Who receives psychiatry-focused pharmacogenomic testing, and is it associated with prescribing patterns and acute care utilisation in depression? Real-world evidence from a large health system
Authors: Lusi Zhang, Anthony J. Tholkes, Kathryn R. Cullen, Meredith L. Gunlicks-Stoessel, Pawel Mroz, Joel F. Farley, Steven G. Johnson, Jeffrey R. Bishop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDepression is highly prevalent, and antidepressant response and tolerability vary widely. Pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing is increasingly used to support antidepressant selection, but its real-world uptake, equity, and associations with prescribing and healthcare utilisation remain incompletely characterised.MethodsUsing electronic health record data from a large health system (2013–2023), we identified 1563 patients with depression who received psychiatry-focused PGx testing and compared them with 248,017 indexed non-PGx controls. We examined demographic and clinical factors associated with receipt of PGx testing, evaluated changes in antidepressant and adjunctive psychiatric medication use pre/post-testing using within-person and difference-in-differences analyses with propensity-score-matched controls. Associations between PGx testing and acute care utilisation, including emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalisations, were examined.FindingsPGx testing was more common among youth and adults with complex clinical histories but less frequent among older adults and racially minoritized groups; Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Asian/Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander patients had 55–65% lower odds of testing than patients identifying as White. Testing clustered among individuals with greater psychiatric comorbidity burden and prior antidepressant trials. Following testing, antidepressant prescribing patterns shifted away from CYP2C19/2D6-sensitive SSRIs and toward agents less dependent on these pathways. Adjunctive anxiolytic and hypnotic prescribing showed modest pre/post declines but these patterns did not significantly differ compared to matched controls. Psychiatric ED visits showed no significant overall difference-in-differences association with PGx testing when compared with matched controls, although exploratory sensitivity analyses suggested a signal of reduced ED utilisation among patients with higher baseline psychiatric complexity. Hospitalisation patterns showed no substantial change.InterpretationIn routine clinical practice, PGx testing is preferentially used in youth and adults with clinically complex histories and is associated with shifts in antidepressant prescribing patterns. Exploratory findings suggest hypothesis-generating signals of reduced psychiatric ED utilisation among patients with higher psychiatric complexity, which requires further confirmation. Observed racial disparities highlight the need for earlier and more equitable implementation. Prospective studies incorporating symptom-level and safety outcomes are needed to determine whether PGx-guided prescribing translates into meaningful clinical benefit.FundingSupported by NIH (UL1TR002494), American Association of Psychiatric Pharmacists Foundation and University of Minnesota College of Pharmacy.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Many individuals with depression do not respond to their first antidepressant medication, and genetic differences can influence medication metabolism, tolerability, and response. Psychiatry-focused pharmacogenomic (PGx) testing is increasingly used to personalise treatment by identifying drug–gene interactions, but its real-world uptake, equity, and associations with prescribing and healthcare utilisation remain incompletely characterised. Prior clinical trials suggest that genetic information may modestly improve outcomes when prescribing is concordant with PGx information. However, these studies were conducted in controlled settings with selective patient populations and structured implementation protocols. We searched major scientific databases from database inception through February 2026 using broad terms related to PGx, mental health, depression, antidepressants, healthcare utilisation, and health equity. There is little real-world evidence describing who receives PGx testing, whether access is equitable across populations, how prescribing patterns change following testing in routine care, or whether testing is associated with downstream healthcare utilisation.

Added value of this study

This study provides one of the largest and most detailed real-world evaluations of psychiatry-focused PGx testing in depression to date. Using ten years of electronic health records from a large U.S. multi-state health system, we characterised demographic and clinical factors associated with receipt of PGx testing and identified persistent disparities by age and race. We further demonstrated that following testing, antidepressant prescribing patterns shifted away from agents with known drug–gene interactions and toward agents less dependent on genetically affected metabolic pathways. Analyses of acute care utilisation showed no significant overall difference-in-differences association between PGx testing and psychiatric emergency department (ED) visits. However, exploratory sensitivity analyses suggested a possible signal of reduced ED utilisation among patients with higher baseline psychiatric complexity. Hospitalisation patterns showed no substantial change. These findings collectively provide a real-world characterisation of how PGx testing is integrated into routine clinical practice beyond controlled trial settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with prior clinical trials and observational studies, our findings suggest that psychiatry-focused PGx testing is associated with meaningful changes in antidepressant prescribing patterns in real-world care. Exploratory findings also suggest a signal of reduced psychiatric ED visits among patients with clinically complex history, although these findings require further confirmation. However, PGx testing remains inequitably utilised, with persistent underuse among older adults and racially minoritized populations. More equitable and systematic implementation, along with earlier integration into care, may help reduce avoidable medication-related complications and acute care encounters. Future prospective studies incorporating symptom-level outcomes, safety measures, and patient- and clinician-level decision-making data will be essential to determine whether PGx-guided prescribing translates into sustained clinical benefit and improved healthcare utilisation across diverse populations.

Introduction

Depression is among the most prevalent mental health disorders, affecting ∼280 million people worldwide.1 It is a leading cause of disability and contributes substantially to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Although antidepressants remain a cornerstone of treatment, response is highly variable, with fewer than 50% of patients responding to first-line agents2 and many discontinuing treatment within 3–12 months due to lack of efficacy or tolerability.3 These modest outcomes underscore the limitations of trial-and-error prescribing and highlight the need for more personalised treatment strategies.

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) offers a promising approach to individualise medication selection and dosing by identifying clinically relevant drug–gene interactions (DGIs).4 Antidepressant selection guidance is a major indication for PGx testing,5,6 as many antidepressants are affected by genetic variations in cytochrome P450 (CYP)2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP2B6 enzymes. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that genetic variation in key drug-metabolising enzymes influences antidepressant plasma exposure and is associated with clinically meaningful differences in treatment response, tolerability, and risk of therapeutic failure.7 Real-world and trial-based studies integrating PGx testing with measured drug levels further support the clinical relevance of PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing.8 These relationships are recognised in U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) labelling9 and supported by evidence-based guidelines from the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC), the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG), and other professional societies.10,11

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses suggest that PGx-guided antidepressant therapy modestly improves depression outcomes.12, 13, 14, 15 These trials have been conducted across North American, European, and East Asian countries and have predominantly evaluated commercial multigene panels that are available for clinician-initiated ordering in routine practice.6,12,13,16, 17, 18 However, most trials were conducted under controlled conditions and may not fully reflect real-world implementation. For example, the Precision Medicine in Mental Health Care (PRIME Care) trial enrolled mostly male veterans (∼75%),13 despite depression being nearly twice as prevalent among females,19 limiting generalisability. Thus, identifying who receives PGx testing in routine depression care and whether access is equitable across demographic and clinical subgroups remains a critical knowledge gap.

Recent large RCTs also indicate that PGx information can influence antidepressant prescribing. In the Genomics Used to Improve Depression Decisions (GUIDED) trial,12 11% of PGx-guided patients (n = 681) changed antidepressants to avoid predicted DGIs, corresponding to greater symptom improvement, response, and remission. In PRIME Care,13 PGx-guided participants (n = 966) were 4.3 times more likely to receive medications without predicted DGIs. However, these trials do not capture how frequently or under what circumstances clinicians act on PGx results in routine practice, where prescribing decisions are shaped by psychiatric comorbidities, concomitant medications, insurance, provider speciality, familiarity with PGx, and perceived clinical utility.20,21 Moreover, evidence linking PGx testing to downstream healthcare utilisation outcomes, such as emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalisations, remains sparse, with only a few recent studies suggesting potential associations between PGx variation or testing and healthcare utilisation patterns.22,23 These outcomes represent clinically meaningful and objectively measurable indicators of treatment instability or adverse effects.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large-scale, longitudinal analysis of psychiatry-focused PGx testing among patients with depression across a U.S. multi-hospital health system. Specifically, we aimed to 1) characterise demographic and clinical factors associated with receipt of PGx testing and assess equity in test utilisation; 2) evaluate changes in antidepressant and other adjunctive psychiatric medication use before and after testing; 3) examine associations between PGx testing and acute care utilisation by assessing ED visits and hospitalisations.

Methods

Data source and setting

This retrospective longitudinal cohort study used de-identified electronic health record (EHR) data from the M Health Fairview system (>10 hospitals and 60+ clinics across Minnesota and Wisconsin). Data were extracted from Epic™ through the University of Minnesota (UMN) Clinical and Translational Science Institute's Best Practices Integrated Informatics Core (BPIC) and analysed in a secure data shelter environment.24

Ethics

The study was approved by the UMN Institutional Review Board (STUDY00016091). The study used de-identified retrospective EHR data, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Study participants and design

Details of PGx report retrieval and data extraction were described previously.25 In brief, PGx-related keywords (e.g., commercial test names, synonyms) were used to identify reports uploaded between January 1, 2013, and May 9, 2023. A total of 3383 patients had a GeneSight® Psychotropic PGx report linked to EHR data. To characterise psychiatric diagnoses, International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes were mapped using the Chronic Condition Indicator Refined (CCIR) and aggregated into broader diagnostic categories through the Clinical Classifications Software Refined (CCSR) framework,26 as described previously.25 Representative ICD-10-CM codes included in the “Depressive Disorders” CCSR category are summarised in Supplementary Table S1.

Patients were included in this study if they met all of the following criteria: 1) a depression diagnosis by the time of PGx testing, 2) no documented bipolar disorder diagnosis, 3) ≥1 antidepressant prescription within six months before or after testing, and 4) ≥1 prescription for any medication in both pre/post-test windows to confirm ongoing engagement in care and ensure complete medication capture. A total of 1563 PGx recipients met all criteria (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PGx cohort selection flow diagram.

PGx cohort selection flow diagram.

A broader clinic comparison cohort with index date mirroring PGx test date was constructed to contextualise test ordering patterns observed in the PGx-tested cohort and support subsequent propensity score matching cohort for comparisons of prescribing and acute care utilisation outcomes (Supplementary Figure S1). This cohort included patients who met the diagnostic inclusion criteria of having depression and no documented bipolar disorder and who had no record of PGx testing during the study period. To align the secular trends of PGx testing and care process, each eligible individual was assigned a pseudo-index date using calendar-year-specific sampling from the empirical distribution of PGx test dates observed in the PGx cohort, thereby mirroring the temporal pattern of testing in routine care. Eligibility for pseudo-index date assignment was restricted to individuals with a documented diagnosis of depression by the indexed calendar year and an age at the pseudo-index date within the range observed in the PGx cohort (6–90 years). Individuals for whom no valid pseudo-index date could be assigned due to inconsistencies in EHR data, timing of diagnosis relative to available PGx test dates, or death prior to diagnosis, were excluded, as detailed in Supplementary Figure S1. The resulting indexed broader clinic cohort consisted of 248,017 PGx-naïve individuals with unipolar depression, each with a valid pseudo-index date defining pre- and post–index observation windows comparable to those used in the PGx-tested cohort.

Propensity-score-matched control cohorts were then generated to mitigate confounding by indications and psychiatric complexity in comparative analyses. Because prescribing outcomes and acute care utilisation represent related but distinct clinical endpoints, two separate propensity score-matched cohorts were constructed: one to support analyses of antidepressant and other psychiatric medication prescribing, and a second to support analyses of acute care utilisation. Separate models were required because the covariate structures most relevant to prescribing trajectories differ from those influencing acute care encounters. Matching was performed separately for adults (aged ≥18 years) and youth (aged 6–17 years) to account for developmental differences in psychiatric presentation and treatment patterns. Propensity scores were estimated using prespecified demographic and clinical covariates (see acute care utilisation outcomes below). For prescribing analyses, models emphasised baseline antidepressant treatment history psychiatric comorbidity burden to align patients at similar points in treatment sequencing. For acute care utilisation analyses, models incorporated factors associated with ED visits and hospitalisations, including baseline psychiatric complexity and related diagnostic categories. Nearest-neighbour matching was applied with three non-PGx control individuals matched to each PGx recipient. Index year was incorporated to align calendar time between groups and minimise bias from secular trends or formulary changes. Balance diagnostics demonstrated good covariate balance between PGx recipients and matched controls across key demographic and clinical characteristics in both matched cohorts (Supplementary Figures S3 and S4). The prescribing-matched cohort was used for antidepressant and other psychiatric medication analyses, whereas the acute care-matched cohort was used for ED and hospitalisation analyses.

Medication outcomes

Primary outcomes were changes in antidepressant use during the six months before versus six months after PGx testing among PGx recipients, and corresponding pre/post–index changes in antidepressant use comparing PGx recipients within propensity score-matched non-PGx control patients. A six-month window, consistent with prior real-world PGx studies,27,28 was selected to capture short-to intermediate-term prescribing changes likely attributable to PGx results, while accommodating variability in clinical follow-up. Prescribing outcomes were derived from outpatient prescription records. Prescriptions discontinued within three days per EHR medication records were excluded to minimise data entry errors. Prescription records for the same antidepressant in each window were grouped as the same trial. Exploratory analyses assessed pre/post changes in non-antidepressant psychiatric medications using the same definitions.

Acute care utilisation outcomes

Acute care utilisation outcomes were defined as ED visits and inpatient hospitalisations occurring during the six months before versus six months after the index date among PGx recipients, and corresponding pre/post–index changes comparing PGx recipients with propensity score-matched non-PGx control patients. A six-month window, consistent with the medication outcome analyses (see section on study participants and design) and prior real-world PGx studies,23,29 was selected to capture short-to intermediate-term changes in healthcare utilisation while accommodating variability in clinical follow-up.

Acute care utilisation outcomes were derived from structured EHR encounter records and evaluated as binary indicators reflecting the occurrence of at least one event within each six-month window. Both psychiatric-specific and all-cause acute care utilisation outcomes were examined. Psychiatric acute care utilisation was defined by ED visits or hospitalisations with a primary diagnosis corresponding to mental, behavioural, or neurodevelopmental disorders (ICD-10-CM F00–F99), suicidal ideation (R45.851), suicide attempt (T14.91), or non-suicidal self-injury or intentional self-harm (R45.88; X60–X84). All-cause acute care utilisation included any ED visit or hospitalisation regardless of diagnosis.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature of factors influencing PGx testing, antidepressant prescribing, and acute care utilisation. All covariates were defined using information available during the baseline period, determined as the interval from the first recorded psychiatric diagnosis or antidepressant prescription up to six months before the index date. The six months immediately preceding the index date were excluded from baseline to prevent overlap with the pre-index analytic exposure window (Supplementary Figure S2).

For within-person pre/post analyses among PGx recipients, models included interaction terms between timepoint (post versus pre) and key covariates, including sex, age, race, and CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 metaboliser status (non-normal versus normal), to evaluate effect modification of prescribing changes following PGx testing. Sex was defined as biological sex at birth as recorded in the EHR. Non-normal metaboliser status was grouped irrespective of direction because the outcome of interest was medication selection or switching rather than dose adjustment, for which both reduced (i.e., intermediate or poor metabolisers) and ultrarapid metabolism may influence prescribing decisions, and phenotype-specific stratification was limited by sparse data and incomplete dose information.

For comparative analyses involving non-PGx controls, two propensity-score-matched cohorts were constructed separately for antidepressant prescribing outcomes and acute care utilisation outcomes to align patients at similar clinical junctures while accounting for outcome-specific confounding structures. Matching used an exact 1:3 ratio (one PGx recipient to three controls) stratified by youth and adult cohorts, yielding 1563 PGx recipients (youth/adults: 192/1371) and 4689 matched controls (576/4113) for both analyses. For antidepressant prescribing analyses, propensity scores were estimated using demographic variables (age, sex, race), index year, baseline number of prior antidepressant trials, baseline psychiatric comorbidity burden, and selected psychiatric conditions for which antidepressants are commonly indicated, including anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), eating disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (Supplementary Figure S3). These covariates were selected to balance baseline treatment trajectory and psychiatric complexity relevant to medication selection, without conditioning on acute care risk variables that may lie on the causal pathway between prescribing and subsequent utilisation outcomes.

For acute care utilisation analyses, similar to the approach for antidepressant prescribing, propensity scores were estimated using age, sex, race, index year, baseline number of prior antidepressant trials, and baseline psychiatric comorbidity burden. Different from the prescribing models, additional matching variables were incorporated to account for psychiatric conditions specifically associated with elevated acute care risk beyond mood disorders, including ASD, substance use disorder (SUD), and psychotic disorders (Supplementary Figure S4).

Covariate balance for propensity-score matching was assessed using absolute standardised mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Across both adult and youth cohorts, propensity score matching substantially improved balance across all included covariates for both prescribing and acute care utilisation analyses (Supplementary Figure S3 and S4).

Statistics

Analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarised descriptively. Associations between sociodemographic predictors of PGx testing, including sex, age, race, insurance status and receipt of PGx testing were first examined using chi-square (X2) tests, followed by multivariable logistic regression models comparing PGx recipients with the indexed broader non-PGx control cohort. Psychiatric clinical characteristics, including individual psychiatric diagnoses, overall psychiatric comorbidity burden (indexed by the total number of psychiatric diagnoses), and prior antidepressant treatment history (indexed by the total number of prior antidepressant trials), were examined in relation to PGx testing status while adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics in multivariable regression models.

Within the PGx cohort, pre/post medication use was compared with McNemar's test for agents with ≥20 users overall and ≥5 discordant pairs, balancing inclusiveness with statistical reliability. Post-hoc analyses stratified by adults versus youth for agents with ≥10 users and ≥5 discordant pairs. Significant results were further examined using conditional logistic regression (CLR; “clogit()” function, “survival” package), specifying within-patient matched pairs (pre- versus post-test use) and modelling interactions between timepoint and relevant covariates.

To compare differences in pre/post–index antidepressant and other psychiatric medication prescribing between PGx recipients and propensity-score matched non-PGx cohorts, difference-in-differences analyses were conducted using generalised estimating equations (GEE)30 implemented with the “geepack” package.31 These models accounted for within-patient correlation and adjusted for prespecified covariates, including index year. To evaluate the robustness of drug-specific prescribing estimates and to identify results susceptible to sparse data bias, stability of estimates was assessed a priori for both CLR and GEE models.32,33 Estimates were classified as unstable if the 95% confidence interval (CI) span ratio (upper bound divided by lower bound) was ≥ 20 or if point estimates were extreme (odds ratio > 10 or < 0.1). These criteria were chosen empirically to flag estimates with extreme imprecision or potential instability due to sparse data while retaining interpretable estimates for description comparison. Estimates not meeting these criteria were considered relatively stable in the sense that they were not dominated by extreme statistical instability; however, they may still have wide CIs and should not be interpreted as highly precise. Unstable estimates are presented for completeness but interpreted cautiously, with primary inferences emphasising directionality and consistency across analytic approaches rather than precise effect size magnitude. Sensitivity difference-in-differences analyses were additionally conducted in a subgroup of patients, both PGx recipients and matched non-PGx controls, meeting a standard definition of treatment-resistant depression (TRD; ≥2 prior antidepressant trials) to further distinguish prescribing patterns temporally associated with PGx testing from broader secular or formulary-driven trends.

Similarly, acute care utilisation outcomes were analysed using parallel analytic approaches. Within the PGx cohort, pre/post–index changes in acute care utilisation (ED visits and hospitalisations) were compared using McNemar's tests and GEEs accounting for timepoint–covariate interactions. For comparative analyses with non-PGx controls, differences in pre/post–index acute care utilisation between PGx recipients and propensity score-matched non-PGx cohorts were evaluated using difference-in-differences models implemented with GEE specifying a binomial distribution with a logit link function and an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated measures within individuals. Models adjusted for relevant covariates as described above, including index year. Post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine whether effect size magnitude varied by psychiatric complexity, defined by cumulative thresholds in the number of baseline psychiatric comorbidities (≥2 through ≥6). These analyses were performed to assess heterogeneity of associations across increasing levels of clinical complexity.

Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05, with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment for multiple comparisons as appropriate.

Role of funders

The funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report. The corresponding author had full access to all data in the study and had final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Participants characteristics

Of 1563 PGx recipients (Table 1), 1371 (87.7%) were adults and 192 (12.3%) were youth. Mean age was 43.8 years in adults and 14.3 years in youth. Most were female (70.7% adults; 64.1% youth) and White (91.7% adults; 84.4% youth). Nearly all (96.0%) had active insurance, most commonly commercial, followed by Medicare/Medicaid. Anxiety disorders were present in ∼90% of both age groups; other frequent conditions included substance use disorders (SUD) in adults (33.8%), ADHD in youth (40.6%), and PTSD in both (17.4% adults; 15.1% youth). Demographic and clinical predictors of receiving PGx testing

PGx recipients differed significantly from the indexed broader clinic non-PGx cohort in sex, age, race, and insurance (all X2 test, all p < 0.01; Supplementary Figure S5). In multivariable logistic regression (Fig. 2A), females were more likely than males to receive testing (OR 1.29; 95% CI, 1.16–1.44; p < 0.001 (Wald test)). Psychiatric comorbidities were strong predictors of testing (Fig. 2B). Adjusting for demographics, anxiety disorders showed the greatest odds (OR 3.96; 95% CI, 3.38–4.67; p < 0.001 (Wald test)). Elevated odds were also observed for OCD, eating disorders, PTSD, ADHD, ASD, and psychotic disorders (ORs 1.5–2.1; all p < 0.05), while SUD showed no association. Psychiatric comorbidity burden demonstrated a dose–response pattern (Fig. 2C), with odds increasing from 2.86 (95% CI, 2.22–3.75; p < 0.001) with two comorbidities to 8.32 (6.42–10.94; p < 0.001) with ≥4, compared with having depression alone. Prior antidepressant treatment burden (Fig. 2D) was also highly predictive. Relative to patients with no or one prior antidepressant trial, odds of PGx testing rose from 3.09 (95% CI, 2.65–3.61) with two trials to 28.28 (24.08–33.22) with ≥5 trials (all p < 0.001).

Adjunct non-antidepressant psychiatric medication use before and after PGx testing

Exploratory analyses of non-antidepressant psychiatric medications showed modest pre/post changes following PGx testing among PGx recipients (Supplementary Figure S10). After FDR correction, significant post-test reductions were observed for zolpidem (0.73-fold) and hydroxyzine (0.79-fold). Nominal reductions were also observed for aripiprazole, alprazolam, lorazepam, and gabapentin (fold changes 0.74–0.89; unadjusted p < 0.05; McNemar's test), although these did not remain significant after FDR adjustment. These patterns were driven primarily by adults, with limited use of non-antidepressant psychiatric medications observed among youth (Supplementary Table S6). CLR models accounting for demographic factors and PGx metaboliser-by-time interactions (Supplementary Table S7) did not identify significant overall pre/post changes for most agents but suggested significant timepoint-by-CYP2D6 interactions for gabapentin and with signals of timepoint-by-CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 interactions for lorazepam. Difference-in-differences analyses comparing PGx recipients with propensity score-matched non-PGx controls did not identify statistically significant differential changes for any non-antidepressant psychiatric medications, suggesting that broader secular or clinical trends cannot be ruled out as contributors to the pre/post prescribing changes observed within the PGx cohort.

Discussion

This study provides a large-scale real-world evaluation that jointly characterises sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with receipt of psychiatry-focused PGx testing, prescribing patterns following testing, and downstream acute care utilisation among patients with depression within a large integrated health system. PGx testing was preferentially used in patients with greater psychiatric complexity and more extensive antidepressant treatment histories, and was substantially more common among youth while less frequent among older adults and racially minoritized populations, highlighting important equity gaps in access. Receipt of PGx testing was associated with changes in prescribing patterns, including increased use of antidepressants less dependent on CYP2D6 or CYP2C19 and decreased use of SSRIs with established PGx considerations, particularly citalopram/escitalopram (see Supplementary Table S8 for summary of clinically relevant antidepressant DGIs). In parallel, we observed changes in acute care utilisation patterns particularly reductions in psychiatric ED visits following testing, with difference-in-differences analyses suggesting greater relative reductions among PGx recipients compared with matched non-PGx controls, especially in patients with higher baseline psychiatric complexity. Together, these findings contextualise PGx-associated prescribing changes within broader patterns of real-world healthcare utilisation.

Although psychiatry-focused PGx testing is increasingly available, real-world data on who receives testing remain limited. Most prior reports originate from academic or integrated systems with dedicated PGx programmes, which may not reflect community practice where tests are often ordered from external laboratories at individual clinician discretion.34,35 Our earlier work revealed that nearly half of tests were ordered by non-psychiatric clinicians and over one-third of the results were imported from outside institutions, underscoring decentralised adoption across care settings.25 These patterns suggest that PGx adoption may vary across clinical contexts and commercial testing platforms, with differences in access, ordering workflows, and institutional oversight potentially shaping utilisation.

PGx testing was less likely among older adults but more likely among youth. Lower uptake in older adults, consistent with prior evidence,36 likely reflects uncertainty about later-life benefit, reimbursement limitations, and knowledge gaps.37 Yet expanding PGx use in geriatric populations remains a growing opportunity,38 given CPIC coverage of nearly 170 medications across multiple therapeutic domains39 and the potential to inform prescribing in multimorbid, polypharmacy contexts.25

In contrast, greater testing among younger patients aligns with increasing interest in PGx-guided youth mental-health care40 and the reality that one-quarter of first depressive episodes occur during adolescence,41 often portending worse long-term outcomes.42 The higher uptake of PGx testing in youth may also reflect a safety-oriented and precautionary approach to prescribing. Clinicians and families may be particularly motivated to minimise avoidable adverse effects and reduce trial-and-error medication exposure in children and adolescents, who may have difficulty recognising or reliably reporting side effects and tolerability concerns. In this context, PGx testing may be viewed as a pragmatic tool to inform initial medication selection and reduce unnecessary risk, even in the absence of definitive paediatric outcome trials. Implementation efforts are expanding within paediatric-focused care settings, including psychiatry,43 with accumulating evidence supporting PGx implications for certain antidepressants,44,45 although professional societies such as the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry continue to advise caution and call for transparent, prospective evidence to better establish clinical validity and utility.46

Patients identifying as Black/African American, AIAN, or Asian/NHPI were substantially less likely to receive PGx testing than patients identifying as White, even after adjusting for insurance, indicating inequities beyond coverage alone. Notably, non-White individuals comprised a smaller proportion of PGx test recipients (9.2%) than expected based on Minnesota's underlying population demographics, where residents of colour represent approximately 23–24% of the total population and more than one-third of children.47 These gaps mirror persistent underrepresentation of non-White populations in PGx implementation and discovery research.48, 49, 50 Structural barriers, including fragmented access, affordability constraints, and limited continuity of care, likely contribute, alongside provider-level factors such as implicit bias, limited familiarity, and lower confidence in interpreting results for non-European ancestry patients.51,52 Additionally, many commercial PGx panels rely heavily on European-ancestry reference data, which may reduce perceived accuracy or relevance in diverse populations.53 Encouragingly, coordinated implementation programmes can help mitigate such gaps; for instance, the Veterans Health Administration's nationwide Pharmacogenomics Testing for Veterans initiative reported higher PGx uptake among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic patients, likely driven by centralised infrastructure and elimination of cost barriers.36

PGx testing was most frequent among patients with multiple psychiatric comorbidities, especially anxiety spectrum disorders, including generalised anxiety, eating disorders, OCD, and PTSD. Smaller but significant associations were seen with ADHD, ASD, and psychotic disorders. Clinicians may be more likely to order testing for patients with depression complicated by overlapping mood or anxiety conditions, where pharmacologic management is challenging. Although antidepressants are indicated for anxiety-related conditions, comorbid anxiety is linked with heightened sensitivity to dose-related side effects (e.g., early SSRI-induced agitation) and worse outcomes,54 for which PGx information may help anticipate DGI-related adverse effects and expedite selection of tolerable regimens.

We also observed clear dose-response relationships of PGx testing and both comorbidity and prior antidepressant trials, suggesting clinicians often reserve testing for complex, treatment-resistant cases. This aligns with previous evidence that nonresponse, multiple failures, and adverse-effect burden are key triggers for testing.53,55 However, reliance on late-course testing represents a missed opportunity, whereby earlier integration could prevent repeated exposure to ineffective therapies and shorten the path to remission.56

Across analytic approaches, antidepressant prescribing patterns following PGx testing shifted away from several commonly used SSRIs with established PGx considerations and toward alternative agents less dependent on CYP2D6 or CYP2C19 metabolism. Within-person pre/post analyses demonstrated large relative increases in desvenlafaxine, vilazodone, and levomilnacipran use, alongside consistent declines in citalopram, escitalopram, sertraline, and fluoxetine. These patterns were directionally consistent in difference-in-differences analyses comparing PGx recipients with matched non-PGx controls accounting for baseline antidepressant treatment history, as well as in sensitivity analyses restricted to the TRD subcohort, supporting an association beyond secular prescribing trends.

For agents with sufficient data and stable effect estimates, PGx testing was associated with reduced use of CYP2C19- and CYP2D6-sensitive SSRIs, including citalopram, escitalopram, and fluoxetine, and increased use of mirtazapine and venlafaxine. In contrast, several newer or less frequently prescribed antidepressants, such as desvenlafaxine, levomilnacipran, and vilazodone, showed large point estimates but wide CIs and were classified as unstable due to sparse data. Accordingly, these findings are interpreted cautiously and viewed as descriptive signals rather than precise estimates of effect magnitude.

CLR models further suggested that some SSRI prescribing changes were modified by metaboliser status. Declines in escitalopram use were concentrated among CYP2C19 non-normal metabolisers, consistent with CPIC and DPWG recommendations to consider alternative therapy or dose modification for poor and ultrarapid metabolisers, as well as modified maintenance dosing for rapid and intermediate metabolisers.10,11 These findings support real-world translation of pharmacokinetic (PK) PGx guidance, albeit with heterogeneity across agents. In contrast, antidepressants that increased following testing are largely considered “PGx-neutral” from a PK perspective. Desvenlafaxine undergoes minimal CYP-mediated metabolism, while vilazodone and levomilnacipran are primarily metabolised via CYP3A4, for which no actionable antidepressant PGx guidance currently exists. Descriptive analyses showing enrichment of non-normal metabolisers among initiators of some of these agents suggest that clinicians may preferentially select alternatives perceived as less susceptible to DGIs, even in the absence of formal guideline recommendations.

Beyond PK markers, many commercial psychiatry-focused panels incorporate pharmacodynamic (PD) genes, such as SLC6A4 (serotonin transporter) and HTR2A (serotonin-2A receptor).57 Although CPIC currently finds insufficient evidence to support clinical actionability for these variants,10 they are frequently embedded within proprietary algorithms that may negatively influence SSRI selection. The observed reduction in SSRI use may therefore reflect a combination of PK guidance and algorithm-driven PD interpretation. This consideration is particularly salient in youth, where the strongest efficacy and safety evidence supports a limited number of SSRIs, including fluoxetine, escitalopram, and sertraline.58 PGx-driven avoidance of these agents could inadvertently reduce evidence-based use of effective agents which underscores the need for careful interpretation and continued evaluation of PGx utility in paediatric populations.46 Taken together, these prescribing shifts are most consistent with clinician responses aimed at mitigating PK safety concerns and avoiding predicted DGIs, while the extent to which such prescribing shifts improve antidepressant efficacy remains unclear.

Overall, several antidepressants that increased following PGx testing are newer or typically used later in the treatment course, suggesting that these shifts may reflect escalation after insufficient response to prior treatments in a clinically complex population, with PGx results serving as one contributing factor rather than the sole driver. For some agents, large relative increases were observed, although effect size estimates were imprecise due to low baseline use and sparse data. Prescribing of these medications may also be further shaped by insurance coverage, prior authorisation, and off-label restrictions, particularly in paediatrics.59 Thus, while PGx provides mechanistic insights to refine prescribing, it remains just one element within a multifactorial clinical process balancing effectiveness, tolerability, and accessibility.34

Exploratory analyses showed modest pre/post changes in adjunctive anxiolytic and hypnotic use following PGx testing, including reductions in hydroxyzine and zolpidem and nominal decreases in benzodiazepines and gabapentin. Use of certain augmentation agents, including aripiprazole, also showed a declining trend following testing within the PGx cohort. These patterns may reflect either reduced need for pharmacologic augmentation as antidepressant selection is optimised or clinician attention to CYP2D6-related information included in psychiatry-focused PGx reports. However, these changes were not statistically significant in difference-in-differences analyses comparing PGx recipients with propensity score-matched non-PGx controls, suggesting that broader secular trends, regression to the mean, natural disease course, or concurrent non-pharmacologic interventions may contribute to the observed patterns. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive prescribing associations rather than unequivocal evidence of improved anxiety or sleep outcomes.

Although reduced use of sedating or dependence-prone agents is consistent with guideline recommendations to minimise polypharmacy and enhance medication safety,60,61 symptom-level data were not available to assess clinical benefit. Future studies incorporating patient-reported outcomes and validated symptom measures will be necessary to determine whether such prescribing changes translate into meaningful clinical improvements beyond antidepressant selection.

Although this study did not directly evaluate treatment response or safety outcomes, the observed prescribing shifts may have downstream clinical relevance consistent with prior PGx RCTs and real-world studies. In the GUIDED trial, patients whose prescribing aligned with PGx recommendations to address baseline DGIs achieved ∼1.5-fold higher remission than the overall PGx-tested group.12,62 In the PRIME Care trial, PGx-tested patients with DGIs addressed showed greater improvements and faster response and remission than those who received usual care.56 Similarly, a large real-world analysis of ∼17,000 adults with depression found that resolving DGIs after testing was associated with 29% fewer psychiatric hospitalisations and 39% fewer ED visits.23

Importantly, these studies, as well as the present analysis, used multigene, combinatorial PGx panels rather than single-gene tests, supporting the premise that integrating information across multiple pharmacogenes and medications may better capture clinically relevant variability in antidepressant response and tolerability. Distinct from many prior studies conducted under controlled trial protocols or within centralised PGx implementation programmes, PGx testing in our health system was ordered through routine clinical care, often from external commercial laboratories long before the establishment of an in-house workflow.63 This pragmatic setting captures clinician responses to psychiatry-focused PGx results under real-world conditions, where report formats, clinical decision support (CDS), and follow-up vary and prescribing decisions are shaped by competing clinical and system-level factors. Taken together, these findings suggest that PGx-guided identification and avoidance of DGIs may support improved tolerability and safety in depression treatment, while the extent to which such prescribing changes translate into sustained clinical benefit warrants further investigation.

To complement prescribing analyses, we evaluated acute care utilisation as an objective outcome captured in the EHR. Within the PGx cohort, psychiatric ED visits declined by more than 38% after testing, and difference-in-differences analyses suggested a trend of larger relative reductions among PGx recipients than matched non-PGx controls, most notably in patients with higher baseline psychiatric complexity. By contrast, we did not observe clear differential changes in hospitalisations, suggesting that any PGx-associated signal may be more evident for lower-threshold events such as ED visits. Hospitalisations were relatively infrequent overall, and absolute pre/post differences were modest, limiting statistical power to detect changes.

The absence of a clear hospitalisation signal differs from some claims-based studies reporting fewer psychiatric hospitalisations after PGx testing, particularly when prescribing became more concordant with PGx results.23 Similarly, Perlis and colleagues, using administrative claims data, observed reductions in all-cause ED visits and hospitalisations following PGx testing.29 Differences in data sources and outcome ascertainment may partly explain this discrepancy. Because our analyses relied on encounter data within a single health system, ED visits and hospitalisations occurring elsewhere, including admissions to specialised inpatient or day treatment programmes, may be missed, which would attenuate associations, especially for hospitalisation outcomes.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. Acute care utilisation is influenced by many clinical and contextual factors, including regression to the mean, changes in care engagement, and concurrent non-pharmacologic interventions. However, the consistency across analytic approaches and the graded pattern by psychiatric complexity support the plausibility of true associations between PGx-related prescribing changes and downstream patterns of acute care use in clinically complex patients with depression.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this study was conducted within a single, albeit large, Midwestern U.S. health system serving a predominantly European-ancestry population, which may limit generalisability to other geographic regions or more diverse care settings. Moreover, models of PGx testing, EHR integration, and CDS vary substantially across health systems and countries; therefore, prescribing responses observed in this pragmatic U.S. setting, where PGx testing was ordered through routine clinical care and often sourced from external commercial laboratories, may not directly translate to settings with centralised or protocol-driven PGx programmes. Multisite studies across health systems serving more heterogeneous populations are needed to better characterise variation in PGx utilisation, prescribing patterns, and downstream outcomes. Second, as with all observational EHR-based studies, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Although we employed within-person pre/post comparisons, propensity score matching, and difference-in-differences analyses to mitigate confounding by indication and secular trends, unmeasured factors, such as patient preferences, clinician familiarity with PGx, perceived utility, and shared decision-making, may still influence prescribing behaviour and are not captured in structured EHR data. Third, medication exposure was ascertained from prescription orders rather than dispensing or adherence data, and prescriptions written or filled outside the health system may not have been fully captured, introducing potential misclassification. Similarly, acute care utilisation occurring outside the health system, including admissions to specialised inpatient or day treatment programmes, may be under ascertained. Finally, this study focused on prescribing and healthcare utilisation patterns and did not directly assess clinical outcomes such as symptom improvement, remission, or adverse effects. Prospective studies linking PGx-guided prescribing decisions to patient-reported outcomes, safety measures, and longer-term clinical trajectories will be essential to more fully define the clinical utility of psychiatric PGx testing, particularly across age groups and levels of clinical complexity.

To our knowledge, this study provides one of the largest and most detailed real-world evaluations of psychiatry-focused PGx testing for antidepressant selection to date, characterising both patient populations who receive testing and subsequent changes in prescribing and healthcare utilisation in routine clinical practice. PGx testing was more common among youth and clinically complex adults but less frequent among older adults and racially minoritized groups, highlighting persistent inequities in access and/or utilisation. Following testing, antidepressant prescribing shifted away from several commonly used SSRIs with CYP2D6, CYP2C19, or CYP2B6 considerations and toward agents less dependent on these metabolic pathways. Exploratory analyses suggested modest pre/post changes in adjunctive anxiolytic and hypnotic prescribing, although no differential changes were observed relative to matched controls. Psychiatric acute care utilisation showed no significant overall change associated with PGx testing relative to controls, although exploratory sensitivity analyses suggested a possible signal of reduced psychiatric ED visits among patients with higher baseline psychiatric complexity, while hospitalisation patterns showed limited change. These findings collectively describe how PGx testing is currently integrated into real-world depression care and underscore the need for earlier, more equitable, and more systematic implementation. Prospective studies incorporating symptom-level and safety outcomes will be essential to determine whether PGx-guided prescribing translates into meaningful clinical benefit, particularly among youth and other clinically complex populations.
