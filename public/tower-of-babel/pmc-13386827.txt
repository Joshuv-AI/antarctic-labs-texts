Development of a new TaqMan real-time PCR for detecting Psittacine adenovirus 1 (PsAdV-1)
Authors: Laura Andrea Charola-Ramos, Nira Vega-Pita, María José Bernal-Guadarrama, Rocío Quilez-Delgado, Ana Rodríguez-Hernández, Richard Heidrich, Nuhacet Fernández-Gallardo, Francesco Grande, Marcia Weinzettl, María Elena Figueroa-Lampo, Rafael Zamora-Padrón, Enrique Martínez-Carretero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsittacine adenoviruses belong to the Adenoviridae family, that includes genera Aviadenovirus, Barthadenovirus, and Siadenovirus and may cause either fatal disease or subclinical infections. Subclinical infections are particularly relevant when infected birds enter the bird trade, as infected birds can act as sources of viral spread. Given the ease of transmission of these viruses, the availability of sensitive and specific diagnostic tools is essential. The purpose of this study was to establish a TaqMan-based quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay for the detection and quantification of Psittacine adenovirus 1 (PsAdV-1) in psittacine samples.ResultsSerial dilutions of a control plasmid and clinical samples were analysed using conventional PCR, SYBR Green qPCR and the newly developed TaqMan qPCR. A psittacine-specific 18 S rRNA TaqMan assay was used as an endogenous control to assess DNA extraction quality and sample suitability, particularly in cloacal swabs. All three methods showed comparable sensitivity when applied to liver samples. However, the TaqMan assay demonstrated improved detection in cloacal swabs compared to conventional PCR, while maintaining high specificity and showing no cross-reactivity with other avian pathogens.ConclusionsThe TaqMan-based qPCR assay developed in this study provides a rapid, sensitive and highly specific method for the detection of PsAdV-1 in clinical samples. Its improved performance in cloacal swabs makes it a valuable tool for routine diagnosis and epidemiological surveillance of psittacine adenovirus infections, particularly in asymptomatic birds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05571-z.

Background

Adenoviruses represent a broad and varied group of non-enveloped viruses that infect a wide range of hosts, including avian species. Their genome consists of double-stranded DNA, typically ranging from 26 to 45 kbp, and viral particles measure between 70 and 90 nanometres in diameter [1, 2]. These viruses are classified into the family Adenoviridae, which is currently divided into six genera: Mastadenovirus, Aviadenovirus, Barthadenovirus (formerly Atadenovirus), Siadenovirus, Ichtadenovirus, and Testadenovirus. Psittacine adenoviruses (PsAdVs) are found across three of these genera: Aviadenovirus, Barthadenovirus, and Siadenovirus [3, 4].

PsAdVs show considerable genetic diversity. PsAdV-1 is classified within the genus Aviadenovirus, together with other avian adenoviruses, including psittacine adenoviruses such as PsAdV-4 and PsAdV-12, whereas other PsAdVs belong to distinct genera and show substantial sequence divergence [4, 5]. This genetic variability highlights the importance of developing type-specific diagnostic assays.

Structurally, adenoviruses possess an icosahedral capsid composed of major structural proteins, including the hexon and penton base, as well as fibre proteins involved in host cell attachment [1, 2]. In psittacine adenoviruses belonging to the genus Aviadenovirus, a distinctive feature is the presence of two fibre proteins per penton base, which are considered important determinants of viral attachment and host specificity [2, 6, 7].

The hexon and fibre proteins are commonly used as molecular targets for adenovirus detection and classification, as they contain conserved and variable regions enabling both broad detection and species-level discrimination [1].

PsAdV infections may range from asymptomatic to fatal disease. Subclinical infections can become clinically apparent under conditions of stress or following co-infection with other pathogens, like Beak and Feather Disease Virus (BFDV), Chlamydia psittaci, or Pseudomonas aeruginosa, among others [3, 8, 9]. When present, clinical signs are non-specific and include lethargy, anorexia, and diarrhoea [10–12]. In addition, psittacine adenoviruses have been associated with hepatitis, conjunctivitis, pneumonia, enteritis, splenitis, pancreatitis and nephritis, as well as multisystemic necrosis in severe cases [8, 9].

Transmission may occur both vertically, through infected eggs, and horizontally, most commonly via faecal contamination, even between birds housed in separate cages [13]. In the context of the global bird trade, this high transmission potential underlines the need for robust diagnostic tools and effective quarantine strategies [8].

Despite this, few studies have focused specifically on PsAdV-1. One Australian survey analysing 118 droppings from captive psittacines detected PsAdV-1 in only 3% of samples [14]. In contrast, several studies have investigated the prevalence of pan-adenovirus in psittacine populations, reporting highly variable detection rates. These range from 76% in a breeding facility in Italy [15], to 16% in captive birds in Slovenia [16], and 2% in China, including both wild birds and breeding facilities [17]. In Australia, where the most extensive research has been conducted, reported prevalence ranges from 2% in wild birds [8] to 76% in a specific breeding institution [9], with other studies generally falling within this range [18, 19]. Interestingly, one recent Australian study analysing 426 cockatoo chicks found no evidence of adenovirus infection [20]. This variability likely reflects differences in geographic origin, sample type and bird population, but overall highlights the increasing frequency with which adenoviruses are detected in psittacine birds and the need for reliable, rapid diagnostic methods to limit their spread.

However, although pan-adenovirus PCR assays have been widely used for broad detection across different species, they typically rely on highly degenerate primers to accommodate the genetic diversity among adenoviruses. This can reduce sensitivity and specificity for individual adenovirus types. This limitation is particularly relevant for psittacine adenoviruses, which are phylogenetically diverse, making targeted, species-specific approaches more suitable for accurate detection.

A variety of diagnostic methods have been developed for the detection of PsAdVs, including virus isolation, electron microscopy, DNA in situ hybridization, immunohistochemistry, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [21, 22]. Although these methods are generally effective, many are expensive, labour-intensive, time-consuming or require access to specialised laboratory equipment and expertise. PCR has emerged as a versatile and widely used approach, particularly when combined with techniques such as sequencing, restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), high-resolution melting (HRM), or quantitative PCR (qPCR).

Real-time PCR is rapid, sensitive, and allows viral DNA quantification without post-PCR processing steps. This approach has been successfully applied to the detection of a range of avian adenoviruses [23–27], as well as psittacine-specific adenoviruses, including PsAdV-1 [28, 29] and PsAdV-3 [12].

Currently, both published PsAdV-1 real-time PCR assays are SYBR Green-based. However, SYBR Green binds indiscriminately to any double-stranded DNA, which can lead to false-positive results and requires careful melting-curve analysis to confirm specificity.

To address these limitations, the present study aimed to develop and validate a TaqMan-based quantitative PCR assay for the accurate detection and quantification of Psittacine adenovirus 1 (PsAdV-1). Specifically, the objectives were: (i) to evaluate the analytical sensitivity and specificity of the TaqMan assay in comparison with conventional PCR and SYBR Green-based real-time PCR; and (ii) to assess its performance in clinical samples, including liver tissues and cloacal swabs from psittacine birds. By directly comparing these diagnostic approaches, this study seeks to determine whether a probe-based qPCR system provides practical advantages for routine diagnosis and epidemiological surveillance of PsAdV-1.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a descriptive and analytical methodological study aimed at the development and validation of a TaqMan-based real-time PCR assay for the detection of PsAdV-1 in psittacine samples. Analytical validation was performed using plasmid standards, followed by clinical validation using liver tissues and cloacal swabs obtained through routine veterinary diagnostic procedures in psittacine birds. Sample processing and molecular analyses were conducted at the Universidad de La Laguna.

Clinical samples

Thirty-four liver samples from psittacine birds of different species, that died from natural causes with clinical suspicion of adenovirus infection, were collected by Loro Parque Fundación for diagnostic purposes. In addition, 254 cloacal swabs were obtained as part of routine veterinary diagnostic procedures. These samples were transported to our laboratory on ice and were stored at -20°C until processing. No animals were euthanised or experimentally infected for the purposes of this study.

Sample collection complied with European (Directive 2010/63/UE) and Spanish legislation (RD 53/2013) on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

No sample size calculation was performed, as this was an observational diagnostic study based on the availability of clinical samples collected during routine veterinary surveillance. No randomisation or blinding procedures were applied, as no experimental groups or interventions were defined.

DNA extraction

Different extraction protocols were used according to the sample type. Viral DNA extraction from liver was performed using Maxwell® 16 Viral Total Nucleic Acid Purification Kit (Promega). Briefly, 100 mg of each sample was homogenised in 200 µl of lysis buffer (containing 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 25 mM EDTA pH 8 and 0.5% SDS) using the TissueRuptor II® (Qiagen), incubated at 65°C with Proteinase K, and transferred to the kit cartridges for DNA extraction. Although this kit is primarily designed for fluid samples, it was successfully applied to tissue homogenates in similar contexts for viral detection. These samples were diluted to 10 ng/µl in DEPC-treated water and analysed for adenovirus DNA.

For cloacal swabs, DNA was extracted using Maxwell® 16 Mouse Tail DNA Purification Kit (Promega). Similarly, the swabs were incubated with 200 µl of lysis buffer, followed by incubation at 65°C with Proteinase K, and then transferred to the kit cartridges. This kit was selected to improve DNA recovery and reduce the impact of potential PCR inhibitors from complex matrices, as cloacal swabs are commonly associated with lower viral loads and a higher presence of inhibitory substances.

Conventional PCR

Conventional PCR (cPCR) assays were performed employing the primer pairs previously described: Hex L1-s/Hex L1-as 1 and qPAV-1/qPAV-2 [28], amplifying a product of approximately 590 and 146 base pairs, respectively. Both primer pairs amplify a region within the loop 1 (L1) of the PsAdV-1 hexon gene.

Each reaction was set up in a final volume of 25 µl, including 5 µl of DNA, 0.4 µM of each primer, 250 µM of each dNTP and 0.625 U of Taq DNA polymerase.

PCR conditions for the Hex L1-s/Hex L1-as primers were as previously described [1]. The qPAV-1/qPAV-2 primers, originally described for SYBR Green real-time PCR, were used here in conventional PCR with optimised thermal cycling conditions. The final protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 45 seconds, annealing at 67°C for 30 seconds and elongation at 72°C for 30 seconds, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The amplification products were separated by electrophoresis in a 1.5% agarose gel, stained with REALSAFE® Nucleic Acid Staining Solution (Real Laboratory) and visualized under UV transillumination.

For PCR product cloning, the Hex L1-s/Hex L1-as primers were modified to include BamHI and HindIII restriction sites to facilitate subsequent cloning: Hex L1-s-RE: 5’-GGATCCATGGGAGCSACCTAYTTCGACAT-3’ and Hex L1-as-RE: 5’-AAGCTTAAATTGTCCCKRAANCCGATGTA-3’. The cyclic conditions consisted of 5 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s and elongation at 72 °C for 45 s, and 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 62 °C for 30 s and elongation at 72°C for 45 seconds.

Generation of standard and control vector

The Hex-L1-s-RE/Hex-L1-as-RE PCR product of an adenovirus positive liver DNA was cloned into pGEM-T® Vector (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This fragment encompasses the region amplified by the qPAV-1/qPAV-2 primers. After the PCR screening of transformants, the positive colony was grown, and plasmid DNA was isolated using the alkaline lysis method and subsequently sequenced.

Plasmid DNA was linearized using the SalI restriction enzyme (Promega) and gel purified with the MicroElute Gel Extraction Kit (Omega bio-tek) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SYBR Green real-time PCR

For SYBR Green real-time PCR, the qPAV-1/qPAV-2 primers were used [28]. Each reaction was set up in a total volume of 10 µl, including 250 nM of each primer, Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) at 1X, and 2 µl of DNA template.

Amplification was carried out on the StepOne System (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: polymerase activation at 95 °C for 10 min, and 30 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 min. After the real-time PCR run, a melt curve was generated to detect nonspecific amplifications. Although the assay was originally performed at an annealing temperature of 56°C [28], in our study, the temperature was adjusted to 60°C to minimise the occurrence of false positives results.

According to the results in liver samples, where all SYBR-positive livers amplified before a quantification cycle (Cq) of 28 (latest positive at Cq 19), a threshold was defined for cloacal swabs. Accordingly, SYBR results were considered positive only when Cq was under 28 and a single melt peak was observed at a temperature consistent with the positive-control peak on the same plate. Signals with Cq greater than 28, with multiple melting peaks or with a melting temperature not compatible with the controls were considered negative. This conservative threshold was selected to minimise false-positive results, as amplification signals at higher Cq values in cloacal swabs were associated with non-specific melting profiles.

TaqMan real-time PCR

To improve the specificity of the real-time PCR assay, a TaqMan probe was designed in our laboratory, based on the PsAdV-1 isolate 18VIR149_ITA_2018 (GenBank accession number MH580295.1) previously characterised [10]. The TaqMan probe sequence was: VIC-5’- CCAACTTTTCTGGCATAAATCCCAATGTCAATTCAGGACCCG-3’-TAMRA.

Primers and probe concentrations used in the reaction were previously optimized. Each reaction was set up in a total volume of 10 µl, including 250 nM of primer qPAV-1, 100 nM of primer qPAV-2, 50 nM of probe, TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) at 1X, and 2 µl of DNA template.

Amplification was carried out on the StepOne System, with UNG activation at 50 °C for 2 min, polymerase activation at 95°C for 10 min, and 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute.

Samples were considered positive when a clear amplification signal was detected within 30 cycles, based on the amplification profiles of positive samples and the absence of amplification in negative controls within this range.

Standard curve and sensitivity of PCR

To generate the standard curve and assess sensitivity, linearized pGEM-HexL1 DNA was diluted to 2.34 ng/µl and ten tenfold serial dilutions were performed using DEPC-treated water (MP Biomedicals). This starting concentration corresponded to the DNA yield obtained after plasmid purification from agarose gel. In the assays of real-time PCR, the dilutions were tested in triplicate and a standard curve was generated by plotting the logarithmic plasmid copy number per reaction against the Cq. Clinical samples were analysed in duplicate.

To imitate real conditions and analyse the influence of mixed host DNA, the standard curve was also performed with adenovirus-negative DNA pool at 17.8 ng/µl as diluent, representing the DNA concentration obtained in the pooled sample.

Repeatability and reproducibility of the TaqMan real-time PCR

To perform the intra-assay variability (repeatability) two positive samples (C + 1 and C + 2) were analysed tenfold at the same time. The inter-assay variability (reproducibility) was performed with two positive samples (C + 1 and C + 2) and two negative samples (C-1 and C-2), with ten measurements at ten different times. The samples were analysed and the mean (x̅), standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) were determined to assess both intra-assay and inter-assay variability.

Specificity of TaqMan real-time PCR

To evaluate the assay’s specificity, DNA samples from other psittacine DNA viruses, including avian polyomavirus (APV), psittacine beak and feather disease virus (PBFDV), and two distinct strains of psittacine herpesvirus (PsHV), were tested using the TaqMan real-time PCR. These samples were kindly donated by the Loro Parque collection.

DNA sequencing

PCR products generated with the Hex-L1-s/Hex-L1-as primers were sequenced by the SEGAI Genomics Service at the Universidad de La Laguna using the same primers. Obtained sequences were initially analysed using BLAST to confirm their identity. Subsequently, sequences were aligned using a multiple sequence alignment approach, and pairwise nucleotide identity was calculated using the Sequence Demarcation Tool (SDT v1.3).

Endogenous control: psittacine 18 S rRNA assay

To verify DNA extraction quality and suitability for PsAdV-1 testing, a TaqMan assay targeting the psittacine 18S gene was designed and used as an endogenous control.

The primers 18Sdir/18Sinv were previously described [29]. The TaqMan probe was designed in our laboratory based on published sequences from several psittacine and avian species (GenBank accession numbers XR_009724310.1, XR_009741753.1, XR_010609395.1, AF173614.1, and XR_011806354.1). A probe region non-homologous to human and mouse 18S (GenBank accession numbers K03432.1, X03205.1, and NR_003278.3) was selected. The TaqMan probe sequence was: 6-FAM-5’-CCAGCTCCAATAGCGTATCTTA-3’-MGBNFQ.

Each reaction was set up in a total volume of 10 µl, including 300 nM of each primer, 50 nM of probe, TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) at 1X, and 2 µl of DNA template. The same cycling programme as for the PsAdV-1 TaqMan PCR was followed.

If sample volume was insufficient to run this assay, samples were considered non-evaluable and omitted from comparative analyses. The 18 S rRNA assay was used as a qualitative endogenous control to assess DNA integrity and the presence of potential PCR inhibitors. Samples were considered suitable when a clear amplification signal was detected within 30 cycles, whereas samples showing no amplification were considered of insufficient quality and excluded from further analysis. No quantitative Cq threshold was defined.

Statistical analysis

Intra-assay and inter-assay variability were assessed by calculating the mean (x̅), standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV). Data handling and calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Results

Limit of detection of adenovirus DNA by conventional PCR

To determine the limit of detection (LOD) of conventional PCR, a standard curve was generated using the linearised plasmid pGEM-HexL1 DNA, diluted either in DEPC-treated water or in an adenovirus-negative DNA pool. This was done for each of the two primer pairs used in the assay: HexL1-s/HexL1-as and qPAV-1/qPAV-2.

Of the ten dilutions tested, when diluted in water, concentrations of 5.9 × 107 and 5.9 × 106 copies/µl yielded strong positive results, dilutions from 5.9 × 105 to 5.9 × 103 copies/µl were weak amplification signals, and no amplification was observed at 5.9 × 102 copies/µl or lower (Fig. 1). Similar results were obtained with the HexL1-s/HexL1-as primers when dilutions were prepared using the adenovirus-negative DNA pool. However, when using qPAV-1/qPAV-2 with this diluent, the lowest detectable concentration was 5.9 × 105 copies/µl (Fig. 1).

Optimization of TaqMan real-time PCR

To determine optimal probe concentration, a range of 25–200 nM of probe was tested with a fixed primer concentration. After establishing 50 nM of probe as optimal, the primers were tested in a range of 50–900 nM of each. The best results were obtained with 50 nM of probe, 250 nM of qPAV-1 and 100 nM of qPAV-2.

Analytical sensitivity of TaqMan real-time PCR

To estimate the sensitivity of the TaqMan assay, serial ten-fold dilutions were performed with the linearized plasmid, from 0.23 ng/µl to 2.3 × 10− 10 ng/µl, which corresponds to 5.9 × 107 to 5.9 × 10− 2 copies/µl, respectively. The last detectable concentration, both when diluted in DEPC water and in adenovirus-negative DNA pool, was 5.9 × 104 copies/µl (Fig. 3a).

To enable quantification, a standard curve was generated (Fig. 3b). Based on the standard curve performed with the adenovirus-negative DNA pool, the Cq were correlated, with a R2 of 0.9994, a slope of -3.6972 and an efficiency of 86.4%.

Detection of adenovirus DNA in liver samples

Liver samples of 34 birds suspected of adenovirus infection were analysed by conventional PCR with primers HexL1-s/HexL1-as, SYBR Green real-time PCR and TaqMan real-time PCR. Twenty-six samples were positive by cPCR, producing amplicons of the expected size; eight were negative. All cPCR-positive samples were also detected by both SYBR Green and TaqMan qPCR, with Cq values ranging from 9.8 to 19.6, and 10.0 to 21.9, respectively. Copy number estimates showed similar viral loads between the two methods, ranging from 2.6 × 106 to 1.9 × 109 copies/µl by SYBR Green and 2.0 × 106 to 3.3 × 109 copies/µl by TaqMan (Table 2).

Detection of adenovirus DNA in cloacal swabs

The 18S extraction control could not be run in 5/254 cloacal swabs due to insufficient volume; these samples were non-evaluable and excluded from subsequent comparative analyses. Cq values for the 18 S-validated swabs ranged from 12.93 to 25.84.

DNA from 249 18S-validated cloacal swabs was analysed using conventional PCR (HexL1-s/HexL1-as), SYBR Green qPCR (qPAV-1/qPAV-2) and TaqMan qPCR (qPAV-1/qPAV-2). All 11 cPCR-positive samples were also detected by qPCR, and all 52 TaqMan-positive samples were detected by SYBR Green qPCR under the Cq 28. Discussion

Adenovirus infections in psittacine birds were first proposed in the 1990s [30–34], but definitive recognition as distinct pathogens required molecular characterisation [1]. Since then, reports of PsAdVs have increased. Given their ease of transmission and frequent subclinical course, there is a clear need for sensitive and specific diagnostic tools.

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of molecular approaches for the detection of Psittacine adenovirus 1 (PsAdV-1) in clinical samples, combining conventional PCR, SYBR Green real-time PCR and a newly developed TaqMan real-time PCR assay. By analysing both liver tissues from birds with suspected adenoviral disease and cloacal swabs collected under field conditions, we directly compared assay performances in matrices with different viral loads and inhibitory backgrounds.

Conventional PCR targeting the hexon L1 region showed adequate analytical sensitivity but limited performance in clinical samples. While this was sufficient to detect all liver samples later confirmed positive by real-time PCR, its performance dropped substantially in cloacal swabs, which typically contain lower viral loads and higher amounts of inhibitors [35]. Thus, cPCR is poorly suited as a stand-alone tool for large‑scale screening, although it remains useful for confirmation in tissues with high viral burden.

The introduction of real-time PCR improved detection, but the two chemistries behaved differently. With a permissive Cq threshold, SYBR Green qPCR increased detection in cloacal swabs more than sevenfold compared to cPCR, consistent with experience in other avian DNA viruses [23, 36, 37]. Although previous studies have reported that SYBR Green qPCR assays for PsAdV-1 can achieve high sensitivity (102 copies/reaction) [28, 38], SYBR’s lack of a target-specific probe means that fluorescence can arise from any double-stranded DNA [39], with precision progressively degrading at higher Cq values and atypical melting profiles becoming more frequent. Consequently, conservative decision rules are required to minimise false positives, at the expense of effective sensitivity. In our dataset, high Cq values of SYBR signals could not be independently confirmed.

To address these limitations, a probe-based TaqMan assay was developed and optimised, targeting the same hexon L1 region. Although the analytical limit of detection on plasmid was comparable to SYBR, performance differed when applied to clinical samples. The TaqMan assay showed high analytical performance (R2 = 0.9994, efficiency 86%), with the lowest concentration yielding a detectable signal of 5.9 × 104 copies/µl. It also exhibited low intra- and inter-assay variability (≤ 0.84% and ≤ 3.15%), and no cross-reactivity with polyomavirus, beak and feather disease virus and herpesvirus was observed, supporting high specificity within the range of viruses tested.

Although the primers used in this qPCR have been previously reported to be specific for PsAdV when tested against a broad panel of avian adenoviruses [28], cross-reactivity with other avian or psittacine adenoviruses was not experimentally assessed in the present study. By eliminating post-PCR steps, the probe-based assay also reduces opportunities for carry-over contamination and shortens turnaround time, making it suitable for routine use.

Differences in analytical sensitivity observed under controlled conditions may be partly explained by methodological and matrix-related factors. In conventional PCR, detection is based on agarose gel visualization and requires a sufficient amount of amplified DNA, so low-level or non-specific amplification may remain undetected, particularly near the detection limit. In this context, the reduced sensitivity observed for the qPAV-1/qPAV-2 assay in the presence of adenovirus-negative DNA (5.9 × 105 copies/µl) likely reflects a matrix effect associated with background nucleic acids and co-extracted components, together with differences in primer performance, including the predicted tendency of qPAV-2 to form self-dimers.

By contrast, the behaviour of SYBR Green and TaqMan assays reflects their distinct detection chemistries. SYBR Green binds to any double-stranded DNA, so non-specific or low-efficiency amplification may contribute to the signal, potentially leading to an apparent increase in sensitivity in the presence of background DNA (5.9 × 103 copies/µl). In contrast, TaqMan relies on sequence-specific probe hybridization, ensuring that only the target amplicon is detected (5.9 × 104 copies/µl). These differences are therefore more likely attributable to the intrinsic properties of the detection systems than to a true increase in analytical sensitivity.

Analysis of liver samples provided internal validation of the workflow. All cPCR‑positive samples (26/34) were strongly positive by both real‑time assays, with low Cq values and high estimated viral loads that were highly concordant between SYBR and TaqMan (≈ 2.6 × 106 to 1.9 × 109 and 2.0 × 106 to 3.3 × 109 copies/µl, respectively). Sequencing of positives confirmed 100% identity with PsAdV-1, supporting the suitability of the targeted region and the accuracy of the assay. Pairwise identity analysis further showed a high degree of similarity among all sequences obtained.

Cloacal swabs were technically more challenging to analyse but proved more informative for epidemiological assessment. The more than fourfold increase in detection achieved by TaqMan qPCR (52/249) compared with cPCR (11/249) highlights the importance of assay choice in prevalence studies and surveillance programmes. Sequencing confirmed PsAdV-1 in all cPCR-positive swabs. By contrast, the higher number of SYBR Green‑positive results (81/249) at relatively high Cq values raises questions about the biological and clinical relevance of very low‑level viral DNA detection, which may reflect transient viral shedding, latent infection or residual environmental DNA rather than active infection. Importantly, all cPCR-positive and all TaqMan-positive samples were detected by SYBR Green when a strict Cq < 28 cut‑off was applied, indicating that the probe-based assay preferentially identifies samples with higher probability of true positivity and offers a more robust balance between sensitivity and specificity in low‑load samples. In this context, the TaqMan assay may represent a pragmatic compromise, prioritising specificity and reproducibility over maximal sensitivity.

The inclusion of a psittacine-specific 18S endogenous control was essential for reliable interpretation of cloacal swab results, as it allowed the exclusion of non‑evaluable samples and ensured that negative results reflected true absence of detectable viral DNA rather than extraction failure or inhibition. Accordingly, 5/254 were excluded and subsequent analyses were restricted to 18S validated extracts. This quality‑control strategy is particularly relevant under field conditions, where sample quality can vary substantially, and is increasingly recommended in veterinary molecular diagnostics [40, 41]. Consistent detection of PsAdV-1 in non-lethal cloacal swabs therefore provides a practical tool for longitudinal surveillance, screening of subclinically infected birds and routine monitoring of aviary biosecurity [15, 16].

Overall, while cPCR remains useful for confirmation in tissues with high viral loads, its diagnostic value in cloacal swabs is limited. Real-time PCR assays substantially improve detection; however, probe-based TaqMan chemistry offers clear advantages in specificity, reproducibility and interpretability in complex clinical samples. These findings highlight the practical sensitivity of the TaqMan assay, particularly in non-invasive samples, supporting early and accurate detection of asymptomatic carriers, which are frequently reported in PsAdV-1 infections [14, 16] and may silently contribute to viral spread in aviaries.

Traditional methods for PsAdV detection, including virus isolation and conventional PCR [9, 22, 28], often require confirmatory testing because of the potential for false-positive results [42–44]. In addition, longer processing times and increased hands-on manipulation reduce their practicality for large-scale monitoring. In contrast, the TaqMan assay combines high specificity with rapid turnaround and minimal handling, making it a practical option for both clinical diagnostics and field-based surveillance. While faecal samples may yield higher detection rates [9], they are less reliable when birds are group-housed, as attribution to a specific individual becomes difficult. Combining non-invasive sample types with a probe-based, target-specific assay may optimise screening strategies in breeding centres and wildlife settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study focused on PsAdV‑1 and did not assess potential cross‑reactivity with more divergent adenovirus species or a broader range of viruses. Second, the sample set was dominated by captive psittacines, so extrapolation to wild populations or other husbandry conditions should be made. Third, clinical follow‑up was limited, preventing firm conclusions about the association between viral load and disease outcome, persistence or transmission risk. Finally, the lack of a true gold standard, such as virus isolation in all samples, prevents definitive classification of equivocal high‑Cq SYBR Green results.

Future work should extend validation across geographical regions and husbandry systems, and include additional sample types such as faeces, with longitudinal sampling to clarify shedding dynamics, duration of infection and the role of subclinical carriers. Correlating quantitative viral loads with clinical and pathological data would help define clinically relevant cut‑offs and improve case definitions. Adapting the probe design to other PsAdV species could expand its diagnostic reach and contribute to improved surveillance and zoonotic risk mitigation in both wild and captive psittacine populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a probe-based TaqMan real-time PCR assay provides a robust, sensitive and highly specific method for the detection of Psittacine adenovirus 1 in clinical samples. While conventional PCR remains useful for confirmation in tissues with high viral loads, its limited performance in cloacal swabs restricts its applicability for surveillance. SYBR Green real-time PCR improves detection sensitivity but requires stringent decision criteria to avoid false-positive results at high Cq values. In contrast, the TaqMan assay offers a reliable balance between sensitivity, specificity and reproducibility, particularly in non-invasive samples with low viral loads.

The implementation of this assay, together with a psittacine-specific endogenous control, enables accurate identification of subclinically infected birds and supports routine diagnosis, epidemiological surveillance and biosecurity monitoring in psittacine populations.
