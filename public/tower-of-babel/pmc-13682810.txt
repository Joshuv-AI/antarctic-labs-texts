Novel targeted therapy and cellular immunotherapy enabling allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Liqin Cao, Daihong Hu, Lijuan Cui, Xiaohui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are characterized by a disastrous prognosis. For most patients with R/R AML, allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative option. It is important to conduct mutational analysis again when the disease relapses to identify any new genetic abnormalities that can be targeted with novel treatments. As part of a conditioning regimen or post-transplant maintenance therapy, new targeted agents support HSCT in patients with R/R AML in various forms, including combination chemotherapy to improve complete remission (CR) prior to HSCT. For R/R AML patients with mutated FMS-related tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3), sorafenib and quizartinib have shown encouraging therapeutic effects either in combination with chemotherapy for bridging to transplantation or as maintenance therapy after HSCT. Ivosidenib and enasidenib, which are inhibitors that target mutated isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) 1 and 2, respectively, have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of IDH1/IDH2-mutated R/R AML. Venetoclax, an inhibitor of B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2), is widely used in the salvage treatment of R/R AML and has better therapeutic effects and controllable drug toxicity than traditional chemotherapy. In addition, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell immunotherapy (targeting CD33, CD123, and CLL1) has achieved encouraging clinical response rates in phase I and phase II clinical trials for R/R-AML, and subsequent bridging HSCT has significantly improved patient survival.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most prevalent form of leukemia in adults.[1–3] In recent years, advances in treatment, including the use of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), multidrug chemotherapy regimens, and novel targeted therapies, have improved the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for AML patients aged 15–39 years from 24% to 63%.[4] Despite these improvements, many AML patients still face poor outcomes, with many experiencing relapse or resistance to initial therapy. This underscores the ongoing challenge of achieving successful outcomes for all AML patients. For those with relapsed or refractory (R/R) AML, treatment options are limited, and the prognosis remains poor, with a 5-year OS rate of less than 10%.[5,6] Intensive chemotherapy (IC) is often used for physically fit patients with the goal of achieving a second complete response to facilitate bridging to HSCT. However, long-term survival rates are still low because the necessary response to enable successful transplantation is often not achieved. Additionally, factors such as comorbidities, age, residual toxicity, and donor availability further limit the feasibility of this approach for many patients. Consequently, R/R AML patients rarely undergo HSCT and achieve recovery.

Recent advances in understanding the biological and molecular complexities of AML have led to the development of new therapeutic strategies and targeted treatments for R/R AML. Gilteritinib has demonstrated greater efficacy than intensive salvage therapies in R/R AML patients with FMS-related tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) mutations.[7] Ivosidenib and enasidenib have demonstrated excellent clinical efficacy in R/R AML patients with mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)1 and 2, with overall response rates (ORR) of 41.6% and 40.3%, respectively.[8,9]

The combination of venetoclax with hypomethylating agents (HMAs) or low-dose cytarabine (LDAC) has led to significant improvements in survival rates among elderly patients.[10] Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell immunotherapy has been effective in treating refractory or relapsed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and is emerging as a promising approach for hematologic malignancies.[11–14] Recently, phase I and phase II clinical trials of CAR T-cell therapy in R/R-AML have achieved encouraging clinical response rates, and subsequent bridging HSCT has significantly improved patient survival.[15–18] In conclusion, the application of targeted therapy and cellular immunotherapy increased the CR rates of R/R AML patients, thus enabling the use of HSCT for physically fit R/R AML patients.

Definition of R/R AML

Primary refractory AML is described as failure to achieve CR after two rounds of intensive induction therapy, as outlined by the European Leukemia Net.[19] Ferguson et al[20] assessed outcomes in 8907 AML patients and introduced a practical definition for primary refractory AML. They classified patients as primary refractory if they showed an inadequate response to the initial induction, which was indicated by a reduction in blast counts of less than 50% and more than 15% residual blasts, or if they did not achieve CR after two induction courses. On the other hand, relapse occurs in AML patients who, after achieving CR, experience a rise in bone marrow blasts to 5% or more, a reappearance of blasts in the blood, or the emergence of extramedullary disease. Notably, molecular relapse has been extensively examined in specific subgroups, such as those with core binding factor leukemia or mutations in nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1).[21] Progressive disease is identified by an increase in the percentage of bone marrow blasts and/or an increase in absolute blast counts in the bloodstream.[19]

Biology of Relapse

Clonal evolution at the cytogenetic level in AML is an important mechanism leading to AML relapse.[22] The clonal evolution of tumor cells during relapse was confirmed by whole-genome sequencing analysis of primary tumor and relapse samples from eight AML patients.[23] There are two main patterns of clonal evolution during AML relapse: (1) the original clone in the primary tumor acquires mutations and becomes a recurrent clone and (2) subclones of the original clone survive the initial treatment, acquire additional mutations, and expand upon relapse. The direction of clonal evolution is related to the pressure on tumor cells receiving chemotherapy drugs in the past. Loss of human leukemia antigen (HLA) after haploidentical-HSCT is another clonal evolutionary mechanism leading to AML recurrence.[24] Luca Vago’s team reported that, out of 17 patients with relapsed AML after haploidentical-HSCT, mutations in pro-leukemic cells were found in five patients. Mutated leukemia cells acquire uniparental diploid chromosome 6, resulting in the loss of HLAs that differ from the donor’s haplotype.[24] T cells from donor and post-transplant patients do not recognize mutated leukemia cells, and immune escape occurs, leading to disease relapse. A similar immune escape mechanism has been reported in AML patients receiving unrelated HSCT.[25]

Leukemia stem cells (LSCs) are important for promoting the recurrence of AML.[26,27] They are able to maintain self-renewal and cell cycle stasis and can evade the attack of traditional chemotherapy drugs, which leads to difficulty in the treatment and recurrence of the disease.[28] Recent studies have shown that LSCs are metabolically reprogrammed and have significant epigenetic differences directly from normal hematopoietic stem cells.[28] Dysregulation of epigenetic expression, such as DNA methylation, abnormal histone modification, and chromatin remodeling, can promote tumor cell proliferation and self-renewal and block differentiation. These mechanisms are critical to chemotherapy resistance and may be responsible for AML relapse.[29]

Mechanism of Refractory AML

The risk factors contributing to primary refractory AML are diverse and intricate. These include having a complex or monosomal karyotype, older age, prolonged duration for blast clearance in either bone marrow or peripheral blood, an elevated white blood cell (WBC) count at the time of diagnosis, secondary AML, and specific molecular mutations, such as tumor protein p53 (TP53).[30] Recent research indicates that failures in initial treatment may also stem from AML clones possessing inherent characteristics of hematopoietic stem cells and a state of quiescence. Li et al[31] discovered that, in contrast to proliferating stem/progenitor-like cells (PSPs), a distinct group of quiescent stem-like cells (QSCs) contributes to chemoresistance and unfavorable outcomes in AML. Through longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses, they revealed that PSPs underwent reprogramming to exhibit a QSC-like expression profile during chemotherapy in patients with refractory AML, marked by increased expression of CD52 and Galectin 1. Additionally, Sachi Horibata and associates[32] conducted transcriptomic analyses on 154 treatment-naive AML cases, identifying that refractory subpopulations exhibited varied expression in pathways related to the cell cycle, transcription, translation, metabolism, and/or stem cell characteristics.

Receptor-Targeted Therapy Enabling HSCT in R/R AML

FLT3 inhibitors

FLT3 gene mutations are found in approximately 30% of AML cases, with internal tandem duplication (ITD) being the most prevalent type (FLT3-ITD), occurring in approximately 25% of AML cases. FLT3-ITD mutation acts as a tumor driver, leading to a high leukemic burden and poor prognosis for affected patients. Conversely, mutations in the tyrosine kinase domain of FLT3 (FLT3-TKD) are less common, affecting approximately 7–10% of AML cases, and their prognostic significance remains unclear.[33–36] A key concept in relapsed AML is clonal evolution. This process involves the emergence of previously undetectable mutations, such as FLT3-ITD, at the time of relapse, which can influence the prognosis of the disease.[37–39]

First- and second-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) inhibit FLT3 activity and are therefore commonly used in chemotherapy regimens for AML patients with FLT3 mutations. Midostaurin is a first-generation TKI, and combination chemotherapy is the first-line treatment for newly diagnosed AML patients with FLT3 mutations.[40] In the randomized phase III RATIFY trial of midostaurin combined with cytarabine and daunorubicin (3 + 7) induction and consolidation, midostaurin improved OS compared with placebo in patients <60 years of age with newly diagnosed FLT3 (ITD and/or TKD) AML.[40] However, midostaurin was not effective in R/R-AML patients. Sorafenib is an inhibitor of several kinases, including FLT3, and has shown activity not only in newly diagnosed patients with FLT3-mutated AML but also in R/R AML patients after HSCT.[41–43] Interestingly, sorafenib promotes graft-versus leukemia (GVL) activity in mice and humans by promoting interleukin (IL)-15 production in leukemia cells with FLT3-ITD mutations.[44] The use of sorafenib combined with azacitide in the treatment of patients with relapsed AML after HSCT resulted in encouraging therapeutic effects; eight patients were included, and four patients achieved remission (50%).[45] In the SORMAIN trial, 83 adult patients with FLT3-ITD-mutated AML who underwent HSCT were randomized to receive either sorafenib maintenance therapy or a placebo. The 1-year relapse-free survival (RFS) rate was significantly greater in the sorafenib group than in the placebo group (85.0% vs. 53.5%, P = 0.002).[41] In a randomized, multicenter, double-blind phase III trial, 202 adult patients with FLT3-ITD-positive AML who achieved hematologic CR following HSCT were randomly assigned to receive either sorafenib (n = 100) or a placebo (n = 102) for a duration of 24 months. With a median follow-up of 60.4 months, the hazard ratio (HR) for relapse or death was 0.55 for the sorafenib group compared with that for the placebo group (P = 0.011). The 5-year OS rate was 72.0% in the sorafenib group, which surpassed the 55.9% reported in the placebo group. Additionally, the cumulative recurrence rate was significantly lower in the sorafenib group (15.0% vs. 36.3%; P = 0.0003).[46] These results suggest that maintenance therapy with sorafenib can significantly improve OS and RFS after FLT3-ITD-positive AML transplantation.

Second-generation TKIs more specifically inhibit FLT3 activity and show promising therapeutic benefits in R/R AML patients.[47–49] In the extensive phase III ADMIRAL trial, 371 patients with relapsed AML harboring FLT3 mutations (FLT3-ITD and FLT3-TKD) were randomly assigned at a 2:1 ratio to receive either gilteritinib or salvage treatment. Among those in the gilteritinib group, 34% achieved CR or CR with incomplete blood count recover (CRi), whereas 15.3% achieved CR or CRi in the salvage chemotherapy group. The median OS was notably extended for patients receiving gilteritinib, at 9.3 months vs. 5.6 months in the control group.[7,50] In the gilteritinib group, 64 of 246 patients had undergone HSCT, and 18 patients survived for more than two years without relapse; of those who did not receive HSCT (182 patients), only eight patients achieved the goal of surviving for more than two years without relapse.[50] The promising results from clinical trials led to the approval of gilteritinib by both the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency for treating FLT3-mutated R/R AML. A retrospective study conducted across 11 medical centers in the United States evaluated the efficacy of gilteritinib in R/R AML patients who had previously received TKIs. The findings revealed that among patients who had been treated with only the 7 + 3 regimen and midostaurin, with or without consolidation, the composite CR rate with gilteritinib was 58%, and the median survival was 7.8 months.[48] Although gilteritinib monotherapy does not cure patients with FLT3-mutated R/R AML, it is an optional treatment bridge to HSCT. After gilteritinib treatment, patients eligible for transplantation should receive HSCT (preferably at CR). In elderly patients with R/R AML who are not candidates for transplantation, gilteritinib can be continued until drug resistance develops.[51]

Quizartinib, another second-generation TKI drug, has shown potent and highly selective FLT3 activity in both FLT3-ITD-mutant and FLT3-wild-type cell lines in cell experiments and xenotransplantation models.[52] In a phase I dose-escalation study involving 76 patients with R/R AML, the safety and efficacy of quizartinib were assessed. The results indicated that patients with FLT3-ITD mutations had a higher ORR than those with wild-type FLT3 (53% vs. 14%). The study demonstrated that quizartinib was clinically effective in treating R/R AML, especially in patients with FLT3-ITD mutations.[53] A subsequent phase II trial validated the effectiveness of quizartinib as a single-agent treatment for R/R AML patients with FLT3-ITD mutations. A previous study reported CR rates of 46–56% among FLT3-ITD-positive patients, whereas rates of 30–36% were reported in those without the mutation.[54] In Japan, a phase III trial compared quizartinib monotherapy with standard salvage chemotherapy for R/R FLT3-ITD AML. With a median follow-up of 23.5 months, patients treated with quizartinib had longer OS than those receiving chemotherapy (6.2 months vs. 4.7 months; HR for death: 0.76; P = 0.02). Additionally, the proportion of patients in the quizartinib group who received HSCT was three times greater than that in the salvage chemotherapy group, indicating that quizartinib may serve as an effective bridge to HSCT.[55]

IDH inhibitors

Approximately 20% of AML patients have IDH1 and IDH2 mutations, and the clinicopathologic characteristics of these patients are associated with older age and an increased incidence of intermediate-risk cytogenetics, FLT3-ITD mutations, and NPM1 mutations.[56] IDH1/2 mutant enzymes generate an abnormal oncometabolite, 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), rather than the normal metabolite α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). The accumulation of 2-HG inhibits the α-KG-dependent enzyme 10–11 translocation 2 (TET2), leading to a hypermethylated genome and impaired differentiation. Several small-molecule inhibitors targeting mutant IDH1 and/or IDH2 have been developed, with ivosidenib (an IDH1 inhibitor) and enasidenib (an IDH2 inhibitor) being the most advanced. In a phase I trial evaluating ivosidenib monotherapy in adult patients with R/R AML harboring IDH1 mutations, 179 participants were treated. The trial reported an ORR of 41.6%, with a median response duration of 6.5 months, a CR rate of 21.6%, and a median duration of remission of 9.3 months.[9] Following these promising clinical findings, the FDA approved ivosidenib in 2018 for treating R/R AML with IDH1 mutations. Additionally, enasidenib has also received FDA approval for use in R/R AML patients with IDH2 mutations. In a phase I/II trial assessing enasidenib in patients with R/R AML harboring IDH2 mutations, the ORR was 40.3%, with a median OS of 9.3 months. The CR rate was 19.3%, and patients who achieved CR had a median OS of 19.7 months.[57] Genthon et al[58] demonstrated that IDH inhibitors, including ivosidenib and enasidenib, can effectively facilitate transplantation as part of a preconditioning regimen. The study included 11 patients with R/R AML harboring IDH1 or IDH2 mutations. Seven patients with IDH2 mutations received oral enasidenib at 100 mg daily, while four patients with IDH1 mutations were administered ivosidenib at 500 mg daily. Among these, seven patients (63.6%) achieved CR or CRi, with a median response time of 60 days (range: 30–330 days). Seven patients (four who achieved CR or CRi before transplantation) subsequently underwent HSCT, five of whom achieved CR following the procedure.

B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL2) inhibition—Venetoclax

BCL2 and its family members play crucial roles in apoptosis and cancer development by regulating the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane and interacting with various apoptosis-related proteins.[59–61] Venetoclax, a selective inhibitor of BCL2, has strong antileukemic effects in vivo and in vitro.[62–64] Venetoclax combined with an HMA composition low-intensity chemotherapy regimen was explored to achieve better clinical efficacy and reasonable drug toxicity in the treatment of R/R AML, which has great clinical significance for R/R AML patients and elderly patients with leukemia.[65–67]

Giacomo et al[68] conducted a retrospective analysis of 67 R/R AML patients treated with venetoclax-based regimens on an off-label basis. Among these patients, 39 (58%) received venetoclax in combination with HMAs or LDAC as part of an intention-to-transplant (ITT) therapy strategy. Among this ITT cohort, the rate of proceeding to HSCT was 66%, with refractory disease being the sole reason for not advancing to HSCT. Zappasodi et al[69] reported the outcomes of 10 R/R AML patients treated with venetoclax + azacytidine (AZA), followed by HSCT if at least a clinical response was obtained. An overall response was obtained in six out of 10 patients (ORR 60%), and among the responders, four achieved CR (66%), one achieved CRi (17%), and one had morphological leukemic-free status (MLFS) (17%); four patients did not respond. HSCT was performed in all responding patients and in a non-responsive patient. Ultimately, five transplanted patients are alive, four of whom achieved CR. In a phase Ib/II study assessing the safety and efficacy of combining fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, and idarubicin with venetoclax for newly diagnosed and R/R AML patients, the transplantation rate in the R/R cohort was 46%. The study demonstrated a significant OS benefit for patients who underwent consolidative HSCT.[70]

Another relevant question is whether venetoclax-based bridging strategies in combination with IC are more effective than venetoclax in combination with HMAs. While there have been reports of a relatively high ORR in patients receiving venetoclax + IC,[70–73] there are significant differences in demographic, clinical, and molecular characteristics among patient populations, which affects the reliability of comparisons. Venetoclax combined with HMAs is currently the preferred treatment for patients with R/R AML who are elderly or not suitable for IC. However, for younger patients,[10,74] it remains unclear whether further increasing the intensity of treatment to improve treatment efficacy is needed. From a safety standpoint, this consideration is crucial, as the use of IC could lead to an increased risk of end-organ damage, potentially affecting eligibility for HSCT.

The efficacy and safety of venetoclax combined with a low-intensity regimen in patients who relapse after HSCT are somewhat inconsistent with that in the current scientific literature. Some retrospective studies have shown a lower ORR after HSCT in patients receiving venetoclax in combination with low-intensity regimens (e.g., AZA),[68,75,76] which may be biased by the enrichment of high-risk cytogenetic/molecular features in the cohort of patients who relapsed after HSCT. However, some other studies have reported encouraging results. Aldoss et al[77] reported that patients who had previously received HSCT had no significant difference in ORR after venetocalx combined salvage therapy compared with patients who had not received HSCT (prior to HSCT, 46.2%; did not receive HSCT, 55.0%; P = 0.73). Byrne et al[78] conducted a study on 21 AML patients who experienced a relapse following HSCT and received treatment with venetoclax, typically in combination with HMAs. The ORR was 42.1%, and the median OS was 7.8 months. Patients who achieved CR or CRi had notably longer OS (P = 0.005). Eventually, all responsive patients required dose reductions of venetoclax due to frequent infectious complications and severe cytopenias graded at four. Joshi et al[79] conducted a study on 29 AML patients who had relapsed after HSCT and were treated with venetoclax. Their findings indicated a 38% ORR. Among responders, the median OS was 403 days, and the median disease-free survival (DFS) was 259 days. The safety profile was consistent with previous research, which recommended reducing venetoclax exposure to 21 days or 14 days to minimize cytopenia duration. These data suggest that venetoclax combined with HMAs has modest efficacy and an acceptable safety profile in the treatment of patients with AML who relapse after HSCT, but dose adjustment in patients is needed to reduce adverse effects.

Recent research has indicated multiple mechanisms contributing to venetoclax resistance in AML: (1) the reliance on other anti-apoptotic members of the BCL-2 family, such as BCL2 related protein A1 (BCL2-A1), myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1), and B-cell lymphoma extra large (BCL-xL), has been identified as significant factors in both primary and adaptive resistance to venetoclax.[80–83] Preclinical studies have shown that AML cell lines sensitive to venetoclax exhibit lower levels of MCL-1 and BCL-xL, while resistant cells display reduced BCL-2 protein levels;[84] (2) the activation of intracellular signaling pathways by mutant proteins such as kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS)/protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 1 (PTPN1) or FLT3 is believed to trigger venetoclax resistance.[83,85] The FLT3-ITD mutation is known to elevate the expression of BCL-xL and MCL-1, potentially leading to resistance;[86,87] and (3) genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) screening has identified BCL2-associated X (BAX), along with TP53 and phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate-induced protein 1 (PMA1P1), as genes whose inactivation can lead to venetoclax resistance.[88,89] The insights gained from preclinical studies regarding the mechanisms of venetoclax resistance have facilitated the development of various combination strategies currently undergoing clinical trials. The combination of venetoclax with FLT-3 inhibitors is being rigorously investigated.[90] In a phase 1B trial, the combination of gilteritinib and venetoclax achieved an ORR of 90% in patients with FLT-3 mutated R/R AML, with similarly high response rates observed in those who had previously failed TKIs.[90,91] A phase I/II study demonstrated that the combination of AZA, venetoclax, and gilteritinib resulted in high rates of CR/CRi, deep FLT3 molecular responses, and encouraging survival in newly diagnosed FLT3-mutated AML.[49] MCL-1 undoubtedly plays a crucial role in mediating venetoclax resistance through various upstream factors, both genomic and epigenetic. Therefore, targeting MCL-1, either directly or indirectly, is a logical approach to enhance sensitivity to venetoclax. S64315, a direct MCL-1 inhibitor, is currently being assessed in early-phase clinical trials in combination with venetoclax,[92,93] with further data on safety and efficacy expected. TP53 mutations are associated with venetoclax resistance and poorer patient outcomes. Eprenetapopt (APR-246) has been developed to covalently bind to mutant TP53, restoring its function and inducing TP53-dependent apoptosis.[94] The safety of the combination of eprenetapopt, venetoclax, and AZA has been proved in a phase 1 clinical trial.[95]

Cellular immunotherapy enabling HSCT in R/R AML

CAR-T-cell therapy is a type of cellular immunotherapy that involves genetically modifying T-cells to incorporate specific antigen recognition domains and T-cell activation signals. This enables the modified T cells to directly recognize and kill tumor cells. Therapy was first proposed by Gross Equals in the late 1980s and entered the preclinical research stage in the early 1990s.[96,97] In the field of hematological malignancies, CAR-T-cell therapy has made significant progress. For example, CD19 CAR-T-cell therapy has achieved unprecedented response rates, and durable remission in patients with relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphomas resulted in its approval by the US FDA in 2017.[98–100] However, in AML, the progress of CAR-T-cell therapy has been relatively slow due to the lack of specific surface antigen targets.[101] In recent years, the efficacy of CAR-T cells targeting CD33, CD123, CD135, C-type lectin-like molecule 1 (CLL1), and natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) has been confirmed in patients with R/R AML. For example, Wang et al[102] conducted an autologous CAR-T-cell clinical trial in which CD33 was targeted, which included a patient with refractory AML. After receiving CD33-targeted CAR-T-cell therapy, the number of bone marrow blasts significantly decreased and persisted for 9 weeks. Tang et al[103] investigated the safety of CD33-CAR-T cells in R/R AML patients and reported that one of three patients had elevated serum IL-6 and IL-10 levels on day 6 after CAR-NK-T-cell therapy. One month after treatment, the number of bone marrow blast cells, minimal residual disease (MRD) level were lower than those before treatment. A phase I study of CAR-T cells targeting CD33 and CLL-1 in R/R AML included a pediatric R/R-AML patient with Fanconi anemia progressing to a complex karyotype and FLT3-ITD mutation, who achieved CR on day 19 of CAR-T-cell infusion, was MRD-negative, and subsequently underwent HSCT.[104] Sallman et al[105] reported a phase I clinical study of targeted NKG2D CAR-T-cell therapy, which included seven patients, three (42%) of whom achieved disease remission. Cao X et al[106] reported that CD7 CAR-T cells were used to treat R/R AML patients with complex karyotypes, TP53 deletion, FLT3-ITD mutation, and src-kinase associated protein (SKAP2)-runt-related transcription factor-1 (RUNX1) fusion gene. Seventeen days after CAR-T-cell infusion, the patients achieved an MLFS. Despite the limited number of R/R AML patients in CAR-T-cell therapy trials, the outcomes have been encouraging, and additional research is anticipated. For R/R-AML patients eligible for HSCT, there are several modalities for CAR-T-cell therapy combined with HSCT. The most common model is CAR-T-cell therapy as a bridge therapy before HSCT. Zhang et al[110] conducted a phase I/II clinical trial of CLL1 CAR-T-cell therapy for R/R AML in children, including eight pediatric AML patients who developed grade 1–2 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) after CAR-T-cell therapy, without any fatal events. Among the eight patients, four achieved MLFS and MRD negativity, one achieved MLFS and MRD positivity, one achieved CRi but MRD positivity, one achieved partial response, and one maintained stable disease status. However, CLL1-positive AML cell clearance was detected; six patients underwent HSCT 2–4 months after CAR-T-cell therapy. Jin et al[16] reported a phase I clinical trial of CLL-1-targeting CAR-T cells for the treatment of adult R/R AML patients, including 10 RR-AML patients. The CR/CRi rate was 70% (n = 7/10). Among these patients, six patients underwent HSCT, and five patients experienced disease remission after transplantation. These studies demonstrated the efficacy and safety of CLL-1 CAR-T cells in the treatment of adult and pediatric R/R AML, providing conditions for further HSCT. Hu et al[15] developed CD7-targeting CAR-T cells (RD13-01) derived from healthy donors and included 12 patients (11 with T-cell leukemia/lymphoma and one with CD7-expressing AML) in a phase I clinical trial. The study revealed no dose-limiting toxicity, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity, or severe CRS (grade ≥3). At 28 days post-infusion, 81.8% of patients (9/11) exhibited an objective response, with a CR rate of 63.6% (7/11, including those with AML). Three patients underwent HSCT. After a median follow-up of 10.5 months, four patients continued to achieve a CR.

The second approach involves incorporating CAR-T-cell therapy into the conditioning regimen for HSCT. Zhang’s team utilized donor-derived CD123 CAR-T cells as part of the conditioning for haploidentical HSCT in a patient with FUS RNA binding protein- ETS transcription factor ERG (FUS-ERG)+ AML. This patient had relapsed within three months of the initial HSCT and had shown no response to multidrug chemotherapy or donor lymphocyte infusion. After 2 weeks of CD123 CAR-T-cell infusion, the tumor load was significantly reduced, secondary transplantation was successfully completed in combination with the dose reduction conditioning regimen, and the disease completely resolved after the graft was fully chimeric.[113] Hu Y et al[112] investigated an innovative “all-in-one” approach involving sequential CD7 CAR-T-cell therapy followed by haploidentical HSCT in 10 patients with R/R CD7-positive leukemia or lymphoma. After achieving a CRi with CD7 CAR-T-cell therapy, patients underwent haploidentical HSCT without the use of pharmacologic myeloablation or GVHD prophylaxis. The results indicated that eight patients achieved full donor chimerism, whereas one patient maintained autologous hematopoiesis. Unfortunately, one patient died from septic shock and encephalitis on Day 13 post-HSCT. Additionally, three patients developed grade 2 HSCT-associated acute GVHD. The median follow-up was 15.1 months (ranging from 3.1 months to 24.0 months) after CAR-T-cell therapy. The estimated 1-year OS rate was 68% (95% confidence interval [CI], 43–100%), and the estimated 1-year DFS rate was 54% (95% CI, 29–100%). These results indicate that CAR-T-cell therapy, which can serve as part of a conditioning regimen or even replace a conditioning regimen, can reduce the intensity of pre-transplantation chemotherapy and increase the safety and effectiveness of transplantation.

The third approach involves the use of CAR-T-cell therapy as either maintenance or salvage treatment after transplantation. To evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of CD38-targeted CAR-T cells, Cui et al[107] enrolled six AML patients who relapsed following HSCT. Before receiving CD38 CAR-T-cell therapy, these patients had a median of 95% (ranging from 92% to 99%) CD38-positive blast cells in their bone marrow. Four weeks after the initial CAR-T-CD38 CAR-T-cell infusion-cell infusion, four out of the six patients (66.7%) achieved either CR or CRi. The median time to CR or CRi was 191 days, with a range of 117–261 days. The recurrence rate at 6 months was 50%, with a median OS of 7.9 months and leukemia-free survival (LFS) of 6.4 months. All six patients experienced manageable side effects, and multiparameter flow cytometry confirmed the effective targeting of CD38+ blast cells by CAR-T cells without off-target effects on monocytes or lymphocytes. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the safety and clinical potential of CD38 CAR-T-cell therapy for treating relapsed AML post-HSCT.

While advancements have been made in the development of CAR-T therapies for the treatment of R/R AML, significant obstacles still exist. Numerous other molecules, which exhibit varying levels of expression across different AML subtypes, are currently being explored in vitro as potential CAR-T cell targets. Nonetheless, as it stands, none of these candidates meet all the necessary criteria to be considered ideal for immunotherapy.[114] Some are only found in specific AML subtypes and may not warrant the expenses associated with large-scale production; others show partial expression in normal hematopoietic stem cells, raising the likelihood of on-target/off-tumor adverse effects. Additionally, certain leukemic antigens may become downregulated in response to treatment, heightening the risk of antigen escape. Identifying newly generated antigens that arise from mutations linked to or caused by AML would be the most desirable target for CAR-T cells.[115] Furthermore, the manufacturing process of CAR-T for AML presents challenges, as the collection, modification, and expansion of autologous T cells is both time-intensive and complex. This complexity is exacerbated by the aggressive nature of the disease and the varying compositions of peripheral T cells, which are influenced by the condition and prior treatments, ultimately impacting the expansion, persistence, and effectiveness of CAR-T cells.[116] To augment their efficacy and overcome limitations, researchers have turned to gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9, transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and meganucleases. The discovery of meganucleases (naturally occurring restriction enzymes that can recognize 12–40 bp DNA sequences) was the initial step toward genome editing, followed by the discovery of zinc finger nucleases (ZFN) in the 1980s.[117] CRISPR/Cas9 is the most recent developed robust genome-editing tool with high precision that can substitute lenti- or retro-viruses for the genome modification of T cells. Phase I clinical trials have been conducted for testing CAR-T cells engineered with CRISPR/Cas9 developed in 2022 by doctor Huang. The results showed anti-CD19 CAR-T cells edited by CRISPR/Cas9 and demonstrated enhanced capabilities in eliminating tumor cells in xenograft models and proved to be effective against R/R aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma.[13] NCT01864902 and NCT03190278 clinical trials aim at evaluating the safety and efficacy of CAR-T targeting CD123 in patients with R/R AML. TALENs are used to disable the T cell receptor Alpha Beta (TCRαβ) gene T cells used to recognize “self” to prevent them from attacking the host.

Other target inhibitors

The TP53 protein, associated with tumors, functions as a transcription factor that regulates alterations in the transcriptional activities of the cell following various events that induce cellular stress.[118] TP53-mutated AML patients have an inferior response to induction/salvage chemotherapy, a very high relapse risk even after HSCT, and a dismal overall prognosis.[119]

APR-246 represents a novel, first-in-class small molecule designed to stabilize mutant TP53, thus restoring the protein’s functional conformation. The effectiveness of APR-246 when used alongside AZA has been evaluated in patients with myeloid neoplasms, including myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and AML featuring TP53 mutations.[120] A phase II, multicenter, open-label study investigated the safety and efficacy of combining APR-246 with AZA as a maintenance treatment following HSCT. Maintenance therapy was administered to 33 patients (14 with AML and 19 with MDS), with a median age of 65 years (ranging from 40 to 74%). The probability of DFS at 1 year was 59.9% (95% CI, 41–74%), while the OS probability for the same period was 78.8% (95% CI, 60.6–89.3%). The mortality rates at 30 days and 60 days post-initial dose were recorded at 0% and 6% (n = 2), respectively. Adverse events related to acute and chronic GVHD (all grades) were observed in 12% (n = 4) and 33% (n = 11) of the patients, respectively. These findings suggest that maintenance therapy with APR-246 combined with azacitidine is well tolerated in patients with TP53-mutated AML and MDS following HSCT, with promising DFS and OS outcomes in this high-risk group.[121]

The anti-CD47 antibody, known as magrolimab, was created following the findings by Majeti et al[122] and Jaiswal et al[123] that human AML expresses CD47 as a mechanism to evade phagocytosis. This antibody became the first of its kind to serve as a macrophage checkpoint inhibitor within the investigational framework for AML. Initially, the design for magrolimab’s registration trials was mutation-agnostic; however, it was later recognized that it demonstrated efficacy in cases of TP53-mutant MDS and AML. In the Phase 1B ENHANCE trial, which evaluated magrolimab in combination with AZA, a CR rate of 40% was observed in patients with TP53-mutant MDS.[124] The median OS for these patients was reported at 16.3 months. For those with TP53-mutant AML, the CR rate was recorded at 31.9%, with a median OS of 9.8 months.[125] The ENHANCE 2 and Phase 3 ENHANCE 3 trials were also in progress to assess magrolimab combined with AZA for AML treatment. However, in February 2024, the ENHANCE 3 trial was halted due to an elevated risk of mortality associated with magrolimab.

Conclusion and Future Directions

Novel targeted therapy and cellular immunotherapy have revolutionized the treatment of AML, especially R/R AML, which is generally characterized by a dramatic prognosis. Novel targeted agents (e.g., venetoclax) offer unprecedented opportunities for salvage therapy with low extra-hematological toxicity, creating conditions for HSCT for candidate patients. CAR-T-cell therapy (targeting CD7, CD33, CD123, CLL1, CD38, and NKG2D), a potential bridge therapy to HSCT, has shown great potential and significant clinical effectiveness in R/R AML [Figure 1]. Transplantation combined with targeted agents and cell therapy is the strategic path to breakthrough treatment of AML. For physically fit patients with R/R AML, targeted therapy or chemotherapy followed by sequential HSCT is the primary treatment option [Figure 2].

In the future, gene therapy and artificial intelligence will be used in the field of medical treatment. Hematopoietic stem cells and CAR-T cells were modified by gene editing technology to enhance the therapeutic effect and reduce side effects. He et al[13] effectively created a two-in-one approach strategy to produce non-viral, gene-targeted CAR-T cells utilizing CRISPR-Cas9 technology. They incorporated an anti-CD19 CAR cassette into the adeno-associated virus integration site 1 (AAVS1) safe-harbor locus and integrated PD1 with a unique variant of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells. This new variant of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells demonstrated enhanced capabilities in eliminating tumor cells in xenograft models and proved to be effective against R/R aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Artificial intelligence uses big data analysis to develop accurate treatment plans for each patient. Warnat-Herresthal et al[126] discuss the application of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, a machine learning technique designed to automatically identify the most predictive features within a dataset and conduct regression analysis, for the automated diagnosis and classification of AML utilizing transcriptomic data aggregated from 105 studies involving 12,029 patients, including those with AML, MDS, ALL, and healthy controls. The model developed by the authors achieved over 99% accuracy in identifying AML using transcriptomic data without the need for additional data or expert insights. Furthermore, it effectively differentiated AML-M3 from other subtypes, demonstrating 97.6% sensitivity and 99.5% specificity. Additionally, research conducted by Gerstung M et al[127] illustrates how genomic data can enhance risk stratification for AML patients, employing gradient boosting trees as an machine learning methodology that produces predictions based on the aggregated results of numerous small decision trees. Gene therapy (G), receptor-targeted therapy (R), artificial intelligence (A), functional cell therapy (F), and transplantation (T), also called “GRAFT”, are deeply integrated and represent future directions for the investigation of R/R AML treatment.
