Rényi-like entanglement probe of the chiral central charge
Julian Gass, Michael Levin

Abstract
We propose a ground state entanglement probe for gapped, two-dimensional quantum many-body systems that involves taking powers of reduced density matrices in a particular geometric configuration. This quantity, which we denote by ω α , β \omega_{\alpha,\beta} , is parameterized by two positive real numbers α , β \alpha,\beta , and can be seen as a “Rényi-like” generalization of the modular commutator – another entanglement probe proposed as a way to compute the chiral central charge from a bulk wave function. We obtain analytic expressions for ω α , β \omega_{\alpha,\beta} for gapped ground states of non-interacting fermion Hamiltonians as well as ground states of string-net models. In both cases, we find that ω α , β \omega_{\alpha,\beta} takes a universal value related to the chiral central charge. For integer values of α \alpha and β \beta , our quantity ω α , β \omega_{\alpha,\beta} can be expressed as an expectation value of permutation operators acting on an appropriate replica system, providing a natural route to measuring ω α , β \omega_{\alpha,\beta} in numerical simulations and potentially, experiments.



I
Introduction

The chiral central charge
c_{-}
is a rational-valued topological invariant that characterizes two-dimensional (2D) gapped quantum many-body systems. The standard definition of
c_{-}
is in terms of the thermal Hall conductance at low temperatures. Specifically,
c_{-}
is defined as the dimensionless ratio
c_{-}=\frac{\kappa_{H}(T)}{\pi^{2}k_{B}^{2}T/3h}
, where
\kappa_{H}(T)
is the thermal Hall conductance at temperature
T
, and
T
is assumed to be much smaller than the bulk gap.
11
;
2
;
16

Although it may not be obvious from the above definition,
c_{-}
is believed to depend only on the bulk ground state.
16
;
12
Therefore, it should be possible in principle to compute
c_{-}
directly from a ground state wave function on an infinite 2D plane.

A concrete proposal for such a bulk formula for
c_{-}
was made by Refs.
13
;
14
. This proposal is based on a quantity known as the “modular commutator.” Let
\rho
be a density operator for a gapped ground state defined on an infinite 2D lattice and let
A,B,C
be three non-overlapping subsets of the lattice, arranged as in Fig.
1
. The modular commutator
J
is defined by

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=i\langle[\ln\rho_{AB},\ln\rho_{BC}]\rangle,

(1)

where
\rho_{R}
is the reduced density operator on region
R
, and
\langle O\rangle=\text{Tr}(O\rho)
.
Refs.
13
;
14
conjectured that in the limit of large
A,B,C
, the modular commutator approaches a universal value proportional to the chiral central charge of the ground state:

\displaystyle J=\frac{\pi}{3}c_{-}.

(2)

This conjecture is supported by several pieces of evidence including (i) a derivation of (
2
)
13
;
14
in the case where
\rho
satisfies the entanglement bootstrap axioms
23
, (ii) a proof in the case where
\rho
is a gapped ground state of a non-interacting fermion Hamiltonian
3
, and (iii) numerical results for a Laughlin-like ground state
14
(see also Refs.

4

In this paper we propose a Rényi-like generalization of the modular commutator. Our Rényi modular commutator,
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

To investigate this Rényi modular commutator, we compute

\begin{gathered}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\pi i}{12}q(\alpha,\beta)c_{-}\right\},\\

More precisely, in the non-interacting fermion case, we show that
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}=\exp[-\frac{\pi i}{24}q(\alpha,\beta)\nu(P)]

\nu(P)

We also find several other pieces of evidence for Eq. (

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

Despite this evidence, the above formula for
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

Instead, the most optimistic scenario is that (

\alpha,\beta

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

II
General properties of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}

II.1
Consistency checks

The Rényi modular commutator
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
has several properties that are necessary for any consistent formula for
c_{-}
. First,
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
is multiplicative under “stacking”, i.e. it is multiplicative under tensoring two decoupled 2D many-body systems in a bilayer geometry:

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho)\cdot\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho^{\prime})

(6)

Second,
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
is odd under time reversal in the sense that

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{T}\rho\mathcal{T}^{-1})=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(\rho)^{*},

(7)

for any antiunitary time reversal transformation
\mathcal{T}
of the form
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{K}U
where
U
is a product of single-site unitaries and
\mathcal{K}
denotes complex conjugation in a local basis.

Finally,
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}=1
for any pure state that is supported on only three of the four regions,
A,B,C,D\equiv(ABC)^{c}
. That is:

\displaystyle\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(|\psi_{ABC}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(|\psi_{ABD}\rangle)=1,

\displaystyle\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(|\psi_{ACD}\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(|\psi_{BCD}\rangle)=1,

(8)

where
|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
denotes a tripartite pure state that is supported only on
A,B,C
and similarly for
|\psi_{ABD}\rangle
,
|\psi_{ACD}\rangle
,
|\psi_{BCD}\rangle
.

Properties (
6
) and (
7
) are important consistency checks for any formula for
c_{-}
, since
c_{-}
is additive under stacking and odd under time reversal. As for property (
8
), this is a general requirement for any 2D topological many-body invariant since any such invariant must take the trivial value for a product state decorated with few-body tripartite states.

We now discuss the derivation of these properties. The first two properties, (
6
) and (
7
), follow straightforwardly from the definition of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
. To derive (
8
), suppose
\rho
is a tripartite pure state,
\rho=|\psi_{ABC}\rangle\langle\psi_{ABC}|
. Then
\rho_{AB}|\psi_{ABC}\rangle=\rho_{C}|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
and
\rho_{BC}|\psi_{ABC}\rangle=\rho_{A}|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
, so

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle=\langle\rho_{C}^{\alpha}\rho_{A}^{\beta}\rangle.

(9)

Next, notice that the operator
\rho_{C}^{\alpha}\rho_{A}^{\beta}
on the right hand side is positive semi-definite since it is a product of two commuting, positive semi-definite operators. Furthermore, one can check that
|\psi_{ABC}\rangle
has no support on the null space of
\rho_{C}^{\alpha}\rho_{A}^{\beta}
. It follows that the right hand side of (
9
) is strictly positive so that
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}=1
. A similar argument applies for the other tripartite states,
|\psi_{ABD}\rangle
,
|\psi_{ACD}\rangle
, and
|\psi_{BCD}\rangle
: in each case we can rewrite
\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle
as an expectation value of the form
\langle\rho_{R}^{\alpha}\rho_{R^{\prime}}^{\beta}\rangle
where
R,R^{\prime}
are disjoint regions.

II.2
Replica representation

We now show that when
\alpha=m,\ \beta=n
, where
m,n
are positive integers,
\omega_{m,n}
can be written in terms of the expectation value of a permutation operator acting in an appropriate replica system. Specifically, consider a replica system built out of
m+n+1
copies of our original state
\rho
. Then we can write

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{AB}^{m}\rho_{BC}^{n}\rangle=\text{Tr}(\pi_{A}^{\sigma_{a}}\pi_{B}^{\sigma_{b}}\pi_{C}^{\sigma_{c}}\rho^{\otimes(m+n+1)})

(10)

where
\pi^{\sigma_{a}}_{A}
,
\pi^{\sigma_{b}}_{B}
,
\pi^{\sigma_{c}}_{C}
are replica permutation operators that permute the
m+n+1
replicas of the lattice sites in regions
A,B,C
according to the following (cyclic) permutations:

\displaystyle\sigma_{a}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&m&m+n+1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&m+n+1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}m+1&m+2&\cdots&m+n+1\end{pmatrix}

(11)

Here,
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{c}
are cyclic permutations of order
m+1
and
n+1
respectively, while
\sigma_{b}
is a cyclic permutation of order
m+n+1
. The precise definition of the replica permutation operators
\pi^{\sigma}_{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma}_{R}=\prod_{i\in R}\pi^{\sigma}_{i}

(12)

where the product runs over lattice sites
i\in R
and where each
\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
is a single-site permutation operator that permutes the
m+n+1
replica sites associated with a single lattice site
i
. For bosonic systems,
\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
is defined by

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma}|q_{1}\cdots q_{m+n+1}\rangle=|q_{\sigma(1)}\cdots q_{\sigma(m+n+1)}\rangle

(13)

where
|q_{1}\cdots q_{m+n+1}\rangle
denotes a state in which the first replica lattice site is in some basis state
|q_{1}\rangle
, the second is in
|q_{2}\rangle
and so on. (Here, we drop the
i
subscript on
\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
for notational simplicity, since the discussion that follows is completely focused on a single lattice site
i
). For fermionic systems,
\pi^{\sigma}
can be defined by its action on the fermion annihilation operators
c_{1},...,c_{m+n+1}
, together with its action on the Fock space vacuum
|0\rangle
. Specifically,
\pi^{\sigma}
leaves the replica vacuum invariant, i.e.
\pi^{\sigma}|0\rangle=|0\rangle
, while it acts on the fermion operators as follows:

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma}c_{j}(\pi^{\sigma})^{-1}=\pm c_{\sigma(j)}

(14)

Here the
\pm
signs can be chosen arbitrarily with the only constraint being that there are an odd (even) number of
-
signs within each cycle of
\sigma
of even (odd) order. This sign choice ensures that expectation values of permutation operators are related to Rényi entropies in the usual way: that is, if
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&k\end{pmatrix}
is a cyclic permutation on
k
replicas, then
\text{Tr}(\pi^{\sigma}_{R}\rho^{\otimes k})=\text{Tr}(\rho_{R}^{k})
.

We should mention that, while the above replica representation (
10
) is a powerful tool, we will not use it in this paper. Instead, our computations follow directly from the density matrix representation of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
(
5
), which applies to any real numbers
\alpha,\beta>0
.

III
Computation for non-interacting fermions

In this section we prove Eq. (
5
) for gapped ground states of non-interacting fermion Hamiltonians. For clarity, we first present our derivation in the special case of charge conserving Hamiltonians (i.e. complex fermion models). We then derive Eq. (
5

III.1
Quasidiagonality and the real-space Chern number

We begin by explaining our basic setup and reviewing some useful concepts. Our main objects of interest are charge conserving fermionic Hamiltonians on an infinite two-dimensional lattice. These Hamiltonians take the form

\hat{H}=\sum_{j,k}h_{jk}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\qquad\{c_{j},c_{k}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{jk},

(15)

where the single-particle Hamiltonian
h
is an infinite-dimensional, Hermitian matrix, and we further assume finite-range hopping:

h_{jk}=0\ \ for\ \ |j-k|\geq r_{0},

for some finite
r_{0}

3
3

3

In fact, our results hold for a more general class of single-particle Hamiltonians, which only have exponential off-diagonal decay:
|h_{jk}|\leq be^{-\gamma|j-k|}
for some
b,\gamma>0
.
. We also assume that the spectrum of
h
is such that
\hat{H}
has a finite energy gap (we need not assume any translational symmetry).

The many-body ground state
|\Psi\rangle
is obtained by filling all of the normal modes corresponding to the eigenvectors of
h
with negative eigenvalues. Non-interacting fermion ground states are particularly nice to work with as their properties are fully determined by the “spectral projector”
P=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}-\operatorname{sgn}(h))
, an operator on the single-particle Hilbert space that projects onto these negative-energy modes. In particular, the two-point correlation function is given by the matrix elements of
P
:
\langle\Psi|c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}|\Psi\rangle=P_{kj}
. Since
\hat{H}
is gapped, the magnitude of
P_{jk}
decays exponentially in the distance between
j
and
k
:

|P_{jk}|\leq be^{-\gamma|j-k|},

(16)

for some constants
b,\gamma>0
. We call operators with such decay properties ‘‘quasidiagonal’’
4
4

4

this is a more restrictive definition than the one used in Ref.
16
, which only assumes polynomial decay in the off-diagonal matrix elements. We expect that most of our results hold for polynomial decay as well, above some suitable critical exponent.
. In the next section, we will use the following properties of quasidiagonal operators:

(i)

If
X
and
Y
are quasidiagonal, their sum
X+Y
and product

(ii)

Let

be quasidiagonal with spectrum

a holomorphic function on an open region

containing
\sigma(X)
. Then

is quasidiagonal. Moreover,

depends locally on

: for any quasidiagonal operator
V_{R}
supported in region

such that

, the matrix elements of

decay exponentially outside of region
R
.

See Appendix
A
for derivations of these properties.

An important result that follows from the quasidiagonal property of

III.2

We now compute

Step 1: Single particle formula for

In the first step of our calculation, we find a single particle formula for

Applying these identities to (

\displaystyle(P-\tilde{\mathcal{C}})^{\alpha}(P-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}})(P-\tilde{\mathcal{A}})^{\beta}

\displaystyle+\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha+1}(P-\tilde{\mathcal{A}})^{\beta}+(P-\tilde{\mathcal{C}})^{\alpha}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta+1}

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha+1}(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}})^{\beta}+(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}})^{\alpha}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta+1}

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle O_{2}

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}}+\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha+1}}{(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}})^{\alpha}}+\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\beta+1}}{(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}})^{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{\beta}.

We now use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion to write

\displaystyle=\exp\text{Tr}(iK)

\displaystyle=\exp\frac{\text{Tr}[O_{1},O_{2}]+\text{Tr}[O_{2},O_{3}]+\text{Tr}[O_{1},O_{3}]}{2}.

\text{Tr}[f(\tilde{\mathcal{C}}),g(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})]=\frac{i\nu(P)}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}[f(t)-f(0)]\partial_{t}g(1-t)dt.

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[O_{1},O_{3}]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[\ln\left(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\right)^{\alpha},\ln\left(\mathbbm{1}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{\beta}\right]

\det U_{\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{C}}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\pi i}{48}q(\alpha,\beta)\nu(P)\right\}.

III.3
Majorana fermions

We now derive Eq. (
5
) for gapped Majorana fermion models.
Specifically, we consider gapped quadratic fermion Hamiltonians on the infinite 2D lattice that take the form

\hat{H}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{j,k}h_{jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}\qquad\{\chi_{j},\chi_{k}\}=2\delta_{jk},

(29)

where
h
is a real skew-symmetric matrix with finite-range hopping.

As in the complex fermion case, the first step of the calculation is to find an expression for
\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle
in terms of the spectral projector on the single-particle Hilbert space. We outline how this is done in Appendix
E
: the main difference with the complex case is that expectation values of Gaussian operators are more naturally expressed in terms of Pfaffians, rather than determinants.
For this reason, it is more straightforward to instead compute
squares
of expectation values, which can then be expressed in the more familiar determinant formalism. In particular, we show in Appendix
E
that for the Majorana fermion model (
29
),

\begin{gathered}\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle^{2}=\det M,\\
M=(\mathbbm{1}-P)+P(P_{AB}+P_{CD})^{\alpha}(P_{BC}+P_{AD})^{\beta}P.\end{gathered}

(30)

where
P
is the spectral projector
P=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}-\operatorname{sgn}(h))
.

Next, notice that the above expression for
M
(
30
) has exactly the same form as that appearing in Eq. (
19
), and the spectral projector satisfies the same quasidiagonal properties in the Majorana basis as it does in the complex basis. We can therefore follow the same logic as in the complex case to derive

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\pi i}{12}q(\alpha,\beta)\nu(P)\right\}

for Majorana fermions. Taking the square root introduces a
e^{\pi i}
phase ambiguity, which is fixed by requiring that
\omega_{\alpha,0}=\omega_{0,\beta}=1
, leaving us with

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\pi i}{24}q(\alpha,\beta)\nu(P)\right\}.

The extra factor of
\frac{1}{2}
in the exponent reflects the fact that each Majorana mode constitutes only half of a complex fermion mode. Correspondingly, we have
c_{-}=\frac{\nu(P)}{2}
when
P
is defined the Majorana basis, and we conclude that the value of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
agrees with (
5
) in the Majorana case as well.

IV.1
Review of string-net models

We begin with a brief review of string-net models.

String-net models are exactly solvable 2D lattice models that realize a large class of topological phases with nontrivial anyon excitations. Specifically, these models are believed to realize every topological phase whose boundary with the vacuum can be gapped.

The input data necessary to build a string-net model is a set of string types,

The output of the string-net construction is a lattice spin model with nontrivial anyon excitations.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have proposed a Rényi generalization of the modular commutator,
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
, parameterized by two positive real numbers
\alpha,\beta
. Our quantity
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
takes as input a gapped many-body state defined on an infinite 2D lattice, and produces as output a
U(1)
phase. We have shown that for several classes of systems, this
U(1)
phase
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
takes a universal value related to the chiral central charge
c_{-}
via the formula (
5
). In particular, we have derived (
5
) for (i) gapped ground states of non-interacting fermion systems, and (ii) ground states of string-net models.

As we mentioned in the introduction, the relationship (
5
) between
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
and the chiral central charge
c_{-}
does not hold in complete generality: there are (fine-tuned) counterexamples to (
5
) where
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
takes on spurious values unrelated to the chiral central charge (see e.g. the examples discussed in Ref.
5
). Instead, the best we can hope for is that (
5
) holds for generic gapped states for a suitable definition of “generic.” A natural direction for future work is to understand whether this optimistic scenario holds. That is, do there exist counterexamples to (
5
) that are stable to small perturbations or are all counterexamples fine-tuned? These questions are also open for the original modular commutator
J
, but they may be easier to answer for its Rényi generalization
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Another important question is to find a more direct connection between
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
and the chiral central charge. One possible approach would be to use the alternative definition of
c_{-}
recently proposed in Refs.
24
;
25
in the case of invertible states. This alternative definition is phrased in terms of permutation symmetry defects of replica systems, so it is possible that one can make a direct connection with the replica representation for
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
(
10
).

Finally, we note that if we think of
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
in terms of the replica representation (
10
), then
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
can be regarded as part of a much larger family of quantities defined in terms of the expectation values of replica permutation operators acting on different subsystems. It seems likely that these kinds of replica permutation probes can extract more information than just the chiral central charge. In particular, Ref.
22
proposed a class of replica permutation probes that give information about topological spins and quantum dimensions of anyon excitations. It would be interesting to understand these probes in more generality.

Appendix A
Properties of quasidiagonal operators

In this Appendix we derive properties (i) and (ii) of quasidiagonal operators given in
III.1
. We restate them here for convenience:

(i)

If
X
and
Y
are quasidiagonal, their sum
X+Y
and product
XY
are as well.

(ii)

Let
X
be quasidiagonal with spectrum
\sigma(X)
and
f
a holomorphic function on an open region
D
containing
\sigma(X)
. Then
f(X)
is quasidiagonal. Moreover,
f(X)
depends locally on
X
: for any quasidiagonal operator
V_{R}
supported in region
R
such that
\sigma(X+V_{R})\subset D
, the matrix elements of
f(X+V_{R})-f(X)
decay exponentially outside of region
R
.

We begin with property (i). Since
X
and
Y
are both quasidiagonal, there exists some
b,\gamma>0
such that

|X_{jk}|,|Y_{jk}|\leq be^{-\gamma|j-k|}.

The fact that their sum is quasidiagonal is obvious from the triangle inequality. To see that their product is quasidiagonal, choose some
0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
, and note that

\displaystyle|(XY)_{jk}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l}|X_{jl}||Y_{lk}|

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}\sum_{l}e^{-\gamma(|j-l|+|l-k|)}

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}|j-k|}\sum_{l}e^{-(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})(|j-l|+|l-k|)}

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}\left(\sup_{r}\sum_{l}e^{-(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})|r-l|}\right)^{2}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}|j-k|}.

To show property (ii), we make use of a well-known fact about operators with exponential off-diagonal decay
10
: if
A
is invertible with bounded inverse, then

|A_{jk}|\leq be^{-\gamma|j-k|}\implies|A_{jk}^{-1}|\leq b^{\prime}e^{-\gamma^{\prime}|j-k|},

where
b^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>0
depend only on
b,\gamma,
and
\left|\left|A^{-1}\right|\right|
.
We then define
f(X)
via the holomorphic functional calculus:

f(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\frac{1}{z-X}dz,

where
\Gamma
is a closed, rectifiable curve in
D
that encloses
\sigma(X)
. For every
z\in\Gamma
,
(z-X)
is quasidiagonal and invertible, so there exists
b_{z},\gamma_{z}>0
such that

\left|(z-X)^{-1}_{jk}\right|\leq b_{z}e^{-\gamma_{z}|j-k|}.

Let
b_{0}=\max_{z\in\Gamma}b_{z}
and
\gamma_{0}=\min_{z\in\Gamma}\gamma_{z}
, then

|f(X)_{jk}|\leq\left(\frac{b_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma}|f(z)|dz\right)e^{-\gamma_{0}|j-k|},

so
f(X)
is quasidiagonal. To see the local dependence, choose
\Gamma
so that it encloses both
\sigma(X)
and
\sigma(X+V_{R})
. Then by the second resolvent identity,

\displaystyle f(X+V_{R})-f

\displaystyle(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\left(\frac{1}{z-X-V_{R}}-\frac{1}{z-X}\right)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\frac{1}{z-X-V_{R}}V_{R}\frac{1}{z-X}dz.

Since
(z-X-V_{R})^{-1}
and
(z-X)^{-1}
are quasidiagonal, we find

|(f(X+V_{R})-f(X))_{jk}|\leq ce^{-\lambda\max\{|j-k|,\operatorname{dist}(j,R),\operatorname{dist}(k,R)\}},

for some
c,\lambda>0
.

Appendix B
Derivation of Eq. (
19
)

In this Appendix, we derive Eq. (
19
), which we reprint below for convenience:

\begin{gathered}\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle=\det M,\\
M=(\mathbbm{1}-P)+P(P_{AB}+P_{CD})^{\alpha}(P_{BC}+P_{AD})^{\beta}P.\end{gathered}

(42)

To begin, recall that traces of Gaussian fermionic operators
\exp(\hat{O})=\exp(\sum O_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j})
can be computed via determinants on the single-particle Hilbert space. For Hermitian
O
, we can diagonalize
O
to obtain

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(e^{\hat{O}}\right)=\text{Tr}\left(e^{\sum O_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j})}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left(e^{\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{\dagger}\Gamma_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}\left(1+e^{\epsilon_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbbm{1}+e^{O}).

(43)

This extends straightforwardly to non-Hermitian
O
as well. In particular, since the product of Gaussian operators is also Gaussian, the finite temperature expectation value of a Gaussian operator is

\displaystyle\langle e^{\hat{O}}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{\text{Tr}(e^{\hat{O}}e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\det(\mathbbm{1}+e^{O}e^{-\beta h})}{\det(\mathbbm{1}+e^{-\beta h})},

where
\hat{H}
is defined as in (
15
). Taking the limit
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, we find that the vacuum expectation value can be expressed in terms of the spectral projector as

\langle e^{\hat{O}}\rangle=\det\left[(\mathbbm{1}-P)+Pe^{O}P\right].

(44)

Next, recall that the reduced density matrix
\rho_{R}
of the many-body ground state
|\Psi\rangle
is a Gaussian operator given in terms of
P
by
20

\rho_{R}=\frac{1}{Z_{R}}\exp\left(-\sum_{i,j\in R}k_{R,ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right),\quad\ k_{R}=\ln\frac{\mathbbm{1}-P_{R}}{P_{R}},

where, as in the main text,
P_{R}=RPR
and
R
denotes the spatial projection onto the corresponding region. Using this definition with Eq. (
44
), we find that the expectation value in the numerator of Eq. (
3
) is

\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle=\det\left[(\mathbbm{1}-P)+Pe^{-\alpha k_{AB}}e^{-\beta k_{BC}}P\right]\\
\times Z_{AB}^{-\alpha}Z_{BC}^{-\beta}.

(45)

The normalization constants are determined by requiring
\text{Tr}\ \rho_{R}=1
, and are given by

\displaystyle Z_{R}=\det(\mathbbm{1}+e^{-k_{R}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbbm{1}-RPR)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbbm{1}-PRP)^{-1}.

We also have the identity

\displaystyle Pe^{-\alpha k_{AB}}

\displaystyle=P\left[\left(\frac{P_{AB}}{\mathbbm{1}-P_{AB}}\right)^{\alpha}+PCD\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\frac{PABP}{\mathbbm{1}-PABP}\right)^{\alpha}PAB+PCD\right]

\displaystyle=(\mathbbm{1}-PABP)^{-\alpha}P(P_{AB}^{\alpha}+P_{CD}^{\alpha}),

which can be seen by formally expanding
(\mathbbm{1}-PABP)^{-\alpha}
in a power series and noting that
PABP=P-PCDP
. The analogous identity for
e^{-\beta k_{BC}}P
is

e^{-\beta k_{BC}}P=(P_{BC}^{\beta}+P_{AD}^{\beta})P(\mathbbm{1}-PBCP)^{-\beta}.

Inserting these into Eq. (
45
), we obtain Eq. (
42
) or equivalently Eq. (
19
) from the main text.

Appendix C
Invertibility of
M

In this Appendix, we show that

M=(\mathbbm{1}-P)+P(P_{AB}+P_{CD})^{\alpha}(P_{BC}+P_{AD})^{\beta}P

is invertible for any spectral projector
P
and any choice of regions
A,B,C,D
. This result implies that
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}
is a well-defined
U(1)
phase for general non-interacting fermion systems.

For convenience, throughout this Appendix, we use the abbreviations
X=P_{AB}+P_{CD}
and
Y=P_{BC}+P_{AD}
. We prove that
M
is invertible by showing that
PX^{\alpha}Y^{\beta}P
is bijective on the subspace
P\mathcal{H}
, i.e. the image of
P
.

First, we note that

\displaystyle PX^{\alpha}P

\displaystyle=(PABP)^{\alpha+1}+(PCDP)^{\alpha+1}

\displaystyle=(PABP)^{\alpha+1}+(P-PABP)^{\alpha+1},

which gives

2(PX^{\alpha}P)^{2}-PX^{2\alpha}P=Pf(PABP)P,

where

f(x)=2[x^{\alpha+1}+(1-x)^{\alpha+1}]^{2}-[x^{2\alpha+1}+(1-x)^{2\alpha+1}].

Next we note that
PABP
is a positive semi-definite operator with eigenvalues that lie in
[0,1]
. Also, one can check that
f(x)
is positive for
x\in[0,1]
with a minimum value of
1/2^{2\alpha}
. We therefore have the inequality

2PX^{\alpha}PX^{\alpha}P\geq PX^{2\alpha}P+\frac{1}{2^{2\alpha}}P\\

and therefore

PX^{\alpha}PX^{\alpha}P\geq PX^{\alpha}(\mathbbm{1}-P)X^{\alpha}P+\frac{1}{2^{2\alpha}}P,

where
A\geq B
means that
A-B
is positive semi-definite. We also have the analogous inequality for
Y^{\beta}
. We deduce then that for any unit vector
|v\rangle\in P\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\left|PX^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|^{2}&\geq\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)X^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{2\alpha}}\\
\left|\left|PY^{\beta}|v\rangle\right|\right|^{2}&\geq\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|v\rangle\right|\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{2\beta}}.\end{split}

(46)

In particular, this tells us that
X^{\alpha}
and
Y^{\beta}
are injective on
P\mathcal{H}
and that the principal angles between the subspaces
X^{\alpha}P\mathcal{H}
and
P\mathcal{H}
, as well as the principal angles between the subspaces
Y^{\beta}P\mathcal{H}
and
P\mathcal{H}
are all strictly less than
\pi/4
. This bound on the principle angles proves the claim: it follows that
X^{\alpha}P\mathcal{H}
and
Y^{\beta}P\mathcal{H}
are strictly non-orthogonal to each other, in the sense that there is no
|y\rangle\in Y^{\beta}P\mathcal{H}
that is in the null space of
PX^{\alpha}
and there is no
|x\rangle\in X^{\alpha}P\mathcal{H}
that is in the null space of
PY^{\beta}
.

For an explicit lower bound on the spectrum, note that Eq. (
46
) implies
PX^{\alpha}P
and
PY^{\beta}P
are both invertible on
P\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, for any unit vector
|w\rangle\in P\mathcal{H}
there exists
|v\rangle\in P\mathcal{H}
such that
PX^{\alpha}|v\rangle=PY^{\beta}|w\rangle
. For such a
|v\rangle
, we find from the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities

\displaystyle\left|\left|PX^{\alpha}Y^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|

\displaystyle\geq|\langle v|X^{\alpha}Y^{\beta}|w\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq|\langle v|X^{\alpha}PY^{\beta}|w\rangle|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-|\langle v|X^{\alpha}(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|w\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\left|PY^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|^{2}-|\langle v|X^{\alpha}(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|w\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left|PY^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|^{2}-\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)X^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left|PX^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|\left|\left|PY^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|

\displaystyle\quad-\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)X^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|w\rangle\right|\right|

\displaystyle\geq 1/2^{2\alpha+2\beta+4},

(47)

Here the final bound comes from the following inequalities, which in turn follow from Eq. (
46
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\left|PX^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|&\geq\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)X^{\alpha}|v\rangle\right|\right|+\frac{1}{2^{2\alpha+2}}\\
\left|\left|PY^{\beta}|v\rangle\right|\right|&\geq\left|\left|(\mathbbm{1}-P)Y^{\beta}|v\rangle\right|\right|+\frac{1}{2^{2\beta+2}}.\end{split}

We can see that the

Lemma D.1
.

Let

and
Y

f

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle f(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}f(z)\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X}dz,

\displaystyle g(Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{2}}g(w)\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}dw,

where
\Gamma_{1(2)}
is a closed rectifiable curve in the domain of
f(g)
that encloses the spectrum of
X(Y)
. We have the resolvent commutator identity

\left[\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X},\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}\right]\\
=\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X}\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}[X,Y]\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X},

and it can be shown that
\text{Tr}|AB|\leq\left|\left|A\right|\right|\text{Tr}|B|
. Combining these two facts, we find

\text{Tr}\left|\left[\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X},\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}\right]\right|\\
\leq\left|\left|\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X}\right|\right|^{2}\left|\left|\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}\right|\right|^{2}\text{Tr}\left|[X,Y]\right|.

We therefore have that
\text{Tr}|[f(X),g(Y)]|\leq ab\text{Tr}|[X,Y]|
, where

and
b
are finite constants given by

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}|f(z)|\left|\left|\frac{1}{z\mathbbm{1}-X}\right|\right|^{2}dz,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{2}}|g(w)|\left|\left|\frac{1}{w\mathbbm{1}-Y}\right|\right|^{2}dw.

Appendix E
Majorana derivation

In this Appendix, we derive Eq. (
30
) as well as give an expression for
\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle
for Majorana fermions in terms of Pfaffians. As stated in the main text, a non-interacting Majorana fermion Hamiltonian takes the form

\hat{H}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{j,k}h_{jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}\qquad\{\chi_{j},\chi_{k}\}=2\delta_{jk},

(50)

where
h
is a real skew-symmetric matrix of even dimension that has eigenvalues
\pm i\epsilon_{k}
. Such an operator can be put into the canonical form

\hat{H}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}b_{k}^{\prime}b_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{k}\epsilon_{k}(a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}-1/2),\quad\epsilon_{k}\geq 0

so that the trace of its exponential is given by

\displaystyle\text{Tr}e^{\hat{H}}=\prod_{k}\left(e^{\epsilon_{k}/2}+e^{-\epsilon_{k}/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{k}\left(e^{\epsilon_{k}}-e^{-\epsilon_{k}}\right)}{\prod_{k}\left(e^{\epsilon_{k}/2}-e^{-\epsilon_{k}/2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh(ih)]}{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh(ih/2)]},

(51)

where
\operatorname{pf}
is the Pfaffian. This result is analytic in the matrix elements of
h
, and thus generalizes to arbitrary skew-symmetric matrices.

Let
\hat{O}
be another quadratic Majorana operator defined by
\hat{O}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{j,k}O_{jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}
, where
O
is skew-symmetric. Then

e^{\hat{O}}e^{\hat{H}}=e^{\hat{K}},\qquad\hat{K}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j,k}\ln(e^{iO}e^{ih})_{jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k},

and
\ln(e^{iO}e^{ih})
is skew-symmetric. Therefore the ground state expectation value of
e^{\hat{O}}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle e^{\hat{O}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\text{Tr}(e^{\hat{O}}e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh\ln(e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih})]}{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh\frac{1}{2}\ln(e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih})]}\frac{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh(-\beta ih/2)]}{\operatorname{pf}[\sinh(-\beta ih)]}.

As mentioned in the main text, rather than evaluate this equation further, it is often easier to instead compute the square using the identity
\operatorname{pf}(A)^{2}=\det A
, and then determine the square root via continuity. Inserting this identity above, we find

\displaystyle\langle e^{\hat{O}}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\det[2\cosh\frac{1}{2}\ln(e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih})]}{\det[{2\cosh(-\beta ih/2)}]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\det[\mathbbm{1}+e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih}]}{\det[\mathbbm{1}+e^{-\beta ih}]}\frac{\det[e^{-\frac{1}{2}\ln(e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih})}]}{\det[e^{-\beta ih/2}]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\det[\mathbbm{1}+e^{iO}e^{-\beta ih}]}{\det[\mathbbm{1}+e^{-\beta ih}]}

\displaystyle=\det[(\mathbbm{1}-P)+Pe^{iO}P],

where the third equality comes from the fact that the determinant of an exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is 1.

For non-interacting Majorana fermions, reduced density matrices of the ground state take the form

\rho_{R}=\frac{1}{Z_{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{4}\sum_{j,k\in R}k_{R,jk}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}\right),\ \ ik_{R}=\ln\frac{\mathbbm{1}-P_{R}}{P_{R}}.

Note that
ik_{R}
is skew-symmetric since
P^{T}=\mathbbm{1}-P
. We can therefore replace
e^{\hat{O}}
with
\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}
in the above equations to find expressions for
\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle
and
\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle^{2}
. For the square, we find

\langle\rho_{AB}^{\alpha}\rho_{BC}^{\beta}\rangle^{2}=\det\left[(\mathbbm{1}-P)+Pe^{-\alpha ik_{AB}}e^{-\beta ik_{BC}}P\right]\\
\times Z_{AB}^{-\alpha}Z_{BC}^{-\beta},

which has the same right-hand side as Eq. (
45
) for complex fermions. From this point, we simply repeat the same steps following (
45
) to derive Eq. (
30
).
