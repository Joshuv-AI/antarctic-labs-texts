Detecting non-uniform patterns on high-dimensional hyperspheres
Tiefeng Jiang, Tuan Pham

Abstract
We propose a new probabilistic characterization of the uniform distribution on hyperspheres in terms of its inner product, extending the ideas of [ 12 , 13 ] in a data-driven manner. Using this characterization, we define a new distance that quantifies the deviation of an arbitrary distribution from uniformity. As an application, we construct a novel nonparametric test for the uniformity testing problem: determining whether a set of n n i.i.d. random points on the p p -dimensional hypersphere is approximately uniformly distributed. The proposed test is based on a degenerate U-process and is universally consistent in fixed-dimensional settings. Furthermore, in high-dimensional settings, it stands apart from existing tests with its simple implementation and asymptotic theory, while also possessing a model-free consistency property. Specifically, it can detect any alternative outside a ball of radius n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} with respect to the proposed distance.

Introduction

Testing whether a sample from an unknown distribution is uniformly distributed over a domain is a classical problem in statistical theory. In the discrete case, this problem has been extensively studied by statisticians, computer scientists, and probabilists; see
[
5
,
2
]
and references therein. For the continuous case, one of the most common and intriguing settings is the unit hypersphere, not only because of its rich mathematical structure but also due to its importance in statistical analysis on non-Euclidean spaces. Below, we briefly formulate the problem and review relevant literature.

Consider the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|x\|_{2}=1\right\}
, where
\|.\|_{2}
is the Euclidean distance. The observed data points are denoted by
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},...,\bm{X}_{n}
with
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
for all
i=1,2,...,n

p=p_{n}
is a sequence diverging to infinity. Assume that the data
\bm{X}_{i}

\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. The uniform distribution on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is denoted by
\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
. The uniformity testing problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle H_{0}:\mu=\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})\ \ \ \mbox{against}\ \ \ H_{1}:\mu\neq\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}).

(1)

In arbitrary but fixed dimensions, the class of Sobolev-based tests were introduced in

In the era of big data, there has been an increasing interest in studying high-dimensional directional statistics, which assumes the dimensions diverge to infinity. For example, in shape analysis and nonparametric statistics, a popular approach is to consider sign-based procedures, in which one projects the observations onto the hyperspheres and carries out statistical inference based on the projected data. This approach is robust in high dimensions since the concentration of measure phenomenon implies that the majority of information from the data is captured by the directions rather than the magnitudes of the observations. Let us give a brief overview about the high-dimensional directional statistics literature below.

In
[
18
]
, the author investigates the asymptotic properties of high-dimensional spherical distributions and their applications to brain shape modeling. Specifically, the study involved statistical modeling of a sample of
n=74
MRI images of adult brains. After normalization, each brain image was represented as a unit vector with dimension
p=62,501
. A natural question in this modeling task is whether some simple, well-known distributions (such as the uniform distribution) provide a good fit for the data.
Clustering analysis on large dimension hypersphere has been studied in
[

3

. Potential applications of high-dimensional the uniformity tests were illustrated in
[

, in which the authors relate the multivariate outliers detection problem to uniformity testing problem. Sign-based procedures in high dimensions have been considered in
[
51

in the context of sphericity testing and in
[
48

, where the authors propose a high-dimensional nonparametric mean test.

From a different perspective than the directional statistical viewpoint discussed above, our primary motivation for studying the high-dimensional analog of (
1
) arises from deep learning theory. In overparameterized neural networks, regularization is crucial for preventing overfitting and improving generalization. In
[
50
]
, it was shown that optimizing one-hidden-layer neural networks with approximately uniformly distributed neurons can help avoid spurious local minima. Furthermore, empirical studies in
[
37
,
38
]
have shown that regularization methods promoting uniformity among neurons effectively reduce the generalization error in deep networks. Such methods are fundamentally tied to the question of whether a random set of points on the unit hypersphere is approximately uniformly distributed. The overparameterized nature of deep networks makes it natural to study this question in the high-dimensional settings. Given the complexity of many deep networks, including heavy-tailed or strongly correlated structures (see
[
39
]
for details), we focus on detecting non-uniformity in a non-parametric manner. This perspective shifts the attention away from traditional parametric modeling goals—such as optimality and asymptotic local power within a parametric class of distributions—towards prioritizing simplicity of implementation and universal consistency.

Despite the vast literature concerning fixed-dimensional tests, much less is known about the uniformity testing problem in the high-dimensional context with diverging dimensions. When the dimension diverges to infinity, many of the existing procedures require highly non-trivial adjustments to work properly. Moreover, there is usually no tractable limiting distribution under uniformity, and the power is typically low due to the curse of dimensionality. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are only three high-dimensional tests that have been investigated in literature. We give a short overview of such tests below.

1.

Rayleigh test in
[

]
and
[

]
. This test can be formulated in terms of a U-statistic of the data points with the inner product kernel, i.e.

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{2p}}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}.

(2)

2.

Bingham test in
[
15
,

]
and
[

]
. This test is also based on a U-statistic of the data points, but with a quadratic inner product kernel, i.e.

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{p}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Big[\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{p}\Big].

(3)

3.

Packing test in
[

This test is based on the smallest angle, i.e.

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle:=p\cdot\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\right)^{2}-4\log n+\log\log n.

(4)

The asymptotic distributions of these test statistics, as well as their non-null behaviors have been studied rigorously over the last few years; see, for example,
[
14
,

,

,

,

. It is known that the Rayleigh test

, both

,

[

]
).

Each of these three tests have their own advantages and disadvantages.
However, a common disadvantage of these three tests is that they depend heavily on parametric or semiparametric assumptions of the distribution classes. Each of them only works well against certain classes of alternatives and might be powerless when the parametric assumptions are violated. This is a shortcoming in practice since one can rarely verify such model assumptions. Moreover, given the inherently nonparametric nature of the uniformity testing problem, it becomes evident that designing testing procedures should prioritize robustness and consistency against a broad range of alternatives.

The primary objective of this article is to address the aforementioned issue by approaching the problem (
1
) from a probabilistic perspective, rather than relying on the likelihood inference framework commonly used in statistics. We introduce a novel procedure, denoted as the test
\phi_{n}
, defined in (
11
) below. Unlike existing tests that are often based on likelihood inference, our test is based on a novel probabilistic characterization of the uniform distribution through the realizations of its inner product; see Proposition
1
below.
This characterization, based on symmetry, allows us to define a
pseudometric
between probability measures on hyperspheres, providing an analytical means to quantify deviations from uniformity; see (
13
) for details. Our proposed test is universally consistent in the fixed-dimensional settings. Notably, a key advantage of our test is its model-free nature: it imposes no assumptions on the underlying classes of distributions since it is not based on likelihood inference.

We would also like to point out that there are other approaches in the literature that are not based on likelihood inference, such as the Sobolev tests originally proposed in
[
27
]
and the projection-based tests introduced in
[
12
]
, with further developments in recent works
[
25
,
26
,
21
]
. Each of these approaches uses a different characterization of the uniform distribution: the Sobolev tests rely on the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, while the projection-based tests are based on the one-dimensional distributions obtained by projecting the data onto all possible directions. However, none of these tests extend easily to high-dimensional settings, as they either involve tuning parameters or require resampling methods to implement. In contrast, our proposed test offers a simple and interpretable asymptotic theory in high-dimensional settings, which we discuss below. A detailed comparison between our test and the class of projection-based tests is provided in Section
3
.

In the high-dimensional settings, our proposed test shares the doubly robust property with that of the Rayleigh test and the Bingham test, yet it is of a nonparametric nature. It is easy to implement, has no tuning parameters and does not require resampling methods to approximate the critical values. Remarkably, our proposed test enjoys a very simple asymptotic theory in the high-dimensional case. It also enjoys a consistency theory that is not restricted to any parametric class of alternatives. This feature can be considered as an analogue of the universal consistency property of Sobolev-based tests proposed by
[
27
]
and holds in both fixed and high-dimensional settings. To the best of our knowledge, only the Sobolev-based tests exhibit such a property but there is no known results regarding the extensions of Sobolev tests to the high-dimensional settings.

2.1
Notation and preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we consider the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|x\|_{2}=1\right\}
, where
\|.\|_{2}
is the Euclidean distance. We always assume without stating that the dimension
p=p_{n}
diverges to infinity. The observed data points are denoted by
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},...,\bm{X}_{n}
with
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
for all
i=1,2,...,n
. We assume that
\bm{X}_{i}
’s are drawn independently from an unknown distribution
\mu
supported on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. The uniform distribution on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is denoted by
\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
.

For a pair of data points
(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})
, we denote by
\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}
the inner product formed by
\bm{X}_{i}
and
\bm{X}_{j}
. Under
H_{0}
, the distribution of
\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}_{2}
is known to have density (see, for example, Lemma 11 and 12 in
[
8
]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a\leq\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}_{2}\leq b\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)}\cdot\int_{a}^{b}\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{\frac{p-3}{2}}d\rho.

(5)

In the formula (
5
) above,
a
and
b
are taken to be in
(-1,1)
. The CDF of a standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
will be denoted by
\Phi(t)
.

2.2
A characterization of the uniform distribution

Let us start with an important observation regarding the random inner product of two i.i.d. points on the hypersphere.

Proposition 1

Let
\nu
be any Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, and let
\mu_{0}
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. Suppose
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{Y}_{1}
are drawn independently from
\nu
, and
\bm{X},\bm{Y}
are drawn independently from
\mu_{0}
. If

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y},

(6)

then
\nu\equiv\mu_{0}
.

Proposition
1
establishes the identifiability of the uniform distribution in terms of the inner product, asserting that the distribution of the inner product uniquely characterizes
\text{Uni}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
. To the best of our knowledge, this characterization is new and may be of independent interest. Notably, we do not impose any regularity assumptions on the measure
\nu
in the statement of Proposition
1
, and the result holds even if
\nu
is highly singular. For instance, the characterization applies to probability measures supported on sets of lower dimensions. Proposition
1
allows us to construct tests that work against any type of alternative, distinguishing it from other omnibus tests in the literature, which typically require alternatives to have
L^{2}
-integrable densities with respect to
\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
.

A related but distinct characterization of the uniform distribution in terms of projections was proposed in
[
12
]
; see Section
3
for further details and comparisons with the class of projection-based tests, which are built upon this characterization. Some recent extensions of this characterization to other types of distributions can be found in
[
22
,
24
,
23
]
. Specifically, the characterization in
[
12
]
is as follows. Let
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}
be random variables on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
for some
p\geq 1
and
U\sim\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
, then under some regularity conditions,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{X}_{2}^{\top}\bm{U}\Leftrightarrow\bm{X}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{X}_{2}.

(7)

Broadly speaking, the characterization (
7
) relies on projections onto independent, uniformly distributed directions. To construct testing procedures using (
7
), one often needs to sample the direction
\bm{U}
repeatedly. In contrast, the left-hand side of (
6
) does not involve any uniformly distributed direction and is completely data-driven. Consequently, our method requires neither integrating over all directions
\bm{U}
, as was done in
[
19
,
26
,
21
]
nor sampling
\bm{U}
repeatedly, as was done in
[
12
]
.

The key challenge in proving Proposition
1
is that the arguments used to establish (
7
) are no longer applicable. Specifically, the proof of the characterization (
7
) in
[
13
,
12
]
relies on a sharp version of the Cramér–Wold device, which cannot be applied to (
6
) because
\bm{Y}_{1}
and
\bm{Y}
follow different laws. The proof of Proposition
1
, instead, relies on a subtle application of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, which will be detailed in Section
5.1
.

The first motivation for our model-free testing comes from Proposition
1
above. Intuitively, instead of carrying out inference directly on the data points, one can construct tests based on some unique features of the random inner product under
H_{0}
. Here we choose the CDF as such a feature to construct the test, which is based on estimating the CDF of
\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}
from the data and reject
H_{0}
if the estimated CDF differs too much from the true CDF
F_{0}
. Under
H_{0}
, we know that the CDF of
\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}
has a beta-type distribution with specific parameters (see equation (
9
) below). Another advantage of the random inner products is that, they are one-dimensional objects, which require low computational cost. Moreover, they become “asymptotically independent” as the dimension gets larger. The last phenomenon has been observed in a number of works related to Haar matrices; see, for examples,
[
29
,
30
,
17
]
and the references therein.

Another motivation for our model-free test is based on the following observation regarding three exsiting tests
R_{n}
,
B_{n}
and
P_{n}
defined in (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
), respectively. It is reasonable to argue that the primary cause of their model-dependent issue is the inability to capture
all the information available from the data
. In particular, the Rayleigh test
R_{n}
only takes advantage of the linear kernel, which is powerless for models involving axial data, such as the Watson distributions. On the other hand, the Bingham test
B_{n}
uses a quadratic kernel and performs poorly on non-axial data. The packing test
P_{n}
uses extreme-values, and as pointed out in
[
8
]
, it does not examine whether there is a gap in the data or not.

To fully utilize all information available from the data, we will look at the empirical distributions generated from all random inner products, instead of using a particular U-statistic. To be more precise, let
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}
be the data, we define

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\delta_{\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}}.

In the light of Proposition
1
, the empirical measure
\hat{\mu}_{n}
intuitively capture the characteristics of the underlying unknown distribution. One can then compare
\hat{\mu}_{n}
and
\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\Big|_{H_{0}}
, which is the distribution of
\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}
under
H_{0}
via the Kolomorgrov distance to see how severely the data is away from uniformity. To define the test formally, we write

\displaystyle T_{n}:

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}\Big|\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\leq t\right\}}-m(t)\Big|,

(8)

where

\displaystyle m(t):=\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\leq t\Big|H_{0}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(1-2U\leq t\right),

(9)

and
U\sim\mbox{Beta}\left(\frac{p-3}{2},\frac{p-3}{2}\right)
.

2.3
Testing procedure

In what follows, we assume
p
depends on
n
and sometimes we write
p=p_{n}
for clarity. Recall the Brownian bridge
\left\{B_{t};0\leq t\leq 1\right\}
has the same distribution as
\left\{W_{t}-tW_{1};0\leq t\leq 1\right\}
, where
\left\{W_{t}\right\}
is a standard Brownian motion. Our next result establishes the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
under
H_{0}
, namely

Theorem 1

Let
\left\{B_{t}\right\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
be a standard Brownian bridge on
[0,1]
. If
\min\left\{n,p\right\}\to\infty
, then

\sqrt{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\max_{t\in[0,1]}|B_{t}|

where
T_{n}
is defined in (
8
).

The asymptotic distribution in Theorem
1
is the same as that of the classical nonparametric Kolomorgrov-Smirnov test. The exact expression of the Brownian bridge’s maximum is known to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{t\in[0,1]}|B_{t}|>x\right)=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k+1}\exp\left(-2k^{2}x^{2}\right);

(10)

see, for example,
[
41
]
.

At first glance, one can see that
T_{n}
is the supremum of a degenerate U-process. Moreover, the underlying distribution of the U-process is also allowed to change with the dimension. To the best of our knowledge, this situation has not been explored in literature, although limit theory for non-degenerate U-processes is well-studied. To establish the null distribution of
T_{n}
, we rely on a special property of the uniform distribution on the sphere: under
H_{0}
, the normalized inner product
\sqrt{p}\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}
’s are pairwise independent and asymptotically normal. Moreover, as discussed above, the dependence between them is weaker as the dimension increases. Thus, one should expect the asymptotic distribution of
T_{n}
is the same as that of the classical Kolomorgrov-Smirnov test under the i.i.d. settings. Remarkably, Theorem 1 presents a stark departure from classical results concerning degenerate U-processes with a
fixed distribution
. Typically, the asymptotic distributions of such statistics lack closed-form expressions; see (
12
) below. However, the divergence of
p
gives a convenient asymptotic distribution as demonstrated in Theorem
1
.

Thanks to Theorem
1
and the expression (
10
), one can easily calculate the critical value
c_{\alpha}
for the
\alpha
-level test. The test rejects the null hypothesis if
T_{n}>c_{\alpha}/\sqrt{2/n(n-1)}
, where
c_{\alpha}
is chosen such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{t\in[0,1]}|B_{t}|\leq c_{\alpha}\right)=1-\alpha.

The quantile
c_{\alpha}
of the Kolomogrov-Smirnov distribution is well understood in literature and can be calculated precisely via (
10
), for example,
c_{0.95}=1.36
. This is also the critical value we choose in the simulation in Section
A
. From now on, we will use
\phi_{n}
to indicate the
\alpha
-level test based on
T_{n}
, which is

\displaystyle q_{n}(\alpha):=\frac{\sqrt{2}c_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n(n-1)}}~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~\phi_{n}:=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{T_{n}\geq q_{n}(\alpha)\right\}},

(11)

where
T_{n}
is defined in (
8
).

From Theorem
1
, one can see that the test
\phi_{n}
is doubly robust: there is no restriction on the way
p
diverges to infinity. Among the three known high-dimensional tests, only the Rayleigh test
R_{n}
in (
2
) and the Bingham test
B_{n}
in (
3
) satisfy this property. The Packing test
P_{n}
requires a mild regularity condition
p/(\log n)^{2}\to\infty
and thus, is not doubly robust. It’s worth noting that the test
\phi_{n}
is also valid in the fixed
p
scenario, albeit without the asymptotic distribution provided in Theorem
1
. Indeed, when
p
is fixed, it is known that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\max_{t\in[0,1]}|Q_{t}|

(12)

where
T_{n}
is defined in (
8
) and
\left\{Q_{t}\right\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is a stochastic process whose marginal distributions equal to a linear combination of chi-squared distributions. The asymptotic distribution in (
12
) does not have a tractable expression and Monte Carlo simulation is needed to approximate the test’s critical value.

2.4
Model-free consistency

For any two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
supported on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, define the pseudometric

\displaystyle d(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{1}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}\leq t\right)\Big|

(13)

where
\bm{X}_{1}
,
\bm{Y}_{1}
are drawn independently from
\mu
and
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Y}
are drawn independently from
\nu
.

The distance
d
in (
13
) is only a pseudometric, in the sense that
d(\mu,\nu)=0
does not imply
\mu\equiv\nu
. However, in the light of Proposition
1
, we can see that
d(\mu,\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right))=0
yields
\mu\equiv\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right)
. Thus, the pseudometric
d
can be used as a quantitative measure for the deviation from the null. Based on this pseudometric, we define a consistency criteria, namely the
\sqrt{n}
-separation condition
. The precise definition can be formulated as follows.

Condition 1
(
\sqrt{n}
-separation condition)

Given a sequence
\left\{(n,p_{n});{n\geq 1}\right\}
. Let
\mu_{n}
be a sequence of probability measures on
\mathbb{S}^{p_{n}-1}
, we say that the sequence
\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies the
\sqrt{n}
-separation condition if

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\cdot d\left(\mu_{n},\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p_{n}-1}\right)\right)\to\infty

(14)

where
d
is the pseudo metric defined in (
13
).

The
\sqrt{n}
-separation condition (
14
) measures the departure from the null hypothesis in terms of the pseudometric
d
, which is the Kolomogrov distance between the random inner products drawn under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. In particular, it requires that this distance is larger than the usual convergence rate of the central limit theorem, which is
n^{-1/2}
. Intuitively, under
H_{1}
, the data points are not uniformly distributed and are more likely to have gaps at some regions on the hypersphere. Condition (
14
) measures such gaps by looking at the Kolomogrov distance between the random inner products under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. Interestingly, condition (
14
) is of nonparametric nature and requires neither parametric assumptions nor regularities: the sequence of alternatives
\mu_{n}
may or may not have densities with respect to the uniform measure
\mu_{0}
, and it is not restricted to any parametric class of distributions that contains
\mbox{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
.

We do not require
p_{n}
converge to infinity in (
14
), and the fixed
p
setting is also covered in (
14
). The assumption that
p
is diverging is only required to control the size of the test via the asymptotic result in Theorem
1
. In the fixed
p
scenario, one can use Monte Carlo simulation to approximate the test’s critical value as stated in (
12
). If we keep
p
fixed and consider a fixed alternative, then by Proposition
1
, (
14
) always holds. Therefore, in the fixed-dimensional case, (
14
) is the same as the universally consistency property of the Sobolev tests. Furthermore, condition (
14
) remains valid in the high-dimensional settings, making it a natural analogue of the universal consistency property that operates in both fixed and high-dimensional cases. Next, given the
\sqrt{n}

is consistent.

Theorem 2

Let
\mu_{n}
be a sequence of probability measures on

which satisfies the
\sqrt{n}
-separation condition (

). Then,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}(\phi_{n}=1)=1
. Here
\phi_{n}=\phi_{n}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},\dots,\bm{X}_{n})
is the test in (

3
Comparison with projection-based tests

At a high level, our newly developed procedure follows the philosophy of projection-based tests, initially developed in
[
12
]
. Their test relies on the characterization (
7
).
This characterization can be shown using a variant of the Cramér-Wold device, although the same argument does not apply to Proposition
1
. Based on (
7
),
[
12
]
proposed a test that rejects for large values of

\displaystyle D_{n,\bm{U}}:=\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{U}\leq x\right\}-\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\bm{U}\leq x\right)\right|,

(15)

where
\bm{U}\sim\text{Uni}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
is drawn independently from the data and
\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
.

The test in
[
12
]
uses the same critical value as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. In practice,
\bm{U}
is drawn multiple times from
\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p_{n}-1}\right)
and one gets a corresponding
p
-value for every such
\bm{U}
. The test rejects if the smallest
p
-value is below a threshold. More specifically, one picks a large number
k
and draws
\bm{U}_{1},\dots,\bm{U}_{k}
independently from
\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p_{n}-1}\right)
. The test in
[
12
]
rejects at
\alpha
-level if

\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n,\bm{U}_{i}}>K_{\alpha}\Big|\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}\right)\leq c_{\alpha}

where
K_{\alpha}
is the critical value of the Kolomogrov–Smirnov test and
c_{\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the left-hand side. However, as the asymptotic theory for this test remains unresolved, computationally intensive Monte Carlo methods are often required to approximate
c_{\alpha}
.

Subsequent works, such as
[
19
,
26
,
25
]
(see also the references therein), addressed this issue by integrating over all possible directions
\bm{U}
, resulting in test statistics of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{U}}\left[\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{U}\leq x\right\}-\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{e}_{1}^{\top}\bm{U}\leq x\right)\right)^{2}w(x)dx\right],

(16)

for some weight function
w(x)\in L^{2}\left([-1,1]\right)
, where the expectation above is taken with respect to
\bm{U}
.

Test statistics like (
16
) exhibit desirable properties, similar to the Anderson–Darling and Cramér–von Mises tests. However, the expectation with respect to
\bm{U}
in (
16
) often lacks a closed-form expression, requiring Monte Carlo simulations for approximation. Additionally, their asymptotic distributions frequently involve weighted sums of chi-squared distributions, complicating the computation of tail probabilities. For example, the tests in
[
25
,
26
]
rely on Imhof’s method to approximate the critical value. Our proposed test offers two key advantages over projection-based tests:

1.

Reduction in computational cost:
Unlike projection-based methods, our test avoids sampling random directions or integrating over all possible directions, which often requires complex procedures to approximate the critical values. Theorem
1
demonstrates that when the dimension is large, the tail probabilities of our test statistic are much simpler to approximate, eliminating the need for Monte Carlo simulation.

2.

Flexibility in the high-dimensional settings:
While existing projection-based tests are valid only in fixed-dimensional scenarios, our test extends seamlessly to high-dimensional settings, including cases where
p
is large and
n
is small. Extending projection-based tests to such settings is highly non-trivial, as it requires understanding how the eigenvalues of the associated Hilbert–Schmidt operator shrink to zero as the dimension increases.

4
Conclusions and remarks

In this paper, we propose a novel test for uniformity in the high-dimensional context, which aims to relieve the model-dependent issues that existing tests suffer from. Our proposed test utilizes Kolomorgrov distance and exploits the asymptotic independence between random inner products under uniformity. It is completely model-free and doubly robust: it requires a single condition that both sample size and dimension diverge to infinity, no other restriction is imposed on the ratio between
p
and
n
. We also propose a criteria for the consistency of the test, which measures the departure from uniformity. The proposed condition is model-free and require no assumptions on the distribution class. Simulation results confirm empirically our theoretical findings. We now make some remarks.

1.

It would be of significant interest to investigate other types of distances, such as the weighted-
L^{2}
distance in Cramer-von Mises statistic and Anderson-Darling statistics. The limiting distribution under
H_{0}
can be similarly derived as was done under the Kolomogrov distance in Theorem
1
, and optimal properties can be established by choosing appropriate weight functions. We leave this as a future project.

2.

Studying the optimal properties of the test
\phi_{n}
is an interesting topic for future research. It is reasonable to conjecture that our proposed test
\phi_{n}
is optimal among the class of rotationally invariant tests and over the class of alternatives which satisfy the condition (
14
). This would likely require some new tools, especially an equivalent and more convenient formulation of the condition (
14
).

3.

It is of independent interest to extend Proposition
1
to other type of spherical distributions. We conjecture that the conclusion of Proposition
1
is still true for two arbitrary Borel probability measures, up to an orthogonal transformation.

s_{n}^{2}=n^{2}(1+o(1))\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})
and thus,

\displaystyle s_{n}^{4}=n^{4}(1+o(1))\cdot\sigma^{4},

(36)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the limit in (
28
). To bound the
4
-th moment terms in (
35
), we first note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{4})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{4})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{4})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

due to the assumption
\mathbb{E}h_{n}(\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}_{2})=0
and Lemma
2
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{3}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{3}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}^{3}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big|\bm{X}_{1}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=0.

Consequently, for some universal constant
C
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y_{n,i}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j})+C\cdot\sum_{1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{r})\cdot h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle=(i-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})+Ci^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\cdot h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{3})\Big]

\displaystyle=(i-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})+Ci^{2}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big]\right)^{2}.

where the last assertion follows from the second statement of Lemma
2
. Sum up the above display over all
2\leq i\leq n
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}Y_{n,i}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\cdot\Big(n^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})+n^{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(C_{1}+1)\cdot n^{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}),

for some universal constant
C_{1}
. This in turn yields

s_{n}^{-4}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}Y_{n,i}^{4}\leq(C_{1}+1)\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{4}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big]}{n\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}\Big[h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\Big]}

by (
34
) and (
36
). The last term in the above display goes to
0
by assumption (
29
) and thus, implies (
35
). This in turn conclude the proof of (
33
).

We now prove (
34
). Recall
Q_{n}
defined in (
32
), which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j})\Big|\bm{X_{j}}\right)+\sum_{1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1}\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{r})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{t})\Big|\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}(i-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1}\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{r})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{t})\Big|\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t}\right).

Let
\bm{X},\bm{Y},\bm{Z}
be i.i.d realizations of the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. Define

\displaystyle H_{n}(\bm{X},\bm{Y}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}\left(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Z}\right)\cdot h_{n}\left(\bm{Y}^{T}\bm{Z}\right)\Big|\bm{X},\bm{Y}\Big].

(37)

It is easy to see that for any
1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1
, we have

H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left(h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{r})\cdot h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{t})\Big|\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t}\right).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}(i-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1}H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t})

\displaystyle=s_{n}^{2}\cdot(1+o(1))+Q_{n}^{*},

where

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{1\leq r\neq t\leq i-1}H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t}).

To prove (
34
), we only need to show that
\mbox{Var}\left(Q_{n}^{*}/s_{n}^{2}\right)\to 0
. Let
A=\left\{(r,t):1\leq r\neq t\leq n-1\right\}
. Note that for any two pair
(r,t)\in A
and
(r^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in A
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t})\cdot H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r^{\prime}},\bm{X}_{t^{\prime}})\Big]=0,

unless
\left\{r,t\right\}=\left\{r^{\prime},t^{\prime}\right\}
. This is due to the assumption
\mathbb{E}h_{n}(\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}_{2})=0
and Lemma
2
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}\left(Q_{n}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{Var}\Big(\sum_{(r,t)\in A}\Big[n-\max\left\{r,t\right\}-1\Big]\cdot H_{n}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(r,t)\in A}\Big[n-\max\left\{r,t\right\}-1\Big]^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}_{r},\bm{X}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq O(n^{4})\cdot\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}),

where we use the fact that
|A|\leq n^{2}
in the last bound. By (
36
),
s_{n}^{4}=\sigma^{4}n^{4}(1+o(1))
so it suffices to verify that
\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2})\to 0
, which is the content of Lemma
4
in Section
6
. This concludes the proof of (
34
), which together with (
33
) implies (
30
).

Step 2: Convergence of finite dimensional distribution.
Fix
t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{k}
in
(0,1)
and define

\displaystyle K_{n}(t):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}}\cdot\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\right)\leq t\right\}}-t\right),

(38)

where
F_{n}
is the distribution funciton of
\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}
. We will show that

\displaystyle\Big(K_{n}(t_{1}),K_{n}(t_{2}),\dots,K_{n}(t_{k})\Big)\xrightarrow{d}\Big(B_{t_{1}},B_{t_{2}},\dots,B_{t_{k}}\Big),

(39)

where
\left\{B_{t}\right\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is the Brownian Bridge with covariance structure
\mbox{Cov}(B_{t},B_{s})=\min\left\{s,t\right\}-st
. Let
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
be the covariance matrix of the vector
(B_{t_{1}},B_{t_{2}},\dots,B_{t_{k}})
. It is easy to see that
\left\{K_{n}(t_{i})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
has the covariance structure

\displaystyle\mbox{Cov}\left(K_{n}(t_{u}),K_{n}(t_{v})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j})\leq t_{u}\right\}}-t_{u}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j})\leq t_{v}\right\}}-t_{v}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\leq t_{u}\right\}}-t_{u}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})\leq t_{v}\right\}}-t_{v}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\min\left\{t_{u},t_{v}\right\}-t_{u}t_{v},

which is the same as the Brownian bridge process
\left\{B_{t}\right\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
. Thanks to the Cramer-Wold device, (
39
) is equivalent to showing that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}K_{n}(t_{i})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_{a}^{2}),

for every
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\sigma_{a}^{2}=a^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}a
.

To prove (
39
), we apply (
30
) to the function
f_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle f_{n}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{F_{n}(x)\leq t_{i}\right\}}-t_{i}\right).

Note that
\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|a_{i}|
, thus the uniform boundedness asumption required in the previous step holds. We also need to verify (
29
), which is equivalent to

\frac{\mathbb{E}f_{n}^{4}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\right)}{n\cdot\Big[\mathbb{E}f_{n}^{2}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\right)\Big]^{2}}\to 0.

To see why the convergence above is true, we first note that the numerator is bounded since
f_{n}
’s have uniformly bounded
L_{\infty}
-norm. For the denominator, note that
\left\{B_{t_{i}}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
has the same covariance structure as
\left\{K_{n}(t_{i})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
as shown in the above. Hence it holds that

\mathbb{E}f_{n}^{2}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2}\right)=\sigma_{a}^{2}=a^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}a>0.

Therefore, (
39
) holds and hence, Condition
2
is verified.

Step 3: Tightness in
D[0,1]
.

By Lemma
6
in Section
6
, we have the bound

\mathbb{E}\left(K_{n}(s)-K_{n}(t)\right)^{4}\leq C\left((s-t)^{2}+\frac{|s-t|}{n^{2}}\right)

for some universal constant
C>0
and
K_{n}(t)
is defined in (
38
). We will use the chaining argument, the
4
-th moment bound above and Lemma
7
in Section
6
to show (
27
) for the sequence
\left\{K_{n}(t);n\geq 1\right\}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, we need to show that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\sup_{t_{i}\leq t<t_{i+1}}\Big|K_{n}(t_{i})-K_{n}(t)\Big|>\delta\right)<\varepsilon.

Fix a grid
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{m}=1
with the grid length
\Delta=\max_{0\leq i\leq m-1}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})
to be chosen later.
In the argument below,
n
is taken large enough so that
n^{-1}\leq\min\left\{\varepsilon,\delta\right\}
. We introduce a finer grid as follows. In each sub-interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, partition them into smaller sub-intervals of length
l_{n}=10^{-1}\delta n^{-1}
and set
s_{j}(i)=t_{i}+jl_{n}
, for all
0\leq j\leq N:=\lfloor(t_{i+1}-t_{i})/l_{n}\rfloor
. We adopt the convention that
s_{N}(i)=t_{i+1}
. Next, We claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t_{i}\leq t<t_{i+1}}\Big|K_{n}(t_{i})-K_{n}(t)\Big|>\delta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq j\leq N}\Big|K_{n}(t_{i})-K_{n}(s_{j}(i))\Big|\geq\delta/2\right).

(40)

To see this, note that by the monoticity of the CDF, if
s_{j}(i)\leq t<s_{j+1}(i)
, we get

\displaystyle K_{n}(t)-K_{n}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{t_{i}<F_{n}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\right)\leq t\right\}}+t_{i}-t\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n(n-1)}}\bigg[\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\left\{t_{i}<F_{n}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}_{i}\bm{X}_{j}\right)\leq s_{j+1}(i)\right\}}+t_{i}-s_{j+1}(i)+s_{j+1}(i)-s_{j}(i)\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq K_{n}(s_{j+1}(i))-K_{n}(t_{i})+\sqrt{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\cdot l_{n}\leq K_{n}(s_{j+1}(i))-K_{n}(t_{i})+\frac{\delta}{2}.

Similarly, one has

K_{n}(t)-K_{n}(t_{i})\geq K_{n}(s_{j}(i))-K_{n}(t_{i})-\frac{\delta}{2}.

The two bounds above implies (
40
). Moreover, for any
0\leq u<v\leq N
, Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|K_{n}(s_{u}(i))-K_{n}(s_{v}(i))\Big|\geq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{x^{4}}\mathbb{E}\Big[K_{n}(s_{u}(i))-K_{n}(s_{v}(i))\Big]^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{x^{4}}\cdot\bigg|\left(s_{u}(i)-s_{v}(i)\right)^{2}+\frac{|s_{u}(i)-s_{v}(i)|}{n^{2}}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{x^{4}}\cdot\Big(s_{u}(i)-s_{v}(i)\Big)^{2},

where we use the fact that
n^{-2}\leq(v-u)l_{n}=|s_{u}(i)-s_{v}(i)|
in the last line. One can then apply Lemma
7
with
u_{k}=s_{k+1}(i)-s_{k}(i)
to get an upper bound on the right-hand side of (
40
) as

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq j\leq N}\Big|K_{n}(t_{i})-K_{n}(s_{j}(i))\Big|\geq\delta/2\right)\leq\frac{16K^{{}^{\prime}}}{\delta^{4}}\cdot(t_{i}-t_{i+1})^{2},

where
K^{{}^{\prime}}
is the constant in Lemma
7
. Consequently, union bounds gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\sup_{t_{i}\leq t<t_{i+1}}\Big|K_{n}(t_{i})-K_{n}(t)\Big|>\delta\right)\leq\frac{16K^{{}^{\prime}}}{\delta^{4}}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(t_{i}-t_{i+1})^{2}\leq\frac{16K^{{}^{\prime}}\Delta}{\delta^{4}}\leq\varepsilon,

provided
\Delta\leq\delta^{4}\varepsilon(16K^{{}^{\prime}})^{-1}
. This concludes the proof of the tightness and thus (
27
) is verified. The proof is completed by applying the continuous mapping theorem.
\hfill\square

Technical results

Lemma 2

Let
\bm{X}
,
\bm{Y}
and
\bm{Z}
be i.i.d realizations of the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable function. Then, we have

•

\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})\Big|\bm{Y}\right)=\mathbb{E}f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})

almost surely.

•

\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y}
and
\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Z}
are independent.

Proof of Lemma
2
. The first claim is a consequence of the rotational invariant property. Conditioning on
\bm{Y}
, there exists an orthogonal matrix
O
such that
O^{T}\bm{Y}=\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
. Thus, with probability one, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})\Big|\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f\left(\bm{X}^{T}O^{T}\bm{Y}\right)\Big|\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{e}_{1})\Big|\bm{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X^{T}}\bm{e}_{1})\right),

where we use the fact that
\bm{X}^{T}\bm{e}_{1}
is independent from
\bm{Y}
in the last equality. Similarly, one can also show that
\mathbb{E}f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})=\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X^{T}}\bm{e}_{1})\right)
. This concludes the proof of the first claim.

For the second claim, take any bounded measurable functions
f
and
g
, by conditioning on
\bm{X}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})g(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Z})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\left(f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})\Big|\bm{X}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(g(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Z})\Big|\bm{X}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}f(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Y})\cdot\mathbb{E}g(\bm{X}^{T}\bm{Z}),

where we use the conclusion of the first statement in the last equality. This concludes the proof.

\hfill\square

A direct consequence of Lemma
2
is that, for any bounded measurable function
h
, the term
Z_{n}
defined in (
31
) are degenerate U-statistics of order
1
(see, for example, Section 5.3 of the monograph
[
16
]
for a comprehensive introduction to U-statistics and its limit theories). This fact was used frequently throughout the proof of Theorem 1. The next lemma is used in checking the conditions of martingale CLT, which was used in the proof of Theorem 1. It gives a simpler form of the distribution of joint angles.

Lemma 3

Let
p\geq 2
be an integer. Assume
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
are fixed vectors. Let
\bm{X}
be a random vector uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{p}
be i.i.d. standard normal. Set
\bm{\xi}=\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{p}\right)^{\top}
. Then, for any bounded measurable function
f(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{X},\bm{b}^{T}\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}f\Big(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b})\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b})^{2}}\,\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\Big).

Proof of Lemma
3
.
We can assume

\bm{X}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{p})^{\top}/\|\bm{\xi}\|
without loss of generality. Suppose
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
are constant vectors with
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{d}=0
. Let
A
be an orthogonal matrix of which the first two rows are
\bm{c}^{\top}
and
\bm{d}^{\top}
, respectively. Then, by the Haar-invariance of the uniform distribution on sphere,
A\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}
are identically distributed. In particular the top two entries of
A\bm{X}
, that is,
(\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{X},\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{X})
have same distribution as that of
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{\top}/\|\bm{\xi}\|.
Write

\displaystyle\bm{b}=(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})\bm{a}+\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\bm{b}-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})\bm{a}}{\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})^{2}}}.

The advantage of doing so is the trivial observation that
\bm{a}
and
\frac{\bm{b}-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})\bm{a}}{\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})^{2}}}
are orthogonal unit vectors. Hence
\bm{a}^{T}\bm{X}
and
\frac{b-(a^{\prime}b)a}{\sqrt{1-(a^{\prime}b)^{2}}}\bm{X}
have the same law as that of
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})^{\prime}/\|\bm{\xi}\|.
This implies that
(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{X},\bm{b}^{T}\bm{X})
have the same law as that of

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{b})^{2}}\,\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\Big).

As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{X},\bm{b}^{T}\bm{X})=\mathbb{E}f\Big(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b})\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-(\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b})^{2}}\,\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\Big),

where the last expectation is taken over
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{p}
, hence it is a function of
\bm{a}^{T}\bm{b}
.
\hfill\square

Lemma 4

Let
H_{n}
be defined in (
37
) for a sequence of bounded, measurable functions
h_{n}:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. Assume additionally that
\|h_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}
for some constant
C_{1}
independent of
n
, and
\mathbb{E}h_{n}(\bm{X}^{\top}_{1}\bm{X}_{2})=0
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X_{1}},\bm{X}_{2})\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof of Lemma
4
. Let
\bm{Y}
be drawn from the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
independently from
\bm{X}_{1}
and
\bm{X}_{2}
. Thanks to Lemma
3
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X_{1}},\bm{X}_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}^{2}\Big[h_{n}\left(\bm{X}_{1}^{T}\bm{Y}\right)\cdot h_{n}\left(\bm{X}_{2}^{T}\bm{Y}\right)\Big|\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}^{2}\bigg[h_{n}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\cdot h_{n}\left(\bm{X}_{1}^{T}\bm{X}_{2}\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-(\bm{X}_{1}^{T}\bm{X}_{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\Big|\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}\bigg],

where
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{p})^{\top}
is a vector consisting of i.i.d. standard normal. Set
U=\bm{X}_{1}^{T}\bm{X}_{2}
and let
f(u)
be the density of
U
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}H_{n}^{2}(\bm{X_{1}},\bm{X}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(u)\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}\bigg[h_{n}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\cdot h_{n}\left(u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\bigg]du

\displaystyle=\int_{|u|\leq\varepsilon}f(u)\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}\bigg[h_{n}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\cdot h_{n}\left(u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)\bigg]du

Lemma 5

Let
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{p})^{\top}
is a vector consisting of i.i.d. standard normal, then for any bounded, measureable function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and

\Big|\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)-\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{p}},\frac{\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\Big|\leq C\|g\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{p}+|u|\right)

for some universal constant
C>0

Proof of Lemma
5
. The conclusion follows from the following two total variation distance bounds

\displaystyle d_{TV}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{p}\cdot\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},\frac{\sqrt{p}\cdot\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right),\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\right)\right)\leq\frac{C}{p}

(41)

and

\displaystyle d_{TV}\Big(N\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&u\\
u&1\end{pmatrix}\right),N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})\Big)\leq C|u|

(42)

for some universal constant
C
and for all
p
large enough in the first estimate. We next explain (
41
) and (
42
).

The first estimate (
41
) is a consequence of Diaconis-Freeman theorem (see Theorem 2.8 in the monograph
[
42
]
and also the paper
[
17
]
). It provides a sharp bound in terms of total variation between the joint distribution of the first few entries of
\mbox{Unif}\left(\mathbb{S}^{p-1}\right)
and the standard multivariate normal random vector of the same length. The second estimate (
42
) is elementary and can be proven directly by estimating the difference between the two corresponding densities. To see this, write

\displaystyle d_{TV}\Big(N\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&u\\
u&1\end{pmatrix}\right),N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right\}-\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}-2uxy}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|dxdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right\}\cdot\bigg|1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x^{2}+y^{2})u^{2}}{2(1-u^{2})}+\frac{uxy}{1-u^{2}}\right\}\bigg|dxdy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2},

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigg|1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\bigg|\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right\}dxdy,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right\}\cdot\bigg|1-\exp\left\{-\frac{(x^{2}+y^{2})u^{2}}{2(1-u^{2})}+\frac{uxy}{1-u^{2}}\right\}\bigg|.

The term
I_{1}
is obviously of order
O(|u|)
as
u\to 0
and thus, we only need to bound
I_{2}
. In polar coordinates,

can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}r\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right\}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg|1-\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}u^{2}-u\sin 2\theta}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|d\theta dr.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|1-\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}u^{2}-u\sin 2\theta}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|1-\exp\left\{\frac{u\sin 2\theta}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|

\displaystyle+\bigg|\exp\left\{\frac{u\sin 2\theta}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|\cdot\bigg|1-\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}u^{2}}{2(1-u^{2})}\right\}\bigg|

where we use the elementary inequalities

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\mbox{const})\cdot\frac{|u|}{4\pi(1-u^{2})^{3/2}}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}r\cdot\exp\left\{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right\}\cdot\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\sin 2\theta|+r^{2}ud\theta\bigg)dr

\displaystyle\leq(\mbox{const})|u|.

This concludes the proof of (

Now we are ready to prove the main estimate in Lemma

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)-\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{p}},\frac{\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|},u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\|\bm{\xi}\|}\right)-\mathbb{E}g\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{p}},u\cdot\frac{\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{p}}+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\cdot\frac{\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\Big|

Lemma 6

Recall the processes
\left\{K_{n}(t)\right\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
defined in (
38
). Then, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(K_{n}(s)-K_{n}(t)\right)^{4}\leq C\left((s-t)^{2}+\frac{|s-t|}{n^{2}}\right),

for some universal constant
C>0
.

Proof of Lemma
6
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
0\leq s<t\leq 1
. Set

\displaystyle\eta^{(n)}_{ij}:=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{s<F_{n}(\hat{\theta}_{ij})\leq t\right\}}-(t-s).

Recall that
F_{n}
is the CDF of
\hat{\theta}_{12}
and hence, each
\eta^{(n)}_{ij}
takes two values and is bounded by
1
in absolute value. By Lemma
2
, we know that
\left\{\eta^{(n)}_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}
are pairwise independent, identically distributed centered Bernoulli random variables with parameter
t-s
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\left\{(a,b):1\leq a<b\leq n\right\}
and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(K_{n}(s)-K_{n}(t)\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=O(n^{-4})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\eta^{(n)}_{ij}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=O(n^{-4})\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{n}^{4}}\mathbb{E}\left(\eta^{(n)}_{{a_{1}}a_{2}}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{{a_{3}}a_{4}}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{{a_{5}}a_{6}}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{{a_{7}}a_{8}}\right),

where the sum is taken over all tuples in
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{4}
. The pairwise independence property makes a lot of terms in the summand above vanish. Let us perform a careful analysis on all possible terms below. Set
I=\left\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{7},a_{8}\right\}
. We consider the following cases

1.

If
|I|=2
, then all the terms have the form
\mathbb{E}\left(\eta^{(n)}_{a_{1}a_{2}}\right)^{4}
. The total contribution of such terms are of order
O(n^{-2})\cdot|s-t|
.

2.

If
|I|=3
, then up to a permutation, we can bound the expectation of the three possibilities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left(\eta^{(n)}_{12}\right)^{2}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{23}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{31}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 4|s-t|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left(\eta^{(n)}_{12}\right)^{3}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{23}\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left(\eta^{(n)}_{12}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\eta^{(n)}_{23}\right)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big[(s-t)(1-s+t)\Big]^{2}\leq(s-t)^{2}.

In this case, the total contribution of all terms of the above three types are of order
O\left(n^{-1}\cdot|s-t|^{2}\right)
.

3.

If
|I|=4
, then there are two scenarios: (i) each index shows up exactly twice and (ii) there exists at least one index that shows up exactly one time. For scenario (i), up to a permutation, all the terms have the form
\eta^{(n)}_{12}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{23}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{34}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{41}
, which we can bound its expectation as

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[\eta^{(n)}_{12}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{23}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{34}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{41}\Big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big|\eta^{(n)}_{12}\Big|\cdot\Big|\eta^{(n)}_{23}\Big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\mathbb{E}\Big|\eta^{(n)}_{12}\Big|\right)^{2}\leq 16(s-t)^{2}.

The total contribution of such terms in scenario (i) is of order
O((s-t)^{2})
. For scenario (ii), without loss of generality, we can assume the index is
a
with the corresponding
\eta^{(n)}_{ab}
. The power of
\eta^{(n)}_{ab}
can be
1,2
or
3
. If it is
3
, the term is of the form
\left(\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\right)^{3}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}
and thus, has expectation
0
. If the power of
\eta^{(n)}_{ab}
is
1
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{gh}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{gh}\Big|\left\{\bm{X}_{s}\right\}_{s\in I\setminus\left\{a\right\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\Big[\eta^{(n)}_{ab}|\bm{X}_{b}\Big]\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{gh}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

So the only non-zero terms in scenario (ii) are of the form
\left(\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\right)^{2}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}
. If
(c,d)\neq(e,f)
, the expectation is also
0
by the same conditioning argument and pairwise independence between
\eta^{(n)}_{cd}
and
\eta^{(n)}_{ef}
.

Consequently, the only non-zero terms in the case
|I|=4
are of the form
\left(\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\right)^{2}
. The total contribution of such terms is thus, of order
O((s-t)^{2})
.

4.

If
5\leq|I|\leq 8
, then there exists at least 2 indices that appear exactly one time. Moreover, the terms in this case have to be the product of at least three
\eta^{(n)}
’s. Let
a
and
b
be these two indices. If they belong to two different
\eta^{(n)}
’s, then at least one of them has power

and the expectation vanishes by conditioning on all
\bm{X}_{i}
’s except that index. If they belong to the same
\eta^{(n)}_{ab}
, since
|I|\geq 5
, the only possible terms must have the form

\left(\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\right)^{2}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}

or

\eta^{(n)}_{ab}\cdot\Big[\eta^{(n)}_{cd}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{ef}\cdot\eta^{(n)}_{gh}\Big].

In either case, the expectation is
0
thanks to the pairwise independence property and thus, the total contribution in the case

is exactly
0
.
The proof is completed.
\hfill\square

Appendix A
Simulation results and background on spherical distributions

A.1
Common classes of high-dimensional alternatives

As noted in the survey paper
[
47
]
, the uniformity testing problem (
1
) is of nonparametric nature and the class of alternatives is enormous. Despite this nonparametric nature, one often assumes concrete parametric classes of distributions which include
H_{0}
as a special case and derive testing procedures that aim to work well within those specific classes. In the following, we recall a few popular classes of alternatives in directional statistics. An exhaustive list of popular alternatives can be found in the monograph
[
40
]
. We list below several common classes of distributions that show up frequently in the directional statistics literature. An extensive simulation will be conducted in Section
A
below to compare the power of the tests across these classes of alternatives with that of existing tests.

Let us start with the class of Fisher-von Mises-Langevin (FvML) distributions. For
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and
\kappa>0
, we denote by
\mbox{FvML}(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
the class of Fisher-von Mises-Langevin distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, which can be described precisely as follows. Given a fixed pair of
(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
, the distribution of
\bm{X}\sim\mbox{FvML}(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
has the density

\displaystyle f_{FvML}(\bm{x})=C_{\kappa,p}\exp\left(\kappa\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}\right)

(43)

where
C_{\kappa,p}
is a normalizing constant and
x\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
.

Class 1
(Fisher-von Mises-Langevin distributions)

Given
n
, let
\mbox{FvML}(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
be the class of distributions with density function defined in (
43
). We allow the unknown concentration parameter
\kappa=\kappa_{n}
to be
n
-dependent and the location parameter
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{n}
to be unknown and non-random.

The FvML distributions are the simplest model for non-axial data. The parameter
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is known as the location parameter, and the parameter
\kappa>0
is known as the concentration parameter. Intuitively, if one increase
\kappa
, the distribution concentrates more and more around the location
\bm{\mu}
. A generalization of the FvML distributions in the semiparametric context was considered in
[
14
]
, and is defined via the family of density functions

\displaystyle\bm{x}\mapsto c_{\kappa,p,f}\cdot f\left(\kappa\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}\right)

(44)

where
f
is positive, strictly increasing and
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=1
. Obviously, setting
f(x)=e^{x}
gives the FvML distributions in Class
1
.

Basic properties and estimation methods for FvML distributions can be found in the monographs
[
40
,
36
]
. For testing uniformity within the FvML distributions in Class
1
, it is known that the Rayleigh test
R_{n}
defined in (
2
) is asymptotically the most powerful rotationally invariant test; see
[
14
]
. The detection rate achieved by the Rayleigh test is
\kappa\sim p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}
. Note that it is unclear in literature whether the rate
p^{3/4}/\sqrt{n}
is optimal for model (
44
), which is much boarder than the FvML distributions in Class
1
. Next, we recall a related, popular model in the settings of axial data, which is the Watson distributions.

Class 2
(Watson distributions)

The class of Watson distributions, denoted by
\mbox{Wat}(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
, is the class of distributions on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
with the density function given by
\exp\left(\kappa(\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x})^{2}\right)d\bm{x}
. Here the integration
d\bm{x}
is taken over the uniform distribution on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
. We allow the unknown concentration parameter
\kappa=\kappa_{n}
to be
n
-dependent and the location parameter
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{n}
to be unknown and non-random.

The Watson distributions are analogs to the FvML distributions in Class
1
, but they are in the axial data context. As the concentration parameter
\kappa
increases, Watson distributions concentrate more and more around the two opposite points
\bm{\mu}
and
-\bm{\mu}
. In the semiparametric context, an analogous model for the Watson distributions is

\displaystyle\bm{x}\mapsto c_{\kappa,p,f}\cdot f\left(\kappa\left(\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}\right)^{2}\right)

(45)

where the unspecified
f
is required to satisfy the same conditions as that of model (
44
). It is clear that the special case
f(x)=e^{x}
recovers the Watson distributions.

Basic properties about the Watson distributions can be found in the monographs
[
40
,
36
]
. Regarding testing uniformity, it has been shown that the Bingham test
B_{n}
, defined in (
3
), is rate-optimal within the class (
45
), and the optimal rate is known to be
p^{3/2}/\sqrt{n}
; see
[
15
]
. It is also known that the Rayleigh test
R_{n}
, defined in (
2
), is completely blind to Watson alternatives, regardless of the values of
\kappa
; see
[
14
,
15
]
.

Class 3
(Spiked covariance distributions of rank
1
)

Draw
\bm{X}\sim N(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p}+\lambda\bm{\beta}\bm{\beta}^{\top})
, where
\lambda=\lambda_{n}>0
is the signal parameter and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is the rank
1
spike vector. We say that the distribution of
\bm{X}/\|\bm{X}\|
belongs to the class of spiked covariance, denoted by
\mbox{Sp}\left(\lambda,\bm{\beta}\right)
.

Class
3
contains the projections onto the hypersphere of rank one and spiked covariance distributions. The spiked covariance distributions are one of the most common models in multivariate analysis, and the optimal detection rate for the sphericity tesing problem
H_{0}:\lambda=0
against
H_{1}:\lambda>0
is known to be
\sqrt{p}/\sqrt{n}
, under the condition that
p
and
n
are proportional; see
[
10
,
43
]
and the references therein. Projections of spiked covariance distributions are of interest in high dimensions, especially when there are outliers in the data. In this case, projection-based tests onto
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
is preferred, as pointed out by
[
14
]
, where the authors argue that “
in the high-dimensional case, since
the concentration-of-measure phenomenon there implies that information lie much more in the directions of the observations from the origin than in their distances from the origin”
, see also
[
32
]
.

Class 4
(Projected distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
)

Suppose
\nu
is a probability distribution supported on
\mathbb{R}
. Draw an i.i.d. sequence
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{p}
from
\nu
and let
\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{p})
. The random vector
\bm{Z}/\|\bm{Z}\|
is then a distribution supported on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, which is the projected distribution generated from
\nu
and we shall denote by
\mbox{Proj}(\nu)
the class of such distributions.

Projected distributions are essentially distributions obtained by radial projection of distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For more details and properties regarding projected distributions, the readers are referred to Section 9.3.3 of the monograph
[
40
]
and the references therein. In the present paper, we focus on projected distributions with i.i.d. components. The case where
\nu
is a heavy-tailed distribution is particularly interesting and we will see in the simulation studies conducted in Section
A
that the Rayleigh test and the Bingham test show trivial power while our proposed test is powerful. It is worth noting that heavy-tailed distributions are ubiquituous in many high-dimensional datasets. For example, it is believed that the underlying distributions of gene expression levels are heterogeneous, heavy-tailed and exhibit complex dependence structures; see
[
11
]
.

A.2
Simulation studies

We perform a Monte Carlo simulation study to verify the size and power of our proposed testing procedure
\phi_{n}
. Let us start with the size’s simulation. Recall the tests

Case 4: Fisher-Bingman distributions.
This class of alternative is a natural generalization of the FvML distributions, which has the density function being proportional to

\exp\left(\kappa\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}\right)d\mu_{0}(\bm{x}),

where
\mu_{0}
is the usual uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
,
\kappa>0
and
A
is a symmetric matrix of size
p
. More details and applications about Fisher-Bingham distributions can be found in page 175 from Section 9.3.3 in the monograph
[
40
]
. For this simulation, we choose
A
to be an equicorrelated covariance matrix, i.e.
A
has the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&\rho&\dots&\rho\\
\rho&1&\dots&\rho\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\rho&\rho&\dots&1\end{array}\right),

which only depends on the parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
. We will choose
(\kappa,\rho)\in\left\{(0.5,0.5),(9,0.3)\right\}
. The first pair
(0.5,0.5)
corresponds to low concentration
\kappa
and high correlation
\rho
, which means
\bm{A}
is more distinct from the identity matrix. The second pair
(9,0.3)
corresponds to high concentration
\kappa
but lower correlation
\rho
, meaning that
\bm{A}
is closer to
\bm{I}_{p}
. The result is presented in Table
5
below.

From Table

, we can see that the proposed test maintains superior power in both cases while the other tests suffer from low power in different scenarios. The Rayleigh test works very well when the concentration parameter
\kappa
is large but is blind to the correlation parameter
\rho

Case 5: Mixture of FvML and Watson distributions.
Recall the Watson distribution defined in
[
36
]
, equation (2.19), page
36
. We adopt the notation used there and denote by
\mbox{Wat}(\bm{\mu},\kappa)
to be the Watson distribution with location parameter
\bm{\mu}
and concentration parameter
\kappa
. The data we considered in this case is a Bayesian mixture of FvML distributions and Watson distributions. Although this example is not covered in our theory, the proposed test
\phi_{n}
nevertheless works very well and outperforms all existing tests. Let us provides the details of the simulation below.

Define
\bm{\mu}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
and
\bm{\mu}_{2}=(0,1,0,\dots,0)
be the two location parameters. For the concentrations parameters, we choose three sets of values
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})\in\left\{(6,20),(10,50),(15,60)\right\}
. The data is drawn in the following way: draw a Bernoulli random variable
z
with probability
1/2
. Conditional on
z
, we draw the data as

\left\{\bm{X}_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big|z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}z\cdot\mbox{FvML}(\bm{\mu}_{1},\kappa_{1})+(1-z)\cdot\mbox{Wat}(\bm{\mu}_{2},\kappa_{2}).

In other words, the data is a mixture of FvML distributions and Watson distributions. Note that this type of data does not satisfy any parametric assumptions that existing tests assume. We will see below that the power of existing tests is significantly lower due to this model misspecification. The result is presented in Table
6
below. From the table, we can see that, apart from the Bingham test
B_{n}
, none of the existing tests do much better than random guessing. The Bingham test appears to be consistent against this mixture altenatives but its power converges to unity much slower than the proposed tests
\phi_{n}
and
\phi^{F}_{n}
.
