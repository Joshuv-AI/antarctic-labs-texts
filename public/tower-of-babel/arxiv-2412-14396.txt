Fingerprinting Codes Meet Geometry: Improved Lower Bounds for Private Query Release and Adaptive Data Analysis
Xin Lyu, Kunal Talwar

Abstract
Fingerprinting codes are a crucial tool for proving lower bounds in differential privacy. They have been used to prove tight lower bounds for several fundamental questions, especially in the “low accuracy” regime. Unlike reconstruction/discrepancy approaches however, they are more suited for query sets that arise naturally from the fingerprinting codes construction. In this work, we propose a general framework for proving fingerprinting type lower bounds, that allows us to tailor the technique to the geometry of the query set. Our approach allows us to prove several new results, including the following. • We show that any (sample- and population-)accurate algorithm for answering Q Q arbitrary adaptive counting queries over a universe 𝒳 \mathcal{X} to accuracy α \alpha needs Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ | 𝒳 | ⋅ log ⁡ Q α 3 ) \Omega(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|}\cdot\log Q}{\alpha^{3}}) samples, matching known upper bounds. This shows that the approaches based on differential privacy are optimal for this question, and improves significantly on the previously known lower bounds of log ⁡ Q α 2 \frac{\log Q}{\alpha^{2}} and min ⁡ ( Q , log ⁡ | 𝒳 | ) / α 2 \min(\sqrt{Q},\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|})/\alpha^{2} . • We show that any ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -DP algorithm for answering Q Q counting queries to accuracy α \alpha needs Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ | 𝒳 | ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ​ log ⁡ Q ε ​ α 2 ) \Omega(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|\log(1/\delta)}\log Q}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}) samples, matching known upper bounds up to constants. Our framework allows for proving this bound via a direct correlation analysis and improves the prior bound of [ BUV14 ] by log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)} . • For privately releasing a set of random 0 0 - 1 1 queries, we show tight sample complexity lower bounds in the high accuracy regime. In the low accuracy regime, the picture is more complex. For random queries, we show that there is a discontinuity in the sample complexity. For d d random queries over a universe 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , the sample complexity grows as Θ ε , δ ​ ( 1 α 2 ) \Theta_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}) , with no dependence on d d or | 𝒳 | |\mathcal{X}| . This new sample complexity bound, based on sparse histograms, is asymptotically better than known lower bounds for CDP. However, at α ≈ log ⁡ | 𝒳 | / d \alpha\approx\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|}/\sqrt{d} , the sample complexity jumps to Θ ε , δ ​ ( d / α ) \Theta_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\sqrt{d}/\alpha) .

Introduction

Differential Privacy
[
DMNS17
]
is standard notion of privacy in statistical databases. Differentially Private (DP) algorithms have been deployed by the US Census Bureau for publishing tables
[
AACM+22
]
, the Israeli Department of Health for publishing birth records
[
HC24
]
, and by several companies for training machine learning models and sharing statistics (see e.g.
[
App17
,
DKY17
,
XZA+23
,
ZRX+23
]
). While there is now a large body of literature on designing differentially private algorithms for numerous machine learning and statistical tasks, algorithms for answering statistical (SQ) queries date back to some of the earliest work on Differential Privacy. In this work, we revisit the question of answering statistical/counting queries under a differential privacy constraint.

In a bit more detail, we consider a dataset of size
n
where each user comes from a universe
\mathcal{X}
. A counting query
q
is defined by a function
p:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[-1,1]
and the desired answer to such a query on a dataset
D=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\}
is
q(D)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(d_{i})
. We would like to design an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm that answers
Q
counting queries, and we will measure the error of a mechanism
M
on a dataset
D
by either the
\ell_{\infty}
error
\max_{i\in Q}|M(D)_{i}-q_{i}(D)|

or the (normalized)
\ell_{2}
error
\sqrt{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{i=1}^{Q}(M(D)_{i}-q_{i}(D))^{2}}
. There has been a large body of work on understanding upper and lower bounds for counting queries under differential privacy (see
Section

1.2
).

Lower bounds for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP broadly fall into a few classes. Reconstruction arguments
[
DN03
]
show that an algorithm that is too accurate allows for reconstructing the input dataset. This approach was generalized by Muthukrishnan and Nikolov
[
MN12
]
who recognized the hereditary discrepancy of the query set as the primary object of interest. Nikolov, Talwar and Zhang
[
NTZ13
]
connected this to the geometry of the so-called
sensitivity polytope
, and showed that for
every
set of queries, the lower bound from hereditary discrepancy is within polylogarithmic factors of an appropriate Gaussian noise mechanism with a carefully chosen covariance, when
n
is large enough. For the large dataset regime, this reconstrution/discrepancy approach yields nearly tight bounds not just in the worst case, but for any given set of queries.

For small datasets, this approach is suboptimal by polylogarithmic in
|\mathcal{X}|
factors. Indeed, for a query set as simple as one-way marginals, the right achievable error rate was open until the seminal work of Bun, Ullman, and Vadhan
[
BUV14
]
. This work showed that fingerprinting codes from cryptography can be adapted to prove tight lower bounds for certain sets of queries, including the tight bounds for one-way marginals. Further, they showed that these lower bounds can be “composed” with other lower bounds to get tight worst-case bounds. This approach has been used to prove worst-case lower bounds for several problems in differential privacy and in adaptive data analysis
[
HU14
,
SU15
]
. It is natural to ask if there is a geometric query-specific generalization of the fingerprinting codes technique.

In this work, we make progress towards pushing this technique to a broader class of problems. We develop a new abstract framework for proving lower bounds using the underlying approach in fingerprinting codes. Our approach has two main technical ingredients. First, we use an
exponential tilt
to define a family of distributions over an arbitrary set of points. This yields a family of distributions over the polytope
K
of choice, that comes from the exponential family and is thus more amenable to the use of fingerprinting tools
[
KMS22
]
. Second, we relax the need for the “parameter vector” to come from a hyper-rectangle as in most previous work, using Stokes’ theorem to control the appropriate “score”. Recent work by Portella and Harvey
[
PH24
]
used Stokes’ theorem for a specific family of distributions via the Stein-Haff identity. Our work shows that this approach can be used to prove lower bounds for a large family of problems.

1.1
Our Results

Adaptive data analysis over a bounded domain.

Adaptive Data Analysis addresses the question of statistical validity of query answers in the face of adaptivity. While
k
non-adaptive counting queries can be answered to accuracy
\alpha
using
O(\log k/\alpha^{2})
samples, these bounds break down when the queries can be adaptive. Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold and Roth
[
DFH+15c
]
first showed that
O(\sqrt{k})
samples suffice to answer
k
queries. Using the private multiplicative weights algorithm of Hardt and Rothblum
[
HR10
]
, subsequent work by Bassily, Nissim, Smith, Steinke, Stemmer and Ullman
[
BNS+16
]
showed that one can answer
Q
statistical queries over a universe
\mathcal{X}
using a sample of size
O(\frac{\sqrt{\log(|X|)}\log(Q)}{\alpha^{3}})
. The currently known lower bounds of
\frac{\log Q}{\alpha^{2}}
(folklore) and
\min(\sqrt{Q},\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|})/\alpha^{2}
due to
[
HU14
,
SU15
,
NSS+18
]
leave a significant gap.
Using our new framework, we strengthen known lower bounds on private query releasing and derive new results on adaptive data analysis, closing this gap up to a
\log\frac{1}{\alpha}
factor.

Theorem 1

(Informal version of Theorem
11
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm for answering statistical linear queries over a domain
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}
operates on at most
o(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|}\log(m)}{\alpha^{3}\log(1/\alpha)})
samples. Then, there is a
(\log|\mathcal{X}|)
-round adaptive attack against
\mathcal{A}
, which sends at most
m
queries to
\mathcal{A}
and makes
\mathcal{A}
fail to be either sample- or distributional-accurate to within error
\alpha
on at least one query. The attack succeeds with probability
\Omega(\alpha)
.

Removing the sample-accurate assumption.

Although all the state-of-the-art algorithms for adaptive data analysis offer accuracy with respect to both sample and distribution, by definition, an ADA algorithm does not need to be sample-accurate. For example, suppose one splits their data set into a couple of subsets and uses them in a sophisticated manner. It could be possible that somehow the outputs of the algorithm fail to be sample-accurate w.r.t. the whole data set, but its outputs generalize nonetheless.

We prove the following lower bound against algorithms that are only distributional accurate. The bound we obtain is weaker than Theorem
1
by a factor of
\frac{1}{\alpha}
.

Theorem 2

(Informal version of Theorem
12
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the domain. Any ADA algorithm
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{X}
operating on
o(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|}\log(Q)}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)})
samples cannot answer more than
Q
adaptively generated statistical queries to within generalization error
\alpha
. Moreover, any such algorithm can be broken into
O(\log|\mathcal{X}|)
adaptive rounds.

Theorem
2
still exhibits the right dependence on the universe size and the number of queries, but is off from the upper bound by a factor of
\frac{1}{\alpha}
. On the other hand, Theorem
1
shows that a nearly tight lower bound can be achieved by additionally assuming the algorithm is accurate w.r.t. samples. Removing the “sample-accurate” assumption while retaining the correct dependence on
\alpha
is an intriguing open question.

Attack with bounded adaptivity.

The subtle distinction on whether the algorithm is sample-accurate or not has been thoroughly explored in the large-universe few-query regime. In particular, when
\mathcal{X}
is unbounded, the Gaussian mechanism can use
\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\alpha^{2}}
samples to answer
k
adaptive queries to within error
\alpha
. On the lower bound side, following
[
HU14
]
, the interactive fingerprinting code of
[
SU15
]
proved a sample complexity of
\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\alpha}\right)
. A subsequent work by Nissim, Smith, Steinke, Stemmer and Ullman
[
NSS+18
]
showed a stronger lower bound of
\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\alpha^{2}}\right)
,
assuming the algorithm is both sample-accurate and distributional-accurate
. It has been an important open question to close this gap and prove the same lower bound for algorithms only promised to be distributional accurate.

We make progress toward resolving this question by giving an attack with a bounded round of adaptivity.

Theorem 3

(Informal version of Theorem
13
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an unbounded domain. Any ADA algorithm
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{X}
operating on
o(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)})
samples cannot answer more than
\frac{k}{\alpha^{2}}
queries to within generalization error
\alpha
. Moreover, any such algorithm can be broken in
O(k)
adaptive rounds.

In Theorem
3
, let
m=\frac{k}{\alpha^{2}}
be the number of queries. In terms of
m
, this is still a
\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\alpha}
lower bound. However, the upshot is that we can achieve the attack only with
k=O(\alpha^{2}m)
rounds of interaction, where each round sends
\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
queries. If one can push our framework further by reducing the number of queries in each round to a constant, this would fully resolve the open question.

Composition of query-releasing lower bounds.

The remarkable work of Bun, Ullman, and Vadhan
[
BUV14
]
showed a nearly tight lower bound on the sample complexity for privately releasing arbitrary counting queries. Specifically, let
\mathcal{X}
denote the universe,
Q
the number of queries asked, and
\alpha
the desired
\ell_{\infty}
accuracy parameter. It is shown that any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm must use
n
samples where

n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|}\log Q}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}\right).

This is nearly tight, in the sense that there are known algorithms (
[
HR10
]
) achieving:

n\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{X}|\log(1/\delta)}\log Q}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}\right).

A natural question is whether the additional
\log\frac{1}{\delta}
factor is inherent. Given stronger lower bounds against pure-DP algorithms, the sample complexity must somehow depend on
\delta
. However, it was not known whether one can improve the sample complexity by “decoupling” the dependence on
\delta
from every other factor.

Via our framework, we bridge this final gap and show that this
\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}
multiplicative factor is inherent, for all reasonable range of
\delta
.

Theorem 4

(Informal version of Theorem
8
)
.

There is a workload matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{Q\times\mathcal{X}}
such that, for all reasonable choices of
0<\varepsilon,\alpha,\delta<1
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP query releasing algorithm for
A
requires

n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{X}|)\log(1/\delta)}\log(Q)}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}\right)

samples to answer
Q
queries to within
\ell_{\infty}
error of
\alpha
.

We can take
\delta\approx\frac{1}{\mathcal{X}}
in Theorem
4
, and recover the best-known lower bounds against pure-DP algorithms
[
Har11
]
.

A similar improvement of
\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}
can be shown for the class of two-way marginal queries. Like previous works, an average-error (
\ell_{2}^{2}
-metric) lower bound can be proved in this case.

Theorem 5

(Informal version of Theorem
9
)
.

Let
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d^{2}\times 2^{d}}
be the two-way marginal query matrix on
d
attributes. For all reasonable choices of
0<\varepsilon,\alpha,\delta<1
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP query releasing algorithm for
A
requires

n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\alpha^{2}\varepsilon}\right)

samples to answer
d^{2}
queries to within
\ell_{2}^{2}
error of
\alpha^{2}d^{2}
.

Theorem
5
is also tight up to constants. For a matching upper bound, see, e.g.
[
DNT15
]
.

The sample complexity of privately releasing random linear queries.

Suppose there are
N
types of users (i.e., the universe is of size
N
). A workload of
d
linear queries can be described by a matrix
A\in[-1,1]^{d\times N}
where
A_{i,j}
is the contribution of a type-
j
user to the
i
-th query. The private query release problem is to publish the counting queries defined by the rows of
A
, up to a small
\ell_{2}
error. It is easy to see that this problem is equivalent to outputting the mean of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where each point is constrained to be one of the columns of
A
.

Nikolov, Talwar and Zhang
[
NTZ13
]
studied this problem and showed that for every
A
, a carefully chosen Gaussian mechanism is within
\text{polylog}\,(d)
of the optimal when
n\geq d
. For smaller
n
, they showed that projecting the output of a Gaussian mechanism to the convex hull of the columns of
A
is within
\text{polylog}\,(d,N)
of the optimal. Removing the
\text{polylog}\,(N)
dependence here was open, and it would have been reasonable to conjecture that the lower bound should be improved. Using our framework, we show that for random
A\in\{-1,1\}^{d\times N}
, the lower bound can indeed be improved for a range of small-error settings.

Theorem 6

(Informal version of Theorem
14
)
.

Let
N,d\geq 0
be two integers such that
d\ll N\ll 2^{d}
. With probability
1-o(1)
over a random matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
, the following is true: for all
\alpha<o\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, any approximate-DP algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for query releasing on workload matrix
A
needs at least
\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha}\right)
samples to achieve
\ell_{2}^{2}
-error of
\alpha^{2}d
.

Theorem
6
discusses the complexity of query releasing from a sample-complexity perspective. Equivalently, we can approach the problem from an error-complexity perspective and write Theorem
6
in an equivalently way: any private algorithm with
n\geq\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
samples incurs an error of
\alpha\geq\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n})
, implying that the Gaussian mechanism is optimal when
n\geq\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
. Previously, a lower bound of the form
\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n}
was only known for
n\geq d
via hereditary discrepancy
[
MN12
,
NTZ13
]
.

For a much smaller
n
, we do not expect
\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n}
to be a lower bound. Indeed, the projection mechanism of
[
NTZ13
]
gives an upper bound of
\tilde{O}(\frac{(\log N)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n}})
for the whole range of
n
, which outperforms the Gaussian mechanism (whose error is
\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n}
) when
n\leq\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
. In light of Theorem
6
, the threshold
\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
does not appear to be coincidental: it is reasonable to conjecture that
\Omega(\frac{(\log N)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n}})
serves as a lower bound for
n\leq\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
, meeting with the
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n})
lower bound at
\frac{d}{\sqrt{\log N}}
. For some classes of structured query sets (e.g., two-way marginals), the conjecture has been affirmatively confirmed
[
BUV14
]
. See also Theorem
5
and the right part of Figure
1
.

Surprisingly, though, we show that it is the
upper bound
that can be improved for smaller
n
(corresponding to larger
\alpha)
. In particular, for all
n<\frac{d}{\log N}
, there is an error upper bound of
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
.

15
)
.

Let
N,d\geq 0
be two integers such that
d\ll N\ll 2^{d}

For

.

Future directions.

Our work leaves several open research directions. We have demonstrated that this general framework can help prove new lower bounds in differential privacy and adaptive data analysis. Our work shows that fingerprinting tools can allow us to extend instance-specific lower bounds to a wider range of parameters for random queries. However, the low-accuracy regime can sometimes admit new algorithms, and we leave to future work a better understanding of the geometric properties that determine the sample complexity of a given query set. Adaptivity is a challenge beyond data analysis, e.g. in streaming algorithms
[
BEJWY22
,
HKM+20
]
, sampling
[
BEY20
]
and dynamic data structures
[
BKM+22
]
and some of the tools developed in our work may help prove lower bounds for adaptivity in those settings as well.

[

[

Fingerprinting Codes Meet Geometry

Notation.

We will work in the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use
v_{i}
to denote the
i
-th coordinate of
v
. We will let
v_{\leq r}
denote the first
r
coordinates of
v
, and let
v_{-i}
denote all but the
i
-th coordinate. Similarly we use
v_{>r},v_{<r}
etc. For a list of vectors
v^{1},\dots,v^{m}
, we use superscript (e.g.,
v^{j}
) to index individual vectors. We write
a\circ b
to denote the concatenation of two objects in a natural manner (be it lists, vectors, or Boolean strings, etc.). For a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
covariance matrix
of
\mathcal{D}
is defined as

\mathrm{cov}(\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim\mathcal{D}}[(v-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{D}])(v-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{D}])^{\top}].

A zero-mean random variable
A
is
K
-subgaussian
if
\|A\|_{p}\leq K\sqrt{p}
for every
p\geq 1
. Equivalently,
A
is
O(K)
-subgaussian if
\Pr[|A|>Kt]\leq 2\exp(-t^{2})
for every
t\geq 1
. We say two random variables
X,Y
are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable
, if
\Pr[X\in S]\in[e^{-\varepsilon}(\Pr[Y\in S]-\delta),e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[Y\in S]+\delta]
for every measurable set
S
.

We use the following convention to ease our asymptotic analysis: We frequently write
c
(resp.
C
) to denote a small (resp. large) but absolute constant. Their appearance in different contexts might have different values. Generally, when we write a statement like “for
n\geq c\cdot m
, something is true”, what we mean is that there exists an absolute constant
c>0
, such that with
n\geq c\cdot m
, the said statement is true.

2.1
Geometry Preliminaries

Let
S
be a closed surface in the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, and
V
be the region enclosed by
S
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector field. The
divergence
of
f
at a point
x
is defined as

\mathrm{div}f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x)_{i}.

The divergence theorem then says that

\iiint_{V}\mathrm{div}f(x)dV=\oiint_{S}\langle f(x),\vec{n}\rangle dS.

Here,
\vec{n}
denotes the normal vector of
S
at a point
\theta\in S
. It might be helpful to review some typical examples of the divergence theorem:

•

A simple example is where
V
is the
\ell_{\infty}
ball of coordinate-wise bound
R/2
(namely, a hypercube centered at the origin with side length
R
), and
S
its boundary (union of
2d
faces). Since
\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}{\mathrm{Area}(S)}=\frac{R^{d}}{2dR^{d-1}}=\frac{R}{2d}
, we obtain

\text{Average divergence inside $V$}=\frac{2d}{R}\cdot\text{Average of $\langle f,\vec{n}\rangle$ on the surface}.

The divergence theorem is intuitive here: since
V
is a product region, the “contribution” to the divergence integral from the
d
coordinates is independent, allowing us to integrate each
i\in[d]
separately. Each
i\in[d]
is naturally associated with a pair of faces of
S
, namely
\{x:\|x\|_{\infty}\leq R/2,x_{i}=\pm R/2\}
. To compare the integrals on two sides, we can condition on
x_{-i}
and apply the fundamental theorem of calculus on
x_{i}
. In this proof, no knowledge of multi-variate calculus is required.

•

A more interesting case is that of
V
being an
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
R
and
S
its enclosing sphere. In this case, one can verify that
\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}{\mathrm{Area}(S)}=\frac{R}{d}
. Hence,

\text{Average divergence inside $V$}=\frac{d}{R}\cdot\text{Average of $\langle f,\vec{n}\rangle$ on the surface}.

2.2
The Exponential Family

The first step of the fingerprinting argument is designing a suitable family of distributions. We will work with a family of distributions known as “exponential family”, defined as follows.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set of vectors. We consider
K
as the possible inputs to the private algorithm and define our “base distribution” as the uniform distribution over
K
. In this work, we will largely be concerned with the case of finite
K
though the approach extends easily to the case of
K
being a bounded convex set, say, or even more broadly to an arbitrary base distribution. For every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define an
\theta
-tilt distribution
D_{\theta}(K)
over
K
as:

\Pr_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[x=v]\propto\exp(\theta^{\top}x).

Intuitively, compared with the uniform distribution over
K
, we put more “favor” on points with a large inner product with
\theta
. When the base set
K
is clear from context, we will use
D_{\theta}
to denote
D_{\theta}(K)
for brevity.

Now, let
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an algorithm that receives
n
samples from
K
and returns approximately their average. We would like to understand the privacy-utility tradeoff of such algorithms. For every
\theta
, we may define

g(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{v^{1},\dots,v^{n}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(v)]

to be the average output of
\mathcal{A}
on a dataset drawn from
D_{\theta}^{n}
. We also define

\mu_{\theta}=\mu(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{v\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[v]

as the “true mean” of the distribution
D_{\theta}
. Note that both
g(\theta)
and
\mu(\theta)
are understood as vector fields over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any not-too-small
n
, a generalization argument implies that
\mu(\theta)
and
g(\theta)
are close, assuming the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is accurate w.r.t. samples.

Defining the “score”.

The reason we work with exponential families is crystallized in the following proposition, which says that the derivative of
g(\theta)
with respect to
\theta
is related to how well
\mathcal{A}(v)
correlates with
v
. A version of this proposition appeared in
[
KMS22
]
.

Proposition 1
.

For every
i\in[d]
, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}g(\theta)_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{v^{1},\dots,v^{n}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\left[\mathcal{A}(v)_{i}\cdot\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}v^{j}_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i}\right]\right].

Summing up all
i\in[d]
, we obtain

\mathrm{div}g(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{v^{1},\dots,v^{n}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\left[\left\langle\mathcal{A}(v),\sum_{j=1}^{n}v^{j}-\mu_{\theta}\right\rangle\right].

Proof.

We use
v^{-j}
to denote a list of
(v^{1},\dots,v^{j-1},v^{j+1},\dots,v^{n})
. Using the chain rule of calculus, we consider the “impact” of differentiating
\theta
to each
v^{j}\sim D_{\theta}
. Namely, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}g(\theta)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{-j}\sim D_{\theta}^{n-1}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{j}\sim D_{\theta}}[\mathcal{A}(v)_{i}]\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{-j}\sim D_{\theta}^{n-1}}\left(\sum_{u\in K}\frac{\partial\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]}{\partial\theta_{i}}\mathcal{A}(v^{-j}\circ u)_{i}\right)

(1)

We calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{\exp(\langle u,\theta\rangle)}{\sum_{u^{\prime}}\exp(\langle u^{\prime},\theta\rangle)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\langle u,\theta\rangle)\cdot u_{i}}{\sum_{u^{\prime}}\exp(\langle u^{\prime},\theta\rangle)}-\frac{\exp(\langle u,\theta\rangle)\sum_{u^{\prime}}\exp(\langle u^{\prime},\theta\rangle)\cdot u^{\prime}_{i}}{\left(\sum_{u^{\prime}}\exp(\langle u^{\prime},\theta\rangle)\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]\cdot u_{i}-\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}[u^{\prime}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]\left(u_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i}\right).

Back to the derivation before, we proceed as

\displaystyle\eqref{equ:before-differentiate-Pr}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{-j}\sim D_{\theta}^{n-1}}\left(\sum_{u\in K}\Pr[D_{\theta}=u]\mathcal{A}(v^{-j}\circ u)_{i}\cdot(u_{i}-(\mu_{\theta}))_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{-j}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{j}}\left[\mathcal{A}(v)_{i}\left(v^{j}_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v}\left[\mathcal{A}(v)_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}v^{j}_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i}\right)\right].

This completes the proof of the first equation. For the second equation, we simply sum up all
i
’s and appeal to the definition of divergence.
∎

A more general formulation of this exists, that holds beyond exponential families. Indeed the term
(v^{j}-\mu_{\theta})
can in general be replaced by the
S_{\theta}(v^{j})=\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{D_{\theta}}[v^{j}]
(see
[
CWZ23
]
). Since this generality will not be needed in our work, we restrict our attention to exponential families.

Looking ahead, we will define
\langle v^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle
as the
score
of
v^{j}
with respect to the algorithm’s output
\mathcal{A}(v)
. We prove our desired lower bounds by deriving contradicting upper and lower bounds on the score by utilizing the privacy and accuracy guarantee of the algorithm, respectively.

2.3
Template Overview

In this subsection, we briefly introduce how to derive upper and lower bounds on the
score
of inputs.

On upper bounding the score.

Given the output of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}(v)
, it is unlikely to correlate well with a freshly sampled
v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
(since the algorithm has never seen
v^{\prime}
before). On the other hand, due to the privacy property of
\mathcal{A}
, its output is unlikely to change too much when we replace one input
v^{j}
with
v^{\prime}
. These two facts combined would give an upper bound on the score of in-sample data points.

We establish the following proposition, which would imply that the “score” of an in-sample point
v^{j}
cannot be significantly larger than that of an independent sample.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
X,X^{\prime}
are a pair of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable random variables supported on
[-B,B]
such that
\mathrm{Var}[X]=b^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[X^{\prime}]\leq O((e^{\varepsilon}-1)b+B\delta).

Proof.

We pay a price of
B\delta
to “change”
X^{\prime}
into a random variable that is
(\varepsilon,0)
-indistinguishable with
X
. For this new
X^{\prime}
, using integration by parts we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X^{\prime}-X]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{B}\left(\Pr[X^{\prime}\geq t]-\Pr[X\geq t]\right)dt+\int_{-B}^{0}\left(\Pr[X\leq t]-\Pr[X^{\prime}\leq t]\right)dt

\displaystyle\preceq(e^{\varepsilon}-1)+\int_{-B}^{-b}\left(\Pr[X\leq t]-\Pr[X^{\prime}\leq t]\right)dt+\int_{b}^{B}\left(\Pr[X^{\prime}\geq t]-\Pr[X\geq t]\right)dt

\displaystyle\preceq(e^{\varepsilon}-1)+\int_{-B}^{-b}\frac{\varepsilon b^{2}}{t^{2}}dt+\int_{b}^{B}\frac{\varepsilon b^{2}}{t^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\leq O((e^{\varepsilon}-1)b).

To justify the derivation, the first inequality used the fact
\Pr[X^{\prime}\geq t]-\Pr[X\geq t]\leq(e^{\varepsilon}-1)
for every
t
, and the second inequality used Chebyshev’s inequality. This completes the proof.
∎

We will use Proposition
2
to compare
\langle v^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle
with
\langle v^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle
where
v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
is independently drawn. These two random variables are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable by the privacy property of
\mathcal{A}
. Also, it is easily seen that the latter random variable has zero mean. Therefore, to use Proposition
2
, it remains to upper bound the variance (equivalently, the second moment) of
\langle v^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle
. One way to proceed is the following: Conditioning on
\mathcal{A}(v)
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\langle v^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle^{2}]=\mathcal{A}(v)^{\top}\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta})\mathcal{A}(v).

Therefore, it suffices to upper bound the spectral norm of the covariance of
D_{\theta}
. The way we accomplish this will depend on the geometry of
K
and how it interplays with the exponential tilt. The details are deferred to future application sections.

On lower bounding the score.

Roughly, we will lower bound
\langle v^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(v)\rangle
by utilizing Proposition
1
together with the accuracy property of
\mathcal{A}
. The first step is to consider a randomly chosen
\theta
from a region
V
and relate the score with an integral of divergence (by Proposition
1
). The latter is further related to a surface integral (on the surface
S
that encloses
V
) by the divergence theorem. Namely, for a random
\theta
drawn uniformly from a closed region
V
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\left[\sum_{j}\langle\mathcal{A}(v),v^{j}-\mu_{\theta}\rangle\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\iiint_{V}\mathrm{div}g(\theta)d\theta

(Proposition
1
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\oiint_{S}\langle g(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle d\theta

(the divergence theorem)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}[\langle g(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle].

(2)

Here, we use
\vec{n}_{\theta}
to denote the normal vector of
S
at the point
\theta
.

We further lower bound (
2
) in an application-specific way. We will always replace
g(\theta)
in (
2
) with
\mu(\theta)
(this is possible because the algorithm is assumed to be accurate, which means the difference between
g(\theta)
and
\mu(\theta)
is minor). Finally, by utilizing the definition of
D_{\theta}
and choosing an appropriate
S
, we can give a desired lower bound on
\langle\mu(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle
. For example, by choosing
V
to be an
\ell_{2}
ball and
S
be its boundary (i.e., a sphere), we get that
\vec{n}_{\theta}=\frac{\theta}{\|\theta_{2}\|}
. Since
D_{\theta}
is defined by favoring points that have a large inner product with
\theta
, we conclude that
\langle\mu(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle=\frac{1}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}}[v],\theta\rangle
is large as well.

Thus the framework needs only a few application-specific ingredients. The set
K
and the body
V
that
\theta
lies in will depend on the application. We will need to prove for each application an upper bound on the spectral norm of the covariance
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta})
, and prove the lower bound on the
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\in S}[\langle\mu(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle]
. In some of our applications, it will be convenient to deviate slightly from this general framework, in which case we may need to redo the proofs of slight variants of some of the steps in this general recipe.

2.4
Proof for the Hypercube

In this subsection, we (re-)prove the lower bound for answering one-way marginal queries using our framework. We hope it serves as a warm-up to the more complicated applications later on.

We work with an equivalent formulation of the problem, which is the task of releasing the mean of
n
vectors from a
d
-dimensional Boolean hypercube. In this case, we have the set
K=\{\pm 1\}^{d}
. Let
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{10}
. We aim to prove that there does not exist an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm which, on input
n\leq c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}
(for some small absolute
c>0
) Boolean vectors
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\in K
, with probability
1
returns a vector
\tilde{x}
such that
\|\tilde{x}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}x^{j}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{d}
. Namely, the algorithm makes constant error per query on average.

Assume for contradiction that such an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
exists. We give contradicting upper and lower bounds on the “score” of the inputs to
\mathcal{A}
. Let
V
be an
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
R=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
. We consider the exponential family
\{D_{\theta}\}
on
K
parameterized by
\theta\in V
.

Upper bound the score.

Let
\theta\in V
be arbitrary. Conditioning on
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
and the output
\mathcal{A}(x)
, it is easy to see that

\mathrm{Var}_{v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}\left[\langle v^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(x)\rangle\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}_{v^{\prime}}[(v^{\prime}_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i})\cdot\mathcal{A}(x)_{i}].

This is because, due to the structure of
K
and the definition of
D_{\theta}
, different coordinates of
v^{\prime}
are independent (basically, we have
\Pr[v^{\prime}_{i}=+1]=\frac{\exp(\theta_{i})}{\exp(\theta_{i})+\exp(-\theta_{i})}
independently for every
i
).

Note that we can assume
\mathcal{A}(x)_{i}\in[-1,1]
(if not, we can truncate it into this range without increasing error). Now,
(v^{\prime}_{i}-(\mu_{\theta})_{i})\cdot\mathcal{A}(x)_{i}
is a bounded random variable, which has variance at most
1
. Hence, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}_{v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}\left[\langle v^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(x)\rangle\right]\leq d.

By Proposition
2
, this implies that for every
v^{j}
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(v)}[\langle\mathcal{A}(v),v^{j}-\mu_{\theta}\rangle]\leq O((e^{\varepsilon}-1)\sqrt{d}+\delta d)=O(\varepsilon\sqrt{d}+\delta d).

(3)

This bound holds for every fixed
\theta
.

Lower bound.

To establish a lower bound on the score, we appeal to (
2
), which tells us that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v,\mathcal{A}(v)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\mathcal{A}(v),v^{j}-\mu_{\theta}\rangle\right]\right)=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}[\langle g(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle].

(4)

If
S
is a sphere, we have that
\vec{n}_{\theta}=\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}
. Assuming
\mathcal{A}
is accurate, we have

\|g(\theta)-\mu_{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{d}.

Hence,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}[\langle g(\theta),\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle]\geq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\langle\mu_{\theta},\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle]-\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{d}.

Let us study the term
\langle\mu_{\theta},\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle
closely. By definition of
\mu_{\theta}
, we have

\langle\mu_{\theta},\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}\left[v^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\frac{\theta_{i}}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right].

Recall that we have
\Pr[v^{\prime}_{i}=+1]=\frac{\exp(\theta_{i})}{\exp(\theta_{i})+\exp(-\theta_{i})}
, meaning that the sign of
v^{\prime}_{i}
agrees with
\theta_{i}
more often than not, which means that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}\left[v^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\frac{\theta_{i}}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right]\geq 0
is always true. Next, for every
\theta_{i}
with
|\theta_{i}|>1
, we have
\Pr[v^{\prime}_{i}=\textbf{sgn}(\theta_{i})]>\frac{e}{e+e^{-1}}>\frac{2}{3}
, which implies
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[v^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\frac{\theta_{i}}{\|\theta\|_{2}}]>\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right)
. Lastly, recall that
\theta
is a random point on a sphere of radius
R=5\sqrt{d}
. Hence, on average, there will be
\Omega(d)
coordinates
i
with
|\theta_{i}|>1
. All in all, we conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}\left[v^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\frac{\theta_{i}}{\|\theta_{i}\|}\right]>\Omega(d)\cdot\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{R}\right).

Hence, we obtain

(
4
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\cdot\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\langle\mu_{\theta},\vec{n}_{\theta}\rangle]-\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{d}\right).

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{R}\cdot\Omega\left(\frac{d}{R}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(d).

(5)

Wrap-up.

We now compare (
5
) with (
3
). Observe that (
5
) says that the total score over all inputs must be at least
\Omega(d)
for the algorithm to be accurate, while (
3
) says that each input contributes at most
O(\varepsilon\sqrt{d})
to the score (assuming
\delta
is negligible). This immediately yields that there has to be at least
n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
samples as desired.

3
Composition of Query Lower Bounds

In this section, we extend the framework introduced in Section
2
to prove our results on the “composition” of query releasing lower bounds. Compared with
[
BUV14
]
, the main innovation in our proof is to write the whole lower bound as a correlation analysis through the geometric fingerprinting framework. This section also serves as a warm-up to later applications of adaptive data analysis.

The main results covered in this section are as follows.

Theorem 8
.

Let
M,N
be such that
M\gg\log(N)
and
N\gg\log(M)
. There is a workload matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
such that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1),\alpha\in(2^{-\log^{1/9}(N)},1)
,
\delta\in(\frac{1}{N},\frac{1}{\log(N)\log(M)})
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP query releasing algorithm for
A
requires

n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)\log(1/\delta)}\log(M)}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}\right)

samples to answer all
M
queries within
\ell_{\infty}
-error at most
\alpha
.

We define two-way marginal queries. Construct a matrix
A_{TW}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d^{2}\times 2^{2d}}
where the rows are indexed by pairs
(i,j)\in[d\times d]
and columns indexed by
(x,y)\in\{\pm 1\}^{d+d}
. Then, set
(A_{TW})_{(i,j),(x,y)}=x_{i}\cdot y_{j}
. This is slightly different than the standard two-way marginals: here, every data point
(x,y)
has
2d
attributes, but we only query all the correlations crossing
x
and
y
. However, since these queries are roughly half of all two-way marginal queries over
(x,y)
, lower bounds against
A_{TW}
lift to lower bounds against the standard two-way marginal query family up to a constant factor.

Theorem 9
.

Let
A_{TW}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d^{2}\times 2^{2d}}
be the two-way marginal query matrix. For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1),\alpha\in(d^{-0.49},1/100)
,
\delta\in(2^{-\frac{d}{\log^{2}(d)}},\frac{1}{d^{2}})
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP query releasing algorithm for
A
requires

n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}\right)

samples to achieve
\ell_{2}^{2}
-error of
\alpha^{2}d^{2}
.

The proofs of both theorems are largely similar, with Theorem
8
being slightly more complicated. We prove Theorem
8
in Sections
3.1
to
3.3
. Next, we explain the necessary modifications to prove Theorem
9
in Section
3.4
.

3.1
Setup for the Fingerprinting Argument

Given
N,M,\alpha,\varepsilon,\delta
as in Theorem
8
, we choose some
m=\Theta(\log(M))
and
d=\Theta(\log(N))
(constants to be specified). We also set
k=\frac{c}{\alpha^{2}}
for some small constant
c>0
. Let
\{e^{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the standard basis and
\{u^{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a collection of
k
pairwise orthogonal Boolean vectors (e.g., Hadamard basis properly scaled). Consider now the following ensemble of vectors:

K=\left\{e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v:i\in[m],j\in[k],v\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}\right\}.

We choose
m,d
properly so that
N=2^{d}\times k\times m
and
M=2^{m}\times d\times k
.

Construction of the query matrix.

We construct the query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
now.

•

Every vector
e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v
identifies a column of
A
.

•

The rows of
A
are indexed by tuples
(h,p,q)
where
h:[m]\to\{\pm 1\}
is a Boolean predicate,
p\in[k]
and
q\in[d]
are two indices. Note that there are
2^{m}\times d\times k
such tuples.

•

Finally, for an entry of
A
indexed by row
(h,p,q)
and column
(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v)
, set the entry to be
h(i)\cdot u^{j}_{p}\cdot v_{q}
.

We aim to prove Theorem
8
for the constructed
A
, via the geometric fingerprinting framework.

Setup of the fingerprinting argument.

We define a family of distributions, which is slightly different than the standard exponential family. In particular, we choose the space of
\theta
to be

V=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}:\sum_{i}|\theta_{i}|\leq R\right\}.

We will choose
R
to be
\frac{mkd}{\sqrt{k}}
. In this way, typically every entry of
\theta
is roughly
\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
. For every
\theta
, we define a
type-conditioned
exponential distribution
D_{\theta}
as follows:

•

To sample from
D_{\theta}
, first choose a random
e^{i}
and a random
u^{j}
.

•

Then select
v\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
with probability proportional to
\exp(\langle\theta,e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v\rangle)
.

In defining
D_{\theta}
, we insist that each
e^{i}
and
u^{j}
have equal probability of being chosen. For this reason, the standard divergence-to-score lemma (Proposition
1
) does not apply as is. Still, for every
x\in\mathrm{supp}(D_{\theta})
, define the
type
of
x
as the pair
(i,j)
if
x=e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v
for some
v
. Then, let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm operating on
n
iid samples from
D_{\theta}
and outputting an
(mkd)
-dimensional vector. We can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

For every
i\in[mkd]
, it holds that

\frac{\partial\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(x)_{i}]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\left[\mathcal{A}(x)_{i}\cdot\left(x^{j}_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}\mid type(x^{\prime})=type(x^{j})}[x^{\prime}_{i}]\right)\right].

Consequently, it holds that

\mathrm{div}~\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}(x)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in[n]}\langle\mathcal{A}(x),x^{j}-\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}:type(x^{\prime})=type(x^{j})}[x^{\prime}]\rangle\right]

Proof sketch.

The proof is nearly identical to that of Proposition
1
, except for one detail: in the new setup, for every
x=e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pr[D_{\theta}=x]}{\partial{\theta_{p}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{p}}\left(\frac{1}{mk}\cdot\frac{\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)}{\sum_{v^{\prime}}\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{mk}\left(\frac{\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v)_{p}}{\sum_{v^{\prime}}\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)}\right.-

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)\sum_{v^{\prime}}\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v^{\prime},\theta\rangle)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v^{\prime})_{p}}{\left(\sum_{v^{\prime}}\exp(\langle e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v,\theta\rangle)\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr[D_{\theta}=x]\cdot\left(x_{p}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}:type(x^{\prime})=type(x)}[x^{\prime}_{p}]\right).

To prove Proposition
3
, we repeat the lines of the proof for Proposition
1
and use the new derivative formula to replace the appearance of “
\frac{\partial\Pr[D_{\theta}=x]}{\partial\theta_{i}}
” there as appropriate.
∎

Proposition
3
suggests defining the score of data points in a
type-dependent
way: given a vector
q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}
and the distribution
D_{\theta}
, for every
x\in\mathrm{supp}(D_{\theta})
, we define the score of
x
with respect to
q
as

score(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}:type(x^{\prime})=type(x)}[\langle q,x-x^{\prime}\rangle].

This new score definition enjoys all the properties we need: the score of a fresh sample is zero on average and well concentrated, while the average score of in-sample data points is significantly larger, as we will show in a moment.

3.2
“Surgery” on the Query-Releasing Algorithm

To prove Theorem
8
, we assume there is a sample-efficient private algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and try to derive a contradiction. However, the output of
\mathcal{A}
is an
M
-dimensional vector (since there are
M
queries in total), while the fingerprinting argument requires the dimension of
\theta
and that of the algorithm to match. To reconcile the mismatch, we post-process the output of
\mathcal{A}
, and get an estimate to the mean of
D_{\theta}
.

In this subsection, we interpret
K=\mathrm{supp}(D_{\theta})
as both a discrete set with support size
mk2^{d}
(the discrete perspective) and an ensemble of vectors living in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}
(the geometric perspective).

Understanding the output of
\mathcal{A}
.

Taking the first interpretation, by inspecting the design of
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
, we see that
\mathcal{A}
is answering the following ensemble of statistical queries:

\left\{h(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v)\coloneqq g(i)\cdot(u^{j})_{\ell}\cdot v_{r}\mid g:[m]\to\{\pm 1\},\ell\in[k],r\in[d]\right\}.

Note that there are
2^{m}\cdot k\cdot d
queries in total. Say we have received
\alpha
-accurate answers to these queries. Let
\{\hat{a}_{g,\ell,r}\}
be the collection of answers.

Now, we switch from the discrete perspective on
\mathrm{supp}(D_{\theta})
to a geometric perspective. Before we start to post-process
\{\hat{a}_{g,\ell,r}\}
, we denote
\mu_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}[D_{\theta}]
to be the mean of
D_{\theta}
and observe the following useful properties about it:

•

For every
i\in[m],\ell\in[k],r\in[d]
, we have
|(\mu_{\theta})_{i,j,k}|\in 0\pm\frac{1}{m}
. This is simply because each
e_{i}
is sampled by
D_{\theta}
with probability
\frac{1}{m}
.

•

For every
i\in[m]
and
r\in[d]
, we have
|\langle(\mu_{\theta})_{i,*,r},u^{j}\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{\langle u^{j},u^{j}\rangle}{k}\leq\frac{1}{m}
. This follows because
\{u^{j}\}
are mutually orthogonal, and each
u^{j}
gets sampled with probability
\frac{1}{k}
.

We apply the following two post-processing on
\{\hat{a}_{g,\ell,r}\}
to find a “nice”
\hat{\mu}
that approximates
\mu_{\theta}
well.

The reconstruction argument.

First, for every
\ell\in[k]
and
r\in[d]
, we can find a vector
\tilde{\mu}_{*,\ell,r}\in[\pm 1/m]^{m}
such that

\|\tilde{\mu}_{*,\ell,r}-(\mu_{\theta})_{*,\ell,r}\|_{1}\leq 2\alpha.

To achieve this, note that for every
g:[m]\to\{\pm 1\}
, the output
\hat{a}_{g,\ell,r}
places an inner-product restriction of
\mu_{\theta}
by requiring that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{\theta})_{i,\ell,r}\cdot g(i)\in\hat{a}_{g,\ell,r}\pm\alpha
. In light of this observation, all we need to do is to find a vector
\tilde{\mu}_{*,\ell,r}
that is consistent with all the restrictions from
\{\hat{a}_{*,\ell,r}\}_{g}
up to additive error
\alpha
. Such a vector exists because
(\mu_{\theta})_{*,\ell,r}
is one. Moreover, since every statistical test
g:[m]\to\{\pm 1\}
cannot tell
(\mu_{\theta})_{*,\ell,r}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{*,\ell,r}
apart, we know the two vectors are close in
\ell_{1}
distance
1
1

1

We note that this is essentially the reconstruction argument for query classes with large VC dimension: c.f.
[
BUV14
, Section 5.1.1]
.

A projection step.

To bound the “variance” of the score later, we want to apply one more surgery on
\tilde{\mu}
. Specifically, for every
i\in[m]
and
r\in[d]
, we consider the vector
\tilde{\mu}_{i,*,r}
and project it to the following space by minimizing
\ell_{1}
-movement:

H\coloneqq\left\{\frac{1}{km}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}u^{j}:\lambda\in[-1,1]^{k}\right\}.

Denote the resulting vector to be
\hat{\mu}_{i,*,r}
. To analyze the error, note that
(\mu_{\theta})_{i,*,r}
lies in the set
H
and it is close to
\tilde{\mu}_{i,*,r}
in
\ell_{1}
-distance. Hence, an application of triangle inequality shows that

\|\hat{\mu}-\mu_{\theta}\|_{1}\leq 2\|\tilde{\mu}-\mu_{\theta}\|_{1}\leq 4\alpha kd.

We remark here that this argument uses the assumption that the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is accurate for the population, rather than just for the sample.

Summary.

We compose the post-processing procedure above with the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. This gives us an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
which, on input
n
iid samples from
D_{\theta}
, returns an approximate average
\hat{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}
with the following guarantees:

•

For every
i\in[m]
, every
r\in[d]
and
u^{j}
, we have
|\langle\hat{\mu}_{i,*,r},u^{j}\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{m}
.

•

Letting
\mu_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}[D_{\theta}]
be the true mean, we have
\|\mu_{\theta}-\hat{\mu}\|_{1}\leq 4\alpha kd
.

For convenience, we define
\hat{\mu}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[\mathcal{B}(x)]
to be the average output of
\mathcal{B}
on a data drawn from
D_{\theta}
. We understand
\hat{\mu}
as a vector field over
\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
.

3.3
Proof of Theorem
8

We are ready to run the fingerprinting argument and finish the proof of Theorem

.

Score Lower Bound.

First, let us lower bound the surface integral and, consequently, the average divergence. Recall the space of
\theta

V=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}:\|\theta\|_{1}\leq\frac{mkd}{\sqrt{k}}\right\}.

Let
S=\partial V

•

For

\ell_{1}
-ball of radius
R
and
S

\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}=\frac{d\sqrt{d}}{R}
.

•

Back to our example,
V
is a
(mkd)

\ell_{1}
-ball of radius
\frac{mkd}{\sqrt{k}}
. Hence, it holds that
\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}=\frac{mkd\sqrt{mkd}\cdot\sqrt{k}}{mkd}=k\sqrt{md}
.

•

For every
\theta\in S
, the unit normal vector at
\theta

is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{mkd}}\textbf{sgn}(\theta)
.

Fix a
\theta

\mathrm{score}(x;q)\coloneqq\left\langle x-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}\mid type(x)=type(x^{\prime})}[x^{\prime}],q\right\rangle.

Using Proposition
3
and the divergence theorem, we obtain:

\displaystyle~~~~\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\sim D_{\theta},\mathcal{B}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j},\mathcal{B}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\left[\mathrm{div}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{B}(x)}[\mathcal{B}(x)]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left[\left\langle\frac{\textbf{sgn}(\theta)}{\sqrt{mkd}},\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\mathcal{B}(x)]\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left[\left\langle\frac{\textbf{sgn}(\theta)}{\sqrt{mkd}},\mu_{\theta}\right\rangle\right]-\frac{4\alpha kd}{\sqrt{mkd}}\right).

(6)

Here, the last inequality is true because we have shown that
\|\hat{\mu}(\theta)-\mu(\theta)\|_{1}\leq 4\alpha kd
in Section
3.2
.

Now let us study the term
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left[\langle\textbf{sgn}(\theta),\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]\rangle\right]
. By definition of
D_{\theta}
, we can first sample and condition on
e^{i}
and
u^{j}
. Then, we would like to understand the average of the following inner product (the “dot” below denotes tensor multiplication):

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left\langle\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}),\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v)\sim D_{\theta}\mid e^{i}\otimes u^{j}}[v]\right\rangle.

(7)

Over a random
\theta\sim S
, we see that
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})
is a
d
-dimensional vector that is entry-wise
\Theta(\sqrt{k})
.
2
2

2

To see this, interpret
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)
as stacking of
m

.
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot e^{i}
restricts onto one of the matrices, and
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})
further takes a signed summation of the
k

. The claim that
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})
is entry-wise

follows from the randomness of
\theta
.
Also, recall that

\Pr_{(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v\sim D_{\theta})\mid e^{i}\otimes u^{j}}[v]\propto\exp(\langle\theta\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}),v\rangle).

Over a random
\theta\sim S

is entrywise

, implying that each entry of

\textbf{sgn}(\theta\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}))
. Since
\textbf{sgn}(\theta\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}))
and
\textbf{sgn}(\theta)\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})

\displaystyle\eqref{equ:IP-after-conditioning-ei-ej}\geq\Omega(d)\cdot\Omega(\sqrt{k})\geq\Omega(d\sqrt{k}).

(8)

\eqref{equ:composition-surface-integral}\geq\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\left(\frac{d\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{mkd}}-\frac{4\sqrt{c}\cdot\sqrt{k}d}{\sqrt{mkd}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(k\sqrt{md}\cdot\frac{d\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{mkd}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(kd\right).

Score upper bound.

We now derive an upper bound on the score. We could easily repeat the argument in Section
2.4
, but then we would end up with a lower bound of the same order as
[
BUV14
]
without achieving the additional
\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}
factor.

To unleash the full power of our framework, we borrow a trick from
[
SU16
]
. In particular, we use the following connection established via group privacy.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
SU16
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta<1
. Suppose there is a
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm for private query releasing on
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{N\times M}
with sample complexity
n
. Then, for every
p\geq 1
, there is a
(p\varepsilon,\frac{e^{p\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\delta)
-DP algorithm for the same task with sample complexity
\frac{n}{p}
.

Back to our example, we pick
p=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{4\varepsilon}
. In order to prove Theorem
8
, by Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that there is
no

(\log(1/\delta),\sqrt{\delta})
-DP algorithm with sample complexity
n\leq o\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)}\log(M)}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}\alpha^{2}}\right)
, as this would “lift” to a
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)\log(1/\delta)}\log(M)}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}})
lower bound for the original parameter setting.

Suppose for contradiction that such an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
exists. The lower bound part tells us the total score must be
\Omega(kd)
. To establish the score upper bound, we need a stronger proposition than Proposition
2
, utilizing the concentration property on the score of an independent point. Namely, we claim:

Proposition 4
.

Suppose
X
and
X^{\prime}
are a pair of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable random variables supported on
[-B,B]
, such that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[X]=0
and
\Pr[X>t]\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{C^{2}}}
for every
t\geq 4C\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[X^{\prime}]\leq O(\delta B+C\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Again, we pay a price of
B\delta
to consider a random variable
X^{\prime}
that is
(\varepsilon,0)
-indistinguishable from
X
. For this new
X^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{B}\Pr[X^{\prime}\geq t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 4C\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\int_{4C\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{B}\Pr[X^{\prime}\geq t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 4C\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\int_{4C\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{B}\exp(-\frac{t^{2}}{C^{2}}+\varepsilon)dt

\displaystyle\leq O(C\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Consider now the random variable
\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime};\mathcal{B}(x))
, where the randomness is over
x^{\prime},x
and
\mathcal{B}(x)
. We first condition on
x,\mathcal{B}(x)
and
type(x^{\prime})
. Suppose
type(x^{\prime})=e^{i}\otimes u^{j}
. It remains to sample the “
v
” part and calculate the score of
x^{\prime}=e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v
. After conditioning on
e^{i}\otimes u^{j}
, the score can be equivalently written as

\langle v-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[v^{\prime}],\mathcal{B}\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})\rangle.

As
v
is sampled from a Boolean cube according to an exponential tilt, it is clearly seen that each coordinate of
v
contributes independently to the score. By the argument of Section
3.2
, each coordinate of
\mathcal{B}\cdot(e^{i}\otimes u^{j})
is bounded by
\frac{1}{m}
. Therefore, we conclude that the score of a fresh point is
T
-subgaussian with
T=\Theta(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{m})
. Using Proposition
4
, this implies that, for every in-sample data point
x^{j}
, it holds that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\mathcal{B}(x)}[\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{B}(x)]\leq O(\frac{\delta d}{m}+\frac{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}\sqrt{d}}{m})\leq O(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{m}).

But remember, the total score must be at least
\Omega(kd)
for the algorithm to be accurate. As such, we conclude that the number of samples uses is at least

\Omega\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{m}\right)^{-1}\cdot kd\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}mk}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\right),

which is what we desired, because we had set
d=\Theta(\log(N))
,
m=\Theta(\log(M))
and
k=\Theta(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}})
.

Remark

One might wonder whether one can take
\delta
to be
2^{-d/\alpha^{2}}
, and prove a lower bound of
\frac{\sqrt{\log N}\log M}{\alpha^{3}\varepsilon}
(this would imply a new lower bound against pure-DP algorithms). This is not possible under the reduction of Lemma
3.1
. Because by taking
p=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}
there, the sample complexity lower bound we aim for is of order
\frac{\log M}{\alpha}
. However, this number is so small that a sample-accurate algorithm does not necessarily generalize, and the projection step in our argument would fail.The lowest

we can “afford” is
\delta\approx 2^{-d}
, which asks us to prove a lower bound of order
\frac{\log(M)}{\alpha^{2}}
. In this regime, any sample-accurate algorithm still generalizes, and our proof technique applies.

3.4

In this section, we prove Theorem

•

•

Since we are interested in

3.4.1

Recall that
\alpha\gg\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
. We set

. Let

distance. An application of triangle inequality shows that
\|\hat{\mu}-\mu_{\theta}\|_{2}\leq 2\alpha\sqrt{kd}
.

3.4.3
Correlation Analysis

Now, let us give contradicting upper and lower bounds on the score of the algorithm.

Score lower bound.

In terms of lower bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{A}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left[\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(x)],\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left[\left\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}],\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right\rangle-\alpha\sqrt{kd}\right].

(9)

We use a similar argument as in Section
3.3
: to understand
\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}],\theta\rangle
, we first sample and condition on
u^{j}
(independent of
\theta
). Then, we would like to lower bound

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{u^{j}\otimes v\sim D_{\theta}\mid u^{j}}[v],\theta\cdot u^{j}\rangle].

Recall that
\theta\sim\partial B_{2}(0,\sqrt{d})
. Hence, each entry of
\theta
is typically
\Theta(1/\sqrt{k})
. Consequently, the vector
\theta u^{j}
is typically entry-wise
\Theta(1)
. Hence, the inner product above is
\Omega(d)
. Since
\|\theta\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, we conclude that

\eqref{equ:two-way-score-lb}\geq\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}[\Omega(\sqrt{d})-\alpha\sqrt{kd}]\geq\Omega(\frac{kd}{\sqrt{d}})\cdot\Omega(\sqrt{d})\geq\Omega(kd).

Here, the second inequality is valid so long as we choose
k=\frac{c}{\alpha^{2}}
with a sufficiently small
c>0
, so that
\alpha\sqrt{kd}
is dominated by the first term
\Omega(\sqrt{d})
.

Score upper bound.

The rest of the argument is largely similar to that in Section
3.3
. Condition on the output of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}(x)
, we argue that the score of a fresh point
x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
is well concentrated. Note that, conditioning on the type of
x^{\prime}
being
u^{j}
, the score of the point
x^{\prime}=u^{j}\otimes v
can be equivalently written as

\langle v-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v^{\prime}}[v^{\prime}],\mathcal{A}(x)\cdot u^{j}\rangle.

Since
v
is sampled from the Boolean cube according to an exponential tilt, each coordinate of
v
contributes independently to the score. Since each coordinate of
\mathcal{A}(x)\cdot u^{j}
is bounded by
1
(see Section
3.4.2
), we conclude that the score of a random
v
is
K
-subgaussian with
K=O(\sqrt{d})
.

Next, for any desired
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(2^{-\frac{d}{\log^{2}(d)}},1/d^{2})
, we can first prove that, any
(\log(1/\delta),\sqrt{\delta})
-DP algorithm has sample complexity lower bound of

n\geq\Omega(\frac{kd}{\sqrt{d}\cdot\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}})\geq\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}\alpha^{2}}).

We emphasize that, thanks to the choice of
\delta
, our target lower bound is above
\frac{\log(d)}{\alpha^{2}}
, which is the number of samples required for low generalization error. In this regime, the fingerprinting framework applies. However, we cannot set
\delta
to be much lower: when
\delta<2^{-\omega(d)}
, the target lower bound becomes
o(1/\alpha^{2})
, and we can no longer derive a contradiction via the fingerprinting framework.

Lastly, using the group privacy connection of Lemma
3.1
, we obtain a lower bound of

n\geq\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}})

for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithms, proving Theorem
9
.

4
Lower Bounds on Adaptive Data Analysis

In this section, we “lift” the lower bounds proved in Section
3
to lower bounds on the task of adaptive data analysis.

4.1
Overview and Intuition

Intuition.

Recall the proof of Theorem
8
(Section
3.3
). There, we constructed a query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
, defined a family of distribution
\{D_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in V}
and a type-dependent score accordingly. Then, we established the following: letting
\mathcal{A}(x)
be the algorithm’s output on input
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
, the scores of in-sample data points w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}(x)
behave like

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{A}(x))\geq\Omega(dk)=\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}),

while for an independent
x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
, with high probability we have
|\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime};\mathcal{A}(x))|\leq\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{m})
. Moreover, the average score of an independent
x^{\prime}
is zero.

Based on the argument so far, we would easily achieve an ADA lower bound if we could claim the following in addition:

Even-ness

Every in-sample point
x^{j}
has a score bounded by
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{m})
with high probability, just like a fresh data point.

If this was indeed the case, we can craft a query
q
by defining

q(x)\coloneqq\max\left(-1,\min\left(1,\mathrm{score}(x;\mathcal{A}(x))\cdot\frac{m}{100\sqrt{d}}\right)\right).

That is, we simply truncate the score of each
x\in[N]
into the range
\pm O(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{m})
. Then, we scale down the score and use it to define a bounded linear query. If Even-ness holds, the truncation incurs little error to both the distribution and the data set. Hence, we end up with roughly
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[q(x)]\approx 0
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[q(x^{j})]\approx\Theta(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}})=\Theta(\alpha)
(recall that we assume
n\leq\frac{c\sqrt{d}m}{\alpha^{3}}
toward proving an ADA lower bound). We see that the query
q
witnesses a mismatch between the distribution and the data set.

Challenge and its resolution.

Unfortunately, Even-ness does not necessarily hold. As an example, if all the queries were given in a batch, the algorithm could simply use its first
\Theta(\frac{\log(M)}{\alpha^{2}})
samples to evaluate the queries and ensure low generalization error. For such algorithms, the scores of the first few samples are huge (their privacy may be completely compromised), but the scores of other samples are small and have zero mean. We cannot exploit the tiny fraction of exposed data, as we must construct a linear query to witness a mismatch between the distribution and the whole data set.

To resolve the issue, we will design an adversary that sends the queries to
A
in
d
adaptive rounds. Meanwhile, we design a strategy to “force” the algorithm to use its samples evenly (and, consequently, distribute the score evenly).

At a very high level, we adopt a well-known trick, sometimes referred to as a “one-time pad” or a “random mask”. In its most basic form, the idea is to generate a random Boolean string
r\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}
and sample a data point
x^{j}=(e^{i_{j}}\otimes u^{i^{\prime}_{j}}\otimes v^{j})
as usual. However, instead of giving
x^{j}
to the algorithm directly, we give algorithm the “encrypted” point
(e^{i_{j}}\otimes u^{i^{\prime}_{j}}\otimes(r\text{ xor }v^{j}))
. As
r
is random, the algorithm has no clue what the real
v^{j}
is. Then, over the
d
rounds of interaction, the adversary reveals
r
, and thus
v^{j}
, bit by bit. Throughout the process, the adversary keeps track of the score of
x^{j}
. Once it finds that the score is too large, it stops revealing future masks on
v^{j}
. In this way, the algorithm effectively loses access to
x^{j}
.

This oversimplified intuition hides many details, which can be found in the formal argument, to appear in the rest of the section.

4.2
Partial Score and Fair Algorithms

In this section, we consider the same setup as in Section
3.1
, which we quickly review here. We have the set of vectors

K=\left\{e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v:i\in[m],j\in[k],v\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}\right\}.

We choose
V=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k\times d}\right\}
, for every
\theta\in V
, we define the type-conditioned exponential distribution
D_{\theta}
.

The set
K
induces a query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
as in Section
3.1
. Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for answering statistical queries from
A
, we can apply the post-processing procedure of Section
3.2
. In the following, we will always work with mean-estimation algorithms
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
. It should be clear that such an algorithm is a direct product of any query-releasing algorithm via the reduction in Section
3.2
.

Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
, we can run
\mathcal{A}
on an input
x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
and obtain
\mathcal{A}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
. For every input point
x^{j}
, we define the score of
x^{j}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}(x)
as

\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{A}(x))\coloneqq\left\langle x^{j}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}:type(x^{\prime})=type(x^{j})}[x^{\prime}],\mathcal{A}(x)\right\rangle.

Slicing queries and Partial score.

Recall the query ensemble is
\{h(e^{i})\cdot u^{j}_{p}\cdot v_{r}\}_{h:[m]\to\{\pm 1\},p\in[k],r\in[d]}
. We split the queries into
d
slices. For each
r\in[d]
, the
r
-th slice consists of all queries
\{h(e^{i})\cdot u^{j}_{p}\cdot v_{r}\}_{h:[m]\to\{\pm 1\},p\in[k]}
. Similarly, for a mean-estimation algorithm
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
, we think of the output of
\mathcal{A}
as consisting of
d
slices, each of dimension
mk
. Namely, the
r
-th slice is
\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,r}
.

We introduce a notion of partial score. For every
1\leq r\leq d
, define the
r
-partial score of a point
x\in K
with respect to
q\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
as

\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x;q)\coloneqq\left\langle x_{*,*,\leq r}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}:type(x^{\prime})=type(x)}[x^{\prime}_{*,*,\leq r}],q_{*,*,\leq r}\right\rangle.

That is, the
r
-partial score just sums up the contribution to the score from the first
r
slices. Note that the partial score only depends on the first
r
slices of the input and output. For this reason, given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{mk\cdot r}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{mk\cdot r}
, we may slightly abuse notation by writing
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x;q)\coloneqq\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(\overline{x},\overline{q})
where
\overline{x}
and
\overline{q}
are arbitrary completion of
x
,
q
into
mkd
-dimensional vectors.

Fair algorithms.

We now define a class of query-releasing algorithms, that we call fair algorithms.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
be a vector mean-estimation algorithm. Assume
\mathcal{A}
operates in
d
stages. In the
r
-th stage, the algorithm outputs
\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,r}
. Then,
\mathcal{A}
is informed of the
r
-th slice of
\theta
(i.e.,
\theta_{*,*,r}
).

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau
-fair, if the following holds true for every stage
r\in[d]
.

1.

Conditioning on
\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,<r}
, the distribution of
\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,r}
only depends on the first
r
slices of inputs and
\theta
. Moreover:

2.

For any input
x^{j}
, conditioning on
\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,\leq r}=q
such that
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x^{j};q)>\tau
, the distribution of
\mathcal{A}(x)\mid(\mathcal{A}(x)_{*,*,\leq r}=q)
is independent of the
(r+1)
-th to the
d
-th slice of
x^{j}
.

A
d
-stage query-releasing algorithm
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to[-1,1]^{M}
is
\tau
-fair, if its induced vector mean estimation algorithm
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
(via Section
3.2
) is
\tau
-fair.

Let us digest Definition
4.1
. Item
1
says that a fair algorithm, during any stage
r
, will not access the
(r+1)
-th to the
d
-th slice of its inputs. Item
2
further asserts that the algorithm will not access
v_{>r}
of a data point
x^{j}
once its
r
-partial score has reached a certain threshold
\tau
. The name “fair” intuitively captures the fact that the algorithm uses its data in a somewhat fair manner. We observe an implication of Item 2: for the joint random variable
(x,\mathcal{A}(x))
, conditioning on
x^{j}_{*,*,\leq r}
and
\mathcal{A}(x)
such that
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x^{j},\mathcal{A}(x))\geq\tau
, the slices
x^{j}_{*,*,>r}
still have maximum uncertainty.

We also note that, since the algorithm is informed of
\theta_{*,*,\leq r}
after the conclusion of the
r
-th stage, it can compute
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x;\mathcal{A}(x))
by itself. Hence, in principle, we can convert any reasonable algorithm to a
\tau
-fair one. Here, having access to
\theta_{*,*,\leq r}
is crucial. Otherwise, the algorithm cannot compute
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}
by itself, and it is not clear how to ask the algorithm to stop accessing an input
x^{j}
at the “right” moment.

Remark on the divergence-to-score lemma.

One may notice that the setup of Definition
4.1
is slightly different than the setup considered in Section
3
. Here, the algorithm publishes its output slice by slice. After the algorithm commits to its estimation for a slice, it is informed of the “correct mean” of that slice, a piece of information that may be utilized to answer future queries. Hence, it is natural to wonder whether the divergence-to-score connection (Proposition
3
) still holds.

We now verify that Proposition
3
holds in the new setup verbatim. Indeed, for every
i\in[mkd]
, consider an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
that has access to every
\theta_{j}
other than
\theta_{i}
and a bunch of input
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
. Suppose
\mathcal{B}
outputs a real value. We claim that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}[\mathcal{B}(x,\theta_{-i})]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\mathcal{B}(x,\theta_{-i})}\left[\mathcal{B}(x,\theta_{-i})\cdot\left(x^{j}_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}\mid type(x^{\prime})=type(x^{j})}[x^{\prime}_{i}]\right)\right].

(10)

To prove the equation, we repeat the proof for Proposition
3
, noting that allowing
\mathcal{B}
to access
\theta_{-i}
has no effect on the derivation (since we do not differentiate
\theta_{-i}
, and the equation holds for every fixed
\theta_{-i}
anyway).

Back to our context, let
\mathcal{A}
be the
d
-stage query-releasing algorithm. During the
r
-th stage, the algorithm can access its inputs and all
\theta_{*,*,<r}
. Since the algorithm cannot access the
r
-th slice of
\theta
, the
r
-th slice of the output is subject to a version of Equation (
10
). Adding up all slices concludes the proof of Proposition
3
for our new setup.

Having confirmed Proposition
3
, the score lower bound from Section
3.3
applies to the algorithms considered in this section, so long as
\mathcal{A}
is accurate.

4.3
Lower Bound Against Fair Algorithms

In this section, we prove a lower bound against all fair algorithms.

Theorem 10
.

Let
n=\frac{c\sqrt{d}m}{\alpha^{3}\log(1/\alpha)}
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to[-1,1]^{M}
is an algorithm for the workload matrix

. Assume
\mathcal{A}
is
\alpha
-accurate (w.r.t.
\ell_{\infty}
-norm) and
\tau
-fair with
\tau\geq C\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}
. Then, there is a
\theta\in V
and an adversary
\mathcal{B}
which, upon seeing the output
\mathcal{A}(x)
, crafts a query

with the following on-average guarantee:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}~\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q\leftarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}(x),\theta)}\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[q(x^{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[q(x)]\right|\geq\Omega(\alpha).

Proof.

Choose
C>0
to be a large constant. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:K^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
be the composition of
\mathcal{A}
with the post-processing described in Section
3.2
. From the argument of Section
3.3
we see that, assuming
\mathcal{A}
(and hence
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
) is accurate, we obtain

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}~\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))\right]\geq\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}).

By averaging principle, we fix a
\theta
for which the score lower bound holds true.

For a realization of
(x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))
, define a query
q:K\to[-1,1]
by

q(x)\coloneqq\max\left(-1,\min\left(1,\mathrm{score}(x;\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))\cdot\frac{m}{2C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}\right)\right).

The average of
q
on an independent point.

For an independent
x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
, we have that
|\mathrm{score}(x;\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))|\leq\frac{2C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}
with probability
1-\alpha^{C^{2}}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x),x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}}[q(x^{\prime})]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x),x^{\prime}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))\cdot m}{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}\right]\pm 2\cdot\Pr_{x^{\prime}}\left[|\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))|>\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha^{C^{2}}.

(11)

The average of
q
on in-sample points.

For each
j\in[n]
, let us examine the distribution of
\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))
. As before, we understand
x^{j}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
as consisting of
d

slices
, each of dimension
m\times k
. Consider observing
x^{j}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
slice by slice. Before the partial score of
x^{j}
exceeds
\tau
, we do not have good control over the growth of the score. However, once
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x^{j},\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))
reaches
[\tau,\tau+\frac{1}{m})
3
3

3

Since the score from each slice is bounded by
\frac{1}{m}
, there cannot be a sudden jump of partial score from one slice to the next.
for some

and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)
become independent by the fair property of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
. Write

and

is independent of

\displaystyle\Pr_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\left[|\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x))|\geq\tau+C\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}\right]<\alpha^{3}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}q(x^{j})\cdot\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}\right)-O\left(\frac{n\cdot C\alpha^{3}\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}\right)-O\left(d\right).

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j\sim[n]}\left[q(x^{j})\right]\geq\Omega(\alpha).

(12)

Combining (

4.4
Lower Bounds Against All Algorithms

In this section, we lift Theorem

to prove a lower bound against all algorithms for answering adaptively generated linear queries.

4.4.1
Obfuscating Inputs

Let
W=(\frac{\log(N)\log(M)}{\alpha^{3}})^{O(1)}
. We enlarge the universe
N
by a factor of
W
. Now, we identify every point
x\in[N\times W]
in the universe by a tuple
x=(w,e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v)
. Here,
(e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v)
has the same geometric interpretation as before (i.e., it is associated with a vector
e^{i}\otimes u^{i^{\prime}}\otimes v\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
, and we newly introduce

as a “name” for

.

For every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
, we extend the definition to

to the new setting: to sample

, we sample the tuple
(e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v)
as before, and sample a name

uniformly at random. The idea is, with our setting of

, we ensure that for an i.i.d. data set

, with probability

, every data point

has a distinct name.

Now, consider a set of random mappings of the form:

\Pi_{r}:[W]\times[m]\times[k]\times\left(\{\pm 1\}^{0}\cup\{\pm 1\}^{1}\cup\dots\cup\{\pm 1\}^{r-1}\right)\to\{\pm 1\}.

Given a data point
x=(w,e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v)
, let its
\Pi
-

be a point
\Pi(x)=(w,e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v^{\prime})
, defined as follows. As the notation suggests, the name and type of
\Pi(x)
are the same as

. However, for every

, we define
v^{\prime}_{r}=\Pi_{r}(w,i,i^{\prime},v_{<r})\oplus v_{r}
where

denotes the XOR of two bits. Since
\Pi
only changes the

-part of

, we write

to denote the “
v
”-part of
\Pi(x)
. Note that the obfuscation is invertible. Write
\Pi^{-1}
to be its inverse. Namely,
\Pi^{-1}
is such that
\Pi^{-1}(\Pi(x))=x
for every
x=(w,e^{i},u^{i^{\prime}},v)
. Finally, for any distribution
D
over
[N\times W]
, let
\Pi(D)
be the distribution of
\Pi(x)
where

is drawn from

.

4.4.2
Making an ADA Algorithm Fair

We describe a reduction to make an ADA algorithm “fair”. The idea is to compare the following two experiments:

•

For an unknown
\theta
, draw
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
and perform ADA with respect to
D_{\theta}
.

•

For an unknown
\theta
and
unknown

\Pi
, draw
(\Pi(x^{1}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n}))\sim\Pi(D_{\theta})^{n}
and perform ADA with respect to
\Pi(D_{\theta})
.

If
\Pi
is known, the two tasks are equivalent as
\Pi
is a bijection. However, if
\Pi
is unknown to the algorithm, from
(\Pi(x^{1}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n}))
, the algorithm only learns the type of each data point. The proof strategy is to design an interaction between the algorithm and the adversary, through which the algorithm gradually learns each slice of its inputs. By designing the adversary properly, we can ensure that the algorithm behaves fairly.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the ADA algorithm. We design the adversary below.

•

At the start, some
\theta
is chosen, and a random
\Pi
is generated. A data set
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
is drawn.
\mathcal{A}
receives
\Pi(x^{1}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n})
. Note that this is equivalent to drawing
\Pi(x^{1}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n})\sim\Pi(D_{\theta})^{n}
in the first place.

•

The adversary interacts with
\mathcal{A}
for
d
stages. In the
r
-th stage, consider the set of queries

H^{r}=\{h(e^{i})\otimes u^{j}_{p}\otimes v_{r}\}_{h:[m]\to\{\pm 1\},p\in[k]}

We understand
H^{r}
as an ensemble of statistical queries w.r.t.
D_{\theta}
. We assume
H^{r}
is publicly known to both
\mathcal{A}
and the adversary. The equivalent of
H^{r}
with respect to
\Pi(D_{\theta})
is given by

H^{\Pi,r}=\left\{g(w,e^{i},u^{j},v)\coloneqq h(e^{i})\cdot u^{j}_{p}\cdot v(\Pi^{-1},(w,e^{i},u^{j},v))_{r}\right\}_{{h:[m]\to\{\pm 1\},p\in[k]}}.

Namely, given a query
g\in H^{r}
, there is a corresponding query
g^{\prime}\in H^{\Pi,r}
defined by
g^{\prime}(x)=g(\Pi^{-1}(x))
.

For a moment, let us consider letting the adversary send the queries
H^{\Pi,r}
to
\mathcal{A}
in the
r
-th stage. By comparing
H^{r}
with
H^{\Pi,r}
,
\mathcal{A}
can learn
\Pi(w,i,j,v_{<r})
for every
w,i,j,v_{<r}
. From this, it learns the
r
-th slice of
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}
. However, it is still clueless about the
(r+1)
-th to
d
-th slices of the inputs.

•

In each stage, the adversary receives responses from
\mathcal{A}
. These responses are answers to
H^{\Pi,r}
with respect to
\Pi(D_{\theta})
. They can be directly translated into answers to
H^{r}
with respect to
D_{\theta}
. By further running the post-processing from Section
3.2
, the adversary obtains a vector
q_{*,*,r}\in\mathbb{R}^{mk}
that approximates
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]_{*,*,r}
. Concatenating all
q_{*,*,r^{\prime}}
for
r^{\prime}\leq r
would allow for tracking
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r)}(x;q)
for every
x\in[N\times W]
.

Now we come to a crucial part: At the start of each stage
r
, for each
x\in[N\times W]
, if
\mathrm{pscore}^{(r-1)}(x;q)>\tau
, we mark
x
as
compromised
. As soon as a point
x
is compromised, the adversary will
not
reveal the correct evaluation of
g(\Pi(x))
in the future. Namely, for each
g\in H^{\Pi,r}
, define
g^{\prime}
as
g^{\prime}(\Pi(x))=g(\Pi(x))
if
x
not compromised, and
g^{\prime}(\Pi(x))=1
otherwise. Let
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,r}
be the collection of all the
g^{\prime}
s. Instead of giving
\mathcal{A}
the query set
H^{\Pi,r}
, the adversary actually sends the set
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,r}
.

As we will argue in a moment, queries from
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,r}
have distributional means similar to their counterparts in
H^{\Pi,r}
. Hence, although the adversary sends queries from
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,r}
and receives responses about them, it can post-process these responses just as if they were queries about
H^{\Pi,r}
.

4.4.3
Analysis

The last section presented our adversary design. We now discuss its correctness. The interaction between
\mathcal{A}
and the adversary as a whole constitutes a query-releasing procedure
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
D_{\theta}
. The procedure
\mathcal{P}
takes
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
as input and outputs
q\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
. We now argue that
\mathcal{P}
is both accurate and fair.

Fairness.

We start by analyzing fairness. Item
1
of Definition
4.1
is satisfied, as the adversary reveals
\Pi
“bit-by-bit” by our design. Regarding Item
2
, note that if a point
x=(w,e^{i},u^{j},v_{\leq r-1}\circ v_{\geq r})
is compromised in the
(r-1)
-th round, so is every point of the form
x^{\prime}=(w,e^{i},u^{j},v_{\leq r-1}\circ v^{\prime}_{\geq r})
. Take one such
x^{\prime}
, and we compare the behavior of the algorithm on, e.g.,
(\Pi(x),\Pi(x^{2}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n}))
versus
(\Pi(x^{\prime}),\Pi(x^{2}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n}))
. Over a random
\Pi
, we know
\Pi(x)
and
\Pi(x^{\prime})
are identically distributed, even after conditioning on the first
(r-1)
slices of
\Pi
. Should
x
be not compromised, the algorithm could have distinguished between
\Pi(x)
and
\Pi(x^{\prime})
by examining
g(\Pi(x))
and
g(\Pi(x^{\prime}))
for some query
g
from
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,\geq r}
. However, since
x
has been compromised, we have
g(\Pi(x))=g(\Pi(x^{\prime}))=1
for every
g\in\tilde{H}^{\Pi,\geq r}
. Therefore, from the
r
-th stage onward, the algorithm cannot distinguish between the input being
x
or
x^{\prime}
. That is, the algorithm behaves the same on every
x^{\prime}
of the form
(w,e^{i},u^{j},v_{\leq r-1}\circ v^{\prime}_{\geq r})
, meaning that the
r
-th to
d
-th slice of the input is independent of the algorithm’s output.

If the names (the “
w
” part) of
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}
are distinct (which happens with probability
1-\frac{1}{W^{\Omega(1)}}
), their random masks do not interfere with each other, and we can apply the argument for each input separately. This shows the procedure is
\tau
-fair with probability
1-\frac{1}{n^{\Omega(1)}}
.

Accuracy.

We now prove the procedure is accurate with respect to
D_{\theta}
. The promise of
\mathcal{A}
says that its responses are
\alpha
-accurate for the query family
\tilde{H}^{\Pi,*}
. If we can argue that these responses are
(1+o(1))\alpha
-accurate to the closely related query family
H^{\Pi,*}
, then the adversary can just directly translate these responses into accurate responses for
H^{*}
(with respect to
D_{\theta}
). Then, the accuracy of
q=\mathcal{P}(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
follows from accurate answers to
H^{*}
together with the post-processing from Section
3.2
.

Indeed, every
g\in H^{\Pi,r}
is associated with a query
g^{\prime}\in\tilde{H}^{\Pi,r}
. By definition, we have

\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[g(x)]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[g^{\prime}(x)]\right|\leq\Pr_{x}[x\text{ is compromised before stage $r$}].

Therefore, it suffices to prove that the fraction of compromised points is bounded by
\alpha
across all stages. At a stage
r\in[d]
, conditioning on
q_{*,*,<r}
, we have

\Pr_{x}[x\text{ is compromised before stage $r$}]=\Pr_{x}\left[\exists r^{\prime}<r,\mathrm{pscore}^{(r^{\prime})}(x,q)\leq\tau\right].

Take
\{\mathrm{pscore}^{r^{\prime}}(x,q)\}_{r^{\prime}<r}
as a random walk with
(r-1)\leq d
steps. The movement of each step has zero mean, and the length of each step is bounded by
\frac{1}{m}
. Since
\tau=\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}
, with probability
1-\alpha^{\Omega(C^{2})}
over
x
, the random walk stays below the threshold
\tau
across the
r-1
steps. This means the fraction of compromised inputs is always below
\alpha^{\Omega(C)}
across the
d
stages, regardless of the partial output
q_{*,*,\leq r}
. This completes the accuracy analysis.

4.4.4
Proof of Theorem
1

We are now ready to prove one of the main results of the paper, Theorem
1
. We formulate a formal version of Theorem
1
below.

Theorem 11
.

Let
N,M\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1/10)
be such that
N\ll 2^{M}
,
M\ll 2^{N}
and
\frac{1}{\alpha}\ll\min(N,M)
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm for answering statistical linear queries over the domain
[N]
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}
operates on at most
o(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)}\log(M)}{\alpha^{3}\log(1/\alpha)})
samples. Then, there is a
O(\log N)
-round adaptive attack against
\mathcal{A}
, which sends at most
m
queries to
\mathcal{A}
and makes
\mathcal{A}
fail to be either sample- or distribution-accurate within error
\alpha
on at least one query. The attack succeeds with probability
\Omega(\alpha)
.

Let us quickly review the relevant parameter settings. We have
d=\Theta(\log N),m=\Theta(\log M)
,
k=\Theta(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}})
such that
2^{d}\cdot mk=N
and
2^{m}\cdot kd=M
. In Section
4.4.1
, we also set up a new parameter
W=(\frac{\log(N)\log(M)}{\alpha^{3}})^{O(1)}
and use it to design an “obfuscating scheme”. To keep notational consistency with previous sections, here we choose to work with a slightly large domain
[N\times W]
in proving Theorem
11
, noting that this does not change our conclusion as
\log(N)=\Theta(\log(NW))
. Having verified the parameter consistency, the upcoming proof will use the constructions and designs from prior sections (such as the set
K
, the mapping
\Pi
, the matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
, the distribution
D_{\theta}
, etc.) without further notice.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is an ADA algorithm working over the universe
[N\times W]
, operating on
n\leq\frac{c\sqrt{\log(N)}\log(M)}{\alpha^{3}}
points. Consider an adversary
\mathcal{B}
interacting with
\mathcal{A}
as in Section
4.4.2
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}
the whole procedure.
\mathcal{P}
takes as input
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
and the description of
\Pi
. It simulates the interaction between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
and outputs an estimate of
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]
, denoted by
q\in\mathbb{R}^{mkd}
. We have argued that
\mathcal{P}
is fair w.r.t.
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
with probability
1-\frac{1}{W^{\Omega}(1)}
.

Depending on how often the output of
\mathcal{A}
is accurate, we consider two cases.

Case 1.

Let
c>0
be sufficiently small. If, with probability
c\cdot\alpha
,
\mathcal{A}
fails to return
\alpha
-accurate answer (w.r.t.
\Pi(D_{\theta})
) to at least one query, the adversary
\mathcal{B}
is the desired attack to
\mathcal{A}
.

Case 2.

Now we assume that with probability
1-c\alpha
, all outputs of
\mathcal{A}
are
\alpha
-accurate w.r.t.
\Pi(D_{\theta})
. We argue that the output of
\mathcal{P}
is close to
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]
on average. First, from Section
4.4.3
, we see that
\alpha
-accurate responses from
\mathcal{A}
induce an output
q
of
\mathcal{P}
such that
\|q-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]\|\leq O(\alpha kd)
. Second, regardless of
\mathcal{A}
being accurate or not, the output
q\leftarrow\mathcal{P}(x)
always satisfies that
\|q\|_{1}\leq O(\sqrt{k}d)
. To see this, for every
i\in[m]
and
r\in[d]
, there is
\lambda\in[-1,1]^{k}
such that

\|q_{i,*,r}\|_{1}=\frac{1}{m}\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}u^{j}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}u^{j}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{m}

Summing over all
i,r\in[d\times m]
verifies the claim. With this in mind, we calculate

\left\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{P}(x)]-\mu_{\theta}\right\|_{1}\leq O(\alpha kd)+\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ not accurate}]\cdot O(\sqrt{kd})\leq O(\alpha kd).

Hence,
\mathcal{P}
is both accurate and natural, placing itself under the regime of Theorem
10
. By Theorem
10
, we can fix a
\theta
and run the following attack. For every output
q
of
\mathcal{P}
, we can define a query
\phi
accordingly, such that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim\mathcal{P}(x),\phi}\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[\phi(x^{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{D_{\theta}}[\phi(x)]\right|\geq\Omega(\alpha).

Since the deviation of the sample-mean from the distributional mean is always bounded by
1
, by the reverse Markov’s inequality, with probability
\Omega(\alpha)
, it holds that:

\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[\phi(x^{j})]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{D_{\theta}}[\phi(x)]\right|\geq\Omega(\alpha).

For any
\phi
for which the above holds, define
\phi^{\prime}(x)\coloneqq\phi(\Pi^{-1}(x))
accordingly. Then, it follows that

\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[\phi^{\prime}(\Pi(x^{j}))]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\Pi(D_{\theta})}[\phi^{\prime}(x)]\right|\geq\Omega(\alpha).

We see that
\phi^{\prime}
witnesses a mismatch between the data
(\Pi(x^{1}),\dots,\Pi(x^{n}))
and the distribution
\Pi(D_{\theta})
, rendering itself a desired attack query against
\mathcal{A}
.

∎

4.5
On Removing the Sample-Accurate Assumption

In this section, we present several lower bounds against algorithms that are
only
required to be accurate with respect to the distribution. The price we pay, however, is that the bound usually becomes smaller by a factor of
\frac{1}{\alpha}
.

Lower bounds in the many-query regime.

We record relevant parameters here for quick reference:
N
is the size of the universe,
M
the number of queries, and
\alpha
the desired accuracy. We have set
d=\Theta(\log(N)),m=\Theta(\log(M)),k=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
so that
2^{m}\cdot kd=M
and
2^{d}\cdot km=N
. We have constructed a set of vectors
K=\{(e^{i}\otimes u^{j}\otimes v)\}
of size
|K|=mk2^{d}=N
, and an associated query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
.

As before, we start by analyzing the class of fair algorithms.

Proposition 5
.

Let
n=\frac{c\sqrt{d}m}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)}
and
\tau=\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}
. There is no
\tau
-fair algorithm
\mathcal{A}:K^{n}\to[-1,1]^{M}
that can answer all queries for the workload matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{M\times N}
to within
\ell_{\infty}
generalization error
\alpha
with probability
1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that such an
\mathcal{A}
exists. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
be the composition of
\mathcal{A}
with the post-processing of Section
3.2
. Recall our parameter setting that
k=\frac{c}{\alpha^{2}}
for some small
c
. Then, for any
\theta
and its induced distribution
D_{\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}[\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq O(\alpha kd)+\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ not accurate}]\cdot O(\sqrt{k}d)

\displaystyle\leq O(\alpha kd).

In particular, this means
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
is subject to the score lower bound from Section
3.3
. Namely,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x^{j}))\right]\geq\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}).

On the other hand, assuming
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau
-fair, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)}[\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(x)]\leq\tau+O(\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m})\leq O(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}}{m}).

If
n<\frac{c\sqrt{d}m}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)}
, the score lower bound and upper bound becomes contradictory. This completes the proof.
∎

Section
4.4.2
has described a reduction, roughly saying the following. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an ADA algorithm working over the domain
[N\times W]
. One can combine
\mathcal{A}
with the reduction procedure and obtain a
\tau
-fair query releasing algorithm, which works over a smaller universe
[N]
, enjoys the same sample complexity, and incurs a slightly higher error (higher by a
(1+o(1))
factor). Furthermore, the reduction interacts with
\mathcal{A}
for at most
d
adaptive rounds. Since all fair algorithms have been ruled out by Proposition
5
. We can thus combine the reduction with Proposition
5
and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 12
.

Let
N,M\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1/10)
be such that
2^{N}\gg M
,
2^{M}\gg N
and
\frac{1}{\alpha}\ll\min(N,M)
. For some
n=\Theta(\frac{\sqrt{\log(N)}\log(M)}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)})
, the following is true: any ADA algorithm, over the universe
[N]
and operating on
n
samples, cannot answer
M
adaptively generated statistical queries to within generalization error
\alpha
.

Furthermore, there is an attack against
\mathcal{A}
, which breaks its accuracy within
O(\log N)
rounds of adaptivity with constant probability. The attack sends at most
M
queries.

Remark

If we “unpack” the proof of Theorem
12
, we will find out that the attack strategy is similar to prior works
[
HU14
,
SU15
]
. Namely, we assign a “score” to every point in the universe. We deem any point with a large score likely in the data set. As such, we remove their contribution to future queries. At a certain moment, almost all data points of the algorithm are exposed. Consequently, the algorithm fails to evaluate new queries accurately. However, due to the limitation of traditional fingerprinting lemmas (which required a Boolean hypercube structure), prior works cannot push the technique to the many-query small-universe regime. In contrast, our geometric fingerprinting technique allows for working with a richer class of geometric structures, enabling us to make further progress in understanding the limitations of efficient adaptive data analysis.

Lower bounds in the few-query regime.

So far, we are interested in the regime where the number of queries is large (compared with log-universe-size). For a smaller number of queries, we establish the following theorem.

Theorem 13
.

Let
n=\frac{c\sqrt{m}}{\alpha\log(1/\alpha)}
and
N=2^{O(\alpha^{2}m)}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an ADA algorithm over the universe
[N]
where
N=2^{O(\alpha^{2}m)}
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}
receives only
n
samples. Then, it cannot answer
m
adaptively generated statistical queries within generalization error
\alpha
.

Furthermore, there is an attack against
\mathcal{A}
that breaks its accuracy within
O(\alpha^{2}m)
rounds of adaptivity with constant probability. The attack sends at most
m
queries.

Theorem
13
recovers the main information-theoretic lower bound of
[
SU15
]
. Our theorem is slightly stronger in the sense that our attack breaks the algorithm within
\alpha^{2}k
rounds of adaptivity, where in each round, the attacker sends
\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
queries in a batch. Prior constructions require full adaptivity, and they send queries one by one.

Proof sketch.

We lift the query-releasing lower bound of Theorem
9
(whose proof appeared in Section
3.4
) to an ADA lower bound.

In particular, recall that
k=\frac{c}{\alpha^{2}}
. For the ensemble of vectors

K=\{u^{j}\otimes v:v\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{kd}

and its associated query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{kd\times(k2^{d})}
, we have shown that any
\alpha
-accurate (in
\ell_{\infty}
metric w.r.t.
D_{\theta}
) algorithm
\mathcal{A}
satisfies that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{A}(x))\right]\geq\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}}).

If we further assume that
\mathcal{A}
is
\tau=C\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}
-fair w.r.t. the slicing of
\mathbb{R}^{kd}
into
d
slices, then we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x,\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\mathrm{score}(x^{j};\mathcal{A}(x))\right]\leq\tau+O(\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)})\leq O(\sqrt{d\log(1/\alpha)}).

The score upper and lower bounds give a lower bound of
n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha^{2}\log(1/\alpha)}\right)
. In terms of the number of queries
m=kd
, this is an
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\alpha\log(1/\alpha)})
lower bound. The universe size is
k2^{d}=2^{O(d)}=2^{O(\alpha^{2}m)}
.

We have shown a lower bound against fair algorithms. We can use a version of the reduction from Section
4.4.2
to obtain a lower bound against all algorithms. The reduction enlarges the universe size by a factor of
W=n^{O(1)}
, which is negligible for a typical parameter regime where
2^{\alpha^{2}m}\gg n
.
∎

5
Lower bounds on Random Query Releasing via Fingerprinting

In this section, we prove the following lower bound for a set of random queries.

Theorem 14
.

Let
N,d\geq 0
be two integers such that
N\gg d
and
\log(N)\leq o(d)
. Consider a random matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
where each entry of
A
is independently set to
\pm 1
with equal probability.

Then, with probability
1-o(1)
over
A
, the following is true for all
\alpha<\frac{c\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{d}}
: for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta<\frac{\alpha}{d}
, any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for query releasing with workload matrix
A
needs at least
\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon\alpha}\right)
samples to achieve a mean-squared error of
\alpha^{2}d
.

We will prove Theorem
14
via our geometric fingerprinting framework. Here, both the upper and lower bounds on the “score” are not straightforward, and we will use randomness of
A
in an essential way to establish both bounds.

We also highlight that Theorem
14
is the first application of our framework on a set
K
that does not appear to contain a large hypercube. In contrast, the lower bounds presented in Sections
3
and
4
all depend heavily on an embedded hypercube structure.

5.1
Basic Facts on Rademacher Sums

We need tight control on the tail bounds of Rademacher sums. To begin with, the following tail upper bound is a direct consequence of the Hoeffding inequality.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for every
t\geq 1
, it holds that

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}}[\langle a,x\rangle\geq t\|a\|_{2}]\leq\exp(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}).

We also need the following tail
lower
bound.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that there are at least
d/2
coordinates
i\in[d]
with
|a_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{5\sqrt{d}}\|a\|_{2}
. Then, there is an absolute
c>0
such that for every
t\in[0,c\sqrt{d})
, it holds that

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}}[\langle a,x\rangle\geq t\|a\|_{2}]\geq\exp(-ct^{2}).

We include a proof of Lemma
5.2
below. To start, for any vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t>0
, define

K_{1,2}(a,t)=\inf\{\|a^{\prime}\|_{1}+t\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}:a^{\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}=a\}.

For small
t
, the quantity
K_{1,2}
behaves like
t\|a\|_{2}
while for large
t
(in particular for
t>\sqrt{d}
), the quantity converges to
\|a\|_{1}
. The following lemma instantiates this intuition for all “good” vectors, which are of interest to us.

Lemma 5.3
.

There is a small constant
c>0
for which the following is true. Suppose
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that there are at least
d/2
coordinates
i\in[d]
with
|a_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{5\sqrt{d}}\|a\|_{2}
. Then, for
t<c\sqrt{d}
, it holds that

t\|a\|_{2}\geq K_{1,2}(a,t)\geq c\cdot t\cdot\|a\|_{2}.

Proof.

The upper bound on
K_{1,2}
is obvious. We establish the lower bound here. Suppose
K_{1,2}(a,t)=\|a^{\prime}\|_{1}+t\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}
. Among all the coordinates
i\in[d]
such that
|a_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{5\sqrt{d}}\|a\|_{2}
, if there are half of them such that
a^{\prime}_{i}>|a_{i}|/2
, then it is clear that
\|a^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{10\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{d}{4}\cdot\|a\|_{2}>ct\|a\|_{2}
(recall that
t<c\sqrt{d}
).

Otherwise, there are at least half of
i
’s with
|a^{\prime\prime}_{i}|\geq|a_{i}|/2
. Then, we have

\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{d}{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{10\sqrt{d}}\right)^{2}\cdot\|a\|_{2}^{2}>c^{2}\|a\|_{2}^{2}.

Consequently, we have
t\|a^{\prime\prime}\|_{2}\geq ct\|a\|_{2}
as desired.
∎

We also need the following anti-concentration inequality from
[
MS90
]
.

Lemma 5.4

(
[
MS90
]
)
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that for every
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}}[\langle x,a\rangle\geq c^{-1}K_{1,2}(a,t)]\geq c^{-1}e^{-ct^{2}}.

Now, it is clear that Lemma
5.2
is a direct consequence of Lemma
5.4
combined with Lemma
5.3
(The constant “
c
” appearing in the three lemma statements are not necessarily the same).

5.2
Lower Bounding the Score

Expanding vector family.

We state the following “expanding” property on a collection of vectors.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. A collection of
d
-dimensional vectors
S=\{s^{1},\dots,s^{N}\}
is
(r,\eta)
-expanding, if the following is true:
with probability
1-\frac{1}{d^{5}}
over
\theta\sim\partial B_{2}(0,r)
, the exponential tilt
D_{\theta}
of
S
satisfies that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}(S)}[\langle v,\theta\rangle]\geq\eta.

A matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
is
(r,\eta)
-
expanding
, if the ensemble of column vectors of
A
is
(r,\eta)
-expanding.

Looking ahead, an expanding family of vectors gives a query matrix
A
for which we can prove the score lower bound (c.f. Section
2.3
). The following proposition states that a random query matrix is expanding with desired parameters with high probability.

Proposition 6
.

For all sufficiently small
c>0
, the following is true. Suppose
N\gg d
and
\log(N)\leq o(d)
. Let
A\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
be chosen uniformly at random. With probability
1-o(1)
, the matrix
A
is
(c\sqrt{\log N},\Omega_{c}(\log(N)))
-expanding.

Proof.

Let
r=c\sqrt{\log N}
. We claim that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\sim\partial B_{2}(0,r)\\
A\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}\end{subarray}}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}(A)}[\langle v,\theta\rangle]\geq\eta\right]\leq\frac{1}{d^{10}}.

(13)

To justify (
13
), we first sample
\theta
and condition on the event that half of
\theta_{i}
satisfies that
|\theta_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{5\sqrt{d}}\|\theta\|_{2}
, which happens with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(d))
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
, for any small
c_{dev}>0
, there is a suitable
c_{prob}=O({c_{dev}}^{2})
such that

\Pr_{s\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}}\left[\langle s,\theta\rangle>\frac{c_{dev}\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{d}}\|\theta\|_{1}\right]\approx\frac{1}{N^{c_{prob}}}.

It follows that with probability
1-\exp(-N^{\Omega(1)})
over a random matrix
A\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
, there are
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{N}{N^{c_{prob}}}=\frac{1}{2}N^{1-{c_{prob}}}
column vectors
A_{*,j}
with
\langle\theta,A_{*,j}\rangle>\frac{c_{dev}\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{d}}\|\theta\|_{1}\geq\frac{c_{dev}\log N}{4}
. For such matrices
A
, if we were to sample a column uniformly at random, with probability
\frac{1}{2N^{c_{prob}}}
we would get a column
A_{*,j}
such that
\langle A_{*,j},\theta\rangle\geq\frac{c_{dev}\log N}{4}
. Next, we make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X
be a random variable with density
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Suppose that
\Pr[X\geq\eta]\geq\delta
. Define a random variable
Y
with density proportional to
q(y)\coloneqq p(y)\cdot\exp(y)
. Then, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y]\geq\eta-2\log(1/\delta).

We defer the proof of Lemma
5.5
to the end of the subsection. Assuming its truth and applying it to the random variable
\langle A_{*,j},\theta\rangle
(where the randomness is over
j\sim[N]
), we get that for the exponential tilt
D_{\theta}(A)
, it holds that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}}[\langle v,\theta\rangle]\geq\frac{c_{dev}}{4}\log N-2c_{prob}\log(N)\geq\Omega(\log N).

Here, we choose
c_{dev}\in(0,1)
to be sufficiently small so that we have
c_{prob}=\Theta({c_{dev}}^{2})\ll c_{dev}
.

To wrap up the proof of Proposition
6
, note that we have shown (
13
) (even with a stronger probability bound of
\exp(-\Omega(d))+\exp(-N^{\Omega(1)})
on the right hand side). Next, by Markov’s inequality, this means that with probability
1-o(1)
over the sampling of
A
, we have

\Pr_{\theta}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim D_{\theta}(A)}[\langle v,\theta\rangle]\leq\Omega(\log N)\right]<\frac{1}{d^{5}}.

(If not, there would be at least
\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{1}{d^{5}}
fraction of
\theta
-
A
pairs that are “bad”, which would contradict to (
13
)). This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We fill in the last gap of the proof by proving Lemma
5.5
below.

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

Without loss of generality, we consider the case that
X\leq\eta
with probability one. Namely, it suffices to prove the lemma for the random variable
\min(X,\eta)
. We also observe that
Y
has an exponentially decaying tail bound. Namely for every
t<\eta
, it holds that

\Pr[Y\leq t]\leq\frac{\Pr[X\leq t]}{\Pr[X\geq\eta]}\cdot\frac{\exp(t)}{\exp(\eta)}\leq\frac{\exp(t-\eta)}{\delta}.

Given the observation, we simply use integration by parts on
Y
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y]

\displaystyle=\eta-\int_{-\infty}^{\eta}\Pr[Y\leq t]dt

\displaystyle\geq\eta-1.9\log(1/\delta)-\int_{-\infty}^{\eta-1.9\log(1/\delta)}\Pr[Y\leq t]dt

\displaystyle\geq\eta-1.9\log(1/\delta)-\int_{-\infty}^{\eta-1.9\log(1/\delta)}\frac{\exp(t-\eta)}{\delta}dt

\displaystyle\geq\eta-1.9\log(1/\delta)-\frac{\exp(-1.9\log(1/\delta))}{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\eta-2\log(1/\delta).

This completes the proof.
∎

5.3
Upper Bounding the Score

Regular vector family.

In this subsection, we define and study the following “regularity” condition on a collection of Boolean vectors.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. A collection of
d
-dimensional Boolean vectors
S=\{s^{1},\dots,s^{N}\}
is
r
-regular, if the following is true:
with probability
1-\frac{1}{d^{5}}
over
\theta\sim B_{2}(0,r)
, the exponential tilt of
S
satisfies that

\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(S)))<O\left(1\right).

A matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
is
r
-regular, if the ensemble of column vectors of
A
is
r
-regular.

Looking ahead, a regular family of vectors gives a query matrix
A
for which we can prove the score upper bound via our framework (c.f. Section
2.3
). We prove the following proposition, stating that a random query matrix is regular with desired parameters.

Proposition 7
.

For all sufficiently small
c>0
, the following is true. Suppose
N\gg d
and
\log(N)\leq o(d)
. Let
A\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
be chosen uniformly at random. With probability
1-o(1)
, the matrix
A
is
(c\sqrt{\log N})
-regular.

Proof.

Let
r=c\sqrt{\log N}
. We claim that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\sim B_{2}(0,r)\\
A\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}\end{subarray}}\left[\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A)))>2\right]\leq\frac{1}{d^{10}}.

(14)

To prove (
14
), we first sample and condition on
\theta
. We advise readers to keep in mind that
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq c\sqrt{\log N}
where
c
is sufficiently small.

Let
B=\{\pm 1\}^{d}
be the Boolean cube. It is easy to see that
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B))
is a diagonal matrix with
\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B)))\leq 1
. Our proof strategy is to show that
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A))
is extremely close to
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B))
. This happens because
A
can be understood as sampling
N
random vectors from
B
with replacement. Since
N
is extremely large, concentration inequalities apply, and we reach the desired conclusion.

We give the formal details here. First, define

B^{\prime}=B\setminus\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}:\langle x,\theta\rangle>3\sqrt{\log N}\|\theta\|_{2}\}

to be a pruning of
B
where we remove all vectors that have too large an inner product with
\theta
. We claim that
D_{\theta}(B^{\prime})
is close to
D_{\theta}(B)
in total variation distance. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D_{\theta}(B^{\prime}),D_{\theta}(B))

\displaystyle=\Pr_{v\sim D_{\theta}(B)}[v\notin B^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{3\sqrt{\log N}}^{+\infty}\Pr_{x\sim\{\pm 1\}^{d}}[\langle x,\theta\rangle>t\|\theta\|_{2}]\cdot\exp(t\|\theta\|_{2})~dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{3\sqrt{\log N}}^{\infty}\exp(-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+t\|\theta\|_{2})~dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}.

It then follows that
\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B^{\prime})))<1+o(1)
. Furthermore, when we sample the column vectors of
A
, with probability
1-\frac{1}{N}
, all the vectors will be in the set
B^{\prime}
. We condition on this event.

We recall a basic fact on concentration: for any bounded function
f:B^{\prime}\to[-N^{1/5},N^{1/5}]
, with probability
1-\exp(-N^{\Omega(1)})
over
s^{1},\dots,s^{N}\sim B^{\prime}
, we have:

\sum_{i\sim[N]}f(s^{i})\in N\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{v\sim B^{\prime}}[f(v)]\pm O\left(N^{3/4}\right).

We apply this fact to the following functions:

•

Z(s^{1},\dots,s^{N})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\langle s^{i},\theta\rangle)
.

•

M_{u}(s^{1},\dots,s^{N})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\langle s^{i},\theta\rangle)\cdot s^{i}_{u}
for every
u\in[d]
.

•

C_{u,v}(s^{1},\dots,s^{N})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\langle s^{i},\theta\rangle)\cdot s^{i}_{u}\cdot s^{i}_{v}
, for every
u,v\in[d]
.

Note that all these functions are bounded by
N^{1/5}
, because we have that
\langle v,\theta\rangle<3\sqrt{\log N}\|\theta\|_{2}<\frac{1}{5}\log(N)
for all
v\in B^{\prime}
. Hence, we get that with high probability over
\{s^{1},\dots,s^{N}\}
, each of these functions is close to the following functions:

•

Z^{*}=N\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim B^{\prime}}[\exp(\langle s,\theta\rangle)]
.

•

M^{*}_{u}=N\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim B^{\prime}}[\exp(\langle s,\theta\rangle)\cdot s_{u}]
for every
u\in[d]
.

•

C^{*}_{u,v}=N\cdot E_{s\sim B^{\prime}}[\exp(\langle s,\theta\rangle)\cdot s_{u}\cdot s_{v}]
for every
u,v\in[d]
.

Denote
A=[s^{1},\dots,s^{N}]
. Note that the covariance matrix of
A
is

\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A))=\frac{C(s)}{Z(s)}-\frac{M(s)M(s)^{\top}}{Z(s)^{2}}.

Observe that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{s\sim B^{\prime}}[\exp(\langle s,\theta\rangle)]\geq\Omega(1)
. Then, it is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A))-\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{C(s)}{Z(s)}-\frac{M(s)M(s)^{\top}}{Z(s)^{2}}\right)-\left(\frac{C^{*}}{Z^{*}}-\frac{M^{*}(M^{*})^{\top}}{(Z^{*})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(C(s)\left(\frac{1}{Z(s)}-\frac{1}{Z^{*}}\right)-M(s)M(s)^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{Z(s)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(Z^{*})^{2}}\right)\right)-

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{C(s)-C^{*}}{Z^{*}}-\frac{M(s)M(s)^{\top}-M^{*}(M^{*})^{\top}}{(Z^{*})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{O(N^{3/4})\cdot J}{Z^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq O(N^{-1/4})\cdot J.

This shows that the distance between
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A))
and
\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(B^{\prime}))
is negligible with high probability, implying that
\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A)))<1+o(1)
.

Now we have shown that (
14
) is true. By Markov’s inequality, this implies that with probability
1-o(1)
over
A
, we have

\Pr_{\theta}\left[\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}(A)))>2\right]<\frac{1}{d^{5}}.

(Again, if not, there would be at least
\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{1}{d^{5}}
fraction of
\theta
-
A
pairs that are bad, contradicting to (
14
)). This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.4
Proof of Theorem
14

We are ready to conclude the proof of Theorem
14
. We condition on a matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
that is
(c\sqrt{\log(N)},\Omega(\log(N)))
-expanding and
(c\sqrt{\log N})
-regular for some absolute constant
c>0
. There are all but
o(1)
-fraction of such matrices by Propositions
6
and
7
. Given
A
, we use
a^{i},i\in[N]
to denote its column vectors.

Let
\alpha<\frac{c^{\prime}\sqrt{\log N}}{\sqrt{d}}
be a desired accuracy parameter, where
c^{\prime}<c
is sufficiently small. Let
n=\frac{c^{\prime}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon\alpha}
. Fix
\delta<\frac{1}{d}
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}:[N]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm with
\ell_{2}^{2}
error of at most
\alpha^{2}d
. We derive a contradiction via the correlation analysis.

To set up the fingerprinting argument, choose
V=B_{2}(0,c\sqrt{\log(N)})
and let
S
be its surface. For every
\theta\in V
, define the exponential tilt
D_{\theta}
as usual. Namely,
\Pr[D_{\theta}=a^{i}]\propto\exp(\langle\theta,e^{i}\rangle)
. Write
\mu_{\theta}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\theta}]
as the mean of
D_{\theta}
.

Score lower bound.

Since the mean-squared error of
\mathcal{A}
is
\alpha^{2}d
, by Jensen’s inequality, we have that
\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(x)]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j\sim[n]}[x^{j}]\|_{2}\leq\alpha\sqrt{d}
for every data set
x=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
. In light of this observation, we apply Proposition
1
and the divergence theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle x^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(x)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Area}(S)}{\mathrm{Vol}(V)}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}[\mathcal{A}(x)],\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{c\sqrt{\log N}}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j\sim[n]}[x^{j}],\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right\rangle-\alpha\sqrt{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{c\sqrt{\log N}}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim S}\left\langle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}}[x],\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}\right\rangle-\alpha\sqrt{d}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{c\sqrt{\log N}}\left((1-d^{-5})\cdot\Omega_{c}(\frac{\log N}{\sqrt{\log N}})-O(d^{-5}\cdot d)-\alpha\sqrt{d}\right)

\displaystyle\text{(expanding of $A$)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{c\sqrt{\log N}}\left(\Omega_{c}(\sqrt{\log N})-c^{\prime}\sqrt{\log N}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega_{c}(d).

To achieve a tight dependence on
\alpha
, we define the score slightly differently. In particular, we observe that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle x^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mu_{\theta}\rangle\right]=0.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle x^{j}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta}\rangle\right]\geq\Omega_{c}(d).

(15)

We define the score of
x^{\prime}\in[N]
w.r.t.
\mathcal{A}(x)
as
\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime};\mathcal{A}(x))=\langle x^{\prime}-\mu_{\theta},\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta}\rangle
. As we will soon see, shifting
\mathcal{A}(x)
by
\mu_{\theta}
allows for reducing the “variance” of scores, making it feasible to prove a tight upper bound on the score.

Score upper bound.

To establish the score upper bound, we will use Proposition
2
. The first step is to understand the variance of
\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime},\mathcal{A}(x))
where the randomness is over
x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)
and
x^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}
. Since
\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime},\mathcal{A}(x))
has zero mean, we just calculate its second moment. We proceed as

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime},\mathcal{A}(x))^{2}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta})^{\top}\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta})(\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta})\right]\leq\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}))\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\|\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}].

By triangle inequality, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\|\mathcal{A}(x)-\mu_{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq 2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n},\mathcal{A}(x)}\left[\|\mathcal{A}(x)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[x^{j}]\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim D_{\theta}^{n}}\left[\|\mu_{\theta}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{j}[x^{j}]\|_{2}^{2}\right].

The first term is bounded by
\alpha^{2}d
since
\mathcal{A}
has a low mean-squared error. The second term is also bounded by
O(\alpha^{2}d)
,
provided that
n\geq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
. We can assume this is the case without loss of generality (i.e.,
\frac{1}{\alpha}<\sqrt{d}
). This is because we can appeal to a folklore reduction, saying that any algorithm with
n
samples and error
\alpha
implies an algorithm with
\frac{n}{k}
samples and error
k\alpha
. We prove this fact at the end of the section for completeness. (The right lower bound in the regime where
\alpha<\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
can also be proven using hereditary discrepancy approaches.)

Back to our discussion, we have shown that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{score}(x^{\prime},\mathcal{A}(x))^{2}]\leq O\left(\alpha^{2}d\cdot\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}))\right).

Now, we utilize the regular property of
A
, which tells us that
\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}))\leq O(1)
with probability
1-d^{-5}
over
\theta\sim V
. For every such
\theta
, we may use Proposition
2
to conclude that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{score}(x^{j},\mathcal{A}(x))]\leq O(\alpha\varepsilon\sqrt{d}+\delta d)\leq O(\alpha\varepsilon\sqrt{d})

for every
j\in[n]
.

For those
\theta
such that
\lambda_{max}(\mathrm{cov}(D_{\theta}))
is too large, we use the naive bound of
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{score}(x^{j},\mathcal{A}(x))]\leq O(d)
. Combining both cases, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim V}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{score}(x^{j},\mathcal{A}(x))]\leq O(\alpha\varepsilon\sqrt{d}).

(16)

Unlike proofs presented in Sections
2
to
4
, here we are only able to establish the score upper bound on average over
\theta
. Nevertheless, this is sufficient to carry out the proof.

Finally, we compare (
15
) with (
16
). The upper and lower bounds on the score imply the desired lower bound
n\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\alpha\varepsilon\sqrt{d}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha\varepsilon}\right)
, concluding the proof.

Trading off accuracy for smaller sample size.

We present the promised reduction that allows one to reduce the sample size at the price of a higher error. Suppose
\mathcal{A}:[N]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\alpha
-accurate and
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. We design an algorithm
\mathcal{B}:[N]^{n/k}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is
\alpha k
-accurate and retains
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. Denote
m=n/k
. Say
(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})
is the input to
\mathcal{B}
. Take
z
to be arbitrary. Make
n-m
copies of
z
and feed
\mathcal{A}
with the input
(x^{1},\dots,x^{m},z,\dots,z)
. Let
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the output of
\mathcal{A}
. We let
\mathcal{B}
output
\frac{n}{m}\left(q-\frac{n-m}{n}\cdot z\right)
.
\mathcal{B}
is clearly private. Regarding accuracy, we have

\left\|\frac{n}{m}(q-\frac{n-m}{n}\cdot z)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\frac{n}{m}\left(q-\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}x^{j}+(n-m)\cdot z\right)\right)\right\|_{2}.

Note that the right hand side measures the error of
\mathcal{A}
on
(x^{1},\dots,x^{m},z,\dots,z)
and multiply it with
\frac{n}{m}=k
. Hence, assuming
\mathcal{A}
has error
\alpha
, it follows that
\mathcal{B}
has error
k\alpha
.

Releasing Random Linear Queries via Sparse Histogram

In this section, we present our algorithm for answering random linear queries with near-optimal sample complexity in the low-accuracy regime. The main result covered in this section is Theorem
15
, to appear in Section
6.3
.

6.1
The Structure of Random Queries

We need the following structural result concerning random matrices. Roughly, it says given a random query matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
, any small number of column vectors behave like mutually orthogonal vectors.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
N,d\geq 0
be two integers. Consider a random matrix
A:\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
where each entry of
A
is independently set to
\pm 1
with equal probability.

Then, with probability
1-o(1)
over
A
, the following is simultaneously true for all
k\in\left[1,c\cdot\frac{d}{\log(N)}\right]
: for all possible subset of
k
distinct columns
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[N]
, the column vectors
A_{*,i_{1}},\dots,A_{*,i_{k}}
satisfy
\left\|\sum_{j}A_{*,i_{j}}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2kd}
.

Before starting the proof, we introduce one useful concentration inequality about quadratic forms of sub-gaussian random variables, known as the Hanson-Wright inequality.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
RV13
]
)
.

There is a universal
c>0
for which the following is true. Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a vector of independent random variables that are all zero-mean and
K
-subgaussian. Let
M
be an
n\times n
matrix. Then, for every
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\Pr[|X^{T}MX-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[X^{T}MX]|>t]\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{4}\|M\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{K^{2}\|M\|_{op}}\right)\right).

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Fix one
k
. Let
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
be a list of fixed indices. Observe that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{*,j}
is a vector of
d
independent entries where each entry is
\sqrt{k}
-subgaussian (this follows because the sum of
k
Bernoulli random variables is
\sqrt{k}
-subgaussian). As such, we use Lemma
6.2
by taking
M
there to be
I
, and derive that

\Pr\left[\left\|\sum_{j}A_{*,j}\right\|_{2}^{2}-kd>t\right]<2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{k^{2}d},\frac{t}{k}\right)\right).

By taking
t=kd
, we see that

\Pr\left[\left\|\sum_{j}A_{*,j}\right\|_{2}>\sqrt{2kd}\right]=\Pr\left[\left\|\sum_{j}A_{*,j}\right\|_{2}^{2}>2kd\right]<\exp(-\Omega(d)).

We can then union-bound over all possible choices of
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
. Note that there are
N^{k}=2^{\frac{cd}{\log(N)}\cdot\log(N)}<2^{c\cdot d}
of them. Lastly, we union-bound over all possible
k
’s (there are at most
\sqrt{d}
of them).
∎

6.2
Private Sparse Histogram

We need the following well-known result from the differential privacy literature.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a (possibly unbounded) universe and
n\geq 1
be a finite integer. There is an algorithm which, on input a non-negative vector
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{X}}
with
\|x\|_{1}=n
, with probability one returns a vector
\hat{x}
with
\|\hat{x}\|_{1}=n
such that

\|x-\hat{x}\|_{\infty}<O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(1/\delta)\right).

Furthermore, the algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with respect to any adjacent inputs
x,x^{\prime}
with
\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}<1
.

Most previous works using or designing sparse histogram algorithms are concerned with the typical definition of add-remove or change-one privacy. For our purpose, we need a version of the sparse histogram algorithm to handle
\ell_{1}
-adjacent data sets. For completeness, we include a proof of Lemma
6.3
below.

Proof.

We start by introducing the truncated Laplace mechanism and giving a “fine-grained” privacy analysis for it, taking the distance between two near inputs into account.

Truncated Laplace mechanism.
For
v,\varepsilon>0
, let
\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
be a truncated Laplace random variable, defined as the following. To sample from
\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
, one
repeatedly
4
4

4

There is another popular way of defining truncated Laplace noise by drawing a single sample from
\mathrm{Lap}(1/\varepsilon)
and truncating it into the range
[-v,v]
. However, for our algorithm and its analysis, the rejection sampling version is needed.
draws
A\sim\mathrm{Lap}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
, and returns the first
A
such that
A\in[-v,v]
.

Now, let
\varepsilon,\delta
be given as in Lemma
6.3
. We choose
v=\frac{5\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}
and make the following claim: if two real numbers
X,Y
are such that
|X-Y|=\Delta\leq 1
, then
X+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
and
Y+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
are
(\Delta\varepsilon,\Delta\delta)
-indistinguishable. To briefly justify this, consider the output distribution of, e.g.,
X+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
. We consider getting a sample from
\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
with value inside
[-v,-v+\Delta]\cup[v-\Delta,v]
as a privacy failure. The probability of failure is then easily shown to be at most
\Delta\delta
. If the failure event does not happen, the output
X+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(1/\varepsilon)
is equally likely (up to a multiplicative factor of
e^{\Delta\varepsilon}
) to occur as well when we sample from
Y+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
instead.

Algorithm.
Let us design the algorithm. For each
u\in\mathcal{X}
, we tentatively set
\tilde{x}_{u}=\max(0,x_{u}+\mathrm{TruncLap}_{v}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))
.
This gives us a vector
\tilde{x}
which satisfies that

\|\tilde{x}-x\|_{\infty}\leq v.

Currently,
\tilde{x}
does not necessarily satisfy that
\|\tilde{x}\|_{1}=n
. Consider the set
H=\{y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathcal{X}}:\|y\|_{1}=n\}
. We project
\tilde{x}
onto
H
by minimizing
\ell_{\infty}
movement. Denote the resulting point by
\hat{x}
. Since the vector
x
is in
H
, and
x
is close to
\tilde{x}
in
\ell_{\infty}
distance, an application of triangle inequality shows that

\|\hat{x}-x\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|\tilde{x}-x\|_{\infty}=2v.

Privacy.
The above concludes the description of the algorithm as well as its utility analysis. In terms of privacy, let
x,x^{\prime}
be a pair of vectors such that
\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 1
. For every element
u\in\mathcal{X}
, the value
\tilde{x}_{u}
and
\tilde{x^{\prime}}_{u}
will be
(\varepsilon|x_{u}-x^{\prime}_{u}|,\delta|x_{u}-x^{\prime}_{u}|)
-indistinguishable. Hence, using the basic composition of differential privacy, we conclude that the two vectors
\tilde{x},\tilde{x^{\prime}}
are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable. Since
\hat{x}
and
\hat{x^{\prime}}
are post-processing of
\tilde{x},\tilde{x^{\prime}}
, this projection step does not change our privacy analysis.
∎

6.3
The Query Releasing Algorithm

We are ready to design our query-releasing algorithm for random linear queries.

Theorem 15
.

Let
N,d\geq 0
be two integers. Consider a random matrix
A\in\{\pm 1\}^{d\times N}
where each entry of
A
is independently set to
\pm 1
with equal probability.

Then, with probability
1-o(1)
over
A
, the following is simultaneously true for all
\alpha\in\left(C\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{d}},1\right)
: for every
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
there is a bound
n=O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha^{2}\varepsilon}\right)
and an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that for every input vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\|x\|_{1}\geq n
,
\mathcal{A}
with probability one returns a
\hat{y}
such that
\left\|A\frac{x}{\|x\|_{1}}-\hat{y}\right\|_{2}<\alpha\sqrt{d}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{A}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP w.r.t.
\ell_{1}
-adjacent data sets.

Proof.

We first state the algorithm, and then give its privacy and utility analysis.

Algorithm.
Given input
x
, we first scale it properly to make it the case that
\|x\|_{1}=n
(namely we change
x
to
\frac{nx}{\|x\|_{1}}
). Then, we use the sparse histogram algorithm of Lemma
6.3
on
x
to find a vector
\hat{x}
such that
\|x-\hat{x}\|_{\infty}<O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log(1/\delta))
. Finally, we output
\frac{A\hat{x}}{\|\hat{x}\|_{1}}
.

Privacy.
To understand the privacy of the algorithm, note that for two adjacent
x,x^{\prime}
with
\min(\|x\|_{1},\|x^{\prime}\|_{1})\geq n
, the scaling step increases their
\ell_{1}
distance by at most
1
:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{nx}{\|x\|_{1}}-\frac{nx^{\prime}}{\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{nx}{\|x\|_{1}}-\frac{nx^{\prime}}{\|x\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\frac{nx^{\prime}}{\|x\|_{1}}-\frac{nx^{\prime}}{\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\|x\|_{1}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{1}+n\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}\cdot\left|\frac{1}{\|x\|_{1}}-\frac{1}{\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{n\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{1}\cdot\|x^{\prime}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2.

Therefore, to ensure the final algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, we can work with slightly smaller privacy parameters
(\varepsilon/2,\delta/2)
when invoking Lemma
6.3
. This only blows up the relevant parameters by a constant factor.

Utility.
We now prove the utility of the algorithm. We assume the matrix
A
is such that the conclusion of Lemma
6.1
holds (which happens with probability
1-o(1)
). We also assume the input
x
has
\|x\|_{1}=n
. Let
\hat{x}
be the algorithm’s output. From Lemma
6.3
we see that

\|\hat{x}-x\|_{1}\leq\|\hat{x}\|_{1}+\|x\|_{1}\leq 2n

and

\|\hat{x}-x\|_{\infty}\leq v

for some
v\leq O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon})
.

For every subset
J\subseteq[N]
of
|J|\leq\frac{2n}{v}
columns, let
e_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a vector which takes value
1
on coordinates from
J
and equals zero elsewhere. It is easy to see that
\hat{x}-x
can be written as a convex combination of
\{\pm v\cdot e_{J}:J\subseteq[N],|J|\leq 2n/v\}
. Here, we assume
\alpha>C\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{d}}
for a large enough
C
, so that by setting
n=\frac{C^{\prime}\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}
for an appropriate
C^{\prime}
, the cardinality of
J
(i.e.,
\frac{2n}{v}=\frac{C^{\prime}}{C^{2}}\frac{d}{\log N}
) falls under the regime of Lemma
6.1
. Then, for every
J
, by Lemma
6.1
we have

\|Ae_{J}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{d|J|}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{dn}{v}}.

Finally, by an averaging argument, we see that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{Ax}{\|x\|_{1}}-\frac{A\hat{x}}{\|\hat{x}\|_{1}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|A(x-\hat{x})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sup_{J}\{\|v\cdot Ae_{J}\|_{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot v\cdot\frac{4\sqrt{dn}}{\sqrt{v}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{v}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\sqrt{d}.

The last inequality is valid so long as we choose
C^{\prime}
to be large enough, so that with
n=\frac{C^{\prime}\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon\alpha^{2}}
and
v=O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)
(this big-Oh hides a constant independent of
\alpha,n,d
and
N
), we still have
\frac{v}{n}<\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}
. This completes the proof.
∎
