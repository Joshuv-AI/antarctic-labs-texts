Natural dyes of mulberry leaf enriched with carbon dots as greener methodology to improve the optical properties of chitosan biopolymer
Authors: Hawkar A. Mohammed, Dara M. Aziz, Shujahadeen B. Aziz, Govar H. Hamasalih, Dana S. Muhammad, Sambasivam Sangaraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents an innovative, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective approach to enhancing the optical properties of the chitosan (CS) biopolymer. By utilizing a natural dye extracted from autumn-fallen mulberry leaves (MuL) which serves as a sustainable precursor naturally enriched with carbon quantum dots (CQDs); this work illustrates a successful fabrication of polymer composite films via simple casting technique. The structural, optical, and optoelectronic characteristics of the pristine and composite films were extensively evaluated. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) analysis confirmed that MuL dye enriched with CQD contains abundant NH, OH, and C=O functional groups. The naturally MuL dye enriched with CQDs exhibited distinct blue photoluminescence with an emission peak at 461 nm and a quantum yield of 1.76%, showcasing their viability for eco-friendly optical uses. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy investigation on MuL dye enriched with CQD particles illustrated clear peaks associated π → π* and n → π*. The FTIR study of the films confirmed strong physical interactions and hydrogen bonding between the abundant functional groups present in the MuL dye enriched with CQD particles and the CS matrix. UV–Vis study revealed that enriching the CS matrix with the MuL dye enriched with CQDs significantly shifted the absorption edge to lower photon energies. Most notably, the electronic band gap (Eg) drastically decreased from 5.45 eV in pristine CS down to 2.1 eV in the doped composites. The doping process increased the refractive index from 1.16 to 1.22 and enhanced the dielectric constant. Advanced modeling using the Wemple–DiDomenico (WDD), Tauc, and Absorption Spectra Fitting (ASF) methods provided deeper insights into dispersion energies and the nature of the electronic transitions. The study successfully connected these macroscopic optical parameters, such as phase velocity, to microscopic properties like charge carrier mobility. Other key parameters in optical properties including; N/m*, ε∞, τ, μopt, ρopt, and ωp were evaluated, while the WDD model provided energies of dispersion (Ed), oscillator (Eo), and static index of refraction (no). Optoelectronic parameters such as thermal emissivity (εth) and sheet resistance (Rs) from Kirchhoff’s function were evaluated and studies as a function of wavelength. The figure of merit (FOM) main peak appeared was used to separate the region of photons of electromagnetic radiation that are inefficient to transfer the electrons from the valence band to conduction band.

Introduction

Researchers have spent the last thirty years looking for materials that may be employed as optical limiters. Because of their strong non-linear optical characteristics, ease of molecular design, architectural flexibility, affordability, and high damage threshold, organic compounds outperform the other materials in this category1. In addition, as compared to metals and ceramics, organic compounds have notably worse optical and electrical properties2. Researchers’ attention has recently been drawn more and more to examining the optical and electrical characteristics of polymers3–5. Numerous technologies, including optical devices, depend on their exceptional polarization, anti-reflection, reflection, and interference properties6,7. It is well recognized that appropriate doping may enhance polymers’ electrical and optical characteristics to a desirable degree8. Dopants used in polymers include organic colors, quantum dots, nanoparticles, and other additives9. Transparency, a wide energy gap, a low index of refraction, and dielectric qualities are all provided by polymeric materials. However, with certain optical characteristics and structures, fillers may alter and enhance these qualities in filmmaking10. An electrical and strong interaction connection happens between the polymer and the dopant; also, the functional groups in the host polymer exhibit a greater electron affinity, altering their absorption characteristics, particularly by enhancing the absorption intensity, so this is beneficial for various domains where regulating absorption characteristics is crucial, including organic electronics and optoelectronic devices6. The investigation of the absorption spectrum of light in solids can yield critical insights on the band structure and electronic Eg of crystalline and non-crystalline materials; also, the high-energy section of the spectrum provides insights into the electronic states of the atom, in contrast, the low-energy segment of the absorption spectra may be analyzed to gain an understanding of atomic vibrations11. The need for clean, renewable energy is becoming urgent as the globe confronts environmental and health challenges. Researchers are investigating methods to mitigate global pollution, focusing on using natural colors derived from plants for optoelectronic applications11; however, because of their chemical composition and structure, synthetic dyes are not biodegradable, which is detrimental to the environment, and most are artificial dyes, which are environmentally hazardous, carcinogenic, and resistant. Consequently, scientists are focusing more on natural dyes made from various sources9. Natural dyes are said to be good for the environment since they can be used over and over again, break down in the environment, are safe for skin, and may even be good for health. Natural colors originate from organic sources and can be categorized as plant, animal, mineral, or microbial pigments, depending on their source. Plants provide the main supply of natural dyes12. Because of their superior optical qualities, organic dyes provide attractive materials for photonic devices13. Dye-doped polymers are becoming more popular since they provide many benefits. They may also be employed in photonic devices that are both linear and nonlinear. Dye-doped polymer materials exhibiting appropriate optical properties are suitable candidates for photonic devices, optical fibers, solar cells, electronic sensors, and laser media14,15. The concept of a dye-doped polymer matrix might enhance the stability of opto-chemical and opto-physical characteristics, together with the density of fluorescent or absorptive centers16. The dye must be doped with a polymer to achieve this objective, as numerous prior studies have shown that dyes doped with polymers, including the polymethyl methacrylate polar polymer (PMMA)15, polar polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)17, chitosan CS18, and methylcellulose (MC)6, exhibit superior optical limiting properties compared to dyes alone. Dyes used as dopants in polymers may produce emission over the visible spectrum, exhibiting improved excitation efficiency and low power operation6. Furthermore, robust sources of polyphenols included in plant extracts induce various interactions with biopolymers. Enhanced optical qualities reduce the electronic gap by modifying the film’s absorption and changing its wavelength to a higher value14,19,20. CS is a polymer that naturally appears derived from the deacetylation of chitin, which includes repeating smaller unit molecules. Natural polymers are highly regarded in medical applications for their accessibility, affordability, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, with few exceptions21. CS is generated by the deacetylation of chitin, and due to its chemical makeup, which incorporates hydroxyl (OH) and amino (NH2) groups in its backbone structure, CS exhibits significant ionic conductivity21. A plethora of studies exists about the enhancement of the optical properties of CS polymer by the application of organic dye. Mamand et al.21 demonstrated that natural dyes derived from natural Cosmos Sulphureus Cav (CSC) flowers substantially enhance the optical and structural properties of chitosan, decreasing the polymer’s Eg from 5 to 2.2 eV. Mamand et al.10, in a separate work, incorporate orange peel color into chitosan. The author discovered that the orange dye enhances the optical characteristic of the polymer, altering the bandgap from 5.11 to 2.93 eV. Natural colors made from hollyhock waste flowers (HWFs) were added to CS, causing the energy gap to shift from 5.27 to 2.50 eV9. According to the present study, combining natural dye extracts from mulberry leaves (MuL) flowers and CS biopolymers was affordable and safe to deliver biopolymer composites with improved optical and optoelectronic characteristics. This work establishes that dyes extracted from tree leaves at autumn and winter seasons are enriched with carbon dots based on PL and UV results. This work indicates that the brown coloration of senescent mulberry leaves arises from chlorophyll degradation under natural environmental conditions. As a result, these biomasses serves as a sustainable carbon precursor and establish that dye extracts of leaves with brownish color in autumn and winter season are enriched with CQDs which they have enough oxygen-containing surface functional groups, which may be advantageous for future functional applications, particularly in optical and environmentally relevant material design. The research indicates that the CS refractive index increased from 1.16 to 1.22, while its electronic gap decreased to 2.1 eV, suggesting its potential for many optoelectronic applications. The results reveals that Mulberry leaf (MuL) dye extracts contains flavonoids, carbon precursors, and polyphenols, which are enriched with CQDs. The MuL dye enriched with CQDs have bright photoluminescence and strong UV–vis absorption. The strong absorption of such dye enriched with CQD helps them incorporate with biopolymers like CS, enhancing the absorption of the composite and the band structure. Dispersion of MuL dye enriched with CQDs in the CS matrix facilitates possible charge transfer and the formation of hydrogen bonds with CS host matrix. The interaction between MuL dye enriched with CQDs and CS may occur via surface interactions of OH and NH functional groups of the CS polymer and dye functional groups containing CQD particles. The optical band gap of composite films decreases to near 2 eV, changing the insulator phase of CS into the semiconductor phase of composite films. These enhancements promise the composite films for optoelectronic and biophotonic applications.

Experimental details

Raw materials

Mulberry fallen leaves are used as the natural precursor for the extracted dye. Chitosan (medium molecular weight, degree of approximately deacetylation ~ 80–85%), sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Iceland). Analytical-grade glacial acetic acid, 100% (CH₃COOH) (60 g/mol) from Merck (Germany), was used to prepare the solvent for dissolving chitosan. During the extraction, synthesis, and film preparation steps, distilled water (DW) was used. All chemicals and reagents were used without further purification.

Dye extraction methodology and polymer composite preparation

A solvent-based technique was used to extract a natural color from decomposing mulberry leaves. After thoroughly cleaning the fallen leaves with distilled water to remove surface impurities, they were air-dried and pulverized into small fragments. During the extraction procedure, 100 g of leaf fragments was immersed in 500 mL of distilled water and heated for 30 min with constant agitation; the mixture was then centrifuged and filtered through a Whatman No. 1 filter paper, then passed through a microfilter syringe (PTFE) with a 0.22 µm pore size to obtain a clear, concentrated dye extract.

The film-forming matrix of both pristine and doped chitosan was produced using the solution casting technique; as such, precisely 1.5 g of chitosan powder was amalgamated with 148.5 mL of distilled water, and to facilitate the dissolving of chitosan, 1.5 mL of acetic acid was gradually introduced to the solution while subjected to magnetic stirring. Then the mixture was continuously agitated for 24 h at ambient temperature to ensure complete dissolution and uniform dispersion of the CS chains. The sample allocated for pristine samples and CS included 10 mL and 20 mL of MuL dye enriched with CQD-based material solution, respectively.

To improve the flexibility and mechanical stability of the films, 0.2 mL of glycerol, used as a plasticizer, was included in each beaker, and each combination was agitated for an additional 1.5 h to guarantee full homogeneity of the dye and plasticizer within the chitosan matrix. Then upon completion of the mixing, the solutions were transferred onto polystyrene Petri dishes (9 cm in diameter) and allowed to dry at ambient temperature, so this prolonged drying technique gradually evaporated the solvent, resulting in homogeneous, flexible biopolymer sheets with varying quantities of leaf color. The polymer composite films had an average thickness of 0.055, 0.095, and 0.15 mm. The films are stored in a desiccator to prevent moisture absorption. Results and discussion

Photoluminescence properties and origin of mulberry-leaf-derived CQDs

Mulberry leaves naturally undergo a progressive pigment transformation during the seasonal transition from summer to autumn, leading to observable color changes from green to yellow and, ultimately, brown before abscission. This transformation is attributed to the oxidative degradation of chlorophylls and the concomitant accumulation of carotenoids and xanthophylls, such as lutein and zeaxanthin, which are oxygenated derivatives of carotenes known collectively as xanthophylls23,24. During senescence (Fig. 1), chlorophyll a (C₅₅H₇₂O₅N₄Mg) and b (C55H70MgN4O6) molecules are cleaved by chlorophyllase and pheophytinase, yielding chlorophyllide and pheophytin, respectively. Subsequent dechelation of the central Mg2⁺ ion and oxidation of the porphyrin macrocycle produce colorless catabolites and liberate carbon-rich aromatic fragments25. These conjugated carbon fragments, rich in oxygenated functional groups (–OH, –COOH, and C=O), act as nucleation precursors for carbon quantum dot (CQD) formation26. In the present study, fallen mulberry leaves collected at the late-autumn stage were used as a natural carbon precursor for the CQD-enriched MuL dye. The leaves were extracted by a solvent-based aqueous method in which 100 g of air-dried, powdered leaves were refluxed in 500 mL of distilled water for 30 min under stirring. During this process, residual xanthophylls, flavonoids, and polyphenolic pigments underwent partial carbonization and dehydration, forming nanoscale carbon cores stabilized by oxygen-containing surface moieties. These moieties not only render the CQDs hydrophilic but also introduce localized electronic states responsible for photoluminescence emission.Fig. 1Mechanistic pathway illustrating chlorophyll-to-CQD transformation via enzymatic degradation, Mg2⁺ de-chelation, and oxidative carbonization during leaf senescence. The photoluminescence (PL) behavior of the dye extract-derived CQDs is presented in Fig. 2A,B. The excitation spectrum exhibits a maximum at 340 nm, which can be attributed to π → π* transitions of aromatic sp2-conjugated domains, while the emission maximum appears at 461 nm, corresponding mainly to n → π* transitions associated with oxygen-containing surface groups such as carbonyl and hydroxyl functionalities. These features are characteristic of biomass-derived CQDs containing both graphitic core states and surface-related emissive traps27. A key feature of CQDs is their excitation-dependent emission behavior, which is commonly observed due to surface heterogeneity, size distribution, and the presence of multiple emissive trap states. In such systems, changing the excitation wavelength results in a noticeable shift of the emission peak because different emissive sites are selectively activated. In the present study, however, the emission spectra recorded at excitation wavelengths from 300 to 400 nm (Fig. 2B) show that the emission maximum remains centered within a relatively narrow blue-emission region, with only a slight spectral variation accompanied mainly by changes in emission intensity. This behavior suggests that the prepared CQDs exhibit weak excitation dependence (or quasi excitation-independent emission) rather than strong excitation-dependent fluorescence. Such behavior indicates a relatively uniform distribution of emissive surface states, together with limited surface heterogeneity and a comparatively narrow size distribution of the carbonized domains. The relatively stable emission position implies that the radiative recombination process is governed predominantly by a similar family of surface energy states rather than by a broad distribution of independent emissive centers. At the same time, the moderate variation in intensity observed across the excitation range suggests that some degree of heterogeneity is still present, which is expected for CQDs obtained through a mild green synthesis route. Therefore, the optical response of the present mulberry-leaf-derived CQDs can be interpreted as arising from predominantly homogeneous but not perfectly identical surface emissive sites, which is consistent with partially carbonized, oxygen-functionalized CQD systems27. Under optimal excitation at 340 nm, the CQDs exhibited a strong blue emission centered at 461 nm, visually observed as intense blue luminescence under UV irradiation. Using fluorescein as the reference standard and applying the comparative method under identical measurement conditions, the QY of the obtained CQDs was estimated to be 1.76%. Although this value is relatively modest, it remains within the range commonly reported for biomass-derived CQDs prepared under mild green synthesis conditions, where residual oxygen-containing surface groups and non-radiative recombination pathways may reduce the overall emission efficiency28. Thus, the relatively low QY can be attributed to partial quenching by residual oxygenated groups and non-radiative decay pathways, yet it still demonstrates sufficient fluorescence intensity for potential optical and bioimaging-related applications. Collectively, these findings reveal that the natural photochemical degradation of chlorophyll into xanthophyll derivatives during leaf senescence provides an eco-sustainable carbon framework for CQD formation. Moreover, the environmental transformation pathway from green chlorophyll to yellow xanthophyll to carbonized CQDs illustrates a remarkable example of a biogenic carbon cycle, converting waste biomass into photoluminescent nanomaterials with promising optical properties.Fig. 2(A) Excitation and FL spectra of the dye. UV–Vis absorption analysis of mulberry-leaf CQDs

The aqueous dispersion of CQDs displays three features in the UV region with a weak tail into the visible (Fig. 3): a robust band at ≈ 210 nm, a shoulder at wavelength region ≈ 275 nm, and a broader band at ≈ 320 nm. The intense 210 nm band is assigned to π → π* transitions of high-energy aromatic C=C chromophores in very small sp2 domains and residual polyphenolic fragments inherited from the biomass precursor29–31. The shoulder at ≈ 275 nm corresponds to π → π* transitions of the graphitic sp2 core domains embedded in the predominantly sp3 matrix, a hallmark signature of CQDs prepared from plant matter32. The third, broader feature at ≈ 320 nm is attributed to n → π* transitions of carbonyl-containing surface states (C=O/C–O–), frequently coupled with transitions of conjugated C–N/C=N (pyrrolic/pyridinic/pyrrolidone) functionalities when nitrogenous species are present; these surface states provide HOMO → LUMO excitations that red-shift into the 300–350 nm window33. The long-wavelength absorption tail extending into the visible arises from heterogeneous surface states and edge defects that create a continuum of sub-gap levels, commonly observed for biomass-derived CQDs34.Fig. 3UV–Vis absorption spectrum of mulberry-leaf-derived carbon quantum dots (CQDs) showing characteristic π → π* and n → π* transitions.

FTIR study

The mulberry leaf dye enriched with CQDs, pristine chitosan (CS), and composite films including 10 and 20 mL of MuL dye, all have FTIR spectra that show strong molecular communications between the inserted CQD molecules and the polar polymer matrix. While the MuL dye enriched with CQD extract exhibits strong absorption properties associated with phenolic, carbonyl, and aromatic groups typical of flavonoid compounds, the characteristic FTIR bands of chitosan are related to its principal functional groups—hydroxyl (–OH), amino (–NH2), and residual carbonyl (C=O) groups. When the MuL dye enriched with CQD is introduced into the chitosan matrix, the resulting composite spectra show notable shifts, intensity variations, and band broadening in specific regions, confirming strong physical interactions between the functional groups of both components. The FTIR spectrum of MuL dye enriched with CQD- (Fig. 4a) exhibits characteristic bands indicative of flavonoid and polyphenolic constituents. The O–H stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups participating in hydrogen bonding in phenolic and alcoholic compounds is represented by a broad band centered at 3350–3270 cm−1. The chemical bands at 2927 and 2854 cm−1 are ascribed to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations35. A strong absorption near 1645 cm−1 is ascribed to C=O stretching or aromatic C=C vibrations of conjugated phenolic compounds such as quercetin and rutin, which are abundant in mulberry leaves36. Supplementary peaks around 1510–1460 cm−1 are ascribed to C=C stretching and C–H distortion in aromatic rings, whilst peaks at 1100–1020 cm−1 are ascribed to C–O stretching vibrations in phenolic or ether groups37, so these functional groups provide multiple binding sites capable of interacting with the amino and hydroxyl functionalities of chitosan through hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole attractions. The FTIR spectra of pristine chitosan (Fig. 4b) displays a large absorption band between 3364 and 3282 cm−1, which results from the overlapping stretching vibrations of ( hydroxyl O–H) and (amino N–H) groups involved in significant intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the chitosan chains38. The peaks observed at 2927 and 2867 cm−1 are ascribed to asymmetric and symmetric stretching of aliphatic C–H groups (–CH2 and –CH3)39. The band at about 1657 cm−1 corresponds to the amide I vibration resulting from C=O stretching of the remaining N-acetyl groups, while the band at 1557 cm−1 (amide II) is attributed to N–H bending combined with C–N stretching40. The peaks located near 1405–1379 cm−1 are related to CH₂ bending, and the bands around 1317–1308 cm−1 represent amide III and C–N vibrations41. The absorption features at 1151, 1093, and 1027 cm−1 correspond to the asymmetric stretching of C–O–C linkages and C–O stretching in the β-(1 → 4) glycosidic bonds, confirming the polysaccharide backbone of chitosan42.Fig. 4FTIR spectra of (a) extracted dye, (b) pristine chitosan, (c) 10 mL and (d) 20 MuL dye enriched with CQD–doped chitosan films.

FTIR spectra of (a) extracted dye, (b) pristine chitosan, (c) 10 mL and (d) 20 MuL dye enriched with CQD–doped chitosan films.

The integration of MuL dye enriched with CQD-into chitosan (Fig. 4c,d) results in notable spectrum alterations that unequivocally demonstrate robust physical interactions between the MuL dye enriched with CQD-and the CS matrix, so the most notable characteristic is the displacement of the broad O–H/N–H stretching band from 3364 cm−1 in pristine chitosan to lower wavenumbers (approximately 3252 cm−1 for 10 mL and 3242 cm−1 for 20 mL of MuL dye enriched with CQD-. This redshift, along with the observed increase in band intensity, substantiates the establishment of more robust hydrogen bonds between the O–H and N–H groups of chitosan and the phenolic or carbonyl groups of the dye molecules10. The hydrogen bonding network formed between -NH₃⁺ of protonated chitosan and –O−/C=O sites in the dye extract stabilizes the composite system and reduces the availability of free amino and hydroxyl groups (Fig. 5), thus weakening the native intramolecular hydrogen bonds within chitosan, so this interaction results in the reorganization of the polymeric structure, producing more open binding sites for the dye molecules and altering the vibrational environment of functional groups.Fig. 5Schematic diagram physical interactions of MuL extracted dye molecule functional groups with chitosan chain functional groups (–OH, –NH2 and –NH3+).

Schematic diagram physical interactions of MuL extracted dye molecule functional groups with chitosan chain functional groups (–OH, –NH2 and –NH3+).

Furthermore, the amide I band at 1657 cm−1 in pristine chitosan shifts to 1638 cm−1 in the doped films, and the amide II band slightly decreases in intensity while remaining near 1554 cm−1. These modifications indicate hydrogen bonding and possible electrostatic interactions between the carbonyl groups of the dye and the amino moieties of chitosan21. The appearance and gradual strengthening of the aromatic C=C band at around 1514 cm−1, which becomes more pronounced with higher dye loading, provide further evidence of the successful incorporation of the MuL dye enriched with CQD within the CS matrix, and the intensification of this band corresponds to the contribution of aromatic rings from the MuL dye enriched with CQD, confirming physical blending and molecular-level interaction rather than simple surface adsorption. Also, the shift of the amide I band and the development of new aromatic absorptions together suggest the formation of non-covalent complexes stabilized by hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking between the dye’s aromatic system and the chitosan backbone43. In the lower wavenumber region, the characteristic glycosidic and C–O–C stretching bands at 1151, 1093, and 1027 cm−1 are preserved in all composite films, confirming that the fundamental structure of chitosan remains intact after doping44, however, these peaks exhibit noticeable broadening and slight shifts in position, indicating secondary communications between the dye’s molecules and the oxygen atoms of the polysaccharide chains. The slight shift of the 1027 cm−1 band toward 1029 cm−1 and its increased width suggest hydrogen bonding between the dye’s hydroxyl groups and the ether oxygen atoms in the chitosan network. These subtle modifications demonstrate that dye incorporation induces local structural disorder and influences the microenvironment of the chitosan functional groups without breaking its glycosidic backbone.

Optical study

Absorption and transmittance investigation

Polymeric substances are capable of absorbing light energy within the UV–VIS regions of the electromagnetic (EM) spectrum, so this absorption process involves the excitation of electrons, which move from their low-energy (ground states) to higher-energy (excited states). Also, the electronic configuration of the polymer, especially the arrangement of its molecular state of orbital bonding (σ), delocalized pi (π), and nonbonding (n) orbitals containing lone pairs determines the specific energy levels available for these electronic transitions. Within molecules, atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals that govern optical behavior. Also, most optical transitions associated with impurities occur in the visible region, often leading to color-related defects. Then, exposure to ultraviolet radiation introduces high-energy photons that can trigger chemical and structural transformations, creating additional defects. Such modifications in the polymer’s electronic framework can generate new charge states, thereby affecting its conductivity and charge transport characteristics18,45. Figure 6a displays the UV/visible spectra of both pristine and altered chitosan (CS) samples. The pristine CS film exhibits no noticeable absorption peak in the visible region, indicating its transparency since the incident light is insufficient to trigger electronic transitions. In contrast, doping significantly narrows the energy band gap of the CS matrix, leading to enhanced optical absorption in the CS/MuL dye enriched with CQD-composites. The components of these polymer composites absorb considerable photon energy within the ultraviolet and visible ranges, promoting electron excitation from lower to higher energy levels associated with the π, σ, and n molecular orbitals46. The UV–visible spectrum is the region where chitosan, a biopolymer produced from shrimp shells, demonstrates absorption. It generally reaches a wide peak around 280–310 nm. The (n → σ*) electron transition of the hydroxyl OH group in the polar CS polymer correlates with this absorption band18. Previous investigations show that the absorption band at about 250 nm comes from π → π* electronic transitions that are connected to unsaturated bonds, including C=C and C=O groups, that are in the CS’s backbone or at the ends47. The absorption region of the doped films is then shifted into the visible region when the extracted MuL dye enriched with CQD mixture is added to CS. Most organometallic compounds are known to exhibit distinct optical absorption and emission in the 400–800 nm spectral region. Modifying the structural and electrical characteristics of polymer materials is possible, as they are closely linked to their light-interaction property. Therefore, polymers have lately shown great promise for use as essential components in various applications48. The π electrons in the chitosan and alter samples come from absorption maxima at longer wavelengths, namely in the 400–800 nm region. The absorption spectra of the doped samples shift to longer wavelengths, indicating a decrease in the degree of crystallinity and the formation of new localized electronic states within the forbidden energy gap. These changes are associated with higher amorphous fractions and may exhibit narrow band gaps3,18,49. Since only polymers with many conjugated double bonds may display a wide absorbance zone, this shift can be ascribed to the production of these bonds. These findings show that samples may absorb a large portion of the sun spectrum, making them suitable as raw materials for the production of optoelectronic devices17,50. A greater optical density is linked to a lower light transmittance because, as Fig. 6b illustrates, a higher density makes a material more effective in attenuating light. Because of component interactions, an increase in the ratio of MuL dye enriched with CQD to CS doping may result in lower light transmittance, higher optical density, and enhanced scattering and absorption. Optical bandgap (Eg) study

The electronic Eg refers to a band of energy devoid of electron states in materials. These states exist between the VBs and CBs, often impeding transitions; in other words, they demonstrate forbidden Eg energy. The shift between these two bands necessitates energy equal to the forbidden energy; consequently, electrons may traverse freely as mobile charge carriers between the valence and conduction bands, although insulators typically display energy band gaps of around 10 eV, whereas semiconductors possess band gaps of 1 eV.

Conversely, conductors exhibit an overlap between the VB and CB. A complete understanding of electron transition mechanisms in semiconducting polymers and charge transfer complexes remains tricky. Incoming photons have to possess sufficient energy to cross the forbidden Eg, enabling the transition of electrons from the VB to the CB. Phonons must simultaneously deliver the necessary momentum62,69. Kirchhoff’s functions

The emissivity of most polymer materials generally ranges from 0.7 to 0.9, making them less appropriate for optoelectronic applications. Conversely, applications like solar-control optical coatings and spaceship thermal management systems need materials with low thermal emissivity values (ε < 0.6)92. The thermal emissivity parameter (εth) is the comparative capacity of a material’s exterior to emit thermal radiation, principally linked to photon-induced electron excitation at the surface of the film93. In optics, the two primary properties characterizing a thin film surface may be assessed as functions of wavelength. The Rs and εth arise from the interaction between incoming photons and surface electrons. Conclusion

In conclusions; this study successfully established a sustainable pathway for fabricating dye-doped polymer composites by integrating polar chitosan (CS) with natural dye extracted from mulberry leaf (MuL). By leveraging biomass naturally enriched with carbon quantum dots (CQDs), we provide a greener, more cost-effective alternative to synthetic dopants. This approach offers a greener, safer, and more economical alternative to traditional synthetic dopants, effectively converting agricultural waste into a valuable material for optical applications. Structural and optical characterizations confirmed the successful integration of the CQD-enriched dye into the polymer matrix. The surface functional groups, such as –NH, C=O, and -OH, highlighted by FTIR, exhibited significant interaction among MuL dye enriched with CQD and the CS polymer; also, the resulting CQDs showed strong blue emission, confirming the effectiveness of natural leaf pigments as precursors. FTIR analysis revealed strong physical interactions, primarily through hydrogen bonding, between the abundant hydroxyl, amino, and carbonyl functional groups of the MuL dye and the CS backbone. Furthermore, the biomass-derived CQDs exhibited characteristic blue photoluminescence with an emission peak at 461 nm and a quantum yield of 1.76%, underscoring their viability for eco-friendly optical applications. The incorporation of the MuL dye profoundly enhanced the optical and optoelectronic properties of the pristine chitosan films. Most notably, the electronic band gap experienced a dramatic reduction from 5.45 eV in pristine CS down to 2.1 eV in the highly doped composites. This band gap narrowing was consistently validated across multiple analytical frameworks, including Tauc’s method, dielectric loss evaluation, and the Absorption Spectra Fitting (ASF) approach. Additionally, the introduction of the dye increased the structural disorder and amorphous nature of the composite, as evidenced by an increase in the Urbach energy tail from 0.4150 eV to 0.5350 eV. A key achievement of this work is the establishment of a clear correlation between microscopic electronic properties and macroscopic optical parameters. As the dye concentration increased, the refractive index rose from 1.16 to 1.22, and the dielectric constant showed a substantial enhancement. Conversely, both the phase velocity of light through the polymer and the optical mobility of charge carriers decreased, directly reflecting the influence of the MuL enriched with CQDs on the polymer’s electronic structure. Lower sheet resistance and thermal emissivity highlight the potential of these materials for use in advanced optical and optoelectronic applications. The figure of merit main peak wavelength was used to estimate the optical band gap of the films and their consistency with other models revealed the crucial role of this parameter to specify the types of electron transition. Consequently; these findings confirm that extracted MuL dye enriched with CQD is an effective biodegradable additive for the development of eco-friendly materials suitable for applications.
