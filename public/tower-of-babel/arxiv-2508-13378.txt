Applications of Small Language Models in Medical Imaging Classification with a Focus on Prompt Strategies
Yiting Wang †, Ziwei Wang †, Jiachen Zhong †, Di Zhu †, Weiyi Li †

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in natural language processing and multi-modal understanding. However, their high computational cost, limited accessibility, and data privacy concerns hinder their adoption in resource-constrained healthcare environments. This study investigates the performance of small language models (SLMs) in a medical imaging classification task, comparing different models and prompt designs to identify the optimal combination for accuracy and usability. Using the NIH Chest X-ray dataset, we evaluate multiple SLMs on the task of classifying chest X-ray positions (anteroposterior [AP] vs. posteroanterior [PA]) under three prompt strategies: baseline instruction, incremental summary prompts, and correction-based reflective prompts. Our results show that certain SLMs achieve competitive accuracy with well-crafted prompts, suggesting that prompt engineering can substantially enhance SLM performance in healthcare applications without requiring deep AI expertise from end users.

1
Introduction

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 have achieved impressive results in domains ranging from scientific discovery to healthcare diagnostics
Brown et al. 2020
. They exhibit strong reasoning, contextual understanding, and cross-domain adaptability, making them valuable for complex medical tasks such as the generation of radiology reports reports and automated clinical decision support. However, their substantial computational requirements, high operational costs, and reliance on cloud infrastructure limit accessibility for smaller clinics, low-resource hospitals, and rural healthcare facilities
Panayides et al. 2020
.

Rapid advancement of LLM has spurred the development of small language models (SLMs) such as LLaMA 2
Touvron et al. 2023
, Gemma 3
Team et al. 2024
, many of which are accessible through platforms such as Ollama. Leveraging advances in model compression, quantization, and efficient transformer architectures
Frantar et al. 2022
, SLMs can run locally on consumer-grade hardware, preserve patient data privacy by avoiding external cloud services, and reduce costs, making them attractive for healthcare environments where infrastructure and budget constraints are common. While SLMs provide clear advantages in cost, accessibility, and privacy, they also face important challenges: limited medical knowledge compared to LLMs, weaker performance in complex reasoning tasks, and high sensitivity to prompt formulation. Overcoming these limitations is essential for their successful adoption in healthcare.

In addition, prompt engineering has emerged as a powerful method for improving the performance of SLM. Carefully designed prompts can guide model reasoning and reduce error rates by explicitly structuring tasks
Wang et al. 2023
. Structured prompts that break tasks into sequential reasoning steps can mitigate the limited inference capacity of smaller models. Iterative refinement, in which the model justifies or corrects its own predictions, has been shown to improve output reliability in medical imaging applications.

In this work, we present a comparative evaluation of multiple SLMs in a representative medical imaging task: classifying chest X-ray images into anteroposterior (AP) or posteroanterior (PA) positions. We test three distinct prompt strategies to assess their impact on classification accuracy. Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We benchmark the performance of different SLMs on the AP vs. PA chest X-ray classification task, offering empirical guidance for selecting models that maintain acceptable accuracy despite reduced size and training scope.

2.

We design and evaluate structured prompt strategies that help mitigate the reasoning limitations of SLMs in medical imaging classification.

3.

We quantify the effects of prompt design and provide practical guidelines for healthcare practitioners without AI expertise to achieve consistent, reliable results.

Related Work

Although much of the attention in medical AI has focused on large-scale deep learning and vision–language models (VLMs), recent advances spotlight the growing application of small-language models (SLMs) in diverse imaging tasks
Razzak et al. 2017
. For example, RadPhi-3
Ranjit et al. 2024
, a 3.8 B-parameter SLM, is fine-tuned from Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct for radiology workflows. It excels at summarizing chest X-ray impressions, extracting semantically meaningful sections, and tagging pathologies, demonstrating that compact models can perform specialized radiology tasks efficiently. Complementing these advances, Müller-Franzes et al. conducted a comprehensive benchmark of open-source small and multimodal models, including Qwen2.5-VL and Gemma3, across diverse medical imaging modalities
Müller-Franzes et al. 2025
. Their study found that Qwen2.5-VL achieved the highest accuracy for chest radiographs and retinal fundoscopy, while Gemma3 performed competitively across other imaging tasks.

Compact VLMs are also enabling broader diagnostic support. MiniGPT-Med serves as an LLM-based general interface for radiology, tackling tasks like report generation, visual question answering (VQA), and disease identification across modalities such as X-rays, CTs, and MRIs
Alkhaldi et al. 2024
. OmniV-Med further scales medical VLM by unifying understanding and inference of 2D / 3D imaging with a model with 1.5 B parameters, demonstrating lightweight multitask capability
Jiang et al. 2025
. Additional evaluation of small open source VLMs in acute care scenarios underscores both their potential and challenges, these ’smaller footprint’ models show lower accuracy compared to GPT-4, but remain valuable for resource-constrained settings
Kurz et al. 2025
.

In lung cancer diagnostics, AutoRad-Lung, a radiomic-guided autoregressive vision–language model, improves cross-modal interpretability in the prediction of lung nodule malignancy based on CT aligning the image with textual features
Khademi et al. 2025
. Extending beyond disease-specific models, a CLIP-based VLM has shown strong performance in predicting lung malignancy at one year, while providing explanations tied to nodule characteristics such as margin and attachment
Zhuang et al. 2025
.

Building on these developments, our work systematically evaluates SLMs for chest X-ray classification and tests targeted prompt-engineering strategies to address known limitations, aiming to bridge the gap between research prototypes and practical clinical tools.

We evaluate three representative small language models (SLMs) from the Ollama platform: LLaMA 2–7B, Mistral 7B, and Gemma 7B. These models were selected for their balance between performance, parameter efficiency, and accessibility for local deployment. All experiments are run locally on a single consumer-grade GPU to ensure results reflect realistic deployment constraints in healthcare environments where high-end computational resources may be unavailable.

4
Experiment

4.1
Dataset and Task

We use the NIH Chest X-ray dataset
1
1

1

https://cloud.google.com/healthcare-api/docs/resources/public-datasets/nih-chest
, which contains frontal chest X-ray images labeled with the projection position: anteroposterior (AP) or posteroanterior (PA). All images are preprocessed to a consistent resolution before input to the models. The classification task is to determine whether a given chest X-ray image is AP or PA view.

4.2
Prompt Designs

In all cases, the textual prompt
p
is carefully crafted to guide the model toward accurate classification. Different prompt designs are evaluated to analyze their impact on performance across models.
We compare three prompt strategies:

•

Prompt v0 (Baseline)
: A standard instruction prompt directly asking the model to classify the input image as AP or PA.

•

Prompt v1 (Incremental Summary)
: Same as v0, but after every 10 training images, the model is prompted to summarize its reasoning process and classification criteria.

•

Prompt v2 (Correction-based Reflection)
: For 40% of the training data, the model predicts AP/PA and is then shown the correct label, followed by a reflection prompt to analyze its errors. The remaining 40% of training data is presented as direct instruction.

4.3
Procedure

For each combination of small language model and prompt design, the dataset is randomly split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. The model is trained using its assigned prompt strategy until convergence, after which performance is evaluated on the held-out test set.

In the baseline configuration (Prompt v0), the model receives only the standard classification instruction for distinguishing between AP and PA chest X-ray views. For Prompt v1, the same training process is followed, but every 10 training images the model is prompted to generate a concise summary of its reasoning process. This incremental reasoning step is intended to reinforce decision-making patterns over the course of training.

Prompt v2 introduces a correction-based reflective learning approach. During training, approximately 40% of the examples are used for direct instruction, while another 40% follow a reflection protocol: the model first predicts the label, then receives the correct answer, and is prompted to reflect on and explain any discrepancies. The remaining 20% of the dataset is reserved for evaluation, where no corrective feedback is given. Model performance is quantified by classification accuracy on the test set, allowing for direct comparison across models and prompt strategies.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we evaluated several small language models (SLMs) for the task of chest X-ray view classification and systematically compared three prompting strategies. Our experiments demonstrate that SLM performance can be substantially improved through careful prompt design. Specifically, the best-performing model in our study was LLaMA 2, and the most effective prompting strategy was the correction-based reflection prompt (v2). These results highlight that prompt engineering is not only a practical tool for enhancing model accuracy but also a crucial component when applying SLMs to medical imaging tasks.

6
Limitations and Future Directions

This work has several limitations. First, our evaluation is restricted to three small language models and a single chest X-ray classification task (AP vs. PA). While this setting allows for controlled benchmarking, it does not fully capture the complexity of real-world medical imaging scenarios, such as multi-class disease detection or multi-view reasoning. Second, the models were tested in isolation without integration into clinical workflows, which means factors such as interoperability, human-AI collaboration, and usability in fast-paced healthcare environments remain unexplored. Third, we focused on three prompt strategies, leaving other potentially effective techniques—such as chain-of-thought reasoning, retrieval-augmented prompting, and few-shot in-context learning—unexamined.

Future research will address these gaps by expanding the scope to include additional SLM architectures, broader medical imaging datasets (e.g., CT, MRI), and disease-specific diagnostic tasks such as pneumonia and lung cancer detection. We also plan to investigate multimodal integration of imaging with radiology reports, assess long-term model stability across institutions, and refine prompt-engineering guidelines tailored for healthcare professionals without AI expertise. Finally, incorporating actionable recommendations alongside classification results and evaluating their impact on clinical decision-making could further enhance the practical utility of SLMs in healthcare.
