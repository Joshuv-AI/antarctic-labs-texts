A preliminary study on the development of a chronic disease self-management curriculum for disability support workers: educational needs analysis
Authors: Han Nah PARK, Hye Jin NAM, Haesun LEE MSN, Sujin LEE, Bohye KIM, Ju Young YOON
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBecause disability support workers play a key role in supporting the healthcare of people with disabilities, it is important to educate them on how to assist persons with disabilities in managing chronic diseases. In this context, chronic disease self-management refers to empowering persons with disabilities to actively manage their own health and illnesses, with support from trained disability support workers. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the educational needs and priorities for developing a chronic disease self-management curriculum designed for disability support workers working in supportive housing.MethodsThis study utilized an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, combining focus group interviews and a survey for disability support workers working in supportive housing for people with disabilities for at least three months. First, six focus group interviews with 32 participants were conducted from July 19 to September 15, 2023. Second, a survey was conducted with 193 participants from November 20 to December 15, 2023. Educational needs analysis was conducted using a paired t-test, the Borich Needs Assessment Model, and the Locus for Focus Model.ResultsThirteen items were identified as high priorities for educational needs: “Representative physical activity programs according to disability type,” “Physical activity based on individual functional status, disease, and preference,” “Specific action strategies to promote physical activity in daily life,” “Diet for overweight/obese people with disabilities,” “Skin disease,” “Mental disease,” “Basic knowledge of disabilities,” “Advanced knowledge of disabilities,” “Communication,” “Aggressive and challenging behavior,” “Sex education,” “Psychological support program for disability support workers,” and “Assistive devices.”ConclusionsThe results of this study can be used as a basis for developing a chronic disease self-management education program for disability support workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25659-9.Keywords: Disability support workers, Education, Needs assessment

Background

Globally, the concept of aging in place has emerged, highlighting the importance of older adults living independently in the community. Similarly, there is a growing recognition that persons with disabilities should also live independently in the community rather than in hospitals [1]. To support independent living for persons with disabilities, supportive housing for people with disabilities (hereafter, supportive housing) was introduced. Supportive housing refers to transitional facilities that assist individuals in adapting to community life following hospital discharge [1]– [2]. In accordance with the evolving supportive framework for independent living for individuals with disabilities in the international community, Korea also introduced supportive housing in 2019 [3]. Supportive housing serves as a stable living space for individuals with physical or mental disabilities, providing integrated services tailored to health management and daily living maintenance on an individual basis [2].

Persons with disabilities are at a higher risk of chronic diseases, leading to elevated mortality rates [4]. In South Korea (Korea hereafter), 70.6% of persons with disabilities experience chronic illnesses lasting over three months [5]. They face a 1.34-fold higher risk of hypertension, and 1.51-fold higher risk of diabetes compared to non-disabled individuals. Additionally, around 60.7% of individuals with disabilities are overweight or obese, which is closely linked to metabolic chronic diseases [4]. These findings highlight the importance of chronic disease management among this population [5]. In particular, for people with disabilities living in supportive housing, the role of disability support workers is important in managing chronic conditions [3, 6].

Disability support workers, who closely interact with individuals with disabilities, play a crucial role in healthcare management by assessing medical needs, accompanying them to medical appointments, and facilitating access to healthcare services [3]. As advocates for health-promoting behaviors, they assist with daily activities such as meal preparation and create opportunities for social activities and participation [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to provide chronic disease management education related to supportive housing for disability support workers who directly or indirectly influence the healthcare of individuals with disabilities [7].

The current curriculum provided to disability support workers in Korea primarily focuses on theory. Thus, it lacks practical training such as movement and position changes for severely disabled individuals. The field learning is conducted informally, and the short training duration makes it challenging for learners to fully grasp the content [8, 9]. In contrast, in countries like Canada, Australia, and the United States, disability support worker education courses are offered by universities or nationally recognized educational institutions. In Canada, courses range from 37 weeks to 1 year, covering various aspects such as types of disabilities, management, treatment, communication, personal hygiene, and daily life care [10]. In Australia, individuals can work as disability support workers after completing education and field training, typically consisting of 7 to 9 courses and requiring around 120 h of field training. The certification process generally takes about 6 months to 1 year [11]. In the United States, disability support worker education is offered through multiple pathways, including community college certificate programs, university degree programs, apprenticeships, and federally or state-funded training programs. For example, Monroe Community College offers a 1-year certificate program covering both theory and practical fieldwork [12]. Compared to these countries, the education period in Korea is shorter, typically lasting around 2 weeks (40 h of theoretical and practical training, along with 10 h of field training). Thus, completing Korea’s required curriculum may not offer workers a comprehensive understanding of disabled individuals’ characteristics and the required services. Moreover, the short education period lowers the entry barrier to jobs and may contribute to labor devaluation [9].

Educational needs assessment analyzes the gap between the current and desired levels of learners’ knowledge, competence, and skills to determine educational priorities. This process ensures that education addresses the actual needs of learners and contributes to solving real-world problems [13]. In addition, survey-based approaches such as the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the Locus for Focus Model provide objective data to establish priority rankings, facilitate consensus among stakeholders, and offer valuable information for policymakers [14]– [15].

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to identify educational needs and priorities for developing a chronic disease self-management curriculum for disability support workers who work in supportive housing.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, beginning with qualitative focus group interviews (FGIs) followed by a survey. This approach is known to be useful in understanding phenomena and developing hypotheses or surveys in situations where existing knowledge is limited [16]. Given the lack of prior research on disability support workers’ educational needs, this approach was adopted.

Educational needs analysis

Common methods used to analyze educational needs include mean difference analysis, the Borich Needs Assessment Model, and Locus for Focus Model [14]. While mean difference analysis compares current performance to desired levels, it may lack direction. The Borich Needs Assessment Model goes beyond a simple comparison to prioritizing needs based on urgency, but the model lacks clear criteria for determining the extent of consideration for each priority [14]– [15]. To address these limitations, the Locus for Focus Model is often combined with the Borich Needs Assessment Model, providing visual information on the extent of consideration for each priority, and offering a macroscopic benchmark for prioritization [17].

The Borich needs assessment formula calculates educational needs by multiplying the difference between the required competency level (RCL) and the present competency level (PCL) by the required competency level (RCL), and then dividing the result by the total number of participants (N). A higher score indicates a greater educational need [15].

The Locus for Focus model helps determine priority areas using a conceptual four-quadrant framework. The horizontal axis represents the desired competency level (importance), and the vertical axis represents the degree of discrepancy between the desired and present competency levels. The mean importance score of all items forms the midpoint of the horizontal axis, and the mean discrepancy score forms the midpoint of the vertical axis. The first quadrant (high discrepancy/high importance, HH) indicates the highest priority area, where both importance and discrepancy exceed the mean. The second quadrant (high discrepancy/low importance, HL) represents items with high discrepancy but low importance, suggesting the need to examine why importance is perceived as low despite large gaps. The third quadrant (low discrepancy/low importance, LL) includes items with both discrepancy and importance below the mean, indicating the lowest priority. The fourth quadrant (low discrepancy/high importance, LH) includes items with low discrepancy but high importance, representing areas where current competency levels are relatively sufficient [14, 17].

Study participants

FGI and survey recruitment documents were placed in 6 supportive housing in S City with the cooperation of the Welfare Foundation, which manages supportive housing. The FGI and survey were conducted only for those who expressed interest in participating in the study through the researcher’s contact details provided in the recruitment document.

Inclusion criteria for study participants were: (1) currently working in supportive housing for at least 3 months, (2) capable of reading and writing; understanding the study’s purpose, and (3) agreeing to participate. Exclusion criteria included individuals with no experience supporting people with developmental disabilities (intellectual disability and autism).

Step 1. FGI

Six sessions were conducted with 32 participants, with 4–7 participants in each FGI.

Step 2. Survey

Initially, 212 people participated, but 2 individuals lacked the required minimum work experience of 3 months, and 17 respondents only provided general characteristics. This resulted in a final sample of 193 participants for analysis, which meets the minimum requirement of 160 participants calculated using the G*Power 3.1 program with a significance level of 0.05, effect size of 0.15, and power of 0.95.

Data collection

Step 1. FGI

Six FGI sessions were conducted from July 19 to September 15, 2023, across three supportive housing locations in S City. Based on participants’ preferences, five FGIs were conducted face-to-face, with one virtual FGI via Zoom. Each FGI session lasted approximately 1 h and 30 min, with all interviews recorded and transcribed with participants’ consent. The corresponding author was the moderator for all FGIs, using a semi-structured questionnaire to guide the discussion. The first author was the co-moderator and took notes. Both authors have extensive experience conducting FGIs and have attended qualitative research workshops. Participants freely shared their opinions in a comfortable environment with ample time to answer the questions. Debriefing sessions with the participants immediately followed each FGI to ensure content objectivity. Recordings were transcribed within a week and then deleted.

Step 2. Survey

Surveys were conducted from November 20, 2023, to December 15, 2023, in five supportive housing locations in S City. The survey questionnaire consisted of 41 items, and the average time to complete the survey was about 20 min.

Measures

Step 1. FGI

The semi-structured interview guide was developed through discussions among six research team members, based on the framework of DeJonckheere and Vaughn (2019) [18]. The purpose and scope of the study were defined, participants were identified, and ethical issues were addressed prior to data collection. The interview questions were open-ended, neutral, and clear, using simple and familiar language. Follow-up and probing questions were adjusted according to participants’ responses, beginning with general questions and gradually moving to more in-depth topics. The key questions included in the questionnaire were: “Have you ever received any education to support people with disabilities in managing their health?” “If so, what kind of education did you receive, how did you learn it, and what aspects of it helped you to support people with disabilities in managing their health?” “How do you support people with disabilities in managing their health?” “What are the facilitators and barriers to supporting people with disabilities?” and “What kind of education do you think is needed to support people with disabilities in managing their health?”

Step 2. Survey

After conducting the FGIs, six researchers independently performed a content analysis of the transcripts. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and key themes were identified. Based on these themes, the research team developed the survey questionnaire. The content validity of the questionnaire was reviewed by two experts—nurses working in community-based disability health management. The survey consisted of 41 questions, including 9 questions on general characteristics, 27 questions on educational content, and 5 questions on educational methods. An English language version of the questionnaire is available as additional file 1.

General Characteristics

Nine questions asked about general characteristics of the participants. Items included gender, age, education level, work experience in current institution, total work experience related to caring for the elderly or disabled, current position, average working hours per week, and number and type of disabled people currently supported.

Educational content

The survey included 27 questions, categorized as follows: 3 questions on physical activity, 5 questions on diet, 6 questions on disease management, 4 questions on health information, 5 questions on disability, and 4 questions on other topics. Each question was evaluated for both performance and importance. Performance measures the current level of competency for each item, assessed on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “very difficult to teach” (1 point) to “able to teach very well” (5 points). A higher score indicates a higher level of competency. Importance assesses the need for the item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “no education needed at all” (1 point) to “very necessary” (5 points). A higher score signifies a greater aneed for educational content.

Educational method

The survey asked five questions to measure the experience and importance of each educational method. Experience with the educational method was measured as either “yes” or “no.” Importance assesses how much participants think the educational method is needed, rated on a 5-point Likert scale from “not needed at all” (1) to “very needed” (5). Higher scores indicate a greater need for the educational method.

Statistical analysis

Step 1. FGI

Interview data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis following the steps outlined by Erlingsson and Brysiewicz (2017) [19]. Six researchers independently and repeatedly read the transcribed interview texts to gain a comprehensive understanding of the data. Meaning units were identified, condensed, and coded, and the resulting codes were organized into categories and, where applicable, overarching themes. Any disagreements during the categorization or theme development stages were discussed until consensus was reached among the research team. Qualitative rigor was ensured by adhering to Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) [20] criteria of credibility, fittingness, auditability, and confirmability.

Step 2. Survey

The SPSS 26.0 program was utilized, applying the educational needs analysis method proposed by Cho (2009) [14]. General characteristics were analyzed by calculating percentages, means, and standard deviations, while t-tests and ANOVA analyses were conducted to determine differences in performance and importance according to general characteristics. The educational needs analysis employed a paired t-test, the Borich Needs Assessment Model, and the Locus for Focus Model. Finally, priority items were determined by examining the overlap between the top-ranked items in the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the items in the first and second quadrants of the Locus for Focus Model.

Missing value analysis

Missing data for performance and importance that were necessary for educational needs analysis were imputed using multiple imputation (MI). MI is a common method for addressing missing values [21]. A total of five imputations were performed, with linear regression models used as the MI models.

Results

FGI study results

The general characteristics of the research participants in the FGIs are shown in additional file 2. The study participants were divided into two groups according to their positions: managers (e.g., center directors and team leaders), and practitioners, (e.g., activity supporters, coaches, and coordinators). In terms of empowering people with disabilities to manage their own health, practitioners talked about specific training content and methods, while managers focused more on strategies and institutional support.

The 32 participants in the FGIs identified six essential topics as necessary educational content to support self-care for health for people with disabilities: physical activity, diet, disease management, health information, disability, and other.

In physical activity, participants noted a need for education on representative physical activity programs tailored to each type of disability, such as spinal cord injury, brain lesions, and hemiplegia. Additionally, participants stressed a need for physical activity plans based on each user’s functional status, illness, and preferences, along with specific practical strategies for increasing physical activity in daily life.

Regarding diet, participants showed interest in learning about various aspects of diet, including healthy meal and snack options that were suitable for different dietary needs such as those who are underweight, overweight, or diabetic. Additionally, they sought guidance on portion sizes and dietary considerations to better manage residents’ nutritional intake.

For disease management, participants desired an overview of common conditions found in supportive housing, such as hypertension, diabetes, constipation, obesity, skin diseases, and mental illness, along with strategies to manage these diseases.

Health information encompassed guidance on obtaining accurate health information, a list of hospitals and community resources available to people with disabilities, recommended health screenings throughout the life cycle, instructions for correct medication usage, and medication information.

In terms of disabilities, participants wanted more knowledge about basic disabilities, including autism, intellectual and developmental disabilities, which are prevalent in supportive housing settings. Additionally, participants sought guidance on effective communication with people with disabilities, strategies for building trusting relationships, understanding disability rights, and responding to violent or challenging behavior by people with disabilities.

Other educational needs included sex education, psychosocial support programs for disability support workers, guidance on creating safe and comfortable living environments, such as appropriate sleeping conditions and stimulating ways to distinguish between night and day, as well as training on assistive devices, such as wheelchairs.

The educational methods participants described as effective included case-based education featuring successful case management, role-playing exercises, training sessions aimed at facilitating communication among disability support workers, hands-on training to acquire practical skills and methods, and meeting time with healthcare professionals to discuss the needs of the individuals with disabilities they support. Additionally, there was a strong emphasis on the perceived need for repeated and regular education.

Educational needs analysis

To identify the priorities of educational needs, the Borich needs ranking (Table 2) and the Locus for Focus Model (Fig. 1) were utilized. First, the Locus for Focus Model was applied to examine the gap between the RCL and the PCL. Items located in the first quadrant (HH) and the second quadrant (HL) were considered as priority areas for education. As a result, 13 priority items were identified—nine in the HH quadrant and four in the HL quadrant.

The Locus for Focus Model presents the educational needs of disability support workers regarding chronic disease self-management education. HH represents High discrepancy and High importance; HL represents High discrepancy and Low importance; LL represents Low discrepancy and Low importance; and LH represents Low discrepancy and High importance

Subsequently, the top 13 items from the Borich Needs Assessment (derived from Table 2) were selected for comparison. The 13 items from the Locus for Focus Model exactly matched the top 13 ranked items in the Borich analysis. Therefore, these overlapping 13 items were determined to represent the highest educational needs (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study examined educational needs and priorities for developing a chronic disease self-management curriculum for disability support workers using the Borich Needs Assessment Model and the Locus for Focus Model.

Educational needs analysis

This study identified thirteen priorities for chronic disease self-management education for disability support workers. Before working as a disability support worker, it is crucial to comprehend the various types of disabilities and their unique characteristics. The curriculum should include both “basic and advanced knowledge of disabilities,” with a specific focus on autism, intellectual, and developmental disabilities, which are prevalent in supportive housing in Korea. Additionally, a comprehensive understanding of “mental disease” is imperative, as it poses significant challenges in providing effective support. Mental health education should be provided separately from other diseases, given the complexity of mental health. Enhanced abilities, skills, and awareness in identifying and managing mental health issues early can greatly improve outcomes for individuals with conditions such as schizophrenia or alcoholism [22].

The educational need for understanding “skin diseases” was high. While skin diseases may not present life-threatening symptoms compared to systemic diseases, they affect patients’ emotional well-being, interpersonal relationships, and social life. Qualitative research on individuals with severe skin conditions has revealed the emotional distress and social challenges they face. Patients may also encounter stigma and misconceptions, with their conditions occasionally mistaken for infectious diseases. Moreover, the pain and itching associated with skin diseases can disrupt daily activities such as meal preparation and food intake. Therefore, education on understanding and managing skin diseases is essential [23].

Three educational items related to “physical activities” were included, indicating high educational needs. Physical activity is important for people with disabilities as it enhances their daily living skills and overall health. It also positively impacts psychological aspects such as self-esteem and confidence [24]. For individuals with disabilities, the requirements for physical activity vary depending on the type of disability, disability rank, and physical characteristics. Therefore, it is necessary to develop physical activity education that considers these factors [25].

We also identified a need for sufficient education on “assistive devices.” These devices enable people with disabilities to live more independently and reduce physical and mental strain for caregivers. By providing education on the operation, selection, and application of assistive devices tailored to each type of disability, disability support workers can better anticipate the needs of the individuals they care for and use assistive devices effectively in various situations [26].

“Diet for overweight/obese people with disabilities” was identified as a high priority. Findings from the FGIs confirmed that many disabled individuals experience sudden weight gain upon discharge from hospitals or facilities, making the management of overweight and obesity a significant task for disability support workers. Diet al.so plays a crucial role in the health of people with disabilities. They are at higher risk of physical inactivity due to their disabilities, which can lead to overweight or obesity, consequently, increasing the incidence of chronic diseases and risking poor health outcomes and premature death. Education on correct eating habits, menus, and dietary strategies for overweight or obese individuals with disabilities is essential [27].

“Sex education” and “aggressive and challenging behavior” were identified as high priorities. Sexuality is integral to quality of life, reflected in recent policies aiming for a high quality of life for disabled individuals. With the deinstitutionalization policy, many adults with developmental disabilities now rely on community welfare services for support and education on sex and love. However, disability support workers often lack adequate training in this area, leading to a reported lack of confidence in providing appropriate sex education. Therefore, providing sex education for disability support workers is essential so they can effectively support individuals with developmental disabilities [28]. “Challenging behavior” that is generally harmful to one-self or others, has a negative impact on the disabled person’s relationship formation with colleagues and friends as well as employment. Effective management relies on understanding the causes and implementing appropriate response methods. Thus, education should include training on managing aggressive and challenging behaviors [29].

“Psychological support program for disability support workers” was also identified as a high priority. Disability support workers who are responsible for the overall daily care of disabled individuals often face excessive stress, leading to feelings of helplessness, a negative self-concept, physical exhaustion, and negative emotions towards their jobs and lives [30]. Implementing a professional counseling psychology program for disability support workers can alleviate burnout and stress, which can ultimately increase job satisfaction, and, in turn, enhance the quality of services provided to people with disabilities [31].

“Communication” was also a high priority. Communication is a fundamental right that everyone should enjoy, and it is crucial for people with disabilities to be able to utilize their communication potential and interact with others. However, there is a lack of awareness and education among both people with disabilities and experts, as well as with disability support workers, about the rights and methods of communication for individuals with disabilities. Therefore, communication must be included in the education for disability support workers [32].

Educational methods

Our findings indicated that, in terms of education methods, expert consulting, practice, case management, group activities, and situational or role plays were all important to the participants. Learning is more effective when a variety of teaching and learning methods are applied rather than just theoretical lectures. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a learner-led curriculum by applying various teaching and learning methods. Previous research has shown similar trends, highlighting that expert consultation is particularly effective, with participants expressing a strong desire to meet healthcare professionals to discuss intervention and management strategies for individuals with disabilities [33]. Disability support workers found practical application and observation beneficial, although they mentioned that the number of allocated 10 h was insufficient. They also asked for exposure to various field training organizations catering to different disability types, necessitating curriculum reform [33]. Case management with problem-solving through real-world scenarios was emphasized to enhance learners’ active participation and decision-making skills [34]. Additionally, they requested group activities that foster cooperative learning and skill development, forming a supportive network for disability support workers post-education [35]. Role plays, known for their effectiveness in changing attitudes, should also be integrated into the curriculum. They provide valuable opportunities for learners to engage in cognitive and social interaction. Role plays also facilitate attitude shifts and improve the overall effectiveness of the training [36].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected using a self-report survey method, which may cause bias in the responses. Thus, it is necessary to interpret the results of the study with caution. Second, the study was conducted in a specific supportive housing context in an urban region in Korea, limiting the generalizability of the findings to disability support workers in other regions. Future research should employ sampling methods that ensure representativeness across diverse contexts. Third, although the survey questionnaire was developed based on the FGI results and its content validity was reviewed by two experts, no formal pilot testing or cognitive interview testing was performed before the survey. Conducting pilot testing in future studies could further enhance the rigor and validity of the survey.

Conclusion

Thirteen priorities were identified for chronic disease self-management education for disability support workers. Based on the study findings, future research is warranted to develop chronic disease self-management education reflecting these priorities and enhancing the effectiveness of the curriculum. Specifically, systematic education programs are needed to address support workers’ difficulties and enhance their capacities, alongside national-level policy support for ongoing education.
