Leptogenesis in the presence of density perturbations
Kenta Hotokezaka 1, Ryusuke Jinno 2, Rin Takada 1

Abstract
We point out a new effect on the freeze-out process of heavy particles induced by density perturbations in the early universe, which we call “acoustically driven freeze-out.” This beyond-linear effect is caused by the exponential decoupling of heavy particles from the thermal bath in the presence of density perturbations, and already at moderately large values δ ​ T / T ¯ = 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 − 2 ) \delta T/\bar{T}=\mathcal{O}(10^{-2}) it cannot be captured by linear perturbation theory. We illustrate this effect with leptogenesis taking the decay and inverse decay of heavy neutrinos into account, and discuss its phenomenological implications. We found that perturbations always enhance the (spatially averaged) values of the final lepton asymmetry, and as a result, constraints on the mass of heavy neutrinos are found to be relaxed in the presence of perturbations.

II
Leptogenesis in the presence of cosmological perturbations

II.1
Prerequisites for leptogenesis

Leptogenesis

[
18
]
is a scenario in which the CP-violating decays of heavy right-handed neutrinos, together with the sphaleron process in the SM, generate the baryon asymmetry of the universe.
In leptogenesis, the SM is extended with right-handed neutrinos with heavy Majorana masses that violate the lepton number.
These right-handed neutrinos have Yukawa interactions with the Higgs and light leptons, whose complex phases give rise to CP violation.
Their CP-violating decays produce lepton asymmetry, part of which is then converted into baryon asymmetry through the SM sphaleron process.
The Majorana masses of the right-handed neutrinos at the same time explain the observed light masses for left-handed neutrinos via the seesaw mechanism.

Leptogenesis scenarios can be classified into two classes: thermal and non-thermal (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
38
]
).
The former assumes thermal abundance for the right-handed neutrinos as one of the initial conditions, while in the latter the right-handed neutrinos are produced via various non-thermal processes.
One of the attractive features of the former class is its robust predictivity: the number of free parameters is significantly smaller, thereby allowing for more robust experimental predictions.
For this reason we focus on thermal leptogenesis in this paper.

We consider a simple model so-called vanilla leptogenesis, in which heavy right-handed neutrinos
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
, and
N_{3}
with masses in the order
M_{1}<M_{2}<M_{3}
are added to the SM.
In this model, the Majorana mass terms and Yukawa couplings of the right-handed neutrinos

\mathcal{L}=-\dfrac{M_{\alpha}}{2}\bar{N}_{\alpha}N_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha\beta}\phi^{\ast}\bar{l}_{\alpha}N_{\beta}+{\rm h.c.},

(2)

are added to the SM Lagrangian together with their kinetic terms.
Here
M_{\alpha}
and
y_{\alpha\beta}
denote the Majorana masses and Yukawa couplings, respectively, while
\phi
and
l_{\alpha}
are the Higgs and lepton doublets of the SM, respectively.
The summation over lepton flavors
\alpha,\beta=1,2,3
is implicit.
The light neutrino masses are generated from the type-I seesaw mechanism
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

The lightest right-handed neutrino
N_{1}
decays into a lepton doublet and Higgs doublet pair or its CP-conjugate:

N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi,\qquad N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}.

(3)

In this paper, only the decay and inverse decay of
N_{1}
are included, and scattering processes are ignored.
The decays of
N_{2}
and
N_{3}
are assumed to be negligible in the final asymmetry, and they appear only as internal lines in the diagrams of
N_{1}
decay.
This simplified model is sufficient to capture the beyond-linear effect we point out in the present study.
The
B-L
asymmetry is generated from the CP-violating decays (
3
) as

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{\Gamma(N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi)}{\Gamma(N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi)+\Gamma(N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast})}=\dfrac{1+\varepsilon_{1}}{2},\\[12.50002pt]
\dfrac{\Gamma(N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast})}{\Gamma(N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi)+\Gamma(N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast})}=\dfrac{1-\varepsilon_{1}}{2}.\end{array}

(4)

Here the parameter
\varepsilon_{1}(<0)
describing CP asymmetry is given by (see Appendix
D
)

\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon_{1}=\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=2,3}\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}f(x),\qquad x\equiv\dfrac{M_{\gamma}^{2}}{M_{1}^{2}}\\[17.50002pt]
f(x)\equiv\sqrt{x}\,\biggl[1+\dfrac{1}{1-x}-(1+x)\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{x}\biggr)\biggr].\end{array}

(5)

In the following, we consider the time evolution of the ratios
N_{N_{1}}\equiv n_{N_{1}}/n_{\gamma}
and
N_{B-L}\equiv(n_{B}-n_{L})/n_{\gamma}
, where
n_{N_{1}}
,
n_{\gamma}
,
n_{B}
, and
n_{L}
denote the number densities of right-handed neutrinos, photons, baryons, and leptons, respectively.
Their initial conditions are given at sufficiently high temperatures

by

N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(T\gg M_{1})=\dfrac{n_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}}{n_{\gamma}^{\rm eq}}=\dfrac{3}{4},\qquad N_{B-L}^{\rm eq}(T\gg M_{1})=0.

(6)

After the decay of right-handed neutrinos, the negative lepton number
L
is converted into positive
B
and negative
L
through the sphaleron process
[
39
,
40
]
.
Since
B-L
is conserved in this process, the final baryon number
B
is related to the value of
B-L
generated by
N_{1}
decay as
[
41
,
42
]

B=\dfrac{8\nu_{f}+4\nu_{s}}{22\nu_{f}+13\nu_{s}}(B-L)=\dfrac{28}{79}(B-L),

(7)

where
\nu_{f}=3
is the number of fermion generations and
\nu_{s}=1
is the number of Higgs doublets.

II.2
Prerequisites for cosmological perturbations

In an inhomogeneous universe, the time evolution of the right-handed neutrinos and baryon and/or lepton numbers occurs in the presence of cosmological perturbations.
In this paper, we follow Refs.
[
43
,
44
]
for the formulation of cosmological perturbations.

The background metric is given by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric

ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j},

(8)

where
g_{\mu\nu}
is the metric tensor and
a=a(t)
is the scale factor of the universe.
We assume that the universe is radiation-dominated in the relevant epoch, and in this case the Friedmann equation is

\displaystyle 3M_{\rm P}^{2}H^{2}=\rho_{r},

(9)

with

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2t},\qquad\rho_{r}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{30}g_{*}T^{4}.

(10)

Here
M_{\rm P}=1/\sqrt{8\pi G}
is the reduced Planck mass,
H\equiv\dot{a}/a=a^{\prime}/a^{2}
is the Hubble parameter, and
\rho_{r}
is the radiation energy density.
During the radiation-dominated epoch, the conformal time
\eta
defined by
d\eta=dt/a
is related to the cosmological time as
\eta\propto t^{1/2}
.
In what follows the dot denotes differentiation with respect to the physical time
t
, and the prime denotes differentiation with respect to the conformal time
\eta
.

The scalar perturbations of the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
are expressed in terms of four independent functions of time and space,
A
,
B
,
C
, and
E
, as
[
44
]

\displaystyle g_{00}

\displaystyle=-a^{2}(1+2A),

(11)

\displaystyle g_{0i}

\displaystyle=-a^{2}B_{,i},

(12)

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle=a^{2}\biggl[(1+2C)\delta_{ij}+2\biggl(E_{,ij}-\dfrac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla^{2}E\biggr)\biggr].

(13)

On the other hand, perturbation in the matter sector appears in the temperature fluctuation
\delta_{T}
defined by

T=\bar{T}+\delta T,\qquad\delta_{T}\equiv\dfrac{\delta T}{\bar{T}},

(14)

where
\bar{T}
is the spatially averaged temperature of the universe.
With these definitions, it is useful to introduce new variables:

\displaystyle\bar{z}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{\bar{T}},

(15)

\displaystyle z_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{\bar{T}+\delta T}=\dfrac{\bar{z}}{1+\delta_{T}}.

(16)

In the following sections, we will use
\bar{z}
and
z_{T}
instead of physical time
t
and physical temperature
T
, respectively.

II.3
Boltzmann equation

The evolution of the number density of each particle species in the presence of collision processes is governed by the Boltzmann equation.
After integrating over the momentum space, it takes the form (see Appendix
B
)

{\mathcal{N}^{\mu}}_{;\mu}=\dfrac{g_{\rm deg}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{d^{3}p}{E}\;C[f],

(17)

where
\mathcal{N}^{\mu}
is the number density current defined by
\mathcal{N}^{\mu}\equiv nU^{\mu}
with
n
and
U^{\mu}
being the number density and the 4-velocity, respectively,
g_{\rm deg}
is the degrees of freedom of the species,
f
is the distribution function, and
C[f]
is the collision term.
The left-hand side of equation (
17
) can be written as

(nU^{\mu})_{;\mu}=\bar{z}H(1-A)\biggl(\dfrac{dn}{d\bar{z}}+\dfrac{3}{\bar{z}}n\biggr)+\dfrac{n}{a}({v^{i}}_{,i}+3C^{\prime}),

(18)

where
v^{i}
is the 3-velocity, the latter of which is first order in perturbation.
Here we used

U^{0}=a^{-1}(1-A),\qquad U^{i}=a^{-1}v^{i},

(19)

and equations (
104
)–(
113
).

For the right-handed neutrinos
N_{1}
, we take only the decay and inverse decay into account.
In this case, assuming that the right-handed neutrinos are in kinetic equilibrium, the thermal average of the right-hand side of equation (
17
) yields

\displaystyle\biggl\langle\dfrac{g_{N_{1}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{d^{3}p_{N_{1}}}{E_{N_{1}}}\;C\biggr\rangle

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{4\pi^{3}g_{l}g_{\phi}|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}{M_{1}}\cdot\dfrac{K_{1}(z_{T})}{K_{2}(z_{T})}\cdot\bigl[n_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-n_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr]

\displaystyle\equiv-\Gamma_{D}(\bar{z}=\infty)\biggl\langle\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\biggr\rangle(z_{T})\bigl[n_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-n_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr].

(20)

The collision term (
20
) is invariant under coordinate transformations: indeed, when the collision term is in the Local Inertial Frame Instantaneously at Rest with respect to the Comoving Observer (LIFIRCO), the cross sections have the same expressions as in the Minkowski space, and the collision term contains
no metric fluctuations

[
45
]
.
Any metric fluctuation is contained in, and arises from, the Liouville term, i.e., the left-hand side of equation (
17
).
As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\biggl(\dfrac{dn_{N_{1}}}{d\bar{z}}+\dfrac{3}{\bar{z}}n_{N_{1}}\biggr)+\dfrac{n_{N_{1}}}{a\bar{z}H}({v^{i}}_{,i}+3C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq-(1+A)\dfrac{\Gamma_{D}(\bar{z}=\infty)}{\bar{z}H}\biggl\langle\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\biggr\rangle(z_{T})\bigl[n_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-n_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr]

\displaystyle=-(1+A)\bar{z}K\biggl\langle\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\biggr\rangle(z_{T})\bigl[n_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-n_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr],

(21)

where
K\equiv\Gamma_{D}(\bar{z}=\infty)/\bar{z}^{2}H
is the so-called decay parameter.
The evolution of the number density ratio
N_{N_{1}}=n_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})/n_{\gamma}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})
becomes

\dfrac{dN_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})}{d\bar{z}}=-(1+A)D(z_{T})\bigl[N_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr],

(22)

where

D(z_{T})\equiv K\bar{z}\biggl\langle\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\biggr\rangle(z_{T})=K\bar{z}\dfrac{K_{1}(z_{T})}{K_{2}(z_{T})},

(23)

and

N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})=\dfrac{3}{8}z_{T}^{2}K_{2}(z_{T}).

(24)

For
N_{B-L}
, the Liouville term is evaluated in the same way as before.
The collision term contains the sourcing of lepton asymmetry from the CP asymmetry
\varepsilon_{1}
through the difference between the non-equilibrium and equilibrium abundances of right-handed neutrinos.
As a result, the evolution of
N_{B-L}
is governed by

\displaystyle\dfrac{dN_{B-L}(\bar{z})}{d\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{1}(1+A)D(z_{T})\bigl[N_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-(1+A)W_{\rm ID}N_{B-L},

(25)

where

W_{\rm ID}(z_{T})=\dfrac{1}{4}K\bar{z}z_{T}^{2}K_{1}(z_{T}),

(26)

is the washout factor without scattering
[
37
]
.

To solve equations (
22
) and (
25
), we choose the conformal Newtonian gauge
A=\Psi
,
C=-\Phi
and
B=E=0
.
The anisotropic stress is assumed to be negligible, and in this case

Equations (

\dfrac{dN_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})}{d\bar{z}}=-(1+\Psi)D(z_{T})\bigl[N_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr],

(27)

\displaystyle\dfrac{dN_{B-L}(\bar{z})}{d\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{1}(1+\Psi)D(z_{T})\bigl[N_{N_{1}}(\bar{z})-N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}(z_{T})\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-(1+\Psi)W_{\rm ID}N_{B-L}.

(28)

The time evolution of
\Psi=\Phi
in a radiation dominated universe is given by

]

\Psi(\eta,\bm{k})=2\mathcal{R}_{i}\dfrac{\sin\varphi-\varphi\cos\varphi}{\varphi^{3}},

(29)

In a radiation-dominated epoch,

\varphi=\dfrac{k\eta}{\sqrt{3}}\equiv\dfrac{\bar{z}}{\bar{z}_{H}}.

Since

\displaystyle\Psi(\eta,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\Psi(\eta,\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\Psi(\eta,\bm{k})+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\Psi^{*}(\eta,\bm{k})\right]

(32)

For each wavenumber

, different values of the phase parameter

The temperature fluctuation

Substituting equation (

\displaystyle\delta_{r}

\displaystyle=8|\mathcal{R}_{i}|\cos\delta\,\dfrac{\sin\varphi-\varphi\cos\varphi-\varphi^{2}\sin\varphi+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{3}\cos\varphi}{\varphi^{3}},

\displaystyle\delta_{T}

\displaystyle=2|\mathcal{R}_{i}|\cos\delta\,\dfrac{\sin\varphi-\varphi\cos\varphi-\varphi^{2}\sin\varphi+\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{3}\cos\varphi}{\varphi^{3}}.

II.4
Neutrino mass parameters

In our setup, the amount of baryon asymmetry depends on the heavy neutrino mass
M_{1}
, the decay parameter
K
, and the the degree of CP asymmetry
\varepsilon_{1}
.

determines the temperature at which the abundance of

The latter two parameters

In this subsection, we introduce various neutrino mass parameters that are useful to interpret the results (see, e.g., Ref.

The masses of light neutrinos are denoted by

, and

m_{3}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}>m_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}
or
m_{3}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}<m_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}
, the dependence of
m_{3}
on
m_{1}
is fixed.
In this paper we analyze the normal hierarchy
m_{1}<m_{2}<m_{3}
only.
In this case, the relations
m_{3}^{2}-m_{2}^{2}=\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}
and
m_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}=\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}
lead to

\begin{array}[]{l}m_{3}^{2}=m_{1}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2},\\[5.0pt]
m_{2}^{2}=m_{1}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2},\\[5.0pt]
\overline{m}^{2}=3m_{1}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}+2\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}.\end{array}

(43)

These relations can be rewritten as

\begin{array}[]{l}m_{1}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{3}(\overline{m}^{2}-\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}-2\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}),\\[10.00002pt]
m_{2}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{3}(\overline{m}^{2}-\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}),\\[10.00002pt]
m_{3}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{3}(\overline{m}^{2}+2\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}+\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}).\end{array}

(44)

According to the neutrino oscillation experiments (see Ref.
[
47
]
and references therein), the mass differences are known to be

\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}=(2.453\pm 0.033)\times 10^{-3}\,{\rm eV^{2}},\\[5.0pt]
\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}=(7.53\pm 0.18)\times 10^{-5}\,{\rm eV^{2}}.\end{array}

(45)

Thus
\overline{m}
takes its minimum value

\overline{m}=\sqrt{\Delta m_{\rm atm}^{2}+2\Delta m_{\rm sol}^{2}}\simeq 0.051\,{\rm eV},

(46)

for
m_{1}=0
.
Furthermore, CMB observations together with baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) measurements constrain the sum of the mass eigenvalues of light neutrinos from above as
[
11
]

m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}<0.13\,{\rm eV}

(47)

at the
2\sigma
level.
Substituting equation (
44
) and using equation (
45
), we find

\overline{m}<0.078\,{\rm eV}.

(48)

This upper limit is more relaxed when CMB alone is used without BAO
[
10
]

m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}<0.26\,{\rm eV}

(49)

at the
2\sigma
level.
In this case, we obtain

\overline{m}<0.15\,{\rm eV}.

(50)

In the following we present the allowed regions in the
(\tilde{m}_{1},M_{1})
plane
for the lower limit on
\overline{m}
(
46
),
the two types of upper limits (
48
) and (
50
), and also
\overline{m}=\overline{m}_{\rm max}\equiv 0.19\,{\rm eV}
, the last of which corresponds to the value just before the allowed region disappears when scattering is taken into account.

III
Implications to leptogenesis

III.1
Intuitive picture

Before proceeding to the numerical results, we describe the qualitative behavior of the evolution of right-handed neutrinos in the presence of density perturbations.
When the right-handed neutrinos decouple from the thermal bath at temperature
T
, the equilibrium abundance of right-handed neutrinos
N_{1}
can be approximated by the Boltzmann distribution

N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}\sim\exp\biggl(-\dfrac{M_{1}}{T}\biggr).

(51)

Substituting equation (
14
) into (
51
),
N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}
behaves as

N_{N_{1}}^{\rm eq}\sim\exp\biggl(-\dfrac{M_{1}}{\bar{T}}\biggr)\exp\biggl(\dfrac{M_{1}}{\bar{T}}\delta_{T}\biggr).

(52)

Therefore, if the temperature fluctuation is sufficiently large

\delta_{T}\gtrsim\dfrac{\bar{T}}{M_{1}},

(53)

the linear expansion of
\exp(M_{1}\delta_{T}/\bar{T})
causes a significant error.
For the strong washout regime, the actual abundance
N_{N_{1}}
closely follows this equilibrium abundance, and hence the linear approximation also breaks down for
N_{N_{1}}
.
The condition (
53
) translates to
\delta_{T}\gtrsim 0.05
given that the freeze-out of
|N_{B-L}|
occurs at
\bar{z}=M_{1}/\bar{T}\sim 10-20
.

If we linearize with respect to
\delta_{T}
, the baryon number would never increase because the average over the phase
\delta
will cancel out the contributions from positive
\delta_{T}
and those from negative
\delta_{T}
.
Therefore, the increase in the baryon number (Figure
2
) represents a
beyond-linear
effect related to
\delta_{T}
, which we call “acoustically driven freeze-out.”

IV
Discussion and conclusions

In this paper, we studied the effect of primordial density fluctuations on the freeze-out of heavy particles.
Primordial density fluctuations within the Hubble horizon propagate as sound waves during the freeze-out of these particles.
Sound waves cause temperature oscillation at each spatial point, driving oscillation in the abundance of massive particles with respect to radiation.
Using leptogenesis as an illustrative framework, we have derived the governing equations that describe the evolution of these heavy particles in the presence of fluctuations, and found that the exponential dependence of the abundance of heavy particles on the local temperature causes a beyond-linear effect in the system,
see equations (
51
) and (
52
).

As a proof of concept, we applied the formulation to the scenario of vanilla leptogenesis and demonstrated how temperature fluctuations affect the abundance of the right-handed neutrino and the resulting
B-L
asymmetry.
We indeed observed oscillations in the equilibrium abundance of the right-handed neutrino
N_{1}
and in the baryon-minus-lepton number
N_{B-L}
(Figure
2
).
Interestingly, the density fluctuations always increase the final baryon-to-photon ratio compared to the case without fluctuations, even after taking spatial average (Figure
3
).
This suggests that the effect of oscillations cannot be captured within linear perturbation.
Qualitatively, the equilibrium value of the right-handed neutrino is governed by the Boltzmann factor
\exp(-E/T)\simeq\exp(-E/\bar{T})\exp(E\delta T/\bar{T}^{2})
, and the fast temperature oscillation makes it harder for the non-equilibrium abundance of
N_{1}
to follow this equilibrium value.
If the temperature oscillation occurs with the right phase, the freeze-out of
N_{1}
is dominantly driven by the oscillation, not by the slow decrease in the average temperature, and the resulting
|N_{B-L}|
is enhanced (top panel of Figure
3
, around
\bar{z}\simeq 15
).
We call this effect “acoustically driven freeze-out”.
Of course, each spatial point has its own oscillation phase, and thus the enhancement in
|N_{B-L}|
does not occur uniformly.
However, the exponential enhancement of
|N_{B-L}|
in some regions cannot be fully canceled out by the contributions from other regions.
As a result, we get a net enhancement of the baryon asymmetry even after spatial average.

From the consideration above, the allowed parameter space for leptogenesis gets enlarged in the presence of fluctuations.
We presented the allowed regions
\eta_{b}\geq(\eta^{\rm CMB}_{b})_{\rm low}
in the
(\tilde{m}_{1},M_{1})
plane for a given absolute neutrino mass scale
\overline{m}=\sqrt{m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2}}
(Figure
4
).
The effective neutrino mass
\tilde{m}_{1}
is linearly proportional to the decay parameter
K
and thus characterizes the weak and strong washout regimes (Figure
4
,
\tilde{m}\lesssim 10^{-3}\,{\rm eV}
and
\tilde{m}\gtrsim 10^{-3}\,{\rm eV}
, respectively).
When temperature fluctuations are included, we indeed found an enlarged allowed regions.
The difference arises mainly in the strong washout regime, because in the weak washout regime the actual
N_{1}
abundance is almost unaffected by its oscillating equilibrium value (bottom panel of Figure
3
).
While the quantitative difference is not dramatically large, given that the neutrino oscillation experiments point toward the strong washout regime
[
47
]
, our findings will be relevant when we find relatively large
\bar{m}
from cosmological observations
[
11
,
10
]
.

We conclude by mentioning several possible future directions.
For the microphysics side, we took only decay and inverse decay into account in this study.
However, it is well known that
\Delta L=2
scattering imposes an (
\overline{m}
-dependent) upper bound on
M_{1}
and thus closes the allowed region in the
(M_{1},\tilde{m}_{1})
plane
[
46
]
.
Such scattering processes will not bring qualitative changes in the behavior of acoustically driven freeze-out since they do not affect the oscillating equilibrium abundance itself, which is the main reason for the net enhancement in the asymmetry.
Nevertheless, identifying the allowed parameter space including all the known microphysical effect will be important in preparing for future precision measurements.
For the macrophysics side, in this paper we injected monochromatic sound waves for simplicity.
In reality, however, fluctuations are a superposition of waves with various wavenumbers characterized by the power spectrum of the primordial curvature perturbation.
Thus the effect of fluctuations on freeze-out will ultimately be described by the power spectrum, and it would be interesting to construct analytical formulations for it.
In addition, it has been pointed that the sound waves can evolve to shock waves in the long run
[
55
]
.
Shock waves by definition have discontinuities in thermodynamic parameters and hence are expected to enhance the sudden freeze-out of heavy particles.
Incorporating all these aspects will be important and interesting future steps, to which we will come back elsewhere.

Appendix B
Derivation of the Boltzmann equation

B.1
The Boltzmann equation

We begin with the Boltzmann equation
[
45
]
:

\dfrac{df}{d\lambda}=C[f]\quad\Rightarrow\quad P^{0}\dfrac{df}{d\eta}=C[f],

(56)

where
f
is the distribution function,
\lambda
is the affine parameter,
C[f]
is the collision term, and
P^{0}
is the zeroth component of the energy-momentum 4-vector
P^{\mu}
.
We integrate the former equation using the integral measure in momentum space defined as

\pi=g_{\rm deg}\dfrac{d^{3}p}{E},

(57)

where
g_{\rm deg}
represents the internal degrees of freedom of the species, and
E
and
p^{i}
are the energy and 3-momentum of the particle evaluated in the local inertial frame, respectively.

B.2
Equivalence of integral measures

The integral measure (
57
) evaluated in an arbitrary coordinate system is given by
[
45
]

\pi\equiv g_{\rm deg}\dfrac{\sqrt{-g}\,d^{3}P}{P_{0}}=g_{\rm deg}\dfrac{d^{3}P}{P^{0}\sqrt{-g}}.

(58)

To check this, note that the relation between the 4-momenta evaluated in the general coordinate system and in the local inertial system is

P^{\mu}={e^{\mu}}_{\nu^{\prime}}p^{\nu^{\prime}},

(59)

where
{e^{\mu}}_{\nu^{\prime}}
is the tetrad, and
p^{0}=-p_{0}=E
is the instantaneous energy of the particle with respect to the local observer.
The tetrad satisfies
{e^{\mu}}_{\alpha^{\prime}}{e^{\nu}}_{\beta^{\prime}}g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}
.
Using (
59
) and
E\;dE=p_{j}\;dp^{j}
, we may rewrite
dP
as

\displaystyle dP^{i}

\displaystyle={e^{i}}_{0}\dfrac{\partial p^{0}}{\partial p^{j}}\;dp^{j}+{e^{i}}_{k}\dfrac{\partial p^{k}}{\partial p^{j}}\;dp^{j}

\displaystyle={e^{i}}_{0}\dfrac{p_{j}}{E}\;dp^{j}+{e^{i}}_{j}\;dp^{j}=\left({e^{i}}_{0}\dfrac{p_{j}}{E}+{e^{i}}_{j}\right)dp^{j}.

(60)

Substituting this into
dP
in (
58
) and using

P_{0}=g_{0\mu}{e^{\mu}}_{\nu^{\prime}}p^{\nu^{\prime}}=\eta_{\nu^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}{(e^{-1})^{\sigma^{\prime}}}_{0}p^{\nu^{\prime}},

(61)

the integral measure indeed reduces to

\pi=g_{\rm deg}\dfrac{d^{3}p}{E}.

(62)

B.3
Equivalence of (
17
) and the integral of (
56
)

In this subsection we show the equivalence of equation (
17
) and the integral of (
56
) following Ref.
[
56
]
.
The momentum integral of the Boltzmann equation (
56
) is

\dfrac{g_{\rm deg}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{\sqrt{-g}\;d^{3}P}{P_{0}}\;\dfrac{df}{d\lambda}=\dfrac{g_{\rm deg}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\dfrac{d^{3}p}{E}\;C[f],

(63)

where we used the equivalence of the integral measures (
58
) and (
62
).
We aim to show that this equation is equivalent to equation (
17
).
For this purpose it is sufficient to consider the collisionless Boltzmann equation, since the collision terms are already identical.
Let
f
denote the distribution function, and consider

-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(\phi^{+})^{\dagger}\\
(\phi^{0})^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}

\displaystyle=\bar{\nu}_{\rm L}(\phi^{0})^{\dagger}-\bar{e}_{\rm L}(\phi^{+})^{\dagger},

(121)

which can be rewritten as

\bar{l}_{\rm L}\circ\phi^{\dagger}=\overline{P_{\rm L}l}\circ\phi^{\dagger}=(\bar{l}P_{\rm R})\circ\phi^{\dagger}=(\bar{l}\circ\phi^{\dagger})P_{\rm R}.

(122)

The conjugate of the above gives
\phi\circ l_{\rm L}
:

\displaystyle\phi\circ l_{\rm L}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{+}&\phi^{0}\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}\circ\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{\rm L}\\
e_{\rm L}\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{+}&\phi^{0}\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{\rm L}\\
e_{\rm L}\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{SU}(2)}

\displaystyle=\nu_{\rm L}\phi^{0}-e_{\rm L}\phi^{+},

(123)

which can be rewritten as

\phi\circ l_{\rm L}=P_{\rm L}(\phi\circ l).

(124)

Note that the sign of the operation
\circ
changes depending on whether the fields are
\mathrm{SU}(2)
fundamental or
\mathrm{SU}(2)
anti-fundamental.

The Majorana field
N
is the Majorana representation of the right-chiral Weyl field
\nu_{\rm R}
given by

N=\begin{pmatrix}(\nu_{\rm R})^{c}\\
\nu_{\rm R}\end{pmatrix}_{\rm Dirac},

(125)

where the charge conjugation operator
c
is defined as

\begin{array}[]{l}(\mbox{left-chiral})^{c}=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{2}(\mbox{left-chiral})^{\dagger},\\[7.5pt]
(\mbox{right-chiral})^{c}=-\mathrm{i}\sigma^{2}(\mbox{right-chiral})^{\dagger},\end{array}

(126)

thereby
(\nu_{\rm R})^{c}=-\mathrm{i}\sigma^{2}\nu_{\rm R}^{\dagger}
.
Thus the relevant Yukawa couplings become

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=-y_{\alpha\beta}^{(\nu)}[(\bar{l}_{\rm L})_{\alpha}\circ\phi^{\dagger}]N_{\beta}+{\rm h.c.}

\displaystyle=-y_{\alpha\beta}^{(\nu)}(\bar{l}_{\alpha}\circ\phi^{\dagger})P_{\rm R}N_{\beta}-y_{\alpha\beta}^{(\nu)\ast}\bar{N}_{\beta}P_{\rm L}(\phi\circ l_{\alpha}).

(127)

Henceforth, we denote
y_{\alpha\beta}^{(\nu)}
by
y_{\alpha\beta}
.

D.3
Calculation of tree diagrams

In this subsection we calculate the tree diagrams.
Hereafter we assume that the masses of the leptons and the Higgs boson are negligible compared to the mass of the heavy neutrino
M_{1}
.
We often use

p_{1}^{2}=p_{2}^{2}=0,\qquad p_{1}\cdot p_{2}=\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{2},

(128)

for the momenta of the lepton
p_{1}
and the Higgs boson
p_{2}
.
Here the second equation is derived from
(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=M_{1}^{2}
.

First we consider
N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi
.
Let
s
and
t
be the spins of the initial and final states of
N_{1}
and
l_{\alpha}
, respectively.
The Lagrangian in equation (
127
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle\supset-y_{\alpha\beta}[\bar{\nu}_{\alpha}(\phi^{0})^{\dagger}-\bar{e}_{\alpha}(\phi^{+})^{\dagger}]P_{\rm R}N_{\beta}.

(129)

Thus there are two possibilities for the final state:
\nu_{\alpha}\phi^{0}
and
e_{\alpha}\phi^{+}
, originating from the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
doublet.
Here the matrix element
\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{(s,t)}
is defined for each channel, and hence the result obtained below must be multiplied by a factor of two when calculating the total decay rate.
The total matrix element is the sum of the tree and the one-loop:

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}(s,t)}=\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}.

(130)

The squared magnitude of the matrix element at the tree level is obtained by taking the sum over spins
s
and
t
and then averaging over the spin
s
of the initial state

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}|\bar{u}_{l_{\alpha}}^{t}(p_{1})(-\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}P_{\rm R})u_{N_{1}}^{s}(p_{1}+p_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}\tr[(\not{p}_{1}+\not{p}_{2}+M_{1})P_{\rm L}\not{p}_{1}P_{\rm R}]

\displaystyle=|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})=\dfrac{1}{2}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}M_{1}^{2},

(131)

where we used the spin sum formula (
199
), the trace formulas (
186
), (
187
), (
190
), and (
179
), as well as the approximation in equation (
128
).

Next, for
N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}
, a similar calculation yields

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}|\bar{v}_{l_{\alpha}}^{t}(p_{1})(-\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}P_{\rm L})u_{N_{1}}^{s}(p_{1}+p_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}\tr[(\not{p}_{1}+\not{p}_{2}-M_{1})P_{\rm R}\not{p}_{1}P_{\rm L}]

\displaystyle=|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})=\dfrac{1}{2}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}M_{1}^{2}.

(132)

D.4
Calculation of vertex diagrams

In this subsection we calculate vertex diagrams and the resulting interference with the tree-level diagrams.
We first consider
N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi
.
The combined contribution of the tree and one-loop diagrams is

\displaystyle\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}}\Bigr|^{2}\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\simeq\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl[\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr)^{\ast}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr)+{\rm c.c.}\Bigr].

(133)

The interference part is

\displaystyle I_{\rm vertex}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr)^{\ast}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;\dfrac{-\frac{1}{2}M_{1}^{3}M_{\gamma}-M_{1}M_{\gamma}(p_{1}\cdot q)}{(q^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2})(-p_{2}-q)^{2}(-p_{1}+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}D_{\rm vertex},

(134)

where we used equation (
182
), the spin sum formula (
199
), and the trace formulas (
186
), (
187
), (
189
).
Furthermore, performing the
q
-integration using Feynman parametrization (
202
) and the dimensional regularization formulas (
203
) and (
204
), we get

\displaystyle D_{\rm vertex}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{4}q}{\mathrm{i}(2\pi)^{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\dfrac{M_{1}^{3}M_{\gamma}+2M_{1}M_{\gamma}(p_{1}\cdot q)}{(q^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)(-p_{2}-q+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)^{2}(-p_{1}+q+\mathrm{i}\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{1-x}dy\;\dfrac{M_{1}M_{\gamma}(1-y)}{M_{\gamma}^{2}(1-x-y)-M_{1}^{2}xy-\mathrm{i}\epsilon},

(135)

where
-\mathrm{i}\epsilon
in the denominator comes from the
\mathrm{i}\epsilon
prescription.

We define
m\equiv M_{1}/M_{\gamma}
and perform the integration over
y
in equation (
135
).
Considering the complex
y
-plane, there exists a pole at
y=(1-x)/(1+m^{2}x)\equiv a
within the range
0\leqslant y\leqslant 1-x
on the real axis, which we avoid by taking an upper semicircle.
We divide the integration range of
y
into three intervals
[0,a-\epsilon^{\prime}]
,
[a-\epsilon^{\prime},a+\epsilon^{\prime}]
, and
[a+\epsilon^{\prime},1]
.

Figure 11:
Contour of
D_{\rm vertex}
.

In the middle interval
[a-\epsilon^{\prime},a+\epsilon^{\prime}]
, we perform the variable transformation
y=a+\epsilon^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
(where
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
is a small quantity and
0\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi
).
The imaginary part of
D_{\rm vertex}
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\Im(D_{\rm vertex})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{a-\epsilon^{\prime}}^{a+\epsilon^{\prime}}dy\;\dfrac{m(1-y)}{1-x-y-m^{2}xy-\mathrm{i}\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\;\dfrac{m}{1+m^{2}x}\int_{a-\epsilon^{\prime}}^{a+\epsilon^{\prime}}dy\;\dfrac{y-1}{y-\frac{1-x}{1+m^{2}x}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\;\dfrac{m}{1+m^{2}x}\int_{\pi}^{0}\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\;d\theta\;\dfrac{a-1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\prime})}{\epsilon^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\epsilon^{\prime}\downarrow 0]{}-\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\;(-\mathrm{i}\pi)\dfrac{m}{1+m^{2}x}\biggl(\dfrac{1-x}{1+m^{2}x}-1\biggr),

(136)

where the
+\mathrm{i}\epsilon
in the denominator specifies that the integration contour lies in the upper half-plane.

Upon performing the
x
-integration, we find

\Im(D_{\rm vertex})=\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl[1-\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{m^{2}}\biggr)\ln(1+m^{2})\biggr]\biggr\}.

(137)

Thus equation (
134
) becomes

\displaystyle I_{\rm vertex}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}\Im(D_{\rm vertex}).

(138)

We next consider
N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}
.
Similar calculation as above gives

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{\rm vertex}

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr)^{\ast}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast}\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi}\biggl\{\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl[1-\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{m^{2}}\biggr)\ln(1+m^{2})\biggr]\biggr\}.

(139)

Regarding the external lines, while the anti-lepton part changed from
u_{l_{\alpha}}^{t}
to
v_{\bar{l}_{\alpha}}^{t}
, the
N_{1}
part remained as
u_{N_{1}}^{s}
.
This is due to the Majorana nature of
N_{1}
.

Combining above results, the CP asymmetry
(\varepsilon_{1})_{\rm vertex}
is given by

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{1})_{\rm vertex}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{\alpha}\Bigl(\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}}\Bigr|^{2}-\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)}{\sum_{\alpha}\Bigl(\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}+\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}F(m),\qquad m\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{M_{\gamma}},

\displaystyle F(m)

\displaystyle\equiv\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl[1-\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{m^{2}}\biggr)\ln(1+m^{2})\biggr].

(140)

As seen from this expression, in order for
\varepsilon_{1}
to be non-zero,
D_{\rm vertex}
must have an imaginary part.

D.5
Calculation of wavefunction diagrams

Regarding the wavefunction diagrams, there appears two types of contributions: wavefunction I and wavefunction II.
However, only the former contributes to the CP asymmetry (this will be verified later).
Additionally, be mindful of the factor two which appears below.
This arises when two different particle species belonging to the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
group appear in the loop.
For example, when fix the decay channel to
N_{\alpha}\to\nu_{\alpha}\phi^{0}
,
the particles appearing in the triangular loop in the vertex diagrams are fixed to
\phi^{0}
and
\nu_{\beta}
, since the vertex is connected to the final state.
On the other hand, in the wavefunction diagrams, there is no constraint on the particles entering the loop from the final state, and hence both
\nu_{\alpha}\phi^{0}
and
e_{\alpha}\phi^{+}
contribute.

The interference term of the wavefunction diagram I is

\displaystyle I_{\rm wave}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr)^{\ast}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}\dfrac{2M_{1}M_{\gamma}}{M_{1}^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}}\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;\dfrac{p_{1}\cdot q}{q^{2}(p_{1}+p_{2}+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}D_{\rm wave}^{(1)},

(141)

where we used (
182
), the spin sum formula (
199
), and the trace formulas (
186
), (
187
) and (
190
).
Furthermore, applying Feynman parametrization (
201
) and the dimensional regularization formula (
204
), we perform the
q
-integration and extract the imaginary part, obtaining

\displaystyle D_{\rm wave}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{2M_{1}M_{\gamma}}{M_{1}^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}}\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{4}q}{\mathrm{i}(2\pi)^{4}}\;\dfrac{p_{1}\cdot q}{q^{2}(q+p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\dfrac{M_{1}^{3}M_{\gamma}}{M_{1}^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\;x\log\bigl[-M_{1}^{2}x(1-x)-\mathrm{i}\epsilon\bigr],

(142)

i.e.,

\Im(D_{\rm wave}^{(1)})=\dfrac{1}{32\pi}\dfrac{M_{1}^{3}M_{\gamma}}{M_{1}^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}}.

(143)

Here note that
\log\bigl[-M_{1}^{2}x(1-x)-\mathrm{i}\epsilon\bigr]
is specified with the integration contour lying in the lower half-plane, thus yielding
-\mathrm{i}\pi
instead of
+\mathrm{i}\pi
.
From this and equation (
142
), we obtain

I_{\rm wave}^{(1)}=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi}\dfrac{m}{m^{2}-1},\quad m\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{M_{\gamma}}.

(144)

The interference term of the wavefunction diagram II is

\displaystyle I_{\rm wave}^{(2)}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{s,t}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}(s,t)}\Bigr)^{\ast}\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{M}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1-loop)}(s,t)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast}\dfrac{2M_{1}^{2}}{M_{1}^{2}-M_{\gamma}^{2}}\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\;\dfrac{p_{2}\cdot q}{q^{2}(p_{1}+p_{2}+q)^{2}},

(145)

where we have taken into account equation (
182
), the spin sum formula (
199
) and the trace formulas (
188
) and (
191
).
Applying Feynman parametrization (
201
) and the dimensional regularization formula (
204
), we perform the
q
-integration in a similar way to those in equation (
144
), obtaining

I_{\rm wave}^{(2)}=\mathrm{i}y_{\alpha 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast}\dfrac{M_{1}^{2}}{32\pi}\dfrac{m^{2}}{m^{2}-1},\quad m\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{M_{\gamma}}.

(146)

By summing equations (
144
) and (
146
),
similarly to equation (
140
), we find

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{1})_{\rm wave}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{\alpha}\Bigl(\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}}\Bigr|^{2}-\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree+1-loop)}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)}{\sum_{\alpha}\Bigl(\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}\phi}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}+\Bigl|\mathrm{i}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}\phi^{\ast}}^{\mbox{\scriptsize(tree)}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{m}{m^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{m^{2}}{m^{2}-1}.

(147)

Here note that

\displaystyle\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma})

\displaystyle=\Im\Biggl[\sum_{\alpha}(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast})\sum_{\beta}(y_{\beta 1}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma})\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\Im\Biggl(\biggl|\sum_{\alpha}y_{\alpha 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}\biggr|^{2}\Biggr)=0,

(148)

from which follows that the contribution from wavefunction diagram II is zero.
Thus we find

(\varepsilon_{1})_{\rm wave}=\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{m}{m^{2}-1},\qquad m\equiv\dfrac{M_{1}}{M_{\gamma}}.

(149)

D.6
Final result and approximate expression

By combining equations (
140
) and (
149
), the expression for the CP asymmetry is obtained as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl[1+\dfrac{m^{2}}{m^{2}-1}-\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{m^{2}}\biggr)\ln(1+m^{2})\biggr].

(150)

At this point we switch to
x\equiv M_{\gamma}^{2}/M_{1}^{2}
in place of
m\equiv M_{1}/M_{\gamma}
to match the literature

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})}{8\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}f(x),\qquad x\equiv\dfrac{M_{\gamma}^{2}}{M_{1}^{2}},

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{x}\,\biggl[1+\dfrac{1}{1-x}-(1+x)\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{1}{x}\biggr)\biggr].

(151)

Note that
\Im(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\beta 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast})=0
and hence
\varepsilon_{1}=0
for
\gamma=1
.
Thus the contributions from the second and third generations are necessary to generate nonzero
\varepsilon_{1}
.

When the magnitude of the CP asymmetry
\varepsilon_{1}
is much smaller than one,
x=M_{\gamma}^{2}/M_{1}^{2}
is expected to be much larger than unity.
In this case, we perform a Taylor expansion of
f(x)
for
1/x\ll 1
:

f(x)=-\biggl[\dfrac{3}{2\sqrt{x}}+\dfrac{5}{6x^{3/2}}+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{x^{5/2}}\biggr)\biggr],

(152)

from which we define
\tilde{f}
taking the leading term

\tilde{f}(x)\equiv-\dfrac{3}{2\sqrt{x}}.

(153)

The plots for
f(x)
and
\tilde{f}(x)
are shown in Figure
12
.
When approximating
f(x)
with
\tilde{f}(x)
, the CP asymmetry (
151
) is approximated as

\varepsilon_{1}\simeq-\dfrac{3M_{1}}{16\pi\sum_{\alpha}|y_{\alpha 1}|^{2}}\Im\biggl(y_{\alpha 1}y_{\alpha\gamma}^{\ast}\dfrac{1}{M_{\gamma}}y_{\beta 1}y_{\beta\gamma}^{\ast}\biggr).

Appendix E
Derivation of the maximal CP asymmetry
|\varepsilon_{1}|^{\rm max}

From the Lagrangian (
2
), the neutrino masses are given by

[
59
]

\mathcal{L}_{m}=-\dfrac{M_{\alpha\beta}}{2}\bar{N}_{\alpha}^{c}N_{\beta}-y_{\alpha\beta}\phi^{\ast}\bar{l}_{\alpha}\bar{N}_{\beta}+{\rm h.c.},

(155)

where
M
is the heavy neutrino mass matrix.
Here
(m_{\rm D})_{\alpha\beta}=y_{\alpha\beta}v
from the Higgs mechanism, where
m_{\rm D}
is the Dirac mass matrix,
y_{\alpha\beta}
is the Yukawa coupling, and
v=\langle\phi\rangle
is the VEV of the Higgs field
\phi
.
We adopt the mass eigenstate basis for the heavy neutrinos, in which
M
is diagonal with real positive eigenvalues
M_{1}\leqslant M_{2}\leqslant M_{3}
.

Through the seesaw mechanism, the light neutrino mass matrix
m_{\nu}
can be expressed in terms of
m_{\rm D}
and the inverse of
M
,

m_{\nu}=-m_{\rm D}\dfrac{1}{M}m_{\rm D}^{\intercal},

(156)

where higher order terms in
1/M
are neglected.

m_{\nu}
can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix
U^{(\nu)}

U^{(\nu)\dagger}m_{\nu}U^{(\nu)\ast}=-\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&0&0\\
0&m_{2}&0\\
0&0&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}\equiv-D_{m},

(157)

with real positive eigenvalues
m_{1}\leqslant m_{2}\leqslant m_{3}
.
Substituting equation (
156
) into equation (
157
), we get

\displaystyle U^{(\nu)\dagger}\bigl(-m_{\rm D}M^{-1}m_{\rm D}^{\intercal}\bigr)U^{(\nu)\ast}

\displaystyle=-U^{(\nu)\dagger}(yv)D_{M}^{-1}(yv)^{\intercal}U^{(\nu)\ast}

\displaystyle=-v^{2}U^{(\nu)\dagger}yD_{M}^{-1}y^{\intercal}U^{(\nu)\ast}=-D_{m},

(158)

where
D_{M}
is the diagonalized heavy mass matrix
M
.
Equation (
158
) means that

\Omega\equiv vD_{m}^{-1/2}U^{(\nu)\dagger}yD_{M}^{-1/2}

(159)

is an orthogonal matrix
\Omega\Omega^{\intercal}=\bm{1}
.
This implies that
\Im(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)_{11}=0
, which yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Im(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)_{11}

\displaystyle=\Im\bigl(vD_{M}^{-1/2}y^{\intercal}U^{(\nu)\ast}D_{m}^{-1/2}\cdot vD_{m}^{-1/2}U^{(\nu)\dagger}yD_{M}^{-1/2}\bigr)_{11}

\displaystyle=v^{2}\dfrac{1}{M_{1}}\Im\bigl(D_{m}^{-1}y^{\intercal}U^{(\nu)\ast}\cdot U^{(\nu)\dagger}y\bigr)_{11}

\displaystyle=v^{2}\dfrac{1}{M_{1}}\sum_{\alpha=1,2,3}\dfrac{1}{m_{\alpha}}\Im\bigl(U^{(\nu)\dagger}y\bigr)_{\alpha 1}^{2},

(160)

that is,

\dfrac{1}{m_{1}}\Im\bigl(U^{(\nu)\dagger}y\bigr)_{11}^{2}=-\sum_{\alpha\neq 1}\dfrac{1}{m_{\alpha}}\Im\bigl(U^{(\nu)\dagger}y\bigr)_{\alpha 1}^{2}.

(161)

On the other hand, the CP asymmetry
\varepsilon_{1}
is obtained from equation (
154
)

\varepsilon_{1}\simeq-\dfrac{3}{16\pi}\dfrac{M_{1}}{(y^{\dagger}y)_{11}}\Im\biggl(y^{\dagger}y\dfrac{1}{M}y^{\intercal}y^{\ast}\biggr)_{11},

(162)

where
M
is the heavy neutrino mass matrix.
Substituting equation (
157
) and equation (
161
) into (
162
), and expressing it in terms of

\tilde{y}=U^{(\nu)\dagger}y

(163)

instead of
y
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Im\biggl(\tilde{y}^{\dagger}\tilde{y}\dfrac{1}{M}\tilde{y}^{\intercal}\tilde{y}^{\ast}\biggr)_{11}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{v^{2}}\Im\biggl(\tilde{y}^{\dagger}U^{(\nu)\dagger}m_{\nu}U^{(\nu)\ast}\tilde{y}^{\ast}\biggr)_{11}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{m_{1}}{v^{2}}\Im(\tilde{y}_{11}^{2})-\sum_{\alpha\neq 1}\dfrac{m_{\alpha}}{v^{2}}\Im(\tilde{y}_{\alpha 1}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{v^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\neq 1}\dfrac{\Delta m_{\alpha 1}^{2}}{m_{\alpha}}\Im(\tilde{y}_{\alpha 1}^{2}),

(164)

that translates into

\varepsilon_{1}=\dfrac{3}{16\pi}\dfrac{M_{1}}{v^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\neq 1}\dfrac{\Delta m_{\alpha 1}^{2}}{m_{\alpha}}\dfrac{\Im(\tilde{y}_{\alpha 1}^{2})}{(\tilde{y}^{\dagger}\tilde{y})_{11}},

(165)

where
\Delta m_{\alpha 1}^{2}\equiv m_{\alpha}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}
.

Now, consider the normalized Yukawa couplings
[
46
]

z_{\alpha}=\dfrac{\tilde{y}_{\alpha 1}^{2}}{(\tilde{y}^{\dagger}\tilde{y})_{11}}=X_{\alpha}+\mathrm{i}Y_{\alpha},

(166)

which follow

0\leqslant|z_{\alpha}|\leqslant 1,\qquad\sum_{\alpha}|z_{\alpha}|=1.

(167)

From the orthogonality condition
(\Omega^{\intercal}\Omega)_{11}=1
, the additional constraint is given by

\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\tilde{m}_{1}}{m_{\alpha}}z_{\alpha}=1,

(168)

where
\tilde{m}_{1}
is the effective neutrino mass (
37
).
From equation (
165
), the CP asymmetry reads in the new variables

\varepsilon_{1}=\dfrac{3}{16\pi}\dfrac{M_{1}}{v^{2}}\biggl(\dfrac{\Delta m_{21}^{2}}{m_{2}}Y_{2}+\dfrac{\Delta m_{31}^{2}}{m_{3}}Y_{3}\biggr).

(169)

Since
m_{3}>m_{2}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Delta m_{31}^{2}}{m_{3}}-\dfrac{\Delta m_{21}^{2}}{m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m_{3}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}}{m_{3}}-\dfrac{m_{2}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}}{m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(m_{3}-m_{2})(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}m_{3})}{m_{2}m_{3}}>0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\dfrac{\Delta m_{31}^{2}}{m_{3}}

\displaystyle>\dfrac{\Delta m_{21}^{2}}{m_{2}}.

(170)

From this, we can presume that the maximal CP asymmetry
is achieved when
|Y_{3}|
is maximal.

The additional condition (
168
) yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{Y_{1}}{m_{1}}+\dfrac{Y_{2}}{m_{2}}+\dfrac{Y_{3}}{m_{3}}=0,

(171)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\tilde{m}_{1}}{m_{1}}X_{1}+\dfrac{\tilde{m}_{1}}{m_{2}}X_{2}+\dfrac{\tilde{m}_{1}}{m_{3}}X_{3}=1.

(172)

Since
m_{3}>m_{2}>m_{1}
, we can set
X_{2}=X_{3}=Y_{2}=0
for maximal
|Y_{3}|
.
Then, it follows that

Y_{1}=-\dfrac{m_{1}}{m_{3}}Y_{3},\qquad X_{1}=\dfrac{m_{1}}{\tilde{m}_{1}}.

(173)

The second equation in (
167
) gives

\sqrt{X_{1}^{2}+Y_{1}^{2}}+|Y_{3}|=1.

(174)

The conditions (
173
) and (
174
) determine

as a function of
m_{1}
,
m_{3}
, and
\tilde{m}_{1}

\displaystyle\biggl(1-\dfrac{m_{1}^{2}}{m_{3}^{2}}\biggr)|Y_{3}|^{2}-2|Y_{3}|+1-\dfrac{m_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{m}_{1}^{2}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad|Y_{3}|=1-\dfrac{m_{1}}{m_{3}}\sqrt{1+\dfrac{m_{3}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{m}_{1}^{2}}},

(175)

where
|Y_{3}|<1
is taken into account.
Substituting
Y_{2}=0
and equation (
175

169

|\varepsilon_{1}|^{\rm max}=\dfrac{3}{16\pi}\dfrac{M_{1}m_{3}}{v^{2}}\Biggl[1-\dfrac{m_{1}}{m_{3}}\biggl(1+\dfrac{m_{3}^{2}-m_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{m}_{1}^{2}}\biggr)^{1/2}\Biggr],

(176)

where

This gives the upper bound for
|\varepsilon_{1}|
.

F.1
Formulas for
\gamma

F.1.1
Definition and properties of
\gamma
matrices

The
\gamma
matrices consist of four
n\times n
matrices
\gamma^{\mu}
(where
\mu=0,1,2,3
) and satisfy the following anticommutation relations:

\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}\bm{1}_{n\times n}.

(177)

Equation (
177
) is called the Clifford algebra.

F.1.2
Definition and properties of chirality
\gamma_{5}

We define
chirality

\gamma_{5}
as

\gamma_{5}\equiv\mathrm{i}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}.

(178)

\gamma_{5}
satisfies

(\gamma_{5})^{2}=\bm{1},\qquad(\gamma_{5})^{\dagger}=\gamma_{5},\qquad\gamma_{5}\gamma^{\mu}=-\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5},

(179)

for
\mu=0,1,2,3
.

The eigenspaces of
\gamma_{5}
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
are referred to as left-chiral and right-chiral, respectively.
Let
P_{\rm L}
and
P_{\rm R}
denote the projection operators onto the left-chiral and right-chiral eigenspaces, respectively.
These satisfy

P_{\rm R}=\dfrac{\bm{1}+\gamma_{5}}{2},\qquad P_{\rm L}=\dfrac{\bm{1}-\gamma_{5}}{2},

(180)

and decompose the Dirac spinor
\psi
into

\psi_{\rm R}=P_{\rm R}\psi,\qquad\psi_{\rm L}=P_{\rm L}\psi.

(181)

These projection operators satisfy

P_{\rm R}^{2}=P_{\rm R},\quad P_{\rm L}^{2}=P_{\rm L},\quad P_{\rm R}P_{\rm L}=P_{\rm L}P_{\rm R}=0.

(182)

F.1.3
Feynman slash notation

The contraction between the 4-vector
a^{\mu}
and the
\gamma
matrix
\gamma^{\mu}
is denoted by

\not{a}\equiv\gamma^{\mu}a_{\mu}=\gamma_{\mu}a^{\mu},

(183)

from which it follows that

\not{a}^{2}=a^{2}.

(184)

Traces of gamma matrices are given by

\displaystyle=4,

(185)

\displaystyle\tr(\not{a})

\displaystyle=\tr(\not{a}\not{b}\not{c})=\cdots=0,

\displaystyle\tr(\not{a}\not{b})

(187)

\displaystyle\tr(\not{a}\not{b}\not{c}\not{d})

\displaystyle=4[(a\cdot b)(c\cdot d)

\displaystyle\qquad-(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)+(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c)],

(188)

F.2
Properties of Dirac fermions

The positive frequency solution
u^{s}(p)
of the Dirac equation satisfies

(\not{p}-m)u^{s}(p)=0,

(192)

while the negative frequency solution
v^{s}(p)
satisfies

(-\not{p}-m)v^{s}(p)=0,

(193)

where
p
is the four-momentum, and
s
denotes the spin eigenvalue.
From the normalization condition of the solution

\bar{u}^{s}(p)u^{s^{\prime}}(p)=2m\delta_{ss^{\prime}},

(194)

\displaystyle\bar{v}^{s}(p)v^{s^{\prime}}(p)

\displaystyle=-2m\delta_{ss^{\prime}},

(195)

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{s}(p)v^{s^{\prime}}(p)

\displaystyle=\bar{v}^{s}(p)u^{s^{\prime}}(p)=0,

(196)

\displaystyle u^{s\dagger}(\bm{p})v^{s^{\prime}}(-\bm{p})

\displaystyle=v^{s\dagger}(\bm{p})u^{s^{\prime}}(-\bm{p})=0,

(197)

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{s}(p)\gamma^{\mu}u^{s^{\prime}}(p)

\displaystyle=\bar{v}^{s}(p)\gamma^{\mu}v^{s^{\prime}}(p)=2p^{\mu}\delta_{ss^{\prime}}.

(198)

Also,
u^{s=\pm}(p)
and
v^{s=\pm}(p)
form complete two-dimensional solution spaces satisfying
\not{p}=m
and
-\not{p}=m

The projection operators onto these solution spaces are given by
(\pm\not{p}+m)/2m
, which yield

\displaystyle\sum_{s}u^{s}(p)\bar{u}^{s}(p)

\displaystyle=\not{p}+m,

(199)

\displaystyle\sum_{s}v^{s}(p)\bar{v}^{s}(p)

\displaystyle=\not{p}-m.

(200)

The proportionality coefficients are determined by taking into account the normalization conditions given in equations (
194

F.3.1
Feynman parametrization

Feynman parametrization is given by

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{ab}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dx\;\dfrac{1}{[ax+b(1-x)]^{2}},

(201)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{abc}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{1}dy\int_{0}^{1}dz\;\dfrac{2\,\delta(x+y+z-1)}{(ax+by+cz)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{1-x}dy\;\dfrac{2}{[ax+by+c(1-x-y)]^{3}}.

(202)

F.3.2
Momentum integrals with dimensional regularization

Momentum integrals with dimensional regularization are given by

\displaystyle\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{n}k}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\dfrac{1}{(m^{2}+2k\cdot p-k^{2})^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}\varGamma\bigl(\alpha-\frac{n}{2}\bigr)}{(4\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}\varGamma(\alpha)}\dfrac{1}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{n}{2}}},

(203)

\displaystyle\int\!\!\dfrac{d^{n}k}{(2\pi)^{n}}\;\dfrac{k^{\mu}}{(m^{2}+2k\cdot p-k^{2})^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.00012pt=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}\varGamma\bigl(\alpha-\frac{n}{2}\bigr)}{(4\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}\varGamma(\alpha)}\dfrac{p^{\mu}}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\alpha-\frac{n}{2}}}.

(204)

Setting
\varepsilon\equiv(4-n)/2\ll 1
, the gamma function gives

\varGamma(\varepsilon)=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}-\gamma+\dfrac{1}{2}\biggl(\gamma^{2}+\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{6}\biggr)\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(205)

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
