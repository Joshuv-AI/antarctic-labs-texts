Dissecting the essential role of luxS-relevant small RNAs in biofilm formation of Streptococcus mutans
Authors: Meng-Ying Mao, Yan-Jing Liang, Ju-Xiu Chen, Yu-Jie Zhang, Wei-Ping Li, Zheng-Wei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus mutans is considered the key contributor to human dental caries. The LuxS/AI-2 quorum‑sensing (QS) system in S. mutans plays a crucial role in the development of cariogenic oral biofilms. Many QS systems rely on regulation carried out by small RNAs (sRNAs) to achieve optimal performance. However, the identities and functions of sRNAs in S. mutans QS system are not well elucidated. Here, we identify two novel sRNAs named SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 (Streptococcus mutans quorum-sensing-related small RNA 2 and 4), which respond to luxS expression and support biofilm formation by mediating exopolysaccharides (EPS) synthesis. We showed that overexpression of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in S. mutans, respectively, resulted in declined growth rates, altered biofilm architecture, increased EPS production, and upregulated expression of gtfB/C/D genes. Transcriptome analysis revealed that several genes related to carbohydrate utilization were differentially expressed, with ccpA being markedly upregulated in both SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 overexpression strains. Specifically, SmqsRs promoted ccpA transcript, and CcpA triggered transcription of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 via direct binding, thus forming a positive feedback loop. We speculate that SmqsRs augment LuxS-mediated biofilm matrix production, most likely through the activation of gtfB/C/D expression and the reprogramming of S. mutans central carbon metabolism by ccpA. In summary, we have confirmed that SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 function as supporting factors that maintain the optimal status of the LuxS/AI-2 system in S. mutans, which is important for cell growth, EPS synthesis, and biofilm formation. Hence, sRNA activity may represent a promising target to modulate S. mutans cariogenicity.

Introduction

Streptococcus mutans is a critical pathobiont involved in the development of dental caries, a major public health concern, due to its ability to be instrumental in the biofilm dysbiotic process [1–3]. The success of S. mutans in oral biofilms is enhanced not only by proficient carbohydrate utilization but also by its ability to quickly respond to fluctuations in the changing environment [4]. The LuxS/AI-2 circuit is the typical quorum‑sensing (QS) system in S. mutans, which affects the information exchange among biofilm bacteria, allowing them to adapt and survive in the microenvironment. It has been reported that the LuxS/AI-2 system in S. mutans plays an important role in biofilm formation, which is closely related to its cariogenicity [5,6]. Previously, we applied RNA-seq to obtain gene expression profiles of S. mutans with LuxS/AI-2 system disruption, and found a high abundance of discrete small RNAs (sRNAs) that were differentially expressed between the mutant and wild-type strains [7]. sRNAs are critical regulatory elements that have been the focus of investigation in pathogens, for their roles in mediating the prompt and energetically virulent expression of virulence attributes [8–10]. Previous observations have indicated that sRNAs take potential roles in pathogenesis regulation of oral pathogens, including S. mutans, Enterococcus faecalis, and periodontal pathogens [11]. Additionally, in many bacterial pathogens, sRNAs have been identified as auxiliary regulators that directly or indirectly affect components of the QS machinery [12]. Studies on the sRNAs of LuxS/AI-2 system in oral pathogens, however, are in their infancy.

Thus far, studies of sRNAs in bacteria have demonstrated that they are important gene regulators and involved in various biological processes [13]. Due to their low energetic cost and fast action, sRNAs are considered ideal response regulators when a bacterium is experiencing environmental stress [14,15]. It has been identified that sRNAs may be crucial for the proper integration of the numerous environmental signals that combine to govern biofilm formation in the well-studied Escherichia coli [16]. Recent evidence suggests that regulatory networks governing the decision of bacteria whether to form biofilms or remain as planktonic cells are further subject to regulation by sRNAs [17]. The cariogenic bacterium, S. mutans, has an obligate biofilm lifestyle that enables it to persist in the oral cavity. The highly structured exopolysaccharide (EPS) produced by glycosyltransferase enzymes (Gtfs) of S. mutans promotes the buildup of biofilms by way of cellular attachment to dental surfaces and other oral microorganisms [18,19]. A number of investigations have indicated that several regulatory nucleotides are taking parts in S. mutans biofilm formation, suggesting a coordinated process dependent on internal and external signals [20,21]. A recent study has identified 15 novel sRNAs in S. mutans and provided evidence that a specific sRNA is involved in regulating carbohydrate transport and metabolism, which implicates that sRNAs may regulate biofilm formation [22]. Interestingly, apart from sRNAs, microRNA-size small RNAs (msRNAs) and antisense RNAs (ASs) have both been found to play roles in exopolysaccharide (EPS) synthesis and biofilm organization in S. mutans [23,24]. Surprisingly little is known about the utility of sRNA in S. mutans biofilm formation. We propose that sRNAs may coordinate a response to the LuxS/AI-2 machinery via the nuanced control of biofilm formation.

In the present study, we identified a subset of sRNAs that were differentially expressed in the wild-type S. mutans UA159 strain compared to its luxS-deficient mutant, and two of which, named SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 (Streptococcus mutans quorum-sensing related small RNA 2 and 4), were tested in response to luxS expression. We focused our attention on these two sRNAs by investigating their potential involvement in the LuxS/AI-2 machinery and exploring how they contribute to the virulence potential of S. mutans. Further analysis demonstrated that overexpression of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4, respectively, could increase EPS production and subsequently alter biofilm formation. We also demonstrated that SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 were involved in carbohydrate utilization and were associated with the cariogenic ability of S. mutans. Taken collectively, our results add new insights to the complexity of regulating the expression of genes involved in the cariogenicity of S. mutans and provide a potential target for controlling dental caries.

Results

Identification and validation of novel sRNAs

We used a transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq)-based approach to analyze the differential expression profiles of UA159ΔluxS_vs_UA159 and identified several novel sRNAs, named SmqsRs for “Streptococcus mutans quorum-sensing related small RNAs.” The detailed filtering steps are shown in Figure 1(C). We finally extracted two putative molecules as validated SmqsRs, which correspond to SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. SmqsR2 localizes to an intergenic region (IGR) between the S. mutans hisC and ezrA genes, whereas SmqsR4 maps to an IGR adjacent to glgABCD genes (Figure 1(A)). Briefly, both SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 have putative promoter sites, and SmqsR2 has a predicted rho-independent terminator at its 3’ end, indicating these are authentic sRNAs. mFold predictions with negative ∆G values support the presence of a stable secondary structure within both SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 (Figure 1(B)). SmqsR2 overlapped with previously predicted sRNA (psRNA-123) that was regulated by catabolite control protein A (CcpA) and catabolite repression. We used TargetRNA2 and IntaRNA to reveal the predicted mRNA targets of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4. Both targets of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 include annotated genes that mediate carbohydrate transport and metabolism, EPS synthesis, and biofilm formation (Supplementary Tables 4–7). Although not present in the genome-wide predictions, gtfB, gtfC, and gtfD were recognized as SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 targets via IntaRNA (Supplementary Table 8). SmqsRs were differentially detected at mid-exponential phase and responded to luxS expression

Northern blotting was performed with specific probes directed against SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in RNA preparations from UA159 WT and ΔluxS at both mid- and late-exponential phase. The predicted size of SmqsR2 (approximately 156 bp) and SmqsR4 (approximately 212 bp) were detected. It is worth noting that the significantly differential expression patterns of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 between ΔluxS and UA159 were observed at mid-exponential phase (Figure 2(A)). Compared to UA159, expressions of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in luxS deletion strain at mid-exponential phase quantified by RT-qPCR decreased by approximately 80% and 60%, respectively (Figure 2(B)). In particular, we constructed a luxS complementation strain (ΔluxS/pDL278_luxS) to evaluate the expression levels of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 following luxS. The transcription levels of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 decreased in UA159ΔluxS strain, whereas they increased in ΔluxS/pDL278_luxS strain, indicating that the expression of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 were positively correlated with luxS. We next evaluated the differences in SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 abundance in planktonic cells vs biofilms. Total RNA was prepared from UA159 strain grown as planktonic or biofilm cultures in BHI without and with added sugar (to final concentrations of 1% sucrose). Expressions of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 were highest in biofilms grown with sucrose, increasing approximately 6.15- and 8.37-fold, respectively. The levels of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in biofilm cultures were higher than those in planktonic cultures, especially with added sucrose (Figure 2(D)). It is supposed that SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 might be involved in S. mutans biofilm formation.

SmqsR2 and SmqsR4-fostered biofilm formation of luxS deletion strain

The biological functions of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 on luxS-mediated QS system in S. mutans were investigated by overexpressing SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in luxS deletion strain, respectively. Unlike the condensed biofilm formed by UA159, UA159ΔluxS bacteria appeared to scatter, forming larger channels surrounded by a reduced extracellular matrix. However, in ΔluxS_SmqsR2 and ΔluxS_SmqsR4 strains, cells were densely packed within the enriched extracellular matrix, which were similar to UA159 (Figure 3(A)). Concomitantly, the biomass quantified by crystal violet assay showed UA159ΔluxS significantly decreased biofilm growth, whereas ΔluxS_SmqsR2 and ΔluxS_SmqsR4 both increased (Figure 3(C)). In UA159, the exopolysaccharides and bacteria formed a compact reticular structure. UA159ΔluxS strain exhibited a reduced capacity to synthesize EPS, while more bacterial clusters wrapped with enriched EPS in the ΔluxS_SmqsR2 and ΔluxS_SmqsR4 strains (Figure 3(B)). These findings were further confirmed by quantitation of data revealing that ΔluxS_SmqsR2 and ΔluxS_SmqsR4 exhibited increased EPS/bacterial biomass volume ratio relative to UA159ΔluxS (Figure 3(D)). A similar consequence was observed by the anthrone assay, the real production of WSGs and WIGs in ΔluxS_SmqsR2 and ΔluxS_SmqsR4 strains were elevated compared to UA159ΔluxS (Figure 3(E)). Overexpression of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in luxS deletion strain, respectively, resulted in concomitant upregulation of the gtfB and gtfC genes (Figure 3(F)). Taken together, the detected SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 can promote EPS production and proportion, and may function as the effectors of luxS-mediated QS system in S. mutans. Overexpression of SmqsRs-altered biofilm structure and composition

Next, we evaluated the specific effects of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 overexpression on the ability of the strain to form biofilms. We separately overexpressed SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 in UA159, which we named UA159_SmqsR2 and UA159_SmqsR4, respectively. Growth-curve analysis revealed altered growth in which both UA159_SmqsR2 and UA159_SmqsR4 strains exhibited an extended lag phase and reduced exponential growth rates, but reached similar final bacterial yields relative to UA159 (Figure 4(A)). SEM detected the morphogenesis of both UA159 and mutant strain biofilms. Unlike the UA159, the overexpression strains exhibited a structural abnormality in the form of more blank areas, which the bacteria preferred to gather into dense clusters and the reduced extracellular matrix was interspersed among blank areas. Besides, no obvious abnormality was observed in cell morphology (Figure 4(C)). Furthermore, the biofilm biomass was measured, and the quantification data confirmed that overexpression of SmqsRs remarkably enhanced the ability of S. mutans to form biofilm (Figure 4(B)). Correspondingly, in the UA159_SmqsR2 and UA159_SmqsR4 strains, the expressions of gtfB/C/D and gbpB transcripts were elevated, but the expression of dexA transcript was repressed (Figure 4(D)). Overall, overexpression of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 altered the ability of S. mutans to form biofilm, indicating its essential role in regulating the virulence attributes of S. mutans. Since we found that SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 overexpression significantly affected EPS synthesis and planktonic growth of S. mutans, the transcriptomes of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 overexpression mutants were analyzed to identify changes in whole-gene expression. Specifically, gene enrichment and functional annotation clustering analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were conducted. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that DEGs primarily belonged to intrinsic/integral component of membrane and carbohydrate transport in both UA159_ SmqsR2_vs_UA159 and UA159_ SmqsR4_vs_UA159 groups (Figure 5(D)). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that DEGs both enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, with 31 DEGs in UA159_ SmqsR2_vs_UA159 group and 36 DEGs in UA159_ SmqsR4_vs_UA159 group (Figure 5(E)). Particularly, in UA159_ SmqsR2_vs_UA159 group, DEGs were mainly involved in phosphotransferase system (PTS) and fructose/mannose metabolism. In contrast, pathways of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and fructose/mannose metabolism were enriched in UA159_ SmqsR4_vs_UA159 group (Supplementary Fig. 2). Meanwhile, metabolic pathway analysis showed that DEGs primarily enriched in carbohydrate and energy metabolism in both UA159_ SmqsR2_vs_UA159 and UA159_ SmqsR4_vs_UA159 groups (Supplementary Fig. 3).

SmqsRs-enhanced ccpA transcript and CcpA positively regulated SmqsRs expression

Compared to UA159, there were 93 significantly upregulated genes and 87 downregulated genes in the UA159_SmqsR2 strain, 94 significantly upregulated genes and 105 downregulated genes in the UA159_SmqsR4 strain (Figure 6(A)). Volcano plots revealed that several genes related to cariogenic factors and carbohydrate utilization were differentially expressed. ccpA gene, coding for CcpA functioned as a transcriptional regulator in carbon catabolite repression (CCR) in S. mutans, was detected significantly upregulated in both UA159_SmqsR2 and UA159_SmqsR4 strains. By contrast, genes associated with fructose metabolism, including ftf and fruA/B/C/D, were downregulated in the overexpression strains. Expression level of ccpA gene was calculated using RT-qPCR, it increased 2.65- and 2.49-fold in the UA159_SmqsR2 and UA159_SmqsR4 strain, respectively (Figure 6(B)). Discussion

In the present study, we propose that S. mutans uses sRNAs to regulate biofilm formation and virulence gene expression in response to LuxS/AI-2 QS circuit. Consistent with this hypothesis are sRNAs that have been proved to link information from QS to regulation of biofilm behavior, such as the well-studied Qrr (Quorum Regulatory RNAs) and VqmR sRNAs in Vibrio cholerae [25,26]. Herein, we identify and characterize two sRNAs in S. mutans that are LuxS-responsive and contribute to EPS production, biofilm formation, and carbohydrate utilization in S. mutans. Our findings deepen the understanding of the roles of sRNAs in the LuxS/AI-2 circuit and the regulation of associated virulence attributes in this important cariogenic pathogen. These findings highlight an extra sRNA-centered QS signal amplification strategy in S. mutans communities, and may provide insights for future research on S. mutans pathogenesis and targeted treatment.

SmqsRs regulate biofilm development of S. mutans

Intriguingly, we find SmqsR2 overlaps with previously reported psRNA-123, which is in response to carbohydrate metabolism in S. mutans [27]. Similarly, overexpression of SmqsR2 significantly changes the carbon metabolism pathways, including the PTS, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and fructose and mannose metabolism pathways, as revealed by KEGG and GO analysis in our present study (Figure 5(D,E)). Additionally, ccpA is confirmed to be highly expressed in UA159_SmqsR2 by both RNA-seq and RT-qPCR (Figure 6(A,B)). It is reported that ccpA plays a critical role in the response to carbon source availability by affecting the stability of biofilms in S. mutans, and the gtfB/C genes require ccpA for optimal expression [28]. Furthermore, CcpA is a pleiotropic regulator involved in many important cellular processes and plays a major role in carbon catabolite repression (CCR), which enables bacteria to utilize carbon sources efficiently [29]. CCR is important for competition in natural environments, as the selection of the preferred carbon source is a major determining factor in growth rate and, therefore, competitive success with other microorganisms [30]. The genomics contexts of sRNAs likely have implications for their functions. We notice that SmqsR4 localizes to the glg operon (Figure 1(A)), which encodes enzymes responsible for intracellular polysaccharide (IPS) synthesis and degradation. S. mutans has two genes (glgC and glgD), respectively, encoding putative ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylases (ADP-Glc PPase), a key enzyme for glycogen synthesis in most bacteria. We then apply the IntaRNA and TargetRNA platforms to predict the downstream targets of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4. The in-silico target predictions suggest that many of them are involved in processes of carbohydrate metabolism and biofilm formation, including gtfB/C/D and glgB (Tables S2-S6). As we know, S. mutans converts extracellular sucrose into EPS mainly by Gtfs (encoded by gtfB/C/D) for forming homopolymers of glucose called glucans and internalizes sucrose as IPS for fermentation through the PTS, further metabolizing IPS by enzymes encoded by the glg operon [31,32]. Thus, it is anticipated that SmqsRs’ activity plays a potential role in the cariogenic phenotypes of S. mutans.

Genes that are differentially expressed between biofilm and planktonic states are considered to be highly associated with biofilm formation [33]. The ASvicR RNA transcript was found to be elevated in planktonic cultures, and overexpression of ASvicR led to a reduction in biofilm formation in S. mutans [24,34]. In Acinetobacter baumannii, sRNA13573 was expressed at a higher level in biofilms than in planktonic states, and its involvement in biofilm formation was verified [35]. Similar phenomenon was observed in our study, expressions of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 were higher in UA159 cells grown as biofilms in BHIS compared with planktonic cells in the same medium (Figure 2(D)). It is worth noting that an impressive number of sRNAs establish an additional, posttranscriptional layer of control over the expression of key biofilm components and regulators in E. coli, including well-studied ArcZ, McaS, OmrA, and OmrB [36–38]. Subsequently, SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 were highly differentially expressed at mid-exponential phase between wild-type and luxS-deletion strains (Figure 2(A,B)). It has been shown that protein production in the stationary phase was almost 121% higher in comparison with the cells analyzed at the exponential phase. And prior to protein expression, various gene expressions in the bacteria are active at mid-exponential phase [39,40]. We further constructed SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 overexpression strains, respectively, and identified that SmqsRs could foster the synthesis of both water-soluble/insoluble EPS, biofilm formation, and carbohydrate utilization. Thus, it is anticipated that SmqsRs’ activity plays a potential role in the cariogenic phenotypes of S. mutans, including biofilm formation and carbohydrate utilization.

SmqsRs link LuxS/AI-2 QS circuit to biofilm formation

A hallmark of nearly all QS systems is that they enable single-celled organisms to act in unison, and this feature is crucial to many important collective microbial behaviors, particularly biofilm formation, which requires the tight coordination of signaling events and robust gene regulation control [41,42]. sRNAs can either enhance or repress gene expression through multiple mechanisms, and reports in the literature ascribe an emerging role for sRNAs in the QS systems [43]. Indeed, many QS systems rely on sRNAs to achieve optimal performance [44,45]. The prime example is Qrr sRNAs in V. cholera, which function at the center of QS pathways and regulate multiple mRNA targets that encode regulators of biofilm formation [25]. In the present study, we discovered that two novel sRNAs are LuxS-responsive and further identified that SmqsRs promote the expression of gtfB/C/D and ccpA genes, which indicates that SmqsRs may function as effectors of the luxS-mediated QS system in S. mutans. Both SmqsRs and Qrr sRNAs operate at the same level of the QS response cascades, namely, directly downstream of the response regulator, whose phosphorylation level reflects the extracellular AI concentration, and upstream of the majority of the QS regulon [12]. Indeed, the SmqsRs may solve a long-standing conundrum in the S. mutans LuxS/AI-2 pathway, i.e., how does S. mutans manage to integrate and internalize the environmental AI-2 signals to govern biofilm formation? The SmqsRs provide a simple yet elegant answer to this question, as they can regulate multiple mRNA targets, which encode proteins involved in regulating QS signal transduction dynamics, global signal integration, and virulence-attribute expression. In the present study, we found that the expression levels of gtfB/C/D and ccpA genes were increased in SmqsRs overexpression strains by RNA-seq and RT-qPCR. As the most abundant class of post-transcriptional regulators, the majority of sRNAs inhibit translation, whereas some of them activate translation [46]. We speculate that the positive regulation of SmqsRs on gtfB/C/D may based on unfolding the sequestration of RBS or stabilization of mRNAs. However, we still have much to learn about sRNA-based regulation of bacterial collective behaviors. The mechanism behind the interaction of LuxS and SmqsRs has not yet been elucidated, and further explorations are necessary.

Because sRNA regulators are crucial to the expression of virulence attributes, it is unsurprising to find that the LuxS/AI-2 system relies on sRNAs to achieve optimal performance in S. mutans. We demonstrate that SmqsRs functions by regulating the expression of gtfB/C/D and ccpA genes, thereby fostering the synthesis of both water-soluble/insoluble EPS, biofilm formation, and carbohydrate utilization (Figure 7). Notably, our data not only provide new hypotheses for previously confirmed LuxS-mediated biofilm formation but also help predict the biological functions of so far uncharacterized sRNA regulators in the LuxS/AI-2 QS circuit. In summary, our study supports a role for sRNAs as crucial regulators that coordinate a response to LuxS/AI-2 machinery via the nuanced control of S. mutans biofilm formation. Further insights into these regulatory mechanisms may provide new opportunities to develop potential therapies to prevent dental biofilm formation. Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

All bacterial strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. All primers designed using MacVector 7.0 software were purchased commercially (Sangon Biotech) and were based on the sequence of the S. mutans UA159 genome (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/AE014133.2) [47]. S. mutans UA159 and its derivatives were routinely grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid) or on BHI agar at 37°C in an anaerobic incubator (10% H2, 5% CO2, and 85% N2). The medium (BHIS) used for the biofilm assay of S. mutans was BHI supplemented with 1% sucrose (weight/volume).

Construction of recombinant SmqsRs plasmid

The shuttle vector pIB169, harboring SmqsRs and promoter regions, was used to overexpress SmqsR2 and SmqsR4, respectively. Recombinant plasmid pIB169_SmqsR2 and pIB169_SmqsR4 were constructed by cloning 508-bp and 564-bp amplicons containing the SmqsR2/4-coding sequences and S. mutans endogenous promoter region of vicR gene into the EcoRI and XhoI sites of plasmid pIB169, respectively [24]. The resulting recombinant was transformed into E. coli DH5a, and transformants were selected on BHI supplemented with chloramphenicol (BHI-Chl). Plasmid DNA isolated with a Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen) was then used to overexpress the SmqsR2/4 in S. mutans UA159 and luxS deletion strains in trans. Transformation occurred in the presence of competence-stimulating peptide as described above [48], and transformants resistant to chloramphenicol were selected on BHI-Chl agar plates (Chl, 20 μg/mL).

sRNA target prediction

Both IntaRNA [49,50] and TargetRNA2 [51] freeware were used with default parameters to predict the downstream mRNA targets of S. mutans SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 at a genome-wide level as previously described with some modifications [43]. IntaRNA was also used to examine whether SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 bind to the gtfB/C/D genes when each of the genes was entered manually; here, a seed region of at least 7 nt was used.

Northern blotting

Detection of SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 was carried out by Northern blotting as previously described with some modifications [34]. Briefly, equal amounts (10 μg) of total RNA was purified from S. mutans cultures. Samples were separated by electrophoresis in 1% formaldehyde–agarose gels and transferred to positively charged Nylon membranes (Amersham). The probe sequence listed in Supplementary Table 2 was obtained by oligonucleotide synthesis (Sangon Biotech). The probes were labeled using the DIG Luminescent System (Roche). Blots were incubated with 10 ng/mL probes at 52°C, and signals were detected using the CSPD Star substrate (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and RT-qPCR analysis

For the SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 transcripts, S. mutans UA159 and UA159 ΔluxS cells were harvested at mid-exponential phase (optical density 0.5 at 600 nm) in BHI alone or supplemented with erythromycin (10 μg/mL). Biofilm growth was achieved as previously described with modifications [23]. Briefly, an overnight culture of UA159 was diluted into fresh BHI and grown to mid-exponential phase, followed by diluting 0.1 mL culture into 2 mL of BHI alone or supplemented with sucrose (1%). Biofilms were established in 24-well plates for 24 h and cells were harvested by scraping. For RT-qPCR, overnight cultures of S. mutans cells were diluted into fresh BHI medium containing erythromycin (10 μg/mL) and/or spectinomycin (300 μg/mL) and/or chloramphenicol (20 μg/mL) and grown to mid-exponential phase. Total RNA was extracted using the classic TRIzol-chloroform protocol (Invitrogen). Contaminating genomic DNA was removed by using Turbo RNase-free DNase I (Ambion). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). RNA quality and integrity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). Quantitative RT-PCR was conducted by the QuantiTect SYBR-Green PCR kit (Qiagen) and the LightCycler 480 (Roche). RT-qPCR was performed using primers for the specific genes listed in Supplementary Table 2, with gyrA as a reference gene. Real-time cycling conditions were 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s, and then dissociation. RT-qRCR reactions were carried out in triplicate. Threshold cycle values (CT) were quantified, and the expression of each gene was normalized relative to that of gyrA.

Planktonic growth assay

Overnight culture of S. mutans strains was subcultured into fresh BHI, grown until mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5), and diluted (1:100) using fresh BHI broth. These planktonic bacteria were cultured in 96-well flat-bottom polystyrene microtiter plates (Corning) and grown at 37°C, with antibiotics added as necessary, as previously described [52]. The medium alone (without bacteria) was maintained as the blank. The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured every hour using a Multiskan Spectrum (Thermo Scientific). A quantity of 5 μL of sterile mineral oil was added to the surface of the liquid to prevent it from being contaminated by oxygen. Three independent experiments were conducted, with results obtained in triplicate.

Biofilm formation assay

The crystal violet (CV) assay for analyzing biofilm biomass was performed as previously described [48]. Each well of a 96-well plate was inoculated with 2 μL of exponential bacterial cultures (OD600 0.5) and 200 μL of BHIS. After anaerobic incubation for 24 h, the formed biofilms were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.2) to remove nonadherent cells, air-dried, and stained with 0.1% (w/v) CV for 15 min. The bound dye was extracted from the stained cells with 1 mL destaining solution (ethanol:acetone 8:2). Biofilm formation was then quantified by measuring the optical density of the solution at 575 nm.

Exopolysaccharide measurement

Water-insoluble glucan (WIG) and water-soluble glucan (WSG) from S. mutans biofilms were isolated, purified, and analyzed using the anthrone-sulfuric method as previously described with some modifications [53]. Exopolysaccharides from 24 h S. mutans bacterial biofilms (UA159, UA159 ΔluxS, ΔluxS_SmqsR2, ΔluxS_SmqsR4, UA159_SmqsR2, UA159_SmqsR4) were extracted and purified according to a previous protocol [53]. For the anthrone method, standard curves were prepared with a dextran standard using various concentrations (Supplementary Figure 1). Four milliliters of the anthrone reagent was added to 1 mL sample solutions and heated at 95°C for 6 min. The absorbance of each sample at 625 nm was monitored on a microplate spectrophotometer (Shimadzu atm9700). The corresponding polysaccharide concentration was calculated according to the standard curve. All assays were performed in triplicate from at least three different experiments.

Structural imaging of biofilm architecture

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) were conducted to evaluate the characteristics of S. mutans biofilms [32]. Overnight cultures of S. mutans strains were subcultured into fresh BHI, grown until the mid-exponential phase (OD600 nm = 0.5), and diluted (1:100) with BHIS as described above. The cultures were then inoculated anaerobically on glass coverslips for 24 h at 37°C. The biofilms were fixed with 2.5% (wt/vol) glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 12 h, rinsed with sterile PBS, and then dehydrated serially (30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) in ethanol. They were subsequently sputter-coated with gold. The sample was examined using SEM (Inspect F50; FEI) at magnifications of ×1000, ×5000, and × 20,000, and representative images are shown. For analyzing exopolysaccharide production and distribution within the mutant and parent S. mutans biofilms, we used CLSM for exopolysaccharide/bacterial staining. Briefly, 1 μM Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen) and 2.5 μM SYTO 9 (Invitrogen) were used to label exopolysaccharide and bacterial cells, respectively, in situ. The biofilms were observed by using Ar (514/488 nm) and He–Ne (543 nm) lasers. Green indicated bacteria and red indicated exopolysaccharide. Images of six random fields of each group were captured. A three-dimensional reconstruction of the biofilms was analyzed using Imaris 7.0.0 software (Bitplane), and the exopolysaccharide-to-bacteria ratio was calculated using Comstat software.

RNA-sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptome analysis of UA159_ SmqsR2_vs_UA159 and UA159_ SmqsR4_vs_UA159 groups was performed by RNA-sequencing as previously described with some modifications (33749354). Overnight cultures of S. mutans were subcultured into fresh BHI and grown until the mid-exponential phase (OD600 0.5) under anaerobic conditions. This culture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min at 4°C, and the cell pellet was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen until needed. For RNA extraction, nine independent cultures of UA159, UA159_SmqsR2, and UA159_SmqsR4 strains were collected and treated with RNAprotect (Qiagen). Total RNA was isolated using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen), then transferred to PowerLyzer 24 (Qiagen) at 2000 rpm for 5 min. RNA quality and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). After removal rRNA, cDNA synthesis, and adaptor ligation, DNA fragments with ligated adaptor molecules on both ends were selectively enriched using Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail via PCR (15 cycles). PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using Agilent high-sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The library was sequenced on HiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina). Transcripts with |log2FoldChange| >1 and p-value ˂ 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) were performed as described previously [54] to determine whether CcpA binds to SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 directly and whether the region of binding shares overlap with a proposed promoter region. PCR amplicons of each candidate promoter region were generated from S. mutans UA159 chromosomal DNA using primers listed in Supplementary Table 2. The DNA probes were purified and labeled with the biotin. Prior to the binding reaction, recombinant CcpA protein was incubated (30 min, 25°C) with 50 mM acetyl phosphate for phosphorylation. The binding reactions were incubated with the mixture of labeled DNA fragments (20 pmol) and phosphorylated recombinant CcpA (40 pmol), and 200-fold excess of unlabeled DNA fragments (cold DNA) as specific competitor, in Tris-based buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol) for 30 min at room temperature. Protein–DNA complexes were added into a 6% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× Tris–borate–EDTA buffer and separated at 4°C (100 V, 60 min). Then, the proteins were transferred to Biodyne® B nylon membranes (PallCorporation) and visualized by ChemiDoc XRS (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Construction of ccpA deletion strain and detection SmqsRs expression

The ccpA deletion strain was conducted by a two-step homologous recombination procedure adapted from the previously published method [55]. Positive transformants were isolated from BHI-erythromycin agar plates (erythromycin, 10 μg/mL) and subsequently BHI-p-Cl-Phe plates (p-Cl-Phe, 4 mg/mL). The mutant strain was verified by PCR and sequencing.

For the SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 transcripts, S. mutans UA159 and UA159 ΔccpA cells were harvested at mid-exponential phase (optical density 0.5 at 600 nm) in BHI. Total RNA was extracted using the classic TRIzol–chloroform protocol (Invitrogen). Contaminating genomic DNA was removed by using Turbo RNase-free DNase I (Ambion). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). RNA quality and integrity were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). RT-qPCR was performed using primers for SmqsR2 and SmqsR4 listed in Supplementary Table 2, with gyrA as a reference gene.

Statistical analyses and study period

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate and reproduced three separate times. Statistical analyses of the data were performed using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk and Bartlett tests were used to assess whether the data were parametric or not. For parametric testing, Fisher tests and one-way ANOVA were used to detect significant effects of variables. For nonparametric testing, the Kruskal–Wallis test and least significant difference multiple comparisons were used. Differences between means of data were considered significant if the p value was <0.05. All experiments were performed from October 2021 to March 2025.
