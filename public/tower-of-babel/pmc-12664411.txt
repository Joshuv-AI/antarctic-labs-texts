The association of workload during healthcare service disruption with the mental health of prehospital emergency medical service providers in Korea
Authors: Yonghwan Moon, Min Seung Choi, Soo Jin Kim, Hyekyung Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn 2024, a large-scale healthcare service disruption occurred in Korea due to ongoing conflict between the government and medical professionals over the proposed expansion of medical school admissions. This study examined how workload burdens experienced by prehospital emergency medical service (EMS) providers during the disruption affected their mental health.MethodsWe analyzed associations between workload factors, including delayed return to base, urgent administrative tasks, and missed meals, and mental-health outcomes, including depressive symptoms, burnout, and sleep problems, in a sample of 851 prehospital EMS providers.ResultsThe results show that EMS providers who experienced delayed return to base were more likely to report depressive symptoms (OR = 2.22, 95 % CI: 1.52, 3.23), those involved in urgent administrative tasks reported higher levels of burnout (OR = 1.83, 95 % CI: 1.28, 2.61), and those who frequently missed meals were more likely to experience sleep problems (OR = 1.89, 95 % CI: 1.33, 2.67).ConclusionsThese findings imply that different types of workload burdens during healthcare service disruptions are significantly associated with mental health outcomes among prehospital EMS providers, highlighting the need for institutional strategies to protect their psychological well-being during similar crisis situations.Keywords: Healthcare disruption, Prehospital EMS providers, Mental health, Workload, Depression, Burnout, Sleep problems

Introduction

On February 6, 2024, the South Korean government initiated a healthcare reform by announcing a 65 % increase in the medical school admission quota relative to the existing capacity (de Beer et al., 2024). This abrupt policy shift provoked strong opposition from the medical community, resulting in mass resignations and walkouts by resident doctors across the country, and causing widespread disruptions in medical service delivery (de Beer et al., 2024; You et al., 2025). These disruptions led to delays in emergency care and difficulties in securing inpatient beds, thereby underscoring the critical importance of the emergency medical system. As a core component of the healthcare infrastructure that requires immediate and uninterrupted operation, the emergency medical system must remain functional even amid systemic breakdowns. During such crises, the working conditions of emergency medical service (EMS) providers become markedly more challenging (Abir et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025). Irregular shifts, unpredictable patient volumes and acuity, and high-pressure clinical situations impose significant psychological stress and mental strain on EMS providers (Kim et al., 2008).

Healthcare disruption refers to the temporary or prolonged suspension or reduction of medical services, often resulting in delays in emergency patient transport and follow-up care (Fatani et al., 2024). In such situations, EMS providers face not only their core clinical responsibilities but also additional burdens, including administrative coordination, Delayed return to base times, and difficulties in securing receiving hospitals. These cumulative demands significantly heighten occupational stress and contribute to burnout (World Health Organization, 2020; Lawn et al., 2020). Shifts in work conditions under these circumstances can directly impact the mental health of prehospital EMS providers. Previous studies have found that during healthcare disruptions, feelings of guilt and helplessness stemming from unsuccessful patient outcomes are closely associated with increased anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances (Lai et al., 2020).

Prehospital EMS providers routinely operate in unpredictable and time-sensitive environments in which they must engage in rapid, high-stakes clinical decision-making. As a result, they are subject to intense psychological pressure, often exceeding that experienced in standard clinical settings (Feng et al., 2023). The life-or-death nature of their responsibilities imposes not only physical fatigue but also recurrent emotional exhaustion and substantial occupational stress (Meyer et al., 2024). Such psychological strain increases the risk of chronic mental-health conditions, including depression and burnout, which may ultimately impair job performance and heighten turnover intentions (Feng et al., 2023; Alanazy and Abdullah, 2023). Previous studies have consistently shown that EMS providers face significantly greater risks to their mental health compared to professionals in other occupational groups (Jenkins et al., 2025).

To date, most studies on the mental health of prehospital EMS providers have focused primarily on individual characteristics or psychosocial factors. However, there remains a lack of empirical research investigating the impact of structural crises, such as healthcare service disruptions, on mental health through changes in occupational environments. This study, therefore, aimed to examine systematically how specific workload burdens experienced by EMS providers during periods of healthcare disruption influenced mental-health outcomes, including depression, burnout, and sleep disturbances. The findings are expected to inform the development of policy-level interventions to safeguard the mental well-being of EMS providers and to provide a foundation for establishing a more resilient emergency medical system capable of responding effectively to future disruptions.

Methods

Data source and study participants

This study utilized data from the “Korean EMS providers' Working Environment in Situations of Medical Disruption Survey” conducted between November and December 2024. The survey comprised 94 items, including questions pertaining to prehospital emergency medical activities during the healthcare disruption. Among the 855 survey respondents, one individual who did not provide informed consent for academic study and three individuals with invalid age data were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 851 prehospital EMS providers (Fig. 1).

The survey targeted EMS providers nationwide who were operating under conditions in which systematic emergency medical services could not be adequately provided due to conflict between the government and the medical community. The objective of the survey was to assess the actual status of EMS provision and the health conditions of EMS providers during this crisis, to generate evidence to inform policy reform. The survey items were developed to reflect the actual working conditions during the healthcare disruption. Relevant literature were reviewed, and initial items were drafted through interviews with EMS providers, dispatchers, on-site personnel, and policy officials, A pilot survey was conducted to assess item clarity and relevance, after which the final version was refined and confirmed. Reliability testing indicated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.86).

Measurement variables

Dependent variables

The outcome variables in this study were depression, burnout, and sleep problems. Depression was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale with scores below 16 categorized as “no depression” and scores of ≥16 categorized as “depression present.” Burnout was measured using the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory scores below 50 were classified as “no burnout,” while scores of ≥50 indicated burnout. Sleep problems were assessed using three items from the fifth Korean Working Conditions Survey: difficulty falling asleep, waking up repeatedly during the night, and waking up feeling extremely fatigued. Each item was rated on a five-point scale ranging from “never” (0) to “everyday” (4), and responses were dichotomized into “no sleep problem” and “sleep problem present” based on quartile distribution.

Independent variables

Independent variables included both sociodemographic characteristics and work-related factors. Sociodemographic variables included sex (male/female), marital status (single, married, divorced/separated/widowed, and non-response), household size (1, 2, 3, 4 or more), education level, and annual household income. Income was categorized as: < KRW 30 million (< 21,000 USD), KRW 30–39.99 million (21,000–27,999 USD), KRW 40–49.99 million (28,000–34,999 USD), KRW 50–59.99 million (35,000–41,999 USD), KRW 60–69.99 million (42,000–48,999 USD), and ≥ KRW 70 million (≥ 49,000 USD). Duration of shift work experience in EMS was classified as: < 1 year, 1–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, 15–19 years, 20–24 years, and ≥ 25 years.

Job rank was categorized into five levels: sobang-sa, sobang-gyo, sobang-jang, sobang-wi, and sobang-gyeong. Job position was classified as: emergency medical technician, ambulance driver, emergency call dispatcher or counselor, and EMS administrator. Current assignment types included: general, air-rescue EMS unit, special EMS unit, and EMS command and control center. Work type was categorized as: day shift, shift work, and other field work. Region of assignment was divided into capital region and provinces.

To assess workload burden, three specific variables were included: delayed return to base, experience of urgent administrative tasks, and missing meals during duty. Delayed return to base time was categorized as either <1 h or ≥ 1 h. Experience of urgent administrative tasks and missing meals were both assessed as binary variables (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and chi-squared tests were used to examine the associations between sociodemographic and work-related factors and the presence of depression, burnout, and sleep problems. To ensure the stability and interpretability of the regression models, correlations among independent variables were reviewed in advance. Hierarchical logistic regression models were employed to identify factors associated with each mental-health outcome. Model 1 included only sociodemographic and workload-burden variables, while Model 2 additionally incorporated work-related variables to assess their incremental explanatory power. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kongju National University (Approval No. KNU_IRB_2025–053). To ensure participant confidentiality and anonymity, all personal data were coded with random identifiers before analysis, making individual identification difficult. Digital research materials were securely stored on a password-protected computer in a locked facility, accessible only to the researcher. All data will be retained for three years post-study completion and then immediately destroyed.

Results

Significant differences in mental-health outcomes were observed based on participants' characteristics and key variables (Table 1). Burnout (p < 0.01) and sleep problems (p = 0.02) were more prevalent among male EMS providers. Marital status was significantly associated with depression (p < 0.01). Job rank was associated with burnout (p = 0.01) and sleep problems (p = 0.01), while job position was significantly related to burnout (p = 0.03) and sleep problems (p = 0.02), with a higher prevalence among emergency medical technicians and ambulance drivers. The work region showed a significant association with all three mental-health outcomes, i.e., depression, burnout, and sleep problems (p < 0.01). Missing meals during duty was also significantly associated with depression (p = 0.02), burnout (p < 0.01), and sleep problems (p < 0.01). Delayed return to base was significantly associated with depression (p < 0.01) and burnout (p < 0.01). Experiencing urgent administrative duties was associated with depression (p = 0.02), burnout (p < 0.01), and sleep problems (p = 0.01).

Discussion

This study utilized data from the Korean Prehospital EMS providers' Working Environment in Situations of Medical Disruption Survey to examine the association between occupational burden and mental health among prehospital EMS providers during the 2024 healthcare service disruption in Korea. Depression, burnout, and sleep problems were designated as dependent variables, while workload burden factors were included as key independent variables. These findings lead to the following points of discussion.

Prehospital EMS providers working in the capital region were found to be at higher risk of mental-health problems compared to those working in the provinces. This appears to be closely related to the structural characteristics of the capital region, where high population density and elevated emergency demand result in sustained pressure during patient transport and treatment processes (Feng et al., 2023; Poulton et al., 2018). During healthcare service disruptions, inadequate patient redistribution and medical resource shortages further intensify this burden (Kim et al., 2023). Previous studies have reported that EMS providers in capital region are more vulnerable to emotional exhaustion, depression, and poor sleep quality, implying that regional disparities in working conditions have tangible effects on mental health (Kim et al., 2024). Although studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic found no significant regional differences in mental-health outcomes, this may be attributable to the nationwide increase in workload at that time, which likely obscured underlying disparities (Feng et al., 2023). In contrast, healthcare disruptions disproportionately affect high-demand regions such as the capital, and this asymmetry may be unique to the nature of service shutdowns. These findings suggest the importance of targeted interventions such as guaranteed rest periods, personnel redistribution, and sustained psychological support systems for EMS providers in the capital region both during routine operations and under crisis conditions.

EMS providers who experienced delayed return to base and urgent administrative responsibilities were at higher risk of depression and burnout. After transporting emergency patients, providers often encounter delays in returning to base due to administrative procedures, including bed confirmation, coordination with caregivers, and completion of documentation (California Hospital Association, 2017). These interruptions limit opportunities for physical recovery and prolong psychological tension even after clinical tasks have been completed, which may be associated with emotional exhaustion and depressive symptoms (Cooney et al., 2011). During periods of healthcare service disruption, emergency departments frequently experience bed shortages, causing extended patient handover delays and necessitating additional coordination to locate available receiving facilities. These operational barriers further exacerbate fatigue and hinder psychological recovery, ultimately diminishing quality of life (Jafari et al., 2021).

The problem extends beyond physical workload and includes significant administrative burden. Even after transportation tasks have been completed, prehospital EMS providers must perform activities such as assigning beds, coordinating inter-facility transfers, communicating with caregivers, and completing extensive documentation. This accumulation of responsibilities can be associated with role overload and psychological distress (Zhu and Eisenberg, 2024). These findings indicate that delayed return to base and administrative burdens are not merely operational inefficiencies but may be associated with the physical and emotional well-being of EMS providers. While previous research has highlighted administrative strain primarily among hospital staff, this study clarified its relevance in the prehospital context. The results support the need for structural reforms, including the deployment of dedicated administrative support staff in the field.

EMS providers who were unable to eat during duty shifts were more likely to report burnout and sleep problems. In the fast-paced environment of prehospital emergency care, where patient transport and treatment occur continuously, it is often difficult to find time for meals. This not only results in cumulative physical fatigue but also emotional instability caused by unmet basic physiological needs (Mansouri et al., 2022). Previous studies have found that emotional exhaustion is exacerbated when EMS providers cannot maintain control over essential recovery activities such as eating and sleeping (Mansouri et al., 2022; Cornelius et al., 2021). These findings imply that ensuring access to regular meals serves as a protective factor for mental health. Consequently, creating an environment in which mealtimes are guaranteed should be recognized as a basic requirement for mental-health support in EMS work and addressed through policy and organizational measures.

Sleep problems were reported more frequently by EMS providers working in the capital region and among those who missed meals. Due to rotating shifts, irregular dispatch schedules, and unpredictable emergency calls, EMS providers face structural obstacles to obtaining adequate, high-quality sleep (Patterson et al., 2023). Poor sleep quality impairs cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and energy levels, thereby contributing to mental-health issues such as depression, burnout, and reduced empathy (Shechter et al., 2023). For EMS providers, sufficient sleep is critical to maintaining clinical decision-making capacity and rapid responsiveness in high-stress environments. However, even after completing shifts, many providers experience ongoing sleep disruption due to residual administrative responsibilities or the anticipation of being called back into service (Jenkins et al., 2025). This implies that sleep disturbances are not isolated events but cumulative, ongoing challenges.

Although none of the additional work-related variables reached statistical significance, the inclusion of these factors in Model 2 led to an increase in Nagelkerke R2 compared with Model 1. This improvement in explanatory power indicates that, beyond sociodemographic characteristics, other structural aspects of EMS work may still play a background role in explaining variations in mental health outcomes. Previous studies have reported that organizational and contextual factors show significant associations with mental health outcomes among emergency and public safety workers (Edgelow et al., 2022; Schneider et al., 2019). These findings suggest that the mental health of EMS providers may not be determined solely by immediate task demands but may also be influenced by how organizational and contextual frameworks structure those demands within the workplace.

This study contributes to the literature by incorporating workload-burden variables such as administrative burden and missed meals into the analysis of sleep problems, providing a more comprehensive perspective than prior studies that focused solely on shift schedules (Shechter et al., 2023). These findings suggest the importance of the need for institutional policies that guarantee minimum rest periods between shifts, protected recovery time, and structured support for sleep health.

This study had several limitations. First, the use of cross-sectional data limited the ability to establish causal relationships between workload-burden factors and mental-health outcomes. Second, as the data were collected during a period of healthcare service disruption, the findings may not be generalizable to typical emergency medical settings. Third, although the survey was conducted nationwide, regional imbalances in respondent distribution may have influenced the results and introduced potential bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides meaningful empirical evidence on how task burdens affect the mental health of EMS providers during a structural crisis. In particular, it offers a comprehensive analysis of the association between delayed return to base time, urgent administrative duties, and missed mealtimes, factors that have been relatively underexplored in previous research. These findings may inform future policy interventions aimed at protecting the mental health of prehospital EMS providers and enhancing the sustainability and resilience of emergency medical systems during future healthcare disruptions.
