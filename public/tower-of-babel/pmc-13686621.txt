Severe Infectious Keratitis Among Inpatients at an Emergency Tertiary Hospital Ophthalmology Department in France: Associations Between Known Predisposing Factors and Patient Demographic, Clinical, and Microbiological Characteristics
Authors: Julie Dovigo-Ané, Vincent Soler, Jacqueline Butterworth, Pierre Fournié
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Describe the epidemiological, clinical, and microbiological patterns in severe infectious keratitis (IK) and identify variables associated with known risk factors.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed inpatient hospitalizations for severe IK (2016–2021) in our tertiary emergency ophthalmology department (France). Chi-squared and Student's t-tests compared demographic, clinical, and microbiological variables according to the presence of known risk factors. Associations between known risk factors (model outcomes) and our chosen variables (predictors) were analyzed by multivariate logistic regression.Results:A total of 861 participants were included (mean age 56 years, 50.3% female) among which 96.2% harbored ≥1 risk factor. Contact lens wear (32.2%) and ocular trauma (14.3%) were the most common risk factors. Contact lens wear–related IK was associated with female sex (adjOR2.91 [2.08–4.10]; P=0.0001) and younger age (56 years and younger) (adjOR7.81 [5.50–11.2]; P=0.0001), whereas ocular trauma–related IK was associated with male sex (adjOR4.55 [2.86–7.14]; P=0.0001) and younger age (adjOR1.65 [1.10–2.49]; P=0.017). Contact lens wear–related (adjOR1.97 [1.38–2.82], P=0.0002) and anterior segment surgery–related (adjOR1.74 [1.07–2.79]; P=0.02) IK were associated with summer admission. Contact lens wear was associated with gram-negative bacilli infection (adjOR4.32 [2.9–6.46]; P=0.0001).Conclusions:Prevention strategies are required for younger female contact lens users, patients undergoing anterior segment surgery in summer, and younger men with ocular trauma.

Infectious keratitis is characterized by the proliferation of intracorneal microorganisms, resulting in local inflammation and tissue destruction. It is the fifth most common cause of legal blindness at the global level.1 The incidence of infectious keratitis varies according to geographical location and degree of industrialization: 0.5 to 79.9 per 10,000 individuals per year.2 Bacterial infections are the most common form in developed countries (e.g., the United States and the United Kingdom), but there are also fungal, parasitic, and viral infections.3 The diagnosis of infectious keratitis is primarily clinical, and based on signs of the presence of infiltrates, corneal ulcers, and conjunctival hyperemia. Microbiological analysis of corneal scrapings is then used to confirm infectious keratitis and identify the causative microorganism. After, antimicrobial treatment can be guided.

Patients with a severe form of infectious keratitis generally require hospitalization for treatment and meet at least one of the following local criteria described.4 The well-characterized 1-2-3 rule5 determines severe infectious keratitis by a Tyndall effect >+1, ulcer diameter >2 mm, and ulcer location within <3 mm of the optical axis. Other local criteria for poor visual prognosis include corneal perforation or threat of perforation, infection deterioration despite 48 hr of topical treatment, associated endophthalmitis, prior corneal surgery, corneal transplant, and bilateral infection. General criteria include patients with monocular vision, systemic diseases, immune depression, or when outpatient treatment cannot be indicated.4

A local and/or systemic predisposing factor was identified among most cases (>93%) of confirmed bacterial keratitis in two studies performed in France over 10 years ago.6,7 Similar findings have also been reported in a recent study on 354 participants in a center in Paris (France).8 Among local risk factors identified, contact lens wear was the most frequent, found among 45% to 50% of bacterial keratitis cases.6–8 The presence of an ocular surface disease, particularly dry eye syndrome, has also been identified as a frequent risk factor.3 Indeed, dry eye syndrome leads to deregulated immune defense mechanisms that favor the development of infectious keratitis.9 Other local risk factors include neurotrophic keratitis, a degenerative corneal disease promoting corneal ulceration and epithelial erosion,2 as well as static eyelid disorders and blepharitis.4 Blepharitis has been identified as one of the predisposing factors for Moraxella keratitis, with 25% of Moraxella-infected patients presenting with blepharitis among a cohort of patients (n=101) in Paris (France) between 2016 and 2018.10 External risk factors include ocular trauma and a history of ocular surgery, with half of all cases of ocular surgery–related infectious keratitis being secondary to corneal transplantation.3 General risk factors include diabetes mellitus and various causes of immune depression. For instance, HIV-infected people carry a higher risk of developing infectious keratitis than healthy people.2

Most studies to date in France have only focused on more severe forms of bacterial keratitis.6–8 Therefore, the aim of this study was first to examine the epidemiological, demographic, clinical, and microbiological data of patients hospitalized on an inpatient basis with severe infectious keratitis (bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic) in our center in the South of France; hence, within a population from a developed (or industrialized) country with a temperate climate. We then investigated the presence of the previously mentioned known risk factors within our cohort. Finally, we tested for associations between any known risk factors identified in our cohort (taken as model outcomes) with the following chosen variables (taken as predictors): participant sex, age, season of admission, visual acuity, length of hospital stay, and Gram staining (bacterial keratitis).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

We conducted a monocentric, retrospective, cohort study of all patients (children and adults) hospitalized on an inpatient basis for the management of severe infectious keratitis in the emergency ophthalmology department of Toulouse University Hospital (France) between the January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2021. An official waiver of ethical approval was granted from the Institutional Review Board of Toulouse University Hospital given the retrospective and noninterventional nature of the study as asserted by the French Ethical and Regulatory Jardé law (Study Reference RnIPH 2024-97). All the procedures performed were part of routine care, and both in accordance with institutional guidelines and with the principles and regulations of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and all data have been anonymized for publication.

Participants and Data Collection

Participant data were collected from electronic records using the ORBIS software (Strasbourg, France). A search for all inpatients hospitalized for severe keratitis (all causes) through the following ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th version) discharge classifications was performed: H160: corneal ulcer; H161: other superficial keratitis without conjunctivitis; H163: interstitial and deep keratitis; H168: other keratitis. During the study period, hospitalization was based on local severity criteria (1-2-3 rule, deterioration within 48 hr of initiating topical treatment, corneal perforation or risk of perforation, endophthalmitis, prior corneal surgery/graft, bilateral involvement) and general criteria (monocular, systemic disease, immunosuppression, inability to manage outpatient treatment). Exclusions were patients with noninfectious corneal ulcers, endophthalmitis without corneal involvement, noninfectious nonpenetrating ocular trauma (including burns, corneal abrasions, corneal foreign bodies, and hyphema), and patients treated for the prevention of postcorneal transplant infection. Importantly, only patients for whom we could confirm the presence or absence, from clinical records, of the following known local and external risk factors were included: contact lens wear, ocular trauma, neurotrophic keratitis, ocular surface diseases, anterior segment surgery, static eyelids, blepharitis, and systemic diseases (diabetes, chronic alcoholism, immunosuppression, psychiatric disorders). The following data were also collected for each participant: sex, age, infected side, length of hospital stay, season of admission, symptoms, corneal location, visual acuity (VA) at admission and at the last follow-up, corneal sample microorganism identified, Gram staining for bacterial keratitis. In brief, for microbiological analysis, corneal scrapings were immediately and systematically performed without anesthesia using a sampling kit for bacteria, fungi, and viruses (e-Swab, Copan Diagnostics, Murrieta, California, USA) or for parasites (Beaver Sclerotome Blade and Knife 375,701, Beaver Visitec International Ltd, Abington, Oxfordshire, UK). Samples were sent to the Microbiology Laboratory (Toulouse University Hospital) for immediate routine analysis.

Outcomes Measures

The primary outcome was the distribution of the aforementioned known risk factors for severe infectious keratitis in our cohort. We then compared our chosen variables of season, sex, Gram staining (bacterial keratitis), age, VA gain, length of hospital stay, and microorganism type according to known risk factor subgroups. We restricted this analysis to known risk factors found within ≥5% of our participants. From here, we delineated the associations between these chosen variables and the known risk factors identified. The secondary outcome was VA gain, calculated as the difference between VA on hospital admission to the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) recorded during the last follow-up. Values for VA given in decimal scale were converted to logarithm of minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). The gain in VA was multiplied by 10 to obtain the number of lines gained.

Statistical Analyses

Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data as means±SD. The incidence of emergency inpatient hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis was estimated during the study period for the population of the Occitanie-Ouest region.11 Toulouse University Hospital is the reference health structure in this region and is home to the only emergency ophthalmology department within the region. The region comprises the geographical administrative divisions of the: Haute-Garonne, Tarn, Aveyron, Tarn-et-Garonne, Haute-Pyrénées, Gers, Lot, and Ariège.

To test for differences in our chosen variables according to known risk factor subgroups, the chi-squared test was used for comparisons of categorical variables (season, sex, Gram staining, microorganism types) and the unpaired Student t-test for continuous variables (age, VA gain, length of stay). We applied a Yates correction to the chi-squared test when the number of participants was too small.

Multivariate logistic regression was performed to assess for associations between each known risk factor (set as the dependent variable/outcome in each model) and our aforementioned chosen variables (set as the predictors). We did not apply any statistical variable selection procedures for our models given that our outcomes (known risk factors identified within ≥5% of the cohort) and our chosen variables (i.e., age, sex) were specified a priori based on both previous literature and clinical relevance. Note that data were checked for multicollinearity (Belsley–Kuh–Welsch technique), heteroskedasticity (White test), and normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) (data not shown). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the online EasyMedStat software (version 3.35.1, Paris, France; www.easymedstat.com).

RESULTS

Admission Patterns and Incidence of Emergency Inpatient Hospitalization for Severe Infectious Keratitis

A total of n=1,344 patients were hospitalized for severe infectious keratitis according to electronic chart discharge classifications. Within these, exclusions were: n=207/1,322(15.7%) participants with noninfectious corneal ulcers; n=69/1,322(5.2%) participants with endophthalmitis without corneal involvement; n=53/1,322 participants for preventative postcorneal transplant treatment. Next, participants were excluded if they had substantial missing data from their charts, meaning the diagnosis of severe infectious keratitis could not be confirmed (i.e., unknown infection location and corneal sampling negativity) and/or the presence/absence of known risk factors could not be confirmed (n=132/1,332; 9.9%). Overall, n=861 participants were included for study. Please refer to the study flowchart in Figure 1.

Study flowchart.

The number of inpatient admissions per year was: n=128/861 (15%) participants in 2016, n=162/861 (19%) in 2017, n=185/861 (21%) in 2018, n=157/861 (18%) in 2019, n=117/861 (14%) in 2020, and n=112/861 in 2021 (13%). Regarding incidence, a 1.012% general population rise was reported for each year between 2016 and 2021 for the Occitanie-Ouest region.11 This allowed us to calculate the following population estimates: 2,936,461 habitants (2016); 2,973,948 habitants (2017); 3,011,435 habitants (2018); 3,048,922 habitants (2019); 3,086,409 habitants (2020); 3,123,896 habitants (2021).11 Based on these population estimates and the admission numbers per year mentioned above, we were able to calculate the change in estimated incidence for emergency inpatient hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis between 2016 and 2021 for the region (Fig. 2). Note the peak in incidence in 2018 (6.1 cases per 100,000 habitants), followed by a progressive decline between 2018 and 2021 (to 3.6 cases per 100,000 habitants). Based on our cohort of 861 participants, the overall median incidence of emergency inpatient hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis per year was estimated at 4.75 cases per 100,000 habitants for the region during the 6-year study period. The breakdown of participant admissions over the six years by season was: n=201/861 (23.3%) participants in winter, n=209/861 (24.3%) participants in spring, n=242/861 (28.1%) participants in summer, and n=209/861 (24.3%) participants in autumn.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The mean age of the cohort was 56±23.8 years (1–101 years) and the sex ratio was equal (50.3% female and 49.7% male). The left side was affected among n=450/861 (52.3%) participants and there was n=5/861 (0.6%) bilateral participants. A total of 10.2% (n=88/861) participants had ≥2 quadrants infected, with 4.5% (n=39/861) participants harboring diffuse infection across all four quadrants. For the whole cohort (n=861), the quadrants infected in order of most frequent were: central for n=312/861 (36.2%) participants, inferior temporal for n=195/861 (22.6%) participants, inferior nasal for n=192/861 (22.3%) participants, superior temporal for n=103/861 (12%) participants, and superior nasal for n=76/861 (8.8%) participants. Infection location was unknown for n=32/861 (3.7%) participants, but infection positivity was checked for and confirmed in the medical charts of these participants. The mean hospitalization stay was 7.6±5 days and median follow-up time was 2.0 months (0–60 months). The mean VA gain at the last follow-up was 0.48 logMAR or 4.8 lines. This was calculated on data from n=656/861 (76.2%) participants and included transplanted participants. In brief, among these 656 participants, n=28 (4.3%) had undergone anterior lamellar keratoplasty, n=21 (3.2%) penetrating keratoplasty, and n=3 (0.46%) endothelial grafting.

Regarding microbiology, corneal scrapes were taken from n=844/861 (98%) participants; that is, samples were not taken from n=17/861 (2%) participants. Among participants sampled, no microorganisms were detected for n=17/844 (2%) participants despite laboratory confirmation of infection (technical laboratory problems resulting in lost results), and no microorganisms were detected for n=330/844 (39%) participants (negativity). Microbiological cultures were Gram-stained for n=518/844 (61%) participants with a total of 616 bacterial detections; n=102/844 (12.2%) participants harbored multiple bacterial isolates. Participants were predominantly infected by Gram-positive cocci (n=222/518; 42.9%) and then Gram-negative bacilli (n=166/518; 32%), followed by Gram-positive bacilli (n=78/518; 15.1%) and then Gram-negative cocci (n=52/518; 10%). Finally, n=98/844 (11.6%) participants tested positive for either fungal, parasitic, or viral infectious keratitis, with four participants harboring multiple nonbacterial isolates. Overall, the rate of microbiological identification was n=616/861 (72%).

Known Risk Factors Identified in Our Cohort

At least one known risk factor was identified in n=828/861 (96.2%) participants, two risk factors in n=49/861 (5.7%) participants, and ≥three risk factors in n=7/861 (0.8%) participants. No known risk factors were identified among n=33/861 (3.8%) participants. The two most common risk factors were external (Table 1). Contact lens wearing had the highest frequency and was identified among approximately one-third of the cohort: n=277/861 (32.2%) participants. This was followed by ocular trauma among n=123/861 (14.3%) participants. Next, with similar but lower frequency, neurotrophic keratitis was identified among n=103/861 (12%) participants, ocular surface diseases among n=95/861 (11%) participants, anterior segment surgery among n=81/861 (9.4%) participants, static eyelids among n=73/861 (8.5%) participants, and blepharitis among n=71/861 (8.2%) participants. We additionally identified with less than 5% frequency: n=38/861 (4.4%) participants with diabetes, n=19/861 (2.2%) participants with endothelial decompensation, n=17/861 (2%) participants with rosacea, n=17/861 (2%) participants with substantial loss of autonomy (e.g., post-stroke patients, dementia), n=10/861 (1.2%) participants with limbal stem-cell deficiency, n=7/861 (0.8%) participants with immunosuppression, n=4/861 (0.5%) participants with confirmed chronic alcoholism, and n=3/861 (0.3%) participants with neglected personal hygiene.

Differences in Demographic and Clinical Variables according to the Presence of Known Risk Factors

Age, Visual Acuity Gain, and Length of Stay

We further explored the known risk factors identified among ≥5% participants of our cohort. In brief, comparing each known risk factor subgroup to the remaining cohort (Table 1), participants with contact lens-related infection (41.2±17.1 years; P=0.001), followed by participants with ocular trauma–related infection (51±19.7 years; P=0.028), were significantly younger than the remaining cohort. On the other hand, participants with neurotrophic keratitis–related (74.4±16; P=0.001), static eyelid disorder–related (73.4±21.8; P=0.001), ocular surface disease–related (69.8±23.6; P=0.001), or blepharitis-related (61.9±25.2; P=0.047) infection were significantly older. Mean hospital stay length was significantly shorter for participants with ocular trauma–related (6.2±4.3; P=0.003) and significantly longer for participants with static eyelid disorder–related (9.2±5.2; P=0.008) infection.

The mean visual acuity gain at the last follow-up for the overall cohort was 0.48±0.71 logMAR (Table 1). Comparing each known risk factor subgroup with the remaining cohort, participants with infection due to contact lens wear had a significantly greater visual acuity gain (0.66±0.78; P=0.002) versus the remaining cohort. Participants with neurotrophic keratitis–related (0.27±0.64; P=0.01), ocular surface disease–related (0.28±0.60; P=0.018), static eyelid disorder–related (0.23±0.52; P=0.038), or anterior segment surgery–related (0.24±0.47; P=0.012) infection showed significantly lower visual acuity gains at the last follow-up.

Season of Admission, Sex, and Bacterial Gram Staining

The presence of the different known risk factors (those identified among ≥5% of participants) according to season of participant admission, sex, and bacterial Gram staining (categorical variables) is summarized in Table 2. First, there were significantly more participants with contact lens-related severe infectious keratitis admitted in summer than participants with other known risk factors. We also found significantly more female contact lens wearers (63.2% v 36.8% for males), but significantly more men with ocular trauma (78.9% v 21.1% for women) or blepharitis (64.8% v 35.2% for women). For participants with bacterial keratitis (n=518/861; 60.2%) (Table 2), contact lens wearers were significantly more prone to gram-negative bacilli infection (59.2%), predominantly by Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Enterobacter species (please refer to Table 3). Participants with ocular trauma (50.9%), having undergone anterior segment surgery (69.7%), or with blepharitis (51.3%) were significantly more prone to gram-positive cocci infection (Table 2). Most gram-positive cocci-infected participants were infected by Staphylococcus species (78.4%), followed by Streptococcus species (19.8%), and this was regardless of risk factor presence (Table 3). Finally, we found a lower rate of corneal sample positivity for participants with ocular trauma (46.7%; P<0.002 (data not shown)).

Variables Associated with the Known Risk Factors Present in Our Cohort

Results from multivariate logistic regression analyses (Table 5) confirm that infection related to contact lens wear was positively associated with female sex (adjOR 2.91 [2.08; 4.10]; P=0.0001) and younger age (adjOR 7.81 [5.50; 11.2]; P=0.0001). Contact lens wear was also positively associated with shorter hospital stays (adjOR 1.47 [1.03; 2.10]; P=0.033) and admission in summer (adjOR 1.97 [1.38; 2.82]; P=0.0002). DISCUSSION

We assessed the associations between previously described risk factors present in at least 5% of our cohort with different demographic, clinical, and microbiological variables among 861 inpatient hospitalizations for severe infectious keratitis in our tertiary emergency ophthalmology department in the South of France between 2016 and 2021. Overall, we show the presence of known risk factors for infectious keratitis among 96.2% participants. The two most common known risk factors identified were contact lens wear (32.2% participants) and ocular trauma (14.3% participants), factors that we demonstrate are significantly associated with our chosen variables of sex, age, and seasonality, as well as harboring specific microbiological profiles.

Regarding incidence, we estimated an overall median incidence of emergency inpatient hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis of 4.75 cases per 100,000 habitants/year for the Occitanie-Ouest region from the beginning of 2016 to the end of 2021. Notably, this estimation declined after 2018 (Fig. 2). The initial decline after 2018 can be partly explained by changes in our practices, including a shift toward the ambulatory management of topical therapies by our internal hospital pharmacy. The continual fall in incidence observed in 2020 and 2021 can be mainly explained by the COVID-19 pandemic and associated lockdowns. A Spanish team has shown similar trends, with a marked reduction in emergency ophthalmology department visits and a reduction in hospital admissions during the same time period.12 Importantly, while we can expect fewer patients to have presented to our emergency department during the COVID-19 pandemic, we can also expect our abrupt switch from inpatient to outpatient treatment during this period to have contributed to fewer hospitalizations. Indeed, outpatient treatment was decided on a case-by-case basis during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The incidence of emergency inpatient hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis reported in our study is much lower than the 34.7 per 100,000 population/year reported for infectious keratitis in the City of Nottingham (UK) in 2021.13 This can be explained by several factors. First, by the fact that we report specifically on severe cases requiring inpatient management only. Next, we can expect some patients living far from our center to have been hospitalized in neighboring regions to the Occitanie-Ouest region, and patients with milder visual symptoms (i.e., noncentral locations) may have sought treatment elsewhere without subsequently needing to attend our emergency department.

We found slightly more participants were hospitalized during summer (28% of participants included) than other seasons (≤24.3% of participants included). A significant positive association was found between admission in summer and anterior segment-related infection (adjOR 1.74; P=0.02). We also found a significantly higher number of infected contact lens wearers admitted in summer, confirmed by the significant positive association between contact lens wear keratitis and summer months (adjOR 1.97; P=0.0002) in our cohort. Gorski et al.14 corroborate this finding, showing a predominance for infectious keratitis in contact lens wearers during the summer months. The summer season is the most popular time of year for outdoor and aquatic activities in France, when contact lenses are more frequently misused and thus increasing the number of infectious keratitis cases.15

Regarding the influence of sex, significantly more women than men were admitted for contact lens wear–related severe infectious keratitis as previously described,8,16 with men being approximately three-times less likely of being admitted for this cause in our study. Conversely, we found that men were 4.5-times more likely (P=0.0001) to be admitted for ocular trauma–related infectious keratitis than women. Indeed, more men have physical professions, and likely more men have “riskier” outdoor leisure activities than women, thus increasing their overall risk of ocular trauma.17

The mean participant age was 56 years, similar to other mean ages found in other studies. For instance, Ting et al.18 report a mean age of 54.4 years in a cohort of 283 patients with bacterial keratitis in England in 2021. We found mean participant age highly varied among the known risk factors examined. For example, contact lens wearers (mean age 41.2±17.1 years)8 and participants suffering from ocular trauma (mean age 51±19.7 years)17 were significantly younger. This was further confirmed by the strong independent positive association between contact lens wear and younger age in our cohort (adjOR 7.81; P=0.0001), with infected contact lens wearers having an almost 8-times greater likelihood of being 56 years or younger compared with noncontact lens wearers. These results are consistent with the fact that contact lens wear is more widespread among younger adults.16 Ocular trauma occurs mainly in an active and thus younger population, confirmed by our positive association between younger age and ocular trauma-related infection (adjOR 1.65; P=0.017).

The central quadrant was predominantly infected in our cohort, accounting for 36.2% of participants, followed by 22.6% for the lower temporal quadrant and 22.3% for the lower nasal quadrant. Diffuse and global involvement accounted for only 4.5% of participants. These findings are similar to those from Bourcier et al.19 in 2003. The authors report 23% of patients with infected central quadrants, 22% with inferior temporal, 24% with inferior nasal, and 4% with diffuse involvement among 300 cases of bacterial keratitis. Note our higher proportion of participants with central infections. Indeed, confirmed infection of the central quadrant was a criterion for inpatient hospitalization in our center, and thus ultimately resulted in study inclusion. Adding to this participant selection bias, we can also expect patients with central infections to be more prone to symptoms of severe visual defects. We can consequently expect these patients to be more likely to directly consult an emergency ophthalmology department in a tertiary hospital setting, also contributing to a higher proportion of central location infections in our study. In line with us, Ting et al.18 report a higher rate for central infection location (38.9%) for bacterial keratitis in a similar tertiary setting in England. We can imagine that at least some patients with milder visual symptoms, and hence noncentral infection locations, could initially seek treatment elsewhere.

Bacterial infection was the most frequent cause of infectious keratitis in our cohort with a positivity rate of 60.2%. Toulouse University Hospital uses sampling kits with indirect laboratory inoculation. Interestingly, bacterial positivity rates between 45.2% and 82.5% have been reported,8,18,19 with Ancèle et al.6 finding a similar 57% positivity rate in our center between 2006 and 2007. We report an overall microbiological identification rate of 72%, similar but slightly lower than the 82.5% rate reported by Bertret et al.8 from a different French center. We found gram-positive bacteria (43% of positive cultures) were most frequently isolated as already reported by Ting et al.3 for infectious keratitis. Gram-negative bacilli infection was strongly associated with contact lens wear (adjOR 4.32; P=0.0001), with the most common bacteria being P. aeruginosa. A history of corneal surgery was found strongly positively associated with gram-positive cocci bacterial keratitis (adjOR 3.45; P=0.0001), especially Staphylococcus spp. and Streptococcus spp. Blepharitis was also found positively associated with gram-positive cocci infection (adjOR 3.06; P=0.005), with Moraxella spp. infection also found among a total of 41% of participants with blepharitis.10 Moraxella spp. are opportunistic commensal bacteria of mucous membranes, particularly of the ocular surface. In pathological conditions, especially blepharitis, these bacteria grow and can cause infectious keratitis.10 Finally, Acanthamoeba spp. were only found among 1.3% of our total cohort and exclusively among contact lens wearers. Contact lens wear–related infectious keratitis was predominant in summer for our cohort; a time period in the South of France where the population is more exposed to outdoor water. Freshwater exposure is a major factor required for the development of parasitic keratitis and can thus explain this link.20

In addition to the selection bias described above for participants with central location infections, we cannot forget the inherent limitations that come with retrospective and monocentric study designs. For instance, a substantial amount of missing data during follow-up meant we were unable to provide reliable findings on infection treatment and resolution, as well as the need for any surgery and outcomes. In addition, we can only for now claim for adjusted associations between previously known risk factors and our chosen variables in our cohort, without being able to determine any causal effects across different populations. To do so would require a challenging multicentric design, with matched participants harboring the known risk factors, but both with and without severe infectious keratitis (i.e., CL wearers with and without infection). On the other hand, we report on one of the largest cohorts of patients to date comprising all causes of infectious keratitis, without focusing only on bacterial keratitis. Finally, our incidence estimation for emergency hospitalization for severe infectious keratitis in the Occitanie-Ouest region, South-of-France, was based on inpatient admissions for treatment of severe infectious keratitis. A better representation of this real-world incidence of severe infectious keratitis could base estimates on residents of the region only with laboratory-confirmed infection in a prospective setting. In addition, collecting accurate data on patient residency in future studies would also allow for determination of the effects of distance from our center on initial presentation and visual outcomes.21

In conclusion, contact lens wear and ocular trauma emerged as the most commonly identified known risk factors in our cohort, both found strongly associated with younger age but opposite sexes. Our findings underscore the importance of targeted prevention strategies, particularly for both young contact lens wearers and patients undergoing anterior segment surgery during the summer months, as well as for younger men with ocular trauma. The continuous monitoring of our incidence estimation could help in improving the care pathway for our region, for instance by supporting resource planning through the adaptation of inpatient bed numbers.
