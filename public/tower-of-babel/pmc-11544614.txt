Electrochemical Formation of C2+ Products Steered by Bridge-Bonded *CO Confined by *OH Domains
Authors: Haibin Ma, Enric Ibáñez-Alé, Futian You, Núria López, Boon Siang Yeo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring the electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (eCO2RR) on copper catalysts, linear-bonded CO (*COL) is commonly regarded as the key intermediate for the CO-CO coupling step, which leads to the formation of multicarbon products. In this work, we unveil the significant role of bridge-bonded *CO (*COB) as an active species. By combining in situ Raman spectroscopy, gas and liquid chromatography, and density functional theory (DFT) simulations, we show that adsorbed *OH domains displace *COL to *COB. The electroreduction of a 12CO+13CO2 cofeed demonstrates that *COB distinctly favors the production of acetate and 1-propanol, while *COL favors ethylene and ethanol formation. This work enhances our understanding of the mechanistic intricacies of eCO(2)RR and suggests new directions for designing operational conditions by modifying the competitive adsorption of surface species, thereby steering the reaction toward specific multicarbon products.

Introduction

The electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (eCO2RR) to multicarbon products on copper-based catalysts has emerged as a promising approach for recycling carbon and thus reducing our reliance on fossil fuels.1 This process involves primarily the electroconversion of CO2 into adsorbed *CO.2 The *CO intermediate then participates in CO-CO coupling, which is generally accepted to be the rate-determining step toward the formation of more complex products such as ethylene, acetate, 1-propanol, etc. The adsorption configuration of *CO and the presence of other coadsorbates can thus significantly impact the efficiency of eCO2RR, since the rate for subsequent reactions can be altered by the electronic and geometric interactions between the *CO and the catalytic environment.

CO produced from eCO2RR mainly binds on Cu surfaces in a linear configuration (*COL), exhibiting a broad C≡O stretching band (νC≡O) centered between 2000 and 2100 cm–1.3−5 Some works have deconvoluted this broad band and assigned it to *COL bonded on Cu sites with different coordination numbers.4,5 Weckhuysen and co-workers further proposed that *COL with a high νC≡O frequency (∼2090 cm–1) tends to desorb directly, while *COL with a low frequency (∼2060 cm–1) tends to undergo coupling, forming C2+ products like ethylene.6

While many works have reported that the *COL species is active for CO-CO coupling, resulting in *COL frequently serving as the basis for computational models, others have shown that *CO can adopt a bridge adsorption configuration (*COB) under specific conditions during eCO2RR.7−9 For instance, both *COL and *COB species were identified during eCO2RR on an amine-terminal, dendrimer-functionalized Cu catalyst, on which acetate was formed with a high Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 47%.10 Acetate and propanol selectivity has also been promoted on Cu by controlling CO partial pressure during eCO2RR, where *COB was observed.11

*COB can be further detected when CO is directly supplied as a feedstock.12 An in situ infrared spectroscopy study has shown that during eCORR, *COB (νC≡O =∼ 1865 cm–1) became the dominant species for prolonged periods from −0.1 to –1.5 VSCE (SCE: saturated calomel electrode).13 Liu et al. have suggested that directly supplied CO can reconstruct the Cu surface, which favors the formation of *COB.14 Some works have proposed that the *COB are inactive, as their signal intensities do not appear to decrease under negative potentials.15,16 Xu and co-workers have further observed that during eCORR, when the electrolyte was changed from KHCO3 to K2CO3, and then to KOH, the CO adsorption configuration completely shifted from linear to bridge.17,18 Here, we note that even when the aforementioned Cu catalyst was completely covered by supposedly inactive *COB, it exhibited high C2 selectivity. Specifically, a 9% FE for C2+ products was found on this *COB-covered surface. In comparison, the FE for C2+ products was only 1.6% at the same potential (vs standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)) when the surface was covered by *COL.18

While numerous spectroscopic studies have investigated the nature of *CO on Cu surfaces and its impact on reactivity, these are based on Cu disks placed in traditional H-cells, which are known for their low CO2 mass transfer.19,20 Consequently, the results obtained may not be directly relatable to reactivity data from flow cells, which are increasingly considered for industrial-scale eCO2RR. In this work, we employ a combination of in situ Raman spectroscopy, product analyses, and deuterium- and 13C- isotope labeling experiments on Cu-based gas diffusion electrodes (GDE) mounted in H-cells and flow cells. Our approach allows us to unveil the factors influencing the binding configuration of *CO species and, consequently, their reactivity to specific C2+ products during eCO2RR. The presence of Cu-OH species was found to correlate with the shifting of *COL to *COB, and with the changes in the product selectivity from ethylene and ethanol to acetate and 1-propanol. Density functional theory (DFT) simulations elucidate how the arrangement of *OH at high coverage forms stripe-structures that break regular *COL domains, thereby generating local *COB domains with a lower CO-CO coupling barrier, which are more likely to produce acetate and 1-propanol. This work enhances our understanding of the features affecting the *CO adsorption configuration and its reactivity, paving the way for a more rational design of Cu catalysts and optimization of the conditions for eCO2RR.

Results and Discussion

Comparison of CO2R and COR in Flow Cells

We begin with in situ Raman spectroscopic- and product-analyses of eCO2RR and eCORR (Figures S1–S3). Constant-potential electrolysis was conducted on a Cu GDE in 1 M KOH electrolyte using a homemade Raman flow cell. The same type of Cu GDE was used to collect data throughout this work so that the results could be better compared. All Raman spectra were normalized to the low-frequency background at 100 cm–1 (from the silica optics) to compensate for differences in signal intensities due to varying laser focus.21 The results for eCO2RR are shown in Figure 1a. At the open circuit potential (OCP), bands at 516 and 614 cm–1 were observed. These features can be assigned to a native overlayer of Cu2O (Table S1).22 These bands diminished rapidly upon electrode polarization from −0.6 to −1.4 VSHE. All potentials cited hereafter are referenced to the SHE since the formation of C2+ products is independent of pH.23−26 At −0.9 VSHE, a band at ∼525 cm–1 appeared. This signal is typically attributed to Cu-OH species.21,27,28 At −1.2 VSHE, two bands centered at 2011 and 2086 cm–1 emerged, which can be attributed to the *COL bonded on Cu surface with different coordination numbers, in line with previous reports.3,4 Two bands at 278 and 361 cm–1, corresponding respectively to the frustrated rotation and stretching of Cu-*COL, also become apparent.29 Additionally, a band at 1065 cm–1 can be observed. This signal is due to the formation of CO32– by the reaction of CO2 with OH– in the electrolyte.27,30

Spectra were collected at potential intervals of 0.1 V. (c) Faradaic efficiencies of major eCO2RR and eCORR products and (d) *CO-related product selectivity (CPS) of acetate and 1-propanol on a Cu GDE at −1.4 VSHE. In contrast, when CO was utilized as feedstock, Cu-OH species appeared immediately upon applying an electrode potential of −0.6 VSHE, as indicated by its signal at 530 cm–1 (Figure 1b).21,27,28 As the bias changed from −0.6 to −1.4 VSHE, the Cu-OH species evolved to give three distinct bands at 399, 540, and 626 cm–1 (at −1.4 VSHE) with the band at 540 cm–1 exhibiting the strongest intensity. H/D isotopic substitution experiments showed that these bands shifted to 389, 533, and 617 cm–1 (at –1.4 VSHE), further indicating that they belong to Cu-OH (Figure S4). The Cu-OH band exhibited a lower intensity during eCO2RR, as compared to eCORR. This is likely due to the acidic CO2 consuming the OH– in the electrolyte to form carbonate and H+, which lowers the local pH and suppresses the formation of Cu-OH.18,31 CO also adsorbed on the Cu surface immediately upon application of an electrode potential. However, unlike eCO2RR which yielded *COL, directly supplied CO adsorbs differently. At a relatively positive potential of −0.6 VSHE, CO adsorbed as both a linear (νC≡O = 2072 cm–1) and bridge (νC≡O = 1901 cm–1) species. The signals of these two *CO species decrease after the potential shifts to −0.7 VSHE. This might result from a disordered layer of Cu-OH formed at this potential, which blocks the CO adsorption sites (Figure S5). As the potential shifts from −0.8 to −1.4 VSHE, the signal from *COL at 2072 cm–1 disappeared, leaving behind *COB with a νC≡O band of 1826–1901 cm–1. It is noteworthy that the shift from *COL to *COB follows the increasing intensities of the three bands associated to Cu-OH. Herzog et al. have recently shown using pulse CO2 electrolysis that the coverage of this *OH layer can be tuned for enhancing ethanol production by changing the anodic and cathodic pulse lengths.28 Our findings suggest that using CO as the feedstock leads to a significantly higher *OH coverage and a change in CO adsorption configurations compared to what was observed in Herzog’s work. A consequent change in the eCORR reactivity can thus be anticipated.

To elucidate the impact of the CO adsorption configuration on the reaction selectivity, product analysis was conducted in a flow cell containing a Cu GDE in 1 M KOH electrolyte (Figure S2). A potential of –1.4 VSHE was selected, as it favors the formation of C2+ products.23 The FEs of the major products are presented in Figure 1c (Tables S2-S3). Significant differences are found for acetate and 1-propanol selectivities. Specifically, the FE for acetate via eCO2RR is 1.4%, while it is 10.4% via eCORR. Similarly, the FE of 1-propanol increases from 6.2 to 11.2% when the feedstock was switched from CO2 to CO.

To better compare the differences in product selectivity formed by eCO2RR and eCORR, we introduce “*CO-related product selectivity” (CPS) as a performance metric (Supporting Note 1). We define CPS as the ratio of the formation rate of a given product to that of all of the eCO2RR products, all of which involve *CO as an intermediate. CPS can be used to evaluate products such as ethylene, ethanol, acetate, and 1-propanol.32 Formate is not included, as it is typically formed via the HCOO* species.33 Upon switching from CO2 to CO feedstock, the CPS for acetate increased from 3.4 to 25.1%, and for 1-propanol, from 6.7 to 8.9% (Figure 1d). Conversely, the CPS for other major products such as ethylene and ethanol declined from 59.0 to 44.5% and from 24.7 to 17.4%, respectively, when switching from CO2 to CO (Figure S6). The difference between eCO2RR and eCORR has been previously studied.34−36 These results indicate that compared to eCO2RR, direct-supplied CO tends to produce more acetate and 1-propanol, which aligns with our current findings. While previous studies generally attribute these differences to variations in pH and CO coverage, our results suggest that the adsorption configuration of CO may also play a role. Specifically, *COB appears to favor the production of acetate and 1-propanol, whereas *COL seems to favor the formation of ethylene and ethanol.

Comparison of COR Using H-cell and Flow cell

We have previously shown that eCORR on Cu catalysts yielded more acetate in flow cells than in H-cells and have attributed this difference to a higher *CO coverage which favors the reduction of the *CHCO to CH2CO(g), leading to acetate.8 Here, we hypothesize that the type of adsorbed *CO formed from eCORR and the presence of coadsorbed *OH might also influence acetate selectivity. We therefore investigated the surface species formed on Cu GDE in an H-cell and flow cell. 0.5 M K2SO4 electrolyte was chosen in lieu of a bicarbonate electrolyte, as we want to completely eliminate the presence of CO2.

In the H-cell setup, where CO bubbles approach the electrode from the electrolyte, *COL, characterized by its νC≡O band at ∼2064 cm–1 was observed from −0.6 to −1.1 VSHE (Figure 2a). Shifting the potential to −1.2 VSHE led to the attenuation of the *COL signal, indicating a lower *COL coverage, likely due to a constrained CO mass transfer within the H-cell. A weak Cu-OH band at ∼532 cm–1 was also measured from −1.0 to −1.4 VSHE, suggesting a low *OH coverage. In contrast, when eCORR was performed in a flow cell (where CO flows to the Cu catalyst from the microporous layer side of the carbon paper), a more pronounced Cu-OH band emerged at −0.7 VSHE (Figure 2b). This signal also intensified with a negative shift of the potentials. While the *COL signal at 2064 cm–1 was observed from −0.6 to −1.0 VSHE, it vanished from −1.1 to −1.4 VSHE, leaving *COB at 1842–1865 cm–1.

Spectra were acquired at potential intervals of 0.1 V. (c) Faradaic efficiency of major eCORR products (insert: enlarged part of the FEs in H-cell) and (d) CPS of acetate and 1-propanol formed on a Cu GDE in H-cell and flow cell at −1.4 VSHE in 0.5 M K2SO4 electrolyte.

The FEs and CPS of major eCORR products formed at –1.4 VSHE are presented in Figure 2c,d (Tables S4 and S5; Figures S2 and S3). Given the poor CO mass transfer in the H-cell, hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) expectedly dominates with the FE of H2 exceeding 90%. Switching from H-cell to flow cell increased the CPS for acetate (5.5 to 10.4%) and 1-propanol (5.5 to 8.5%), while the CPS for ethylene and ethanol slightly declined (49.9 to 49.1% and 29.0 to 27.1%, respectively) (Figure S7).

The different current densities exhibited during constant potential electrolysis in the H-cell (∼−14 mA/cm2) and flow cell (∼−49 mA/cm2) might cause a difference in the local pH at the Cu surface, therefore changing the product distribution. To rule out the effect of varying currents and local pH on the eCORR product distribution, we conducted constant current electrolysis in these two types of cells. Both sets of results are consistent with each other: specifically, using constant current electrolysis at −20 mA/cm2, eCORR in an H-cell still presents lower CPS values of acetate (2.5% to 19.1%) and 1-propanol (2.0% to 11.5%) and higher CPS values of ethylene (52.8% to 47.4%) and ethanol (20.3% to 15.7%), as compared to that in a flow cell (Figure S8). These findings imply a preference to produce acetate and 1-propanol when eCORR was performed within a flow cell configuration with *COB adsorbed on the Cu surface. In contrast, *COL formed in the H-cell setup favors the generation of ethylene and ethanol.

Comparing COR at Different pH Values

The pH of the electrolyte highly impacts eCORR performances.25,26 To explore how pH affects CO adsorption configurations on Cu, Raman spectroscopy was conducted on the Cu GDE in 1 M KH2PO4 (adjust by H3PO4 to pH 2) and in 1 M KOH (pH 14) electrolytes, each within a flow cell (Figure S1).

At pH 2, due to the removal of the oxide layer in the acidic electrolyte, *COL can be observed at 2104 cm–1 at the OCP. Once the Cu GDE was polarized, bands at 386, 526, and 608 cm–1 attributable to Cu-OH species were detected (Figure 3a). Three signals at 884, 988, and 1078 cm–1 were also observed. The bands at 884 and 1078 cm–1 can be ascribed to H2PO4– ions, while that at 988 cm–1 corresponds to HPO42– ions (Table S1).37 When the electrode was polarized from −0.6 VSHE to −1.4 VSHE, two bands at 2065 and 1874 cm–1 were observed. These belong respectively to *COL and *COB, with *COL being more dominant. In contrast, at pH 14, *COB was the only *CO species detected from −0.8 to −1.4 VSHE, with its vibrational frequency shifting with potentials from 1884 to 1826 cm–1 (Figure 3b).

Spectra were collected at potential intervals of 0.1 V. (c) Faradaic efficiency of major eCORR products (insert: enlarged part of FE at pH 2) and (d) CPS of acetate and 1-propanol formed on a Cu GDE mounted in a flow cell at −1.4 VSHE in 1 M KH2PO4 + H3PO4 and 1 M KOH electrolytes. Product analysis reveals that at −1.4 VSHE, the formation of eCORR products dominates in KOH electrolyte, while H2 formation dominates in phosphate electrolyte (Figure 3c and S2; Tables S3 and S6). A notable increase in the CPS of acetate from 2.8% to 25.1% and 1-propanol, from 1.2% to 8.9%, upon changing the electrolyte from phosphate (pH 2) to KOH (pH 14), was found (Figure 3d). Interestingly, despite the adsorption of *CO being mainly in a linear mode in the phosphate electrolyte, the CPS for ethylene and ethanol were 4.9% and 4.1%, respectively, lower than those in KOH (43.5% for ethylene and 16.8% for ethanol). We attribute this reduction in CPS to an increase of H+ concentration in phosphate electrolyte, which facilitates methane generation.23 This proposition is supported by a large increase in methane CPS from 0.3% to 59.8% when the electrolyte was switched from alkaline KOH (pH 14) to acidic phosphate (pH 2) (Figure S9).

A change in CO adsorption was also observed when we switched from phosphate to sulfate electrolyte while maintaining the same bulk pH of 2. In the sulfate electrolyte, a *COL signal at 2045–2150 cm–1 was detected at OCP (Figure 3e). As the electrode potential became more negative, *COB predominantly adsorbed onto the Cu surface. We conjectured that this shift in CO adsorption configuration may be due to the local pH turning more alkaline in sulfate electrolyte during the eCORR.18 This is because H2SO4/K2SO4 electrolyte is not as good a buffer as H3PO4/KH2PO4.38,39 Product analysis further reveals an increase in the FEs of eCORR products formed in the sulfate electrolyte, as compared to in the phosphate electrolyte (Table S7 and Figure S10). There is also an enhancement in CPS for acetate and 1-propanol, which at 13.4 and 8.9% respectively in the sulfate environment, marks a significant increase from the 2.8 and 1.2% observed in the phosphate electrolyte (Figure 3d and Figure S11).

It is noteworthy that during our Raman experiment in sulfate electrolyte, the Cu-OH formed during the electrolysis persisted even after the electrolysis had ended (blue spectrum, Figure 3f). Intriguingly, *COB at 1930 cm–1 could also be detected, evidence of an alteration in the stable *CO adsorption configuration by *OH species on the Cu surface. Collectively, these observations indicate that the presence of Cu-OH species shifts the binding of *CO from linear to bridged (Figure 3e,f). Upon reapplication of the potential at −1.4 VSHE, a pronounced decrease in the intensity of *COB was observed, indicating its electroreduction (green-colored spectrum, Figure 3f). These findings not only highlight the importance of Cu surface characteristics in determining CO adsorption configurations but also affirm the involvement of *COB as active eCORR intermediates.

Electrochemical Reduction of CO+CO2 Feeds

The electrochemical reduction of a CO+CO2 feed has been found effective for boosting the production rate of C2+ products.40−43 Gao et al. suggested that eCORR and eCO2RR could have taken place on different Cu sites.42 To understand the CO adsorption configuration in a flow cell during the electrolysis of a CO+CO2 feed, Raman spectroscopy was performed on a Cu GDE mounted in a flow cell with a 1 M KOH electrolyte. The Raman spectrum of the electrode biased at −1.4 VSHE was first recorded, with pure CO2 being supplied from the gas chamber. A mixture of CO+CO2 gas at a molar ratio of 1:3 then replaced the CO2 feed. When pure CO2 was supplied, the generated CO intermediate adsorbed on the Cu surface linearly, as indicated by its νC≡O signal at 2093 cm–1 (Figure 4a). A weak band at 530 cm–1 that can be attributed to Cu-OH was also observed. When the gas feed was switched to a CO2+CO mixture, the intensity of the Cu-OH band at 530–538 cm–1 increased. Two additional bands of Cu-OH at 393 and 623 cm–1 also became discernible over time. Concurrently, the 2093 cm–1 band of *COL attenuates, while a *COB band at 1821 cm–1 emerges. The attenuation of the *COL signal might result from the evolution of Cu-OH.

To determine the proportion of *COB originating from directly supplied CO versus CO produced from CO2, 13CO2 and 12CO was utilized. A constant potential of −1.4 VSHE was applied. When pure 13CO2 was used, the *13COL generated from 13CO2 displayed a distinct band at 2030 cm–1, red-shifted from 2093 cm–1 of *12COL due to mass difference between the isotopes (Figure 4b). Upon introduction of 12CO into the 13CO2 at a molar ratio of 1:6, a decline in the intensity of the *13COL band was observed, in comparison to the pure e13CO2RR experiment. A further increase in the concentration of 12CO resulted in the complete disappearance of *13COL signals. *12COB was detected instead, as signified by its signal at 1821 cm–1. Strikingly, the *12COB band maintained the same vibration frequency at 1821 cm–1, even with a 12CO:13CO2 molar ratio of 1:3, indicating that all *COB was derived exclusively from 12CO.

The spectra measured during the co-electrolysis of N2 and CO at −1.4 VSHE were also compared (Figures S12 and S13). When pure N2 was utilized, a Cu-OH band was present at 533 cm–1, suggesting that Cu-OH was formed during HER. The introduction of a small proportion of CO, at a CO:N2 molar ratio of 1:6 resulted in a significant enhancement of the Cu-OH band, as compared to that in pure N2. Spectra measured at a constant applied current of –50 mA/cm2 in CO- and N2-saturated 1 M KOH electrolyte (thereby keeping the local pH similar) also showed that directly supplied CO better facilitates the formation of Cu-OH (Figure S14). Meanwhile, *COB was adsorbed on the Cu surface during the co-electrolysis of CO and N2 at ratios from 1:6 to 1:1, as evident by its signal at 1821 cm–1 (Figure S12). This observation further indicates that the *COL measured during CO+CO2 electrolysis originates from CO2, while *COB is derived from directly supplied CO.

To elucidate how different CO adsorption configurations and formation of Cu-OH impact product selectivity, we electrolyzed a 12CO+13CO2 mixture on a Cu GDE at −1.4 VSHE in a flow cell and analyzed the liquid products using 1H NMR spectroscopy. The presence of 13C in the products resulted in NMR spectral splitting distinct from that of standard 12C acetate and ethanol (Figure 4c and Figure S15). This isotopic splitting allows us to quantify acetate and ethanol derived from 13CO2 and 12CO (Figure 4d and Table S8). We found a smaller presence of 13C in acetate compared to ethanol, suggesting that acetate primarily originates from directly supplied 12CO, whereas ethanol is largely derived from 13CO produced via e13CO2RR. Taking the electrolysis of a 12CO:13CO2 mixture with a molar ratio of 1:1 as an example, the coupling of directly fed 12CO contributes to 48% of the total acetate yield. In contrast, only 20% of acetate originates from the coupling of 13CO-13CO produced from 13CO2. Conversely, only 21% of ethanol comes from the coupling of directly fed 12CO, whereas a substantial 40% of ethanol is generated from 13CO formed from 13CO2.

The composition of 1-propanol with different amounts of 13C makes peak assignment and quantification challenging. Consequently, we could only give an estimate by integrating the broad peaks of 13C and 12C of 1-propanol (Figure S16, Table S9). The findings reveal that, similar to acetate, the proportion of 13C in 1-propanol is smaller (52.8%) compared to that of ethanol (60.8%) when cofeeding 12CO:13CO2 with a molar ratio of 1:1. This suggests that compared to *COL, *COB converts more easily to 1-propanol.

Modeling the Cu Surface with Coadsorbed *OH and *CO

To assign the Raman bands, we simulate the linear, bridge, and hollow adsorption of *OH on the Cu(100) and Cu(111) facets (Supporting Note 2). The experimentally detected band at 525–545 cm–1 can be assigned to the Cu-OH bending mode (ν1) of both bridged *OHB on Cu(100) and hollow *OHH on Cu(111) (Figure S17), with the former species being slightly more stable (Table S10). The band at 608–640 cm–1 also corresponds to the ν1 mode for *OHH, albeit it is adsorbed on Cu(100). The band at 386–406 cm–1 can be assigned to the Cu-OH stretching mode (ν2) of *OHB on either Cu(100) or Cu(111). Overall, we consider *OHB on Cu(100) to be the dominant species.

To further understand how *OH at a higher coverage arranges on surfaces, we computed the differential Gibbs free energies (dΔG*OH) of a second *OHB, and we found that their coadsorption is highly stabilized due to hydrogen bonding (Figure S18). Differential adsorption energies of subsequent *OH species (third and fourth) show that at even higher coverages, *OH is adsorbed on Cu(100) with enhanced stability in linear stripes; however, no significant stabilization effect could be observed for *OH on Cu(111) (Figures S18b and S19). Considering the lateral repulsion between two *OH at adjacent bridge sites sharing one single Cu atom (Structure 1 in Figure S18c, ΔΔGads differential = 0.53 eV with respect to the adsorption energy of a single *OHB), the bridge sites near the *OH stripes are likely to be unoccupied. Therefore, we build two periodic models with one and two empty spaces between stripes (θOH = 1/2 and 1/3 ML, respectively) (Figure 5a). To assess how the presence of these *OH stripes affects *CO adsorption, we computed the adsorption energies of *COL and *COB species on models with zero, medium, and high *OH coverages (θOH = 0, 1/3, and 1/2 ML; Figure 5b). As *OH hinders *CO adsorption at distances smaller than 3.0 Å (Figure S20a), in the narrow space between stripes at θOH = 1/2 ML, *COL adsorption sites are blocked and only *COB adsorption is feasible (ΔGads = −0.44 eV). Therefore, *COL domains are broken at high *OH coverages, thus confining CO adsorption to only bridge sites, in line with the *COL shift to *COB observed in our experiments (Figure 3f). Note that this shift is not expected for *CO originating from the eCO2RR, as adsorbed CO2 inhibits *OH adsorption on its vicinities, thus limiting the formation of *OH stripes (Figure S21).

To further clarify how *CO enhances *OH adsorption, we conducted DFT simulations at different *COL and *COB coverages (0–0.5 ML) on Cu(100). Bader charge analysis indicates a positive polarization of these surfaces as the amount of *CO increases (Figure S22), which suggests *CO will promote the adsorption of an anion such as OH– due to ionic interactions.44 While a previous work has suggested that the effect of CO in promoting Cu-OH formation is due primarily to the decomposition of surface water facilitated by eCORR, our results indicate that surface polarization also plays an important role in Cu-OH formation.21

We computed the coadsorption energies of *COL + *COL, *COB + *COB, and *COL + *COB, which showed no repulsion at distances greater than 3.0 Å. This indicates that on these confined domains *COL or *COB can stably coadsorb close enough to each other to facilitate further CO-CO coupling (Figure S20b). The computed and scaled νC≡O frequencies for *COL and *COB on Cu(100) also agree with detected Raman bands (2027 and 1884 cm–1 for *COL and *COB, respectively; Table S11).

DFT Simulation of CO-CO Coupling to C2+ Products

We computed the CO-CO coupling energy barriers for *COB and *COL domains in θOH = 0, 1/3, and 1/2 ML, using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method (Figures 5c and Figures S23–S25). For a Cu(100) surface without *OH, the lowest energy barriers were observed for coupling reactions involving one or two *COL (COL-COL and COL-COB), in line with previous results.29 At θOH = 1/3 ML, the COB-COB coupling became favored. For the confined *COB domains at θOH = 1/2 ML, we found the lowest CO-CO coupling energy barrier of Ea = 0.77 eV and a more stable final state configuration of *COCO than *OCCO (Figures S24 and S25). In contrast, the *OCCO intermediate was more stable than *COCO on surfaces with lower *OH coverage (θOH = 0 and 1/3 ML). We note that these findings can explain the observed decrease in FE of ethanol and ethylene when using CO as a feedstock compared to CO2 (Figure 1c), as *COB on confined domains will not evolve easily to the *OCCO intermediate which leads to ethylene and ethanol.32

To further elucidate how changes in *CO adsorption configurations affect the reactivity, we computed the adsorption energies of key intermediates leading to acetate, ethylene/ethanol, and 1-propanol on Cu(100) surfaces with the three *OH coverages θOH = 0, 1/3, and 1/2 ML (Figure 5d). From the common intermediate *CHCO, the energies of a further hydrogenation step to *COCH2 or *OCHCH serve as indicators of acetate and ethylene/ethanol selectivity, respectively.32 The *OCHCH intermediate is favored over *COCH2 for θOH = 0 and 1/3 ML. However, *OCHCH becomes less stable at θOH = 1/2 ML, compared to θOH = 0 and 1/3 ML (ΔΔG = 0.46 and 0.51 eV). *COCH2, instead, becomes the dominant species, thus shifting selectivity toward acetate (Figure 5e). The energy profiles for these competing reaction pathways for acetate and ethanol indicate that both are thermodynamically downhill at U = −1.4 VSHE, with acetate becoming more favored at θOH = 1/2 ML (Figure S26).

Interestingly, both *COCH2 (acetate pathway) and *OCHCH (ethanol pathway) intermediates can further couple with a neighboring *CO (generating *COCHCOH and *OCHCHCOH, respectively), leading to 1-propanol. For all three studied θOH coverages, we found that *COCHCOH is more stable than *OCHCHCOH, which indicates that the acetate pathway is more likely to contribute to 1-propanol formation. While both *COCHCOH and *OCHCHCOH are not thermodynamically favored at θOH of 0 and 1/3 ML, the coupling reaction to *COCHCOH becomes exergonic (ΔG = −0.31 eV) at θOH = 1/2 ML (Figure 5f). Collectively, this means that as the *OH coverage increases to 1/2 ML, active *COB domains are generated, favoring both acetate and 1-propanol production (Scheme 1), which aligns well with our experimental observations of reactivity (Figures 1d, 2d, and 3d).

We also consider a newly reported mechanism for acetate production, which states that *CO first couples with *OH to form *COOH, which then further couples with a second *CO to give *COCOOH.45 The first coupling with *OHB showed lower barriers for *COB (Ea = 0.52 eV for θOH = 0 ML, and Ea = 0.61 eV for θOH = 1/2 ML) than for *COL (Ea = 0.72–0.82 eV); the second CO-COOH coupling was more favored in θOH = 1/2 ML (Ea = 0.83 eV) confined domains compared to that in θOH = 0 ML (Ea = 1.18 eV). The complete reaction pathway is energetically favorable for both θOH = 0 and 1/2 ML (Figure S27), indicating that acetate can alternatively be formed from this path.

Conclusion

We have revealed the critical role of *COB as an active intermediate in the eCO2RR on Cu catalysts. Combining in situ Raman spectroscopy and product analysis, supported by isotope-labeled 12CO+13CO2 co-electrolysis experiments, we demonstrate that *COB distinctly reacts to give acetate and 1-propanol, while *COL favors ethylene and ethanol formation. DFT simulations further unveiled that *CO promotes the formation of Cu-OH, and high *OH coverages confine CO adsorption to a bridge configuration. Within these confined domains, the activation energy for COB-COB coupling significantly decreases, as compared to on surfaces without *OH. This confinement further facilitates the formation of *COCH2 from the CO-CO dimer and stabilizes the *COCHCOH intermediate, thus respectively favoring the production of acetate and 1-propanol. These insights not only advance our understanding of the mechanistic pathways occurring during eCO2RR but also highlight the potential of optimizing reaction conditions or catalysts to steer the formation of specific multicarbon products.

Experimental Section

Electrochemical Deposited Cu GDE Preparation and Characterization

An ∼200 nm thick Cu (99.99%, Latech Scientific Supply) film was sputtered (Denton Instrument; sputtering radio frequency power of 100 W for 1000 s) onto a carbon gas diffusion layer substrate (GDL, Sigracet 28 BC). To ensure good reproducibility of the results and improve the strength of the Raman signal, another Cu layer was then electrochemically deposited on the GDL at a constant current density of −200 mA/cm2 for 20 s in 0.4 M CuSO4 (98.5%, GCE) + 0.2 M H2SO4 (95%, VWR) solution. After Cu deposition, the electrode surface was protected by a thin layer of ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ, Sartorius) and quickly transferred to the cell for electrochemical measurements and Raman spectroscopy. The morphologies of the Cu GDE samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM 6710 F; 1.91 × 10–5 Pa; accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV; probe current of 8 pA; working distance of 7.9 mm) (Figure S28).

Electrochemical Measurements

Electrochemical measurements were conducted using either a flow-cell or a H-cell setup (Figures S2 and S3). The cathodic and anodic compartments of the cell were separated by an anion exchange membrane (Selemion AMV, AGC, Asahi Glass) when the electrolyte was alkaline and neutral or a cation exchange membrane (Nafion 117, DuPont) when the electrolyte was acidic. Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl, Pine) was employed as the reference electrode. When testing in alkaline electrolyte, Ni foam (99.0%, 120 ppi, 1.0 mm thickness, Xiamen Lith Machine Limited) was used as the counter electrode. When testing in acidic and neutral electrolytes, an IrO2/Ti foam counter electrode was used.

A potentiostat (Gamry reference 600) was used to control and measure the electrochemical activity. The current interrupt mode was used to compensate for the iR drop during constant potential electrolysis. CO/CO2 electrolysis was conducted in 1 M KOH (pH = 14, 99.999%, Meryer Chemical Technology), 0.5 M K2SO4 (pH = 7, 99.999%, Meryer Chemical Technology), or 1 M KH2PO4+H3PO4 (pH = 2, 99.999%, Meryer Chemical Technology) electrolyte. CO (99.97%, Linde)/CO2 (99.999%, Linde) was introduced to the flow cell at a flow rate of 10 mL min–1 using a mass flow controller (Alicat Scientific). The flow rates of the inlet and outlet gases were monitored to avoid any gas leak. The electrolysis period was 43 min to ensure at least 4 injections of gas products into the GC. All potentials in this work are referenced to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) using the average potential of the Ag/AgCl electrode (EAg/AgCl)

For eCORR H-cell testing, a homemade H-cell (3 mL catholyte) was used for the electrochemical measurements and product analysis (Figures S3 and S29). An anion membrane (Selemion AMV, AGC Asahi Glass) was used to separate the anolyte and catholyte. CO was bubbled into the H-cell at a flow rate of 5 mL min–1 (Alicat Scientific).

For the 13C experiment, the same protocol as that used for the 12C experiment was applied. The ratio of 13CO2 (13C, 99%, Cambridge Isotope Lab) and 12CO (99.97%, Linde) was controlled by a mass flow controller (Alicat Scientific).

Product Analysis

Gaseous products formed during eCORR and eCO2RR were analyzed using an online gas chromatograph (Shimadzu GC2014) equipped with one thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and two flame ionization detectors (FIDs). One of the FIDs has a methanizer. Four GC injections of gas samples (intervals of 810 s between consecutive injections) were made. To ensure that the reported data are from a system under equilibrium, only measurements from the second to fourth injections were used for analyses. The product concentrations were stable between these injections. Each set of data was collected from measurements made on at least three independently prepared samples. The liquid products were analyzed by a headspace gas chromatograph (Headspace GC, Agilent 7697A) with a FID (detection limit <0.2 μM). Acetate and formate were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent-1260 Infinity Series; Aminex HPX-87H column; variable wavelength detector; and 0.5 mM H2SO4 mobile phase).

GC chromatograms of standards and a representative eCO2RR sample are shown in Figure S30. The liquid-phase product analyses from a Raman electrochemical cell (Figure S1) and a typical flow cell (Figure S2) were also compared. While small differences in Faradaic efficiencies of the products were observed (Figure S31a), the overall product distribution in the liquid phase remained consistent (Figure S31b). This suggests that the results from the Raman cell are representative of those in a typical flow cell.

For 13C measurements, 1H NMR (Bruker Avance DRX500(2D)) were used to quantify the product ratio of 13C and 12C in CH3COOH and CH3CH2OH. Typically, 520 μL of the catholyte was mixed with 20 μL of an internal standard consisting of 25 mM phenol (99.6%, VWR) and 5 mM dimethyl sulfoxide (99.9%, Quality Reagent Chemical). 60 μL of D2O (99.9% deuterium, Merck Millipore) was then added into 0.5 mL of this mixture. Solvent suppression protocol was used to decrease the intensity of the water peak. 516 continuous scans were used to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

In Situ Raman Experiment

A LabRAM HR Evolution Raman microscope (Horiba) equipped with a He-Ne laser (λ = 633 nm) was employed. A homemade Raman electrochemical cell was used (Figure S1). A Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl, Pine) was used as the reference electrode, and a Ni foam (for alkaline electrolyte) or Pt wire (for an acidic electrolyte) was used as the counter electrode. In the flow cell test, the gas was introduced into the gas chamber at a flow rate of 10 mL min–1. During the H-cell type test, the electrolyte was presaturated with CO for 30 min and CO was bubbled into the electrolyte throughout the experiment. A water immersion objective (LOMO, 40×, numerical aperture = 1.23) was used to focus and collect the incident and scattered laser.22 A 0.013 mm thick optically transparent Teflon film (American Durafilm) was tightly wrapped over the objective to protect it from any corrosive electrolyte. Each spectrum was acquired over 8 s. Ten spectra were collected and averaged to improve the signal-to-noise ratio of the presented spectrum.

Computational Details

Density functional theory (DFT) simulations were carried out with Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4) to obtain adsorption energies of key intermediates.46,47 The exchange functional used was the Perdew–Burke–Emzerhof (PBE).48 Dispersion was included though the DFT-D2 method49,50 with our reparametrized C6 coefficients for Cu atoms.51 Inner electrons were represented by projector augment wave (PAW),52,53 while the valence monoelectronic states were expanded as plane waves with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV. For all the investigated systems, structures were relaxed using convergence criteria of 0.03 eV/Å and 10–5 eV for the ionic and electronic steps, respectively. The vacuum thickness between periodic repetitions on the z direction accounted for at least 10 Å. We sampled the Brillouin zone by a Γ-centered k-point mesh from the Monkhorst–Pack method54 with a reciprocal grid size smaller than 0.03·2π Å–1. The slab models were asymmetric, and thus the dipole correction was applied.55 Reported adsorption energies were obtained using CO2(g), H2(g), H2O(g), and clean surfaces as energy references. The computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) was used for obtaining the relative energy between H+ and gas-phase H2 at U = 0.0 V and pH = 0.56,57 To find transition states for different CO-CO coupling reactions, the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was employed.58 Their transition state nature was confirmed by computing numerical frequencies with a step size of ± 0.015 Å.
