The Role of Sex in Ocular Biometry: A Large-Scale Study of Cataract Candidates in Greece
Authors: Dimitra Sakka, Athanasios Zisimopoulos, Efthymia Kalogera, Efstathios Athanasopoulos, Kalliopi Diamantopoulou, Ioannis Halkiadakis, Kalliroi Konstantopoulou, Christina Kourentis, Ioannis Markopoulos, Iakovos Michail, Efstratios Paroikakis, Vasileios Peponis, Michael Karampelas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccurate intraocular lens (IOL) selection in cataract surgery relies on precise ocular biometry, yet variability between patients remains a challenge in achieving optimal refractive outcomes. Sex-related differences in biometric parameters and age-related changes in corneal astigmatism may contribute to this variability. This study aimed to evaluate sex-related differences in ocular biometry and age-related astigmatic changes in Greek cataract candidates using swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT).MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 6258 eyes from 3726 adult patients. Patients were scheduled for cataract surgery between July 2024 and July 2025 at a tertiary eye hospital in Athens, Greece. Axial length (AL), anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT), keratometry (K1, K2), corneal astigmatism (CA), and white-to-white (WTW) were measured using the Argos SS-OCT biometer. Predicted IOL power distribution was evaluated using the Hoffer Q formula for descriptive purposes, targeting emmetropia. Sex- and age-stratified analyses were performed.ResultsMale eyes demonstrated longer AL (by 0.46 mm), deeper ACD (by 0.13 mm), and larger WTW (by 0.20 mm) (p < 0.01), whereas female eyes had steeper K1 (by 0.78 D) and K2 (by 0.70 D) (p < 0.01). An age-related shift from with-the-rule (WTR) to against-the-rule (ATR) astigmatism was observed in both sexes, occurring approximately one decade earlier in male eyes. Female eyes were overrepresented below the 5th (5.54% vs. 4.00%, p < 0.01) and above the 95th (4.42% vs. 2.88%, p < 0.01) percentiles of the predicted IOL power distribution.ConclusionSex-related differences in ocular biometry and age-related changes in corneal astigmatism were observed in this cohort. These findings provide population-specific data and highlight variability in biometric characteristics among patients with cataracts which may be relevant for preoperative assessment and surgery planning.Keywords: Cataract surgery, Corneal astigmatism, Intraocular lens, Ocular biometry, Sex differences, Swept-source optical coherence tomography

Introduction

Cataract remains the leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide, according to the World Health Organization. Ongoing demographic ageing, population growth, and lifestyle patterns are expected to result in a continuous increase in the number of individuals affected by cataract-related visual impairment [1]. Surgical removal of the opacified crystalline lens with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation remains the standard of care and is a highly cost-effective intervention [2].

In recent years, patient expectations have evolved from mere restoration of vision toward achieving optimal refractive outcomes and reduced dependence on spectacles [3]. Accurate prediction of postoperative refraction is therefore fundamentally dependent on precise preoperative ocular biometry as even minor inaccuracies in measurements can result in clinically meaningful refractive errors [4]. Key biometric parameters, including axial length (AL), keratometry (K), anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT), and white-to-white (WTW) corneal diameter, play a central role in IOL power calculation [5].

Ocular biometric characteristics are known to vary across geographic regions, and several studies have described the range of biometric parameters across populations, reflecting anatomical, ethnic, and environmental influences [6–14]. In addition, accumulating evidence indicates that sex-related differences exist in ocular biometry, with distinct biometric profiles reported between male and female patients [6, 8, 13]. However, relatively few studies have examined sex-related differences in ocular biometry across age groups [8, 15], particularly Southern European and Balkan populations.

This study aimed to evaluate the role of sex in ocular biometry in a large cohort of cataract surgery candidates in Greece using swept-source optical biometry (SS-OCT), with particular emphasis on age-related patterns of corneal astigmatism and combined sex–age analysis in a population for which such data remain limited.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective, single-center, cross-sectional study of consecutive patients scheduled for cataract surgery at the public, tertiary, Specialized Eye Hospital, Ophthalmiatreio Athinon, in Athens, Greece, between July 2024 and July 2025. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Ophthalmiatreio Athinon (Approval No. 565/02-02-2026). As a result of the retrospective nature of the study, the need for informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional regulations.

Patient Selection

All patients aged ≥ 18 years underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including visual acuity testing with Snellen charts, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and dilated fundus examination. Inclusion criteria were the presence of visually significant cataract resulting in correcting distance visual acuity (CDVA) of ≤ 0.5 (decimal notation). Exclusion criteria included previous intraocular surgery, corneal ectatic disorder, dense cataracts requiring ultrasound biometry, ocular surface disorder, penetrating eye trauma, or missing biometric data.

Ocular Biometry and Corneal Astigmatism

Ocular biometric measurements were obtained by a trained ophthalmologist for all cataract surgery candidates using Argos (Alcon Laboratories Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA), a SS-OCT biometer, which uses a 1060-nm-wavelength swept-source technology to collect two-dimensional OCT data of the entire eye [16].

The following parameters were extracted and analyzed: AL, ACD, LT, K, corneal astigmatism (CA), and WTW distance. Corneal power was recorded for both flat (K1) and steep (K2) meridians. CA was calculated as the absolute difference between K1 and K2, and the astigmatism axis was defined as the axis of the steep meridian.

CA orientation was classified as with-the-rule (WTR) when the steep meridian was oriented within ± 30° of the vertical meridian (90°) and as against-the-rule (ATR) when the steep meridian was oriented within ± 30° of the horizontal meridian (180°). All remaining axes were classified as oblique. Analyses of astigmatism orientation primarily focused on WTR and ATR patterns because of their clinical relevance. For age analyses, patients were stratified into eight groups: 18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80–89, and ≥ 90 years.

IOL Power Calculation

IOL power calculations were performed as a secondary, exploratory analysis, based on the available preoperative biometric data. The Hoffer Q formula was used, as it is an open-access, non-proprietary, widely accepted formula [17], targeting a postoperative spherical equivalent of 0.00 diopters (D). A single IOL model (MX60E; Bausch + Lomb, Rochester, NY, USA) with a lens constant of 119.2 was used, and power values were calculated in 0.50-D increments. The IOL power closest to emmetropia was chosen. This analysis was performed for descriptive purposes and was not intended to evaluate refractive accuracy.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted primarily at the eye level, consistent with large biometric studies. Age and sex distributions were summarized at the patient level to avoid duplication in bilateral cases. To account for potential inter-eye correlation, a sensitivity analysis was performed using one randomly selected eye per patient. Data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (version 2024, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), and statistical analysis was conducted using Python software (version 3.9.0, Python Software Foundation).

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Given the large sample size, additional evaluation of distributional characteristics was performed using skewness, kurtosis, and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Variables demonstrating marked non-normality (skewness > 2 and/or kurtosis > 7) were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR) and were not subjected to parametric testing. The Mann-Whitney U test was used in these cases for inferential comparisons. Between-sex comparisons for continuous variables with acceptable distributional properties were performed using Welch’s independent-samples t test.

Age-stratified analyses were performed according to predefined age categories. Categorical variables and distributional comparisons were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test. To account for multiple comparisons across primary biometric parameters, a Bonferroni correction was applied, with statistical significance defined as an adjusted α = 0.00625.

Results

General Population

A total of 6258 eyes from 3726 patients were included; 3685 eyes (58.9%) were from female patients and 2573 eyes (41.1%) from male patients. The mean age was 73.36 ± 8.41 years and did not differ significantly by sex (p = 0.65).

Overall ocular biometric distributions are summarized in Table 1. Mean AL was 23.77 ± 1.21 mm, ACD 3.23 ± 0.39 mm, LT 4.67 ± 0.40 mm, and WTW 11.92 ± 0.42 mm. Mean keratometry values were K1 43.11 ± 1.66 D and K2 44.10 ± 1.74 D. Mean anterior CA was 0.99 ± 0.94 D (median 0.78 D), showing a right-skewed distribution (Table 1).

Sex-Related Ocular Biometric Differences

Sex-related differences were observed across several parameters (Table 2). Male eyes had larger ocular dimensions, with longer AL by 0.46 mm, deeper ACD by 0.13 mm and greater WTW by 0.20 mm (all p < 0.01). Female eyes had steeper corneas, with K1 higher by 0.78 D and K2 higher by 0.70 D (both p < 0.01). CA magnitude was slightly lower in female eyes by 0.08 D (p < 0.01), while LT did not differ significantly between sexes (p > 0.99).

Sex- and Age-Related Astigmatism Differences

Astigmatism axis orientation demonstrated a marked age-dependent shift from WTR to ATR astigmatism (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1). In female eyes, WTR astigmatism predominated through the sixth decade, accounting for 77.3% of eyes in the 50–59-year group and 63.7% in the 60–69-year group. The prevalence of ATR astigmatism increased progressively with age, becoming the dominant orientation from the seventh decade onward and reaching 55.0% in the 80–89-year group.

Distribution of anterior corneal astigmatism orientation by age decade in female (a) and male (b) eyes. WTR with-the-rule, ATR against-the-rule

In contrast, male eyes demonstrated an earlier and steeper transition toward ATR astigmatism. WTR prevalence declined to 71.3% in the 50–59-year group and 54.2% in the 60–69-year group, while ATR prevalence increased to 15.7% and 29.0%, respectively. ATR became the dominant orientation one decade earlier than in female eyes, accounting for 57.3% of eyes in the 70–79-year group and increasing further to 69.6% in the 80–89-year group. Sex-related differences in astigmatism axis distribution were statistically significant in the 60–69-year (p = 0.01), 70–79-year (p < 0.01), and 80–89-year (p < 0.01) age groups (Fig. 2).

Graphical representation of age-related trends in anterior corneal astigmatism orientation by sex. WTR with-the-rule, ATR against-the-rule

Oblique astigmatism accounted for 15.9% of eyes and did not show a marked variation across age groups.

Prediction of IOL Power Distribution

The predicted IOL power distribution using the Hoffer Q formula was unimodal and centered in the low 20-D range (Fig. 3). The median predicted IOL power was 21.0 D, with a mean of 20.72 ± 3.52 D. The 5th and 95th percentile thresholds were 14.0 D and 25.5 D, respectively. The most frequent predicted IOL powers were 22.0 D (n = 499), 21.5 D (n = 498), and 22.5 D (n = 455).

Distribution of predicted intraocular lens (IOL) power calculated using the Hoffer Q formula. The distribution is unimodal and centered in the low 20-D range

Sex-stratified analysis demonstrated significant differences at the extremes of the predicted IOL power distribution (Fig. 4). Female eyes were over-represented both below the 5th percentile (5.54% vs. 4.00%, p < 0.01) and above the 95th percentile (4.42% vs. 2.88%, p < 0.01) compared with male eyes.

This secondary analysis was descriptive and intended to illustrate the distribution of predicted IOL power within the study population.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis using one randomly selected eye per patient yielded results consistent with the primary eye-level analysis, with no material differences in the observed associations.

Discussion

This study evaluated sex-related differences in ocular biometry and age-related changes in corneal astigmatism in a large cohort of cataract candidates in Greece. Population-based biometric data from Southern European and Balkan cataract populations remain limited, and comprehensive analyses incorporating both biometric parameters and astigmatism characteristics are scarce [6, 8, 18, 19]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize ocular biometry and predicted IOL distribution in a Greek population using a SS-OCT biometer.

The overall mean age (73.36 ± 8.41 years) was higher than that reported in studies from Asian populations, including China, South Korea and Iran [9, 12, 13]. In contrast, populations from Europe and Israel have reported similar age distributions [6, 8, 10]. These differences likely reflect variations in population demographics, life expectancy, referral patterns, and access to cataract surgery across healthcare systems.

The overall mean AL (23.77 ± 1.21 mm) was closely aligned with those reported in other European and Middle Eastern cataract populations [6, 8, 10, 13]. In contrast, larger AL have been consistently reported in East Asian populations, where mean AL often exceeds 24.5 mm [7, 9, 11, 14, 20]. This difference may partly reflect the higher prevalence of axial myopia in East Asia [21] which has been associated with environmental and lifestyle factors such as increased near-work activities and reduced outdoor exposure during childhood [22, 23]. In agreement with previous studies, male eyes in our cohort exhibited longer AL than female eyes, with a mean difference of 0.46 mm [6, 10, 13].

The overall mean ACD (3.23 ± 0.39 mm) was deeper than that reported by Jiménez-García et al. and Zvorničanin et al. while surprisingly almost equal to that reported by Song et al. [6, 8, 24]. Male eyes presented deeper ACD by 0.13 mm, a difference consistent with previous biometric studies [6, 8, 13]. Although statistically significant, this difference is relatively small and may have limited clinical impact when considered in isolation.

LT in our cohort (4.67 ± 0.40 mm) fell within the range reported in large cataract cohorts [6–8]. Although small sex-related differences in LT have been described previously, these are often of limited clinical relevance [6, 10]. Consistent with this, LT did not differ significantly between sexes, suggesting that sex alone may not be a major determinant of this parameter.

The mean WTW value in the present study was 11.92 ± 0.42 mm. Beyond providing insight into anterior segment dimensions, WTW is incorporated into several IOL power calculation formulas such as Barrett Universal II and Holladay 2 [25]. WTW was significantly larger in male eyes supporting the concept of larger overall ocular dimensions in men reported in previous studies [6, 8, 13].

The mean magnitude of CA (0.99 ± 0.94 D) is consistent with values reported across multiple studies, where average astigmatism levels cluster around 1.0 D [6, 8, 10, 13]. This finding is clinically relevant as CA of ≥ 0.75 D is commonly considered when evaluating candidates for toric IOL. A recent study demonstrated that toric IOL implantation provides more accurate astigmatism correction than alternative surgical methods [26].

Female patients exhibited steeper corneal curvature than male patients, as reflected by higher keratometric values. Despite this difference, the magnitude of CA was slightly lower in female eyes. This apparent discrepancy is unlikely to be clinically meaningful, as the observed difference was relatively small (0.08 D). The cornea is a hormone-responsive tissue and changes in sex hormone levels may influence corneal curvature and biomechanics [27]. One possible mechanism underlying women’s steeper corneas may be hormonal changes associated with the postmenopausal period, during which most of our participants were evaluated. Additionally, unmeasured systemic factors such as body stature may also play a role, as previous studies have demonstrated that height and weight are associated with AL, vitreous chamber depth, and corneal curvature. These findings suggest that overall physical development may influence ocular biometry [28, 29].

Accurate assessment of CA is influenced by tear film stability and blinking dynamics, which are recognized factors affecting measurement accuracy [30]. Previous studies have shown that tear film instability may introduce variability in keratometric measurements, particularly in older populations and in the presence of ocular surface dysfunction [31, 32]. Ocular surface disease, including dry eye disease, is common among patients with cataracts [31] and its exclusion may limit the direct generalizability of these findings to typical clinical populations.

Beyond differences in corneal curvature magnitude, the orientation of CA demonstrated age- and sex-dependent patterns. While both sexes predominantly exhibited WTR astigmatism in younger age groups, a progressive transition toward ATR astigmatism was observed with advancing age. This shift occurred earlier, by one decade, and more prominently approximately in the seventh decade in male patients and in the eighth decade in female patients. The later onset and lower frequency of ATR astigmatism observed in female patients may be influenced by hormonal factors affecting corneal stiffness and curvature [33].

Similar age-driven transitions from WTR to ATR astigmatism have been consistently reported in previous studies [13, 34, 35]. Hayashi et al. and Kim et al. also described an earlier onset and greater prevalence of ATR astigmatism in male patients [33, 36]. Although the mechanisms underlying this age-related change are not fully understood, age-dependent alterations in corneal biomechanics and eyelid movement have been proposed as potential contributors [35, 37].

Sex-related differences were observed at the extremes of the predicted IOL power distribution. According to the Hoffer Q formula, female eyes were more frequently represented at both the lower and, particularly, the higher ends of the IOL power spectrum. Given the inverse relationship between AL and IOL power, this finding may in part suggest a higher prevalence of very short and very long eyes among female patients [38]. However, in the present cohort it was not fully explained by AL distribution alone, suggesting that additional biometric factors may contribute. These observations highlight the potential relevance of sex-related biometric variability when evaluating patients at the extremes of ocular dimensions.

Recent SS-OCT biometry studies have confirmed consistent sex-related differences in ocular dimensions. However, their impact on refractive outcomes remains unclear [6, 39, 40]. While Zhang et al. identified sex as an independent predictor of refractive error [39], Yasar et al. found no significant differences in IOL power between sexes [40]. Thus, although our findings align with established biometric patterns, their clinical relevance remains uncertain and requires further investigation using postoperative data.

Limitations include cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to infer causal inference between age, sex and ocular biometric parameters. Moreover, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Measurements were under routine clinical conditions and parameters such as tear breakup time were not systematically recorded which may have influenced CA measurements. Also, posterior CA was not assessed, which may lead to incomplete characterization of total CA and affect interpretation of astigmatic patterns. Calculations of IOL power were performed using the Hoffer Q formula; although it was selected for descriptive purposes the absence of comparison with newer-generation formulas may limit contemporary applicability. Lastly, postoperative refractive outcomes were not available, limiting the ability to evaluate the clinical impact of the observed biometric differences.

Conclusion

Male eyes exhibited larger ocular dimensions, whereas female eyes had steeper corneas and were more frequently represented at the extremes of the IOL power spectrum. An age-related shift from WTR to ATR astigmatism was observed, occurring approximately one decade earlier in male eyes. These findings highlight consistent sex and age-related patterns in ocular biometry and corneal astigmatism and may be relevant when interpreting preoperative measurements and planning cataract surgery.
