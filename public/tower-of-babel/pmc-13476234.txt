New paradigm of q-CAR drug development targeting disease-specific protein conformations
Authors: Libin Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug discovery has progressed to using advanced AI-driven platforms. These systems rely heavily on static structures and overlook an essential aspect: protein motion—conformational transitions and local fluctuations—is essential for function, as “no motion, no function.” Thus, this review discusses recent progress in measuring protein conformations and dynamics and introduces a new concept, “quantitative conformation-activity relationship (q-CAR),” aimed at developing drugs that target disease-specific protein conformations in the future.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Structural biology

Introduction

The history of drug discovery spans from the ancient trial-and-error use of raw plants and minerals for healing to modern genomics and large-scale, structure-based, computation-assisted drug screening platforms1–4. Medication forms vary widely, encompassing small molecules, peptides, proteins, and receptor domains, with ongoing evolution towards radiolabeled and nanobody-based therapies5–8. With advances in many technologies, drug discovery is continually being revolutionized, aiming to overcome the high cost, long timelines, and high failure rates of the traditional drug development process and to deliver more precisely targeted and effective medications9,10. These revolutions allow us to divide the history of drug discovery into several distinct, chronologically progressive phases, moving from opportunistic observation to highly targeted, technology-driven design11,12. The key concepts and technological innovations from each period will be summarized in this review. In addition, we propose a novel drug development concept, in conjunction with ongoing technological innovations, to enhance drug development by leveraging our understanding of protein function through quantitative conformational dynamics.

Traditional trial-and-error discovery to early modern medications

Ancient drug discovery mainly relied on opportunity-based trial-and-error observations of the therapeutic effects of plants, minerals, and animal parts, with these materials applied directly to humans13. These practices have led to the development of early pharmacopeias in China, Egypt, and India, among other countries14. For instance, a plant called San Qi (三七, Panax notoginseng) was used to treat external injuries and remains popular in China nowadays15. A willow bark was recorded in the Ebers Papyrus that was used for treating aches and pains, which contains salicin, the precursor to aspirin16,17.

The emerging of modern medication development starting along with the decline of the Roman Empire during the Middle Ages, two pandemics occurred on the European and American continents: from 1347 to 1353, 50–60% Europe’s population, around 50 million, were killed by the Black Death, which was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis18 while the Columbian Exchange during 1492-1600 led to 90% Native Americans vanishing due to the introduction of diseases19. While these devastating pandemics did not directly cause modern drug development, they revealed the vulnerabilities of medieval medicine. As a result, they led to the development of early public health measures, such as quarantine20–22. They also shaped human genetics, which guides modern drug research, and were followed by advances in public health care, hygiene, and sanitation23. In 1796, Edward Jenner’s successful smallpox inoculation paved the way for the development of vaccines to treat infectious diseases24. At that time, a set of medicines extracted from raw materials, rather than used directly, was also developed, including Digitalis for cardiac conditions25, Quinine for malaria26, Ipecacuanha for dysentery27, and Aspirin for fever16,17. One of the landmarks in these drug developments was Alexander Fleming’s discovery of Penicillin to treat Staphylococcus, with large-scale production becoming available in the 1940s28.

Cell-based HTS drug development

Trial-and-error-based drug discovery was extremely tedious and inefficient for developing modern medications, resulting in only a few drugs targeting specific diseases, as it relies entirely on opportunistic observations, as discussed in the previous section. In addressing this deficit, some semi-automated and miniaturized (96-well plate) high-throughput drug screening (HTS) platforms emerged in the 1960s with an aim of increasing the opportunistic observations. From this standpoint, the early stage of HTS screening is an upgraded version of “trial-and-error,” except that it is not directly applied to humans. The HTS were further transformed into robotic, cell-based HTS systems (cb-HTS) in the late 1980s and early 1990s29,30. The cb-HTS was developed over many decades as an automated method to quickly test large compound libraries on living cells31,32. Its goal is to find “hits” that exhibit desired biological effects, usually using signaling output or protein activity as indicators, measured via fluorescence energy transfer (FRET), fluorescence correlation spectroscopy, cell proliferation, cAMP, and even RNAi33–37. The purpose of this type of screening is to identify compounds that interact with the target protein in a cellular environment to achieve optimal IC50 or EC50 values, determined by a dose-response curve38. A significant turning point in cb-HTS was the development of the NCI-60 human tumor cell line in 1980s, which shifted the screening process from a “compound-oriented” approach using a general cell line such as HEK293 to a “disease-oriented” approach39 (Fig.1). Structure-based HTS drug discovery (sb-HTS)

A signaling process mediated by a protein typically involves three steps: (1) the ligand interacts with the protein; (2) the protein responds to the ligand; and (3) the catalytic activity or signaling efficiency changes due to the protein’s conformational allostery45, depicted by a signaling allostery of the A1R receptor46

(Figure 2). In the cell-based drug screening process, the approach usually integrates steps 1 and 3 without the involvement of step 2. The advancement of X-ray and cryo-EM techniques for determining the structures of numerous (patho)physiological proteins offers a new platform for drug screening47. The approach utilizes these structures within structure-based drug discovery, in conjunction with biological activity assessments, forming the foundation of quantitative structure-activity relationships (QSAR) that guide the optimization of lead compounds in drug development48. Advances in computational tools are accelerating the development and adoption of QSAR systems, enabling the study of ligand-protein interactions in virtual environments and reducing or avoiding the often labor-intensive benchwork that is impractical for ultra-high-throughput screening49. Structure-based HTS (sb-HTS) is thus a mainstream approach for discovering and optimizing potential drug molecules by analyzing the three- dimensional (3D) structure of a biological target to identify its binding sites50. Over the last few decades, a significant accumulation of experimental 3D structures has provided an unprecedented foundation for sb-HTS (Fig. 3). There are more than 240,000 PDB entries, including an estimated 18,000 membrane protein structures. Within a single category of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), over 2,000 structures have been resolved, and this number continues to grow rapidly51.

A general procedure for sb-HTS starts with the (1) preparation of an existing X-ray or cryo-EM structure of the protein of interest51; if an experimental structure is unavailable, computational modeling can be used to generate a 3D structure of the target protein, such as AlphaFold52,53. The structure is then prepared for screening by removing extraneous elements, such as water molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, and assigning atomic charges54. Due to the Fation of computational resources, in most scenarios, one or two potential ligand binding pockets on the target’s surface will be identified using computational algorithms that usually locate cavities suitable for ligand binding55. Followed by the prepared target protein, (2) a ligand library will be prepared. In the sb-HTS, HTS is primarily carried out via large-scale virtual screening. The ligand library can include small molecules, peptides, proteins, or even compound fragments. Therefore, building a comprehensive ligand library is a key part of sb-HTS’ objectives, and significant progress has been made over the past decades. The virtual compound libraries can contain billions of commercially available or synthetically on-demand accessible molecules, such as V-SYNTHES56, Enamine REAL57, ZINC2258, PubChem59, and ChEMBL60, partially listed in Table 1. With advancements in computational hardware and software, the docking process has become more efficient by effectively leveraging large-scale libraries, advancing several candidates into clinical trials, especially through the integration of artificial intelligence (AI)61 and accelerated MD approaches62–64. This includes a small molecule inhibitor, Rentosertib (INS018_055), that is used to treat idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a chronic lung disease65, and DSP-1181, as the first AI-designed drug to treat obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)66. However, AI-based drug discovery still falls under computation- or structure-based drug discovery, where AI algorithms are better at analyzing complex, large datasets and identifying patterns that traditional computational methods might miss67.

In addition, a major issue for AI-based HTS is the lack of sufficient, high-quality experimental data, as these data were usually generated across different laboratories under varying conditions69–71. So, it is important to create a dataset with consistent experimental conditions and then use it to build an AI model to avoid massive false positives. As pointed out, the application of AI in drug discovery is still in its early stages and not that successful, despite the hype, but it can be foreseen its progress.

Emergence of quantitative conformation-activity-relationship-based drug discovery (q-CAR)

Structure-based drug discovery (SBDD) continues to be a prominent approach, driven by increased access to protein structures, the ongoing expansion of chemical space, and advancements in computational technology72. The advantages of using QSAR in drug development are widely recognized. AI-driven computation accelerates progress by shortening the hit-digging cycle and improving the precision of hit identification73. As additional experimental data is incorporated into training AI systems, the accuracy of hit discovery is expected to improve. However, the limitations of QSAR-based drug discovery are also paramount: (1) A static structure is often insufficient in depicting a protein’s behavior at a specific moment of its function, as it lacks the motion information that is essential for understanding proteins functioning74,75; (2) Thermostabilized strategies employed to aid in structural elucidation can often hinder the ability to determine how well the structure represents its functional state in nature76–79; (3) The computational simulation based on force fields to replicate the physiological environment is still a significant challenge, particularly for membrane proteins, due to the unique nature of native membrane and membrane proteins and limitation in current modeling techniques80; (4) while general computational power has increased dramatically as the significant advances in GPU technology, conducting full, flexible docking on both ligand and proteins simultaneously remains resource-intensive81,82; (5) The limitation of using a single apo or ligand-bound conformation in computational simulation is a recognized challenge in the drug discovery as well, as it fails to capture the protein’s inherent multi-state equilibrium observed in experiments. This discrepancy will lead to inaccurate ligand binding, especially for targets that undergo significant conformational changes upon ligand and signaling partner bindings83. This also makes it difficult to evaluate how one-structure-based computational data align with pharmacological evaluation that is a convergent signaling from multiple states; (6) The lack of sufficient, diverse, and high-quality protein dynamics data is a significant hurdle for training AI84, which leads to the current situation is to use the computation created data to train AI and how these data represent the “real data” is a big question mark85,86, etc.. Therefore, the next step for us is to develop a drug platform that can quantifiably measure protein motion—including multi-conformational transitions and dynamics—to better understand the protein’s function and aid in designing more effective drugs. This paper presents a new concept: Quantitative Conformation-Activity-Relationship-based Drug Discovery (q-CAR). It recognizes that protein function relies on crucial dynamic conformational changes that are not captured by existing models in drug development87,88. This concept of innovation is essential for a protein with many interaction partners, since their signaling preferences depend on the conformational selectivity of both the target protein and its transducers, such as the GPCR system. The proposal for q-CAR is primarily based on recent advances across multiple technologies, including the development of high-field NMR spectroscopy, the feasibility of capturing conformational ensembles using state-of-the-art cryo-EM, advances in 19F probes for quantifying and mapping conformational ensembles or landscapes using 19F-qNMR, and, particularly, advances in AI-based computation that enable the use of minimal datasets to train AI models, which is essential for the broad application of Bio-NMR, as it will benefit minimal sample preparation for model building. In the following section, we will focus on advances in 19F-qNMR that make this paradigm shift feasible.

In this regard, recent advances in mapping and measuring the conformational landscape have emerged as a frontier across multiple fields, including GPCRs89–96 and transporters97. Although some progress has been made, creating a reliable method for mapping the free-energy landscape with identifiable multi-conformational states remains challenging. Particularly in 2016, a study revealed that a typical neurological GPCR—the A2AR receptor—can be characterized into four conformational states using 19F quantitative NMR (19F-qNMR). These states, labeled S1 through S3’, span from inactive to active (-like) states along the activation pathway. The study also analyzed their measurable transitions triggered by ligand binding89,90. The conformational resolution of the A2AR receptor was improved to five states (S1 to S5) by creating mutants that lock the receptor into specific conformations93. This advance enabled us to determine the first GPCR-G protein complex in its intermediate state78. More importantly, we can now investigate the role of a specific conformational state in signaling transduction, which was previously infeasible because the fully activated state always signals in the wild-type construct, obscuring the functions of other states78. This success makes it possible to link a particular conformation to its function. This showed that q-CAR development is possible by linking different conformations and functions, and subsequently to ligand pharmacophores. To develop a q-CAR system, the following steps are necessary: (1) delineate and quantify the protein’s conformational landscape using a quantitative biophysical approach; (2) quantitively analyze conformational transitions and dynamics; (3) evaluate the protein’s functions during interactions with various substrates or mutants through biochemical or pharmacological assessments, and relate these findings to the conformations and dynamics identified in steps (1) and (2). In general, this involves recognizing the conformational states of a target protein, the population of each substate, its local dynamics, and the transitions among the states, as all of these elements influence the protein’s behavior and function.

Quantify Conformations: Over the past few years, a set of approaches has been developed to delineate conformational states, including single-molecule Forster Resonance Energy Transfer (smFRET)98, double electron-electron resonance (DEER)/pulse electron double resonance (PELDOR)99, 19F-qNMR87,89, time-resolved serial X-ray crystallography (TRSX) using photo-switchable ligands100–104, and time-resolved cryo-EM105, as shown in Fig. 4. Each method relies on different spectroscopic mechanisms. All approaches can profile subpopulations in various conformational states (in a simplified way, the figure illustrates only two states), where smFRET measures two-domain distances via fluorescence transitions between two fluorophores106, DEER via dipolar interactions between two nitroxide groups107, and TRSX measures the isomerization of photosensitive ligands103. Cryo-EM can also be used to estimate conformational subpopulations by analyzing particle heterogeneity within a single frozen sample108. It is worth highlighting that the recent development of 19F-qNMR for characterizing and profiling protein conformational states and studying their dynamics has made it an emerging and powerful tool87,89–91,94,109–111. It enables simultaneous, quantitative analysis of protein conformational transitions, including measurement of subpopulations of a protein that respond to various ligands, downstream partners, or changes in the microenvironment87. This advance offers an opportunity to profile the receptor’s conformational states, trap individual states, and study their functions to establish the correlation between the ligand pharmacophore88, the receptor’s conformational response to a ligand, and pharmacological outputs, thereby guiding conformation-based drug discovery, as demonstrated in the publications78,93 — a new era of precision drug development on the basis of q-CAR might be open that is able to target a disease-related protein conformational state instead of the entire protein88.

From the practical perspective, we would like to point out that, when mapping the conformational states of a protein, each method has its limitations, and thus it should be judiciously selected based on the purpose of the study. The smFRET

usually needs to introduce relatively bulky fluorophores (often ~1-2 nm in diameter, such as Cy3 and Cy5) and relies on distance distributions between these two bulky fluorophores, with a FRET transfer distance maximized to 10 nm112,113. On the other hand, this also produces the challenges to routinely manage data and controversy in explaining the dataset in quantifying the angstrom level distance distinction between protein conformers, as the smFRET efficiency is largely influenced by fluctuation of the bulky donor and acceptor fluorophores and photobleaching in the system, etc114,115. In the application of membrane proteins, the intracellular labeling and the effect of hydrophobic fluorophores are also major setbacks for the method116. In the DEER spectroscopy, the rotamerization of the probe itself blurs the distance between labeled backbone atoms, leading to the measured distance often not reflect a single fixed distance but a distance distribution117. This led to difficulty in resolving the conformational distinction for a conformational movement in a single digital Angstrom level. In addition, DEER experiment needs to be performed at cryogenic temperatures (often 50–80 K), which prevents studies of real-time dynamics at physiological temperatures. For the TRSX, a huge sample consumption is a setback in addition to the substrate or ligand diffusion into the crystal, which can be slower than the reaction itself, blurring the time resolution. The structural heterogeneity of the biological sample also makes it difficult to extract clear functional conformational information118. The application of time-resolved cryo-EM technology, in combination with innovations in cryo-EM toolkits such as 3DVA in cryoSPACR119 and AI-Variable Autoencoders in cryoDRGN120, has also made strides in sorting out the conformational ensembles of macromolecular complexes, including, more recently, the resolution of a series of GPCR-G protein complexes121. However, it has some setbacks, including low signal-to-noise ratios, an inability to detect sparsely populated and dynamic states, and a majority of particles are not counted during the process. As a result, it is biased toward stable structures, in addition to being significantly affected by the data processing procedures of the individuals122. This makes it challenging to capture transient intermediates or continuous, flexible movements. The limitations of 19F-qNMR will be extensively discussed in the section “Open Questions and Future Perspective”.

Quantify Dynamics: It is clear that proteins are not fixed structures but exist in various conformational ensembles, with continuous transitions among these states across length scales (from tenths of an angstrom to nanometers) and timescales (from picoseconds to seconds)123. These motions ultimately result in unique functions, a particular signaling level of a receptor, or a specific catalytic capacity of an enzyme124,125. In general, protein dynamics include two aspects: global dynamics (including conformational transitions) and local dynamics within a single conformational state that exhibit fluctuations126, which include transitions between different states and dynamics within each state. While transitions among states are key to function, local dynamics that directly affect interactions with ligands or downstream signaling partners are also key. Transitions among conformational states affect interactions between conformational states and partner proteins, and the dynamics of each conformational state will also influence how the protein engages with its partners and, thus, the functional consequences. One application of conformational transitions is the design of a biased drug in the GPCR field, given that different conformational states engage distinct downstream signaling partners (Fig. 5). Although signaling bias originates from conformational bias, the roles of both overall and localized dynamics in shaping signaling effectiveness have not been comprehensively studied. Designing a drug to target a specific signaling pathway with optimal therapeutic efficacy and selectivity remains central to drug discovery. Progress in developing q-CAR

To achieve this, the field has begun quantitatively characterizing protein conformational states beyond the simple two-state (ON/OFF) model128–130, recognizing that protein activation typically involves multiple states, each of which is associated with signaling bias and efficacy131. A key example of this is the GPCR. So far, only a limited number of GPCRs have been quantitatively examined for their conformational transitions using 19F-qNMR, including A2AR, β2AR, NTSR, and β1AR receptors29,31–33,36,49–51. The A2AR is the most extensively studied and offers the highest level of conformational resolution. This will be discussed in the next section to highlight the future potential of the q-CAR strategy in drug development.

After a decade of efforts, we’ve developed a five-state activation model for the GPCR—the A2AR receptor — as illustrated in Fig. 6a. This model includes two inactive states (S1 and S2), two intermediate states (S3 and S4), and a fully activated state (S5)93. Furthermore, these distinct conformational states can be stabilized in different mutants through interactions with related ligands and transducers. This process is guided by a conformational landscape probed by 19F-qNMR. We refer to these as conformation-trapped constructs78,93. This advancement enabled us to study the functions and structures of individual states, especially those between the fully inactive and fully active states, which were previously difficult to analyze78. This progress provides a foundation for linking specific conformational states to their signaling functions and, consequently, their pharmacological effects35,40. It is also worth noting that the creation of mutants itself could affect the protein’s functionality. Therefore, mutation sites should be judiciously selected, and the functional assay should be utilized as a checkpoint for the constructs. 19F-qNMR can more intuitively and quantitatively analyze a protein’s conformational landscape than cryo-EM, as cryo-EM relies heavily on sample preparation and data processing, and not all conformational states, especially dynamic ones, can be well resolved. 19F-qNMR also makes it easy to observe all conformational transitions at once during ligand titration and signaling interactions, without the need to resolve all particles and sort out subpopulations in response to different ligand and transducer binding. In particular, Cryo-EM’s statistical determination of conformational subpopulations can be biased because the majority of particles cannot be processed and are therefore excluded from counts132, mostly because of their dynamics. In this regard, the 19F-qNMR profiling of conformational subpopulations does not have these limitations, as it captures information on all states simultaneously (Fig.6). Furthermore, the transitions among different conformational states in 19F-NMR can also be easily monitored in response to various ligands or signaling partners using a set of NMR relaxation experiments such as CPMG, and CEST95,133, etc, along with their dynamics during the conformational transitions — the essence of the q-CAR concept. These advantages are unparalleled among other biophysical tools, without damaging the protein samples in solution, including the capture of less populated, invisiblehigh-energy conformational states .

Compared to the single static structure-derived QSAR, the q-CAR provides a more detailed and comprehensive dynamic vision of how proteins respond to ligand binding, rather than just a one-point snapshot. A drug-elicited signaling process involves two key components closely related to signaling efficacy: conformation and dynamics. This can be likened to a person playing basketball, where the pose (conformation) influences whether the ball can be cast into the basket, while the rate of hand movement affects how many shots can be completed per minute. Unfortunately, the current drug discovery system lacks these two pieces of information, even though the static structure provides a conformation that is strongly influenced by the structure-determination process. For instance, a thermostabilized mutation or the addition of a thermostabilized protein to aid structural analysis will likely affect the final structure or alter the receptor’s original functional shape. A misalignment of just 0.1 degree or less can cause a missed shot. This raises questions about how well these resolved structures reflect their native conformations in solution and about their effectiveness in drug discovery campaigns. As a result, using these structures in a SAR-based drug discovery system for screening will likely lead to uncertainty in identifying target ligands. It remains unclear how this affects our ongoing evaluation of candidates within a chemical space library of 100 billion. However, this remains a concern in the field as we work to improve methods for detecting hits, whether through existing computational docking techniques or AI training, both of which depend on precise, comprehensive raw data for effective model development.

Strategy of q-CAR implementation and effectiveness-validation

In the q-CAR model, the population of each conformational substate is quantified and correlated with signaling strength, as illustrated in Fig. 7a. To simplify, we assume a single signaling state, defined as a conformational state in a multi-state protein that can interact with a downstream signaling partner and induce associated signaling. The conformational population change in this signaling state is directly correlated with signaling strength. We can then imagine that any compound that drives the conformational equilibrium toward this substate will increase or decrease corresponding signaling. Accordingly, one can design the drug based on the effects of ligand pharmacophores on conformational re-equilibrium, which ultimately leads to changes in the strength of signaling output. Generalization of q-CAR beyond GPCRs

While most current research on quantifying the conformational landscape has focused on a limited number of GPCRs, these receptors are also the most extensively studied in this context using 19F-qNMR. However, the concept of q-CAR can be generalized to any protein exhibiting multiple states, or even to a two-state (folded or unfolded) system, to map transitions and dynamics among these states and their correlations with signaling or enzymatic activity. In enzymology, where engineering a high-activity protein remains an open question, despite site-directed evolution having significantly advanced the field in the past decades134,135. Creating a conformational landscape using 19F-qNMR and studying the dynamics of conformational transitions can guide the design of a mutant to trap the enzyme’s high-activity transition state93,136. This strategy has been demonstrated in our recent GPCR study to study the function of a particular state78,93. An additional application of q-CAR, based on the delineated and quanitified conformational states, is to guide ligand screening or design to maintain a half-open efflux system for drug-resistant ATP-binding Cassette transporters, thereby not only preserving ATP binding and hydrolysis but also maintaining drug concentrations in the cells to exert their effects137,138. This will help us control the ABC efflux transporter and overcome multidrug resistance by identifying a compound that traps it in its partially open state.

Open questions and future perspectives for q-CAR

Innovative drug discovery has become a central focus of the global pharmaceutical industry, driven by advances across many sectors, from biomedical technologies to unmet medical needs139–142. It has evolved through various discovery generations, as summarized in this review and others143–148, from early Trial-and-Error drug discovery to modern structure-based and AI-assisted platforms (Fig.8a). Although our knowledge of how these physiological proteins activate and transform into signaling molecules is still elusive—mainly due to the complexity of protein activation, new approaches of comprehending the protein behavior is thus the key, which proposes — ‘What are crucial in future drug development for precision medicine?’149. As the scientific community began to understand how protein motion quantitatively influences activation, the concept of q-CAR is thus coined herein (Fig.8b). However, to establish universal Bio-NMR or other methods for quantitatively analyzing conformational landscape and dynamics of a protein and its interaction partners, several obstacles must be conquered, using 19F-qNMR as an exemplified tool discussed below.

How to overcome NMR instrumentation limitations? As one of the most powerful tools for studying dynamics across the full spectrum of timescales, NMR is inherently less sensitive than other biophysical techniques, such as cryo-EM and fluorescence spectroscopy150,151. This raises a necessary question: how can a biological sample preparation system be developed to produce proteins, especially membrane proteins, at levels sufficient for Bio-NMR? The challenge is heightened in 19F-qNMR because the chemical-shift anisotropy (CSA) of the fluorine nucleus in macromolecules results in significantly linewidth broadening, thereby reducing resonance intensity, compared to 13C, 15N, or 1H152,153. Recently, developing an approach to boost the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of NMR measurements has become a hotspot in the field. In particular, the development of dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP)-enhanced NMR has consistently eliminated the sensitivity barriers in bio-NMR, making it possible to perform in-cell NMR at a high SNR154,155. Under the ideal conditions of electron-nuclear transfer, the NMR signal enhancement for proteins can at most be 659, corresponding to a time-saving factor of 434,000 for a solution NMR experiment156. 19F nucleus has a close gyromagnetic moment, which should have a close time-saving factor compared to the proton. The implementation of the NMR automation system and AI-assisted data collection and analysis will facilitate NMR use in the future. These progresses will significantly reduce the requirements for sample preparation, making it feasible for a broad application of NMR to study the sparsely expressed proteins like GPCRs in the cellular environment.

How to address the limitations of the current 19F-tag? Because conformational landscapes vary widely across receptors and individuals, a highly sensitive 19F-NMR probe is urgently needed. Screening for the next-generation fluorinated probe remains challenging due to the wide variety of 19F compounds, making it akin to finding a needle in a haystack and requiring considerable effort. In this regard, several efforts have been observed in recent years. A highly sensitive mono-fluorinated compound, monofluoroethyl (mFE), was developed that exhibits a narrow linewidth and ultrahigh sensitivity to conformational changes, with a chemical shift dispersion of 9 ppm97, although it has not yet been validated in our system. The aromatic 19F-13C TROSY was also developed to improve resolution among different conformational states157. Though the monofluorinated tag has advantages in linewidth compared to the tri-fluorinated tag, the difficulty of membrane protein preparation often makes it less appealing. In addressing this, a few groups focus on searching for tri-fluorinated probes. For example, the probe 6-CTFPA exhibited a slight advantage over the tag BTFMA158. Considering the resolution achieved so far, the development of a new type of tri-fluorinated probe is still a long way from meeting the requirements for profiling a protein’s conformational landscape at high resolution, which will possess both intensity and resolution of conformational states of the targeted protein.

How to address the challenge of structure-function disruption? Since allosteric signaling pathways in (membrane) proteins like GPCRs are super subtle, attaching a large fluorophore to the receptor could interfere with its structure and function. As a consequence, (1) developing a fluorine tag that minimally affects the receptor is still in progress, despite dedicated efforts from various research groups in the past years87,97,158,159; (2) developing an effective fluorinated unnatural amino acid (UAA) genetic incorporation system would be a plus. However, protein expression often faces challenges in the UAA system because the orthogonal tRNA (O-tRNA) and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (O-aaRS) struggle to efficiently incorporate the target unnatural amino acid during translation. This inefficiency leads to dramatically reduced protein yields, further complicating work with low-production proteins.

How to develop a widely applicable NMR method like cryo-EM? Conformational dynamics occur over a broad spectrum of timescales, from picoseconds to seconds160,161. NMR can detect all forms of protein motion; however, each timescale demands specific pulse sequences, making it challenging for non-experts to execute these experiments and process the data162. Therefore, creating a standardized toolkit of pulse sequences—whether commercial or academic—that non-specialists can easily use will be crucial for increasing the accessibility of these techniques.

How to create a more physiologically relevant Bio-NMR environment? The best way to profile the conformational landscape of a protein to match its cell-based signaling measurements is to perform in-cell NMR experiments. Recently, several groups have implemented strategies to perform in-cell 19F-NMR, primarily focused on fluorinated ligands163 or soluble proteins that haven’t reached a desirable conformational resolution, or in-membrane protein systems164–166. Alternatively, in-membrane NMR is a valuable approach in which the receptor remains in its native membrane, allowing it to best reflect the receptor’s natural conformational states and interactions with ligand167,168.

How to establish a q-CAR-based-HTS system? NMR was notoriously low-throughput, even though it provides ample conformational and dynamic information about proteins and their complexes with ligands or binding partners. Bridging low-throughput NMR data collection into a scalable, modern screening pipeline would be key to its broader application. Integrating AI-based landscape fitness169–171, trained on experimental conformational landscapes, would be an avenue to address this challenge, as it requires only a minimal experimental dataset that is feasible nowadays, given innovations in sample preparation and NMR spectroscopy. The combination of AI-based landscape fitness and the conformational landscape dataset generated by 19F-qNMR of the protein will enable large-scale q-CAR drug discovery in the future.

In traditional thinking, establishing high-throughput NMR-based drug screening is usually impossible due to the large amount of protein required, which is even more challenging for membrane proteins. However, as discussed above, advances in many technologies, from sample preparation to spectroscopic upgrades, have made this possible. In particular, our lab recently established a native in-membrane-based ligand screening platform for membrane proteins, including GPCRs, using in-membrane Water-LOGSY167. Using 5 mm NMR tubes and an automation system, the approach allows us to screen more than 1000 compounds with minimal cell culture (250 mL) on a 600 MHz magnet to identify the hits. This method not only tells us whether the virtual-screened compounds bind to the receptor but also provides information on the orientation of their bindings in the pocket. Training a reasonable number of datasets (tens to hundreds) to build an AI-based model is also feasible, given advances in ChatGPT, Claude, and other AI systems in development; we expect such a model to emerge in the next few years and will significantly drive q-CAR development.
