An influenza HA mRNA-LNP vaccine induces potent responses in newborn nonhuman primates that enhance protection from challenge
Authors: Courtney L Page, Beth C Holbrook, Kali F Crofts, Mohamad-Gabriel Alameh, Benjamin Davis, David Caudell, Drew Weissman, Martha A Alexander-Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza virus infection poses a significant health risk to newborns, with this population experiencing higher hospitalization and mortality rates compared to older children. The heightened vulnerability of this age group results from a combination of an altered immune system and lack of a licensed vaccine for children under six months of age. mRNA-LNP vaccines have shown remarkable efficacy, including the capacity to induce antibodies in poorly responding populations. This makes them a promising candidate for addressing the unique immunological environment of newborns. Here, we leveraged the close immunological and physiological similarity of NHP to evaluate the efficacy of an influenza hemagglutinin mRNA-LNP vaccine in newborns. Our findings show the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine elicits robust, multi-functional antibody responses in newborn NHP that result in significantly reduced viral load and disease severity following challenge. These results highlight the potential of mRNA-based vaccines as a transformative approach to protect the vulnerable newborn population against influenza. Continued development and optimization of this platform could address the critical gap in influenza virus and other pathogen vaccine coverage for infants under six months of age.Subject terms: Influenza virus, RNA vaccines

Introduction

Influenza virus infection places a significant burden on the health of newborns. The prevalence of lower respiratory tract infection-associated hospitalizations is four times greater in children under one year of age compared to those between 1 and 4 years1, with those less than 6 months 2-6 times more likely to succumb to influenza virus infection compared to their older counterparts2,3. These data highlight the exacerbated disease that is experienced by newborns compared to older infants. The rate of infection has the potential to increase given the decline in adult vaccination in recent years4, enhancing the circulation of influenza viruses.

The enhanced disease in infants under 6 months of age is the result of the naïve immune status of newborns coupled with their altered immune responsiveness. Presently, there is no licensed influenza virus vaccine for children under six months of age, as current vaccination approaches fail to elicit robust antibody responses in these individuals5–8. This is likely the combined result of maternally derived influenza virus-specific antibodies, which can decrease antibody production following vaccination9–12, and the immune alterations present in young infants13,14. Given the disproportionate mortality of influenza virus infection in newborns, there is a critical need to establish vaccine strategies that can elicit protective responses against influenza virus in this age group.

Infants exhibit diminished production of high affinity IgG through the first year of life15,16. This may, at least partially, result from the decreased T follicular helper (Tfh) cell number and function that has been reported in newborns17,18. Previous work in many models, including humans and NHPs, has shown that Tfh cells play a crucial role in B cell maturation and efficient IgG production19–22. The germinal center (GC) is the site of high affinity antibody generation, and GC formation is dependent on a robust Tfh cell response23,24. Newborns not only have reduced Tfh cell responses compared to adults following vaccination17,18, but also face impaired B cell differentiation and survival as a result of decreased costimulatory molecule expression25. B cells isolated from human cord blood demonstrated reduced expression of survival signals such as BCMA and BAFF-R compared to those isolated from adults26. The decreased presence of these molecules on newborn B cells is a barrier to generating a protective antibody response. Accompanying this, the BCMA ligand APRIL, which is needed for survival and differentiation into long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs)27, is decreased in the stromal cells of newborn bone marrow, further contributing to diminished long-lived antibody28.

The effectiveness of mRNA lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic has propelled exploration of this approach for other pathogens, including influenza virus. Recent studies aimed at optimizing the stability, efficacy, and delivery methodology of mRNA-based vaccines have significantly advanced this technology29. Multiple influenza vaccine phase I clinical trials employing the mRNA technology are currently underway in adults30. mRNA-LNP vaccines are notable in their ability to induce potent responses in populations that are difficult to effectively vaccinate, e.g., elderly individuals31. As noted above, young infants represent a population that has proven challenging with regard to generation of protective antibody against influenza virus with current seasonal vaccines. At present, mRNA-LNP vaccines are approved for those >6 months of age, where it has proven safe and effective32,33. While the utility of this vaccine in older infants is clear, whether it is effective in newborns, whose immune systems differ significantly from older infants, is unknown. The immunostimulatory potential of this platform makes it an attractive candidate for overcoming the immune alterations present in younger infants.

To explore this possibility, we utilized the highly translatable newborn African green monkey (AGM) model. We have utilized this model to study a variety of experimental influenza vaccines34–42. NHP represent the most relevant pre-clinical model available to investigate vaccine responses in the context of newborn immunity due to the high similarity between human and AGM immune function and lung physiology43,44, together with a prolonged period of infancy compared to small animal models, e.g., mice or ferrets. This allows for prime, boost, and challenge analyses during infancy. Furthermore, AGM are advantageous as a model for influenza virus due to their similarity to humans in sialic acid distribution in the respiratory tract45. Here, we report findings from the first assessment of this vaccine platform in the newborn NHP. We have performed a comprehensive analysis of antibody titer and function, antigen-specific B and T cell responses in the draining lymph node, and protection following challenge. We find hemagglutinin (HA) mRNA-LNP elicits robust responses in newborn AGM that possess a broad array, both neutralizing and non-neutralizing, of antibody effector activities. Critically, the vaccine-elicited immune response results in reduced viral load and disease following challenge. The promising results presented here support the continued pursuit of this vaccine as an effective approach for protecting newborns.

Results

A Ca09 HA mRNA-LNP vaccine generates high level HA-specific antibodies in newborn NHP

This study aimed to test the efficacy of an A/California/07/2009 H1N1 (Ca09) HA-expressing mRNA-LNP influenza vaccine in newborns. Three-to-five-day old (approximating a 12–20 day old human44) AGM were vaccinated intramuscularly (im) with 3 µg of Ca09 HA mRNA-LNP in the left and right deltoid region (totaling 6 µg). Delivery in both arms allowed for isolation and analysis of a vaccine-draining lymph node (LN) following boost, while ensuring that the response continues in the contralateral LN. Our control cohort of newborns received either PBS or a luciferase-expressing mRNA-LNP (Supplementary Table 1). An equal number of male and female animals were included in both groups. Newborns received a boost dose on d39 or 40 (d39/40) post vaccination (pv). The vaccination and sampling timeline is shown in Fig. 1A.

As a measure of systemic inflammation, we quantified C-reactive protein (CRP) in plasma obtained on d1pv. No significant increase in CRP levels was observed following HA mRNA-LNP administration (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Further, no negative impact on body weight or temperature was detected across the study (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Inspection of the vaccine site over the first 24 h showed no adverse reaction. These data suggest vaccination with the HA mRNA-LNP is well tolerated in newborn AGM.

We next evaluated HA-specific antibodies generated by vaccination. HA mRNA-LNP administration resulted in HA-specific IgM that further increased following boost (Fig. 1B). HA-specific IgG was also rapidly induced following the initial dose (d10pv) (Fig. 1C). IgG levels continued to increase through d39/40pv and reached maximum levels at d10/11 post boost (pb) (Fig. 1C). Male and female newborns did not differ in the magnitude of the antibody response. While widely considered a benefit of the platform, the high level of antigen expression and immunostimulatory properties of mRNA-LNP vaccines have been speculated to potentially promote autoantibody generation46. We found no evidence of increases in anti-nuclear IgG and IgM in vaccinated newborns (Supplementary Fig. 1D, E). Together, these results show that the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine induces a rapid and robust HA-specific antibody response in newborn AGM with no indication of adverse effects.

Vaccination of newborn NHP with Ca09 HA mRNA-LNP induces stem binding antibodies that are broadly reactive against group 1 HA molecules

The generation of stem-reactive antibodies is a sought-after goal in next generation influenza vaccine development. The HA stem region is a target of such responses as it is relatively conserved compared to the head, where mutations in HA most often occur47. Robust induction of stem-specific antibodies through vaccination could provide protection against both drifted and pandemic strains of influenza virus.

Generating stem-specific antibodies through vaccination has historically been challenging, as the head region is immunodominant48,49. Interestingly, administration of an HA mRNA-LNP vaccine to adult mice resulted in improved responses to the stem region50. Thus we asked whether this platform could drive stem-specific responses in newborn AGM, using a headless HA stem construct to quantify these antibodies51. Newborns exhibited robust induction of stem-specific IgG (Fig. 2A). These antibodies were rapidly generated, significantly increasing by d10 following the initial vaccine dose and continuing to increase through d10/11pb (Fig. 2A). These data suggest that even in the presence of the immunodominant HA head, the mRNA-LNP platform can promote a strong stem-specific antibody response in newborns. To investigate the potential of these antibodies for broad HA recognition, we evaluated binding to a panel of group 1 (H1, H2, H5, H9) and group 2 (H3, H7) HA molecules. Homosubtypic H1 and heterosubtypic H5 and H9 molecules were recognized at significant levels (H2 trended higher vs. controls but did not meet significance, p = 0.07) (Fig. 2B). Group 2 molecules were not above controls (Fig. 2B). This finding is not unexpected given previous studies showing that stem-reactive antibodies elicited by H1 stem are predominantly restricted to recognition of group 1 HA molecules52. These data establish the ability of HA mRNA-LNP to efficiently induce both head and stem-specific IgG antibodies in newborns that are broadly reactive against group 1 HA molecules.

Boosting with the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine drives improved antibody avidity

A significant benefit of secondary antigen exposure is increased antibody avidity53,54. This process is often the result of selection of B cells with higher affinity in the GC. Given previous data showing newborns are reduced in their ability to form GC, we examined whether a second dose of vaccine would drive increases in avidity. Boosting with HA mRNA-LNP significantly increased the avidity of HA-specific IgG antibody presents at d41/45pb (Fig. 3A), consistent with affinity maturation and thus higher quality antibody.

Vaccination of newborn AGM with HA mRNA-LNP results in antibody with a broad range of effector functions

Mounting evidence supports the contribution of multiple antibody functions in the control of influenza virus infection including neutralization, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD), and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)55–60.

Neutralization is the most frequently assessed and has long been evaluated as a correlate of protective responses. Microneutralization (MN) assays comprehensively assess neutralizing activity, i.e., inhibition of both infection and spread. Using this approach, we found that all vaccinated newborns had neutralizing antibodies at d41/45pb (Fig. 3B). Five of the eight animals had detectable neutralizing antibodies following a single vaccine dose and boosting drove further increases in neutralizing titers. HAI activity, which assesses antibodies that block the receptor binding site on HA, was present in the plasma of all infants by d39/40pv and was also significantly increased following boost (Fig. 3C).

We next explored the ability of HA mRNA-LNP to induce antibodies that can promote non-neutralizing activities, i.e., ADCC, ADCD, and ADCP. Antibodies present following the initial dose exhibited ADCP activity that was significantly increased relative to controls (Fig. 3D). Although ADCD and ADCC activity were also elevated at this time, the increase did not reach significance until following the boost dose, which resulted in a significant rise in all three of the non-neutralizing effector functions (Fig. 3D–F). These data show the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine generates an antibody response capable of neutralizing, as well as a broad array of non-neutralizing functions.

Vaccination with HA mRNA-LNP results in increased HA-specific IgG+ B cells in the draining lymph node at d10/11 following boost

Given our finding of significantly increased antibody quality following the boost dose of HA mRNA-LNP, we evaluated B cell activation and maturation in the draining axillary LN (AxLN) on d10/11pb. HA-specific B cells were identified using well-described fluorescently labeled HA probes from Ca09 (H1) and Indo05 (H5)51,61. Cells with B cell receptors (BCRs) that bind HA head are H1+H5-, while those that bind stem are H1+H5+ (Supplementary Fig. 2). On d10/11 pb, we detected a significantly higher number of head-specific IgG+ B cells in the AxLN of vaccinated newborns relative to controls (Fig. 4A). There was no detectable difference in head-specific IgM+ cells between vaccinated and control groups (SupplementaryFig. 3A). This aligns with the expectation that a boost dose drives IgG class switching. We next assessed the stem-specific B cell responses by gating on the H1+H5+ B cell populations. As with the head-specific response, there was no difference in the number of IgM+ stem-specific B cells between the vaccinated and control groups. There was an appreciable trend towards increased IgG+ stem-specific B cells in the vaccinated cohort, but this did not reach significance (p = 0.109) (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Vaccination with HA mRNA-LNP is associated with decreased numbers of T regulatory cells and T follicular regulatory cells

Among the immune alterations that impact newborns is the presence of an increased regulatory T cell population (Tregs)62–64, including T follicular regulatory (Tfr) cells65. Tfr are a subset of regulatory cells that has been reported to influence the size and specificity of the GC response66. Although the impact of Tfr-mediated GC is incompletely understood in humans, studies in mice showed Tfr cells can inhibit proliferation and cytokine expression in Tfh (non-Tfr) cells as well as class switching and antibody production67,68.

Given these findings, we asked whether the vaccine-induced environment in the AxLN altered the presence of Treg or Tfr populations (for gating, see Supplementary Fig. 4). Tregs were identified by FoxP3 expression. Vaccination of newborns with HA mRNA-LNP was associated with reduced FoxP3+ T cells present at d10/11pb (Fig. 4B). This included a significant reduction in Tfr (Fig. 4C). These data raise the intriguing possibility that a decrease in the number of regulatory populations may contribute to the ability of mRNA-LNP vaccines to generate a potent immune response in newborns.

Analysis of Tfh in the draining lymph node following boost

Given the important role Tfh cells play in B cell maturation and efficient IgG production19–22, we were interested in whether HA mRNA-LNP could promote these responses in newborns. We first quantified Tfh cells in the draining lymph node (dLN). No significant difference in the total number of Tfh was found (Supplementary Fig. 5). This may reflect a failure to generate these responses, their low representation that did not significantly impact the total Tfh number, or egress of these cells from the LN. Given the limitation in dLN sample available for further Tfh analysis, we assessed antigen-specific Tfh cells in circulation by measuring IL-21, the hallmark cytokine produced by Tfh given the reported correlation between circulating Tfh and GC/antibody responses69,70. PBMC collected d10/11pb were stimulated with a Ca09 HA peptide pool. Seven of eight vaccinated animals had detectable HA-specific Tfh cells (Fig. 4D). This finding suggests Tfh are generated in the vaccinated newborns, although the response at this timepoint appears to be modest. This would be in keeping with the reported challenge in newborns in developing robust Tfh responses17,18.

Vaccination with HA mRNA-LNP reduces viral burden and pathology in the lungs of newborns following influenza virus challenge

Our findings thus far have demonstrated the ability of the HA mRNA-LNP influenza vaccine to generate a high level of functional, HA-specific antibodies capable of diverse clearance activities in newborns. To interrogate the protective capacity of these responses, we challenged the newborns with the homologous Ca09 virus. Viral genomes were quantified by qRT-PCR in tracheal wash (TW) obtained on days 2, 4, and 7 post challenge (pc) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) obtained on d7pc. Vaccinated newborns exhibited decreased viral burden in both the trachea (Fig. 5A (viral load each day) and B (AUC across the time course)) and the lung ( > 30-fold reduction) at this time (Fig. 5C). These data strongly support the ability of the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine to induce responses that reduce viral burden in newborns following challenge.

Limiting damage in the lung is an important component of the protective capacity of a vaccine. To evaluate disease, a blinded assessment (pneumonia lesions, edema, inflammatory infiltration, and lesion distribution) of the lung was performed at d7pc. HA mRNA-LNP vaccinated newborns had significantly reduced disease compared to control animals (Fig. 5D). We also measured the level of IL-6 as this cytokine has been correlated with increased disease severity during influenza virus infection in children71. Vaccinated newborns had significantly reduced levels of IL-6 in the BAL compared to the control cohort (Fig. 5E). Taken together, our post-challenge analyses demonstrate that vaccination with HA mRNA-LNP provides significant protection to newborns.

Protective responses on day 7 post-challenge correlate with the number of IgG + HA head-specific B cells in the draining lymph node at d10/11pb

The comprehensive analyses described above provided an opportunity to probe immune outcomes that may be associated with increased protection. To this end, we generated a Spearman correlation matrix to identify vaccine-induced responses that correlated with disease outcomes (Fig. 6). This analysis revealed that the overall number of H1 + H5-IgG+ B cells in the LN at d10/11pb correlated with multiple aspects of protection, including TW AUC (Fig. 7A), viral titer in the BAL (Fig. 7B), and disease score (Fig. 7C). We appreciate that our correlation analysis may be limited by small sample size as several parameters neared significance, allowing for the possibility that increased sample sizes resulting in increased power would have revealed additional significant correlations. However, our findings from the correlation analysis suggest antigen-specific B cells in the draining site following boost have a strong predictive value for the protective capacity imparted by this vaccine in newborn AGM.

Discussion

Current influenza vaccination strategies have limited immunogenicity in newborns. As a result, there is no licensed vaccine for this vulnerable population. Given that newborns are disproportionately susceptible to severe disease and death caused by influenza virus infection, an effective vaccine for this population would represent a major advance. mRNA-LNP vaccines have shown efficacy across many age groups, ranging from older infants to the elderly, for an array of mRNA-LNP vaccines (SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, ZIKA, etc.)11,32,72–74. This includes a study wherein 2-month-old infant NHPs were vaccinated with a SARS-CoV-2 mRNA-LNP vaccine, which resulted in protection out to one year following vaccination73. Two-month-old NHP infants approximate an 8-month-old human infant, reflecting the age group for which mRNA-LNP vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 have recently been approved75. This important study provided early data that this platform could be safe and effective in infants. Here we continued to explore this platform, asking whether it could serve as a vaccine for infants at a much earlier point following birth. It is clear that the immune system steadily evolves over the first year of life76. As a result, the responsiveness of newborns is distinct from that of older infants, posing additional hurdles for eliciting a protective immune response. Here, we utilized our highly translatable newborn AGM model, the most similar animal model with regard to newborn lung physiology and immune system77–82, to assess the efficacy of an HA-expressing mRNA-LNP vaccine in the setting of the newborn immune environment. This study demonstrates the ability of an HA expressing mRNA-LNP vaccine to mitigate the reduced vaccine responsiveness found in newborns.

We find newborns vaccinated with HA mRNA-LNP generated high levels of HA head and, notably, stem-reactive antibodies. Furthermore, vaccine-elicited antibodies displayed broad recognition across a panel of group 1 HA molecules, supporting the potential benefit for infection with divergent strains of influenza virus. This vaccine drove substantial levels of high-quality antibody that not only had neutralizing activity, but also a broad array of potent non-neutralizing activities. These data are compelling given the increasing number of studies demonstrating the protective potential of antibodies that function outside of neutralization56,57,59,83–85. The vaccine-induced response resulted in enhanced protection in these newborns following challenge with influenza virus. While these results are highly encouraging, the limited Tfh response detected in animals suggests this may be an area for improvement in next-generation vaccines that employ this platform. Nonetheless, our findings demonstrate the ability of this vaccine platform to induce a rapid and robust antibody pool possessing multiple mechanisms that can contribute to protection in this vulnerable population.

Our investigation of the dLN at d10/11pb revealed that the HA mRNA-LNP vaccine was associated with a reduced number of Tregs and Tfr cells. This was of significance, as increased numbers of Tregs have been shown to play a role in the tolerogenic immune state present in newborns86–89. Tregs have been implicated in the diminished vaccine responses observed in young infants87. In adult mice, vaccination with OVA323–339 peptide was associated with an increase in the number of regulatory T cells90. However, addition of CpG diminished the generation of these cells. It is possible that the immunostimulatory effects of the mRNA-LNP platform mimic these TLR agonist effects. Our findings lead to an intriguing model where the potent antibody responses observed in vaccinated newborn AGM may, in part, be the result of the ability of the vaccine to limit the number of regulatory T cells in the draining lymph nodes. It is tempting to speculate that the environment created by the mRNA-LNP may decrease the reported propensity of newborn cells to differentiate along the Treg pathway91.

Given the array of antibody response parameters assessed in our study, we sought to understand which of these might correlate with protection. Our analysis revealed the number of head-specific IgG+ B cells in the dLN site at d10/11pb was more strongly correlated with reduced disease severity following challenge (r = -0.621, p = 0.034) compared to neutralization (r = -0.364, p = 0.366), HAI (r = 0.290, p = 0.479), or antibody effector function (ADCC r = -0.346, p = 0.401, ADCP r = -0.299, p = 0.471, ADCD r = -0.252, p = 0.549). Although we cannot draw causative inferences from these data, we speculate that memory B cells generated as a result of vaccination and boost are rapidly recalled in the lung-draining lymph node after infection. The rapid recall of influenza-specific B cells is supported by a study showing influenza-specific plasmablasts peak in the blood at 7-14 days post-vaccination92. The results of this analysis suggest vaccine-elicited memory B cells may be a crucial contributor to protection against disease caused by influenza virus infection in newborns.

Our study does have limitations, including the lack of maternal antibodies present in the newborns at the time of vaccination. We acknowledge this limitation, but highlight that the goal of this study was to determine whether the mRNA-LNP platform could induce potent responses in the face of the altered newborn immune system. Our evaluation here is a critical and necessary step in exploring this vaccine approach for use in this age group. With that said, it is noteworthy that in mice, the mRNA-LNP platform appears to mitigate the negative regulation that can be imparted by maternal antibody72. We are also cognizant of the cohort size. However, despite the small number and the outbred nature of the NHP model, our study demonstrated robust vaccine responses across both males and females, further supporting the potent immunostimulatory capacity of this vaccine platform and its potential for use in the human population. The lack of a comparator group that received the licensed seasonal influenza vaccine is also a limitation of the current investigation. Such an analysis would be of interest as exploration of this platform in newborns continues. Furthermore, the vaccine assessed here expresses a single HA molecule, which differs from seasonal formulations, which are multivalent. Our primary goal here was to assess the immunogenicity and protective capacity of the mRNA-LNP platform in the newborn setting. Future studies utilizing multivalent formulations will be necessary to determine how this may impact the vaccine response. Finally, our analyses suggested this vaccine is well tolerated in the newborn AGM. However, further evaluation of this vaccine platform will be needed in humans, where additional assessments of the safety of the vaccine can be evaluated.

Overall, our data suggest this vaccine is effective in inducing a potent antibody response in newborns that is capable of limiting infection and disease. Our findings serve as foundational evidence of the ability of the mRNA-LNP vaccine platform to drive enhanced vaccine responses in this population and provide compelling evidence for the continued pursuit of the HA mRNA-LNPs as an effective vaccine for newborns.

Methods

Study design

African green monkeys (vervets) were housed, immunized, and sampled at the Vervet Research Colony at the Wake Forest University School of Medicine. Animals live in social groups. Each housing unit has indoor and outdoor areas. Newborn AGM were immunized at 3–5 days of age. To be enrolled, animals must be healthy as evaluated by a veterinarian and weigh at least 300 g. Infants were randomly assigned to PBS and luciferase mRNA-LNP (non-HA vaccinated control, “Ctrl”) (n = 5) and Ca09 HA mRNA-LNP (n = 8). Animal assignments ensured an equal number of males and females in each vaccine group. Newborns received 3 μg Ca09 HA mRNA-LNP (Weissman Lab, University of Pennsylvania) intramuscularly in the deltoid muscle of both left and right arms (6 μg of HA mRNA-LNP total dose). Vaccine was produced as previously described93. Animals were boosted with the same dose at d39/40pv. Plasma samples were collected at d1pv, d10pv, d39/40pv, d10/11pb, d41/45pb, and d7pc. Body weights and temperatures were also measured at all sampling time points. On d41/45pb newborn AGM were challenged with 1 × 107 TCID50 of A/California/07/2009 H1N1 (Ca09) (BEI Resources). TW samples were harvested at d2, 4, and 7pc, and BAL was harvested at d7pc. Isoflurane was used as an anesthetic and was administered as an inhalant (2-5% inhalant) during blood draws. Prior to euthanasia, infants were administered an intramuscular dose of ketamine (10 mg/kg) as a sedative in addition to isoflurane (2–5% inhalant) followed by an intravenous injection of a lethal dose (100 mg/kg) of sodium pentobarbital. BAL samples were acquired after infants had been euthanized.

ELISA for the detection of antibody

Analysis of HA molecule or Ca09 stabilized trimeric stem (NIH VRC)-specific antibodies was performed by ELISA. Microplates (96-half well) (Greiner bio-one) were coated with 100 ng/well of trimeric recombinant full-length HA proteins: A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (H1) (BEI Resources, NR-48873), A/California/07/2009 H1(BEI Resources, NR-42635), A/Puerto Rico/08/1934 (H1) (BEI Resources, NR-19240), A/Singapore/1/1957 (H2) (BEI Resources, NR-52249), A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (H3) (BEI Resources, NR-49237), A/Vietnam/1204/2004 (H5) (VRC, NIH), A/Anhui/1/2013 (H7) (BEI Resources, NR-44365), and A/Hong Kong/33982/2009 (H9) (BEI Resources, NR-41792) HA stem in sodium carbonate/bicarbonate coating buffer (pH 9.5) overnight at 4°C. Plates were blocked with 1X blocking buffer (10X Casein Blocking Buffer, Sigma-Aldrich) plus 2% goat serum (Lampire Biologicals) for 1 hour and then washed with buffer containing PBS with 0.1% Tween 20. Plasma samples were serially diluted in 1X blocking buffer. Wells that contained no antigen served as a negative control. HRP-conjugated antibodies specific for monkey IgM (Fitzgerald, #43R-IG074hrp) (1:10,000) and IgG (Fitzgerald, #43C-CB1603) (1:5,000) were used to detect bound antibodies. Plates were developed using 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine dihydrochloride (TMB) (Sigma-Aldrich), and subsequently, the reaction was stopped with 2 N sulfuric acid. Plates were read at 450 nm on a BioTek Elx800 Microplate Reader. For each dilution, the OD450 from the non-antigen-coated wells was subtracted from the antigen-coated wells. Threshold titer was defined as the value that reached 3X the assay background, i.e., wells that only received 1X blocking buffer + coating antigen (no sample).

Microneutralization assay

Microneutralization assays were performed on Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells as previously described94. Briefly, MDCK cells were plated at 4 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium plus GlutaMAX with 7% fetal calf serum and allowed to reach confluency. Plasma samples were heat inactivated and 2-fold dilutions incubated with 100 TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose) of H1N1 A/California/07/2009 influenza virus. Samples were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Following incubation, plasma and virus mixtures were added to the MDCK cells in quadruplicate, then maintained at 37 °C. Cytopathic effects were assessed after 5 days by crystal violet staining following methanol fixation.

Hemagglutination Inhibition assay

AGM plasma was treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (SEIKEN) of Vibrio cholera filtrate overnight at 37 °C. Samples were then heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 45 min. Samples were then brought to final starting dilution (1:10) using sterile saline. The RDE-treated plasma was then serially diluted and incubated with 8 HAU/well of Ca09 for 30 min before transferring to turkey red blood cells (tRBC) (Lampire Biologicals) for 45 min in U-bottom plate at room temperature. HAI titers were determined by the last column of wells where tRBCs formed a button. Samples were run in quadruplicate.

Antibody-Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity

KHYG-1 cells transduced with rhesus macaque CD1695 were used to perform ADCC assays [adapted from96]. ELISA plates (Corning) were coated with 50 µL (2 µg/mL in PBS) of recombinant HA protein (A/California/07/09) (BEI Resources) and left to incubate at 4°C overnight. Plates were washed, and then blocked with PBS + 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 30 minutes. Blocking solution was removed and heat-inactivated plasma samples were serially diluted (starting dilution 1:10) and left to incubate at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h. Plates were washed, and then 50 µL (1×105 cells/well) KHYG-1 MmCD16 cells were added in media (RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% L-glut, 0.2% Primocin). Recombinant human IL-2 (Biolegend, #589104) (10 U/mL, 50 µg/mL) was added to cells. CD107a PE (Biolegend, #328608) (1:50) was added to 25 µL of warmed media and then added to plates containing cells. Golgi Plug (1:1000) and Golgi Stop (1:1500) (BD Biosciences) were added to cells in 25 µL of warmed media. Cells (100 µL total) were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 5 hours. Cells were transferred to a 96 V-bottom plate, washed with PBS, and stained with Zombie Violet (Biolegend) for 15 mins at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Cells were washed with PBS + 1% FBS (FMF) and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA). ADCC was assessed using flow cytometry on a BD X20 Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with BD FACSDiva Software (BD Biosciences). The endpoint titer for ADCC was defined as the first plasma dilution where the percentage of CD107a+ cells was ≥ 5%.

Antibody dependent cellular phagocytosis

THP-1 phagocytosis of HA-coated beads was performed as previously described97. Biotinylated HA A/California/07/2009 H1 (Duke CIVIC Vaccine Center, Core of NIAID Collaborative Influenza Vaccine Innovation Centers (CIVICs)) was adsorbed onto 1.0 µm of fluorescently labeled beads (FluoSpheres™ NeutrAvidin™) (yellow-green fluorescent (505/515)) at a ratio of 10 µg protein to 10 µl beads and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Then, in a 96-round bottom plate, 10 µl of HA-coated beads was incubated with 10 µl of 2-fold heat-inactivated plasma dilutions (starting dilution 1:800) diluted in PBS for 2 hours at 37 °C. Any unbound antibody was washed away, and THP-1 cells (RPMI + 10% FBS + 1% L-glut, 1% P/S) (5 × 104 cells/well) were added and incubated for 16 hours at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with 2% PFA. Phagocytosis was assessed using flow cytometry on a BD X20 Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with BD FACSDiva Software (BD Biosciences). Phagocytic scores were determined by the (bead-positive cells x the geometric mean)/1000 at 1:800 dilution.

Antibody dependent complement deposition

Antibody-dependent complement deposition of HA-coated beads was performed as previously described98. HA-coated 1 μm non-fluorescent NeutrAvidin™ beads were prepared as in the ADCP assay and incubated with diluted, heat-inactivated plasma samples (2-fold dilutions, starting dilution 1:5) for 2 h at 37 °C. Lyophilized guinea pig complement (Cedarlane, #CL4051) was reconstituted in ice-cold dH20, then diluted in 1:60 veronal buffer with 0.1% gelatin (GVB + +, Boston BioProducts, #IBB-300X) added. Next, 150 μl of the diluted complement was added to the opsonized beads and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. Beads were washed with 15 mM EDTA and stained with anti-guinea pig C3-FITC (MP Biomedicals, #855385). Samples were washed, and ADCD was assessed using flow cytometry on a BD X20 Fortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with BD FACSDiva Software (BD Biosciences). Complement scores were calculated using the (percentage of C3+ beads x GMFI)/1000 at 1:20 dilution.

Flow cytometry for the detection of Ca09 HA B cell responses

Draining axillary lymph nodes were thawed, and 1 ×106 cells were added to round bottom 96-round bottom plates for staining. Cells were pelleted and stained with the Zombie Aqua fixable viability dye (BioLegend) to exclude dead cells. Subsequently, 2.5 µg of Fc block (BD Biosciences, #564220) was added to prevent nonspecific binding. To identify influenza HA head- and HA stem-specific B cells, the cells were stained with fluorescently labeled HA probes: A/California/07/2009 (Ca09) (H1) and A/Indonesia/05/2005 (IN05) (H5). HA stem-specific B cells were identified as double-positive (H1⁺H5⁺), while HA head-specific B cells stained positive only for the H1 probe (H1⁺H5⁻)51,61. Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, #563794) was used to optimize staining.

The surface antibody panel included: BUV395 anti-IgM (G20-127) (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), BV510 anti-IgG (G18-145) (BD Biosciences), FITC anti-CD38 (AT-1) (STEMCELL), PeVIO770 anti-CD3 (10D12) (Miltenyi Biotech), AF700 anti-CD20 (2H7) (BD Biosciences), V450 anti-CD45 (D058-1283) (BD Biosciences), BV786 anti-CD11b (M1/70) (BD Biosciences), PE conjugated H1 Probe, and APC conjugated H5 probe.

Intracellular staining was then performed to assess Ki67 and BCL-6 expression and intracellular binding of HA probes to BCRs/antibodies. Antibodies used for intracellular staining include: BUV395 anti-IgM (G20-127) (BD Biosciences), BV510 anti-IgG (G18-145) (BD Biosciences), BUV737 anti-Ki67 (B56) (BD Biosciences), PerCpCY5.5 anti-BCL-6 (K112-91) (BD Biosciences), PE conjugated H1 Probe, and APC conjugated H5 probe. Samples were acquired on a BD LSR Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with BD FACSDiva Software (BD Biosciences).

Flow cytometry for the detection of Ca09 HA T cell responses

Draining axillary lymph nodes were thawed, and 1 ×106 cells were added to round bottom 96 flat bottom plates for stimulation in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1X non-essential amino acids, 100 U/ml penicillin/100 μg/ml streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES (Gibco), 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma Aldrich) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Atlanta Biologics). These primers were designed as part of a multiplex assay to quantify SARS-CoV-2, Influenza B, and Influenza A viruses. For this study, only those primers specific for Influenza A viruses were used. Primers and probes of the CDC recommended sequence (see below) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The probe contained the reporter molecule 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) at the 5’ end with an internal ZEN™ quencher and the Iowa Black FQ quencher (IABkFQ) at the 3’ end. Immunohistochemical analysis on newborn AGM lung sections

Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a minimum of 24 hours at a volume of 1:20 ratio tissue to fixative. Tissues were then placed in labeled tissue processing cassettes and were moved to 70% ethanol for holding and delivery. Cassettes were loaded onto an automated vacuum infiltration tissue processing instrument (Excelsior AS) (Fisher Scientific). Samples were run on an overnight program as follows: 80% ethanol – holding solution until processing cycle begins, 85% ethanol – begins dehydration, 90% ethanol – continues dehydration, 95% ethanol - continues dehydration, 100% ethanol – removal of all not bound water in the samples, 100% ethanol - continues removal of all not bound water in the samples, 100% ethanol – completes dehydration of samples, Xylene – begins removal of all ethanol in the samples, Xylene – continues clearing of samples, Xylene – completes clearing of samples. Infiltration Paraffin – heated to 60-61°C to remain melted. Samples were removed from the tissue processor and were embedded in metal molds creating paraffin blocks for sectioning. Paraffin blocks were sectioned on a Leica RM 2255 semi-automatic microtome at 4 microns. Sections were mounted from a warm water bath on labeled glass slides and air-dried overnight. Slide drying and H&E staining were then performed on a Leica Spectra ST automatic stainer (Leica Biosystems, IL) according to the following program: Oven drying at 60°C (1 hour), Xylene for deparaffinization (2 changes), 100% Ethanol to remove xylene (2 changes), 95% Ethanol to introduce water to sections (1 change), Run tap water rinse, Gill’s Hematoxylin II (Poly Scientific R&D Corp), run tap water to rinse, ammonia water (bluing solution for hematoxylin), run tap water rinse, Eosin / Phloxine Stain, 95% Ethanol, 100% Ethanol for dehydration (2 changes), Xylene (2 changes), last Xylene – slides remained here until coverslips were applied using Permount Mounting Medium (# SP15-500, Fisher Scientific). Finished slides dried at room temperature overnight to secure the coverslips. One section of Hematoxylin and Eosin-stained vervet lung taken from the left and right cranial, middle, and caudal lobes were examined by light microscopy in a blinded fashion using a custom scoring system developed by two veterinary pathologists. Pneumonia lesions were scored and graded based on their distribution throughout the pulmonary parenchyma, lesion patterns, consolidation, and disease stage. The distribution score was defined as normal (0), <25 = 1, 26-50 = 2, 51-75 = 3, and >75% = 4 of parenchyma affected. Lesion pattern scores were defined as normal (0), focal (1), multifocal (2), coalescing (3), or diffuse (4). The product of the distribution score and pattern score were added to the stage score for a total lesion score. Disease stage was defined as normal (0), Emerging (1), Early (2), Progressive (3), and Established (4). The maximum score was then ranked into a Final Grade (FG) (0-16) to reflect overall disease severity.

IL-6 quantification

IL-6 was quantified using a human inflammatory cytokine bead array (BD Biosciences) performed on BAL collected from newborns d7pc per the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were acquired on a BD Fortessa X20 (BD Biosciences); data were analyzed using FCAP Array software (BD Biosciences).

C-Reactive Protein Quantification

C-reactive protein levels in the plasma were assessed at 24 h post vaccination using a human C-Reactive Protein ELISA kit KR9710s (30-9710S) (ALPCO Diagnostics) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The plate was read at 450 nm on a BioTek Elx800 Microplate Reader. Amounts were calculated based on the standard curve generated using the control provided in the kit.

ELISA for the Detection of Monkey Anti-Nuclear Antibodies

Monkey anti-nuclear IgM (# 670-115-ANM) and IgG (# 670-110-ANM) (Alpha Diagnostic International) were detected as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma samples were diluted at 1:50. The monkey ANA IgM and IgG ELISA kits is based on the binding of monkey antibodies to extractable nuclear antigens (ENA). ENA antigen in these kits is a combination of several nuclear autoantigens (dsDNA, SSA/Ro, SSB/LA, Scl70, Sm, RNP, and Jo-1). Plates were read at 450 nm on an Elx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader (BioTek). The dotted line represents the limit of detection of the assay (blank).
