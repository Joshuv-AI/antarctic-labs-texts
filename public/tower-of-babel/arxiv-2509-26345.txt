SafeBehavior : Simulating Human-Like Multistage Reasoning to Mitigate Jailbreak Attacks in Large Language Models Journal: Neural Networks Note: These authors contributed equally to this work.
Qinjian Zhao \fnmark [1], Jiaqi Wang \fnmark [1], Zhiqiang Gao, Zhihao Dou, Belal Abuhaija, Kaizhu Huang

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved impressive performance across diverse natural language processing tasks, but their growing power also amplifies potential risks such as jailbreak attacks that circumvent built-in safety mechanisms. Existing defenses including input paraphrasing, multi step evaluation, and safety expert models often suffer from high computational costs, limited generalization, or rigid workflows that fail to detect subtle malicious intent embedded in complex contexts. Inspired by cognitive science findings on human decision making, we propose SafeBehavior , a novel hierarchical jailbreak defense mechanism that simulates the adaptive multistage reasoning process of humans. SafeBehavior decomposes safety evaluation into three stages: intention inference to detect obvious input risks, self introspection to assess generated responses and assign confidence based judgments, and self revision to adaptively rewrite uncertain outputs while preserving user intent and enforcing safety constraints. We extensively evaluate SafeBehavior against five representative jailbreak attack types including optimization based, contextual manipulation, and prompt based attacks and compare it with seven state of the art defense baselines. Experimental results show that SafeBehavior significantly improves robustness and adaptability across diverse threat scenarios, offering an efficient and human inspired approach to safeguarding LLMs against jailbreak attempts.

2
Related work

2.1
Jailbreak Attack

Jailbreak attacks are a form of adversarial prompting that aim to circumvent the built-in safety safeguards of LLMs, manipulating them into producing prohibited or harmful outputs
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Gong et al. 2025
)
. Jailbreak attacks against LLMs can be broadly categorized into two major classes: white-box attacks and black-box attacks, depending on the attacker’s level of access to the model. White-box attacks assume full or partial access to internal model parameters (e.g., gradients, logits, weights), while black-box attacks operate solely through input-output queries without any knowledge of the model internals. Each category encompasses several sub-classes with distinct methodologies and representative works.

White-box attacks exploit internal knowledge of the target large language model (LLM) to craft effective adversarial prompts and are typically categorized into three subtypes. First, gradient-based attacks append or optimize adversarial suffixes by leveraging model gradients to induce harmful responses. Representative methods include GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, AutoDAN
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
, ARCA
(
Jones et al. 2023
)
, and ASETF
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
. Second, logits-based attacks manipulate the prompt to steer the output token distribution toward unsafe generations, as demonstrated in methods such as COLD
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
, DSN
(
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
, and decoding manipulation strategies
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
. Third, attacks based on fine-tuning retrain the LLM on a small set of malicious examples to undermine its safety alignment. Effective strategies include LoRA-based tuning
(
Lermen et al. 2023
)
, data poisoning
(
Zhan et al. 2023
)
, and few-shot jailbreak fine-tuning
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. While these methods are highly effective, they require substantial access to model internals, limiting their practicality in restricted deployment settings.

Black-box attacks, in contrast to white-box methods, do not require access to the internal parameters or architecture of the target LLM and instead rely solely on input-output interactions to craft adversarial prompts. These attacks can be broadly categorized into three subtypes. First, template completion attacks embed harmful intents within benign task templates to bypass safety filters, with representative techniques including scenario nesting (e.g., DeepInception
(
Li et al. 2024
)
), context-based prompting (e.g., ICA
(
Wei et al. 2023
)
, Pandora
(
Deng et al. 2024b
)
), and code injection strategies (e.g., CodeChameleon
(
Lv et al. 2024
)
). Second, prompt rewriting attacks transform malicious prompts into less detectable forms by employing ciphers (e.g., CipherChat
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
), low-resource or underrepresented languages
(
Deng et al. 2024c
)
, and genetic algorithm-based mutation frameworks (e.g., AutoDAN-HGA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
). Third, LLM-based generation attacks leverage the generative capabilities of LLMs themselves to autonomously produce effective jailbreak prompts. These methods range from single-model pipelines (e.g., MASTERKEY [Deng et al., 2023]) to collaborative multi-agent systems (e.g., PAIR
(
Chao et al. 2025
)
, Evil Geniuses
(
Tian et al. 2023
)
) in which LLMs take on roles such as generator, evaluator, and optimizer. Collectively, black-box attacks underscore the increasing feasibility of adversarial prompt construction even in settings where model internals remain inaccessible.

2.2
Defense of jailbreak attack

To address the growing threat of jailbreak attacks on LLMs, researchers have proposed a variety of defense strategies. These methods can be broadly categorized into
prompt-level defenses
and
model-level defenses
, depending on whether the defense involves modifying the model parameters. Prompt-level defenses operate by detecting or manipulating user inputs without altering the model itself, while model-level defenses aim to fundamentally enhance the model’s robustness through fine-tuning or internal behavior analysis. In what follows, we summarize both categories and review representative techniques within each.

Prompt-level defenses operate by manipulating or analyzing input prompts to prevent jailbreak attacks without modifying the underlying model. These approaches are lightweight and suitable for black-box settings. One major line of work is prompt detection, which identifies adversarial prompts based on heuristics such as perplexity or sequence length
(
Yi et al. 2024a
;
Hu et al. 2023
;
Alon and Kamfonas 2023b
)
. For example, LightGBM
(
Alon and Kamfonas 2023b
)
trains a classifier using prompt-level statistics to detect malicious queries. Another common strategy is prompt perturbation, which applies transformations -such as token masking
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
, character-level edits
(
Rao et al. 2024
)
, or semantic rewriting
(
Ji et al. 2024
;
Jain et al. 2023a
)
- to generate variants of the input and observe output divergence. Methods like JailGuard
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
and erase-and-check
(
Kumar et al. 2024
)
further improve reliability by examining consistency under these perturbations. A third line of defense is system prompt safeguard, which strengthens the internal instructions of the model to reinforce safe behavior. Techniques such as SMEA
(
Zou et al. 2024
)
employ genetic algorithms to optimize robust system prompts, while others embed secret tokens to resist fine-tuning-based attacks
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
. Although prompt-level defenses are efficient, they remain vulnerable to adversarial obfuscation techniques such as cipher prompts or multilingual rewrites
(
Deng et al. 2024c
;
Yuan et al. 2024
)
, and thus are often combined with model-level defenses.

Model-level defenses, by contrast, aim to enhance the intrinsic safety alignment of the LLM through fine-tuning or inference-time control. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) augments the model with safety-aligned data to improve its rejection of harmful prompts
(
Bianchi et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2024a
)
. For example, Vicuna-Safe
(
Bhardwaj and Poria 2023
)
uses Chain-of-Utterance-style conversations to teach the model to refuse unsafe requests, while adversarial prompt mining techniques construct safety datasets from red-teaming interactions. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is another widely adopted method, used in models like Claude and GPT-4
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, where human preferences guide model behavior through a reward model. Alternatives like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Gallego 2024
)
have emerged as lightweight substitutes to traditional RLHF. Additionally, gradient and logit analysis approaches analyze internal activations during decoding, identifying harmful intent via gradient similarity
(
Xie et al. 2024
)
or modifying token probabilities via mixed-logit decoding
(
Xu et al. 2024b
)
. Refinement-based defenses rely on the model’s self-correction ability, prompting it to iteratively revise potentially unsafe responses
(
Phute et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
. Finally, proxy defenses delegate safety checks to external models, such as LlamaGuard
(
Chi et al. 2024
)
or AutoDefense
(
Zeng et al. 2024
)
, which monitor prompts and responses to prevent misuse. While model-level defenses are more computationally intensive, they provide significantly stronger protection against sophisticated jailbreak attacks.

3
Problem Statement

Threat model:

The threat model adopted in our work aligns with the methodology proposed in previous research
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Gong et al. 2025
)
.
Specifically, we assume a powerful adversary capable of crafting arbitrary prompts and submitting them to LLMs to launch jailbreak attacks. These attacks can be broadly categorized into three types: optimization-based attacks, which iteratively generate adversarial prompts using automated or gradient-guided methods to maximize the likelihood of harmful model outputs; contextual manipulation attacks, which construct specific conversational scenarios or role identities (e.g., inducing the model to play a “hacker” or triggering a “developer mode”) to bypass built-in safety constraints; and prompt-based attacks, which embed adversarial instructions or manipulative queries directly into the input to override system prompts or elicit internal configurations (such as prompt injection and prompt leakage). In this study, we adopt a fully permissive prompt construction setting, allowing the adversary to generate diverse and complex malicious prompts. This setup enables a comprehensive evaluation of our method’s robustness and safety under highly complex and varied jailbreak scenarios.

Defender’s knowledge and goal:

Our primary goal is to design a reliable inference strategy for LLMs to defend against jailbreak attacks. This strategy simulates human-like reasoning and detects potential jailbreak attempts through adaptive analysis, without relying on any prior knowledge of the attacker. The proposed detection mechanism is expected to meet the following key objectives:

•

Robust output integrity:

In non-adversarial settings (i.e., when there is no attacker interference), the model should produce outputs consistent with normal inference behavior, ensuring task performance remains unaffected.

•

Effective threat mitigation:

In adversarial scenarios, the model should be able to detect and analyze maclious prompts introduced by jailbreak attacks during the generation process, regardless of the attack type (e.g., prompt injection, optimized-based attacks) and mitigate their impact on the final response.

•

Efficient execution:

The defense strategy should maintain high efficiency, introducing minimal additional computational overhead, with inference time close to that of the standard, non-defended model.

4
Our
SafeBehavior

This work proposes
SafeBehavior
, a novel LLM reasoning defense framework inspired by the human cognitive process in handling inappropriate content. The method models a three-stage reasoning process: Intent inference, Self-introspection, and Self-revision, and integrates it directly into the LLM’s inference phase. Unlike previous static defense methods,
SafeBehavior
introduces a hierarchical defense strategy that dynamically detects and addresses potentially harmful prompts. When inappropriate content is identified during reasoning, the model can halt or engage in deeper reflection on its response accordingly. This design achieves both effective moderation and high efficiency by avoiding unnecessary overhead when there is no threat. The algorithm can be seen in Algorithm
1
.

4.1
Intent inference

Given a user query
q
, our initial task is to perform a coarse-grained assessment to determine whether the query exhibits malicious intent. This early-stage evaluation aims to quickly filter out questions that may potentially lead to policy-violating content, allowing the system to immediately terminate the response process.
Inspired by recent work
(
Yi et al. 2023
;
Phute et al. 2024
)
, we introduce a human-like intuitive judgment mechanism as the first line of defense, referred to as
Intent Inference
.

Specifically, the template
T_{u}
instructs the model to generate a self-summary of the input query
q
denoted as
S_{tr}
, using the LLM’s language understanding capabilities to extract the core goals and intentions from the query, rather than relying on superficial information influenced by potential disguises. The purpose of this is that some attackers may use code words, cross-lingual expressions, or additional irrelevant background to disguise harmful queries as harmless, making it difficult for direct judgment methods to distinguish them. Direct judgment methods are often limited by these disguises, leading to false positives where the query is misclassified as harmless. By first generating an intention summary, the model can filter out these distractions, enabling a more accurate judgment of the query’s true intent and effectively identifying potential harmful content, thus avoiding misjudgments caused by these disguises. In this process, the LLM relies on the potentially sensitive terms within the input query
q
and their contextual logic to outline the overall intent, while the way these terms are distributed and where they appear can subtly influence the final judgment. Once the input query
q
is determined to be harmful, the generation process is immediately terminated in order to prevent unsafe content from being further produced or propagated.

This stage excels at performing
early-stage risk screening
by leveraging shallow semantic and structural cues, without incurring the computational cost of deep contextual reasoning. It enables the model to flag potential jailbreak attempts efficiently and provides a reliable coarse-grained signal to guide subsequent stages of the multi-tier defense pipeline.

4.2
Self-introspection

For an input query
q
that passes the
Intention Inference
stage, the model can only semantically infer that the query is likely non-malicious.
However, some adversarially crafted input queries
q
may contain almost no harmful terms, or only a few sensitive expressions placed in secondary positions, yet they can still induce the LLM to generate inappropriate content. Such attacks are inherently covert, and relying solely on analyzing the input query often makes it difficult to uncover their true intent. Therefore, it becomes essential to further examine and analyze the model’s output response in order to more comprehensively identify and defend against potential attacks.

To address this, we consider the model’s response
r
, which is generated based on the input
q
. At this stage, a further fine-grained safety verification of
r
is required to ensure that the model output adheres to safety guidelines and we define this action step as
Self-introspection
as follow:

S_{r}=\mathrm{LLM}\bigl(T_{r}(r)\bigr),

(1)

where
T_{r}(\cdot)
and
S_{r}
represent the introspection template and the jailbreak confidence score, respectively. The template
T_{r}
prompts the model to perform self-introspection on the response
r
, generating a structured summary that includes a brief summary, harmful entities, potential impacts, possible security policy violations, supporting evidence, and confidence in judgment. This structured summary allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the output response, enabling the detection of potential harmful content and assessing whether the response adheres to predefined security policies. By breaking down the response into its components, such as harmful entities and supporting evidence, it provides a more detailed and systematic analysis of the response, making it easier to identify subtle risks that may otherwise go unnoticed.

By considering the summarized content, the LLM generates a confidence score
S_{r}
, providing a quantifiable measure of the model’s certainty about the response’s safety. This allows for more nuanced decision-making and better handling of uncertain cases. The confidence scores
S_{r}
range from 0 to 1. A score closer to 0 indicates higher safety, while a score closer to 1 reflects an increasing likelihood that the response has been compromised by a jailbreak attack.
To more effectively determine whether a response
r
triggers a jailbreak policy based on the confidence score
S_{r}
, we adopt a defensive strategy called the
Refusal Mechanism
. Specifically, we define a safety threshold
\tau
and a corresponding risk threshold
1-\tau
, which are used to decide whether the response should be refused. The details can be seen as:

\text{Output}=\begin{cases}\text{Refuse resposne}&\text{if }S_{r}>1-\tau,\\
\text{Return response $r$}&\text{if }S_{r}<\tau,\\
\text{Uncertainty and require furture action}&\text{Otherwise}.\end{cases}

The above decision rule defines the core logic of the
Refusal Mechanism
, which determines the appropriate action based on the jailbreak confidence score
S_{r}
. If
S_{r}<\tau
, the response
r
is considered safe and returned; if
S_{r}>1-\tau
, it is regarded as high-risk and thus refused. When
S_{r}
falls within the uncertain region between the safety threshold
\tau
and the risk threshold
1-\tau
, it typically indicates that the response
r
is not overtly harmful but difficult for the model to confidently judge. Such borderline cases are challenging: an outright refusal may lead to unnecessary rejection of acceptable content, while blind acceptance could risk overlooking subtle violations. To handle this delicately, the model engages in a human-like behavior called
Self-Revision
—revisiting and modifying the response
r
based on the original query and the jailbreak policy to ensure compliance with safety standards.

The Refusal Mechanism offers a nuanced and adaptable framework that improves both safety and usability by allowing the model to reject, accept, or revise responses based on calibrated confidence scores, rather than relying on rigid binary decisions. Such binary approaches often result in overblocking harmless content or inadvertently allowing borderline unsafe outputs to slip through, making the flexible, confidence-driven strategy of Refusal Mechanism a more effective safeguard.

4.3
Self-revision

For responses
r
that fall within the uncertainty interval, it typically means that the large language model, even after careful reasoning, is still unable to definitively determine whether the response constitutes a jailbreak attempt. These responses usually do not contain explicit violations of the jailbreak policy, but their linguistic expressions exhibit clear borderline tendencies, carrying potential misleading or sensitive implications. Such ambiguous language already poses safety risks.

To mitigate the potential risks posed by such borderline language while avoiding unnecessary suppression of legitimate outputs, we propose a strategy called
Self-Revision
. The core idea is inspired by how humans often navigate sensitive or uncomfortable topics—not by stating them directly, but by expressing them in more indirect or euphemistic ways. Similarly, when the model detects that a response
r
falls within a gray area of safety assessment (e.g., within the bounds
\tau\leq S_{r}\leq 1-\tau
), it neither accepts nor rejects the response outright.
This content often lies on the borderline. It is not entirely unacceptable but contains some sensitive information. The best approach for such language is to retain the useful information while removing the harmful parts. This method aims to balance the retention of valuable content with the removal of inappropriate or harmful elements, ensuring that the output is both useful and compliant with safety or ethical standards.
Therefore, it is essential to modify the response
r
that lies in the gray area into a valid revision response
r_{\text{revision}}
. We design newly generated response
r_{\text{revision}}
which can be represented as:

r_{\text{revision}}=\text{LLM}\left(T_{v}(q,r,T_{p},S_{tr})\right),

(2)

which
T_{v}
is the revision template prompt that guides the LLM to revise its response by integrating the input query, the jailbreak policy, and the original response. Specifically, during this stage, the LLM is constrained by the acceptance policy guidelines to avoid terms that may lead to a jailbreak. By incorporating the previous summary
S_{tr}
and the input query
q
, the original response
r
is modified to comply with the guidelines. The LLM utilizes its ability to adapt and refine content, ensuring that the revised response adheres to safety protocols while retaining the necessary information. This revision process ensures the response remains within acceptable boundaries without compromising on the core message.

5
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of our
SafeBehavior
framework against a variety of baseline defense methods. We compare multiple attack scenarios, model architectures, and performance metrics to demonstrate both robustness and efficiency.

5.1
Experimental Setup

5.1.1
Datasets and base model

150 malicious scenarios from AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
are used to construct GCG, Papillon, IFSJ, and DeepInception, while the datasets provided by the authors of IFSJ and PPPI are utilized. In addition, 150 borderline scenarios are generated using GPT-3. These borderline scenarios may be rejected by the LLM but are designed to comply with the OpenAI safety policy.

Following prior work
(
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Ouyang et al. 2025
;
Hua and Wei 2025
)
, we chose two representative base models for our experiments, namely Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
and Mistral-7B-Instruct
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
.

5.1.2
Attack Methods

The five state-of-the-art jailbreak attacks we consider are GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, Papillon
(
Gong et al. 2025
)
,
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
, DeepInception
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, and PPPI
(
Sestito 2025
)
. Among these attacks, they collectively represent different categories. At the same time, we also evaluated the robustness of
SafeBehavior
by testing it against Siege
(
hua2025selfinstructfewshotjailbreakingdecompose
)
, which is a multi-turn attack.

Specifically, these attacks cover different categories: gradient optimization-based attacks such as GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, contextual manipulation attacks such as DeepInception
(
Li et al. 2024
)
and Papillon
(
Gong et al. 2025
)
, and prompt-based attacks such as PPPI
(
Sestito 2025
)
and IFSJ
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
. Each attack can be described in detail as follows:

GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
:
GCG is a gradient-based discrete optimization jailbreak attack. This method iteratively constructs adversarial token suffixes within the input sequence and leverages gradient signals in the discrete token space to guide the search process, thereby gradually enhancing the input’s control over the model’s generation trajectory.

DeepInception
(
Li et al. 2024
)
:

DeepInception is a prompt-based jailbreak method. This approach constructs a virtual, nested scenario that guides the model into a kind of “role-playing” self-immersion, thereby loosening its built-in safety constraints and ultimately inducing it to generate harmful content.

IFSJ
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
:
IFSJ is a prompt-based adversarial jailbreak method.
It also achieves efficient circumvention of safety alignment mechanisms in LLMs by introducing system tokens into the input and using a demo-level random search strategy, which significantly enhances the attack success rate under limited-shot conditions.

Papillon
(
Gong et al. 2025
)
:
Papillon is a black-box jailbreak method.
It autonomously generates adversarial prompts through a fuzz testing process and employs a two-stage attack structure to progressively optimize the jailbreak. In addition, Papillon leverages GPT-assisted mutation strategies and a two-level judging mechanism, enabling it to achieve efficient and stealthy jailbreaks across multiple large language models while maintaining short token lengths.

PPPI
(
Sestito 2025
)
:

PPPI is a prompt injection–based jailbreak technique that disguises malicious inputs as structured “policy files” combined with role-playing, tricking the model into treating them as legitimate rules.
This approach achieves a universal bypass of alignment safeguards in major LLMs, enabling the generation of sensitive or high-risk content that would otherwise be blocked.

Siege
(
hua2025selfinstructfewshotjailbreakingdecompose
)
:
Sigie jailbreak is modeled as a multi-turn tree search problem: in each round, an attacker LLM generates a stronger adversarial prompt based on the dialogue history, while the assistant’s response is evaluated by a “partial compliance” scorer (0–10). If the score exceeds a threshold, the search stops early and the jailbreak is considered successful. The search expands branches using BFS or beam search under a fixed budget of maximum turns and query limits.

5.1.3
Baselines

To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the robustness of
SafeBehavior
, we adopt seven different defense strategies as baseline comparisons and conduct a systematic comparison with
SafeBehavior
, as detailed below:

Paraphrase
(
Jain et al. 2023a
)
:

By default aligned with
(
Jain et al. 2023a
)
, we employ GPT-3.5 to paraphrase user queries, and the resulting paraphrased text is subsequently passed as input to the target language model.

PPL Calculator
(
Alon and Kamfonas 2023a
)
:

PPL Calculator is an input detection method that measures how predictable a query is under a language model. Queries with abnormally high perplexity are likely adversarial or out-of-distribution. Formally,

\text{PPL}(x_{1:n})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(x_{i}\mid x_{1:i-1})\right).

Following
(
Alon and Kamfonas 2023a
;
Xu et al. 2024b
)
, we use GPT-2 to compute perplexity. The threshold is set to the maximum perplexity of harmful queries in Advbench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, ensuring these queries are not misclassified as benign while rejecting other abnormal inputs.

SafeDecoding
(
Xu et al. 2024b
)
:
SafeDecoding is a mitigation based defense method that introduces safety aware decoding during the generation phase to resist jailbreak attacks. It first applies lightweight fine tuning on the original model with a small amount of safety data to obtain an expert model. During inference, the original model and the expert model are invoked simultaneously to compare their token distributions, increasing the probabilities of safety related tokens and decreasing those of attack related tokens, thereby guiding the model to produce refusals or safe responses.

Retokenization
(
Jain et al. 2023b
)
:
Retokenization transforms a token into several smaller units. For example, the word “playing” can be divided into “play” and “ing”. To implement this, we employ BPE-dropout
(
Provilkov et al. 2020
)
, which randomly skips a fraction p of merge operations during the tokenization process. In line with
(
Jain et al. 2023b
)
, we set
p=0.2
in our setup.

Self-Examination
(
Phute et al. 2024
)
:

This approach is an output detection method that leverages the language model itself to determine whether the generated content is harmful. For implementation, we adopt the prompting strategy introduced by
(
Phute et al. 2024
)
.

Self-Remainder
(
Yi et al. 2023
)
:

Self-Reminder augments the input by appending additional prompts that explicitly instruct the language model to reply in a responsible manner.

Intention Analysis (IA)
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
:

IA is a defense method that analyzes the intention of user inputs to guide models toward safe and policy-aligned responses. By first identifying the core intention and then generating a response accordingly, it suppresses jailbreak prompts and reduces harmful outputs while preserving helpfulness.

5.2
Evaluation Metrics

In evaluating defense methods, we consider three dimensions. The first is robustness, measured by the Attack Success Rate (ASR), which reflects the effectiveness of resisting jailbreak attacks—a lower ASR indicates stronger robustness. The second is reliability, assessed by the False Positive Rate (FPR), which captures the system’s tendency to mistakenly flag benign inputs as attacks—a lower FPR means less disruption to normal usage. The third is efficiency, quantified by the Detection Time, where faster detection implies greater practicality for real-world deployment.

a) Attack success rate (ASR):

ASR measures the proportion of jailbreak attacks that successfully bypass the defense, reflecting the robustness of the system against adversarial prompts. It can be represented as:

\text{ASR}=\frac{\#\text{of responses aligned with jailbreak attack}}{\#\text{of input queries to LLM}}

b) False positive rate (FPR):

FPR denotes the fraction of benign inputs that are mistakenly flagged as jailbreak attacks, which is defined as:

\text{FPR}=\frac{\#\text{ of benign inputs misclassified as jailbreak attacks}}{\#\text{ of benign inputs}}

c) Detection time (Time):

Detection time quantifies the efficiency of the defense by measuring the average time required to detect a jailbreak attempt. Lower detection latency indicates higher practicality for real-world deployment.

d) Reasoning performance metrics:

To examine whether
SafeBehavior
compromises the model’s reasoning capability, we evaluate its reasoning performance. The evaluation consists of two categories of metrics. The first category, reasoning performance metrics (IRT, PIRT, GPIRT), directly measures the model’s reasoning ability under different settings, where higher scores indicate stronger reasoning competence. The second category, retain ratio metrics (IRT Retain, PIRT Retain, GPIRT Retain), reflects how well the reasoning performance is preserved relative to the undefended baseline. A retain ratio of 1.0 means the defense causes no degradation, while values below 1.0 indicate performance loss.

Robustness of different defense methods under various jailbreak attacks

SafeBehavior
not only provides generalizable and consistent robustness against diverse jailbreak attacks but also ensures high reliability in real-world deployment. Overall, these results highlight the clear advantage of

in terms of both robustness and practical feasibility.

This highlights not only its superior ability to suppress attack success but also its remarkable stability across turns, showing strong cross-turn robustness. Since real-world jailbreak attempts typically unfold through sustained multi-turn interactions rather than single queries, these results emphasize the distinct advantage of
SafeBehavior
in providing effective and durable protection, offering a promising direction for building safer and more reliable dialogue systems.

5.4.3
Ablation analysis

Table
4
presents the ablation results of
SafeBehavior
’s individual and combined stages, evaluated by ASR. Overall, the inclusion of Self-Introspection (S2) consistently achieves the lowest ASR values across most attackers, with an average of 0.03, indicating that this stage plays a particularly strong role in defending against adversarial inputs. Intent Inference (S1) also shows notable effectiveness, with an average ASR of 0.06, while Self-Revision (S3) performs less effectively in isolation, yielding the highest average ASR of 0.10. However, when stages are combined, the defense is substantially strengthened, as shown by ASR values close to zero in most pairwise settings (S1+S2, S1+S3, S2+S3). This suggests that the synergy among multiple stages is crucial, with combinations outperforming single-stage defenses by a clear margin. 5.4.4
Computation time comparision

Figures 3 and 4 report the computation costs across different defenses on Mistral-7B-instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-instruct. Overall, lightweight methods such as PPL or Self-Examination, which either terminate generation early or produce very short refusals, achieve the lowest latency. In contrast, approaches that require an additional forward pass like Intention Analysis or Paraphrase are consistently slower, while single-pass methods such as Retokenization or Self-Reminder fall in between. In GCG and DeepInception,
SafeBehavior
achieves the fastest average time because most malicious prompts are intercepted at Stage 1. In PPPI, it also remains efficient by quickly rejecting policy-style prompts in the early stages, whereas IA and Paraphrase are slowed by long context processing.

Papillon and IFSJ show more nuanced patterns. In Papillon, Paraphrase, IA, and PPL are slowest due to handling long and stealthy prompts, while SafeBehavior falls in the middle since it is less likely to trigger early stopping. In IFSJ,
SafeBehavior
is extremely fast on Mistral, where demonstrations trigger early refusals, but slower on Qwen, where long contexts push many cases into deeper stages. Overall, defenses that can stop early and generate short refusals are fastest, and
SafeBehavior
maintains near-lightweight efficiency by applying deep inspection only to ambiguous cases.

Sensitivity analysis of hyperparameter
\tau

Figure
5

SafeBehavior

SafeBehavior
is essentially insensitive to the choice of
\tau
, meaning the model can robustly resist jailbreak attacks without requiring delicate parameter tuning. In particular, even under strong adversarial scenarios such as IFSJ and Papillon, the ASR remains close to zero, highlighting the inherent robustness and reliability of our method. These findings validate that
SafeBehavior
provides stable and parameter-independent protection, ensuring practical deployability in real-world systems

6
Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper proposes
SafeBehavior
, a hierarchical jailbreak defense framework that simulates human multistage reasoning through intention inference, self-introspection, and self-revision. Extensive experiments across diverse jailbreak attacks demonstrate that
SafeBehavior
achieves near-zero attack success rates with minimal false positives while preserving reasoning ability and efficiency, highlighting its robustness and practicality for building safer and more trustworthy large language models.
