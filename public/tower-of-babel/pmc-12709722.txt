Intestinal colonization of carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria in pediatric hematology patients: risk factors and molecular characteristics, a multicenter case-control study
Authors: Huangdu Hu, Weiyi Huang, Shuwei Ruan, Junxin Zhou, Yinping Wang, Rui Weng, Qiucheng Shi, Yan Jiang, Xi Jin, Yunsong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAims to investigate the prevalence, risk factors, and molecular characteristics of intestinal colonized carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria (CR-GNB) in pediatric hematology inpatients across China.MethodsIn this multicenter case control study, 412 pediatric inpatients with hematological diseases across China were screened for CR-GNB intestinal colonization. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing were performed on colonizing isolates. Logistic regression models were used to identify risk factors and construct a predictive nomogram. Comparative analyses between pediatric and adult patients were conducted to assess differences in colonization rates, resistance phenotypes, and resistance gene profiles.ResultsA total of 13.3% (55/412) of pediatric inpatients were colonized with CR-GNB, with carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) being the dominant group (10.0%, 41/412), followed by carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) (3.2%, 13/412) and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) (0.2%, 1/412). Independent risk factors for colonization included younger age, acute leukemia, lymphoma, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Phylogenetic analyses showed no clonal dissemination and no regional clustering. NDM enzyme was prevalent in carbapenem-resistant E. coli (CREC) and Enterobacter spp., while CRKP isolates were mostly non-carbapenemase-producing, with resistance likely driven by extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) overexpression and porin loss. Compared with adults, pediatric CRE isolates exhibited significantly lower resistance to aminoglycosides and β-lactamase inhibitor combinations.ConclusionsThis large-scale multicenter study highlights the substantial burden and molecular diversity of CR-GNB colonization in children with hematological diseases. Our findings underscore the need for pediatric-focused surveillance and infection control strategies that reflect species-specific resistance patterns and clinical vulnerabilities unique to this population.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, registration number NCT05002582. Background

Carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (CR-GNB), including carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) were considered by World Health Organization (WHO) as critical or high priority antimicrobial resistant bacteria in 2024 [1]. Due to the limited treatment options and rising resistant rates, CR-GNB are associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, posing significant global public health threat [2, 3]. The gastrointestinal tract serves as a critical reservoir for CR-GNB isolates, which subsequently cause pathogens invasion into blood and abdominal leading to infections [4]. Intestinal colonization with CR-GNB is particularly concerning in immunocompromised populations like patients with hematological malignancies (HM), where it can lead to bloodstream infection (BSI) and increasing mortality [5].

CR-GNB infections are responsible for most life-threatening complications in pediatric patients with HM, due to their prolonged hospital stays and immunosuppression [6]. Intestinal CR-GNB colonization in HM patients has been identified as a risk factor for subsequent infections [7, 8]. Meanwhile, children had a greater incidence than adults [9] and the mortality was significantly higher among CRE colonized children [10]. Despite the clinical importance, data on the epidemiology and risk factors of intestinal CR-GNB colonization in pediatric patients with hematological diseases remain insufficient, especially in large-scale, multicenter settings.

In this study, we conducted a multicenter, case control investigation of pediatric inpatients with hematological diseases across China. We aimed to determine the prevalence of CR-GNB intestinal colonization, identify regional distribution patterns, analyze clinical risk factors, and characterize the molecular features of colonizing isolates through whole-genome sequencing. Our findings provide valuable insights into the epidemiology and resistance mechanisms of CR-GNB colonization in a high-risk pediatric population, with implications for infection control and antimicrobial stewardship strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter case control study was conducted from July 20 to December 31, 2021, across 92 hospitals in China, focusing on intestinal colonization of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (CR-GNB) in patients with hematological diseases. Rectal swabs or feces were collected from all inpatients of the Hematology Ward at a certain hospital on a specific day. Sample collection and identification were detailed in our previous research [11]. Pediatric patients (age ≤ 18) were included in this study.

Clinical data

The clinical data were collected retrospectively, including (1) general information: age, gender; (2) risk factors: diagnosis of hematological disease, time of illness, treatment within 6 months (chemotherapy, HSCT or only symptomatic and supportive treatment), antimicrobial agents use within 6 months, deep vein catheterization, gastrointestinal symptoms (abdominal pain, diarrhea, gastrointestinal bleeding) within one week, sign of infections (blood or other parts) within one week.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation). Binary logistic regression analysis was used, and the results included odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Variables with P < 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Prediction Model was conducted in R v4.3.2 using the following packages car, pROC, rms, Hmisc and rmda.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

The AST was detailed in our previous study [11]. Briefly, the AST for carbapenems were performed using agar dilution method, while for other antimicrobial agents, customized susceptibility plates (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) were used for broth microdilution method. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 were used for quality control. The results for tigecycline were interpreted according to the criteria proposed by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) while those for other antimicrobial agents were interpreted following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [12].

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA of all CR-GNB isolates were extracted using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Short-read sequencing (Illumina, San Diego, USA) and genomes were de novo assembled using shovill v0.9.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) results were analyzed using mlst v2.19.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst), and antimicrobial resistance genes were analyzed using ABRicate v1.0.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with the NCBI database. Prokka v1.14.6 (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka) was used for open reading frame (ORF) annotation. Alignment of core genes from CR-GNB isolates were achieved with panaroo v.1.2.7 (https://github.com/gtonkinhill/panaroo). The maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree of CREC, CRKP, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter spp. and CRPA isolates were inferred by IQ-TREE v2.1.2 using the best-fit model according to BIC (http://www.iqtree.org/). The phylogenetic tree was visualized using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/). The copy number of ESBL genes were analyzed using ccne v1.1.2 (https://github.com/biojiang/ccne).

Results

Intestinal colonization rate of CR-GNB

During the study periods a total of 412 pediatric inpatients were included in the final analysis, as shown in Fig. 1. Among them, 55 (13.3%, 55/412) were found to be intestinal colonized with CR-GNB. Specifically, 41 (10.0%, 41/412) were colonized with CRE, 13 (3.2%, 13/412) were colonized with CRPA and only one (0.2%, 1/412) was colonized with CRAB. Additionally, we compared the colonization rate between pediatric and adult patients, as shown in Table S1, though the colonization rate was slightly higher in pediatrics than adults, but the difference was not statistically significant in CR-GNB, CRE and CRPA isolates (P > 0.05).

The highest colonization rates were recorded in Shanghai (32.3%, 10/31) and Hebei (26.3%, 5/19). Similarly, the colonization rate of intestinal CRE in pediatric inpatients followed the same geographical pattern, being more prevalent in North, East, and Central China while remaining lower in Northeast and Southwest China. Risk factors for intestinal colonization of GR-GNB in pediatric inpatients

Pediatric inpatients were classified into either the colonization group or the non-colonization group based on the presence or absence of intestinal CR-GNB colonization. In total, 55 colonized and 357 non-colonized pediatric inpatients were included in the analysis. The demographics and clinical characteristics of these patients with hematological diseases are presented in Table 1. Both groups exhibited a male predominance. Regarding underlying hematological conditions, acute leukemia was the most common diagnosis in both groups, with a slightly higher prevalence in the colonized group (65.5% vs. 65.0%).

As shown in Table 2, univariate analysis identified age, sex, diagnosis, therapy and use of antimicrobial agents (P < 0.2) as potential risk factors for CR-GNB intestinal colonization. After multivariate analysis, several significant independent risk factors were identified. for CR-GNB intestinal colonization. Younger age was associated with an increased risk (OR: 0.929, 95% CI: 0.875–0.986, P = 0.015). Patients diagnosed with acute leukemia (OR: 2.678, 95% CI: 1.048–6.841, P = 0.040) or lymphoma (OR: 3.801, 95% CI: 1.152–12.546, P = 0.028) had a significantly higher likelihood of colonization compared to those with nonneoplastic hematologic diseases. Furthermore, HSCT was identified as an independent risk factor. Transplant recipients faced 2.3-fold greater colonization likelihood than chemotherapy-treated patients (OR = 2.34, 95% CI:1.20–4.54, P = 0.012).

Notably, although combined carbapenem/β-lactam exposure showed a risk elevation trend (OR = 1.79, P = 0.248), no antimicrobial regimen reached statistical significance. Similarly, invasive procedures like deep vein catheterization recent gastrointestinal symptoms/infections were not predictive of colonization.

Additionally, we used variables maintained statistical significance in the predication model and drew a nomogram based on the logistic regression model as shown in Figure S1. The ROC analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.658 (95% CI 0.578–0.738), indicating moderate discriminative capacity. Calibration analysis revealed systematic overestimation of risk, evidenced by a significant intercept shift (corrected intercept = -0.282) and slope reduction (corrected slope = 0.837).

Distribution of colonized CR-GNB isolates and AST results

Among 41 intestinal colonized CRE isolates, 22 (53.7%, 22/41) were identified as CREC, 12 (29.3%, 12/41) as CRKP and 7 (17.0%, 7/41) as carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter spp. The distribution of carbapenemase enzymes and antimicrobial resistance patterns revealed distinct profiles among the CRE isolates. CREC and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter spp. exhibited remarkably similar resistance mechanisms, with NDM enzyme detected in 77.3% (17/22) and 57.1% (4/7) of isolates, respectively. This enzymatic profile correlated with high resistance to novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (BLBLIs), including ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA), meropenem-vaborbactam (MEV), and imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam (I/R), as shown in Fig. 3A and B. In contrast, CRKP isolates displayed a different resistance profile, with 91.7% (11/12) of isolates lacking carbapenemase, and only one strain carrying the IMP enzyme. This likely contributed to higher susceptibility rates to novel BLBLIs, with 91.7% of CRKP isolates remaining susceptible to CZA, MEV and I/R. And tigecycline (TGC) showed good susceptibility in CREC and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter spp., but had a 33.3% (4/12) resistance rate in CRKP isolates.

Additionally, polymyxin B (POL), fosfomycin (FOS), and amikacin (AK) showed good susceptibility against all CRE isolates, suggesting their potential as alternative therapeutic options. In contrast, fluoroquinolones, including ciprofloxacin (CIP) and levofloxacin (LEV), exhibited high resistance rates across all CRE species.

Furthermore, CRPA isolates exhibited a distinct resistance pattern, remaining susceptible to multiple antipseudomonal agents, with no detected carbapenemase genes, indicating a non-carbapenemase-mediated resistance mechanism. And for the CRAB strain, it carried both OXA-23 and OXA-66 enzymes and was resistant to cefoperazone-sulbactam (F/S, 1:1 and 1:2), CIP and AK, while only susceptible to POL and TGC.

As shown in Table S2, comparative analysis of antimicrobial resistance rates between pediatric and adult patients revealed distinct profiles. Among CRE isolates, resistance to β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (including cefoperazone-sulbactam and piperacillin-tazobactam) and aminoglycoside were lower in pediatrics compared to adults (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed for other antimicrobial categories in CRE. While, CRPA isolates exhibited uniformly low resistance rates across all antimicrobial classes in both pediatrics and adults, with no significant disparities.

Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and phylogenetic analysis

As shown in Fig. 4A, phylogenetic analysis revealed that intestinal colonizing CR-GNB isolates exhibited diverse sequence types (STs), with no evidence of clonal transmission. These isolates were derived from various regions across China, without apparent geographical clustering.

Among the CREC isolates, the majority (17/22, 77.3%) carried the NDM carbapenemase gene, consistent with their high-level resistance to carbapenems and other β-lactams. In contrast, only one CRKP isolate was carbapenemase-positive. Despite lacking carbapenemase genes, the meropenem/imipenem MIC elevations were driven by synergistic effects of ESBL overproduction (particularly CTX-M variants gene amplification) and porin deficiency (OmpK35/OmpK36 loss) as shown in Fig. 4B.

The resistance gene profiles of both CREC and CRKP isolates included extended-spectrum β-lactamases (CTX-M variants, DHA-1, SHV-12), quinolone resistance genes (qnrS, qnrB), and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes. Notably, CRKP strains exhibited a more variable and scattered resistance gene profile, with generally fewer resistance determinants than CREC.

Among the carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter spp. isolates, most (4/7, 57.1%) carried the NDM enzyme. These isolates demonstrated widespread resistance to carbapenems and novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. They also harbored a broad range of resistance genes, including CTX-M variants, AmpC β-lactamases (ACT variants) and additional quinolone and aminoglycoside resistance genes, reflecting a multidrug-resistant phenotype.

The CRPA isolates also showed considerable phylogenetic diversity, with no clear clustering, despite sharing similar resistance gene profiles. No carbapenemase genes were detected in any of the CRPA isolates. Instead, resistance appeared to be primarily mediated by non-enzymatic mechanisms, with OprD inactivation observed in 53.8% (7/13) of isolates, and one isolate also demonstrating MexAB-OprM efflux pump overexpression. Resistance determinants in CRPA included intrinsic β-lactamases (PDC variants, OXA-50 family) and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes such as aac(6’)-Ib and aph(3’).

Additionally, we compared the number of carbapenemase and the ARGs in isolates obtained from pediatrics and adults, as shown in Figure S2. There was no statistically significant difference in the median number of antimicrobial resistance genes between the two groups (Mann-Whitney U test, P > 0.05). However, significant difference was observed in prevalence of carbapenemase in CRKP isolates between pediatric and adult patients (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.05), suggesting pediatric CRKP isolates are more likely to be carbapenemase negative.

Discussion

Our multicenter investigation revealed an intestinal CR-GNB colonization rate of 13.3% in pediatric inpatients with hematological diseases, with CRE (10.0%) being the predominant group. This colonization rate was lower than previous reported single- or multicenter studies, including 18.4% in Hangzhou, China [13], 25% in Colombia [14], and 17.48% in Guangzhou, China [15]. Regional variation was notable, with higher rates observed in North, East, and Central China. Notably, the colonization rates of CRE mirrored those of overall CR-GNB, whereas CRPA displayed a more uniform distribution across regions, with no colonization detected in Northeast and Southwest China. And as the results showed in Table S1, the colonization rates appeared slightly higher in children, but no statistically significant difference were observed between adult and pediatric patients. These findings suggest active surveillance should be maintained in both populations, with regional specific strategies.

Risk factor analysis identified younger age, acute leukemia, lymphoma, and HSCT as independent predictors of CR-GNB colonization. Previous research also identified acute leukemia, lymphoma and HSCT as risk factors due to the impact of immunosuppressive therapy caused immunodeficiency [13, 16]. And we also identified younger age as an independent risk factor, which had not been identified in adult population. This may be explained by the relative immaturity of the intestinal microbiota [17] and antibiotic exposure patterns. Although prior antibiotic exposure showed a trend toward increased risk, it did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the widespread use of broad-spectrum agents in this vulnerable patient population. Furthermore, antimicrobial prescribing patterns differed between pediatric and adult hematology patients. Aminoglycosides, tetracycline and fluoroquinolones were rarely used in pediatric patients. The lower selection pressure might partially explain the reduced resistance rates in pediatric isolates compared with adult cohorts. These distinctions underscore the importance of age-specific antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance strategies to prevent colonization and transmission of CR-GNB in pediatric hematology wards.

Additionally, the prediction model provides initial risk stratification for pediatric CR-GNB colonization based on the training set and used the bootstrap internal validation method to evaluate the model performance. Although the nomogram demonstrated only moderate discriminative ability (AUC = 0.658), it still provides a practical tool for preliminary screening and risk stratification of CR-GNB colonization among pediatric hematology patients. The moderate predictive power may be attributed to the limited number of clinical variables included and the relatively small pediatric sample size in each participating center. Future improvements could be achieved by incorporating dynamic clinical indicators including changes in neutrophil counts, prior colonization history, duration of antibiotic exposure, and ICU admission history. Moreover, expanding the model with a larger, multicenter dataset and employing machine-learning–based approaches may further enhance predictive accuracy. Despite its limitations, the current model can still assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients for targeted surveillance and preventive interventions in pediatric hematology wards.

Phylogenetic analysis showed CRE and CRPA isolates in our study were sporadic colonization without clonal dissemination. CRE was the most common colonizing group, followed by CRPA, consistent with previous surveillance studies involving both pediatric and adult populations [18, 19]. Within the CRE group, CREC was more prevalent than CRKP, contrasting with earlier studies which focused on bloodstream infection (BSI) or adults cohort, where CRKP is typically the dominant pathogen [20–23]. This suggests that the distribution of CRE species may vary by patient population, and that surveillance priorities should be tailored accordingly, like pediatric hematology patients.

At the molecular level, NDM was the predominant carbapenemase in CREC and Enterobacter spp., consistent with prior reports from pediatrics or hematological cohorts [24, 25]. Notably, most CRKP isolates in our study were carbapenemase negative, and compared to adult patients, CRKP isolates from pediatrics are more likely to be carbapenemase negative, which was quite different from some nationwide surveillance, where ST11 CRKP producing KPC-2 predominates [26, 27]. Only three ST11 CRKP isolates were detected in our study (one IMP-producing and two carbapenemase-negative), and the overall sequence types were highly diverse. This suggests that the intestinal colonized CRKP isolates may be selected by antibiotic pressure rather than by clonal transmission. Genomic analysis further revealed that most carbapenemase-negative CRKP isolates exhibited high-level expression of ESBL enzymes or AmpC enzymes, predominantly of the CTX-M family, accompanied by outer membrane porins inactivation (OmpK35 and/or OmpK36). These strains typically display moderate carbapenem MICs. In pediatric hematology patients, where broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure is common, mainly involving third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems, such mechanisms may facilitate long-term intestinal persistence and selective enrichment under antimicrobial pressure. This emphasizes the need for molecular diagnostic approaches to detect carbapenemase-negative resistant strains and for refined antimicrobial stewardship to prevent their silent dissemination.

Although CREC was dominant in colonization, CRKP has been widely reported as the leading cause of subsequent infection [28–30]. This might reflect differences in invasive potential, with K. pneumoniae known to exhibit greater virulence and environmental persistence [31, 32]. Moreover, resistance phenotypes differ substantially between these CRE species, especially for new BLBLIs. Interestingly, although the total number of ARGs was comparable between pediatric and adult isolates, we observed lower resistance rates in pediatric CRE isolates to most antimicrobial agents except for cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, especially in β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (cefoperazone-sulbactam and piperacillin-tazobactam) and aminoglycosides (P < 0.05, Table S1). This may be reflected by different antimicrobial exposure patterns, such as the limited use of aminoglycosides and the wildly use of cephalosporins in children. However, fluoroquinolone resistance rates were similarly high in both populations, despite their limited use in children. This may be explained by the co-selection of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance genes, such as qnr and aac(6’)-Ib-cr, which are often co-transferred with β-lactam resistance genes [33]. These findings emphasize the complexity of genotype-phenotype relationships and highlight the need for both genetic and phenotypic resistance surveillance to guide therapy and control efforts. These differences reinforce the need for species-level and mechanism-based resistance surveillance to inform appropriate empiric therapy and infection control strategies.

In conclusion, our study highlights the high prevalence and molecular complexity of CR-GNB intestinal colonization in pediatric inpatients with hematological diseases. The identification of risk factors and resistance mechanisms provides a foundation for targeted screening strategies. Ongoing surveillance is warranted to mitigate the clinical impact of CR-GNB in vulnerable pediatric populations.
