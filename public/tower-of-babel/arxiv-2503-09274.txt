Non-Hermitian Linear Electro-Optic Effect Through Interactions of Free and Bound Charges
Sylvain Lannebère 1, Nader Engheta 2, Mário G. Silveirinha 3

Abstract
In recent years, there has been significant interest in non-Hermitian phenomena, particularly optical gain, stemming from electro-optic effects. Typically, these effects are attributed to nonlinear interactions arising from the “anomalous velocity” of Bloch electrons. Here, we introduce a novel mechanism for achieving optical gain through nonlinear interactions between free and bound electrons. We propose a semiclassical phenomenological microscopic model that encompasses a broad spectrum of nonlinear interactions, consistent with passivity and with the principles of microscopic reversibility. Our analysis shows that under non-equilibrium conditions and within low-symmetry systems, the linearized electromagnetic response can display both nonreciprocal and gain properties. We examine the stability of these electrically-biased systems and illustrate their potential applications, including the development of optical isolators and traveling wave amplifiers.

As previously mentioned, the study of the NHEO effect has been centered on the nonlinear interactions involving drifting (free) electrons based on the anomalous velocity. In this paper, we expand on this by demonstrating that the NHEO effect can also emerge from the nonlinear coupling between bound and free electrons. Our analysis employs a semiclassical model, wherein free electrons are governed by standard transport and continuity equations, and bound charges adhere to Lorentzian dynamics. These dynamics are nonlinearly coupled through local fields, with the constraint that the interaction must satisfy to the principles of microscopic reversibility and passivity. We illustrate that under a static electric bias, the system’s linearized response is inherently non-Hermitian and nonreciprocal, with the type of response controlled by the symmetry of the material. This analysis paves new avenues for engineering robust NHEO effects in realistic materials.

II
Phenomenological model for the NHEO effect with Berry curvature dipoles

To begin with, we introduce a phenomenological semiclassical model for the NHEO effect, considering only the contribution from free electrons to the response function. To this end, we consider an hypothetical system where the transport of electrons is described by a modified Drude-type model:

\displaystyle\frac{{d{\bf{p}}}}{{dt}}+\Gamma{\bf{p}}=q\left[{{\bf{E}}+\frac{{\bf{p}}}{m}\times{\bf{B}}}\right].

(1)

where
q=-e
is the electron charge,
m
is an effective mass,
\Gamma
is a collision frequency, and
\bf{p}
may be understood as a crystal quasi-momentum. It is supposed that due to the interactions with the ionic lattice the electron velocity and the crystal quasi-momentum are related as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf{v}}=\frac{1}{m}\left[{{\bf{p}}+\left({\overline{\zeta}\cdot{\bf{p}}}\right)\times{\bf{E}}}\right]

(2)

where
\overline{\zeta}
is some system dependent tensor. The structure of the equations (
1
) and (
2
) is reminiscent of the semiclassical equations for Bloch electrons in a crystal with a nontrivial Berry curvature
Xiao et al. 2010
. In particular, the nonlinear interaction of the electrons with the crystal lattice originates a contribution to the velocity which is proportional to the electric field
\bf{E}
. In the context of Bloch electrons, this term is known as the anomalous velocity contribution and depends on the geometry (Berry curvature) of the crystal bands
Xiao et al. 2010
.

In our model, the transport equations (
1
) and (
2
) are coupled to the Maxwell equations,

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf{E}}=-{\mu_{0}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{H}},\quad\quad\nabla\times{\bf{H}}={\varepsilon_{\rm{b}}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{E}}{\rm{+}}{\bf{j}}_{\rm{c}}

(3)

through an electric current density,

\displaystyle{\bf{j}}_{\rm{c}}={n_{0}}q{\bf{v}}

(4)

where
n_{0}
is the electron density and
\varepsilon_{\rm{b}}
is the (non-dispersive) permittivity of the background due to the bound electrons.

II.1
Passivity and conservation laws

Next, we show that the proposed model leads to a passive material response. To begin with, we note that from Eq. (
2
) it follows that
{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{v}}={\bf{E}}\cdot\frac{{\bf{p}}}{m}
. Then, calculating the dot product of both members of (
1
) with
\bf{p}
it is found that

{\bf{p}}\cdot\frac{{d{\bf{p}}}}{{dt}}+\Gamma{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}=qm{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{v}}
. Thereby, the system satisfies:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{{dt}}\left[{\frac{{{n_{0}}}}{{2m}}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}}\right]+\frac{{{n_{0}}\Gamma}}{m}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}={\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{j}}_{\rm{c}}.

(5)

From the Poynting theorem
Kong 1986
, it is well-known that
\nabla\cdot{\bf{S}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{EM}}}}=-{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{j}}_{\rm{c}}
, with
{\bf{S}}={\bf{E}}\times{\bf{H}}
the Poynting vector and
{W_{{\rm{EM}}}}=\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon_{\rm{b}}}{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{E}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mu_{\rm{0}}}{\bf{H}}\cdot{\bf{H}}
the electromagnetic energy density. Combining this result with Eq. (
5
), it is found that the proposed phenomenological model adheres to the conservation law:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf{S}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}=-Q,\quad\quad Q=\frac{{{n_{0}}\Gamma}}{m}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}},

(6)

\displaystyle{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}=\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon_{\rm{b}}}{\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{E}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mu_{\rm{0}}}{\bf{H}}\cdot{\bf{H}}+{n_{0}}\frac{1}{{2m}}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}.

(7)

The term
{n_{0}}\frac{1}{{2m}}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}
is related to the kinetic energy density of the free electrons.
Therefore,
W_{\text{tot}}
represents the total energy density within the medium, and
Q>0
symbolizes the dissipated power per unit volume. This shows that our model describes a passive response. Particularly, in the absence of lattice collisions (
\Gamma\to 0^{+}
), the dynamics of the system are conservative, and the system’s response remains time-reversal invariant in this limit. Note that the conservation law is independent of the tensor
\overline{\zeta}
that governs the anomalous velocity contribution.

II.2
Linearized dynamical response

Let us now consider the scenario where the material is biased by a static electric field
{\bf{E}}_{0}
created by some external generator. We write the total field as the sum the DC component and a weak dynamic field:
{\bf{E}}={{\bf{E}}_{0}}+\left({{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}{e^{-i\omega t}}+\rm{c.c.}}\right)
. As Eq. (
1
) is linear, the corresponding quasi-momentum has a similar decomposition,
{\bf{p}}={{\bf{p}}_{0}}+\left({{{\bf{p}}_{\omega}}{e^{-i\omega t}}+\rm{c.c.}}\right)
with

\displaystyle{{\bf{p}}_{\omega}}=\frac{q}{{\Gamma-i\omega}}{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}},\quad\quad{{\bf{p}}_{0}}=\frac{q}{\Gamma}{{\bf{E}}_{0}}

(8)

For simplicity, we neglect the magnetic field (
\bf{B}
) in the effective transport equation [Eq. (
1
)]. It should be noted that the presence of collisions (
\Gamma\neq 0
) is essential to ensure that the drift velocity (
{{\bf{v}}_{0}}\approx{{\bf{p}}_{0}}/m
) remains finite.

Combining Eqs. (
2
) and (
4
), it is evident that the (linearized) dynamical current is given by
{{\bf{j}}_{{\rm{c,}}\omega}}=\frac{{{n_{0}}q}}{m}\left[{{{\bf{p}}_{\omega}}+\left({\overline{\zeta}\cdot{{\bf{p}}_{\omega}}}\right)\times{{\bf{E}}_{0}}+\left({\overline{\zeta}\cdot{{\bf{p}}_{0}}}\right)\times{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}}\right]
. Then, from Eq. (
8
), it follows that the linearized current is related to the dynamical field as
{{\bf{j}}_{{\rm{c,}}\omega}}=\left({{{\overline{\sigma}}_{\rm{D}}}+{{\overline{\sigma}}_{{\rm{EO}}}}}\right)\cdot{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}
, where the
{{{\overline{\sigma}}_{\rm{D}}}+{{\overline{\sigma}}_{{\rm{EO}}}}}
is the optical conductivity. It consists of a Drude-like response (
{{\overline{\sigma}}_{\rm{D}}}
) and an electro-optic response that originates from the static-bias (
{{\overline{\sigma}}_{\rm{EO}}}
):

\displaystyle{\overline{\sigma}_{\rm{D}}}={\varepsilon_{0}}\omega_{p}^{2}\frac{1}{{\Gamma-i\omega}}{{\bf{1}}_{3\times 3}},\quad\quad\quad{\overline{\sigma}_{{\rm{EO}}}}={\varepsilon_{0}}\omega_{p}^{2}\left[{\frac{1}{\Gamma}\left({\overline{\zeta}\cdot{{\bf{E}}_{0}}}\right)\times{{\bf{1}}_{3\times 3}}+\frac{{-1}}{{\Gamma-i\omega}}{{\bf{E}}_{0}}\times\overline{\zeta}}\right]

(9)

where
{\omega_{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{{{n_{0}}{q^{2}}}}{{{\varepsilon_{0}}m}}}
is the plasma frequency of the electron gas. The electro-optic conductivity piece is proportional to the magnitude of the electric bias.

Interestingly, the term
{{\overline{\sigma}}_{\rm{EO}}}
shares the same structure as the electro-optic conductivity derived using the Boltzmann formalism in
Morgado et al. 2024
. Indeed, the results from the phenomenological model align precisely with those calculated using Boltzmann’s approach when the tensor
\overline{\zeta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\overline{\zeta}=-\frac{{{e^{3}}}}{{{\varepsilon_{0}}\omega_{p}^{2}{\hbar^{2}}}}{\overline{\bf{D}}^{T}}.

(10)

Here,
\overline{\mathbf{D}}
represents the Berry curvature dipole of the material. This similarity confirms that the phenomenological model accurately describes the NHEO effect. As extensively discussed in prior research
Morgado et al. 2024
;
Rappoport et al. 2023
, an electrically biased material may exhibit optical gain. The phenomenological model elucidates how this property is consistent with the material’s global passivity [Eq. (
6
)] and highlights that the optical gain originates from the nonlinear interactions between DC and dynamic fields.

II.3
Lagrangian formulation

Importantly, as demonstrated in Appendix
A
, linearizing any system described by a Lagrangian subjected to a time-independent bias—essentially, a system in equilibrium—inevitably results in a Hermitian linearized response. Consequently, the system of equations (
1
)-(
4
) cannot be derived from a Lagrangian density, even when approaching the limit
\Gamma\to 0^{+}
. More broadly, we conclude that a nonlinear conservative system in equilibrium cannot exhibit gain upon linearization, thereby precluding its use for implementing the NHEO effect. Consistent with this finding, the analyses in
Morgado et al. 2024
;
Rappoport et al. 2023
demonstrate that the Berry curvature dipole can only be nonzero in metallic systems—specifically, systems where a static electric bias induces a drifting current, representing a nonequilibrium scenario.

Therefore, the NHEO effect can only occur in nonlinear systems out of equilibrium. As seen in Sec.
II.1
, these systems can exhibit conservative-type dynamics (in the
sense that a Poynting theorem can be written for the overall response), but their dynamics
do not derive from a Lagrangian.

In this context, it is noteworthy that the anomalous velocity contribution in Eq. (
2
) is represented by a tensor lacking transposition symmetry,
-\frac{1}{m}{\bf{E}}\times\overline{\zeta}
. In contrast, the term describing the effective mass is always a symmetric tensor (as exemplified here by
\frac{1}{m}{{\bf{1}}_{3\times 3}}
, which corresponds to a scalar). Thus, the correction due to the anomalous velocity cannot be regarded as a field-dependent effective mass. This underscores the unique characteristics of the (nonlinear) term associated with the NHEO effect.

III
Model of free and bound charges nonlinearly coupled

Next, we explore alternative physical mechanisms that may potentially contribute to the NHEO effect, beyond the influence of the anomalous velocity. Specifically, we demonstrate that the NHEO effect can emerge from the interaction between nonlinearly coupled free and bound charges, even without the presence of an anomalous velocity term. Our microscopic model adheres to the principles of microscopic reversibility and passivity, even though the system dynamics is not described by a Lagrangian.

III.1
Description of the model

We consider a hypothetical material, schematically represented in Fig.
1
, that consists of free carriers and bound charges in interaction.

Figure 1:
Schematic of a material formed by free carriers and bound charges that are nonlinearly coupled. The figure represents the situation where the system is biased by a static electric field
\mathbf{E}_{0}
. For simplicity, only free carriers with a negative charge are represented.

For simplicity, the free carriers are chosen to be all of the same type with a mass
m
and a charge
q
.
The bound charges are modelled through a standard anisotropic Lorentzian model. It is supposed that the two types of charges are coupled through local fields created by the conduction and polarization currents. Thus, the dynamics of the two types of carriers is described by:

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\Gamma\right)\cdot\mathbf{p}=q\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle m_{b}\left(\partial_{tt}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\sum_{j=x,y,z}\omega_{0j}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\right)\mathbf{P}=q^{2}n_{b}\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\cdot\frac{qn_{0}}{m}\mathbf{p}\right)

(12)

In the above,
\mathbf{P}
is the polarization vector associated with the bound charges,
\omega_{0j}
are the resonance frequencies (transverse optical phonon frequencies) along each axis,
\gamma
is the phonon collision frequency,
m_{b}
is the effective mass of the bound charges and
n_{b}
their density. The notations
\partial_{t}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
and
\partial_{tt}\equiv\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}
are used for brevity. The vectors
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}
,
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{y}
,
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
are the unit vectors along the Cartesian axes and
\otimes
represents the tensor product of two vectors.
For simplicity, we neglect the effect of the magnetic component of the Lorentz force in Eq. (
11
), which is justified when the drift velocity of the free carriers satisfies
|\mathbf{v}_{0}|\ll c
. The quasi-momentum distribution
\mathbf{p}
of the free charges is related to the drift velocity as in Eq. (
2
).

In our model, the free carriers and the bound charges are coupled via two generic tensors,
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}
and
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
, with units of
\mathrm{Vm/A}
. The physical origin of the coupling is the following: the current densities of bound charges
\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
and of free carriers
qn_{0}\mathbf{v}\approx\frac{qn_{0}}{m}\mathbf{p}
induced by an external field
\mathbf{E}
generate secondary local electric fields given by
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\cdot\frac{qn_{0}}{m}\mathbf{p}
, respectively. These secondary local fields give rise to electric forces that interact with the free and bound charges, and that are at the origin of the coupling. The role of the tensors
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}
and
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
is analogous to the Green’s function in electromagnetism, which mathematically translates the current densities (source terms) into radiated electric fields
Kong 1986
. Physically, the coupling between the bound and free charges depends on the actual microscopic distribution of charges and fields. Thus,
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}
and
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
may be functions of the different dynamic variables of the system.

In the absence of coupling,
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}=\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}=0
, and of an anomalous velocity term,
\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0
, equations (
11
) and (
12
) reduce to the standard Drude and Lorentz models. For completeness, in Appendix
B
we present a slightly refined version of the proposed model that ensures that the conduction current satisfies the continuity equation. As discussed in Appendix
B
, the corrections arising from such refinement are usually negligible.

III.2
Allowed form of nonlinear coupling

To make further progress, next we impose restrictions on the allowed forms of the coupling based on the passivity of the system.

The analysis of Sec.
II.1
can be readily generalized to a material described by Eqs. (
11
)-(
12
). To this end, first we calculate the inner product of both sides of Eq. (
11
) with
\mathbf{p}
. Taking into account that
{\bf{E}}\cdot\frac{{\bf{p}}}{m}={\bf{E}}\cdot{\bf{v}}
, it is found that:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{{\partial t}}\left[{\frac{{{n_{0}}}}{{2m}}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}}\right]+\frac{{{n_{0}}\Gamma}}{m}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}={\bf{E}}\cdot q{n_{0}}{\bf{v}}+\frac{{q{n_{0}}}}{m}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\overline{\bf{C}}_{12}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}.

(13)

Similarly, calculating the inner product of both sides of Eq. (
12
) with
{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}
, one obtains:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{{\partial t}}\left[{\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{2{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}+\frac{m_{b}}{{2{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}\sum\limits_{j}{\omega_{0j}^{2}{\bf{P}}\cdot{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}\otimes{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}}\cdot{\bf{P}}}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle+\gamma\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}={\bf{E}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}+{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\overline{\bf{C}}_{21}}\cdot\frac{{q{n_{0}}}}{m}{\bf{p}}

On the other hand, from the Poynting theorem we have that
\nabla\cdot{\bf{S}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{EM}}}}=-{\bf{E}}\cdot{{\bf{j}}_{{\rm{tot}}}}
, where
W_{\rm{EM}}
is defined as in Sec.
II.1
and
{{\bf{j}}_{{\rm{tot}}}}
is the total current coupled to Maxwell’s equations. It is now given by the sum of the conduction and polarization currents:
{{\bf{j}}_{{\rm{tot}}}}={n_{0}}q{\bf{v}}+{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}
. Using Eqs. (
13
)-(
14
), it is readily found that:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf{S}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}=-Q+\frac{{q{n_{0}}}}{m}\left[{{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{{\overline{\bf{C}}}_{21}}\cdot{\bf{p}}+{\bf{p}}\cdot{{\overline{\bf{C}}}_{12}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}}\right]

(15)

where we introduced

\displaystyle{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}

\displaystyle={W_{{\rm{EM}}}}+{\frac{{{n_{0}}}}{{2m}}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}}+\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{2{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}\left({\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}+\sum\limits_{j}{\omega_{0j}^{2}{\bf{P}}\cdot{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}\otimes{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}}\cdot{\bf{P}}\right),

(16)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{{{n_{0}}\Gamma}}{m}{\bf{p}}\cdot{\bf{p}}+\gamma\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}.

(17)

The terms of
W_{\rm{tot}}
represent the electromagnetic field energy density, the energy density of the free carriers, and the kinetic and potential energy density of the bound charges, respectively.

The right-hand side of Eq. (
15
) represents the power transferred from the material to the wave. For a passive system it must be strictly negative for any system state. This is possible only if the term in rectangular brackets vanishes.
This can be enforced by imposing that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}^{T}=-\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21},

(18)

where the superscript
T
represents the transpose operator.
When the above constraint is fulfilled, Eq. (
15
) simplifies to the standard law of energy conservation
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
with a light and a matter part
\nabla\cdot{\bf{S}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}=-Q
, where
Q>0
represents the heat dissipated per unit of volume.
The condition (
18
) will be assumed in the rest of the article.

III.3
Microscopic reversibility

The vast majority of physical systems obey the principle of microscopic reversibility, i.e. they are time-reversal (TR) invariant at the microscopic level
Sachs 1987
;
Asadchy et al. 2020a
. Here, we impose that the coupling terms in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
) are compatible with such a principle and remain invariant under a TR symmetry transformation.
The terms
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\cdot\mathbf{p}
and
-\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}^{T}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
must transform as “forces”, and thus must be even under a TR symmetry transformation (similar to the other terms in the equations, with exception of the dissipative terms). Because
\mathbf{p}
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
are both odd under a TR symmetry transformation, it follows that
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
must also be odd under a TR, implying that
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
cannot be a constant matrix. Thus, to respect microscopic reversibility,
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
has to depend only on the state variables that are odd under a TR, namely:
\mathbf{v}
,
\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
,
\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}
or
\mathbf{B}
.
In particular, it follows that the coupling terms associated with the tensors
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\cdot\mathbf{p}
represent nonlinear interactions between the bound and free charges. Such a nonlinearity is essential to generate the NHEO effect
Lannebère et al. 2022
. We will demonstrate later in Sec.
IV
that the
linearization of these nonlinear terms at the bias point may lead to a nonreciprocal and non-Hermitian electromagnetic response, even in the absence of a Berry curvature dipole
\left(\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0\right)
.

Since we are only interested in the linearized electromagnetic response of the system, we can restrict the expansion of
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
to
linear
combinations of the variables odd under a TR symmetry (
\mathbf{v}
,
\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
,
\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{B}
).
As previously noted, the interactions between the free and bound charges are expected to depend on the dynamical state of the system, i.e., on the displacements of the two charge distributions from the equilibrium point. Thereby, it is reasonable to assume that
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
is a linear function of
\mathbf{v}
and
\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}
only.
In these conditions, the coupling matrix is of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ijl}\left(a_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\mathbf{v}+b_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}

(19)

where the dummy indices
i
,
j
and
l
run over
x,y,z
and the coupling coefficients
a_{ijl}
and
b_{ijl}
are real-valued coefficients (54 in total) with units of
\mathrm{Vs/A}
and
\mathrm{m^{3}V/A^{2}}
, respectively. Note that in the lossless limit (
\gamma\to 0^{+}
and
\Gamma\to 0^{+}
) the full dynamical equations become TR-symmetric.

III.4
Linearization

Next, we linearize the nonlinearly coupled differential equations (
11
) to (
12
) with the coupling matrix
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
given by Eq. (
19
). The nonlinear response is determined both by
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
and by the “anomalous” velocity term described by the tensor
\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}
.
For weak nonlinearities, the two contributions to the linear electro-optic effect are independent and are combined additively. The effect of the anomalous velocity term was already characterized in Sec.
II
[see Eq. (
9
)]. Thus, to determine the contribution from the interactions of free and bound electrons, we can set
\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0
and use
\mathbf{p}=m\mathbf{v}
in Eqs. (
11
)-(
12
). This leads to the following system of differential equations:

\displaystyle m\left(\partial_{t}+\Gamma\right)\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=q\left(\mathbf{E}-\sum_{ijl}\left(a_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\mathbf{v}+b_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)

(20a)

\displaystyle m_{b}\left(\partial_{tt}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\sum_{j}\omega_{0j}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\right)\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=q^{2}n_{b}\left(\mathbf{E}+\sum_{ijl}\left(a_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\mathbf{v}+b_{ijl}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot qn_{0}\mathbf{v}\right)

(20b)

Let us first ignore the weak nonlinear terms associated with the coefficients
a_{ijl}
and
b_{ijl}
.Then, the velocity and polarization vector response to a time-harmonic field (
{\bf{E}}={{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}{e^{-i\omega t}}
) is determined by:

\displaystyle{\bf{v}}_{\omega}^{0}=\frac{q}{m}\frac{1}{{\Gamma-i\omega}}{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}},

(21a)

\displaystyle{\bf{P}}_{\omega}^{0}=\varepsilon_{0}\sum\limits_{j}{\frac{\omega_{b}^{2}}{{\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega\left({\omega+i\gamma}\right)}}{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}\otimes{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}}\cdot{{\bf{E}}_{\omega}}

(21b)

where
\omega_{b}^{2}=\frac{q^{2}n_{b}}{\varepsilon_{0}m_{b}}
.
In particular, the response to a static bias (
{\bf{E}}={{\bf{E}}_{0}}
) is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{m\Gamma}\mathbf{E}_{0},

(22a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\sum_{j}\frac{\omega_{b}^{2}}{\omega_{0j}^{2}}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{0}.

(22b)

The static electric bias
\mathbf{E}_{0}
induces a drift current characterized by the velocity
\mathbf{v}_{0}
, as well as a macroscopic polarization vector.

To perform the linearization of equations (
20
), it is assumed that a generic dynamic variable of the system
\mathbf{K}
—where
\mathbf{K}
represents
\mathbf{v},\,\mathbf{P}
or
\mathbf{E}
—can be decomposed into
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}_{0}+{\mathbf{K}_{\omega}}{e^{-i\omega t}}
where
\mathbf{K}_{0}
is a time-independent vector induced by the static electric bias and
\mathbf{K}_{\omega}{e^{-i\omega t}}
is a time harmonic vector associated with the dynamical field. Similar to
Lannebère et al. 2022
, it is assumed that the time-harmonic signal has a small amplitude with respect to the static bias such that
|\mathbf{K}_{\omega}|\ll|\mathbf{K}_{0}|
.

Substituting
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}_{0}+{\mathbf{K}_{\omega}}{e^{-i\omega t}}
in Eqs. (
20
) and retaining only the terms linear in the dynamical fields one obtains:

\displaystyle m\left(-i\omega+\Gamma\right){\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}=q\left({\mathbf{E}_{\omega}}+i\omega\sum_{jlr}a_{jlr}v_{0r}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}\right)

(23a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma\omega\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}=

(23b)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\varepsilon_{0}\omega_{b}^{2}\left({\mathbf{E}_{\omega}}+qn_{0}\sum_{jlr}v_{0r}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}\cdot\left[\left(a_{jlr}+a_{jrl}\right){\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}-i\omega b_{jrl}{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}\right]\right)

where
v_{0r}=\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{r}
.

The linearized permittivity tensor
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}
is defined through the relation between the dynamic electric field
{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega}
and the total dynamic polarization
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{tot},\omega}=\frac{n_{0}q}{-i\omega}{\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}+{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{tot},\omega}=\varepsilon_{0}\left(\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}-\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega}.

(24)

For a weak nonlinearity,
{\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}
and
{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}
can be calculated using the replacements
{{\bf{v}}_{\omega}}\to{\bf{v}}_{\omega}^{0}
and

{{\bf{P}}_{\omega}}\to{\bf{P}}_{\omega}^{0}
in the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
23
), with
{\bf{v}}_{\omega}^{0}
and
{\bf{P}}_{\omega}^{0}
given by Eqs. (
21
). A straightforward analysis shows that the permittivity can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}=\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{\rm{L}}+\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}+\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-f}},

(25)

where
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{\rm{L}}
determines the material response without the bias,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{\rm{L}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{jj}^{0}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}

(26a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{jj}^{0}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\omega\left(\omega+i\Gamma\right)}+\frac{\omega_{b}^{2}}{\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\omega\gamma}

(26b)

and
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}
and
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-f}}
represent the electro-optic response due to the interaction of bound and free electrons and due to the “anomalous velocity”, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{jlr}\left[g_{lr}a_{ljr}-g_{jr}\left(a_{jlr}+a_{jrl}\right)-f_{jlr}b_{jrl}\right]\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{l}

(27a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{{{\mathbf{\overline{\sigma}}}_{{\rm{EO}}}}}}{{-i\omega{\varepsilon_{0}}}}.

(27b)

The term due to the anomalous velocity was added by hand to the final result, because as noted previously the different nonlinear contributions combine additively. In the above, the
\omega
-dependent functions
g_{jr}
and
f_{jlr}
are defined by

\displaystyle f_{jlr}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{0}qn_{0}v_{0r}\omega\frac{\omega_{b}^{2}}{\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega\left(\omega+i\gamma\right)}\frac{\omega_{b}^{2}}{\omega_{0l}^{2}-\omega\left(\omega+i\gamma\right)},

(28a)

\displaystyle g_{jr}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}v_{0r}\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}\omega_{b}^{2}}{\left(\omega+i\Gamma\right)\left[\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega\left(\omega+i\gamma\right)\right]}.

(28b)

The linearized permittivity (
25
) is a dispersive function that in the absence of coupling (
a_{ijk}=b_{ijk}=0
for all indices and
\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0
) reduces to a diagonal matrix. As seen, the electro-optic effect may induce non-diagonal terms in the permittivity tensor. Remarkably, as discussed in the next section, for low-symmetry materials such non-diagonal terms may result in a permittivity tensor lacking transpose symmetry and may give rise to nonreciprocity and optical gain.
In the rest of the article, we focus our analysis in the free and bound electrons interactions, and thereby we will ignore possible anomalous velocity contributions
\left(\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0\right)
.

IV
Spatial symmetries

Next, we discuss the spatial symmetries compatible with nontrivial nonlinear couplings and derive explicitly the linearized permittivity for materials belonging to the
2mm
symmetry group.

IV.1
Constraints due to spatial symmetries

Next, we study how the invariance of the material under rotations, inversions, or their combinations, constrains the elements of the tensor
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
.

The tensor
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
links “acceleration” and “velocity” distributions. Both acceleration and velocity transform identically under rotations and reflections:

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})\to\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})=\mathcal{R}\cdot\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{R}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{r})

with
\mathbf{A}
standing for the relevant field. Here
\mathcal{R}
is an element of the orthogonal group
O(3)
, so that
{{\mathcal{R}}^{T}}\cdot{\mathcal{R}}={{\bf{1}}_{3\times 3}}
. Thus, if
\mathcal{R}
is a symmetry of the material, the coupling matrix
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\left(\mathbf{v},\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)
is constrained to satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\left(\mathcal{R}\cdot\mathbf{v},\mathcal{R}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\left(\mathbf{v},\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)\cdot\mathcal{R}^{T}

(29)

This invariance under specific spatial symmetries restricts the form of the coupling tensor, potentially reducing the number of nonzero coupling coefficients significantly.

For example, for a system invariant under inversion symmetry (corresponding to
\mathcal{R}=-\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}
) the above equation combined with Eq. (
19
) implies that

\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}=0
. This means that a non-centrosymmetric structure is required to implement the NHEO effect.

Among the non-centrosymmetric systems that possess an axis of rotation, it can be checked that rotations by an angle of
\pi
,
2\pi/3
,
\pi/2
, and
\pi/3
are all compatible with the existence of a nontrivial coupling tensor
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
.
For example, we checked that a nontrivial
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
is compatible with the symmetries of the
2
,
222
,
2mm
and
32
crystallographic groups (Hermann–Mauguin notation).

IV.2
Linearized permittivity for the
2mm
symmetry group

In the rest of the paper, we focus on material belonging to the
2mm
symmetry group. Materials in this crystallographic group have two mirror planes and are invariant under a 2-fold rotation symmetry about some specific axis. We note in passing that a MOSFET transistor exhibits the same type of symmetries
Lannebère et al. 2022
.

We assume, without loss of generality, that the axis of rotation of the structure is parallel to the
z
direction. The nontrivial elements of the
2mm
symmetry group consists of a rotation by an angle of
\pi
(
\mathcal{R}=\text{diag}\left\{-1,-1,1\right\}
) and two mirror symmetries with respect to planes containing the rotation axis (here taken as
\mathcal{R}=\text{diag}\left\{-1,1,1\right\}
and
\mathcal{R}=\text{diag}\left\{1,-1,1\right\}
). When these three symmetries are individually imposed to our model, it can be shown that most coupling coefficients in Eq. (
19
) must vanish. The most general form of the coupling tensor is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}^{2mm}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{xxz}v_{z}+b_{xxz}\partial_{t}P_{z}&0&a_{xzx}v_{x}+b_{xzx}\partial_{t}P_{x}\\
0&a_{yyz}v_{z}+b_{yyz}\partial_{t}P_{z}&a_{yzy}v_{y}+b_{yzy}\partial_{t}P_{y}\\
a_{zxx}v_{x}+b_{zxx}\partial_{t}P_{x}&a_{zyy}v_{y}+b_{zyy}\partial_{t}P_{y}&a_{zzz}v_{z}+b_{zzz}\partial_{t}P_{z}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(30)

Thus, only 14 coupling coefficients out of the 54 possible are allowed to be different from zero.

Next, we calculate the linearized permittivity (
25
) for a material that exhibits
2mm
-symmetry. Importantly, two cases can be distinguished depending on whether the direction of the drift velocity is parallel or orthogonal to the axis of rotation of the structure. For a drift velocity parallel to the axis of rotation (
\mathbf{v}_{0}=v_{0z}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
), it can be shown that the linearized permittivity (
25
) is always a diagonal tensor and therefore describes a reciprocal response. This case will not be further considered in this paper.

Instead, we focus on the situation where the drift velocity is orthogonal to the rotation axis and assume, without loss of generality, that it is along the
x
direction:
\mathbf{v}_{0}=v_{0x}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}
.
It is interesting to examine further the set of linearized differential equations (
23
) for this example. It reduces to:

\displaystyle m\left(-i\omega+\Gamma\right){\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle=q\left[{\mathbf{E}_{\omega}}+i\omega v_{0x}(a_{zxx}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}+a_{xzx}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x})\cdot{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}\right]

(31a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left(\omega_{0j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma\omega\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\omega_{b}^{2}\left\{{\mathbf{E}_{\omega}}+qn_{0}v_{0x}\left[(2a_{zxx}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\left(a_{xzx}+a_{xxz}\right)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z})\cdot{\mathbf{v}_{\omega}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 91.04872pt\left.\left.-i\omega\left(b_{xxz}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}+b_{zxx}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{P}_{\omega}}\right]\right\}

(31b)

This set of equations explicitly shows the form of linearized local field induced by the coupling between the free and bound electrons. As seen, the induced local fields involve the interaction between the
x
component of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
or
-i\omega\mathbf{P}_{\omega}
with the
z
component of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
or
-i\omega\mathbf{P}_{\omega}
, or vice-versa, similar to what happens in standard MOSFET transistors
Sze et al. 2021
;
Lannebère et al. 2022
.

Furthermore, it is clear from the above equation that for a drift velocity orthogonal to the material rotation axis, only 5 coefficients out of the 14 coefficients appearing in Eq. (
30
) play a role in the linearized response. The other coefficients give rise to higher order responses and disappear upon linearization.
In fact, from (
25
) and (
27a
), one readily finds that for materials belonging to the
2mm
symmetry group with a drift orthogonal to the rotation axis, the linearized permittivity tensor is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}^{0}&&0&&i\left(f_{1}b_{xxz}-g_{zx}a_{zxx}+g_{xx}\left[a_{xzx}+a_{xxz}\right]\right)\\
0&&\varepsilon_{yy}^{0}&&0\\
i\left(f_{1}b_{zxx}+2g_{zx}a_{zxx}-g_{xx}a_{xzx}\right)&&0&&\varepsilon_{zz}^{0}\end{pmatrix}

(32)

where
f_{1}(\omega)=f_{xzx}=f_{zxx}
and the remaining functions are given by Eq. (
28
).

In the presence of nonlinear coupling between the free and bound electrons, when some of the coefficients
a_{ijk}
and
b_{ijk}
are different from zero, the permittivity tensor is non-diagonal. Additionally, due to collisions (
\Gamma>0
and
\gamma>0
), the functions
\varepsilon_{ii}^{0}
,
f_{1}
and
g_{ij}
(with
i,j=x,y,z
) are all complex-valued.
Remarkably, all types of coupling result in a permittivity tensor that is asymmetric, leading to a nonreciprocal (
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}\neq\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{T}
) electromagnetic response. In particular, the linearized electro-optic response is not TR symmetric (
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}\neq\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}^{\ast}
); note that the tensor
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}
is formed by the out-of-diagonal elements of
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}
. This behavior is expected from the presence of a drift current, which is inherently odd under TR symmetry.
The broken TR symmetry distinguishes our system from the idealized model described in Ref.
Lannebère et al. 2022
, aligning it more closely with the behavior observed in actual MOSFET transistors, which also support a DC current
Fernandes et al. 2024
.

Additionally,
all couplings
result in a non-Hermitian electro-optic response (
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}\neq\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}^{\dagger}
), Furthermore, it can be checked that the tensor
{\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{{\rm{NH}}}}=\frac{1}{{2i}}\left({{{\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}}_{{\rm{f-b}}}}-\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{{\rm{f-b}}}^{\dagger}}\right)
is an indefinite matrix. This indicates that the electro-optic interaction may result in either dissipation or in gain
Lannebère et al. 2025b
. This idea will be further developed in the next section.

Therefore, the nonlinear interactions between the bound and free electrons provide an alternative mechanism to implement the NHEO effect, not directly relying on the Berry curvature dipole. It is interesting to note that the frequency dependence of the tensor
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\rm{f-b}}
is qualitatively similar to that of the
\chi^{(2)}
tensor derived from band theory
Boyd 2008
(Sec. 3.2). Thus, in principle it is possible to link the coefficients
a_{ijk}
and
b_{ijk}
with the electronic band-structure of the material. Such analysis will not be pursued here, and is left for future work.

It is relevant to note that the coupling associated with the coefficient
a_{xzx}
, gives rise to a gyrotropic response, reminiscent of that of a magnetized plasma
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
;
Altman and Suchy 2011
. However, different from passive gyrotropic media, for a sufficiently strong electric bias this coupling can originate chiral gain, analogous to the case of tellurium discussed in Ref.
Morgado et al. 2024
.

In summary, in this section it was demonstrated that a metallic system with the symmetry of the
2mm
group, with the static electric bias orthogonal to the rotation axis of the structure, can be used to implement the NHEO effect and achieve nonreciprocity and gain. Different from other platforms based on drifting electrons
Borgnia et al. 2015
;
Duppen et al. 2016
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2018
;
Wenger et al. 2018
;
Bliokh et al. 2018
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2020
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Dong et al. 2021
, our solution does not rely on Doppler shifts and on waves with extremely short wavelengths (plasmons). Furthermore, in our system the drift velocity does not need to be a significant fraction of the light velocity.

We expect that our model can be implemented either in engineered semiconductor superlattices
Rosencher et al. 1996
or in natural materials. For example, potential candidates could be found in the class of the polar/ferroelectrics metals
Zhou and Ariando 2020
, materials that are both conducting and polarizable. WTe
2
, a polar metal (as well as a Weyl semimetal) with the symmetry of the
2mm
symmetry group could be a possible candidate.

V
Wave propagation

To illustrate the properties of our system, next we study the wave propagation in a material described by the linearized permittivity (
32
). For simplicity, we assume an isotropic Lorentz model such that
\omega_{0j}=\omega_{0}
for all
j
. Also we focus on a specific type of nonreciprocal and non-Hermitian response and assume that
a_{zxx}
is the only nonzero coupling coefficient in Eq. (
19
). Thus, the coupling matrix is
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}=a_{zxx}v_{x}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
, so that it couples the
x
and
z
components of the velocity and polarization current. From Eq. (
32
), the linearized permittivity satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{d}&&0&&-i\varepsilon_{c}\\
0&&\varepsilon_{d}&&0\\
i2\varepsilon_{c}&&0&&\varepsilon_{d}\end{pmatrix}

(33)

where
\varepsilon_{d}=\varepsilon_{jj}^{0}(\omega)
and
\varepsilon_{c}=g_{zx}(\omega)a_{zxx}
, with
\varepsilon_{jj}^{0}
and
g_{zx}
given by Eq.(
28

In the rest of this section, we are interested only in weak
signals described by the linearized response, and thus, to
keep the notations short, we drop the subscript
\omega
(
{\mathbf{E}_{\omega}}\to\mathbf{E}
), and denote the dynamic electric field simply
by
\mathbf{E}
.

V.1
Band structure and stability

To begin with, we characterize the photonic band diagrams in the absence of coupling (
\varepsilon_{c}=0
). In this case, the permittivity tensor is isotropic
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}=\varepsilon_{d}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}
and the plane wave solutions of the homogeneous wave equation
\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\varepsilon_{d}\mathbf{E}
can be separated into a longitudinal mode and a doubly degenerated transverse electromagnetic (TEM) mode.

3
(a) and high-frequency bands in Fig.
3
(b)] the TM waves split into two sub-bands. The TM waves can be understood as resulting from the hybridization, due to
\varepsilon_{c}\neq 0
, between one of the TEM waves of the unbiased system with the longitudinal wave.

In Figs.

\omega^{\prime}(\mathbf{k})+i\omega^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{k})
across all angles
\theta
.
When collisions are ignored [Fig.

\omega^{\prime\prime}<0
) and unstable modes (
\omega^{\prime\prime}>0
). The complex spectrum exhibits mirror symmetry with respect to the real-frequency axis.
Therefore, in these conditions the material response is unstable, suggesting potential applications for lasing.

The effect of collisions can stabilize the material response and ensure that the frequency spectrum is fully confined to the lower-half frequency plane for all
k

\omega^{\prime\prime}\leq 0

V.2
Indefinite gain response

In order to explain the directional dependence of the gain response of the material, next we characterize the tensor
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}-\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\dagger}}{2i}
, which determines the power exchanged between the material and the wave.
Lannebère et al. 2022
;
Lannebère et al. 2025b
;
Lannebère et al. 2025a
. For the model of Eq. (
33
), it is given by
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{Im}\left(\varepsilon_{d}\right)\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\text{NH}}
where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\text{NH}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\left(\varepsilon_{c}\right)-i\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left(\varepsilon_{c}\right)\\
0&0&0\\
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\left(\varepsilon_{c}\right)+i\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{Im}\left(\varepsilon_{c}\right)&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

For a standard passive material, all the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
are required to be positive to guarantee that the energy of the wave is irreversibly dissipated in the material. Interestingly, in our problem

\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\text{NH}}
is an indefinite matrix, meaning that it has both positive and negative eigenvalues. Specifically, its nontrivial eigenvalues are
\pm\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{0}|
, with
\alpha_{0}=\mathrm{Re}(\varepsilon_{c})-3i\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{c})
, and the corresponding eigenvectors are
\pm\frac{\alpha_{0}}{|\alpha_{0}|}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
.
The positive eigenvalue corresponds to an electro-optic response that results in increased dissipation, whereas the negative eigenvalue to an electro-optic response that results in reduced dissipation.

As
\mathbf{\overline{\chi}}_{\text{NH}}
trivially commutes with the response of the unbiased material (a scalar), the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
are

\lambda_{\pm}\equiv\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{d})\pm\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{0}|
, corresponding to the eigenvectors
\pm\frac{\alpha_{0}}{|\alpha_{0}|}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
, and
\lambda_{0}\equiv\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{d})
corresponding to the eigenvector
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{y}
.
Thus, in the presence of an electric bias the level of dissipation depends on the wave polarization in the material. In our system, the parameter
\alpha_{0}
is predominantly real-valued. Thereby, the polarizations that activate the maximum (minimum) dissipation are such that
\mathbf{E}\sim s\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
and
\mathbf{E}\sim-s\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
, with
s={\mathop{\rm sgn}}\left({{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits}\left\{{{\varepsilon_{c}}}\right\}}\right)
. This explains why the maximum dissipation/gain occurs for angles
\theta=\mp\pi/4
: for such directions of propagation the electric field of the TM wave is roughly oriented along
\pm\pi/4
, matching closely one of the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}^{\prime\prime}
.

Eventually, when the bias velocity is strong enough such that in some frequency range
\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{0}|>\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{d})
, the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}
can become negative. In such a case, the material exhibits an indefinite gain response
Lannebère et al. 2025b
, as it can provide both dissipative and gain interactions, depending on the wave polarization.
When
\lambda_{-}<0
, the electromagnetic waves polarized as
-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{|\alpha_{0}|}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
are amplified.

can reach negative values, indicating that the associated eigenpolarization can be amplified by the material. As seen, the electro-optic response is stronger in the low-frequency band, where

for all real-valued wave vectors). This occurs because the eigenpolarization of the TM-polarized plane wave,

\omega_{0}
of natural materials is typically in the range of the tens of THz

. Thus, the NHEO effect should be the strongest for frequencies in the THz range.

In the low-frequency band, the frequency that gives the most negative

V.3
Traveling wave amplifier

Next, we demonstrate that the electrically biased material can be used as a traveling wave amplifier. To this end, we examine the Poynting vector of a TM wave. Different from subsection
V.1
, we consider plane wave solutions with a real-valued frequency
\omega
and a complex-valued wave vector
k_{\text{TM}}=k_{\text{TM}}^{\prime}+ik_{\text{TM}}^{\prime\prime}
. This models for example the wave propagation in an open system of finite dimensions.

The Poynting vector
\mathbf{S}_{\text{TM}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\mathbf{E}_{\text{TM}}\times\mathbf{H}_{\text{TM}}^{\ast}\right\}
with
\mathbf{H}_{\text{TM}}=\frac{1}{i\omega\mu_{0}}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\text{TM}}
, is given explicitly by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\text{TM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A_{\text{TM}}|^{2}}{2\omega\mu_{0}}\mathrm{Re}\left\{k_{\text{TM}}\left[\cos(\theta)+\nu\sin(\theta)\right]\left(\nu^{\ast}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}\right)\right\}\text{e}^{-2k_{\text{TM}}^{\prime\prime}\left(\sin(\theta)x+\cos(\theta)z\right)}.

(36)

This vector exhibits different behavior depending on the sign of the imaginary part of
k_{\text{TM}}
. When
k_{\text{TM}}^{\prime\prime}
is positive, the Poynting vector experiences exponential decay, corresponding to a wave attenuated by the material, whereas when
k_{\text{TM}}^{\prime\prime}<0
, the Poynting vector grows exponentially, corresponding to a wave amplified by the material. The threshold electric bias required to have amplification (
k_{\text{TM}}^{\prime\prime}<0
) is the same as the stability threshold for the closed system discussed in subsection
V.1
. It is important to note that while such a system may be unstable under closed boundary conditions, a system with open boundaries—lacking the feedback loop provided by periodic boundary conditions—can, in principle, remain stable.

Note that similar to passive anisotropic media
Kong 1986
, the Poynting vector
\mathbf{S}_{\text{TM}}
is not parallel to the direction of propagation
\mathbf{k}
.
Provided
\left|\varepsilon_{c}\right|\ll\left|\varepsilon_{d}\right|
, so that the material response is quasi-isotropic, the angle between both vectors is quite small. For example, in the numerical example discussed below the angle is typically smaller than 1.

To illustrate the gain effect, we represent in Fig.

VI
Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper introduces a novel mechanism to implement the NHEO effect, achieving optical gain through nonlinear interactions between free and bound electrons. This mechanism operates independently of the anomalous velocity of Bloch electrons and the Berry curvature dipole. Our semiclassical phenomenological model shows that under non-equilibrium conditions and within specific low-symmetry systems, the linearized electromagnetic response becomes not only nonreciprocal but also capable of exhibiting polarization-dependent gain.

Our model ensures global passivity and adheres to the principles of microscopic reversibility. By considering the specific spatial symmetries of the model, we can significantly narrow down the permissible forms of nonlinear coupling. A notable aspect of the NHEO response is that it necessitates broken inversion symmetry. We demonstrate that an electrically biased material with
2mm
group symmetries can exhibit a robust nonreciprocal and non-Hermitian response.
We identify (natural) materials belonging to the class of polar/ferroelectric metals with the
2mm
group symmetry, such as for example WTe
2
, as potential candidates to implement this effect experimentally.

Furthermore, we analyzed the wave propagation within these systems and evaluated the application of our electrically biased material in devices such as kinetic Faraday rotators, Faraday isolators, and traveling wave amplifiers. In addition, the strength of the electric bias can be precisely adjusted to compensate for material dissipation, facilitating the realization of robust waveguides for nanophotonic applications. Our findings open new pathways for the use of electrically biased systems in photonic circuits, presenting a potentially interesting alternative to traditional methods reliant on magnetic effects or optical pumping.

Appendix A
Linearization of nonlinear conservative systems described by a Lagrangian

In this Appendix, we demonstrate that the linearization of nonlinear conservative systems, described by a Lagrangian and subjected to a time independent bias (equilibrium situation), results invariably in a Hermitian response.

We consider a general Lagrangian system in which the electromagnetic fields interact with matter. According to the classical theory of fields
Landau and Lifshitz 1980
, the action
S
in this system is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle S=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}\left(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}\right)dxdt

(41)

where
\partial_{x}=\partial/\partial x
,
\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\partial\mathbf{q}/\partial t
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}
is the Lagrangian density and
\mathbf{q}
represents either the matter fields (such as the displacement of the negative charge “cloud” with respect to the ion background, which is proportional to the polarization vector) or the components of the electromagnetic four-potential. For simplicity of notation, we consider only one spatial dimension (
x
) in Eq. (
41
), but the generalization to three spatial dimensions is straightforward.

The
N
equations of motion [
N\equiv\text{dim}(\mathbf{q})
] of the system are obtained from the Euler-Lagrange equations
Landau and Lifshitz 1980

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right),\quad 1\leq i\leq N

(42)

Except when the Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}
is a quadratic function of
\bf{q}
, the system (
42
) is inherently nonlinear.

Let us suppose that the system is subject to an external, time-independent bias. The equilibrium bias point is defined by
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}_{0}
,
\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
and
\partial_{x}\mathbf{q}=\partial_{x}\mathbf{q}_{0}
.
We aim to linearize the equations of motion around this bias point. To this end, the terms
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{i}}
,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}
in (
42
) are expanded into a Taylor series. To characterize the linear dynamics it is enough to limit the expansion to the first order. For a generic function
u\left(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}\right)
we have that:

\displaystyle u\left(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}\right)\approx u\left(\mathbf{q}_{0},\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}_{0}}\right)+\sum_{j}\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial q_{j}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})

(43)

where
\delta q_{j}
,
\delta\dot{q}_{j}
and
\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})
are respectively the
j
th component of the vectors
\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{0}
,
\dot{\mathbf{q}}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
and
\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}-\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}_{0}
. The subscript
0
indicates that the functions are evaluated at the bias point.
Substituting the Taylor expansions of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{i}}
,
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}
in Eq. (
42
) and taking into account that the equilibrium state satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equations,
\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\right)
, we find that the linearized equations of motion are

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})\right).

(44)

The sum over repeated indices is implicit.
This set of equations describes the dynamics of the linearized system.
Importantly, it is possible to find a quadratic Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}})
, that leads to the same set of linearized equations of motion. To do so, we simply impose that the Euler-Lagrange equations for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
(i.e. Eq. (
42
) with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}
substituted by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
) yields(
A
). Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
must satisfy the following conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})

(47)

Evidently, the Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}\delta q_{i}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial\dot{q}_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}\delta\dot{q}_{i}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial(\partial_{x}q_{j})\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{j})\delta(\partial_{x}q_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{ij}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial q_{i}}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}\delta q_{i}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial q_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta q_{j}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{i})+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}}{\partial\dot{q}_{j}\partial(\partial_{x}q_{i})}\right|_{0}\delta\dot{q}_{j}\delta(\partial_{x}q_{i})

(48)

As seen,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
is a quadratic polynomial of the three variables
\mathbf{q}
,
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
and
\partial_{x}{\mathbf{q}}
. For a bias point that is time independent, i.e. in an equilibrium situation, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
does not depend explicitly on time. Then, it can be inferred from fundamental Lagrangian mechanics that the system described by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
is a conservative system
Landau and Lifshitz 1976
. From this, we deduce the important result that the linearized system is conservative and as a consequence it can only describe an Hermitian system with an Hermitian linearized permittivity.
It is interesting to note that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}
coincides with the second order terms of a Taylor expansion of the original Langrangian density
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NL}}
.

Appendix B
Generalized microscopic model

In this Appendix, we extend the model of Sec.
III
to ensure that the conduction current satisfies the continuity equation. For simplicity, we ignore the effect of the “anomalous velocity”
\left(\mathbf{\overline{\zeta}}=0\right)
, so that
\mathbf{p}=m\mathbf{v}
.

Charge conservation can be included into the model, by replacing Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
) by
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
:

\displaystyle m\left(\partial_{t}+\Gamma+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\right)\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=q\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}\cdot\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}\right)

(49a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n+\nabla\cdot(n\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=0.

(49b)

\displaystyle m_{b}\left(\partial_{tt}+\gamma\partial_{t}+\sum_{j=x,y,z}\omega_{0j}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\right)\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=q^{2}n_{b}\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}\cdot qn\mathbf{v}\right)

(49c)

Here,
n
is the free electron density, which is related to the velocity through the continuity equation [Eq. (
49b
)]. It can be easily checked that provided
\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{12}^{T}=-\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{21}
[Eq. (
18
)], the fields are still constrained by a conservation law of the type
\nabla\cdot{{\bf{S}}_{{\rm{tot}}}}+{\partial_{t}}{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}=-Q
, with

\displaystyle{{\bf{S}}_{{\rm{tot}}}}

\displaystyle={\bf{E}}\times{\bf{H}}+\frac{1}{2}nm\left({{\bf{v}}\cdot{\bf{v}}}\right){\bf{v}},

(50a)

\displaystyle{W_{{\rm{tot}}}}

\displaystyle={W_{{\rm{EM}}}}+\frac{{{1}}}{{2}}nm{\bf{v}}\cdot{\bf{v}}+\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{2{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}\left({\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}+\sum\limits_{j}{\omega_{0j}^{2}{\bf{P}}\cdot{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}\otimes{{{\bf{\hat{e}}}}_{j}}}\cdot{\bf{P}}\right),

(50b)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle={{mn}\Gamma}{\bf{v}}\cdot{\bf{v}}+\gamma\frac{{{m_{b}}}}{{{n_{b}}{q^{2}}}}{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}\cdot{\partial_{t}}{\bf{P}}.

(50c)

Hence, this model also ensures that the material response remains passive (
Q>0
). Note that now both the Poynting vector (
\mathbf{S}_{\rm{tot}}
) and the energy density (
W_{\rm{tot}}
) have field and matter components.

This refined model may be relevant for field distributions that vary fast in space, specifically when the gradient of the velocity is significant.
The velocity gradients create
\mathbf{k}
-dependent terms in the linearized permittivity (spatial dispersion), which can be attributed to a Doppler shift due to the electron motion. Such terms can also originate a non-Hermitian and nonreciprocal response, but are only relevant for large drift velocities and for waves that vary fast in space (e.g., surface plasmon type excitations)
Morgado and Silveirinha 2018
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2021
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2017
. Otherwise, one can safely assume that
n(\mathbf{r},t)\approx n_{0}=const.
, as assumed in the main text.

Appendix C
Transmission and reflection matrices for a nonmagnetic material slab under plane wave illumination

In this appendix, we derive the reflection and transmission matrices for a homogeneous nonmagnetic material slab subjected to plane wave incidence with an arbitrary incidence direction. The slab thickness along the
y
direction is
d
(see Fig.
10
).

Figure 10:
Geometry of a material slab with permittivity
\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}
embedded in a dielectric of permittivity
\varepsilon_{\text{ext}}
under plane wave illumination. The slab is infinitely extended along the
x
and
z
directions.

The material is described by the permittivity tensor

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{xx}&0&\varepsilon_{xz}\\
0&\varepsilon_{yy}&0\\
\varepsilon_{zx}&0&\varepsilon_{zz}\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

C.1
Solutions of Maxwell equations

In the scattering problem, the electromagnetic fields in the
x
and
z
directions have a spatial variation of the form
\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
with
\mathbf{k}_{t}=k_{x}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{x}+k_{z}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
. The vector
\mathbf{k}_{t}
is determined by the spatial variation of the incident plane wave.

Then, the Maxwell equations

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}

(52)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{\overline{\varepsilon}}\cdot\mathbf{E}

(53)

for a material described by the permittivity tensor (
51
) can be cast into a form similar to a Schrödinger’s equation,

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\overline{M}}\cdot\mathbf{f},

(54)

where
\mathbf{f}\equiv\left(E_{x}\;E_{z}\;H_{x}\;H_{z}\right)^{T}
is the state vector and

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{M}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\frac{k_{x}k_{z}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yy}}&\omega\mu_{0}-\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yy}}\\
0&0&-\omega\mu_{0}+\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yy}}&-\frac{k_{x}k_{z}}{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{yy}}\\
-\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{zx}-\frac{k_{x}k_{z}}{\omega\mu_{0}}&-\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{zz}+\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{\omega\mu_{0}}&0&0\\
\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xx}-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{\omega\mu_{0}}&\omega\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{xz}+\frac{k_{x}k_{z}}{\omega\mu_{0}}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(55)

The solution of Eq.(
54
) is

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(y)=\text{e}^{-iy\mathbf{\overline{M}}}\cdot\mathbf{f}(0)

(56)

where the
4\times 4
matrix
\text{e}^{-iy\mathbf{\overline{M}}}
is obtained by taking the exponential of the matrix of
-iy\mathbf{\overline{M}}
. Equation (
56
) relates the state vectors
\mathbf{f}(y)
and
\mathbf{f}(0)
at two distinct points of the material in terms of the transfer matrix
\text{e}^{-iy\mathbf{\overline{M}}}
.

C.2
Reflection and transmission matrices

Next, we characterize the reflection and transmission matrices when the material slab is subjected to plane wave incidence.
As represented in Fig.
10
, the material slab interfaces are
y=0
and
y=d
.

Using the Maxwell equations in the dielectric region, it is feasible to relate the transverse components of the electric
\mathbf{E}_{t}=\left(E_{x}\;E_{z}\right)^{T}
and magnetic
\mathbf{H}_{t}=\left(H_{x}\;H_{z}\right)^{T}
fields associated with a plane wave through an admittance matrix
Morgado and Silveirinha 2016
;
Silveirinha et al. 2018
;
Latioui and Silveirinha 2019
;
Rappoport et al. 2023
. The result is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\pm},

(57)

where
\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}=\frac{c}{k_{y}\omega}\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{ext}}}{c^{2}}-k_{z}^{2}&k_{x}k_{z}\\
k_{z}k_{x}&\dfrac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{ext}}}{c^{2}}-k_{x}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(58)

with
\eta_{0}
the vacuum impedance and
k_{y}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{ext}}}{c^{2}}-k_{x}^{2}-k_{z}^{2}}
. In Eq. (
57
) the superscript
\pm
discriminates between plane waves propagating along the
+
or
-y
direction, respectively.

In the region
y<0
, the electromagnetic fields are a superposition of an incident wave and a reflected wave. We relate the reflected and incident fields at the
y=0
interface through a reflection matrix:
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{re}}=\mathbf{\overline{R}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}
with
\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{re}}=\left(E_{x}^{\text{re}}\;E_{z}^{\text{re}}\right)^{T}
. Then, the state vector
\mathbf{f}
for
y\leq 0
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(y\leq 0)=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}\text{e}^{ik_{y}y}+\mathbf{\overline{R}}\text{e}^{-ik_{y}y}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\\
\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}\text{e}^{ik_{y}y}-\mathbf{\overline{R}}\text{e}^{-ik_{y}y}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\end{pmatrix}.

(59)

Similarly, for
y\geq d
, the transmitted field can be expressed in terms of a transmission matrix as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(y\geq d)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\overline{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\text{e}^{ik_{y}(y-d)}\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\\
\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\text{e}^{ik_{y}(y-d)}\text{e}^{i\mathbf{k}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

Inside the slab, the state vectors
\mathbf{f}(y)
at
y=0
and
y=d
are linked by the relation (
56
). Then, enforcing the continuity of the tangential component of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
at the interfaces
y=0
and
y=d
, which is equivalent to enforce the continuity of the state vector
\mathbf{f}(y)
, we find that

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-id\mathbf{M}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}+\mathbf{\overline{R}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\\
\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}_{2\times 2}-\mathbf{\overline{R}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\overline{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\\
\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{T}}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{t}^{\text{inc}}\end{pmatrix}.

(61)

It is convenient to write the transfer matrix as

\displaystyle\text{e}^{-id\mathbf{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\overline{A}}&\mathbf{\overline{B}}\\
\mathbf{\overline{C}}&\mathbf{\overline{D}}\end{pmatrix}

(62)

where
\mathbf{\overline{A}},\,\mathbf{\overline{B}},\,\mathbf{\overline{C}}
and
\mathbf{\overline{D}}
are
2\times 2
matrices. After some algebra, one can find the following explicit formulas for the reflection and transmission matrices:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{R}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}+\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}-\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{T}}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{\overline{A}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}+\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}+\frac{2}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{\overline{B}}\cdot\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}+\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}

(64)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\left(-\mathbf{\overline{D}}\cdot\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}+\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{B}}\cdot\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\overline{Q}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\overline{C}}-\frac{1}{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\mathbf{\overline{A}}

(66)

For normal incidence (
k_{x}=k_{z}=0
) and a permittivity tensor as in (
33

64

38
) of the main text.
