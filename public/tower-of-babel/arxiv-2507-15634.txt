Caustics in a Near-Resonant Quantum Kicked Rotor
Yi Cao, Shaowen Lan, Bin Sun, Jie Liu

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the dynamics of the quantum kicked rotor in the near-resonant regime and observe a particular recurring cusp caustic of the wave functions’ accumulation. We then derive the path integral expression of the quantum evolution and analytically determine the conditions for the caustic formation and recurring period. A scaling power law with Arnold index of 1 / 4 1/4 relating to the wave amplitude enhancement depending on the kicking strength as well as the resonant detuning parameter, has been derived and verified. We also discuss classical-quantum correspondence of the caustics and find that chaos can disrupt the phase matching leading to the destruction of the caustic structure. Finally, possible experimental observations and some implications of these findings are discussed.



I
Introduction

Light propagating in media can give rise to some interesting optical phenomena through ray-focusing. Among these, the caustics such as glories and rainbows stand out for their vivid visual displays and profound physical origins
Berry and Upstill 1980
;
Arnol’d 1983
;
Stavroudis 2012
;
Berry 2015
. Such caustic effects, however, are not limited to optics. In other physical systems, generalized caustics arise when multiple particle trajectories converge, producing singularities that enhance some observable effects dramatically
Kravtsov and Orlov 1983
;
Safari et al. 2017
;
Xia et al. 2018
;
Liao et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2024
. The caustics also manifest as highly localized wave density peaks corresponding to the phase singularities
Ford and Wheeler 1959
;
Mumford et al. 2019
;
Kirkby et al. 2019
;
Kirkby et al. 2022
, where classical ray-focusing behavior is modulated by quantum interference
Berry and O’Dell 1999
;
O’Dell 2012
;
Goldberg et al. 2019
. Mathematically, the enhancement magnitude of the observables obeys a universal scaling law described by catastrophe theory, in which the scaling exponent is given by the Arnold index according to different classifications of the catastrophe singularity
Thom 1975
;
Arnold 1976
;
Arnold 1992
.

On the other hand, the kicked rotor (KR), as a paradigmatic model of Floquet systems, provides a fundamental platform to explore the quantum transport, energy diffusion and nonlinear dynamics of classical-quantum correspondence
Izrailev 1990
;
Santhanam et al. 2022
. Classically, the system displays interesting chaotic diffusion in momentum space, while the quantum effects (i.e., the quantum kicked rotor, QKR) give rise to richer dynamical phenomena including Anderson localization
Fishman et al. 1982
;
Raizen 1999
;
Billy et al. 2008
and quantum resonances
Izrailev and Shepelyanskii 1980
;
Moore et al. 1995
. The simplicity in form and experimental feasibility of the QKR model make it highly valuable for studying periodically-driven quantum dynamics
Casati et al. 1979
;
Adachi et al. 1988
;
Wimberger et al. 2005
;
Wang and García-García 2009
;
Chen and Tian 2014
;
Stöber et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
. Recently, the QKR model has been extended to nonlinear
Zhang et al. 2004
;
Liu et al. 2006
;
Zhao et al. 2014
;
Zhao et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2025
and non-Hermitian
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Zhao 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Zhao and Liu 2024
systems.

The QKR dynamics exhibit remarkable parallels with optical systems, where a kicking potential can be effectively modeled by periodic optical gratings (or thin lenses)
Nye 1999
;
Hecht 2016
. This analogy is further supported by previous studies showing that a quantum rotor under single delta-pulse excitation can generate characteristic caustic structures, similar to the focusing of optical thin lenses
Averbukh and Arvieu 2001
;
Leibscher and Averbukh 2002
;
Mumford et al. 2017
. Based on the similarity between optics and QKR systems, the well-established formation mechanism of optical caustics
Berry and Upstill 1980
;
Berry 1976
thus suggests that similar structural features might also emerge in the QKR phase space, governed by analogous catastrophe theory. This theoretical correspondence enables the QKR model to bridge optical caustics and nonlinear dynamical systems. The study of caustics in the QKR system could offer valuable insights into the universal mechanisms involved in the regularity-to-chaos transition.

In this paper, we investigate the quantum rotor dynamics driven by continuous delta-kick sequences and observe the caustic structures with remarkable spatiotemporal periodicity in the near-resonant regime. We establish a comprehensive path integral framework employing the stationary phase approximation to provide rigorous analytical justification for these numerical findings. We further discuss the implications of these findings and their possible experimental observations. The paper is structured as follows. Sec.
II
presents the dynamical evolution of the QKR through numerical simulations. Sec.
III
provides the theoretical interpretation of the recurring cusp caustics, and Sec.
IV
summarizes our conclusions.

II
Recurring Cusp Caustics in QKR

Dynamical evolution of the QKR (top) and its classical counterpart (bottom). The quantum system is initialized as the uniform state
\psi(\theta_{0},t_{0})=1/\sqrt{2\pi}
, while classical trajectories are initialized with

uniformly sampled. Left and right panels correspond to
\Delta=\sqrt{2}
and
\Delta=0.0001
, respectively. Other parameters are fixed at

and

|\psi(\theta_{n},t_{n})|

The dimensionless Hamiltonian of the QKR is given by
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2+K\cos\hat{\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT)
, where
\hat{\theta}
is the angle position operator with eigenvalues confined to
[0,2\pi)
, and
\hat{p}
is the angular momentum operator. Due to the periodic boundary conditions in
\theta
, the eigenvalues of
\hat{p}
are quantized as integers (in units of
\hbar=1
). Here,
K
represents the kicking strength, and
T
denotes the kicking period. When the kicking period satisfies
T=4N\pi
with
N
being a positive integer, the system exhibits quantum resonance
Santhanam et al. 2022
. In this work, we focus on the near-resonant regime and therefore introduce a detuning
\Delta
from a resonant condition by setting the kicking period to
T=4\pi+\Delta,\Delta/4\pi\ll 1
.

In the classical limit
\hbar\to 0
, the system’s dynamics can be analyzed using the Poincaré surface of section, reducing the continuous-time Hamiltonian to a discrete-time mapping. Specifically, the evolution follows the mapping:
\theta_{j+1}=\theta_{j}+p_{j}T~(\text{mod}~2\pi),~p_{j+1}=p_{j}+K\sin\theta_{j+1}
, where
(p_{j},\theta_{j})
denote the angular momentum and angle just after the
j
-th kick. Applying the momentum scaling
\tilde{p}_{j}=p_{j}T
, this mapping becomes equivalent to the standard mapping (or Chirikov-Taylor mapping)
Chirikov 1979
, characterized by an effective kicking strength
K_{\text{eff}}=KT
. The system undergoes a transition from regular to globally chaotic motion when
K_{\text{eff}}
exceeds the critical value
K_{\text{c}}\approx 0.9716

Chirikov 1979
;
Greene 1979
. Notably, in the quantum resonant regime, phase coherence effectively suppresses chaotic diffusion even when the effective kicking strength exceeds the classical Chirikov threshold, i.e.,
K_{\text{eff}}>K_{\text{c}}

Lepers et al. 2008
.

To examine detuning effects, we first numerically simulate the QKR and its classical counterpart (discrete-time mapping) under varying detuning at a fixed kicking strength
K=5
. The QKR is initialized as the uniform plane wave state,
\psi(\theta_{0},t_{0})=1/\sqrt{2\pi}
.
In contrast, the classical dynamics are sampled from five trajectories with initial positions uniformly distributed in
[0,2\pi)
at initial momentum
p_{0}=0
.

For large detuning
\Delta=\sqrt{2}
as shown in Figs.
1
(a) and (c), the classical dynamics displays global chaos since the effective kicking strength
K_{\text{eff}}
in the standard mapping substantially surpasses the Chirikov threshold
K_{\text{c}}
. In the quantum case, the far-from-resonant condition leads to a dynamical competition between dispersion-induced wave packet spreading and kick-driven phase modulation, which destroys regular quantum evolution and produces distinct quantum chaotic dynamics.

In the near-resonant regime
\Delta=0.0001
, as illustrated in Figs.
1
(b) and (d), a marked deviation emerges between quantum and classical dynamics. Although
K_{\text{eff}}\gg K_{\text{c}}
still holds, the classical system remains chaotic whereas the quantum evolution exhibits the regular pattern of recurring cusp caustics. This behavior differs from the caustics observed under single-pulse excitation
Mumford et al. 2017
, where the time-translation symmetry of free evolution decouples pulse timing from dynamical evolution and restricts the system to forming only a single cusp caustic structure, with subsequent free evolution displaying distinct Talbot interference features. Moreover, even under periodic kicks, no caustics are observed at quantum resonance. Instead, the system exhibits optical-Talbot-like interference when the initial wave packet is Gaussian
Lepers et al. 2008
. The interesting recurring cusp caustic structures have also been observed in the two-mode Bose-Hubbard model, which is a time-independent interacting many-body system
Kirkby et al. 2022
. In contrast, the QKR system has a periodically driven external potential that is usually expected to be non-integrable and chaotic. How the recurring cusp caustic pattern forms in this typical Floquet system is an interesting issue that calls for insightful investigations.

III
Theoretical Analysis

III.1
Path Integral Method

Since our model is time-periodic, Floquet theory implies that the evolution operator over one period can be expressed as
\hat{U}(T)=\hat{\mathcal{T}}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}\hat{H}dt\right)=\exp(-iK\cos\hat{\theta})\exp\left(-i\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2}T\right)
, where
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is the time-ordering operator. For the discrete time series
\{t_{j}\}
with uniform spacing
t_{j+1}-t_{j}=T
, the evolution operator reduces to

\hat{U}(t_{j+1};t_{j})=\exp\left(-iK\cos\hat{\theta}\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2}\Delta\right).

(1)

To characterize the quantum dynamical behavior, we employ the Feynman path integral formalism
Feynman et al. 2010
. The dynamical evolution of the initial quantum state
\psi(\theta_{0},t_{0})
to time
t_{n}
is described by

\psi(\theta_{n},t_{n})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(\theta_{n},t_{n};\theta_{0},t_{0})\psi(\theta_{0},t_{0})d\theta_{0},

(2)

where the propagator
G(\theta_{n},t_{n};\theta_{0},t_{0})
takes the form,

G(\theta_{n},t_{n};\theta_{0},t_{0})=\bigg\langle\theta_{n}\bigg|\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{U}(t_{j+1};t_{j})\right)\bigg|\theta_{0}\bigg\rangle.

(3)

For deriving the path integral representation, we express the propagator in the position basis through insertions of the identity operator
\int d\theta_{k}\ket{\theta_{k}}\bra{\theta_{k}}=1
, where
d\theta_{k}
denotes the angle position volume element at each intermediate time
t_{k}
(from
k=1
to
n-1
). The resulting propagator is then given by

G(\theta_{n},t_{n};\theta_{0},t_{0})=\int\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}d\theta_{k}\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\bra{\theta_{j+1}}\hat{U}(t_{j+1};t_{j})\ket{\theta_{j}}.

(4)

The matrix element
M_{j+1,j}\equiv\bra{\theta_{j+1}}\hat{U}(t_{j+1};t_{j})\ket{\theta_{j}}
can be represented by inserting a complete set of momentum eigenstates,

\begin{split}&M_{j+1,j}=\int dp_{j}\langle\theta_{j+1}|\hat{U}(t_{j+1};t_{j})\ket{p_{j}}\bra{p_{j}}\theta_{j}\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dp_{j}\exp\left[-iK\cos\theta_{j+1}-i\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2}\Delta+i(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})p_{j}\right].\end{split}

(5)

Here,
\langle\theta_{j}|p_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{i\theta_{j}p_{j}}
has been considered. This integral takes the form of a Gaussian integral, with the well-known solution

M_{j+1,j}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi i\Delta}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})^{2}}{2i\Delta}-iK\cos\theta_{j+1}\right].

(6)

Now, the propagator can be written as

\begin{split}&G(\theta_{n},t_{n};\theta_{0},t_{0})=\int\mathcal{D}\theta\exp\bigg\{i\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\bigg[\frac{(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})^{2}}{2\Delta}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad-K\cos\theta_{j+1}\bigg]\bigg\}\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\theta\exp\left(iS\right),\end{split}

(7)

where
\mathcal{D}\theta
denotes the path integral measure defined by
\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi i\Delta}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi i\Delta}}d\theta_{k}
, and
S
represents the discrete action.

III.2
Semiclassical Caustic Trajectory

Within the Feynman path integral framework, the primary contribution to the propagator arises from the classical path
\{\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}}\}
, and quantum effects emerge as perturbative corrections around this extremal path
Feynman et al. 2010
;
Berry and Mount 1972
. Employing a semiclassical expansion about the classical path, we decompose the path as
\{\theta_{j}\}=\{\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}}\}+\{\eta_{j}\}
, where
\{\eta_{j}\}
represents quantum fluctuations that satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions
\eta_{0}=\eta_{n}=0
. The discrete action
S
can be expanded in a Taylor series about the classical path:

S[\theta]=S[\theta^{\text{cl}}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\theta_{j}\partial\theta_{k}}\bigg|_{\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}},\theta_{k}^{\text{cl}}}\eta_{j}\eta_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),

(8)

where the quadratic term characterizes Gaussian fluctuations around the classical path, and higher-order terms account for non-Gaussian quantum corrections. In this subsection, we restrict our analysis to classical paths, with quantum corrections to be systematically addressed later. This semiclassical expansion approach requires the action to be extremized with respect to all
\theta_{j}
, leading to the discrete-time Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{\theta_{j+1}^{\text{cl}}-2\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}}+\theta_{j-1}^{\text{cl}}}{\Delta}=K\sin\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}},

(9)

which corresponds to the discrete mapping

\displaystyle\theta_{j+1}^{\text{cl}}=

\displaystyle\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}}+p_{j}^{\text{cl}}~~(\text{mod}~2\pi),

(10a)

\displaystyle p_{j+1}^{\text{cl}}=

\displaystyle p_{j}^{\text{cl}}+K\Delta\sin\theta_{j+1}^{\text{cl}}.

(10b)

The similar classical mapping in the near-resonant regime has also been derived in Refs.
Wimberger et al. 2004
;
Wang et al. 2011
, with the help of a fictitious classical limit. Our deduction here is based on the classical-quantum correspondence of the path integral method. As shown in Fig.
2
, classical paths with zero initial momentum and uniformly distributed initial positions across
[0,2\pi)
accurately reproduce the quantum caustic structure.

In the continuous limit
\Delta\to 0
(i.e., the near-resonant condition), the discrete sequence
\{\theta_{j}^{\text{cl}}\}
converges to a continuous trajectory
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}(t)
with
t=j\Delta
. Under this approximation, the Euler-Lagrange equation (
9
) then reduces to the nonlinear pendulum equation:

\ddot{\theta}_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\frac{K}{\Delta}\sin\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}},

(11)

where the dot denotes differentiation with respect to time
t
. This equation has a general solution

\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}(t)=\pi+2\arcsin\left[k\cdot\frac{\mathrm{cn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}{\mathrm{dn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}\right],

(12)

where
k=\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}-\pi}{2}\right)
, with
\mathrm{cn}(u,k)
and
\mathrm{dn}(u,k)
being the Jacobi elliptic cosine and delta amplitude functions. Figure
3
compares the discrete mapping simulations with the continuous analytical solution Eq. (
12
) at sampling times
t=n\Delta
. Excellent agreement is observed for small detuning (
\Delta\ll 1
), but this consistency deteriorates with increasing
\Delta
, and the approximation fails completely in the large-detuning regime.

) and Eq. (

In the classical dynamical system, caustics emerge as fundamental singularities in phase space where families of classical trajectories focus. Mathematically, these singular points are characterized by the vanishing derivative condition
{\partial\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}}/{\partial\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}}=0

Averbukh and Arvieu 2001
, which gives rise to the following caustic equation:

\frac{\mathrm{cn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}{\mathrm{dn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}+k\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\frac{\mathrm{cn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}{\mathrm{dn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)}=0.

(13)

The exact analytical solution to this equation remains challenging to derive, yet symmetry considerations clearly indicate that the or cusp points must lie on the
\theta=\pi
axis. By examining the properties of the arcsine function and the behavior of the Jacobi elliptic functions, we find that the caustic time (i.e., the time of cusp formation) can be well approximated by setting
\mathrm{cn}\left(t\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}},k\right)=0
, which implies

n_{\text{cau}}=(2m+1)\frac{\mathcal{K}(|k|)}{\sqrt{K\Delta}},~~m=0,1,2,\dots,

(14)

where
\mathcal{K}(|k|)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. Although
n_{\text{cau}}
formally depends on the initial angle
\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}
, the asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{K}(|k|)
shows that the dominant contribution comes from the
k\to 0
limit, where caustic features are most pronounced. For a system with the uniform initial angle distribution, we estimate the dominant caustic time by averaging over all possible values of
\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}
:

\langle n_{\text{cau}}\rangle=\frac{(2m+1)\pi}{2\sqrt{K\Delta}},~~m=0,1,2,\dots.

(15)

This result is shown in Fig.
3
, marked by red crosses. Furthermore, we numerically solve the caustic equation Eq. (
13
) and substitute the obtained solutions into the analytical expression Eq. (
12
) for final evaluation. The resultant profiles, displayed as magenta solid curves in Fig.
2
, not only capture the characteristic features of classical caustics but also exactly reproduce the quantum caustic structures. This operational procedure confirms that the quantum caustic dynamics can be completely reconstructed through classical solutions.

III.3
Quantum Caustics and Scaling Law

The semiclassical analysis establishes a clear correspondence between theoretically derived semiclassical caustics and their numerically observed quantum manifestations. We now demonstrate how these quantum caustics emerge fundamentally from the underlying propagator structure. In the continuous limit
\Delta\to 0
under stationary phase approximation, the quantum propagator takes the following form

G(\theta,t;\theta_{0},t_{0})=e^{iS[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]}\int\mathcal{D}\eta e^{\frac{i}{2}\delta^{2}S[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]},

(16)

where

\begin{split}\delta^{2}S[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]=&\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\left(\dot{\eta}^{2}+\frac{K}{\Delta}\cos\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}\eta^{2}\right),\end{split}

(17)

and a continuous functional measure

\mathcal{D}\eta=\lim_{\Delta\to 0}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi i\Delta}}\prod_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi i\Delta}}d\eta(t^{\prime}).

(18)

Applying integration by parts to the action variation, we can express
\delta^{2}S[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]
in a compact quadratic form

\delta^{2}S[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\eta\hat{O}\eta,

(19)

where the operator
\hat{O}
is defined as

\hat{O}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{\prime 2}}+\frac{K}{\Delta}\cos\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}.

(20)

Finally, using the Gaussian functional integral formula, the propagator simplifies to

G(\theta,t;\theta_{0},t_{0})=\sqrt{2\pi i}e^{iS[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]}(\det\hat{O})^{-1/2}.

(21)

The functional determinant
\det\hat{O}
for this second-order differential operator can be computed using the Gelfand-Yaglom method
Gel’fand and Yaglom 1960
;
Ossipov 2018
, which requires solving the following initial value problem:

\hat{O}u(t)=0,\quad u(t_{0})=0,~~\dot{u}(t_{0})=1.

(22)

The determinant is then given by

\det\hat{O}=u(t).

(23)

By differentiating the classical equation of motion Eq. (
11
) with respect to the initial condition
\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}
, we obtain the quantity
u(t)={\partial\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}}/{\partial\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}}
which holds only at caustics and demonstrates the classical-quantum correspondence. Importantly, when the condition
\det\hat{O}\to 0
is satisfied, it not only marks the failure of the stationary phase approximation but also reflects the emergence of structural singularities in the propagator’s phase space representation. These singularities enhance quantum fluctuations and produce strongly localized probability densities with delta-function-like peaks, forming the quantum caustic structures.

The wave function in the continuous limit is obtained by substituting Eq. (
21
) into Eq. (
2
):

\psi(\theta,t)=\mathcal{C}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{iS[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]}d\theta_{0},

(24)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a time-dependent normalization factor and the continuous-limit action is

S[\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\left[\frac{(\dot{\theta}_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}})^{2}}{2}-\frac{K}{\Delta}\cos\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}+K\dot{\theta}_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}\sin\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}\right].

(25)

We consider small perturbations around the equilibrium position
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi
by setting
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi+\delta
and
\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}=\pi+\delta_{0}
. Using the equation of motion Eq. (
11
), the wave function in Eq. (
24
) can be expressed in terms of these deviations as:

\begin{split}\psi(\theta,t)=&\mathcal{C}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\delta_{0}\exp\bigg\{i\bigg[\frac{2K}{\Delta}t\cos\delta-\frac{K}{\Delta}t\cos\delta_{0}\\
&-K\sin\delta(\delta-\delta_{0})\bigg]\bigg\}.\end{split}

(26)

Here,
\delta
and
t
are regarded as control parameters rather than variables. Taylor expanding
\cos\delta_{0}
around
\delta_{0}=0
to
\mathcal{O}(\delta_{0}^{4})
yields
\cos\delta_{0}=1-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\delta_{0}^{4}
. From Eq. (
26
), the coefficient of
\delta_{0}^{4}
is identified as
i\lambda\equiv-i\frac{K}{\Delta}t\frac{1}{24}
. Considering the behavior near the first cusp point, we set
t=\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{K}}
, so that
\lambda=-\frac{\pi}{48}\sqrt{\frac{K}{\Delta}}
. Based on the framework of catastrophe theory, near the cusp point, the field amplitude follows a power-law scaling behavior
Berry 1977
;
Arnold 1992
;
Kirkby et al. 2019
;
Mumford et al. 2019
:

|\psi(\theta,t)|\propto|\lambda|^{1/4}\propto\left(\frac{K}{\Delta}\right)^{1/8},

(27)

where
1/4
is called Arnold index. We take the maximum amplitude of the wave function to approximate the value at the cusp point, and the comparison between the numerical simulation results and the analytical scaling law is shown in Fig.
4
.

III.4
The Effects of Chaos and Nonlinearity

Applying the variable transformation
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi+\delta
, the equation of motion Eq. (
11
) transforms into
\ddot{\delta}+(K/\Delta)\sin\delta=0
. For the small-angle case
|\delta|\ll 1\text{rad}
, the sine function can be linearized as
\sin\delta\approx\delta
, with higher-order nonlinear terms becoming negligible. This linearized approximation yields a simple harmonic oscillator
\ddot{\delta}+(K/\Delta)\delta=0
characterized by an intrinsic oscillation period
T=2\pi\sqrt{\Delta/K}
that remains independent of initial conditions. For initial states localized within a neighborhood of
\theta_{0}^{\text{cl}}=\pi
, the linearized solution predicts periodic focusing at
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi
at discrete time instances
t=(2m+1)\pi/(2\sqrt{K/\Delta})
(with
t=n\Delta
). These focusing events correspond to the formation of cusp points that match our earlier theoretical predictions. This fundamental behavior extends naturally to other regular points in phase space, thereby constructing the complete caustic curves, as shown in Figs.
5
(a) and (d).

The above findings reveal a fundamental correlation between the caustic structures and the Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser invariant tori in the phase space. The critical transition occurs when the control parameter
K\Delta
surpasses the well-established Chirikov threshold of 0.9716
Chirikov 1979
, at which point significant overlap occurs in resonance bands away from the
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi
region, leading to the breakdown of invariant tori and the emergence of chaotic motion. This dynamical characteristic is demonstrated in Fig.
5
(b), while quantum simulations further confirm the corresponding destruction of quantum caustic structures in this region. A particularly noteworthy feature emerges from the inherent canonical symmetry of the nonlinear
\sin\delta
term, which preserves a robust one-dimensional invariant manifold structure in the immediate neighborhood of
\theta_{\text{con}}^{\text{cl}}=\pi
, thereby maintaining the integrity of caustic formations in this localized domain, see Fig.
5
(e). Upon further increasing

Our previous studies have investigated the conventional QKR, demonstrating distinct recurring caustic features in the near-resonant regime. For quantum many-particle systems, where interparticle interactions are typically non-negligible, examining their influence on dynamical evolution becomes crucial. Within the mean-field approximation framework, the QKR incorporates an additional nonlinear term of the form
g|\psi|^{2}
that significantly alters its dynamical behavior
Zhao et al. 2014
;
Zhao et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2025
. The nonlinear term introduces significant phase modulation effects that suppress subsequent caustics beyond the initial one, including the cusp singularities at
\theta=\pi
, by disrupting the quantum coherence essential for caustic development through density-dependent potential effects. Moreover, a striking asymmetry emerges between attractive (
g<0
) and repulsive (
g>0
) interactions. Figures
6

|g|=0.25
, attractive interactions completely eliminate all caustic features while repulsive interactions show weaker suppression. This asymmetry stems from their opposing influences on effective kinetic energy and quantum localization. Additionally, attractive interactions induce anomalous non-caustic focusing behind original caustic positions, representing transient quasi-stable configurations arising from competing nonlinear self-focusing and periodic driving.

IV
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have thoroughly investigated the dynamics of the celebrated QKR model and found the striking recurring cusp caustics in the near-resonant regime. Within the path integral framework and with the help of the Gelfand-Yaglom method, we have obtained the analytical expression for the recurring cusp period and the position of the caustic patterns. We have also established the scaling law of the amplitude at the first cusp point through catastrophe theory. The effects of chaos and nonlinearity are discussed.

The QKR model is a fundamental model that has been experimentally realized in various systems, such as optical systems
Prange and Fishman 1989
;
Fischer et al. 2000
;
Long and Zhang 2015
, ultra-cold atoms
Moore et al. 1995
;
Chabé et al. 2008
;
Lemarié et al. 2009
and femtosecond laser-controlled molecular polarization
Karle and Lemeshko 2024
. Our findings might have implications in quantum control, quantum simulation and will stimulate the related experiments.
