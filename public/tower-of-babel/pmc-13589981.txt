Microneedle-Enabled Exosome Therapy: From Tumor Targeting to Precision Treatment of Complex Diseases
Authors: Yuhui Fang, Hao Song, Dongxian Li, Yongyue Jiang, Kehan Zhao, Xinru Qiu, Chongguang Zhao, Xiaoyun Yu, Yumei Li, Xicheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes are nanoscale extracellular vesicles (EVs) that mediate intercellular communication by transporting proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity and intrinsic ability to protect labile cargo make them attractive therapeutic carriers. Yet their clinical translation remains constrained by heterogeneous isolation protocols, variable product purity, inefficient cargo loading, rapid systemic clearance and limited tissue-selective delivery. Engineering strategies, including parental-cell preconditioning, genetic modification, post-isolation cargo loading and surface functionalization, have improved the potency and targeting of exosome-based therapeutics but have not fully solved delivery-related bottlenecks. Microneedle (MN) systems provide a complementary solution by breaching the stratum corneum in a minimally invasive manner and depositing exosomes directly within defined tissue compartments. When integrated with dissolving, hydrogel, cryogenic, core-shell, Janus, threaded or stimulus-responsive MN architectures, exosomes can be retained locally, released in a programmed manner and protected from rapid degradation. This Review first summarizes the biological basis, source-dependent functions and engineering strategies of exosomes, and then outlines the design principles of MN platforms relevant to vesicle delivery. On this basis, we discuss representative studies in which MN systems have been explored to improve the local retention, controlled release and tissue-specific delivery of exosomes in cancer, wound repair, neurological injury, cardiovascular disease, immune-mediated disorders and other regenerative settings. We further summarize the translational barriers that remain for this emerging strategy, including vesicle characterization, potency assays, sterility control, scalable manufacturing, long-term safety, storage stability and batch-to-batch reproducibility.Keywords: microneedles, exosomes, extracellular vesicles, transdermal delivery, cancer immunotherapy, regenerative medicine

Biological Properties and Engineering Strategies of Exosomes as Therapeutic Carriers

This section outlines the biological rationale for using exosomes as therapeutic carriers and defines the main obstacles that currently limit their translation: inconsistent isolation and characterization, low and variable drug-loading efficiency, insufficient targeting specificity and uncertain potency metrics. We first summarize exosome origin, composition and source-dependent function, and then discuss engineering approaches based on parental-cell modification, physical cargo loading and chemical surface functionalization. The central criterion is not simply whether exosomes can be modified, but whether a given strategy preserves vesicle integrity while improving purity, yield, cargo stability and delivery precision.

Discovery and Fundamental Properties of Exosomes

In the early 1980s, small membrane-bound vesicles (~50 nm) were first observed in tumor cell culture media, with surfaces exhibiting 5’-nucleotidase activity.1 Subsequent work in reticulocytes suggested that these vesicles originate from late-stage endosomes; intraluminal vesicles accumulate within multivesicular bodies (MVBs), which can fuse with the plasma membrane and are enriched in transferrin receptors and other components.2 Electron microscopy further supported this release mechanism by visualizing extracellular vesicles (EVs) following MVB-plasma membrane fusion. After isolation by ultracentrifugation, these vesicles were termed “exosomes”.3 In current usage, exosomes are commonly discussed as small EVs, usually enriched in particles of ~50-150 nm, although authoritative EV guidelines emphasize that operational definitions should be based on source, separation method and characterization rather than size alone.4–6 Key advantages include nanoscale size, high biocompatibility, the capacity for cargo protection and intercellular communication, immunomodulatory activity, and amenability to engineering. Collectively, these properties underpin the growing interest in exosomes as therapeutic carriers and support their broad potential in disease treatment.7 A schematic overview of exosome biogenesis, engineering strategies, and their diverse functions in intercellular communication is illustrated in Figure 1.

Molecular Composition and Information Transfer Function

Natural and engineered exosomes can transport proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and small-molecule drugs, thereby enabling therapeutic intervention across multiple disease contexts.8 For example, engineered exosomes have been used to deliver siRNA or shRNA targeting oncogenic KRASG12D in pancreatic cancer models.9 At the molecular level, exosomes contain membrane proteins involved in adhesion and fusion, including integrins and tetraspanins, as well as regulatory molecules that influence metabolism and signal transduction. Their luminal cargo includes microRNAs (miRNAs), messenger RNAs (mRNAs), deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragments and long non-coding RNAs, which can reshape target-cell behavior through transcriptional, post-transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms.10 However, these biological advantages are meaningful only when vesicles reach the appropriate tissue at sufficient concentrations, which makes efficient and tissue-selective delivery a central translational requirement.

Cell-Specific Origin

The therapeutic activity and safety profile of exosomes are strongly influenced by their cell of origin.11 B-cell-derived exosomes can carry MHC molecules and support antigen presentation, illustrating how immune-cell origin determines vesicle function.12 Tumor-derived exosomes may contain invasion- and metastasis-related components, indicating that vesicle origin can also introduce disease-promoting risks.13 Tumor-derived vesicles can further reprogram stromal or mesenchymal cells within the tumor microenvironment through transferred proteins and miRNAs.14,15 Therefore, source selection is not a technical detail but a determinant of therapeutic potency, biodistribution, MN compatibility and translational risk.11,13,15 The major exosome sources relevant to MN-assisted delivery are compared in Table 1.

Comparison of Major Exosome Sources Relevant to MN-Assisted Delivery

Stem Cell-Derived Exosomes

Stem cell-derived exosomes are widely investigated as cell-free therapeutic candidates because they may retain part of the regenerative potential of their parent cells while reducing risks associated with live-cell transplantation.16,59 MSC-Exo are particularly relevant to regenerative medicine because mesenchymal stem cells possess immunomodulatory properties and broad translational potential.16,60 Neural stem cell-related evidence also supports the broader concept that stem-cell-derived products may be useful in neural regeneration.61 In hair-regeneration models, MSC-derived vesicles have been associated with Wnt/beta-catenin-related signaling and hair-follicle stem-cell activity.62–64 MSC-Exo can activate antioxidant defense pathways such as the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, which is relevant to oxidative-stress-associated tissue injury.17,25,26 MSC-Exo and preconditioned MSC-derived vesicles can modulate macrophage polarization and inflammatory signaling, supporting their use in inflammation-associated repair.18,33–36 MSC-Exo may also promote angiogenesis and epithelial repair through pro-regenerative signals such as VEGF-related, miR-126-related and Wnt4-associated pathways.20–23 At the same time, MSC-Exo can limit excessive fibrosis by regulating fibroblast activation and extracellular-matrix deposition.18,22,24

In addition to MSC-Exo, adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSC-Exo) can recapitulate several reparative properties of their parent cells. ADSC-Exo have been explored for skin ageing and pigmentation-related indications through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and anti-melanogenic effects.27–31,65 Mitochondria-enriched extracellular vesicles from ADSC may support chronic wound repair by improving mitochondrial function in damaged tissues.32

Immune Cell-Derived Exosomes

Immune cell-derived exosomes are important mediators of immune-cell communication because they can transfer proteins, lipids, mRNAs and miRNAs between immune and target cells.66,67 Macrophage-derived exosomes display functional plasticity that reflects the phenotype of their parent macrophages.41,42 M1 macrophage-derived exosomes can contain pro-inflammatory mediators such as miR-155 and may sustain inflammatory signaling.41 By contrast, M2 macrophage-derived exosomes have been linked to angiogenesis, macrophage phenotype switching and cutaneous wound repair.42,43,45 M2 macrophage-derived exosome-loaded MN systems have further been used to enhance diabetic wound healing, supporting their relevance to local immune microenvironment remodeling.44

DC-derived exosomes carry antigen-presentation and co-stimulatory molecules, including MHC I/II and CD86, making them relevant to cancer immunotherapy.46 Clinical studies of dendritic-cell-derived exosomes in melanoma and non-small-cell lung cancer suggest acceptable safety and immune activation potential in selected settings.47,48

Treg-derived exosomes can transfer immunosuppressive miRNAs and suppress pathogenic T helper responses.49 Treg-derived exosome-based MN systems have been explored for psoriasis, indicating their potential in local immune regulation.50

NK-cell-derived exosomes contain cytotoxic proteins such as perforin, granzyme B and FasL and can induce tumor-cell apoptosis in preclinical models.51 Recent studies further support NK-cell-derived exosomes as a potential cell-free platform for cancer therapy, although translational standardization remains challenging.52

For MN-assisted delivery, immune cell-derived exosomes are most relevant to local immunotherapy, inflammatory skin disease, wound repair and tumor immune modulation, but their immune potency requires careful control to avoid context-dependent inflammation, excessive immune suppression or off-target immune effects.41,49,51,52,66,67

Plant-Derived Exosome-Like Nanoparticles

Plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles have attracted interest as potentially scalable and low-immunogenic vesicle sources.56 Extracellular vesicles derived from plant leaves and stems have been reported to exert anti-melanogenic effects in melanoma cells and human skin models.29 Plant-derived vesicles from Polygoni Multiflori Radix have been linked to hair-growth-associated pathways through putative miRNA cargo.57

Ginseng-derived exosome-like nanoparticles have been reported to modulate the tumor microenvironment and exert anti-glioma activity.58 Therefore, plant-derived vesicle-like nanoparticles may be relevant to dermatological, inflammatory and topical or MN-assisted applications.29,56–58 However, their nomenclature, purity standards, batch consistency, cross-kingdom mechanisms and long-term safety remain less clearly defined than those of mammalian-cell-derived exosomes.56–58

Exosomes from Other Sources

HUVEC-derived exosomes have been implicated in vascular homeostasis and diabetic wound repair by improving endothelial function and promoting M2 macrophage polarization.53 GelMA/PEGDA MN patches loaded with HUVEC-derived exosomes have also been reported to promote diabetic wound healing, supporting their compatibility with MN-assisted vascular repair strategies.54 Tumor-derived exosomes can carry matrix-remodeling enzymes and other tumor-associated signals that promote invasion, metastasis and angiogenesis.13,55 Ovarian cancer cell-derived exosomes and chronic lymphocytic leukemia cell-derived exosomes can induce stromal or mesenchymal cells to acquire tumor-supporting phenotypes.14,15 Although tumor-derived vesicles may have diagnostic, antigen-delivery or tumor-homing relevance, their pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive risks require careful safety assessment before therapeutic use.13–15,55

Exosome Engineering, Cargo Loading and Isolation Strategies

As source- and context-dependent functions of exosomes become clearer, engineering strategies have been developed to enhance therapeutic performance.68 These strategies can be broadly divided into parental-cell engineering before vesicle release and post-isolation modification after vesicle purification.69 Parental-cell engineering often preserves membrane integrity and supports stable production of functional vesicles, whereas post-isolation loading allows direct incorporation of therapeutic cargo but may compromise vesicle integrity, reproducibility or purity depending on the method.69,70 The major engineering and loading strategies are compared in Table 2.

Parental-Cell Engineering and in situ Modification

Pretreatment of parental cells with defined stimuli can alter exosomal protein and RNA cargo without directly disrupting the vesicle membrane.69,71 Circadian cues, inflammatory stimulation and three-dimensional culture conditions may reshape vesicle cargo and thereby tune regenerative or immunomodulatory functions.72–74 Genetic engineering enables parental cells to generate vesicles that carry therapeutic nucleic acids or display targeting ligands, including peptides and antibodies.9,75 Surface-display scaffolds such as CD9, CD63, CD81, LAMP-2b and PDGFR transmembrane domains have been used to improve vesicle targeting.76,77 For neurological delivery, LAMP-2b fused to rabies virus glycoprotein peptide has been used to enhance neuronal targeting and blood-brain-barrier penetration.75,78,79 In cancer models, ligand-displaying exosomes can improve tumor accumulation through mechanisms such as EGFR or RGD-associated targeting.76,80 These strategies increase the design space of exosome therapeutics, but genetic modification also introduces concerns regarding off-target effects, transgene stability, potency drift and manufacturing control.5,6,70

Physical and Chemical Cargo-Loading Approaches

Electroporation, sonication, extrusion, incubation, freeze-thaw cycles, saponin-assisted permeabilization, click chemistry and liposome-exosome fusion have all been used to load or modify exosomes.69,81,84 Ultrasonication transiently disrupts the lipid bilayer to facilitate cargo entry and has been used to load miRNA cargo into BMSC-derived exosomes in disc-degeneration-related models.40 Cellular nanoporation can generate large quantities of exosomes enriched with exogenous nucleic acids and targeting peptides, making it attractive for scalable nucleic-acid delivery.83 Liposome-exosome fusion combines the loading capacity of liposomes with selected biological properties of exosomes and has been used to generate hybrid carriers, including ziconotide-loaded liposome-exosome systems integrated with MN arrays.84,85 Chemical modification can attach antibodies, peptides or aptamers to exosome surfaces, including through click chemistry; however, residual reagents such as copper must be controlled to reduce cytotoxicity.81 Antibody-modified exosomes targeting the CD47-SIRPalpha axis illustrate how surface functionalization can be used to alter tumor immune interactions.86

Isolation, Characterization and MISEV-Related Considerations

Post-isolation modification requires reliable exosome purification before cargo loading or surface functionalization.69 Differential ultracentrifugation remains widely used, but it is time-consuming and can co-isolate protein aggregates or other EV subpopulations.87,88 Density-gradient separation can improve purity but increases processing time and may reduce throughput.87 Ultrafiltration and polymer precipitation are operationally simple but often require additional clean-up to improve purity.87,88 Immunocapture can enrich defined marker-positive vesicles but may not represent heterogeneous EV populations and is difficult to scale.87 Emerging acoustic, microfluidic and electromagnetic approaches provide rapid processing for small volumes but require validation for standardized production.87,88 In accordance with MISEV guidance, isolation methods should be reported together with particle characterization, protein markers, purity indicators and operational definitions rather than relying on size alone.4–6 A comparison of exosome isolation methods is provided in Table 3.

Artificial Exosomes, Biomimetic Vesicles and Hybrid Nanocarriers

Artificial exosomes, exosome mimetics, biomimetic vesicles and exosome-nanoparticle hybrids broaden the design space beyond naturally secreted vesicles.68,69 Exosome-mimetic hybrid vesicles can combine membrane-derived biological cues with engineered nanocarrier properties, potentially improving drug loading, tumor targeting or manufacturability.68,69 Liposome-exosome hybrids can increase cargo capacity while retaining selected vesicle-associated compatibility, as illustrated by liposome-exosome fusion strategies for peptide or nucleic-acid delivery.84,85 These systems may be useful when native exosomes have insufficient cargo loading, limited scalability or heterogeneous potency, but their biological identity, safety profile and regulatory classification may be more complex than those of native EVs.5,6,69,70 For MN-assisted delivery, hybrid carriers are especially relevant when local retention, vesicle protection and controlled release must be balanced with higher drug-loading capacity.

Synergistic Effects of Exosomes and Microneedle Delivery Systems

This section explains why MNs are particularly well suited to exosome delivery. Conventional administration routes are limited by rapid systemic clearance, poor penetration across biological barriers, low local retention and, in the case of repeated injections, tissue trauma and infection risk.89–93 MN-based transdermal or local delivery addresses these limitations by creating controlled microchannels and depositing exosomes within defined tissue layers.90,93 A representative approach is the incorporation of exosomes into hyaluronic-acid-based MN patches, which combine minimally invasive delivery with local retention and sustained release. Intravenous administration can distribute vesicles systemically but is constrained by rapid clearance and poor biodistribution.89 Local injection can increase regional exposure but may cause tissue trauma, leakage and inconsistent retention.91 Passive topical or transdermal delivery is limited by the stratum corneum, which permits passive diffusion mainly of small and sufficiently lipophilic molecules; the 500-Da rule illustrates this barrier for passive skin absorption.92 Hydrogel-based local delivery can improve retention but may still be limited by tissue penetration and dose precision.94,95 By contrast, MNs can bypass the stratum corneum, reduce injection-associated pain and create localized depots for sustained release.90,93 Compared with conventional hypodermic needles, MNs enable minimally invasive access across the epidermal barrier while limiting penetration into deeper tissues (Figure 3). For exosome therapy, the key advantage of MNs is not merely tissue penetration but the ability to preserve vesicle bioactivity while reshaping local concentration, residence time and release kinetics. Specific Microneedle Design

Region-specific therapy addresses the spatial heterogeneity of human tissues by designing hierarchical distributions of physical structures or functional components, thereby achieving anatomical adaptability and localized treatment. Depth-specific designs are relevant because epidermal and mucosal thickness varies substantially across anatomical regions; adjustable MN systems have therefore been developed to accommodate different penetration-depth requirements.90,93,99 Temperature-specific designs are important because many exosome formulations are thermosensitive, and fabrication at room temperature or above 30 ℃ may compromise vesicle activity. Cryogenic MNs protect labile biologics by low-temperature molding and cold storage, although cold-chain logistics remain a major translational barrier.100 Functional specificity can be achieved through Janus, core-shell, hollow or retention designs that regulate tissue adhesion, phase-specific release, sampling or sustained implantation.18,20,101,102 Structure-specific MNs, including threaded or hook-shaped arrays, can improve anchorage and local retention in soft tissues or anatomically specialized sites such as the annulus fibrosus or tendon.40

Stimulus-Responsive Microneedles

Stimulus-responsive MNs integrate bioresponsive materials with micro- and nanofabrication to enable spatiotemporal control over drug release.104–106 Exogenous stimuli such as light, ultrasound, electricity, magnetic fields or heat can provide temporal control, although clinical practicality depends on stimulus penetration depth, device availability and safety.104–106,122 Photo-, ultrasound- or NIR-responsive MNs can trigger drug release, local heat or ROS generation and are most suitable for superficial lesions when penetration depth is limited.107–110 Electro-, mechanical-, magnetic- and heat-responsive MNs can modulate cargo release by external fields, strain or temperature-responsive materials.111–114 Endogenous stimulus-responsive MNs use disease-associated cues such as acidic pH, enzyme activity, glucose or redox gradients to provide closed-loop release without external devices.115–118 For exosome delivery, these systems are valuable only if responsiveness is reproducible and compatible with vesicle integrity, which makes scalable quality control, stimulus sensitivity and long-term biosafety essential translational requirements.

Design Parameters Governing Exosome Loading, Release and Delivery Performance

Needle length and tip geometry determine whether exosomes are deposited in the epidermis, dermis, mucosa, wound bed or deeper tissue compartments.90,93,97 Matrix composition influences both mechanical strength and vesicle stability during fabrication, storage and insertion.98–100 Dissolution or degradation kinetics control early burst release, sustained release or phase-specific release, as seen in core-shell and hydrogel-based MN systems.18,19,102 Loading mode is equally important: exosomes may be embedded within the matrix, concentrated in the tip, adsorbed onto porous materials or retained within hybrid hydrogel networks.18,19,123 Tissue adhesion and retention structures can reduce leakage and increase local residence time, which is relevant for uterine, tendon, wound and intervertebral-disc applications.20,40,98,101 Therefore, MN-mediated exosome therapy should be analyzed as a combined design problem that integrates vesicle source, engineering method, MN platform, loading strategy, release profile and disease microenvironment.

Precision Applications of Microneedle-Delivered Exosome Systems in Tumor and Disease Treatment

This section evaluates how MN-exosome systems can be adapted to disease-specific barriers, including tumor heterogeneity, chronic inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, fibrosis, ischemia, immune dysregulation and tissue-specific anatomical constraints. The relevant benchmarks are targeted accumulation, local retention, programmed release, therapeutic mechanism, comparative advantage over standard delivery and feasibility for translation. Therapeutic studies are distinguished as proof-of-concept, preclinical or clinical where possible, because most current evidence remains at the proof-of-concept or small-animal preclinical stage.

Cancer Treatment

Exosomes are attractive carriers for tumor therapy because they can promote intracellular delivery and can be engineered to modulate immune responses in the tumor microenvironment.124 MN-based delivery adds local deposition and controlled release, potentially increasing tumor-site exposure while reducing systemic toxicity.119,125–127 However, the current evidence base remains largely preclinical. Tumor heterogeneity, dense extracellular matrix, abnormal vasculature and limited deep-tumor penetration remain major barriers to effective MN-exosome therapy for cancer.

Drug Delivery

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy enhances antitumor responses by inhibiting immunosuppressive pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, but response rates remain suboptimal and immune-related adverse events remain clinically important.128–131 Localized delivery of ICB agents has been proposed to maximize tumor-local immune activation while reducing systemic adverse effects.125,126 Han et al summarized local and targeted strategies that combine vesicle engineering and MN platforms for immune-checkpoint modulation (Figure 5).127 In representative systems, engineered vesicles can provide immune-modulatory signals, whereas MNs support transdermal deposition, controlled release and prolonged local retention.119,127 The pancreatic cancer microenvironment, which is characterized by fibrosis and immune suppression, illustrates why additional matrix-penetrating and immunomodulatory strategies may be required beyond conventional nanodelivery.132

A critical limitation is that patch-like MN systems are best suited for skin, mucosa, superficial tumors, accessible lesions or postsurgical tumor beds. For deep-seated tumors such as pancreatic cancer, conventional transdermal MNs cannot directly reach the tumor mass, and delivery may require intraoperative placement, endoscopic assistance, implantable MN-like systems, or combination with systemic nanocarriers.125,132 Therefore, cancer applications should be interpreted according to anatomical accessibility rather than assuming that MN-based exosome delivery is broadly applicable to all solid tumors.

Tumor Detection

Tumor detection increasingly uses molecular biomarkers in biofluids, including circulating tumor DNA, miRNAs, circulating tumor cells and exosomes.133,134 Interstitial fluid has attracted attention as a diagnostic matrix because it contains tissue-proximal proteins and vesicles that may reflect local disease states.135 Park et al developed hydrogel MNs for extracting exosomes from interstitial fluid in colorectal cancer model mice.121 Exosomes captured by glypican-1 antibodies were recovered and used for cancer diagnosis, supporting the proof-of-concept value of MN-assisted exosome sampling.121 A schematic summary of MN-based immune-checkpoint delivery and MN-assisted ISF exosome extraction is shown in Figure 5.

Skin and Wound Repair

Skin Scar, Oral Ulcers and Diabetic Wound Healing

MN-exosome systems can promote skin repair by coordinating inflammation resolution, angiogenesis, fibroblast activity, matrix remodeling and re-epithelialization.136 In scar repair, core-shell MNs can coordinate early release of mangiferin with prolonged release of MSC-Exo, supporting phase-specific inflammation control and tissue regeneration.18 In oral ulcers, silk fibroin MNs loaded with LPS-preconditioned BMSC-Exo achieved deep mucosal penetration and promoted wound healing, illustrating the relevance of MNs for mucosal vesicle delivery.39 In diabetic wounds, adaptable indwelling MNs containing MSC-Exo improved local delivery and wound repair20, whereas MOF-based MNs loaded with exosomes combined electrostatic loading, acidic microenvironment-responsive release, Zn2+-associated antibacterial effects and pro-angiogenic signaling.123 Exosome- and antibiotic-loaded MN systems have also been used to remodel high-glucose-associated macrophage dysfunction and clear biofilms in diabetic MRSA infection models.137 These examples remain primarily preclinical but show how MN architecture can link release kinetics, immune remodeling and tissue regeneration. Tendon-Bone Interface Healing

Tendon-bone interface healing is limited by insufficient mechanical integration and inflammatory microenvironmental dysregulation. A triphasic MN delivery system combined optimized exosomes with layered MN architecture to enhance tendon-to-bone healing.98 Grapefruit-derived exosome-loaded MN systems have also been reported to reduce oxidative stress, promote collagen synthesis and induce M2 macrophage polarization in tendinopathy models.138 These studies highlight the need to integrate tissue anchorage, sustained release and microenvironmental modulation in musculoskeletal applications.

Alkaline Corneal Burns

Alkaline corneal burns involve epithelial injury, sustained inflammation, apoptosis, necrosis and fibrosis.139–141 ADSC-derived exosomes provide immunomodulatory and regenerative signals, but eye drops and subconjunctival injection often show poor retention and limited ocular bioavailability.142,143 Dissolvable MNs can improve engineered exosome penetration and retention in the corneal stroma.120 Anti-TNF-alpha-antibody-conjugated ADSC-Exo-loaded MNs illustrate how exosome engineering and MN delivery can be integrated for local, multi-targeted ocular therapy.120

Neurological Disorders

Spinal Cord Injury

After spinal cord injury, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation and glial scar formation create an inhibitory microenvironment that limits neural regeneration.144,145 MSC-Exo can modulate this microenvironment, and 3D-culture-derived exosomes may better preserve donor-cell phenotypes than conventional 2D-culture-derived vesicles.94,146,147 Han et al developed a GelMA MN hydrogel platform loaded with 3D-MSC-Exos for in situ sustained release.19 Compared with injection, this system increased exosome retention and maintained release for up to 72 h in rat SCI models, improving functional recovery and reducing neuroinflammation.19 The overall design of the GelMA-MN@3D-Exo system is shown in Figure 7.

Cardiovascular Disease

Cardiac Fibrosis Following Myocardial Infarction

Cardiac fibrosis following myocardial infarction is mediated in part by myofibroblast differentiation and abnormal extracellular-matrix deposition.148–150 Current biomaterial and drug-delivery approaches remain limited by insufficient targeting and stability.96 Yuan et al developed an exosome-based MN patch for localized myocardial delivery of miR-29b.82 HUCMSC-derived exosomes were loaded with miR-29b mimics by electroporation and integrated into a biodegradable MN array, improving local retention and controlled release in a myocardial infarction mouse model.82 This approach demonstrates how exosome cargo engineering and MN-mediated local delivery can be combined to address fibrotic remodeling. A schematic representation of the miR-29b-loaded exosome MN patch is shown in Figure 7.

Myocardial Infarction

An MN-microbot hybrid system using asymmetric CaCO3 particles has also been developed for exosome delivery after myocardial infarction.151 The platform moves within the acidic infarct microenvironment, supports deeper tissue delivery, enables rapid VEGF release and improves cardiac function in animal models.151 Although promising, this strategy remains preclinical and requires further validation of biodistribution, repeat-dose safety and manufacturability.

Immune-Mediated Diseases

Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis is characterized by chronic inflammation mediated in part by TNF-alpha.102 Wen et al developed cryomicroneedles loaded with milk-derived exosomes encapsulating TNF-alpha siRNA for percutaneous acupoint administration. In this system, exosomes serve as siRNA carriers, whereas cryogenic MNs enable transdermal delivery and reduce the drawbacks of injection-based administration.102 This example demonstrates how MNs can combine local tissue access with nucleic-acid-loaded vesicles for inflammatory disease, although clinical translation will require potency, safety and dosing validation.

Psoriasis

Local immune dysregulation in psoriasis creates a need for lesion-directed therapies that avoid systemic immune suppression. rExo@DMF MNs integrate regulatory T-cell-derived exosomes with dimethyl fumarate and suppress keratinocyte hyperproliferation and local inflammation while promoting Treg differentiation.50 A dual-segment light-guiding MN system containing MXene photothermal materials and M2 exosomes has also been developed for localized photothermal and immunomodulatory treatment of psoriasis.152 These examples illustrate how MN structure can be adapted to skin-specific optical and immune constraints. Treatment for Other Diseases

Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

Ischemia-reperfusion injury involves oxidative stress, inflammation and cell death. GelMA MNs loaded with 3D-culture-derived exosomes improved local retention and supported sustained release for up to 72 h in ischemia-reperfusion models.17 A related exosome-loaded MN patch has been reported to ameliorate renal ischemia-reperfusion injury after transplantation.153 These studies support a general design principle: exosome potency and local delivery must be optimized together.

Intrauterine Adhesions

Intrauterine adhesions can cause infertility and menstrual dysfunction, and conventional local delivery systems may show weak tissue adhesion and rapid degradation.95,154–156 A Janus MN patch combining an inner adhesive, exosome-loaded layer with an outer anti-adhesion barrier improved MSC-Exo retention in the uterine environment and supported epithelial proliferation and angiogenesis.101 This dual-function design addresses both tissue regeneration and recurrence prevention.

Chronic Pain

Chronic pain management is limited by opioid tolerance, dependence and adverse effects.157 A GelMA MN array delivering ziconotide-loaded liposome-exosome hybrid carriers improved delivery efficiency and sustained analgesic activity in preclinical pain models.85 This example illustrates how exosome-hybrid carriers and MNs can be integrated to improve local peptide delivery.

Intervertebral Disc Degeneration

Intervertebral disc degeneration is characterized by extracellular-matrix degradation, annulus fibrosus injury and impaired mitochondrial quality control.158 BMSC-derived exosomes have been explored for IVDD therapy but are limited by low delivery efficiency and rapid clearance.37 miR-378 can regulate mitochondrial homeostasis by modulating the PDK-Akt pathway and mitophagy.38 Hu et al loaded miR-378 into BMSC-derived exosomes and incorporated the engineered vesicles into threaded MNs designed for annulus fibrosus repair.40 The threaded architecture improved anchorage and local retention, whereas the biodegradable porous matrix supported sustained release.40 Disease-specific applications, therapeutic payloads, MN designs and evidence stages are summarized in Table 6.

Translational Roadmap, Future Outlook and Limitations

MN-exosome systems offer a compelling strategy for localized and precision therapy, but their clinical translation requires a rigorous development framework. First, exosome identity and potency must be defined with standardized characterization, impurity profiling and release assays in accordance with EV reporting principles.4–6 Second, MN materials and architectures must be optimized not only for insertion strength and release kinetics but also for preservation of vesicle integrity during fabrication, storage and use.98–100 Third, scalable manufacturing remains unresolved: batch-to-batch consistency, sterility assurance, cost-effective production, cryostability or lyophilization, packaging and cold-chain requirements must be addressed before commercialization.70 Fourth, preclinical studies should move beyond short-term small-animal efficacy and include biodistribution, cellular uptake, dose-response relationships, repeat-dose toxicity, immunogenicity, local tissue reactions, degradation products and long-term functional outcomes. Finally, regulatory translation will require clear definitions of the active substance, mechanism-based potency assays and clinically meaningful endpoints.
