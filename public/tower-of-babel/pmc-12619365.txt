Lactate and gastric cancer immunotherapy from regulatory mechanisms to therapeutic strategies: a critical review
Authors: Yuanzhu Hao, Yanan Zhang, Yu Zhang, Duo Yang, Dan Ren, Yixing Yue, Qian Li, QingLing Fan, Chao Sun, Manli Cui, Mingxin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with current immunotherapies exhibiting limited therapeutic efficacy owing to the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME).Main bodyLactate, a hallmark metabolic by-product of tumour glycolysis, also functions as a critical metabolic driver. By promoting TME acidification, immunosuppression, and epigenetic reprogramming, lactate emerges as a pivotal regulator in reshaping GC immune evasion and therapeutic resistance, while also strongly correlating with chemotherapy resistance. Furthermore, the dysregulated expression of lactate-associated metabolic genes represents a promising therapeutic target.ConclusionThis review elucidates the dual roles and clinical value of lactate in gastric cancer as both an energy substrate and a signalling molecule within the tumour microenvironment and positions lactylation as a critical metabolic–epigenetic bridge. We also discuss innovative strategies that integrate lactate metabolism-targeting immune checkpoint inhibitors to overcome therapeutic resistance in GC.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Lactate metabolism, Tumour microenvironment, Immunotherapy, Lactylation, Immune evasion

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is a leading malignancy worldwide [1]. Over the past decade, immunotherapy has emerged as a major focus in anti-tumour treatment [2]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and its antagonist (anti-CTLA-4), as well as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antagonists (anti-PD-1/PD-L1), have brought new hope to cancer treatment. However, their benefits for most patients are limited.

The limited efficacy of ICIs in several GC patients is primarily attributable to tumour intrinsic heterogeneity and [3] the immunosuppressive metabolic reprogramming of the tumour microenvironment (TME) [4]. Tumour heterogeneity—through altered antigen presentation, immune cell infiltration, and genomic landscape modifications—creates an immunologically “cold” TME, a phenotype synonymous with intrinsic resistance to ICIs [5]. Concurrently, metabolic reprogramming, characterised by lactate overproduction, nutrient depletion, and TME acidification, directly impairs cytotoxic T cell function while promoting the expansion and immunosuppressive activity of regulatory cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) [6, 7].

Lactate reflects tumour cell growth and serves as a key metabolite across different tumour types [8–11]. During tumour progression, lactate in the TME continuously nourishes and promotes tumour cell proliferation by promoting the expansion of tumour-infiltrating Tregs [9], driving M2 macrophage polarisation [11], strengthening melanoma invasiveness through a lactate dehydrogenase A-dependent secretion mechanism [8], and supporting the tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolism that enhances melanoma metastatic potential [10], thereby playing a crucial role in tumorigenesis. During lactate metabolic reprogramming, regulatory T cells shift from glucose metabolism to lactate metabolism by upregulating lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) Solute Carrier Family 16 member 1 (SLC16A1) gene expression [9], and M2 macrophages utilise lactate for mitochondrial metabolism and regulate histone acetylation [11]. Highly metastatic melanoma cells adapt to glucose-deficient environments using lactate as an alternative carbon source [10]. This process reshapes nutrient utilisation, and targeting lactate metabolism reverses these pro-tumour effects, highlighting its therapeutic potential [8, 10, 11].

Lactic acid’s role in TME and as an immunotherapy target has been explored. However, the existing studies have primarily focused on the role of lactic acid in metabolic reprogramming, and a comprehensive review addressing its systematic association with immune evasion in GC remains scarce. Furthermore, research on lactate modification in GC is limited, underscoring the need to integrate its mechanisms with epigenetic regulation to more clearly highlight the “research gap” in lactic acid studies.

This study systematically analysed the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of lactic acid metabolism in the immune microenvironment of GC, elucidating the network system linking lactic acid metabolism, immunity, and epigenetics. This review also summarises the latest therapeutic strategies that combine lactic acid metabolism targeting with ICIs, offering key insights into immune evasion mechanisms of GC. This study provides a solid theoretical framework for future development of combination therapies targeting metabolic checkpoints. This study aimed to advance the clinical treatment of GC treatment towards a more precise and effective manner.

Lactate: rethinking its role beyond a glycolytic byproduct

Biological basis of lactate metabolism

In aerobic environments, glucose is metabolized into pyruvate via glycolysis in the cytoplasm, yielding a small amount of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) [12]. Pyruvate then enters the mitochondria, where it is oxidatively decarboxylated in the mitochondrial matrix via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, resulting in the production of a substantial amount of NADH and reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2). These reducing equivalents are oxidised in the electron transport chain, simultaneously generating large amounts of ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. Under hypoxic conditions, pyruvate is further reduced to lactate. Lactate production appears to serve the purpose of maintaining the supply of oxidised nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD +) during glycolysis, rather than directly participating in energy production. Therefore, lactate has long been considered a metabolic by-product.

Lactate levels in tumour cells is significantly higher than in normal tissues [13], primarily because of the abnormal tumour vasculature, which limits oxygen supply. This unique enhancement of glycolysis in tumour cells is closely associated with the occurrence, progression, invasion, metastasis, drug resistance, and poor prognosis of GC [14]. The “Warburg effect” represents an adaptive strategy employed by cancer cells through various mechanisms. Its core mechanisms include mitochondrial dysfunction or aberrant activation of oncogenic signalling pathways that inhibit the entry of pyruvate into the TCA cycle, thereby forcing pyruvate to be converted to lactate by LDHA. Glycolytic intermediates can be diverted into the synthesis pathways of nucleotides, lipids, and amino acids, facilitating the rapid proliferation of cancer cells, and the rapid energy supply from glycolysis enhances tolerance to hypoxia and provides cancer cells with a survival advantage in poorly vascularised regions [15]. Furthermore, lactate is a key factor that promotes lactylation, and studies have suggested that, as compared to normal tissues, the levels of lactylation in tumour tissues are significantly higher and are associated with poor patient prognosis [16].

Lactate as a signalling molecule: roles in metabolic and cellular crosstalk

Recent research has revealed that lactate plays a more significant role than merely serving as a metabolic by-product. It functions as an important signalling molecule in tumour metabolism (Fig. 1). The TME plays a crucial role in the progression of GC, and lactic acid concentrations are significantly elevated within the TME. Several studies mention the role of lactic acid in the TME and have shown that lactic acid not only serves as a traditional energy source in tumour metabolism, but also acts as a key signalling molecule that regulates tumorigenesis and progression through various molecular mechanisms.

Studies indicate that lactic acid accumulation in the TME acts as an agonist of G protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81), promoting angiogenesis, immune evasion, and chemoresistance through the sustained activation of the GPR81/Giα-Gβγ-RhoA-ROCK1-p38 signalling axis [17, 18]. The inhibition of LDH-A suppressed tumour growth in preclinical models [19, 20]. Lactic acid-induced lactylation promotes tumour immune evasion by creating an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment, regulating the function of immunosuppressive cells, and upregulating PD-L1 expression in cancer cells [21]. Moreover, metabolic reprogramming can significantly regulate the phenotypic transformation of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) and tumour-associated neutrophils (TANs) [20]. This process ultimately leads to the suppression of anti-tumour immune responses, whereas lactic acid/lactate-induced interactions with stromal and endothelial cells enhance metabolic reprogramming within the tumour [22]. These studies indicate that the inhibition of lactic acid production holds promise for preventing the progression of tumour cells, representing a novel approach for cancer treatment.

Specifically, studies have confirmed that adipocyte-derived lactic acid binds to prolyl hydroxylase domain-containing protein 2 (PHD2) and inhibits its activity [23]. We hypothesise that a similar mechanism exists in tumour cells, where lactic acid, as a metabolic by-product, inhibits PHD2 while simultaneously reducing its hydroxylation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), thereby stabilising HIF-1α and activating the HIF-1α/BNIP3/Beclin-1 signalling axis [24]. This mechanism promotes autophagy of granulocytes under hypoxic conditions [25], supporting the formation of the TME.

It activates cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), promotes the secretion of interleukin-8 (IL-8) by CAFs [26], recruits macrophages, and promotes the polarisation of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [27, 28], thereby enhancing the immunosuppressive TME. On the other hand, studies have revealed that lactic acid modifies the membrane-organising extension spike protein (MOESIN) in Tregs, improving the interaction between MOESIN and the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signalling receptor and downstream Smad3 signalling, stabilising the expression of forkhead box protein P3 (Foxp3), and promoting the metabolic reprogramming of Tregs, thereby enhancing their immunosuppressive function [29]. Furthermore, lactic acid is absorbed by Tregs via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), facilitating the translocation of nuclear factor of activated T-cells 1 (NFAT1) to the nucleus, which enhances PD-1 expression and leads to improved Treg function [30].

The presence of lactic acid strengthens the relationship between metabolism and epigenetics [22], and research on lactic acid modification of histones and non-histones has become increasingly clear. Histone lactylation is a novel type of histone acylation modification discovered in the recent years that is involved in important biological processes, such as transcription and metabolism. Numerous studies have shown that under conditions of hypoxia, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), lipopolysaccharides (LPS), or bacterial infection, histone lactylation accumulates in the cytoplasm, leading to the production of lactic acid [31].

In summary, promotion of tumour immune evasion and acceleration of disease progression may be targeted by inhibiting lactic acid production, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy for GC and improving prognosis. Lactylation plays a role in the occurrence and development of various cancers, as evidenced by tumour initiation, progression, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming of tumour cells [31–33]. Altering cellular metabolism as a novel approach offers hope for cancer treatment, specifically by identifying methods to inhibit or modify metabolic processes within the TME to regulate immune metabolism, which may significantly enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy [34].

Lactate and immune evasion in gastric cancer

Role of lactate in modulating immune cell function

CD8 + T lymphocytes

Interactions between lactic acid and immune cells can impair cell differentiation, reduce immune responses, facilitate immune evasion, and lead to defects in treatment sensitivity, thus playing a critical role in the occurrence and progression of GC [14, 17]. Recent studies have indicated that histone lactylation using lactic acid as a substrate indirectly affects the metabolism and functional regulation of CD8 + T lymphocytes (CD8 + T cells) [35]. CD8 + T cells are key cells responsible for executing immune surveillance functions [36], killing tumour cells by releasing perforin and granzyme [37], expressing Fas ligand (FasL) that binds to the Fas receptor present on tumour cells [38], and secreting tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IFN-γ, and interleukin-2 (IL-2) [37]. These actions directly or indirectly induce apoptosis in tumour cells, inhibit tumour proliferation, and enhance the sensitivity of tumour cells to immune cells. Previous studies have suggested that the acidic environment generated by the accumulation of lactic acid in the TME can affect the function and activity of CD8 + T cell surface receptors [39], inhibiting the expression of monocarboxylate transporter 11 (MCT11), and thereby reducing the secretion of cytokines and granzymes [40]. CD8 + T cell exhaustion is promoted by lactate under repeated tumour antigen stimulation; however, when the exposure time is prolonged without antigen stimulation, stem cell-like differentiation is driven by histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27) acetylation through the Transcription Factor 7 (TCF7) locus. Furthermore, anti-tumour immunity can be enhanced when targeting lactate metabolism is combined with ICIs, thereby demonstrating synergistic effects [41].

Tregs

Helper T cells (Th cells or cluster of differentiation 4-positive T lymphocytes (CD4 + T cells)) play a crucial role in assisting the body to eliminate tumour cells [42, 43], whereas regulatory Tregs within the CD4 + T cell family occupy a central position. The role of lactic acid in tumour cells is closely related to that of regulatory Tregs, which are associated with the occurrence and progression of GC. In the TME of GC, Tregs are divided into three distinct subgroups, among which effector Tregs (eTregs) perform sustained immunosuppressive functions [44]. Qiang Zou et al. revealed that lactic acid induces the upregulation of Foxp3 expression, which in turn increases the expression of ubiquitin-specific peptidase 39 (Usp39). Usp39 promotes spliceosome assembly by binding to the splicing factor 3b complex (SF3b), a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism that ensures effective splicing of the CTLA-4 precursor messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA). This process ultimately enhances the expression of the immune checkpoint protein CTLA-4, significantly increasing the immunosuppressive capacity of tumour-infiltrating Tregs [45].

The activation of the TNF-α/TNFR2 signalling pathway enhances the functionality of Tregs, resulting in a high infiltration level of TNFR2 + regulatory Tregs within the GC TME. Tregs not only exhibit an immunosuppressive phenotype but also perform immunosuppressive functions, which are considered independent risk factors and prognostic markers for GC [46]. M2 tumour-associated macrophages (M2 TAM) and N2 tumour-associated neutrophils (N2 TAN) secrete various chemokines. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) promote the polarisation of M2 TAM, thereby increasing the number of Tregs within the TME and facilitating the growth of tumour-associated vasculature [44]. These studies indicated that Tregs play a crucial role in the progression of GC. Therefore, modulating the immunosuppressive function of Tregs through direct or indirect pathways represents a novel approach for the treatment of GC. Lactic acid lowers the pH of the TME and creates an excessively acidic environment [47] that oxidises lactic acid, thereby enhancing cell survival and proliferative capacity [48]. As potential therapeutic targets, studies have demonstrated that reducing the pH of the TME can inhibit tumour progression [34, 49].

TIMs

Similar to the microenvironment of other tumour cells, the TME of GC is composed of various cell types, including inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and nerve cells. These cells promote tumour initiation and growth by secreting various molecules that activate growth signals in cancer cells or remodel the surrounding tissue [50]. The TME of GC also contains various other immune cell types, including helper T cells, dendritic cells, TAMs, TANs, mesenchymal stem cells, MDSC, and associated inflammatory pathways [12, 13] that possess the ability to produce inhibitory factors with profound implications for the development of GC. Within the GC TME, TAM M2 polarisation is driven by tumour-derived lactate through multiple mechanisms: HIF-1α stabilisation (which upregulates Arg-1/VEGF), GPR132/GPR65/GPR81 activation, and H3K18la induction. In addition, macrophage glycolysis is suppressed by non-histone lactylation (e.g. PKM2 lactylation), thereby forming a metabolic–epigenetic loop that enhances GC immune evasion. Conversely, TAM immunosuppression can be reversed by targeting the lactate/lactylation pathway, which synergises with ICIs [51].

TAMs, MDSCs, and TANs are all classified as tumour-infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs). With the accumulation of lactic acid in the TME, protein lactylation regulates N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of TIMs mediated by methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), thereby promoting the immunosuppressive functions of TIMs and facilitating tumour immune evasion [52] (Fig. 2).

Lactic acid reprograms immune checkpoint signalling: metabolic crosstalk in the TME

Lactic acid may indirectly influence the efficacy of ICIs by regulating the expression or function of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4. The fundamental mechanism of immune checkpoint therapy (ICT) is to block inhibitory signals that suppress T cell activation, thereby achieving tumour suppression [53]. In the past decade, ICT has developed rapidly and played a significant role in the treatment of various tumours [54, 55]. To date, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has approved 12 ICIs: two CTLA-4 inhibitors (ipilimumab and tremelimumab), six PD-1 inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab, retifanlimab, and toripalimab), three PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, durvalumab, and avelumab), and one lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) inhibitor (relatlimab). These agents are indicated for monotherapy or combination therapy in first-, second-, third-, and subsequent-line cancer treatments [56, 57]. Moreover, neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has been experimentally applied to various cancer types, which not only enhances the resectability of tumours but also boosts the body’s anti-tumour immunity by targeting the elimination of distant micro-metastases to prevent postoperative recurrence [58].

Although this mechanism has not been explicitly addressed in GC, studies have shown that in lung cancer cells, the accumulation of lactic acid in the TME activates GPR81, leading to a decrease in intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels and further inhibition of protein kinase A (PKA). This process activates the transcriptional activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), which in turn promotes the activation of the PD-L1/PD-1 immune checkpoint pathway, thereby impairing T cell function [59], suggesting that similar mechanisms may exist in GC. Additionally, a prospective cohort study involving 117 patients with HER2-negative advanced GC or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma found that patients with a higher relative abundance of lactic acid bacteria exhibited greater microbial diversity and a better response to PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy [60], suggesting a correlation between lactic acid and the efficacy of immunotherapy. CAF-derived IL-8 upregulates PD-L1 expression in GC cells through the p-p38, p-JNK, and p-NF-κB pathways. Furthermore, repertaxin, an IL-8 receptor inhibitor, attenuates the CAF-mediated protective effects of GC cells on CD8 + T cells, enhances T cell cytotoxicity, and improves anti-PD-L1 antibody efficacy by targeting IL-8/CXCR1/2 signalling [62].

Studies have analysed GC samples and found that glycolysis-related gene sets are significantly enriched in tumour tissues with high PD-1 expression. In addition, in vitro cellular and in vivo animal model experiments were conducted to establish different models for observing tumour-related indicators and the effects of PD-1 inhibitors. Ultimately, we concluded that in a high-glycolysis TME, lactic acid could promote the expression of PD-1 in Treg cells and inhibit the expression of PD-1 in effector T cells. Furthermore, PD-1 blockade in a high lactic acid environment activates Treg cells, enhancing their immunosuppressive activity and leading to suboptimal efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy. This study also suggested that lactic acid serves as a unique metabolic checkpoint for Tregs and may be used as a predictor of the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade therapy [30]. In GC, PD-L1 is upregulated by the lactate transporter SLC16A7 (MCT2). SLC16A7 silencing reduces PD-L1 expression, disrupts PD-1/PD-L1 binding, enhances T cell cytotoxicity, and inhibits M2 macrophage polarisation. GC prognosis and ICI response can be predicted using a lactylation gene signature that includes SLC16A7, with lower-risk patients demonstrating a higher tumour mutational burden (TMB)/microsatellite instability (MSI) and improved clinical outcomes [61].

In addition to PD-1/PD-L1, lactic acid may influence the expression of other immune checkpoint molecules. Lactic acid enhances the expression of the immune checkpoint CTLA-4, thereby increasing the immunosuppressive function of Tregs. The combined use of PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors can enhance anti-tumour efficacy in patients with various types of solid tumours [62, 63]. Cadonilimab is a PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody that exhibits higher binding activity in tumour tissues [64, 65]. The incidence of related adverse events is low [48, 49], and there is a favourable clinical response and safety profile [66]. It has been approved for use in combination with oxaliplatin and capecitabine as a first-line treatment for unresectable locally advanced recurrent or metastatic gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma [67–71]. Although ICIs have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic efficacy in cancer patients, their effectiveness remains limited to certain cancer types or subsets of patients. This heterogeneity in treatment outcomes may be associated with cancer type, intrinsic tumour characteristics, and specific components of the TME [4, 72]. Elevated lactic acid levels in the TME may constitute a critical mechanism contributing to resistance against ICIs or suboptimal therapeutic efficacy, which is primarily attributed to the lactate-mediated modulation of PD-1 expression in immune cells [30], This mechanistic insight suggesting that pharmacological inhibition of lactic acid production within the TME could potentiate the clinical effectiveness of ICIs in cancer treatment.

Lactate and lactylation: bridging metabolism and epigenetics

However, lactate functions far more than a metabolic waste product or signalling molecule through surface receptors. These multifunctional regulatory factors orchestrate metabolic reprogramming, modulate protein lactylation, mediate immune suppression, drive the development of drug resistance, promote epigenetic modifications, and ultimately facilitate tumour metastasis through interconnected molecular cascades [73]. Studies have confirmed that elevated lactate concentrations in the TME modulate the lactylation of various proteins within tumour cells. As a canonical tumour suppressor protein, p53 undergoes lactate-induced lactylation, which prevents its binding to response elements, reduces its activity, and impairs cell cycle regulation [74], thereby facilitating tumour progression (Fig. 3).

Lactylation plays a key role in the interactions between tumour metabolism and epigenetics. Key enzymes and metabolites involved in lactylation are crucial for this process [75]. Acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2) converts lactate to lactyl-CoA, whereas lysine acetyltransferase 2 A (KAT2A) catalyses histone H3 lactylation [76]. Histone acetyltransferases, including P300, lysine acetyltransferase 8 (KAT8), lysine acetyltransferase 7 (KAT7), and lysine acetyltransferase 5 (KAT5), function as “writers” that promote protein lactylation; Histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) and sirtuin (silent mating type information regulation 2 homolog) 3 (SIRT2-3) serve as “erasers” that remove lactyl groups [75]. Alanyl-transfer tRNA synthetase 1/2 (AARS1/2) can function as both a lactate sensor and a lactyltransferase, participating in lactylation. Furthermore, the lactyl groups involved in lactylation are derived from two primary sources: L-lactyl-CoA and S-D-lactyl glutathione, which mediate enzymatic and nonenzymatic lysine lactylation, respectively [75]. In tumour cells, lactate is metabolised into key compounds that serve as substrates for lactylation modification and subsequently influence tumour initiation and progression. Similar regulatory mechanisms may exist in GC; however, current research has not clearly elucidated these processes.

Lactate exerts critical effects on tumour (DNA) damage repair and chemotherapy resistance by enhancing lactylation of the Nijmegen breakage syndrome protein 1 (NBS1) [77]. As mentioned previously, the accumulation of lactate within the TME regulates m6A modification through protein lactylation, thereby promoting the immunosuppressive functions of TIMs and mediating tumour immune evasion [52].

In recent years, lactylation, a novel post-translational modification, has emerged as a critical nexus between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation. Lactylation not only serves as the terminal product of glycolysis but also functions as a metabolic signalling molecule that accumulates abundantly in the TME through the Warburg effect. The lactylation modification formed via its covalent binding to lysine residues of proteins plays essential roles in gene expression regulation, cell fate determination, and disease progression. In 2019, Zhao Yingming's team first reported histone lactylation as a post-translational modification, revealing a novel mechanism through which lactate regulates gene transcription via an epigenetic pathway. This study demonstrated that under hypoxic or inflammatory conditions, enhanced glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation, which activates acyltransferases such as CREB-binding protein (CBP)/p300 to catalyse lactylation modifications of both histone (e.g. H3K18la) and non-histone proteins (e.g. meiotic recombination 11 homologue A (MRE11), p53) [31].

In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, H3K18la upregulates TTK protein kinase (TTK) and BUB1B mitotic checkpoint serine/threonine kinase (BUB1B) gene expression through a positive feedback loop, promoting glycolysis and malignant tumour progression [78]. Furthermore, lactylation dynamically competes with acetylation, and demethylating enzymes such as histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) can reverse this process, forming a sophisticated metabolic–epigenetic regulatory network [79].

Lactylation directly participates in DNA damage response. Lactylation of MRE11 at lysine 673 enhances its DNA-binding capacity and promotes homologous recombination repair, thereby conferring chemotherapy resistance, whereas pharmacological inhibition of CBP or LDHA blocks this modification, significantly increasing sensitivity to cisplatin and poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors [80], suggesting that targeting lactation is a new strategy to overcome drug resistance.

Phosphorylation at serine 267 (S267 phosphorylation) and nuclear translocation of ACSS2 (acetyl-CoA synthetase 2) can be induced by EGFR-ERK signalling activation. ACSS2 then acts as a lactyl-CoA synthetase to convert lactate into lactyl-CoA and subsequently forms a complex with histone KAT2A. Through histone lactylation at H3K14/K18 sites, the expression of Wnt/β-catenin and NF-κB pathway genes, as well as PD-L1 (CD274), is regulated, ultimately promoting tumour immune escape. Tumour growth can be significantly inhibited, and CD8 + T cell infiltration can be enhanced by combining a peptide targeting the ACSS2-KAT2A interaction with anti-PD-1 antibody treatment [76].

Studies have revealed a pivotal role of lactate-driven NBS1 lactylation in facilitating DNA repair and conferring chemotherapeutic resistance. Stilbestrol can inhibit LDHA, decrease lactate production, and lower the level of NBS1 lactylation, thereby disrupting DNA repair and rendering drug-resistant cancer cells more sensitive to DNA-damaging treatments (such as cisplatin and radiotherapy). In mouse models and patient-derived organoid experiments, the combination of stilbestrol with cisplatin and radiotherapy significantly inhibited tumour growth and extended mouse survival [77]. Overall, these findings underscore the significance of inhibiting lactate production and lactylation as a breakthrough strategy for improving drug resistance in GC.

Targeting lactate and lactylation: a key direction for next-generation immunotherapy

As GC treatment continues to face numerous challenges, including delayed diagnosis, intricate biological characteristics, the complexity of surgical procedures, chemotherapy and drug resistance issues, bottlenecks in immunotherapy, exorbitant costs associated with new drug research, poor prognosis, and survival rates, as well as the majority of patients experiencing cachexia in advanced stages, significantly affecting quality of life [81–84], there is an urgent need for a novel drug to enhance current therapeutic approaches [85].

The Glo1 (glyoxalase 1)-mediated metabolic pathway has recently been identified as a key upstream source of protein D-lactylation. This pathway begins with the production of methylglyoxal (MgO), a by-product of glycolysis. S-D-lactyl glutathione (LGSH) is synthesised by Glo1 using MgO as a substrate, and this compound can act as a direct donor to induce the D-lactylation of proteins through a unique nonenzymatic mechanism [86–88]. Unlike p300/CBP-mediated enzymatic lactylation, this process relies on adjacent cysteine (Cys) residues as catalytic intermediates to transfer the lactyl group to spatially adjacent lysine (Lys) residues, exhibiting a high degree of structural selectivity [86].

LGSH accumulation is precisely regulated under inflammatory conditions and within the TME. For example, the expression of glyoxalase 2 (GLO2), its degrading enzyme, is downregulated by immune activation through NF-κB signalling, while GLO1, its synthase, is upregulated by the high glycolytic characteristics of tumour cells, which together leads to LGSH accumulation, thereby activating D-lactylation [86]. Functionally, D-lactylation is an important negative immune regulatory mechanism. K310 and other sites of p65, a key subunit of NF-κB, can be modified by D-lactylation, and its transcriptional activity can be inhibited, thus driving macrophages to polarise toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and establishing a negative feedback loop of “inflammation-lactylation-inhibition-inflammation” [86].

In tumour biology, this mechanism plays a pivotal role. D-lactylation enhances the immunosuppressive function of TAMs by modifying histones (such as H3K18la) or signalling proteins [87, 88]. Moreover, chemoresistance can be induced by upregulating genes such as the glutamate–cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC) subunit in colorectal cancer stem cells [89]. Clinically, activation of this pathway is associated with the severity of inflammatory diseases and the prognosis of patients with tumours [86], providing a new strategy for targeting the metabolic-immune crosstalk.

As a pivotal molecule in tumour metabolism, lactate plays a crucial role in the formation of the TME, interplay between metabolic and immune networks, and the precise regulation of lactylation modifications, gradually transitioning from fundamental mechanisms to clinical advancement. Studies have demonstrated significant breakthroughs in cancer treatment through regulatory strategies targeting lactate metabolic reprogramming in the TME. By employing specific interventions such as lactate dehydrogenase inhibitors and monocarboxylate transporter blockers, in conjunction with ICIs or chemotherapeutic agents, these strategies can synergistically reverse the immunosuppressive TME and enhance drug sensitivity [90, 91]. The underlying mechanisms encompass the modulation of the HIF-1α/PD-L1 signalling axis [92–94], the remodelling of T-cell metabolic adaptability, and the activation of immunogenic cell death pathways [95–97].

As for the impact of TME acidification caused by lactic acid on immune cells, we speculate that in the future, we can improve the degree of acidification in the GC TME, weaken its impact on immune cells, and allow autoimmune cells to play a role in inhibiting tumour cell growth, improving cancer prognosis, and prolonging survival. Regarding the effect of lactic acid on immune checkpoints, as mentioned above, targeting LDH combined with ICIS has achieved good results in animal models [77]; however, further in vivo model validation is expected in the future.

Lactate modification has emerged as a promising breakthrough in reversing the lactate-mediated drug resistance mechanism, with inhibition of lactate production capable of restoring sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs [77]. Regarding its role in predicting prognosis, a meta-analysis assessing overall survival revealed a significant correlation between elevated LDH levels and poor overall survival, suggesting that LDH levels may play a crucial role in predicting GC survival in future research. We hypothesised that higher LDH levels in patients with advanced GC may indicate a heightened metabolic state and more severe tumour infiltration.

With the rise of follow-up artificial intelligence (AI), significant achievements have been made in oncology, particularly in tumour research [98], diagnosis, and treatment [99, 100]. AI technology can mine and analyse large amounts of data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. Therefore, AI can be used for cancer diagnosis and treatment prediction and as a tool to study basic biological problems. AI in GC can be used to assist in the discovery of early GC using endoscopy, intelligent diagnosis, pathological diagnosis of GC, and prediction of the survival cycle and prognosis [101]. Surprisingly, it has been reported that AI can be used to evaluate the TME, therapeutic effect [102], and efficacy of immunotherapy [103]. This suggests that the future is an era of precise tumour treatment, with immunotherapy combined with AI emerging as a trend.

This review indicates that lactate transcends beyond its role as a metabolic by-product and emerges as a central regulator within the GC TME. Immunosuppression is driven by lactate accumulation through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including acidification of the TME, direct modulation of immune cell function (particularly CD8 + T cells, Tregs, and TAMs), and metabolic reprogramming, which favour tumour progression and immune evasion. Furthermore, lactate serves as a precursor for lactylation, a novel post-translational modification that bridges metabolic activity and epigenetic regulation, thereby influencing gene expression, DNA damage repair, and chemotherapy resistance. The intricate signalling networks orchestrated by lactate, including the GPR81-mediated pathway and MOESIN lactylation in Tregs, highlight its potential as a therapeutic target. Targeting lactate metabolism through inhibition of LDHA or monocarboxylate transporters holds promise for reversing the immunosuppressive TME and enhancing the efficacy of existing immunotherapies, such as ICIs. Future research should focus on elucidating specific lactylation targets in GC and validating combination therapies in clinical settings. Integration of metabolic targeting with immunotherapy represents a promising strategy for overcoming therapeutic resistance and improving the outcomes of patients with GC. Furthermore, utilizing AI can enhance patient stratification and treatment prediction, advancing personalised therapeutic approaches.
