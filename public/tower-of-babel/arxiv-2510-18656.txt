Constraints on Attractor Models of Inflation and Reheating from Planck , BICEP/Keck, ACT DR6, and SPT-3G Data
John Ellis, Marcos A. G. Garcia, Keith A. Olive, Sarunas Verner

Abstract
We analyze the latest cosmic microwave background (CMB) constraints on the scalar spectral index n s n_{s} and tensor-to-scalar ratio r r from Planck 2018, BICEP/Keck 2018, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope Data Release 6 (ACT DR6), and the South Pole Telescope (SPT-3G) data, focusing on their implications for attractor models of inflation. We compare systematically observational bounds with theoretical predictions for both E-model ( α \alpha -Starobinsky) and T-model potentials. The observational constraints accommodate E-models with α ≲ 25 \alpha\lesssim 25 , with the canonical Starobinsky model ( α = 1 \alpha=1 ) predicting n s = 0.958 − 0.963 n_{s}=0.958-0.963 for reheating temperatures between 100 − 10 10 100-10^{10} GeV, in good agreement with Planck 2018 data and within the 95% CL region determined by the Planck -ACT-SPT combination, but below the 95% confidence region of the Planck -ACT-DESI combination. Higher reheating temperatures from near-instantaneous reheating improve the compatibility. T-models predict slightly lower n s n_{s} values (0.956-0.961), in some tension with Planck 2018 data, and we find an upper limit of α ≲ 11 \alpha\lesssim 11 in these models. We extend our analysis to generalized α \alpha -attractors with monomial potentials V ⁡ ( φ ) ∝ φ k V(\varphi)\propto\varphi^{k} near the minimum, demonstrating that models with k ≥ 6 k\geq 6 naturally predict n s ≃ 0.965 − 0.968 n_{s}\simeq 0.965-0.968 for typical number of e e -folds, in better agreement with the ACT DR6 data. We also consider deformed E- and T-models, which allow significantly higher values of n s n_{s} for low values of α ≃ 1 \alpha\simeq 1 . VIII
Conclusions

Currently, models of inflation are constrained by CMB observations that determine the spectral tilt of scalar perturbations,
n_{s}
, and bound the tensor-to-scalar ratio,
r
. The former indicates a definite departure from purely scale-free perturbations, as is expected in many models of single-field inflation. The convergence of a value of
n_{s}
near 0.97 began with WMAP
wmap
, and
Planck
2018 data
Planck
reached a precision exceeding 0.5 %. Among the well studied and relatively simple single-field models of inflation, the Starobinsky model
Staro
appeared to be in best agreement with
Planck
2018 data. For example, for
N_{*}\sim 52
(corresponding to a reheating temperature

GeV), this model predicts
n_{s}\sim 0.963

Planck

,
which predict a slightly lower value for
n_{s}
for the same reheating temperature.

These models are special cases of a more general class of model known as
\alpha
-attractors. The Starobinsky model (and its T-model cousin) can be formulated in the context of no-scale supergravity
ENO6
;
Cecotti
;
GKMO
with a Kähler potential given by Eq. (
8
) with
\alpha=1
. The Kähler potential determines the metric in field space, and the no-scale potential characterizes a field space of constant curvature (
R=2/3
). As is evident from the form of Eq. (
8

\alpha\neq 1
yields field spaces of arbitrary constant curvature
R=2/3\alpha

\alpha
-attractor models
ENO7
;
Kallosh:2013hoa
;
T-model
;
KLR

\alpha>1
predict similar (though slightly higher) values of
n_{s}

but significantly higher values of
r

N_{*}=52

predicts

Planck
/BICEP/Keck upper limit of
r<0.036

BICEP2021

\alpha=25
in the E-model, and
\alpha=11
in the T-model.

-attractors generated by the prefactor of the Kähler potential in Eq. (
8

we have considered generalized versions of the E- and T-models as described by Eqs. (
V.1

C
Numerical Computation of Inflationary Observables

While the slow-roll approximations presented in Section
IV
provide valuable analytical insights, precise comparison with CMB data requires numerical integration of the perturbation equations. This is particularly important for models near the boundary of observational constraints, where percent-level accuracy in
n_{s}
can determine viability.

We compute the primordial scalar power spectrum by solving the equation of motion for gauge-invariant curvature perturbations. In the uniform-density gauge, the Mukhanov-Sasaki variable is defined as:

Q_{k}=\delta\varphi_{k}+\frac{\dot{\varphi}}{H}\Psi_{k},

(C.1)

where
\delta\varphi_{k}
and
\Psi_{k}
are the Fourier modes of the inflaton and metric perturbations, respectively. The evolution equation for
Q_{k}
follows from the perturbed Einstein equations
Lalak:2007vi
;
egno3
:

\ddot{Q}_{k}+3H\dot{Q}_{k}+\left[\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}+m_{\text{eff}}^{2}\right]Q_{k}=0\,,

(C.2)

where the effective mass term is:

m_{\text{eff}}^{2}=V_{\varphi\varphi}-\frac{\dot{\varphi}^{4}}{2H^{2}}+\frac{2\dot{\varphi}V_{\varphi}}{H}+3\dot{\varphi}^{2}\,.

(C.3)

We impose Bunch-Davies initial conditions in the sub-horizon limit:

Q_{k}=\frac{1}{a\sqrt{2k}}e^{-ik\tau}\quad\text{for}\quad k\gg aH\,,

(C.4)

where
\tau=\int dt/a
is the conformal time. The comoving curvature perturbation is related to the Mukhanov-Sasaki variable by

\mathcal{R}_{k}=\frac{H}{\dot{\varphi}}Q_{k}\,.

(C.5)

The dimensionless power spectrum, defined through

\langle\mathcal{R}_{k}\mathcal{R}_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\rangle=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k)\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}}),

(C.6)

is evaluated numerically at horizon crossing (
k=aH
) for each mode. The spectral index is then computed from its definition at the end of inflation:

\displaystyle n_{s}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{d\ln\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}}{d\ln k}\bigg|_{k=k_{*}}.

(C.7)

Analogously, we solve numerically for the tensor perturbations, using the standard transverse, traceless perturbation. The equation of motion satisfied by the tensor mode functions is given by

\ddot{h}_{k,\gamma}+3H\dot{h}_{k,\gamma}+\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}h_{k,\gamma}\;=\;0\,.

(C.8)

where
\gamma=+,\times
denotes the two polarization states. The corresponding power spectrum is defined as

\sum_{\gamma=+,\times}\langle h_{k,\gamma}h^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime},\gamma}\rangle\;=\;\frac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{3}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}(k)\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}}),

(C.9)

with the tensor-to-scalar ratio computed then as follows,

r=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}}{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}}\bigg|_{k=k_{*}}.

(C.10)

Our numerical results reveal appreciable corrections to the slow-roll predictions. Fig.
9
illustrates the relationship between
n_{s}
and

computed using three methods:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}=\frac{\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{2M_{P}^{2}H^{2}},

(C.11)

\displaystyle\eta_{H}

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon_{H}-\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{H}}{2\varepsilon_{H}H}.

(C.12)

The potential slow-roll approximation exhibits systematic errors of
\Delta N_{*}\gtrsim 1
when matching to a given value of

This discrepancy arises from violations of slow-roll near the end of inflation, higher-order gradient corrections in the perturbation equations, and the evolution of slow-roll parameters during horizon crossing. For precision cosmology, however, full numerical integration remains essential, particularly when confronting models with tight observational constraints such as those from ACT DR6.
