Hom-unitality and hom-associative structures
Germán García Butenegro, Abdennour Kitouni, Sergei Silvestrov

Abstract
We study hom-associative structures on general, possibly non-associative algebras, focusing on one-sided and two-sided unital algebras. New characterizations and aspects of these structures, along with some important subclasses, are explored for non-associative algebras. By exploiting the observation that the twisting linear map in the hom-associativity axiom of one-sided unital hom-associative algebras is a left or right multiplication operator by an element of the algebra (obtained by the action of the twisting map on a corresponding one-sided unity), a new characterization of the multiplicative twisting operators (or, in other words, the multiplicative hom-associative algebras) is established for one-sided unital algebras. This demonstrates a strong connection between multiplicativity in hom-associative structures and the idempotents of the algebra, thereby further enhancing our understanding of the structure and special nature of multiplicative hom-associative algebra structures as a special subclass of arbitrary hom-associative structures with arbitrary linear twisting maps. Furthermore, new insights into subspaces and a subalgebra of hom-unities, algebra elements that induce hom-associativity by multiplication, are obtained. Moreover, since non-unital hom-associative algebras need not be twisted by a multiplication operator, a unitalization process is employed to describe a subalgebra of two-sided hom-unities that induce hom-associative structures on such algebras. This is formulated in terms of eigenspaces of multiplication operators within the algebra. Additionally, the obtained insights and general results about the structure and characterization of hom-algebra structures are applied to some important known general classes of non-associative algebras, such as commutative, possibly non-associative algebras, Cayley-Dickson algebras, Leibniz algebras, and hom-Leibniz algebras.

: hom-associative algebras, unity, hom-unity

Introduction

Hom-algebra structures provide a unified framework for the joint study of seemingly unrelated classes of algebraic structures by relating their defining axioms through linear twisting maps (unary operations) that deform these axioms in specific, non-trivial ways. Such deformations establish connections between the axioms of various related but distinct known and new classes of algebraic structures, enabling their simultaneous consideration and classification, and uncovering common or distinct properties and relationships between their substructures and morphisms. In particular, hom-algebra structures of a given type include their classical non-twisted counterparts for special choices of twisting maps in the defining identities, and they create additional opportunities for deformations, extensions of cohomological structures, and representations.

The area of hom-algebra structures originated with the work of Hartwig, Larsson, and Silvestrov

Unitality in hom-associative algebras
[
5
,
6

[

While classical unitality ensures that multiplication interacts with the identity element in a strict way, hom-unitality relaxes this requirement by incorporating the twisting map, making it compatible with the hom-associative structure. Hom-unities play an important role in the structural analysis and construction of hom-associative algebras. They often arise naturally in deformation processes, where classical identities are replaced by twisted analogues to maintain consistency with the underlying hom-structure. For example, in Ore-type extensions and hom-analogues of Weyl algebras
[

3

[

Beyond theoretical interest, these constructions have applications in quantum deformations and non-commutative geometry, where hom-type identities model symmetries that are no longer strictly associative but remain coherent under twisting.

The goal of this article is to study in-depth the connection between unitality and hom-unitality in arbitrary algebras and establish hom-associativity conditions in terms of one-sided (hom-)unities and multiplication operators within the algebra.

The constructions and results of this article aim to approach this problem in full generality of arbitrary non-associative algebras.

We generalize associativity conditions for hom-associative algebras established by Frégier, Gohr and Silvestrov
[
6
]
. In that paper, associativity conditions are derived from two-sided unitality. We provide conditions for left- and right-unital algebras which imply those in
[
6
]
in the two-sided unital case. Frégier and Gohr
[
5
]
use nucleus and ideals to describe a subalgebra of a given two-sided unital algebra which encodes all hom-associative structures of the algebra. We describe this subalgebra explicitly in terms of commutation, association and zero division relations, that is, via center, nucleus and annihilators. We extend their results to one-sided unital algebras by describing a subspace of one-sided multiplication operators which contains a subalgebra encoding all hom-associative structures of a given one-sided unital algebra. Furthermore, we examine the problem in non-unital algebras by describing a subspace encoding hom-associative structures given by multiplication operators, and use a unitalization process to describe a subalgebra encoding hom-associative structures given by multiplication by central elements.

Basic notions, notations and conventions

An algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
is a linear space
A
over
\mathbb{F}
with a bilinear multiplication operation called a product or multiplication.
The notations for the products, such as
\mu\colon A\times A\rightarrow A
,
(x,y)\mapsto x\cdot y
, often simplified to juxtaposition
xy
when no confusion arise. The algebra is
associative
if
x\cdot(y\cdot z)=(x\cdot y)\cdot z
for all
x,y,z\in A
, and it is
commutative
if
x\cdot y=y\cdot x
for all
x,y\in A
.
If no confusion is possible, we identify the algebra with the associated linear space
A
. An element
e\in A
is
idempotent
if
e\cdot e=e
. The set of all idempotents is denoted
E(A)
.
Commuting elements in non-commutative algebras are studied in terms of commutators.
The commutator is the bilinear operator on an algebra
A

defined by
[x,y]_{-}=x\cdot y-y\cdot x
. Algebras where the commutator is always
0
are
commutative
.
The zeroes of the commutator indicate how wide the commutativity relation is in an algebra or a certain subset of it: the
center

Z(A)=\{a\mid[a,x]_{-}=0\ \forall x\in A\}
of an algebra
A
is the subspace of elements of
A
that commute with all its elements, and generally, the
centralizer
of a subset
S\subseteq A
is the subspace of elements of
A
that commute with all elements of
S
, that is,
[a,s]_{-}=0
for all
s\in S.

In any algebra, the commutator map
[,]_{-}:A\times A\rightarrow A
is bilinear and skew-symmetric,
[x,y]_{-}=-[x,y]_{-}
, and hence the underlying linear space of any algebra is a skew-symmetric algebra with the commutator map as the new product.
In general, for non-associative algebras, it is a rare and highly restrictive condition for the commutator operation to be associative on the whole algebra.
For example, in any Lie algebra
(A,[\cdot,\cdot])
, by skew-symmetry of
[\cdot,\cdot]
, the Jacobi identity can be rewritten as
[[x,y],z]-[x,[y,z]]=[[x,z],y]
, implying thus that
(A,[\cdot,\cdot])
is associative if and only if
[A,A]\subseteq Ann_{A}(A)=\{a\mid[a,x]=[x,a]=0\ \forall x\in A\}
, that is, if and only if
(A,[\cdot,\cdot])
is a nilpotent algebra of degree
3
meaning that the products of any three elements are zero, since

[A,A]\subseteq Ann_{A}(A)\Leftrightarrow[[A,A],A]=0,\ [A,[A,A]]=0
by skew-symmetry of
[\cdot,\cdot]
. This shows that, for Lie algebras, as a class of not necessarily associative algebras, associativity is indeed a rare and highly restrictive condition. The annihilators of a subset
S\subseteq A
are the subspaces
Ann_{A}^{l}(S)=\{a\in A\mid a\cdot s=0\ \forall s\in S\}
(left annihilator) and
Ann_{A}^{r}(S)=\{a\in A\mid s\cdot a=0\ \forall s\in S\}
(right annihilator), and the subspace
Ann_{A}(A)
is known as annihilator of
A
. Note that in any skew-symmetric algebra
(A,[\cdot,\cdot])
,
[x,y]_{-}=[x,y]-[y,x]=[x,y]+[x,y]=2[x,y]
by skew-symmetry, and hence
[x,y]_{-}=0\Leftrightarrow[x,y]=0
, that is,
Ann_{A}(A)=Z(A)
in any skew-symmetric algebra over a field of characteristic different from
2
.
The commutator is often seen as the simplest example of a not necessarily associative bilinear product on associative algebras. The commutator product on any associative algebra
(A,\cdot)
not only satisfies skew-symmetry but also obeys the Jacobi identity
[x,[y,z]_{-}]_{-}+[y,[z,x]_{-}]_{-}+[z,[x,y]_{-}]_{-}=0
for all
x,y,z\in A
, yielding thus a Lie algebra

(A,[\cdot,\cdot]_{-})
. This Lie algebra is associative if and only if it is nilpotent of degree
3
, meaning that

[[A,A]_{-},A]_{-}=0,\ [A_{-},[A,A]_{-}]_{-}=0
, which is equivalent to

[x,[y,z]_{-}]_{-}=x\cdot y\cdot z-y\cdot x\cdot z-z\cdot x\cdot y+z\cdot y\cdot x=0
holding for all
x,y,z\in A
. This identity holds for all commutative associative algebras, but is clearly quite a restrictive for non-commutative associative algebras, singling out a special subclass of non-commutative associative algebras which satisfy this special identity.

The associator on an algebra
A
is the trilinear operator defined by
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=(x\cdot y)\cdot z-x\cdot(y\cdot z).

The associator can be seen as the commutator of multiplication operators
L_{x}
and
R_{z}
in the following sense:

[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=(x\cdot y)\cdot z-x\cdot(y\cdot z)\\
=R_{z}(x\cdot y)-L_{x}(y\cdot z)=R_{z}(L_{x}(y))-L_{x}(R_{z}(y))=[R_{z},L_{x}]_{-}(y).

The zeroes of the associator correspond to associating elements of the algebra.

Definition 1
.

The relative nucleus of
S\subseteq A
is the subspace of
A
consisting of elements that associate with all elements in
S
, that is,

\displaystyle N_{A}(S)

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid[a,x,y]_{\mathrm{as}}=[x,a,y]_{\mathrm{as}}=[x,y,a]_{\mathrm{as}}=0\ \forall x,y\in S\}

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid[a,S,S]_{\mathrm{as}}=[S,a,S]_{\mathrm{as}}=[S,S,a]_{\mathrm{as}}=0\}.

The left, right and middle relative nuclei of
S\subseteq A
are the subspaces of
A
consisting of elements that associate with all
S
in the corresponding position:

\displaystyle N_{A}^{l}(S)

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid\left[a,s,t\right]=0\ \forall s,t\in S\}

\displaystyle N_{A}^{m}(S)

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid\left[s,a,t\right]=0\ \forall s,t\in S\}

\displaystyle N_{A}^{r}(S)

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid\left[s,t,a\right]=0\ \forall s,t\in S\}

If
S=A
, we denote them
N(A),N^{l}(A),N^{m}(A)
and
N^{r}(A)
, respectively.

Hom-algebras appear as natural deformations of usual algebras by the action of a linear map. A hom-algebra is an algebra equipped with a linear map
\alpha\colon A\rightarrow A
which deforms the fundamental identities of the algebra.

Definition 2

(​​
[
17
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

A hom-associative algebra is a triple
(A,\mu,\alpha)
where
A
is a linear space,
\mu\colon A\times A\rightarrow A
is a bilinear product operation denoted by dot or juxtaposition, and
\alpha\colon A\rightarrow A
is a linear map such that

\alpha(x)\cdot(y\cdot z)=(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)

for all
x,y,z\in A.
If no confusion is possible, we identify a hom-algebra with the pair
(A,\alpha).
The twisting map
\alpha
is called trivial if
\alpha=\lambda\mathrm{id}_{A},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{F}
, and non-trivial otherwise.

A hom-associative algebra is
multiplicative
if
\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y)
for all
x,y\in A
. A hom-associative algebra where
\alpha=\mathrm{id}_{A}
is associative. A common way to obtain hom-associative algebras is to twist an associative algebra by a homomorphism
[
24
, Theorem 2.3]
: if
(A,\mu)
is associative, then
(A,\alpha\circ\mu,\alpha)
is hom-associative.

Example 3
.

Consider
\mathbb{C}
as a linear space over
\mathbb{R}
,
x\ast y=\overline{x\cdot y}
and
\alpha\colon x\mapsto\bar{x}
, where
\bar{x}
is the complex conjugate of
x
. The triple
(\mathbb{C},\ast^{\prime},\alpha)
is a hom-associative algebra. Indeed, for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\alpha(x)\ast^{\prime}(y\ast^{\prime}z)

\displaystyle=\overline{\alpha(x)\cdot(y\ast^{\prime}z)}=\overline{\bar{x}\cdot\overline{{y\cdot z}}}=x\cdot y\cdot z

\displaystyle=\overline{\overline{x\cdot y}\cdot{\bar{z}}}=\overline{(x\ast^{\prime}y)\cdot\alpha(z)}=(x\ast^{\prime}y)\ast^{\prime}\alpha(z).

This procedure is compatible with more general algebras and linear maps: the hom-associator of a twisted algebra
(A,\alpha\circ\mu,\alpha)
can be expressed in terms of the original product as
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}^{\alpha}=\alpha(\alpha(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)-\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y\cdot z))
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(A,\mu)
be an algebra,
\alpha\colon A\rightarrow A
be a linear map. Then
(A,\alpha\circ\mu,\alpha)
is hom-associative if and only if, for all
x,y,z\in A
,
\alpha(\alpha(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)-\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y\cdot z))=0
.

Example 5
.

Let
(\mathbb{C}[t],\mu)
denote complex polynomials in
t
with usual product and the multiplication operator
L_{t}\colon p(t)\rightarrow tp(t)
. For any three generators
t^{n},\ t^{m},\ t^{l}
, the hom-associator with respect to
L_{t}
is

L_{t}(L_{t}(t^{n}\cdot t^{m})\cdot L_{t}(t^{l})-L_{t}(t^{n})L_{t}(t^{m}\cdot t^{l}))=L_{t}(t^{n+m+1}\cdot t^{l+1}-t^{n+1}\cdot t^{m+l+1})\\
=L_{t}(t^{n+m+l+2}-t^{n+m+l+2})=0.

and thus
(\mathbb{C}[t],L_{t}\circ\mu,L_{t})
is hom-associative.

Similarly to the associator in associative algebras, an algebra will be hom-associative if the corresponding
hom-associator
is always zero.

Definition 6
.

The hom-associator
[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathrm{as}}^{\alpha}\colon A\times A\times A\rightarrow A
is the trilinear operator defined by
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}^{\alpha}=(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)-\alpha(x)\cdot(y\cdot z).

A unity of a (hom-)algebra
(A,\alpha)
is an element
1_{\star}\in A
such that, for all
x\in A
,
1_{\star}\cdot x=x
(left unity) or
x\cdot 1_{\star}=x
(right unity) or both (two-sided unity). A hom-algebra equipped with a unity is known as unital and denoted
(A,\alpha,1_{\star})
.

Two-sided unital hom-associative algebras are very close to being associative: it suffices that the twisting map is injective or surjective
[
6
]
. In particular, the quotient algebra
A/\ker\alpha
is associative. It is then necessary to introduce a generalized notion of unity for hom-algebras.

Definition 7
.

A hom-unity or weak unity of a hom-algebra
(A,\alpha)
is an element
\omega_{\star}\in A
such that, for all
x\in A
,
\omega_{\star}\cdot x=\alpha(x)
(left hom-unity),
x\cdot\omega_{\star}=\alpha(x)
(right hom-unity) or both (two-sided hom-unity). A hom-algebra with a hom-unity is known as hom-unital or weakly unital and denoted
(A,\alpha,\omega_{\star})
.
A hom-algebra being left or right hom-unital is equivalent to
\alpha=L_{\omega_{\star}}
or
\alpha=R_{\omega_{\star}}
respectively.

3
One-sided unital hom-associative algebras

From this section on, we mainly prove results for left-unital algebras, as the right-unital counterparts are analogous, and easily obtained by considering the opposite algebra
A^{\mathrm{op}}=(A,\mu_{\mathrm{op}},1_{r})
of a given right-unital algebra
A=(A,\mu,1_{r})
. The opposite product is given by
\mu_{\mathrm{op}}(x,y)=y\cdot x
. The opposite algebra
(A^{\mathrm{op}},\mu,1_{r})
is left-unital if and only if
(A,1_{r})
is right-unital. It then suffices to study left-unital algebras.

Remark 11
.

Observe that a left unital hom-associative algebra
(A,\alpha,1_{l})
is hom-unital with hom-unity
\alpha(1_{l})
– indeed, by hom-associativity, we have

\alpha(x)=(1_{l}\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(x)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot(1_{l}\cdot x)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot x,

which is the hom-unitality axiom.

Lemma 12
.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{l})
be a left-unital hom-associative algebra. Then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

(i)

\alpha(x)\cdot z=(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(z)
,

(ii)

\alpha(x)\cdot x=(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(x)
,

(iii)

\alpha(x)\cdot 1_{l}=(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(1_{l})
,

(iv)

\alpha(1_{l})\cdot x=\alpha(x)
,

(v)

1_{l}\cdot\alpha(1_{l})=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot 1_{l}
,

(vi)

\alpha(x\cdot y)=x\cdot\alpha(y)
,

(vii)

\alpha=L_{\alpha(1_{l})}
.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{r})
be a right-unital hom-associative algebra. Then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

(viii)

x\cdot\alpha(z)=\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{r}\cdot z)
,

(ix)

x\cdot\alpha(x)=\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{r}\cdot x)
,

(x)

1_{r}\cdot\alpha(x)=\alpha(1_{r})\cdot(1_{r}\cdot x)
,

(xi)

x\cdot\alpha(1_{r})=\alpha(x)
,

(xii)

\alpha(1_{r})\cdot 1_{r}=1_{r}\cdot\alpha(1_{r})
,

(xiii)

\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x)\cdot y
,

(xiv)

\alpha=R_{\alpha(1_{r})}
.

Proof.

We apply hom-associativity and one-sided unitality conditions:

(i)
\colon

(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(z)=\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{l}\cdot z)=\alpha(x)\cdot z
,

(ii)
\colon

(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(x)=\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{l}\cdot x)=\alpha(x)\cdot x
,

(iii)
\colon

(x\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(1_{l})=\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{l}\cdot 1_{l})=\alpha(x)\cdot 1_{l}
,

(iv)
\colon

(1_{l}\cdot 1_{l})\cdot\alpha(x)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot(1_{l}\cdot x)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot x
,

(v)
\colon

\alpha(1_{l})\cdot 1_{l}=\alpha(1_{l})=1_{l}\cdot\alpha(1_{l})
,

(vi)
\colon

\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot(x\cdot y)=(1_{l}\cdot x)\cdot\alpha(y)=x\cdot\alpha(y)
,

(vii)
\colon

\alpha(x)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot x\ \forall x\in A\Rightarrow\alpha=L_{\alpha(1_{l})}
,

(viii)
\colon

\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{r}\cdot z)=(x\cdot 1_{r})\cdot\alpha(z)=x\cdot\alpha(z)
,

(ix)
\colon

\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{r}\cdot x)=(x\cdot 1_{r})\cdot\alpha(x)=x\cdot\alpha(x)
,

(x)
\colon

\alpha(1_{r})\cdot(1_{r}\cdot x)=(1_{r}\cdot 1_{r})\cdot\alpha(x)=1_{r}\cdot\alpha(x)
,

(xi)
\colon

\alpha(x)\cdot(1_{r}\cdot 1_{r})=(x\cdot 1_{r})\cdot\alpha(1_{r})=x\cdot\alpha(1_{r})
,

(xii)
\colon

1_{r}\cdot\alpha(1_{r})=\alpha(1_{r})=\alpha(1_{r})\cdot 1_{r}
,

(xiii)
\colon

\alpha(x\cdot y)=(x\cdot y)\alpha(1_{r})=\alpha(x)\cdot(y\cdot 1_{r})=\alpha(x)\cdot y
,

(xiv)
\colon

\alpha(x)=x\cdot\alpha(1_{r})\ \forall x\in A\Rightarrow\alpha=R_{\alpha(1_{r})}
.

∎

3.1
Association relations

One-sided unities need not be unique. Indeed, for any two different one-sided unities
1_{l}
and
1_{l}^{\prime}
we have
1_{l}\cdot x=x=1_{l}^{\prime}\cdot x
, and thus
(1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l})\cdot x=0
, that is,
1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l}
is a universal left zero divisor of
A
or
1_{l}-1_{l}^{\prime}\in Ann_{A}^{l}(A)
. Due to this, any unity in a domain is unique. The element
1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l}
is also nilpotent with order
2
, as
(1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l})\cdot(1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l})=1_{l}^{\prime}\cdot 1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l}^{\prime}\cdot 1_{l}-1_{l}\cdot 1_{l}^{\prime}+1_{l}\cdot 1_{l}=1_{l}^{\prime}-1_{l}-1_{l}^{\prime}+1_{l}=0
. If there are left and right unities, then the unity is two-sided and unique: for any two one-sided unities
1_{l}
and
1_{r}
,
1_{l}=1_{l}1_{r}=1_{r}
.

The twisting map
\alpha
of a unital hom-associative algebra
(A,\alpha,1_{\star})
is multiplication by
\alpha(1_{\star})
(see
Lemma

12
). This property applied to the hom-associators provides associativity conditions which extend those in
[
5
, Proposition 1.1]
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{l})
be a left-unital hom-associative algebra. Then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

[x,y,\alpha(z)]_{\mathrm{as}}=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}).

Moreover, if
\alpha
is injective, then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

[x,y,\alpha(z)]_{\mathrm{as}}=0\Leftrightarrow[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0,\textnormal{ that is, }\alpha(z)\in N^{r}(A)\Leftrightarrow z\in N^{r}(A)

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{r})
be right-unital hom-associative algebra. Then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

[\alpha(x),y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}).

Moreover, if
\alpha
is injective, then, for all
x,y,z\in A
,

[\alpha(x),y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0\Leftrightarrow[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0,\textnormal{ that is, }\alpha(x)\in N^{l}(A)\Leftrightarrow x\in N^{l}(A)

Proof.

In left-unital hom-associative algebras, it follows from
Lemma

12
that, for all
x,y\in A
,
\alpha(x\cdot y)=x\cdot\alpha(y)
. Apply this property to elements
\{x\cdot y,z\}
:

(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)=\alpha((x\cdot y)\cdot z)\Rightarrow[x,y,\alpha(z)]_{\mathrm{as}}=(x\cdot y)\cdot\alpha(z)-x\cdot(y\cdot\alpha(z))\\
=\alpha((x\cdot y)\cdot z)-x\cdot\alpha(y\cdot z)=\alpha((x\cdot y)\cdot z)-\alpha(x\cdot(y\cdot z))=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}).

If
\alpha
is injective, then
[x,y,\alpha(z)]_{\mathrm{as}}=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}})=0\Leftrightarrow[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0
.

In right-unital hom-associative algebras, it follows from
Lemma

12
that, for all
x,y\in A
,
\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x)\cdot y
. Apply this property to elements
\{x,y\cdot z\}
:

\alpha(x)\cdot(y\cdot z)=\alpha(x\cdot(y\cdot z))\Rightarrow[\alpha(x),y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=(\alpha(x)\cdot y)\cdot z-\alpha(x)\cdot(y\cdot z)\\
=\alpha(x\cdot y)\cdot z-\alpha(x\cdot(y\cdot z))=\alpha((x\cdot y)\cdot z)-\alpha(x\cdot(y\cdot z))=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}})

If
\alpha
is injective, then
[\alpha(x),y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}})=0\Leftrightarrow[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0.

∎

In the two-sided unital case, both statements of
Proposition

15
hold. It follows that
\alpha((x\cdot y)\cdot z)=\alpha(x\cdot(y\cdot z))
for all
x,y,z\in A
, that is,
\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}})=0
. Applying this property to
Proposition

15
gives
[
5
, Proposition 1.1]
, which can be used to prove the following identity:

[\alpha(x),y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=[x,\alpha(y),z]_{\mathrm{as}}=[x,y,\alpha(z)]_{\mathrm{as}}=\alpha([x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}})=0,

and thus
\alpha(A)\subseteq N(A)
.
This identity applied to a surjective
\alpha
implies that
(A,1_{\star})
is associative. This is another proof to
[
5
, Corollary 1.1]
.

3.2
Multiplicativity and associativity conditions

In the literature, hom-algebras are often considered to be multiplicative
[
1
,
19
,
2
,
16
]
or constructed that way by twisting the product of an algebra by an algebra homomorphism
[
3
,
4
,
24
]
: doing so allows a very smooth generalization of many relevant algebraic properties including cohomology
[
1
]
and deformations
[
2
]
, and multiplicative hom-associative algebras are moreover classified in low dimension
[
19
]
.
Multiplicativity in one-sided unital algebras behaves as follows:

Theorem 16
.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{\star})
be a unital hom-associative algebra. The following statements are equivalent:

1)

\alpha
is multiplicative,

2)

\alpha
is idempotent,

3)

\alpha^{2}(1_{\star})=\alpha(1_{\star})
,

4)

\alpha(1_{\star})
is idempotent.

Proof.

We prove
1

\Rightarrow

2

\Rightarrow

3

\Rightarrow

4

\Rightarrow

1
for a left-unital algebra
(A,1_{l})
:

1
\Rightarrow
2
: If
\alpha
is multiplicative, then
x\cdot\alpha(y)=\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y)
. It follows from here that
x\cdot\alpha(y)=\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y)
, and by contracting
x\mapsto 1_{l}
we obtain
\alpha(y)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot\alpha(y)=\alpha^{2}(y)
and thus
\alpha^{2}=\alpha.
That is,
\alpha
is idempotent.

2
\Rightarrow
3
: If
\alpha^{2}=\alpha
, then
\alpha(1_{l})^{2}=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot\alpha(1_{l})=\alpha^{2}(1_{l})=\alpha(1_{l})
. It follows that
\alpha^{2}(1_{l})=\alpha(1_{l})^{2}=\alpha(1_{l}).
That is,
\alpha(1_{l})
is idempotent.

3
\Rightarrow
4
: From
\alpha(1_{l})^{2}=\alpha^{2}(1_{l})
it follows that
\alpha(1_{l})^{2}=\alpha(1_{l})\Leftrightarrow\alpha^{2}(1_{l})=\alpha(1_{l})
.

4
\Rightarrow
1
: If
\alpha^{2}(1_{l})=\alpha(1_{l})
, then
\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y)=(\alpha(1_{l})\cdot x)\cdot\alpha(y)
. By hom-associativity, this is equal to
\alpha^{2}(1_{l})\cdot(x\cdot y)=\alpha(1_{l})\cdot(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x\cdot y)
. It follows that
\alpha(x\cdot y)=\alpha(x)\cdot\alpha(y).
That is,
\alpha
is multiplicative.

The right-unital case is analogous. This completes the proof.
∎

The twisting map of any unital hom-associative algebra is a multiplication operator, and thus determined (albeit not always uniquely) by the factor
\alpha(1_{\star})
. That is, there is a correspondence between multiplicative twisting maps and a certain subset of idempotents of the algebra (see
Theorem

38
).

Remark 17
.

Multiplicativity of
\alpha
can be determined by computing
\alpha(1_{\star})^{2}
. This allows a very streamlined classification of multiplicative hom-associative structures, as will be seen on the sequel, and can moreover be used to rule out hom-associative structures on certain algebras.

Corollary 18
.

The following equivalent statements hold:

1)

No multiplicative hom-associative algebra
(A,\alpha)
with
\alpha^{2}\neq\alpha
is unital.

2)

A unital multiplicative hom-associative algebra without non-trivial idempotents is either trivial or associative.

Example 19
.

The
3
-dimensional multiplicative hom-associative algebras
A_{4}^{3}
,
A_{5}^{3}
,
A_{6}^{3}
and
A_{11}^{3}
, classified in
[
19
]
, have non-idempotent twisting maps (see
[

]
), and thus are non-unital.

Example 20
.

Any division algebra has no non-trivial idempotents, and thus it cannot be seen as multiplicative hom-associative unless
\alpha(1_{\star})=1_{\star}
, in which case it is associative.

Example 21

)
.

Let
R
be a unital ring without non-trivial idempotents,

Corollary 22
.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{\star})
be a unital hom-associative algebra. If any of the statements of
Theorem

16
holds and
\alpha
is injective or
1_{\star}\in\alpha(A)
(and in particular if
\alpha
is surjective), then
(A,1_{l})
is associative.

Proof.

Let
(A,\alpha,1_{\star})
be a one-sided unital hom-associative algebra. If any of the statements of
Theorem

16
holds, then
\alpha
is idempotent. Moreover,

(i)

If
\alpha
is injective:
From
\alpha
idempotent folows that
\alpha(\alpha(x)-x)=0
. By injectivity of
\alpha

\alpha(x)=x
, and thus

(ii)

If

:
there is

4
Hom-unities in two-sided unital algebras

Definition 23
.

Given an algebra
A
, a linear map
\alpha\colon A\to A
is
HA-compatible
or a
hom-associative structure of
A
if
(A,\alpha)
is hom-associative.

An element
a\in A

induces a hom-associative structure
on
A
if the multiplication operator
\alpha=L_{a}
or
\alpha=R_{a}
is HA-compatible.

If no nonzero map is HA-compatible, we say that
A
has no non-trivial hom-associative structures.

The twisting map
\alpha
of a left-unital hom-associative algebra is a multiplication operator
L_{a}
, and thus
a
satisfies the hom-unitality axiom
\alpha(x)=a\cdot x
. The search for hom-associative structures is then one with the search of hom-unities of the algebra which induce hom-associative structures.

In two-sided unital algebras, the subspace of HA-compatible maps consists of left multiplications by elements in the following subalgebra
[
5
, Proposition 1.1]
:

AC(A)=\{a\in Z(A)\mid Aa\subseteq N(A)\textnormal{ is a two-sided ideal}\}.

Any
a\in AC(A)
is central, and thus speaking of left or right multiplication is up to preference.
AC(A)
is the subalgebra consisting of all hom-unities of
(A,1_{lr})
which induce hom-associative structures.

Theorem 24
.

Let
(A,1_{lr})
be a two-sided unital algebra. With the notations above,

AC(A)=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}).

(3)

Proof.

For any
a\in AC(A)
, it follows from
Aa\subseteq N(A)
that
a\in N(A)
, and thus
AC(A)\subseteq Z(A)\cap N(A)
. Moreover,
Aa\subseteq N(A)
is a two-sided ideal only if the following association relations hold for all
x,y,z\in A
:

\displaystyle[ax,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}

\displaystyle=((ax)y)z-(ax)(yz)=(a(xy))z-a(x(yz))

\displaystyle\hskip 120.92421pt=a((xy)z)-a(x(yz))=a[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}},

\displaystyle[x,ay,z]_{\mathrm{as}}

\displaystyle=(x(ay))z-x((ay)z)=((xa)y)z-x(a(yz))

\displaystyle\hskip 35.56593pt=((ax)y)z-(ax)(yz)=(a(xy))z-a(x(yz))

\displaystyle\hskip 120.92421pt=a((xy)z)-a(x(yz))=a[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}},

\displaystyle[x,y,za]_{\mathrm{as}}

\displaystyle=(xy)(za)-x(y(za))=((xy)z)a-x((yz)a)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=((xy)z)a-(x(yz))a=[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}a=a[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}.

These relations indicate that
Aa\subseteq N(A)
is a two-sided ideal if and only if
a[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=0
for all
x,y,z\in A
, that is,
a\in Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})
. Lastly, ideal conditions of
Aa
follow from
a\in Z(A)\cap N(A)
:

\displaystyle\textbf{Left ideal: }\text{for all }b\in A,\ b(ax)=b(xa)=(bx)a\in Aa.

\displaystyle\textbf{Right ideal: }\text{for all }b\in A,\ (xa)b=x(ab)=x(ba)=(xb)a\in Aa.

This completes the proof.
∎

Since this subalgebra consists entirely of central elements, it can be rewritten in any of the following forms indistinctly:

\displaystyle AC(A)=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})

\displaystyle=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{r}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})

\displaystyle=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})

.

Example 25
.

Any two-sided unital associative algebra
(A,1_{lr})
satisfies
N(A)=A
and
[A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}=\{0\}
, and thus

AC(A)=Z(A)
. That is, (the product in)
(A,\alpha,1_{lr})
is hom-associative if and only if the twisting map is
\alpha=L_{\omega}
for some
\omega\in Z(A)
. Then,
(A,L_{\omega},\omega)
is two-sided hom-unital hom-associative. The algebra in Example
13
is a case of this, with hom-unity
\omega=t
.

Example 26
.

Any two-sided unital simple algebra with non-trivial HA-compatible maps is associative. Indeed, if
(A,1_{lr})
is two-sided unital simple and
AC(A)\neq\{0\}
, then
Aa
is a two-sided ideal for some
a\neq 0
in
AC(A)
. This implies one of the following:

\displaystyle Aa

\displaystyle=\{0\}\overset{a\in Z(A)}{\Longrightarrow}L_{a}=0\Rightarrow Twist(A)=\{0\}\Rightarrow a=0,

\displaystyle Aa

\displaystyle=A\overset{Aa\subseteq N(A)}{\Longrightarrow}N(A)=A\Rightarrow(A,1_{lr})\textnormal{ is associative.}

By associativity, as seen in Example
25
, we have
AC(A)=Z(A)
.

Corollary 27
.

No HA-compatible map of a two-sided unital non-associative algebra is injective. Moreover, if there exist
x,y,z\in A
such that
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}
is one-sided regular, then
AC(A)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in AC(A)
. Then
a\in Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})
. We can write this in the form
a\cdot[A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}=\{0\}
, or
[A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}\subseteq\ker L_{a}
. Since
A
is non-associative,
\ker L_{a}
is non-trivial. If, for some
x,y,z\in A
,
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}
is (without loss of generality) right-regular, then there is no nonzero
a\in A
such that
[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}\in\ker L_{a}

6
Hom-unities in non-unital algebras

In unital algebras, finding hom-associative structures is equivalent to finding hom-unities: the unity grants bijection between them and compatible twisting maps. This bijection is lost in non-unital algebras. Thus, for non-unital algebras it is unclear if or when the equality

AC(A)=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})

holds, where
AC(A)
is the subspace
\{a\in Z(A)\mid Aa\subseteq N(A)\textnormal{ is a two-sided ideal}\}
. We cannot grant that these are all the compatible two-sided hom-unities, and moreover we cannot grant that all elements of
AC(A)
are hom-unities. This section aims to construct a subspace of hom-unities using both the original definition of
AC(A)
and the formula computed for unital algebras. Consider to that end the following subspaces:

\displaystyle HU_{t}(A)=\{a\in A\mid(A,L_{a})\textnormal{ is hom-associative or }(A,R_{a})\textnormal{ is hom-associative}\}.

\displaystyle HU_{t}^{l}(A)=\{a\in A\mid(A,L_{a})\textnormal{ is hom-associative}\}.

\displaystyle HU_{t}^{r}(A)=\{a\in A\mid(A,R_{a})\textnormal{ is hom-associative}\}.

\displaystyle HU_{n}(A)=Z(A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}).

\displaystyle HU_{n}^{l}(A)=Z_{A}(A\cdot A)\cap N^{l}(A)\cap N^{m}(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}).

\displaystyle HU_{n}^{r}(A)=Z_{A}(A\cdot A)\cap N^{m}(A)\cap N^{r}(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{r}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}).

\displaystyle HU_{n}^{l}(A)\cap HU_{n}^{r}(A)=Z_{A}(A\cdot A)\cap N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}).

The set
HU_{t}(A)
contains all possible hom-unities of
A
, while
HU_{n}(A)
is the formulation of the subalgebra
AC(A)
explored in
Section

4
. It is then instrumental to understand how these sets interact with one another and describe explicit algebraic relations which highlight the structure of
AC(A)
,
HU_{n}(A)
or subspaces of them.

. Conclusion follows from
a\notin HU_{n}(A)
.
∎

AC(\tilde{A})
being a subalgebra of
(\tilde{A},\cdot,(0,1)^{T})
indicates that it is closed by products. The product of any two elements
(a,\lambda)^{T},(b,\mu)^{T}\in AC(\tilde{A})
is then in
AC(\tilde{A})
, that is,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}b\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ab+\lambda b+\mu a\\
\lambda\mu\end{pmatrix}\in AC(\tilde{A})\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}ab+\lambda b+\mu a\in Z(A)\cap N(A),\\
\left[A,A,A\right]_{\mathrm{as}}\subseteq Eig_{-\lambda\mu}(L_{ab+\lambda b+\mu a}).\end{array}\right.

Example 70

(Skew-symmetric algebras)
.

In any skew-symmetric algebra with left unity
1_{1}
element,
a=[1_{l},a]=[1_{l},1_{l}]a=-[[1_{l},1_{l}],a]=-[1_{l},a]=-a\Rightarrow 2a=0
, and analogousely the same conclusion
a=-a,2a=0
holds in any skew-symmetric algebra with right unity.
Since
2a=0\Rightarrow a=0
for all elements in any algebra over fields of characteristic different from
2
, the only such algebra is the trivial zero algebra
L=\{0\}
. For algebras over fields of characteristic
2
, this conclusion does not hold since
a=-a,2a=0
holds for all
a\neq 0
.
This implies also that a skew-symmetric algebra over a field of characteristic
2
is also symmetric since
x=-x\Rightarrow[x,y]=-[y,x]=[y,x]
, while it does not necessarily yields product being zero.
Unlike in algebras over fields of other characteristics, in characteristic
2
, a non-zero algebra can be both skew-symmetric and unital. For example, any field
F
of characteristic
2
(such as
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) is a nonzero, unital, skew-symmetric algebra over itself with the multiplication which is commutative and satisfies
xy=yx=-yx
.

Example 71

(Leibniz algebras)
.

Leibniz algebras
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot])
are those where every product operator is a derivation on the algebra. We distinguish between left and right Leibniz algebras according to which products are derivations.

\displaystyle\textbf{Left Leibniz: }[a,[b,c]]=[[a,b],c]+[b,[a,c]]

\displaystyle\textbf{Right Leibniz: }[[a,b],c]=[[a,c],b]+[a,[b,c]]

Leibniz algebras with non-trivial, that is, non-zero multiplication,
cannot be unital, as any one-sided unity collapses the product:

Left-unital:

\displaystyle[b,c]=[[1_{l},b],c]=[[1_{l},c],b]+[1_{l},[b,c]]=[c,b]+[b,c]\Longrightarrow[c,b]=0,

Right-unital:

\displaystyle[a,b]=[[a,b],1_{r}]=[[a,1_{r}],b]+[a,[b,1_{r}]]=[a,b]+[a,b]\Rightarrow[a,b]=0.

Note that for Leibniz algebras this incompatibility of existence of non-zero unity with product being non-zero holds inependently of characteristic of the field, while in skew-symmetric algebras it guarantied holds over the fields of characteristic different from
2
, while it often fails over the fields of characteristic
2
. The incompatibility of existence of non-zero unity with product being non-zero holds in Lie algebras over fields of any characteristic, as they are a subclass of Leibniz algebras.

So, for Leibniz algebras one must rely on the unitalization process:
HU_{n}(\mathcal{L})
is a subspace of
AC(\mathcal{L})
which we can compute.

Remark 72
.

In the literature, for example in
[
20
]
, the centers of a Leibniz algebra are given by
Z_{l}(\mathcal{L})=\{a\in\mathcal{L}\mid[a,\mathcal{L}]=\{0\}\}
and
Z_{r}(\mathcal{\mathcal{L}})=\{a\in\mathcal{L}\mid[\mathcal{L},a]=\{0\}\}
and
Z(\mathcal{L})=Z_{l}(\mathcal{L})\cap Z_{r}(\mathcal{L}).

In context of our article, we emphasise that these sets are the
annihilators

Z_{l}(\mathcal{L})=Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{l}(\mathcal{L})
,
Z_{r}(\mathcal{\mathcal{L}})=Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{r}(\mathcal{L})
and
Z(\mathcal{L})=Ann_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{L})
. In order to avoid notational overlap with the commutative center we use this for the classical centers of Leibniz algebras, while we denote the commutative center by
C(\mathcal{L})=\{a\in\mathcal{L}\mid[a,x]=[x,a]\ \forall x\in\mathcal{L}\}
.

Proposition 73
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot])
be a Leibniz algebra. Then
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot],L_{a})
is hom-associative for all
a
in the following subspace:

HU_{n}(\mathcal{L})=C(\mathcal{L})\cap Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{l}([\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}])=C(\mathcal{L})\cap Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{r}([\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}])=C(\mathcal{L})\cap Ann_{\mathcal{L}}([\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]).

Every element in
HU_{n}(\mathcal{L})
is moreover 3-nilpotent.

Proof.

Compute the associator of three elements
a,b,c\in\mathcal{L}
using the Leibniz rule:

\displaystyle\textbf{Left: }[a,[b,c]]=[[a,b],c]+[b,[a,c]]\Rightarrow[a,b,c]_{\mathrm{as}}=-[b,[a,c]].

\displaystyle\textbf{Right: }[[a,b],c]=[[a,c],b]+[a,[b,c]]\Rightarrow[a,b,c]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[a,c],b].

It follows that
[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}],\mathcal{L}]
(left) and
[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]_{\mathrm{as}}=[\mathcal{L},[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]]
(right).

Any
a\in N(\mathcal{L})
satisfies, for all
b,c\in\mathcal{L}
, the following relations if
\mathcal{L}
is left-Leibniz:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}0=[c,b,a]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[c,b],a]-[c,[b,a]]=-[b,[c,a]]\\
0=[b,c,a]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[b,c],a]-[b,[c,a]]=-[c,[b,a]]\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \ [[b,c],a]=[c,[b,a]]=0

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}0=[a,b,c]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[a,b],c]-[a,[b,c]]=-[b,[a,c]]\\
0=[b,a,c]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[b,a],c]-[b,[a,c]]=-[c,[a,b]]\\
\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{matrix}[[a,b],c]=[[b,a],c]=0\\
[a,[b,c]]=[b,[a,c]]=0\end{matrix}\right.

and the following relations if
\mathcal{L}
is right-Leibniz:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}0=[a,b,c]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[a,b],c]-[a,[b,c]]=[[a,c],b]\\
0=[a,c,b]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[a,c],b]-[a,[c,b]]=[[a,b],c]\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\ \ [a,[b,c]]=[[a,c],b]=0

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}0=[c,b,a]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[c,b],a]-[c,[b,a]]=[[c,a],b]\\
0=[c,a,b]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[c,a],b]-[c,[a,b]]=[[c,b],a]\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{matrix}[c,[b,a]]=[c,[a,b]]=0\\
[[c,b],a]=[[c,a],b]=0\end{matrix}\right.

Every triple product involving
a
is 0. In particular,
a
commutes with any
[b,c]
and is 3-nilpotent. That is,
N(\mathcal{L})
, and in particular
C(\mathcal{L})\cap N(\mathcal{L})
, is a subspace of the subspace of 3-nilpotent elements of
\mathcal{L}
. By the Lebniz identity,

[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]_{\mathrm{as}}=[[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}],\mathcal{L}]\subseteq[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]
and thus
a\in N(\mathcal{L})\Rightarrow a\in Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{l}([\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}])\subseteq Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{l}([[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}],\mathcal{L}])=Ann_{\mathcal{L}}^{l}([\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]_{\mathrm{as}})
. This indicates that
C(\mathcal{L})\cap N(\mathcal{L})
cancels
[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}]
on both sides by centrality, so we can use left, right or two-sided annihilator notation indistinctly. This completes the proof.
∎

The Proposition
73
illustrates also that the study of hom-associative structures is linked to the existence of
3
-nilpoitent elements in the algebra.

Example 74

(Hom-Leibniz algebras, non-unital)
.

The discussion of hom-associative structures in hom-Leibniz algebras seen in Example
58
can continue in two directions:

(i)

Compute
AC_{l}(A)\cdot 1_{l}
and examine its existence.

(ii)

Examine non-unital hom-Leibniz algebras.

We start by examining non-unital hom-Leibniz algebras twisted by multiplication operators: if
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot],\alpha)
is non-unital, but
\alpha=L_{a}
is a multiplication operator such that
a=[a,w]=\alpha(w)
for some
w\in\mathcal{L}
(for example, if
a
is idempotent), then

\displaystyle[[w,x],\alpha(y)]

\displaystyle=[[w,y],\alpha(x)]+[\alpha(w),[x,y]]=[[w,y],\alpha(x)]+\alpha([x,y]),

\displaystyle\alpha([x,y])

\displaystyle=[[w,x],\alpha(y)]-[[w,y],\alpha(x)].

Observe that then

\alpha([y,x])=[[w,y],\alpha(x)]-[[w,x],\alpha(y)]\\
=-\big([[w,x],\alpha(y)]-[[w,y],\alpha(x)]\big)=-\alpha([x,y]).

The product
\alpha\circ[\cdot,\cdot]
is skew-symmetric, and in particular
\alpha([x,x])=0.
We then have that
(\mathcal{L},L_{a}\circ[\cdot,\cdot],L_{a}\circ L_{a})
is a hom-Lie algebra if
L_{a}
is multiplicative. This hom-Lie algebra is moreover multiplicative, due to being a Yau twist.

Proposition 75
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{L}
be such that
a=[a,w]
for some
w\in\mathcal{L}
. The Yau twist of a multiplicative right hom-Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot],L_{a})
by

(\mathcal{L},L_{a}\circ[\cdot,\cdot],L_{a}\circ L_{a})

a=[w,a]
for some

(\mathcal{L},R_{a}\circ[\cdot,\cdot],R_{a}\circ R_{a})
, is a multiplicative hom-Lie algebra.

Consider now a left-unital, right hom-Leibniz algebra
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot],\alpha,1_{l})
, let
\beta=L_{b}
be HA-compatible, that is,
(\mathcal{L},[\cdot,\cdot],\beta,1_{l})

Right hom-Leibniz:

\displaystyle[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}^{\alpha}=[[x,z],\alpha(y)],

hom-associative:

Example 76

(Commutative algebras, non-unital)
.

Let
A
be a commutative algebra. If we look for hom-unities, all of them are immediately two-sided, and moreover any element that cancels all associators does so on both sides. We can then describe the subspace
HU_{n}(A)
of hom-unities through the unitalization process: it is the subspace of

AC(\tilde{A})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}\mid a\in N(A),\ [A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}\subseteq Eig_{-\mu}(L_{a})\right\}

corresponding to
\mu=0
, or
N(A)\cap Ann_{A}^{l}([A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}})
.

First of all, observe that these algebras are always flexible:

[x,y,x]_{\mathrm{as}}=(xy)x-x(yx)=(xy)x-(yx)x=(xy)x-(xy)x=0.

The associator of three elements is

[x,y,z]_{\mathrm{as}}=(xy)z-x(yz)=z(xy)-x(zy)=[L_{z},L_{x}](y),

and thus
[A,A,A]_{\mathrm{as}}=\{[L_{z},L_{x}](y)\mid x,y,z\in A\}
. By using the unitalization process, we obtain a subspace of hom-unities given by the eigenspaces which contain all associators. In terms of
L_{a}
, this can be expressed as

\displaystyle(L_{a}\circ[L_{z},L_{x}])(y)=-\mu([L_{z},L_{x}](y))\Rightarrow(L_{a}+\mu\cdot\mathrm{id}_{A})([L_{z},L_{x}](y))=0.

Now,
HU_{n}(A)
is isomorphic to the subspace of
AC(\tilde{A})
with
\mu=0
, that is

HU_{n}(A)=\{a\in N(A)\mid L_{a}\circ[L_{z},L_{x}]=0\ \forall x,z\in A\},

or explicitly in terms of elements as

HU_{n}(A)=\{a\in N(A)\mid a(x(yz))=a(z(yx))\ \forall x,y,z\in A\}.
