Advance care planning uptake in the severely frail
Authors: Kim San Lim, Htin Kyaw Khine, Barbara Helen Rosario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with severe frailty (Clinical Frailty Scale [CFS] 7–9) have high short-term mortality and are often approaching the end of life. Despite this, uptake of Advance Care Planning (ACP) remains low. This study aimed to examine the rate and timing of ACP referral and completion among hospitalized patients with severe frailty.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study of patients aged ≥ 65 years with documented CFS scores of 7–9 admitted to the geriatric medicine wards of a public tertiary hospital in Singapore between July and September 2024. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with completed ACPs. Secondary outcomes included ACP referral and completion rates, time from admission to ACP referral and completion, mortality at 30 days and 6 months, stated care preferences, and place of death. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and outcomes.ResultsAmong 86 patients with severe frailty (mean age 84.8 years; 72.1% female), 14 (16.3%) had completed ACPs prior to admission. Of the remaining 72 patients, 17 (23.6%) were referred for ACP within 90 days, of whom 6 (35.3%) completed ACP discussions. In-hospital mortality was 5.8%, and 6-month mortality was 31.4%. Among patients who died within 6 months, 69.5% died in hospital. Patients with completed ACPs more frequently died at home or with home hospice care.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that hospitalized patients with severe frailty have high short-term mortality but low rates of ACP referral and completion. Earlier and systematic initiation of ACP at the point of frailty identification may improve alignment of end-of-life care with patient preferences and reduce unwanted hospital deaths.

Background

Frailty is a condition characterized by increased vulnerability to stressors, resulting from the gradual deterioration of multiple physiological systems over a lifetime, which impairs the body’s ability to maintain or restore homeostasis [1]. Epidemiological data suggest that the prevalence of frailty is rising as populations age [2]. Longitudinal analyses further demonstrate that individuals tend to transition toward greater frailty severity over time, with an increasing proportion reaching severe frailty [3].

Severely frail older adults represent a particularly high-risk population in the healthcare system. Frailty is linked to serious adverse health outcomes such as falls [4], delirium [5], and mortality [6–8]. This is especially so for those categorised as severely frail, with a mortality rate of up to 19.1% at 1 year [9]. Identifying those at greatest risk requires reliable frailty assessment tools, such as the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), a 9-point clinical judgment scale. The CFS was originally developed by Rockwood et al. [10] as a tool to assess frailty based on clinical judgment, integrating multiple health domains into a single score. Systematic reviews of epidemiological data show that older people with severe frailty have a dramatically elevated risk of mortality with a hazard ratio of 20.08 for those with CFS scores 8–9 compared to those with CFS scores 1–4 [11]. Frailty is also associated with increased utilization of hospital services [9] and readmissions [8].

Given the high burden of frailty-related morbidity and mortality, advance care planning (ACP) is an essential component of patient-centred care for this population. ACP is a process in which patients, in conversation with healthcare providers and often their families, articulate their values, goals, and preferences regarding future medical care, including end-of-life care, and may document their wishes in an advance directive or care plan [12–14]. ACP is associated with care that is more aligned to patient preferences [15–17], improved caregiver and spokesperson experience [18, 19], and reduced unwanted aggressive interventions at end-of-life [17, 20]. Patients who engage in ACP also report feeling more respected and heard [21–24]. This may particularly benefit frail older people, who across multiple international studies have been shown to frequently prefer less aggressive, comfort-focused care over life-prolonging treatments [25, 26].

However, prior research indicates that ACP completion rates in frail populations are low. A systematic review, predominantly comprising studies from Western settings, noted that although around 74–84% of capable older inpatients would be receptive to discussing ACP, less than 5% engaged in ACP discussions or had documented plans during their hospital stay [25]. This disparity between patient willingness and actual uptake points to significant missed opportunities, where openness to ACP is not being translated into action in clinical practice. Contributing factors may include healthcare professionals not initiating ACP due to uncertainty in disease trajectory, discomfort with the discussions, or time constraints in acute care [27].

In this context, we aimed to study the uptake of Advance Care Planning in severely frail patients (CFS 7–9) hospitalized in Changi General Hospital, a public tertiary hospital in Singapore. Specifically, we examined the rate of ACP referral and completion this high-risk group. For those with ACP referred, we assessed if they were initiated prior to hospitalization or during the hospital stay. We also collected data on outcomes in this cohort, particularly in-hospital and short-term post-discharge mortality, to underscore the urgency of care planning in this population.

Ultimately, our goal was to identify gaps and inform strategies to improve ACP uptake for severely frail older adults. Given that frailty can render the end-of-life course unpredictable, proactive planning is essential to ensure that care is aligned with patient wishes and to avoid aggressive interventions that may not benefit these patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a single-centre retrospective study conducted at the geriatric medicine wards of a public tertiary hospital in Singapore. Data were collected through reviewing the electronic health records (EHR) for patients admitted to geriatric medicine wards from 22 July to 22 September 2024.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 65 or older, and had a documented CFS score of 7 to 9 recorded during their index admission.

Data collection

Frailty severity assessment (Clinical frailty scale)

Patients with CFS scores of 7–9 are severely frail, meaning they are completely dependent on others for personal care and typically have multiple comorbidities. CFS 7 indicates “severe frailty” (extensive dependence for Activities of Daily Living (ADL)), CFS 8 indicates “very severe frailty” (completely dependent, approaching end-of-life), while CFS 9 is “terminally ill” (Approaching the end of life with a life expectancy < 6 months, but are not otherwise evidently frail) [10].

In our hospital, clinicians are expected to record the CFS for each admission, typically performed at the start of the admission. We noted whether a CFS score was documented and, if so, its value.

Advance care planning (ACP) assessment

We defined ACP completion as the presence of a formal written Advance Care Plan, recorded in the Agency for Integrated Care (AIC) national registry, accessible through the national electronic health records (NEHR) system. In Singapore, an ACP is a formal but non-legally binding document in which a person documents their values, goals, and preferences for future medical care, typically with the guidance of a trained and certified ACP facilitator [28]. Preferred Plan of Care (PPC) is a specific type of ACP document used for patients with a life-limiting illness and a prognosis of less than 12 months, designed to record their goals, preferred place of care, and treatment preferences near the end of life. Both ACP and PPC documents registered in the NEHR were included in our definition of ACP completion. Verbal discussions about goals of care that were not formally documented in the registry were not counted as completed ACPs. While ACP referral is recommended but not mandated by institutional policy, it could be initiated by any member of the medical team at any point during or after admission via an electronic order on the hospital’s EHR system. This referral directed a trained and certified ACP facilitator to contact the patient and family to schedule and conduct ACP discussions. The completed ACP document would subsequently be registered in the NEHR.

For each patient, we recorded whether ACP was completed (yes/no) or referred within 90 days of hospital admission. ACP was considered complete if undertaken within 6 months of the index hospital admission.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, ACP referral rates, and ACP completion rates. Frequencies and proportions were calculated for categorical variables. Comparisons between subgroups were performed using Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. The cohort was stratified by CFS scores (e.g., CFS 7 vs. CFS 8 vs. CFS 9) to explore potential differences in ACP referral and completion rates across varying levels of frailty.

Time intervals (e.g., from admission to ACP referral or completion) were analysed to assess delays in the ACP process. Mortality outcomes at 30 and 60 days were examined descriptively, and their associations with ACP completion were explored to identify trends.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 674 individual patients who were admitted to geriatric medicine wards in a tertiary hospital in Singapore were screened, of whom 366 (54.3%) patients had a documented CFS score on admission. 86 of these patients (23.5%) patients had documented CFS scores of 7 or 8 (severely frail) and were included in this study. The breakdown by frailty grade was: 76 patients with CFS 7 and 10 patients with CFS 8. In this cohort, no patients were categorized as CFS 9 (terminally ill).

Of the 86 with CFS 7 and 8, 62 were female (72.1%) and the mean age was 84.8 years old (Table 1).

Advance care planning outcomes

Only 14 out of the 86 severely frail patients (16.3%) had documented completed ACP discussion prior to the index hospitalisation. Three of those with previous ACP (21.4%) were re-referred for ACP again. For the 3 cases re-referred for ACP discussion, an average of 561 days had lapsed since the last ACP discussion.

For those with no previous completed ACP discussions (n = 72), 13 (18.1%) were referred during the index hospitalisation and a further 4 patients were referred following discharge giving a total of 17 (23.6%) referred within 90 days. The mean number of days between admission and date of ACP referral was 12.5 days with range of 2 to 32 days.

Of those referred, only 6 (35.3%) eventually completed ACP discussion within 90 days, with an average of 24.3 days (range 6–42) days between ACP referral and completion. There was only one patient with ACP referred and completed during the index hospitalisation.

Of the 86 severely frail patients, 26 (14 + 6+6) or 30% had a completed ACP. ACP completion rates were similarly low across CFS grades (CFS 7: 15.8%; CFS 8: 20%; p = 0.663). Full details are summarised in Table 2.

Discussion

Our study reports a 16.3% uptake of advance care planning among hospitalized patients with severe frailty. It highlights a significant gap with more than 80% of our severely frail patients having no previous ACP. This finding mirrors prior observations in the literature, where in practice, frail older people often have low rates of ACP [25].

Despite the index hospitalisation posing as an opportunity to consider ACP, the referral rate remains low at 18.1%. Our real-world hospital data put those statistics into context, among those who are recognizably at the highest risk (CFS 7–8), clinicians are frequently not engaging in ACP. This suggests that severe frailty which could be considered a trigger for ACP referral is being underutilized.

The six-month mortality rate of 31.4% in our cohort underscores the poor prognosis of severe frailty. This is consistent with existing literature demonstrating that higher CFS scores are independently associated with significantly increased mortality risk.¹¹ These findings highlight the urgency of timely ACP, given the limited window for meaningful care planning conversations in this population.

Our study population did not have any patients who were CFS 9, a group associated with six-month mortality rates of up to 60% [29]. CFS 9 is used to describe those approaching end-of-life with a life expectancy of less than 6 months and who are not otherwise evidently frail [10]. Its prevalence was similarly found to be very low in other studies, with only one patient CFS 9 within a cohort of 145 patients [30] and none in a cohort of 167 hospitalised patients [31]. Indeed, in our clinical practice, patients with a prognosis of less than six months who remain mobile are occasionally encountered, albeit not during this study period. The infrequent use of CFS 9 highlights challenges in identifying terminal stages in frail older adults and suggests a need for clearer guidance and education around the use of frailty tools in palliative care settings, to ensure those CFS 9 are offered an ACP.

The findings of low rates of ACP referral and completion, combined with the high short term mortality rates seen in those who are severely frail, highlight the importance of early ACP referral and completion so that care preferences can be ascertained and measures are put in place to respect them.

Barriers to ACP referral in the severely frail

Despite a six-month mortality of 31.4% in severely frail patients, ACP referral rates in our cohort remained low at 18.1%, suggesting that severe frailty is not being consistently recognised as a trigger for care planning. Several contextual barriers, well-described in the literature, are likely to underlie this gap.

Firstly, prognostic difficulties and failure to recognise severe frailty as an end-of-life condition can make it challenging for clinicians to identify a clear trigger point for care planning [32] and ACP discussions [27]. Unlike the relatively linear decline seen in advanced cancer, or the episodic deterioration characteristic of organ failure, frailty is typically characterised by a prolonged, gradual decline punctuated by acute illness episodes with uncertain recovery [33]. This unpredictable trajectory [34] may contributed to the underrecognition of severely frail patients as being in the last phase of life [35, 36]. This was reflected in our cohort where even patients with CFS 8 were no more likely to be referred for ACP than those with CFS 7 (p = 0.663). Waiting for prognostic certainty risks deferring ACP until it is too late, when patients are hospitalised for acute critical illnesses [37] or on the day of death [38]. This is a pattern reflected in our cohort, where 69.5% of those who died did so in hospital without a documented ACP.

Secondly, discomfort with telling difficult news [39] and fear that end-of-life discussions may cause unnecessary alarm or diminish hope [40, 41] may further discourage clinicians from initiating ACP without a clearer prognostic signal. While these factors were not directly studied in our cohort, they represent plausible barriers operative in our setting and are consistent with the low referral rates observed.

Thirdly, time and workload constraints in the acute inpatient setting may deprioritise ACP referral in favour of immediate clinical management [42, 43]. This is reflected in our finding that the mean time from admission to ACP referral was 12.5 days, suggesting that care planning was not being initiated early in the admission.

Lastly, beyond individual clinician factors, cultural context of our predominantly East Asian cohort can also shape patients’ and families’ openness to ACP discussions. Religion and spirituality are known to impact health by helping patients and families cope with illness, fostering a positive mindset, and supporting emotional well-being [44]. However, levels of religiosity and comfort in death-related discussions can play a significant role in the adoption of ACP [45, 46], which includes discussion on end-of-life issues such as withdrawal of care [47]. In East Asian cultures influenced by Confucianism, family values [48–51] and filial piety, end-of-life discussions may be considered taboo, [52, 53] and decision-making frequently involves family members rather than patients themselves [54–56]. This places particular demands on ACP facilitators to engage families sensitively and early, balancing family expectations with patients’ own preferences [53].

Benefits of ACP uptake

Notwithstanding these barriers, our findings provide important preliminary evidence for the value of ACP in this population. Patients with a completed ACP were significantly less likely to die in hospital (33.3%) compared to those without (82.3%; p = 0.045), and half of those with ACP who died at home had explicitly documented home as their preferred place of care and death, demonstrating direct alignment between stated preferences and actual outcomes. A “good death” is defined by an understanding of the dying person’s wishes and taking meaningful steps to honour them [57], and this appears more achievable when ACP is in place.

In our cohort, a majority of those without ACP died in hospital (82.3%), whereas those with ACP had a trend toward more likelihood of dying out of hospital (67.7%). This aligns with evidence that ACP is associated with reduced hospitalizations at end-of-life [58, 59] and a higher likelihood of dying in one’s preferred place​, which is most commonly at home [60, 61]. Beyond individual benefit, home-based end-of-life care has been associated with better bereavement outcomes for family members, [62, 63] and ACP more broadly with more efficient use of healthcare resources through reductions in unwanted intensive care treatments and hospitalisations [64, 65]. This reinforces the case for systematic ACP implementation in this population.

Future directions and proposed interventions

Our findings underscore that severe frailty should be treated as a trigger for ACP. Severe frailty essentially signals that the patient is in the final phase of life. We advocate for a systematic approach: for any patient with CFS 7–9 (or an equivalent assessment of severe frailty), healthcare teams should proactively initiate ACP conversations, ideally early in the hospitalization or even upstream in outpatient settings. This could be operationalized through automated EHR alerts for patients with CFS ≥ 7 to prompt a goals-of-care discussion, or automatic referral to a palliative care or geriatric team. Combining such triggers with accountability, such as tracking ACP discussion rates for frail inpatients as a quality metric, can incentivize improvement and drive sustainable change at the institutional level.

To improve ACP uptake, we also need to address the barriers directly. Education and training for clinicians is vital, both in recognizing when frailty signals limited prognosis and in communication skills to discuss goals of care. Examples include workshops on how to conduct ACP conversations tailored for frailty, emphasizing function and quality of life goals. Involving multidisciplinary teams will be integral: nurses, social workers, and therapists often have close rapport with patients and can be instrumental in starting or reinforcing ACP discussions. Another strategy is to engage patients and families earlier in the continuum, where primary care physicians and geriatricians should ideally begin ACP when a patient first meets criteria for severe frailty, rather than waiting for a crisis during hospitalisation.

Limitations

This study was retrospective and single-centre, which limits generalizability, with a relatively small cohort of 86 patients, which limits power to detect differences.

While CFS levels were typically recorded by the medical team at the start of the admission, comprehensive geriatric assessments were only performed later. This may have resulted in inaccurate initial frailty scores being based on limited or incomplete information. Such static scoring can affect clinical decision-making, especially when frailty is used to guide care planning or prognostication.

Discussions on patient’s goals of care, values and care preferences may have occurred but ACP was not referred or completed. In one patient’s case, Preferred Place of Care was explained and discussed with family members during hospitalisation, and their preference was for care to be provided in the patient’s home. However, this was not formally documented in the national registry, as the patient was terminally ill and passed on before ACP could be formally documented.

The use of our institutions EHR system to screen for ACP referrals may potentially underestimate the number ACP referrals. This is because referrals made from other institutions after hospitalisation will not be identifiable.

We did not delve into nuanced aspects such as the content or quality of ACP discussions or patient/family satisfaction, which may be areas for future research in the local context.

Our definition of ACP completion was specific to the Singapore context, requiring formal registration in the national NEHR. Given the significant international variation in how ACP is defined and operationalised, direct comparisons with studies from other settings should be made with caution.

Despite these limitations, our study provides a real-world insight into current practice in an acute hospital setting and highlights areas for future improvement. This resonates with broader findings that ACP can reduce hospitalizations at end-of-life, and increase the likelihood of dying in one’s preferred place.

Conclusion

Many hospitalized patients with severe frailty face high 6-month mortality, yet most lack documented ACPs, indicating missed opportunities to align care with patient and family preferences. Contributing factors include poor recognition of frailty as a terminal condition, uncertainty about prognosis, and limited time for discussions. Proactive ACP discussions at the time of frailty identification, supported by system-level prompts and clinician education, can improve ACP completion. Prioritizing ACP for this growing population is essential to ensure more compassionate, patient-centred outcomes.
