Determining the Causal Effect of Neurotrophic Tropomyosin Receptor Kinase Gene Fusions on Survival Prognosis for Adult Patients With Advanced Cancers
Authors: Emanuel Krebs, Nirupama Tamvada, Deirdre Weymann, Howard J Lim, Stephen Chia, Cheryl Ho, Dean A Regier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeUncertainty about the prognostic significance of neurotrophic tropomyosin receptor kinase (NTRK) fusions complicates clinical decisions and interpretation of survival outcomes in oncology care. Drawing on the largest cohort of NTRK fusion–positive (NTRK+) patients, we establish the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions in adults with advanced cancers.MethodsWe used a large real-world clinicogenomic research database from the United States to emulate a pragmatic randomized trial that determines the causal effect of harboring an NTRK gene fusion (NTRK+) on survival prognosis for adult patients with advanced solid tumors. We 1:1 matched NTRK+ patients to patients with no identified NTRK genomic alterations (NTRK–) using a machine learning matching approach. Excluding patients who received TRK inhibitors, we conducted stratified matching within common tumor groups. We used Kaplan-Meier and Weibull regression survival analyses and estimated the difference in restricted mean survival time (RMST) over 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year study periods.ResultsWe included 68,145 NTRK– patients for matching to 73 NTRK+ patients within seven tumor groups, including colorectal (n = 22), non–small cell lung (n = 13), sarcoma (n = 13), breast (n = 10), head and neck (n = 7), neuroendocrine (n = 4), and prostate (n = 4), and 14 NTRK+ patients representing eight other cancers. The difference in overall survival over 1 year was statistically significant using the Kaplan-Meier estimate (P = .008), Weibull regression (hazard ratio, 2.105 [95% CI, 1.207 to 3.673]), and RMST (–50 days [95% CI, –96 to –5]; P = .029). Differences were also statistically different at 2 years and 3 years. At 5 years, no survival differences were statistically significant.ConclusionIn this large real-world target trial emulation, NTRK gene fusions were associated with clinically meaningful reductions in overall survival among adults with advanced cancers not treated with TRK inhibitors. These findings clarify the prognostic role of NTRK fusions and inform clinical interpretation of outcomes in routine oncology.

Introduction

Fusions in the neurotrophic tropomyosin receptor kinase (NTRK) gene represent one of the rarest actionable oncogenic drivers in adult oncology, yet they have broad implications across multiple tumor types.1 Fusions involving NTRK1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 occur in fewer than 1% of most common adult cancers, including lung (0.2%), breast (0.4%) and colorectal (0.2%) malignancies, although they are enriched in a small number of rare tumor subtypes (eg, ≥90% in secretory carcinoma).1,2 These rearrangements result in constitutive TRK signaling and can be detected in routine clinical practice using next-generation sequencing (NGS).

The tumor-agnostic approvals of first-generation TRK inhibitors, including larotrectinib and entrectinib (the latter currently approved with conditions in the United States, Europe, and Canada),3,4 have expanded the clinical role of NGS for NTRK fusions. The rarity of these fusions and their occurrence across a broad range of tumor types limit the feasibility of traditional trial designs5-7 and constrain understanding of their natural history under standard clinical care.8 As a result, uncertainty persists regarding their independent prognostic significance in adults with advanced cancers,9 complicating clinical decisions and treatment recommendations. In this setting, generating direct comparative evidence among NTRK fusion–positive (NTRK+) patients is inherently challenging. Quantifying the intrinsic prognostic effect of NTRK fusions supports the use of appropriately constructed external controls that reflect tumor-specific heterogeneity among NTRK fusion–negative (NTRK−) patients, thereby reducing uncertainty in the interpretation of comparative outcomes in routine oncology.

A recent meta-analysis reported lower overall survival among patients with NTRK+ tumors based on three previously published studies.9 Interpretation of these findings is constrained by methodological challenges common to the included studies, including heterogeneous index and censoring dates, unsatisfactory confounder control, overall and by tumor type, and small sample sizes.10-13 Notably, the largest study included only 28 NTRK+ patients.13 Furthermore, evidence that the proportional hazards (PH) assumption was not met in some of the included studies suggests caution in interpreting Cox model estimates included in meta-analysis.9,14 These limitations leave substantial uncertainty regarding the prognostic role of NTRK fusions9 and the attribution of survival benefits to TRK inhibitors in the absence of appropriate comparative evidence.

Using large real-world clinicogenomic data with a target trial emulation, we determined whether NTRK gene fusions independently influence overall survival in adults with advanced cancers. By comparing NTRK+ and NTRK– patients with similar tumor-specific clinical characteristics across multiple tumor types, we estimated the causal, tumor-agnostic prognostic effect of NTRK fusions as a directly interpretable difference in average survival. To contextualize and assess the robustness of our results, we replicated the three studies included in the recent meta-analysis of the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions by mirroring study design, inclusion criteria, and matching approaches.

Methods

Study Design, Data Sources, and Study Population

We used real-world data to mirror a pragmatic randomized trial that would determine the causal effect of harboring an NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3 gene fusion (NTRK+) on survival prognosis for adult patients with advanced solid tumors.15,16 Real-world data for our target trial emulation came from the Flatiron Health Research Database, a US-wide retrospective longitudinal database that encompassed more than 280 academic and community oncology clinics.17 These data captured structured and unstructured longitudinal demographic, clinical, and health care utilization information for patients diagnosed with cancer in the United States who sought care within the Flatiron Health network. Patient records were linked to the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine Clinico-Genomic Database, with all patient genomic data derived from Foundation Medicine comprehensive genomic profiling tests (ie, FoundationOne CDx, FoundationOne, FoundationOne Liquid CDx, and FoundationOne Liquid).18-20

Eligibility Criteria

This retrospective study included all patients age 18 to 85 years who had an advanced or metastatic solid tumor cancer diagnosis between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2023, received NGS, and were alive at the time of the testing report. Patients were excluded if they had previously participated in a clinical trial, received TRK inhibitors, or received their NGS report before an advanced or metastatic diagnosis. We divided patients into three groups: those with a NTRK gene fusion (NTRK+); those with no NTRK genomic alterations identified (NTRK–); and those with other NTRK genomic alterations. We excluded any patients with tumors not represented among the population of NTRK+ patients.

Outcomes and Follow-Up Period

The primary outcomes were tumor-agnostic overall survival over 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year study periods for patients from common tumor groups. Secondary outcomes included tumor-specific and NTRK1/2/3 gene-specific overall survival over 1- and 2-year study periods for patients from common tumor groups. We observed all patients included in our study cohort from the date of their NGS report (ie, time zero of follow-up) until a maximum period of 5 years or up to the first of either their date of death, end of the study period, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring. Loss to follow-up occurred when more than 90 days elapsed since a patient's last contact date. Administrative censoring occurred if patients received a TRK inhibitor, any clinical trial drug, or if they were alive at the end of the follow-up period, defined as the first of either the end of the study period or March 31, 2024.

Assignment Procedures, Matching, and Balance Assessment

To emulate random assignment and determine the tumor-agnostic causal effect of NTRK+ status on overall survival, we conducted stratified matching within tumor types, ensuring equal numbers of NTRK+ and NTRK– patients by tumor type were included in the tumor-agnostic analytical sample.

For stratified tumor-specific matching, we 1:1 matched NTRK+ patients with NTRK– patients using genetic algorithm–based matching, a nonparametric, semisupervised machine learning approach.21

We identified covariates used for matching based on clinician input and a directed acyclic graph (DAG).22-24 DAG-based sociodemographic covariates (Data Supplement, Fig S5) used for matching included sex, age at diagnosis, race/ethnicity (categorized as White, Black/African American, Hispanic, Asian, and other), socioeconomic status (SES) quintiles (defined at the census block group level based on patient's last known address, we also included a covariate capturing when SES was missing), history of smoking, time since diagnosis, and year of their diagnosis and of their NGS report. Clinical and treatment history covariates included number of lines of prior systemic therapy, indicators for any prior surgery, for any prior surgery with prior radiation therapy, for Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score at diagnosis, for stage at diagnosis, and for the type of practice where patients received care.

We followed best practice recommendations to evaluate balance, using standardized mean differences, and variance ratios, including balance assessment on polynomial and interaction terms in tumor-specific and tumor-agnostic settings.25-28 We determined satisfactory balance as achieving a matched sample where all standardized mean differences were <0.1 and where variance ratios were >0.5 or <2.29 We performed genetic matching using the Matching package in R.21,28

Causal Contrast

The causal contrast in our study was the per-protocol effect equivalent to the effect that would have been observed if having an NTRK gene fusion could be randomly assigned to patients as would be specified in a pragmatic randomized trial protocol. Our study design did not require patients to subsequently receive treatment. Our estimates thus correspond to the real-world causal effect of NTRK gene fusions on survival, regardless of standard care or receipt of any non–TRK-inhibitor treatment.

Survival Analysis

To estimate the relative causal effect of NTRK gene fusions on survival, after matching, we used nonparametric Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and parametric Weibull regression survival analysis, relaxing the assumptions of proportionality of the semiparametric Cox PH model and accounting for the possibility of a time-varying hazard. To provide the directly interpretable difference of the causal effect of NTRK gene fusions on average survival, we estimated the difference in restricted mean survival time (RMST).30 Overall survival time was determined from time zero of follow-up (ie, the date of each patient's NGS report) up to the first of either their date of death, end of the study period, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring, as previously defined above.

First, we estimated the tumor-agnostic effect of NTRK gene fusions on survival over 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year study periods for the most common tumor groups. Second, we estimated the tumor-specific effect of NTRK gene fusions on survival over 1- and 2-year study periods. Finally, we estimated the tumor-agnostic effect of NTRK gene fusions on 1- and 2-year survival for the most common tumor groups, stratifying patients by NTRK1/2/3 genes. We report results using P < .05 as a conventional reference point for indicating statistical significance.

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of our tumor-agnostic results, we conducted a sensitivity survival analysis including all NTRK+ patients, irrespective of tumor type. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we censored patients at the time of receiving any standard care treatment after their NGS report.

Finally, to situate our results, we replicated the three studies11-13 used in a recent meta-analysis9 on the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions. Detailed methods are provided in the Data Supplement. Broadly, we mirrored the study design, inclusion criteria, and matching approaches. We applied each protocol to our retrospective study cohort using each study's respective index date, including date of diagnosis for Bridgewater et al (Data Supplement, Replication A),11 the date of locally advanced/metastatic diagnosis for Hibar et al (Data Supplement, Replication B),13 and the date of first treatment after biopsy for Santi et al (Data Supplement, Replication C).12 We additionally conducted each analysis using the index date from our study, the date of NGS report. We used Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Weibull regression survival analysis to provide a test of the stability of the reported effects that had been estimated using Cox PH models.

Matching was done in R (version 4.4.3) using the Matching package,21,28 survival analysis was done in Stata (version 16.1), and we used strmst2 for estimating differences in RMST.31

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia-BC Cancer Research Ethics Board (H21-00284).

Results

During our study period, there were 91,314 adult patients who had an advanced or metastatic solid tumor cancer diagnosis, received NGS, and were alive at the time of the testing report (Fig 1). We included 68,145 NTRK– patients for matching to 73 NTRK+ patients within seven tumor groups (Table 1), including colorectal, non–small cell lung, sarcoma, breast, head and neck, neuroendocrine, and prostate, and 14 NTRK+ patients representing eight other cancers. Among the 87 NTRK+ patients, 58 (66.7%) had a NTRK1 fusion, 5 (5.7%) had a NTRK2 fusion, and 24 (27.6%) had a NTRK3 fusion. Before matching, the most frequent tumor types among NTRK– patients for the same seven tumor groups (n = 47,480; 69.7%) included NSCLC (n = 15,473; 32.6%), colorectal cancer (n = 11,032; 23.2%), and breast cancer (n = 10,346; 21.8%).

Survival Analysis

Tumor-agnostic Kaplan-Meier overall survival curves for the matched cohorts from common tumor groups are presented in Figure 3. The difference on overall survival over the 1-year study period (Fig 3A) was statistically significant using both the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the survivor function (log-rank test P value: .008) and Weibull regression (hazard ratio [HR], 2.105 [95% CI, 1.207 to 3.673]). The RMST difference of –50 days (95% CI, –96, –5) was statistically significant from no difference in survival (P = .029). Similarly, over 2- and 3-year periods, we found differences on overall survival with the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the survivor function that were statistically different at P < .05 (Figs 3B and 3C), with log-rank test P-values of 0.036 and 0.046, respectively. Estimated HRs from Weibull regression of 1.640 (95% CI, 1.028 to 2.625) over 2 years and of 1.589 (95% CI, 1.008 to 2.506) over 3 years were also statistically significant (Table 2). Survival difference in RMST were statistically different at 2 years (–123 days [95% CI, –221 to –25]; P = .014) and at 3 years (–161 days [95% CI, –314 to –8]; P = .039). At 5 years, no survival differences were statistically significant at P < .05.

Sensitivity Analysis

In replicating the analyses11-13 included in a recent meta-analysis on the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions,9 we found evidence suggesting that the PH assumption underlying Cox models was not met in all three studies. Using each study's index date also suggested immortal time bias (Data Supplement, Figs S9, S10, S12, and S13) and selection bias (Data Supplement, Figs S15 and S16), further threatening the validity of each study's estimates. Using the date of NGS report as the index date, consistent with our study, Weibull survival analysis produced substantially weaker signals of worse prognosis for NTRK+ patients, with all estimates not statistically different from no difference (Data Supplement, Figs S11, S14, and S17). Our estimated prognostic effects over the respective study periods were consistently smaller than originally reported (1.05 v 1.47 for Replication A over 48 months; 1.32 v 1.60 for Replication B over 36 months; and 1.02 v 1.44 for Replication C over 50 months). We provide further details on each replication in the Data Supplement. Finally, additional sensitivity analyses demonstrated robustness of our findings (Data Supplement, Figs S7 and S8, and Tables S3 and S4).

Discussion

Drawing on a cohort of NTRK+ patients more than three times larger than prior studies, our study provides a comprehensive estimate of the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions for patients with advanced cancer. We estimated mortality risk to be more than two-fold higher within the first year after receiving NGS (HR, 2.1 [95% CI, 1.2 to 3.7]), suggesting a larger short-term prognostic effect than previously reported. Survival was, on average, 50 to 123 days shorter over 1- and 2-year periods, providing clinically meaningful estimates of the prognostic value of NTRK fusions among patients not receiving TRK inhibitors. Building on a literature characterized by small, heterogeneous cohorts, our study examines clinically comparable patients with and without NTRK fusions across several common cancers. Although stratified analyses were not statistically significant, tumor-specific prognostic estimates are novel in this setting and may provide a hypothesis-generating starting point for understanding prognosis across tumor types.

Our findings enhance the clinical interpretability and validity of evidence indicating poorer overall survival among NTRK+ adult patients. The 64% and 59% increases in mortality risk we observed over 2 and 3 years, respectively, are of larger magnitude compared with the 51% increased mortality risk previously reported in a recent meta-analysis.9 Our estimates are derived from short- to medium-term observation periods, while the meta-analysis summarized survival over a 10-year horizon from diagnosis or treatment initiation, drawing on studies with median follow-up of <3 years.9,11-13 Replication of prior prognostic studies showed that reported effects were sensitive to model assumptions and selection of study index date, with analyses aligned to our design yielding consistently attenuated estimates. Our short- to medium-term findings of survival differences are therefore more closely aligned with the period over which outcomes are directly observed and most relevant to clinical decision making. Given that few patients remained under observation beyond 3 years, the attenuated survival difference at 5 years should be interpreted cautiously, as statistical precision decreases with the number of patients remaining at risk and does not necessarily indicate a diminution of the underlying prognostic effect. Our RMST findings contribute to the literature by providing estimates of absolute survival benefit that are directly expressible in units of time, facilitating interpretation over time horizons that are meaningful for patients. This study's clear and causal estimates of the tumor-agnostic prognostic effect of NTRK fusions thus improve interpretability for clinicians and patients while enhancing our understanding of important considerations for the validity of real-world external comparators.32,33

Although uncertainty remains, by providing tumor-specific estimates of overall survival in adults with NTRK+ solid tumors, our study advances understanding of prognosis across cancer types where outcomes have previously been poorly characterized.9 These findings contextualize observed heterogeneity in survival by suggesting differences in the prognostic value of oncogenic drivers,34,35 which may inform treatment considerations in routine oncology practice.36,37 The strong overall balance in patient characteristics achieved across tumor types supports causal interpretation under assumptions commonly accepted in randomized trials,16,38 while highlighting the need for tumor-specific inference within tumor-agnostic frameworks to manage persistent evidentiary uncertainty and support informed decision making in precision oncology.37

By replicating prior studies using our large clinicogenomic data, our findings offer additional insights into the transportability of real-world findings of the prognostic effect of NTRK fusions. Our findings were attenuated but consistent with Hibar et al13 using a larger, more recent cohort from the same US database, whereas differences from studies conducted in the United Kingdom and the Netherlands likely reflect limited comparability due to fundamental differences in study design, including index date definitions and confounder adjustment, as well as underlying population characteristics across countries.11,12

To manage evidentiary uncertainty in precision oncology, regulators and payers are increasingly signaling the acceptability of real-world evidence (RWE).39,40 Realizing the promise of decision-grade RWE will require harmonized guidance on fit-for-purpose analytic standards that are grounded in causal study design.41 Across tumor types, heterogeneity in comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness can reflect differences in prognosis and available treatment options,36,37 suggesting that tumor-agnostic market approval and reimbursement decisions may need to be tailored to clinically relevant subgroups.42 Persistent uncertainty driven by limited comparative evidence and gaps between identifying actionable alterations and delivering matched therapies complicates clinical decision making for patients in routine care.37,43 A lifecycle assessment approach embedded within learning health systems can leverage real-world evidence to support patient-centered treatment decisions while informing regulatory and reimbursement processes that balance timely access with responsible evidence development.44

This study had limitations. First, the overall sample of NTRK+ patients remains small, hindering inference regarding heterogeneity of effects. Second, NGS testing was performed selectively in clinical practice in patients with advanced disease or refractory to prior therapies, limiting generalizability to nonadvanced cancer settings. Nonetheless, our findings are based on the broadest cohort of NTRK+ patients with advanced cancers examined to date, with a study design that addressed key methodological limitations of prior real-world analyses. Third, although our intention-to-treat estimand did not disentangle intrinsic prognostic differences from effects mediated by subsequent treatment initiation, it remains appropriate for estimating prognosis as it reflects survival outcomes as observed in routine clinical practice. Finally, the inclusion of laboratory markers data was limited. Although matching achieved excellent balance on measured sociodemographic and clinical covariates, the potential for residual confounding on other genomic markers cannot be fully ignored. Good covariate balance overall and within tumor types reduced concerns about bias and supports the generalizability of our findings. More complete genomic data collection will be essential for unbiased outcome evaluation in future studies.

In conclusion, in this large real-world target trial emulation, NTRK gene fusions were associated with reduced short-term overall survival among adults with advanced cancers not treated with TRK inhibitors. By contributing novel tumor-specific prognostic evidence that accounts for heterogeneity of key clinical and sociodemographic characteristics across tumor types, our findings provide additional context for reliable tumor-agnostic inference. Collectively, these findings clarify the prognostic role of NTRK fusions and inform clinical interpretation of outcomes in routine oncology.
