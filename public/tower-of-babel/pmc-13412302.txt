A comparative investigation of transcranial pulsed and direct current stimulation on motor learning and brain oscillation patterns in older adults with mild cognitive impairment: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mohaddeseh Sadat Hafez Yousefi, Nastaran Ghotbi, Kazem Malmir, Ali Maleki, Fatemeh Ehsani, Shapour Jaberzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge-related declines in motor performance are associated with reduced cortical excitability and impaired neuroplasticity. Noninvasive transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) can improve motor learning, but the comparative efficacy of anodal transcranial direct current stimulation (a‑tDCS) and anodal transcranial pulsed current stimulation (a‑tPCS) in older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is unclear. This study aimed to compare the effects of a-tDCS and a-tPCS on motor learning and cortical activity in older adults with MCI.MethodsThirty‑nine older adults (age 60–75 years; Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) 18–23) were randomized to a‑tPCS (n = 13), a‑tDCS (n = 13), or sham (n = 13). Stimulation (2 mA, 20 min) was applied over left M1 during five daily sessions of a Serial Reaction Time Task (SRTT). Behavioral outcomes were reaction time (RT) and error rate (ER). Electroencephalography (EEG) absolute and relative spectral power (theta, alpha, beta), magnitude‑squared coherence (MSC), and imaginary coherence (ICoh) between C3–C4 were measured at baseline, after five intervention sessions, and at two‑week follow‑up.ResultsBoth a-tPCS and a-tDCS significantly increased alpha and theta spectral power, increased MSC, decreased ICoh, and reduced RT and ER relative to sham (p < .001). a-tPCS produced significantly greater initial changes than a-tDCS in absolute alpha power, relative theta power, alpha and theta MSC, and RT (p < .001). At follow-up, a-tPCS maintained significant changes compared to sham in absolute alpha power, absolute theta power, alpha MSC, and RT (p < .001). In contrast, the effects of a-tDCS showed notable decline over time, with only modest lasting benefits primarily observed in absolute theta and alpha power and beta MSC.ConclusionsBoth techniques facilitate motor learning in older adults with MCI, but a‑tPCS caused stronger and more enduring neurophysiological and behavioral effects in this sample.Trial registrationThe protocol of this trial was registered at Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (registration number: IRCT20151228025732N79; July 30, 2023) available at https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/search/result?query=IRCT20151228025732N79.

Background

The global population is aging rapidly, with the number of individuals over 60 years increasing faster than any other age group [1, 2]. Aging is accompanied by extensive quantitative and qualitative changes in the motor cortex, including reduced cortical excitability, decreased cortical plasticity, and various neurochemical alterations [3]. One of the key functions affected by aging is motor performance, which plays a crucial role in maintaining independence and quality of life in older adults [4, 5]. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) was characterized in 1997 by Petersen et al. as an abnormal memory function, more than in normal aging, that does not impair general cognitive function and daily living activities, the patient being self-aware and complaining about the deficit in the absence of dementia [6]. It is situated between unimpaired cognition and dementia, surpassing the natural age-related decline in cognition [7]. MCI is associated with mild impairments in memory, attention and executive function, which can impair cortical plasticity and motor memory consolidation, leading to a more rapid decline in functional independence [8]. The prevalence of MCI in the elderly population is globally over 15% [9]. Although motor function generally declines with age, especially in older adults with MCI, the capacity for motor learning remains largely preserved, and substantial motor performance can often be regained through training and rehabilitation [10, 11]. Therefore, acquiring new motor skills or recovering lost abilities through rehabilitation is essential to maintain an independent and healthy lifestyle in the elderly population with MCI [12, 13]. Neuroimaging and neurophysiological studies have shown that motor learning engages several brain regions, including the primary motor cortex (M1), supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, and cerebellum [14, 15]. Among these, a systematic review by Wang et al. (2021) highlights the central role of M1 in motor learning [14].

Motor learning—defined as adaptive and lasting changes in motor behavior—arises from structural and functional modifications across various brain regions [16, 17]. This process is initiated through experience gained during one or more training sessions [18]. Behaviorally, motor skill learning can be classified based on temporal dynamics: improvements occurring within a single session are referred to as online learning [19], while consolidation and reinforcement of motor memory across multiple sessions constitute the consolidation phase [20]. Even after consolidation, stable long-term motor memories must remain flexible and adaptable to environmental demands [21].

Given the multiple factors influencing motor learning, various therapeutic interventions have been developed to enhance this process. Among noninvasive neuromodulation techniques, transcranial Electrical Stimulation (tES) has attracted considerable attention due to its ability to modulate cortical excitability and influence neuronal activity in targeted brain regions [22, 23]. Importantly, each tES modality exerts distinct neurophysiological effects [24], and as new techniques emerge, understanding their differences has become essential [25]. Four primary tES modalities have been identified: transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS), and transcranial pulsed current stimulation (tPCS) [26].

Among tES techniques, anodal tDCS (a-tDCS) over the M1 or cerebellum reliably enhances motor learning in older adults by boosting cortical excitability and synaptic plasticity, as evidenced by meta-analysis showing moderate-to-large effect size on skill acquisition tasks [14]. On the other hand, tDCS has been the most widely studied modality for motor learning, and studies have examined the behavioral and neurophysiological effects of a-tDCS [23, 27, 28]. For example, the results of a 2023 systematic review examining 16 randomized controlled trial studies showed that a-tDCS can improve motor learning in older adults with MCI [29]. In addition, a study by Talimkhani et al. (2025) showed that a-tDCS can improve motor learning in older adults with both low and high activity levels [28]. Furthermore, one study reported that a-tDCS increased cortical spectral power, which was associated with improved motor learning [23].

Similarly, anodal tPCS (a-tPCS) (akin to cortical current modulation) targets cortical motor neurons to amplify motor neuron excitability and motor output, offering complementary benefits in age-related motor decline [30]. These modalities were prioritized over cathodal stimulation, which primarily inhibits excitability and yields inconsistent motor learning gains in aging populations [31], or alternatives like tRNS, which has limited and unstable evidence in seniors [32], and tACS, which is better suited for oscillatory disorders such as Parkinson’s disease and has less standardized protocols for motor learning [33]. This comparative design addresses a key research gap by evaluating two excitatory, mechanistic approaches optimized for geriatric motor plasticity.

However, recent evidence suggests that a-tPCS may be more effective than a-tDCS in increasing cortical excitability [25, 34]. tPCS applies pulsed currents that are characterized by specific pulse duration, frequency, and intervals between pulses, in contrast to tDCS, which provides a constant, continuous current [33]. Thibaut et al. found that the neurophysiological effects of a-tPCS were more dramatic than those of a-tDCS in reducing chronic pain [34]. On the other hand, in another study, Thibaut et al. showed that both a-tDCS and a-tPCS increased spectral power and coherence in multiple frequency bands [25]. In addition, a study using local field potentials to assess the effect of a-tPCS on brain plasticity found that low-intensity current and long-term a-tPCS can cause a large calcium response in astrocytes, thereby inducing an enhanced cortical excitability [35]. tPCS may improve both cortical plasticity and functional recovery by modulating brain network connections. One study has shown that when tPCS stimulates the M1, it can weaken the connection between the M1 region and the surrounding cortex and increase the resting-state functional connectivity with the thalamus [36].

Given the limited studies investigating the effect of a-tPCS directly on motor learning and the heterogeneity of the available evidence regarding the different mechanisms of action of these two types of currents and the extent of their effectiveness, a comparative study of these two stimulation modalities on motor learning in older adults with MCI seemed necessary [25, 37]. In older adults with MCI, due to reduced neuroplasticity, slower learning speed, and greater rehabilitation needs, it is important to find a current that has a longer-lasting effect at a faster rate.

However, no study has directly compared the effects of a-tDCS and a-tPCS on motor learning in older adults with MCI using comprehensive behavioral and neurophysiological measures. To comprehensively evaluate motor sequence learning using the Serial Reaction Time Task (SRTT) at both behavioral and neurophysiological levels, we incorporated high-density electroencephalography (EEG) to capture underlying neural dynamics in older adults with MCI—a population prone to age-related declines in oscillatory synchrony and cortico-spinal connectivity [38]. Absolute Power (AP) and Relative Power (RP) in theta (4–8 Hz; error monitoring and learning), alpha (8–13 Hz; inhibition), and beta (13–30 Hz; motor execution) bands were used as sensitive markers of motor adaptation, consistent with their established modulation during implicit sequence learning tasks such as the SRTT.

Importantly, in addition to spectral measures, we focused on interhemispheric functional connectivity between the primary motor cortices. Interhemispheric coherence between left and right M1 is a well-established electrophysiological marker of motor skill acquisition, consolidation, and interlimb transfer [39, 40]. Resting-state EEG studies have further shown that functional connectivity involving the left M1 can predict subsequent motor learning performance [41], whereas this connectivity is often reduced in older adults, contributing to slower motor adaptation [42]. Given that the intervention targeted the left primary motor cortex (M1), connectivity analysis was restricted to its homologous contralateral region.

In EEG studies, the C3 and C4 electrodes are widely accepted proxies for left and right primary motor cortices within the international 10–20 system [43, 44]. Therefore, C3–C4 connectivity was selected as a hypothesis-driven region-of-interest measure to specifically assess interhemispheric motor network interactions during SRTT performance, rather than performing whole-brain exploratory connectivity analysis. Accordingly, Magnitude Squared Coherence (MSC) between C3 and C4 was used to quantify overall functional coupling between bilateral M1 regions.

To further characterize interhemispheric interactions while minimizing the influence of volume conduction, Imaginary Coherence (ICoh) analysis was also applied [45]. ICoh specifically reflects non-zero-lag phase relationships between signals and is sensitive to time-lagged functional coupling. Therefore, ICoh values should be interpreted in terms of changes in delayed interhemispheric interactions rather than as direct indicators of improved neural coordination. Consequently, increases in ICoh are generally associated with stronger time-lagged connectivity, whereas decreases require cautious interpretation and may reflect alterations in the temporal structure of coupling rather than enhanced functional integration [46, 47].

The hypothesis was that a-tPCS, compared with a-tDCS and sham stimulation, would improve motor learning and alter cortical oscillations, as demonstrated by increased spectral power and coherence, decreased imaginary coherence, and better behavioral outcomes (faster Reaction Time (RT) and lower Error Rate (ER)) immediately after the intervention and at the two-week follow-up. We also hypothesized that a-tDCS would produce significant improvements in behavioral and neurophysiological parameters compared with the sham stimulation group, thereby improving motor learning in older adults with MCI. Furthermore, a-tPCS was expected to have significantly stronger and greater retention neurophysiological and behavioral effects than a-tDCS at both time points.

Methods

Study design

This randomized, double-blind controlled trial assigned 39 participants (n = 13 per group) to one of three intervention groups, a-tDCS with SRTT, a-tPCS with SRTT, or sham stimulation with SRTT, using random allocation software with a 1:1:1 ratio and a block size of six. To prevent selection bias, group allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque envelopes, and both the participants and the outcome assessor were blinded to the assignments.

Ethical approval and trial registration

This study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (approval code: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1402.038; 29 May 2023) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was then registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (registration number: IRCT20151228025732N79; 30 July 2023).

Experimental procedure

Participants

The required sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7) for a repeated measures ANOVA. Pilot data confirmed effect sizes of 0.498 for ER, 0.83 for absolute alpha power, 0.71 for relative alpha power, 0.46 for MSC, and 0.55 for ICoh, yielding minimum sample sizes of 18, 9, 12, 18, and 15 participants, respectively. To enhance statistical power, we screened 51 older adults aged 60 to 75 years; nine were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria or declining participation, and three others (one per group) withdrew before intervention. The remaining 39 randomized participants completed all assessments and interventions, while no serious adverse events were reported.

Inclusion criteria comprised: age 60–75, right-handedness, and a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of 18–23 indicating mild cognitive impairment [48]. Exclusion criteria were: impactful neurological or psychiatric disorders, recent CNS-affecting drugs or sedative use, amnesia, depression, upper limb dysfunction, color blindness essential for the Color Matching Test (CMT) task, or dizziness [49]; participants also refrained from stimulants like tea or coffee on assessment mornings. Education levels ranged from high school to postgraduate (80% with a high school diploma). The study adhered to CONSORT checklist criteria (Fig. 1).

Immediately after enrollment, participants were provided with detailed information about the study method and signed a written informed consent form. Written informed consent for publication of the images and associated data was obtained from all participants. A trained therapist administered the stimulation interventions, while a second investigator, blinded to group assignment, performed all behavioral and electrophysiological assessments. Participants were also blinded to the stimulation conditions and device settings. They remained unaware of their assigned intervention (a-tPCS, a-tDCS, or sham) as well as the conditions of the other groups. Blinding integrity was evaluated after the session by asking participants to guess their intervention type (active a-tPCS, active a-tDCS, sham, or unsure). Guess distributions across the three arms were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test (χ²). This randomized controlled trial was conducted in the research center of Semnan University, Semnan, Iran.

Participants completed five intervention sessions on consecutive days over the course of one week. Assessments were conducted at three time points: before the intervention (baseline), after five intervention sessions, and at a two-week follow-up. During intervention sessions, they performed the SRTT—presented as a ten-block CMT—while receiving brain stimulation; no EEG was recorded during stimulation. During assessment sessions only (baseline, post-interventions and follow-up), EEG was recorded while participants performed a two-block SRTT. Task events were trigger-locked to EEG acquisition via synchronized TTL pulses from Psychtoolbox of MATLAB. All sessions were held at the Faculty of Electrical Engineering, Semnan University, at a fixed time of day for each participant. (Fig. 2).

Transcranial electrical stimulation

In the experimental groups, stimulation was delivered at 2 mA for 20 min using an ActivaDose II device (Nurostim2, Medinateb), with saline‑soaked 5 × 5 cm sponge electrodes [50]. For a‑tPCS, pulses were delivered at a randomly varying frequency between 6 and 10 Hz, automatically generated by the stimulator’s internal algorithm (monophasic and non‑sinusoidal). The pulse width was fixed by the device manufacturer at 400 µs. The inter‑pulse interval varied between 100 and 166 ms, corresponding to the randomly generated frequency (calculated as 1/frequency minus pulse width), and a 50% direct current (DC) offset of the maximum current amplitude was applied [50]. For a‑tDCS, a constant anodal current was applied with 10‑s ramp‑up and 10‑s ramp‑down. In the sham condition we used a fade‑in, short stimulation, fade‑out procedure: current was ramped up for 30 s, held for 30 s, then ramped down [51] to avoid any sudden starting or stopping of the stimulation. In all three groups (a-tDCS, a-tPCS, and sham), the anode was positioned over the left (dominant) M1 (C3, International 10–20 system) and the cathode was located over the right contralateral supraorbital area [52] (Fig. 3).

All participants were monitored for adverse effects throughout the interventions using a standardized questionnaire to evaluate common sensations such as itching, tingling, burning, headache, and pain, with severity rated on a numerical scale ranging from 0, indicating no sensation, to 10, indicating the worst imaginable sensation [52].

Serial reaction time task

We employed the Color Matching Test (CMT), a custom-designed software application, to evaluate ER and RT [53]. This software effectively simulated an SRTT environment, characterized by a second-order structural sequence [53]. The SRTT holds widespread recognition as a dependable instrument for assessing motor learning, integrating components of both motor execution and cognitive processing [53].

During the CMT, colored squares appeared in a sequential manner at the computer screen’s central display. Participants were tasked with pressing the corresponding keyboard key for each color using their right index finger. The assessment during the pre-test stage involved two blocks. The second block’s data served as the baseline measurement, considering that the first block was dedicated to practice and participant familiarization. Subsequent to the primary training session, two additional blocks were administered to ascertain offline learning effects: immediate effect was quantified after five post-training sessions, and short-term retention was determined two weeks thereafter (Fig. 4). The presentation of colors occurred in ten series. For every trial within these series, the software captured RT and the number of errors. The average RT and average number of errors across all ten series were then calculated and presented.

Motor learning was operationally defined as improvement in RT and ER on the repeated SRTT sequence from pre- to post- intervention, accompanied by retention of effects at two-week follow-up indicative of sustained learning, alongside consistent EEG changes across sessions; however, the absence of random-sequence blocks precludes strict separation of general practice effects from sequence-specific learning.

EEG acquisition and signal processing

Cortical activity was assessed using EEG [54]. We used a 32-channel high-density EEG system (WEEG V.32, BayaMed Technologists, Tehran, Iran). The 21 electrodes used were positioned according to the international 10–20 system (Fig. 5). EEG was recorded simultaneously during the SRTT task. Data were sampled at 250 Hz, and electrode impedances were maintained below 10 kΩ. A notch filter was used to remove power-line interference (50–60 Hz) in order to improve the clarity of the true neural signal. EEG signals were band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 50 Hz to remove slow drifts and high-frequency noise, normalized to a pre-task resting state baseline to control for inter-session variability. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was performed using EEGLAB (v2022.1) running in MATLAB (R2022b; The MathWorks Inc.) with the extended Infomax algorithm. Artifactual components (mean 5 ± 1 per participant; range 4–7; ~ 20–25% of total components) were identified through visual inspection of spatial topographies, time courses, and power spectra, and manually excluded. These primarily included eye blinks, lateral eye movements and muscle artifacts. Neural components were retained to optimize signal-to-noise ratio without compromising task-related cortical activity. Fast Fourier Transformation (FFT) was used to calculate both AP (µV²/Hz) and RP (%) in the EEG bands theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz) [25, 37]. Additionally, both AP and RP were calculated using MATLAB (MATLAB R2022b; The MathWorks Inc). Moreover, MSC and ICoh were computed for these frequency bands using the bilateral electrode pairs C3/C4. These areas were precisely the areas of stimulation in the present study, which are also the most effective learning areas. The frequency domain analysis was performed using the FFT algorithm with a Hamming window of 4 s and 50% overlap, achieving a frequency resolution of 0.125 Hz, to calculate absolute power (AP, µV²/Hz) and relative power (RP, %) densities within each of the bands. The AP of a band is the integral of all of the spectral power values within its frequency range. RP for each frequency band was computed as the absolute power of that band divided by the total absolute power summed across the delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz) bands, and then multiplied by 100. Thus, although only theta, alpha, and beta relative power were analyzed as outcomes, the denominator included the delta band to represent the full spectral power captured within the 0.5–50 Hz range. As a result, the sum of the reported relative power values does not reach 100% because a substantial proportion of the total power resides in the delta band. Also, MSC and ICoh were calculated using established formulas from the literature [46, 55]. Furthermore, EEG analyses were conducted using predefined spectral features. For spectral power analysis, absolute and relative power values were calculated for each frequency band and then averaged across all 21 electrodes to obtain a global estimate of cortical activity. This approach was used to reduce spatial dimensionality and avoid multiple channel-wise comparisons [56]. Functional connectivity was assessed using MSC and ICoh [57]. Coherence analyses were restricted to a predefined pair of electrodes over M1 selected based on their relevance to motor cortical activity during motor learning.

Outcome Measures

Behavioral and neurophysiological aspects of motor learning were evaluated. Behavioral parameters, recorded by the task software, included RT and ER; a decrease in these values indicated improved motor learning [27, 28]. Neurophysiological parameters included the mean absolute and relative spectral power of 21 EEG channels (Fp1, Fpz, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, Oz, O2) in the alpha, beta, and theta bands; modulations in spectral power within these frequency bands have been widely associated with cortical processes involved in motor control, sensorimotor integration, and motor learning [25, 35]. In addition, the MSC and ICoh were measured for the C3 electrode pair, which was the stimulated area, and its corresponding pair in the right hemisphere, C4 [40, 41]. Increased MSC means increased synchrony and coordination of signals reaching the two hemispheres and indicates increased motor learning [39]. However, because this synchrony may be due to signal transmission from adjacent areas, we used imaginary coherence, which indicates the synchrony of signals reaching the two cerebral hemispheres. ICoh reduces the influence of volume conduction and is therefore considered a more reliable indicator of true functional connectivity between brain regions [46, 47].

Statistical analysis

The main objective of this randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial was to examine the effects of transcranial electrical stimulation on motor learning performance. Therefore, the behavioral motor learning measures assessed across the three time points (pre-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up) were considered the primary outcomes of the study. EEG measures were included as secondary outcomes to explore the possible neurophysiological mechanisms associated with motor learning. All analyses were done in SPSS v.22 with a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05. Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test; for baseline demographic comparisons we used one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test when normality was not met. To assess blinding integrity, participants’ guesses regarding their allocated intervention (real a-tPCS, real a-tDCS, or sham) were compared across groups using a Chi-square test. The main test was mixed-model ANOVA framework with Time (Pre, Post, Follow-up) as the within-subject factor and Group (a-tPCS, a-tDCS, Sham) as the between-subject factor; separate 3 × 3 ANOVAs were performed for each dependent measure, and neurophysiological analyses were performed separately for each frequency band (theta, alpha, beta) from 21 electrodes that were selected a priori based on previous literature [25, 37]. The analyses of the EEG data were designed to explore potential underlying mechanisms. For this reason, they were treated as secondary, exploratory objectives, not as primary outcomes intended to confirm a hypothesis. Sphericity was tested with Mauchly’s test and, where violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied to adjust degrees of freedom and p-values. For significant effects, follow-up pairwise comparisons were performed and primary contrasts were adjusted using Bonferroni correction to control Type I error. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared (ηp²), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large, respectively.

Results

Participant and baseline characteristics

A total of thirty-nine participants were randomly allocated to one of three groups: a-tPCS (n = 13), a-tDCS (n = 13), or sham (n = 13). The groups were well-matched at baseline, with no significant differences in age, gender distribution, or any of the behavioral or neurophysiological parameters assessed (p > .05) (Table 1).

Effects of transcranial stimulation

We used mixed-effects ANOVAs with 3 groups (a-tPCS, a-tDCS, Sham) × 3 times (Pre, Post, Follow-up). Behavioral performance outcomes showed significant improvements following stimulation. For reaction time (RT), a significant Group × Time interaction was observed (p < .001, partial η² = 0.753). Post-hoc between-group comparisons at the post-intervention time point (Table 3) revealed that the a-tPCS group had a significantly faster RT than the Sham group (Mean Difference = -1.531 s, p = .003). While the a-tDCS group also showed a reduction in RT compared to Sham, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Mean Difference = -1.032 s, p = .058). Notably, within-group changes from baseline (Table 4) confirmed that both active stimulation groups significantly improved their RT post-intervention (a-tPCS: -3.77 s, p < .001; a-tDCS: -1.32 s, p < .001), whereas the Sham group did not (0.04 s, p = .625). For ER, a significant main effect of Group was found (p < .001, partial η² = 0.450). Post-hoc tests confirmed that the Sham group committed significantly more errors than both the a-tPCS group (Mean Difference = 0.749, p < .001) and the a-tDCS group (Mean Difference = 0.818, p < .001) across the assessment periods.

Spectral Power analyses showed significant changes over groups and time. Absolute alpha power demonstrated a significant group × time interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.860), with the a-tPCS group exhibiting higher power than both a-tDCS (Mean Difference = 1.459 µV², 95% CI [1.142 to 1.776], p < .001) and Sham (Mean Difference = 2.519 µV², 95% CI [2.202 to 2.836], p < .001) after the intervention, indicating a strong and precise effect. Similarly, absolute theta power showed a significant interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.786), where a-tPCS exceeded a-tDCS (Mean Difference = 1.090 µV², 95% CI [0.035 to 2.145], p = .041) and Sham (Mean Difference = 2.055 µV², 95% CI [1.000 to 3.110], p < .001). Absolute beta power likewise demonstrated a significant group by time interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.824), and post-hoc tests indicated immediate increases in absolute beta for both active stimulations versus sham (a-tPCS vs. sham Mean Difference = 1.947 µV², 95% CI [1.416 to 2.478], p < .001; a-tDCS vs. sham Mean Difference = 1.525 µV², 95% CI [0.994 to 2.056], p < .001) while a-tPCS versus a-tDCS was not significant (p = .161); by follow-up these beta absolute-power increases had partially declined. For relative power, alpha band showed a significant group × time interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.916), with a-tPCS showing a notable increase compared to Sham (Mean Difference = 13.800%, 95% CI [10.852 to 16.749], p < .001). Relative theta power also showed a significant interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.955). Post-hoc analysis confirmed that a-tPCS produced significantly higher power than both the a-tDCS group (Mean Difference = 5.287%, 95% CI [2.161 to 8.413], p < .001) and the Sham group (Mean Difference = 13.908%, 95% CI [10.782 to 17.034], p < .001). Relative beta power did not show a significant group by time interaction (p = .387), and no significant between-group or within-group changes were observed.

Functional connectivity was significantly affected by the interventions. Alpha coherence showed a significant group × time interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.560), indicating a large effect, with post-hoc analyses showing that a-tPCS increased coherence more than sham (Mean Difference = 0.221, 95% CI [0.116 to 0.325], p < .001). Theta coherence also exhibited a significant interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.848), another large effect, with a-tPCS again producing higher coherence than sham (Mean Difference = 0.205, 95% CI [0.136 to 0.274], p < .001). Beta coherence changed significantly (p < .001, partial η² = 0.544), with both active groups increasing beta coherence post–pre (p < .001); a-tDCS increased more than sham (mean difference 0.146, p = .001) and more than a-tPCS (a-tPCS vs. a-tDCS mean difference − 0.094, p = .032). Conversely, alpha imaginary coherence showed a significant interaction (p < .001, partial η² = 0.687), marked by a post-intervention decrease in the a-tPCS group compared to Sham (Mean Difference = -0.042, 95% CI [-0.059 to -0.024], p < .001). Beta imaginary coherence showed a significant group by time interaction (p < .001, partial ηp² = 0.410). Active stimulation caused small but significant drops compared to sham (a-tPCS vs. sham: mean difference − 0.016, 95% CI [− 0.029, − 0.002], p = .019; a-tDCS vs. sham: mean difference − 0.015, 95% CI [− 0.028, − 0.001], p = .030), with partial decline at follow-up.

Blinding integrity

Participants were asked to guess their intervention type (active a-tPCS, active a-tDCS, sham or unsure). Responses related to the active and sham interventions were included:

In the a‑tPCS group, 11 guessed active, 1 sham, and 1 unsure; in the a‑tDCS group, 12 guessed active, 0 sham, and 1 unsure; and in the sham group, 12 guessed active, 1 sham, and 0 unsure. The distribution of guesses (active, sham, or unsure) did not differ significantly between the three intervention groups (χ² = 2.05, df = 4, p = .725), suggesting that participants were not systematically able to distinguish the type of intervention across groups.

Assessment of side effects

A formal assessment of side effects was conducted. The frequencies of adverse effects (tingling, itching, burning) were compared between the a-tPCS, a-tDCS, and Sham groups using chi-square tests (Table 5). No significant differences were observed (χ2 tingling = 0.27, p = .87; χ2 itching = 0.21, p = .90; χ2 burning = 0.46, p = .80), confirming that mild sensations were similar across conditions.

Discussion

This study found that both a-tDCS and a-tPCS, when paired with motor training, changed cortical oscillations and improved behavioral performance in older adults with MCI. Compared with sham, both active stimulations increased alpha and theta band power and changed interhemispheric connectivity parameters; a-tPCS produced larger effects and greater retention than a-tDCS on several behavioral and neurophysiological outcomes.

Behavioral outcomes

Both a-tPCS and a-tDCS significantly improved behavioral performance, as demonstrated by reduced RT and ER compared with the sham group. The sustained reduction in RT and ER at 2-week follow-up in the a-tPCS group suggests short-term neuroplastic effects, whereas the effects of a-tDCS on RT were not sustained for up to 2 weeks after intervention. A 2022 systematic review by Shuo Qi et al. showed that a-tDCS can significantly reduce RT and thus improve motor learning in young and older adults [58]. A 2025 study by Talimkhani et al. also showed that 5 sessions of a-tDCS over the M1 region significantly reduced RT and ER in older adults with low and high physical activity immediately and one week after intervention [28]. The results of this study differed from our findings at follow-up, which may be due to the difference in the duration of follow-up, which was longer in our study (two weeks). Taken together, these results suggest that noninvasive electrical stimulation enhances motor-cognitive integration and attentional processing, leading to improved task performance in older adults with MCI [50, 58, 59]. The small, nonsignificant changes in the sham group likely reflect the effects of training or placebo rather than true physiological adaptations.

Spectral power modulation

Both a-tPCS and a-tDCS increased absolute and relative alpha power versus sham, with a-tPCS producing larger immediate and follow-up changes. This pattern is consistent with prior reports that stimulation near the alpha range can improve cortical synchrony and support motor and cognitive processing [25, 60]. Sustained alpha enhancement likely reflects long-term synaptic plasticity and motor learning consolidation in aging populations [61, 62]. In addition, an increase in absolute and relative power in the alpha band leads to an increase in sensory-motor integration and motor readiness [62]. Moreover, the alpha band is related to memory consolidation, which is confirmed by entering the consolidation phase in the a-tPCS group [63]. These findings indicate that both interventions effectively modulated neural processes associated with the initiation and consolidation phases of motor learning in older adults with MCI.

Beta power spectral increases were transient and most prominent following a-tDCS, a pattern that aligns with beta’s role in short-term motor activation and engagement. Although these effects dissipate quickly, their presence immediately after stimulation indicates that a-tDCS can momentarily enhance the preparatory motor state that facilitates task execution [64, 65]. While not sufficient for short-term retention on their own, these beta modulations may prime the motor system for learning when combined with repeated practice.

Theta spectral power increased in both active groups—especially a-tPCS—with effects maintained at the two-week follow-up. Theta enhancement is typically linked to improved attentional control, error monitoring, and working-memory processes that are essential components of motor learning [66, 67]. In older adults, strengthening these cognitive–motor interfaces is particularly valuable, as impairments in attention and working memory often limit their ability to acquire and generalize new motor skills [1, 2]. Therefore, the sustained increase in theta is likely due to more stable maintenance and transfer of trained motor behavior and is related to neuroplasticity processes.

Overall, the results showed that a‑tPCS enhanced alpha and theta band power, while a‑tDCS produced an immediate effect on theta modulation. It is also important to consider the contribution of practice-related learning and expectancy effects across the five training sessions. Repeated task exposure is known to produce modest behavioral and neurophysiological changes even in sham conditions, likely reflecting familiarization, procedural learning, and nonspecific engagement with the task. Accordingly, the small changes observed in the sham group may be attributed to these practice-related improvements rather than neuromodulatory effects. However, the magnitude, persistence, and frequency-specific nature of the EEG changes observed in the active stimulation groups—particularly the sustained alpha and theta enhancements following a-tPCS and the immediate post-stimulation effects following a-tDCS—exceeded what would be expected from practice alone. This pattern suggests that active stimulation was associated with neural modulations beyond general learning or placebo-related effects, supporting the specificity of the observed EEG–behavior associations.

It is important to note that EEG patterns may underlie or accompany behavioral improvement. In other words, these parallel changes indicate a close relationship between brain signals and motor learning.

Functional connectivity (Coherence)

Analysis of MSC and ICoh between bilateral M1 regions revealed increased interhemispheric coupling and reduced phase lag following both a-tPCS and a-tDCS relative to sham. In the alpha band, a-tPCS produced greater MSC increases and stronger ICoh reductions, indicating improvement of interhemispheric synchronization and motor learning efficiency. These results support previous findings that a-tPCS promotes two-week maintenance synchronization through rhythmic pulsed activation of cortical networks [25, 34]. In addition, a 2025 study by Zhao et al. showed that coherence increases after performing a motor task similar to the SRTT [39]. Lu and colleagues in 2023 suggested that the acquisition and transfer of motor skills, including interhemispheric connectivity, may be relevant for both simple and complex tasks. This is consistent with the notion that theta and alpha bands are most relevant to motor learning [15].

In contrast, a-tDCS showed a more pronounced and sustained effect in the beta band, suggesting improved coordination within motor-related beta networks and enhanced top-down control [68, 69]. While increases in MSC may indicate enhanced overall functional connectivity, interpretation of ICoh requires particular caution. As originally described by Guido Nolte et al., the imaginary part of coherence selectively captures time-lagged phase interactions while minimizing zero-lag correlations arising from volume conduction. Accordingly, increases in ICoh are typically interpreted as reflecting stronger delayed functional coupling. In contrast, decreases in ICoh cannot be directly interpreted as improved phase alignment or enhanced information transfer; rather, they may indicate reduced time-lagged interactions, a relative increase in zero-lag components, or other changes in signal properties. Therefore, the ICoh changes observed in the present study are interpreted as reflecting alterations in the temporal characteristics of interhemispheric communication, rather than unequivocal improvements in neural coordination [46, 47].

Theta-band coherence was also significantly enhanced, particularly under a-tPCS, which may reflect the engagement of cortical–subcortical loops and improved integration of attention, memory, and motor processes [70, 71]. Collectively, these findings suggest that both techniques enhance neural connectivity, with a-tPCS producing broader subcortical-cortical effects and a-tDCS producing more localized cortical modulation.

Although both a-tPCS and a-tDCS modulate brain activity, their underlying mechanisms differ substantially. a-tDCS alters neuronal excitability by shifting resting membrane potentials, thereby facilitating synaptic efficacy but without directly entraining oscillatory activity [25]. Conversely, a-tPCS delivers random pulsed currents that engage deeper structures such as the thalamus and brainstem, enhancing endogenous oscillations via stochastic facilitation [69]. By modulating alpha–theta rhythms and strengthening M1–premotor connectivity, a-tPCS may induce spike-timing–dependent plasticity and facilitate long-term motor learning [34]. Thus, a-tPCS provides broader dynamic synchronization, whereas a-tDCS exerts targeted top-down modulation of cortical excitability. These neurophysiological changes collectively point to motor improvement, as increased interhemispheric connectivity and decreased phase lag indicate more efficient acquisition, consolidation, and execution of motor skills. Importantly, these findings have direct clinical relevance to older adults with MCI, a population in which motor mechanisms are compromised due to impaired cortical-subcortical connectivity and reduced network receptivity [42]. The observed improvements in interhemispheric coherence and information transfer efficiency may translate into better control, faster adaptation to novel motor tasks, and improved functional mobility in activities of daily living.

It is important to note that our study is limited by the lack of electric field modeling or neuroimaging data and relies on EEG, and these results are discussed in light of previous findings to propose possible mechanisms of electrical currents.

The parallel changes in behavioral outcomes (faster RTs and reduced errors) and in EEG parameters (increased alpha/theta spectral power and coherence, reduced ICoh) provide compelling evidence for a tight coupling between neural synchronization and motor learning performance. The enhanced alpha–theta synchronization observed after stimulation likely facilitated more efficient information processing, improved attentional engagement, and smoother integration of sensory and motor commands—core components of motor learning. These electrophysiological signatures, together with behavioral gains, indicate that stimulation strengthened the neural mechanisms underlying task acquisition, error correction, and performance stabilization.

Importantly, the sustained behavioral and neurophysiological improvements in the a-tPCS group highlight its superior ability to drive deep and enduring neuroplastic adaptations in aging brains with MCI. For older adults with MCI, such improvements may translate into more effective learning of daily motor routines and greater resilience against functional decline. Thus, beyond demonstrating neural modulation, these results underscore the clinical potential of a-tPCS as a practical intervention to enhance motor learning capacity, preserve independence, and reduce the risk of mobility-related complications in individuals.

This study has some important limitations: the five-session protocol and two-week follow-up only show short-term effects and cannot prove long-term stability; stimulation targeted only left M1 while motor learning relies on wider cortical-subcortical networks; EEG gave useful timing information but lacks the spatial resolution to detect deep brain changes; MoCA screening may not have captured subtle cognitive differences that affect learning; practice effects could have influenced behavior despite sham control; and we did not measure neurochemical markers (e.g., glutamate, GABA), which limits mechanistic conclusions; moreover, results from older adults with MCI may not generalize to other clinical groups. Future studies should use longer interventions and follow-up, test multi-site montages (for example M1–cerebellum or prefrontal targets), combine EEG with imaging (fMRI, fNIRS) or source analyses to map networks, apply electric-field modeling to optimize parameters, account for individual differences (cortical excitability, genetics), and directly compare a-tPCS and a-tDCS with other methods such as TMS to strengthen translational and mechanistic claims. Also, future studies with larger and more educationally diverse samples are warranted to explicitly investigate how educational attainment and clinical heterogeneity within MCI may modulate responsiveness to tES.

Conclusion

In older adults with MCI, both a-tPCS and a-tDCS combined with motor training produced measurable improvements in task performance and cortical oscillations compared with sham. a-tPCS caused larger and more persistent effects than a-tDCS: it produced greater increases in alpha and theta spectral power, stronger changes in cortical coherence measures, and more durable behavioral gains at two-week follow-up. a-tDCS mainly increased beta-band activity and produced immediate but less durable behavioral benefits, suggesting it may better support immediate motor engagement and stabilization rather than short-term retention and skill consolidation. Clinically, these results indicate that a-tPCS is a promising adjunct to rehabilitation in older adults with MCI. It may improve motor learning and the retention of new motor skills, which could translate into better performance of daily activities and potentially reduce the risk of Alzheimer’s disease when combined with task-specific training. However, before clinical adoption, larger and longer trials are needed to replicate these findings, optimize pulse parameters and montage using electric-field modeling, confirm safety and tolerability in aging populations, and link neural changes to meaningful functional outcomes (ADL, mobility, and cognition). In summary, a-tPCS shows greater promise than a-tDCS for promoting longer-lasting neuroplasticity and motor learning in older adults with MCI, but further work is required to move from proof-of-concept to routine clinical use.
