The canonical form, scissors congruence and adjoint degrees of polytopes
T. Baumbach, A. Freyer, J. Weigert, M. Winter

Abstract
. We study the canonical form Ω \Omega as a valuation in the context of scissors congruence for polytopes. We identify the degree of its numerator – the adjoint polynomial adj P \adj_{P} – as an important invariant in this context. More precisely, for a polytope P P we define the degree drop that measures how much smaller than expected the degree of the adjoint polynomial of P P is. We show that this drop behaves well under various operations, such as decompositions, restrictions to faces, projections, products and Minkowski sums. Next we define the reduced canonical form Ω 0 \Omega_{0} and show that it is a translation-invariant 1-homogeneous valuation on polytopes that vanishes if and only if P P has positive degree drop. Using it we can prove that zonotopes can be characterized as the d d -polytopes that have maximal possible degree drop d − 1 d-1 . We obtain a decomposition formula for Ω 0 \Omega_{0} that expresses it as a sum of edge-local quantities of P P . Finally, we discuss valuations Ω s \Omega_{s} that can distinguish higher values of the degree drop.

1.
Introduction

The
canonical form

\Omega
of a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a rational function that measures the volume of the polar dual as a function of the polarization point
x\in\Int(P)
:

\Omega(P;x):=d!\vol(P-x)^{\circ}=\frac{\adj_{P}(x)}{\prod_{F\subset P}L_{F}(x)}.

Its numerator
\adj_{P}
is known as the
adjoint polynomial
of
P
. The canonical form was initially introduced by Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam
[
4
]
in the study of positive geometries and scattering amplitudes in quantum field theory.
The adjoint polynomial in turn arose in geometric modeling, initially defined for polygons by Wachspress
[
21
]
, and later generalized to general polytopes and polyhedra by Warren
[
22
]
. Both concepts have since emerged in a number of other geometric and algebraic contexts, including geometric rigidity theory
[
23
]
, intersection theory
[
2
,
3
]
, and algebraic statistics
[
14
]
.

In this article we focus on the non-trivial fact that the canonical form defines also a
simple valuation
on convex polytopes.
In the form relevant to us, this means that whenever
P_{1},...,P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are convex polytopes with disjoint interiors, so that
P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
is convex as well, we have

\Omega(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})=\Omega(P_{1})+\cdots+\Omega(P_{n}).

A number of famous results on polytope decompositions and scissors invariance have their roots in valuation theory, perhaps most notably, Max Dehn’s resolution of Hilbert’s third problem on the equidecomposability of 3-polytopes
[
7
,
11
]
.
One objective of this article is to explore what similarly flavored results can be derived using specifically the valuative nature of the canonical form.
The following recurring example should give a good idea of what we are aiming for:

Example 1.1
.

Let
P_{1},...,P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
centrally symmetric
polytopes (that means,
P_{i}
and
-P_{i}
are translates of each other).
It is a remarkable and somewhat surprising fact that if a larger
convex
polytope
P=P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
is built from the
P_{i}
without overlapping interiors, then it is itself centrally symmetric.

This can be shown in different ways, but
one of the most elegant proofs involves valuation theory: let
\mathrm{S}(P)
be the so-called
surface area measure
of
P
. That is,
\mathrm{S}(P)
is the measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
that to each subset
N\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
assigns the area of the part of the surface of
P
that has unit normals that lie in
N
.
In our case, where
P
is a polytope,
\mathrm{S}(P)
is the sum of finitely many Dirac measures, one per facet of
P
.
Based on this we define the
odd surface area measure

\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P):=\mathrm{S}(P)-\mathrm{S}(-P)
.
One can show that
\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}
defines a simple valuation.
Clearly, since all
P_{i}
are centrally symmetric, we have
\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P_{i})=0
.
Less obvious however, a converse holds as well: if
\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P)=0
, then
P
is centrally symmetric.
This can be proven using Minkowski’s uniqueness theorem.

The fact that
P=P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
inherits its central symmetry from the pieces
P_{i}
is now reduced to a one-line argument:

\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P)=\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})=\sum_{i}\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P_{i})=0.

This technique can actually show something stronger: suppose that
P
and
Q
are
translation scissors congruent
, that is,
P=P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
and
Q=Q_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\dots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}Q_{n}
so that
P_{i}=Q_{i}+t_{i}
are related through translations along vectors
t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Clearly,
\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}
is
translation-invariant
, that is
\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P+t)=\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Through

\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P)=\sum_{i}\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(P_{i})=\sum_{i}\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(Q_{i}+t_{i})=\sum_{i}\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(Q_{i})=\smash{\mathrm{\tilde{S}}}(Q)

we therefore find that
P
is centrally symmetric if and only if
Q
is.
More remarkably, we made no assumptions about whether the
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
are centrally symmetric.
We say that central symmetry is a
translation scissors invariant
.

When attempting to prove similar decomposition results using the canonical form,
initially two obstacles are encountered:

()

A typical way to obtain translation scissors invariants is by identifying them as the vanishing of a valuation (such as central symmetry is characterized as the vanishing of the symmetrized surface area measures in
Example

1.1
).
However,
\Omega
only vanishes on polytopes with empty interior.

()

\Omega
is
not
translation-invariant, and hence one might not expect new results about translation scissors congruence from its study.

In this article we shall overcome both of these obstacles and thereby discover interesting new mathematics.

For (
i
) we show that instead of the vanishing of
\Omega
, the degree of the numerator (
i.e.,
the degree of the adjoint polynomial
\adj_{P}
) can be controlled in decompositions and presents itself as a new and interesting polytope invariant.
From this we derive the quantity
\drop(P)
, a purely affine notion that measures how much “smaller than expected” the adjoint degree is.
It turns out that this
adjoint degree drop
is subtly related to parallelisms in the face structure of
P
and in many ways behaves better than the adjoint degree itself.
Lastly, we note that the adjoint degree comes up independently as a relevant quantity also in other contexts, such as in the construction of polynomial barycentric coordinates
[
5
]
.

To overcome (
ii
) we construct from
\Omega
a new truly translations-invariant valuation
\Omega_{0}
.
It will serve us as a powerful tool in the study of adjoint degrees and will also yield some results on translation scissors congruence.
For example, we will show that being a zonotope in dimension three is translations scissors invariant.
We will also prove a decomposition formula for
\Omega_{0}
that allows us to compute its value from data that is only local to the edges of
P
(that is, edge lengths and tangent cones).

2.
Dual volume and the canonical form
\Omega

For a convex
d
-dimensional polyhedron
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

P^{\circ}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\text{$\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1$ for all $y\in P$}\}.

denotes the
polar dual
with
polarization center
at the origin.
In this sense,
(P-x)^{\circ}
is the polar dual with polarization center at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
x\in\Int(P)
, the polar dual is a convex polytope (in particular, bounded) and its volume is a rational function in
x
.
Based on this, we define the
canonical form
\Omega(P;x)
as the unique rational function that evaluates to

(2.1)

\Omega(P;x)=d!\vol(P-x)^{\circ},

for
x\in\Int(P)
, and is constant
\Omega(P)\equiv 0
if
\Int(P)=\varnothing
.
Here,
\vol
denotes the Euclidean volume, and the factor
d!
is included to align with the definition given in
[
9
, Definition 2.5]
where the measure is normalized to evaluate to 1 on the standard simplex.
This is moreover consistent with the definition of canonical form in Positive Geometry
[
4
]
where it is more commonly defined as a rational
d
-form.
As we are solely concerned with its algebraic properties, we prefer to work with
\Omega
as a rational function.
The precise relation is clarified by
[
9
, Theorem 5.3]
.

If
\Int(P)\not=\varnothing
and
x
approaches the boundary of
P
, the volume of the polar dual diverges.
Hence
\Omega(P;x)
has poles along the facet hyperplanes.
One can show that these are the only poles, and by factoring them out we obtain a polynomial known as the
adjoint polynomial
or just
adjoint
\adj_{P}
of
P
:

(2.2)

\Omega(P;x)=\frac{\adj_{P}(x)}{\prod_{F\subset P}L_{F}(x)}.

Here the product in the denominator iterates over all facets
F
of
P
, and
L_{F}(x):=h_{F}-\langle u_{F},x\rangle
is the facet-defining linear form, with
u_{F}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
being the facet’s unit normal vector (pointing outwards), and
h_{F}\in\mathbb{R}
the height of
F
over the origin.

Computing the canonical forms and adjoints for simplices and simplicial cones is a standard exercise.
Here we state the result (see
Appendix

A
for derivations):

Example 2.1
.

For a simplex
\Delta:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\leq h_{i}\}
we have

(2.3)

\Omega(P;x)=\frac{\adj_{\Delta}}{\;\;\,\prod_{i}(h_{i}-\langle x,u_{i}\rangle)},\quad\text{with }\!\adj_{\Delta}=\left|\,\det\!\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
h_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&h_{d}\end{pmatrix}\right|.

Similarly, for a simplicial cone
C:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\leq 0\}
we have

(2.4)

\Omega(C;x)=\frac{\adj_{C}}{(-1)^{d}\prod_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle},\quad\text{with }\!\adj_{C}=\left|\,\det\!\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{1}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
\end{pmatrix}\right|.

Note that according to
Example

2.1
the canonical form of a simplex or simplicial cone is independent of the length of the normals.
The adjoint however is a number and scales with the lengths of the normals.
In the following, whenever we refer to the precise value of an adjoint without specifying normals, we shall assume that they are normalized to length one.
This is relevant, for example, when we discuss geometric interpretations for the adjoint (see
Section

5.2
and
Appendix

G
).

A most remarkable fact about canonical forms, and core motivation for this article, is that
\Omega
defines a
simple valuation
on convex polyhedra. That it is a
valuation
means that whenever
P,Q,P\cap Q
and
P\cup Q
are convex polyhedra, then

\Omega(P)+\Omega(Q)=\Omega(P\cap Q)+\Omega(P\cup Q).

It is
simple
because it vanishes on polytopes with empty interior.
That dual volumes yield such simple valuations had been observed before (
e.g.
proven by Ludwig
[
16
]
),
but was rediscovered in the context of Positive Geometry by Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam
[
4
, Equation 3.6]
.
For us, the relevant consequence of these properties (which one may as well take as the definition of simple valuation) is the following:

Theorem 2.2
.

Whenever
P_{1},...,P_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
are convex polyhedra (where
\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}
denotes that the
P_{i}
have pair-wise disjoint interior), then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Omega(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})=\Omega(P_{1})+\cdots+\Omega(P_{n}).

In particular, the canonical form (and adjoint) of any polytope or polyhedron can be computed by decomposing it into simplices and simplicial cones, computing
\Omega
on the pieces using
Example

2.1
, and adding up the results according to
(
2.5
)
.

The canonical form behaves furthermore well under restriction to facets.
If
F\subset P
is a facet of
P
, then the canonical form of
F
(considered here as a full-dimensional polytope in the affine span of
F
) can be obtained as the residue of
\Omega(P)
at
F
.
That means, we drop the factor
L_{F}
from the denominator and restrict to
F
:

(2.6)

\Omega^{d-1}(F)=\frac{\adj_{P}|_{F}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\subset P\\
G\not=F\end{subarray}}L_{G}|_{F}}.

We include here superscripts to indicate that
\Omega^{d-1}(F)
is computed within the ambient space
\aff(F)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
​.
This distinction is important since
\Omega^{d}(F)=0
.
We
will usually not include these superscripts when they are clear from context.
Recall also that the adjoint is only defined if
\Int(P)\not=\varnothing
, and so an analogous indication of dimension is not necessary.
Finally, we highlight the subtlety that
(
2.6
)
holds without a scaling factor only because we assume the normal
u_{F}
to be of unit length (since we found our setting to be not quite standard, a proof of this is given in
Appendix

B
).

Finally, for later use we also record here the behavior of
\Omega
under affine transformations.

Lemma 2.3
.

For an invertible linear transformation
S\in\GL(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds

\Omega(SP+t;Sx+t)=|\!\det(S)^{-1}|\,\Omega(P;x).

Proof.

This follows from a straightforward computation:

\displaystyle\Omega(SP+t;Sx+t)

\displaystyle=d!\vol((SP+t)-(Sx+t))^{\circ}

\displaystyle=d!\vol(SP-Sx)^{\circ}

\displaystyle=d!\vol(S(P-x))^{\circ}

\displaystyle=d!\vol(S^{-\top}(P-x)^{\circ})

\displaystyle=d!\,|\!\det(S^{-\top})|\vol(P-x)^{\circ}

\displaystyle=|\!\det(S)^{-1}|\,\Omega(P;x).

We used the well-known fact that
(SP)^{\circ}=S^{-\top}P^{\circ}
.
∎

In order to use
\Omega
for matters of scissors congruence, we would need to identify certain special values or characteristics of
\Omega
that are preserved under composition.
As mentioned before,
\Omega
does not vanish on full-dimensional convex polytopes.
Thus, even though
\Omega(P)=0
is preserved under composition, this yields no interesting insights at this point.
In the next section we shall explore how instead the degree of the adjoint polynomial can serve as a useful characteristic.

3.
Adjoint degrees and degree drop

From here on, let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex
d
-dimensional polytope with
m
facets. It is known that “generically” (or projectively) the degree of the adjoint of a polytope is
precisely

m-d-1
(see

[
22
, Theorem 1]
or also
[
13
]
).
This holds, for example, if
P
is a simplex (
cf.

Example

2.1
).
For general polytopes this
expected degree
is still an upper bound on
\deg(\adj_{P})
, the actual degree might however be much smaller.

Example 3.1
.

If
\square_{d}:=[-1,1]^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional cube, then one can show that

\Omega(\square_{d};x)=\frac{\vol(\square_{d})}{\prod_{i}(1-x_{i})^{2}}.

The expected adjoint degree is
2d-d-1=d-1
, whereas the actual degree is
0
.

This deficiency in degree turns out to be an interesting and well-behaved invariant that we shall call the
adjoint degree drop
of
P
, or just
drop
for short. We write

\drop(P):=\overbrace{(m-d-1)}^{\mathclap{\text{expected degree}}}-\overbrace{\deg(\adj_{P})}^{\mathclap{\text{actual degree}}}\geq 0.

With the degree of a rational function defined as
\deg(p/q):=\deg(p)-\deg(q)
, one can express the drop also as

(3.1)

\drop(P)=-d-1-\deg(\Omega(P)).

Note that the drop, like the adjoint polynomial itself, is defined only for polytopes with non-empty interior. We shall restrict to the affine span if necessary.

The following is an immediate consequence of the valuative nature of the canonical form and motivates the study of the degree drop as a tool for investigating polytope decompositions.

Lemma 3.2
.

\drop(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})\geq\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
.

Proof.

Using
Theorem

2.2
, we have

\deg\Omega(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})\overset{\smash{\text{\ref{res:Omega_valuation}}}}{=}\deg\Big(\sum_{i}\Omega(P_{i})\Big)\leq\max_{i}\deg\Omega(P_{i}),

where the inequality is the usual behavior of rational degrees under addition.
The claim then follows by substituting
(
3.1
)
.
∎

This is already conceptionally similar to
Example

1.1
: like central symmetry, a lower bound on the drop, such as
\drop(P)\geq s
, is a property that a composed polytope inherits from its pieces.
To fully appreciate or make use of this fact one needs to understand what geometric and combinatorial properties of
P
are encoded by the degree drop.
Thus, we consider the following as the central question in the study of adjoint degrees:

Question 3.3
.

What geometric/combinatorial properties characterize polytopes with a particular adjoint degree drop?

Example 3.4
.

According to
Example

3.1
the
d
-dimensional cube has
\drop(\square_{d})=d-1
.
In fact, this holds for every parallelepiped (see
Theorem

3.5

()
‣
3.5
below).
By
Lemma

3.2
every polytope composed from parallelepipeds has drop at least
d-1
.
This includes all zonotopes.

We collect some elementary properties of the adjoint degree drop.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
P,P_{1},...,P_{n}
be polytopes of dimensions
d,d_{1},...,d_{n}\geq 1
.
Then

()

\drop(P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{n})=\sum_{i}\drop(P_{i})+n-1
.

()

if
F\subset P
is a facet, then

\drop(F)\geq\drop(P)-1.

If equality hold, then
P
has a facet
G\not=F
parallel to
F
.
Moreover, if
f\subset P
is a face of dimension
d-k
, then
\drop(f)\geq\drop(P)-k
.

()

\drop(P)\leq d-1
.

()

the degree drop is affinely invariant, that is, if
S

, then

\drop(SP+t)=\drop(P).

()

if
\pi
is the projection onto a
(d-k)

()

\drop(P_{1}+\cdots+P_{n})\geq(d-1)-\sum_{i}(d_{i}-1)+\sum_{i}\drop(P_{i})
, where

denotes the Minkowski sum of polytopes.

()

if
P
is centrally symmetric then

\drop(P)\text{ is }\begin{cases}\text{even}&\text{if $d$ is odd,}\\
\text{odd}&\text{if $d$ is even.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For
()
‣
3.5
set
P:=P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{n}
.
It is a standard exercise to show that
\deg(\adj_{P})

=\sum_{i}\deg(\adj_{P_{i}})
(we included a proof in
Appendix

C
).
We likewise have
d=\sum_{i}d_{P_{i}}
and

(where

and

\displaystyle\drop(P)

\displaystyle=m-d-1-\deg\adj_{P}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}m_{P_{i}}-\sum_{i}d_{P_{i}}-1-\sum_{i}\deg\adj_{P_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(\underbrace{m_{P_{i}}-d_{P_{i}}-1-\deg\adj_{P_{i}}}_{=\,\drop(P_{i})})+n-1.

For
()
‣
3.5
we use the residue formula
(
2.6
)
to obtain

\displaystyle\deg\Omega^{d-1}(F)

\displaystyle=\deg\left(\frac{\adj_{P}(x)|_{F}}{\prod_{G\not=F}L_{G}|_{F}}\right)=\deg(\adj_{P}(x)|_{F})-\deg\Big(\prod_{G\not=F}L_{G}|_{F}\Big)

The product on the right has
m-1
factors.
Each

has degree one, but becomes constant on restriction to

if and only if
G
and

are parallel.
Only one facet can be parallel to

, so this introduces a case distinction:

\displaystyle\deg\Omega^{d-1}(F)

\displaystyle=\deg(\adj_{P}(x)|_{F})-\begin{cases}m-1&\text{if $P$ has no facet parallel to $F$}\\[2.15277pt]
m-2&\text{if $P$ has a facet parallel to $F$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\deg(\adj_{P}(x))-m+2

\displaystyle=(m-d-1-\drop(P))-m+2=-\drop(P)-d+1

Comparing with

(from
(
3.1
)
) results in the claimed inequality.
For equality the case distinction must evaluate to
m-2
, that is, there must be a parallel facet.
The general statement with
f

For
()
‣
3.5

Then

Item
()
‣
3.5

\displaystyle\drop(SP+t)

\displaystyle=-d-1-\deg(\Omega(SP+t;x))

4.
The reduced canonical form
\Omega_{0}
and translation-invariance

A major disadvantage of
\Omega
that makes it unsuited for questions of scissors congruence is that it is not translation-invariant.
In this section we introduce a translation-invariant modification.
In a first step we define the
homogenized canonical form
, and subsequently derive from it the
reduced canonical form

\Omega_{0}
.

4.1.
The homogenized canonical form

There are two equivalent ways to define the
homogenized canonical form
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
for a polytope – geometrically or more algebraically.
We shall mainly work with the latter.

We construct
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
by homogenizing both the numerator and denominator of
\Omega
to their respective “expected degrees”.
More precisely, we homogenize the numerator (which is the adjoint) to degree
m-d-1
, and the denominator to degree
m
(recall that
m
is the number of facets).
Formally, this yields

\displaystyle\Omega(P;x_{0},x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{x_{0}^{m-d-1}\adj(x/x_{0})}{x_{0}^{m}\prod_{F}L_{F}(x/x_{0})}=x_{0}^{-d-1}\,\Omega(P;x/x_{0}).

If
\adj_{P}(x_{0},x):=x_{0}^{m-d-1}\adj_{P}(x/x_{0})
denotes the
homogenized adjoint
, then

(4.1)

\Omega(P;x_{0},x)=\frac{\adj_{P}(x_{0},x)}{\prod_{F}(h_{F}x_{0}-\langle u_{F},x\rangle)}

Observe that
\drop(P)
is the highest power of
x_{0}
that divides
\adj_{P}(x_{0},x)
.

Geometrically, this construction agrees with applying the canonical form to the homogenization of the polytope
P
:

(4.2)

\Omega(P;x_{0},x)=\Omega(P^{\hom};(x_{0},x)),\quad\text{where }P^{\mathrm{hom}}:=\cone(\{1\}\times P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

The equivalence can be shown by triangulating
P
and proving the statement on simplices (
cf.

Example

2.1
). For details, see
Lemma

D.1
in the appendix.

4.2.
The reduced canonical form

The
reduced canonical form
\Omega_{0}
is obtained from
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
by setting
x_{0}=0
:

(4.3)

\Omega_{0}(P;x):=\Omega(P;x_{0},x)|_{x_{0}=0}=(-1)^{m}\frac{\adj_{P}(x_{0},x)|_{x_{0}=0}}{\prod_{F}\langle x,u_{F}\rangle}.

In other words,

\Omega_{0}(P)
is obtained from
\Omega(P)
by restricting both numerator and denominator to the monomials of the expected maximal degree.
In the numerator this means restricting to monomials of degree
m-d-1
, and in the denominator to monomials of degree
m
.

The geometric interpretation of this construction is that
\Omega_{0}(P)
is the restriction of the homogeneous rational function
\Omega(P^{\hom};(x_{0},x))
to the “hyperplane at infinity”
H^{\infty}=\{x_{0}=0\}
.
This restriction clearly preserves the property of being a simple valuation, and so
\Omega_{0}
too is a simple valuation on convex polytopes. Its crucial new property is translation-invariance:

Lemma 4.1
.

\Omega_{0}
is translation-invariant, that is

\Omega_{0}(P+t;x)=\Omega_{0}(P;x),\quad\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.3
we have
\Omega(P+t,x)=\Omega(P;x-t)
.
Observe that substituting
x\mapsto x-t
into a polynomial does not change its monomials of maximal degree.
Since
\Omega_{0}
only retains the expected top degree terms of the numerator and denominator of
\Omega
, it is therefore unaffected by this modification.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In contrast to
\Omega
, the reduced canonical form cannot be extended, as a valuation, to general
unbounded
polyhedra.
The algebraic construction of the homogenized canonical form
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
fails because for unbounded polyhedra the adjoint degree can be higher than
m-d-1
and so cannot be homogenizing to degree
m-d-1
(
e.g.
simplicial cones have adjoint degree
m-d
, see
Example

2.1
).
The geometric construction of
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
does work, but yields an expression with a non-zero power of
x_{0}
in the denominator.
This prevents us from setting
x_{0}=0
, and so
\Omega_{0}
cannot be constructed.

4.3.
Applying
\boldsymbol{\Omega_{0}}

If the adjoint
\adj_{P}
is not of the expected degree
m-d-1
, then restricting to the monomial of this degree yields zero. Hence

Observation 4.3
.

\Omega_{0}(P)=0
if and only if

This gives us a first translation scissors invariant based on adjoint degrees:

\drop(P)=0

Once again, to make use of this fact directly we would need to understand what a drop of zero encodes geometrically or combinatorially (see

The intuition is that drop zero is the “generic case”, though a precise alternative formulation is not available in all cases (for

We can now answer the question raised by

Proof.

That zonotopes have maximal drop

For the converse, assume that

The statement is clear for

5.
Homogeneity

A translation-invariant valuation
\phi

if for all
\lambda>0

\phi(\lambda P)=\lambda^{k}\phi(P).

In this section we prove that
\Omega_{0}
is homogeneous and explore the implications.

Lemma 5.1
.

\Omega_{0}
is 1-homogeneous.

Proof.

The statement is clear if
\Omega_{0}(P)=0
.
Otherwise,
\Omega_{0}(P)
is a homogeneous rational function of degree
-d-1-\drop(P)
.
Using
Corollary

4.11
and

we obtain

\Omega_{0}(\lambda P;x)\,\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\ref{res:Omega0_trafo}}}}{=}\,\lambda^{-d}\Omega_{0}(P;x/\lambda)=\lambda^{-d}\lambda^{d+1}\Omega_{0}(P;x)=\lambda\Omega_{0}(P;x).\qed

Homogeneity gives us access to a variety of results from valuation theory.
For example, 1-homogeneous translation-invariant valuations are
Minkowski additive
(see
[

, Remark 6.3.3]
):

Corollary 5.2
.

\Omega_{0}(P_{1}+\cdots+P_{n})=\Omega_{0}(P_{1})+\cdots+\Omega_{0}(P_{n}).

So far, our repertoire of polytopes with a non-zero drop is rather small.
In particular, in dimension three we are limited to zonotopes.
We can use Minkowski additivity to construct many more examples:

Example 5.3
.

Let
P_{1},...,P_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be convex polytopes with empty interior.
For them
\Omega_{0}(P_{i})=0
, and so
\Omega_{0}(P_{1}+\cdots+P_{n})=0
by Minkowski additivity.

Consider for example the 3-dimensional polytope
P
shown in
Figure

2
(left) which is constructed as the Minkowski sum of two triangles.
Since
P
is not a zonotope, it is our first 3-dimensional example for which we can deduce
\drop(P)=1
.
Interestingly, this

can also be obtained as “half of a zonotope”​ by cutting the latter with a central hyperplane (see

This will later give us a second proof for
\Omega_{0}(P)=0
(see

).

The polytope on the right is a zonotope with a plane that dissects it into two identical halves, both of which are isometric to the polytope on the left.

5.1.
A decomposition formula for
\boldsymbol{\Omega_{0}}

The following is a deep theorem due to McMullen (see
[
20
, Theorem 6.4.7]
): a valuation

\phi
on convex polytopes
is simultaneously translation-invariant, 1-homogeneous and weakly continuous if and only if it permits a decomposition

(5.1)

\phi(P)=\sum_{e\subset P}\ell_{e}\psi(N_{P}(e)),

where the sum is over the edges
e
of
P
,
\ell_{e}
is the length of the edge
e
,
\psi
is a valuation defined on
(d-1)
-dimensional cones, and
N_{P}(e)
is the normal cone at
e
.
Weak continuity
means that
\phi
is continuous under translation of facet hyperplanes.

To apply McMullen’s result to
\Omega_{0}
we would first need to verify weak continuity, and we would get no information for what valuation
\psi
to chose.
Instead we managed to prove a decomposition as in
(
5.1
)
independently.

Theorem 5.4
.

For a convex polytopes
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds

(5.2)

\Omega_{0}(P;x)=-\frac{1}{\|x\|^{2}}\sum_{e\subset P}\ell_{e}\,\Omega^{d-1}(T_{e};\pi_{e}x),

where the sum is over the edges
e
of
P
,
\ell_{e}
is the length of edge
e
,

\pi_{e}
is the orthogonal projection onto
e^{\bot}
, and
T_{e}:=\pi_{e}T_{P}(e)\subset e^{\bot}
is the projected tangent cone at
e
.

We note that
Theorem

5.4
uses the projected tangent cone
\pi_{e}T_{P}(e)
as opposed to the normal cone
N_{P}(e)
as in
(
5.1
)
.
The theorem can nevertheless be understood as an instance of
(
5.1
)
because
N_{P}(e)
and
T_{P}(e)
are related through polar duality, and valuations on cones can be interpreted as valuations on their polar duals.

Alternatively, we can express
(
5.2
)
as a polynomial identity in terms of adjoints by substituting in
(
2.2
)
and
(
4.3
)
.
The canonical form of
T_{e}
can be written as

(5.3)

\Omega^{d-1}(T_{e};\pi_{e}x)=(-1)^{m_{e}}\frac{\adj_{T_{e}}(\pi_{e}x)}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
F\supset e\end{subarray}}\langle x,u_{F}\rangle},

where
m_{e}
is the number of facets of
T_{e}
, and we used
\langle\pi_{e}x,u_{F}\rangle=\langle x,u_{F}\rangle
.
Multiplying

(

)
through with

and
\prod_{i}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle
we arrive at a polynomial identity equivalent to
Theorem

5.4
:

Corollary 5.5
.

For a convex polytopes
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds

\adj_{P}^{0}(x)\cdot\|x\|^{2}=-\sum_{e\subset P}(-1)^{m-m_{e}}\ell_{e}\adj_{T_{e}}\!(\pi_{e}x)\!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
F\not\supset e\end{subarray}}\!\langle x,u_{F}\rangle,

where
\adj_{P}^{0}(x):=\adj_{P}(x_{0},x)|_{x_{0}=0}
is the homogenized adjoint evaluated at
x_{0}=0
.

To prove
Theorem

5.4
we can employ a standard strategy from valuation theory: since both sides of

)
are valuative, we can decompose the polytope into simpler pieces (typically simplices) and prove the statement only on these simple pieces.
It does however turn out that already on simplices

is a rather non-trivial statement that is interesting in its own right.
We therefore first explore the simplex case in

, and postpone a proof to

.

5.2.
Theorem

5.4
for simplices

For a

-dimensional simplex

the adjoint is of expected and actual degree
m-d-1=(d+1)-d-1=0
.
That is,
\adj_{\Delta}
is a number and is invariant under restriction to monomials of expected maximal degree.
Likewise, tangent cones at edges of
\Delta
are simplicial, and their adjoints too are just numbers.
Lastly, each edge lies in all but two facets.
If we denote by
e_{ij}
the edge that does
not
lie in facets
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
, then

Corollary

5.5
simplifies to

(5.4)

\adj_{\Delta}\|x\|^{2}=-\sum_{i<j}\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\langle x,u_{j}\rangle,

where
\ell_{ij}
and
T_{ij}
are the length and projected tangent cone at
e_{ij}
respectively. This is a curious identity:
on the right hand side we construct a quadratic form by summing over the edges of a simplex; but the left hand side shows that this form reduces to a multiple of
\|x\|^{2}
.
Alternatively, this can be expressed as a matrix identity:

\adj_{\Delta}\Id_{d}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i<j}\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}(u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}\!+u_{j}u_{i}^{\top}).

Our next goal is to rewrite
(
5.4
)
more explicitly by substituting in the known expressions for adjoints of simplices and simplicial cones (
cf.

Example

2.1
). For convenience (and later generalization) we shall assume that
u_{0},...,u_{d}
form a positive affine basis,
i.e.,
the determinant in
(
2.3
)
is non-negative and the absolute value can be dropped:

(5.5)

\adj_{\Delta}=\det\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\cdots&u_{d}\\
|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
h_{0}&\cdots&h_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

For the simplicial cone
T_{ij}

\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we cannot use
(
2.4
)
right away, but need to adjust for the fact that
T_{ij}
is of dimension
d-1
.
To express
\smash{\adj_{T_{ij}}}
as a
d\times d
-determinant as in
(
2.4
)
, we have to introduce an additional column that is orthogonal to the others.
For this we can use the (normalized) edge direction
\vec{e}_{ij}
, oriented from
i
to
j
. Conveniently, this allows us to also include the edge length into the determinantal expression.
More precisely, if
\Delta
has vertices
v_{0},...,v_{d}
(with
v_{i}
being opposite to the facet with normal
u_{i}
), and
\hat{u}_{i}
denotes the absence of
u_{i}
from the list
u_{0},...,u_{d}
, then we can write

\displaystyle\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}\,

\displaystyle\equiv\,\ell_{ij}\cdot\det\!\begin{pmatrix}&|&&|&&|\\
\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!&\vec{e}_{ij}\\
&|&&|&&|\end{pmatrix}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\,\det\!\begin{pmatrix}&|&&|&&|\\
\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!&v_{j}-v_{i}\\
&|&&|&&|\end{pmatrix}

where
\equiv
denotes identity up to sign.
Tracking the signs in these identities is somewhat cumbersome.
In the following we shall instead use the following more elegant determinantal expression, which is equivalent
including signs
:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}=-\det\!\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}|&&|&&|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&v_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&v_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|&&|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
0&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&1&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&1&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&0\end{pmatrix}}_{=:D_{ij}}.

Here,
D_{ij}
is the
(d+1)\times(d+1)
-matrix whose columns are
(u_{k},0)^{\top}\!
for
k\not\in\{i,j\}
, and
(v_{j},1)^{\top}\!
otherwise.
The equivalence of these expressions is shown in
Lemma

E.1
.

Substituting
(
5.5
)
and
(
5.7
)
into
(
5.4
)
we obtain a curious identity for quadratic forms involving determinants:

Theorem 5.6
.

If
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
d
-dimensional simplex with normal vectors
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, heights
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and vertices
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

(5.8)

\det\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
h_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&h_{d}\end{pmatrix}\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{i<j}\det\begin{pmatrix}|&&|&&|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&v_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&v_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|&&|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
0&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&1&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&1&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&0\end{pmatrix}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\langle x,u_{j}\rangle.

In
Theorem

5.6
we do no longer need that the
u_{i}
are normalized.
This is because both sides are linear in
(u_{i},h_{i})^{\top}\!
. We also do not need to assume a specific order for the indices to guarantee positive determinants.
This is because both sides flip signs under a swap of indices.
So far our only proof of
Theorem

5.6
relies on the valuation properties of
\Omega_{0}
(see
Section

5.3
).
Due to its elementary statement, a purely matrix-theoretic proof would be desirable.

We also remark on special case
d=2
, that is,
\Delta
is a triangle.
Here the projected edge tangent cones
T_{ij}
are 1-dimensional and so
\adj_{T_{ij}}=1
.
Therefore
Theorem

5.6
further simplifies to

(5.9)

\adj_{\Delta}\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{i<j}\ell_{ij}\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\langle x,u_{j}\rangle.

Note that even though the entries
h_{i}
of
\adj_{\Delta}
depend on the translation of
\Delta
, the adjoint itself does not.
The adjoint therefore expresses some geometric property of
\Delta
.
In dimension
d=2
we identified the identity

\adj_{\Delta}=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)}{\operatorname{Circumradius}(\Delta)}.

A proof is given in
Appendix

G
.
We are not aware of an analogously geometric interpretation in dimensions
d\geq 3
.

Question 5.7
.

What does
\adj_{\Delta}
express geometrically if
d\geq 3
?

5.3.
Proof of
Theorem

5.4

As discussed previously, we prove
Theorem

5.4
by decomposing
P
into simpler polytopes.
Since the statement is already complicated on simplices (see
Theorem

5.6
), we need to choose even simpler pieces.

An
ortho-simplex
(also known as an
orthoscheme
) is a simplex
\Delta
(or any simplex isometric to it) whose vertices are of the form

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle=(0,0,0,...,0),

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{1},0,0,...,0),

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},0,...,0),

\displaystyle v_{3}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},...,0),

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle v_{d}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},...,\ell_{d}),

for some positive numbers
\ell_{1},...,\ell_{d}>0
(see
Figure

3
).

Figure 3.
Ortho-simplices of dimension two and three.

Crucially, every polytope
P
has an
ortho-simplex decomposition
, that is, it can be written as a
signed
sum of ortho-simplices.
This is standard, but we give a quick description of the decomposition.
First, fix a point
x_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For each face
F\subset P
let
x_{F}
be the orthogonal projection of
x_{P}
onto
\aff(F)
.
Then for each flag
\mathcal{F}=\{P\supset F_{d-1}\supset\cdots\supset F_{1}\supset F_{0}\}
the convex hull of
\{x_{F_{i}}\mid F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}\}
is an ortho-simplex
\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}
(see
Figure

4
).
Let
u_{i}\in\aff(F_{i})
be the outwards pointing normal vector to
F_{i-1}
considered as a facet of
F_{i}
and let
k(\mathcal{F})
count the number of
i\in\{1,...,d\}
for which
\langle x_{F_{i-1}}-x_{F_{i}},u_{i}\rangle<0
.
One can show that

\phi(P)=\sum_{\mathcal{F}}(-1)^{k(\mathcal{F})}\phi(\Delta_{\mathcal{F}}),

whenever
\phi
is a simple valuation, such as
\Omega
or
\Omega_{0}
.
The goal here is to prove
Theorem

5.6
for ortho-simplices, which gives us the full statement by the above ortho-simplex decomposition.

Figure 4.

An ortho-simplex decomposition of a quadrangle.
Left: a positively signed ortho-simplex in the decomposition.
Right: a negatively signed ortho-simplex in the decomposition.

First, one verifies that the (non-normalized) normal vectors of an ortho-simplex
\Delta
are given by

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{0},0,0,...,0,0),

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=(-\ell_{2},\ell_{1},0,...,0,0),

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=(0,-\ell_{3},\ell_{2},...,0,0),

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=(0,0,-\ell_{4},\ell_{3},...,0,0),

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle u_{d-1}

\displaystyle=(0,0,0,...,-\ell_{d},\ell_{d-1}),

\displaystyle u_{d}

\displaystyle=(0,0,0,...,0,-\ell_{d+1}),

where
u_{i}
is the normal of facet
F_{i}
opposite to vertex
v_{i}
. We introduced additional parameters
\ell_{0},\ell_{d+1}>0
for notational convenience; one might set
\ell_{0}=\ell_{d+1}=1
.

The height of each facet over the origin is
0
, except for the height of
F_{0}
, which is
\ell_{0}\ell_{1}
.
Following
(
5.5
)
we can express the adjoint of
\Delta
as

\displaystyle\adj_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}|&\!\!\!\!|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}&\!\!\!\!u_{1}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&\!\!\!\!|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
\ell_{0}\ell_{1}&\!\!\!\!0&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&0\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{d+1}\ell_{0}\ell_{1}\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{1}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&u_{d}\\
|&&|\\[4.30554pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d+1}\ell_{0}\ell_{1}\cdot\det\left(\;\begin{matrix}-\ell_{2}\\
\phantom{+}\ell_{1}&\mathllap{-}\ell_{3}\\[-5.16663pt]
&\ell_{2}&\!\!\!\ddots\!\!\!\!\\[-5.16663pt]
&&\!\!\!\ddots\!\!\!\!&-\ell_{d}\\
&&&\ell_{d-1}&\,\mathllap{-}\ell_{d+1}\end{matrix}\;\right)=\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{d+1}.

A likewise elementary but unlike more involved computation yields an expression for
\det(D_{ij})=\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}
as defined in
(
5.7
)
:
for
i<j
we obtain

\det(D_{ij})=\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}\ell_{j+1}}\cdot\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{d+1}.

The details of this computation can be found in
Appendix

F
.

Lastly, we observe
\langle x,u_{i}\rangle=\ell_{i}x_{i+1}-\ell_{i+1}x_{i}
, where we set
x_{0}=x_{d+1}=0
. After substituting all of the above expressions into
(
5.4
)
and canceling
\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{d+1}
on both sides, we are left with the following identity that we need to verify:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{d}\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}\ell_{j+1}}(\ell_{i}x_{i+1}-x_{i+1}x_{i})(\ell_{j}x_{j+1}-x_{j+1}x_{j})

\displaystyle=-\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{d}(\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2})\Big(\frac{x_{i+1}}{\ell_{i+1}}-\frac{x_{i}}{\ell_{i}}\Big)\Big(\frac{x_{j+1}}{\ell_{j+1}}-\frac{x_{j}}{\ell_{j}}\Big).

We can slightly simplify by substituting
y_{k}:=x_{k}/\ell_{k}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\ell_{k}^{2}y_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{d}(\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2})(y_{i+1}-y_{i})(y_{j+1}-y_{j}).

This identity can now be verified by sorting the terms on the right side according to
\ell_{k}^{2}
, and recognizing two telescoping sums:

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i<j\end{subarray}}^{d}(\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2})(y_{i+1}-y_{i})(y_{j+1}-y_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\!\sum_{k=1}^{d}\ell_{k}^{2}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=0\\
i<k\leq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\!\!(y_{i+1}-y_{i})(y_{j+1}-y_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=-\!\sum_{k=1}^{d}\ell_{k}^{2}\!\!\underbrace{\sum_{0\leq i<k}\!\!(y_{i+1}-y_{i})}_{=\,y_{k}-y_{0}\,=\,y_{k}}\underbrace{\sum_{k\leq j\leq d}\!\!(y_{j+1}-y_{j})}_{=\,y_{d+1}-y_{k}\,=\,-y_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\ell_{k}^{2}y_{k}^{2}.

This finishes the proof of
(
5.4
)
for ortho-simplices, and hence also the proof of
Theorem

5.4
.

6.
Central inversion

In this section we consider the behavior of
\Omega_{0}
under central inversion
P\mapsto-P
.

Lemma 6.1
.

\Omega_{0}(-P)=(-1)^{d+1}\Omega_{0}(P)
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to
Lemma

5.1
.
The statement is clear if
\Omega_{0}(P)=0
.
Otherwise,
\Omega_{0}(P)
is a homogeneous rational function of degree
-d-1
.
Using
Corollary

4.11
we obtain

\Omega_{0}(-P;x)\,\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\ref{res:Omega0_trafo}}}}{=}\,\Omega_{0}(P;-x)=(-1)^{-d-1}\,\Omega_{0}(P;x).\qed

As previously advertised in
Example

5.3
, we can now confirm that indeed “half of a centrally symmetric polytope” has a drop in odd dimensions:

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
d
be odd and let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a centrally-symmetric polytope with
\drop(P)>0
.
Let
H
be a central hyperplane (i.e., it is passing through the symmetry center of
P
) with halfspaces
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
.
Then

\drop(P\cap H_{+})=\drop(P\cap H_{-})>0.

Proof.

Set
P_{\pm}:=P\cap H_{\pm}
.
Since
d
is odd,
Lemma

6.1
yields
\Omega_{0}(P_{-})=\Omega_{0}(-P_{+})=\Omega_{0}(P_{+})
. Using
Corollary

5.2
we obtain

0=\Omega_{0}(P)=\Omega_{0}(P_{+}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{-})\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\ref{res:Minkowski_additivity}}}}{=}\Omega_{0}(P_{+})+\Omega_{0}(P_{-})=2\Omega_{0}(P_{+}),

and analogously for
P_{-}
.
∎

Figure 5.
A selection of halves of zonotopes in dimension
d=3
, which have a non-zero drop by
Corollary

6.2
. Left: halves of the rhombic dodecahedron. Middle: halves of the cube. Right: halves of the permutahedron.

Corollary 6.3
.

If
Z=Z_{+}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}Z_{-}
is a central dissection of a 3-dimensional zonotope into two halves (as in
Corollary

6.2
), then
Z_{+}+Z_{-}
is a zonotope.

Proof.

Z_{+}+Z_{-}
is centrally symmetric, hence
\drop(Z_{+}+Z_{-})\in\{0,2\}
according to
Theorem

3.5

()
‣
3.5
.
Using
Corollary

6.2
we have
\Omega_{0}(Z_{+}+Z_{-})=\Omega_{0}(Z_{+})+\Omega_{0}(Z_{-})=0
.
Hence
\drop(Z_{+}+Z_{-})=2
, and the claim follows from
Theorem

4.5
.
∎

There are other ways to arrive at
Corollary

6.3
.
For example, one can show that
Z_{+}+Z_{-}=Z+Z_{0}
, where
Z_{0}=Z\cap H
is the section of
Z
with the central dissecting hyperplane
H
.
Since for
d=3
the section
Z_{0}
is a centrally symmetric polygon, it is also a zonotope, and so
Z+Z_{0}
must be a zonotope as well.
This also shows that an analogue of
Corollary

6.3
in higher dimensions will fail: in
d\geq 4
the central section
Z_{0}
of a zonotope needs not be a zonotope, but
Z_{0}
will appear as a face of
Z_{+}+Z_{-}
.

We are now ready to prove a 3-dimensional analogue of
Corollary

4.6
that provides a geometric interpretation for each value of the degree drop:

Theorem 6.4
.

If
P
is a 3-dimensional polytope, then

\drop(P)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $P+(-P)$ is \lx@text@underline{\smash{not}} a zonotope,}\\
1&\text{if $P+(-P)$ is a zonotope, but $P$ itself is \lx@text@underline{\smash{not}},}\\
2&\text{if $P$ is a zonotope.}\end{cases}.

Note that
P+(-P)
is a zonotope precisely if for each facet
F\subset P
and opposite face
F^{\prime}\subset P
(
i.e.,

F
and
F^{\prime}
are touched by parallel hyperplanes),
F+(-F^{\prime})
is itself a zonotope. For
d=3
the latter means checking whether
F+(-F^{\prime})
is centrally symmetric, which is easy to do.
Hence,
Theorem

6.4
provides a practical geometric way to determine the drop in dimension three.

Proof of
Theorem

6.4
.

Using Minkowski additivity and central inversion, we have

\Omega(P+(-P))\,\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\ref{res:Minkowski_additivity}}}}{=}\,\Omega(P)+\Omega(-P)\,\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\ref{res:central_inversion}}}}{=}\,2\Omega(P).

That is,
\drop(P+(-P))=0
if and only if
\drop(P)=0
.
Note also that
P+(-P)
is centrally symmetric, hence by
Theorem

3.5

()
‣
3.5
its drop is
0
or
2
.
So

\displaystyle\drop(P)=0\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\drop(P+(-P))=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\drop(P+(-P))\not=2\;\overset{\smash{\ref{res:zonotopes_iff_drop_d_1}}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\;\text{$P+(-P)$ is not a zonotope}.

The equivalence “
\drop(P)=2

\Longleftrightarrow

P
is a zonotope” is precisely
Theorem

4.5
.
The remaining case
\drop(P)=1
is necessarily capturing what remains, that is, that
P
is not zonotope, while
P+(-P)
is a zonotope.
∎

7.
Valuations for higher degree drops

Over the course of the last sections we established the reduced canonical form
\Omega_{0}
as a helpful tool in the study of degree drops. Its major drawback is that it cannot quantify this drop.
The goal of this section is to introduce and investigate a new family of valuations
\Omega_{s},s\geq 0
, where
\Omega_{s}
can testify a degree drop of
s
or more.

From
(
4.1
)
we see that the degree drop of
P
is the order of vanishing of
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
at a generic point of the form
(0,x)
. Indeed, since the denominator of
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
is not divisible by
x_{0}
we may expand
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
as a Taylor series at
(0,x)
:

(7.1)

\Omega(P;x_{0},x)=\sum_{s\geq 0}\Omega_{s}(P;x)x_{0}^{s},

which shall serve as a definition of
\Omega_{s}
as a function mapping convex polytopes to
\mathbb{R}(x)
.
This notation is consistent with our previous definition of
\Omega_{0}
as
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
evaluated at
x_{0}=0
.
As it turns out, all
\Omega_{s}
together completely describe the degree drop of
P
. We obtain the following generalization of
4.3
:

Theorem 7.1
.

\Omega_{s}(P)=0
for all
s\leq s_{0}
if and only if
\drop(P)>s_{0}
.

Proof.

We assume
s_{0}\geq 0
.
We can write the homogenized adjoint as a polynomial in
x_{0}
with coefficients
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
. Then

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{k}a_{k}(x)x_{0}^{k}=\adj_{P}(x_{0},x)&\,=\;\prod_{F}(h_{F}x_{0}-\langle x,u_{F}\rangle)\cdot\Omega(P;x_{0},x)\\
&\,\overset{\mathclap{\smash{\text{\hyperref@@ii[eq:omegasdef]{{{(\ignorespaces\ref*{eq:omegasdef}\/)}}}}}}}{=}\;\prod_{F}(h_{F}x_{0}-\langle x,u_{F}\rangle)\cdot\sum_{s\geq 0}\Omega_{s}(P;x)x_{0}^{s}\end{split}

The proof now proceeds by comparing coefficients on both sides.

Suppose first that
\drop(P)>s_{0}
. This is, by definition, equivalent to
a_{k}\equiv 0
, for all
0\leq k\leq s_{0}
.
Hence, the corresponding coefficients of
x_{0}^{s}
on the right hand side of
(
7.2
)
must vanish as well.
For
s=0
, this coefficient is
\prod_{F}(-\langle x,u_{F}\rangle)\Omega_{0}(P)
, that is,
\Omega_{0}(P)=0
.
Proceeding by induction on
s
, we see that for each
s\leq s_{0}
the coefficient is
\prod_{F}(-\langle x,u_{F}\rangle)\Omega_{s}(P)
.
Thus,
\Omega_{s}(P)=0
whenever
0\leq s\leq s_{0}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\Omega_{s}(P)=0
for all
0\leq s<s_{0}
.
Then performing the previous argument backwards, the corresponding coefficients of
x_{0}^{s}
on the right hand side of
(
7.2
)
vanish, and we conclude
a_{k}\equiv 0
for all
0\leq k\leq s_{0}
.
∎

Next, we observe that
\Omega_{s}
is a valuation:
in view of
(
7.1
)
we have

\Omega_{s}(P)=\frac{1}{s!}\left[\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial x_{0}^{s}}\Omega(P;x_{0},x)\right]_{x_{0}=0},

so
\Omega_{s}
arises as the composition of the valuation
P\mapsto\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
with the linear map
f\mapsto\tfrac{1}{s!}[\tfrac{\partial^{s}}{\partial x_{0}^{s}}f]_{x_{0}=0}
and thus inherits the valuation property. For the same reason,
\Omega_{s}
is a
simple
valuation,
i.e.,
it vanishes if
P
has empty interior.
It moreover exhibits the same linear equivariance as
\Omega_{0}
(
cf.

Corollary

4.11
):

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex polytope and let
S\in\GL(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We have

\Omega_{s}(SP,Sx)=|\det(P)|^{-1}\,\Omega_{s}(P;x).

Proof.

The proof is analogous to
Corollary

4.11
, except that in the last step we do not set
x_{0}=0
, but expand both sides at
x_{0}=0
and compare coefficients.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

For any convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the rational function
x\mapsto\Omega_{s}(P;x)
is homogeneous of degree
-(d+1+s)
.

Proof.

Using the geometric definition of
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
from
(
4.2
)
together with the linear equivariance
Lemma

2.3
, we find

\begin{split}\smash{\sum_{s\geq 0}}\Omega_{s}(P;\lambda x)x_{0}^{s}&=\Omega(P^{\hom};(x_{0},\lambda x))=\lambda^{-d-1}\,\Omega(\lambda^{-1}P^{\hom};(\lambda^{-1}x_{0},x))\\
&=\lambda^{-d-1}\Omega(P^{\hom};(\lambda^{-1}x_{0},x))=\lambda^{-d-1}\smash{\sum_{s\geq 0}}\Omega_{s}(P;x)\lambda^{-s}x_{0}^{s}.\end{split}

Comparing coefficients concludes the proof.
∎

This provides a generalization of
Lemma

5.1
:

Corollary 7.4
.

\Omega_{s}
is
(s+1)
-homogeneous (in the sense of
Section

5
).

Proof.

Combining
Lemmas

7.2
and
7.3
we obtain

\Omega_{s}(\lambda P;x)=\lambda^{-d}\,\Omega_{s}(P;\lambda^{-1}x)=\lambda^{-d}\lambda^{d+1+s}\,\Omega_{s}(P;x).\qed

A shortcoming of the
\Omega_{s},s>0
as compared to
\Omega_{0}
is that they are not translation-invariant.
Nevertheless some statements about their behavior under translation can be made.

Theorem 7.5
.

For any convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function

\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}(x),\quad t\mapsto\Omega_{s}(P+t;x)

is a polynomial of degree at most
s
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}(x)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the statement for a simplex
\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\leq h_{i}\}
,
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and then use the valuation property. In this case, the adjoint of
\Delta
is a constant. We have

\begin{split}\Omega(\Delta;x_{0},x)&=\frac{\adj_{\Delta}}{\prod_{i}(h_{i}x_{0}-\langle x,u_{i}\rangle)}=\frac{\adj_{\Delta}}{\prod_{i}(-\langle x,u_{i}\rangle)}\cdot\frac{1}{\prod_{i}\big(1-\tfrac{h_{i}}{\langle x,u_{i}\rangle}x_{0}\big)}\\
&=\Omega_{0}(\Delta;x)\prod_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{s\geq 0}\left(\frac{h_{i}}{\langle x,u_{i}\rangle}\right)^{s}\!x_{0}^{s}\\
&=\Omega_{0}(\Delta;x)\sum_{s\geq 0}\left[\sum_{s_{0}+\cdots+s_{d}=s}\,\prod_{i=0}^{d}\left(\frac{h_{i}}{\langle x,u_{i}\rangle}\right)^{s_{i}}\right]\!x_{0}^{s}.\end{split}

Comparing coefficients yields,

\Omega_{s}(\Delta;x)=\Omega_{0}(\Delta;x)\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{s_{0}+\cdots+s_{d}=s}\,\prod_{i=0}^{d}\left(\frac{h_{i}}{\langle x,u_{i}\rangle}\right)^{s_{i}}\!\!\!.

Since
\Delta+t=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle x,u_{i}\rangle\leq h_{i}+\langle u_{i},t\rangle\}
we obtain

\Omega_{s}(\Delta+t;x)=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{s_{0}+\cdots+s_{d}=s}\,\prod_{i=0}^{d}\left(\frac{h_{i}+\langle t,u_{i}\rangle}{\langle x,u_{i}\rangle}\right)^{s_{i}}

which is a polynomial in
t
.
∎

Valuations that exhibit a polynomial behavior under translation have been studied in great detail by Khovanskiĭ and Pukhlikov
[
19
]
, see also
[
18
]
. They appear naturally in the context of tensor valuations
[
1
,
6
,
12
,
17
]
.

Finally, we show that
\Omega_{s}
exhibits translation-invariance at least on polytopes of a sufficiently high degree drop:

Theorem 7.6
.

If
\drop(P)\geq s
, then
\Omega_{s}(P+t)=\Omega_{s}(P)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
A_{t}\in\GL(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
be the linear transformation given by
(x_{0},x)\mapsto(x_{0},x+x_{0}t)
. Clearly
\det A_{t}=1
and
A_{t}^{-1}=A_{-t}
. Moreover, we have
(P+t)^{\hom}=A_{t}P^{\hom}
.
Using the geometric definition
(
4.2
)
of
\Omega(P;x_{0},x)
, as well as
Lemma

2.3
, we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{r\geq 0}\Omega_{r}(P+t;x)x_{0}^{r}&=\Omega((P+t)^{\hom};(x_{0},x))\\[-9.90276pt]
&=\Omega(P^{\hom};(x_{0},x-x_{0}t))=\sum_{s\geq 0}\Omega_{r}(P;x-x_{0}t)x_{0}^{r}.\end{split}

Since
\drop(P)\geq s
,
Theorem

7.1
yields
\Omega_{r}(P)=0
for all
r<s
.
We can then divide both sides of the above identity by
x_{0}^{s}
to arrive at

\sum_{r\geq s}\Omega_{r}(P+t;x)x_{0}^{r-s}=\sum_{r\geq s}\Omega_{r}(P;x-x_{0}t)x_{0}^{r-s}.

Setting
x_{0}=0
yields the claim.
∎

We close this section with an application to polytope decompositions.
Recall that by
Lemma

3.2
we have
\drop(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})\geq\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
.
We can now make a statement about the equality case: if for given pieces
P_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
it is possible to built at least one polytope whose drop attains the lower bound
\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
, then all polytopes built from these pieces will attain the bound.

Lemma 7.7
.

Given
P=P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n}
and
Q=(P_{1}+t_{1})\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}(P_{n}+t_{n})
, then

\drop(P)=\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
if and only if
\drop(Q)=\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
.

Proof.

Assume
s:=\drop(P)=\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})
.
By
Theorem

7.1
we have
\Omega_{s}(P)\not=0
, and by
Theorem

7.6
we have that
\Omega_{s}
is translation-invariant on the
P_{i}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\Omega_{s}(Q)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{s}((P_{1}+t_{1})\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}(P_{n}+t_{n}))

\displaystyle=\Omega_{s}(P_{1}+t_{1})+\cdots+\Omega_{s}(P_{n}+t_{n})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{s}(P_{1})+\cdots+\Omega_{s}(P_{n})=\Omega_{s}(P_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}P_{n})=\Omega_{s}(P)\not=0

By
Theorem

7.1
we can conclude
\drop(Q)\leq s
.
But by
Lemma

3.2
we also have
\drop(Q)\geq\min_{i}\drop(P_{i})\geq s
, and hence
\drop(Q)=s
.
∎

One can read this as follows: the drop is a translation scissors invariant as long as we only decompose into pieces of sufficiently high drop.

Appendix A
Computing
\Omega
for simplices and simplicial cones

In this section we explicitly compute the formulae for the canonical forms of simplicial cones and simplices as presented in
Example

2.1
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
C:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle u_{i},y\rangle\leq 0\}
be a simplicial cone with facet normals
u_{1},...,u_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Its canonical form is given by

\Omega(C;x)=(-1)^{d}\frac{|\det(u_{1},...,u_{d})|}{\prod_{i}\langle u_{i},x\rangle}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Int(P)
, then
(C-x)^{\circ}
is bounded. In this case we have

C-x=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle u_{i},y\rangle\leq-\langle u_{i},x\rangle\big\}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\middle|\,\left\langle\frac{u_{i}}{-\langle u_{i},x\rangle},y\right\rangle\leq 1\right\}.

Its dual
(C-x)^{\circ}=\conv(\{0\}\cup\{u_{i}/(-\langle u_{i},x\rangle)\mid 1\leq i\leq d\})
is a simplex, the volume of which we can express via a determinant:

\Omega(C;x)=d!\vol(C-x)^{\circ}=\left|\,\det\!\left(\frac{u_{i}}{-\langle u_{i},x\rangle}\,\middle|\,1\leq i\leq d\right)\right|=\frac{|\det(u_{1},...,u_{d})|}{\prod_{i}(-\langle u_{i},x\rangle)}.

Note that in the last step we once again used
x\in\Int(C)
, which guarantees that the factors
-\langle u_{i},x\rangle
are positive and can be pulled out of the absolute value. ∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\Delta:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle u_{i},x\rangle\leq h_{i}\}
be a simplex with facet normals
u_{0},...,u_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and facet heights
h_{0},...,h_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
. Its canonical form is given by

\Omega(\Delta;x)=\frac{\left|\det\!\left(\!\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
h_{i}\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,0\leq i\leq d\right)\right|}{\prod_{i}(h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle)}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Int(\Delta)
.
Similar to the proof of
Proposition

A.1
we have

(\Delta-x)^{\circ}=\conv\left\{\frac{u_{i}}{h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle}\,\middle|\,0\leq i\leq d\right\}.

A convenient way to compute the volume of the dual simplex is by computing the volume of a pyramid of height 1 with
(\Delta-x)^{\circ}
as a base.
More precisely, let
\overline{T}:=\conv(\{0\}\cup(\{1\}\times(\Delta-x)^{\circ})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Then,
\vol\overline{T}=\tfrac{1}{d+1}\vol(\Delta-x)^{\circ}
and we obtain

\begin{split}\Omega(\Delta;x)&=d!\vol(\Delta-x)^{\circ}=(d+1)!\vol(\overline{T})=\left|\det\!\left(\!\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{u_{i}}{h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,0\leq i\leq d\right)\right|\\
&=\frac{\left|\det\!\left(\!\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,0\leq i\leq d\right)\right|}{\prod_{i}(h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle)}=\frac{\left|\det\!\left(\!\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
h_{i}\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,0\leq i\leq d\right)\right|}{\prod_{i}(h_{i}-\langle u_{i},x\rangle)}.\end{split}

The last equality is obtained by a row operation:
if the first
d
rows of the matrix are labeled
r_{1},...,r_{d}
, we obtain the final expression by adding
x_{i}r_{i}
to the last row.
∎

Appendix C
Adjoint degrees for polytope products

The following is needed in the proof of
Theorem

3.5

()
‣
3.5
: given polytopes
P_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
P_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, it holds

\deg(\adj_{P_{1}\times P_{2}})=\deg(\adj_{P_{1}})+\deg(\adj_{P_{2}}).

To show this, we first compute the canonical form of
P_{1}\times P_{2}
.
Since this
involves com
puting the volume of the dual of
P_{1}\times P_{2}
, we recall the following facts:

(P_{1}\times P_{2})^{\circ}=P_{1}^{\circ}\oplus P_{2}^{\circ}:=\conv\big((P_{1}^{\circ}\times\{0\}^{d_{2}})\cup(\{0\}^{d_{1}}\times P_{2}^{\circ})\big)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}.

where
\oplus
denotes the
direct sum
of convex polytopes. It is also well-known that

\vol(P_{1}\oplus P_{2})=\frac{d_{1}!d_{2}!}{(d_{1}+d_{2})!}\cdot\vol(P_{1})\vol(P_{2}).

If
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
, then

\displaystyle\Omega(P_{1}\times P_{2};(x_{1},x_{2}))

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+d_{2})!\cdot\vol((P_{1}\times P_{2})-(x_{1},x_{2}))^{\circ}

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+d_{2})!\cdot\vol((P_{1}-x_{1})\times(P_{2}-x_{2}))^{\circ}

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+d_{2})!\cdot\vol((P_{1}-x_{1})^{\circ}\oplus(P_{2}-x_{2})^{\circ})

\displaystyle=d_{1}!d_{2}!\cdot\vol(P_{1}-x_{1})^{\circ}\vol(P_{2}-x_{2})^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\Omega(P_{1};x_{1})\,\Omega(P_{2};x_{2}).

Recall that if
F_{1}\subset P_{1}
is a facet of
P_{1}
with linear form
L_{F_{1}}(x_{1}):=h_{F_{1}}-\langle x_{1},u_{F_{1}}\rangle
, then
F:=F_{1}\times P_{2}
is a facet of
P_{1}\times P_{2}
which has the essentially same linear form
L_{F}(x_{1},x_{2}):=h_{F_{1}}-\langle(x_{1},0),(u_{F_{1}},0)\rangle=L_{F_{1}}(x_{1})
. Analogous statements hold if we start from a facet
F_{2}\subset P_{2}
.
Moreover, all facets of
P_{1}\times P_{2}
are of this form.
Hence

\displaystyle\frac{\adj_{P_{1}\times P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\prod_{F}L_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})}

\displaystyle=\Omega(P_{1}\times P_{2};(x_{1},x_{2}))=\Omega(P_{1};x_{1})\,\Omega(P_{2};x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\adj_{P_{1}}(x_{1})}{\prod_{F_{1}}L_{F_{1}}(x_{1})}\frac{\adj_{P_{2}}(x_{2})}{\prod_{F_{2}}L_{F_{2}}(x_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\adj_{P_{1}}(x_{1})\adj_{P_{2}}(x_{2})}{\prod_{F_{1}}L_{F_{1}\times P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\prod_{F_{2}}L_{P_{1}\times F_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\adj_{P_{1}}(x_{1})\adj_{P_{2}}(x_{2})}{\prod_{F}L_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})}.

Comparing numerators, we indeed obtain
\adj_{P_{1}\times P_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\adj_{P_{1}}(x_{1})\adj_{P_{2}}(x_{2})
, from which follows the additivity of degrees.

Appendix E
Computing the adjoint of edge tangent cones

For the notation and setting, see
Section

5.2
.
We prove the following:

Lemma E.1
.

If
u_{0},...,u_{d}
is a positive affine basis, and if
i<j
, then

(E.1)

\displaystyle\ell_{ij}\adj_{T_{ij}}=-\det\!\begin{pmatrix}|&&|&&|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]

We use

\displaystyle U:=\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\[-2.15277pt]

\Delta
so that

. In particular, since

We inspect the entries of

\displaystyle\equiv\det\!\begin{pmatrix}&|&&|&&|\\
\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots&v_{j}-v_{i}\\

\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{i}&\!\!\!\!\cdots\!\!\!\!&\hat{u}_{j}&\!\!\!\!\cdots&\vec{e}_{ij}\\
&|&&|&&|\end{pmatrix}

The last determinant agrees with

in absolute value by
(

)
.
∎

, but we move the last column
v_{j}-v_{i}
to index
j+1
.
Plugging in the values for
v_{i}
and
u_{i}
, we are left with computing the determinant of the following block matrix:

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc|cccc|cccc}\ell_{0}&\!\!-\ell_{2}&&&&&&&&&&\\[-5.16663pt]
&\!\!\phantom{+}\ell_{1}&\!\!\ddots&&&&&&&&&\\[-5.16663pt]
&&\!\!\ddots&\mathllap{-}\ell_{i}&&&&&&&&\\
&&&\ell_{i-1}&&&&&&&&\\[3.01385pt]
\hline\cr&&&&-\overset{\phantom{.}}{\ell}_{i+2}&&&\ell_{i+1}&&&&\\[-5.16663pt]
&&&&\phantom{+}\ell_{i+1}&\!\!\ddots&&&&&\\[-5.16663pt]
&&&&&\!\!\ddots&\mathllap{-}\ell_{j}&\raisebox{5.0pt}{\smash{\vdots}}&&&&\\
&&&&&&\ell_{j-1}&\ell_{j}&&&&\\[3.01385pt]
\hline\cr&&&&&&&&-\overset{\phantom{.}}{\ell}_{j+2}&&\\[-5.16663pt]
&&&&&&&&\phantom{+}\ell_{j+1}&\!\!\ddots&\\[-5.16663pt]
&&&&&&&&&\!\!\ddots&\!\!\!\!-\ell_{d}&\\
&&&&&&&&&&\!\!\!\!\phantom{+}\ell_{d-1}&\!\!-\ell_{d+1}\end{array}\right)

Conveniently, this matrix is block-diagonal with square blocks of dimension
i
,
j-i
and
d-j
respectively.
Its determinant can therefore be computed by multiplying the determinants of the diagonal blocks.
Evidently, the determinants of the top-left and bottom-right blocks multiply to
\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{i-1}\ell_{j+2}\cdots\ell_{d+1}
(up to a sign).
If we extract the middle block as

M_{ij}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\overset{\phantom{.}}{\ell}_{i+2}&&&\ell_{i+1}\\[-5.16663pt]
\phantom{+}\ell_{i+1}&\!\!\ddots\!\!&\\[-5.16663pt]
&\!\!\ddots\!\!&\mathllap{-}\ell_{j}\!\!\!\!&\raisebox{5.0pt}{\smash{\vdots}}\\
&&\ell_{j-1}\!\!\!\!&\ell_{j}\end{pmatrix}

it suffices to prove the following:

Lemma F.1
.

\det(M_{ij})=(-1)^{j-i-1}\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}\ell_{j+1}}\cdot\ell_{i}\cdots\ell_{j+1}.

The sign is included here because it is needed for the inductive proof.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction on
j-i
.
For
j-i=1
the statement becomes
\det(\ell_{i+1})=(-1)^{0}\ell_{i+1}
which clearly holds.
For
j-i\geq 2
we apply Laplace expansion to the last row of
M_{ij}
.
When deleting the row and column of
\ell_{j-1}
we are left with
M_{i(j-1)}
whose determinant we know by induction hypothesis.
When deleting the row and column of
\ell_{j}
, we are left with a lower-triangular matrix whose determinant is
(-1)^{j-i-1}\ell_{i+2}\cdots\ell_{j}
.
The proof then finishes with the following computation:

\displaystyle\det(M_{ij})

\displaystyle=-\ell_{j-1}\det(M_{i(j-1)})+(-1)^{j-i-1}\ell_{j}\cdot\ell_{i+2}\cdots\ell_{j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j-i-1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\ell_{j-1}}\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j-1}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\ell_{j-1}}\ell_{j}}\cdot\ell_{i}\cdots\ell_{j}+(-1)^{j-i-1}\ell_{j}\cdot\ell_{i+2}\cdots\ell_{j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j-i-1}\Big(\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j-1}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell_{j+1}}}\cdot\ell_{i}\cdots\ell_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\ell_{j+1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt+\frac{\ell_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\ell_{j}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}\ell_{j+1}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}}\ell_{i+2}\cdots\ell_{j}{\color[rgb]{0,0.88,0}\ell_{j+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j-i-1}\frac{\ell_{i+1}^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{j}^{2}}{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j}\ell_{j+1}}\cdot\ell_{i}\cdots\ell_{j+1}.\qed

Appendix G
Geometric interpretation of the adjoint of a triangle

The adjoint polynomial of a triangle is of degree zero, that is, it is a number. This number is well-defined if we assume that normal vectors in
(
2.2
)
are normalized. It is moreover invariant under translation of the triangle, as can be inferred, for example, from
(
5.9
)
.
Here we show that the adjoint of a triangle
\Delta
can be expressed as

\adj_{\Delta}=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)}{\operatorname{Circumradius}(\Delta)}.

Figure 8.
Visualization for the proof of
Appendix

G
.

Let
\Delta
be a triangle with vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
and
v_{3}
.
Let
u_{i}
and
h_{i}
be the normal vector and height of the edge opposite to
v_{i}
.
By translation invariance we can assume that
\Delta
is translated so that its circumcenter is the origin.
The circumradius we denote by
R
.
The three line segments connecting the origin to the vertices dissect
\Delta
into three triangles with areas
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
, that sum up to
\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)
.
Below we compute the area
A_{1}
(see
Figure

8
).

Let
\alpha:=\measuredangle(u_{2},u_{3})
be the angle between the normal vectors
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
.
As shown in
Figure

8
, the angle
\beta
at
v_{1}
lies opposite to
\alpha
in a quadrangle with two right angles.
Hence
\beta=\pi-\alpha
.
By the inscribed angle theorem, the angle
\gamma=\measuredangle(v_{2}0v_{3})
is twice as large as
\beta
.
Thus,
\gamma=2\pi-2\alpha
.
The highligted triangle
v_{2}0v_{3}
is isosceles, and so its area
A_{1}
evaluates to

\displaystyle A_{1}=Rh_{1}\sin(\nicefrac{{\gamma}}{{2}})=Rh_{1}\sin(\pi-\alpha)=Rh_{1}\sin(\alpha).

Analogous formulas hold for
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
.
The proof then concludes with the following computation:

\displaystyle\adj_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}|\!\!\!\!&|\!\!\!\!&|\\[-2.15277pt]
u_{0}\!\!\!\!&u_{1}\!\!\!\!&u_{2}\\
|\!\!\!\!&|\!\!\!\!&|\\[2.15277pt]
h_{0}\!\!\!\!&h_{1}\!\!\!\!&h_{2}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=h_{1}\sin\measuredangle(u_{2},u_{3})+h_{2}\sin\measuredangle(u_{3},u_{1})+h_{3}\sin\measuredangle(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{1}}{R}+\frac{A_{2}}{R}+\frac{A_{3}}{R}=\frac{A}{R}.

For the first equality we used Laplace expansion in the last row and the fact that the determinant of a
(2\times 2)
-matrix is the sine of the angle between its columns.
