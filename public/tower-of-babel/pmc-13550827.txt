Yiqi Huoxue Yangyin Decoction Ameliorates Diabetic Nephropathy by Targeting AMPK/mTOR-Mediated Autophagy: A Network Pharmacology-Guided in vivo and in vitro Study
Authors: ZhiWei Xu, JinXing He, Yangli Zhang, JianHong Jin, Fei Pan, Ai Mi, XueQian Peng, WenHong Liu, YanFang Yang, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the protective effects and underlying mechanisms of Yiqi Huoxue Yangyin Decoction (YHY), a multi-herb formula composed of Astragalus membranaceus, Ligusticum chuanxiong, Pueraria lobata, Rehmannia glutinosa, and Trichosanthes kirilowii, against diabetic nephropathy (DN), focusing on its role in modulating autophagy and attenuating podocyte senescence via the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway.Materials and MethodsUsing network pharmacology and UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, this study examined the active constituents and targets of YHY. The type 2 diabetes db/db mouse model (leptin receptor-deficient BKS.Cg-Lepr<db>/+ mice, random blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L) was used to construct a DN model in vivo. Mice received daily oral gavage of YHY or vehicle for 4 weeks (n=10). YHY’s protective benefits were assessed by physiological measurements, serum insulin and HbA1c by ELISA, histopathological evaluation of glomerular and tubular regions, glomerular basement membrane thickness by TEM, and protein expression in renal cortex by immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blot. In vitro, using a high-glucose-induced MPC-5 model, CCK-8, wound healing assays, qRT-PCR, and Western blot were performed (n=3-6) to investigate the mechanisms of YHY.ResultsYHY demonstrated significant protective effects against DN. The expression levels of autophagy markers (ULK1, P62, and LC3B) in renal tissue were restored following YHY intervention. Through the AMPK/mTOR pathway, YHY regulated the expression of autophagy-related genes (ULK1, Beclin-1, P62, LC3B, ATG5, ATG7) and senescence-related genes (P21, P53), and improved the survival and migratory capabilities of high-glucose-induced MPC-5 cells. Furthermore, YHY enhanced the expression of podocyte structural proteins, including Nephrin, NPHS2, and SYNPO.ConclusionYHY treatment was associated with activation of autophagy and attenuation of podocyte senescence, potentially through modulation of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, a finding that requires validation in future mechanistic studies.

Introduction

One of the most common chronic metabolic diseases is diabetes mellitus (DM). It occurs when there is either insufficient insulin generated by the body or resistance to insulin develops. One of the dangerous side effects of diabetes is DN. Worldwide, about 10% of adults have diabetes. Thirty to forty percent of them will develop DN.1 The present management of DN involves the use of several drug classes. These include antidiabetic drugs, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, lipid-lowering treatments, and hypertension medications. Blood glucose control is aided by these treatments. They can aid in delaying the course of renal illness. However, there are currently limited options for DN-specific treatment.2,3 Further clarification of the pathophysiology of DN is necessary to develop novel therapeutic strategies.

Hyperglycemia is the core factor in the development of DN, and the metabolic disturbances caused by prolonged high blood glucose lead to kidney cell injury and structural changes.4 Glomerular damage is a key pathological feature in the early stages of DN, and as the disease progresses, the function of the glomerular filtration barrier may be compromised, resulting in clinical manifestations such as proteinuria.5 Podocytes, which are critical for maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, are key contributors to proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis when damaged or dysfunctional, directly impacting the kidney’s filtration capacity.6 Podocyte senescence has been identified as one of the major mechanisms contributing to the breakdown of the glomerular filtration barrier.7,8 Autophagy is an essential homeostatic regulation and quality control mechanism that maintains cellular integrity.9 Increasing evidence suggests that autophagy plays a pivotal role in DN by maintaining podocyte lysosomal homeostasis, preventing podocyte loss, and reducing proteinuria, with autophagy dysfunction being a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of DN.10,11 Classical autophagy pathways are closely associated with podocyte autophagy dysfunction. These include mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, which regulates protein synthesis, degradation, and the start of autophagy.12–14 In addition, research has demonstrated that reduced autophagy is a significant mechanism of podocyte senescence,15 leading to the accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles, resulting in functional loss and being closely associated with glomerular diseases and proteinuria.16–18 Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the function and mechanism of podocytes in the initiation of DN. Despite the promise of autophagy modulation as a therapeutic strategy, current approaches face significant challenges. Direct mTOR inhibitors such as rapamycin have narrow therapeutic windows and can cause metabolic side effects including hyperlipidemia and insulin resistance.19,20 Moreover, agents that non-specifically enhance autophagy may disrupt other cellular processes or lead to off-target effects.21 Thus, identifying safer, multi-targeted interventions remains a priority.

We hypothesized that YHY ameliorates DN by activating autophagy through the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby attenuating podocyte senescence. To test this hypothesis, we first evaluated the renoprotective effects of YHY in db/db mice, then determined whether YHY modulates autophagy in renal tissues and involves the AMPK/mTOR pathway, and finally validated these findings in high-glucose-induced MPC-5 podocytes.

“Yiqi Huoxue Yangyin Decoction” (YHY) is a combination of the classical formulas Si Wu decoction and Yu Ye decoction, consisting of Sheng Huangqi (Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge. var. mongholicus (Bge.) Hsiao root), Chuanxiong (Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. rhizome), Gegen (Pueraria lobata (Willd.) Ohwi root), Shengdi (Rehmannia glutinosa Libosch. root), and Tianhuafen (Trichosanthes kirilowii Maxim. root). According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, DN is characterized by Qi deficiency, blood stasis, and Yin deficiency. Accordingly, in YHY, Sheng Huangqi tonifies Qi; Chuanxiong invigorates blood and removes stasis; Gegen generates fluids and quenches thirst; Shengdi nourishes Yin and cools blood; and Tianhuafen clears heat and promotes fluid production. From a modern pharmacological perspective, these herbs contain bioactive compounds with documented renoprotective effects: astragaloside IV and astragalus polysaccharide from Astragalus enhance podocyte autophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO1 and AMPKα pathways;22,23 rehmannioside from Rehmannia restores autophagic activity;24 puerarin from Pueraria reduces oxidative stress and inhibits renal fibrosis; and ligustrazine from Ligusticum improves renal microcirculation and reduces inflammation.25 A previous clinical study reported that YHY combined with sitagliptin improved renal function in early DN patients.26 However, because that study used YHY as an adjunct to sitagliptin, the specific effects and mechanisms of YHY monotherapy in DN remain to be elucidated.

In summary, the pathogenesis of DN is complex and involves podocyte dysfunction and autophagy defects. While current treatment options can slow the progression of DN to some extent, there remains a lack of effective interventions targeting these underlying mechanisms. This study therefore investigated whether YHY, a multi-herb formula, exerts renoprotective effects by modulating autophagy and podocyte homeostasis.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Drugs

The herbal formulation YHY comprises five constituent medicinal plants: Sheng Huangqi (Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge. var. mongholicus (Bge.) Hsiao root), Chuanxiong (Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. rhizome), Gegen (Pueraria lobata (Willd.) Ohwi root), Shengdi (Rehmannia glutinosa Libosch. root), Tianhuafen (Trichosanthes kirilowii Maxim. root). All botanical materials were procured from Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Herbal Pieces Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

For preparation of the aqueous extract, precisely weighed quantities (24.0 g Huang Qi, 6.0 g Chuan Xiong, 12.0 g Di Huang, 12.0 g Ge Gen, and 6.0 g Tian Hua Fen) were combined in strict accordance with the standardized YHY formulation ratio. The herb mixture was initially macerated in 1.0 L of ultrapure water at 25°C for 30 min, followed by decoction with supplementary 3.0 L ultrapure water. After primary filtration through 0.22 μm membranes, the marc was subjected to secondary extraction under identical conditions. The combined filtrates were subsequently concentrated to 3 g crude herb equivalents/mL using rotary evaporation (60°C, 80 rpm). Aliquot portions were either immediately allocated for in vivo studies or lyophilized and cryopreserved at −80°C for subsequent analyses. For comparative purposes, dapagliflozin (Dapa; AstraZeneca Pharmaceuticals Ltd., Cambridge, UK) was prepared as an aqueous working solut at a concentration of 1.3 mg/mL.

Collection and Identification of Active Components in YHY

Lyophilized YHY powder was reconstituted in 50% methanol-water to 10 mg raw material/mL, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. UPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis was performed on a CORTECS T3 C18 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.6 μm; Waters) using a gradient elution of 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile at 0.3 mL/min. Mass spectrometry was conducted using ESI in positive and negative ion modes with MSE full scan (50–1200 m/z). Data were processed with UNIFI 2.0 software.

Mice and Treatments

This study used BKS. Cg-Dock7m + / + Leprdb/JNju (db/db) male mice with random blood glucose levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L. Age-matched BKS heterozygote db/+ mice (random blood glucose level <11.1 mmol/l) served as the control group. All mice were 8 weeks old and were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The mice were housed under controlled conditions with a temperature range of 20–25°C, humidity of 40–70%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice had free access to food and water and were housed at the Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Experimental Animal Research Center. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Committee of Animal Care and Use of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Animal Ethics No: IACUC-20240513-32). One week after adaptive feeding, the diabetic db/db mice were randomly divided into five groups based on blood glucose levels and body weight: (i) diabetic model group (Model), (ii) low-dose YHY group (YHY-L), (iii) medium-dose YHY group (YHY-M), (iv) high-dose YHY group (YHY-H), and (v) western medicine Dapagliflozin Group (Dapa), the control db/+ mice served as the normal group (Normal), with 10 mice in each group and 2 to 3 mice in each cage. Mice in the YHY-L (1.56 g/mL), YHY-M (3.12 g/mL), and YHY-H (6.24 g/mL) groups were gavaged with YHY water extract, while mice in the Normal and Model groups received an equal volume of purified water. Mice in the Dapa group were gavaged with 1.3 mg/mL of Dapagliflozin. The gavage volume for all groups was consistently maintained at 50 µL per 10 g of body weight, administered once daily for a duration of 4 weeks. Every week, measurements of body weight (BW) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were made. In order to measure 24-hour urine volume, 24-hour urinary protein, and 24-hour urinary albumin, urine samples were taken using metabolic cages after four weeks. All mice were put to sleep with CO2 at a flow rate of 2–5 L/min after biochemical and physical assessments. This study has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments) Guidelines.

Cell Culture

We bought MPC-5 mouse podocytes from Shanghai, China’s iCell Bioscience Inc. In addition to 10% fetal bovine serum (Cellmax, Beijing, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA), MPC-5 cells were cultivated in high-glucose DMEM media.

Preparation of YHY-Containing Serum

YHY-containing serum was prepared following established protocols for dose conversion based on body surface area.27 Briefly, adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–220 g) were orally administered YHY water extract at a dose of 6.24 g crude herb/mL (the high-dose used in the in vivo mouse study, converted to rat equivalent dose) twice daily for 3 consecutive days. Two hours after the final administration, blood was collected from the abdominal aorta under isoflurane anesthesia. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and stored at −80°C. For in vitro experiments, MPC-5 cells were cultured with medium containing 10% YHY-containing serum. Blank control serum was obtained from rats gavaged with an equal volume of purified water using the same protocol.

In vitro Experiment

To investigate the effects and mechanisms of YHY, MPC-5 cells were cultured in high glucose (HG) and treated with YHY-containing serum in vitro. Based on preliminary data screening, the HG model was established with a glucose concentration of 55 mmol/L and a treatment duration of 48 hours. The cell groups and treatments were as follows: (i) NG group (cultured in normal DMEM with 25 mM glucose+10% blank serum intervention), (ii) HG group (cultured in DMEM containing 50 mM glucose+10% blank serum intervention), and (iii) YHY group (cultured in DMEM containing 50 mM glucose+10% YHY-containing serum intervention).

Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the intervened MPC-5 cells using the trizol method, and RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). According to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, Osaka, Japan), total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a thermal cycler (Langji Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit (Selleck Chemicals, TX, USA) on the Mastercycler ep realplex instrument (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) with a 20 μL reaction system. The specific protocol was as follows: 95°C pre-incubation for 5 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C denaturation for 15 seconds, 60°C annealing for 30 seconds, and 72°C extension for 30 seconds. GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and the primer sequences for all genes are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2(−∆∆Ct) method. The autophagy-related genes (ULK1, Beclin-1, LC3B, ATG5, ATG7) and senescence markers (P21, P53) were selected based on their established roles as key regulators of autophagosome formation28 and cellular senescence,29 respectively.

Western Blotting

Protein expression in MPC-5 cells and kidney tissues was detected by Western blot (WB) analysis. For kidney tissue protein extraction, kidney samples were obtained from three mice in the normal, model, YHY-H, and Dapa groups. After grinding in liquid nitrogen, radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added, and the samples were incubated on ice for 30 minutes with vortexing every 5 minutes. The lysates were then centrifuged at 4°C, 12,000 g for 15 minutes, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). For MPC-5 cells, protein was extracted by collecting cells with a cell scraper and following the same procedure as for kidney tissue. Protein was separated using 7.5% or 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to NC membranes (Cytiva, Sweden). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) secondary antibody at 4°C for 2 hours. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagents (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and images were captured with the ChemiDoc XRS+ Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Band density was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with goat serum at 37°C for 30 minutes. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against P62 (0.1 µg/mL, AB109012, Abcam), ULK1 (1:100, A8529, ABclonal), and p-mTOR (0.1 µg/mL, AB109268, Abcam), then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and visualized with DAB. For immunofluorescence (IF), sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with LC3B antibody (0.1 µg/mL, AB192890, Abcam), followed by fluorescent secondary antibody and mounted with DAPI-containing antifade medium. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

For quantification, five randomly selected non-overlapping fields per section were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Regions containing large vessels or artefacts were excluded. All analyses were performed blinded to group assignment by two independent investigators who were not involved in the animal experiments. Intra-observer and inter-observer variabilities were less than 10%. Staining intensity was quantified as integrated optical density (IHC) or mean fluorescence intensity (IF).

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) based on preliminary data (effect size f = 0.40, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), yielding a minimum of 7 mice per group; to account for dropouts, we initially included 10 mice per group. Normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, and all data met parametric assumptions.

For longitudinal data (fasting blood glucose, body weight), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction was applied. For other comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or partial η2) and 95% confidence intervals are reported where applicable. Data are presented as means ± SD, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001. For different experimental endpoints, sample sizes were: n=10 per group for physiological and urinary measurements; n=5 for histopathological analyses (HE, PAS, Masson, TEM) due to the labor-intensive nature of these procedures; and n=3-6 for molecular analyses (Western blot, IHC, IF, qPCR).

Network Pharmacology Analysis of YHY Against DN

Network pharmacology analysis was employed to systematically uncover the multi-target mechanisms underlying YHY’s anti-DN effects. After screening 25 chemical components of YHY using SwissADME, 540 protein targets were predicted via the Swiss Target Prediction website. A total of 2932 DN-related targets were identified from the GeneCards, TTD, and OMIM databases, with 271 common targets shared between YHY and DN (Figure 2A). Target interactions were obtained from the STRING database (Figure 2B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to explore the potential mechanisms underlying YHY’s therapeutic effects on DN, visualizing the top 10 BP, CC, MF, and top 20 KEGG pathways. The BP analysis primarily identified key processes such as positive regulation of MAPK cascade, response to xenobiotic stimulus, and peptidyl-serine phosphorylation. CC results emphasized neuronal cell body, synaptic membrane, and membrane raft. MF analysis revealed key functions including protein serine/threonine kinase activity, protein serine kinase activity, and protein tyrosine kinase activity (Figure 2C). KEGG enrichment highlighted pathways impacted by YHY against DN, including autophagy, AMPK and mTOR signaling (Figure 2D). Collectively, these in predictions strongly suggest that YHY exerts its renoprotective action through modulating autophagy and metabolic signaling pathways, providing a mechanistic foundation for subsequent experimental validation. YHY Improved Renal Dysfunction in Diabetic Mice

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of YHY on diabetic manifestations, we conducted a systematic assessment of metabolic and renal parameters in accordance with the experimental design illustrated in the schematic diagram (Figure 3A). Compared to the normal group, the model group mice had significantly increased FBG and BW [P < 0.01] (Figure 3B). Following YHY treatment, FBG decreased in all dosage groups: YHY-L, YHY-M, and YHY-H [P < 0.01], with the YHY-H group showing a reduction similar to the Dapa group. After four weeks of YHY treatment, BW was significantly reduced in all dosage groups. Compared to the normal group, insulin levels were decreased in the model group, whereas the YHY-H and Dapa groups showed a significant increase in insulin levels [P < 0.05 or P < 0.01] (Figure 3D). Metabolic cage analysis demonstrated a significantly higher 24-hour urine volume in the model group compared to the normal group [P < 0.001]. YHY treatment significantly reduced urine volume in all dosage groups [P < 0.05 or P < 0.01] (Figure 3C). Biochemical analysis of urine revealed that YHY treatment significantly reduced 24-hour urinary protein excretion [P < 0.001] and albumin excretion [P < 0.05], exhibiting a trend comparable to that of the Dapa group. However, no significant reduction in urinary albumin was observed in the YHY-L group (Figure 3E and F). Moreover, YHY-H and Dapa groups significantly reduced HbA1c compared to the model group [P < 0.05 or P < 0.01] (Figure 3G). These results indicate that YHY treatment has significant efficacy in controlling blood glucose, reducing body weight, improving insulin levels, and alleviating kidney damage in diabetic mice. YHY Alleviated Renal Histological Damage in Diabetic Mice

To elucidate the renoprotective effects of YHY at the histopathological and ultrastructural levels, we performed comprehensive morphological analyses. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Periodic acid-schiff (PAS) staining results showed that the kidney tissue of model group mice exhibited glomerular hypertrophy, significant inflammatory infiltration, mesangial thickening, glycogen deposition, and glomerular capillary dilation. Compared to the model group, the YHY treatment groups and the Dapa group showed marked pathological improvement, with significantly reduced pathological scores [P < 0.001] (Figure 4A, B and E). Masson’s trichrome staining indicated substantial collagen deposition in the kidney tissue of model group mice, along with tubular interstitial fibrosis. In contrast, collagen deposition was significantly reduced in the YHY treatment groups and the Dapa group [P < 0.001] (Figure 4C and F). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations revealed thickening of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) in the model group. However, in the YHY treatment groups and the Dapa group, GBM thickness was significantly reduced [P < 0.05, P < 0.01 or P < 0.001] (Figure 4D and G). These findings demonstrate that YHY treatment significantly attenuated diabetic kidney injury by reducing inflammatory infiltration, glycogen and collagen deposition, and GBM thickening, thereby exhibiting marked renoprotective effects. YHY Ameliorates DN Injury via Autophagy Regulation

Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that YHY treatment effectively restored autophagy flux in DN, as indicated by key autophagy markers. Compared to the normal group, the model group exhibited significantly increased expression of P62 [P < 0.001] and significantly decreased expression of ULK1 [P < 0.001]. In contrast, the YHY-L, YHY-M, and YHY-H groups showed significant restoration of P62 expression [P < 0.001] and ULK1 expression [P < 0.01 or P < 0.001] (Figure 5A–C). LC3B, a classic marker of autophagy, showed significantly reduced fluorescence expression in the kidney tissue of the model group [P < 0.001], whereas fluorescence levels of LC3B gradually recovered in all YHY treatment groups [P < 0.01 or P < 0.001] (Figure 5D). Dose-dependent effects of YHY were observed for these autophagy markers, with the high-dose group showing the most substantial restoration of ULK1 and LC3B expression and reduction of P62 (Figure 5). Taken together, these results provide evidence that YHY ameliorates DN injury by activating renal autophagy. To mechanistically validate that YHY ameliorates DN via the AMPK/mTOR pathway in vivo, we performed immunohistochemical and immunoblotting analyses. IHC results showed that compared to the normal group, p-mTOR expression was significantly increased in the model group [P < 0.001]. In contrast, p-mTOR expression was reduced in the YHY-treated and Dapa groups, with the YHY-H group showing the lowest expression [P < 0.001] (Figure 6A and B). WB analysis revealed that, compared to the normal group, the protein expression levels of Nephrin [P < 0.05], ULK1 [P < 0.01], and Beclin-1 [P < 0.01], as well as the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio [P < 0.01], were decreased in the model group. Meanwhile, the protein expression of P62 [P < 0.001] and the p-mTOR/mTOR ratio [P < 0.01] were increased. Notably, YHY treatment significantly reversed these dysregulated expressions [P < 0.05, P < 0.01 or P < 0.001] (Figure 6C and D). These findings suggest that YHY modulates the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway in vivo, correlating with enhanced autophagy and podocyte protection. Effect of YHY on the Viability and Migration Ability of MPC-5 Cell

The onset and progression of DN are closely related to podocyte injury. Therefore, in vitro validation of the protective mechanism of YHY on podocytes is crucial for revealing its potential therapeutic effects on DN. To simulate the hyperglycemic microenvironment in diabetic patients, HG-induced MPC-5 cell injury was used, with 55 mM glucose treatment for 48 hours as the HG condition (Supplementary Figure 1). The results showed that in the HG group, the viability of MPC-5 cells was significantly inhibited [P < 0.001]; however, after treatment with YHY-containing serum, cell viability was significantly restored [P < 0.01] (Figure 7A). In addition, compared to the NG group, the HG group suppressed the migration ability of MPC-5 cells, with significantly reduced wound healing area at both 12 and 24 hours [P < 0.001]. After treatment with YHY-containing serum, the wound healing area was significantly increased [P < 0.01 or P < 0.001] (Figure 7B–D), and the migration ability was restored to a level close to the NG group. These in vitro findings collectively demonstrate that YHY-containing serum significantly mitigates HG-induced podocyte damage by enhancing cell viability and migration capacity, thereby providing functional evidence for its renoprotective role in DN. Discussion

The pathogenesis of DN is complex and multifactorial. It is widely believed that DN is an immune-inflammatory disease triggered by metabolic disorders, which primarily include the activation of protein kinase C, the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, activation of the polyol pathway, oxidative stress, and the activation of cytokines.30–32 The development of DN is also linked to the dysfunction of podocyte autophagy, which plays an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, energy balance, and renal protection.33 In mice with HG-induced podocyte damage, the levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3-II/LC3-I were reduced, while the expression of the autophagy substrate P62/SQSTM increased, indicating HG-mediated podocyte damage and autophagy loss, thereby promoting DN progression. Inhibition of podocyte autophagy (via 3-MA or Beclin-1 siRNA) reduced the expression of Nephrin and CD2AP, leading to damage of the filtration barrier and worsening proteinuria. Conversely, the autophagy enhancer rapamycin restored autophagic function, improved filtration barrier function, and alleviated proteinuria.34 These findings highlight the crucial role of podocyte autophagy in maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, and autophagy dysfunction is one of the key mechanisms in the occurrence and progression of DN. However, whether YHY targets and activates autophagy to treat DN remains unclear. Therefore, this study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of YHY in a db/db mouse model of DN and preliminarily explored its underlying mechanisms. Our study suggests that YHY treatment in db/db mice significantly ameliorates hyperglycemia, improves glycemic control as reflected by reduced glycated hemoglobin, attenuates proteinuria (Figure 3), and alleviates histopathological injury including glomerular inflammation, mesangial proliferation, and fibrosis (Figure 4). Importantly, we found that in db/db mice, autophagy function (ULK1, P62, LC3B) was significantly suppressed, while YHY treatment effectively restored autophagy levels (Figure 5). These findings combined imply that the autophagy pathway may be connected to YHY’s kidney protective function.

The mTOR pathway is a major regulator of autophagy in DN. Overactivation of mTOR contributes significantly to podocyte injury and a decline in glomerular filtration rate.35 At the same time, AMPK acts as a central regulator of cellular energy metabolism. It maintains energy balance by activating processes like glucose uptake and breakdown. AMPK also inhibits energy-consuming biosynthetic pathways.36 It is significant to note that AMPK inhibits the mTOR signaling pathway. AMPK is a crucial upstream controller of mTOR because of this connection. Existing research is associated with Emodin and Neuregulin-4 ameliorate high glucose-induced podocyte injury by regulating the AMPK/mTOR-mediated autophagy signaling pathway.37,38 Similarly, Kaempferol and the compound XueShuanTong have been shown to attenuate DN injury by promoting autophagy through the AMPK/mTOR pathway.39,40 As a result, the AMPK/mTOR pathway is critically involved in controlling autophagy in DN.41 However, unlike these single agents, YHY is a multi-herb formula with documented clinical use, which may offer translational advantages despite challenges in mechanistic dissection. In this study, we used network pharmacology to analyze the multi-target mechanisms of YHY. We discovered that YHY works by regulating several signaling pathways. Among these, the AMPK/mTOR pathway serves as a core mechanism. It regulates autophagy and influences the progression of DN (Figure 2). In vivo experiments showed that p-mTOR levels were significantly lower in the kidney tissues of db/db mice. This suggests that the mTOR pathway may be suppressed in DN. However, treatment with YHY promoted AMPK phosphorylation. This further inhibited mTOR phosphorylation, leading to negative regulation of the mTOR pathway. By regulating the AMPK/mTOR pathway, YHY activated the autophagy process. This was reflected in changes in proteins such as ULK1, P62, and Beclin-1. Additionally, YHY increased the expression of Nephrin protein. This helped maintain podocyte structure and function. Ultimately, YHY protected the kidneys and slowed the progression of DN (Figure 6). These findings suggest that YHY may play an important role in treating DN, potentially through regulating the AMPK/mTOR pathway and enhancing autophagy.

When autophagic function is impaired, intracellular waste and damaged components cannot be efficiently cleared, leading to their accumulation, which disrupts cell function and accelerates senescence.42 This mechanism is particularly evident in terminally differentiated podocytes, as they play a crucial role in the glomerular filtration barrier and are highly sensitive to aging.43 Damaged podocytes are always accompanied by a decrease in autophagic flux, exhibiting typical senescence phenotypes such as increased cell volume, impaired function, and cell cycle arrest.15 Studies have shown that in mice with specific knockout of ATG5 in podocytes, the aging process of podocytes is significantly accelerated, leading to renal functional damage.44 In the context of DN, the HG environment further exacerbates this process. HG induces podocyte hypertrophy and stress responses, causing a greater metabolic burden, while excessive accumulation of advanced glycation end products accelerates the deterioration of the intracellular environment. Podocytes in this HG environment are more likely to undergo aging and lose their normal functions, including maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier.45 Therefore, the decline in podocyte autophagic function leads to podocyte senescence, which plays an important role in the progression of DN. In this study, we further validated the molecular mechanism of YHY treatment in podocytes at the cellular level using a HG-induced MPC-5 cell model. In vitro results showed that HG induced a significant decrease in cell viability and migration ability, while YHY-containing serum intervention effectively reversed these adverse changes (Figure 7). Consistent with in vivo findings, YHY treatment was associated with activation of autophagy and modulation of the AMPK/mTOR pathway, correlating with restored podocyte function (Figure 8). P21 and p53, as classic markers of senescence, are upregulated to promote cell aging.46,47 This study found that YHY significantly reduced the expression of P21 and P53 in HG-induced MPC-5 cells, suggesting its potential in delaying podocyte senescence. Furthermore, YHY was able to restore the expression of Nephrin, NPHS1, NPHS2, WT-1 and the protein level of Nephrin in high glucose-induced MPC-5 cells (Figure 9), indicating that YHY ameliorates podocyte aging, repairing their function and structure. The relationship between autophagy and senescence, however, is complex and bidirectional. While autophagy dysfunction accelerates senescence, excessive autophagy can also promote senescence under certain conditions.48 Additionally, senescent cells exhibit reduced autophagic flux, creating a vicious cycle.49 Our finding that YHY both enhances autophagy and reduces senescence markers may reflect a role in breaking this cycle, rather than a simple linear relationship.

This study has several limitations. While our data show associations between YHY treatment and AMPK/mTOR pathway modulation, we did not perform inhibitor-based experiments to establish causality. Additionally, the specific active constituents responsible for YHY’s effects have not been identified, and the precise molecular targets through which YHY fine-tunes autophagy in vivo remain to be elucidated. Despite these limitations, our findings demonstrate that YHY, as a multi-herb formula with documented clinical use, exerts coordinated effects on both autophagic and senescent pathways in DN. Given the limited therapeutic options currently available for this progressive disease, these results provide a mechanistic rationale for further preclinical and clinical development of YHY as a potential adjunct therapy for DN.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study suggests that YHY exhibits therapeutic potential in the treatment of DN. YHY treatment was associated with reduced blood glucose levels, alleviated proteinuria, and improved kidney structure and function in db/db mice. Mechanistically, these effects were associated with modulation of the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway. The observed activation of autophagy in podocytes correlated with improved cellular function and structure, attenuated podocyte senescence, and restored integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier. Furthermore, YHY enhanced the expression of key proteins such as Nephrin, NPHS1, and WT-1, which are critical for podocyte function (Figure 10). These findings offer new evidence endorsing the translational potential of YHY in DN treatment. However, further studies using pathway-specific inhibitors are needed to establish causality.
