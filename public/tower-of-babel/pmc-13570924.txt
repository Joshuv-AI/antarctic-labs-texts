Fecal Microbiome and Serum Metabolome Profiles of the Ovarian Failure Mouse Model
Authors: Yiyao Fu, Honghong Jiang, Dingchuan Peng, Zheqiao Bai, Sishuo Wang, Hengchang Liu, Wendan Zhang, Wei Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian dysfunction is closely associated with reproductive aging and systemic metabolic disturbances; however, the underlying microbial and metabolic mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we analyzed fecal microbiome and serum metabolome profiles in young (7-week-old) and aged (12-month-old) female C57BL/6J mice using shotgun metagenomic sequencing and untargeted ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Microbial and metabolic data were processed using QIIME2, HUMAnN, and MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Differential taxa and metabolites were identified using DESeq2 and linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), and their associations were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Our results showed that aged mice exhibited significant alterations in gut microbiota composition, including a decreased abundance of Firmicutes and an increased abundance of Bacteroidetes, along with enrichment of the genera Alistipes and Akkermansia. Serum metabolomic profiling identified 246 differential metabolites, primarily involved in amino acid and energy metabolism pathways. Integrated analysis revealed that tryptophan metabolism represents a key pathway linking microbial dysbiosis with systemic metabolic alterations. Notably, enriched microbial taxa, including Akkermansia muciniphila and species within the genus Alistipes, were strongly correlated with tryptophan-related metabolites. These findings indicate that ovarian failure is associated with coordinated alterations in the gut microbiome and serum metabolome, converging on tryptophan metabolism. This study provides new insights into host–microbiome–metabolite interactions in ovarian failure and highlights potential microbial and metabolic targets for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12010-026-05747-1.Keywords: Ovarian failure, Gut microbiome, Serum metabolome, Tryptophan metabolism, Microbe-metabolite interaction

Introduction

Ovarian function is critical for female reproductive health, endocrine homeostasis, and systemic metabolism [1]. The decline or premature loss of ovarian function is closely related to ovarian failure, which is characterized by estrous cycle disorders, decreased fertility, and altered hormone levels [2]. In addition to impaired reproductive function, ovarian failure is associated with metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and an increased risk of osteoporosis [3, 4]. The biological mechanisms underlying the correlation between ovarian failure and systemic metabolic changes have not been elucidated.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the gut microbiome regulates host physiology, especially nutrient metabolism, immune response, and endocrine signaling [5–7]. Dysbiosis is associated with the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases and female reproductive disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome and premature ovarian insufficiency [8–10]. Ovarian failure or reproductive aging is reported to be associated with decreased microbial diversity and Lactobacillus abundance and the enrichment of pro-inflammatory genera, such as Alistipes and Akkermansia, which can exacerbate systemic inflammation and hormonal imbalance [11–13]. These findings suggest that alterations in the gut microbiota directly or indirectly affect ovarian function through metabolic and immune pathways.

Ovarian dysfunction is also associated with metabolomic alterations. In addition to local hormonal dysregulation, ovarian failure is characterized by systemic metabolic remodeling [14]. Patients with ovarian insufficiency exhibit dysregulation of amino acid and energy metabolism, including tryptophan, arginine, and lipid pathways [15–17]. These metabolic shifts modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and steroid hormone synthesis, influencing follicular development and ovulatory function. Furthermore, serum metabolites reflect the dynamic interplay between the gut microbiota and the endocrine system, serving as biochemical links between peripheral metabolism and reproductive health. Therefore, profiling serum metabolite changes can aid in identifying potential biomarkers and elucidating the systemic pathophysiology of ovarian failure. However, few studies have performed integrated analyses of the gut microbiome and serum metabolome data in ovarian failure. Additionally, the mechanistic links between ovarian aging, dysbiosis, and host metabolic alterations have not been systematically investigated.

In this study, young and aged mice were subjected to fecal metagenomics and serum metabolomics analyses. This study aimed to identify key microbial groups, metabolic signatures, and pathways associated with ovarian failure through comparative microbial community composition analysis, functional pathway prediction, and serum metabolite profiling. Additionally, correlation analyses were performed to explore potential interactions between distinct gut microbes and altered serum metabolites. This integrated approach provides novel insights into the microbial and metabolic alterations associated with ovarian failure and improves our understanding of the complex host-microbiome-metabolite interactions underlying female reproductive health.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Experimental Design

Adult female C57BL/6J mice aged 7 weeks (W7 group) and 12 months (M12 group) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mice were maintained under the following conditions: circadian cycle: 12 h light/dark cycle; temperature: 22 °C ± 2 °C; relative humidity: 55% ±10%; diet: standard rodent diet; and access to filtered water ad libitum. The animals were allowed to acclimatize to the laboratory environment for 7 days.

The mice were randomized into the following two groups: the W7 group (aged 7 weeks), which represents mice at physiological reproductive age, and the M12 group (aged 12 months), which represents mice exhibiting age-associated ovarian functional decline. Age groups were selected based on previous studies, which revealed that female C57BL/6J mice exhibit decreased ovarian follicle counts and estradiol (E2) levels at about 12 months of age, which is consistent with reproductive aging phenotypes [18, 19].

In this study, 11 mice were randomized into the W7 (n = 5) and M12 groups (n = 6). After sample collection, all the animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia. The studies involving animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Seventh Medical Center of Chinese Pepole’s Liberation Army General Hospital (S2025-024–01). The animal studies were performed according to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th Edition, 2011).

Sample Collection

Fecal and serum samples were collected at 7 weeks and 12 months of age, corresponding to the reproductive and ovarian failure stages, respectively. To collect the fecal samples, each mouse was placed in a clean, sterile cage and allowed to defecate freely for 30 min. The fresh feces were immediately collected in sterile Eppendorf tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. After euthanasia, blood samples were obtained via cardiac puncture using non-heparinized tubes. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and then centrifuged at 1,500 g and 4 °C for 10 min to separate the serum. The serum aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until metabolomic and biochemical analyses. All procedures were conducted under sterile conditions to prevent contamination.

Histological Analyses

The ovarian tissues from the W7 and M12 groups were collected immediately after euthanasia and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Next, the tissues were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sect. (5 μm thick) were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and mounted on glass slides. To perform hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained sequentially with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and mounting, the sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan) to assess ovarian morphology, follicle number, and structural integrity. The presence of healthy growing follicles, atretic follicles, and corpora lutea was recorded to evaluate ovarian function.

Fecal Metagenomic Analysis

Total fecal DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany; Cat. No. 51504) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). This study aimed to profile the taxonomic and functional composition of the gut microbiome. Thus, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) to ensure high-depth coverage. The raw reads were quality-filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39. The host DNA contamination was removed by mapping the reads to the Mus musculus GRCm39 reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.4.5. The clean reads were taxonomically classified with MetaPhlAn 4 against the ChocoPhlAn database (2023). Functional annotation was performed using HUMAnN 3 with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and UniRef90 databases. Microbial alpha diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) indices were calculated using QIIME2 v2023.5. Differentially abundant taxa between groups were identified using DESeq2 (adjusted p < 0.05). Discriminant taxa were further validated using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA score > 3.0, p < 0.05) as previously described [20].

Serum Metabolomic Analysis

Serum metabolomic profiling was performed using untargeted ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) with a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled to an AB TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, USA). Analyses were conducted in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes to capture a broad spectrum of metabolites. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase C18 column with a gradient elution program. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient was set as follows: 5% B at 0–2 min, 5–95% B at 2–15 min, and 95% B at 15–17 min, followed by re-equilibration to initial conditions. The mass spectrometer was operated with an ion spray voltage of ± 5.5 kV, a source temperature of 550 °C, a curtain gas at 35 psi, and an ion source gas 1 and gas 2 at 50 psi. Data were acquired in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode over an m/z range of 50–1,200. Raw LC-MS data were processed in PeakView v2.2 for peak detection, deconvolution, and alignment. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), were conducted using SIMCA v16.0 (Umetrics, Sweden) for exploratory analysis. Differentially abundant metabolites were defined based on a combined criterion of variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0 and p < 0.05 (Wilcoxon test), with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied where applicable. VIP values were used to rank metabolite importance rather than as the sole criterion for significance. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 with reference to the KEGG and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) for metabolite annotation [21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and R 4.2.2. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Means between two groups were compared using Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon test, while those between more than two groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Validation of Ovarian Failure Phenotype

In this study, the pathological features of ovarian dysfunction in aged mice were evaluated. The ovarian tissues from the two groups were subjected to HE staining. As shown in Fig. 1A, mice in the W7 group exhibited an intact ovarian architecture with multiple follicles, including primary, secondary, and mature antral follicles, at various developmental stages in the cortex. Physiological corpus luteum structures were observed in some individuals. The ovarian stroma appeared dense with well-distributed vascularization. In contrast, the ovaries in the M12 group exhibited decreased volume with a significant decrease in the number of follicles within the cortex. Mature follicles were limited, while atretic and empty follicular structures were abundant. Some follicles contained granulosa cells that had shed, undergone degeneration, or were necrotized. The stroma exhibited fibrotic changes with reduced vascularity. These findings indicate significant structural degeneration in the ovaries of aged mice, which is consistent with the typical histological features of decreased ovarian function.

Characteristics of the Fecal Microbiome in the W7 and M12 Groups

As shown in TableS1, high-quality metagenomic sequencing data were obtained from the W7 and M12 groups and were characterized by sufficient numbers of clean bases (≥ 5.66 GB), high base accuracy (Q20 ≥ 96.39% and Q30 ≥ 91.52%), and effective removal of host DNA contaminants (≤ 10%). These findings indicate that all the samples were suitable for downstream microbiome analyses. Next, sequencing data quality and inter-sample consistency were evaluated (FigureS1). As shown in FigureS1A, the distribution of sequence abundance across different samples was balanced, indicating sufficient sequencing depth and no significant bias. Most sequence lengths clustered between 400 and 800 bp (FigureS1B), indicating high library quality and successful assembly. Inter-sample correlation analysis (FigureS1C) revealed that the correlation coefficients within groups were significantly greater than those between groups, indicating good consistency among biological replicates within each group. A Venn diagram (FigureS1D) further revealed shared core genomes (Core: 102,975 sequences) between the two mouse fecal microbiota groups, as well as distinct sequences specific to each group. These findings confirm the reliability of the metagenomic sequencing data in this study, with good reproducibility across samples. Thus, these data are suitable for subsequent analyses of microbial community structure and function. Additionally, we analyzed the taxonomic diversity and species composition of the microbiome across the W7 and M12 groups. The number of identified species ranged from 5,108 to 6,550 per sample, with an average of 5,626.9 ± 471.7 species per sample. The annotation rates at various taxonomic levels were also calculated and are summarized as follows: 100.00% at the kingdom level, 96.17% at the phylum level, 86.18% at the class level, 86.20% at the order level, 82.73% at the family level, 79.73% at the genus level, and 91.39% at the species level. These results highlight the high-quality taxonomic annotation achieved in our metagenomic analysis, which ensures comprehensive taxonomic profiling across both groups.

The diversity and community structure of the fecal microbiota in the W7 and M12 groups were examined (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A, the Shannon, Simpson, and inverse Simpson indices of the M12 group were not significantly greater than those of the W7 group (p = 0.082). This suggests a slight increase in the α diversity of the microbiota in aged mice. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances (Fig. 2B) revealed that PCoA1 and PCoA2 explained 85.74% and 9.26% of the community variance, respectively, with clear separation of samples along the PCoA1 axis. PCA (Fig. 2C) indicated that principal component 1 (PC1) and PC2 explained 75.04% and 15.06% of the variance, respectively. The samples of the W7 group clustered distinctly from those of the M12 group. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (Fig. 2D) further validated the differences in microbial community structure between the two groups. The stress value was 0.0315, indicating that this model adequately reflected the overall differences between samples. At the phylum level (Fig. 2E), the fecal microbiomes of the W7 and M12 groups were predominantly composed of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. To rigorously evaluate group-level differences, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed for the dominant taxa (Fig. 2H), revealing that the abundance of Firmicutes significantly decreased, whereas that of Bacteroidetes significantly increased in the M12 group. At the genus level (Fig. 2F), Lactobacillus, Clostridium, and Bacteroides were the predominant microbes in the W7 group. Wilcoxon-based differential analysis (Fig. 2I) further revealed Akkermansia and Desulfovibrio as the most significantly altered genera between groups. At the species level (Fig. 2G), a statistical comparison using Wilcoxon tests (Fig. 2J) revealed that Akkermansia muciniphila and Alistipes sp. CHKCI003 were among the most significantly differential species. These results indicate that a decrease in ovarian function is associated with statistically significant shifts in the gut microbial composition across multiple taxonomic levels. To further elucidate the association patterns and relative abundance distributions among the differentially abundant microbial communities, a co-occurrence network was constructed. The differentially abundant species were visualized using LEfSe analysis (FigureS2). Network analysis (FigureS2A) revealed complex co-occurrence relationships among microbial communities across different groups. The LDA score plot (FigureS2B) revealed that the strains enriched in the M12 group were primarily composed of Akkermansia muciniphila and Enterococcus hirae, whereas those enriched in the W7 group were mainly Clostridium sp. CAG226 and Lachnotalea sp. AF33 28.

Functional Prediction of the Gut Microbiome

As shown in Fig. 3A, the annotation based on eggNOG revealed significant differences in functional gene categories between the two groups. The enrichment of transcription, replication, recombination, and repair was similar between the W7 and M12 groups (10%–15%). Moreover, the enrichment of replication, recombination, and repair in the M12 group was slightly lower (about 2% difference). The enrichment of carbohydrate transport and metabolism in the W7 group was greater than that in the M12 group. Unknown functions accounted for about 20% of the genes in both groups. Overall, eggNOG-based functional annotation suggested a relative shift from carbohydrate-related functions in young mice toward other metabolic functions in aged mice. In the KEGG grade 2/3 pathway (Fig. 3B), carbohydrate metabolism pathways were enriched in the W7 group, whereas energy metabolism and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins were more abundant in the M12 group, indicating age-associated functional remodeling of the gut microbiome. In contrast, energy metabolism pathways were enriched in the M12 group (an increase in abundance of about 3%). Immune system pathways were enriched in the W7 group (about 1%), whereas the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins was enriched in the M12 group (about 3%). These results further indicate that age-associated functional remodeling of the gut microbiome is characterized by reduced carbohydrate metabolism and increased energy-related metabolic potential in aged mice. Further analysis of the specific KEGG pathways (Fig. 3C and FigureS3) revealed different population-specific enrichments. Folate biosynthesis and lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis were significantly enriched in the M12 group, whereas ABC transporters and quorum sensing were enriched in the W7 group. These pathway-level differences suggest altered microbial metabolic activities and host-microbe interactions during aging. Carbon cycle analysis (Fig. 3D) highlights the changes in the major contributing species. During the organic carbon oxidation process, the contribution rate of Lactobacillus murinus in the W7 group was about 85%, while that of Blautia sp. An249 in the M12 group was about 15%. With respect to carbon fixation, Clostridium sp. CAG:1024 and Clostridium sp. D5 were the predominant microbes in the W7 (about 30%) and M12 groups (about 10%), respectively. Fermentation in the W7 and M12 groups was driven mainly by Lactobacillus murinus (60–80%). However, the contribution rate of Bacteroides sp. CAG:545 to fermentation in the M12 group was about 15%. These findings indicate that aging is associated with a reorganization of key microbial contributors involved in carbon cycling processes. Antibiotic resistance gene analysis (Fig. 3E) revealed that the contribution of Bacteroides acidifaciens to β-lactam resistance in the M12 group was about 10% greater than that in the W7 group. Uncultured Flavonifractor sp. was the predominant contributor to tetracycline resistance in both groups. The contribution rate of Clostridium algidixylanolyticum to vancomycin resistance in the M12 group was about 10% greater than that in the W7 group. These findings suggest the increased potential for antibiotic resistance in the gut microbiome of aged mice. Taken together, the results of functional predictions based on eggNOG, KEGG, carbon cycle, and antibiotic resistance analyses consistently indicate that aged mice exhibit increased amino acid biosynthesis, energy metabolism, carbon cycle remodeling, and antibiotic resistance potential, whereas young mice exhibit increased activity of carbohydrate metabolism and immune-related pathways. To provide statistical support for functional differences, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied to key functional pathways. At KEGG level 3, several pathways, including the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and the adipocytokine signaling pathway, were significantly altered between the W7 and M12 groups (Fig. 3F). Further Wilcoxon-based analyses of carbon cycle-related enzymes (Fig. 3G) demonstrated that mannan endo − 1,4 − beta−mannosidase and beta−galactosidase were significantly differentially abundant, indicating age-associated remodeling of microbial carbon utilization. Similarly, Wilcoxon analysis of ARGs revealed that ermB, multidrug-transporter, and other ARGs were significantly altered in the M12 group (Fig. 3H), suggesting that the antibiotic resistance potential of aged mice was enhanced. Taken together, the results of functional predictions based on eggNOG, KEGG, carbon cycle, and ARG analyses, which are supported by the results of non-parametric statistical tests, consistently indicate that aged mice exhibit increased amino acid biosynthesis, energy metabolism, carbon cycle remodeling, and antibiotic resistance potential, whereas young mice exhibit increased activity of carbohydrate metabolism and immune-related pathways.

Serum Metabolomic Profiles of the W7 and M12 Groups

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis revealed 1,234 metabolites. Among these, 246 exhibited significantly different abundances between the W7 and M12 groups (146 upregulated and 100 downregulated metabolites). The top 20 differentially abundant metabolites are summarized in Table S2. As shown in Fig. 4A, the volcano diagram based on the HMDB category revealed significant changes in multiple categories, including alkaloids, derivatives, and benzenoid compounds. A detailed volcano diagram with annotations (Fig. 4B) highlights that the levels of key metabolites, such as isoleucyl-hydroxyproline and fumigaclavine C, were significantly downregulated, whereas those of uric acid were upregulated in the M12 group. PCA (Fig. 4C) revealed that PC1 and PC2 explained 27.71% and 16.36% of the total variance, respectively. The metabolites of the W7 group clustered distinctly from those of the M12 group. PLS-DA validation through permutation tests (Fig. 4D) indicated that the R2Y and Q2 values significantly decreased with increasing permutation. Additionally, the Q2 regression line was truncated below zero, indicating a lack of model overfitting. The PLS-DA score plot (Fig. 4E) demonstrated significant differences between the W7 and M12 groups (t1: 25.7% variance; t2: 13.5% variance). The corresponding loading plots (Fig. 4F) revealed metabolites with a VIP > 1. Dihydrocorticosterone and capryloyl glycine contributed the most to group segregation. OPLS-DA further supported these findings. The permutation test (Fig. 4G) confirmed the robustness of the OPLS-DA model. The OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. 4H) revealed a clear separation between groups (orthogonal T score of 1: 19.1% and T score of 1: 19.8%). The corresponding load plots (Fig. 4I) revealed metabolites with a VIP > 1. Isoleucyl-hydroxyproline and fumigaclavine C were the main contributors, which was consistent with the results of the PLS-DA. Thus, the serum metabolome of aged mice exhibits distinct metabolic profiles. To further explore the internal structure and coordinated alterations of the metabolomic data beyond multivariate discrimination, correlation analysis of the top 10 differentially abundant metabolites was performed (Fig. 4J). The correlation heatmap revealed strong positive and negative associations among metabolites involved in amino acid activity and related metabolic pathways, indicating coordinated metabolic remodeling in aged mice. These findings suggest that ovarian aging is associated with systematic and interrelated alterations in serum metabolic profiles.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis of Differential Metabolites

As shown in Fig. 5A, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed important pathways related to differentially abundant metabolites. Among these pathways, pathways related to D-amino acid biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism were significantly enriched after multiple testing correction, whereas several other pathways were not significantly enriched. These results indicate that ovarian aging is primarily associated with coordinated alterations in amino acid and lipid metabolism, with tryptophan metabolism emerging as one of the most consistently enriched pathways in aged mice. Pathway topology analysis (Fig. 5B) indicated that D-amino acid biosynthesis was highly significant and strongly influenced by pathways. Thus, D-amino acid biosynthesis is a major contributor to metabolic differences. At the KEGG functional category level (Fig. 5C), metabolic pathways were the most enriched, especially amino acid metabolism and energy metabolism. Further subdivision (Fig. 5D) indicated that D-amino acid and carbon metabolism were the most significantly enriched subcategories in the M12 group. Metabolite-pathway associations were visualized in a chord diagram (Fig. 5E). Key amino acid metabolites, such as L-tryptophan and methionine were closely related to amino acid biosynthesis and tryptophan metabolism. The Sankey plot (Fig. 5F) further illustrates the flow of metabolites to pathways and KEGG classes. Amino acid metabolites contribute primarily to metabolism, especially amino acid metabolism. The cyclic enrichment map (Fig. 5G) highlights the significant enrichment of aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and central carbon metabolism in cancer, supporting their consistent participation in population differences. The differential abundance scores (Fig. 5H) revealed that amino D-acid biosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation were significantly upregulated, whereas carbon metabolism was downregulated in the M12 group. Z score analysis (Fig. 5I) revealed that the central carbon metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism pathways of cancer exhibited stable differences in samples from different age groups, confirming the robustness of the alterations in these pathways.

Correlation Between Gut Microbiota and Serum Metabolites

Compared with that in the W7 group, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes was significantly greater in the M12 group (Fig. 6A). At the genus level (Fig. 6B), the proportions of Alistipes and Akkermansia were significantly increased in the M12 group. At the species level (Fig. 6C), Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes sp. CAG435, and Alistipes sp. CHKCI003 were identified as the most prominently enriched taxa in the M12 group, suggesting age-associated remodeling of the gut microbial structure. To explore the potential microbiota-metabolite interplay underlying ovarian failure, we further integrated gut microbiome shifts (Fig. 6) with serum metabolomic alterations (Fig. 4and Fig. 5). Notably, pathway enrichment of differential serum metabolites consistently highlighted tryptophan metabolism as one of the most affected metabolic modules (Fig. 5D), indicating that systemic amino acid metabolism is substantially altered in M12 mice. Importantly, the functional profiling of the gut microbiome also supported this observation. LEfSe analysis based on metagenomic functional annotation revealed tryptophan metabolism as one of the key pathways differentiating between the W7 and M12 groups (Fig. 3D), demonstrating that the microbial functional potential is concordant with the metabolomic pathway-level disturbance. Taken together, the findings of the concurrent enrichment of Akkermansia and Alistipes taxa and the consistent alteration of tryptophan metabolism at both the metabolite and microbial functional levels suggest a coordinated shift along the gut microbiota-tryptophan metabolism axis in aged mice. To visualize these associations, a mechanistic diagram was constructed (Fig. 7). This schematic diagram indicates that the increased abundance of Alistipes and Akkermansia in the M12 group, especially A. muciniphila, Alistipes sp. CAG435, and Alistipes sp. CHKCI003, may contribute to altered tryptophan metabolism. The proposed microbiota-metabolite interactions underlying the metabolic imbalance in mice with ovarian failure are shown in Fig. 7.

Discussion

A decline in ovarian function represents a key shift in female reproductive biology, with regulatory effects on endocrine balance, systemic metabolism, and overall health [22–25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ovarian failure is not an isolated reproductive disorder but a systemic disease related to metabolism and the microbiota [26, 27]. Therefore, the simultaneous analysis of microbial and metabolic alterations may provide useful insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of ovarian dysfunction.

In this study, the fecal metagenome and serum metabolome of the W7 and M12 groups were compared. Our results showed that the microbial and metabolic profiles varied between the two groups. Ovarian failure was associated with decreased Firmicutes proportions, increased Bacteroidetes proportions, and enrichment of Alistipes and Akkermansia. At the species level, the proportions of Akkermansia muciniphila and Alistipes spp. were significantly increased in the M12 group. Moreover, serum metabolomics revealed 246 significantly altered metabolites. Several of these metabolites were involved in amino acid and energy metabolism. Pathway enrichment analysis suggested that tryptophan metabolism is a key node in the microbial and metabolic datasets, indicating a strong microbiota-metabolite interaction in ovarian failure.

Interestingly, although previous studies have reported reduced gut microbial diversity during reproductive aging or ovarian failure, we observed a mild increase in α diversity in aged mice. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in animal models, age windows, housing conditions, or sequencing strategies. In addition, increased α diversity does not necessarily indicate a healthier microbiome, as aging-associated dysbiosis may involve the expansion of low-abundance or opportunistic taxa, leading to higher diversity indices but altered community structure and function. Similar observations have also been reported in other aging-related microbiome studies. Metagenomic analysis revealed that the gut microbiota of the M12 group underwent remodeling. The increased abundance of Bacteroides and decreased abundance of Firmicutes in aged mice are consistent with the findings of previous studies reporting the correlation of microbial dysregulation with reproductive aging and ovarian insufficiency [28]. For example, Wen et al. reported that a decreased Firmicutes proportion was associated with ovarian dysfunction in mouse models [29]. The enrichment of Alistipes and Akkermansia in aged mice has also been reported in previous studies, which have demonstrated that metabolic stress and inflammatory conditions increase the levels of these taxa [30–32]. Akkermansia muciniphila is beneficial for mucosal barrier function and host energy homeostasis [33, 34]. In this study, ovarian failure was associated with increased proportions of Akkermansia muciniphila. This discrepancy highlights the context-dependent roles of microbial taxa. Although A. muciniphila typically contributes to mucosal protection, its expansion during reproductive aging may represent a compensatory response to epithelial stress or altered immune signaling rather than a purely beneficial event. Furthermore, estrogen depletion alters gut mucin thickness and epithelial renewal, establishing ecological conditions that selectively favor Akkermansia growth. Recent studies have demonstrated that estrogen deficiency suppresses MUC2 secretion, which expands the ecological niche for mucin-degrading bacteria. Thus, the upregulation of A. muciniphila in aged mice may be a secondary consequence of estrogen withdrawal rather than a primary cause of ovarian decline. This mechanism has been observed in ovariectomized models but has rarely been reported in naturally aged mice, highlighting the novelty of the findings.

However, the downregulation of Lactobacillus murinus abundance in aged mice was not consistent with its well-established role in supporting reproductive health. Previous studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus species are predominant in the vaginal and intestinal microbiota of healthy females and that they are involved in estrogen metabolism and mucosal protection [35–38]. Thus, the depletion of Lactobacillus in the M12 group may have exacerbated both local and systemic hormonal imbalances. This shift can disrupt estrogen-dependent microbial feedback mechanisms, further amplifying reproductive endocrine decline. The downregulation of Lactobacillus abundance is also associated with altered β-glucuronidase activity, a reduction in enterohepatic estrogen recycling, and an acceleration of estrogen downregulation, which is characteristic of ovarian failure. This mechanistic link between Lactobacillus depletion and reduced estrogen bioavailability provides a plausible pathway consistent with the findings of this study.

Serum metabolomic analysis revealed systemic metabolic alterations associated with reproductive aging. Among the 246 differentially abundant metabolites, several were enriched in amino acid and energy metabolism pathways. This finding is consistent with the findings of recent studies, which demonstrated that amino acid dysregulation was closely linked to ovarian dysfunction [39–41]. In particular, tryptophan metabolism was the central altered pathway in this study. In addition to serving as a precursor for serotonin and kynurenine, tryptophan regulates immune and endocrine functions [42, 43]. Previous studies have demonstrated that disruption of tryptophan metabolism alters ovarian hormone regulation and reproductive aging [44–46]. Mechanistically, the increased abundance of microbial taxa (Alistipes and Akkermansia) known to express tryptophanase and indole-producing enzymes suggests a shift in tryptophan catabolism toward pro-inflammatory kynurenine metabolites. Alistipes promotes kynurenine production via tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO)-mediated cleavage. Kynurenine and its downstream metabolites activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, which inhibits granulosa cell proliferation and suppresses ovarian steroidogenic enzymes, such as CYP19A1. Therefore, the findings of this study support a microbe-driven kynurenine overproduction model that may directly repress ovarian steroidogenesis.

In this study, we hypothesized that ovarian dysfunction-associated physiological stress triggers the overgrowth of taxa, such as Alistipes and Akkermansia, which enhance the microbial conversion of tryptophan toward the kynurenine branch. This metabolic redirection promotes local ovarian inflammation and interferes with estrogen receptor signaling, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle between dysbiosis and ovarian decline. Metabolomic analysis also revealed elevated levels of D-amino acids, which are reported to be associated with oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in oocytes. These changes support the hypothesis that microbial amino acid metabolism contributes to the deterioration of ovarian microenvironments. The downregulation of uric acid and the upregulation of metabolites, such as ergothioneine, further suggest that oxidative stress modulation is involved in ovarian aging [47, 48]. The upregulation of antioxidants, such as ergothioneine, may reflect a compensatory response to chronic oxidative stress. However, the suppression of carbohydrate metabolism pathways in aged mice, which is distinct from the hyperactivated glycolysis reported in chemically induced ovarian failure, suggests that natural reproductive aging may involve an energy-conservation phenotype. This metabolic suppression resembles the “energy-saving mode” reported in aged intestinal microbiota and may indicate reduced mitochondrial efficiency or impaired nutrient absorption.

The integration of microbiome and metabolome data revealed that tryptophan metabolism is a convergent pathway linking microbial and systemic metabolic changes. The enrichment of Alistipes and Akkermansia and their correlation with tryptophan-derived metabolites suggested a potential mechanism through which the gut microbiota influenced host metabolic reprogramming during reproductive aging. These findings were consistent with those of previous studies reporting that microbial tryptophan metabolism regulates estrogen signaling and the immune response [49]. For example, Crump et al. demonstrated that microbial metabolites derived from tryptophan regulate ovarian function via the PD-L1 pathway, providing a mechanistic basis supporting the findings of this study [50]. However, some studies have reported opposite trends. For example, the proportion of Lactobacillus and the activity of serotonin-derived tryptophan pathways are reported to be upregulated in aged animals. These discrepancies can be attributed to differences in age definitions (11 months vs. >18 months), diet composition, or sequencing depth. The findings of this study suggest that the early phase of ovarian decline (perimenopausal equivalent) favors kynurenine-dominant metabolism. In contrast, late-life ovarian failure may favor serotonin-dominant pathways, indicating a stage-dependent metabolic pattern. Moreover, altered microbial contributions to carbon cycle processes may have reinforced the systemic energy imbalances detected in the serum metabolome. In particular, the increased abundance of Chlamydia abortus (4.33%) in the M12 group must be carefully interpreted. This may represent a true microbial shift linked to immune suppression during reproductive aging or reflect environmental contamination or transient colonization [51]. C. abortus is a known reproductive pathogen. The detection of C. abortus may indicate that latent infection can exacerbate ovarian dysfunction through chronic inflammation [52]. Alternatively, the occurrence of C. abortus may be related to age-dependent immune weakening, increasing susceptibility to low-grade opportunistic colonization. C. abortus is rarely reported in murine fecal samples. Thus, further validation via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction or reanalysis of mapping specificity is essential to ensure that this is not a strain-level misclassification.

In summary, reproductive aging is characterized by coordinated remodeling of the gut microbiota and serum metabolome, which converge on amino acid metabolism, especially tryptophan-related pathways. This microbe-metabolite interaction provides a mechanistic framework in which dysbiosis aggravates ovarian decline by promoting inflammatory and metabolic stress through the kynurenine pathway. The findings of this study suggest that alteration of microbial tryptophan metabolism through various mechanisms, such as inhibition of IDO/TDO activity, supplementation with Lactobacillus murinus, or enrichment of indole-producing commensals, may attenuate ovarian aging. Future interventional studies must focus on probiotics, prebiotics, or targeted microbial metabolite inhibitors to determine the causality of the gut-ovary axis.

This study has several limitations. The sample size of the study was small, and only two time points were evaluated, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and prevent longitudinal comparisons. Additionally, this study focused on the gut microbiome and serum metabolome without directly examining ovarian microenvironment metabolites, such as those in the follicular fluid that are potentially related to ovarian function. Future studies should incorporate local metabolomic profiling and causal validation models to further clarify the gut-ovary metabolic axis.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence of pathway-level integration for the association of mouse reproductive aging with distinct alterations in the gut microbiome and serum metabolome. Metagenomic analysis revealed decreased Firmicutes proportions, increased Bacteroidetes proportions, and enrichment of Alistipes and Akkermansia. Moreover, metabolomic profiling revealed significant alterations in amino acid and energy metabolism. Pathway-level integration analyses consistently highlighted tryptophan metabolism as a convergent pathway linking microbial dysbiosis to systemic metabolic reprogramming. These findings expand our understanding of the host-microbiome-metabolite interactions underlying ovarian functional decline and suggest that specific gut microbial taxa and metabolic signatures are potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Future studies should validate these mechanisms and explore microbiome-based or metabolite-based interventions to preserve female reproductive health.
