Abstract
We report the measurement of the Bi 214 {}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi} beta-decay spectrum to the ground state of Po 214 {}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po} using the XENONnT detector. This decay is classified as first-forbidden non-unique, for which theoretical predictions require detailed nuclear structure modeling. A dedicated identification algorithm isolates a high-purity sample of ground-state beta-decays, explicitly excluding events with associated gamma-rays emission. By comparing the measured spectrum, which covers energies up to 3.27 MeV 3.27\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV} , with several nuclear models, we find that the prediction based on the conserved vector current (CVC) hypothesis provides the best description of the data. Using this dataset, we additionally derive charge and light yield curves for electronic recoils, extending detector response modeling up to the MeV scale.

Understanding the nuclear structure of isotopes like

II
Theoretical Framework

Beta-decays are interactions mediated by massive
W^{\pm}
bosons between hadronic and leptonic currents. Assuming the decaying nucleon does not interact with the other
A-1
nucleons, the probability density of the beta-decay electron being emitted with kinetic energy
E_{e}
can be expressed as
Commins 2007
:

\displaystyle P(E_{e})dE_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{\text{F}}}{(\hbar c)^{6}}\frac{1}{2\pi^{3}\hbar}C(E_{e})

\displaystyle\times p_{e}cE_{e}(E_{0}-E_{e})^{2}F_{0}(Z,E_{e})dE_{e},

(1)

where
p_{e}
is the momentum of the electron,
E_{0}
the beta-decay endpoint energy,
Z
the proton number,
G_{F}
the Fermi constant, and
F_{0}(Z,E_{e})
the Fermi function. The nuclear structure information is encoded by the shape factor
C(E_{e})
, which can generally be decomposed into vector, axial-vector, and mixed vector-axial-vector components:

\displaystyle C(\omega_{e})

\displaystyle=g_{V}^{2}C_{V}(\omega_{e})+g_{A}^{2}C_{A}(\omega_{e})+g_{V}g_{A}C_{VA}(\omega_{e}),

(2)

where the unitless kinematic quantity
\omega_{e}=W_{e}/m_{e}c^{2}
is used, and
g_{V}
and
g_{A}
are the corresponding coupling constants for the vector and axial-vector components
Feynman and Gell-Mann 1958
. The contribution of the shape factors
C_{V}
,
C_{A}
, and
C_{VA}
is characterized by the nuclear matrix elements (NMEs).

The theoretical description of the beta-decay has undergone significant advancements in recent years, which have been essential for the background modeling of the XENON experiments
Haselschwardt et al. 2020
;
Mougeot 2016
, addressing the challenges when moving towards heavier nuclei and higher beta-decay Q-values. The complications include incorporating a large number of different transition modes in NMEs
Ramalho and Suhonen 2024
, modeling strong quenching of the weak coupling constant
g_{A}
due to many-nucleon correlations
Zhi et al. 2013
involving numerous approaches
Deppisch and Suhonen 2016
;
Kostensalo and Suhonen 2017
;
Toivanen and Suhonen 1998
;
Caurier et al. 2012
;
Iachello and Isacker 1991
, and applying the mesonic enhancement correction from the effects of nuclear medium
Kostensalo and Suhonen 2018
.

For the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay, the transition to the ground state is classified as first-forbidden non-unique, which requires non-diagonal NMEs for its description
Ejiri et al. 2019
. The simplest reference is the zeroth-order spectrum, calculated analytically under the allowed approximation using the BetaShape code
Mougeot 2016
, and shown in Fig.
2
as
Allowed
. A more complete theoretical description combines nuclear shell-model calculations with next-to-leading-order corrections under the conserved vector current (CVC) hypothesis. This approach enforces isospin symmetry of the strong interaction, fixes the weak vector coupling constant to
g_{V}=1.0
, and directly constrains the relevant vector form factors
Hardy and Towner 2005
;
Haaranen et al. 2017
;
Kumar et al. 2021
. Building on the same framework, a recent study introduced a fit procedure in which the small relativistic vector NMEs (sNMEs) are varied simultaneously across all transitions to reproduce measured branching ratios
Ramalho and Suhonen 2024
. From this study, we obtain the three additional spectra shown in Fig.
2
: the
CVC
prediction, and the two degenerate solutions of the sNME fit, labeled
sNME1
and
sNME2
. These results illustrate that the choice of sNME values can substantially modify the predicted spectral shape, making experimental input essential to discriminate between models.

III
Experimental Method

III.1
XENONnT Detector and Datasets

The XENONnT experiment
Aprile et al. 2024a
uses xenon as both the target and detection medium inside a cylindrical dual-phase time projection chamber (TPC) that is
1.33\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
in diameter and
1.49\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
tall. The TPC contains
5.9\text{\,}\mathrm{t}
of liquid xenon (LXe), with a thin layer of gaseous xenon (GXe) at the top. An interaction with xenon atoms inside the TPC produces both prompt scintillation light, defined as the S1 signal, and ionization electrons. Meshed electrodes are used to apply an electric drift field in LXe (
23\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
) and an extraction field in the region of the liquid-gas interface (
2.9\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
). This allows the free electrons to drift upward towards the LXe surface, where they are extracted into the GXe and generate secondary scintillation via electroluminescence, defined as the S2 signal. A total of 494 Hamamatsu R11410-21 photomultiplier tubes (PMTs)
Antochi et al. 2021
are arranged at the top and bottom of the TPC to capture both the S1 and S2 signals, measured in units of photoelectrons (PE). A MeV beta-electron in the TPC generates an S1 with a signal area of approximately
10^{4}
PE and an S2 of order
10^{6}
PE, while a MeV alpha generates an S1 of several
10^{4}
PE and an S2 of order
10^{4}
PE.

An event consists of a pair of S1 and S2 signals. The pairing enables reconstruction of the deposited energy and the three-dimensional interaction position, where the xy-position is derived from the S2 scintillation photon hit pattern on the top PMT array and the z-position from the time difference between S1 and S2. The position reconstruction permits the definition of a fiducial volume, selecting only the data from the interior of the TPC, and thereby avoiding the background events originating from the decay of radioactive contaminants accumulated on the TPC wall. The dual signal also allows the discrimination between interactions with atomic electrons and with nuclei. The former is referred to as electronic recoil (ER) events, and the latter as nuclear recoils (NR) events. In dark matter searches, WIMP signals have the NR signature. In this work, the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay signal of interest has the ER signature.

To achieve a minimal background, the XENONnT experiment is operated at the INFN Laboratori Nazionali del Gran Sasso (LNGS) underground laboratory. The cryostat containing the TPC is the innermost of three nested detectors. It is enclosed by a neutron veto
Aprile et al. 2025b
that is itself nested within a muon veto. Both veto detectors are water Cherenkov detectors and are optically separated. All detector construction materials were selected for low radioactivity
Aprile et al. 2022b
. The LXe is constantly purified via both gaseous and liquid purification systems
Aprile et al. 2024a
that remove electronegative impurities. The noble gas
{}^{\makebox[7.97226pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 85$}}_{\makebox[7.97226pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Kr}
beta-decay isotope, which is intrinsically present in xenon, was removed via a dedicated cryogenic distillation system
Aprile et al. 2022c
with the remnant measured
Lindemann and Simgen 2014
. For the radon background, a radon removal system also based on cryogenic distillation was installed to continuously remove the radon remnant from the TPC
Aprile et al. 2017a
. Finally, degreasing, etching, passivating, and screening were performed to ensure low radon emanation and electronegative impurities for all TPC components
Aprile et al. 2022b
. With all these background removal measures, XENONnT has achieved the lowest ever ER background among dark matter detectors
Murra et al. 2022
.

This study includes 95.1 days of data from the first science run (SR0) and 186.5 days from the second (SR1) of the XENONnT experiment. These datasets contain
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decays from natural
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
emanation from detector materials at a
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
rate of approximately
1.8\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro Bq}
of LXe for SR0 and
1.0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro Bq}
for SR1
Aprile et al. 2025c
.

To obtain sufficient statistics for this study, the most relevant XENONnT dataset is from a calibration campaign during SR1 using a
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
source (SR1
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 226$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Ra}
into a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}

\times

2\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}

{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}

This tagging allows the determination of the alpha rate by isolating and counting the alpha events with negligible background. The derivation of the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}

3

\sim

. The event is first triggered by the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
S1 signal and is shortly followed by a significantly larger S1 signal from
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
after a time difference of
\Delta t
. The S2s arrive a drift time later. The
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
S2 signal is suppressed as expected for an alpha event. The
\Delta t
is effectively preserved by the S2s because the alpha signals are localized in position: the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
alphas travel on an order of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
in LXe from the preceding
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decay.

A complication arises when considering the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay into excited states that subsequently produce gammas from nuclear de-excitation. Given that the de-excitation half-life is in the nanosecond range, the S1s of these gammas are always merged with the S1 of the beta. However, unlike alpha particles, the gamma-rays can travel a few centimeters before interacting with another xenon atom
Berger et al. 1999
. This shift in positions of the interaction site results in
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
events with more than two S2s, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Notably, however, among all the de-excitation gammas of the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay as illustrated in Fig.
1
, there is always one gamma photon with an energy at least
609\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}
. We can effectively use these high-energy gamma-decays to tag the excited states.

III.3
Event Selection

This analysis relies on the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
tagging algorithm
, a method that exploits the time coincidence between the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay and the subsequent
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
alpha-decay. Although
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
tagging is commonly employed in low-background experiments
Aprile et al. 2024b
;
Gando et al. 2016
;
Arnold et al. 2015b
, our implementation specifically addresses the complexity introduced by gamma emissions accompanying
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decays into excited nuclear states. These emissions produce events with multiple signals, complicating straightforward time-based matching. To reliably pair the correct S1 and S2 signals, our algorithm compares the time differences
\Delta t
between all combinatoric pairs formed by the three largest S1 signals (to account for possible spurious signals) and up to ten largest S2 signals. We identify events in which one S1 pair and one S2 pair have matching
\Delta t
values within a tolerance of
\pm
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
.
Peaks from S2 signals with areas below
1\text{\,}500\text{\,}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}
, unphysical top-bottom PMT area distributions, or inappropriate timing (such as spurious pulses that occur shortly after the main pulse and can be reconstructed as separate peaks), are excluded from the matching procedure. We use simulations, described in Sec.
III.4
, to quantify the matching efficiency, which is
>91\%
for true ground-state events, providing validation of the matching approach.

Following the matching procedure, we apply a series of
quality selection criteria
to ensure high-quality event reconstruction and minimize contributions from backgrounds or poorly reconstructed signals. Events must pass the fiducial volume constraint, defined by a vertical depth at least
5\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}
away from the top and bottom boundaries of the TPC to avoid regions with field inhomogeneities
Aprile et al. 2024c
. A minimum separation of
25\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
between the two primary S1 signals is required to prevent potential merging of the corresponding S2 signals, given a typical S2 width at
90\%
of the area of up to
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
. This criterion results in only a modest loss of approximately
3\%
of events, as shorter S1 intervals typically produce merged, unresolved S2 signals. This requirement also eliminates the background contribution of
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 212$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
events originating from the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 220$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{R}
n decay chain, with a similar topology as the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
but with a half-life of
300\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
. Additional criteria based on the alpha S1 signal area are employed to remove events originating from other alpha-decays. These selection steps reduce the dataset to approximately
165\text{\,}000\text{\,}\mathrm{e}
vents, which include both ground-state and excited-state decays.

The final
ground-state selection
step specifically isolates
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decays directly to the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
ground state. We require exactly two S2 signals above
1\text{\,}500\text{\,}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}
, corresponding to the beta-decay and the subsequent alpha-decay. To suppress events with additional gamma interactions, any third S2 is required to be smaller than
10\text{\,}000\text{\,}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}
. This selection effectively excludes most excited-state decays, which, due to selection rules, de-excite through high energy gamma-decays  leading to clearly identifiable additional S2 signals. Although further criteria, such as a selection on the S2 width to remove partially merged signals, were tested, they provided no significant improvement beyond the initial matching procedure. This selection yields
56\text{\,}965\text{\,}\mathrm{e}
vents, corresponding to 31% of the events that passed the quality criteria.

Using detailed simulations (described in Sec.
III.4
), we evaluate the performance and purity of the selection procedure. After applying matching and quality criteria, the requirement of precisely two S2 signals achieves a signal acceptance of around
94\%
(the fraction of true ground-state events passing the final selection) and a background rejection of approximately
93\%
(the fraction of excited-state events removed by the selection).

The fraction of excited-state events that pass the ground-state selection criteria (approximately
20\%
of the total selected events) predominantly belongs to two categories of roughly equal proportions. The first category consists of events in which the emitted gammas completely escape the active detection volume. These events are reconstructed at energies lower than genuine ground-state events, as only the energy deposited by the beta electron is observed. The second category comprises events where gamma-induced signals fully merge with the beta electron signals, resulting in artificially higher reconstructed energies due to the combined gamma and beta contributions. Both categories distort the measured shape of the ground-state
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decay energy spectrum. As we compare simulated
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay spectra to measured data, we model these leakage events from decays to excited states in addition to the ground-state decays.

III.4
Simulations and Signal Modeling

Simulations for this analysis are produced using the XENONnT framework
XENON Collaboration et al. 2025a
, which incorporates a full-chain simulation from energy deposition to final event reconstruction. Initial interactions, including both the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
and subsequent
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
decays, are simulated with Geant4
Agostinelli et al. 2003
;
Allison et al. 2006
. This step considers all relevant nuclear decay characteristics, including the full decay scheme of
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
, associated branching ratios, and emitted gamma energies. To investigate different theoretical models of the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
ground-state beta-decay spectrum introduced in Fig.
2
, we adjusted the input beta energy distribution provided to Geant4 accordingly, creating simulation datasets for each considered model.

Subsequent stages of the simulation that include clustering of deposited energies, generation and propagation of photons and electrons using detector-specific efficiency maps, electron drifting, and waveform simulation are performed using the fuse package
XENON Collaboration et al. 2025a
. These simulated waveforms closely replicate those recorded by the data acquisition system of the XENONnT detector, allowing for the use of an identical processing pipeline as for the real data with the straxen package
XENON Collaboration et al. 2025b
.

For accurate modeling of events with energies up to
3.27\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
, which is substantially higher than the typical range targeted by the dark matter search analyses (
<
100\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
)
Aprile et al. 2025d
, we extended the existing fuse framework with a specialized simulation workflow. Two critical improvements were implemented: a more physically motivated clustering algorithm for energy depositions, and a data-driven yield model specifically tailored for high-energy electron recoils. The latter describes the photon and electron yields per unit energy.

Clustering of energy depositions is necessary because the data-driven yield models, such as the ones provided by NEST
Szydagis et al. 2011
;
Szydagis et al. 2025
, require input energies to attribute the correct amount of photons and electrons generated by individual interactions. Due to the incomplete understanding of the underlying microphysics at these scales, energy depositions must be aggregated into physically meaningful clusters before applying the yield model. The default clustering method used in XENONnT
Aprile et al. 2025d
groups energy depositions solely based on spatial and temporal proximity. While adequate at lower energies, this method is inadequate in describing complex high-energy event topologies. The new framework employs a lineage clustering method inspired by NEST, which groups interactions based on their physical properties and interaction history. Energy depositions from electron tracks are grouped into single clusters, except for Bremsstrahlung photons, which form separate clusters. Gamma interactions instead initiate new clusters for each distinct interaction site, effectively differentiating gamma from beta events.

Unlike the default NEST model, which employs separate yield models for gamma and beta interactions, our approach uses a single, unified yield model. The apparent difference between the two cases originates from the underlying physics: gamma-rays can interact via Compton scattering and therefore deposit their energy at multiple spatially separated sites, while electrons typically deposit all energy in a contained interaction cluster. Each site contributes its own cluster with energy
E_{i}
, and since the charge and light yields depend non-linearly on
E_{i}
, the combined yield of these clusters differs from that of a single deposition with the same total energy. In our analysis, this distinction emerges naturally from clustering: the yield difference between beta-decays and gamma-decays is not imposed through separate models, but arises directly from the physical interaction topology when combined with the clustering method.

We extract a new parametrisation of the ER yield model using single beta-decay events, which provide well-defined single-cluster energy depositions. For this purpose, we use
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 212$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Pb}
decays from
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 220$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
calibrations together with SR0
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
decays from this analysis, ensuring broad coverage across the full energy range. The yield parameters are obtained by fitting the ten-parameter NEST function
Szydagis et al. 2011
to the measured charge yield, with the light yield calculated as the complementary fraction of quanta.

We account for residual leakage of excited-state events into the ground-state selection by comparing yields in simulation from truth-level ground-state beta-decay events with those obtained when the excited-state events that passed the selection are included. The observed difference is implemented as an effective correction to the charge and light yield in data. Since the leakage events concentrate above
1.8\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
, this region is excluded from the fit.

We validated the resulting yield model using two independent datasets: the clean single-cluster beta selection described above, and the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
events including excited-state decays that produce combined beta and gamma topologies. These events, with multiple interaction sites and complex clustering, provide a stringent test of the clustering and yield framework. Although the yield parameters were derived only from clean beta-decays below
1.8\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
, the model reproduces the measured S1 and S2 distributions at the percent level across the full energy range, up to the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
endpoint. By contrast, simulations based on the default NEST yields and spatial clustering showed discrepancies of up to
50\%
in S2 for beta–gamma events above a few hundred keV. The agreement obtained here therefore supports the changes in both the clustering method and the yield parametrisation: reproducing multi-cluster topologies at high energy is only possible if both components are accurate.

The clustering procedure and yield parametrisation introduced above are designed specifically to achieve accurate reproduction of the individual S1 and S2 distributions between data and simulations. They do not directly guarantee agreement in the reconstructed energy spectra, which is instead addressed in the following section through dedicated corrections.

IV.1
Models to Data Comparison

In this analysis, the event selection is rendered effectively free of background contamination as a result of the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
identification algorithm. The primary remaining background arises from gamma leakage from excited-state decays into the ground-state selection. Since this leakage is accurately modeled by our simulations, which incorporate well-measured gamma emissions from
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
, we can compare data directly to full-chain simulations.

We combine data from the three experimental datasets: SR0 (
7\text{\,}708\text{\,}\mathrm{e}
vents), SR1 (
5\text{\,}542\text{\,}\mathrm{e}
vents), and the SR1
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
(
37\text{\,}835\text{\,}\mathrm{e}
vents). The consistency of the ratio between quality-selected and ground-state-selected events across these datasets (
\sim 30\%
) allows combining them into a single dataset. The energies of each dataset have been individually corrected for the energy bias described in Sec.
III.5
.

IV.2
Systematic Uncertainties

We address potential systematic uncertainties arising from mismatches between simulation and data, particularly those with energy-dependent effects. We identify and evaluate three primary sources of uncertainty.

First, we consider uncertainties due to gamma-event leakage into the beta selection. While waveform properties like the S1/S2 amplitudes and signal widths are well matched between simulations and data, slight mismatches could still affect the fraction of excited-state events leaking into the ground-state selection. To account for this, we apply a
1\%
variation to the total number of leakage events. This choice is motivated by the observed stability (within 1%) of the ratio of selected ground-state events to all quality-selected events across multiple datasets.

Second, we examine uncertainties related to the energy scale of excited-state events. While gamma energies are well known from precise nuclear spectroscopy, the associated beta spectra are more difficult to measure and model. To assess the stability of the result, we apply an effective
5\%
energy shift to all excited-state events. This corresponds to approximately
150
keV at the beta endpoint, compatible with the spectral shifts observed in the ground-state spectrum studied in this work. The effect on the reconstructed spectrum is limited by the relatively small contribution of excited-state events and the dominant role of the well-measured gamma energies.

Finally, we evaluate the overall energy scale uncertainty. The reconstructed energy is corrected using energy-bias curves derived from calibration peaks, for which the fitted peak positions have uncertainties below
0.25\%
. To account for possible residual effects, we apply a conservative
0.5\%
global energy shift. At the beta endpoint, this corresponds to a less than
20\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}
shift, which is smaller than the bin widths.

These three sources of uncertainty were combined by evaluating their impact on the simulated energy spectrum in each energy bin. The resulting variations were added in quadrature and incorporated as an additional uncertainty on the simulated prediction, on top of the statistical uncertainties from data. The combined systematic uncertainty remains below
1\%
across the entire energy range and is subdominant to the statistical error. In the high-energy tail, where event statistics are low, systematic and statistical effects become correlated, but statistical uncertainties dominate.

V
Conclusion and outlook

We presented a measurement of the energy spectrum of the
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Bi}
beta-decay to the ground state of
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Po}
using data from the XENONnT experiment. The event selection employs a dedicated
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 214$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{BiPo}
tagging technique, combining background data with a
{}^{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle 222$}}_{\makebox[11.95839pt][r]{$\scriptstyle$}}\mathrm{Rn}
calibration campaign. By developing an improved high-energy simulation pipeline, we successfully modeled ER signals up to
3.27\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}
, well beyond the low-energy range defined for WIMP searches. Our analysis strongly favors the theoretical spectrum calculated under the conserved vector current (CVC) hypothesis, significantly rejecting the allowed approximation and alternative nuclear models. These results demonstrate the capability of dual-phase xenon detectors to perform high-precision beta spectroscopy, providing valuable validations for theoretical predictions and reinforcing their potential for probing nuclear processes relevant to fundamental physics and astrophysics.
