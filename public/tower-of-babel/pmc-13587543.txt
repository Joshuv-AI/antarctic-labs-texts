Left Write Hook: proof of concept for a survivor-designed, and peer-led programme incorporating expressive writing and boxing to support mental health in adult survivors of child sexual abuse
Authors: Emma Veltman, Alicia Smith, Donna Lyon, Caitlin Hitchcock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Existing approaches to supporting victim-survivors (herein referred to as ‘survivors’) of childhood sexual abuse (CSA) are resource-intensive and traditionally led by mental health professionals. Left Write Hook (LWH) is a novel survivor-designed, peer-led programme for survivors of CSA. By combining trauma-related expressive writing and non-contact, trauma-informed boxing, the programme promotes self-empowerment and supports survivors to rewrite their stories and reclaim their bodies.Objective: This study sought to demonstrate proof of concept for LWH as an intervention to enhance survivors’ mental health and wellbeing.Method: 17 women and gender diverse survivors completed an 8-week programme of weekly 2-hour sessions and completed both pre- and post-intervention assessments.Results: From pre- to post-intervention, we observed a significant decrease in self-reported symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder, complex post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety and stress. We also observed a significant decrease in maladaptive post-traumatic cognitions and fragmentation of trauma memories, and a significant increase in emotional wellbeing and engagement in vigorous physical activity from pre- to post-intervention.Conclusion: Though evaluated in a small sample, these findings build on early pilot research (2020) and provide further support for LWH as a peer-led mental health intervention. LWH offers a multifaceted, empowering, and survivor-centred approach. This preliminary evidence illustrates the promise of LWH to support survivors.KEYWORDS: Child sexual abuse, trauma, exercise, trauma-informed boxing, expressive writing, peer-led intervention

Introduction

Childhood sexual abuse (CSA) is a pervasive public health issue worldwide. UNICEF (2024) reports that 1 in 5 women and 1 in 7 men experience CSA in their lifetime. Estimates of violence against women more broadly are even higher (World Health Organisation, 2021), with CSA significantly increasing the risk of gender-based violence (GBV) later in life (Dube et al., 2005; Papalia et al., 2021). The harmful impact of both CSA and GBV on physical and mental health over the life course is well documented, demonstrating both short- and long-term consequences (Hailes et al., 2019; Stubbs & Szoeke, 2022). CSA is associated with poor mental health outcomes, including chronic post-traumatic stress (e.g. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) or Complex Post-Traumatic-Stress Disorder (CPTSD) symptoms), depression, and anxiety (Hailes et al., 2019; Heise et al., 2002). PTSD is a mental health disorder that can develop in response to exposure to a traumatic event, and is characterised by re-experiencing symptoms, avoidance and changes in mood or cognition and arousal. CPTSD, as outlined in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11; World Health Organisation, 2018), is characterised by the aforementioned PTSD symptoms, in addition to difficulties with affect regulation, negative self-concept and interpersonal relationships. According to the ICD-11 criteria, an individual can be given a diagnosis of PTSD or CPTSD, but not both. Physical health impacts of CSA may include permanent disability, poor sexual and reproductive health, chronic pain, gastrointestinal disorders, and obesity (Heise et al., 2002; Stein et al., 2025; World Health Organization, 2014). This increased risk of chronic disease results from both direct and indirect processes. Higher stress levels drive development of chronic disease, but survivors are also more likely to engage in health risk behaviours such as smoking, alcohol misuse, overeating and high-risk sex (Brown et al., 2021; Chartier et al., 2009; Heise et al., 2002). Given these well-documented and long-term impacts of trauma, there is a clear need for effective interventions to support survivors.

Traditional interventions designed to support survivors have focused on addressing ongoing psychological distress. For example, gold-standard treatment modalities for PTSD include trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapies (TF-CBT), eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), cognitive processing therapy (CPT), and prolonged exposure therapy (PET; American Psychological Association, 2025; National Institute for Care and Excellence, 2018). As these interventions are delivered by trained mental health professionals, they are resource-intensive, which presents significant barriers to access (de Boer et al., 2022). Moreover, a significant number of survivors continue to experience trauma-related symptoms upon treatment completion, suggesting that existing interventions may not address all lasting impacts of trauma (Wesseling et al., 2025). A multidimensional approach that considers both physical and psychological processes may help to promote better mental health outcomes within this population. Moreover, elevating the voices of those with lived/living experience is essential to ensure that interventions meet the needs of, and have meaningful impacts for, survivors of CSA and GBV. Left Write Hook (LWH) is one such intervention that has been designed by survivors, for survivors, and which integrates both physical (i.e. exercise) and psychological (i.e. expressive writing) therapeutic techniques. Empirical support for this approach comes from literature highlighting expressive writing and exercise as promising therapeutic approaches to support survivors (DeJesus et al., 2024; Rosenbaum et al., 2015).

Research supports the use of writing as a valuable modality that can help to address post-traumatic stress. Written exposure therapy (WET) is a brief, structured intervention that uses trauma-focused writing as a method of exposure to reduce the distress associated with trauma memories, and to facilitate both cognitive and emotional processing of traumatic events (Sloan & Marx, 2024). Research has shown that although less intensive than many other treatments for PTSD, WET appears to be similarly effective for reducing PTSD symptoms and has lower dropout rates (DeJesus et al., 2024). Other expressive writing interventions for those with PTSD have been found to be associated with reduced anger, tension, and neuroendocrine responses to trauma memories, as well as promote positive post-traumatic growth (Smyth et al., 2008). While expressive writing alone has been found to alleviate PTSD symptoms, treatment outcomes appear to be further enhanced when combined with other psychological therapies (Gerger et al., 2022). Similar benefits of expressive writing have been illustrated among women survivors of CSA and GBV (Koopman et al., 2005; Meston et al., 2013). Some of the key mechanisms thought to be underlying this effect are the aspects of cognitive processing that enable one to develop a coherent narrative and make meaning about one’s experiences (Baikie & Wilhelm, 2005; Park & Blumberg, 2002). These mechanisms reflect those that are also targeted in gold-standard treatments for trauma-related stress (NICE, 2018).

It is well-established that in addition to its impact on mental health, trauma also negatively impacts physical health. Trauma can result in sensory re-experiencing symptoms (i.e. reliving sensory details of the experience) and contribute to observed changes in the biological stress response (e.g. threat sensitivity, stress hormone reactivity) (Pitman et al., 2012; Ressler et al., 2022). Dissociation, including disconnection from the body, is a common response to trauma meaning that survivors can struggle with feeling agency over their bodies (Herman, 2015). Body-based interventions such as physical activity thereby present an opportunity to lower the risk of chronic disease as well as improve mental health outcomes (Pebole et al., 2020).

Exercise, including resistance training, aerobic exercise and yoga, has been found to be associated with reductions in PTSD, anxiety and depression symptom severity (Rosenbaum et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2023; Whitworth et al., 2019). Exercise has also been used as either a standalone treatment or an adjunct to other psychological interventions in survivors of sexual violence, resulting in observed reductions in PTSD symptoms and rumination, and increased self-compassion (Crews et al., 2016; Shors et al., 2018; Smith, 2016; van der Kolk et al., 2014). However, these studies have exclusively focused on low-intensity exercise such as walking and yoga. Studies have shown that some survivors would prefer to engage in moderate to high intensity exercise, and that martial arts are appealing as they enhance confidence around one’s ability to protect themselves (Pebole et al., 2022; Smith-Marek et al., 2018).

Boxing is a more vigorous form of exercise that appears to have mental health benefits for women survivors of GBV. Shape Your Life (van Ingen, 2011a) is a trauma-informed, non-contact boxing programme designed to support survivors of violence. Through both quantitative and qualitative research, initial evidence suggests that Shape Your Life increases survivor’s sense of empowerment and location of healthy aggression, as well as their sense of agency, resilience, self-esteem, and physical ability (Gammage et al., 2022; van Ingen, 2011b). Given the breadth of potential mental health benefits for survivors, boxing is a promising exercise modality that could be incorporated into exercise-based interventions to support survivors.

Taken together, existing evidence suggests that a programme which combines both psychological (e.g. building self-efficacy, promoting processing of trauma-related memories and meaning) and physical (e.g. exercise) components could have a synergistic effect, generating greater changes in wellbeing within this at-risk population than existing interventions that focus on one of these components alone. The development of novel interventions enables survivors to have greater choice of treatment options, thus increasing the acceptability and approachability of treatments. Expressive writing and trauma-informed, non-contact boxing are modalities that offer particular promise for survivors of CSA and GBV.

Peer-led interventions offer additional benefits as they can be delivered by individuals who are not qualified mental health professionals but have targeted training relevant to the intervention alongside expertise from lived/living experience. In addition to increasing treatment accessibility, interventions delivered by lived experience experts can dismantle power imbalances often present in traditional models of therapy and may increase the sense of connectedness and empowerment generated by the programme (Konya et al., 2025). LWH is a survivor-designed, peer-led programme that combines trauma-related expressive writing and non-contact boxing. Principles of trauma-informed care, including safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment, underpin treatment design and delivery. LWH is distinct from other existing interventions (e.g. WET and Shape Your Life) in that it combines both writing and boxing modalities into an 8-week manualised treatment approach. The programme is designed to empower survivors to rewrite their stories and reclaim their bodies and it’s peer-led nature positions LWH as a more accessible and scalable intervention. This is compared with those modalities traditionally offered to survivors, given that survivors (rather than mental health professionals) can deliver the intervention with relatively brief training (compared to that of mental health professionals). The LWH intervention was originally developed and implemented by a survivor-founder with a background in creative arts practice, boxing and peer-led recovery. The programme was created from lived experience and a genuine desire to connect with other survivors through creative expression, boxing and peer support. At the time of its development, there were no known programmes combining writing and boxing in a peer-led setting for survivors of CSA and GBV. An initial mixed-methods pilot study (N = 8) of the LWH programme provided preliminary evidence to support acceptability of the intervention, and survivors who completed the programme experienced a large increase in assertiveness and emotional, social, and psychological wellbeing from pre- to post-programme. Qualitative data also highlighted themes of empowerment and body connectedness among participants (e.g. ‘I want to fix my body and my mind and feel strong’) (Lyon et al., 2020). Further research was needed to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the potential benefits of the LWH programme on the mental health of survivors of CSA.

The current study

To build on existing findings regarding the treatment potential of the LWH programme and unpack broader impacts on mental health, this investigation sought to examine changes in mental health outcomes commonly associated with experiences of CSA and GVB, notably PTSD, CPTSD, depression, and anxiety, from pre- to post-intervention. We also examined changes in post-traumatic cognitions (Schumm et al., 2015) and trauma memory quality (Reed et al., 2023), both of which have been proposed as cognitive mechanisms underpinning PTSD symptoms. Specifically, changes in post-traumatic cognitions and trauma memory quality are thought to produce changes in PTSD symptoms (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Finally, we sought to examine changes in participant’s engagement in physical exercise from pre- to post-programme, to explore whether the LWH programme might promote positive behaviour change with potential benefits for survivors’ physical health. The current research study was co-produced alongside the survivor-founder, to ensure that the research methodology was appropriate, and that the outcomes measured accurately reflect what is most important to survivors. Results from the current proof of concept study have not been reported elsewhere.

Method

Power calculation

A power analysis predicting the within-person effect of LWH on the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-5 (d = 1.022), observed by Lyon et al. (2020), indicated that 15 participants would provide 95% power (two-tailed, alpha = .05).

Participants

Participants were recruited through the LWH charity and were invited to take part in the current study. Research participation was voluntary and separate from their involvement in the programme. Enrolled participants were 23 individuals who took part in an 8-week LWH programme in the community during 2023 or 2024. Self-reported gender identity was assessed, with the majority of the sample identifying as women (83%), while 17% identified as non-binary. Biological sex was not measured as the current study’s primary hypotheses concerned gendered social experiences rather than biological mechanisms. Participant ages at baseline ranged from 24 to 70 years, with an average age of 40. Information about ethnicity was not collected. Criteria for inclusion in a LWH programme were that individuals were at least 18 years of age and self-identified as a survivor of CSA. Exclusion criteria included ongoing intimate partner violence (IPV) and high suicidality in need of consistent crisis management.

Measures

International Trauma Questionnaire

The International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; Cloitre et al., 2018) is a self-report measure that assesses PTSD and CPTSD as outlined in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Edition (World Health Organisation, 2018). The ITQ is comprised of 12 items, six indexing PTSD symptoms (e.g. ‘having upsetting dreams that replay part of the experience or are clearly related to the experience’) and six indexing Disturbances in Self Organisation (DSO; e.g. ‘when I am upset, it takes me a long time to calm down’), which, alongside PTSD symptoms, characterise CPTSD. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). The ITQ was scored both dimensionally and diagnostically, with probable diagnostic status determined using the procedure specified by Cloitre et al. (2018) for ICD-11 diagnostic criteria for CPTSD and PTSD. Probable diagnosis is not based on the total score, but rather the combination of symptoms that are present (see Cloitre et al., 2018). Use of the term ‘probable diagnostic status’ reflects that the ITQ is a self-report screening tool; definitive diagnosis would necessitate a comprehensive assessment by a qualified mental health professional (e.g. structured clinical interview and evaluation of functioning and comorbid conditions). Individual item scores for the six PTSD items were summed to calculate a dimensional PTSD symptom score, while the 6 items from the DSO scale were summed to generate a dimensional DSO symptom score for each participant. Symptom scores range from 0–24, with higher scores indicative of greater symptom severity. Diagnostic scoring was also used to determine whether participants were likely to meet diagnostic criteria for PTSD or CPTSD, with a score of 0 indicating the individual likely did not meet criteria, and a score of 1 indicating the individual likely met diagnostic criteria.

Trauma Memory Quality Questionnaire

The Trauma Memory Quality Questionnaire (TMQQ; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2007) is a self-report measure of coherence and vividness of trauma memories. The measure is comprised of 11 items regarding trauma quality (e.g. ‘when I remember the frightening event I feel like it is happening right now’, ‘my memories of the frightening event are mostly pictures or images’), which are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (disagree a lot) to 4 (agree a lot). Individual item scores were summed to obtain a total score. Scores can range from 11–44, with a higher score indicating greater memory fragmentation (i.e. incoherence and vivid sensory details) which is linked to greater post-traumatic stress. In the current study, item 8 was inadvertently not administered meaning that total scores were generated from a total of 10 items and could range from 10–40.

Post-Traumatic Cognitions Inventory

The Posttraumatic Cognitions Inventory (Foa et al., 1999) is a self-report questionnaire that indexes maladaptive beliefs about the self and world after experiences of trauma (e.g. ‘I have no future’, ‘people can’t be trusted’). These beliefs are measured across 33 items with a 7-point Likert scale response format ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 7 (totally agree). A response score of 4 was indicative of a neutral response. A total score was obtained by summing the individual item scores. Possible scores range from 33–231, with a higher total score indicative of more negative cognitions about the self, the world, and self-blame in relation to traumatic experience(s).

Depression Anxiety Stress Scales

The Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS; S. H. Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995) are self-report scales designed to measure the emotional states of depression, anxiety and stress. A shortened 21-item version known as the DASS-21 (P. F. Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995) was used in the current study. This version is comprised of seven items used to index each emotional state, including depression (e.g. ‘I felt that life was meaningless’), anxiety (e.g. ‘I felt I was close to panic’) and stress (e.g. ‘I found it difficult to relax’). Responses were scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 3 (often). In line with evidence suggesting that the DASS-21 is best used as an indicator of overall psychological distress (Osman et al., 2012; Shaw et al., 2017; e.g. Zanon et al., 2021), rather than examining individual subscale scores, the current study focused on changes to total distress. Results pertaining to subscale scores are presented in the online supplemental materials. Total distress scores were calculated by summing the individual item scores across all three scales and could range from 0 to 63, with a higher score indicating greater emotional distress.

Mental Health Continuum Short Form

The Adult Mental Health Continuum Short Form (Keyes, 2002) is a self-report scale that measures wellbeing. Across 14 items, the measure asks participants to indicate how often they have experienced aspects of wellbeing over the past month. Items index emotional wellbeing (e.g. ‘happy’), social wellbeing (e.g. social integration ‘that you belonged to a community, like a social group, or your neighbourhood’, social acceptance ‘that people are basically good’), psychological wellbeing (e.g. self-acceptance ‘that you liked most parts of your personality’, and personal growth ‘that you had experiences that helped you to grow and become a better person’). Responses are provided on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (every day). A total wellbeing score was obtained by summing individual item scores. Scores could range from 0–70, with a higher score indicative of greater overall wellbeing.

International Physical Activity Questionnaire

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ; Craig et al., 2003) is a self-report measure of engagement in physical activity. The IPAQ has seven items that ask the respondent to report the number of days in the last week they had engaged in physical activities for 10 min or more, and the amount of time spent doing the activity. The activities assessed include vigorous activities (i.e. make you breathe much harder than normal and may include heavy lifting, digging, aerobics, or fast bicycling), moderate activities (i.e. those that cause you to breathe somewhat harder than normal and may include carrying a light load, bicycling at a regular pace, or double tennis), walking and sitting. To reduce participant burden, selected items from the IPAQ short-form were used, namely the number of days the participants had engaged in vigorous activity, moderate activity and walking in the previous week. Scores ranged from 0 days to 7 days.

Procedure

LWH is a registered charity based in Victoria, Australia. The charity and the LWH programme it delivers were established as part of a survivor-led, community-based initiative to bring together survivors of CSA and GBV to continue their healing journey in community. The charity aims to empower survivors to rewrite their stories and reclaim their bodies through delivery of the LWH programme to survivors of CSA and GBV within Victoria, and more recently other Australian states. LWH was therefore consumer-developed and implemented in a similar manner to other consumer-led initiatives to improve mental health care (e.g. Young et al., 2005), rather than developed using a science-driven approach (Scheuplein et al., 2023). Figure 1 provides a timeline of the development of LWH and research evaluating the intervention to date, to contextualise the current study.

Timeline of the development and empirical evaluation of LWH.

Participants in the current study were recruited through the LWH charity, which, in accordance with their normal recruitment processes, advertised upcoming programmes on social media platforms and via their pre-established mailing list. Interested individuals completed an expression of interest form via the charity’s website and were supported by the LWH programme manager (a survivor themselves) to judge their own readiness to participate in the programme. To help the individual decide if they felt ready, the manager shared common experiences in the programme (e.g. an increase in anxiety or stress as use of avoidance strategies is reduced). This conversation also included an assessment of key eligibility criteria within a scope of practice limited to a peer-led intake setting. These criteria were not experiencing acute suicidality in need of consistent crisis management, no ongoing IPV, and professional mental health support in place. All participants who enrolled in a LWH programme during the recruitment window (2023–2024) were invited to participate in this study by their programme facilitator until we received complete datasets for 15 participants. Participation in the study was voluntary, meaning data was not available for all of those enrolled in a given programme. Those interested were provided with a copy of the study’s plain language statement detailing key study details, and written informed consent was obtained prior to engagement in research activities.

Once enrolled in the research, study participants completed online questionnaires via Qualtrics survey software to assess their mental health and wellbeing in the week prior to the first session. Of the 23 participants enrolled, 17 completed both pre- and post-programme assessments. Participants received LWH merchandise as reimbursement for assessment completion. One participant had only 28 days between the completion of the pre- and post-programme assessments. As the programme spans eight weeks and the main analyses involved pre- and post-programme comparisons, the data for this individual were excluded from the main analyses resulting in a final N = 16.

Principles of trauma-informed care (i.e. safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment) as described by Butler et al. (2011) underpinned both the writing and boxing components of every session. Emotional and physical safety was prioritised through the avoidance of re-traumatisation by creating a welcoming tradition, ensuring venues were private and booked exclusively, co-creating ground rules at the start of the programme, teaching synchronicity of movement with breath to promote calmness and groundedness, introducing pad work slowly acknowledging that this can be activating for some, and routinely obtaining consent before providing adjustments. An environment of trustworthiness and transparency was created through the provision of clear and consistent information, a structured format to shape participant expectations, and explaining, demonstrating, and practicing boxing movements together, before offering improvements. Empowerment, collaboration, and choice were a central focus throughout all activities. Individualisation was promoted through the use of invitational language, offering multiple writing prompts and levels of boxing difficulty, and allowing participants to decide the depth and pace of their engagement. Participants could complete expressive writing exercises through various methods (handwritten or digital), in the language in which they feel most comfortable, and exercises within the boxing component of the session were modified to suit different ranges of mobility or physical fitness. Facilitators fostered an environment where participants felt comfortable expressing their preferences or sharing any difficulties as they arose, avoiding any pressure and offering alternatives or modifications to cater treatment components to their individual needs and preferences. As a survivor-designed, peer-led intervention, LWH promotes relational healing through peer support. Facilitators engaged in expressive writing and sharing, they demonstrated and practiced boxing movements alongside participants, and more broadly modelled recovery and healing as a continuum therefore reducing power imbalances. Finally, strong emphasis is placed on participant strengths and achievements throughout the programme.

The LWH programme consisted of weekly two-hour sessions over an eight-week period. Sessions were led by peer-facilitators, who were survivors of CSA and/or GBV who had participated in the LWH programme previously, had expressed interest in becoming a peer-facilitator, and completed the charity’s Train-the-Champion programme. The Train-the-Champion programme was delivered by the charity and focuses on trauma-informed principles and their application in the context of the LWH programme. The training programme is comprised of self-directed online learning followed by shadowing an experienced facilitator to learn the practical application of theoretical learning.

In the first LWH session, the group (n = 4–8 people) collaboratively established guidelines that were followed in subsequent sessions. Each session adhered to a consistent structure throughout the programme. The first hour was the expressive writing component. Participants were encouraged to write freely in response to prompts that explored different aspects of how they experienced trauma and its impact on their lives (see M. Butler et al., 2025 for more details). They were then given the opportunity to share their writing and/or reflect on their experience during the writing activity. The second hour was the non-contact, trauma-informed boxing component. This part of the session focused on teaching participants the physical activity of boxing. Each session, participants learnt a new boxing technique (e.g. stance, punch, or combination of punches) and were given the chance to practice this technique through either bag work or pad work with a partner. In doing so, participants were invited to connect with their bodies through grounding, stance, and focus on purposeful movement and expression.

The post-programme assessments were distributed immediately following the last session and were completed within one week of distribution. This research project was approved by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (Project ID: 27131).

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics for variables included in the main analyses are presented in Table S1 of the Online Supplemental Material. Paired-samples t-tests were conducted to compare mean scores for each of the outcome measures from pre- to post-programme to identify any significant differences in mental health and physical activity. Significance was determined based on a p-value of less than .05. Cohen’s d was calculated as a standardised measure of effect size. A d = 0.3 indicates a small effect, d = 0.5 indicates a moderate effect, and d = 0.5 indicates a large effect. The main analyses focused on total scores across the outcome measures assessed; differences in the subscale scores are presented in the Online Supplemental Material and will not be repeated here. Descriptive statistics were analysed to detect change in probable PTSD and CTPSD diagnostic status from pre- and post-programme completion. Measures with a minimum possible score >0 were transformed to have a minimum score of 0 to be more clearly presented in Figures 2–4. The data analysis code and corresponding dataset can be accessed online at https://github.com/aliciasmith1/Left-Write-Hook.

Results

Post-traumatic stress symptoms

As shown in Figure 2, significant changes were observed for PTSD and DSO symptoms from pre- to post-programme. PTSD symptom severity significantly decreased from pre- (M = 16.44, SD = 4.55) to post-programme (M = 9.63, SD = 3.50) t(15) = −6.86, p < .001. A large effect size accompanied this difference d = −1.72, 95% CI [−2.49, −0.92], with the mean difference suggesting reliable change on the PTSD subscale of the ITQ (Cloitre et al., 2021). Similarly, DSO symptom severity significantly decreased from pre- (M = 16.00, SD = 4.97) to post-programme (M = 8.31, SD = 4.60) t(15) = −6.66, p < .001. A large effect size was observed for this difference d = −1.67, 95% CI [−2.42, −0.89], with the mean difference suggesting reliable change on the DSO subscale of the ITQ (Cloitre et al., 2021). Changes in probable diagnostic status from pre-programme to post-programme were also observed. Three out of four (75%) individuals who met probable PTSD criteria at pre-programme no longer met probable criteria at post-programme. Similarly, seven out of ten (70%) individuals who met probable CPTSD diagnostic criteria pre-programme no longer met probable criteria at post-programme. More specifically, of those who met probable CPTSD diagnostic criteria pre-programme, three individuals (30%) met probable PTSD criteria at post-programme, and four (40%) did not meet any probable diagnostic criteria at post-programme. There was an insufficient number of participants at each probable diagnostic status at pre-programme to allow to permit valid statistical tests of clinically significant change in probable PTSD and CPTSD diagnoses from pre-programme to post-programme (i.e. several cells contained very small counts).

Post-traumatic cognitions

Figure 3 illustrates the observed changes in trauma memory quality and post-traumatic cognitions from pre- to post-programme. Specifically, a decrease in fragmentation of trauma memories was observed from pre- (M = 31, SD = 4.84) to post-programme (M = 27.44, SD = 3.14), t(15) = −2.88, p = .01. A medium effect size was found to accompany this difference d = −0.72, 95% CI [−1.26, −0.16]. Post-traumatic cognitions scores also decreased significantly from pre- (M = 160, SD = 45.57) to post-programme (M = 116.94, SD = 36.55), indicating that participants' unhelpful cognitions regarding their experiences of trauma decreased following LWH, t(15) = −4.53, p < .001. This was a large effect size, d = −1.13, 95% CI [−1.75, −0.49].

Broader mental health

As shown in Figure 4, differences were also observed on broader mental health and wellbeing. Depression, anxiety and stress total scores significantly decreased from pre- (M = 31.5, SD = 14.70) to post-programme (M = 17.44, SD = 7.44) t(15) = −5.41, p < .001. This difference was found to have a large effect size, d = −1.35, 95% CI [−2.03, −0.66]. In terms of overall wellbeing, participants reported greater wellbeing after the programme (M = 38.75, SD = 13.68), compared to before the programme (M = 30.19, SD = 19.03) t(15) = 2.72, p = .02. This difference was found to have a medium effect size, d = 0.68, 95% CI [0.12, 1.22].

Physical activity

Finally, as shown in Figure 5, significant changes were observed for engagement in vigorous physical activity, but not moderate physical activity or walking from pre- to post-programme. Specifically, the number of days people engaged in vigorous physical exercise significantly increased from pre- (M = 1.63, SD = 1.75) to post-programme (M = 2.88, SD = 1.78) t(15) = 4.70, p < .001. This difference was found to have a large effect size, d = 1.17, 95% CI [0.52, 1.81]. The difference in engagement in moderate exercise increased from pre- (M = 2, SD = 1.93) to post-programme (M = 2.88, SD = 2.06) but this difference was not significant t(15) = 2.15, p = .05. This difference was found to have a medium effect size, d = 0.54, 95% CI [0.00, 1.06]. Similarly, no significant difference was found in participant’s engagement in walking from pre- (M = 4.19, SD = 1.68) to post-programme (M = 5.06, SD = 1.95), t(15) = 1.62, p = .13. This difference was found to have a small effect size, d = 0.41, 95% CI [−0.11, 0.91].

Discussion

Here we demonstrate proof of concept for LWH, a survivor-designed and delivered intervention. After participation in LWH, participants reported experiencing fewer symptoms of PTSD, CPTSD, depression, anxiety and stress. In addition to reduced symptoms, in line with trauma-informed care principles that value the whole person, LWH was associated with positive changes in wellbeing – an outcome highly valued by survivors in other research (Stige et al., 2013). Engagement in vigorous exercise was also found to increase from pre- to post-programme. Overall, the observed medium-large effect sizes offer early indications that LWH may be associated with improvements in a range of positive mental health outcomes for survivors of CSA and promote changes in health behaviours that have known physical health benefits.

Peer-led delivery of a combination of physical and psychological intervention techniques are defining features of LWH. There is increasing recognition of the important contribution of peer support in mental health care. Peer support specialists are already employed by some agencies (e.g. Veteran Peer Support Specialists in mental health clinics within the Veterans Health Administration). Our work suggests that this workforce could be expanded to include CSA survivors. LWH combines two treatment modalities that have independently been shown to be effective in improving the mental health of trauma survivors, such as WET and boxing programmes like Shape Your Life. Importantly, our results suggest that a multidimensional approach that combines both psychological and physiological processes has the potential to offer a variety of mental health benefits. This aligns with literature highlighting the possible benefits of complementary and integrative care by combining traditional psychological therapies with other mind–body interventions (e.g. breathing biofeedback, exercise) (Michael et al., 2019; Niles et al., 2018, 2023). Our preliminary findings also align with prior research demonstrating the acceptability of other multidimensional approaches (e.g. Chapman et al., 2017) and the utility of physiological modalities such as exercise in augmenting traditional psychological therapies (e.g. exposure therapy) to further reduce PTSD and depressive symptomatology (Bryant et al., 2023; Michael et al., 2019; Nord et al., 2023). CSA survivors are at increased risk of a range of chronic physical health conditions, and thus LWH’s potential effects on both psychological health and engagement in exercise suggest that an integrated approach may yield a multidimensional impact on survivor health. Further exploration of downstream impacts of LWH on physiological risk factors (e.g. physical fitness, stress biomarkers, indicators of metabolic syndrome) is now warranted. Offering one programme that can improve both trauma processing and meaning-making and physical health outcomes may offer a streamlined, time- and cost-effective approach to supporting survivor wellbeing.

LWH is designed and delivered by survivors, for survivors, and embodies key principles of trauma-informed care. For instance, the programme takes active steps to foster a sense of trust, as well as emotional and physical safety for participants through both practical considerations and delivery approach. Session components are delivered in ways that promote choice and collaboration and can be readily modified to suit individual needs, acknowledging the diverse experiences of survivors. Additionally, the peer-led nature of LWH is inherently empowering, and the ability to adapt session components to the diverse needs of the survivor population takes important steps to improve treatment accessibility and acceptability for this population.

The current study emphasises the potential power of centring survivors at all stages of an intervention’s development and implementation. Survivor-centric delivery and governance models as employed by LWH empower survivors in their healing journey and offer a unique opportunity to further strengthen the lived/living experience workforce. Employing survivors to deliver the intervention also increases scalability. Across mental health outcomes, we observed medium to large pre-to-post intervention effect sizes (from d = 0.68 to d = −1.72) of LWH. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the study’s small sample size, such findings are promising as these effect sizes are comparable to those in gold-standard psychological therapies such as TF-CBT (g = 1.26) and EMDR (g = 1.01) delivered by mental health professionals (Watts et al., 2013). Despite other examples of their success (Fukui et al., 2010; Lucksted et al., 2009; McCormack & Katalinic, 2016), peer-led interventions are not yet widely available. More research is needed to elucidate the potentially wide-reaching mental and physical health benefits of LWH, and other peer-led programmes. Formal evaluation in randomised controlled trials such as that laid out by Butler et al. (2025) is important to generate the funding that is sorely needed to support community-based and survivor-led initiatives.

Limitations

Our findings should be considered alongside a number of limitations. First, we acknowledge that the large number of analyses conducted increases the risk of Type I error (false positives). Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in future studies using appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons. Some measures, namely the TMQQ and IPAQ, were not administered in full, meaning that the findings relating to these scales should be interpreted with some caution, and require replication with full measures. For example, single items were used to index engagement in physical activity, which may only provide a small snapshot of physical activity that one engages in. Additionally, given that self-report measures were completed in the days following completion of the programme, and engagement in physical activity was measured over the previous week, it is possible that involvement in the programme itself may have impacted these results. More rigorous empirical investigation of these outcomes, including follow-up in the weeks or months after programme completion, is warranted in the future. Furthermore, future work will need to determine whether the number of sessions completed impacts the treatment effect.

Second, it is important to highlight the lack of comparison condition, the small sample size, and that we did not collect information about programme completion (i.e. number of sessions attended) or intercurrent events (e.g. changes in psychopharmacological or other treatments over the course of the programme). While these limitations are reflective of the pilot nature of this study, future investigations are now needed using more controlled methods, to isolate the potential treatment effects of LWH. Similarly, future studies should consider a more comprehensive set of measures indexing both mental health and physical health outcomes to improve the generalisability of the findings. It will be important for such studies to include both qualitative and quantitative methods of assessment, and to consider both self-report and objectively assessed indicators of change. We demonstrated that LWH was associated with benefits for posttraumatic cognitions and trauma memory quality – well-established mechanisms underpinning psychological symptom change in the aftermath of trauma (Reed et al., 2023; Schumm et al., 2015). Future work should seek to isolate the active components and mechanisms of change that might underlie the effects of LWH. To drive successful implementation and scalability of LWH, the perspectives of key stakeholders such as participants, programme facilitators, and other community stakeholders should be considered to support wider roll-out of community-based programmes such as LWH.

Conclusion

This proof-of-concept study suggests that LWH may have a number of potential mental health benefits for adult survivors of CSA. Further research, and in particular, randomised controlled trials that compare LWH to no treatment, and to existing interventions, are needed to further establish the effectiveness of this intervention in promoting the mental and physical health of survivors (M. Butler et al., 2025). Multidimensional and peer-led treatment approaches may offer choice to survivors, and reduce barriers to evidence-based treatments, helping to reduce widespread healthcare inequity.
