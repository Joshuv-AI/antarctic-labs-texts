Hemi-Hamate Arthroplasty in Delayed Proximal Interphalangeal Joint Fracture-Dislocations: Technical Considerations and Early Outcomes From a Case Series
Authors: Hung Quoc Vu, Long Bao Trong Hoang, Luan Khoi Tran, Sang Anh Nguyen, Doanh Quoc Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the early clinical and radiographic outcomes of hemi-hamate autograft (HHA) reconstruction for unstable dorsal proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) fracture-dislocations with severe comminution involving more than 50% loss of the volar articular surface.MethodsA retrospective case series of 4 patients treated with HHA reconstruction between June and December 2025 was performed. All injuries involved highly comminuted fractures that were unsuitable for conventional open reduction and internal fixation. The surgical technique aimed to restore the volar buttress of the middle phalanx using an osteochondral hamate graft fixed with bicortical lag screws. Early protected active motion was initiated within the first postoperative days using a dorsal extension-block splint. Clinical outcomes included active PIPJ range of motion, grip, and key pinch strength relative to the contralateral side, visual analog scale pain score, and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score. Radiographic assessments evaluated joint alignment and graft integration.ResultsAt 3–6 months, the mean active PIPJ arc of motion was 81.8° ± 7.9°. Grip strength recovered to 86% ± 5% of the contralateral side. The mean Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score improved from 38.3 ± 6.3 at 6 weeks to 7.5 ± 4.0 at final follow-up. Pain was minimal, with a mean visual analog scale score of 0 at rest and 0.5 during motion. Radiographs demonstrated maintained concentric reduction and successful graft incorporation in all cases without hardware complications or donor-site morbidity.ConclusionsIn this small case series, HHA reconstruction showed promising early functional outcomes for delayed, severe dorsal PIPJ fracture-dislocations. Fracture-dislocations of the proximal interphalangeal joint (PIPJ) represent a challenging category of hand injuries that frequently result in long-term functional impairment if not managed appropriately. These injuries can lead to chronic joint instability, progressive stiffness, and ultimately posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Biomechanically, the stability of the proximal interphalangeal joint is critically dependent on the concave “cupping” morphology of the volar base of the middle phalanx, which functions as a bony buttress resisting dorsal translation. Previous biomechanical and clinical studies by Hastings and Carroll demonstrated that fractures involving more than 40% to 50% of the volar articular surface disrupt this stabilizing mechanism, rendering the joint inherently unstable.1,2

In many regions, particularly within the Asia–Pacific population, these injuries are commonly associated with high-energy trauma such as industrial accidents and motorcycle-related injuries. Such mechanisms frequently produce complex “pilon-type” fracture patterns characterized by central articular impaction and severe comminution of the middle phalangeal base.3 Although small avulsion fractures may be treated successfully with extension-block splinting or percutaneous pinning, highly comminuted fractures present a considerably greater reconstructive challenge. In these situations, open reduction and internal fixation is often not technically feasible because the volar lip fragments are too small to accommodate stable screw fixation. As a result, reconstructive or salvage procedures have historically been employed, including volar plate arthroplasty and dynamic external fixation systems such as the Suzuki frame.4

Despite their widespread use, these techniques have inherent biomechanical limitations. Volar plate arthroplasty reconstructs the articular defect using the volar plate as an interpositional tissue; however, the resulting surface consists of fibrocartilage rather than native hyaline cartilage, which has inferior mechanical durability and unpredictable long-term remodeling.5 Clinical studies have therefore reported recurrent dorsal instability and flexion contractures, particularly when the articular defect exceeds half of the joint surface.6 Similarly, dynamic external fixation relies on ligamentotaxis to maintain reduction and facilitate early motion, but it does not anatomically restore the joint’s native osteochondral contour. Consequently, the reconstructed joint may remain mechanically incongruent despite appearing concentrically reduced, potentially predisposing the joint to degenerative changes over time.7

Hemi-hamate autograft (HHA) arthroplasty was first described by Hastings et al8 in 1999 as an anatomical reconstructive technique to address these limitations. Transplantation of this osteochondral graft restores the volar buttress with native hyaline cartilage, permitting early active range of motion.

Although hemi-hamate arthroplasty has been well described in prior studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up (such as the recent 35-patient series by Smith et al9), these reports predominantly focus on acute reconstruction or mixed cohorts. In contrast, delayed presentations remain less well characterized, despite being frequently encountered in clinical practice. These delayed cases are associated with distinct challenges, including joint stiffness, fibrosis, and altered soft tissue conditions, which may notably influence both surgical technique and postoperative rehabilitation. Therefore, the present study aims to provide focused insight into the technical considerations and early functional outcomes of hemi-hamate reconstruction, specifically in delayed cases (4–12 weeks postinjury).

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective observational case series evaluating the clinical and radiographic outcomes of HHA reconstruction for unstable dorsal fracture-dislocations of the PIPJ. The study was conducted at a tertiary referral center specializing in hand and upper-extremity surgery between June 2025 and December 2025.

All cases were identified through the institutional surgical database and electronic medical records. Patients were included consecutively during the study period to minimize selection bias.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. According to institutional policy, retrospective studies using anonymized data and involving standard clinical treatments are exempt from formal institutional review board approval. The surgical procedures for each patient were reviewed and approved by the hospital’s scientific and technical council, as documented in papers numbered 25.P.0079567, 25.P.0199074, 25.P.0399143, and 25.P.0224214. All patients provided individual written informed consent for both treatment and the publication of their clinical data and images.

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) dorsal fracture-dislocation of the proximal interphalangeal joint, (2) severe articular comminution involving ≥50% of the volar articular surface of the base of the middle phalanx, (3) persistent dorsal instability of the PIPJ, and (4) fractures unsuitable for standard open reduction and internal fixation because of fragment comminution. Both acute and delayed presentations were included.

Exclusion criteria included (5) simple avulsion fractures amenable to conventional fixation, (6) fractures involving <40% of the articular surface, (7) previous degenerative arthritis of the PIPJ, and (8) incomplete follow-up data.

Four patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Patient demographic characteristics, injury mechanisms, and surgical timing were obtained from clinical records.

Recruitment

Patients were identified through hospital electronic medical records and the upper limb department’s operative logs. All patients were referred for surgical management by emergency physicians or orthopedic specialists after radiographic confirmation of unstable PIPJ fracture-dislocation.

No financial incentives were provided to patients for participation in this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for publication of clinical information and images.

Preoperative evaluation and optimization

All patients underwent preoperative clinical and radiographic evaluation. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the affected finger were obtained to confirm severe comminution and the loss of a stable volar buttress, which rendered internal fixation unsuitable. Computed tomography was performed in all cases to assess the extent of articular comminution better, identify occult sagittal splits, and guide surgical planning, particularly when early callus formation obscured the fracture geometry in delayed presentations.

Preoperative assessment also included evaluation of soft tissue condition, neurovascular status, and range of motion of adjacent joints.

In the adolescent patient, closure of the proximal phalangeal phyzes was confirmed radiographically before performing rigid bicortical screw fixation.

Patients received routine preoperative counseling regarding surgical risks, postoperative rehabilitation, and expected functional recovery.

Surgical intervention

All procedures were performed under regional anesthesia with a pneumatic arm tourniquet. Patients were positioned supine with the affected limb placed on a hand table.

Recipient site preparation

Access to the PIPJ was achieved via a palmar V-shaped incision. Neurovascular bundles were identified and retracted. The flexor tendon sheath was longitudinally incised between the A2 and A4 pulleys (elevating the A3 pulley as a flap) to allow lateral retraction of the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus tendons. The volar plate was distally detached from the base of the middle phalanx and proximally mobilized. The accessory collateral ligaments were released to permit a 150° dorsal hyperextension of the PIPJ. Compromised volar fragments were meticulously debrided. Using a micro-oscillating saw and a fine osteotome, the recipient bed was prepared into a geometric step-cut, ensuring a perfectly flat, bleeding cancellous surface while preserving the dorsal cortex and the insertion of the central slip. The 3-dimensional defect was measured using a microcaliper (Fig. 1).

Intraoperative preparation of the recipient site. The PIPJ is accessed via a palmar V-shaped incision and exposed using a “shotgun” (hyperextension) maneuver. The highly comminuted, unstable volar base of the middle phalanx is meticulously debrided to create a flat, bleeding cancellous geometric step-cut, preserving the dorsal cortex.

Hamate autograft harvest

A dorsal longitudinal incision was made over the fourth and fifth carpometacarpal (CMC) joints. The dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve and extensor tendons were protected. Following a longitudinal capsulotomy, the dorsal articular ridge of the hamate was exposed.

Based on recipient site measurements, the required osteochondral graft was mapped. Drawing upon institutional experience and recommended surgical principles, the graft was intentionally oversized by 0.5–1.0 mm in all dimensions. This deliberate oversizing provides a crucial margin of error, allowing for precise contouring during the inset phase without compromising structural integrity. Harvest was performed using a micro-oscillating saw in the sagittal and coronal planes, followed by a fine osteotome to avoid thermal necrosis of the cartilage (Fig. 2).

Graft tailoring and fixation

The oversized hamate autograft was brought to the recipient bed and contoured with a high-speed burr to precisely match the defect. Special attention was given to recreating the volar cup-shaped buttress of the middle phalanx to prevent recurrent dorsal subluxation. For definitive fixation, the osteochondral graft was secured with two to three 1.5-mm titanium screws, achieving stable compression. Although a formal lag screw technique was not used because of instrumentation limitations, adequate compression and stability were achieved intraoperatively, which was critical for allowing early controlled mobilization. This configuration provided robust, rigid compression while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic fragmentation within the graft. Screws were directed volar-to-dorsal, capturing the intact dorsal cortex. Fluoroscopy confirmed concentric joint reduction, anatomical articular stepoff, and appropriate screw length to avoid dorsal tendon impingement. The volar plate was reattached to the graft and collateral ligaments, and the flexor sheath was loosely repaired (Fig. 3).

Operator details

All procedures were performed by a single experienced hand surgeon, ensuring strict consistency in operative technique across all cases.

Quality control

To maintain consistency across cases, all surgeries were performed using a standardized operative protocol including (1) identical surgical exposure technique, (2) controlled graft oversizing, (3) fixation using bicortical lag screws, and (4) standardized rehabilitation protocol.

Intraoperative fluoroscopy was routinely used to verify reduction and hardware placement.

Postoperative care and rehabilitation

To mitigate the risk of PIPJ stiffness, we employed an accelerated active motion protocol. After surgery, the digit was immobilized in a dorsal extension-block splint, with the PIPJ flexed to 20° to 30° to neutralize tension on the volar plate repair.3 Given the delayed presentation of all cases and the associated high risk of joint stiffness, an early controlled mobilization protocol was adopted. Controlled active flexion of the PIP joint was initiated within 3–5 days after surgery under the protection of the splint, whereas extension remained restricted to prevent dorsal subluxation. During the first 2 weeks, patients performed frequent, supervised, home-based active motion exercises. Formal hand therapy was initiated at 2–3 weeks after surgery, focusing on progressive active and gentle passive range of motion. The extension-block was gradually reduced over 4–6 weeks, and unrestricted active motion was typically allowed after 6 weeks, depending on clinical stability.

Outcome measures

Clinical outcomes were evaluated at 6 weeks and 3–6 months after surgery.

The following parameters were recorded as follows: (1) range of motion: maximum PIP joint flexion, extension deficit, total arc of motion; (2) strength assessment: grip strength and key pinch strength were measured and expressed as a percentage of the contralateral uninjured hand; and (3) patient-reported outcomes: pain was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), functional disability was evaluated using the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) questionnaire.

Radiographic evaluation

Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs were obtained at each follow-up visit. Radiographic outcomes were assessed based on joint congruity, graft incorporation, hardware integrity, and maintenance of articular alignment. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, a semiquantitative evaluation was performed, specifically including the assessment of articular stepoff (with a threshold of no stepoff greater than 1 mm) and the absence of visible graft subsidence or recurrent dorsal subluxation.

The hamate donor-site was also evaluated clinically for potential complications, including pain, instability, or weakness.

Data analysis

Given the descriptive nature of this case series and the small sample size, only descriptive statistics were performed. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and range. No inferential statistical tests were performed.

Reporting guidelines and ethics

This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS 2025 guideline.10 Our institution does not require ethical approval to report individual cases or case series. Our patient was fully informed about the surgical procedure and signed a consent form agreeing to the proposed treatment method. Additionally, before the surgery, the surgical approach was approved by the hospital’s deputy director.

The cohort consisted of 3 men and 1 woman, with a mean age of 41.8 ± 18.1 years (range, 16–58 years). Patient Demographics and Surgical Characteristics

The interval between injury and definitive surgical treatment ranged from 4 to 12 weeks, with a mean of 8.0 ± 4.6 weeks. In all cases, more than 50% of the volar articular surface of the base of the middle phalanx was involved, resulting in severe comminution that precluded primary internal fixation. The operative time ranged from 85 to 95 minutes (mean: 89.5 minutes). During surgery, the harvested hamate osteochondral grafts were carefully contoured and secured using two 1.5-mm bicortical titanium lag screws in all patients. This fixation strategy provided immediate structural stability and avoided intraoperative graft fragmentation.

At the 6-week postoperative follow-up, all reconstructed PIPJ demonstrated clinical stability under both coronal and sagittal stress testing (Table 2). Functional assessment revealed a mean active PIPJ arc of motion of 47.5° ± 9.6° (range, 40° to 60°), consisting of a mean flexion of 63.8° ± 4.8° (range, 60° to 70°) and a mean extension deficit of 16.3° ± 4.8° (range, 10° to 20°). Grip strength recovered to an average of 55% ± 6% (range, 50% to 65%) of the contralateral uninjured hand, whereas key pinch strength reached 60% ± 4% (range, 55% to 65%).

Functional outcomes improved substantially at the final follow-up between 3 and 6 months after surgery. The mean active arc of PIPJ motion increased to 81.8° ± 7.9° (range, 75°to 90°). Patients achieved a mean flexion of 89.3° ± 4.6° (range, 85° to 95°), with only a minimal extension deficit averaging 7.5° ± 2.9° (range, 5° to 10°) (Fig. 4). Correspondingly, grip strength improved to a mean of 86% ± 5% (range, 80% to 92%) of the contralateral side, and key pinch strength recovered to 88% ± 5% (range, 82% to 93%).

Postoperative clinical and functional outcomes. Clinical photographs of a patient at the 3-to-6-month final follow-up demonstrate excellent restoration of the functional arc of motion.

Patient-reported outcome measures demonstrated progressive improvement during the follow-up period. At 6 weeks, the mean VAS pain score was 1.25 ± 0.5 at rest (range, 1–2) and 3.25 ± 0.5 during active motion (range, 3–4). By the final follow-up, pain levels had decreased substantially, with a mean VAS score of 0 at rest and 0.5 ± 0.6 during motion (range, 0–1). Similarly, functional disability assessed using the DASH questionnaire improved markedly, decreasing from a mean score of 38.3 ± 6.3 (range, 30–45) at 6 weeks to 7.5 ± 4.0 (range, 3–12) at the final follow-up. All patients returned to their preinjury occupational activities and reported satisfaction with the surgical outcome.

Radiographic evaluation demonstrated maintained concentric joint reduction in all cases throughout the follow-up period. All osteochondral grafts showed successful osseous integration without loss of alignment (Table 3). No cases of graft subsidence, hardware failure, dorsal screw migration, or recurrent dorsal subluxation were observed. The joint space remained preserved in all patients, with no radiographic evidence of early posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Additionally, no donor-site morbidity was identified. None of the patients reported symptoms of carpal instability or persistent pain at the fourth and fifth CMC joint harvest sites.

Discussion

Hemi-hamate arthroplasty has been widely reported as an effective surgical option for unstable PIPJ fracture-dislocations, particularly in the acute setting, with generally favorable functional outcomes. However, most existing studies primarily focus on early reconstruction, and the management of delayed presentations remains less clearly defined. In clinical practice, delayed cases are not uncommon and are frequently associated with additional challenges, including capsular contracture, fibrosis, and joint stiffness. The present study specifically examines this subset of patients, with all cases undergoing reconstruction between 4 and 12 weeks after injury.

In prior reports (Table 4),7,9,11,12 the mean arc of motion following hemi-hamate reconstruction typically ranges from approximately 70° to 85°, as summarized in several case series and systematic reviews. A recent large cohort study by Smith et al9 involving 35 patients reported a mean PIPJ arc of motion of 62°. Notably, their subset of patients undergoing delayed or secondary HHA achieved a substantially reduced arc of only 35°. In the present series, the final arc of motion ranged from 75° to 90° (mean 81.8°), which appears highly favorable. However, direct comparisons with larger studies should be interpreted with caution, given this study’s small sample size and relatively short follow-up. Rather than suggesting definitive superiority, these results indicate that satisfactory functional outcomes may still be achievable even in delayed presentations if managed aggressively.

A notable observation in this series is a reduced range of motion in patients with longer delays to surgery. Cases treated at 12 weeks had lower final motion than those treated earlier (4 weeks), suggesting that timing influences postoperative recovery. This finding is consistent with the known pathophysiology of delayed PIP joint injuries, where prolonged dislocation and articular incongruity lead to capsular contracture, fibrosis, and adaptive shortening of periarticular structures. From a technical perspective, delayed reconstruction presents distinct intraoperative challenges. In our series, varying degrees of callus formation were observed, requiring careful debridement during joint preparation. Furthermore, these cases necessitated more extensive soft tissue release and aggressive arthrolysis to restore joint congruity and mobility, thereby considerably increasing the technical difficulty of the procedure compared with acute reconstruction.

Despite these challenges, stable fixation of the osteochondral graft was achieved in all cases, enabling early controlled mobilization. Initiating active motion within 3–5 days after surgery was prioritized to mitigate stiffness, a well-recognized complication in delayed reconstructions. The favorable functional outcomes observed may largely reflect this emphasis on early controlled mobilization.

Furthermore, early functional outcome measures, such as DASH scores at 6 weeks (mean 38.3), should be interpreted with caution. These early scores likely reflect the effects of postoperative surgical pain, tissue swelling, and temporary splint immobilization rather than true functional recovery. The dramatic improvement in DASH scores at 3–6 months (mean 7.5) was influenced by both the resolution of surgical pain and the progressive restoration of joint motion.

Limitations

Despite its advantages, hemi-hamate reconstruction is not without limitations. Harvesting an osteochondral graft from the hamate introduces the potential risk of donor-site morbidity, including pain, weakness, or long-term degenerative changes at the fourth and fifth CMC joints. However, recent systematic reviews report low rates of clinically significant donor-site morbidity (6.8%).13 Furthermore, in resource-limited health care systems, the use of an autologous osteochondral graft offers distinct socioeconomic advantages by eliminating implant-related costs and avoiding concerns regarding prosthetic wear or revision surgery associated with synthetic arthroplasties.14 As a nonvascularized osteochondral graft, there remains a theoretical risk of graft resorption, collapse, or progressive joint degeneration over time. Although no donor-site morbidity or graft subsidence was observed in our cohort during the follow-up period, the relatively short duration limits our ability to assess these long-term risks.

Additionally, this study’s findings must be interpreted in light of its small sample size (n = 4) and retrospective design. The variability in imaging protocols precluded standardized quantitative radiographic measurements (eg, exact joint space width in millimeters), making only semiquantitative assessment feasible. Therefore, these results should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

In this small case series, hemi-hamate autograft reconstruction showed promising early functional outcomes and stable anatomical restoration in delayed PIP joint fracture-dislocations. The combination of controlled graft oversizing, stable fixation, and early mobilization may optimize recovery in these technically demanding cases. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size and short follow-up. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these results and assess the long-term durability of the nonvascularized grafts.
