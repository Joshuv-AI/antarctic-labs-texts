Symplectic groups over Lie subgroups of involutive algebras
Eugen Rogozinnikov

Abstract
. We introduce the symplectic group Sp 2 ⁡ ( G , σ ) \Sp_{2}(G,\sigma) associated to a Lie subgroup G G of a (possibly noncommutative) associative algebra A A equipped with an anti-involution σ \sigma . Our construction recovers several classical Lie groups as special cases, and in particular provides new realizations of spin groups as instances of Sp 2 ⁡ ( G , σ ) \Sp_{2}(G,\sigma) for suitable subgroups G G of the Clifford algebra. This case is not covered by the framework of [ 1 ] , which focuses on the specific situation G = A × G=A^{\times} , and is thus of particular interest. We construct and study geometric spaces on which Sp 2 ⁡ ( G , σ ) \Sp_{2}(G,\sigma) acts. In particular, we define the space of G G -isotropic elements and the corresponding space of G G -isotropic lines, which generalize the classical projective line. We analyze the group action on these spaces and introduce natural invariants, such as the notion of positive triples and quadruples of G G -isotropic lines and a generalized cross–ratio of positive quadruples of G G -isotropic lines. Finally, when the Lie algebra of G G is Hermitian, we define the associated Riemannian symmetric space of Sp 2 ⁡ ( G , σ ) \Sp_{2}(G,\sigma) and provide several models for it.

Introduction

The construction and study of symplectic groups over associative, possibly noncommutative, involutive unital algebras was systematically developed in
[
1
]
. That paper introduces
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(A,\sigma)
for an associative (possibly noncommutative) unital algebra

\sigma
. The authors further construct the space of isotropic
A
-lines, define generalized cross–ratios and Kashiwara–Maslov type indices, and provide several models of the symmetric space in the Hermitian case. This approach already realizes many classical Lie groups (e.g.
\Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{R})

\SO(2,n)
or their spin covers) — are not obtained directly by the constructions presented in
[
1
]
.

A very recent article
[
12
]
further develops the study of groups over noncommutative involutive algebras and, complementing the results of
[

]
, constructs symmetric-space models for several group constructions (such as
\mathrm{O}_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
,

, and

The goal of this article is to introduce and analyse the group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(G,\sigma)
associated with a Lie subgroup
G

A^{\times}
of a unital real finite-dimensional associative involutive algebra
(A,\sigma)

y&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;x\in B,\;y,z\in B^{\sigma}\right\}.

2.
Hermitian Lie algebras with anti-involution

2.1.
Lie subalgebras of involutive algebras

Let
A
be a unital associative, possibly non-commutative, finite-dimensional semisimple algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
anti-involution
on
A
is a
\mathbb{K}
-linear map
\sigma\colon A\to A
such that

•

\sigma(ab)=\sigma(b)\sigma(a)
;

•

\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
.

An
involutive
\mathbb{K}
-algebra
is a pair
(A,\sigma)
, where
A
is an
\mathbb{K}
-algebra as above and
\sigma
is an anti-involution on
A
.

Remark 2.2
.

Sometimes in the literature, the maps satisfying Definition
2.1
are called just
involutions
. We add the prefix “anti” in order to emphasize that they exchange factors and to be consistent with
[
1
,
13
,
12
]
.

The algebra
A
can be turned into a Lie algebra with the Lie bracket
[x,y]=xy-yx
. Let
B\subseteq A
be a Lie subalgebra that is closed under
\sigma
. We define:

B^{\sigma}:=\Fix_{B}(\sigma),

B^{-\sigma}:=\Fix_{B}(-\sigma).

Proposition 2.3
.

[
1
, Proposition 2.30]
If
B
is a Lie subalgebra of
A
containing
1
, then
B^{\times}
is open and dense in
B
and
(B^{\sigma})^{\times}:=B^{\times}\cap A^{\sigma}
is open and dense in
B^{\sigma}:=B\cap A^{\sigma}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A Lie subalgebra
B
is called
of Jordan type
(with respect to
\sigma
), if for every
x,y\in B^{\sigma}
,
xy\in B
.

Proposition 2.5
.

A Lie subalgebra
B
is of Jordan type if and only if for every
x\in B
and for every
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
\sigma(x)b+bx\in B^{\sigma}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Let
B
be of Jordan type.
We take
x^{s}:=\frac{x+\sigma(x)}{2}
,
x^{a}:=\frac{x-\sigma(x)}{2}
, then
x=x^{s}+x^{a}
,
x^{s}\in B^{\sigma}
and
\sigma(x^{a})=-x^{a}
. Then we can write

\sigma(x)b+bx=(x^{s}-x^{a})b+b(x^{s}+x^{a})=(x^{s}b+bx^{s})+(bx^{a}-x^{a}b).

Since
B
is of Jordan type,
x^{s}b,bx^{s}\in B
and so
x^{s}b+bx^{s}\in B^{\sigma}
. Further,
bx^{a}-x^{a}b=[b,x^{a}]\in B
and
\sigma(bx^{a}-x^{a}b)=-x^{a}b+bx^{a}
, i.e.,
bx^{a}-x^{a}b\in B^{\sigma}
. So we obtain,
\sigma(x)b+bx\in B^{\sigma}
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Let
x,y\in B^{\sigma}
, then

2xy=xy+yx+xy-yx=\sigma(x)y+yx+[x,y]\in B.

Therefore,
B
is of Jordan type.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Let
B
be of Jordan type. Notice that for
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
b^{k}\in B^{\sigma}
for every
k
. However, for
b\in B^{-\sigma}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}
,
b^{k}
is not necessarily an element of
B
.

Remark 2.7
.

The following properties hold:

[B^{-\sigma},B^{-\sigma}]\subseteq B^{-\sigma},\;\;[B^{-\sigma},B^{\sigma}]\subseteq B^{\sigma},\;\;[B^{\sigma},B^{\sigma}]\subseteq B^{-\sigma}.

In particular,
B^{-\sigma}
is a Lie subalgebra of
B
.

2.2.
Weakly Hermitian Lie subalgebras

Let
B
be a Lie subalgebra of a real involutive algebra
(A,\sigma)
. We denote by
B_{\geq 0}^{\sigma}
the closed convex cone in
B^{\sigma}
generated by squares of elements in
B^{\sigma}
.

Definition 2.8
.

A Lie subalgebra
B
of Jordan type (with respect to
\sigma
) is called
weakly Hermitian
, if:

(1)

1\in B
;

(2)

The convex cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is proper, i.e., it does not contain lines;

(3)

The space
B^{\sigma}
does not contain nilpotent elements, i.e., for every
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
b^{2}=0
if and only if
b=0
.

If
B
is real weakly Hermitian, we define
B^{\sigma}_{+}:=(B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0})^{\times}.

Then
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is the topological closure of
B^{\sigma}_{+}
. In this case,
B^{\sigma}_{+}
and
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
are proper convex cones in
B^{\sigma}
. Notice that a convex cone is proper if and only if for every two its elements
b_{1},b_{2}
,
b_{1}+b_{2}=0
if and only if
b_{1}=b_{2}=0
.

Let
A_{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
A
. Slightly abusing our notation, we denote by
\sigma\colon A_{\mathbb{C}}\to A_{\mathbb{C}}
the complex linear extension
\sigma\colon A\to A
, and we denote by
\bar{\sigma}\colon A\to A
the composition of the complex anti-involution
\sigma
and the complex conjugation in
A_{\mathbb{C}}
. Notice that
\bar{\sigma}
is a real anti-involution on
A_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let
B_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Lie subalgebra of
A_{\mathbb{C}}
which is closed under the complex conjugation and under
\sigma
. Notice that
B
is always a complexification of a unique real Lie subalgebra
B\subseteq A
closed under the anti-involution
\sigma\colon A\to A
. As above, we denote by
(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bar{\sigma}})_{\geq 0}
the closed convex cone in
B^{\bar{\sigma}}
generated by elements of the form
\bar{b}b\in B^{\bar{\sigma}}
where
b\in B^{\sigma}
, and by
(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bar{\sigma}})_{+}:=(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bar{\sigma}})_{\geq 0}^{\times}
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
B
be a Lie subalgebra of
(A,\sigma)
closed under
\sigma
.

(1)

The Lie subalgebra
B
is of Jordan type with respect to
\sigma
if and only if
B_{\mathbb{C}}
is of Jordan type with respect to
\sigma
.

(2)

The cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is proper if and only if the cone
(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}
is proper.

Proof.

(1)
Let
B
be of Jordan type. Let
b,b^{\prime}\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}
such that
b=b_{1}+b_{2}i
,
b^{\prime}=b^{\prime}_{1}+b^{\prime}_{2}i
where
b_{1},b_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}
. Then

bb^{\prime}=(b_{1}b_{1}^{\prime}-b_{2}b_{2}^{\prime})+(b_{1}b_{2}^{\prime}+b_{2}b_{1}^{\prime})i\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}.

If
B_{\mathbb{C}}
is of Jordan type, then clearly
B=B_{\mathbb{C}}\cap A
is it as well.

(2)
Let the cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
be proper. By contradiction, we assume that there exists
0\neq c\in(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}
such that
ct\in(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We can write
c=\bar{c}_{1}c_{1}+\dots+\bar{c}_{k}c_{k}
where
c_{i}=c_{i}^{\prime}+c_{i}^{\prime\prime}i
,
c_{i}^{\prime},c_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in B^{\sigma}
for all
i
. Then

\Ree(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left((c_{i}^{\prime})^{2}+(c_{i}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\right)\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}.

Therefore,
\Ree(c)\neq 0
, otherwise,
c_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}^{\prime\prime}=0
for all
i
because
B^{\sigma}_{+}
is a proper cone, i.e.,
c=0
. Therefore, the line
\Ree(c)\mathbb{R}
is contained in
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
. This contradicts to the properness of the cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
.

Let the cone
(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}
be proper. Since
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\subseteq(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}
, the cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
contains no line, i.e., it is proper.
∎

Notice that, if
B
is of Jordan type or weakly Hermitian with respect to
\sigma
, then, in general, it is not of Jordan type with respect to
\bar{\sigma}
(cf. Remark
7.3
).

We recall the definition of Jordan algebra and formally real Jordan algebra.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(V,\circ)
be an possibly non-associative algebra over some field
\mathbb{K}
.
(V,\circ)
said to be a
Jordan algebra
if for all
x,y\in V

(1)

x\circ y=y\circ x
;

(2)

(x\circ y)\circ(x\circ x)=x\circ(y\circ(x\circ x))
(Jordan identity).

A Jordan algebra
(V,\circ)
is called
formally real
if for all
x,y\in V
,
x^{2}+y^{2}=0
implies
x,y=0
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
B
be a Lie subalgebra of an involutive algebra
(A,\sigma)
.

•

If
B
is of Jordan type, then the space
(B^{\sigma},\circ)
is a Jordan algebra where

x\circ y=\frac{xy+yx}{2}.

•

If additionally
B
is weakly Hermitian, then the Jordan algebra
(B^{\sigma},\circ)
is formally real.

Proof.

Since for all
x,y\in B^{\sigma}
,
xy\in B
, so we obtain
x\circ y\in B^{\sigma}
. Moreover,
x\circ y=y\circ x
is clear. Further, the Jordan identity holds:

(x\circ y)\circ(x\circ x)=\frac{xy+yx}{2}\circ x^{2}=\frac{xyx^{2}+yx^{3}+x^{3}y+x^{2}yx}{4}=

=\frac{xyx^{2}+x^{3}y+yx^{3}+x^{2}yx}{4}=x\circ\frac{yx^{2}+x^{2}y}{2}=x\circ(y\circ(x\circ x)).

Thus
(B^{\sigma},\circ)
is a Jordan algebra.

Assume now
B
to be weakly Hermitian. Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in B^{\sigma}
, then
a_{i}^{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
. The convex cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is proper, so
a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}
vanishes if and only if
a_{1}^{2}=a_{2}^{2}=0
. Therefore,
a_{1}=a_{2}=0
by (
3
) in Definition
2.8
.
∎

2.4.
Spectral theorem

In this section, we assume
B
to be weakly Hermitian. As we have seen,
(B^{\sigma},\circ)
is a formally real Jordan algebra.

We are going to state the first versions of the spectral theorem for formally real Jordan algebras. But before we do it, first, we give some necessary definitions:

Definition 2.14
.

•

An element
c\in B^{\sigma}
is called an
idempotent
if
c^{2}=c
.

•

Two idempotents
c,c^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}
are called
orthogonal
if
c\circ c^{\prime}=0
.

•

A tuple
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
of pairwise orthogonal idempotents is called a
complete orthogonal system of idempotents
if
c_{1}+\dots+c_{k}=1
.

Remark 2.15
.

Every idempotent
c\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
.

Theorem 2.16

(Spectral theorem, first version
[
7
, Theorem III.1.1]
)
.

For every
b\in B^{\sigma}
, there exist a unique
k\in\mathbb{N}
, unique real numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, all distinct, and a unique complete system of orthogonal idempotents
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in B^{\sigma}
such that

b=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}c_{i}.

Corollary 2.17
.

For
b\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
, the numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0
. For
b\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
, the numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}>0
. In particular,

B^{\sigma}_{+}=\{b^{2}\mid b\in(B^{\sigma})^{\times}\},\;B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}=\{b^{2}\mid b\in B^{\sigma}\},

i.e., the sets of squares already build cones.

Corollary 2.18
.

The set of all invertible elements
(B^{\sigma})^{\times}
of
B^{\sigma}
consists of elements such that all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
. If all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
, then

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}c_{i}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{-1}c_{i}.

Corollary 2.19
.

The cone
B^{\sigma}_{+}
is open in
B^{\sigma}
, open and closed in
(B^{\sigma})^{\times}
. The cone
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is closed in
B^{\sigma}
.

Corollary 2.20
.

For every (continuous or smooth) function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the (continuous, resp. smooth) map

\hat{f}\colon B^{\sigma}\to B^{\sigma}

can be defined: if

b=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}c_{i},

then

\hat{f}(b):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(\lambda_{i})c_{i}.

This map is well defined because the spectral decomposition is unique. Analogously, for any function
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
or
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\hat{f}\colon B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\to B^{\sigma}
resp.
\hat{f}\colon B^{\sigma}_{+}\to B^{\sigma}
can be defined.

In particular, for every
b\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
, the element
b^{t}\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
for
t>0
is well-defined. This definition is compatible with integer powers of element.

Corollary 2.21
.

The space
B^{\sigma}_{+}
is homeomorphic to
B^{\sigma}
, more precisely
\exp(B^{\sigma})=B^{\sigma}_{+}
. In particular,
B^{\sigma}_{+}
is open in
B^{\sigma}
and contractible.
\{1\}\subset B^{\sigma}_{+}
is a deformation retract of
B^{\sigma}_{+}
.

Proof.

As an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space,
B^{\sigma}
is contractible.
\{0\}\subset B^{\sigma}
is a deformation retract of
B^{\sigma}
. The map
\hat{f}\colon B^{\sigma}\to B^{\sigma}_{+}
for
f(t)=\exp(t)
provides a homeomorphism.
∎

To state the second version of the spectral theorem, we need to give some additional definitions:

Definition 2.22
.

•

An idempotent
0\neq c\in B^{\sigma}
is called
primitive
if it cannot be written as a sum of two orthogonal non-zero idempotents.

•

A complete orthogonal system of primitive idempotents
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
is called a
Jordan frame
.

Theorem 2.23

(Spectral theorem, second version
[
7
, Theorem III.1.2]
)
.

Suppose,
B^{\sigma}
has rank
n
. For every
b\in B^{\sigma}
there exist a Jordan frame
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
and real numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}e_{i}.

The numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
(with their multiplicities) called
eigenvalues
of
b
are uniquely determined by
b
. In particular, they do not depend (up to permutations) on the Jordan frame
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\in B^{\sigma}
.

Remark 2.24
.

A Jordan frame
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\in B^{\sigma}
associated to the element
b\in B^{\sigma}
as in Theorem
2.23
is, in contrast to the complete system of orthogonal idempotents from Theorem
2.16
, in general not unique. In fact, it is unique if and only if all eigenvalues of
b
are distinct.

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
B
be weakly Hermitian.
Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in B^{\sigma}
be two commuting elements. Then there exists an element
b\in\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]
such that
\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]=\mathbb{R}[b]
. in particular,
b,b_{1},b_{2}
share a Jordan frame.

Proof.

The associative
\mathbb{R}
-algebra
\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]
is commutative and does not contain any nilpotent elements because
\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]\subseteq B^{\sigma}
. This implies that
\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]
is semisimple, and by Wedderburn’s theorem, it is isomorphic to a finite direct sum of copies of
\mathbb{R}
and copies of
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, there exists a generating element
b\in\mathbb{R}[b_{1},b_{2}]
. Since
b\in B^{\sigma}
, we apply the spectral theorem:
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}e_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
a Jordan frame. Since
b_{1},b_{2}
are polynomials in
b
, they share the Jordan frame
e
.
∎

Definition 2.26
.

Let

and

are all its eigenvalues (with multiplicities). We define the

and the

\tr(b):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i},\;\det(b):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}.

2.5.

As before, let

be an

-algebra with an anti-involution. The space

\displaystyle G^{\sigma}_{0}

\displaystyle:=G_{0}\cap A^{\sigma},

\displaystyle\OO_{1}(G_{0},\sigma):=\OO(G_{0},\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in G_{0}\mid\sigma(u)u=1\}.

The Lie algebra of

. Moreover,

Definition 2.29

Proposition 2.30
.

Let
B
be of Jordan type. For every
g\in G_{0}
and for every
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
\sigma(g)bg\in B^{\sigma}
.

Proof.

We consider the following map:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F\colon\OO(G_{0},\sigma)\times\exp(B^{\sigma})&\to&G_{0}\\
\quad(u,b)&\mapsto&ub\end{array}

We notice that since for all
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
b^{2}\in B^{\sigma}
, we have
b^{n}\in B^{\sigma}
for all positive integers
n
. In particular,
\exp(B^{\sigma})\subseteq G^{\sigma}_{0}\cap B^{\sigma}
. Moreover,
B^{\sigma}
is closed in
A
, therefore,
\exp(b)-1\in B^{\sigma}
. Since
\exp
is a local diffeomorphism at
0\in B^{\sigma}
,
T_{1}\exp(B^{\sigma})=B^{\sigma}
.

The differential of
F
at
(1,1)
is a bijection. Indeed:

D_{(1,1)}F(x,y)=x+y\in B

where
x\in B^{-\sigma}=\Lie(\OO(G_{0},\sigma))
,
y\in B^{\sigma}=T_{1}\exp(B^{\sigma})
.
Therefore, in a small neighborhood

of
(1,1)\in\OO(G_{0},\sigma)\times B^{\sigma}_{+}
, the map

is a homeomorphism. In particular,
G_{0}
is generated by

, i.e., for every
g\in G_{0}
, there exist
r\geq 0
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{r}\in\OO(G_{0},\sigma)
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}\in\exp(B^{\sigma})
such that
g=u_{1}b_{1}\dots u_{r}b_{r}
.

Since
\sigma(u)=u^{-1}
for
u\in\OO(G_{0},\sigma)
,
\sigma(u)bu=u^{-1}bu=\mathrm{Ad}(u^{-1})b\in B^{\sigma}
for all
b\in B^{\sigma}
.

Let
b\in B^{\sigma}
,
b^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
, then
b^{\prime}=1+b_{0}
for
b_{0}\in B^{\sigma}
.

\sigma(b^{\prime})bb^{\prime}=b^{\prime}bb^{\prime}=(1+b_{0})b(b+b_{0})=b+b_{0}b+bb_{0}+b_{0}bb_{0}.

By Proposition
2.5
,
b_{0}b+bb_{0}=\tilde{b}
for
\tilde{b}\in B^{\sigma}
. Therefore,
b_{0}bb_{0}=b_{0}\tilde{b}-(b_{0})^{2}b\in B
and, since
b^{\prime}bb^{\prime}\in A^{\sigma}
, we obtain
b^{\prime}bb^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}
.

Finally, by induction, we obtain
\sigma(g)bg\in B^{\sigma}
for all

.
∎

Thus, the following maps define a right and a left actions of
G_{0}
on
B^{\sigma}
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi\colon G_{0}&\to&\Aut(B^{\sigma})\\
g&\mapsto&[\psi(g)\colon b\mapsto\sigma(g)bg],\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\psi^{\prime}\colon G_{0}&\to&\Aut(B^{\sigma})\\
g&\mapsto&[\psi^{\prime}(g)\colon b\mapsto gb\sigma(g)].\end{array}

Proposition 2.31
.

If
B
is weakly Hermitian, then
B^{\sigma}_{+}\subseteq G_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
b\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. Take its spectral decomposition:
b=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}c_{i}
where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}>0
,
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
is a complete system of orthogonal idempotents. Then

\displaystyle\log(b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log(\lambda_{i})c_{i}\in B^{\sigma}

and
\exp(\log(b))=b\in G_{0}
because the map
\exp
defined on
\mathbb{R}
extended to
B^{\sigma}
and the exponential map
\exp\colon B\to G
restricted to
B^{\sigma}
defined by the same power series and thus they agree.
∎

Corollary 2.32
.

Let

be weakly Hermitian.

•

For every
b\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
and for every

,
\sigma(g)bg\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
.

•

For every

and for every

It is clear that

for

and

is in the connected component of

The second one follows from the fact that

in

.

Theorem 2.33

(Polar decomposition for
G_{0}
)
.

Let
B
be weakly Hermitian. The following maps are homeomorphisms:

\begin{matrix}\pol\colon&\OO(G_{0},\sigma)\times B^{\sigma}_{+}&\to&G_{0}\\
&(u,b)&\mapsto&ub,\end{matrix}

\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\pol}\colon&B^{\sigma}_{+}\times\OO(G_{0},\sigma)&\to&G_{0}\\
&(b,u)&\mapsto&bu.\end{matrix}

Proof.

We prove the statement for the map
\pol
.
This map is well-defined because
B^{\sigma}_{+}\subseteq G_{0}
.

First, we prove surjectivity: Let
g\in G
, then
\sigma(g)g\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. We define
b:=(\sigma(g)g)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, then
u:=g(\sigma(g)g)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in\OO(G_{0},\sigma)
. Indeed,

\sigma(u)u=(\sigma(g)g)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma(g)g(\sigma(g)g)^{-\frac{1}{2}}=1.

Further, we prove injectivity: Let
g=ub=u^{\prime}b^{\prime}
where
u,u^{\prime}\in\OO(G,\sigma)
,
b,b^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. Then
\sigma(g)g=(b^{\prime})^{2}=b^{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. We take the spectral decompositions of
b
and
b^{\prime}
:

b=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}c_{i},\;b^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}\lambda_{i}^{\prime}c_{i}^{\prime}

where all

,
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}^{\prime}>0
and
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
,
(c^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}
complete orthogonal systems of idempotents of

. Then

Because of the uniqueness of the spectral decomposition,

and, up to reordering, all

. But all

\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

and

Finally, by definition,

is continuous. Moreover,

\pol^{-1}(g)=(g(\sigma(g)g))^{-\frac{1}{2}},(\sigma(g)g))^{\frac{1}{2}})

∎

Proposition 2.38

is a normal subgroup of

Proof.

It is enough to show that

for all

b_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}e_{i}
and take a group

S_{e}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}e_{i}\mid\varepsilon_{i}\in\{1,-1\}\right\}\subset(B^{\sigma})^{\times}\cap\OO(G,\sigma).

Then, as we have seen in the proof of Theorem
2.39
, every connected component of
G
contains an element form
S_{e}
. Moreover, since
\OO(G,\sigma)
is a deformation retract of
G
, and
S_{e}\in\OO(G,\sigma)
, every connected component of
\OO(G,\sigma)
contains an element of
S_{e}
. Therefore, there exists
s\in S_{e}
such that
u=u_{0}s
for an
u_{0}\in\OO(G_{0},\sigma)
. Then

g=ub_{0}=u_{0}sb_{0}=:u_{0}b

for
b:=sb_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\lambda_{i}e_{i}\in(B^{\sigma})^{\times}
.
∎

Remark 2.47
.

The map
\pol^{\prime}
is in general not injective. For example, if we take

B=A=\Mat(2,\mathbb{R})

with
\sigma
to be the transposition, then

\OO(G_{0},\sigma)=\SO(2,\mathbb{R}),\;B^{\sigma}=\Sym(2,\mathbb{R}).

Then the matrix

\Id=u_{1}b_{1}=u_{2}b_{2}

for
u_{1}=b_{1}=\Id
,
u_{2}=b_{2}=-\Id
. The reason for that is the fact that
\OO(G_{0},\sigma)\cap B^{\sigma}\neq\{1\}
.

Let
\bar{G}
be the topological closure of
G
in
A
.

Proposition 2.48
.

\bar{G}
is a monoid.

Proof.

Let
g,g^{\prime}\in\bar{G}\subseteq A
, then
gg^{\prime}\in A
. We want to show that there exists
\{h_{i}\}\subset G
such that
\lim h_{i}=gg^{\prime}
. Since
g,g^{\prime}\in\bar{G}
, there exist
\{g_{i}\},\{g^{\prime}_{i}\}\subset G
such that
\lim g_{i}=g
,
\lim g^{\prime}_{i}=g^{\prime}
. Take
h_{i}=g_{i}g^{\prime}_{i}\in G
, then

\lim h_{i}=\lim g_{i}g^{\prime}_{i}=\lim g_{i}\lim g^{\prime}_{i}=gg^{\prime}.\qed

By taking closure in the polar decomposition, we get the following map:

\begin{matrix}\bpol\colon&\OO(G,\sigma)\times B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}&\to&\bar{G}\\
&(u,b)&\mapsto&ub.\end{matrix}

This map is not a homeomorphism anymore, but it is surjective. If
B
is Hermitian, it is also proper because
\OO(G,\sigma)
is compact. We define the following surjective map:

\begin{matrix}\theta\colon&\bar{G}&\to&B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\\
&g&\mapsto&\sigma(g)g.\end{matrix}

The map
\theta
maps surjectively
G
to
B^{\sigma}_{+}
.

Proposition 2.49
.

If
B
is Hermitian, the map
\theta\colon\bar{G}\to B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
is proper.

Proof.

Let
K\subset B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
be a compact subset. Then

\theta^{-1}(K)=\{ub^{\frac{1}{2}}\mid u\in\OO(G,\sigma),b\in K\}=\bpol(\OO(G,\sigma)\times K).

Since
\OO(G,\sigma)\times K
is compact in
\OO(G,\sigma)\times B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
and the map
\bpol
is continuous, the set
\theta^{-1}(K)
is compact.
∎

Proposition 2.50
.

If
B
is a Hermitian subalgebra in
A
, the following spaces

\displaystyle D(\bar{G},\sigma):=

\displaystyle\{a\in\bar{G}\mid 1-\sigma(a)a\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\}\subseteq\bar{G}

\displaystyle D(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma},\bar{\sigma}):=

\displaystyle\{a\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid 1-\bar{a}a\in(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bar{\sigma}})_{\geq 0}\}\subseteq B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}

are compact.

Proof.

First, we need the following Lemma:

Lemma 2.51
.

Let
C
be a closed proper convex cone in some finite-dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
V
. Then for every
c\in V
, the set
K:=C\cap(c-C)
is compact.

Proof.

By contradiction, we assume
K
is not compact. We fix some norm
\|\cdot\|
on
V
. Since
K
is closed, by Heine-Borel it must be unbounded, i.e., there exists a sequence
(x_{n})
such that
\|x_{n}\|\to\infty
. Since
y_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}}{\|x_{n}\|}\in S^{1}
and for finite-dimensional
V
,
S_{1}
is compact, there exists a limit point
y
of
(y_{n})
. Since
C
is a closed cone,
y\mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq C\cap(c-C)
and, therefore
c-y\mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq C\cap(c-C)
. Analogously,
c+y\mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq C\cap(c-C)
and, therefore
-y\mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq C\cap(c-C)
. That means,
y\mathbb{R}\in C\cap(-C)
, so
y=0
. This contradicts to
y\in S^{1}
. Therefore,
K
is compact.
∎

By Lemma
2.51
, the set

\displaystyle K:=\{x\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\mid 1-x\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\}=B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\cap(1-B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0})

is compact. Since
\theta^{-1}(K)=D(\bar{G},\sigma)
and
\theta
is proper,
D(\bar{G},\sigma)
is compact.

Further, notice that
D(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma},\bar{\sigma})
is closed. Let now
c=c_{1}+c_{2}i\in D(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma},\bar{\sigma})
where
c_{1},c_{2}\in B^{\sigma}
. Then

1-\bar{c}c=(1-c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2})-[c_{1},c_{2}]i\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}+B^{-\sigma}i.

In particular,
1-c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}
. This means,
c_{1},c_{2}
are elements of the compact domain
D(\bar{G},\sigma)
, i.e.,
D(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma},\bar{\sigma})
is compact.
∎

Corollary 2.52
.

Let
G
be the minimal extension of
G_{0}
. The map

\begin{matrix}\overline{\pol^{\prime}}\colon&\OO(G_{0},\sigma)\times B^{\sigma}&\to&\bar{G}\\
&(u,b)&\mapsto&ub\end{matrix}

is surjective and continuous. In particular,
\bar{G}
is connected.

3.
Symplectic group over
G

3.1.
Symplectic group
\Sp_{2}(A,\sigma)
over an involutive algebra

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive algebra. The symplectic group over
(A,\sigma)
was introduced and studied in
[
1
]
. In this section, we recall the definition of this group and state its properties that will be needed in the sequel of this chapter.

Definition 3.1
.

A
\sigma
-sesquilinear form

\omega
on a right
A
-module
V
is a map

\omega\colon V\times V\to A

such that for all
x,y,z\in V
and for all
r_{1},r_{2}\in A

\omega(x+y,z)=\omega(x,z)+\omega(y,z),

\omega(x,y+z)=\omega(x,y)+\omega(x,z),

\omega(x_{1}r_{1},x_{2}r_{2})=\sigma(r_{1})\omega(x_{1},x_{2})r_{2}.

We denote by

\Aut(\omega):=\{f\in\Aut(V)\mid\forall x,y\in V:\omega(f(x),f(y))=\omega(x,y)\}

the group of symmetries of
\omega
. We also define the corresponding Lie algebra:

\End(\omega):=\{f\in\End(V)\mid\forall x,y\in V:\omega(f(x),y)+\omega(x,f(y))=0\}

with the usual Lie bracket
[f,g]=fg-gf
.

We now set
V=A^{2}
. We view
V
as the set of columns and endow it with the structure of a right
A
-module. The anti-involution
\sigma
extends to an involutive map on
A^{2}
and on the space
\Mat_{2}(A)
of
2\times 2
-matrices with coefficients in
A
componentwisely.

Definition 3.2
.

We make the following definitions:

(1)

A pair
(x,y)
for
x,y\in A^{2}
is called a
basis
of
A^{2}
if for every
z\in A^{2}
there exist
a,b\in A
such that
z=xa+yb
.

(2)

The element
x\in A^{2}
is called
regular
if there exists
y\in A^{2}
such that
(x,y)
is a basis of
A^{2}
.

(3)

l\subseteq A^{2}
is called a
line
if
l=xA
for a regular
x\in A^{2}
. We denote the space of lines of
A^{2}
by
\mathbb{P}(A^{2})
.

(4)

Two regular elements
x,y\in A^{2}
are called
linearly independent

if
(x,y)
is a basis of
A^{2}
.

(5)

Two lines
l,m
are called
transverse
if
l=xA
,
m=yA
for linearly independent
x,y\in A^{2}
.

(6)

An element
x\in A^{2}
is called
isotropic
with respect to
\omega
if
\omega(x,x)=0
. The set of all isotropic regular elements of
(A^{2},\omega)
is denoted by
\Is(\omega)
.

(7)

A line
l
is called isotropic if
l=xA
for a regular isotropic
x\in A^{2}
. The set of all isotropic lines of
(A^{2},\omega)
is denoted by
\PIs(\omega)
.

From now on, we assume
\omega(x,y):=\sigma(x)^{t}\Omega y
with
\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
for
x,y\in A^{2}
. The form
\omega
is called the
standard symplectic form
on
A^{2}
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive algebra. If
\omega
is the standard symplectic form, then the group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(A,\sigma):=\mathrm{Aut}(\omega)
is called the
symplectic group

\mathrm{Sp}_{2}
over
(A,\sigma)
.

We have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Sp}_{2}(A,\sigma)&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,\sigma(a)c,\,\sigma(b)d\in A^{\sigma},\,\sigma(a)d-\sigma(c)b=1\right\}\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_{2}(A).\end{aligned}

We can determine the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}_{2}(A,\sigma)
of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(A,\sigma)
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{sp}_{2}(A,\sigma)&=\left\{g\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(A)\,\middle|\,\sigma(g)^{t}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}g=0\right\}\\
&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x&z\\
y&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,x\in A,\;y,z\in A^{\sigma}\right\}.\end{aligned}

A basis
(x,y)
of
A^{2}
is called symplectic if
\omega(x,x)=\omega(x,y)=0
and
\omega(x,y)=1
.

3.2.
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}_{2}(B,\sigma)

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive real algebra. Let
B\subseteq A
be a Lie subalgebra.

Consider the vector space

\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x&z\\
y&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,x\in B,y,z\in B^{\sigma}\right\}\subseteq\spp_{2}(A,\sigma)

In general, it is not a Lie algebra. We need to take the following additional assumption:

Proposition 3.4
.

The vector space
\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)
is a Lie subalgebra of
\spp_{2}(A,\sigma)
if and only if
B
is of Jordan type.

Proof.

Matrices

r(z):=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\,l(x):=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
y&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }d(z):=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}

generate
\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)
as a vector space. The vector space
\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)
is a Lie subalgebra of
\spp_{2}(A,\sigma)
if and only if all Lie bracket of these elements are in
\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)
.

For
y,z\in B^{\sigma}

[r(z),l(y)]=\begin{pmatrix}zy&0\\
0&-yz\end{pmatrix}\in\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)

so
zy,yz\in B
and we need the condition that
B
is of Jordan type.

For
a\in B
,
z\in B^{\sigma}
:

[d(x),r(z)]=\begin{pmatrix}0&xz+z\sigma(x)\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)

so
xz+z\sigma(x)\in B^{\sigma}
. This holds for
B
of Jordan type by Proposition
2.5
.

For
a\in B
,
y\in B^{\sigma}
:

[l(y),d(x)]=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\sigma(x)y+yx&0\end{pmatrix}\in\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)

so
\sigma(x)y+yx\in B^{\sigma}
. This holds for
B
of Jordan type by Proposition
2.5
.
∎

3.2.1.
Symplectic Lie algebra over Jordan algebras

The symplectic Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}_{2}
can be defined in a more general context in terms of Jordan algebras and their derivations without the assumption for them to be embedded into an associative algebra.

Let
(J,\circ)
be a Jordan algebra. Let
\Aut(J)
be the group of automorphisms of
J
. The Lie algebra of
\Aut(J)
is the Lie algebra
\Der(J)
of all derivations of
J
(with respect to the commutator bracket).

The space
B:=J\oplus\Der(J)
admits a structure of a Lie algebra: for
x,y\in J
,
[x,y]\in\Der(J)
is the derivation acting on
z\in J
as follows

[x,y]z:=x\circ(y\circ z)-y\circ(x\circ z)

and for
D\in\Der(J)
and
x\in J
,
[D,x]=Dx\in J
. This provides a Lie algebra structure on
B
(for more details see
[
7
, Section II.4]
). We define the anti-involution
\sigma\colon B\to B
by the rule
\sigma(x)=x
for
x\in J
and
\sigma(x)=-x
for
x\in\Der(J)
.

The following space of matrices:

\spp_{2}(B,\sigma):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,x\in B,\;y,z\in J\right\}.

is a Lie algebra. If
B
is a Lie subalgebra of an associative involutive algebra
(A,\sigma)
, then this definition agrees with the definition from Section
3.2
.

In this way, the exceptional Hermitian Lie algebra of tube type
\mathfrak{e}_{7}^{-25}
can be realized as follows: let
J=\Herm_{3}(\mathbb{O})
be the exceptional Jordan algebra of all Hermitian
3\times 3
-octonionic matrices. Its automorphism group agrees with the compact exceptional Lie group
F_{4}
. Therefore,
\Der(J)=\mathfrak{f}_{4}=\Lie(F_{4})
and
B=\mathfrak{f}_{4}\oplus\Herm_{3}(\mathbb{O})\cong\mathfrak{e}_{6}^{-26}\oplus\mathbb{R}
. However, the exceptional Jordan algebra
J
cannot be embedded into an associative algebra (cf.
[
11
, Corollary 2.8.5]
). Thus we cannot realize it in the way provided in Section
3.2
. This fact will also prevent us from describing the corresponding Lie group in terms of
2\times 2
-matrices with coefficients in an appropriate submonoid of
A
.

3.2.2.
Indefinite orthogonal Lie group over involutive algebras

Similarly as the symplectic group, the indefinite orthogonal group
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
over an involutive algebra
(A,\sigma)
can be defined.

Let
V=A^{2}
be the right
A
-module as above, let
h(x,y):=\sigma(x)^{t}Hy
, where
x,y\in V
and
H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, be the standard indefinite orthogonal. Then the group
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
is defined as the group
\Aut(h)\subset\GL_{2}(A)
. Alternatively, one can take
H=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, the corresponding group
\Aut(h)
is then conjugated to
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
.

The group
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
and its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{o}_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
can be described explicitly:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{O}_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,\sigma(a)c,\,\sigma(b)d\in A^{-\sigma},\,\sigma(a)d+\sigma(c)b=1\right\}\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_{2}(A),\\
\mathfrak{o}_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)&=\left\{g\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2}(A)\,\middle|\,\sigma(g)^{t}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}g=0\right\}\\
&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x&z\\
y&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,x\in A,\;y,z\in A^{-\sigma}\right\}.\end{aligned}

For more details about the group
\mathrm{O}_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
we refer to
[
12
, Part 1]
. However, for a Lie subalgebra
B\subset A
of Jordan type, the vector space

\mathfrak{o}_{(1,1)}(B,\sigma)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x&z\\
y&-\sigma(x)\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,x\in B,\;y,z\in B^{-\sigma}\right\}

is (in general) not a Lie algebra, in contrast to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sp}_{2}(B,\sigma)
(cf. Proposition
3.4
). The obstruction to being a Lie algebra is the fact that for
x,y\in B^{-\sigma}
, their product is, in general, not contained in
B
.

3.3.
First definition of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive real algebra. Let
G\leq A^{\times}
be a Lie subgroup of
A^{\times}
which is closed under
\sigma
with the Lie algebra
B:=\Lie(G)
such that
(B^{\sigma})^{\times}\subseteq G
. The connected component of the identity element in
G
is denoted by
G_{0}
. Then
B=\Lie(G_{0})=\Lie(G)\leq A
. If
B
is weakly Hermitian, we always take
G
to be the minimal extension from Section
2.6
.

We consider the following matrices:

(3.4)

D(x):=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&\sigma(x)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\;L(y):=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
y&1\end{pmatrix},\;R(z):=\begin{pmatrix}1&z\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

where
x\in G
,
y,z\in B^{\sigma}
. These matrices, acting on
A^{2}
, preserve the standard symplectic form

\omega
. Therefore, the set
\{L(y)D(x)R(y)\mid y,z\in B^{\sigma},\;x\in G\}
is contained in
\Sp_{2}(A,\sigma)
.

Definition 3.5
.

We denote by
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
the topological closure of the space

\{L(y)D(x)R(z)\mid y,z\in B^{\sigma},\;x\in G\}

in
\Sp_{2}(A,\sigma)
. Matrices of the form
L(y)D(x)R(z)
for
y,z\in B^{\sigma}
,
x\in G
are called
generic
.

Proposition 3.6
.

An element
M:=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
is generic if and only if
a\in A^{\times}
. In this case,
a\in G
,
ca^{-1},a^{-1}b\in B^{\sigma}
. Moreover, there is a neighborhood of the identity element in
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
which consists of generic elements.

Proof.

First, notice that if
a\in A^{\times}
, then
M=L(ca^{-1})D(a)R(a^{-1}b)
with
ca^{-1},a^{-1}b\in A^{\sigma}
. Since
M\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
, there exists a sequence
M_{i}=L(y_{i})D(x_{i})R(z_{i})
such that
x_{i}\in G
,
y_{i},z_{i}\in B^{\sigma}
, and
\lim(M_{i})=M
. Since the decompositions of
M_{i}
and
M
into
L
-,
D
-, and
R
-matrices is unique,
\lim(x_{i})=a
, i.e.,
a\in\bar{G}
and
a\in A^{\times}
, i.e.,
a\in G
. Similarly,
\lim(y_{i})=ca^{-1}\in B^{\sigma}
and
\lim(z_{i})=a^{-1}b\in B^{\sigma}
. Moreover, this implies that there is a neighborhood of
M
containing only generic matrices. In particular, it holds for
M=\Id
.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
B
be of Jordan type such that
1\in B
. Then the space
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
is a Lie group.

Proof.

First, we prove the following two Lemmata:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
1+y
is not invertible for some
y\in A
. Then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
0\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
t\in U\setminus\{0\}
,
1+y(1+t)
is invertible.

Proof.

By Proposition
[
1
, Proposition 2.24]
,
A
can be embedded as a subalgebra into
\Mat(r,\mathbb{R})
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We identify
B
with a Lie subalgebra of
\Mat(r,\mathbb{R})
. Since
1+y
is not invertible,
-1
is an eigenvalue of
y
. Since
y
has only finitely many eigenvalues, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
0\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
t\in U\setminus\{0\}
,
1+y(1+t)
is invertible.
∎

We remind that the topological closure
\bar{G}
of
G
in
A
is a monoid.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
y,z\in B^{\sigma}
then
1+zy\in\bar{G}
. In particular, if
1+zy
is invertible, then
1+zy\in G
.

Proof.

First, assume
z\in(B^{\sigma})^{\times}\subset G
. Then
1+zy=(z+zyz)z^{-1}\in\bar{G}
because
z^{-1}\in G
,
z,zyz\in B^{\sigma}\subseteq\bar{G}
.

If
z
is not invertible, take a sequence of invertible
(z_{i})
such that
\lim z_{i}=z
. Then all
1+z_{i}y\in\bar{G}
and
\lim(1+z_{i}y)=1+zy
. Since
\bar{G}
is closed,
1+zy\in\bar{G}
.
∎

Let now
a:=\lim L(y_{i})D(x_{i})R(z_{i})\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
,
b:=\lim L(y^{\prime}_{i})D(x^{\prime}_{i})R(z^{\prime}_{i})\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
for some sequences
\{x_{i}\},\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}\subset G
and
\{y_{i}\},\{y^{\prime}_{i}\},\{z_{i}\},\{z^{\prime}_{i}\}\subset B^{\sigma}
. We want to show that there exist sequences
\{y^{\prime\prime}_{i}\},\{z^{\prime\prime}_{i}\}\subset B^{\sigma}
,
\{x^{\prime\prime}_{i}\}\subset G
such that
ab=\lim L(y^{\prime\prime}_{i})D(x^{\prime\prime}_{i})R(z^{\prime\prime}_{i})
.

Since limits for
a
and
b
exist, we can write:

ab=\lim L(y_{i})D(x_{i})R(z_{i})L(y^{\prime}_{i})D(x^{\prime}_{i})R(z^{\prime}_{i}).

Consider the term

R(z_{i})L(y^{\prime}_{i})=\begin{pmatrix}1&z_{i}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
y^{\prime}_{i}&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}&z_{i}\\
y^{\prime}_{i}&1\end{pmatrix}.

If
1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}
is invertible then by the Lemma
3.9
,
1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}\in G
, then we can write

R(z_{i})L(y^{\prime}_{i})=L(z_{i}(1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i})^{-1})D(1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i})R((1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i})^{-1}y^{\prime}_{i}).

If
1+z_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}
is not invertible in
B
then we take a sequence
\{t_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\lim t_{i}=0

0&1\end{pmatrix}
where
b\in B^{\sigma}
. Thus,
v^{\prime\prime}=vb+v^{\prime}
where
b\in B^{\sigma}
. Now,
\omega(w,v^{\prime})\in B
if and only if
\omega(w,v^{\prime\prime})=\omega(w,v^{\prime})+\omega(w,v)b\in B
because
\omega(w,v)b\in B
if
\omega(w,v),b\in B^{\sigma}
by the property to be of Jordan type for
B
.
∎

Proposition 4.26
.

Let
l\in\PIs_{G}(\omega)
. Then

(4.5)

\displaystyle T_{l}\PIs_{G}(\omega)=\left\{Q\in\Hom_{A}(l,A^{2}/l)\mid\omega(w,v)\in B^{\sigma}\text{ for $v\in\Is_{G}(\omega)$ such that $l=xA$}\right\}.

Proof.

We consider the differential of the quotient map
q\colon\Is_{G}(\omega)\to\PIs_{G}(\omega)
. Let
v\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
then the kernel
\Ker(d_{v}q)=vA\cap T_{v}\Is_{G}(\omega)
. Thus for
w\in T_{v}\Is_{G}(\omega)
, we can identify the element
d_{v}q(w)
with the
A
-linear map
Q\colon l\to A^{2}/l
which sends
w
to
w+vA
. Since
\omega(w,v)=\omega(w+vA,v)\in B^{\sigma}
, we obtain the description of the tangent space (
4.5
).
∎

Corollary 4.27
.

For every
v\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
, the tangent space
T_{v}\Is_{G}(\omega)
is isomorphic to
B\oplus B^{\sigma}
, and
T_{vA}\PIs_{G}(\omega)
is isomorphic to
B^{\sigma}
.

Remark 4.28
.

Notice that the space
\PIs_{G}(\omega)
is a compact Riemannian symmetric space. The linear transformation
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
induces an inversion symmetry at the line
(1,0)^{t}A\in\PIs_{G}(\omega)
. In contrast,
\Is_{G}(\omega)
is not a symmetric space. Indeed, for every
v\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
,
v\in T_{v}\Is_{G}(\omega)
. Therefore, every
Q\in\End_{A}(A^{2})
fixing
v
cannot act as
-\Id
on
T_{v}\Is_{G}(\omega)
.

5.
Models for the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive finite dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-algebra. The goal of this section is to construct different models of the symmetric space for
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
for
\Lie G=B
where
B
is a Hermitian Lie subalgebra
A
.

5.1.
Space of complex structures

The first model we construct is the space of complex structures.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
V
be a right
A
-module, and
J\in\End_{A}(V)
. The map
J
is called a
complex structure
on
V
if
J^{2}=-\Id
.

Let
V=A^{2}
and
\omega
be the standard symplectic form in
A^{2}
. For every complex structure
J
on
A^{2}
, we can define the following
\sigma
-sesquilinear form

\begin{matrix}h_{J}\colon&A^{2}\times A^{2}&\to&A\\
&(x,y)&\mapsto&\omega(J(x),y).\end{matrix}

Definition 5.2
.

A
\sigma
-sesquilinear form
h
on
(A^{2},\omega)
is called
(G,\sigma)
-symmetric
if
h
is
\sigma
-symmetric and for all
v\in\bar{G}^{2}
,
h(v,v)\in B^{\sigma}
; it is called a
(G,\sigma)
-inner product
if additionally for all
v\in\bar{G}^{2}
,
h(v,v)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. For a
(G,\sigma)
-inner product
h
, a basis
(v,w)
of
A^{2}
is called
h
-orthonormal
if
h(v,v)=h(w,w)=1
and
h(v,w)=0
.

We consider the following space:

\mathfrak{C}:=\left\{J\text{ complex structure on $A^{2}$}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}J(\Is_{G}(\omega))=\Is_{G}(\omega),\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-inner product}\end{array}\right\}.

Definition 5.3
.

The
standard complex structure
on
A^{2}
is the map

\begin{matrix}J_{0}\colon&A^{2}&\to&A^{2}\\
&(x,y)&\mapsto&(y,-x).\end{matrix}

Remark 5.4
.

The form
h_{J_{0}}
is the standard
(G,\sigma)
-inner product on
A^{2}
, i.e., for
x,y\in A^{2}
,
h_{J_{0}}(x,y)=\sigma(x)^{t}y
. By Proposition
4.3
,
J_{0}\in\mathfrak{C}
. Moreover,
\Aut(h_{J_{0}})=\OO_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
J
be a complex structure on
A^{2}
.
J\in\mathfrak{C}
if and only if there exists
w\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
such that
(J(w),w)
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Let
J\in\mathfrak{C}
and
w^{\prime}\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
. We denote
b:=h_{J}(w^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
and
w:=w^{\prime}b^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, then
h_{J}(w,w)=1
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\omega(J(w),J(w))

\displaystyle=h_{J}(w,J(w))=\sigma(h_{J}(J(w),w))=\sigma(\omega(w,w))=0,

\displaystyle\omega(J(w),w)

\displaystyle=h_{J}(w,w)=1.

Therefore,
(J(w),w)
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis.

(
\Leftarrow
) Let
w\in A^{2}
such that
(J(w),w)
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis. Then,

\displaystyle h_{J}(w,w)

\displaystyle=\omega(J(w),w)=1,

\displaystyle h_{J}(J(w),J(w))

\displaystyle=\omega(J^{2}(w),J(w))=-\omega(w,J(w))=1,

\displaystyle h_{J}(J(w),w)

\displaystyle=\omega(J^{2}(w),w)=-\omega(w,w)=0.

Therefore,
(J(w),w)
is an orthonormal basis for
h_{J}
, so
h_{J}
is an
(G,\sigma)
-inner product.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

The group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{C}
by conjugation. The stabilizer of the standard complex structure is
\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{C}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Proof.

1. First, we prove that
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts on
\mathfrak{C}
by conjugation. Let
J\in\mathfrak{C}
and
g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. Consider
J^{\prime}:=g^{-1}Jg
. Then,
(J^{\prime})^{2}=g^{-1}J^{2}g=-\Id
so
J^{\prime}
is a complex structure on
A^{2}
. For
x\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
,
g(x)\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
and we obtain

\displaystyle h_{J^{\prime}}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\omega(J^{\prime}(x),x)=\omega(g^{-1}Jg(x),x)

\displaystyle=\omega(Jg(x),g(x))=h_{J}(g(x),g(x))\in B^{\sigma}_{+}.

Moreover, the complex structure
J^{\prime}
preserves
\Is_{G}(\omega)
. Therefore,
h_{J^{\prime}}
is a
(G,\sigma)
-inner product on
A^{2}
, i.e.,
J^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}
.

2. Second, we prove that this action is transitive. Let
J\in\mathfrak{C}
, and
(J(w),w)
be a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis from Proposition
5.5
. By Proposition
4.11
, the group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts transitively on
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic bases, i.e., there exists
g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
which maps the standard symplectic basis to
(J(w),w)
. That means,
g
maps the standard complex structure
J_{0}
to
J
. So the action is transitive.

3. Finally, we compute the stabilizer of
J_{0}
. An element
g\in\Stab_{\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)}(J_{0})
if and only if

g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
and
g\in\Aut(h_{J_{0}})=\OO_{2}(A,\sigma)
, i.e.

g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\cap\OO_{2}(A,\sigma)=\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma).\qed

Corollary 5.7
.

The orbit map

\begin{matrix}\pi_{\mathfrak{C}}\colon&\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)/\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\to&\mathfrak{C}\\
&M\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\mapsto&MJ_{0}M^{-1}\end{matrix}

is an
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-equivariant homeomorphism.

The space
\mathfrak{C}
is a smooth manifold with the following tangent space at
J\in\mathfrak{C}
:

T_{J}\mathfrak{C}=\left\{L\in\End_{A}(A^{2})\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}JL+LJ=0,\\
L(\Is_{G}(\omega))\subseteq\overline{\Is_{G}(\omega)}\subseteq\bar{G}^{2},\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-symmetric}\end{array}\right\}.

The group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts by conjugation on the tangent bundle
T\mathfrak{C}
, i.e., for
g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
,
g.(J,L)=(gJg^{-1},gLg^{-1})
.
For the proof of this fact, we refer to
[
12
, Proposition 6.3]
.

5.2.
Projective space models

Let
B_{\mathbb{C}}:=B\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\subseteq A\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=A_{\mathbb{C}}
be the complexification of
B
and
G_{\mathbb{C}}
the Lie subgroup of
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
as in Section
2.6
.

Slightly abusing our notation, we denote by
\sigma\colon A_{\mathbb{C}}\to A_{\mathbb{C}}
the
\mathbb{C}
-linear extension of
\sigma\colon A\to A
, i.e., for every
x,y\in B

\sigma(x+iy)=\sigma(x)+i\sigma(y)

and by
\bar{\sigma}
the
\mathbb{C}
-antilinear extension of
\sigma
, i.e., for every
x,y\in B

\bar{\sigma}(x+iy)=\sigma(x)-i\sigma(y).

Notice that in general neither
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
nor
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\bar{\sigma})
is Hermitian. Moreover, the Lie algebra
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
is never Hermitian but it is always of Jordan type. However,
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\bar{\sigma})
is even not always of Jordan type (cf. Remark
7.3
).

We extend
\omega
in the complex linear way to
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
on
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Every complex structure
J
on
A^{2}
extends to an
A_{\mathbb{C}}
-linear operator on
A^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
in the complex linear way. We denote this extension by
J_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

For every
J\in\mathfrak{C}
, there exist regular isotropic elements
x,y\in A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
J_{\mathbb{C}}(x)=ix
,
J_{\mathbb{C}}(y)=-iy
. The lines
xA_{\mathbb{C}}
,
yA_{\mathbb{C}}
are uniquely determined by
J
.

Proof.

Since
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{C}
, it is enough to prove the proposition for the standard complex structure
J_{0}
.

Since
J_{0}(a,b)^{t}=(b,-a)^{t}
,
(b,-a)^{t}=i(a,b)^{t}
if and only if
b=ai
, i.e.,
x=(1,i)^{t}a
for
a\in A_{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
xA_{\mathbb{C}}
is uniquely defined. Analogously,
y=(i,1)^{t}a
for
a\in A_{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
yA_{\mathbb{C}}
is uniquely defined. Moreover,
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(x,x)=\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(y,y)=0
, so
x
are
y
are isotropic.
∎

For a complex structure
J\in\mathfrak{C}
, we denote by
l_{J}
the line
yA_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
J_{\mathbb{C}}(y)=-yi
.

Remark 5.9
.

For every
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis
(w,u)
of
(A^{2},\omega)
, elements
u+wi,w+iu
are isotropic.

Definition 5.10
.

We call the spaces

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}:=\mathfrak{P}^{+}:=

\displaystyle\{(u+wi)A_{\mathbb{C}}\mid(w,u)\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-symplectic basis of $A^{2}$}\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}^{-}:=

\displaystyle\{(w+ui)A_{\mathbb{C}}\mid(w,u)\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-symplectic basis of $A^{2}$}\}=\overline{\mathfrak{P}^{+}}

the
projective space models
of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

These spaces are smooth manifolds, and, similarly as in Proposition
4.26
, one can describe the tangent space at
l\in\mathfrak{P}^{\pm}
:

T_{l}\mathfrak{P}^{\pm}=\left\{Q\in\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(l,A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}/l)\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(Q(x),x)\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\text{ for $x=u+wi$ where $(w,u)$ is}\\
\text{a $(G,\sigma)$-symplectic basis of $A^{2}$}\end{array}\right\}.

The group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts on the spaces
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
by the left matrix multiplication. Moreover, complex conjugation provides an
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-equivariant homeomorphism between
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
.

The following proposition justifies the name of
\mathfrak{P}^{\pm}
:

Proposition 5.11
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{cccl}F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}\colon&\mathfrak{C}&\to&\mathfrak{P}\\
&J&\mapsto&l_{J}\end{array}

defines a homeomorphism that is equivariant under the action of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{P}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Proof.

1. First, we show that
l_{J}\in\mathfrak{P}
. It is again enough to prove this for the standard complex structure
J_{0}
. We take
v:=(i,1)^{t}=(0,1)^{t}+(1,0)^{t}i
, then
l_{J_{0}}=vA_{\mathbb{C}}
,
((1,0)^{t},(0,1)^{t})
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis.

2. We show that
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}
is surjective. Let
v=u+wi
for a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis
(w,u)
of
(A^{2},\omega)
. We define the following complex structure:
J(u)=w
,
J(w)=-u
. By Proposition
5.5
,
J\in\mathfrak{C}
.
Since

J_{\mathbb{C}}(v)=J_{\mathbb{C}}(u+iw)=w-iu=-i(u+iw)=-iv,

we obtain
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}(J)=vA_{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}
is surjective.

3. We show that
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}
is injective. Let
l_{J}=l_{J^{\prime}}=yA_{\mathbb{C}}
for
J,J^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
y=y_{1}+y_{2}i\in A^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
J(y_{1})=J^{\prime}(y_{1})=-y_{2}
,
J(y_{2})=J^{\prime}(y_{2})=y_{1}
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
is a basis of
A^{2}
, i.e.,
J=J^{\prime}
.

4. Finally, we show the equivariance of
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}
. Let
M\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
,
J\in\mathfrak{C}
and
(w,u)
be a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis of
(A^{2},\omega)
such that
w:=J(u)
,
J(w)=-u
. Then for
v=u+wi
,
J_{\mathbb{C}}(v)=-iv
.

Moreover,
MJM^{-1}(Mu)=Mw
,
MJM^{-1}(Mw)=-Mu
where
(Mw,Mu)
is also a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis of
(A^{2},\omega)
. Then
(MJM^{-1})_{\mathbb{C}}(M_{\mathbb{C}}v)=-iM_{\mathbb{C}}v
and

F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}(MJM^{-1})=(M_{\mathbb{C}}v)A_{\mathbb{C}}=M_{\mathbb{C}}(vA_{\mathbb{C}})=M_{\mathbb{C}}F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}(J),

i.e.,
F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}}
is equivariant with respect to the
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-action.
∎

Similarly, we obtain the following Proposition:

Proposition 5.12
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{cccl}F_{\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{P}^{-}}\colon&\mathfrak{C}&\to&\mathfrak{P}^{-}\\
&J&\mapsto&\overline{l_{J}}\end{array}

defines a homeomorphism that is equivariant under the action of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Since
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
are related by complex conjugation, in what follows we will only discuss
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
keeping in mind that the corresponding statements apply equally to
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
.

Corollary 5.13
.

The orbit map

\begin{matrix}\pi_{\mathfrak{P}}\colon&\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)/\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\to&\mathfrak{P}\\
&M\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\mapsto&M(i,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}\end{matrix}

is an
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-equivariant homeomorphism.

Consider the following
\bar{\sigma}
-sesquilinear form on
A^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
:

(5.6)

h(x,y):=i\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(\bar{x},y).

It is
\bar{\sigma}
-symmetric:

h(y,x)=i\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(\bar{y},x)=\bar{\sigma}((-i)(-\omega_{\mathbb{C}}(\bar{x},y)))=\bar{\sigma}(h(x,y)),

and in the basis
e_{1}:=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{t}
,
e_{2}:=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{t}
, the form
h
is represented by the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
.

The following lemma is immediate:

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
G_{\mathbb{C}}
be a Lie subgroup of
A^{\times}_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\Lie(G_{\mathbb{C}})=B_{\mathbb{C}}
and
(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\times}\subseteq G_{\mathbb{C}}
. In this case, the group
\Sp_{2}(G_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
is well defined. For
v:=u+wi\in A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
(w,u)
is a basis of
A^{2}
, the basis
(w,u)
is
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic if and only if
h(v,v)=2
and

.

Remark 5.15
.

The group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts on
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
preserving both forms
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
and
h
. So we can see
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
as a subgroup of

.

5.3.
Precompact model

In this section, we identify the symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
as an open subset of
B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
with a compact closure.

We define the following domain
\mathfrak{B}
:

\mathfrak{B}\!:=\{c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid 1-\bar{c}c\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}\}.

This is an open domain in
B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
, therefore, it is naturally a complex manifold, and the tangent space
T_{c}\mathfrak{B}=B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
at every
c\in\mathfrak{B}
. Moreover, its closure agrees with the compact domain
D(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma},\bar{\sigma})
from Proposition
2.50
, i.e., it is precompact.

We consider the following
\Sp_{2}(G_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
-transformation:

T:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
i&1\end{pmatrix}\in\Sp_{2}(G_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma).

It maps
h
(cf. (
5.6
)) to the indefinite form represented by the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
: i.e.,
\bar{\sigma}(T)^{t}[h]T=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Since
T\in\Sp_{2}(G_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
, it stabilizes the set
\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
.

Proposition 5.16
.

We consider the map
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}:=\Phi\circ T^{-1}\colon\mathfrak{P}\to\mathfrak{B}
where

\begin{array}[]{cccl}\Phi\colon&T^{-1}\mathfrak{P}&\to&\mathfrak{B}\\
&\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}A_{\mathbb{C}}&\mapsto&v_{1}v_{2}^{-1}.\end{array}

Both maps
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}
and
\Phi
are homeomorphisms.

Proof.

Let
vA_{\mathbb{C}}\in\mathfrak{P}
for
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{t}\in\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
and
v=u+wi
where
(w,u)
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis of
(A^{2},\omega)
. Then by Lemma
5.14
:

2=h(v,v)=-\bar{\sigma}(x_{1})x_{1}+\bar{\sigma}(x_{2})x_{2}\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}

where
T^{-1}v=:(x_{1},x_{2})^{t}
. Therefore,
\bar{\sigma}(x_{2})x_{2}=\bar{\sigma}(x_{1})x_{1}+2\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}
because
(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}
is a convex cone. This means that
x_{2}
is invertible, i.e.,
x_{2}\in G
. We define

(c,1)^{t}:=(x_{1}x_{2}^{-1},1)^{t}\in\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}}).

Then
(c,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}=(T^{-1}v)A_{\mathbb{C}}
. By Corollary
4.12
,
(c,1)^{t}\in\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
if and only if
c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover,

h((c,1)^{t},(c,1)^{t})=1-\bar{c}c\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}.

Therefore,
\Phi(T^{-1}v)\in\mathfrak{B}
.

The map
\Phi
is injective because
T
is injective and, if

x_{1}x_{2}^{-1}=\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})^{t}=\Phi(y_{1},y_{2})^{t}=y_{1}y_{2}^{-1},

then
(y_{1},y_{2})^{t}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{t}x_{2}^{-1}y_{2}
, i.e.,
(x_{1},x_{2})^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}=(y_{1},y_{2})^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Finally, the map
\Phi
is surjective because for every
c\in\mathfrak{B}
,
(c,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}=(T^{-1}v)A_{\mathbb{C}}
where
v:=T(c,1)^{t}\sqrt{2}(1-\bar{c}c)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Then
v\in\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
and
h(v,v)=2
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.14
,
v=u+wi
where
(w,u)
is a
(G,\sigma)
-symplectic basis of
(A^{2},\omega)
. Therefore,
vA_{\mathbb{C}}\in T^{-1}\mathfrak{P}
.
∎

Remark 5.17
.

The group
T^{-1}\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)T<\Sp_{2}(G_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{B}
by Möbius transformations. Thus
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}
is equivariant with respect to this action of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
on
\mathfrak{B}
and the action on
\mathfrak{P}
.

5.4.
Compactification and Shilov boundary

Let
(B,\sigma)
be a Hermitian Lie subalgebra of
A
. Then

\mathfrak{B}:=\{c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid 1-\bar{c}c\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{+}\}

is precompact. We take the topological closure of
\mathfrak{B}
in
B^{\sigma}
:

\overline{\mathfrak{B}}:=\{c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid 1-\bar{c}c\in(B^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\mathbb{C}})_{\geq 0}\}.

The boundary of
\overline{\mathfrak{B}}
contains the following compact subspace:

\check{S}(\mathfrak{B}):=\{c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}\mid 1-\bar{c}c=0\}=\OO(G_{\mathbb{C}},\bar{\sigma})\cap B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}.

Definition 5.18
.

We call
\check{S}(\mathfrak{B})
the
Shilov boundary
of the precompact model
\mathfrak{B}
.

Remark 5.19
.

The map
\Phi^{-1}
extends to the boundary of
\overline{\mathfrak{B}}
and remains continuous and bijective. Since the boundary is compact, it is a homeomorphism. Therefore, we can see the boundary also in the projective space model. In particular, we can see the Shilov boundary there.

The following proposition describes the Shilov boundary in the projective space model.

Proposition 5.20
.

The preimage of the Shilov boundary
\check{S}(\mathfrak{B})
in
\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega)
under the map
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}
gives a compact subset of the boundary of the projective space model. It consists of all lines of the form
xA_{\mathbb{C}}
such that
x\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
.

Proof.

Note that the line
l\in\Is_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega)
is of the form
xA_{\mathbb{C}}
for some
x\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
if and only if
\bar{l}=l
.

Let
c\in\check{S}(\mathfrak{B})
, then
\bar{c}^{-1}=c
and

F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}\left(\overline{F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}(c)}\right)=\Phi\left(\!\!\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{c}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right)=\Phi\left(\!\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\bar{c}i\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right)=\bar{c}^{-1}=c,

i.e.,
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}(c)=xA_{\mathbb{C}}
for
x=(c,1)^{t}\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
.

Let
xA_{\mathbb{C}}
be a line spanned by
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{t}\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
, then

c:=F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}(xA_{\mathbb{C}})=(x_{1}-ix_{2})(-ix_{1}+x_{2})^{-1}.

By Corollary
4.12
,
x\in\Is_{G}(\omega)
if and only if
c\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{c}c

\displaystyle=(x_{1}+ix_{2})(ix_{1}+x_{2})^{-1}(x_{1}-ix_{2})(-ix_{1}+x_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=i(x_{1}+ix_{2})(x_{1}-ix_{2})^{-1}(x_{1}-ix_{2})(-ix_{1}+x_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=i(x_{1}+ix_{2})(-ix_{1}+x_{2})^{-1}=(x_{1}+ix_{2})(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{-1}=1.

Therefore,
F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{B}}(xA_{\mathbb{C}})\in\check{S}(\mathfrak{B})
.
∎

Corollary 5.21
.

The space
\PIs_{G}(\omega)
embedded into
\PIs_{G_{\mathbb{C}}}(\omega_{\mathbb{C}})
as:

xA\mapsto xA_{\mathbb{C}}

is the Shilov boundary in the projective space model. This is a compact orbit of the action of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
on the boundary of the projective space model.

5.5.
Half-space models

The last models, we discuss in this chapter are the upper half- and lower half-space models. They generalize the upper half-plane model for the hyperbolic plane and the Siegel space model for the symmetric space for the group
\Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 5.22
.

The
upper (lower) half-spaces
are defined by

\mathfrak{U}:=\mathfrak{U}^{+}:=\{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}\mid\Imm(z)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}\}

and

\mathfrak{U}^{-}:=\{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}\mid\Imm(z)\in B^{\sigma}_{-}\}

respectively.

The spaces
\mathfrak{U}^{\pm}
are open domains in
B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}
. Therefore, they are naturally complex manifolds, and the tangent spaces
T_{z}\mathfrak{U}^{\pm}=B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}
, for every
z\in\mathfrak{U}^{\pm}
.

Note that
\mathfrak{U}^{-}
and
\mathfrak{U}^{+}
are related by complex conjugation. Thus, in what follows we will only discuss
\mathfrak{U}=\mathfrak{U}^{+}
. The corresponding statements for
\mathfrak{U}^{-}
can be easily reformulated.

Proposition 5.23
.

The group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts on
\mathfrak{U}
via Möbius transformations:

z\mapsto M.z=(az+b)(cz+d)^{-1}\text{, where }M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Since
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
is generated by the matrices

\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&\sigma(a)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

where
a\in G
,
b\in B^{\sigma}
, we prove that
M.z\in\mathfrak{U}
on these generators.

If
M:=\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
with
b\in B^{\sigma}
, then
M.z=z+b\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\Imm(M.z)=\Imm(z)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
.

If
M:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
,
then
M.z=-z^{-1}\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
. If
z=x+yi
, then

z^{-1}=y^{-1}x(y+xy^{-1}x)^{-1}-(y+xy^{-1}x)^{-1}i,

i.e.,
\Imm(M.z)=(y+xy^{-1}x)^{-1}
. For
y\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
, also
y^{-1}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
.

Let
y^{-1}=\sigma(p)p
for some
p\in B^{\times}
, then

y+xy^{-1}x=y+\sigma(px)px\in B^{\sigma}_{+}.

Therefore,
\Imm(M.z)=(y+xy^{-1}x)^{-1}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}.

If
M:=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&\sigma(a)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

for
a\in G
, then
M.z=az\sigma(a)\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
because
B^{\sigma}
is preserved by action of
G
.
\Imm(M.z)=a\Imm(z)\sigma(a)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
because
B^{\sigma}_{+}
is closed by action of
G
.
∎

Proposition 5.24
.

The orbit map

\begin{matrix}\tilde{\pi}_{\mathfrak{U}}\colon&\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\to&\mathfrak{U}\\
&M&\to&M.1i\end{matrix}

is continuous, proper and surjective, i.e.,
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{U}
. The stabilizer of
1i
is
\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Proof.

Let
z=x+yi\in\mathfrak{U}
then
y=u^{2}
for some
u\in(B^{\sigma})^{\times}
. Then

\pi\left(\!\!\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u&0\\
0&u^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right)=\pi\left(\!\!\begin{pmatrix}u&xu^{-1}\\
0&u^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right)=x+yi=z.

A matrix
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

stabilizes
1i
if and only if

1i=M.1i=(ai+b)(ci+d)^{-1}=(ai+b)(-c+di)^{-1}i.

Thus,
a=d
and
c=-b
, i.e.,
M\in\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.
∎

Corollary 5.25
.

The map
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathfrak{U}}
induces a homeomorphism

\begin{matrix}\pi_{\mathfrak{U}}\colon&\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)/\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\to&\mathfrak{U}\\
&M\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)&\mapsto&M.1i\ .\end{matrix}

This homeomorphism is equivariant under the action of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
by left multiplication on
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)/\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
and by Möbius transformations on
\mathfrak{U}
. In particular, the space
\mathfrak{U}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
.

Corollary 5.26
.

The map

\begin{array}[]{rccc}F_{\mathfrak{P},\mathfrak{U}}\colon&\mathfrak{P}&\to&\mathfrak{U}\\
&\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\end{pmatrix}A_{\mathbb{C}}&\mapsto&a_{1}a_{2}^{-1}\end{array}

provides an
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-equivariant homeomorphism between the upper half-space and the projective space model.

The domain
\mathfrak{U}
admits a Riemannian metric as described in
[
7
, Excercise 8, Chapter XIII]
which is
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
-invariant and together with the natural complex structure on
\mathfrak{U}
, it defines a structure of Hermitian symmetric space on
\mathfrak{U}
.

Before we define the metric, we remind the definition of the
quadratic representation
(see
[
7
, Section II.3]
): let
(J,\circ)
be a Jordan algebra, for
x,y,z\in J
, we denote

\{x,y,z\}:=z\circ(x\circ y)+x\circ(z\circ y)-(x\circ z)\circ y.

Then the Riemannian metric on
\mathfrak{U}
can be expressed as follows: for
z=x+yi\in\mathfrak{U}
,
v,w\in T_{z}\mathfrak{U}=B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
v=v_{x}+v_{y}i
,
w=w_{x}+w_{y}i
:

\displaystyle g_{z}(v,w):=

\displaystyle\tr\left(\Ree\left(\{y^{-\frac{1}{2}},\{\bar{v},y^{-1},w\},y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tr\left(\{y^{-\frac{1}{2}},\{v_{x},y^{-1},w_{x}\},y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}+\{y^{-\frac{1}{2}},\{v_{y},y^{-1},w_{y}\},y^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}\right).

Using the isomorphisms between models, this metric can by transferred to all four models of the symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
that we discussed before.

Notice that if
z=i
, then the metric becomes particularly easy:

g_{i}(v,w)=\tr(v_{x}\circ w_{x}+v_{y}\circ w_{y})=\beta(v_{x},w_{x})+\beta(v_{y},w_{y})

where
\beta
is the inner product on
B^{\sigma}
defined in (
2.27
).

6.
Models for the Riemannian symmetric space of
G

Let
(A,\sigma)
be an involutive finite dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-algebra. The goal of this section is to construct different models of the symmetric space for
G
for
\Lie G=B
where
B
is a Hermitian Lie subalgebra
A
, seen as sub-manifolds of the corresponding models of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. For this, we use the identification
G\cong\hat{G}=\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\cap\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
discussed in Section
3.6.2
. Moreover, we will not discuss explicitly the maps between models and the Riemannian metric because they are simply the restrictions of the corresponding ones from Section
5
.

Let
V=A^{2}
and
\omega
be the standard symplectic form in
A^{2}
. We define the following
\sigma
-sesquilinear,
(G,\sigma)
-symmetric form
h
: for
x,y\in A^{2}
,
h(x,y):=\sigma(x)^{t}Hy
, where
H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Then
\OO(h)=\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
.

6.1.
Space of complex structures

The first model we construct is the space of almost orthogonal complex structures.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
J\in\End_{A}(V)
.
J
is called
almost orthogonal
if
h(Jx,Jy)=-h(x,y)
.

An almost orthogonal endomorphism is not an element of
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
. However, a product of two of them is an element of
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
.

Let now
J
be a complex strucure on
A^{2}
(cf. Definition
5.1
). As before, we can define the following
\sigma
-sesquilinear form

\begin{matrix}h_{J}\colon&A^{2}\times A^{2}&\to&A\\
&(x,y)&\mapsto&\omega(J(x),y).\end{matrix}

We consider the following space:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}:=

\displaystyle\;\left\{J\text{ complex structure on $A^{2}$}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}J(\Is_{G}(\omega))=\Is_{G}(\omega),\\
h(J(\cdot),J(\cdot))=-h(\cdot,\cdot),\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-inner product}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left\{J\text{ complex structure on $A^{2}$}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}J(\Is_{G}(\omega))=\Is_{G}(\omega),\\
J(\Is(h))=\Is(h),\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-inner product}\end{array}\right\}.

Notice that the standard complex structure
J_{0}
on
A^{2}
is almost orthogonal, i.e.,
J_{0}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The group
\hat{G}
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
by conjugation. The stabilizer of the standard complex structure is
\OO(G,\sigma)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\hat{G}
.

Proof.

Let
J\in\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
. Since
J
is an almost orthogonal complex structure, then
g_{J}:=JJ_{0}
is an element of
\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)
and of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. Therefore,

g_{J}=\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&\sigma(g)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\OO_{(1,1)}(A,\sigma)\cap\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)=\hat{G},

where
g\in G
, i.e.,
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-g\\
\sigma(g)^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}
.
The condition that
h_{J}
is a
(G,\sigma)
-inner product implies that
-g\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. Let
b\in B^{\sigma}_{+}\subseteq G
such that
b^{2}=-g
. Then

\begin{pmatrix}b&0\\
0&b^{-1}\end{pmatrix}J_{0}\begin{pmatrix}b&0\\
0&b^{-1}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=J.

Thus the action of
\hat{G}
on
\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
is transitive. The stabilizer of the standard complex structure agrees with the subgroup
\OO(\hat{G},\sigma):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&g\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\,g\in\OO(G,\sigma)\right\}
which is a maximal compact subgroup of
\hat{G}
.
∎

The space
\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
is a smooth manifold with the following tangent space at
J\in\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
:

T_{J}\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}=\left\{L\in\End_{A}(A^{2})\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}JL+LJ=0,\\
L(\Is_{G}(\omega))\subseteq\overline{\Is_{G}(\omega)}\subseteq\bar{G}^{2},\\
h(L(\cdot),\cdot)+h(\cdot,L(\cdot)=0,\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-symmetric}\end{array}\right\}.

The group
\hat{G}
acts by conjugation on the tangent bundle
T\mathfrak{C}
, i.e., for
g\in\hat{G}
,
g.(J,L)=(gJg^{-1},gLg^{-1})
.
For the proof of this fact, we refer to
[
12
, Proposition 4.3]
.

6.2.
Projective space models

We define the following spaces:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}:=\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{+}:=

\displaystyle\{uA\mid h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}\}\cap\PIs_{G}(\omega)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{-}:=

\displaystyle\{uA\mid h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{-}\}\cap\PIs_{G}(\omega)=-\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{+}.

Notice that in fact to define
\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}
, it is enough to take the intersection of
\{uA\mid h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{\pm}\}
with
\PIs(\omega)
because the conditions
h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
and
\omega(u,u)=0
imply that
uA\in\PIs_{G}(\omega)
. The spaces
\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}
are smooth manifolds, and one can describe the tangent space at
l\in\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}
:

T_{l}\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}=\left\{Q\in\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(l,A/l)\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}h(Q(x),x)\in B^{\sigma}\text{ for $x\in\Is_{G}(\omega)$}\\
\text{such that $l=xA$ and $h(x,x)\in B^{\sigma}_{\pm}$}\end{array}\right\}.

The group
\hat{G}
acts on the spaces
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
by left matrix multiplication. Moreover, multiplication by
-1
provides a
\hat{G}
-equivariant homeomorphism between
\mathfrak{P}^{+}
and
\mathfrak{P}^{-}
.

The following proposition justifies the name of
\mathfrak{P}^{\pm}
:

Proposition 6.3
.

The group
\hat{G}
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}
. The stabilizer of the line
(1,1)^{t}A
agrees with
\OO(G,\sigma)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\hat{G}
.

Proof.

First notice that the line
\ell:=(1,1)^{t}A\in\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}
because
h((1,1)^{t},(1,1)^{t})=2\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
and
(1,1)^{t}\in\Is(\omega)
.

Let
u=(u_{1},u_{2})^{t}\in\Is(\omega)
such that
h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
which spans a line
\ell_{u}\in\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}
. This implies
\sigma(u_{1})u_{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. In particular, this means that
u_{1},u_{2}
are invertible, and we can assume
u_{2}=1
and
u_{1}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}
. Let
g=u_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, then the matrix
\hat{g}:=\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&\sigma(g)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\hat{G}
maps
(1,1)^{t}
to

(u_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}},u_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{t}=(u_{1},1)u_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

That means
\hat{g}
maps
\ell
to
\ell_{u}
, i.e.,
\hat{G}
act transitively on
\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}
.

Let
\hat{g}=\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&\sigma(g)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
for
g\in G
stabilizes
\ell
. This is equivalent to
g=\sigma(g)^{-1}
, i.e.,
g\in\OO(G,\sigma)
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The closure of
\mathfrak{P}^{\pm}_{\hat{G}}
inside
\PIs(\omega)
is compact:

\overline{\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}}:=\{uA\mid\pm h(u,u)\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\}\cap\PIs(\omega).

The group
\hat{G}
acts on
\overline{\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{+}}
. There is a unique compact orbit of this action that is shared by
\overline{\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{+}}
and
\overline{\mathfrak{P}_{\hat{G}}^{-}}
, namely:
\PIs_{G}(\omega)\cap\PIs(h)
.

6.3.
Precompact model

In this section, we identify the symmetric space of
\hat{G}
as an open subset of
B^{\sigma}
with a compact closure.

We define the following domain
\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}
:

\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}\!:=\{c\in B^{\sigma}\mid 1-c^{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{+}\}.

This is an open domain in
B^{\sigma}
, therefore, it is naturally a complex manifold, and the tangent space
T_{c}\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}=B^{\sigma}
at every
c\in\mathfrak{B}
. Moreover, its closure

\overline{\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}}\!:=\{c\in B^{\sigma}\mid 1-c^{2}\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}\}

is contained in the compact domain
D(\bar{G},\sigma)
from Proposition
2.50
, i.e., it is precompact.

We define the following transformation
T:=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. The group
T\hat{G}T^{-1}
acts on
\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}
transitively by Möbius transformations. The action is transitive and the stabilizer of the point
0\in\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}
agrees with the group
T\OO(G,\sigma)T^{-1}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}
is a model of the Riemannian symmetric space of
\hat{G}
.

Furthermore, this action extends to the closure
\overline{\mathfrak{B}_{\hat{G}}}
. There is a unique closed orbit of this action, which is:

\{c\in B^{\sigma}\mid 1-c^{2}=0\}.

6.4.
Half-space models

The last models we discuss in this chapter are the upper half- and lower half-space models. Let
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}:=B^{\sigma}_{+}
respectively
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{-}:=B^{\sigma}_{-}.

The spaces
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}
are open unbounded domains in
B^{\sigma}
. Therefore, the tangent spaces
T_{z}\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}=B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}
for every
z\in\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{\pm}
.

Note that
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{-}
and
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{+}
are related by multiplication by
-1
. Thus, in what follows we will only discuss
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}=\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}^{+}
. The corresponding statements for
\mathfrak{U}^{-}
can be easily reformulated.

The group
\hat{G}
acts on
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}
by Möbius transformations. This is equivalent to saying that
G
acts by
\sigma
-congruence on
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}
, i.e., for
g\in G
and
u\in\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}
,
u\mapsto gu\sigma(g)
. This action is transitive and the stabilizer of the point
1\in\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}
is
\OO(G,\sigma)
. This means, in particular, that
\mathfrak{U}_{\hat{G}}
is a model of the symmetric space of
G
.

Remark 6.5
.

The space of complex almost orthogonal structures
\mathfrak{C}_{\hat{G}}
naturally embeds into the space of complex structures
\mathfrak{C}
. However, in the last three models, to embed the models of the symmetric space of
\hat{G}
into the corresponding model of the symmetric space of
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
, one needs to multiply corresponding elements by
i
. Since these models of the symmetric space of
\hat{G}
can be described as real manifolds, we chose to present them directly, without passing to complexifications. Nevertheless, to obtain the corresponding embeddings, this additional complexification is necessary.

7.
Spin group as
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)

If
(A,\sigma)
is an involutive associative algebra, then
A
becomes a Lie algebra under the commutator bracket. Moreover, since
A
is closed under multiplication, it is automatically of Jordan type. This includes, in particular, matrix algebras over
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{H}
, and other (skew-)fields, which yield numerous classical examples: the groups
\Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\Sp_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathrm{U}(n,n)
,
\GL_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\SO^{*}(4n)
,
\OO(4n)
, and
\Sp(n,n)
can all be realized as
\Sp_{2}(A,\sigma)
for suitable choices of
(A,\sigma)
. We refer to
[
1
]
for details on this “full-algebra” case
B=A
.

The purpose of this section is to go beyond this situation and exhibit examples of proper Lie subalgebras
B\subsetneq A
. Clifford algebras over real vector spaces provide such examples. We focus on our principal case of interest – the spin group
\Spin_{0}(m,n)
– and describe its realization as
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
. When
m=2
, we further construct explicit models of the associated Hermitian symmetric space of
\Spin_{0}(2,n)
.

7.1.
Clifford algebra

Let
V
be a real vector space of dimension
m+n>0
with a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form
b
of signature
(m,n)
. We denote by
\Cl(b)
the Clifford algebra generated by
(V,b)
. We remind,
\Cl(b)
is a unital algebra generated by all elements of
V
subject to the relation
v^{2}=b(v,v)
for
v\in V
. From this relation follows that for
v,w\in V
,
vw+wv=2b(v,w)
.

The Clifford algebra
\Cl(b)
contains a subalgebra
\Cl_{even}(b)
that is generated by elements
\{vw\mid v,w\in V\}
. It is called
even Clifford algebra
.

We fix a linear involutive isometry
\sigma\colon V\to V
with respect to
b
. We denote the
+1
-eigenspace of
\sigma
by
V^{\sigma}
, and the
-1
-eigenspace of
\sigma
by
V^{-\sigma}
. Then
V^{\pm\sigma}=(V^{\mp\sigma})^{\perp_{b}}
, and
V=V^{\sigma}\oplus V^{-\sigma}
is a direct
b
-orthogonal decomposition of
V
. The map
\sigma
extends uniquely to an anti-involution on
\Cl(b)
.

An involutive isometry
\sigma
is called
compatible
with
b
if
b|_{V^{\sigma}}
is positive definite and
b|_{V^{-\sigma}}
is negative definite.

We fix an orthonormal basis
(f_{1},\dots,f_{m},e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
of
V
such that
b(e_{i},e_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,
b(f_{i},f_{j})=-\delta_{ij}
,
b(f_{i},e_{j})=0
and, furthermore, there are
0\leq k\leq m
,
0\leq l\leq n
such that

V^{\sigma}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{k},e_{1},\dots,e_{l}),

V^{-\sigma}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{k+1},\dots,f_{m},e_{l+1},\dots,e_{n}).

This provides an identification of
(V,b)
with
\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
, which is the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
equipped with the standard symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form of signature
(m,n)
. The Clifford algebra corresponding to
\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
is denoted by
\Cl(m,n)
.

We consider the following vector subspace of
\Cl_{even}(b)
:

B(m,n)=B(b)=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(vw\mid v,w\in V).

A direct computation shows that this is a Lie subalgebra by
\Cl(m,n)
with respect to the Lie bracket
[x,y]:=xy-yx
that is closed under
\sigma
. Further,

(7.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} B^{\sigma}(m,n)&=B^{\sigma}(b)=\mathbb{R}\oplus V^{-\sigma}V^{\sigma}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(1,xy\mid x\in V^{-\sigma},y\in V^{\sigma}).\\
B^{-\sigma}(m,n)&=B^{-\sigma}(b)=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(xx^{\prime},yy^{\prime}\mid x,x^{\prime}\in V^{\sigma},y,y^{\prime}\in V^{-\sigma},b(x,x^{\prime})=b(y,y^{\prime})=0)\\
&=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(xx^{\prime}\mid x,x^{\prime}\in V^{\sigma},b(x,x^{\prime})=0)\oplus\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(yy^{\prime}\mid y,y^{\prime}\in V^{-\sigma},b(y,y^{\prime})=0)\\
&=B^{-\sigma}(V^{\sigma})\oplus B^{-\sigma}(V^{-\sigma}).\end{aligned}

Notice that
B^{\sigma}(V^{\sigma})=B^{\sigma}(V^{-\sigma})=\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 7.1
.

The Lie algebra
(B(m,n),\sigma)
is of Jordan type if and only if
k+l\leq 1
or
k+l\leq m+n-1
.

Proof.

The condition is equivalent to:
\dim(V^{\sigma})\leq 1
or
\dim(V^{-\sigma})\leq 1
.

If
\dim(V^{\sigma})=0
or
\dim(V^{-\sigma})=0
, then
B^{\sigma}(V)=\mathbb{R}
is of Jordan type.

If
\dim(V^{\sigma})=1
and
V^{\sigma}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(x)
, then for
y,y^{\prime}\in V^{-\sigma}
,
xy,xy^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}(b)
, and we have
xyxy^{\prime}=-x^{2}yy^{\prime}=-b(x,x)e_{i}e_{j}\in B(b)
. Therefore,
B(b)
is of Jordan type.

The case
\dim(V^{-\sigma})=1
can be proven analogously.

Let
\dim(V^{\sigma})\geq 2
and
\dim(V^{-\sigma})\geq 2
. Let
x,x^{\prime}\in V^{\sigma}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in V^{-\sigma}
pairwisely orthogonal vectors. Then
xy,x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}(b)
. However,
xyx^{\prime}y^{\prime}\notin B(b)
.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

Notice that powers of elements of
B^{-\sigma}(b)
in general do not belong to
B
: let
e_{1},e_{2},e_{2},e_{4}\in V^{\sigma}
be pairwisely orthogonal, then the element
e=e_{1}e_{2}+e_{3}e_{4}\in B^{-\sigma}(b)
. However,

e^{2}=(e_{1}e_{2})^{2}+(e_{3}e_{4})^{2}+e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}e_{4}+e_{3}e_{4}e_{1}e_{2}=-2+2e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}e_{4}\notin B.

Remark 7.3
.

If a real Lie algebra
(B,\sigma)
is of Jordan type, than its complexification
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
is also of Jordan type. However,
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\bar{\sigma})
is in general not of Jordan type. Let us illustrate this phenomenon.

We consider the real Lie algebra
B=B(m,n)
with
\sigma
as above such that
\dim V^{-\sigma}=1
where
V^{-\sigma}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{m})
. We take for simplicity
m=1
and
n\geq 3
, but the argument below remains true also for any
m
.

We consider the complexification
V_{\mathbb{C}}
of
V
. Then

\displaystyle V^{-\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}(f_{1}),

\displaystyle B_{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle=B(1,n)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}(1,e_{l}e_{j},f_{1}e_{k}\mid j,k,l\in\{1,\dots n\}),

\displaystyle B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}(1,f_{1}e_{k}\mid k\in\{1,\dots n\}).

Therefore,
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
is a complex Lie algebra of Jordan type. However,

\displaystyle B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\bar{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(1,f_{1}e_{k},ie_{l}e_{j}\mid j,k,l\in\{1,\dots n\})

and, therefore,
(B_{\mathbb{C}},\bar{\sigma})
is not of Jordan type because
if_{1}e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\notin B_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proposition 7.4
.

The Lie algebra
(B(b),\sigma)=(B(m,n),\sigma)
with
m\leq n
is weakly Hermitian if and only if either
\dim V^{-\sigma}=0
or
m=1
and
\sigma
is compatible with
b
.

Proof.

If
\dim(V^{-\sigma})=0
, then
B^{\sigma}(b)=\mathbb{R}
and thus
(B(b),\sigma)
is weakly Hermitian. Since the property to be weakly Hermitian implies the property to be of Jordan type, by Proposition
7.1
, we can now assume that
V^{-\sigma}=f_{m}\mathbb{R}
.

To prove Proposition
7.4
, we the following Lemma:

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
V^{-\sigma}=f_{m}\mathbb{R}
. If the Lie algebra
(B(b),\sigma)
is weakly Hermitian then
\sigma
is compatible with
b
and
b|_{V^{\sigma}}
is positive definite, i.e.,
b
has signature
(1,n)
.

Proof.

By contradiction, let
x\in V^{\sigma}
be an element such that
b(x,x)\leq 0
. Then
f_{m}x\in B^{\sigma}(b)
and
(f_{m}x)^{2}=b(x,x)\in B^{\sigma}_{+}(b)
. As
B^{\sigma}(b)
contains no nilpotent elements,
b(x,x)<0
. But
1^{2}=1\in B^{\sigma}_{+}(b)
. Therefore,
B^{\sigma}_{+}(b)
contains the line
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
B(b)
is not weakly Hermitian. Thus, if the Lie algebra
(B(b),\sigma)
is weakly Hermitian then
\sigma
is compatible with
b
and
b|_{V^{\sigma}}
is positive definite.
∎

To finish the proof of Proposition
7.4
, it is enough to prove the converse of Lemma
7.5
, i.e., that
B(1,n)
with a compatible anti-involution
\sigma
is weakly Hermitian.

Let
\sigma
be an involutive isometry on
(V,b)=\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. For an element
e\in V^{\sigma}
, we denote its Euclidean norm as:

\|e\|:=\sqrt{b(e,e)}\geq 0.

Obviously, every
x\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
can be uniquely written as follows
x=a_{0}+rf_{1}e
where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
r\geq 0
and
e\in V^{\sigma}
with
\|e\|=1
such that
x=a_{0}+rf_{1}e
.

Lemma 7.6
.

The space

B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)=\left\{t+uf_{1}e\mid t\geq 0,\,0\leq u\leq t,\,e\in V^{\sigma},\,\|e\|=1\right\}.

is a closed proper convex cone.

Proof.

Let
x:=a_{0}+rf_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
r\geq 0
and
e\in V^{\sigma}
with
\|e\|=1
. Then
x^{2}=a_{0}^{2}+r^{2}+2a_{0}rf_{1}e.

Let
t:=a_{0}^{2}+r^{2}\geq 0
and
u:=2a_{0}r=2\sgn(a_{0})\sqrt{t-r^{2}}r
. For fixed
t\geq 0
,
u=u(r)
takes all values between
-t
and
t
. So we get

\displaystyle B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)

\displaystyle=\left\{t+uf_{1}e\mid t\geq 0,\,|u|\leq t,e\in V^{\sigma},\,\|e\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{t+uf_{1}e\mid t\geq 0,\,0\leq u\leq t,e\in V^{\sigma},\,\|e\|=1\right\}.

This is a convex cone. Indeed, take
x=t+uf_{1}e
,
x^{\prime}=t^{\prime}+u^{\prime}f_{1}e^{\prime}
from
B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)
. Then

x+x^{\prime}=(t+t^{\prime})+f_{1}(ue+u^{\prime}e^{\prime})=(t+t^{\prime})+\tilde{v}f_{1}\tilde{e}

where

v=\|ue+u^{\prime}e^{\prime}\|
,
\tilde{e}=\frac{ue+u^{\prime}e^{\prime}}{v}.

Using the triangle inequality we get:

0\leq v=\|v\tilde{e}\|=\|ue+u^{\prime}e^{\prime}\|\leq\|ue\|+\|u^{\prime}e^{\prime}\|=u+u^{\prime}<t+t^{\prime}.

Therefore,
x+x^{\prime}\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)
.

It is also a proper cone because for

x\in B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)\cap(-B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}(1,n)),

t=0
and so
u=0
.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

The space
B^{\sigma}(1,n)
contains no nilpotent elements.

Proof.

We show that for
b\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
,
b=0
if and only if
b^{2}=0
. Let
b=a_{0}+a_{1}fe\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
with
b(e,e)=1
. Then
b^{2}=a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+2a_{0}a_{1}fe=0
, therefore
a_{0}=a_{1}=0
, i.e.,
b=0
.
∎

Thus, we conclude that the Lie algebra
B(1,n)
is weakly Hermitian.
∎

7.2.
Spectral decomposition in the Clifford algebra

In this subsection, we find what do the Jordan frames in the formally real Jordan algebra
B^{\sigma}(1,n)
look like.

Proposition 7.8
.

Every nontrivial idempotent
c\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
is of the following form:
c=\frac{1+e}{2}
for some
e\in V^{\sigma}
such that
\|e\|=1
.

Proof.

Let
c=x+yf_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}(1,n)
be an idempotent. This means, in particular, that
c\in B_{\geq 0}^{\sigma}(1,n)
, i.e.,
0\leq y\leq x
and
\|e\|=1
. Then

c^{2}=(x^{2}+y^{2})+2xyf_{1}e=x+yf_{1}e=c.

Thus
2xy=y
. If
y=0
, then
c=1
is trivial idempotent. If
y\neq 0
, then
x=y=\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

Theorem 7.9
.

Every Jordan frame in
B^{\sigma}(1,n)
is of the following form:
(c_{1},c_{2})
where
c_{1}=\frac{1+e}{2}
,
c_{2}=\frac{1-e}{2}
for some
e\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
,
b(e,e)=1
.

Proof.

Let
c_{1}=\frac{1+f_{1}e_{1}}{2}
,
c_{2}=\frac{1+f_{1}e_{2}}{2}
be two orthogonal idempotents with
\|e_{1}\|=\|e_{2}\|=1
. Then:

\displaystyle 0=c_{1}\circ c_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+f_{1}e_{1}}{2}\circ\frac{1+f_{1}e_{2}}{2}=\frac{(1+f_{1}e_{1})(1+f_{1}e_{2})+(1+f_{1}e_{2})(1+f_{1}e_{1}))}{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+f_{1}e_{1}+f_{1}e_{2}+f_{1}e_{1}f_{1}e_{2}+1+f_{1}e_{1}+f_{1}e_{2}+f_{1}e_{2}f_{1}e_{1}}{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{2+2b(e_{1},e_{2})+2f_{1}(e_{1}+e_{2})}{8}.

Therefore,
e_{1}=-e_{2}
. Moreover,
c_{1}+c_{2}=1
, i.e.,
(c_{1},c_{2})
is a complete system of orthogonal idempotents. Thus, every complete system of idempotents has at most two elements. In particular, all Jordan frames are of the form
(c_{1},c_{2})
where
c_{1}=\frac{1+e}{2}
,
c_{2}=\frac{1-e}{2}
as above.
∎

7.3.
Clifford group and its Lie algebra

In this section, we describe a Lie group of which the Lie algebra is
B(m,n)
.

The group of all invertible elements
\Cl(m,n)^{\times}
of
\Cl(m,n)
acts on
\Cl(m,n)
in the following way

(7.8)

\begin{matrix}\tau\colon&\Cl(m,n)^{\times}\times\Cl(m,n)&\to&\Cl(m,n)\\
&(x,y)&\mapsto&\alpha(x)yx^{-1}\end{matrix}

where
\alpha
is the involution on
\Cl(m,n)
induced by the automorphism
v\mapsto-v
on
\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
.

Definition 7.10
.

The (even)
Clifford group
of signature
(m,n)
is the group of all invertible elements of
\Cl_{even}(m,n)
that stabilize
(V,b)=\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
under the action
\tau
, i.e.

\ClGr(m,n):=\ClGr(b):=\{x\in\Cl_{even}^{\times}(m,n)\mid\tau(x)v\in V\text{ for all }v\in V\}.

We denote by
\ClGr_{0}(m,n)
the connected component of
1
in
\ClGr(m,n)
. We call it also Clifford group.

Remark 7.11
.

The Lie algebra of
\ClGr(m,n)
can be described as follows:

\clgr(m,n):=\clgr(b):=\{x\in\Cl(m,n)\mid\forall v\in V:\alpha(x)v-vx\in V\}.

We recall the following well known properties of the Clifford group (for more details see
[
14
]
). Definition
7.10
implies that
\ClGr(m,n)
acts on
V
by
\tau
preserving
b
so that the following sequences are exact:

1\to\mathbb{R}^{\times}\xrightarrow{\subset}\ClGr(m,n)\xrightarrow{\tau}\SO(m,n)\to 1.

1\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\xrightarrow{\subset}\ClGr_{0}(m,n)\xrightarrow{\tau}\SO_{0}(m,n)\to 1.

In particular,

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ClGr(m,n)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\SO(m,n)=1+\frac{(m+n)(m+n-1)}{2}.

Fact 7.12
.

The following map is well defined:

\begin{matrix}N\colon&\ClGr(m,n)&\to&\mathbb{R}^{\times}\\
&x&\mapsto&x^{t}x\end{matrix}

where
(\cdot)^{t}
is the anti-involution on
\Cl(m,n)
induced by the trivial automorphism on
V
.

We remind the definition of the spin group:

Definition 7.13
.

The
spin group
of signature
(m,n)
is the following subgroup of the Clifford group:

\Spin(m,n):=\{x\in\ClGr(m,n)\mid N(x)=1\},

\Spin_{0}(m,n):=\{x\in\ClGr_{0}(m,n)\mid N(x)=1\}.

Remark 7.14
.

If
m>0
and
n>0
then
\Spin(m,n)
has two connected components. If
m=0
or
n=0
, then
\Spin(m,n)
is connected, i.e.,
\Spin(m,n)=\Spin_{0}(m,n)
.

Corollary 7.15
.

•

For
x\in\ClGr_{0}(m,n)
,
N(x)>0
;

•

The map

x\mapsto\frac{x}{\sqrt{N(x)}}

maps
\ClGr_{0}(m,n)
surjectively to
\Spin_{0}(m,n)
;

•

The map

x\mapsto\frac{x}{\sqrt{|N(x)|}}

maps
\ClGr(m,n)
surjectively to
\Spin(m,n)
;

Proposition 7.16
.

The Lie algebra
\clgr(m,n)
agrees with
B(m,n)
.

Proof.

A direct calculation shows that
B(m,n)\subseteq\clgr(m,n)
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{R}}B(m,n)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{m^{2}-m}{2}+\frac{n^{2}-n}{2}+mn=1+\frac{m^{2}-m+n^{2}-n+2mn}{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(m+n)^{2}-(m+n)}{2}=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\clgr(m,n).

So we obtain that
B(m,n)=\clgr(m,n)
.
∎

Proposition 7.17
.

The maximal compact subgroup
U(\ClGr_{0}(m,n),\sigma)
of
\ClGr_{0}(m,n)
agrees with
\Spin(m)\times\Spin(n)
.

Proof.

Both groups are connected. So it is enough to show that their Lie algebras agree. We refer to (
7.7
):

\displaystyle\Lie(U(\ClGr_{0}(m,n),\sigma))

\displaystyle=B^{-\sigma}(m,n)

\displaystyle=B^{-\sigma}(m,0)\oplus B^{-\sigma}(0,n)

\displaystyle=\spin(m)\oplus\spin(n).\qed

Theorem 7.18
.

Let
\sigma
be a compatible involutive isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. The group
U(\ClGr_{0}(1,n),\sigma)
acts transitively on the set of Jordan frames. In particular,
B(1,n)
is Hermitian.

Proof.

Let
\left(c_{1}=\frac{1+f_{1}e}{2},c_{2}=\frac{1-f_{1}e}{2}\right)
and
\left(c_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{1+f_{1}e^{\prime}}{2},c_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{1-f_{1}e^{\prime}}{2}\right)
be two Jordan frames. Since
b(e,e)=b(e^{\prime},e^{\prime})=1
, there exists an orthogonal transformation
u\in\SO(n)
such that
u(e)=e^{\prime}
. Take some preimage
v
of
u
in
\Spin(n)=U(\ClGr_{0}(1,n),\sigma)
. Then

\sigma(v)c_{1}v=v^{-1}c_{i}v=\frac{1+(v^{-1}f_{1}v)(v^{-1}ev)}{2}=\frac{1+u(f_{1})u(e)}{2}=\frac{1+f_{1}e^{\prime}}{2}=c_{1}^{\prime}.

Similarly,
\sigma(v)c_{2}v=c_{2}
.

Finally, by Proposition
7.4
, the Lie algebra
B(1,n)
is weakly Hermitian, and, by Proposition
7.17
, the group
U(\ClGr(1,n),\sigma)
is compact.
∎

Remark 7.19
.

As we have seen, the Lie algebra
(B(b),\sigma)
such that
V^{\sigma}=V
or
V^{-\sigma}=V
is always weakly Hermitian. In this case,
U(\ClGr_{0}(b),\sigma)=\Spin_{0}(m,n)
where
(m,n)
is the signature of the form
b
, which is not compact unless
m=0
or
n=0
. Therefore,
(B(b),\sigma)
is Hermitian if and only of
m=0
or
n=0
, i.e., if and only if
\sigma
is compatible with
b
.

Remark 7.20
.

Let
\sigma
be a compatible involutive isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. Notice that the space
B^{\sigma}(1,n)^{\times}
is (in general) strictly contained in
\ClGr^{\sigma}(1,n)
. For example, let
n\geq 4
then the elements
f_{1}e_{1}
,
f_{1}e_{2}
,
f_{1}e_{3}
,
f_{1}e_{4}
are in
B^{\sigma}(1,n)^{\times}\subseteq\ClGr^{\sigma}(1,n)
. However, their product is not contained in
B^{\sigma}(1,n)
because

f_{1}e_{1}f_{1}e_{2}f_{1}e_{3}f_{1}e_{4}=e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}e_{4}\notin B.

But it is an element of
\ClGr^{\sigma}(1,n)
and, more precisely, of the
\sigma
-symmetric part of its unipotent subgroup
U(\ClGr(1,n),\sigma)\cap\ClGr^{\sigma}(1,n)
.

7.4.
Lie algebra
\spin(m,n)
as
\spp_{2}(B,\sigma)

In this section, we identify
\spp_{2}(B(m,n),\sigma)
with
\spin(m+1,n+1)
. We recall the definition of
\spin(m,n)
:

\spin(m,n)=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{i}e_{j},f_{k}e_{i},f_{k}f_{l}\mid i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\},k,l\in\{1,\dots,m\})

where
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
build an orthonormal basis of
(V,b)=\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
as before. Note that
B(m,n)\cong\mathbb{R}\oplus\spin(m,n)
, where
\mathbb{R}
is central. Let
\sigma
be an involutive isometry on
\mathbb{R}^{m,n}
such that
\sigma(f_{m})=-f_{m}
and
\sigma(f_{i})=f_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
, and
\sigma(e_{i})=e_{i}
for all
i
.

The following theorem is immediate:

Theorem 7.21
.

The following map is an isomorphism of Lie algebras:

\begin{array}[]{rrcll}\varphi\colon&\spp_{2}(B(m,n),\sigma)&\to&\spin(m+1,n+1)\\
&\diag(a,a)&\mapsto&a&\text{for }a\in B^{-\sigma}(m,n)\\
&\diag(f_{m}e_{i},-f_{m}e_{i})&\mapsto&f_{m}e_{i}&\text{for }1\leq i\leq n\\
&\diag(f_{m}f_{i},-f_{m}f_{i})&\mapsto&f_{m}f_{i}&\text{for }1\leq i\leq m-1\\
&\diag(1,-1)&\mapsto&f_{m+1}e_{n+1}\\
&S(f_{m}v)&\mapsto&f_{m+1}v&\text{for }v\in V^{\sigma}\\
&A(f_{m}v)&\mapsto&ve_{n+1}&\text{for }v\in V^{\sigma}\\
&S(1)&\mapsto&-f_{m}e_{n+1}\\
&A(1)&\mapsto&f_{m}f_{m+1}\end{array}

where
S(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
x&0\end{pmatrix}
,
A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-x\\
x&0\end{pmatrix}
.

7.5.
Group
\Spin_{0}(m,n)
as
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)

In this section, we want to identify the groups
\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)
and
\Sp_{2}(\ClGr(m,n),\sigma)
.

First, note that a generic matrix in
\Sp_{2}(\ClGr(m,n),\sigma)
can be decomposed in a unique way as follows:

(7.9)

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
y&1\ \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\ \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&\sigma(x)^{-1}\ \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&z\\
0&1\ \end{pmatrix}

where
x\in\Spin(m,n)
,
\lambda>0
,
y,z\in B^{\sigma}(m,n)
.

The embedding:

\mathbb{R}^{m,n}=\Span(f_{1},\dots,f_{m},e_{1},\dots,e_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2,n+1}=\Span(f_{1},\dots,f_{m+1},e_{1},\dots,e_{n+1})

induces the embedding of spin groups
\iota\colon\Spin(m,n)\hookrightarrow\Spin(m+1,n+1)
. Moreover, we can embed the entire
\Spin(m,n)
into
\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)
using the following map:

\iota_{0}\colon\Spin(m,n)\to\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)

•

if
x\in\Spin_{0}(m,n)
, then
\iota_{0}(x):=\iota(x)
;

•

if
x\in\Spin(m,n)\setminus\Spin_{0}(m,n)
, then
\iota_{0}(x)=\iota(x)f_{m+1}e_{n+1}
.

The map
\iota_{0}
is an injective group homomorphism.

Theorem 7.22
.

The following map is an isomorphism of Lie groups:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\Phi\colon\Sp_{2}(\ClGr(m,n),\sigma)&\to&\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)&\\
\diag(y,\sigma(y)^{-1})&\mapsto&\iota_{0}(y)&\text{for }y\in\Spin(m,n)\\
\diag(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})&\mapsto&\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}}{2}+f_{m+1}e_{n+1}\frac{\lambda-\lambda^{-1}}{2}&\text{for }\lambda>0\\
R(f_{m}v)&\mapsto&1+\frac{f_{m+1}+e_{n+1}}{2}v&\text{for }v\in V^{\sigma}\\
L(f_{m}v)&\mapsto&1+\frac{f_{m+1}-e_{n+1}}{2}v&\text{for }v\in V^{\sigma}\\
R(1)&\mapsto&1-\frac{f_{m+1}+e_{n+1}}{2}f_{m}\\
L(1)&\mapsto&1-\frac{f_{m+1}-e_{n+1}}{2}f_{m}\end{array}

where
R(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
,
L(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x&1\end{pmatrix}
. The map
\varphi
is the differential of
\Phi
at
\Id
.

Proof.

Because there is a neighborhood of
\Id\in\Sp_{2}(\ClGr(1,n),\sigma)
consisting of generic elements and because of uniqueness of the decomposition (
7.9
), one can see that
\varphi
is the differential of
\Phi
at
\Id
. Because
\varphi
is an isomorphism of Lie algebras,
\Phi
is a group homomorphism and a
k:1
-covering map for some
k\geq 1
. Finally, since this map is bijective on the level of diagonal matrices,
k=1
.
∎

7.6.
Models of the symmetric space of
\Spin_{0}(2,n)

In this section, we construct different models of the symmetric space of
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)\cong\Sp_{2}(\ClGr(1,n),\sigma)
.

7.6.1.
Upper half-space model

We remind:

\displaystyle B(1,n)

\displaystyle=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(1,e_{i}e_{j},f_{1}e_{k}\mid i,j,k\in\{1,\dots n\})

\displaystyle B^{\sigma}(1,n)

\displaystyle=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(1,f_{1}e_{k}\mid k\in\{1,\dots n\})=f_{1}V

\displaystyle B^{\sigma}_{+}(1,n)

\displaystyle=\left\{t+uf_{1}e\mid t>0,\,u\in[0,t),e\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}),\,\|e\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle=f_{1}V_{-}\ ,

where
V_{-}=\{v\in V\mid b(v,v)<0,\;b(f_{1},v)>0\}
. Thus,

\mathfrak{U}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))=\{x+yi\mid x\in B^{\sigma}(1,n),\;y\in B^{\sigma}_{+}(1,n)\}=f_{1}(V+V_{-}i).

Further,
\mathfrak{U}(\ClGr(1,n))=B^{\sigma}_{+}(1,n)=f_{1}V_{-}
. It sits naturally inside
\mathfrak{U}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
as the imaginary part.

7.6.2.
Precompact model

We take the complexification

B_{\mathbb{C}}:=B_{\mathbb{C}}(1,n)=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}\{1,f_{1}e_{i},e_{i}e_{j}\mid i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}

with complex linear extension of
\sigma
denoted also by
\sigma
and the complex antilinear extension of
\sigma
denoted by
\bar{\sigma}
. Then

B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}\{1,f_{1}e_{i}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}.

We consider the space, which is the precompact model of the symmetric space for
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))

\displaystyle=\{b\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}\mid 1-\bar{b}b\in\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\times}\}

\displaystyle=\{x+yf_{1}e\mid 1-(\bar{x}+\bar{y}f_{1}e)(x+yf_{1}e)\in\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\times},\;\|e\|=1\}

\displaystyle=\{x+yf_{1}e\mid 1-(\bar{x}x+\bar{y}y)-(\bar{x}y+\bar{y}x)f_{1}e\in\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\times},\;\|e\|=1\}.

We first find out what the set
\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})
is.
Let
r_{1}\exp(i\phi_{1})+r_{1}\exp(i\phi_{1})f_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0
,
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\|e\|=1
then

\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(r_{1}\exp(i\phi_{1})+r_{1}\exp(i\phi_{1})f_{1}e)=(r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2})+2r_{1}r_{2}\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})f_{1}e.

We denote
r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}=:r
. then

\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(r_{1}\exp(i\phi_{1})+r_{2}\exp(i\phi_{2})f_{1}e)=r+2r_{1}\sqrt{r-r_{1}^{2}}\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})f_{1}e

For fixed
r\geq 0
, the expression
2r_{1}\sqrt{r-r_{1}^{2}}\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})
can take every value in the interval
[-r,r]
, i.e.

\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})=\{r+pf_{1}e\mid r\geq 0,\;p\in[-r,r],\;\|e\|=1\}=B^{\sigma}_{\geq 0}.

Analogously,

\theta_{\mathbb{C}}(B^{\sigma}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\times}=B^{\sigma}_{+}.

Therefore,

\mathfrak{B}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))=\{x+yf_{1}e\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\sigma}\mid 1-(\bar{x}x+\bar{y}y)-(\bar{x}y+\bar{y}x)f_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}_{+},\;\|e\|=1\}

is the precompact model of the symmetric space for
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
. Further,
\mathfrak{B}(\ClGr(1,n))=\{x+yf_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}\mid 1-(x^{2}+y^{2})-2xyf_{1}e\in B^{\sigma}_{+}\}
. Is sits naturally inside
\mathfrak{B}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
as the imaginary part.

7.6.3.
Projective space model

We take the upper half-space model:

\mathfrak{U}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))=\{x+yi\mid x\in B^{\sigma}(1,n),\;y\in B^{\sigma}_{+}(1,n)\}.

We know that the map
z\mapsto(z,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}
is a homeomorphism between the upper half-space models and the projective space model. So we obtain:

\mathfrak{P}(\Spin_{0}(2,n))=\{(x+yi,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}\mid x\in B^{\sigma}(1,n),\;y\in B^{\sigma}_{+}(1,n)\}=

=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}(x_{1}+y_{1}i)+f_{1}(ex_{2}+e^{\prime}y_{2})i\\
1\end{pmatrix}A_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\begin{array}[]{l}x_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\,y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\\
y_{2}\in[0,x_{2}),\,\|e\|=\|e^{\prime}\|=1\end{array}\right.\right\}.

We can also interpret the projective space model for
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
in terms of lines in
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
. First, we note that the stabilizer of the line
(i,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}\subset A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
corresponds under the map
\Phi
(cf. Theorem
7.22
) to the stabilizer of the line
(f_{2}+f_{1}i)\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
where
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
by the (complexified) map
\tau
(cf. (
7.8
)). So we can take the following injective map:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F\colon\mathfrak{P}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))&\to&\{l\in\mathbb{C}P^{n+2}\mid l=v\mathbb{C},\,v\in\mathbb{C}^{n+3},\,b_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)=0\}\\
g(i,1)^{t}A_{\mathbb{C}}&\mapsto&\tau(\Phi(g))(f_{2}+f_{1}i)\mathbb{C}=\Phi(g)(f_{2}+f_{1}i)\Phi(g)^{-1}\mathbb{C}\end{array}

where
g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
and
b_{\mathbb{C}}
the complex bilinear extension of
b
.

Since
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{n+3}
preserving
b
, it acts on
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
preserving
b_{\mathbb{C}}
and the
\mathbb{C}
-sesquilinear extension
\tilde{b}
of
b
, i.e.

\tilde{b}(v_{1}+v_{2}i,w_{1}+w_{2}i):=b(v_{1},w_{1})+b(v_{2},w_{2})+(b(v_{1},w_{2})-b(v_{2},w_{1}))i

for
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+3}
. Notice that the Hermitian form
\tilde{b}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
has signature
(2,n+1)
. Therefore, a direct computation shows that

F
maps injectively
\mathfrak{P}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
onto

\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)):=\left\{l\in\mathbb{C}P^{n+2}\,\middle|\,l=v\mathbb{C},\,v\in\mathbb{C}^{n+3},\,b_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)=0,\,\tilde{b}(v,v)<0\right\}.

Let
v=v_{1}+v_{2}i\in\mathbb{C}^{n+3}
be a vector such that
b_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)=0
,
\tilde{b}(v,v)=-2
. Then
b(v_{1},v_{1})=b(v_{2},v_{2})=-1
,
b(v_{1},v_{2})=0
. There exists an
\SO(2,n+1)
-transformation that maps
(f_{2},f_{1})
to
(v_{1},v_{2})
. Therefore,
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
acts transitively on
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
, so it is a model of the symmetric space of
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
.

The projective space model for the symmetric space of
\ClGr(1,n)
can be similarly interpreted as follows:

\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\ClGr(1,n)):=\left\{l\in\mathbb{C}P^{n+2}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}l=(w+vi)\mathbb{C},\,v\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1})\\
w\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{n+1},f_{2}),b_{\mathbb{C}}(v,v)=0,\,\tilde{b}(v,v)<0\end{array}\right\}.

This space is naturally embedded into
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
. Notice that the subset of
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\ClGr(1,n))
with
w=f_{2}
provides a projective space model for the Riemannian symmetric of
\Spin(1,n)
, which can be also reinterpreted as follows:

\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n)):=\left\{l\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\,\middle|\,l=v\mathbb{R},\,v\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1}),\;b(v,v)<0\right\}.

This is the classical projective space model of the hyperbolic
n
-space.

7.6.4.
Space of complex structures

We consider the space of complex structures for
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\cong\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
:

\mathfrak{C}(\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)):=\left\{J\text{ complex structure on $A^{2}$}\left|\begin{array}[]{l}J(\Is_{G}(\omega))=\Is_{G}(\omega),\\
h_{J}\text{ is a $(G,\sigma)$-inner product}\end{array}\right.\right\}

where
h_{J}(x,y)=\omega(J(x),y)
. This model admits the following equivalent interpretation:

Notice that
\mathfrak{C}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))\subseteq\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
because the standard complex structure
J_{0}\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
and
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
acts on
\mathfrak{C}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
transitively by conjugation.

If we take the standard complex structure
J_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
, then
\Phi(J_{0})=-f_{1}f_{2}
where
\Phi
is the isomorphism from
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
to
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
defined in Theorem
7.22
. For every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n+3}
there exist unique elements
e\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
,
f\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},f_{2})
such that
v=e+f
. Then

\Phi(J_{0})v\Phi(J_{0})^{-1}=e-f.

Let
J
be another element of
\mathfrak{C}
. There exists
g\in\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)
such that
J=g^{-1}J_{0}g
. Let
v=\Phi(g)^{-1}(e+f)\Phi(g)
,
e,f
as above,

\Phi(J)v\Phi(J)^{-1}=\Phi(g)^{-1}\Phi(J_{0})(e+f)\Phi(J_{0})^{-1}\Phi(g)=\Phi(g)^{-1}(e-f)\Phi(g).

Since
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
acts on
(\mathbb{R}^{n+3},b)
preserving
b
, the restriction of
b
to the linear subspace
\Phi(g)^{-1}\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n+1})\Phi(g)
is positive definite and the restriction of
b
to the linear subspace
\Phi(g)^{-1}\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},f_{2})\Phi(g)
is negative definite.

Consider the following space of
b
-orthogonal splittings of
\mathbb{R}^{n+3}
:

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)):=\left\{(V_{+},V_{-})\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{R}^{n+3}=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}\\
b|_{V_{+}}\text{ is positive definite}\\
b|_{V_{-}}\text{ is negative definite}\\
V_{+}\perp_{b}V_{-}\end{array}\right\}.

We have a surjective map
F\colon\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\to\mathcal{D}
,

F(g):=(\Phi(g)^{-1}\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n+1})\Phi(g),\Phi(g)^{-1}\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},f_{2})\Phi(g)).

Notice,

F^{-1}(\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}),\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{1},f_{2}))=\Spin(2)\times\Spin(n+1).

Therefore,
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
is isomorphic to
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)/(\Spin(2)\times\Spin(n+1))
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma))
is the model of the symmetric space of
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
.

To identify the spaces
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\ClGr(1,n))
and
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n))
, we notice that
\iota_{0}(\Spin(1,n))
inside
\Spin_{0}(2,n+1)
stabilizes
\Span(e_{n+1})
and
\Span(f_{2})
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n))
can be identified with the space of the following splittings of
\mathbb{R}^{n+3}
:

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n)):=\left\{(V_{+},V_{-})\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{R}^{n+3}=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}\oplus\Span(e_{n+1})\oplus\Span(f_{2})\\
b|_{V_{+}}\text{ is positive definite, }V_{+}\subset W\\
b|_{V_{-}}\text{ is negative definite, }V_{-}\subset W\\
V_{+}\perp_{b}V_{-}\end{array}\right\},

where we denote
W:=\Span(e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1})
.

However,
\iota_{0}(\ClGr(1,n))
does not preserve
\Span(e_{n+1})
and
\Span(f_{2})
separately, but only the entire subspace
\Span(e_{n+1},f_{2})
. Therefore, we can identify:

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\ClGr(1,n)):=\left\{(V_{+},V_{-},l_{+},l_{-})\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{R}^{n+3}=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}\oplus l_{+}\oplus l_{-}\\
b|_{V_{+}}\text{ is positive definite, }V_{+}\subset W\\
b|_{V_{-}}\text{ is negative definite, }V_{-}\subset W\\
b|_{l_{+}}\text{ is positive definite, }l_{+}\subset\Span(e_{n+1},f_{2})\\
b|_{l_{-}}\text{ is negative definite, }l_{-}\subset\Span(e_{n+1},f_{2})\\
V_{+}\perp_{b}V_{-},\;l_{+}\perp_{b}l_{-}\end{array}\right\},

where as above
W=\Span(e_{1},\dots,e_{n},f_{1})
.

The Riemannian symmetric spaces
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n))
and
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\ClGr(1,n))
naturally embed into
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin_{0}(2,n+1))
as follows:

(V_{+},V_{-},l_{+},l_{-})\mapsto(V_{+}\oplus l_{+},V_{-}\oplus l_{-}),

where
l_{+}=\Span(e_{n+1})
and
l_{-}=\Span(f_{2})
in case of
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\Spin(1,n))
.

Remark 7.23
.

In constructing the models of the Riemannian symmetric space associated to the group

\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\cong\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)
, we restricted attention to the case
m=1
,
since only in this situation the Lie algebra
(B(m,n),\sigma)
is Hermitian.
This allows one to define the positive cone
B^{\sigma}_{+}(m,n)
, which is required to build
these models, and moreover ensures that the stabilizer of every point in the symmetric space,
which is conjugate to the group
\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
, is compact.

In the remaining cases, when
(B(m,n),\sigma)
is of Jordan type but not Hermitian,
the group
\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)\cong\Spin_{0}(m+1,n+1)
and its subgroup

\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
are still well defined, although the latter is no longer compact.
This nevertheless allows one to define the symmetric space

\Sp_{2}(G,\sigma)/\KSp_{2}(G,\sigma)
, which is then of pseudo–Riemannian type.
Models of these pseudo–Riemannian symmetric spaces can also be formulated in terms of

B(m,n)
; however, a detailed study of their geometric structure lies beyond the scope of this article and will be pursued in future work.
