Microglia-associated Galectin-3 expression in prenatal stress-induced cognitive dysfunction in adult offspring
Authors: Qianqian Wang, Huanhuan Luo, Fangfei Fan, Zibin Zhao, Dandan Liu, Libin Liao, Shengbin Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize Galectin-3 expression and its cellular distribution in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring exposed to prenatal stress, and to examine their associations with microglial activation, neuroinflammation, and cognitive dysfunction.MethodsTwelve pregnant Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned to a stress group (n = 6) or a control group (n = 6). Rats in the stress group were subjected to restraint stress from gestational day 15 to day 21, whereas rats in the control group received no intervention. Cognitive function in adult offspring was assessed using the open-field test, elevated plus maze test, novel object recognition test, and Y-maze test. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to measure peripheral blood levels of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and corticosterone (CORT) in dams and offspring. ELISA was used to measure the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in the serum of offspring, while quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was applied to detect the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex of offspring. Immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate microglial activation by assessing Iba1-positive microglial morphology and quantifying Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells, as well as to examine the colocalization of Iba1 with Galectin-3 in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring. qRT-PCR was used to determine Galectin-3 mRNA expression levels in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring. Western blotting was performed to assess the protein expression levels of Galectin-3, NF-κB p65, and the neuronal marker NeuN in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring.ResultsCompared with the control group, offspring in the prenatal stress group exhibited significant behavioral impairments in the open-field test, elevated plus maze test, novel object recognition test, and Y-maze test. ELISA results showed that prenatal stress increased peripheral blood levels of CRH and CORT in both dams (P < 0.05) and offspring (P < 0.001). ELISA and qRT-PCR analyses further revealed significantly increased expression of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in the peripheral blood, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring (P < 0.05). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that prenatal stress induced activation-associated morphological changes in Iba1-positive microglia and significantly increased the numbers of Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (all P < 0.01). The numbers of Iba1/Galectin-3 double-positive cells were also significantly increased in the hippocampus (P < 0.05) and prefrontal cortex (P < 0.001). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed that prenatal stress significantly upregulated Galectin-3 expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring and increased total NF-κB p65 protein expression, accompanied by decreased NeuN expression (all P < 0.05).ConclusionIncreased Gal-3 expression was associated with microglial neuroinflammatory responses and cognitive dysfunction in adult offspring exposed to prenatal stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12868-026-01048-9.

Prenatal stress refers to the sustained or repeated exposure of the mother to psychological, environmental, or physiological stressors during pregnancy, which may disrupt maternal endocrine and immune homeostasis and, in turn, affect intrauterine fetal development [1]. Evidence indicates that the effects of prenatal stress on offspring are multifaceted and long-lasting, involving not only abnormal development of the immune, endocrine, and nervous systems, but also an increased risk of neuropsychiatric and behavioral abnormalities in adulthood, including anxiety, depression, attention deficits, schizophrenia, and cognitive impairment [2, 3]. The hippocampus is one of the brain regions most vulnerable to stress-related injury in the central nervous system and is also a critical structure for learning, memory, and emotional regulation [4, 5]. Studies have shown that prenatal stress reduces the generation of neurons and glial cells in the hippocampus of offspring, thereby impairing hippocampal structure and function. The underlying mechanism may be associated with excessive activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis: maternal stress can increase the secretion of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), leading to elevated glucocorticoid levels; in rodents, this change is primarily reflected by increased corticosterone levels [6, 7]. Glucocorticoids and inflammatory cytokines can cross the placental barrier and enter the fetal brain, thereby affecting fetal brain development and ultimately inducing neuroinflammatory responses in the offspring’s central nervous system, impairing neuronal development, and promoting microglial activation [7, 8].

Microglia are the resident immune cells of the central nervous system. Studies have shown that microglia exhibit marked heterogeneity and plasticity, and their functional states dynamically change in response to alterations in the local microenvironment [9]. Under homeostatic conditions, microglia help maintain the internal stability of the central nervous system by clearing cellular debris, secreting neurotrophic factors, regulating neurogenesis, and mediating synaptic pruning; moreover, they promote neural stem cell differentiation and the maturation of neural network circuits [10]. In contrast, following inflammatory or stress-related stimulation, microglia become activated and release inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TNF-α. This activation is also accompanied by abnormal synaptic pruning and excessive phagocytosis, which may disrupt the normal development and plasticity of neuronal networks, impair the function of cognition-related brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, and ultimately lead to abnormalities in cognitive behaviors, including learning and memory [11–13]. Notably, Gal-3 is typically markedly upregulated in activated microglia, suggesting that it may participate in microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and neuronal injury [14].

Gal-3, encoded by the Lgals3 gene, is localized in intracellular and extracellular compartments and has diverse biological functions involving immune regulation and cellular homeostasis [15, 16]. Primary experimental studies have shown that Gal-3 is upregulated in activated microglia following ischemic brain injury and contributes to microglial activation and proliferation [14, 17]. In experimental models of LPS-induced neuroinflammation and brain ischemia, microglia-secreted Gal-3 has been shown to act as a TLR4 ligand [18], while hippocampal Gal-3 knockdown attenuated TLR4/NF-κB signaling in an LPS-induced neuroinflammation model [19]. However, whether Gal-3 expression is altered following prenatal stress and how such alterations relate to microglial and neuroinflammatory changes in offspring remain unclear. In this study, we established a prenatal restraint stress rat model to characterize changes in Gal-3 expression and microglial morphology in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring and to examine their associations with inflammatory markers, total NF-κB p65 protein expression, NeuN expression, and behavioral outcomes.

(Cat. Animals and prenatal stress procedure

Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from the Animal Experimental Center of Xinjiang Medical University. Following mating, twelve pregnant dams were included in the study and randomly assigned to the control group or prenatal stress group, with six dams per group. Rats in the control group were maintained under standard housing conditions, whereas those in the stress group were subjected to restraint stress from gestational day 15 to 21. The restraint stress procedure was performed according to a previously published protocol [20]. Briefly, pregnant rats were placed in transparent plastic restrainers three times daily at 10:00, 14:00, and 18:00, with each restraint session lasting 45 min. Offspring from the control group (CON) and prenatal stress offspring (PSO) were weaned on postnatal day 21. Thereafter, offspring rats were separated by sex and housed in different cages. For each offspring-based analysis, six adult offspring were included per group, with one offspring selected from each of six different litters. Thus, no more than one offspring from the same litter was included in any given analysis. Both male and female offspring were included, with three males and three females in each group. Sex was not included as a factor in the primary statistical analyses.Sample collection and behavioral testing were performed on postnatal day 60, when the rats reached adulthood. All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the regulations of the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University and were reviewed and approved by the committee.

Sample collection and Euthanasia

At postnatal day 60, offspring rats were deeply anaesthetised with amobarbital sodium at a dose of 50 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection. Adequate anaesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes. Peripheral blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta, and the animals were euthanised by exsanguination under deep anaesthesia. Brain tissues were rapidly removed, and the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex were dissected for subsequent qRT-PCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence analyses. This procedure was performed to minimise animal pain and distress and to preserve tissue integrity for subsequent biochemical, molecular, and histological analyses.

Behavioral analysis

Open field test

Rats were subjected to behavioral testing in an open-field environment, and their behavior was recorded and analyzed using Shanghai Jiliang Animal Behavior Video Analysis software. The open-field apparatus measured 100 cm × 100 cm × 40 cm in length, width, and height, respectively. Before testing, rats were allowed to acclimate to the experimental room for 30 min. Each rat was then placed in the central square of the open field and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. After the test, the rat was returned to its home cage. The exploration area was cleaned with 75% ethanol and allowed to dry completely before testing the next animal. During the test, the distance traveled in the central zone and the total distance traveled were recorded [21].

Elevated plus maze

Behavior was recorded and analyzed using the Shanghai Jiliang Animal Behavior Video Analysis software. Before the experiment, rats were allowed to acclimate to the experimental room for 30 min. Each rat was then placed in the central area of the elevated plus maze, with its head facing an open arm. All animals began the test from the maze’s central platform. During the 5-min test period, the number of entries into the open and closed arms, as well as the time spent in each arm, was recorded. An arm entry was defined as the placement of all four paws into the arm [22].

Y-maze novel arm exploration test

The Y-maze consisted of three arms arranged at 120° angles, each measuring 50 cm in length, 15 cm in width, and 40 cm in height. Animals were allowed to acclimate to the experimental room for 30 min before testing. The experiment comprised two phases: a training phase and a test phase. During the training phase, the designated novel arm was blocked with a partition, and each rat was placed at the end of the start arm and allowed to freely explore the remaining two arms for 5 min under video recording. During the test phase, after a 1-h interval, the partition was removed to provide access to all three arms. Each rat was then placed in the same start arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The number of entries into each arm, distance traveled, and time spent in each arm were recorded. An arm entry was defined as the complete entry of all four paws into an arm. The percentage of distance traveled in the novel arm relative to the total distance and the percentage of time spent in the novel arm relative to the total exploration time during the 5-min test period were calculated [23].

Novel object recognition test

The novel object recognition test was performed using the same square open-field apparatus as that used for the open-field test. Experimental objects with distinguishable shapes and materials that could not be damaged by biting were selected, including two identical objects, A and B, and one novel object, C. To avoid olfactory interference, both the objects and the apparatus were cleaned with 75% ethanol between animals and allowed to dry completely. The experiment consisted of two phases: a training phase (T1) and a test phase (T2). In T1, objects A and B were placed at opposite ends of the diagonal of the arena, and each rat was placed in the center and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The exploration time for objects A and B was recorded. Exploration was defined as the rat directing its nose toward the object and sniffing or whisker scanning within approximately 2–3 cm of the object; climbing or sitting on the object was not counted as exploration. In T2, object B was replaced with the novel object C, while the objects remained in diagonal positions. The rat was placed in the arena from the same starting position and allowed to explore freely for 5 min, during which the time spent exploring objects A and C was recorded. To control for side bias and object preference, the object identity and left/right or diagonal position of objects A and C were fully counterbalanced across animals. If the total exploration time for the two objects in either phase was less than 20 s, that phase was extended to 10 min; animals that still failed to meet this criterion were excluded. Recognition memory was assessed by comparing the exploration time of the novel object C with that of the familiar object A during the test phase [24].

ELISA

Peripheral blood samples were collected from both offspring rats and dams. After serum separation,corticosterone (CORT) and corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) levels were measured using rat CORT and CRH ELISA kits, the concentrations of TNF‑α, IL‑6 and IL‑1β in serum of adult offspring were measured simultaneously, respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Standards and samples were added to the wells at 50 µL per well, followed by the addition of HRP conjugate at 50 µL per well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After washing the plates five times, TMB substrate was added at 90 µL per well, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. The stop solution (50 µL per well) was then added to terminate the reaction, and the absorbance, expressed as optical density (OD), was measured at 450 nm. Sample concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve. Both standards and samples were assayed in duplicate.

qRT-PCR detection of the relative mRNA levels of Gal-3 and the inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of offspring

Total RNA was extracted from the prefrontal cortex and hippocampal tissues of offspring rats using the TRIzol method and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed according to the instructions of the 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit. After preparing the reaction mixture, the samples were placed in a real-time quantitative PCR system. The reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and the relative mRNA expression levels of Gal-3, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were analyzed. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Hippocampal and prefrontal cortical tissues were collected from adult offspring rats. For each sample, 500 µL of RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF at a ratio of 100:1 (v/v) was added, and the tissues were thoroughly homogenized. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto membranes. The membranes were washed with TBST and blocked with rapid blocking solution at room temperature for 35 min. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Gal-3 (1:5000), total NF-κB p65 (1:2000), NeuN (1:10000), and GAPDH (1:10000). The following day, the membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:10000) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. For each biological sample, the intensity of each target-protein band was normalized to the GAPDH band intensity from the corresponding lane to account for residual lane-to-lane variability in protein loading and transfer. All six biological replicates per group were included in the densitometric analysis.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining and colocalization analysis

After blocking with goat serum at room temperature for 45 min, double-label immunofluorescence staining was performed using a dual-label three-color multiplex fluorescence staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Separate tissue sections were stained for CD68/Iba1, Iba1/Gal-3, GFAP/Gal-3, and NeuN/Gal-3 colocalization. The primary antibodies included rabbit anti-Iba1 antibody (1:300), rabbit anti-CD68 antibody (1:200), rabbit anti-Gal-3 antibody (1:200), rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (1:5000), and rabbit anti-NeuN antibody (1:500). The primary antibodies and corresponding fluorescence detection reagents were applied sequentially according to the multiplex staining protocol provided with the kit. CD68, GFAP, and NeuN signals were visualized in green, Gal-3 signals were visualized in red, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and examined under a fluorescence microscope. Double-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

For offspring outcomes, the litter was considered the experimental unit. Because only one offspring per litter was included in each analysis, n = 6 represented six offspring from six independent litters per group. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prenatal stress disrupted HPA axis function in dams and offspring

ELISA was performed to measure peripheral blood levels of CRH and CORT in dams and offspring. Compared with the control group, dams exposed to prenatal stress showed significantly increased peripheral blood levels of CRH and CORT (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1A; P < 0.001, Fig. 1C), indicating abnormal activation of the maternal HPA axis and elevated circulating stress-related hormone levels. The increased maternal CORT level may have further contributed to dysregulation of the HPA axis in offspring. Consistently, compared with the control group, offspring rats in the prenatal stress group exhibited significantly elevated peripheral blood levels of CRH and CORT (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1B; P < 0.01, Fig. 1D), suggesting that prenatal stress induced HPA-axis dysfunction in offspring.

Prenatal stress impairs cognitive function in adult offspring

To determine the effects of prenatal stress on cognitive function and anxiety-like behavior in adult offspring, anxiety-like behavior was assessed on postnatal day 60 (P60) using the open-field test and elevated plus maze test, while cognitive and memory performance was evaluated using the Y-maze novel arm exploration test and the novel object recognition test.

The open-field test showed that, although there was no significant difference in total distance traveled between the two groups within 5 min, offspring in the prenatal stress group predominantly moved along the peripheral area of the arena (Fig. 2A). Compared with the control group, the distance traveled in the central zone within 5 min was significantly reduced in the prenatal stress group (P < 0.001, Fig. 2B). The elevated plus maze test further showed that, compared with the control group, offspring in the prenatal stress group had a significantly lower percentage of open-arm entries (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2D) and a significantly lower percentage of time spent in the open arms (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2D). In the Y-maze novel arm exploration test, the prenatal stress group exhibited significantly lower percentage of time spent in the novel arm (P < 0.001, Fig. 2F) and a lower percentage of distance traveled in the novel arm (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2F) than the control group. In the novel object recognition test, there was no significant difference in the total exploration time for the two objects between the two groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 2H); however, the novel object recognition index was significantly decreased in offspring from the prenatal stress group (P < 0.001, Fig. 2H). Collectively, these behavioral results indicate that adult offspring exposed to prenatal stress exhibited anxiety-like behavior and cognitive impairment involving learning, working memory, and recognition memory.

Prenatal stress enhanced inflammatory responses in offspring

When the HPA axis is dysregulated or persistently affected by chronic stress, its anti-inflammatory effects may be weakened, leading to sustained or exacerbated inflammatory responses. We measured the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the peripheral blood serum of adult offspring by ELISA and detected the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring by qRT-PCR. The results showed that the expression levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were increased in offspring from the prenatal stress group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3), accompanied by elevated total NF-κB p65 protein expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 4). These findings suggest that prenatal stress induces long-term HPA-axis dysregulation and promotes a neuroinflammatory microenvironment in offspring, characterized by increased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and total NF-κB p65 protein expression.

Prenatal stress enhanced microglial activation in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring

To evaluate microglial activation in the offspring brain, Iba1 immunofluorescence staining was first performed to assess microglial morphology, followed by Iba1/CD68 double immunofluorescence staining to provide additional marker-based validation. Compared with the CON group, Iba1-positive microglia in the hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the PSO group exhibited enlarged somata and fewer, shorter, and thicker processes, with some cells displaying a rounded or amoeboid morphology. Similar morphological alterations were observed in the prefrontal cortex (Fig. 5). Quantitative analysis showed that the numbers of activated microglia in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions were significantly increased in the PSO group (all P < 0.0001; Fig. 5A–C). The number of activated microglia in the prefrontal cortex was also significantly increased (P < 0.001; Fig. 5D).

Consistent with these morphological findings, the numbers of Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells were significantly increased in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the PSO group (both P < 0.0001; Fig. 6A, B), as well as in the DG region (P < 0.01; Fig. 6C) and prefrontal cortex (P < 0.0001; Fig. 6D). Together, the morphological alterations in Iba1-positive microglia and the increased numbers of Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells provide complementary evidence of enhanced microglial activation following prenatal stress.

Prenatal stress enhanced Gal-3 expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring

To further clarify the effect of prenatal stress on Gal-3 expression in the brain of adult offspring, qRT-PCR and Western blotting were performed to detect Gal-3 mRNA and protein expression levels, respectively, in both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. The results showed that, compared with the control group, offspring in the stress group exhibited significantly increased Gal-3 mRNA expression in the hippocampus (P < 0.01, Fig. 7A), accompanied by a marked increase in Gal-3 protein expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 7B, C). In the prefrontal cortex, Gal-3 mRNA expression was also significantly elevated in offspring from the stress group compared with the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 7D), and Gal-3 protein expression was likewise markedly increased (P < 0.05, Fig. 7E, F). These results indicate that prenatal stress significantly upregulated Gal-3 expression in both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring.

Prenatal stress increased Gal-3 colocalization with Iba1-positive microglia in the offspring hippocampus and prefrontal cortex

To further characterize the cellular distribution of Gal-3 following prenatal stress, double immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine the colocalization of Gal-3 with Iba1-positive microglia, GFAP-positive astrocytes, and NeuN-positive neurons in the hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG regions and in the prefrontal cortex of adult offspring. Compared with the CON group, the PSO group exhibited enhanced Gal-3 fluorescence signals and significantly increased numbers of Iba1/Gal-3 double-positive cells in all examined regions (Fig. 8). Specifically, the numbers of Iba1/Gal-3 double-positive cells were significantly increased in the CA1 and CA3 regions (P < 0.05; Fig. 8A, B), as well as in the DG region and prefrontal cortex (P < 0.001; Fig. 8C, D).

GFAP/Gal-3 double-positive cell numbers did not differ significantly between the CON and PSO groups in any examined region (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S1). NeuN/Gal-3 double-positive cell numbers also did not differ significantly between groups in the CA1 and CA3 regions (both P > 0.05), but were significantly higher in the DG region and prefrontal cortex of the PSO group (both P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S2). Across the examined regions, GFAP/Gal-3 and NeuN/Gal-3 double-positive cells appeared less frequent than Iba1/Gal-3 double-positive cells. This descriptive observation suggests that Gal-3 immunoreactivity was more frequently detected in Iba1-positive microglia, while colocalization with GFAP-positive astrocytes and NeuN-positive neurons was also observed.

Prenatal stress reduced NeuN expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring

To determine whether prenatal stress was associated with altered neuronal marker expression in the offspring brain, Western blotting was performed to assess NeuN protein expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring. The results showed that, compared with the control group, NeuN protein expression was markedly decreased in the hippocampus of offspring from the stress group (P < 0.05, Fig. 9A, B). In the prefrontal cortex, NeuN protein expression was also significantly lower in the stress group than in the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 9C, D). These findings suggest that prenatal stress may adversely affect neuronal status in both brain regions, as reflected by reduced NeuN protein expression, although this finding alone does not confirm neuronal death or loss.

Discussion

Prenatal stress induces cognitive dysfunction in offspring

Prenatal stress has been recognized as an important adverse intrauterine factor affecting cognitive development in offspring. Both clinical studies and basic experimental research have demonstrated that maternal exposure to stress during pregnancy can exert long-term and persistent effects on the offspring’s cognitive function. Population-based studies have confirmed that the degree of maternal stress during pregnancy is significantly associated with reduced cognitive ability and abnormal structural alterations in the hippocampus of offspring [25]. In animal models, prenatal stress not only causes postnatal growth and developmental retardation in offspring, but also markedly impairs spatial learning and memory [26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that prenatal stress may exert long-lasting, potentially irreversible adverse effects on offspring’s cognitive function by disrupting the normal development and maturation of brain regions involved in cognition, such as the hippocampus.

To verify the effects of prenatal stress on offspring cognitive function, pregnant rats in the present study were subjected to restraint stress from gestational day 15 to gestational day 21, and behavioral tests were subsequently performed to assess cognitive function after the offspring reached adulthood. The results showed that offspring in the stress group exhibited more pronounced anxiety-like behaviors in the open-field test and elevated plus maze test. In addition, in the Y-maze test, which was used to evaluate working memory, and in the novel object recognition test, which was used to assess recognition-related learning and memory, offspring in the stress group showed significantly reduced exploration time and distance in the novel arm, as well as a lower novel object recognition index, compared with the control group. These results suggest that prenatal stress not only disrupts the regulation of anxiety-related emotional behaviors in offspring but also impairs their learning and cognitive function. Cognitive function depends on the integrity of neurons in brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, as well as the connectivity and activity of these neuronal circuits [27]. Therefore, prenatal stress may affect these functionally relevant brain regions and consequently contribute to cognitive dysfunction.

Prenatal stress induces neuroinflammation in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus of offspring

Dysregulation of the neuroendocrine–immune axis is a central mechanism through which prenatal stress impairs cognitive function in offspring. The present findings confirm that stress responses activate the maternal HPA axis and elevate glucocorticoid levels in dams, an effect that may persist into adulthood in offspring [28]. Prolonged exposure to excessive glucocorticoids can prime microglia toward a pro-inflammatory state, characterized by excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and an increased number of activated microglia [29–31].

In the present study, CRH and CORT levels were markedly increased in the peripheral blood of both stressed pregnant rats and their adult offspring. These findings indicate that prenatal stress activates the HPA axis and promotes glucocorticoid release, and that the resulting endocrine disturbance may be transmitted from the mother to the offspring and persist into adulthood. Meanwhile, the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, were significantly increased in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring, accompanied by activation-associated morphological changes in Iba1-positive microglia. Consistent with these morphological findings, the numbers of Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells were also significantly increased in both brain regions, providing additional marker-based evidence of enhanced microglial activation. Together, these findings suggest that prenatal stress-induced neuroendocrine dysregulation is associated with local neuroinflammatory responses in the offspring brain.

Gal-3 has been implicated as a microglia-associated molecule in neuroinflammation. In ischemic brain injury models, Gal-3 is upregulated in microglia and contributes to microglial activation and proliferation [14, 17], whereas Gal-3 deficiency attenuates pathological responses in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [32]. Microglia-secreted Gal-3 has also been shown to act as a TLR4 ligand in experimental neuroinflammation and ischemia models [18]. In Alzheimer’s disease, Gal-3 is increased in the brains of patients and 5×FAD mice and is associated with plaque-associated microglia [33]; Gal-3 has also been shown to promote Aβ oligomerization and toxicity in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model [34].

The whole-tissue qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated an overall increase in Gal-3 expression but did not identify its cellular source. The colocalization analysis showed that prenatal stress increased the numbers of Iba1/Gal-3 double-positive cells in all examined hippocampal subregions and in the prefrontal cortex. In contrast, GFAP/Gal-3 double-positive cell numbers remained unchanged in all examined regions, whereas NeuN/Gal-3 double-positive cells increased only in the DG region and prefrontal cortex. Moreover, GFAP/Gal-3 and NeuN/Gal-3 double-positive cells were observed substantially less frequently than Iba1/Gal-3 double-positive cells. These findings support a predominantly microglia-associated pattern of Gal-3 immunoreactivity in this model, while indicating that Gal-3 is not restricted to microglia and may also be present in astrocytes and neurons.

NF-κB is an important regulator of inflammatory responses and can promote the expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines [35]. Previous studies conducted in other experimental contexts have suggested a possible relationship between Gal-3 and NF-κB signaling. In an LPS-induced neuroinflammation model, Chen et al. reported that Gal-3 knockdown reduced NF-κB activation and its pro-inflammatory effects [19]. Similarly, in a periodontitis model, Gal-3 knockdown reduced LPS-induced inflammatory cytokine expression and NF-κB activation [36]. NF-κB activation has also been observed following restraint stress in rats [37].

In the present study, Gal-3 expression was significantly increased in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of offspring exposed to prenatal stress, accompanied by increased total NF-κB p65 protein expression. These concurrent changes, considered alongside previous experimental evidence, suggest that Gal-3 may be linked to NF-κB-associated inflammatory responses in this model. Further interventional studies are required to determine whether Gal-3 functionally regulates NF-κB signaling under prenatal stress conditions.

Although equal numbers of male and female offspring were included in each group, data were pooled across sexes because each sex-by-treatment subgroup contained only three animals. Thus, the study was not adequately powered to assess sex-specific effects, and potential sex-dependent responses to prenatal stress cannot be excluded.

Taken together, prenatal restraint stress was associated with HPA-axis dysfunction in both dams and offspring. Glucocorticoids may cross the placental barrier and affect fetal brain development; in the present study, prenatal stress was accompanied by increased pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and elevated total NF-κB p65 protein expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of adult offspring. These changes occurred together with activation-associated morphological changes in Iba1-positive microglia, increased numbers of Iba1/CD68 double-positive cells, increased Gal-3 expression, reduced NeuN protein expression, and behavioral impairments. Collectively, these findings suggest that increased Gal-3 expression may participate in microglia-associated neuroinflammatory responses and may contribute to anxiety-like behaviors and cognitive dysfunction following prenatal stress.
