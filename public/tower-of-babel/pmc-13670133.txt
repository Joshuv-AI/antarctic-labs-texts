Telepharmacological Consultation for Polypharmacy Management in Primary Care: A Qualitative Study of General Practitioners' Experiences With Cross‐Sectoral Coordination
Authors: Daniel Pilsgaard Henriksen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe telepharmacological outpatient clinic (TPOC) is a hospital service in which clinical pharmacologists and clinical pharmacists review a patient's full medication regimen and hospital record, then discuss it with the referring general practitioner (GP) by video conference. Seven GPs in the Region of Southern Denmark who had used or been introduced to the service were interviewed to explore how it was used and what prevented wider use. Interviews were semi‐structured and analysed thematically. GPs described practices organised around single diagnoses, with no separate pathway for patients who are difficult because they are complex. This was the setting the service entered. Four themes were identified. Whether the service was used depended on integration into existing clinical software. Its value reached past the referred patient, as GPs applied what they had learned to patients never referred. GPs described a coordination gap for patients attending several outpatient clinics, where no clinician held an overview of the whole treatment. They wanted the intensity of the service matched to the complexity of the case. GPs who wanted to refer more often pointed to awareness and time pressure, not to doubts about the service. Uptake appears to depend on workflow integration.

Introduction

The ageing of European populations has increased the prevalence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In Denmark, nearly half of adults aged 65 years and older transition into polypharmacy over a 5‐year period, and polypharmacy is associated with a nearly four‐fold increased mortality risk as well as elevated rates of hospitalisation, adverse drug reactions and falls [6, 7, 8, 9]. Disease‐specific guidelines frequently conflict in these patients and add to the treatment burden [10, 11].

GPs coordinate the care of these patients, increasingly without formal support, as specialists and hospital departments transfer medication management and coordination tasks to primary care [12]. Time pressure is a consistent occupational stressor in general practice and is associated with burnout across high‐income countries [13, 14]. Care is at the same time fragmented across specialist outpatient clinics, and fragmentation increases with morbidity level and is associated with potentially inappropriate medication and elevated mortality [15]. Specialist outreach models improve communication and access between GPs and specialists, though evidence for improved patient outcomes remains limited [16, 17, 18], and electronic consultation services have shown potential to increase GP knowledge through case‐based learning [19, 20, 21].

Telemedicine‐based specialist consultation is increasingly used to support GPs [22, 23]. Clinical pharmacologists are trained in the safe and cost‐effective use of medicines and are positioned to support GPs in managing complex polypharmacy [24, 25], and structured frameworks for specialist review of complex polypharmacy in primary care have been developed [26]. Clinical pharmacology has been an independent medical specialty in Denmark since 1996 [27], and Danish general practice operates on a list system with GPs as gatekeepers, funded by mixed capitation and fee‐for‐service [28, 29].

To address these challenges, a telepharmacological outpatient clinic (TPOC) was developed at Odense University Hospital. The service operates as a hospital outpatient clinic and requires a formal patient referral, but its output is not a conventional outpatient appointment. A clinical pharmacologist or clinical pharmacist reviews the patient's complete medication regimen together with the hospital record, prescription redemption history, diagnoses and previous treatment attempts. The review is then presented and discussed with the referring GP in a video consultation scheduled for 15–30 min, corresponding to one or two standard consultation slots in general practice. The patient may attend, but this is agreed between GP and patient and is not the default. After the consultation the GP receives a written summary containing the medication overview, identified interactions and adverse effect risks, prescription redemption data, and recommendations. Responsibility for acting on the recommendations remains with the GP.

The aim of this study was to explore GPs' experiences with the TPOC and their approaches to managing patients with multimorbidity and polypharmacy in primary care, with particular attention to organisational conditions required for effective cross‐sectoral collaboration.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A qualitative design with semi‐structured individual interviews was used to explore GPs' experiences with a TPOC and their approaches to managing multimorbidity and polypharmacy in primary care. The author used thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke to identify patterns of meaning across the dataset [30].

The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [31].

Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited from general practices in the Region of Southern Denmark. Eligible participants were GPs who had used or been introduced to the TPOC service, and were approached directly by the author. TPOC requires a formal patient referral but delivers specialist counselling directly to the GP via video conference, with optional patient attendance. The development, clinical setup and implementation of the TPOC have previously been described in detail [32]. Purposive sampling was used to ensure variation in practice size, geographic location and level of TPOC utilisation. Ten GPs were invited and seven participated in the study between March 2024 and January 2025. Sample size was guided by information power rather than a predetermined target [33]: The aim was narrowly defined, all participants had direct experience of the service, and interviews lasted 45–75 min. Data Collection

Individual interviews were conducted by the author, a senior consultant in clinical pharmacology with an established professional relationship with all participants. Most had referred patients to the service repeatedly. At the start of each recorded interview, the author stated this dual role directly, asked participants to answer as honestly as they could, and made clear that nothing said would affect the working relationship afterwards. All participants provided verbal informed consent at the same point. Interviews were conducted by telephone. A semi‐structured interview guide was developed covering three domains: (1) organisational approaches to multimorbidity management in general practice, (2) use of and perspectives on specialist consultation and cross‐sectoral collaboration and (3) experiences with TPOC including service design and future development. The guide included direct questions about barriers to use, about what participants would change if given a free hand, and about why colleagues did not use the service. The study was initiated as part of a service development and evaluation initiative, which facilitated recruitment and participant compensation. Data were stored on a secure server approved for research and quality development purposes by the Region of Southern Denmark. The full interview guide is provided in the supplementary appendix.

Analysis

Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed using AI‐assisted transcription software with subsequent manual review and correction by the author. AI‐based tools also assisted with language editing, but all analytical interpretations, coding decisions and conclusions represent the author's independent scholarly work. Thematic analysis followed the six‐phase process outlined by Braun and Clarke. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline was used to structure this manuscript [34], and the SRQR reporting checklist when editing, included in the supplementary appendix.

Rigour

All interviews were transcribed and read in full, and coding was carried out on the complete dataset once all seven interviews had been conducted. Coding was inductive and manual. Candidate themes were checked against the transcripts to confirm that each was supported across more than one interview, and material that did not fit was recoded or left out rather than fitted to an existing theme. Verbatim quotations are used throughout the Results so that the link between the data and the interpretation is visible to the reader. All coding and interpretation were done by the author alone. No second coder was involved, and no member checking was carried out.

Multimorbidity Management in General Practice: Fragmented Structures and Unmet Needs

The seven participants described different ways of organising the care of patients with multimorbidity, and the arrangements varied from practice to practice. These accounts are reported as the setting the service operates in, not as a survey of how such care is organised in Danish general practice. A common feature across all accounts was the central role of practice nurses in maintaining chronic disease follow‐up, with GPs describing a delegation model in which nurses managed structured annual reviews for defined patient groups, most commonly type 2 diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, while GPs were called upon for complex or deteriorating cases.

Several participants described systematic tools for identifying and prioritising patients with multimorbidity. One participant described a flagging system for patients designated as particularly vulnerable, referred to internally as ‘star patients’, ensuring consistent assignment to a named GP and nurse:

‘The secretary already knew from when the patient stood at the desk or called, this patient has this provider’ (GP7).A similar approach was described by another participant, who distinguished between clinical complexity and diagnostic load: ‘You don't have to be a complex patient just because you have diabetes, you have to be a complex patient because you are complex’ (GP6).

‘The secretary already knew from when the patient stood at the desk or called, this patient has this provider’ (GP7).

Others described floating notes positioned at the top of patient records containing an updated overview of diagnoses, medication rationale and scheduled follow‐up: ‘It floats on top, so even if I wrote it two years ago, it's always the note that comes up first’ (GP4).

Despite these efforts, participants consistently identified the absence of a dedicated organisational structure for patients with multimorbidity as a gap. Annual review systems were organised around single diagnoses rather than patient complexity, meaning that patients with multiple conditions were reviewed in separate disease‐specific encounters without a unifying clinical overview. As one participant noted:

‘There's no field in our annual review schedule called multimorbidity or polypharmacy, or vulnerable patients’ (GP7).The increasing complexity of patients presenting in general practice was attributed in part to earlier discharge from hospital and expanding outpatient activity, which transferred clinical responsibility to GPs without corresponding increases in capacity or support. One participant summarised the resulting experience: ‘The patients aren't getting easier and we're 10% fewer doctors than we were ten years ago’ (GP6).

These accounts describe the setting a service such as TPOC enters. Follow‐up is organised around single diagnoses; practice nurses carry much of the routine work, and there is no pathway for patients whose difficulty is that they are complex rather than that they have any particular condition. The themes that follow concern how a cross‐sectoral pharmacological service is used within that setting, and what limits its use.

Theme 1: Integration as a Structural Prerequisite

Participants consistently described structural and organisational factors as determinants of whether specialist consultation services were used in practice. Referring a patient to TPOC requires the GP to find an available appointment slot and send a referral. Where this could be done from within the practice record system the GP already had open, participants described it as unproblematic. Where it required opening a separate system, it competed with everything else in a consultation running to time. One participant summarised what determines whether a referral is made at all: ‘The fewer clicks, the greater the probability’ (GP1).

Beyond technical integration, visibility within established workflows was emphasised. Participants had learned that the service existed through colleagues rather than through any formal channel, and expected awareness to spread the same way: ‘The “jungle telegraph” has to work a bit, little by little, more and more people will find out that this [the service] is pretty good’ (GP5). Multiple participants confirmed that colleagues were unaware of the TPOC service despite its availability: ‘I don't think it's because people don't think it has a place. It's because we don't know it exists’ (GP4).

Integration in continuing medical education was proposed as a structural solution: ‘There's a course in end‐of‐life care and courses in all sorts of other things, so there should also be telepharmacological education’ (GP6).

Time pressure determined whether a referral was made at all, since referring requires an unhurried moment that is often not available. Several participants distinguished, however, between the time it takes to refer and the mental effort of working through a complex regimen alone. On the second, the service reduced the burden rather than adding to it. One GP described the difference between thinking a complex case through unaided and having it worked through with a specialist: ‘I think plenty in my daily work. With you, I don't have to think’ (GP7). Participants were aware that the service had spare capacity, having been told so during consultations, and one attributed the limited uptake to time rather than to a lack of need: ‘It's an expression of time pressure in general practice, not an expression of lack of need’ (GP3).

Booking System Usability as a Proxy for Structural Integration

Participants' accounts of booking system experiences illuminated the broader integration principle in concrete terms. Consultations were initially arranged through a shared calendar on a public web page, where GPs could see whether a slot was free or taken. The slot was then reserved either by stating it in the referral or by telephone. Participants contrasted this with booking from within the software they already had open: ‘I have no doubt whatsoever that it is a barrier, having to go and look in a Google calendar’ (GP6).

Integration within the practice software system was valued both for reducing friction and for enabling automatic transfer of patient identification data, which several participants framed as a patient safety consideration. Participants identified several specific design features as facilitating or impeding referral: appointment slot intervals matching standard practice scheduling structures, visibility of the referral send‐button and a toggle function specifying patient presence with automatic notification. One participant suggested automatic population of the booked appointment into the GP's own calendar.

Several participants noted that a fully integrated booking system could be operated by practice secretaries, removing the referral step from the GP's workflow entirely: ‘You could easily imagine delegating it, the system just needs to be where we already are’ (GP4).

Theme 2: Value Beyond the Individual Patient

Participants described the value of TPOC as extending substantially beyond the immediate clinical question. A consistent pattern of transferable learning was identified, with participants describing how clinical pharmacological insights gained through TPOC consultations were subsequently applied to other patients. As one participant described: ‘I use it just as much as education as I use it for the specific patient case’ (GP6).

Participants named what they had taken from the consultations. Interactions were mentioned most often, and one participant recalled a specific interaction pattern by name: ‘That's what's so good, learning about the interactions. What was the triple whammy? All those things’ (GP7).

Another described anticholinergic burden as a gap in general practice knowledge that the consultations had helped close: ‘I think a lot of GPs could learn a great deal about anticholinergic burden. I've heard about it many times by now, but I don't think it's something we know much about’ (GP6).

Prescription redemption data were valued because they showed whether a drug was actually being collected, which several participants said they did not check routinely, and which sometimes made the decision to stop a drug straightforward: ‘Lansoprazole, for example, hasn't been collected. So what is she supposed to have it for? Then you might as well stop it’ (GP7).

The comprehensive patient review provided through TPOC was valued as a resource that general practice lacked the capacity to produce independently. One participant articulated this as filling a structural gap in professional networks: ‘We had no “grandfather”, someone with more experience you could just ask. Sometimes we just stood there thinking, what do we do now?’ (GP5).

Each referral concerns one patient, and the written summary the GP receives afterwards documents the review and the advice given for that patient. Several participants used it well beyond that. They copied sections into the patient's floating note, a note held permanently at the top of the record so that it appears first regardless of when it was written, forwarded relevant passages to community nurses, and kept the interaction summaries as reference material when meeting similar combinations in other patients.

Theme 3: The Coordination Gap in Multimorbidity Care

Participants described a structural gap in the management of patients with multimorbidity attending multiple specialist outpatient clinics simultaneously. No single clinician held an overview of the patient's complete treatment trajectory, and the GP was frequently left as the de facto coordinator without formal support. One participant described a patient attending eight different outpatient clinics: ‘You end up sitting a bit alone with it, because it's only me who knows what all the others are doing’ (GP4). Another framed the challenge as a question of ownership: ‘Who is it that actually takes the lead?’ (GP5).

Participants described TPOC as covering ground that other specialist services did not. Unlike organ‐specific services, telepharmacological consultation was experienced as capable of holding the complexity of multimorbidity across diagnostic categories: ‘I don't experience anyone besides you who can contain the complexity of patients with multimorbidity’ (GP2).

The structural consequences of inadequate coordination were described concretely. One participant described a patient whose rate‐controlling medication had been discontinued during an orthopaedic admission without awareness of co‐existing conditions, resulting in uncontrolled atrial fibrillation at home. The GP related this as an example of a change made by one specialty without reference to the rest of the regimen and of being left to discover the consequence herself.

Theme 4: What Participants Asked For

Asked what they would change if given a free hand, participants described the same thing in different words: a service graded by the complexity of the case rather than offered in a single format.

For straightforward medication questions, written consultation without video was proposed as a time‐efficient alternative. A pharmacist‐led consultation was suggested for cases not requiring specialist physician input. For the most complex cases, participants envisioned multidisciplinary video conferences involving multiple specialists simultaneously: ‘You could make some kind of joint venture, how can we all get together and say, what is actually most important for Peter's quality of life?’ (GP4).

Proactive outreach was proposed to address the inverse care phenomenon [35], with TPOC identifying complex patients at hospital discharge and contacting the GP directly: ‘There must be many neglected patients, younger GPs would embrace this’ (GP3).

The role of non‐physician clinical staff as intermediaries was raised by one participant, describing a highly experienced practice nurse capable of independently identifying patients appropriate for TPOC referral: ‘She could easily do it, if she was just trained in what to write and what to look for’ (GP7).

Delegation was raised at two points: of the booking, where an integrated system would allow secretarial staff to handle it, and of the decision to refer, where an experienced nurse might identify suitable patients. Both would extend access without adding to GP workload, and both depend on the service being reachable from the systems the practice already uses.

Finally, participants consistently identified integration into GP specialist training as a prerequisite for systematic uptake: ‘That's where we change our habits, when we're young’ (GP6).

Discussion

This qualitative study explored GPs' experiences with a telemedicine‐based telepharmacological consultation service. GPs described structural and organisational integration as a prerequisite for service utilisation. Participants who described referring less often than they wanted to pointed to awareness and time pressure rather than to doubts about the service. The service generated value beyond the individual consultation through transferable learning and cognitive offloading. A structural coordination gap in multimorbidity care was identified, in which GPs assumed de facto coordination responsibility without formal support. Participants asked for a service graded by the complexity of the case.

Participants distinguished between knowing that a service exists and being able to reach it from within their own workflow, and described both as necessary before it is used. The number of steps required to make a referral was described as decisive.

Booking system design illustrated this at the level of individual technical components. GPs experienced a meaningful qualitative difference between a system embedded in existing clinical software and one requiring navigation to an external platform. Automatic transfer of patient identification data, appointment slot intervals matching practice scheduling structures, and the potential for delegation to secretarial staff were all identified as design features with real impact on referral behaviour. Implementation efforts should attend to this level of technical detail with the same rigour applied to organisational and educational integration.

Informal professional networks were the primary dissemination mechanism, which is another way of saying that current communication strategies are insufficient. Several participants proposed a structural alternative: embedding telepharmacological consultation in postgraduate specialist training for GPs.

GPs applied pharmacological insights from TPOC consultations to patients who had never been referred. Referral counts therefore say little about what the service does, though this study cannot say how much further it reaches. Specialist outreach models have shown a similar effect on GP knowledge beyond the case at hand [16, 17, 18].

The cognitive offloading function matters for a different reason. In a primary care system under sustained workforce pressure, a service that reduces cognitive load without reducing clinical responsibility has value that standard economic analyses do not capture.

Patients with multimorbidity attend multiple outpatient clinics without a designated cross‐specialty coordinator, which causes treatment fragmentation, conflicting recommendations and medication errors [15]. GPs assume this role by default, without formal support to synthesise the specialist inputs. Because TPOC is not organ‐specific, it covers the regimen across diagnostic categories.

What participants asked for was consistent enough to be worth reporting, though it remains what they said they wanted rather than evidence of what would work. A graded service would extend access without taking more specialist time. And the patients least likely to be referred may well be the ones who would benefit most, which is the argument for outreach rather than waiting for referrals [35].

Strengths and Limitations

The sample spans GPs with more than 20 years in independent practice and GPs specialised within the last five, and includes both frequent referrers and GPs who had referred once or not at all. Interviews lasted 45–75 min, and participants spoke at length about a service provided by the interviewer, including its shortcomings.

The study has several limitations. GPs were recruited from a single region, limiting generalisability to areas with different primary care structures. Seven GPs cannot represent the range of practice organisation in Danish general practice, and the accounts of local multimorbidity management are reported as the context participants described, not as a survey of how such care is organised. The interviewer provided the TPOC service and was known to all participants, which introduces a risk of social desirability bias and means the data were interpreted by someone positioned inside the service. This familiarity also gave access to accounts that assumed shared knowledge of referral pathways, remuneration and record systems, and it is unlikely that an outside interviewer would have obtained the same detail at a single interview. The participating GPs freely shared critical views of both the service and the healthcare system, including delays in receiving the written report, the absence of remuneration for cross‐sectoral consultation, and the view that specialist advisory services move complex work into general practice without moving responsibility with it. Finally, the interviewed GPs all had prior TPOC experience, and GPs who have never used the service likely hold different views.

The analysis was carried out by a single researcher. Coding was not checked by a second analyst, and the interpretation therefore rests on one reading of the material.

Conclusions

This study describes how a group of GPs used a telepharmacological consultation service, and what determined whether they used it. Participants described the service as taking a mental load off them, as teaching them things they applied to other patients, and as covering the whole medication regimen in a way they did not experience elsewhere. These functions matter increasingly as primary care assumes growing responsibility for patients with complex medication regimens following earlier hospital discharge and expanding outpatient activity.

Realising this potential is not straightforward. Structural integration into existing clinical workflows appears to be a prerequisite for systematic uptake rather than an optional refinement. Participants applied what they learned to patients who were never referred, which suggests that the value of such a service is not captured by counting referrals, though this study cannot show how far it reaches. For patients attending multiple specialist clinics simultaneously, effective coordination requires an institutional home at the interface between hospital and primary care, not a peripheral add‐on bolted onto existing structures.

Whether these conditions can be met within current healthcare architectures remains an open question. What the data suggest is that voluntary individual uptake is an insufficient mechanism for realising the potential of cross‐sectoral pharmacological consultation, and that deliberate organisational design is the more promising path.
