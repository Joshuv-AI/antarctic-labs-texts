Impact of changes in metabolic health and obesity on incident urolithiasis: A nationwide cohort study
Authors: Won Ku Hwang, Tae Hyun Lee, Hyo Jong Kim, Seon Beom Jo, Jong Wook Kim, Mi Mi Oh, Hong Seok Park, Du Geon Moon, Sun Tae Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate whether time-varying changes in metabolic health and obesity status are associated with incident urolithiasis using a nationwide health-screening cohort.Materials and MethodsThe National Health Insurance Service-National Health Screening database was used for this study. The study cohort consisted of 514,866 health screening examinees between 2009 and 2010. Metabolic health was determined based on triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting glucose, blood pressure, and waist circumference criteria, while obesity was determined by body mass index. The participants were stratified into metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO); metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO); metabolically healthy obese (MHO); metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO). According to the second health screening, each group was divided according to changes in metabolic status and obesity. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 286,175 examinees who contributed 1,958,405 person-years of follow-up were eligible for this cohort study, during which 12,403 subjects developed urolithiasis. Compared with MHNO individuals, aHRs for incident urolithiasis were higher in MUO, MHO, and MUNO groups. Persistent obesity or metabolic unhealthiness was associated with higher aHRs, whereas improvement in either status was associated with lower aHRs.ConclusionsWe found that aHRs for incident urolithiasis were similarly elevated in metabolically healthy and unhealthy obese individuals compared with MHNO individuals, indicating that excess adiposity may be a key driver of stone risk. Maintaining metabolic health and normal weight or improving metabolic health and reducing weight may lead to decreased risk of urolithiasis development.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Obesity, Urolithiasis

INTRODUCTION

Urolithiasis is a common and significant health problem characterized by flank pain, nausea, and hematuria. The incidence, prevalence, and economic burden of urolithiasis are escalating. In South Korea, a cohort study for 11 years shows annual incidence of urolithiasis increased every year [1]. Various factors contribute to the occurrence of urolithiasis, including environmental factors, occupation, nutritional status, and metabolic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and obesity [2,3].

Metabolic health, which encompasses glucose and lipid metabolism, blood pressure (BP) regulation, adipose distribution, and systemic inflammation, plays a pivotal role in shaping an individual’s susceptibility to various health conditions [4]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS), a complex and interrelated condition of metabolic health, has emerged as a significant concern for both public health and clinical practice [5]. Numerous studies have unveiled associations between metabolic health status and the incidence of various diseases. From a urological perspective, accumulating evidence suggests that metabolic health and obesity are associated with diverse urological conditions, ranging from lower urinary tract symptoms such as nocturia to prostate cancer [6,7].

Increasing evidence now indicates that MetS and its individual components are linked to urolithiasis risk. Chang et al. [8] reported that the presence of MetS increased the hazard of incident kidney stones by approximately 24%, and analysis from the UK Biobank similarly demonstrated that the partial traits of MetS were independently associated with urolithiasis [9]. Obesity in particular appears to be a dominant determinant, independent of metabolic abnormalities and insulin resistance [10,11].

The limitation of previous studies is that these studies have treated metabolic status and obesity as fixed conditions, overlooking their dynamic nature. In reality, metabolic profiles shift with lifestyle modification, medication use, and aging. In this point of view, recent studies have focused on looking at the relationship between changes in metabolic health and accompanying diseases, such as cardiovascular disease [12].

In this context, our study aims to elucidate the association between changes in metabolic health status and the incidence of urolithiasis. Using data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS)-National Health Screening database, we seek to dissect these relationships, identify potential risk factors, and explore the impact of variations in metabolic health on urolithiasis development. By unraveling the intricacies of this relationship, our research aims to contribute valuable insights to the broader understanding of urological health and pave the way for more targeted approaches in urolithiasis prevention and management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (approval number: 2022GR0014). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data.

1. Data source and study population

The NHIS in Korea consists of a set of data about approximately 50 million Koreans [13]. Through standardized biannual questionnaires and health examination data, information on the continuous health status of the population could be obtained. The information includes personal information (height, weight, gender, age, socio-economic data, etc.) and medical information (medical history, family history, medication history, etc.). In addition, information on an individual’s disease and medication status can also be obtained based on the ICD-10-CM (the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification). Subjects were selected using stratified random sampling to ensure that the sample was representative of the entire population.

The NHIS-National Health Screening database was used for this study (approval number: NHIS-2022-2-156). Among 514,866 individuals screened between 2009 and 2010, urolithiasis was defined using ICD-10 diagnosis codes: N20.0 (calculus of kidney), N20.1 (calculus of ureter), N20.2 (calculus of kidney with calculus of ureter), and N20.9 (urinary calculus, unspecified).

A total of 228,691 individuals were excluded, including those with any urolithiasis diagnosis recorded between 2002 and 2008 (n=23,851), those with missing values in health screening data (n=157,844), those with impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or less) (n=45,108), and those with other comorbidities (n=1,888); polycystic kidney disease (Q61.0, Q61.1, Q61.2, Q61.8, Q61.9), kidney dysgenesis (Q60.0, Q60.2, Q60.3, Q60.5, Q61.4, Q61.5), congenital kidney deformity (N13.0, N13.1, N13.7, N13.8, Q62.0, Q62.1, Q62.2, Q62.3, Q62.5, Q62.7, Q62.8), renal tumor (C64, C65), or kidney transplantation (Z94.0) (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of study subjects. eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate.

Each participant underwent health screening examinations twice: initial status examined in 2009–2010, and follow-up status examined in 2011–2012. Based on the results of initial and second health screening, the cohorts were divided according to changes in obesity and metabolic health status. Incident urolithiasis was tracked from 2013 to 2020.

2. Definition of metabolic health and obesity

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters. Participants were divided into the obese (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) and non-obese (BMI <25 kg/m2) groups, in accordance with the World Health Organization guidelines [14].

Metabolic health was defined according to the modified criteria by the National Cholesterol Education Program – Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) [5]. Under these criteria, participants were classified as metabolically unhealthy if they met three or more of the following components: triglyceride level ≥150 mg/dL; high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol level <40 mg/dL; fasting glucose level ≥100 mg/dL (or taking anti-diabetic medications); BP ≥130/85 mmHg (or taking antihypertensive drugs); or waist circumference (WC) ≥90 cm, according to the Asian-specific WC cut-off.

Blood samples were drawn after an overnight fast and measured for serum levels of glucose, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL (low-density lipoprotein), and creatinine. Systolic and diastolic BP measurements were taken in a seated position after 5 minutes of rest. Smoking, drinking, exercise, and socio-economic status data were collected by self-report questionnaire. eGFR was calculated using the modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) equation based on serum creatinine, age, and sex, as applied in this NHIS health screening program.

Subjects were stratified into four groups: metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO), metabolically healthy obese (MHO), metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO), and metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO).

To examine changes, participants were classified according to transitions between the two exams. For obesity, transitions were defined as NONO (persistent non-obesity), NOO (development of obesity), ONO (resolution of obesity), or OO (persistent obesity). For metabolic health, transitions were categorized as NMNM (remaining healthy), NMM (becoming unhealthy), MNM (recovering to healthy), or MM (remaining unhealthy).

3. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.3 (SAS Institute). All values in this study were expressed as the mean±standard deviations and proportions for continuous and categorical variables. A multivariate-adjusted Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between urolithiasis and metabolic health status. Age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, exercise, economic status, and eGFR were adjusted. Hypertension and diabetes mellitus were not additionally adjusted for because they were included as components of metabolic health status, and further adjustment could result in overadjustment and attenuation of the associations of interest. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 286,175 examinees who contributed 1,958,405 person-years of follow-up were eligible for this cohort study. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the study population. The number of MHNO, MUNO, MHO, MUO were 161,546 (56.5%), 27,587 (9.6%), 55,529 (19.4%), and 41,513 (14.5%), respectively. Mean BMI (kg/m2) was 22.22±1.84, 23.07±1.54, 26.74±1.66, 27.57±2.09 in the MHNO, MUNO, MHO, and MUO groups, respectively. As expected, BMI and WC were highest in the obese groups (MHO and MUO), whereas systolic and diastolic BP, fasting plasma glucose, and total cholesterol levels were higher in the metabolically unhealthy phenotypes (MUNO and MUO) than in the metabolically healthy phenotypes (MHNO and MHO) (all p<0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide longitudinal cohort study of metabolic health status and urolithiasis, we observed a significant correlation between changes in metabolic health and the incidence of urolithiasis. First, after adjustment for age and other covariates, we found that obese phenotypes—both MHO and MUO—had similarly elevated aHRs for incident urolithiasis compared with MHNO individuals. The point estimate was numerically highest in the MHO group, followed by MUO and MUNO, suggesting that excess adiposity exerts a strong influence even among metabolically healthy individuals. In age-adjusted analyses in our cohort, both higher BMI and elevated WC were among the strongest predictors of incident urolithiasis (Table 3). Because BMI and WC are correlated measures of adiposity, our models cannot completely disentangle their independent effects, and the observed associations should be interpreted as reflecting the combined influence of overall and central obesity. Second, in subgroup analysis, changes over time substantially modify risk: participants who developed obesity or metabolic unhealth had higher incidence, while those who lost weight or regained metabolic health showed reduced risk, supporting the modifiability of stone formation through metabolic improvement.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies linking MetS components and urolithiasis. Rahman et al. [15] showed that MetS was associated with an increased risk of nephrolithiasis with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.769 (95% CI 1.386–2.309). The OR of each MetS traits of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus and obesity were 1.613 (95% CI 1.213–2.169), 1.586 (95% CI 1.007–2.502), 1.552 (95% CI 1.027–2.344), and 1.531 (95% CI 1.099–2.109), respectively. West et al. [16] reported that the presence of 2 or more MetS traits significantly increased the odds of nephrolithiasis. The presence of four or more traits was associated with an approximate 2-fold increase in odds of nephrolithiasis. Fu et al. [17] reported that the risk of severe urolithiasis was associated with MetS grade.

Obesity, one of the components of MetS, itself is well known risk factor of urolithiasis. Taylor et al. [18] reported that obesity increases the risk of kidney stone formation, with the risk ratio for men with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or greater compared to those with a BMI of 21 to 22.9 kg/m2 was 1.33 (95% CI 1.08–1.63). Cohort-study conducted by Kim et al. [10] showed that obesity was associated with a higher incidence of nephrolithiasis, with or without MetS. Obesity was positively associated with an increased risk of incident nephrolithiasis in dose-response manner, with stronger association in metabolically healthy individuals.

The pathophysiological association between urolithiasis and MetS and obesity remains unclear. One important hypothesis is that MetS causes changes in urine composition, resulting in the presence of urolithiasis. Cho et al. [19] reported that the presence of MetS was associated with a 93% increased OR of uric acid calculi compared with metabolic healthy, suggesting that MetS altered urine composition to promote urine calculi formation. Another study examined the relationship between hypertension and urine compositions shows that stone formers with hypertension excreted 25.6 mg per day more urine calcium, corresponding to a 12% increase in urinary calcium excretion compared to normotensive stone formers [20]. This change in urine composition explains the association between MetS and urolithiasis.

Insulin resistance has also been considered one of the mechanisms explaining the correlation between metabolic health and urolithiasis. Insulin resistance is a condition of impaired insulin action by impaired tissue response to insulin and excessive insulin production, forming a vicious circle [21]. Increased serum insulin level is associated with changes in urine composition, such as increased urinary calcium excretion and decreased urinary citrate excretion. Metabolic acidosis resulting from insulin resistance also causes low urine pH, favoring uric acid stone formation.

Suen et al. [22] showed that increased levels of IL-8 in the urine were associated with presentation of urinary stone. Also, it is known that inflammatory diseases such as chronic kidney disease or recurrent urinary tract infection are closely associated with urolithiasis. From this point of view, chronic inflammation is considered a factor explaining the association between urolithiasis and MetS.

Other metabolic components such as hypertension and dyslipidemia have also been implicated in urolithiasis. Hypertension has been linked to stone formation through increased urinary calcium excretion, likely resulting from altered renal sodium handling and pressure-related tubular dysfunction, leading to hypercalciuria [23]. Hypertension also shares common metabolic pathways with obesity and MetS, including insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation, indirectly contribute to a lithogenic urinary environment [24]. Dyslipidemia has been proposed to influence stone formation through the renal lipotoxicity hypothesis, whereby lipid accumulation and lipid-induced oxidative stress impair tubular function and promote crystal adherence [24]. However, compared with obesity, the effects of hypertension and dyslipidemia on stone formation may be less pronounced because obesity represents an upstream driver of multiple metabolic abnormalities and exerts more direct effects on urinary composition, including reduction in urine pH and citrate excretion and increases in lithogenic solutes. In contrast, hypertension and dyslipidemia may reflect downstream manifestations of metabolic dysfunction, with their associations with urolithiasis being partially mediated by obesity and insulin resistance.

As mentioned above, previous studies suggested that metabolic disturbances may differentially influence stone composition. Obesity has been more strongly associated with uric acid stone formation, primarily through persistently acidic urine [18,25]. Other metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance and hypertension, may induce a urinary environment favorable for stone formation through impaired renal tubular function, leading to increased urinary excretion of calcium and oxalate, along with reduced urinary citrate levels and lower urinary pH. These alterations collectively increase urinary supersaturation and favor the formation of calcium oxalate stones [24]. Although stone composition data were not available in the present study, these previously described, stone type-specific mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the observed associations between metabolic health status, obesity, and incident urolithiasis.

Taken together, these findings support an integrated pathophysiological framework in which obesity and adverse metabolic profiles are closely linked to insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and alterations in urinary composition, all of which contribute to stone formation. Although these mechanisms could not be directly evaluated in the present study, our results are consistent with prior experimental and clinical evidence suggesting that urolithiasis is a manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction rather than a purely local urological condition.

From a clinical point of view, the implications are significant. In those at risk of stones, routine metabolic tests should be emphasized, and counseling on weight control, hydration, and dietary control should extend beyond traditional stone formers to patients with metabolic abnormalities or obesity. Because the metabolic and obesity phenotypes are dynamic, periodic re-evaluation can identify patients at increased risk of benefiting from early intervention. A multidisciplinary approach combining urology, endocrinology, and preventive medicine can optimize outcomes.

This study has several strengths, including its nationally representative sample, longitudinal follow-up with repeated measurements, and adjustment for lifestyle factors. However, several limitations are also present. BMI does not fully reflect the heterogeneity of obesity, as it cannot distinguish between lean and fat mass or capture differences in fat distribution, such as visceral versus subcutaneous adiposity, which may have distinct metabolic implications. Consequently, some degree of adiposity misclassification cannot be excluded. Future studies incorporating complementary measures of adiposity may provide a more refined assessment of obesity-related risk. Residual confounding by individual metabolic components cannot be completely excluded, as detailed information on sex-specific factors, dietary factors, medication use, and differences in healthcare utilization were not fully captured or evaluated in the present analysis. Because the NHIS database does not provide information on imaging confirmation or stone composition, the proportion of imaging-confirmed stone events could not be determined and associations between metabolic health status and specific stone types could not be directly evaluated, raising the possibility of some degree of outcome misclassification. Because metabolic health and obesity status were measured at only two discrete time points during the early follow-up period (2009–2010 and 2011–2012), subsequent changes in these parameters during the extended follow-up could not be captured. Thus, the observed patterns primarily reflect early metabolic transitions rather than sustained long-term trajectories, and more nuanced information on the duration or continuity of metabolic health was not available because we dichotomized metabolic status into “healthy” and “unhealthy” categories. Nevertheless, the finding that even short-term changes over a two-year interval were associated with future urolithiasis risk suggests that early improvement in adiposity and metabolic profile may have clinically meaningful benefits for stone prevention. Since metabolic health status was divided into only healthy and unhealthy, the effect on the continuity of metabolic health was overlooked. We were unable to fully separate the independent effects of hypertension and diabetes from metabolic health because these conditions were incorporated into the metabolic health definition, and some residual confounding by their severity or duration may persist. Finally, generalizability to other ethnicities may be limited. Future research should integrate claims data with imaging studies, 24-hour urine analysis data, and dietary data. Also, by checking the change in stone composition according to the change pattern of metabolic health, we can confirm the effect of MetS on stone formation. Interventional trials using metabolic therapies could clarify whether improving metabolic health truly reduces stone incidence.

Our findings support the concept that urolithiasis is closely linked to systemic metabolic health, rather than being a purely local urological condition. It should be noted that improving these metabolic conditions can lower the risk. Thus, stone prevention should be reframed as a long-term metabolic health strategy.

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide cohort, excess adiposity and metabolic unhealthiness were both associated with an increased risk of incident urolithiasis. Obese phenotypes (both metabolically healthy and unhealthy obese) showed similarly elevated aHRs compared with MHNO individuals, suggesting that overall and central obesity play a central role in stone formation. Moreover, early improvements in obesity and metabolic health status over a two-year interval were associated with partial risk reduction, supporting the importance of early metabolic intervention in the prevention of urolithiasis.
