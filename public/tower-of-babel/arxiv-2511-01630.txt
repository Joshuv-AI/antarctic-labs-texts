2D or not 2D:
a “holographic dictionary” for Lowest Landau Levels

Abstract
We consider two-dimensional fermions on a plane with a perpendicular magnetic field, described by Landau levels. It is well-known that, semiclassically, restriction to the lowest Landau levels (LLL) amounts to imposing two constraints on a 4D phase space, which transforms the 2D coordinate space ( x , y ) (x,y) into a 2D phase space, thanks to the non-zero Dirac bracket between x x and y y . A straightforward application of Dirac’s prescription of quantizing LLL in terms of L 2 L^{2} functions of x x (or of y y ) fails because the wavefunctions are clearly functions of x x and y y . We find it possible, however, to construct a different 1D QM, sitting differently inside the 2D QM, which describes the LLL physics. The construction includes an exact 1D-2D correspondence between the fermion density ρ ⁡ ( x , y ) \rho(x,y) and the Wigner distribution of the 1D QM. In an appropriate large N N limit, (a) the Wigner distribution is upper bounded by 1, reflecting the semiclassical intuition that a phase space cell can have at most one fermion (Pauli exclusion principle) and (b) the 1D-2D correspondence becomes an identity transformation. (a) and (b) then imply an upper bound for the fermion density ρ ⁡ ( x , y ) \rho(x,y) which verifies known facts from LLL physics. We also explore the entanglement entropy (EE) of subregions of the 2D noncommutative space which displays behaviour distinct from conventional 2D systems as well as from conventional 1D systems, falling somewhere between the two. The main distinguishing feature of the EE, which is directly attributable to the noncommutative nature of space, is the absence of a logarithmic dependence on the size of the entangling region, even though there is a Fermi surface. In this paper, instead of working directly with the Landau problem, we consider a more general problem, namely 2D fermions in a rotating harmonic trap, which reduces to the Landau problem in a special limit. Among other consequences of the emergent 1D physics, we find that post-quench dynamics of the (generalized) LLL system is computed more simply in 1D terms, which is described by well-developed methods of 2D phase space hydrodynamics (see, e.g. [ 1 ] for a recent application).

C.1
The Weyl correspondence

Classically a particle moving in
R^{1}
is described by a 2D phase space
R^{2}
. Let us make a choice of canonical phase space coordinates
(x,p)
such that
\{x,p\}=1

, such that
[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=i\hbar

f(x,p)=xp
, classically the functions
xp,px
are the same, but the operators
\hat{x}\hat{p},\hat{p}\hat{x}
are different because they are ordered differently. The Weyl correspondence
[
16
]
assigns to the function

a unique operator
\hat{f}
and
vice versa
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{f}=\int{\frac{dx\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}}f(x,p)\hat{g}(x,p)

(C.1)

\displaystyle f(x,p)={\rm Tr}(\hat{f}\,\hat{g}(x,p))

(C.2)

where

\displaystyle\hat{g}(x,p)\equiv\int{\frac{d\alpha\,d\beta}{2\pi\hbar}}\exp\left[i/\hbar\left(\alpha(\hat{x}-x)+i\beta(\hat{p}-p)\right)\right]

(C.3)

Note that (
C.3
) is essentially like a delta function which indicates replacing
x,p
by
\hat{x},\hat{p}
, but with a well-defined ordering prescription. The consistency between (
C.1
) and (
C.2
) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\hat{g}(x,p)\hat{g}(x^{\prime},p^{\prime}))=2\pi\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(p-p^{\prime})

(C.4)

For the function
f(x,p)=xp
, the “Weyl-ordered” operator, as defined in (
C.1
), is the democratic choice
\hat{f}={\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x})
, as is straightforward to verify.

C.1.1
Invariance of the Weyl ordering prescription

Suppose we choose a different pair of phase space coordinates

\displaystyle{\tilde{\xi}}_{i}=M_{ij}\xi_{j}

where det

\displaystyle f(x,p)=xp=cs({\tilde{p}}^{2}-{\tilde{x}}^{2})+(c^{2}-s^{2}){\tilde{x}}{\tilde{p}}\equiv\tilde{f}({\tilde{x}},{\tilde{p}})

The Weyl ordered operators would be

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\xi}}_{i}=M_{ij}\hat{\xi}_{j}

(C.7)

Applying this fact, we find, by a straightforward calculation, that indeed

\displaystyle\hat{f}=\hat{\tilde{f}}

(C.8)

A general proof of (

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{\xi})

\displaystyle\equiv\int{\frac{d^{2}a}{2\pi}}\exp[ia_{i}(\hat{\tilde{\xi}}_{i}-{\tilde{\xi}}_{i})]

C.2
The Wigner distribution

The Wigner distribution for a general mixed state
\hat{\rho}
is defined as the classical phase space function, denoted
u(x,p)
, related by the Weyl correspondence to the state
\hat{\rho}
:

\displaystyle u(x,p)={\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}\,\hat{g}(x,p))

(C.14)

A pure state corresponds to
\hat{\rho}=\hat{P}_{\psi}\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, for which the Wigner distribution becomes

\displaystyle u(x,p)={\rm Tr}(\hat{P}_{\psi}\hat{g}(x,p))=\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\psi^{*}(x_{1})\psi(x_{2})\delta(x-{\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}})\exp[ip(x_{1}-x_{2})/\hbar]

(C.15)

The last expression comes by writing the Trace as

\int dx_{1}\,dx_{2}\,\langle x_{2}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|x_{1}\rangle\langle x_{1}|\hat{g}(x,p)|x_{2}\rangle

and noting that

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}|\hat{g}(x,p)|x_{2}\rangle=\delta(x-{\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}})\exp[ip(x_{1}-x_{2})/\hbar]

(C.16)

which is straightforward to derive by applying the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula to the exponential in
\hat{g}(x,p)
.

A more conventional expression for the Wigner distribution, equivalent to (
C.15
) is

\displaystyle u(x,p)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\eta,\psi^{*}(x+\eta/2)\psi(x-\eta/2)\exp[ip\eta/\hbar]

(C.17)

C.2.1
Properties of the Wigner distribution

It is easy to derive the main property of the Wigner distribution:

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\hat{f}|\psi\rangle=\int{\frac{dx\,dp}{2\pi\hbar}}u(x,p)f(x,p)

(C.18)

To see this, note that the LHS=
{\rm Tr}P_{\psi}\hat{f}
. By using the phase space representations the trace of operator products given in Table
1

The formula (

strictly
a phase space density since it is not positive definite; in fact the negativity of the Wigner distribution carries important information (related to the extent of non-classicality of the wavefunction). The marginal distributions are positive definite, however:

\displaystyle\int{\frac{dp}{2\pi\hbar}}u(x,p)=\rho(x)\equiv|\psi(x)|^{2}

(C.19)

\displaystyle\int dx\,u(x,p)=\tilde{\rho}(p)\equiv|\tilde{\psi}(p)|^{2}

E.1
Fermion density

In the large
N
limit, we claim that the ground state fermion density is given by:

\displaystyle\rho(x,y)=\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\theta(x^{2}+y^{2}-1),

(E.1)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function. The proof goes as follows:

The fermion density can be expressed as (using the form (
2.10
) of the wavefunctions
\psi_{n}(x,y)
) (using Mathematica)

\rho(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\psi^{\dagger}_{n}(x,y)\psi_{n}(x,y)=\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\frac{\Gamma(N,\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{\hbar})}{\Gamma(N)},

where
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
. Here the incomplete gamma function is defined as:

\Gamma(N,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-t}t^{N-1},\quad x=\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{\hbar}

To analyze the large
N
limit, it is useful to define
t=Ns,x=Ny
, and convert the above integral as

\Gamma(N,x)=N^{N}\int_{y}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-N\tilde{f}(s)},\quad\tilde{f}(s)=f(s)+\frac{1}{N}\log s\quad f(s)=s-\log s\quad y=\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{N\hbar}=r^{2}/r_{0}^{2},\;r_{0}^{2}={\frac{1}{m\omega}}

We will solve this integral using saddle point analysis. We Taylor expand
f(s)
as
f(s)=f(s_{0})+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(s_{0})}{2!}(s-s_{0})^{2}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(s_{0})}{3!}(s-s_{0})^{3}...
where
f^{\prime}(s)=0|_{s=s_{0}}
. For the case above,
s_{0}=1
. To evaluate this at large
N
, we need to consider the following two cases.

Case 1:
The saddle point
(s_{0}=1)
lies in between the integration limits i.e
s_{0}\in(y,\infty)\implies\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{N\hbar}\leq 1
. We use the scaling
s-1=\frac{u}{\sqrt{N}}
and express

\displaystyle N^{N}\int_{y}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-N\tilde{f}(s)}

\displaystyle=N^{N}\int_{y}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-Nf(s)}\frac{1}{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\;e^{-N\left(1+\frac{u^{2}}{2N}+\frac{u^{3}}{6N\sqrt{N}}\right)}\left(1-\frac{u}{\sqrt{N}}+\mathcal{O}(1/N)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{N}}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{2\pi}\left(1+O(1/N)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}N^{N}e^{-N}\left(1-O(1/N)\right)

(E.2)

Therefore, for
x<N

\Gamma(N,x)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}N^{N}e^{-N}\left(1-O(1/N)\right)

Case 2:

s_{0}
does not lies between integration limits
\implies\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{N\hbar}\geq 1

In the range
y<s<\infty,f(s)
is monotonically increasing. Hence the integral will be dominated by
f(s)|_{s=y}
. Therefore, for
x>N

\displaystyle\Gamma(N,x)\approx N^{N}e^{-Nf(y)}y^{-1}

(E.3)

The
Stirling’s approximation
of gamma function gives

\displaystyle\Gamma(N)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}N^{N}e^{-N}

(E.4)

We obtain:

\frac{\Gamma(N,x)}{\Gamma(N)}=\begin{cases}1+O(\frac{1}{N})&\text{if }x<N,\\
\sqrt{N}\exp\left(-N\left(\frac{x}{N}-\log(\frac{x}{N})-1\right)\right)x^{-1}\approx O(e^{-N})&\text{if }x\geq N.\end{cases}

We have used the fact that
f(y)>f(s_{0})=1
for the case of

. Thus, the fermion density is:

\rho(x,y)=\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\times\begin{cases}1-O(\frac{1}{N})&\text{if }\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{\hbar}<N,\\
\exp\left(-N\left(r^{2}-\log(r^2)-1\right)\right)r\approx O(e^{-N})&\text{if }\frac{m\omega r^{2}}{\hbar}\geq N.\end{cases}

which gives not only (

) but also the subleading corrections at large
