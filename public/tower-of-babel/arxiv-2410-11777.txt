Measure estimation on a manifold explored by a diffusion process
Vincent Divol, Hélène Guérin, Dinh-Toan Nguyen, Viet Chi Tran

Abstract
From the observation of a diffusion path ( X t ) t ∈ [ 0 , T ] (X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]} on a compact connected d d -dimensional manifold ℳ \mathcal{M} without boundary, we consider the problem of estimating the stationary measure μ \mu of the process. Wang and Zhu (2023) showed that for the Wasserstein metric 𝒲 2 \mathcal{W}_{2} and for d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 , the convergence rate of T − 1 / ( d − 2 ) T^{-1/(d-2)} is attained by the occupation measure of the path ( X t ) t ∈ [ 0 , T ] (X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]} when ( X t ) t ∈ [ 0 , T ] (X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]} is a Langevin diffusion. We extend their result in several directions. First, we show that the rate of convergence holds for a large class of diffusion paths, whose generators are uniformly elliptic. Second, the regularity of the density p p of the stationary measure μ \mu with respect to the volume measure of ℳ \mathcal{M} can be leveraged to obtain faster estimators: when p p belongs to a Sobolev space of order ℓ ⩾ 2 \ell\geqslant 2 , smoothing the occupation measure by convolution with a kernel yields an estimator whose rate of convergence is of order T − ( ℓ + 1 ) / ( 2 ℓ + d − 2 ) T^{-(\ell+1)/(2\ell+d-2)} . We further show that this rate is the minimax rate of estimation for this problem.

occupation measure, kernel estimator, density estimation, optimal transport, Wasserstein distance, Riemannian geometry.

Introduction

The manifold hypothesis has become ubiquitous in the modern machine learning landscape, where it is commonly used to explain the efficiency of nonparametric methods in high-dimensional statistical models
[
BCR+23
]
. This paradigm has motivated statisticians to study inference problems under manifold constraints
[
NSW08
,
GPPVW12b
,
AL18
,
AL19
,
Div21
,
PS22
]
. Given
n
i.i.d. samples from a distribution
\mu
supported by a
d
-dimensional manifold compact
\mathcal{M}
, the task of estimating either
\mu
or geometric quantities related to
\mathcal{M}
naturally arises. The picture is now well-understood. For example, minimax rates are known for the estimation of
\mathcal{M}
with respect to the Hausdorff distance
[
GPPVW12a
]
, for tangent space estimation
[
TVF13
,
AL19
]
, and for curvature estimation
[
AKC+19
,
AL19
,
BHHS22
,
ABL23
]
.
The estimation of the measure
\mu
has been tackled in a pointwise manner
[
BH21
]
, with respect to the Wasserstein distance
[
Div22
,
SAL24
]
, or with respect to more general adversarial losses
[
TY23
]
. Once again, minimax rates of estimation are known and are typically achieved by kernel-like estimators. Yet, the literature is far less abundant when we leave the i.i.d. setting.

However, a framework in which the data is generated through an exploration process is also natural. This setting is especially relevant in scenarios where the manifold is seen as the continuum limit of a large graph, the latter being explored by a random walk. We can think for instance of the famous PageRank algorithm
[
PBMW98
]
, but also of Respondent-Driven Sampling methods to discover hard-to-reach populations such as sexual communities or drug-users
[
Hec97
,
CDTV23
,
TV21
,
Vo20
]
. At the limit, this random walk converges to a continuous time diffusion exploring the manifold. Formally, we will consider that we have access to a sample path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
on
[0,T]
of a diffusion process on a submanifold
\mathcal{M}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, that is generated by a uniformly elliptic
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-differential operator
\mathcal{A}
, essentially self-adjoint with respect to some invariant measure
\mu
. Our goal is to propose reconstruction methods for the measure
\mu
based on the observation of the sample path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. Applications of these results could include asymptotic equivalence
[
DR06
]
, bias correction in sampling schemes based on random walks
[
TV21
]
or manifold regression
[
RR25
]
, for example.

The general framework we work in encapsulates in particular operators of the form
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
given for any test function
f
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
\mathcal{M}
by

\mathcal{A}_{pq}(f):=q\Delta f+{{\left\langle q\nabla\ln(pq),\nabla f\right\rangle}},

(1.1)

where
p,\ q\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
are two positive functions, with
p
being the density of the measure
\mu
with respect to the volume measure
\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathcal{M}
and
\nabla
and
\Delta
denote respectively the gradient and the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
(see e.g.
[
Wan14
]
).
When we take
q=\frac{p}{2}
, we recover the generator

\frac{p}{2}\Delta f+{{\left\langle\nabla p,\nabla f\right\rangle}}

studied in
[
CG22
,
GK06
,
GNT22
]
. When
q=1
, we recover a Langevin diffusion, whose generator
\mathcal{L}
is defined for any test function
f
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
\mathcal{M}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f):=

\displaystyle\Delta f+{{\left\langle\nabla\ln p,\nabla f\right\rangle}}=\Delta f+{{\left\langle\frac{\nabla p}{p},\nabla f\right\rangle}}.

(1.2)

Processes with this kind of generators can be obtained as the limits of random walks (without and with renormalization) visiting points sampled independently with identical distribution (i.i.d.)
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathcal{M}
, see e.g.
[
CG22
,
GK06
,
GNT22
]
.

In
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the question of estimating the invariant measure of a diffusion has been treated abundantly, see
[
Dal17
,
DM17
,
PP12
,
RT96
]
(notice that the problem could also be studied with the different point of view of non-parametric estimation for diffusion processes, see e.g.
[
DR07
]
).
For manifold-valued data, the problem of reconstructing the stationary measure
\mu
from a sample path was first addressed by Wang and Zhu
[
WZ23
]
for the generator (
1.2
). They consider the occupation measure
\mu_{T}
of the process, defined for every bounded measurable test function
f
by

\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)\mu_{T}(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{s})\ \mathrm{d}s.

(1.3)

Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
be the space of probability measures on the compact connected
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
. We introduce the
2
-Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, defined by

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}}\rho(x,y)^{2}\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\right)}}^{1/2},

where
\rho
is the geodesic distance on
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the set of measures on
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
with first marginal
\mu_{1}
and second marginal
\mu_{2}
. Wang and Zhu
[
WZ23
, Theorem 1.2]
showed that for the process with generator
\mathcal{L}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T},\mu)\right]}}\lesssim\begin{cases}T^{-1}&\mbox{ when }d\leq 3\\
T^{-1}\ln(1+T)&\mbox{ when }d=4\\
T^{-\frac{2}{d-2}}&\mbox{ when }d\geq 5,\end{cases}

(1.4)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
stands for the expectation taken from the diffusion process starting at
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
As noticed by Divol
[
Div22
]
, in the context of i.i.d. random variables
X_{1},\dots X_{n}
sampled from
\mu
on
\mathcal{M}
, the rate of convergence can be increased by smoothing the empirical measure. Our purpose here is to extend this result beyond the i.i.d. setting, by studying the convergence properties of an estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
of
\mu
, obtained by smoothing the occupation measure
\mu_{T}
with a kernel
K
of bandwidth
h>0
. When
d\geq 5
and for an appropriate choice of
h
, we obtain the rate of convergence

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu)\right]}}\lesssim T^{-\frac{2\ell+2}{2\ell+d-2}},

(1.5)

where
\mu
has a density of regularity
\ell\geq 2
. The above rate does not only hold for the Langevin diffusion with generator
\mathcal{L}
, but for all diffusion paths
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
whose generator
\mathcal{A}
is a uniformly elliptic
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-differential operator, essentially self-adjoint with respect to
\mu
. Furthermore,
we will show that these rates cannot be improved by providing minimax rates of convergence for this problem.

In Section
2
, we define the estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
and enounce precisely our main result. In Section
3
, we review some useful notions of Riemannian geometry. In Section
4
, we start the proof by treating the stationary case, i.e., when the initial measure of the SDE is
\mu
, and then give a generalization for a general initial measure.

2
Main results

We consider the framework described at the beginning of the paper.
Let
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{M}
with a positive density
p
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, and
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion on
\mathcal{M}
with generator
\mathcal{A}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})
is a
uniformly elliptic
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-differential operator of second order on
\mathcal{M}
, symmetric with respect to the measure
\mu
.

Notice that the operators
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
satisfy Assumption
2.1
as soon as
p
and
q
are of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
(see e.g.
[
Gri09
, Exercise 3.11, p. 69]
for smooth functions, which can be extended to the case where
p
and
q
are of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
).

The diffusion for
\mathcal{A}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
admits
\mu
as stationary measure. In the sequel, this generator is extended, as is usually done, to
L^{2}(\mu)
functions.
We introduce a concept closely associated with second-order differential operators, known as the
carré du champ

\Gamma(f,g)
for the operator
\mathcal{A}
:

\Gamma(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{A}(fg)-f\mathcal{A}(g)-g\mathcal{A}(f)\right)

(2.7)

Given that
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric with respect to
\mu
, for any smooth functions
f
and
g
, it follows that:

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(f,g)\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathcal{A}(g)\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{A}(f)g\mathrm{d}\mu.

(2.8)

Since
\mathcal{M}
is compact, from the regularity of
p
, there exist
p_{\min},\,p_{\max}>0
such that,

\forall x\in\mathcal{M},\quad p_{\min}\leq p(x)\leq p_{\max}.

(2.9)

Furthermore, the uniform ellipticity, the continuity of
\mathcal{A}
and the compactness of
\mathcal{M}
imply that
there exist positive constants
\kappa_{\min}
,
\kappa_{\max}
such that for all functions
f
,

\kappa_{\min}|\nabla f|^{2}\leq\Gamma(f,f)\leq\kappa_{\max}|\nabla f|^{2}.

(2.10)

We denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0}}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu_{0}}
the expectation and probability taken for the diffusion process with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
. When
\mu_{0}
is a Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
, with
x\in\mathcal{M}
, we will simply write
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{x}
.

Let
T>0
be a time horizon, and assume that we observe the diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the time window
[0,T]
. We consider the occupation measure
\mu_{T}
on
[0,T]
of the diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the positive measure defined by (
1.3
).
As explained in the introduction, the occupation measure can be seen as a first naive estimator of the measure
\mu
, that will be improved upon by convolution with a kernel
K_{h}
.

Let
K:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a (signed) Lipschitz-continuous function supported in
[0,1]
, with
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}K(\|u\|)\mathrm{d}u=1
.
We define for
h>0
small enough (see
Lemma

4.2

(ii)
) and
(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}^{2}
,

K_{h}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\eta_{h}(x)}K{{\left(\frac{{{\left\|x-y\right\|}}}{h}\right)}},

(2.11)

with
\displaystyle{\eta_{h}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K{{\left(\frac{{{\left\|x-y\right\|}}}{h}\right)}}\mathrm{d}y}
. We will show later (in
Lemma

4.2
) that
\eta_{h}>0
for
h
small enough, ensuring that the kernel
K_{h}
is well-defined. We consider the following estimator of the density
p
of
\mu
obtained by convolution of the occupation measure
\mu_{T}
with
K_{h}
. For
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle p_{T,h}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K_{h}(z,x)\mu_{T}(\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}K_{h}{{\left(X_{s},x\right)}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\eta_{h}(X_{s})}K{{\left(\frac{{{\left\|X_{s}-x\right\|}}}{h}\right)}}\mathrm{d}s.

(2.12)

Thanks to the definition of
\eta_{h}
, we notice that

\int_{\mathcal{M}}p_{T,h}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1.

However, the function
p_{T,h}
is not necessarily a density, as it may not be nonnegative everywhere (recall that
K
is a signed function). Still, we will show that the function
p_{T,h}
approximates the density
p
, and is therefore a density with high probability.
Let
x_{0}
be an arbitrary fixed point of
\mathcal{M}
. We introduce two random measures
\mu_{T,h}
, and
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
on
\mathcal{M}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{T,h}(\mathrm{d}x)=p_{T,h}(x)\mathrm{d}x,\text{ and }\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}=\begin{cases}\mu_{T,h}&\text{ if }\mu_{T,h}\text{ is a nonnegative measure,}\\
\delta_{x_{0}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.13)

The measure
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
is introduced for purely technical purposes. Indeed, the measure
\mu_{T,h}
may not be a probability measure (with exponentially small probability), so that the risk
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{T,h},\mu)
may not even be defined, whereas
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu)
is always defined.

Remark 2.2
.

It would have been arguably more convenient to work with a kernel based on the geodesic distance
\rho
, i.e. a kernel
\widetilde{K}_{h}
defined by
\widetilde{K}_{h}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\tilde{\eta}_{h}(x)}K{{\left(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{h}\right)}}
,
with
\widetilde{\eta}_{h}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K{{\left(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{h}\right)}}\mathrm{d}y
. For instance, we can easily prove that
{{\left(h^{-d}\widetilde{\eta}_{h}\right)}}_{h>0}
converges to
1
uniformly on
\mathcal{M}
, with a speed of convergence of order
h^{2}
. Such a property also holds for
\eta_{h}
, but only for sufficiently smooth kernels
K
satisfying some moments assumptions (see
Definition

A.2
).
However, for statistical purposes, the use of the Euclidean distance in (
2.11
) seems natural in the context where the manifold
\mathcal{M}
(and hence the geodesic distance
\rho
) is unknown. Yet, the study of the convolution with the kernel
\widetilde{K}_{h}

is of own interest. Moreover, remark that since the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is assumed to be compact, the geodesic distance
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathcal{M}
and the Euclidean distance
\|\cdot\|
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are known to be equivalent, see e.g.
[
GGHS20
, Proposition 2]
.

We are now in position to state our main result, which gives the rate of convergence of
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
to
\mu
when the diffusion path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
exploring the manifold has generator
\mathcal{A}
. More precisely, our purpose is to upper-bound

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu)\right]}}.

(2.14)

For any initial measure
\mu_{0}
, we can write
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0}}
as a mixture
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0}}[\cdot]=\int{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[\cdot]\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)
. Hence, a bound on the uniform risk defined in (
2.14
) automatically implies a bound on the risk for
any
initial measure.
As often, such a bound is obtained by decomposing the loss into a bias term
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)
and a variance term
\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{h}(\mathrm{d}x)=p_{h}(x)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ with}\quad p_{h}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K_{h}{{\left(z,x\right)}}p(z)\mathrm{d}z

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{\eta_{h}(z)}K{{\left(\frac{{{\left\|z-x\right\|}}}{h}\right)}}p(z)\mathrm{d}z.

is the intensity measure of the random measure
\mu_{T,h}
when the distribution of
X_{0}
is the invariant measure
\mu
.

The control of the variance term
\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}
relies on fine spectral properties of the generator of the diffusion. The proof of the following result is detailed in Section
4
for a diffusion starting from its invariant measure
\mu
and in Section
5
for a diffusion starting from a general initial distribution. The bound on the variance in the following theorem depends on the ultracontractivity constant
u_{\mathcal{A}}
of the generator
\mathcal{A}
, defined in
Section

5
.

Theorem 2.3

(Estimation from a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
p
be a positive
\mathcal{C}^{2}
density function with associated measure
\mu
.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Let
T\geq 2
and let
0<h\leq h_{0}
for some constant
h_{0}
depending on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
. Assume that either
K
is nonnegative or that
d\geq 4
and that
Th^{d}\geq c\ln(T)
(in which case,
h_{0}
additionally depends on
p_{\min}
and on the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-norm of
p
). Then,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}\leq c_{0}\frac{u_{\mathcal{A}}p_{\max}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}\begin{cases}\frac{h^{4-d}}{T}&\text{if }d\geq 5\\
\frac{\ln(1/h)}{T}&\text{if }d=4\\
\frac{1}{T}&\text{if }d\leq 3,\end{cases}

(2.16)

where

c_{0}
depends on
\mathcal{M}
, and
c
depends on
\mathcal{M}
,
K
,
p_{\min}
,
p_{\max}
and
\kappa_{\min}
.

The second term in the risk decomposition is the bias term
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)
, which was already studied by Divol in
[
Div22
]
.
Let
\ell\geq 0
. We introduce the Sobolev space
H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
as the completion of the set of smooth functions on
\mathcal{M}
with respect to the norm:

\|f\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}:=\max_{0\leq i\leq\ell}\Big(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla^{i}f(x)\|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x\Big)^{1/2}.

As
\mathcal{M}
is compact, we note that for any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
,
\|f\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
is a subset of
H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
.

Under additional technical conditions on the kernel
K
(recalled in
Section

A.2
), Divol showed that if
p\in H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})
for some
\ell\geq 0
, then

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)\leq c_{1}\frac{\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}h^{2\ell+2},

(2.17)

where
c_{1}
depends only on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
, see
Proposition

A.3
.
As a corollary of Theorem
2.3
and (
2.17
), we obtain a tight control on the risk of the estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 5
and
p
be a positive
\mathcal{C}^{2}
density function with associated measure

. Further assume that

has a controlled Sobolev norm
\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}
for some
\ell\geq 2
. Assume that
K
is a kernel of order larger than

(in the sense of
Definition

A.2
).
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Let
T\geq 2
and let
0<h\leq h_{0}
and assume that
Th^{d}\geq c\ln(T)
, where

,

2.3
. Then,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu)\right]}}\leq 2\ c_{1}\frac{\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}h^{2\ell+2}+2\ c_{0}\frac{u_{\mathcal{A}}p_{\max}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{h^{4-d}}{T}

(2.18)

where
c_{0}
is the constant from
Theorem

2.3
and
c_{1}
is the constant in (
2.17
).
In particular, for
h
of order
T^{-1/(2\ell+d-2)}
, it holds that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu)\right]}}\lesssim T^{-\frac{2\ell+2}{2\ell+d-2}}.

(2.19)

The results of Theorem
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
are non-asymptotic results. Notice that in Corollary
2.4
, the choice of
h
of order
T^{-1/(2\ell+d-2)}
is compatible with the condition
Th^{d}\geq c\ln(T)
required in Theorem
2.3
. It is a remarkable fact that the constants in the previous corollary only depend on the generator
\mathcal{A}
through the uniform ellipticity constant
\kappa_{\min}
and the ultracontractivity constant
u_{\mathcal{A}}
. From a statistical perspective, this implies that the knowledge of the exact SDE satisfied by the sample path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is not needed to estimate the invariant measure
\mu
. Only an a priori estimate on the uniform ellipticity constant
\kappa_{\min}
of the generator
\mathcal{A}
of the sample path and its ultracontractivity constant
u_{\mathcal{A}}
have to be known. For instance, the same reconstruction method will apply for estimating a sample path with either of the generators
\mathcal{L}
or
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
for
q=p/2
mentioned in the introduction.

Comparing with the results of Wang and Zhu in
[
WZ23
]
for the operator
\mathcal{L}
, we note that for

, the rate
T^{-\frac{2\ell+2}{2\ell+d-2}}
is faster than the rate of

that they obtained for the occupation measure
\mu_{T}
, see (

). Actually, our results allow us to recover their rate of convergence, for
any
generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying
Assumption

2.1
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
p
be a positive density function of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Then, for all
T\geq 2
,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T},\mu)\right]}}\leq c_{0}\left(1+\frac{u_{\mathcal{A}}p^{2}_{\max}}{p^{2}_{\min}}\right)\begin{cases}T^{-1}&\mbox{ when }d\leq 3\\
T^{-1}\ln(1+T)&\mbox{ when }d=4\\
T^{-\frac{2}{d-2}}&\mbox{ when }d\geq 5,\end{cases}

(2.20)

for some constant
c_{0}
depending on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Let
K
be a nonnegative Lipschitz-continuous kernel supported in
[0,1]
with
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}K(\|u\|)\mathrm{d}u=1
. As

is positive,

is always a probability measure, so that

. For any probability measure

by

as in (

). We prove in

that for any measure

for some constant

. Hence, both
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T},\mu_{T,h})
and

are of order

. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T},\mu)\right]}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T},\mu_{T,h})\right]}}+4\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}+4\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)

\displaystyle\leq 8c_{0}h^{2}+4\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}.

We pick

for

and

to conclude.
∎

Finally, we address the optimality of our statistical procedure using minimax theory. Consider a class
\mathcal{P}_{T}
of probability distributions of diffusion processes
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. For
{\mathbf{P}}_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{T}
, we let
\mu({\mathbf{P}}_{T})
be the invariant measure of the diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
whose restriction to
[0,T]
has distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{T}
. The associated minimax rate of convergence is defined as

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{T})=\inf_{\hat{\mu}}\sup_{{\mathbf{P}}_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{T}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{T}}[\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu},\mu({\mathbf{P}}_{T}))],

(2.21)

where the infimum is taken over all estimators
\hat{\mu}
, i.e. measurable functions of the observation
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, and where the expectation is over processes
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
distributed as
{\mathbf{P}}_{T}
.
To put it another way, the minimax rate is the best risk an estimator can attain (uniformly on
\mathcal{P}_{T}
) for the problem of estimating the invariant measure of some diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
whose distribution lies in
\mathcal{P}_{T}
.

For parameters
\ell\geq 2
,
\kappa_{\min}
,
p_{\min}
,
p_{\max}
,
u_{\max}
,
L>0
,
we consider the statistical model
\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}=\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}(\kappa_{\min},p_{\min},p_{\max},u_{\max},L)
consisting of all the distribution of diffusion processes
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
on
\mathcal{M}
with arbitrary initial distribution, generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying
Assumption

2.1
with constant
\kappa_{\min}
and ultracontractivity constant

smaller than

, and whose invariant measure
\mu
has a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
density

on
\mathcal{M}
with

and
\|p\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}\leq L
, satisfying

.
Remark that the kernel density estimator

attains (for

) the rate of convergence

uniformly on the class

. Hence, the minimax rate satisfies

. The next proposition, proved in

, states that this rate cannot be improved.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let

be an integer. Then, for

,

,

,

,

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell})\gtrsim\begin{cases}T^{-1/2}&\text{ if }d\leq 4\\
T^{-\frac{\ell+1}{2\ell+d-2}}&\text{ if }d\geq 5.\end{cases}

(2.22)

For

, these rates match with the rates obtained by our kernel-based estimator

, whereas for

, the empirical estimator

attains the minimax rate (up to logarithmic factors), according to the results of Wang and Zhu

]
for the Langevin generator

, or according to

\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
has a density with respect to the volume measure
\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathcal{M}
. As a consequence, computing
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
requires the knowledge of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, prohibiting the use of this method in the situation where the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is unknown. However, we expect that similar methods to the ones developed in
[
Div22
]
will allow us to create an estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{\mathcal{M}}
of the volume measure. Such an estimator of the volume measure is based on a patch-based reconstruction of the underlying manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
developed by Aamari and Levrard
[
AL19
]
. Guarantees for this method are only known in the case of i.i.d. samples, although we believe that the results of
[
AL19
]
could be adapted to the setting of this paper, where a diffusion path is observed. Such an estimator of the volume measure could then be used through a plug-in method to design a new estimator
\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h}
that would not require the knowledge of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

Remark 2.8
.

An arguably more natural variant of the Wasserstein estimation problem is the estimation of the invariant measure
\mu
with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric. Nonetheless, since we consider the most relevant end goal to be the setting where the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is unknown, the Wasserstein metric appears to be more suitable: indeed, unlike the
L^{2}
-metric which is not well-defined for measures with disjoint supports, the Wasserstein metric can be small for two measures supported on nearby manifolds.

However, we believe that estimation with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric is still feasible and, in fact, the analysis is simplified compared to the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-metric for several reasons. First, the
L^{2}
-metric is well-defined even when
\mu_{T,h}
is not a probability measure, so there is no need to control the probability that
\mu_{T,h}
is nonnegative (
Section

4.1
). Second, the proof of
Proposition

4.1
becomes more straightforward, as
Lemma

4.6
and subsequently
Lemma

4.4
can be directly applied to the function
\mu_{T,h}-\mu
, avoiding the more intricate use of
Proposition

4.9
. This approach leads to the conclusion that the variance of
\mu_{T,h}
is of order
h^{2-d}/T
(for
d\geq 3
). Standard Taylor expansions (e.g., using tools such as
[
Div22
, Lemma 10]
) can also be employed to show that the bias of the estimator is of order
h^{\ell}
when the density
p
belongs to
H^{\ell}
. Altogether, this yields a decomposition of the form

{\mathbb{E}}[\|\mu_{T,h}-\mu\|_{L^{2}}]\lesssim h^{\ell}+\frac{h^{1-d/2}}{\sqrt{T}}.

(2.23)

For
h
of the order of
T^{-1/(2\ell+d-2)}
, this leads to an estimation rate of order
T^{-\ell/(2\ell+d-2)}
. This matches the
L^{2}
estimation rates obtained in the Euclidean setting, as studied by
[
DR06
,
DR07
]
and
[
AG25
]
, among others. We leave the details to the interested reader.

3
Preliminaries

We detail in this section some tools, notation, and general results on elliptic operators on compact manifolds. Recall that
\mathcal{M}
is a smooth compact
d
-dimensional connected Riemannian manifold without boundary.

3.1
The Laplace-Beltrami operator

By Green’s theorem, the Laplace-Beltrami operator is symmetric, with for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})
,

\int_{\mathcal{M}}(\Delta f)g\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla g\right\rangle}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(\Delta g)\mathrm{d}x.

(3.24)

The operator
\Delta
defines an essentially self-adjoint operator over
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
with a discrete spectrum (see e.g.
[
Cha84
, Chapter I, Section 3]
).
We denote by

0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots

the eigenvalues of
(-\Delta)
, and by
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
the respective eigenfunctions of
\Delta
(note that they are of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
\mathcal{M}
). The first eigenfunction
\phi_{0}
is constant and the family
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
forms a Hilbert basis of
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
: for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
,

f=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\beta_{i}\phi_{i},\qquad\mbox{ with }\qquad\beta_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\phi_{i}\mathrm{d}x.

The first nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is of particular importance and is called the spectral gap.

The inverse operator
\Delta^{-1}
is defined on
L^{2}_{0}(\mathrm{d}x):={{\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x):\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}x=0\right\}}}
by

\Delta^{-1}(f):=-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}.

(3.25)

We introduce the operator
{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}

defined for
f\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
by

{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}}\phi_{i}.

3.2
Green function of the Laplace Beltrami operator

The Green function
G
of
\Delta
is a linear bounded operator
G:L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)\to L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
which is, in some sense, an inverse of
\Delta
in
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
.

Proposition 3.1

(See Appendix A in
[
AS13
]
, or Theorem 4.13 in
[
Aub82
]
)
.

We define
\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{M})={{\left\{(x,x):x\in\mathcal{M}\right\}}}
.
There exists a unique continuous function
G\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}{{\left(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{M})\right)}}
, which has the following properties

(i)

\forall x\in\mathcal{M}
,
G(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathrm{d}x)
with
\displaystyle{\int_{\mathcal{M}}G(x,\cdot)\mathrm{d}x=0}
;

(ii)

G
is symmetric :
\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}^{2}\setminus\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{M})
,
G(x,y)=G(y,x)
;

(iii)

\forall f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M}),\forall x\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}}G(x,y)\Delta f(y)\mathrm{d}y=f(x)-\int_{\mathcal{M}}f(y)\mathrm{d}y;

(iv)

there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that
\forall(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}^{2}\setminus\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{M})
,

{{\left|G(x,y)\right|}}\leq\kappa\begin{cases}1&\text{ when $d=1$,}\\
1+{{\left|\ln\rho(x,y)\right|}}&\text{ when $d=2$, and}\\
\rho(x,y)^{2-d}&\text{ when $d\geq 3$.}\end{cases}

The function
G
is called the Green function of the operator
\Delta
and it naturally defines an operator from
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
to
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
, which is also denoted by
G
, as follows:
\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
,
\forall x\in\mathcal{M}
,

{{\left(Gf\right)}}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{M}}G(x,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y.

(3.26)

From Proposition
3.1

(iii)
and (
3.26
), we remark that for
f\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)
we have

Gf=\Delta^{-1}(f).

The Green function will play a central role to control the variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{T,h}
, in particular through the following lemma, which controls the behavior of
Gf
for a function
f
localized on a small ball.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
h>0
sufficiently small. For any
(x,z)\in\mathcal{M}^{2}
, any continuous function
f
with support in the ball
\mathcal{B}(x,h)
, there exist constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
depending only on
\mathcal{M}
such that:

•

when
d=1
,
{{\left|(Gf)(z)\right|}}\leq\kappa_{1}{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{\infty}h
,

•

when
d=2
,
{{\left|(Gf)(z)\right|}}\leq\kappa_{1}{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{\infty}h^{2}(1+\ln|h|)
,

•

when
d\geq 3
,

\displaystyle{{\left|(Gf)(z)\right|}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{\infty}h^{2}\quad\text{ when }\rho(x,z)\leq 2h,

\displaystyle{{\left|(Gf)(z)\right|}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{\infty}h^{d}\rho(x,z)^{2-d}\quad\text{ when }\rho(x,z)>2h.

The proof, given in Appendix
B.3
, uses the Riemannian normal parametrization of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

3.3
The elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}

Consider an elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{M}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. As mentioned previously, using the
carré du champ

\Gamma
defined by (
2.7
),
\mathcal{A}
satisfies a Green’s formula (
2.8
).
Furthermore, as a uniformly elliptic operator of second order on a compact manifold without boundary, symmetric with respect to the measure
\mu
, the operator
\mathcal{A}
is essentially self-adjoint with respect to
L^{2}(\mu)
, and its spectrum is discrete (see Appendix
B.6
, also e.g.
[
Eva98
, Chapter 6]
and
[
Ngu25
]
). Consequently, there exist functions
{{\left(\psi_{i}\right)}}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of real numbers
0=\gamma_{0}<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\cdots
such that

(i)

for each
i
,
\psi_{i}
is an eigenfunction of
-\mathcal{A}
associated to the eigenvalue
\gamma_{i}
(counted with multiplicity);

(ii)

{{\left(\psi_{i}\right)}}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Let
L^{2}_{0}(\mu):={{\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mu):\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}\mu=0\right\}}}
. As for the Laplace-Beltrami operator,
we introduce the operators
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
and
{{\left(-\mathcal{A}\right)}}^{-1/2}
defined for
f\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
by

\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f):=-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}\psi_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad{{\left(-\mathcal{A}\right)}}^{-1/2}(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}\psi_{i},

(3.27)

with

\alpha_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\mu
.
At last, we denote by
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the semigroup of a diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with generator
\mathcal{A}
.
Then, for
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,
f=\sum_{i\geq 0}\alpha_{i}\psi_{i}
with
\alpha_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\mu
, we have

\forall x\in\mathcal{M},\ P_{t}f(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[f(X_{t})\right]}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{i}t}\alpha_{i}\psi_{i}(x).

This result is a consequence of the
Dynkin formula
[
Kul18
]
, which implies that for any function
f
in the domain of
\mathcal{A}

\forall x\in\mathcal{M},\ P_{t}f(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}[f(X_{t})]=f(x)+\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\mathcal{A}f(x)\mathrm{d}s.

We now give a Poincaré inequality for the general operator
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

(Poincaré’s inequality for
\mathcal{A}
)
For any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}\mu=0
,

\int_{\mathcal{M}}-f\mathcal{A}f\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\frac{p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}}{p_{\max}}\lambda_{1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
\lambda_{1}
is the spectral gap of
\Delta
, and
\kappa_{\min}
is defined in (
2.10
).

Proof.

By the symmetry of
\mathcal{A}
, Equation (
2.10
) and the Poincaré’s inequality of
\Delta
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}-f\mathcal{A}f\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(f,f)\mathrm{d}\mu\geq p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\geq p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}\lambda_{1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}(f-\overline{f})^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}}{p_{\max}}\lambda_{1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}(f-\overline{f})^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\frac{p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}}{p_{\max}}\lambda_{1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
\overline{f}:=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}x.
We develop the square and use that that
\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\mathrm{d}\mu=0
to obtain the conclusion.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The elliptic operators
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
and
\mathcal{L}
defined in
Section

1
are essentially Laplace operators.
Indeed, the operator
\mathcal{L}
is a weighted Laplacian with weight
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
as defined in
[

, while the operator

is a weighted Laplacian on a weighted manifold, i.e. a manifold

.

4
Variance term for the stationary process

2.3
, we state a simpler version of the theorem, where the initial measure

for the sample path

is replaced by the invariant measure

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[p_{T,h}(y)\right]}}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}K_{h}(z,y)p(z)\mathrm{d}z=p_{h}(y).

(4.28)

We will then explain in Section

how we can extend the result to any initial distribution

using the ultracontractivity of the semi-group

.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

and
p
be a positive
\mathcal{C}^{2}
density function with associated measure
\mu
.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying Assumption

. Let

for some constant

depending on
\mathcal{M}
and

. Assume that either

is nonnegative or that

and that

(in which case,

additionally depends on

and on the
\mathcal{C}^{1}

). Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}\leq c_{0}\,\frac{p_{\max}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}\begin{cases}\frac{h^{4-d}}{T}&\text{if }d\geq 5\\
\frac{\ln(1/h)}{T}&\text{if }d=4\\
\frac{1}{T}&\text{if }d\leq 3,\end{cases}

(4.29)

where

c_{0}
depends on
\mathcal{M}
, and

depends on
\mathcal{M}
,

,
p_{\min}
,
p_{\max}
and
\kappa_{\min}
.

The main difference between
Proposition

4.1
and
Theorem

2.3
is the choice of the initial measure, which is of the form
\delta_{x}
for
Theorem

2.3
and is equal to
\mu
in
Proposition

4.1
. As for any distribution
\mu_{0}
and random variable

,
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0}}[U]=\int{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[U]\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}(x)
,
Theorem

2.3
is stronger, and implies that the convergence holds for
any
initial measure
\mu_{0}
.

The remainder of this section is dedicated to proving
Proposition

4.1
.
We first state some useful properties on
K_{h}
. The following lemma is stated in
[
Div22
, Lemma 10]
under stronger regularity hypotheses on the kernel

. We can relax these assumptions and a proof is given in Appendix

. The first point of the lemma guarantees that

is well defined on
\mathcal{M}^{2}
for

small enough.

Lemma 4.2
.

We first give a lemma that is a direct consequence from a result by Peyre

is defined on

(see Section

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be two probability density functions with respect to the volume measure
\mathrm{d}x
, with
f_{1}
lower bounded by some positive constant

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(f_{1}\mathrm{d}x,f_{2}\mathrm{d}x)\leq\frac{4}{f_{\min}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}{{\left(f_{1}-f_{2}\right)}}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(4.32)

Besides, ellipticity yields the following relation between the general operator
\mathcal{A}
and the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any function

, we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|(-\mathcal{A})^{-1/2}f\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\frac{1}{p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}(fp)\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

From Equation (
2.8
),

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{-1}f,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f)\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f{{\left(-\mathcal{A}^{-1}\right)}}f\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|(-\mathcal{A})^{-1/2}f\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu.

Besides, since
\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and since
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})
is dense in this space,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{-1}f,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f)\mathrm{d}\mu}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(-\mathcal{A}^{-1}f,g)\mathrm{d}\mu:g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\text{ such that }\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(g,g)\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}fg\mathrm{d}\mu:g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\text{ such that }\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(g,g)\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\Big\}.

Then, because the simple fact that the supremum of a given set is always bigger than the supremum on any subset, and using (
2.10
), we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{-1}f,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f)\mathrm{d}\mu}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}(fp)g\mathrm{d}x:g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\text{ such that }\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|\nabla g\right|}}^{2}p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{{\left(p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}\right)}}^{-1/2}\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}(fp)g\mathrm{d}x:g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\text{ such that }\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|\nabla g\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1\Big\}.

Besides, using Green’s theorem, we have that for all
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}(fp)g\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\Delta(\Delta^{-1})(fp)\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left\langle\nabla(\Delta^{-1})(fp),\nabla g\right\rangle}}\mathrm{d}x.

Hence, by Hölder’s inequality, we conclude that:

\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Gamma((\mathcal{A}^{-1})f,(\mathcal{A}^{-1})f)\mathrm{d}\mu}\leq{{\left(p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}\right)}}^{-1/2}\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}(fp)\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x}.\qed

The following standard result is crucial: it bounds the variance of the random variable
\mu_{T}(f)
for some function
f
in terms of the generator
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a diffusion with generator
\mathcal{A}
, starting from its invariant measure
\mu
. We have for any
f\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[{{\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)}}^{2}\right]}}\leq\frac{2}{T}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\mathcal{A}\right)}}^{-1/2}f\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

Recall that
(\gamma_{i})_{i\geq 0}
are the eigenvalues of
-\mathcal{A}
, with
0=\gamma_{0}<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}\leq\cdots
, and
(\psi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
their respective eigenfunctions. We also remind the reader that
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the semigroup of a process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with generator
\mathcal{A}
(see Section
3.3
).

Since
f\in L^{2}_{0}(\mu)
, we write
\displaystyle{f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\psi_{i}}
with
\alpha_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}\mu
, and
\alpha_{0}=0
.
By the Markov property, denoting by
{{\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)}}_{t\geq 0}
the natural filtration of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[{{\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)}}^{2}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}f(X_{t})f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[{\mathbb{E}}{{\left[f(X_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]}}f(X_{s})\right]}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[P_{t-s}f(X_{s})f(X_{s})\right]}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s.

By assumption, the distribution of
X_{t}
is
\mu
for any
t\geq 0
, and computing the expectation using the link between the semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and the generator
\mathcal{A}
of the process, we then obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[{{\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right)}}^{2}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}e^{-\gamma_{i}(t-s)}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}.

The lemma is then proved by definition of
(-\mathcal{A})^{-1/2}
given by Equation (
3.27
).
∎

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(p_{T,h},p_{h})\leq\frac{8}{p_{\min}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}{{\left(p_{T,h}-p_{h}\right)}}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(4.37)

Now, consider the function
R_{h}(x,y)=K_{h}(x,y)-\int_{\mathcal{M}}K_{h}(z,y)\mu(\mathrm{d}z)
. First, we observe that

p_{T,h}(y)-p_{h}(y)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}R_{h}(X_{s},y)\mathrm{d}s.

As

is continuous on a compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we have
R_{h}\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mathrm{d}y)
.
Thus, for each
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
,
R_{h}(x,\cdot)\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{d}y)
and
R_{h}(\cdot,y)\in L_{0}^{2}(\mu)
. Therefore, due to Proposition
4.9
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}{{\left(p_{T,h}-p_{h}\right)}}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}y\right]}}\leq\frac{2}{T}\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\mathcal{A}\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}R_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\mathrm{d}y.

(4.38)

Besides, by Lemma
4.5
, we have:

\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\mathcal{A}\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}R_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{p_{\min}\kappa_{\min}}\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}(M_{p})_{1}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}R_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

(4.39)

where
M_{p}:L^{2}_{0}(\mu)\rightarrow L^{2}_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)
is the bounded multiplication operator
f\mapsto pf
.

Therefore, after Inequalities (
4.37
), (
4.38
), (
4.39
), we have:

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{T,h},\mu_{h})^{2}{\mathbf{1}}_{E_{T,h}}\right]}}\leq\frac{{16}}{p_{\min}^{2}\kappa_{\min}T}\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}(M_{p})_{1}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}R_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Note that
M_{p}
and
\Delta^{-1/2}
are bounded operators, which implies
(M_{p})_{1}
and
(\Delta^{-1/2})_{2}
are commutative. In other words,
(M_{p})_{1}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}R_{h}={{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}(M_{p})_{1}R_{h}
, which means

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{T,h},\mu_{h}){\mathbf{1}}_{E_{T,h}}\right]}}\leq\frac{{16}}{p_{\min}^{2}\kappa_{\min}T}\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}S_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where
S_{h}(x,y)=p(x)R_{h}(x,y)\in L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x\otimes\mathrm{d}y)
.

Recall that
(\lambda_{i})_{i\geq 0}
and
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
are respectively the eigenvalues and the eigenfunctions of
(-\Delta)
. As
p
is upper bounded,
\forall x\in\mathcal{M}

S_{h}(x,.)\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{d}y)
,
\forall y\in\mathcal{M}

S_{h}(.,y)\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
and
S_{h}\in L_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{d}x\otimes\mathrm{d}y)
. Thus, there are
(\alpha_{i,j}(h))_{i,j\geq 0}
such that
S_{h}
has the following decomposition (with
\alpha_{i,0}=\alpha_{0,j}=0
): for all
x,y\in\mathcal{M}

S_{h}(x,y)=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\alpha_{ij}(h)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y),

with
\alpha_{ij}(h)=\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}S_{h}(x,y)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y
. Consequently, for all

\displaystyle{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}S_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{ij}(h)}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y),

\displaystyle{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1}S_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{ij}(h)}{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y),

\displaystyle{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1}S_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{ij}(h)}{\lambda_{j}}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{1}^{-1/2}{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}_{2}^{-1/2}S_{h}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\frac{\alpha_{ij}^{2}(h)}{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}(\Delta_{1}^{-1}S_{h})(\Delta_{2}^{-1}S_{h})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}(G_{1}S_{h})(x,y)(G_{2}S_{h})(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

where
G
is the Green function of
\Delta
introduced in Section
3.2
.
Denoting by
\widetilde{K}_{h}(x,y)=p(x)K_{h}(x,y)
, we have
S_{h}(x,y)=\widetilde{K}_{h}(x,y)-p(x)p_{h}(y)
, and thus,

(G_{1}S_{h})(x,y)(G_{2}S_{h})(x,y)=\\
(G_{1}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)(G_{2}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)-(G_{1}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)p(x)Gp_{h}(y)\\
-(G_{2}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)p_{h}(y)Gp(x)+p(x)Gp(x)p_{h}(y)Gp_{h}(y).

(4.40)

Let

. Using the fact that

has compact support and that the geodesic and Euclidean distances are equivalent, the functions

and

are supported on a geodesic ball of radius

for some

. Hence,

implies that for

sufficiently small and for all

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|G_{1}{\widetilde{K}}_{h}(x,y)G_{2}{\widetilde{K}}_{h}(x,y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq{c\,}{{\left\|{\widetilde{K}}_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}^{2}{{\left(h^{4}\int_{\mathcal{B}(x,2c_{1}h)}\mathrm{d}y+h^{2d}\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{B}(x,2c_{1}h)}\rho(x,y)^{4-2d}\mathrm{d}y\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq{c\,}p_{\max}^{2}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{-2d}{{\left(h^{4+d}+h^{2d}h^{4-d}\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq{c\,}p_{\max}^{2}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}h^{-2d}\times h^{4+d},

(4.41)

where we also use
Lemma

B.2
and
Lemma

4.2
, and the constant

depends only on
\mathcal{M}
and can change from line to line.
Besides, using similar computations, we have, for
h\leq 1
sufficiently small

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|(G_{1}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)p(x)Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq c\,p_{\max}^{2}\|K_{h}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left(h^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}(y,2h)}\mathrm{d}x+h^{d}\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{B}(y,2h)}\rho(x,y)^{2-d}\mathrm{d}x\right)}}{{\left|Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq c\,p_{\max}^{2}\|K_{h}\|_{\infty}h^{d}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y\leq c\,p_{\max}^{2}\|K\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y,

(4.42)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{{\left|(G_{2}\widetilde{K}_{h})(x,y)p_{h}(y)Gp(x)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq c\,p_{\max}{{\left\|p_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}\|K_{h}\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left(h^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}(x,2h)}\mathrm{d}y+h^{d}\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{B}(x,2h)}\rho(x,y)^{2-d}\mathrm{d}y\right)}}{{\left|Gp(x)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq c\,p_{\max}{{\left\|p_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}\|K\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp(x)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x,

(4.43)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|p(x)Gp(x)p_{h}(y)Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\leq p_{\max}{{\left\|p_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp(x)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y.

(4.44)

By Lemma
4.3
,
(p_{h})
converges to

uniformly on
\mathcal{M}
when

. Consequently, for

sufficiently small,
{{\left\|p_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}\leq 2p_{\max}
. Finally, by Proposition
3.1

(iv)
and (

) in Lemma

,
we have, for

sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp(x)\right|}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq c\,p_{\max}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathcal{B}(x,h)}\rho(x,z)^{2-d}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathcal{B}(x,h)}\rho(x,z)^{2-d}\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x\right)}}\leq c\,p_{\max}

(4.45)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|Gp_{h}(y)\right|}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq c\,{{\left\|p_{h}\right\|}}_{\infty}\leq c\,p_{\max}.

(4.46)

Combining Equations (

)-(

), we deduce that, for

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}(G_{1}S_{h})(x,y)(G_{2}S_{h})(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\leq cp_{\max}^{2}{{\left\|K\right\|}}_{\infty}^{2}h^{4-d}.

This proves the proposition when

. The computations for

∎

5
Transition to a general initial measure

In the previous section, we have obtained a control of the variance term
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})
when the stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starts from its invariant measure
\mu
. In this section, we explain how to extend the result to an initial measure of type Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
(which will imply the result for any initial measure).
The main idea is to use the ultracontractivity of the diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Let us first introduce this notion.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
Wan14
, Theorem 3.5.5.]

The semigroup
(P_{t})
associated to the operator
\mathcal{A}

is ultracontractive. In other words, for each
t>0
, there is a minimal positive value
c_{t}>0
, such that for any bounded measurable function
f
, we have

\|P_{t}f\|_{\infty}\leq c_{t}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}.

(5.47)

In
Section

B.5
, an explicit form of the ultracontractivity term
c_{t}
is given.
We denote by
u_{\mathcal{A}}=c_{1}
the ultracontractivity constant at time
t=1
.

Remark 5.2
.

Wang only considers operators of the form
\Delta+\nabla p
in
[
Wan14
]
. However, as explained in
Remark

3.4
(see
Section

B.5
for more details), for any
\mathcal{C}^{2}
second order elliptic differential operator
\mathcal{A}
on a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric with respect to the measure
\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
, there is always a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-Riemannian metric
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
on
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\Delta}+\tilde{\nabla}p,
where
\tilde{\Delta}
and
\tilde{\nabla}
are respectively the Laplacian and the gradient operator of
(\mathcal{M},\tilde{\mathbf{g}})
. Hence,
[
Wan14
, Theorem 3.5.5.]
can readily be applied.

Remark 5.3
.

In
[
Wan14
]
, Wang actually defines the ultra-contractivity of a semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in a slightly different way: for any
t>0
, there should exist
c_{t}>0
such that for any measurable bounded function
f
,
\|P_{t}f\|_{\infty}\leq c_{t}{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{L^{2}(\mu)}
. However, Wang’s definition implies (
5.47
) in our setting with
\mathcal{M}
compact. Assume that
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is ultra-contractive in Wang’s sense, then for
t>0
and for any measurable bounded function
f
,

{{\left\|P_{t}f\right\|}}_{\infty}\leq c_{t/2}{{\left\|P_{t/2}f\right\|}}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq{{\left(c_{t/2}\right)}}^{2}\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}

because
{{\left\|T\right\|}}_{2\to\infty}={{\left\|T\right\|}}_{1\to 2}
for a symmetric operator
T
.

We now use the ultracontractivity constant
u_{\mathcal{A}}
to control the distance
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{M}
. There exist constants
c
,
h_{0}
depending only on
\mathcal{M}
, and on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
respectively such that for any
x\in\mathcal{M}
and any
T>1
, and any
h\leq h_{0}
, we have that:

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]\leq c\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T}+2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})+2u_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu}(E^{c}_{T,h})\\
+2u_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}.

(5.48)

Notice that the last term in the right hand side of (
5.48
) is the expectation starting from the initial distribution
\mu
, thanks to ultracontractivity. This term has been controlled in Proposition
4.1
.

Proof.

Consider functions
F(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}
and
H(x)={\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})
.
Note that the shifted process
(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X_{t+1})_{t\geq 0}
is still a diffusion process with generator
\mathcal{A}
. Therefore, if we consider the following shifted quantities

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}_{T,h}(y)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}K_{h}(X_{s+1},y)\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{p}_{T,h}\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }\widetilde{p}_{T,h}\mathrm{d}x\text{ is positive measure}\\[2.84544pt]
\delta_{x_{0}}&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{T,h}={{\left\{\widetilde{p}_{T,h}\geq 0\right\}}},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X_{1}}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[F(X_{1})\right]}}=P_{1}F(x),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\widetilde{E}^{c}_{T,h})={\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})\right]}}={\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[H(X_{1})\right]}}=P_{1}H(x).

Thus, by ultracontractivity of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, at time

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]\leq{{\left\|P_{1}F\right\|}}_{\infty}\leq u_{\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}F(y)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)=u_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}},

(5.49)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(\widetilde{E}^{c}_{T,h})\leq u_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu}(E^{c}_{T,h}),

(5.50)

where
u_{\mathcal{A}}
has been defined after Lemma
5.1
.

Recall that the Wasserstein distance is controlled by the total variation distance
[
Vil09
, Theorem 6.15]
.
Besides, on the event
E_{T,h}\cap\widetilde{E}_{T,h}
,
p_{T,h}\mathrm{d}x
and
\widetilde{p}_{T,h}\mathrm{d}x
are both positive measures. Hence, on
E_{T,h}\cap\widetilde{E}_{T,h}
, by Lemma
4.2
, when
h
is sufficiently small depending on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h})\leq 2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}|p_{T,h}(y)-\widetilde{p}_{T,h}(y)|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}\frac{1}{T}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|K_{h}(X_{s},y)|\mathrm{d}s+\int_{T}^{T+1}|K_{h}(X_{s},y)|\mathrm{d}s\right)}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}\frac{1}{T}{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|K_{h}(X_{s},y)|\mathrm{d}y\right)}}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{T}^{T+1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|K_{h}(X_{s},y)|\mathrm{d}y\right)}}\mathrm{d}s\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T},

where

\mathcal{M}
and we used
Lemma

B.2
for the last inequality and the equivalence between the Euclidean and geodesic distances.

Thus, by (
5.50
), for
h

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h})\right]}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T}+\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{x}{{\left(E^{c}_{T,h}\cup\widetilde{E}^{c}_{T,h}\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T}+\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})+u_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu}(E^{c}_{T,h}).

(5.51)

Consequently, by triangular inequality, (

), and (

), we deduce that there exists a constant

such that for

sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h})\right]}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\widetilde{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T}+\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})+u_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu}(E^{c}_{T,h})+u_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}{{\left(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h}\right)}}\right]}},

which is the desired conclusion.
∎

Hence, using
Proposition

4.1
and

4.8

is nonnegative. Otherwise we assume that

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume that

. Let

,

\displaystyle p_{T,h}(y)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}K_{h}(X_{s},y)\mathrm{d}s+\frac{T-1}{T}\times\frac{1}{T-1}\int_{0}^{T-1}K_{h}(X_{s+1},y)\mathrm{d}s

where we used ultracontractivity at the last step. By Lemma

By Proposition

4.1

, for any

, for

\displaystyle\leq c\frac{\|K\|_{\infty}}{T}+2\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(E^{c}_{T,h})+2u_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{\mu}(E^{c}_{T,h})+cu_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{p_{\max}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}\|K\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{h^{4-d}}{T}

6
Minimax lower bound

The proof of the minimax lower bound (
Proposition

2.6
) relies crucially on the computation of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the law of two diffusion processes
(X_{t})
and
(X^{\prime}_{t})
on
\mathcal{M}
. Recall that for two probability measures
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{Q}}
, the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}\|{\mathbf{Q}})
is defined as

\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}\|{\mathbf{Q}})=\int\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}}{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{Q}}}\right)\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}

(6.52)

whenever
{\mathbf{P}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\mathbf{Q}}
.

Recall that we have denoted, in
Section

2
, by
\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}=\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}(\kappa_{\min},p_{\min},p_{\max},u_{\max},L)
the class of all the laws of diffusion paths
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
on
\mathcal{M}
with arbitrary initial distribution, generator
\mathcal{A}
satisfying
Assumption

2.1
with constant
\kappa_{\min}
and having an ultracontractivity constant smaller than
u_{\max}
, and invariant measure
\mu
having a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
positive
p
density on
\mathcal{M}
with a Sobolev norm
\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}\leq L
and a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-norm smaller than

, satisfying
p_{\min}\leq p\leq p_{\max}
.

Our minimax lower bound follows from an application of Assouad’s lemma, see
[
Tsy09
, Theorem 2.12]
.

Lemma 6.1

(Assouad’s lemma)
.

Consider a statistical model
\mathcal{P}
. Let
J>0
be an integer and consider a subfamily
\{{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}\subset\mathcal{P}
indexed by
\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{J}
. Define the Hamming distance
d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{j}\neq\tau^{\prime}_{j}\}}
on the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{J}
. Assume that there exists
A
such that for every
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{J}
,
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}),\mu({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}}))\geq Ad_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
and that whenever
d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=1
,
\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\|{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}})\leq 1/2
. Then, the minimax risk over
\mathcal{P}
defined in (
2.21
) satisfies

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{P})\geq\frac{AJ}{4}.

(6.53)

To apply Assouad’s lemma, we build an appropriate family
\{\mu_{\tau}\}
of probability measures on
\mathcal{M}
indexed by the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{J}
by
perturbating the uniform measure by small bumps. We let
p_{\tau}
be the density of
\mu_{\tau}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}
be the law of a diffusion path
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
with initial distribution
\mu_{\tau}
and generator
\mathcal{A}_{p_{\tau}q}
(defined in (
1.1
)) with
q\equiv 1
. In that case, the associated
carré du champ
is equal to
\Gamma(f,f)=|\nabla f|^{2}
, so that we can take
\kappa_{\min}=1
.

Lemma 6.2

(Existence of bumps)
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
be an integer. There exist constants
\kappa
,
{\varepsilon}_{0}>0
depending only on
\ell
and
\mathcal{M}
such that
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
and all
0\leq{\varepsilon}\leq{\varepsilon}_{0}
, there exists a smooth function
\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}:\mathcal{M}\to{\mathbb{R}}
supported on
\mathcal{B}(x,{\varepsilon})
, with
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}y=0
,
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}^{2}\mathrm{d}y={\varepsilon}^{d}
and for all integers
0\leq i\leq\ell
,

\|\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{i}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\kappa{\varepsilon}^{-i}.

(6.54)

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathcal{M}
. Consider a smooth chart
\Psi
around
x
defined on the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
\Psi(0)=x
. As
\mathcal{M}
is smooth and compact,
\Psi
can be choosen as being a bi-Lipschitz map with Lipschitz constant
L
not depending on
x
. In particular, for
{\varepsilon}
small enough, the chart
\Psi:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,L^{-1}{\varepsilon})\to\mathcal{M}
is a diffeomorphism whose image is contained in
\mathcal{B}(x,{\varepsilon})
. Let
\phi_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[-1,1]
be a smooth function of integral
0
, with support included in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,L^{-1})
, equal to
1
on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,L^{-1}/3)
. Let
J\Psi:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,L^{-1}{\varepsilon})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the Jacobian of
\Psi
. We define for
u\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,{\varepsilon}L^{-1})
and
y=\Psi(u)

\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}(y)=C_{x,{\varepsilon}}\frac{\phi_{0}(u/{\varepsilon})}{J\Psi(u)},

and
\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}
is
0
outside
\Psi(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,{\varepsilon}L^{-1}))
. By construction,
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}\mathrm{d}y=C_{x,{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,{\varepsilon}L^{-1})}\phi_{0}(u/{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}u=0
. We choose
C_{x,{\varepsilon}}
so that
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}^{2}\mathrm{d}y={\varepsilon}^{d}
. It remains to bound the
\mathcal{C}^{i}
-norm of
\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}
for
0\leq i\leq\ell
. Note that as
\mathcal{M}
is compact and smooth, all the coefficients of the metric and its inverse have bounded
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-norms for all
k\geq 0
. This implies that for
{\varepsilon}
small enough, the chart
\Psi
can be chosen so that the
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
-norm of the function
J\Psi:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,{\varepsilon}/2)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is uniformly bounded (uniformly with respect to the base point
x
), with
J\Psi(u)\geq c_{0}
for all
u\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,{\varepsilon}L^{-1})
and some constant
c_{0}
depending on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, as
\phi_{0}
is equal to
1
on
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,L^{-1}/3)
, this implies that the constant
C_{x,{\varepsilon}}
is larger than
c_{1}
for some constant
c_{1}
depending only on
\mathcal{M}
. The smoothness of
\phi_{0}
and of
J\Psi
then imply the desired controls on the
\mathcal{C}^{i}
-norm of
\phi_{x,{\varepsilon}}
(applying Leibniz formula for the derivative of a product).
∎

Fix
0<{\varepsilon}\leq{\varepsilon}_{0}
and consider a set of points
x_{1},\dots,x_{J}\in\mathcal{M}
that are all at least
2{\varepsilon}
apart. Note that by a simple covering argument, the number
J
can be chosen to be of order
{\varepsilon}^{-d}
. For
j=1,\dots,J
, write
\phi_{j}=\phi_{x_{j},{\varepsilon}}
. Assume without loss of generality that the manifold
\mathcal{M}
has unit volume. For
\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{J}
, consider the probability measure

p_{\tau}=1+\frac{v}{2\kappa}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{j}\phi_{j}.

for some
0\leq v\leq{\varepsilon}^{\ell}
. Note that according to
Lemma

6.2
,
p_{\tau}
satisfies
1/2\leq p_{\tau}\leq 3/2
and integrates to
1
. According to
Lemma

6.2
, the
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
-norm of
p_{\tau}
is smaller than
1/2
(and therefore so is its Sobolev norm).

Using
Remark

B.6
,
we see that for a choice of
p_{\min}\leq 1/2
,
p_{\max}\geq 3/2
,
\kappa_{\min}\leq 1
,
L\geq 1/2
, all the associated measures
\mu_{\tau}
are in the statistical model
\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}=\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell}(\kappa_{\min},p_{\min},p_{\max},u_{\max},L)
for some
u_{\max}
large enough, as long as
\ell\geq 2
.

Let
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{J}
. Consider the function
f=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{j}\neq\tau^{\prime}_{j}\}}\tau_{j}\phi_{j}
. The function
f:\mathcal{M}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
\kappa{\varepsilon}^{-1}
.
As the 2-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is larger than the 1-Wasserstein distance

, it holds by duality

that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{\tau},\mu_{\tau^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{\tau},\mu_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq\frac{1}{\kappa{\varepsilon}^{-1}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}fp_{\tau}\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathcal{M}}fp_{\tau^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{v}{2\kappa^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{j}\neq\tau_{j^{\prime}}\}}2\tau_{j}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{j}f\mathrm{d}y.

By construction of
f
and as
\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{j}^{2}\mathrm{d}y={\varepsilon}^{d}
, this quantity is equal to
\frac{v{\varepsilon}^{d+1}}{\kappa^{2}}d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
, that is the first condition in Assouad’s lemma holds for
A=v{\varepsilon}^{d+1}/\kappa^{2}
.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence between
{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}}
can be controlled using Girsanov theorem. Indeed, Girsanov theorem provides an explicit formula for the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}||{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{4}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla\ln p_{\tau}-\nabla\ln p_{\tau^{\prime}}\|^{2}p_{\tau}^{2}\mathrm{d}y=\frac{T}{4}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla p_{\tau}-\frac{p_{\tau}}{p_{\tau^{\prime}}}\nabla p_{\tau^{\prime}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla p_{\tau}-\nabla p_{\tau^{\prime}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{T}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\|\nabla p_{\tau^{\prime}}\|^{2}}{p_{\tau^{\prime}}^{2}}(p_{\tau}-p_{\tau^{\prime}})^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Consider
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{J}
with
d_{H}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=1
, and assume without loss of generality that the two vectors differ only by their first entry.
Using the available bound on the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-norm of
p_{\tau}
and the fact that
p_{\tau}\geq 1/2
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}||{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla p_{\tau}-\nabla p_{\tau^{\prime}}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{v^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-2}}{2}T\int_{\mathcal{M}}(p_{\tau}-p_{\tau^{\prime}})^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{2}\frac{v^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{B}(x_{1},{\varepsilon})}\|\nabla\phi_{1}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{v^{4}{\varepsilon}^{-2}}{2\kappa^{2}}T\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{1}^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{2}v^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-2}\int_{\mathcal{B}(x_{1},{\varepsilon})}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{v^{4}}{2\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon}^{-2+d}T\lesssim Tv^{2}{\varepsilon}^{d-2},

where we use at the last line that the volume of a ball of radius
{\varepsilon}
is of order
{\varepsilon}^{d}
for
{\varepsilon}
small enough (see
Lemma

B.2
), and
v\in[0,{\varepsilon}^{\ell}]
.

When
d\geq 5
, choose
{\varepsilon}=cT^{-1/(2\ell+d-2)}
and
v={\varepsilon}^{\ell}
. For
c
small enough,
\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\|{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}})\leq 1/2
.
By Assouad’s lemma, and recalling that we can pick
J
of order
{\varepsilon}^{-d}
, we obtain that

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell})\geq\frac{AJ}{4}=\frac{{\varepsilon}^{\ell+d+1}J}{4\kappa^{2}}\gtrsim{\varepsilon}^{\ell+1}\gtrsim T^{-\frac{\ell+1}{2\ell+d-2}},

(6.55)

concluding the proof of
Proposition

2.6
in this case. For
d\leq 4
, we let
{\varepsilon}={{\varepsilon}_{0}}
and
v=cT^{-1/2}
for
c
small enough, so that
\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}||{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau^{\prime}})\leq 1/2
and
J
is of order
1
. Then, Assouad’s lemma gives

{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{P}_{T,\ell})\geq\frac{AJ}{4}=\frac{v{\varepsilon}^{d+1}J}{4\kappa^{2}}\gtrsim T^{-1/2}.

(6.56)

This concludes the proof.

Appendix A
Wasserstein distance and bias term estimates

A.1
About the Wasserstein distance’s estimate of Peyre and Proof of Lemma
4.4

We provide here a new proof of
[
Pey18
, Theorem 2.1]
, a result we need to prove Lemma
4.4
at the end of this section. The proof given by Peyre indeed holds only under additional assumptions that were not detailed in his paper and that turn out to be unnecessary.

Theorem A.1

(Theorem 2.1 in
[
Pey18
]
)
.

Given a compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
Then, for all probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathcal{M}
, we have:

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\|\mu-\nu\|_{H^{-1}(\mu)},

where

\|\mu-\nu\|_{H^{-1}(\mu)}:=\sup_{g:\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}.

Moreover,

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in Lip(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}},

where
\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M})
denotes the space of all Lipschitz continuous functions on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Consider the Hamilton-Jacobi semigroup
(Q_{t})_{t>0}
on
\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M})
:

Q_{t}\phi(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\phi(y)+\frac{1}{2t}\rho(y,x)^{2}\right\},\quad t>0,\ \phi\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}),

where
\rho
is the geodesic distance of
\mathcal{M}
.
From
[
LV07
, Theorem 2.5]
, for any
\phi
continuous bounded,
Q_{0}\phi:=\lim_{t\to 0}Q_{t}\phi=\phi
,
\|\nabla Q_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}
is bounded for all
t>0
, and
Q_{t}\phi
solves the Hamilton-Jacobi equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Q_{t}\phi=-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2},\quad t>0.

Following the ideas of the proof of
[
Wan14
, Corollary 2.7.3]
and using
[
Wan14
, Proposition 1.3.1]
(see also
[
Rac85
, Theorem 1]
), the Kantorovich dual formula implies that:

\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\phi\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M})\text{ bounded}}{{\left\{\nu(Q_{1}\phi)-\mu(\phi)\right\}}}.

Consider the following curve on the space of measures
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
defined by

\mu_{t}:=(1-t)\mu+t\nu.

By taking the derivative along
t
, we have that for all
\phi
continuous and bounded:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{{\left(\mu_{t}(Q_{t}\phi)\right)}}=-\frac{1}{2}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\right)}}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu).

We now analyze the above term. Let
t\in(0,1)
.

If
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}>0
, by the classical inequality
-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+ab\leq\frac{1}{2}b^{2}
, we have:

-\frac{1}{2}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\right)}}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\right)}}^{2}}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}

If
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}=0
and
\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)=0
, clearly we have

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\right)}}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}.

If
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}=0
and
\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\neq 0
, using the Lipschitz function
\alpha\,Q_{t}\phi
on
\mathcal{M}
for any arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, we remark that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}=\infty,

and clearly

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla Q_{t}\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\right)}}+\int_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{t}\phi\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}.

Hence, for all
t\in(0,1)
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{{\left(\mu_{t}(Q_{t}\phi)\right)}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2},

which implies

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}\ dt.

Besides,
\mu_{t}\geq(1-t)\mu
for all
t\in(0,1)
. Thus, for all
g
with
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}\leq 1
, we have
(1-t)\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1
.
This leads to the fact that for all
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<1
, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t_{0}},\mu_{t_{1}})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
(1-t_{1})\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left((t_{1}-t_{0})\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(t_{1}-t_{0})^{2}}{1-t_{1}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}^{2}.

Hence, for any
n\in\mathbb{N^{*}}
and
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{n}<1
, we have:

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t_{0}},\mu_{t_{n}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t_{i-1}},\mu_{t_{i}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}{\sqrt{1-t_{i}}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}

Thus, for all
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<1
, by convergence of the Riemann sum,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{t_{0}},\mu_{t_{1}})

\displaystyle\leq{{\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-t}}\mathrm{d}t\right)}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}

\displaystyle=2{{\left(\sqrt{1-t_{0}}-\sqrt{1-t_{1}}\right)}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}}.

By taking
t_{0}\rightarrow 0
and
t_{1}\rightarrow 1
, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leq 2\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\text{Lip}(\mathcal{M}):\\
\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1\end{subarray}}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right)}},

which is the desired conclusion.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Before proving the result, let us establish that for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)
,

\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\mathrm{d}x)}^{2}:=\sup_{g:\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}gf\mathrm{d}x\right)}}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(A.57)

This equality is a consequence of Definition 7.24 p.202 in
[
Rud91
]
for example, or can be read from the developments in Section 5.5.2 of
[
San15
]
. We provide a short proof of (
A.57
) below.

Let
f
be a function with
\int f\mathrm{d}x=0
.
Recall that
{{\left\|f\right\|}}_{H^{-1}(\mu)}=\sup{{\left\{{{\left|\int fg\mathrm{d}x\right|}}:{{\left\|\nabla g\right\|}}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)}\leq 1\right\}}}
.
First, for any function
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}}fg\mathrm{d}x\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}}((-\Delta)^{-1/2}f)((-\Delta)^{1/2}g)\mathrm{d}x\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)}\times\|(-\Delta)^{1/2}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x)}.

Besides,

\|(-\Delta)^{1/2}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathrm{d}x))}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}((-\Delta)^{1/2}g)((-\Delta)^{1/2}g)\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathcal{M}}g\Delta g\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore, by definition of,
\|\cdot\|_{H^{-1}(\mathrm{d}x)}
, we have:

\|f\|_{H^{-1}(\mathrm{d}x)}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathcal{M}}|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}f|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

Note also that equality is attained in Cauchy-Schwartz inequality if
(-\Delta)^{-1/2}f=\lambda(-\Delta)^{1/2}g
for some
\lambda>0
. Letting
g=\lambda(-\Delta)^{-1}f
with
\lambda=\|\nabla(-\Delta)^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}}^{-1}
gives (
A.57
).

Let us now prove Lemma
4.4
. From Theorem
A.1
above, with
\mu=f_{1}\mathrm{d}x
,
\nu=f_{2}\mathrm{d}x
and
f_{1}
bounded below by
f_{\min}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}\big(f_{1}\mathrm{d}x,f_{2}\mathrm{d}x\big)\leqslant

\displaystyle 2{{\left\|f_{1}-f_{2}\right\|}}_{H^{-1}(f_{1}\mathrm{d}x)}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\sup_{g:\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}f_{1}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g(f_{1}-f_{2})\mathrm{d}x\right)}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\sup_{g:\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}f_{\min}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g(f_{1}-f_{2})\mathrm{d}x\right)}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2f_{\min}^{-1/2}\sup_{g:\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1}{{\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}g(f_{1}-f_{2})\mathrm{d}x\right)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2f_{\min}^{-1/2}\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{M}}\big|(-\Delta)^{-1/2}(f_{1}-f_{2})\big|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}

where we have used (
A.57
) for the last equality. This concludes the proof.

A.2
Control of the bias term

In kernel density estimation, variance terms can be controlled with minimal assumptions on the kernel
K
(say, boundedness), whereas choosing a kernel having specific properties is required to control bias terms
[
Tsy09
]
. The situation is not different for the estimation of
\mu
: we are able to control the variance term
\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}_{T,h},\mu_{h})\right]}}
with few assumptions on
K
(see
Theorem

2.3
). However, controlling the bias requires the kernel
K
to be of sufficiently high order in the following sense.

Definition A.2

(Order of a kernel)
.

For a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha^{1},...,\alpha^{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{d}
, we denote
|\alpha|:=\alpha^{1}+\cdots+\alpha^{d}
,
z^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}

and
\partial^{\alpha}\mathbf{K}
the partial derivative of
\mathbf{K}
in the direction
\alpha
.

A function
\mathbf{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called kernel of order
r
if the function is of class
\mathcal{C}^{r}
, and
\mathbf{K}
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}\mathbf{K}(z)z^{\tilde{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}z=0,

for any multi-index
\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}
such that
|\alpha|<r
and
{{\left|\tilde{\alpha}\right|}}<r+|\alpha|
, with
{{\left|\alpha\right|}}>0
when
\tilde{\alpha}=0
.

When
K
is of order larger than
\ell+1
, we obtain a tight control of the bias following
[
Div22
]
.

Proposition A.3

(Bias term)
.

Let
K
be a kernel of order larger than
\ell+1
, and let
p
be a density of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with a finite Sobolev norm
\|p\|_{{}_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}}
. Then, for
h
small enough,

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)\leq\frac{c\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}h^{2\ell+2},

(A.58)

where
c
depends on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
.

Proof.

As
\mu
has a lower bounded density, it holds according to
Lemma

4.4
that

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu)\leq 4p_{\min}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}{{\left(p-p_{h}\right)}}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

whereas it is proved in
[
Div22
, Proposition 9]
that

\int_{\mathcal{M}}{{\left|{{\left(-\Delta\right)}}^{-1/2}{{\left(p-p_{h}\right)}}\right|}}^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq c\|p\|_{H^{\ell}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}h^{2\ell+2}

when
K
is a kernel of order larger than
\ell+1
, where
c
depends on
\mathcal{M}
and
K
.
∎

B.5
The ultracontractivity term

We give in this section an explicit control of the ultracontractivity constant introduced at the beginning of
Section

5

To this aim we need to introduce the iterated

.

Definition B.4
.

Given a differential operator
\mathcal{A}
, its iterated carré du champ
\Gamma_{2}
is defined as:

\Gamma_{2}(f,f):=\frac{1}{2}{{\left[\mathcal{A}\Gamma(f,f)-2\Gamma(\mathcal{A}f,f)\right]}},

where
\Gamma
is the carré du champ of
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})
be a positive density on
\mathcal{M}
with respect to the volume measure
\mathrm{d}x
, and
\mathcal{A}
a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-elliptic second-order differential operator, which is symmetric with respect to the probability measure
\mu=p\mathrm{d}x
.
Then, there is a constant
\kappa
, which can be negative, such that:

\Gamma_{2}(f,f)\geq\kappa\Gamma(f,f),

(B.64)

In particular, the associated semigroup
{{\left(P_{t}\right)}}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies, for all
t>0
,

\|P_{t}f\|_{\infty}\leq\exp{{\left[\frac{\kappa\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}}{2(e^{2\kappa t}-1)}\right]}}\times\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

(B.65)

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-elliptic operator of second-order, there is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-metric
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}
on
\mathcal{M}
such that
\Gamma(f,f)={{\left\langle\tilde{\nabla}f,\tilde{\nabla}f\right\rangle}}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}
, where
\tilde{\nabla}
is the gradient of the new metric (see
[
Hsu02
, eq. 1.3.3]
).
Hence, due to the symmetry of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to

,
\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\Delta}+\tilde{\nabla}{\ln(\tilde{p})}
, where
\tilde{\Delta}
is the Laplacian of the new metric and
\tilde{p}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}
, where
\mathrm{vol}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}
is the volume measure in the new metric.

Consequently, by
[
BGL14
, eq. C.5.3]
we have
\Gamma_{2}(f,f)=|\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}f|^{2}+{{\left(\mathrm{Ricc}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}-\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}\ln(\tilde{p})\right)}}{{\left(\tilde{\nabla}f,\tilde{\nabla}f\right)}}
, where

denotes the Ricci tensor.
Therefore, as in
[
BGL14
, eq C.6.3]
, (

) is equivalent to

\mathrm{Ricc}_{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}-\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}\ln(\tilde{p})\geq\kappa.

Hence, from the compactness of
\mathcal{M}
and the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
continuity of both
\mathcal{A}
and
p
, we have the desired conclusion for the first part.

For the second part, we have from the implication
(1)\rightarrow(3)
of Theorem 2.3.3 in
[
Wan14
]
with
p=2
, that for any
x
,

|P_{t}f(x)|^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|P_{t}f(x)|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\leq\int_{\mathcal{M}}P_{t}|f|^{2}(y)\exp{{\left[\frac{\kappa\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}}{e^{2\kappa t}-1}\right]}}\mu(\mathrm{d}y)=\\
\exp{{\left[\frac{\kappa\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}}{e^{2\kappa t}-1}\right]}}\int_{\mathcal{M}}P_{t}|f|^{2}(y)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)=\exp{{\left[\frac{\kappa\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{M})^{2}}{e^{2\kappa t}-1}\right]}}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)},

since
\mu
is the invariant measure of the underlying process. Therefore, we have the second inequality, which is the ultracontractivity of
\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Remark B.6
.

We note that for the operator
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
defined by (
1.1
) with
q\equiv 1
, there is no need to change the metric to obtain the result. Consequently, the constant
\kappa
appearing in
Lemma

B.5
depends only on
\mathcal{M}
, an upper bound on
\|p\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
and
p_{\min}
. This enables the choice of a uniform ultracontractivity constant
u_{\max}
for the minimax lower bound in
Section

6
.

B.6
Essentially self-adjoint operator

Although
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, this does not guarantee that its adjoint
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is symmetric unless
\mathcal{A}
is self-adjoint. The essential self-adjointness of
\mathcal{A}
means that its closure
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is self-adjoint, ensuring that the operator has a unique self-adjoint extension.

Proposition B.7
.

If
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{C}^{2}_{c}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathcal{M})\subset L^{2}(\mu)
is symmetric and the measure
\mu
has a strictly positive density on
\mathcal{M}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is essentially self-adjoint when considered as an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric on the Hilbert space
H=L^{2}(\mu)
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{A}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{c}(\mathcal{M}).

The closure
\overline{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow H
of
\mathcal{A}
generates a semigroup
(P_{t}^{(2)})_{t\geq 0}
on
H
, satisfying
P_{t}^{(2)}f=P_{t}f
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathcal{M})
(see
[
Ngu25
, Theorem 1.5.38]
).

The operator
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is symmetric, being the closure of a symmetric operator. Moreover, since
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
generates
(P_{t}^{(2)})_{t\geq 0}
, we have
P_{t}^{(2)}f\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\mathcal{A}})
for all
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathcal{M})
.

Consider the function

F(s)=\langle P_{s}^{(2)}f,P_{t-s}^{(2)}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)},

for fixed
t>0
and
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{c}(\mathcal{M})
. This function is constant in
s\in[0,t]
. Indeed, for
0<s<t
, we compute by symmetry of
\mathcal{A}

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F(s)=\langle\overline{\mathcal{A}}P_{s}^{(2)}f,P_{t-s}^{(2)}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}-\langle P_{s}^{(2)}f,\overline{\mathcal{A}}P_{t-s}^{(2)}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0.

This implies that

F(s)=F(0)=\langle f,P_{t}^{(2)}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\quad\text{for all }s\in[0,t].

Therefore, the semigroup
(P_{t}^{(2)})
is self-adjoint, meaning that
{{\left(P_{t}^{(2)}\right)}}^{*}=P_{t}^{(2)}
. Consequently, the generator
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is self-adjoint, and since
\mathcal{A}
is densely defined, it follows that
\mathcal{A}
is essentially self-adjoint.
∎

Appendix C
SDEs for the diffusions with generator
\mathcal{A}_{pq}

In this section, we give the SDEs satisfied by the diffusion processes of generator (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).
Recall that the Stratonovich integral (see
[
Pro04
, page 82]
) is defined as follows:

Definition C.1

(Stratonovich integral)
.

Let
X,Y
be two continuous real-valued semimartingales. The Stratonovich integral of
Y
with respect to
X
, denoted by
\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{s}
, is defined by

\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\circ\mathrm{d}X_{s}:=\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}X_{s}+\frac{1}{2}{{\left\langle Y,X\right\rangle}}_{t},

where the first term is the Itô integral of
Y
with respect to
X
and
{{\left\langle.,.\right\rangle}}
is the bracket process (also known as the quadratic covariation process).

With the Stratonovich integral, the classical Itô formula can then be written is the following way (see
[
Pro04
, Theorems 20-21, pages 277-278]
), for a continuous
d
-dimensional semimartingale
X
and a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
:
f(X)
is a semimartingale and

f(X_{t})-f(X_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{i}}(X_{s})\circ\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{s}.

(C.67)

Because of this chain rule, the Stratonovich integral is better fitted to differential calculus on manifolds than the usual Itô integral.

We recall that a vector field
V
on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a family
{{\left\{V(x)\right\}}}_{x\in\mathcal{M}}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{M}
,
V(x)\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
(see for e.g.
[
Lee03
, Chapter 4]
). In local coordinates
(x^{1},x^{2},...,x^{d})
, a smooth vector field
V
can be represented as

V(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}V^{i}(x)\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{x},

where
V^{1},\ldots,V^{d}
are real smooth functions on the domain of the local coordinate system, and where
{{\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right\}}}_{1\leq i\leq d}
denotes a basis of
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition C.2

(Theorem 1.2.9 in
[
Hsu02
]
)
.

Let
l\geq 1
. Consider the Stratonovich SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{l}V_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t}+V_{0}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t

(C.68)

where

(V_{\alpha})_{0\leq\alpha\leq l}
are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
vector fields and
B=(B^{\alpha})_{1\leq\alpha\leq l}
is the standard
l
-dimensional Brownian motion.
Then, there exists a unique strong solution to (
C.68
) (up to explosion time) whose infinitesimal generator is

\mathcal{A}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{l}{{\left(V_{\alpha}^{2}f\right)}}(x)+(V_{0}f)(x),

where
{{\left(V_{\alpha}^{2}f\right)}}(x):={{\left(V_{\alpha}{{\left(V_{\alpha}f\right)}}\right)}}(x)
, and whose carré du champ operator is given by
\Gamma(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{l}V_{\alpha}(f)V_{\alpha}(g)
.

Let
{{\left\{e_{\alpha}\right\}}}_{1\leq\alpha\leq m}
be an orthonormal basis on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For each
x\in\mathcal{M}
, we consider
P_{\alpha}(x)
the orthogonal projection of
e_{\alpha}
to
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. Let us note that
P_{\alpha}
is a vector field on
\mathcal{M}
.
In a local coordinate system
(x^{1},x^{2},...,x^{d})
,

P_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}P^{i}_{\alpha}(x)\left.{\partial\over\partial x^{i}}\right|_{x}.

Then the Laplace-Beltrami operator satisfies (see
[
Hsu02
, Theorem 3.1.4]
)

\Delta=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P^{2}_{\alpha}.

(C.69)

Remark that for two real-valued functions of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
\mathcal{M}
, we have:

\langle\nabla f,\nabla h\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}(P_{\alpha}f)(P_{\alpha}h).

(C.70)

Then, as an application of Proposition
C.2
, there exists a unique strong solution starting at
x\in\mathcal{M}
to the following SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\sqrt{2q(X_{t})}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}{{\left(\frac{1}{2}{{\left(P_{\alpha}q\right)}}(X_{t})+q{{\left(P_{\alpha}(\ln p)\right)}}(X_{t})\right)}}{{\left(P_{\alpha}f\right)}}(X_{t})

(C.71)

for
(B^{\alpha})_{1\leq\alpha\leq m}
independent euclidean 1-dimensional Brownian motions, and whose infinitesimal generator is

\mathcal{A}_{pq}f=q\Delta f+{{\left\langle q\nabla\ln(pq),\nabla f\right\rangle}}.

(C.72)

In the particular case where
q\equiv 1
, we deduce
that the unique solution to the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}{{\left(\ln p\right)}}(X_{t})P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t,

(C.73)

has the infinitesimal generator

\mathcal{L}f=\Delta f+{{\left\langle\nabla\ln p,\nabla f\right\rangle}}.

Proof of (
C.72
).

To compute the infinitesimal generator of (
C.71
), we apply Proposition
C.2
with

V_{\alpha}=\sqrt{2q}P_{\alpha}
and
V_{0}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}{{\left(\frac{1}{2}{{\left(P_{\alpha}q\right)}}+q{{\left(P_{\alpha}(\ln p)\right)}}\right)}}P_{\alpha}
.
From this proposition, we know that the generator of
X
is, for a test function
f
of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{pq}f(x)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}(V^{2}_{\alpha}f)(x)+(V_{0}f)(x).

We have:

\displaystyle{{\left(V_{\alpha}^{2}f\right)}}(x)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{q}P_{\alpha}(\sqrt{q}{{\left(P_{\alpha}f\right)}})(x)=2q(x){{\left(P_{\alpha}^{2}f\right)}}(x)+{{\left(P_{\alpha}q\right)}}(x){{\left(P_{\alpha}f\right)}}(x)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{pq}f(x)

\displaystyle=q(x)\Delta f(x)+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}{{\left(P_{\alpha}q\right)}}(x){{\left(P_{\alpha}f\right)}}(x)+q\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}{{\left(P_{\alpha}(\ln p)\right)}}P_{\alpha}.

We conclude thanks to (
C.70
).
∎

At last, since
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
is self-adjoint with respect to
\mu
, we deduce the following proposition.

Proposition C.3
.

The measure
\mu=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
is invariant for
\mathcal{A}_{pq}
.

Appendix D
Kullback-Leibler divergence of path space measures on manifolds

In this section, the operator
\mathcal{L}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{p}
to highlight its dependence with respect to the density
p
of the measure
\mu
.
Let us denote by
{\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}
the probability measure on
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{M})
, given by the distribution of the diffusion
with generator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
, defined for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M})
by:

\mathcal{L}_{p}f=\Delta f+\langle\nabla f,\nabla\ln p\rangle,

and starting from its invariant measure
\mu=p\mathrm{d}x
.

We will denote by
{\mathbb{E}}_{(p,T)}
the expectation in the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}
and we define the Kullback-Leibler divergence as:

\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}||{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)})={\mathbb{E}}_{(p,T)}{{\left[\ln\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}}{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)}}\right]}}

(D.74)

where
\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}/\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)}
stands for the density of
{\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}
with respect to the measure
{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)}
.

Theorem D.1
.

For any two
\mathcal{C}^{1}
strictly positive probability densities
p
and
q
on
\mathcal{M}
,

\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}||{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)})=\frac{T}{4}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\|\nabla\ln p-\nabla\ln q\|^{2}p^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(D.75)

The proof of
Theorem

D.1
relies crucially on Girsanov’s theorem.

Proposition D.2

(Girsanov’s theorem for embedded manifolds)
.

Consider two continuous tangent vector fields
Z_{1},Z_{2}
that are tangent to
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the Stratonovich SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=Z_{2}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathcal{M},

where
B=(B^{\alpha})_{1\leq\alpha\leq m}
is a
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}
, and
(P_{\alpha})_{1\leq\alpha\leq m}
is the tangent projection of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
on
\mathcal{M}
(see Section
C
).

Then, defining for any
T>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{T}=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Z_{2}-Z_{1})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T}\|Z_{1}(X_{t})-Z_{2}(X_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{d}t\right),

(D.76)

it follows that:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}_{T}]=1

and under
\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{E}_{T}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}
,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a solution to the SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=Z_{1}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}^{\alpha}_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathcal{M},

with
\widetilde{B}
being a Brownian motion under
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof for Proposition
D.2
.

Let
T>0
. The first part follows from the fact that the Itô integral
\int_{0}^{T}(Z_{2}-Z_{1})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t}
is a local martingale with bounded quadratic variation (since the
Z_{i}
’s are continuous and
\mathcal{M}
is compact, hence the
Z_{i}
s are bounded on
\mathcal{M}
).
For the second part, by Girsanov’s theorem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the stochastic process
(\widetilde{B}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
defined by:

\widetilde{B}_{t}=B_{t}+\int_{0}^{T}u(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t,

with
u(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Z_{2}-Z_{1})(x)
, is a standard Brownian motion under
\mathbb{Q}
.
Besides, the Stratonovich SDE for
X_{t}
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{2}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{2}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\left(\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}^{\alpha}_{t}-u^{\alpha}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}^{\alpha}_{t}+(Z_{2}(X_{t})-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\sqrt{2}u^{\alpha}(X_{t})P_{\alpha}(X_{t}))\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=Z_{1}(X_{t})\ \mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}(X_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}^{\alpha}_{t},

hence, we imply the desired conclusion.
∎

Let us now prove Theorem
D.1
.

Proof of Theorem
D.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a solution to the SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\ln p(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}\circ\mathrm{d}B^{\alpha}_{t},

with
X_{0}
uniform on
\mathcal{M}
and with
B
being a
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion under some probability space
\mathbb{P}
. The infinitesimal generator of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
\mathcal{L}_{p}
by (
C.73
).
By
Proposition

D.2
,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is also a solution to the SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\ln q(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}P_{\alpha}\circ\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}^{\alpha}_{t},

with
X_{0}
uniform on
\mathcal{M}
, and
where
\widetilde{B}
is a
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion under the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
defined by
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\mathcal{E}_{T}
with

\mathcal{E}_{T}=\exp\left(-M_{T}+\frac{1}{2}\langle M\rangle_{T}\right),\quad M_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\nabla\ln p-\nabla\ln q\right)(X_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

Therefore, by the definition of Kullback-Leibler divergence,

\mathrm{KL}({\mathbf{P}}_{(p,T)}||{\mathbf{P}}_{(q,T)})=\mathbb{E}{{\left[\ln\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right]}}=\mathbb{E}{{\left[-M_{T}+\frac{1}{2}\langle M\rangle_{T}\right]}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}{{\left[\langle M\rangle_{T}\right]}}.

We have the desired conclusion by computing
\mathbb{E}{{\left[\langle M\rangle_{T}\right]}}
, because the distribution of
X_{t}
is
\mu=p\mathrm{d}x
at each time
t\geq 0
.
∎
