A long-term (2000–2022), high-resolution (0.005°) aboveground biomass dataset of global grasslands
Authors: Pinzhen Wu, Qingling Sun, Daju Wang, Jiahe Si, Baolin Li, Xuetong Zhao, Siyu Zhu, Yong Li, Xiuzhi Chen, Wenping Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrasslands are critical to the global carbon cycle and support livestock production, yet long-term, high-resolution global datasets of grassland aboveground biomass (AGB) remain scarce, since existing products are often spatially coarse, temporally discontinuous, or regionally limited. Here, we present a global grassland AGB dataset spanning 2000–2022 at 0.005° spatial resolution. The dataset was generated by employing an optimized random forest model, which was trained on 11,265 spatially aggregated samples with global distribution. The multi-source predictors included Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), climate variables, atmospheric CO2 concentration, and topographical factors. At the global scale, the model achieved an R2 of 0.60 for both validation and independent testing, with RMSEs of 85.97 and 93.69 g m−2, respectively. When evaluated across all samples, the model exhibited an R2 of 0.93 and an RMSE of 51.85 g m−2. Comparison with other datasets showed its comparable value distributions and interannual trajectories, and closer agreement with field observations. This dataset provides a foundation for advancing research on global grassland carbon dynamics and management practices.

Background & Summary

Grassland ecosystems are a critical component of the terrestrial biosphere, covering 20–40% of the global land surface1,2. As an important part of the global carbon cycle, grasslands store 10–30% of soil organic carbon and over 10% of terrestrial biomass carbon3,4, with a substantial carbon sequestration rate of 0.5 Pg C yr−15,6. Beyond their vital ecological functions, grasslands directly support the livelihoods and food security of over 180 million people worldwide through livestock grazing7. Aboveground biomass (AGB) serves as a key indicator of grassland productivity and carrying capacity, which reflects the growth status, ecological functional support capacity, and environmental response and adaptability of grasslands8. Consequently, accurate, large-scale, and continuous monitoring of grassland AGB is fundamental for estimating the terrestrial ecosystem carbon budgets, guiding sustainable grassland management and development of livestock husbandry, and understanding response of grasslands to climate change9,10.

Currently, there are two main methods to obtain grassland AGB, including field measurements and remote sensing inversion. Traditional field measurements, while accurate at the site scale, are labor-intensive and impractical for continuous monitoring over large areas11. In contrast, remote sensing has revolutionized the estimation of AGB by enabling the acquisition of large-scale vegetation data to support regional and global assessments12–14. Specifically, remote sensing data has enabled the derivation of vegetation indices (VIs), which have become fundamental predictors in grassland AGB estimation models10,15–17. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is the most widely used proxy9,18–20, emphasizing green vegetation while minimizing non-vegetative influences21–23. Early research primarily used statistical regression models to establish empirical regressions between VIs and AGB15,16. Nevertheless, such statistical regressions are often region-specific and have limited capacity to capture the complex, nonlinear relationship between spectral signals and grassland biomass24. To address this limitation, machine learning algorithms have been adopted for their superior ability to capture complex, nonlinear interactions and reduce sensitivity to collinearity and noise13,14,25,26. Among machine learning algorithms, Random Forest (RF)27 can effectively characterize complex ecological relationships, tolerate multicollinearity, and provide favorable generalization, with minimal preprocessing demands28,29. Furthermore, its ability to quantify variable importance facilitates the identification of dominant predictive factors, even for noisy datasets27. Previous studies have demonstrated the advantages of RF for grassland AGB estimation at regional scales17,30,31.

Despite advances in acquisition methods, existing data products remain insufficient to provide spatially explicit, temporally continuous representations of global grassland AGB because they are often spatially coarse, temporally discontinuous, or restricted to specific regions (Table 1). Numerous regional AGB datasets have been developed20,32–34, yet they are typically developed for specific regions, limiting their applicability to global-scale analyses. Globally, datasets explicitly dedicated to grassland AGB remain scarce. Early efforts, such as the Grassland NDVI-Biomass (GNB) model proposed by Xia et al.9, estimate grassland AGB based on an empirical relationship with NDVI. These products were generally developed using a small amount of ground observations with coarse spatial resolutions (8 km). The coarse spatial resolution and static formulation restrict representation of fine-scale heterogeneity and interannual variability in grassland AGB. More recent global biomass products generally target multiple vegetation types rather than focusing specifically on grasslands. For instance, the harmonized global biomass map of Spawn et al.35,36 integrates multiple vegetation-type-specific products derived from different data sources and modeling strategies to generate a single-year global snapshot. Such differences in data sources, model structures, and parameterization schemes may introduce regional inconsistencies and limit the internal comparability of grassland AGB estimates. Satellite-based products derived from active LiDAR and microwave sensors have further advanced global biomass estimation, such as the Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI)37 and Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity (SMOS) AGB products38. However, these products primarily target forests and offer short, discontinuous temporal coverage, limiting its applicability for long-term grassland monitoring. Recently, emerging high-resolution datasets—such as the 10-m global aboveground biomass density (AGBD) dataset39 developed via multi-model deep learning—offer improved spatial detail yet remain limited in temporal coverage and geographic continuity. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for a long-term, temporally continuous, and relatively high-resolution global grassland AGB dataset to support carbon budget estimation, grassland carrying capacity assessment, and evaluation of grassland ecosystem responses to climate change.

To narrow the aforementioned data gap, we developed a long-term (2000–2022), relatively high-resolution (0.005°) annual global grassland AGB dataset based on a RF approach. In this study, an optimized RF model for simulating global grassland AGB was constructed, incorporating variable selection and sample filtering procedures. Model performance was evaluated using cross-validation folds and an independent test set to assess its generalization capability. Multi-source predictors describing vegetation activity, climate and soil conditions, atmospheric CO2 concentration, and topography were accounted for in predicting global grassland AGB. Following sample filtering, 11,265 globally distributed and spatially aggregated field AGB samples were used for model training. The trained optimal RF model was then applied to annual predictor stacks to generate continuous global grassland AGB maps spanning the time period 2000–2022. This newly developed dataset provides a consistent and spatially detailed foundation for carbon stock assessments, grassland management practices, and studies on grassland ecosystem responses to climate change.

Methods

Framework

This study utilized an optimized RF model to develop a dataset of global grassland AGB for 2000–2022 at 0.005° resolution. The predictor variables include satellite-derived vegetation activity, climate variables, atmospheric CO2 concentration, and topography. The overall workflow comprises three key steps: (1) compilation and preprocessing of the observed AGB and environmental predictor data, (2) construction and optimization of the RF model, and (3) application of the optimal RF model to generate global grassland AGB maps, followed by spatiotemporal pattern analyses and comparison with other datasets (Fig. 1).

Framework for the estimation of global grassland AGB in this study.

RF model construction and AGB map generation

Data compilation and preprocessing

A total of 26 candidate predictors were compiled from multi-source remote sensing and environmental datasets, including VIs, climate variables (air temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, soil temperature, soil moisture, and vapor pressure deficit), atmospheric CO2 concentration, topographic factors (DEM and slope), and wetland attributes. All predictors were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.005° × 0.005° using bilinear interpolation. In parallel, field-measured grassland AGB from multiple sources were compiled and harmonized (see Field-measured AGB subsection). The original field AGB measurements were spatially averaged within 0.005° grid cells and matched to the corresponding gridded environmental data, forming an initial sample pool for subsequent model construction and optimization.

Construction and optimization of the RF model

The observed AGB was first natural log-transformed (ln) to reduce its data range and variability, leading to more stable and efficient predictions40,41, though RF model is distribution-free. The initial sample pool was then randomly divided into two subsets: 85% of all samples were used for cross-validation, and the remaining 15% were reserved for independent testing42. Cross-validation was conducted using 10-fold stratified sampling, with AGB divided into five quantile bins to maintain balanced distributions across folds43,44. The independent test set was used exclusively to assess the generalization capability of the models.

Variable selection was performed to identify an optimal predictor subset for model construction. First, to avoid strong multicollinearity within predictor subsets, pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated. Predictor pairs with strong collinearity (|r| ≥ 0.8) were not included in the same subset45,46. Second, multiple non-collinear predictor subsets were generated (|r| < 0.8 for all variable pairs within each subset). Each subset was used to construct a distinct RF model. Model performance was evaluated using cross-validation and an independent test set, quantified by the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE). The predictor subset achieving the relatively highest R2 and lowest RMSE in both cross-validation and independent testing was selected, and the corresponding RF model was designated as the optimal RF model. This procedure resulted in 10 final predictors, ranked by their relative importance in the optimal RF model: annual total precipitation, growing season average NDVI (NDVIg), annual mean atmospheric CO2 concentration, annual mean minimum air temperature, annual mean surface downward solar radiation, annual mean soil temperature, elevation (DEM), annual mean soil moisture, annual mean surface net solar radiation, and slope.

Sample filtering was further conducted to align field AGB samples with remotely sensed vegetation signals by excluding samples with NDVIg < 0.1, as such low values generally correspond to bare soil or extremely sparse vegetation and may introduce non-grass noise into model training47. After variable selection and sample filtering, a total of 11,265 spatially aggregated field AGB samples were retained. These samples were used to train the optimal RF model, which was subsequently applied to generate the global grassland AGB maps. All model predictions (ln(AGB)) were back-transformed to the original biomass scale using the exponential function.

AGB map generation

The trained optimal RF model was applied pixel-by-pixel to the annual global predictor stacks (encompassing 10 selected predictors) for the period from 2000 to 2022. Predictions were restricted to pixels classified as grassland in the corresponding annual MODIS land cover product, ultimately yielding a set of annual global grassland AGB maps with a spatial resolution of 0.005° × 0.005°.

Assessment of data accuracy

The model accuracy was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r, Eq. 1), the coefficient of determination (R2, Eq. 2), the root mean square error (RMSE, Eq. 3), and the linear regression slope (Slope, Eq. 4) between estimated and observed AGB values. r quantifies the strength of linear correlation between estimated and observed AGB, and is used exclusively to assess the model’s ability to capture interannual AGB dynamics. The statistical significance of r was evaluated using two-tailed Pearson correlation tests48. r values closer to 1.0 indicate better consistency in observed and estimated temporal trends49,50. R2 evaluates the goodness of fit by quantifying the proportion of variance in observed AGB explained by the model. RMSE measures the magnitude of prediction errors in the original AGB units (g m−2). The linear regression slope indicates systematic bias, with values close to 1.0 representing unbiased estimates49,50. To further assess the model’s ability to capture temporal dynamics in grassland AGB, a dedicated evaluation was conducted using long-term continuous field observations. Sites with ≥8 consecutive years of AGB measurements were screened from the full set of 11,265 samples, with corresponding AGB predictions extracted from the optimal RF model outputs based on all 11,265 samples. This yielded 42 qualified sites and 515 site-year samples. The accuracy of temporal simulation was quantified primarily using r, supplemented with R2, RMSE, and regression slope between paired estimated and observed annual AGB values.

In addition, the developed dataset was compared with existing grassland AGB products (Table 1) to further evaluate data reliability and consistency. All datasets were resampled to a common 0.005° spatial resolution and constrained to an identical MODIS grassland mask to ensure spatial consistency. We used three complementary comparisons: multi-year pixel-level AGB value distributions, regionally averaged annual time series, and pixel-level comparisons with field AGB samples. Comparison of pixel-level AGB value distributions across overlapping spatial domains and time periods was performed using multi-year averages to assess agreement in central tendencies and overall magnitude. Temporal comparison used regionally averaged annual AGB time series to evaluate the consistency of temporal trajectories and interannual variability. Finally, pixel-level AGB values were extracted from each dataset at the locations of the compiled, spatially aggregated field AGB samples used in this study and compared with these observations, providing a consistent and comparable benchmark across all products.

Spatiotemporal analysis

Spatial patterns and temporal trends of grassland AGB from 2000 to 2022 were analyzed globally and across five Köppen-Geiger (KG) climate zones: tropical, arid, temperate, cold, and polar regions51,52. To characterize long-term spatial distribution, pixel-level multi-year average AGB maps were calculated for the full period and two sub-periods (2000–2011 and 2012–2022). Regional averages were calculated for each KG climate zone. Temporal trends in annual grassland AGB were quantified using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression on the annual mean AGB time series53. The regression slope represents the magnitude and direction of long-term AGB change, and statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed tests48. To identify potential shifts in temporal trajectories, abrupt changes were detected using a sliding window piecewise linear regression (SWPLR) approach with a 6-year window size54,55.

Data Source

Global grassland mask

The spatial extent of global grasslands was acquired using the Terra and Aqua combined Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Land Cover Type product (MCD12Q1, Version 6.1)56 based on the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification scheme57. For each year, pixels classified as grasslands (IGBP class 10) were extracted to generate an annual global grassland mask. According to this product, grasslands accounted for an average of 23.77% of the global ice-free land area during 2000–2022, with relatively small interannual variations. Annual grassland coverage generally ranged between 23.43% and 24.51%, showing a gradual increase during the early 2000s, a pronounced peak around 2010, and relatively stable fluctuations thereafter. The resulting mask encompassed major grassland biomes worldwide, including tropical and subtropical savannas (e.g., African savannas and South American Cerrado), temperate grasslands (e.g., prairies, steppes, and pampas), high-latitude tundra, as well as montane grasslands (e.g., alpine meadows). Together, these grassland ecosystems span a wide range of climatic and topographic conditions, from humid tropical to arid and semi-arid regions, and from low-latitude warm environments to high-latitude cold zones.

Satellite-based NDVI data

NDVI was derived from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Terra MOD09A1 Version 6.1 surface reflectance product58, which provides atmospherically corrected 8-day composite reflectance at 500 m spatial resolution. It was calculated based on surface spectral reflectance for the near-infrared (NIR, 841–876 nm) and red (Red, 620–670 nm) bands:

For each pixel and year, a growing season average NDVI (NDVIg) was derived to represent seasonal vegetation activity relevant to biomass accumulation. The growing season was identified using a globally consistent moving-window approach. Specifically, the 8-day composite period with the maximum NDVI value was identified as the seasonal peak59,60. NDVI values within a temporal window extending 14 composite periods before and 6 periods after the peak were averaged to derive NDVIg60–62, informed by the grassland phenological metrics (start, peak, and end of the growing season) from Wang et al.63 and adjusted to the MODIS 8-day compositing scheme. The MODIS MCD12Q2 vegetation phenology product was also evaluated as an alternative but not adopted primarily due to missing or abnormal values in approximately 20% of global grassland pixels.

Environmental variables

Multi-source environmental data were integrated as the model predictors (Table 2). Annual total precipitation was obtained from the Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) Integrated Multi-satellite Retrievals for GPM (IMERG) Final Run product64, which provides daily accumulated precipitation derived from half-hourly estimates with a spatial resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°. Annual mean minimum air temperature, soil temperature, soil moisture, surface downward solar radiation, and surface net solar radiation were obtained from the ERA5-Land hourly reanalysis data65 at 0.1° spatial resolution, the land component of the fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). Soil temperature and soil moisture (i.e., volumetric soil water) represented mean conditions over the 0–28 cm soil layer, calculated as the average of layer 1 (0–7 cm) and layer 2 (7–28 cm). Hourly ERA5-Land variables were aggregated to annual values prior to model input, with daily means used for most variables and daily minima used for minimum air temperature.

Annual mean atmospheric CO2 concentration was derived using a combination of satellite-based observations and model-based scaling. For 2018–2022, annual CO2 concentration fields were directly obtained from a daily column-averaged CO2 dataset66 integrating observations from the Orbiting Carbon Observatory-3 (OCO-3), the Greenhouse Gas Observing Satellite (GOSAT), and the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) at 0.1° spatial resolution. Daily CO2 values were aggregated to annual means. To extend the CO2 record back to 2000, the spatial pattern for CO2 in 2018 was scaled backward using the annual global average atmospheric CO2 time series from the Trends and Drivers of the Regional Scale Sources and Sinks of Carbon Dioxide (TRENDY) model ensemble67. The spatial pattern of CO2 in 2018 was used as a baseline to reconstruct annual fields for 2000–2017. For each year, values were adjusted proportionally to match the global average CO2 concentration of the target year. This procedure preserves observed large-scale spatial gradients while maintaining temporal consistency across the study period.

Topographic variables were derived from the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans (GEBCO) 2024 Grid at 15 arc-seconds (i.e., approximately 500 m at the equator) resolution68. Slope was calculated from the DEM using the 3D Analyst Tools in ArcGIS. Finally, all environmental variables, together with NDVIg, were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.005° × 0.005°.

Field-measured AGB

Field-measured AGB served as the dependent variable for model training and validation. A total of 21,246 original field AGB measurements were compiled from four sources: (1) original field investigations, (2) the National Grassland Inventory, (3) published literature, and (4) public data platforms, including China Scientific Data (CSD), the National Ecosystem Science Data Center (NESDC), the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center (TPDC), the National Cryosphere Desert Data Center (NCDC), and international repositories (e.g., Dryad, Environmental Data Initiative, the Polar Terrestrial Environmental Systems, the Terrestrial Ecosystem Research Network, and the Arctic Data Center). The full list of sources for the compiled original field AGB measurements is available at Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19664562. All measurements were harmonized to a common unit of g m−2, with carbon density values converted to biomass using a factor of 0.45. After data processing and sample filtering (see Methods), the final sample set comprised 11,265 spatially aggregated field AGB samples. Spatially, the samples span five KG climate zones (Fig. 2). Most samples are located in cold (4,454 samples, 39.54%), arid (3,529 samples, 31.33%), and polar (2,694 samples, 23.91%) regions, followed by temperate (479 samples, 4.25%) and tropical (109 samples, 0.97%) regions. Temporally, these samples cover all years from 2000 to 2022. The annual sample size ranges from 16 (0.14%) in 2022 to 7056 (62.64%) in 2020, with relatively more samples in 2005 (569, 5.05%), 2009 (438, 3.89%), and 2011 (372, 3.30%).

Data Records

The long-term (2000–2022), high-resolution (0.005°) aboveground biomass dataset of global grasslands generated in this study is publicly available at Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1804416369. The dataset is provided in GeoTIFF format with a geographic coordinate system of WGS84 (EPSG: 4326). The dataset consists of 23 annual files covering the period 2000–2022. Each file is named AGB_YYYY.tif, where YYYY denotes the year. Each GeoTIFF contains a single band representing annual grassland AGB at a 0.005° (i.e., approximately 500 m at the equator) spatial resolution, with pixel values expressed in grams per square meter (g m−2). Annual AGB values represent year-specific peak aboveground biomass. Pixels outside the grassland mask are assigned NoData values. A complete table of all candidate predictors considered during model construction is provided in the Zenodo repository: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19664562.

Technical Validation

Performance of the optimal RF model

The optimal RF model demonstrated consistent performance on both the cross-validation folds and the independent test samples (Fig. 3a). For the cross-validation folds, the model achieved an R2 of 0.60, an RMSE of 85.97 g m−2, and a regression slope of 0.56. Comparable accuracy was obtained for the independent test samples (R2 = 0.60, RMSE = 93.69 g m−2, Slope = 0.55), indicating stable generalization to unseen observations. When all retained samples were evaluated together, the model yielded an R2 of 0.93, an RMSE of 51.85 g m−2, and a regression slope of 0.76 (Fig. 3b). The overall agreement between estimated and observed AGB values across the entire biomass gradient supports the reliability of the optimal RF model for global grassland AGB estimation.

The model also well reproduced interannual AGB dynamics across long-term observation sites. For the 42 sites and 515 site-year samples, the model achieved a mean R2 of 0.89, a mean RMSE of 60.69 g m−2, and a mean regression slope of 0.65. All correlations were positive and statistically significant (p < 0.05). There were 34 (80.95%) sites showing r > 0.9 (Fig. 4a). The sites with lower r values (r < 0.8) were distributed across temperate grasslands (the central United States, southeastern Australia, and northern China) and alpine grasslands of the Tibetan Plateau. Overall, the time-series comparison demonstrated that the model reliably captured interannual trends at most sites, although larger deviations occurred under high-AGB conditions and during years with pronounced fluctuations (Fig. 4b).

Additionally, climate zone-specific evaluations further confirmed stable predictive performance across all zones: R2 varied from 0.87 (arid) to 0.96 (tropical), regression slopes ranged from 0.68 (tropical/arid) to 0.76 (cold/polar), and RMSE varied from 36.25 g m−2 (polar) to 143.45 g m−2 (tropical) (Fig. 5). Despite the highest R2, tropical regions showed the largest RMSE, likely due to their wider AGB range and limited sample representation (Fig. 5b). Notably, arid regions showed the lowest R2 and regression slope, which might be associated with greater environmental heterogeneity and stronger impacts from unmodeled disturbances (e.g., grazing and water limitation) in these areas70 (Fig. 5c).

Comparison with other datasets

The dataset developed in this study exhibited generally consistent value distributions and temporal trajectories with existing regional and global grassland AGB products, and demonstrated closer agreement with field observations (Fig. 6). For pixel-level AGB value distributions, multi-year average AGB across overlapping spatial domains and temporal periods showed broadly comparable central tendencies across most products (Fig. 6a). For regional products, relative differences, defined as the percentage deviation from each reference product ranged from −17.94% to 9.32%. The harmonized global biomass map by Spawn et al.36 yielded substantially higher estimates at the global scale, with a relative difference of −48.45%. This discrepancy is consistent with its representation of total vegetation biomass and vegetation-type allocation schemes, which may introduce mixed-pixel effects and include woody components within grassland-dominated regions.

At the temporal scale, regionally averaged annual AGB time series from this dataset and the reference products showed consistent magnitudes and interannual variability across their overlapping domains and periods (Fig. 6b). Strong agreement was observed on the Tibetan Plateau (TP) for 2014–2020 relative to Zhu & Luo34, as well as in Mongolia (Yan et al.)32 and China (Jiao et al.)20. By contrast, larger discrepancies occurred over the TP for 2000–2009 compared with Jin et al.33, characterized by divergent temporal trends and differences in AGB magnitude. These variations likely stemmed from disparities in input data, model structures, and sparse in situ observations during the early satellite era. Temporal trajectories became increasingly consistent after approximately 2009.

Finally, pixel-level comparison between estimated AGB values and spatially aggregated field observations revealed stronger predictive performance for the dataset developed in this study (Fig. 6c). Across all regions and biomass ranges, this dataset achieved higher R2 and lower RMSE values relative to reference products. Regression slopes were generally closer to unity, indicating reduced systematic bias across low to high AGB ranges. While Spawn et al.36 exhibited a regression slope nearer to 1.0, its lower R2 value implied greater pixel-level prediction uncertainty. Performance disparities among products were more pronounced at higher AGB values, as evidenced by wider scatter around the 1:1 line.

Spatial distribution of global grassland AGB

The dataset captured large-scale latitudinal gradients and pronounced spatial heterogeneity in grassland AGB across continents, climate zones, and topographic settings (Fig. 7). At the global scale, grassland AGB exhibited a pronounced latitudinal gradient, with higher values at low latitudes and progressively lower values toward high latitudes. High AGB was mainly concentrated in tropical savannas, whereas cold and polar tundra regions consistently showed low AGB (Fig. 7a,c,e). At the continental scale, elevated AGB occurred in South America, central and western Africa, and northern Australia, where pixels frequently exceeded 400 g m−2 and locally surpassed 1000 g m−2. Relatively high values were also observed in the eastern Great Plains of North America, where tallgrass prairies exhibit higher AGB than other grasslands at similar latitudes. In contrast, arid and semi-arid regions across Central Asia, the Middle East, and inland Australia generally showed low AGB, generally below 250 g m−2. Temperate grasslands in Eurasia and North America exhibited intermediate levels. The Tibetan Plateau formed a distinct low-AGB area despite its mid-latitude location, associated with high elevation and low temperatures. High-latitude grasslands across northern Eurasia, Alaska, and the Arctic consistently showed the lowest values (<100 g m−2).

Averaged across the full period (2000–2022), the global average grassland AGB was 178.39 ± 155.61 g m−2 (Fig. 7b), indicating substantial spatial variability. Tropical grasslands exhibited the highest average AGB (446.77 ± 212.06 g m−2), followed by temperate grasslands (306.81 ± 112.75 g m−2). Arid regions showed lower AGB (132.91 ± 73.03 g m−2). Cold and polar regions had the lowest values (120.13 ± 74.85 g m−2 and 84.94 ± 36.62 g m−2, respectively). Large spatial standard deviations within each climate zone indicate pronounced heterogeneity within each climate zone. Dividing the study period into two sub-periods, the relative ranking among climate zones remained consistent, whereas the regional average AGB differed between the two periods (Fig. 7c–f). The global average AGB decreased from 179.87 ± 164.62 g m−2 in 2000–2011 to 169.66 ± 141.04 g m−2 in 2012–2022, with reductions observed in tropical, temperate, and arid regions. Cold regions showed minimal change, while polar regions saw a slight increase.

Temporal change of global grassland AGB

Over the period 2000–2022, global grassland AGB exhibited a weak overall decline, indicating limited net change over the full study period (Fig. 8a). Across the KG climate zones, long-term trends differed in both magnitude and direction. Tropical grasslands exhibited a significant decrease (Fig. 8b), whereas polar grasslands showed a significant increase (Fig. 8f). In contrast, arid, temperate, and cold regions exhibited weaker, non-significant long-term trends (Fig. 8c–e).

Temporal changes in global and climate-zone grassland AGB from 2000 to 2022. The solid lines indicate fitted trends, with shaded areas representing 95% confidence intervals. Changes in trend direction and magnitude reflect detected significant changepoints.

The dataset effectively captures large-scale temporal variability and climate-related shifts in global grassland biomass, as evidenced by the detected changepoints. These changepoints reveal spatially heterogeneous temporal dynamics over the past two decades, with widespread AGB declines in low- and mid-latitude regions and increases in high-latitude regions. Globally, AGB increased moderately before 2011 but declined afterward (Fig. 8a). This transition temporally coincided with the prolonged 2010–2012 La Niña period71 and a concurrent slowdown in global surface temperature rise71,72. Regionally, tropical grasslands exhibited a pronounced decline after 2012 (Fig. 8b), broadly aligning with the 2010–2012 La Niña and widespread drought conditions reported across tropical grassland and savanna regions73,74. Arid grasslands showed a similar transition around 2010, from a modest rise to decline (Fig. 8c), temporally consistent with enhanced drought conditions associated with the 2009–2010 El Niño75–78. Temperate grasslands exhibited a transition around 2010, shifting from an increasing trend to a rapid decline (Fig. 8d), roughly coinciding with a period characterized by widespread mid-latitude climate extremes, such as extreme heatwaves79–81. Cold grasslands increased before 2015 but declined afterward (Fig. 8e), corresponding with the strong 2015–2016 El Niño82,83. In contrast, polar grasslands remained stable prior to 2016 but increased markedly thereafter (Fig. 8f), coinciding with enhanced Arctic warming84,85 and continued reductions in sea-ice extent86.

Potential implications of the dataset

This dataset provides a consistent basis for quantifying global grassland carbon stocks and their temporal variability from regional to global scales. As a major component of vegetation carbon pools, AGB links ecosystem productivity to carbon allocation and turnover38,87. Annual AGB estimates can thus improve the representation of grassland carbon storage in terrestrial carbon budgets and ecosystem models88,89. In turn, the continuous time series from 2000–2022 enables quantification of interannual variability in carbon stocks and climate-driven changes in biomass, thereby supporting more robust assessments of carbon sources and sinks under varying climatic conditions9,88. Furthermore, the dataset can serve as a benchmark for process-based and data-driven ecosystem models, facilitating model-data fusion and reducing uncertainties in model estimations35,90.

Beyond grassland carbon accounting, the dataset also supports grassland management and ecosystem dynamics research. Spatially explicit AGB estimates provide quantitative information on forage availability, which are directly relevant to livestock carrying capacity assessment and sustainable grazing management7,91. The long-term AGB dataset allows tracking of year-to-year fluctuations in annual biomass, identification of sustained increases or declines in productivity, and evaluation of ecosystem responses to climatic variability92,93. These capabilities enable systematic comparisons between climatic zones or biomes, helping identify areas vulnerable to degradation, and guide targeted conservation, restoration, and adaptation strategies91,94. Collectively, the dataset offers a practical foundation for integrating grassland ecological monitoring, carbon mitigation planning, and evidence-based grassland management.

Uncertainties

Despite the advantages of this dataset, several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, field AGB samples are unevenly distributed across space and time. Spatially, compiled observations are concentrated in cold, polar, and arid regions, with limited coverage in tropical grasslands (Fig. 2). To maximize sample availability, a single global RF model was adopted rather than climate-zone-specific models. This limited representativeness of the training samples may reduce model generalization and increase prediction uncertainty in underrepresented regions, particularly under extreme climatic conditions or unusual combinations of predictors95–97. Temporally, samples are unevenly distributed across the study period, with the majority of observations concentrated in 2020 and fewer samples in other years. This imbalance constrains the model’s ability to capture interannual variability96. In addition to the limitation of sample representativeness, temporal simulation exhibits higher uncertainty than spatial pattern simulation. This primarily stems from two factors: the global scarcity of long-term continuous field AGB observations98, and a structural limitation of the RF model—it is trained on site-year samples that integrate both spatial and temporal signals96. At the global scale, spatial heterogeneity across biomes outweighs temporal fluctuations at individual sites, leading the model to prioritize fitting spatial patterns over temporal dynamics99.

Uncertainty also arises from the predictor datasets themselves. Satellite- and reanalysis-derived variables inevitably contain retrieval errors, sensor noise, and modeling biases introduced during atmospheric correction, gap filling, and data assimilation procedures100–102. Furthermore, the field AGB observations represent actual biomass and therefore inherently reflect the combined influences of climate variability, grazing, and other natural or anthropogenic disturbances103,104. However, explicit variables describing extreme climate events, wildfires, and anthropogenic disturbances (e.g., grazing intensity, prescribed burning, land-use changes) were not included as predictors of the RF model, due to the lack of consistent global datasets. This may be a key reason for the reduced performance in capturing interannual variability at some temperate and alpine grassland sites105–107. Future improvements will focus on expanding long-term continuous field observations globally, with priority given to underrepresented regions, incorporating more long-term continuous grassland monitoring records, adopting spatiotemporal stratified modeling to separate spatial patterns from temporal dynamics, and integrating disturbance- and management-related predictors into the machine learning framework.
