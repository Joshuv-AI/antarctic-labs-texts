Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance of rat adipose-derived stem cells via the PI3K/AKT-SREBF1 signaling axis
Authors: Wenzhong Hu, Zhihao Zhao, Qiuling Zhang, Pei Gong, Xinyu Wang, Cong Han, Xinzhe Gao, Jie Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRat adipose-derived stem cells (rADSCs) are widely used in bone tissue engineering (BTE). Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) can modulate stem cell fate; however, its precise regulatory effects on ADSC lineage commitment remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate how LIPUS regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance of rADSCs. We first performed cell proliferation and differentiation induction assays, which demonstrated that LIPUS significantly enhanced osteogenic differentiation while simultaneously suppressing adipogenesis, without affecting rADSC proliferation. Subsequently, transcriptome sequencing and protein–protein interaction analysis identified sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 1 (Srebf1) as a pivotal regulatory node in LIPUS-modulated osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation. Rescue experiments further confirmed that LIPUS rescued the Srebf1 overexpression phenotype by restoring osteogenic potential and attenuating adipogenesis. Mechanistically, LIPUS downregulated SREBF1 expression and inhibited its nuclear translocation by inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling, as evidenced by synergistic effects with the PI3K-specific inhibitor LY294002. Finally, a rat cranial defect model was established to validate the osteogenic potential of LIPUS in vivo. LIPUS significantly enhanced bone defect repair, even in the presence of Srebf1-overexpressing rADSCs. Collectively, our findings suggest that SREBF1, a canonical transcription factor in lipid metabolism, also functions as a mechanoresponsive regulator in cell fate determination. Furthermore, LIPUS promotes bone regeneration by shifting the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance in rADSCs toward osteogenesis through suppression of PI3K/AKT–SREBF1 pathway, thereby providing a potential therapeutic strategy for BTE.Keywords: adipose-derived stem cells, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound, osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance, PI3K/AKT–SREBF1 signaling pathway, bone tissue engineering

This study demonstrates the potential of low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) combined with rat adipose-derived stem cells (rADSCs) for bone defects reconstruction. The research highlights how LIPUS can shift the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance toward osteogenesis in rADSCs. By integrating stem cell therapy with physical stimulation, the study provides valuable insights into developing advanced strategies for bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

Reconstruction of bone defects represents a significant clinical challenge.1,2 Such defects often originate from trauma, oncologic resection, or congenital anomalies, impairing both function and aesthetics and complicating reconstruction due to anatomical complexity and functional demands.3–5 While autografts remain the clinical gold standard, limitations such as donor-site morbidity, insufficient graft volume, and graft resorption necessitate advanced regenerative strategies.6–8 Bone tissue engineering (BTE), which integrates stem cells, biomaterial scaffolds, and bioactive factors, holds substantial promise for functional bone regeneration.9 Among potential seed cells, adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) are particularly attractive owing to their abundance, accessibility, immunomodulatory properties, and multilineage potential.10,11 The osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation of ADSCs exhibits a seesaw-like reciprocal regulatory balance—enhanced osteogenic differentiation is typically accompanied by suppressed adipogenic differentiation, and vice versa.12,13 However, the precise mechanism modulating this osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance remains poorly understood.

Physical stimuli such as low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) offer noninvasive approaches to directing stem cell fate. LIPUS modulates osteogenesis in various mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)14–16 and enhances bone repair in clinical settings.17 However, the molecular mechanism by which it regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance in ADSCs remains inadequately explored. In this study, we investigated these mechanisms in rat ADSCs (rADSCs). Transcriptome sequencing and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were employed to identify key regulatory genes involved in LIPUS-mediated modulation of osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation. Furthermore, the therapeutic potential of LIPUS in promoting bone healing was validated using a rat cranial defect model with genetically modified rADSCs. This work highlights a novel combinatorial strategy that integrates mechanical stimulation and stem cells for BTE.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Approval No. WCHSIRB-D-2024-562) and included both in vitro cellular experiments and in vivo validation using animal models. In the in vitro phase, rADSCs were isolated from bilateral inguinal fat pads of 4-week-old specific pathogen-free Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats and characterized by flow cytometry, as well as multilineage differentiation assays using Alizarin Red S (ARS), Oil Red O, and Alcian blue staining. Following LIPUS stimulation (1.5 MHz, 30 mW/cm2, 20 min/d) for 7 days, cellular responses were assessed via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays, ARS, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and Oil Red O staining, as well as transcriptome sequencing, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), western blotting (WB), cell transfection, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining. For in vivo validation, critical-sized cranial defects were surgically created in 24 male SD rats (8–10 weeks old) to evaluate the osteogenic repair potential of LIPUS. Animals received daily LIPUS treatment (1.5 MHz, 30 mW/cm2, 20 min/d) for 8 weeks, after which venous blood samples, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys), and cranial specimens were harvested for further analysis. These samples were subjected to comprehensive analyses, including hematology, serum biochemistry, micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, Masson staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and IF assays. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student t-test, while differences among multiple groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple groups, depending on the normality and homogeneity of variance. Full methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Results

Characterization of rADSCs

Primary rADSCs exhibited typical fibroblast-like morphology (Figure S1A-D) and multidirectional differentiation potential. Oil Red O staining confirmed lipid droplet formation (Figure S1E), ARS staining revealed mineralized nodules (Figure S1F), and Alcian blue staining showed proteoglycan-rich cartilage microspheres (Figure S1G, H). Flow cytometry indicated positive expression for MSC-characteristic markers CD44, CD29, and CD90, and negative expression for CD11b_c, CD45, and CD34 in passage 3 rADSCs (Figure S1I).

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound enhances osteogenic differentiation while suppressing adipogenic differentiation in rADSCs

Rat adipose-derived stem cells were subjected to LIPUS stimulation (1.5 MHz, 30 mW/cm2, 20 min/d) for 7 days (Figure 1A-C) followed by CCK-8 assays. No significant differences between the LIPUS and negative control (NC) groups at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (Figure 1D) were observed. However, LIPUS enhanced osteogenic differentiation. Qualitative ALP staining revealed stronger cytoplasmic ALP activity (Figure 1E, F), and quantitative enzymatic assays confirmed activity levels substantially higher than those of controls (Figure 1G). Subsequent ARS staining showed more mineralized nodules in the LIPUS group (Figure 1H, I). Consistent with these findings, qRT-PCR analysis showed significant upregulation of key osteogenic markers (Col1a1, Runx2, Opn, Ibsp) in LIPUS-stimulated cells (Figure 1L). Moreover, WB analysis validated increased protein expression of ALP, RUNX2, and OPN in the LIPUS group (Figure 1M-P). In contrast, adipogenesis was inhibited. Oil Red O staining and semi-quantitative analysis revealed significantly fewer and smaller lipid droplets in the LIPUS group (Figure 1J, K). Correspondingly, qRT-PCR analysis showed downregulation of adipogenic marker genes (Fabp4, Pparg, Cebpa) in the LIPUS group (Figure 1Q), which was corroborated using WB analysis showing reduced protein expression of PPARγ and CEBPα (Figure 1R-T).

Differential gene expression profile of LIPUS on rADSC differentiation under osteogenic or adipogenic induction conditions

Principal component analysis of mRNA expression profiles demonstrated robust experimental reproducibility, with biological replicates clustering tightly. Two principal components (PC1: 96.5%, PC2: 2%) collectively accounted for 98.5% of the total variance (Figure 2A). Clear spatial separation along the PC1 axis distinguished groups subjected to osteogenic induction(OM-7D-NC, OM-7D-LIPUS) from those subjected to adipogenic induction (AM-7D-NC, AM-7D-LIPUS). Correlation heatmaps further supported this separation, showing high intra-group correlations (median >0.99) and lower inter-group correlations, thereby confirming systematic transcriptomic differences between the differentiation conditions (Figure 2B).

Comparative transcriptomic sequencing of osteogenically induced day-7 samples revealed highly consistent gene expression profiles between control (OM-7D-NC) and LIPUS-treated (OM-7D-LIPUS) groups, as shown by via violin plots (Figure 2C). A volcano plot identified only 60 significant DEGs, including 9 upregulated and 51 downregulated genes (Figure 2E), with hierarchical clustering confirming distinct group-specific expression patterns (Figure 2G). GO enrichment of these DEGs identified the top 20 significantly enriched GO terms, 18 of which were associated with biological processes. These included stress-related pathways such as defense response (GO:0006952; P = 4.8 × 10−10) and response to external biological stimuli (GO:0043207; P = 3.3 × 10−8). At the molecular function level, lipoprotein particle binding (GO:0071813; P = 9.4 × 10−7) and protein–lipid complex binding (GO:0071814; P = 9.4 × 10−7) were significantly enriched. These findings suggest that LIPUS may influence cell differentiation by regulating lipid metabolism-related molecular interactions (Figure 2H). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the top 20 significant pathways included AMPK signaling (ko04152; P = 0.013021) and PI3K/AKT signaling (ko04151; P = 0.125136) (Figure 2I). Unexpectedly, the PPARγ pathway was also enriched, potentially reflecting early crosstalk between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation (Figure 2I).

Under adipogenic induction, transcriptomic comparisons between control (AM-7D-NC) and LIPUS-treated (AM-7D-LIPUS) groups revealed markedly different dynamics. Violin plots confirmed high data reproducibility (Figure 2D), and a volcano plot identified 1052 significant DEGs, including 497 upregulated and 555 downregulated genes (Figure 2F). Expression heatmaps demonstrated clear group segregation with coordinated downregulation of master adipogenic regulators (Fabp4, Pparg) (Figure 2J). GO functional enrichment analysis revealed 18 significantly enriched terms in biological processes, including single-organism processes (GO:0044763; P = 1.7 × 10−24) and cellular component organization (GO:0016043; P = 1.3 × 10−19), suggesting that LIPUS may influence cell differentiation by disrupting the intrinsic regulatory network. At the molecular function level, protein binding (GO:0005515, P = 3.2 × 10−19) was significantly enriched, indicating that mechanical stimuli may transmit signals via PPI interfaces. For cellular components, enrichment was observed in the plasma membrane region (GO:0044459; P = 2.4 × 10−18), consistent with mechanical signal transduction mediated by membrane receptors (Figure 2K). Similarly, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for the top 20 significant pathways identified PPARγ signaling (ko03320; P = 8.3 × 10−14), cyclic adenosine monophosphate signaling (ko04024; P = 4.4 × 10−7), AMPK signaling (ko04152; P = 6.5 × 10−7), and fatty acid metabolism (ko01212; P = 8.2 × 10−6) (Figure 2L).

Srebf1 functions as a pivotal regulatory node in LIPUS-mediated osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance in rADSCs

Cross-analysis of DEGs from the two differentiation conditions identified 13 co-regulated genes, including lipid metabolism regulators (Abcg1, Srebf1, Scd1, Fabp4) and signaling mediators (Cxcl6, G0s2) (Figure 2M). Protein–protein interaction network analysis further highlighted Srebf1, Abcg1, Scd1, and Fabp4 as topological hubs, with Srebf1 exhibiting the highest connectivity and significance—underscoring its key regulatory role (Figure 2N). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction validation confirmed consistent downregulation of all four genes following LIPUS stimulation, with Srebf1 showing the most pronounced suppression (Table S3, Figure 2O, P). These findings guided subsequent investigations toward dissecting Srebf1-mediated mechanotransduction in LIPUS-regulated osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance.

Srebf1 bidirectionally regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance in rADSCs

In the knockdown model, small interfering RNAs (siRNA) transfection achieved >90% transfection efficiency across groups, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 3A, B). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction analysis revealed effective knockdown with all three siRNA (si-Srebf1-1/2/3), with si-Srebf1-2 achieving the strongest suppression (70.84%±4.94%) compared to that with si-Srebf1-1 (62.11%±3.35%), and si-Srebf1-3 (46.84%±1.61%) (Figure 3E). Western blotting analysis further validated reduced SREBF1 protein expression, leading to the selection of si-Srebf1-2 for subsequent experiments (Figure 3F). In the overexpression model generated via lentiviral vectors, puromycin selection yielded stable lines, with >90% transfection efficiency confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 3C, D). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction detected ∼17-fold mRNA upregulation with Srebf1 overexpression (Lv-Srebf1) compared to that with negative control (Lv-NC) (Figure 3G), and WB analysis confirmed elevated SREBF1 protein levels (Figure 3H).

During osteogenic differentiation, after 7 days of induction, Lv-Srebf1 downregulated Col1a1, Runx2, Opn, and Ibsp mRNA compared to Lv-NC, whereas si-Srebf1 upregulated these markers relative to negative control siRNA (si-NC) (Figure 3I). Western blotting analysis showed RUNX2 and OPN protein levels decreased with overexpression and increased with knockdown (Figure 3J-L). Immunofluorescence indicated enhanced ALP/RUNX2-positive cell density and fluorescence intensity in si-Srebf1-transfected cells compared to those transfected with si-NC. Opposite effects were observed with Lv-Srebf1 relative to Lv-NC, indicating that Srebf1 suppresses osteogenic differentiation (Figure 3O, P, Figure S2C, D). Conversely, under adipogenic induction, after 7 days, si-Srebf1 downregulated Fabp4, Pparg, and Cebpa mRNA compared with si-NC, whereas Lv-Srebf1 upregulated these genes relative to Lv-NC (Figure 3M). Western blotting analysis revealed parallel decreases in CEBPα and PPARγ protein following knockdown and increases with overexpression (Figure 3N, Figure S2A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed lower CEBPα and PPARγ fluorescence intensity and positive cell density with si-Srebf1 than those with si-NC, whereas greater intensity and density were observed with Lv-Srebf1 than those with Lv-NC, confirming that Srebf1 promotes adipogenic differentiation (Figure 3Q, R, Figure S2E, F).

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound rescues the Srebf1-overexpression phenotype by restoring osteogenesis and suppressing adipogenesis in rADSCs

For osteogenic differentiation, ALP and ARS staining demonstrated that Lv-NC+LIPUS treatment significantly increased mineralized nodule formation compared to that with Lv-NC controls, whereas Lv-Srebf1 reduced ALP activity and calcium deposition (Figure 4A, B, F, G). Notably, LIPUS stimulation partially restored mineralization in Srebf1-overexpressing cells (Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS) compared to unstimulated cells, although mineralization levels remained lower than those observed in Lv-NC+LIPUS. These findings were corroborated by dynamic ALP activity assays, which showed that Lv-NC+LIPUS consistently exhibited the highest activity over time (Figure 4C). At the molecular level, LIPUS suppressed Srebf1 expression and upregulated osteogenic markers (Col1a1, Runx2, Opn) in the control groups, with partial recovery observed in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS compared to that in Lv-Srebf1, albeit below that in Lv-NC+LIPUS (Figure 4H-K). This was confirmed via WB analysis, which revealed elevated OPN and RUNX2 protein levels in Lv-NC+LIPUS, whereas reduced levels were observed in Lv-Srebf1 (Figure 4L, Figure S3A-C). Immunofluorescence analysis showed higher ALP and RUNX2-positive cells density and fluorescence intensity in Lv-NC+LIPUS, with partial restoration in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS. Collectively, these findings indicate that LIPUS reverses Srebf1-mediated inhibition of osteogenesis by suppressing its expression (Figure 4R, S, Figure S3G, H).

In adipogenic differentiation assays, Oil Red O staining revealed reduced lipid droplet accumulation in Lv-NC+LIPUS compared to that in Lv-NC controls, whereas Lv-Srebf1 exhibited enhanced lipid deposition (Figure 4D, E). Notably, LIPUS stimulation significantly attenuated this effect in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS compared to the unstimulated Lv-Srebf1 group, although deposition remained higher than that in Lv-NC+LIPUS. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction confirmed LIPUS downregulated Srebf1 and adipogenic markers (Fabp4, Pparg, Cebpa) expression across groups, with partial recovery of overexpression-induced upregulation in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS compared to that in Lv-Srebf1 (Figure 4M-P). Western blotting analysis showed reduced PPARγ and CEBPα protein levels in LIPUS-stimulated cells (Figure 4Q, Figure S3D-F), consistent with IF analysis showing decreased density and fluorescence intensity of PPARγ-positive cells in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS compared to those in Lv-Srebf1 (Figure 4T, Figure S3I).

Collectively, these results establish that LIPUS counteracts Srebf1-mediated enhancement of adipogenesis by suppressing its expression, confirming Srebf1 as a key regulatory target.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance of rADSCs through the PI3K/AKT–SREBF1 axis

Western blotting analysis revealed LIPUS significantly reduced PI3K and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) levels in Lv-NC+LIPUS compared to those in the Lv-NC controls, indicating LIPUS suppresses PI3K/AKT activity. Although Lv-Srebf1 alone exhibited negligible effects on PI3K/AKT signaling compared to those in Lv-NC, Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS demonstrated markedly decreased PI3K and p-AKT expression compared to that in Lv-Srebf1, suggesting that LIPUS-mediated inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway operates independently of SREBF1 expression levels (Figure 5A-C).

Western blotting analysis also revealed significant downregulation of SREBF1, p-AKT, and PI3K expression in both the LIPUS and LY294002 groups, whereas the LIPUS+LY294002 group exhibited markedly synergistic inhibition of all three proteins (Figure 5D-G). Immunofluorescence (Figure 5H, I) and cell fractionation (Figure 5J-L) demonstrated that the nuclear translocation of SREBF1 was suppressed by LIPUS. These findings indicate that SREBF1 likely functions downstream of the PI3K/AKT pathway and that LIPUS downregulates SREBF1 indirectly via PI3K/AKT inhibition.

For osteogenesis, Lv-NC+LIPUS+LY294002 displayed maximal ALP activity and increased mineralized nodule formation (Figure 5M, N). Lv-Srebf1 exhibited substantially impaired osteogenic capacity, an effect partially rescued in Lv-Srebf1+LY294002. Although Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS+LY294002 showed enhanced mineralization compared to that in Lv-Srebf1+LY294002, the effect remained inferior to that observed in Lv-NC+LIPUS+LY294002. At the molecular level, LY294002 treatment reversed Lv-Srebf1-mediated downregulation of osteogenic genes (Col1a1, Runx2, Opn, Ibsp). Gene expression was further upregulated in Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS+LY294002, with peak levels observed in Lv-NC+LIPUS+LY294002 (Figure 5P-S). OPN and RUNX2 protein levels consistently mirrored these transcriptional patterns (Figure 5W). Adipogenic differentiation demonstrated reciprocal suppression: Lv-NC+LIPUS+LY294002 showed minimal lipid accumulation (Figure 5O), reduced expression of adipogenic genes (Fabp4, Pparg, Cebpa) (Figure 5T-V), and decreased levels of adipogenesis-related proteins (PPARγ and CEBPα) (Figure 5X). While Lv-Srebf1 showed enhanced adipogenesis, this phenotype was attenuated by LY294002 (Lv-Srebf1+LY294002). Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS+LY294002 exhibited further inhibition of adipogenesis compared to that in Lv-Srebf1, though levels remained higher than those in Lv-NC+LIPUS+LY294002.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LIPUS orchestrates osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance of rADSCs through the PI3K/AKT–SREBF1 signaling axis.

Gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel sustains rADSCs viability without compromising differentiation capacity

Live/dead cell assays demonstrated excellent biocompatibility of the rADSCs/gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) complex, with >95% cell viability on days 3 and 5 in both constructs, as observed under confocal microscopy (Figure S4A). Cytoskeletal analysis showed uniform cellular distribution in both groups at day 3, with most cells displaying rounded or short spindle-shaped morphology and limited pseudopod extensions (Figure S4B). By day 7, rADSCs in GelMA hydrogels retained this uniformity while exhibiting significant morphological improvements, including complete spreading and distinct elongated spindle shapes. Alkaline phosphatase and ARS staining indicated markedly reduced enzyme activity and calcium nodule deposition in the Lv-Srebf1 group compared to the Lv-NC group (Figure S4C, D). Following 14 days adipogenic induction, Oil Red O staining confirmed increased lipid droplet accumulation and size in the Lv-Srebf1 group, indicative of enhanced adipogenic potential upon Srebf1 overexpression (Figure S4E). Western blotting analysis was consistent with the phenotypic data, showing upregulated SREBF1 protein expression as well as elevated levels of adipogenic proteins (PPARγ and CEBPα) but reduced osteogenic proteins (OPN and RUNX2) in Lv-Srebf1 hydrogels (Figure S4F, G).

Collectively, these findings validate that GelMA hydrogels effectively preserve the differentiation capacity of rADSCs without compromising morphological characteristics. Importantly, Srebf1 overexpression suppresses osteogenesis and promotes adipogenesis within this microenvironment.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound synergizes with rADSCs/GelMA complex to enhance new bone formation in vivo

Critical-sized cranial defects were successfully created in 24 male SD rats (8–10 weeks; Figure 6A-F), and received daily LIPUS stimulation (1.5 MHz, 30 mW/cm2, 20 min/d) or sham stimulation for 8 weeks beginning postoperative day 1 (Figure 6G, H). No observable toxicity and structural damage were detected in HE-stained sections (Figure 6I). All hematological parameters (WBC, RBC, HGB, and PLT) and serum markers (AST, ALT, CREA, and UREA), remained within physiological ranges across experimental groups (Figure 6J-Q). Subsequently, micro-CT reconstruction quantified cranial bone regeneration, revealing superior bone formation extending the initial defect margins in the Lv-NC+LIPUS group, in contrast to the limited ossification observed in the Lv-NC control and Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS. In comparison, Lv-Srebf1 significantly impaired osteogenesis (Figure 7A). Morphometric analyses corroborated these findings: Lv-NC+LIPUS showed decreased Tb. Sp, and increased BV/TV, Tb. N, and BMD compared with Lv-NC. In contrast, Srebf1 overexpression significantly impaired these parameters; however, LIPUS partially reversed this impairment in the Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS group (Figure 7B-E).

Histopathological evaluation via HE and Masson staining revealed poor bone regeneration in the Lv-Srebf1 group, characterized by fibrotic tissue and inflammation. In contrast, the Lv-NC+LIPUS group displayed substantial new bone formation and reduced inflammation, approaching defect bridging with mature lamellar bone in Masson staining, whereas only nascent bone was observed in the other groups (Figure 7F, G). Notably, no adipose tissue formation was detected in any defects. IHC analysis further elucidated the regulatory mechanisms: Lv-Srebf1 upregulated SREBF1, PI3K, and p-AKT expression compared to that in the controls, while LIPUS significantly suppressed these markers in both the Lv-NC+LIPUS and Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS groups. Notably, total AKT (t-AKT) expression was unaffected by Srebf1 expression but reduced by LIPUS (Figure 7H, Figure S5A-D). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed these results: Lv-NC+LIPUS exhibited upregulated OPN and RUNX2 expression, along with reduced PPARγ levels. In contrast, Lv-Srebf1 downregulated osteogenic markers and elevated PPARγ, while Lv-Srebf1+LIPUS partially mitigated these effects but remained less effective compared to Lv-NC+LIPUS (Figure 7I, Figure S5E-G).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate Srebf1 overexpression inhibits osteogenesis by promoting adipogenic differentiation, whereas LIPUS alleviates Srebf1-mediated osteogenic suppression and regulates the PI3K/AKT-SREBF1 signaling cascade to orchestrate bone regenerative processes.

Discussion

The osteogenic–adipogenic fate determination of stem cells is precisely regulated by external cues, among which mechanical stimuli (eg, shear stress, cyclic stretching, and centrifugal force) serve as critical physical modulators.18 As a distinct form of mechanical stimulation,19–21 LIPUS and its influence on stem cell differentiation have garnered considerable attention.22–29 Functioning as high-frequency pressure waves, it can generate mechanical forces (compression, tension, shear stress, and torque) that activate cellular mechanotransduction pathways.30 Thus, LIPUS acts as a multidimensional mechanical regulator in cell fate determination through these mechanotransduction pathway compared single type of force. Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound primarily operates through nonthermal effects such as acoustic streaming, cavitation, and the formation of microbubbles and microjets induced by mechanical stimulation.31 Since the isolation protocol for ADSCs was established in 2001,32 research on their differentiation mechanisms has advanced substantially; however, the synergistic interactions between ADSCs and extrinsic physical cues, particularly LIPUS stimulation, remain largely underexplored.

The mechanism by which LIPUS regulates cell proliferation remains contentious, a discrepancy likely attributable to cell type differences and variations in LIPUS intensity parameters. Significant proliferation enhancement has been reported in rADSCs (Jiang et al.,23 100 mW/cm2) and MC3T3-E1 cells (Tassinary et al.,33 200 mW/cm2; Katiyar et al.,34 75 mW/cm2). Katiyar et al.34 further demonstrated that the effects of LIPUS on cell proliferation are strongly intensity-dependent: within the 1–500 mW/cm2 range, the most pronounced stimulatory effect occurred at 75 mW/cm2, whereas an inhibitory effect emerged when the intensity increased to 465 mW/cm2. Conversely, no significant effects were observed in rat bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs; Akagi et al.,35 40 mW/cm2) or MC3T3-E1 cells (Veronick et al.,36 30 mW/cm2). In this study, continuous LIPUS stimulation (30 mW/cm2) for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days exerted no significant effect on rADSCs proliferation, consistent with the findings of Akagi et al.35 and Veronick et al.36 Notably, the intensities used in Akagi et al.,35 Veronick et al.,36 and our study (30–40 mW/cm2) were significantly lower than those in Jiang et al.23 (100 mW/cm2), and Tassinary et al.33 (200 mW/cm2). Collectively, these findings suggest that LIPUS-induced biological effects may be constrained within a specific “intensity window,” in which mechanosensitivity thresholds vary by cell type, highlighting the nonmonotonic, intensity-dependent nature of LIPUS bioeffects. Consequently, optimizing these physical parameters is a fundamental prerequisite for harnessing the full regenerative potential of LIPUS. Since its food and drug administration (FDA) approval in 1994, LIPUS parameters have progressively become standardized: most current devices employ a 30 mW/cm2 intensity at 1.5 MHz frequency, a 1 kHz pulse repetition rate, and a 20% duty cycle.17 Given the critical role of parameter optimization and to align with clinical translation prospects, our study strictly adhered to this FDA-standardized parameter system. Subtle thermal cues may modulate cellular responses, but the thermal effect is negligible under this parameter system. In contrast to high-intensity continuous ultrasound, pulsed emission mode with low sonic intensity (<3 W/cm2) means that there is no accumulation of heat in the target area.37–39 An intensity of 50 mW/cm2 does not raise the temperature by more than 1 °C.40 When pulsed ultrasound was applied at A 2400 mW/cm2 for 15 min, the temperature increase remained as low as 0.2 °C, measured using fine-wire thermocouples.40 High-intensity ultrasound (>5000 mW/cm2) causes adverse effects like healing arrest, fibrosis, necrosis, soft tissue damage, and osteocyte necrosis in dog femurs.40 Notably, 30 mW/cm2 LIPUS application for 30 min elevated the temperature from 37 °C to 40 °C, and promoted osteoblast proliferation/differentiation via heat-sensitive protein 70 regulation,41 but the exposure time is more than standardized parameter. Based on the understanding of the principles, this study did not conduct additional experiments to exclude thermal effects separately. Attentionally, the FDA-standardized parameters may be unsuitable for bone defects of varying depths and dimensions, as therapeutic efficacy will differ depending on the axial propagation distance. Therefore, future protocols should be adjusted according to individual differences to achieve a safer and more effective treatment result in the future. The osteogenic-promoting effect of LIPUS is recognized. Kusuyama et al.27 demonstrated LIPUS significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation of ST2 cells while inhibiting adipogenic differentiation of 3T3-L1 and ST2 models at 30 mW/cm2. An et al.22 reported LIPUS elevated expression of Col1a1, Alp, Bglap, and Opn in rat BMSCs at 100 mW/cm2. Hu et al.25 corroborated LIPUS-enhanced ALP activity, osteocalcin production, and mineralization in human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) at 90 mW/cm2, and Kusuyama et al.26 further confirmed that LIPUS promoted osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs under inflammatory conditions. Our findings revealed that LIPUS significantly elevated ALP activity, mineralization capacity, and the expression of key osteogenic genes (Col1a1, Runx2, Opn, Ibsp) as well as proteins (ALP, RUNX2, OPN). These results align with the observations reported by Jiang et al.23 and Yue et al.,24 who employed 100 mW/cm2 LIPUS to treat ADSCs. Regarding adipogenic differentiation, Xu et al.42 observed LIPUS suppressed adipogenic differentiation in rat visceral preadipocytes at 109.44 mW/cm2. Nishida et al.28 documented reduced lipid accumulation and downregulated Pparg and Cebpa expression in C3H10T1/2 cells at 60 mW/cm2 LIPUS. Similarly, Wu et al.29 found that LIPUS promoted the differentiation of BMSCs toward cartilage while inhibiting adipogenic differentiation by regulating BMSC-derived exosomes at 30 mW/cm2. Our findings also support the inhibitory effect of LIPUS on adipogenic differentiation. Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound stimulation markedly reduced lipid droplet formation and decreased the expression levels of key adipogenic genes (Fabp4, Pparg, Cebpa) as well as proteins (PPARγ, CEBPα). Notably, Fu et al.43 reported contrasting results, finding that LIPUS upregulated the mRNA levels of PPAR-γ1 and Adipoq, along with increased expression of PPAR-γ protein in mouse ADSCs at 100 mW/cm2. This discrepancy is potentially attributable to differences in cell types, adipogenic induction protocols and LIPUS intensity. To comprehensively address these inconsistencies, further optimization of LIPUS stimulation protocols and deeper exploration of the molecular mechanism are required.

In this study, transcriptome sequencing and PPI identified srebf1 as a pivotal bidirectional regulator regulating the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance in rADSCs under LIPUS stimulation. SREBF1, conventionally recognized as a central pro-adipogenic driver and key regulator of lipid metabolism,44,45 plays a crucial role in this process. Ji et al.44 demonstrated that dexamethasone promotes adipogenesis in BMSCs by upregulating SREBF1, whereas SREBF1 inhibitors significantly reduce adipogenic potential. Ayala et al.45 further confirmed that Srebf1 knockout suppresses adipogenic markers (Pparγ2, Cebpα, Fabp4) and impedes fat formation. Recent studies have highlighted mechanical stimuli as novel modulators of SREBF1 function.46–48 For instance, Liu et al.46 found that shear stress promotes SREBP1 proteolytic cleavage in endothelial cells, facilitating its nuclear translocation and activation of downstream gene transcription. Romani et al.47 reported that mechanical forces inhibit SREBF1/2 activation by disrupting perimitochondrial F-actin networks. Peng et al.48 demonstrated mechanical suppression of SREBF1 via electroacupuncture alleviates insulin resistance and mitochondrial dysfunction in a polycystic ovary syndrome model. Nevertheless, existing research has primarily focused on chemical or biological regulation or conventional mechanical stimuli such as fluid shear stress and electroacupuncture—the regulatory effects of noninvasive physical modalities (eg, LIPUS) on SREBF1 and its role in MSC fate determination remain largely underexplored. Our study provides novel mechanistic insights, revealing that LIPUS tilts the osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation balance toward osteogenesis through targeted suppression of Srebf1. Importantly, our study deepens the understanding of SREBF1 from a well-established lipid metabolism transcription factor to a mechanoresponsive regulator in cell fate determination as previously described.49,50 This dual role establishes a conceptual link between mechanotransduction and cellular energy metabolism, providing a new framework to explore their functional interplay.

The PI3K/AKT pathway exhibits a distinct environment-dependent paradox in regulating the osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of MSCs. Conventionally, activation of PI3K/AKT directly promotes osteogenesis by upregulating core transcription factors such as Runx2 and Osterix.51 Biomaterial-mediated PI3K/AKT activation further enhances osteogenesis in synergy with angiogenesis.52–54 Paradoxically, recent studies have demonstrated opposing regulatory patterns. For example, low-dose sodium fluoride suppresses AKT phosphorylation, which in turn increases ALP activity and mineralization in dental pulp stem cells, with PI3K inhibitor LY294002 mimicking these pro-osteogenic effects.55 Similarly, FGF2-mediated PI3K/AKT inhibition significantly enhances mineralization and ALP activity in stem cells derived from apical papilla.56 This environment-dependent paradox is also evident in adipogenesis. Activated PI3K/AKT initiates adipogenesis via the mTOR–FOXO1 signaling axis, a process that can be reversed by PI3K inhibitors.57 Additionally, mTOR activation upregulates the expression of PPARγ and CEBPα to accelerate fat formation.58 Notably, SOD3 activates PI3K/AKT to stimulate osteogenesis while simultaneously suppressing adipogenesis—an exceptional case that highlights the plasticity of the PI3K/AKT pathway.59 These paradoxical patterns underscore PI3K/AKT as a central regulatory pathway in osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance, with functional outcomes dependent critically on the cellular microenvironment, activation dynamics, and crosstalk with other pathways.

Existing literature also reveals conflicting PI3K/AKT responses to LIPUS. Pro-activation studies report LIPUS induces proliferation, anti-inflammatory, and pro-osteogenic effects. Specifically, LIPUS activates ERK/AKT phosphorylation to enhance human ADSC proliferation,60 stimulates HaCaT cells and BMSCs proliferation via the PI3K/AKT pathway,61,62 maintains bone homeostasis via PI3K/AKT-Nrf2 axis-mediated oxidative stress relief,63 and activates FAK-PI3K/AKT-Wnt/β-catenin cascades to promote BMSC osteogenesis.64 It also enhances periodontal regeneration by modulating cartilage oligomeric matrix protein expression in extracellular vesicles via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.65 Conversely, LIPUS inhibits PI3K/AKT in other contexts.66,67 For example, LIPUS alleviates neuroinflammation by suppressing the PI3K/AKT-NF-κB axis66 and enhances endometrial repair by inhibiting PI3K/AKT.67 Our findings are consistent with these reports on pathway inhibition. LIPUS suppresses the activity of the PI3K/AKT pathway, leading to downregulation of SREBF1 expression and impairing its nuclear translocation. Consequently, the transcriptional activity of SREBF1 is inhibited, which counteracts the osteogenic suppression and pro-adipogenic effects driven by SREBF1. These findings indicate that the PI3K/AKT–SREBF1 axis acts as a central mediator underlying LIPUS-directed cell differentiation.

To mechanistically validate the translational potential of our findings, we established a cranial defect model using Srebf1-engineered rADSCs combined with GelMA hydrogels. GelMA hydrogels were selected for their well-documented advantages in bone regeneration, including excellent biocompatibility, low cost, a photo-cross-linkable structure, and highly tunable physicochemical properties.68 Notably, the rADSCs/GelMA complex fully preserved cellular genetically programmed cell fate determination—an essential prerequisite for effective in vivo bone regeneration. To minimize experimental variability and ensure therapeutic efficacy, we employed a cranial defect model, which provides a non–weight-bearing environment and eliminates motion-induced confounding factors.69 Compared with the 8-mm trans-sagittal and bilateral 5-mm parasagittal cranial defect models, the unilateral 5-mm model enables self-control and anti-interference by precluding material exchange between bilateral defects and sagittal suture-derived substance diffusion.70,71 Alterations in osteogenesis-related events and PI3K/AKT-SREBF1 pathway proteins were confirmed in the rat cranial defect model, consistent with our in vitro observations. While adipose tissue formation was less readily detectable than bone formation across all experimental groups, shifts in PPARγ IF revealed clear adipogenic suppression. Collectively, these findings confirm that LIPUS steers the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance toward osteogenesis through targeted suppression of the PI3K/AKT-SREBF1 signaling axis.

Beyond elucidating the core mechanism by which LIPUS modulates osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance of rADSCs via the PI3K/AKT-SREBF1 axis, several limitations warrant discussion. First, although preliminary evidence suggests regulatory crosstalk between the PI3K/AKT pathway and SREBF1, direct molecular interactions require further validation through gene editing or co-immunoprecipitation assays. Second, the precise mechanotransduction mechanism by which cells perceive LIPUS-generated mechanical stimuli through membrane receptors and subsequently transduce them into PI3K/AKT pathway inhibition remains incompletely characterized, representing a knowledge gap that limits deeper elucidation of mechano-biochemical signaling cascades.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LIPUS downregulates SREBF1 through inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby shifting the osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation balance toward osteogenesis in rADSCs. Collectively, these findings highlight the noninvasive therapeutic potential of LIPUS-mediated mechanical regulation of stem cell fate, presenting a promising avenue for BTE and regenerative medicine. From the perspective of bone-fat balance, LIPUS may potentially restore this balance disrupted by various bone-related metabolic diseases and systemic disorders, such as obesity, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, and skeletal aging.72 Specifically, our strategy exhibits strong translational potential across diverse clinical scenarios, including but not limited to fracture nonunions, postoperative reconstruction of cystic bony lesions, and complex bone defects complicated by systemic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, all of which pose significant challenges to effective bone healing. However, giving that the energy distribution pattern of the ultrasound field is related to axial propagation distance,73,74 critical challenges must be addressed before clinical translation, including disease-specific mechanisms, evidence-based customized therapeutic strategies, and the development of safe, intelligent wearable home-use devices.40
