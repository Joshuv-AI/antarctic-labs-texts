Water demand forecasting in multiple district metered areas based on a multi-scale correction module neural network architecture
Authors: Qidong Que, Jinliang Gao, Yizhou Qian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Short-term water demand forecasting (STWDF) for multiple spatially and temporally correlated District Metering Areas (DMAs) is an essential foundation for achieving more refined management of urban water supply networks. However, due to the greater uncertainty associated with specific DMA demand compared to overall water usage, accurately predicting STWDF poses significant challenges. This study introduces an innovative network architecture—the multi-scale correction module neural network, built upon Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) enhanced with Attention mechanisms—for simultaneous STWDF with a temporal resolution of one hour over a week for 10 DMAs located in a single city in northern Italy. This framework utilizes multivariate corrections to refine and enhance the output accuracy. The results reveal that, in comparison to traditional Gated Recurrent Unit or LSTM models, the proposed model with integrated correction modules, particularly those that leverage inter-DMA correlations, improves performance across all evaluation metrics by an average of 5 %-20 % per DMA. Additionally, it consistently delivers superior accuracy across three scenarios: single DMA forecasting, total water demand, and extreme conditions, while maintaining stable performance throughout. Furthermore, the interpretability analysis underscores the feasibility of this innovative structure and highlights the contribution of meteorological features to the predictive model in some DMA-level STWDF. The unified input-output framework elegantly simplifies the STWDF process across multiple DMAs, providing new insights and methodologies for future research in this domain.

Introduction

District Metering Area (DMA) is a defined section within a water distribution network, typically isolated by closing sluice valves, often on a permanent basis. This allows water flow to be monitored through one or more meters, enabling effective monitoring of water demand and aiding in leakage management and reduction (Farley and Trow 2005, Pearson 2019). The operation and management of water distribution systems have always been both the core mission and a challenge for water utilities, with a variety of studies in the literature already showing that DMAs are highly beneficial in this regard (Di Nardo et al. 2013, Ferrari and Savic 2015). With the advent of smart metering technologies and the development of the Internet of Things (IoT), more and more water utilities measure water demand at the level of DMA to achieve more refined spatiotemporal control. (Cominola et al. 2015, Di Mauro et al. 2021). This includes managing water distribution across various spatial scales, from individual DMAs to entire urban areas, and adapting to different temporal resolutions, such as hourly, daily, or monthly demand variations (Di Mauro, Cominola et al. 2021).One of the objectives is water demand forecasting (WDF), which poses a significant challenge due to the heterogeneity in characteristics between different DMAs, such as urban land use patterns resulting from the complexity of urban environments (Shandas and Parandvash 2010, Mamade et al. 2014).

Deep learning technologies (DLT) seems to possess the potential for tackling complex DMA-level WDF, having already achieved significant advancements in predictive tasks across various industries owing to its powerful nonlinear processing capabilities (Bedi and Toshniwal 2019, Cao and Wang 2019, Ye et al. 2022, Guo et al. 2024). A multitude of studies in the literature have shown that DLT, especially Recurrent Neural Networks such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) models, significantly outperform conventional linear techniques like AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average and even conventional Machine Learning models such as Random Forest and Support Vector Machines in terms of prediction accuracy and error accumulation in WDF (Donkor et al. 2014, Mu et al. 2020, Nasser et al. 2020, Fu et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2023).

However, it is worth noting that few studies have incorporated the spatiotemporal characteristics between DMAs when utilizing DLT for WDF. (Guo et al. 2018)established separate GRU forecast models for two independent DMAs, with significant differences in their structural parameters, without considering the potential connections between these DMAs within the same city. This approach is also common in most current research on DLT in WDF (Fu, Jin et al. 2022) which lacks universality and is not particularly elegant in its implementation. (Xenochristou et al. 2020, Xenochristou and Kapelan 2020, Xenochristou et al. 2021) included postal codes of regions and household characteristics as input features of the forecast model and utilized various other social indicators to explore the WDF problem across multiple DMAs. These data often involve privacy issues, and evidently, not all water utilities can obtain such detailed data (Parker and Wilby 2013). Simultaneously, (Mu, Zheng et al. 2020) discovered that DMA-level WDF is more susceptible to high uncertainty due to the more variable water demand compared to the total demand of a city, which presents significant challenges to WDF tasks (Seok et al. 2014, Hu et al. 2021).Besides, research on the role of external parameters examines the effectiveness of meteorological features as model inputs, with some researchers arguing that they do not enhance performance,(Seok, Kim et al. 2014, Mu, Zheng et al. 2020, Xenochristou, Hutton et al. 2021), while others suggest they can improve accuracy (Hu et al. 2019, Fiorillo et al. 2021, Zanfei et al. 2022).

In summary, the use of DLT in constructing WDF models lacks the reasonable integration of spatiotemporal information. The present paper aims to explore: (1) whether there is any correlation between multiple DMAs in WDF and if such correlation can be incorporated into the design of the neural network architecture; (2) how to quantify and compare the role of meteorological features in WDF. To address these questions, this article develops a novel neural network architecture to tackle the multi-step WDF across multiple DMAs, which is formally described in the later sections. A dataset, including water demand and meteorological data of 10 DMAs with 1-hour resolution from a city in Italy, is used to build and verify the model. After comparison with conventional LSTM and GRU models, a novel model named Multi-Scale Correction Module Neural Network (MSCMNet) is found to hold promising application prospects for more refined DMA-level WDF.

Results and Discussion

In this chapter, this paper presents the WDF results in terms of single-step and multiple-step forecasting, referring to 24-hour and 168-hour (7-day) predictions, with detailed definitions available in the Method section. Based on this, the performance of all models is comprehensively analyzed using four evaluation metrics from three perspectives: single DMA forecasting, total water demand, and extreme conditions. Next, the impact of the correction module on model training is examined by analyzing the convergence of the loss function on the training set, demonstrating the feasibility of the proposed architecture. Finally, model interpretability analysis is conducted to explore the influence of input data on the models, aiming to provide new insights for future work.

Models equipped with the Correction Module—MSCMNet_WM, MSCMNet_M, and MSCMNet_W—rank as the top performers in 24 out of 40 single-step forecasts and 26 out of 40 multi-step forecasts. Specifically, MSCMNet_WM and MSCMNet_W, which incorporate FC2, perform as the best models in both single and multi-step forecasting, with 18 (12 for the former and 6 for the latter) and 21 times (15 for the former and 6 for the latter), respectively. Moreover, in most cases, for DMA-level WDF, models with the multi-scale correction module closely approach the optimal value even when not the best model for certain metrics, maintaining a diamond shape, indicating stable forecasting performance.

Fig. 2 illustrates that, compared to GRU, LSTM, and MSNet, the models MSCMNet_WM, MSCMNet_M, and MSCMNet_W significantly improve the average forecasting results across all DMAs in terms of MAE, RMSE, and MAPE. Additionally, MSCMNet_WM and MSCMNet_W, which include FC2, show approximately 5 % to 20 % performance improvement over the baseline models, and also demonstrate stronger improvement capabilities compared to MSCMNet_W, which only includes FC1. By comparing Fig. 2(a) and 2(b), it is evident that in multi-step forecasting, models with the correction module show a stronger performance improvement over the baseline models compared to single-step forecasting.

In both single-step and multi-step forecasting, while the other three metrics remain relatively small, some NSE values turn negative (S1). This is likely because predictions are close to the true values for most points, but show significant deviations in a few challenging cases, such as sudden shifts in water demand during holidays or other anomalous periods. This issue remains a persistent challenge for all forecasting models when dealing with high-frequency data (Xu et al. 2019, Pu et al. 2022). This phenomenon is particularly evident in the forecasting of DMA F, indicating that, under these conditions, none of the models outperformed predictions based on the simple mean.

Discussion

In the comparison of model performance, it is undeniable that models with the Correction Module, represented by MSCMNet_W and MSCMNet_WM, outperform the others. Moreover, the performance of MSCMNet_M, which only includes FC1, is inferior to that of MSCMNet_WM and MSCMNet_W with FC2. The latter two exhibit high robustness in both single-step and multi-step forecasting. It is also observed that, although MSCMNet_W demonstrates the best overall performance in total demand, it underperforms compared to MSCMNet_WM in individual DMAs. This indicates that MSCMNet_WM shows greater versatility in handling the heterogeneity across different DMAs. In the comparison of extreme demand DMAs for the multi-step task, it is clear that, due to very low water demand, DMA C exhibits high uncertainty, resulting in much poorer model performance compared to DMA E. This again demonstrates that all models tend to perform worse in DMAs with high uncertainty in water demand. However, MSCMNet_WM and MSCMNet_W still exhibit robustness, further highlighting their superior performance in DMAs with uncertainty.

The result of training convergence analysis suggests that the Correction Module contributes to preventing model overfitting, providing a solid foundation for the model's potential applications.

Fig. 6(a) suggests that selecting input sequence lengths of [7, 14, 21, 28] days for different DMAs ensures high predictability. This finding highlights the predictability of the demand weight time series and provides a basis for determining the input sequence length for FC2. Fig. 6(b) indicates that multivariate inputs, particularly the inclusion of water demand data from other DMAs and certain meteorological features, rescued the model's poor performance on DMA F.

Fig. 6(c) shows that, regardless of the city's division, the impact between water supply zones should not be overlooked when forecasting partitioned water demand. All the supply zones in this study are closely interconnected, further underscoring the importance of FC2 in the model design. Additionally, this paper identifies the dependency of WDF in commercial/industrial districts on meteorological features, and the independence of facility districts, such as hospitals and stadiums, from these features. Moreover, suburban residential areas are more influenced by meteorological factors compared to those near the city center. The SHAP value quantification is used to guide the construction of related forecasting models.

Conclusion

The study innovatively proposes the use of the proportion of each DMA's daily water demand relative to the total daily demand of the region (FC2) and meteorological/temporal features (FC1) to correct model errors. It predicts the Daily water demand with 1 hour resolution of multiple DMAs within the same region and conducts multi-step forecasting over a 168-hour horizon. The results demonstrate that MSCMNet_WM, which incorporates both FC1 and FC2, and MSCMNet_W, which includes only FC2, excel in performance on the WDF at both the single DMA-level and the Total demand level. Compared to other models, these approaches enhance various metrics by approximately 5 %-20 % in both single-step and multi-step predictions. Additionally, the study discusses the roles of FC1 and FC2 specifically from the perspectives of ApEn and SHAP, and examines the conditions under which meteorological features might be effective, offering a novel framework for applying DLT in multi-DMA WDF.

This study also observed the inherent difficulty of machine learning models in fitting high-frequency components in time series data. While the model simultaneously predicts all DMAs’ demands, it is hindered by an excessive number of parameters and increased structural complexity compared to baseline models. In the future, reducing model complexity and overcoming high-frequency fitting issues may be addressed by considering the topological connections between DMAs.

Dataset Description

This study examined a water distribution system in northeastern Italy, serving a diverse range of areas that vary significantly in terms of characteristics, size, and average water demand. The raw data consists of approximately 19,056 entries of water volume and meteorological data with a temporal resolution of 1 hour, spanning from January 1, 2021, to March 5, 2023. This dataset originates from “The Battle of Water Demand Forecasting” Conference (2024).

The water demand across the ten DMAs is assumed to be reflected by the net inflow, thereby capturing all types of water consumption and leakages within these areas. Notably, none of the DMAs are equipped with storage facilities. The meteorological data for the region includes air temperature, rainfall depth, air humidity, and wind speed. They were processed using linear interpolation and the quartile method for outlier exclusion. Multi-Scale Correction Module Neural Network Architecture

Basical model

To conduct Multi-Step Forecasting for ten DMAs with vastly different land characteristics and water usage, and to emphasize the potential interactions between DMAs, multiple variables need to be input into the model. Previous studies, when addressing the issue of multiple variables, often adopt a hybrid framework to construct an encoder-decoder network that captures the temporal characteristics of the input variables (Hu, Tong et al. 2019, Pu, Yan et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2022). Such a framework can also refocus on the importance of different features and enhance the role of input features, such as Attention mechanisms (Zhou, Guo et al. 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Inspired by this, this study proposes a novel predictive network framework. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the core structure of this approach, the Forecast module, consists of a CNN-Attention module (CAM) and an LSTM module (LM).Fig. 7Overview of the multi-scale correction module neural network architecture.Fig 7

Overview of the multi-scale correction module neural network architecture.

The convolutional neural network (CNN) is a feedforward neural network that was initially inspired by the work of (Hubel and Wiesel 1968) and further developed into the widely used CNN architecture through advancements by researchers such as (Cun et al. 1989) and (Fukushima 1980). The core concept of CNNs is to extract local features from data through convolutional layers and abstract them into higher-level features layer by layer, ultimately facilitating tasks such as classification and recognition. This approach has proven highly effective, particularly in the field of image processing (Krizhevsky et al. 2012). Attention mechanism is inspired by the attention characteristics of the human brain (Vaswani et al. 2017). The core idea of the Attention mechanism is to focus on different parts of the input data by assigning weights, thereby capturing important information more effectively. This approach has significantly enhanced model performance and efficiency, particularly in natural language processing and image recognition. The effectiveness of these two models in extracting input data features within WDF has been well demonstrated, leading to their widespread application (Kao et al. 2020, Chen et al. 2022). Model development

The baseline model uses only historical water demand data as input, with{Xd−di:d−1i}i=1N, and separately trains an independent GRU/LSTM model for each of the ten distinct DMAs.

Starting with the analysis of Fig. 7, this study proposes four models to investigate the Water Demand Forecasting (WDF) problem across multiple DMAs. The models are all based on a multi-step network (MSNet) that forecasts the demand for multiple DMAs simultaneously. MSNet uses the history data from DMA 1 to DMA i as input, represented as {Xd−di:d−1i}i=1N,{Md−di:d−1n}n=1Nm,{Td−di:d−1n}n=1Nn, and the uncorrected X^t0,X^t0∈R10×24×1 as the output. For each independent DMA, the CAM-LM Forecast module, with different network structure parameters, is used for prediction. The results are then concatenated under the fully connected layer, allowing the gradient to be calculated together during backpropagation.

If X^t0 is concatenated with {Mdn}n=1Nm,{Tdn}n=1Nn, a single fully connected correction will yieldX^t1, X^t1∈R10×24×1. At this point, based on MSNet, multi-scale correction module net called MSCMNet_M is obtained, and this fully connected layer is referred to as FC1. In the top left of Fig. 7, a module uses a time series with length of min(di), which includes each DMA's daily water demand weight and daily maximum and minimum temperature (Rmin(di)×12×1), as input to predict the next day's water consumption ratio for each DMA, generating a prediction series(R1×10×1). Using this series and X^t1 for a fully connected operation, referred to as FC2, the results are further corrected to obtain X^t2. This model corresponds to the overall structure shown in Fig. 7 and is called MSCMNet_MW.

It is worth noting that to investigate the impact of meteorological features, all meteorological data were removed in MSCMNet_MW, specifically retaining only {Xd−di:d−1i}i=1N and {Td−di:d−1n}n=1Nnin DMA history data. In FC2, the temperature feature was excluded, and in FC1, only {Tdn}n=1Nnremained. This structure is referred to as MSCMNet_W.

In summary, this study innovatively develops three multi-step WDF frameworks for multiple DMAs by modifying the Correction module and input data based on MSNet. For all the above six models, including the baseline model, the 'Hyperopt' (Bergstra, Komer et al. 2015)program was used for Bayesian optimization to ensure optimal performance within the parameter search range (see S2). Benchmarking metrics

Various metrics are used to evaluate models, among which mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), root-mean square error (RMSE), and Nash-Sutcliffe model efficiency (NSE) are widely adopted in the literatures for their comprehensive assessment capabilities (Krause et al. 2005, Guo, Liu et al. 2018). Therefore, this study employs these four metrics to evaluate the performance of all models.(14)MAE=1N∑i=1N|Yi−Y^i|(15)RMSE=1N∑i=1N(Yi−Y^i)2(16)MAPE=1N∑i=1N|Yi−Y^iYi|×100%(17)NSE=1−1N∑i=1N(Yi−Y^i)21N∑i=1N(Yi−Y¯i)2

WhereYi=observed value;Y^i=forecasted value; Y¯i=the mean of the observed value. MAE, RMSE, and MAPE are all positive error metrics, with lower values indicating improved model performance as they approach zero. The NSE range is (−∞,1]; the larger the value, the better, and if it is less than 0, it indicates that the model performs worse than using the mean for prediction (Nash and Sutcliffe 1970). The input and output data resolution of this study is one hour, corresponding to Δi=1, with the above metrics calculated based on the forecast step length.

Interpretability Analysis Methods

Although the application of DLT has significantly improved accuracy in the era of big data (LeCun et al. 2015), the complexity of the models poses challenges even for experts to interpret, which is crucial for model development and improvement (Lundberg and Lee 2017).

Therefore, this study aims to elucidate two key aspects: 1) the rationale behind selecting the weights of each DMA's demand relative to the total demand as the Correction Module, and 2) The influence of various features on the output, with a particular focus on further exploring the extent to which meteorological features play a role. To address the former, ApEn (Pincus 1995) is employed to explore the selection of FC2 and investigate the underlying reasons for its potential effectiveness, while the latter is examined using SHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017) to interpret the impact of different features. The primary contribution of this study lies in addressing the latter issue by, for the first time, using SHAP to provide an objective explanation. In contrast to previous research in the WDF field, which either unilaterally dismissed the significance of this factor (Seok, Kim et al. 2014, Mu, Zheng et al. 2020) or discussed it subjectively based on its divergence from ground truth, this study offers a more rigorous and nuanced analysis (Zanfei, Brentan et al. 2022).

In this study, considering that some features, such as holidays and other one-hot encoded features, are normalized post-input, a uniform value of -2 is used for features excluded from predictions. This ensures scenarios where features don't participate in the model are represented. Thus, -2 is used as the baseline when calculating SHAP values, minimizing the impact of differences in feature input distribution densities.
