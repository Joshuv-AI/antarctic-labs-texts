Finite-Time Convergence Analysis of ODE-based Generative Models for Stochastic Interpolants
Yuhao Liu, Rui Hu, Yu Chen, Longbo Huang

Abstract
Stochastic interpolants offer a robust framework for continuously transforming samples between arbitrary data distributions, holding significant promise for generative modeling. Despite their potential, rigorous finite-time convergence guarantees for practical numerical schemes remain largely unexplored. In this work, we address the finite-time convergence analysis of numerical implementations for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) derived from stochastic interpolants. Specifically, we establish novel finite-time error bounds in total variation distance for two widely used numerical integrators: the first-order forward Euler method and the second-order Heun’s method. Furthermore, our analysis on the iteration complexity of specific stochastic interpolant constructions provides optimized schedules to enhance computational efficiency. Our theoretical findings are corroborated by numerical experiments, which validate the derived error bounds and complexity analyses.



1
Introduction

Stochastic interpolants
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Albergo et al., 2023
)
provide a powerful framework for constructing generative models by learning deterministic or stochastic transformations that continuously map samples from an initial distribution
\rho_{0}
to a target distribution
\rho_{1}
, governed by ordinary or stochastic differential equations (ODEs/SDEs).
To determine the differential equation, the approach constructs stochastic interpolations between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
samples, then estimates a mean velocity field from these paths. With a learned approximation of the velocity field, one can build a generative model by solving the approximated ODE or SDE.
This novel framework unifies flow matching
(
Lipman et al., 2023
)
and score-based diffusion
(
Song and Ermon, 2020
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
)
, offering significant design flexibility through its various choices of the initial distribution and generalized interpolation formulation, making it an important subject for theoretical investigation.

Theoretical guarantees regarding generation error bounds have been established for both ODE-based and SDE-based transformations within the stochastic interpolant framework
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Albergo et al., 2023
;
Benton et al., 2024b
)
. However, these existing analyses mainly examine the continuous-time scenario, relying on the idealized assumption of exact solutions. In practical applications, numerical approximation methods must be employed to solve these equations, introducing additional discretization errors. To develop a complete theoretical understanding of the computational complexity in the generation process, a rigorous analysis of finite-time convergence for discrete-time implementations becomes essential. In this paper, we focus on the ODE-based transformations, and investigate the following research question:

What are the non-asymptotic convergence rates for discrete-time ODE implementations of stochastic interpolants?

This problem is important yet technically challenging. While similar analyses exist for diffusion models and related approaches that transform Gaussian to target distributions (e.g.,
Li et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2024b
;
Huang et al., 2025
), they cannot be directly extended to general stochastic interpolants due to the general data-to-data transformation structures.
Liu et al., 2025
recently established finite-time error bounds for the SDE case; however, their bounds exhibit singular behavior when the SDE degenerates to an ODE, indicating the necessity for novel analytical approaches to establish a complete theoretical foundation.

In this paper, We derive rigorous finite-time error bounds for both first-order and second-order numerical methods in the ODE setting. Building upon an existing framework for total variation (TV) error estimation (Lemma
6
), we introduce several key innovations to address the challenges: (i) novel continuous-time interpolations carefully designed for discrete-time schemes, (ii) improved error decomposition that enables tighter bounds, and (iii) control of higher-order derivatives through refined analytical techniques.

Contributions

This work provides the first systematic analysis for discretized ODE implementations of stochastic interpolants, with three main theoretical contributions:

•

This work establishes the first finite-time error bounds in total variation (TV) distance for discrete-time numerical approximations of stochastic interpolant ODEs. We provide a complete theoretical characterization of both first-order (forward Euler) and second-order (Heun’s) methods, rigorously quantifying the dependence of distributional approximation error on (i) problem parameters (including dimension
d
and properties of initial/target distributions) and (ii) numerical parameters (particularly the choice of step sizes and the order of numerical scheme).

•

Compared to existing analyses of ODE-based flows, our approach employs a novel error partition technique that yield tight bounds under an uncommon yet reasonable regularity assumptions. By introducing a Lipschitz divergence assumption that is typically satisfied for the true velocity functions in practical settings, we can decompose the total error into tractable expectation terms. Notably, for the second-order Heun’s method, our theoretical framework establishes improved results compared to prior works when reducing to diffusion models.

•

We have implemented both the forward Euler method and the Heun’s method to provide extensive numerical validation using both 2D distributions and high-dimensional Gaussian mixtures. These numerical results further validate our theoretical findings on convergence rates, demonstrating the practical implications of our theoretical results.

Related Works

2.1
Stochastic Interpolants Analysis

The stochastic interpolant framework originates from continuous-time normalizing flows, providing a principled approach for constructing data-to-data generative models
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
)
. Subsequent work
(
Albergo et al., 2023
)
extended this framework through the incorporation of Gaussian perturbations, enabling learnable score functions and facilitating the application of stochastic differential equations for data transformation tasks.

Regarding ODE-based formulations,
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
established Wasserstein error bounds for velocity field estimation under Lipschitz continuity assumptions.
Benton et al., 2024b
advanced these results by considering time-dependent Lipschitz constants, thereby obtaining tighter error bounds. Their analysis further examined the control of Lipschitz constants specifically for linear interpolants. Both studies focused exclusively on continuous-time settings.

In the SDE context,
Albergo et al., 2023
derived continuous-time Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence bounds between target and estimated distributions, expressed in terms of the mean squared error of drift estimation.
Liu et al., 2025
made progress by establishing finite-time error bounds for the Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme, representing the first discrete-time analysis within the stochastic interpolant framework. Their work additionally investigated the impact of schedule selection on convergence properties.

2.2
ODE-based Diffusion Models Analysis

Recent theoretical work has made significant progress in analyzing the convergence properties of probability-flow ODE diffusion models.
Chen et al., 2023
developed a provably efficient sampling algorithm incorporating corrector steps within the ODE solver framework, though this approach introduces additional stochasticity to the process.

Theoretical analyses of purely deterministic generation typically adopt two principal approaches. The first directly examines discrete-time density evolution, where
Li et al., 2024b
established a foundational framework for controlling the total variation (TV) distance between target and estimated distributions. Subsequent work
(
Li et al., 2024c
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
extended this framework to derive tighter bounds and accelerated convergence guarantees. The second approach considers equivalent continuous-time processes through partial differential equation (PDE) analysis. While both
Huang et al., 2025
and
(
Li et al., 2025
)
employed PDE techniques to bound TV error evolution, the latter achieved superior results through more refined error decomposition methods.

Current theoretical understanding indicates that first-order methods require
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)
iterations to achieve
\varepsilon
-precision in TV distance
(
Li et al., 2024c
)
. However, existing analyses of higher-order methods remain either non-tight or reliant on overly restrictive assumptions, highlighting the need for more comprehensive theoretical frameworks for ODE-based approaches.

3
Background on Stochastic Interpolants

Consider two probability distributions,
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The stochastic interpolant framework
(
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023
;
Albergo et al., 2023
)
provides an approach for constructing a learnable mapping between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
. Specifically, this is accomplished by identifying a learnable vector field
b(t,x)
such that the solution
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
to the ordinary differential equation

\textnormal{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})\textnormal{d}t

with initial condition
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
, satisfies
X_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
. Notably, this ODE formulation is similar to the flow matching framework.

To determine the vector field
b(t,x)
, we introduce the stochastic interpolant between
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
as a stochastic process defined by

x_{t}=I(t,x_{0},x_{1})+\gamma(t)z,

where
(x_{0},x_{1})\sim\nu
(with
x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
,
x_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
),
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is an independent Gaussian noise term. Notably,
\nu
may accommodate both paired and unpaired data settings. The
C^{2}
-smooth interpolation
I(t,x_{0},x_{1})
satisfies the boundary conditions
I(0,x_{0},x_{1})=x_{0}
and
I(1,x_{0},x_{1})=x_{1}
. The latent term
\gamma(t)z
serves to regularize the marginal distributions
\rho(t,\cdot)
of
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Typically,
\gamma(t)
is chosen such that

•

\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=0
(ensuring
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
and
\rho(1)=\rho_{1}
),

•

\gamma(t)>0
for
t\in(0,1)
(maintaining smoothness in the intermediate states).

A widely used example is the linear stochastic interpolant
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}+\sqrt{2t(1-t)}z
, where
\gamma(t)=\sqrt{2t(1-t)}
. This choice is particularly notable because, when
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the same marginal distributions of
x_{t}
coincide with those of the variance-preserving diffusion model, establishing a direct connection between interpolant-based methods and diffusion processes.

Although
x_{t}
establishes a connection between the two distributions, its computation depends on both
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, making it unsuitable as a generative model yet. However,
Albergo et al., 2023
demonstrated that the marginal density
\rho(t,x)
satisfies the following transport equation (where the notation
\nabla
represents the gradient operator):

\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot(\rho(t,x)b(t,x))=0.

(1)

Here, the velocity field
b(t,x)
is given by

b(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\dot{x}_{t}|x_{t}=x]=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}I(t,x_{0},x_{1})+\dot{\gamma}_{t}z|x_{t}=x].

This transport equation reveals that any process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
satisfying the initial condition
X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
and solving the ODE

\textnormal{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})\textnormal{d}t,

(2)

will share identical marginal distributions with the stochastic interpolant
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, i.e.,
X_{t}\sim\rho(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Crucially, the temporal derivative of
X_{t}
depends only on the current time
t
and position
X_{t}
. Consequently, when initialized with a sample
x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
, solving equation
2
yields
X_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
.

Furthermore, for any non-negative function
\epsilon(t)\geq 0
,
Albergo et al., 2023
shows that the solution to the SDE

\textnormal{d}X_{t}=[b(t,X_{t})+\epsilon(t)s(t,X_{t})]\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2\epsilon(t)}\textnormal{d}W_{t}

also transforms samples from
\rho_{0}
into
\rho_{1}
, where
W_{t}
denotes the standard Wiener process and
s(t,x)=\nabla\log\rho(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\gamma^{-1}z|x_{t}=x]
represents the well-known score function. Notably, the ODE in equation
2
emerges as a special case of this SDE when
\epsilon(t)\equiv 0
.

In practice, an estimator
\widehat{b}(t,x)
of the expected velocity field
b(t,x)
can be obtained by minimizing the quadratic loss:

\mathcal{L}[\widehat{b}]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\widehat{b}(t,x_{t})\|^{2}-b(t,x_{t})\cdot\dot{x}_{t}\right]\textnormal{d}t,

where
x_{t}\sim\rho(t)
denotes the stochastic interpolant process. This loss differs from the mean squared error

\text{MSE}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\widehat{b}(t,x_{t})-b(t,x_{t})\|^{2}\right]\textnormal{d}t

by a constant that is independent of
\widehat{b}
, as seen by expanding the quadratic term. The resulting estimator
\widehat{b}(t,x)
, when used in equation
2
, yields a generative model that transports
\rho_{0}
to
\rho_{1}
.

4
Main Results for Using the Forward Euler Method

In this section, we analyze the forward Euler method, a fundamental first-order approximation technique for ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Initially, we provide a formal definition of the method when applied to the stochastic interpolant case. Given a time discretization, a schedule
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
satisfying
t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{N}
is specified. Let
\widehat{X}_{t_{0}}
denote the initial condition for the equation. At the
(k+1)
-th iteration, the forward Euler method approximates the solution by

\widehat{X}_{t_{k+1}}=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+h_{t_{k}}\cdot\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}),

where
\widehat{b}(t,x)
represents an estimator for the true drift function
b(t,x)
, and
h_{t_{k}}=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
is the step size. If
\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}
denotes the true solution of
\widehat{\rho}(t_{k})
, our objective is to quantify and control the discrepancy between the approximated terminal distribution
\widehat{\rho}(t_{N})
and the true terminal distribution
\rho(t_{N})
.

To facilitate a rigorous analysis of the forward Euler method, we introduce the following set of assumptions, which are requisite for our subsequent derivations.

Assumption 1
.

\underset{(x_{0},x_{1})\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{4}\right]<\infty
. Furthermore, there exist positive constants
C_{I},C_{\gamma}>0
such that for all
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{p}I(t,x_{0},x_{1})\|\leq C_{I}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{p}}{\textnormal{d}t^{p}}\left[\gamma^{2}(t)\right]\right|\leq C_{\gamma}.

Assumption
1
is essential for guaranteeing the requisite regularity of the stochastic interpolant process
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
and the function
b(t,x)
. In the case of a linear interpolant
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}+\sqrt{2t(1-t)}z
, this assumption is satisfied if both
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
possess finite fourth moments
(
Albergo et al., 2023
)
.

Assumption 2
.

The estimator satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}\underset{x_{t_{k}}\sim\rho(t_{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(x_{t_{k}})^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{\text{drift}}^{2}<\infty,

where
\varepsilon_{1,k}(x)=\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-b(t_{k},x)\|
.

Assumption
2
is a standard condition concerning the quality of the estimator, aligning with similar assumptions found in prior works
(
Benton et al., 2024a
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
. Unlike continuous-time mean squared error conditions, this assumption provides a discrete-time formulation, focusing on the error specifically at the time steps utilized by the numerical method.

However, in contrast to models based solely on stochastic differential equations (e.g.,
Liu et al., 2025
), Assumption
2
alone is insufficient to control the overall distribution error
(
Li et al., 2024b
;
Li et al., 2024a
;
Li et al., 2025
)
. Consequently, we introduce further assumptions regarding the properties of
\widehat{b}(t,x)
.

Assumption 3
.

The estimator satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}\underset{x_{t_{k}}\sim\rho(t_{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k}(x_{t_{k}})\right]\leq\varepsilon_{\text{div}}<\infty,

where
\varepsilon_{2,k}(x)=\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-\nabla b(t_{k},x)\right\|_{F}
. Here the notation
\nabla^{p}b(t,x)
represents the tensor consisting of the
p
-th order derivatives of
b
with respect to
x
(e.g., when
p=1
, this notation represents the Jacobian matrix).

Assumption 4
.

\widehat{b}(t,x)
is
C^{2}
w.r.t.
x
. Furthermore, both
\widehat{b}(t,x)
and
\nabla\cdot\widehat{b}(t,x)
are Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
x
. Specifically, there exists a constant
L>0
such that for all
k=0,1,\dots,N
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)\right\|_{F}\leq L,\quad\left\|\nabla^{2}\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)\right\|_{F}\leq L^{3/2}.

In addition, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|\nabla\cdot\widehat{b}(t,x)-\nabla\cdot\widehat{b}(t,y)\|\leq L^{3/2}\|x-y\|.

Assumption
3
extends the requirements to the Jacibian matrices of
\widehat{b}(t,x)
, stipulating its proximity to the true divergence. Assumption
4
imposes a uniform Lipschitz constant on both
\widehat{b}(t,x)
and its divergence. The reasonableness of Assumption
4
can be illustrated by considering cases where the data for both
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are bounded in each dimension; in such scenarios, Lemma
A.7
in the Appendix demonstrates that
L
is of order
O(d)
.

With the preceding assumptions established, we are now ready to present the main theoretical result concerning the application of the forward Euler method.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, suppose the forward Euler method is initialized with
\widehat{X}_{t_{0}}\sim\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})
, and the step sizes satisfy
h_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2L}
. Then,

\displaystyle\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{N}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{0}))+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{div}}+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{drift}}\left(d^{1/2}S(\gamma,t_{0},t_{N})^{1/2}+L^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}^{2}\left[\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}d^{2}+\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}M^{2}\right].}_{\textnormal{Discretization Error}}

Here, the terms are defined as:

\begin{cases}\overline{\gamma}_{k}&:=\inf_{t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}\gamma(t),\\
S(\gamma,t_{0},t_{N})&:=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\gamma^{-2}(t)\textnormal{d}t,\\
M&:=\max\left\{d,L,\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{4}\right]}\right\}.\end{cases}

Theorem
5
provides an comprehensive upper bound for the total variation (TV) distance between the true target distribution
\rho(t_{N})
and the distribution approximated by the forward Euler method,
\widehat{\rho}(t_{N})
. This bound elucidates the influence of several critical factors: the initialization error
\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))
, dimension
d
, the distance between source and target distribution captured by
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{4}]
, the Lipschitz constant
L
, the latent scale term
\gamma(t)
, the estimation errors
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{drift}}
and
\varepsilon_{\textnormal{div}}
, and finally, the step sizes
\{h_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
.

To avoid an unbounded right-hand side in Theorem
5
when
\gamma(0)=0
or
\gamma(1)=0
(which ensures
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
and
\rho(1)=\rho_{1}
in the stochastic interpolant definition), we follow
Liu et al., 2025
and simulate the process within a sub-interval
[t_{0},t_{N}]\subset(0,1)
. This means our sampling starts from an estimation of
\rho(t_{0})
rather than
\rho_{0}
, and similarly aims for an estimation of
\rho(t_{N})
instead of
\rho_{1}
. This approach is justified because
\rho(t_{0})
and
\rho(t_{N})
are close to
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, respectively, when
t
is close to
0
and
t_{N}
is close to
1
. In addition, the initialization error can be made very small when
\rho_{0}
is available (e.g., if
I(t_{0},x_{0},x_{1})=x_{0}
). This technique is also known as early stopping in the context of diffusion models
(
Song et al., 2021
;
Hongrui et al., 2023
;
Benton et al., 2024a
)
.

The step sizes
\{h_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
specifically influence the last term of Theorem
5
, which directly corresponds to the convergence rate of the forward Euler method. Since this error term is proportional to
h_{k}^{2}
, it approaches zero as the step sizes tend to zero. The remaining terms in the theorem are unaffected by the choice of step sizes, representing the inherent quality achievable with an infinite number of steps.

4.1
Complexity of Forward Euler Method

Theorem
5
broadly applies to various definitions of
I
,
\gamma
and step size schedules
\{h_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
, provided the stochastic interpolant assumptions are met. This section explores two specific stochastic interpolant definitions and their corresponding computational complexities.

Stochastic Interpolants with
\gamma(t)=\sqrt{at(1-t)}

This choice, previously examined by
Liu et al., 2025
, is natural as
\gamma^{2}(t)
represents the variance of a Brownian bridge. It satisfies
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=0
and Assumption
1
.

To optimize the schedule, we focus on minimizing the last term in Theorem
5
:

\varepsilon\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}^{2}\left[\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}d^{2}+\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}M^{2}\right].

Following
Liu et al., 2025
, the error’s proportionality to
h_{k}^{2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}
suggests setting
h_{k}\propto\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{2}
. This leads to the following schedule: define a midpoint
m
at
t_{m}=0.5
and select a step size scale parameter
h>0
. The schedule
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
is then given by

\begin{cases}t_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(1-h)^{m-k},&k\leq m;\\
t_{k}=1-\frac{1}{2}(1-h)^{k-m},&k>m.\end{cases}

(3)

This schedule ensures
h_{k}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}=O(h)
, resulting in a total number of steps
N=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{h}\log\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}(1-t_{N})}\right)\right)
.

Building on the schedule defined above, and setting
\delta=\min\{t_{0},1-t_{N}\}
, Theorem
5
yields the following error bound:

\displaystyle\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{N}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{0}))+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{div}}+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{drift}}\left(d^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(1/\delta)+L^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{h\left[d^{2}\log(1/\delta)+M^{2}\right]}_{\textnormal{Discretization Error}}.

This indicates that achieving an
\varepsilon
TV error requires
N=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[d^{2}\log^{2}(1/\delta)+M^{2}\log(1/\delta)\right]\right)
steps.

Variance-Preserving Diffusion Models

Now, consider a stochastic interpolant defined by
x_{1}\sim\rho_{\text{data}}
,
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
x_{t}=tx_{1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z
. Here,
\rho(0)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
\rho(1)=\rho_{\text{data}}
, and the marginal distributions align with those of variance-preserving diffusion models.

Similar to the previous case, we aim for
h_{k}\propto\gamma^{2}=1-t^{2}=\Theta(1-t)
. This leads to the schedule:

t_{k}=1-(1-h)^{k},\quad 0\leq k\leq N,

(4)

where
h>0
controls the step sizes.

If
\delta=1-t_{N}
is the early-stopping time, the number of steps
N
is
N=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{h}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
. Assuming
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{1}\|^{4}]=O(d^{2})
and
L=O(d)
(which holds, e.g., if
\rho_{\text{data}}
is bounded in each dimension), by the previous discussion, achieving an
\varepsilon
error requires
N=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}d^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
steps. This
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
iteration complexity matches prior theoretical findings
(
Li et al., 2024b
;
Li et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2024c
)
. While
Li et al., 2024c
reports a faster
\widetilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)
complexity, their result relies on stochastic localization, which is restricted to Gaussian-to-data scenarios. Our analysis, however, extends to the more general data-to-data case.

4.2
Proof Sketch of Theorem
5

We’ll briefly outline the proof for Theorem
5
; the complete details are in the Appendix. The proof hinges on Lemma
6
(Lemma 3.2 by
Li et al., 2025
), which provides a way to control the TV distance between two processes.

Lemma 6
.

(Lemma 3.2 by
Li et al., 2025
)
For two processes
X_{t}
and
\widehat{X}_{t}
governed by
\textnormal{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})\textnormal{d}t
and
\textnormal{d}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widehat{b}(t,\widehat{X}_{t})\textnormal{d}t
, respectively. Let
\rho(t)
and
\widehat{\rho(t)}
be their corresponding laws. Then:

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t),\widehat{\rho}(t))

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{t}}(\nabla\cdot b(t,x)-\nabla\cdot\widehat{b}(t,x))\rho(t,x)\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{t}}(b(t,x)-\widehat{b}(t,x))\nabla\log\rho(t,x)\rho(t,x)\textnormal{d}x,

where
\Omega_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\widehat{\rho}(t,x)>\rho(t,x)\}
.

To apply Lemma
6
, we must express the discrete-time forward Euler process
\{\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}\}_{k=0}^{N}
as a continuous-time process. This is achieved by the following interpolation:

\widehat{X}_{t}=F_{t_{k}\to t}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+(t-t_{k})\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}).

Provided Assumption
4
holds and the step size
h_{k}
is sufficiently small,
F_{t_{k}\to t}
becomes a diffeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to itself. This allows us to write

\textnormal{d}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})\textnormal{d}t=\widehat{b}(t_{k},F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(\widehat{X}_{t}))\textnormal{d}t.

By defining
\widetilde{b}(t,x)=\widehat{b}(t_{k},F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(x))
, the equation takes the form
\textnormal{d}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widetilde{b}(t,\widehat{X}_{t})
, which is precisely the structure required by Lemma
6
.

The remainder of the proof involves demonstrating the proximity of
\widetilde{b}(t,x)
to
b(t,x)
, and similarly for their divergences. While similar in spirit to
Li et al., 2025
, our approach partitions the error differently. Let
z=F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(X_{t})
. The core idea is a sequential approximation:

For the drift term:

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})

\displaystyle=\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)\overset{\text{(a)}}{\approx}\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\overset{\text{(b)}}{\approx}b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\overset{\text{(c)}}{\approx}b(t,X_{t}),

For the divergence term:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)\cdot\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\right]\overset{\text{(a)}}{\approx}\text{tr}\left[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\cdot I_{d}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\approx}\nabla\cdot b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\overset{\text{(c)}}{\approx}\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t}).

Here, step (a) relies on
z\approx X_{t_{k}}
and
\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(z)\approx I_{d}
. Step (b) accounts for estimation errors, leveraging Assumptions
2
and
3
. Finally, step (c) quantifies the discretization error between time
t_{k}
and
t
.

5
Main Results for Using Heun’s Method

This section delves into Heun’s method, a widely recognized second-order approximation for ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Formally, given a sequence of discrete time steps
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
, let
\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}
denote the estimated solution at time
t_{k}
. Beginning with an initial condition
\widehat{X}_{t_{0}}
, the method proceeds via the following iterative scheme:

\begin{cases}\widetilde{X}_{t_{k+1}}=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+h_{t_{k}}\cdot\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}),\\
\widehat{X}_{t_{k+1}}=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}h_{t_{k}}\left[\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})+\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},\widetilde{X}_{t_{k+1}})\right].\end{cases}

As with the first-order case, our primary objective is to quantify and control the difference between the estimated distribution
\widehat{\rho}(t_{N})
and the true distribution
\rho(t_{N})
, where
\widehat{\rho}(t_{k})
represents the distribution of
\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}
. To accommodate this higher-order numerical method, certain assumptions require modification.

Assumption 1’
.

\underset{(x_{0},x_{1})\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]<\infty
. Furthermore, there exist positive constants
C_{I},C_{\gamma}
such that for all
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{p}I(t,x_{0},x_{1})\|\leq C_{I}\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{p}}{\textnormal{d}t^{p}}\left[\gamma^{2}(t)\right]\right|\leq C_{\gamma}.

Assumption 2’
.

The estimator fulfills the condition

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(x_{t_{k}})^{2}+\varepsilon_{1,k+1}(x_{t_{k+1}})^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{\text{drift}}^{2}<\infty,

where
\varepsilon_{1,k}(x)=\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-b(t_{k},x)\|
.

Assumption 3’
.

The estimator satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k}(x_{t_{k}})^{2}+\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(x_{t_{k+1}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\varepsilon_{\text{div}}<\infty,

where
\varepsilon_{2,k}(x)=\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-\nabla b(t_{k},x)\right\|_{F}
.

Assumptions
1’
,
2’
, and
3’
represent refined versions of Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, respectively, specifically adapted for Heun’s method. Assumption
1’
mandates a finite sixth moment (in contrast to a fourth moment) and requires
I
and
\gamma
to possess bounded derivatives up to the third order (rather than the second). Assumptions
2’
and
3’
incorporate an additional term, reflecting the fact that Heun’s method necessitates two evaluations of
\widehat{b}(t,x)
per step. It’s worth noting that Assumption
3’
utilizes
\varepsilon_{2,k}^{2}
instead of
\varepsilon_{2,k}
; this is equivalent to employing
\varepsilon_{2,k}
when a uniform bound on
\varepsilon_{2,k}
exists, which occurs when both
\nabla\cdot\widehat{b}(t,x)
and
\nabla\cdot b(t,x)
are bounded.

With the preceding assumptions established, we are now prepared to present our main theorem for Heun’s method.

Theorem 7
.

Under Assumptions
1’
,
2’
,
3’
,
4
, if the equation is solved using Heun’s method with an initial condition
\widehat{X}_{t_{0}}\sim\widehat{\rho}(t_{0})
, and provided the step sizes satisfy
h_{k}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{4L},\mathbb{E}[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}]^{-1/3},d^{-1}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{2}\right\}
, then the TV distance is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{N}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{0}))+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{div}}+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{drift}}\left(d^{1/2}S(\gamma,t_{0},t_{N})^{1/2}+L^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}^{3}\left[\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-6}d^{3}+\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}M^{3}\right].}_{\textnormal{Discretization Error}}

Here, the terms are defined as

\begin{cases}\overline{\gamma}_{k}&:=\inf_{t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}\gamma(t),\\
S(\gamma,t_{0},t_{N})&:=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\gamma^{-2}(t)\textnormal{d}t,\\
M&:=\max\left\{d,L,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3}\right\},\end{cases}

Similar to Theorem
5
, Theorem
7
establishes an upper bound for the TV distance between the estimated distribution and the true target distribution. The primary distinction lies in the last term, where the order of the step sizes is enhanced from
h_{k}^{2}
to
h_{k}^{3}
, reflecting the higher-order accuracy of Heun’s method.

5.1
Complexity of Heun’s Method

Similar to the forward Euler case, we analyze the complexity of Heun’s method for the same stochastic interpolant instances.

Stochastic Interpolants with
\gamma(t)=\sqrt{at(1-t)}

For this case, the last term in Theorem
7
is proportional to
h_{k}^{3}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-6}
. Balancing the error again suggests
h_{k}\propto\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{2}
leading to the exponentially decaying schedule equation
3
. Let
\delta=\min\{t_{0},1-t_{N}\}
. Theorem
7
then yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{N}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))

\displaystyle\lesssim\textnormal{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{0}))+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{div}}+\varepsilon_{\textnormal{drift}}\left(d^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(1/\delta)+L^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{h^{2}\left[d^{3}\log(1/\delta)+M^{3}\right]}_{\textnormal{Discretization Error}}

Since
N=\Theta(h^{-1}\log(1/\delta))
, the above inequality shows that achieving an
\varepsilon
-TV error requires

N=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[d^{3/2}\log^{3/2}(1/\delta)+M^{3/2}\log(1/\delta)\right]\right)

steps. This represents a reduction in complexity from
O(1/\varepsilon)
(for forward Euler) to
O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
.

Variance-Preserving Diffusion Models

For
x_{t}=tx_{1}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z
, using schedule equation
4
, we derive a similar complexity of
N=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left[d^{3/2}\log^{3/2}(1/\delta)+M^{3/2}\log(1/\delta)\right]\right)
,
where
\delta=1-t_{N}
is the early-stopping time. When
M=O(d)
, this simplifies to

N=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}d^{3/2}\log^{3/2}(1/\delta)\right)
. This
O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
convergence rate aligns with previous works
(
Li et al., 2024a
;
Huang et al., 2025
)
. Compared to the
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1/2}d^{3})
complexity by
Li et al., 2024a
(for a second-order method assuming bounded data support), our dependence on dimension
d
is lower. This improvement stems from an additional Lipschitzness assumption that holds in many practical scenarios. While
Huang et al., 2025
achieve a comparable
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1/2}d^{3/2})
complexity, their analysis relies on stricter assumptions. Specifically, they require uniform boundedness of derivatives up to the third order for the score estimator
\widehat{s}(t,x)
w.r.t. both
t
and
x
, even though the
t
-derivative for the true score function can be unbounded. In contrast, we only necessitate bounded second-order derivatives of
\widehat{b}(t,x)
w.r.t
x
. Furthermore, their Assumption 2 (analogous to our Assumption
2’
) considers errors across the entire interval
t\in[t_{0},t_{N}]
, whereas our assumption only requires consideration at the discrete time steps
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
actually used by Heun’s method.

5.2
Proof Sketch of Theorem
7

The proof of Theorem
7
, fully detailed in the Appendix, mirrors the first-order case by using Lemma
6
to control the TV distance. This requires converting the discrete-time process into a continuous one. For Heun’s method, we define the interpolation as:

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=G_{t_{k}\to t}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+\left[(t-t_{k})-\frac{(t-t_{k})^{2}}{2(t_{k+1}-t_{k})}\right]\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})+\frac{(t-t_{k})^{2}}{2(t_{k+1}-t_{k})}\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})).

This interpolation implies that
\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\widehat{X}_{t}
linearly interpolates between the estimated derivatives at
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
:

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\widehat{X}_{t}=\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}))
.
This provides a higher-order approximation than the forward Euler interpolation. While other interpolations exist (e.g., the approach of
Huang et al., 2025
), our chosen method simplifies analysis by only requiring access to
\widehat{b}(t,x)
at the discrete time steps
t\in\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
used in the Heun’s iteration.

When the step size is sufficiently small,
G_{t_{k}\to t}
is an diffeomorphism, ensuring
\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}=G_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(\widehat{X}_{t})
is uniquely determined. Write

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widetilde{b}(t,\widehat{X}_{t}):=\partial_{t}G_{t_{k}\to t}(G_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(\widehat{X}_{t}))
.
This allows application of Lemma
6
, requiring us to show that
b(t,x)\approx\widetilde{b}(t,x)
and
\nabla\cdot b(t,x)\approx\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,x)
. Let
z=G_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(X_{t})
, where
X_{t}
is the true solution of the equation equation
2
. We then apply a sequential approximation:

For the drift term:

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})=\partial_{t}G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\approx}\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\approx}\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}b(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(c)}}{\approx}b(t,X_{t}).

For the divergence term:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\bigg[\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z))\cdot\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\approx}\text{tr}\bigg[\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\cdot\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\approx}\text{tr}\bigg[\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla b(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\cdot\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(c)}}{\approx}\text{tr}\left[\nabla b(t,X_{t})\right]=\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t}).

Step (a) uses
z\approx X_{t_{k}}
and
F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\approx G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\approx X_{t_{k+1}}
. Step (b) relies on Assumptions
2’
and
3’
. Finally, step (c) accounts for the discretization error. For step (c), the key is that for a function
f(t)
the interpolation
f(t)\approx\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}f(t_{k})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}f(t_{k+1})
has an
O(h_{k}^{2})
error. Here, we apply this to
f(t)=b(t,X_{t})
and
f(t)=\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)
, respectively. For the divergence term, unlike the first-order case where
\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\approx I_{d}
(due to its
O(h_{k})
error), we group
\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)
and
\nabla b(t,X_{t})
for a combined second-order approximation.

7
Conclusions

This paper presented a finite-time analysis of discrete-time numerical implementations for ODEs derived within the stochastic interpolants framework. We established total variation distance error bounds for both the first-order forward Euler and second-order Heun’s methods, quantifying the discrepancy between true and approximated target distributions. Furthermore, we analyzed the iteration complexity for both methods, elucidating their convergence rates. Numerical experiments corroborated our theoretical convergence findings. However, results from
d
-dimensional Gaussian mixtures suggest the potential for tighter error bounds, motivating future work to validate optimal bounds and develop analyses to achieve them.

Appendix A
Technical Lemmas

In this section, we use the notation
x_{t}
to denote the stochastic process

x_{t}=I(t,x_{0},x_{1})+\gamma(t)z

defined in the stochastic interpolant, and use the notation
X_{t}
to denote the process satisfying the equation

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t})

and
X_{t}\sim\rho(t)
.

Recall that in stochastic interpolants, we have defined
b(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}I(t,x_{0},x_{1})+\dot{\gamma}z|x_{t}=x]
and
s(t,x)=\nabla\log\rho(t,x)=\gamma^{-1}(t)\mathbb{E}[z|x_{t}=x]

.

A.1
The Concrete Form of Derivatives of
s(t,x)
and
v(t,x)

Before introducing the lemmas, we first define the notation
f_{t}=-\frac{\|x-I(t,x_{0},x_{1})\|^{2}}{2\gamma(t)^{2}}
, so that the conditional density
p(x_{t}|x_{0},x_{1})\propto\exp(f_{t})
. The first lemma shows that how the jacobian matrices of
v
and
s
can be expressed.

Lemma A.1
.

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Cov}(\partial_{t}I,\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x),

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}s(t,x)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Cov}(\nabla_{x}f_{t},\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x)-\gamma^{-2}I_{d}.

Proof.

\nabla_{x}v(t,x)=\nabla_{x}\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})\partial_{t}I\textnormal{d}\nu}{\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}.

The conditions for Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem can be easily checked, so the order of differential and integral can be alternated. Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})(\nabla_{x}\partial_{t}I)\textnormal{d}\nu}{\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}+\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})\partial_{t}I\otimes\nabla_{x}f_{t}\textnormal{d}\nu}{\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}

\displaystyle-\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})\partial_{t}I\textnormal{d}\nu\otimes\int\exp(f_{t})\nabla_{x}f_{t}\textnormal{d}\nu}{\left[\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Cov}(\partial_{t}I,\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x).

Here the notation
\otimes
represents the tensor product. Similarly,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}s(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})(\nabla_{x}^{2}f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}{\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}+\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})\nabla_{x}f_{t}\otimes\nabla_{x}f_{t}\textnormal{d}\nu}{\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu}

\displaystyle-\frac{\int\exp(f_{t})\nabla_{x}f_{t}\textnormal{d}\nu\otimes\int\exp(f_{t})\nabla_{x}f_{t}\textnormal{d}\nu}{\left[\int\exp(f_{t})\textnormal{d}\nu\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Cov}(\nabla_{x}f_{t},\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x)+\nabla^{2}_{x}\left(-\frac{\|x-I\|^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Cov}(\nabla_{x}f_{t},\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x)-\gamma^{-2}I_{d}\textnormal{.}

∎

In the above calculations, the key is to change the order of taking differential and integral, and rearrange the final formula into the form of conditional expectations. The higher order derivatives can be obtained similarly, but since the calculation is too long, we will give the results without showing the detailed proof. Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
are similar as Lemma
A.1
, while we consider the second-order and third-order derivatives.

Lemma A.2
.

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{2}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{t}I-v(t,x))\otimes(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))\otimes(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))|x_{t}=x],

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{2}s(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))\otimes(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))\otimes(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))|x_{t}=x],

Note that
v(t,x)
and
s(t,x)
are the conditional expectations of
\partial_{t}I
and
\nabla_{x}f_{t}

.

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{3}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\partial_{t}I-v(t,x))\otimes(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))^{\otimes 3}|x_{t}=x]

\displaystyle-\mathcal{T}(\textnormal{Cov}(\partial_{t}I,\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x)\otimes\textnormal{Cov}(\nabla_{x}f_{t},\nabla_{x}f_{t}|x_{t}=x)),

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{3}s(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x))^{\otimes 4}|x_{t}=x]

Here, the notation
A^{\otimes n}

n

\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d},\quad\mathcal{T}(X)=\mathcal{T}_{1234}(X)+\mathcal{T}_{1324}(X)+\mathcal{T}_{1423}(X),

Lemma A.4

We first provide a uniform upper bound to explain why Assumption

Lemma A.7
.

Suppose that for all
t\in[0,1]
,
P(\|I(t,x_{0},x_{1})\|\leq R)=1
, i.e. the data is bounded. Then under Assumption 1,

\displaystyle\|\nabla b(t,x)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-4}R^{2},

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}b(t,x)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-6}R^{3},

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\nabla\cdot b(t,x))\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-6}R^{3}.

Proof.

According to Lemma
A.1
, since
\gamma\dot{\gamma}=O(1)
, for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by noticing that
\|x\otimes y\|_{F}=\|x\|\cdot\|y\|
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla b(t,x)\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla v(t,x)+\gamma(t)\dot{\gamma}(t)\nabla s(t,x)\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[(\|(\partial_{t}I-v(t,x))\|+\|\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x)\|)\cdot\|\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x)\||x_{t}=x].

Since
\|\partial_{t}I\|\lesssim R
,
\nabla_{x}f_{t}-s(t,x)=-\frac{x-I}{\gamma(t)^{2}}-\mathbb{E}[-\frac{x-I}{\gamma(t)^{2}}|x_{t}=x]=\gamma(t)^{-2}(I-\mathbb{E}[I|x_{t}=x])\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-2}R
, we have

\|\nabla b(t,x)\|\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-4}R^{2}.

Similarly,

\|\nabla^{2}b(t,x)\|_{F}\lesssim(\gamma(t)^{-2}R)^{2}(1+\gamma(t)^{-2})R\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-6}R^{3}.

\|\nabla(\nabla\cdot b(t,x))\|\lesssim(\gamma(t)^{-2}R)^{2}(1+\gamma(t)^{-2})R\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-6}R^{3}.

∎

A.7
justifies the choice of Lipschitz constants in Assumption

Lemma A.8
.

For a Gaussian random variable
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, for any constant
p\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|z\|^{p}\right]\leq C(p)d^{p/2},

where the constant
C(p)>0
only depends on
p
.

Proof.

First, by Jensen’s inequality,

\|z\|^{p}=(\|z\|^{2})^{p/2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}|z_{i}|^{2}\right)^{p/2}\leq d^{p/2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{p}|z_{i}|^{p},

so

\mathbb{E}\left[\|z\|^{p}\right]\leq d^{p/2}\mathbb{E}|z_{1}|^{p}.

Here,
C(p)=\mathbb{E}|z_{1}|^{p}<\infty
is a constant that only depends on
p
since
z_{1}\sim N(0,1)
is a standard 1-dimensional Gaussian variable.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem

B.1
Interpolation of the Discrete-time Process

First, for the forward Euler solver, we apply the following interpolation to reformulate the process as a continuous-time process.

\widehat{X}_{t}=F_{t_{k}\to t}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}):=\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}+(t-t_{k})\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}}),\quad\forall t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}),

that is,

\textnormal{d}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})\textnormal{d}t=\partial_{t}F_{t_{k}\to t}(\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})\textnormal{d}t.

To write
\widehat{b}(t_{k},\widehat{X}_{t_{k}})
as a function of
(t,\widehat{X}_{t})
, we first need to show that
F_{t_{k}\to t}
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to itself.

Lemma B.1
.

Under Assumption
4
, suppose that the step size
h_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2L}
, then
F_{t_{k}\to t}
is a diffeomorphism, and

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)\|\leq 2,\quad\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(x)\|\leq 2.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)=I_{d}+(t-t_{k})\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x).

So,

\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)-I_{d}\|=(t-t_{k})\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)\|\leq h_{k}L\leq\frac{1}{2}<1.

The above inequality shows that the Jacobi matrix
\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)
is invertible, so
F_{t_{k}\to t}
is a local diffeomprhism. At the same time, note that
\|F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)-F_{t_{k}\to t}(y)\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|
, so by Hadamard’s global inverse function theorem, it is a global diffeomorphism on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, the matrix norm of the inverse of its Jacobi matrix satisfies
\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(x)^{-1}\|\leq 2
.
∎

By Lemma
B.1
,
\widehat{X}_{t}
satisfies the ODE

\textnormal{d}\widehat{X}_{t}=\widetilde{b}(t,\widehat{X}_{t})\textnormal{d}t=\widehat{b}(t_{k},F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(\widehat{X}_{t}))\textnormal{d}t.

So according to Lemma
6
,

\displaystyle\text{TV}(\rho(t_{N}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{N}))

\displaystyle\leq\text{TV}(\rho(t_{0}),\widehat{\rho}(t_{0}))

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[|\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t})|\right]\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[\|\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-b(t,X_{t})\|\cdot\|\nabla\ln\rho(t,X_{t})\|\right]\textnormal{d}t.

In the following sections, we denote by
(X_{t})_{t\in[t_{0},t_{N}]}
the true solution of the original ODE. When
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
are given, we denote
z=F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(X_{t})
for simplicity. The rest of the problem is to control the velocity error
\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-b(t,X_{t})
and the divergence error
\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t})
.

B.2
Controlling the Velocity Error

By a simple triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-b(t,X_{t})\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)-\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\|}_{A}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\|}_{B}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\|b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-b(t,X_{t_{k}})\|}_{C}.

Below we discuss the above terms respectively. For simplicity, we use the notation
\varepsilon_{1,k}(x)=\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-b(t_{k},x)\|
to denote the error of
\widehat{b}(t,x)
at
(t_{k},x)
.

For the term
A
, by Assumption
4
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)-\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\|

\displaystyle\leq L\|z-X_{t_{k}}\|

\displaystyle=L\|F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(X_{t})-F_{t_{k}\to t}^{-1}(F_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}}))\|

\displaystyle\leq 2L\|X_{t}-F_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})\|,

where the last inequality uses Lemma
B.1
and the assumption that
h_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2L}
. For the term
C
, we introduce the following lemma:

Lemma B.2
.

\|X_{t}-F_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})\|\leq(t-t_{k})\int_{t_{k}}^{t}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}\left(b(s,X_{s})\right)\right\|\textnormal{d}s+(t-t_{k})\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Consider taking derivatives and then integrate, we obtain that

\displaystyle\|X_{t}-F_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})\|

First, according to the chain rule of derivatives,

For the term

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[\|\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})\|^{6/5}\right]^{5/6}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[\|s(t,X_{t})\|^{6}\right]^{1/6}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-1}d^{1/2}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k+1}})\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle+\gamma(t)^{-1}d^{1/2}Lh_{k}\left[(t-t_{k})^{1/5}\int_{t_{k}}^{t}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}b(s,X_{s})\right\|^{6/5}\textnormal{d}s\right]^{5/6}

\displaystyle+\gamma(t)^{-1}d^{1/2}h_{k}\left[(t-t_{k})^{1/5}\int_{t_{k}}^{t}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}s^{2}}b(s,X_{s})\right\|^{6/5}\textnormal{d}s\right]^{5/6}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(t)^{-1}d^{1/2}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k+1}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle+\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-3}d^{3/2}h_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/2}+d^{3}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-6}

\displaystyle+L\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}d\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3}+Ld^{2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}.

Integrate and write
M=\max\{\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3},L,d\}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[\|\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-b(t,X_{t})\|\cdot\|\nabla\ln\rho(t,X_{t})\|\right]\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{1/2}\varepsilon_{\text{drift}}S(\gamma,t_{0},t_{N})^{1/2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}^{3}\bigg[\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-6}d^{3}+M^{3}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}\bigg].

C.3
Controlling the Divergence Error

Use the notation
\varepsilon_{2,k}(x)=\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)-\nabla b(t_{k},x)\|_{F}
. First,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\bigg[\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z))\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}

\displaystyle-\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)^{-1}\bigg].

Then, by Lemma
C.1
,

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg|\text{tr}\bigg[\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z))\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)

\displaystyle-\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\left|\text{tr}\left[D_{\text{bias}}+D_{\text{est}}+D_{\text{dis}}\right]\right|,

where

\displaystyle D_{\text{bias}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\left[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\bigg[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z))\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})\bigg]

\displaystyle-\left[\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)-\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})\right],

\displaystyle D_{\text{est}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\left[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-\nabla b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\bigg[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})-\nabla b(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})\bigg],

\displaystyle D_{\text{dis}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\nabla b(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle-\nabla b(t,X_{t})\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}}).

Bias Error

For
D_{\text{bias}}
, with Assumption
4
,

\displaystyle\left|\text{tr}[D_{\text{bias}}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}L^{3/2}\|z-X_{t_{k}}\|

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z))-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\right\|_{F}\cdot\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\|_{F}\cdot\left\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)-\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla b(t,X_{t})\|_{F}\cdot\left\|\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})-\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(z)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\lesssim}\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}L^{3/2}\|z-X_{t_{k}}\|+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}L^{3/2}\left\|F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(z)-X_{t_{k+1}}\right\|

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}L\bigg\|h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},z)-\frac{1}{2}h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}}))\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})\bigg\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla b(t,X_{t})\|_{F}\cdot L^{3/2}h_{k}\|X_{t_{k}}-z\|

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\lesssim}\|z-X_{t_{k}}\|L^{3/2}\left(1+h_{k}\|\nabla b(t,X_{t})\|_{F}\right)+L^{3/2}\|F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})-X_{t_{k+1}}\|

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}\|\nabla F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})-I_{d}\|\cdot\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\|_{F}

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(c)}}{\lesssim}L^{3/2}(1+h_{k}\|\nabla b(t,X_{t})\|_{F})\bigg[h_{k}\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})+h_{k}\varepsilon_{1,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})

\displaystyle+h_{k}^{2}L\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}b(s,X_{s})\right\|\textnormal{d}s+h_{k}^{2}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}s^{2}}b(s,X_{s})\right\|\textnormal{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle+L^{3/2}h_{k}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}b(s,X_{s})\right\|\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}\left(\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})+\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})\right)

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}\left(\nabla b(s,X_{s})\right)\right\|_{F}\textnormal{d}s.

The inequality (a) applies Assumption
4
; the inequality (b) rearranges the terms and applies triangle inequalities; the inequality (c) expands the differences into the form of integrals by Lemma
B.2
,
C.2
, and we control the difference
\left\|\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\right\|_{F}
by controlling
\left\|\nabla b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})-\nabla b(t_{k+1},X_{t_{k+1}})\right\|_{F}
.

Notably, in the first term, when
dh_{k}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}\lesssim 1
and
h_{k}d^{1/2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{p}\right]^{1/p}\lesssim 1
(
p>1
is a small constant), we have
h_{k}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla b(t,X_{t})\|^{p}\right]^{1/p}\lesssim 1
,
so based on the assumptions on
h_{k}
, Lemmas
A.13
,
A.15
,
A.14
and Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\text{tr}[D_{\text{bias}}]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{3/2}h_{k}^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-1}d^{1/2}+\overline{\gamma}^{-3}d^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+L^{3/2}h_{k}^{3}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/2}d\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}+d^{5/2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-5}\right]

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}^{2}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3}d\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}+d^{2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}\right]

\displaystyle+L^{3/2}h_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})]\right)

\displaystyle+Lh_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})]\right).

Estimation Error

For
D_{\text{est}}
,

\displaystyle|\text{tr}[D_{\text{estimate}}]|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})\cdot\|\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})+\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}}).

Discretization Error

For
D_{\text{dis}}
, similarly to the analysis on the discretization error of
b(t,X_{t})
,

|\text{tr}[D_{\text{dis}}]|\leq(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}s^{2}}\text{tr}\left[\nabla b(s,X_{s})\nabla G_{t_{k}\to s}(X_{t_{k}})\right]\right|\textnormal{d}s.

We now explicitly write the derivatives of
\nabla G_{t_{k}\to s}
below for further discussion:

\displaystyle\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(x)

\displaystyle=I_{d}+\left[(t-t_{k})-\frac{(t-t_{k})^{2}}{2h_{k}}\right]\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)

\displaystyle+\frac{(t-t_{k})^{2}}{2h_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(x))\cdot(I_{d}+h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)),

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{k+1}-t}{h_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)

\displaystyle+\frac{t-t_{k}}{h_{k}}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(x))\cdot(I_{d}+h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)),

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}t^{2}}\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{k}}\left[\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k+1},F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(x))\cdot(I_{d}+h_{k}\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x))-\nabla\widehat{b}(t_{k},x)\right].

So, by that
h_{k}L\lesssim 1
,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(x)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(x)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim L,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}t^{2}}\nabla G_{t_{k}\to t}(X_{t_{k}})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim L\left(\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})+\left\|\nabla b(t_{k},X_{t_{k}})\right\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{L^{3/2}}{h_{k}}\|F_{t_{k}\to t_{k+1}}(X_{t_{k}})-X_{t_{k+1}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h_{k}}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}\nabla b(s,X_{s})\right\|_{F}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h_{k}}\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})+\frac{1}{h_{k}}\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}}),

The constants omitted by the notation “
\lesssim
" above is uniform for all
x
(or
X_{t_{k}}
) and
t
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\text{tr}[D_{\text{dis}}]\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{k}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}s^{2}}\nabla b(s,X_{s})\right\|_{F}\right]

\displaystyle+L\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}s}\nabla b(s,X_{s})\right\|_{F}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla b(s,X_{s})\right\|_{F}\cdot\left\|\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}s^{2}}\nabla G_{t_{k}\to s}(X_{t_{k}})\right\|_{F}\right]\Bigg\}\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k+1}(X_{t_{k+1}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+L^{3/2}h_{k}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})^{2}]^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(d\gamma(t)^{-2}+d^{1/2}\gamma(t)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+h_{k}^{2}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/2}d^{3/2}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-3}+d^{3}\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-6}

\displaystyle+(L+d\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2})\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3}d\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-2}+\overline{\gamma}_{k}^{-4}d^{2}\right)\bigg]

Therefore, by adding
D_{\text{bias}}
,
D_{\text{est}}
and
D_{\text{dis}}
together and integrating with respect to
t
, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\rho(t)}\left[|\nabla\cdot\widetilde{b}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\cdot b(t,X_{t})|\right]\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{2,k}(X_{t_{k+1}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}L^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k}})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{1,k}(X_{t_{k+1}})\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{k}^{3}\left(d^{3}\overline{\gamma}^{-6}+M^{3}\overline{\gamma}^{-4}\right).

where
M=\max\{\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{0}-x_{1}\|^{6}\right]^{1/3},L,d\}
.

Combining the above discussions, we can get the bounds given in Theorem
7
.
