Isolated Cardiac Hydatid Disease Presenting as Ventricular Remodeling and Tamponade
Authors: Qayoom Yousuf, Aamir Rashid, Naseer Ahmad Choh, Zubair Ashraf Hakeem, Farooq Ahmad Ganie, Rukhsana Akhter, Imran Hafeez, Jan Mohammad Sheikh, Uzair Hilal, Aadil Amin Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac hydatid (echinococcal) disease is rare, and presentation with cardiac tamponade and secondary myocardial remodeling is exceptional.Case SummaryA 16-year-old woman presented with acute pleuritic chest pain on a background of progressive dyspnea. Multimodality imaging revealed a large intact pericardial hydatid cyst with adjacent myocardial thinning, concentric hypertrophy of the uninvolved left ventricular segments, active pericarditis, and pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Intraoperatively, a large intact hydatid cyst was identified along with free-floating laminated membranes within the pericardial space, several of which were adherent to the epicardium, suggesting rupture of an additional occult cyst, likely of myocardial origin. Surgical excision led to complete symptom resolution. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and genetic testing excluded primary cardiomyopathies.DiscussionIsolated cardiac echinococcosis is rare, with myocardial involvement responsible for most clinically significant presentations. Although the cysts usually grow slowly and present insidiously, acute symptoms may arise owing to complications such as cyst rupture or pericardial inflammation. Chronic cyst expansion may induce secondary myocardial remodeling, including left ventricular hypertrophy, and predispose to tamponade physiology.Take-Home MessageThis case illustrates a rare presentation of cardiac hydatid disease complicated by secondary myocardial remodeling, pericarditis, and cardiac tamponade.Key words: hybrid imaging, pericardial effusion, tamponade

History of Presentation

A 16-year-old woman from a rural background presented with 1 day of acute-onset pleuritic chest pain, preceded by several weeks of progressively worsening dyspnea. On examination, she had tachycardia with low-normal systemic blood pressure, elevated jugular venous pressure, and muffled heart sounds.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant medical history, including hypertension, congenital heart disease, tuberculosis, or prior cardiac illness, and there was no relevant family history of cardiac disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included pericarditis, primary cardiomyopathy, and cardiac tumors.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (Figure 1) showed sinus tachycardia with diffuse T-wave inversions, raising concern for extensive myocardial pathology. Chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly. Laboratory tests demonstrated mild eosinophilia; cardiac biomarkers were mildly elevated. Echinococcus IgG level was 4.41 NTU (nephelometric turbidity units), consistent with a negative result (<9 NTU).

Electrocardiogram

Electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia with diffuse T-wave inversions.

Transthoracic echocardiography (Figures 2A to 2D, Video 1) demonstrated a large, well-defined intact cystic lesion adjacent to the distal left ventricular (LV) myocardium with associated focal myocardial thinning and concentric hypertrophy of the uninvolved LV segments, with an interventricular septal thickness of 21 mm (normal range: 4.70-8.94 mm; Z-score >>2) and posterior wall thickness of 19 mm (normal range: 4.59-8.82 mm; Z-score >>2). In addition, some floating internal membranous structures were noted within the pericardial effusion, raising suspicion of another ruptured hydatid cyst. A moderate pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology was present, including a dilated inferior vena cava with absent inspiratory variations and >40% respiratory change on Doppler inflow across the tricuspid valve.

(C) Concentric hypertrophy (yellow arrow) seen in the basal segments. (D) Dilated inferior vena cava (green arrow) with reduced inspiratory variation.

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) (Figure 3A) confirmed a well-circumscribed pericardial cyst with adjacent myocardial thinning and associated pericardial effusion, supporting the diagnosis of cardiac hydatid disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) (Figure 3B) demonstrated a 6.5 × 6.0 × 7.2 cm cystic lesion located near the distal LV apex, hypointense on T1-weighted and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, consistent with a hydatid cyst. Adjacent myocardial thinning and hypokinesia were noted. Diffuse pericardial enhancement with moderate effusion indicated active pericarditis. No late gadolinium enhancement pattern suggestive of hypertrophic, infiltrative, or storage cardiomyopathy was seen. Genetic testing for cardiomyopathy-associated mutations was negative, supporting a secondary cause of hypertrophy.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Given symptomatic tamponade physiology and risk of cyst rupture, surgical excision was planned. Cardiopulmonary bypass was kept on standby but was not required.

Intraoperatively (Figures 4A and 4B), a large intact hydatid cyst was identified and excised. In addition, a significant pericardial effusion containing free-floating laminated membranes was encountered, some of which were adherent to the LV epicardium and associated with adjacent focal myocardial thinning. Given the intact morphology of the primary cyst and the epicardial adherence of membranes, rupture of an additional cyst, most plausibly of myocardial origin, was considered. However, the possibility of rupture of a second pericardial cyst could not have been definitively excluded. An immediate rise in systemic blood pressure was observed after cyst decompression, reflecting relief of pericardial and myocardial constraint. Histopathological (Figures 5A and 5B) examination of the intact cyst and floating membranes demonstrated a laminated acellular cyst wall with an inner germinal layer, confirming echinococcal disease. Once cardiac hydatid disease was suspected, albendazole had been initiated at 10 to 15 mg/kg/d in 2 divided doses. However, given hemodynamically significant tamponade, urgent surgical intervention was performed shortly thereafter. Albendazole was continued postoperatively as adjunctive therapy to reduce recurrence risk, with a planned duration of 3 to 6 months.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At the 8-week follow-up, the patient reported marked symptomatic improvement. Echocardiography showed resolution of tamponade physiology and no residual cyst.

Discussion

Hydatid disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus most commonly involves the liver and lungs, while cardiac involvement is distinctly uncommon, reported in approximately 0.4% to 2% of cases. Among cardiac sites, the LV myocardium is most frequently affected, likely owing to its greater myocardial mass and rich coronary blood supply.1, 2, 3, 4 Because hydatid cysts enlarge slowly, clinical presentation is often delayed, with symptoms arising from progressive mass effect, myocardial injury, or secondary involvement of adjacent cardiac structures. Recent contemporary reports and systematic reviews continue to emphasize the rarity of cardiac echinococcosis, particularly in nonendemic settings, where delayed diagnosis is common.4,5

Serologic testing was negative in our patient; however, seronegativity does not exclude hydatid disease. The sensitivity of serologic assays varies with cyst location and is lower in extrahepatic involvement, including cardiac disease, likely because of limited antigen exposure. Therefore, imaging and intraoperative findings remain central to diagnosis in such cases.2

Transthoracic echocardiography remains the initial diagnostic modality, allowing identification of cystic cardiac lesions, assessment of ventricular function, and detection of pericardial effusion with hemodynamic consequences. CMR provides superior tissue characterization, delineates the relationship between the cyst and surrounding myocardium, and assists in excluding alternative diagnoses such as cardiac tumors, abscesses, or primary cardiomyopathies. In our patient, the absence of late gadolinium enhancement patterns suggestive of hypertrophic, infiltrative, or storage cardiomyopathies on CMR, together with negative genetic testing for cardiomyopathy-associated mutations, strongly argued against a primary cardiomyopathic process as a cause of hypertrophy. Cardiac CT serves as a valuable complementary imaging modality by providing high spatial resolution and precise anatomical delineation of cardiac hydatid cysts. CT is particularly useful for defining cyst size, location, wall characteristics, presence of calcification, and the relationship of the lesion to adjacent myocardium, coronary arteries, and pericardium.2,6,7

Progressive expansion of a cardiac hydatid cyst can lead to localized myocardial fibrosis, focal wall thinning, and alteration of regional ventricular wall stress. Focal myocardial thinning may result from a combination of chronic external compression, local ischemia due to coronary compression, and inflammatory injury from the adjacent cyst. In the present case, chronic regional myocardial compression and altered transmural wall stress likely resulted in compensatory hypertrophy of noninvolved myocardial segments. Given the exclusion of primary cardiomyopathies, we propose that the observed hypertrophy was secondary, resulting from long-standing mechanical compression, altered myocardial loading conditions, and compensatory myocardial remodeling induced by the hydatid cysts. Superimposed rupture of a second, likely myocardial cyst into the pericardial space precipitated acute pericardial inflammation and effusion, leading to acute-on-chronic tamponade physiology and sudden clinical deterioration.

Although preoperative imaging demonstrated only a single definite intact cyst, with echocardiography suggestive of some pericardial membranous structures, the intraoperative finding of free-floating laminated membranes within the pericardial space strongly suggested rupture of an additional hydatid cyst. Ruptured cysts may be difficult to detect on imaging because of decompression, collapse of cyst walls, or masking by pericardial effusion.

Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment for cardiac hydatid disease, even in asymptomatic patients, given the risk of rupture, embolization, anaphylaxis, and sudden death. Complete cyst removal with meticulous precautions to avoid spillage is recommended, with cardiopulmonary bypass support available when required.8 Adjunctive antihelminthic therapy with albendazole is advised to reduce cyst viability and minimize recurrence risk.9

Conclusions

Although rare, cardiac hydatid disease with myocardial involvement can produce secondary myocardial remodeling, including focal thinning and compensatory LV hypertrophy, through chronic mechanical and inflammatory effects. Superimposed cyst rupture and the space-occupying nature of the disease may precipitate acute clinical deterioration. Recognition of these secondary manifestations is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis as primary cardiomyopathy and to enable timely surgical intervention, particularly in patients from endemic regions.
