Surgeons’ stress assessment after surgical mortality: first international validated survey (STRESSURG study)
Authors: Olga Morató, Carlos Peña-Salazar, Luis Grande, Miguel Pera, Eduardo Luque-Villalobos, Patricia Sánchez-Velázquez, STRESSURG group collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnexpected perioperative death is one of the most distressing events in surgical practice and may lead to burnout or post-traumatic stress symptoms, with potential implications for surgeon well-being and patient safety. However, its psychological impact remains insufficiently quantified.AimThe STRESSURG study assessed the prevalence and severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms among surgeons after unexpected surgical deaths and explored associated professional, demographic, and institutional factors.MethodsAn international, cross-sectional online survey endorsed by the IHPBA and ESCP was distributed to surgeons who experienced intraoperative or early postoperative (< = 48 h) patient death. The 42 – item questionnaire included demographics, professional data, and the validated Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R).ResultsA total of 354 surgeons participated. Median IES-R score was 22.5 (IQR, 14–35). Clinically relevant distress (≥ 24) was observed in 48.6%, probable PTSD (≥ 33) in 29.1%, and severe distress (≥ 37) in 24.6%. Distress was higher among intermediate-career surgeons and when acting as primary surgeon. Only 8% received institutional psychological support, although over half believed it should be mandatory.ConclusionsUnexpected surgical deaths impose a substantial psychological burden on surgeons, driven largely by operative responsibility. Structured institutional support is urgently needed.Keywords: Surgeons, Stress, PTSD, IES-R, Perioperative mortality, Second victim

Introduction

Surgeons operate in high-stakes, high-pressure environments in which critical decision-making is routine. Among the most emotionally distressing experiences in surgical practice is the unexpected death of a patient, whether during the operation or in the immediate postoperative period. Although relatively rare, such events are an unavoidable aspect of a surgical career, particularly in complex or high-risk specialities such as oncologic, transplant, trauma or emergency surgery [1, 2].

The psychological impact of these events has received limited attention. A 2019 systematic review by Joliat et al. identified only seven studies investigating the impact of patient death on surgeons, underscoring the limited attention historically given to this issue [3]. Perioperative mortality can trigger profound emotional responses- sadness, anxiety, guilt, self-doubt [4, 5] and at times shame - that may progress to burnout, depression, suicidal ideation [1, 2, 6–8], post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [9, 10], and reducing considerable quality of life [1, 11]. Burnout, in particular, is a major public health concern, affecting up to 50% of surgeons in some series [12]. It is associated with maladaptive coping strategies, including substance misuse [6], reported in 15.4% of American surgeons [13] and increased alcohol consumption in 10.6% of UK surgeons after traumatic events [7]. Beyond its impact on surgeon well-being, burnout has important implications for patient safety. Studies demonstrate that burnout and depressive symptoms are independently associated with higher odds of medical errors, with rising emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation scores significantly increasing the likelihood of reporting a major error [14]. A recent meta-analysis further showed that surgeons experiencing burnout have approximately a 2.5-fold higher risk of self-reported medical errors and worse patient-safety perceptions compared with non-burned-out colleagues [15].

These outcomes are often compounded by moral injury, defined as lasting psychological, social or spiritual harm from actions-or inaction- that violate deeply held moral beliefs [16]. Albert Wu’s concept of the second victim [17], Jameton’s moral distress, and Litz’s expansion of moral injury collectively describe a continuum of morally challenging events in surgical practice [16, 18]. Such experiences can lead to isolation, emotional suppression due to fear of blame, and diminished self-confidence, often without adequate institutional support [1, 19–21]. Female and junior surgeons appear especially vulnerable, tending to personalize adverse outcomes more acutely than peers [5, 15].

Unaddressed psychological trauma jeopardizes both surgeon well-being and patient safety [9, 22]. Despite growing awareness, institutional support structures remain insufficient [7, 23, 24]. A national survey by Akyol et.al highlighted the urgent need for systemic changes to better support surgeons in emotional distress [1], while a recent review found that only a minority receive psychological assistance after traumatic event, with help-seeking hindered by stigma and reluctance to disclose [25].

The STRESSURG study aimed to assess the psychological impact of unexpected perioperative mortality on surgeons and to explore factors associated with increased post-traumatic stress symptoms, including professional role, seniority, and institutional support.

Material and methods

Study design & survey

Study design

This study was designed as an international, observational, cross-sectional survey of surgeons who had experienced the unexpected patient death intraoperatively or within 24–48 h postoperatively. The survey was administered online using Google Forms and comprised 42 items across the three sections: (i) demographics and professional background; (ii) clinical experience with perioperative death; and (iii) psychological impact using the IES-R, alongside additional items on perceived changes in performance, team communication, institution-level review, and psychological support availability (ANNEX 1). The study objectives, inclusion criteria, and analysis plan were prespecified in the protocol.

Survey

Eligible participants were surgeons of any gender, specialty, and seniority who had experienced at least one unexpected patient death occurring either intraoperatively or within 24–48 h after surgery at any point during their professional career. Participation was voluntary, and responses could be anonymous. Surgeons wishing to be acknowledged as collaborators could provide their name, affiliation, and email address.

Definition of IES-R, psychosocial assessment and unexpected postoperative mortality

Psychological impact was evaluated using the IES-R [26, 27], a 22-items validated self-report questionnaire widely used to measure post- traumatic stress symptoms in response to a specific distressing event. Originally developed to evaluate post-traumatic stress symptoms in survivors of war and other severe crises, the IES-R has since been widely used in healthcare to assess clinicians’ psychological responses to traumatic events to quantify post-traumatic stress reactions across three domains: intrusion, avoidance and hyperarousal corresponding to DSM-IV PTSD domains [23]. Although the IES-R assesses symptoms experienced during the previous 7 days, it is designed for retrospective evaluation of distress related to a specific traumatic event and may therefore be administered long after the event occurred.

Each item is rated on a 5-point liker scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”), with a total score ranging from 0 to 88. A score ≥ 33 is commonly used to indicate probable PTSD, while a score of ≥ 24 suggests clinically relevant distress. 37 or more could be enough to weaken your immune system's functioning (even 10 years after an impact event) [28]. Subscale scores were also analysed to identify specific psychological patterns. Although not a definitive diagnostic tool, the IES-R can help identify individuals who might benefit from further psychological evaluation or support. The potential influence of recall bias and temporal distance from the event is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Unexpected postoperative mortality was defined as a death occurring intraoperatively or within 24–48 h after surgery that was not anticipated based on the patient’s preoperative clinical condition or surgical indication. Deaths occurring in palliative or end-of-life contexts were not considered within this definition.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint

Prevalence and severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms measured by the IES-R total score (0–88). Thresholds were predefined at ≥ 24 (clinically relevant distress), ≥ 33 (probable PTSD), and ≥ 37 (very high likelihood of clinically significant PTSD symptoms) as commonly used in the literature and per protocol.

Secondary endpoints

Associations between IES-R (continuous and thresholds) and surgeon characteristics (sex, age group, years of practice, training level, specialty), event handling (discussion with team, M&M), and institutional psychological support.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted for 18 months; between February 2024 and August 2025. The online survey link was distributed through international surgical societies, including the European-African Hepato-Pancreato- Biliary Association (E-AHPBA), which granted Blue Seal endorsement, and the European Society of Coloproctology (ESCP), which also provided endorsement. Additional dissemination occurred via professional networks. To ensure data quality, only fully completed surveys were included in the final analysis. Responses were anonymized unless participants explicitly consented to be identified.

Statistical analysis

Responses were exported to Microsoft Excel and the analyses were performed in Stata/BE 18 (Stata Corp, TX, USA) for statistical analysis. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (n %); continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Comparisons between groups were conducted using appropriate tests: Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for two-group continuous variables. Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across three or more groups. For IES-R thresholds (≥ 24, ≥ 33, ≥ 37), we compared prevalence across sex, age group, years of practice, training level, and surgical specialty (binary indicators for HPB, colorectal, upper-GI, emergency/trauma, transplant, etc.). IES-R total (continuous) was summarized and compared across the same strata. The relationship between exposure to patient deaths and distress was assessed using Spearman’s correlation for the continuous variable and Kruskal–Wallis and Chi-square tests for group comparisons (< 5 vs ≥ 5 deaths) or IES-R totals and PTSD thresholds, respectively. Two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital del Mar, Barcelona, Spain. (Ref. 2024/11693). Completion of the survey constituted consent.

Results

A total of 354 surgeons completed the survey. Most respondents were male (70.9%) and between 40–60 years of age (48%).

Psychological burden

Nearly half of the surgeons experienced clinically relevant psychological distress following perioperative mortality events. Specifically, 48.6% had an IES-R score ≥ 24, 29.1% scored ≥ 33 (probable PTSD), and 24.6% reached the highest severity threshold (IES-R ≥ 37). The overall median IES-R score was 22.5 (IQR, 14–36) (Table 1).

Distress levels did not differ significantly by sex (p = 0.87), geographical region (p = 0.49) or surgical specialty (p = 0.44), at any IES-R threshold (Table 1). However, both age and years of practice were significantly associated with IES-R total scores (p = 0.015 and p = 0.007 respectively). Median IES-R scores were highest among mid-career surgeons aged 40–60 years (26 [IQR,15–38]), compared with those with < 40 years (20 [IQR,10–33]) and > 60 years (23 [IQR, 16–36]); p = 0.015). Similarly, surgeons with 11–20 years of experience or ≥ 20 years, demonstrated higher distress levels (25 [IQR,15–38]) and (25.5 [IQR,17–39]) respectively, than those ≤ 10 years (19 [IQR,10–31]); p = 0.007. Prevalence of probable PTSD did not differ significantly across experience groups (years of practice) (IES-R score ≥ 33: p = 0.13).

Current training level was not associated with distress scores (p = 0.89). When considering the training level at the time of the adverse event, consultants demonstrated a higher prevalence of severe PTSD symptoms (≥ 37 cutoff: p = 0.041), with similar but non-significant trends for lower thresholds (p = 0.09). Surgeons who had been the primary surgeon during the adverse event exhibited significantly higher distress than those assisting (p = 0.01).

The number of previous intra- or peri-operative deaths was not correlated with IES-R total score (p = 0.59). Similarly, when the variable was categorized (< 5 vs ≥ 5) by deaths, was not associated with distress levels (p = 0.87), although the distribution was highly skewed and should be interpreted with caution. When stratified by age and years of practice, the relationship between exposure to deaths and psychological distress remained non-significant.

Following perioperative mortality, most surgeons reported a behavioural change in their professional performance. Nearly half most often increased caution (46.4%) described being more cautions than usual, 12.3% reported being more stressed, and 18% endorsed experiencing both increased caution and stress. Smaller proportions reported being slower (1.4%) or noticing no change (21.9%). Psychological impact differed significantly across behavioural (p < 0.001). Median IES-R scores were highest among surgeons who were both cautious and stressed (32 [IQR,21–44]) and those who were only stressed (32 [IQR, 22–46]), followed by those who were only cautious (21 [IQR,13–31]) and no changes (14 [IQR, 6–28]). Similarly, the prevalence of probable PTSD varied by behavioural pattern (p < 0.001): 69.8% of the cautious + stressed group and 67.4% of the stressed-only group scored ≥ 24 on the IES-R, compared with 43.6% among those who were only cautious and 33.8% among those who reported no change. No significant associations were observed between change-in performance pattern and sex, age, years of practice, or role during the adverse event (p = 0.44, p = 0.56, p = 0.36, and p = 0.25 respectively) (Table 3).

Psychological support attitudes

Attitudes toward psychological support after perioperative mortality varied substantially among respondents. Surgeons who believed institutional psychological support should be mandatory had significantly higher IES-R scores (p < 0.001). Similarly, surgeons willing to seek psychological therapy reported higher IES-R scores than those who would not (p = 0.018). In contrast, discussing the event at M&M was not associated with distress (p = 0.61) (Table 2).

When attitudes toward psychological support were dichotomized 338/354 (95.5%), surgeons felt support was important, very important, or mandatory, whereas 16/354 (4.5%) reported that support was unnecessary. Surgeons who considered psychological support to be necessary (includes responses of important, very important, and should be mandatory) demonstrated significantly higher distress compared with those who felt it was unnecessary (p = 0.024). The median IES-R score was 23 (IQR, 14–36) in the first group versus 14.5 (IQR, 4.5–25) in the latter, suggesting that personal emotional impact may influence willingness to seek support. PTSD prevalence was also numerically higher across all IES-R thresholds, although categorical comparisons did not reach statistical significance. The minority who believed support was unnecessary did not differ significantly by years of practice (p = 0.38) or training level at the time of the event (p = 0.83).

Age-specific patterns emerged when using the three-category variable (< 40, 40–60, > 60 years). Perceptions of institutional psychological support after perioperative mortality varied significantly across age groups (p = 0.026). Surgeons > 60 years were least likely to endorse structured support, with none considering it “mandatory” and a higher proportion reporting that support was “not necessary” (14.3%) compared with younger colleagues (< 40 years: 3.1%; 40–60 years: 4.7%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This international survey provides one of the first comprehensive assessments of the psychological impact of perioperative mortality on surgeons, revealing substantial burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms. Nearly 29.1% of respondents scored ≥ 33 on the IES-R, a threshold often associated with probable PTSD. These findings underscore that unexpected surgical deaths are not only clinical events but also deeply personal experiences that can profoundly affect surgeon’s mental health, self-perception and professional performance.

A key observation is the heightened distress among surgeons who served as the primary surgeon during the adverse event. This findings aligns with previous studies showing that primary operating surgeons experience significantly higher mental workload than assistant [29] – even when the primary surgeon is a trainee [24, 30, 31] – highlighting how decision-making authority and accountability amplify emotional consequences when complications occur. Consultants showed the highest prevalence of severe PTSD symptoms at the ≥ 37 threshold, supporting the notion that senior surgeons may internalize adverse outcomes more intensely due to their sense of ultimate responsibility.

Age and experience further shaped the psychological response. Surgeons in the mid-career phase (40–60 years) exhibited the higher PTSD scores, a pattern that may reflect cumulative emotional burden or greater awareness of professional accountability that develops with career maturity. When examining perceptions of hospital-based psychological support, significant differences emerged across age group. Surgeons aged > 60 years were notably less inclined to value formal support as necessary, with nearly one in five (18.8%) considering it unnecessary, whereas early and mid-career surgeons (< 40 and 40–60 years) more commonly rated support as important or very important. Although this effect diminished when age was analysed as an ordinary variable, the pattern suggests meaningful generational variations in help-seeing behaviour. Senior surgeons may have trained in eras where emotional resilience and distress was expected to be self-managed, and seeking support was culturally discouraged, whereas newer generations of surgeons appear more receptive to institutional mental-health initiatives.

Notably, experience did not uniformly mitigate psychological distress. Surgeons with > 10 years of practice reported significantly higher PTSD scores than those with less experience, despite similar exposure to perioperative deaths. This indicates that psychological impact is influenced more by the nature of the responsibility during the event than by cumulative exposure alone [11]. Being the primary surgeon was not strictly linked to seniority, as consultants and junior surgeons alike often assumed the role of primary surgeons, reinforcing that the emotional impact derives from role-related accountability rather than years of practice.

Surgeons’ reactions to adverse events have been described as progressing through four sequential phases – from the initial “kick” to the long-term impact-each shaping emotional processing and subsequent future clinical judgement [5]. Within this framework, our cohort demonstrated distinct behavioural patterns after perioperative mortality. Most surgeons reported becoming more cautious, indicating a reflective adaption and a constructive attempt to prevent future adverse outcomes, nearly one-fifth demonstrated a dual pattern characterised by both heightened caution and emotional stress. This subgroup experienced the highest IES-R scores and PTSD prevalence, suggesting a state of hypervigilant stress in which increased caution coexists with unresolved emotional arousal. Although such a response may initially enhance situational awareness, sustained hypervigilance risks consolidating anxiety-driven behaviours, impaired clinical confidence, persistent self-blame, and guilty [32]. In contrast, surgeons who reported no change or isolated increases in caution displayed significantly lower distress (lower PTSD rates) indicating that behavioural adaptation alone is not a reliable marker of psychological well-being. Recognising and addressing this mixed “cautious-and-stressed” profile could help institutions identify surgeons at higher risk and implement early, supportive interventions aimed at restoring both wellbeing and optimal performance.

Despite the high emotional burden, institutional support was remarkably scarce. Fewer than 8% of respondents reported receiving psychological help from their hospitals, and over 90% were unlikely to seek support independently. This reflects a systemic failure to normalize and address the emotional consequences of adverse outcomes. Although most participants recognized the importance of in-hospital psychological support – and nearly a quarter advocated for it to be mandatory - actual access and utilization remain limited. This disconnect echoes findings from previous studies calling for structured peer support programs and institutional policies to assist healthcare professionals in distress [7, 33]. Interestingly surgeons expressing the strongest need for mandatory psychological support had the highest distress scores, indicating the personal experience of emotional burden may drive preference for structured institutional support pathways.

In contrast to healthcare, other high-risk professions such as aviation and the military have long established structured support systems following critical incidents, including formal debriefings and peer-support programs. These systems are designed to normalize emotional responses, provide early intervention, and maintain performance under pressure. The relative absence of comparable frameworks in surgical practice highlights a critical gap in institutional support and underscores the need for cultural and structural change within healthcare systems.

Although current institutional responses are inadequate, several successful models demonstrate the structured support in both feasible and effective [15]. One example is the Peer Support Program at Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) which has become a benchmark, offering trained peers to support clinicians following distressing events, promoting early emotional intervention, and reducing the stigma surrounding help-seeking [25]. Similarly, the forYOU Program developed at the University of Missouri [33] provides a structured, tiered response model through dedicated Second Victim Response Teams, connecting affected clinicians to trained responders, mental health professionals, or spiritual care teams as appropriate. Its success has encouraged adoption at multiple U.S. institutions providing that well-resourced, and clearly defined frameworks can be implemented effectively.

Beyond specific interventions, broader organizational initiatives-such as Wellness Committees and institutional counselling services – play a preventive role. A non-punitive culture that emphasizes learning over blame is essential to reduce secondary trauma, encourage open communication, and strengthen trust [15, 23, 34]. Structural barriers such as fear or litigation, lack of standardized reporting systems, and uncertainty about defining intraoperative adverse events – reported by many respondents – further hinder transparency and support-seeking. Overcoming these challenges requires cultural and structural change.

Overall, our findings reveal a clear gap between the recognised psychological impact of perioperative mortality and the availability of meaningful support for surgeons. While most participants acknowledged the importance of emotional and institutional assistance, structured, accessible, and confidential resources remain inconsistently available and underutilised. These results underscore the urgent need for healthcare institutions and surgical societies to move beyond awareness and actively implement culturally competent, stigma-free support systems, embedding psychological care into routine professional practice as a cornerstone of safe and humane surgery [35].

Limitations

Due to the dissemination of the survey through large international societies (IHPBA and ESCP), the exact number of surgeons who received the survey invitation could not be determined; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated. This introduces a potential selection bias, as surgeons experiencing greater psychological impact may have been more likely to participate. Furthermore, although no major differences in IES-R scores were observed across surgical specialties or geographic regions, suggesting that the psychological impact of unexpected perioperative death may represent a universal challenge among surgeons rather than an isolated issue, the international generalizability of this finding remains limited because most respondents were from Europe.

The IES-R is a validated self-report questionnaire widely used to measure post-traumatic stress symptoms in response to a specific distressing event, but it is not a diagnostic tool itself. A definitive diagnosis requires a structured clinical interview conducted by a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, based on DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria and the persistence of symptoms for at least 1 month.

Conclusions

Unexpected perioperative deaths are associated with substantial and often unacknowledged psychological distress among surgeons, particularly among those acting as the primary surgeons. The STRESSURG study provides robust, international evidence of this burden and its behavioural impact. Supporting surgeons’ psychological well-being is essential not only for individual resilience but also for maintaining safe, high-quality patient care. Future directions include conducting prospective studies that administer the IES-R within the first 7 days and again beyond 1 month after the traumatic event, and for participants scoring ≥ 33, incorporating a structured psychopathological interview with a psychiatrist or a clinical psychologist.
