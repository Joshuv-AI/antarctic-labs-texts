Deer antler polypeptides promote osteogenesis for fracture healing via activating neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis
Authors: Yunfan He, Jihao Xi, Haoqiang Dai, Liang Fang, Yihe Yu, Yonggan Ying, Jin Li, Danqing Fu, Chenjie Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeer antler polypeptides (DAPs), one of the most bioactive components of deer antler, have been reported to possess multiple biological functions. However, the role of DAPs in fracture healing remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of DAPs on fracture healing and elucidate their potential pharmacological mechanism.MethodsLC-MS/MS was employed to analyze the peptide compositions and serum metabolites of DAPs. Sensory nerve-specific EP4 receptor knockout (EP4Avil) mice, sensory nerve- and skeletal stem/progenitor cells-specific fluorescence reporter mice were subjected to a transverse tibial fracture followed by oral administration of DAPs at 50 mg/kg/day. In vitro, primary bone marrow cells (BMCs) were incubated with serum containing DAPs, and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons were subsequently treated with the conditioned medium harvested from these BMCs. A series of experimental approaches including gait analysis, μCT, three-point bending test, calcein double-labeling staining, IHC, IF, ELISA, qRT-PCR and western blotting were performed on both in vivo and in vitro models.ResultsLC-MS/MS analysis identified 303 functional peptides and 32 differential metabolites. PGE2 production was significantly increased in the fracture callus of DAPs-treated mice and in BMCs stimulated with DAPs-containing serum, which activated the EP4 receptor in sensory nerves and DRG neurons in vivo and in vtiro. This activation consequently improved hypothalamic CREB phosphorylation and diminished sympathetic tone, thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs. Introduction

Bone fracture is one of the most frequent skeletal disorders accompanied by a high risk of physical disability [1]. It is estimated that more than 170 million new fractures occur annually worldwide, and approximately 5%-10% result in delayed healing or non-union [2]. Current fracture treatments, including immobilization and surgical fixation, can well establish mechanical stability but fail to deliver the essential biological stimulation for bone regeneration [3]. Fracture patients are subjected to prolonged rehabilitation, substantial physical pain and psychological distress, imposing significant burdens on the healthcare system [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies for enhancing fracture healing.

Fracture healing is a complex biological process involving two distinct pathways, intramembranous ossification and endochondral ossification [5]. In clinical, most fractures heal through a combination of both ossification processes. In response to injury, a hematoma enriched with growth factors, inflammatory mediator, and chemokines forms at the fracture site, recruiting skeletal stem/progenitor cells (SSPCs) for bone regeneration [6]. Within mechanically unstable central region of the hematoma, SSPCs undergo chondrogenic differentiation to form a transient cartilaginous template. This template progresses through endochondral ossification, a sequence involving chondrocyte apoptosis, matrix calcification, vascular invasion and osteoblast-mediated bone deposition, and is ultimately replaced by bone [7]. Concurrently, within the periphery of the hematoma, SSPCs directly differentiate into osteoblasts to deposit bone via intramembranous ossification [8]. Therefore, SSPCs-mediated bone formation via both pathways plays a crucial role in successful fracture healing, as the resulting fracture callus not only restores the mechanical integrity of the fractured bone but also enables subsequent bone remodeling [9].

Sensory nerves densely innervate bone, and continuously monitor bone regeneration through receiving and transmitting mechanical, chemical, and biological signals originating from bone [10]. In contrast, denervation substantially delays fracture healing and reduces the mechanical properties of newly formed bone [11]. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a common inflammatory mediator catalyzed by the rate-limiting enzyme cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2). Beyond pain mediation, PGE2 also exerts osteogentic effects for fracture repair [12]. Clinical evidence demonstrated that downregulation of endogenous PGE2 levels contribute to delayed union [13,14], and systemically or locally administered PGE2 stimulates osteogenesis to promote fracture healing [15,16]. Recent findings revealed that PGE2 enhances osteogenesis by activating EP4 receptors on sensory nerves [1,17]. Specifically, the binding of PGE2 to neurosensory EP4 receptors triggers signal transduction through the dorsal root ganglion (DRG), leading to phosphorylation of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) in the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) [18]. This hypothalamic p-CREB attenuates sympathetic tone, thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs for bone formation [19]. Collectively, this neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis is regarded as a potential therapeutic target for enhancing osteogenesis during fracture healing.

Deer antler, known as Lu Rong (鹿茸) in Chinese, is a unique mammalian organ capable of full regeneration [20]. For millennia, it has been utilized as a traditional medicine and health supplement throughout East Asia [21]. According to the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) theory that “the kidney governs bone”, deer antler has kidney-tonifying properties and is used clinically to treat skeletal disorders associated with kidney deficiency, such as bone fractures [22]. Modern chemical profiling has identified a variety of bioactive compounds in deer antler, including proteins, peptides, lipids, polysaccharides, glycoproteins and trace elements [23,24]. Notably, proteins account for approximately 50% of its dry weight, and peptides are derived from these proteins via enzymatic hydrolysis [25]. Compared to the proteins, deer antler polypeptides (DAPs) are typically consist of 2-50 amino acid residues, possessing significantly enhanced gastrointestinal absorption and bioactivity [26]. Consequently, current research has primarily focused on investigating the pharmacological properties and mechanisms of DAPs. Advanced analytical platforms such as liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) have enabled high-resolution characterization of DAP pharmacokinetics [27]. Furthermore, DAPs have been shown to exhibit antioxidant [28], anti-organ fibrosis [29], anti-aging [30], anti-tumor [31], anti-neurological disease [32] and anti-osteoporosis effects [33] in animal and cellular models. Nervertheless, it remains largely unknown the role of DAPs on bone fractures. The present study aimed to elucidate the therapeutic efficacy and molecular mechanisms of DAPs in fracture healing. Here, we hypothesized that DAPs enhance callus osteogenesis for fracture healing by activating the neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis, thereby downregulating sympathetic tone. To test this hypothesis, LC-MS/MS-guided peptidomic and metabolomic analyses on DAPs were conducted, followed by in vivo validation using sensory nerve-specific EP4 receptor knockout mice and a transverse tibial fracture model, and in vitro assessment with primary bone marrow cells (BMCs) and dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons.

Methods and materials

Preparation and LC-MS/MS-guided peptidomic analysis of DAPs

DAPs (Lot No. 20220211001) were obtained from Shanghai Yingxin Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd. The preparation process of DAPs was as follows: The entire fresh deer antler from 2-year-old Cervus nippon was cut into 0.1-0.5 cm pieces, rinsed with distilled water, and ground into a fine powder. The powdered antler was mixed with a pH 3.5 acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 160 g: 1 L, followed by ultrasonic disruption extraction at 4 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, the remaining precipitate was re-extracted under the same ultrasonic conditions. The two supernatants were combined and hydrolyzed first with 6000 U/g neutral protease and papain at 45 °C and pH 7.0 for 4 h, and then with 6000 U/g alkaline protease at 50 °C and pH 9.0 for 6 h. The mixture was then heated to 100 °C for 15 min to inactivate the enzymes, and then immediately cooled to room temperature. After centrifugation, the supernatant was passed through an ultrafiltration membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 KDa (flow rate controlled at 2 m/s) to obtain a filtrate. The filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C under a vacuum of 0.09 MPa, and subsequently freeze-dried into DAPs powder.

The identification of polypeptide components in DAPs was conducted by LC-MS/MS analysis. Briefly, DAPs powder was reconstituted in 10 μL of 0.1% formic acid. After centrifugation at 14,000×g for 20 min, 1 μg of peptide-containing supernatant was injected into a Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a Nanospray Flex™ ion source. Full MS scans were acquired over a range of 350–1500 m/z at a resolution of 120,000 (at 200 m/z). The precursors selected from full scans were fragmented via Top Speed model at 33% normalized collision energy (NCE). The resulting MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 with a maximum injection time of 22 m s. To identify functional peptides, all peptides obtained from LC-MS/MS were compared against known functional peptides recorded in six databases. Subsequent validation was performed by integrating mass spectral matching scores, retention time, and fragment ion information, leading to the final confirmation of their functional identity.

Animals and interventions

C57BL/6 J mice (body weight 22 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Sensory nerve-specific Advillin-Cre (Avil-Cre) mice were acquired from Shanghai Model Organisms Center [34]. SSPCs-specific Gli1-CreERT2 mice [35], Rosa26-tdTomatofx/fx mice, and EP4fx/fx mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou). Gli1-CreERT2;Rosa26-tdTomatofx/wt (TomatoGli1ER) mice were generated by crossing Gli1-CreERT2 mice with Rosa26-tdTomatofx/fx mice, with tamoxifen induction at 14 days of age (1 mg/10 g body weight/day, intraperitoneally for four consecutive days). Avil-Cre mice were crossed with either Rosa26-tdTomatofx/fx mice or EP4fx/fx mice to generate Avil-Cre;Rosa26-tdTomatofx/wt (TomatoAvil) mice, Avil-Cre;EP4fx/fx (EP4Avil) mice and EP4fx/fx littermates. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier conditions, and free access to food and water.

Eight-week-old C57BL/6 J mice (n = 64) and transgenic (TomatoGli1ER, TomatoAvil, EP4Avil and EP4fx/fx) mice underwent transverse tibial fracture, and were then randomly assigned to either Model Group or DAPs Group. Mice in the DAPs group received daily oral gavages of DAPs (50 mg/kg/day, dissolved in pure water) starting from the second day post-fracture [36,37], whereas those in the Model group received an equal volume of pure water. All mice were euthanized at 4, 7, 14, and 24 days post-fracture, and biological specimens including (serum, tibiae, DRG, and hypothalamic tissues) were collected for subsequent analyses. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (IACUC-20240923-01).

Transverse tibial fracture model

An unilateral transverse tibial fracture was established in mice using an established protocol [6]. Briefly, a 1-cm longitudinal incision was made along the surface of tibial crest to expose the tibial shaft. A 26-gauge needle was inserted into the medullary cavity via the tibial plateau, and then removed. The tibia was osteotomized transversely at its midpoint using a No. 11 surgical blade, and the needle was reinserted across the fracture site for fixation. The skin incision was closed with sutures.

Gait analysis

Gait analysis was performed using the DigiGait system (Mouse Specifics, Inc., US). Mice were placed on an enclosed transparent treadmill belt moving at 18 cm/s, and a 10-s video was captured with a high-speed digital camera. Parameters assessed for fractured limb function included stride length (distance between consecutive placements of the same hind paw), swing duration (time the hind paw is elevated off the surface), and paw area (total contact area during stance phase) [38].

Three-point bending testing of fractured tibiae

Tibiae were harvested at 7, 14, and 21 days post-fracture for three-point bending testing. After removal of surrounding soft tissue and fixation pins, samples were mounted on the testing fixture and loaded at 5 mm/min until failure. Maximum load and elastic modulus were calculated using the Axial-Torsion Fatigue Testing System (KN Test, CN) according to standard protocols.

μCT analysis

Fractured tibia samples were scanned using micro-computed tomography (μCT, VNC-102, PINGSENG Healthcare Inc, CN) at a resolution of 12 μm. Image reconstruction was performed using ReconDaemon software with the FDK algorithm, followed by three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction in Avatar3 software. Contiguous 60 slices on each side of the fracture gap (cortical bone excluded) were selected as a region of interest (ROI). Quantitative parameters including total volume (TV, mm3), bone volume (BV, mm3) and mineralized volume fraction (BV/TV, %) within the fracture callus, were measured using Avatar3 software to evaluate fracture healing.

Calcein double-labeling staining

Mice received two intraperitoneal injections of calcein (10 mg/kg; Sigma, USA) at 4 and 11 days post-fracture. Fractured tibiae were harvested at day 14 post-fracture, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in polymethyl methacrylate, and sectioned into 30-μm slices for fluorescence microscopy. Mineral apposition rate (MAR) and bone formation rate per bone surface (BFR/BS) were quantified using Image J software.

Histology and histomorphometry

Fractured tibiae, DRG and hypothalamus samples were processed into 3-μm-thick paraffin sections or 10-μm-thick frozen sections. Frozen sections were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to visualize tdTomato-labeled Avil+ sensory nerves and Gli1+ cells. Paraffin sections were stained with Alcian Blue Hematoxylin (ABH)/Orange G for histological analysis. Cartilage area percentage (Cg.Ar/Ps.CI.Ar, %) and woven bone area percentage (Md.Ar/Ps.CI.Ar, %) within the fracture callus were quantified using OsteoMetrics software (Decatur, GA, USA).

DAPs-containing serum preparation and metabolic analysis

Eight-week-old Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (n = 3 per group) received oral administration of either DAPs (50 mg/kg/day) or pure water for three consecutive days. Whole blood was collected via abdominal aorta puncture, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 200×g for 15 min. DAPs-containing and control serum were analyzed by LC-MS/MS for metabolic profiling. The separation of metabolic profiles between groups was visualized using a PCA score plot. Differential metabolites were categorized by chemical taxonomy and summarized with a pie chart, and further displayed in a bar graph ranked by fold change. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Isolation, intervention and western blot analysis of mouse BMCs

Bone marrow cells (BMCs) were isolated from the tibial and femoral marrow cavities of 8-week-old C57BL/6 J mice. Collected cells were centrifuged at 200×g for 10 min and resuspended in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). After 48 h, non-adherent cells were removed, and adherent BMCs were harvested. BMCs were then treated with 10% FBS, DAPs-free (900 μL), or low-, middle-, or high-concentration DAPs-containing rat serum (300, 600, or 900 μL) for 24 h, followed by western blot analysis.

BMCs from each group were lysed with protein extraction reagent, and total protein was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, USA), and blocked for 20 min. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against COX-2 (1:1000; Abcam, ab15191, UK) or Runx2 (1:500; ABclonal, A11753, CN), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h. Immune complexes were detected using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate and visualized with a Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (Tanon 5200, CN).

ALP staining and western blot analysis of C3H10T1/2 cells

C3H10T1/2 cells were treated with 10% FBS, DAPs-free (900 μL) serum, or DAPs-containing rat serum (600 μL) for 24 h. ALP staining and western blot analysis of Runx2 were then performed on cells from each group as previously described [39].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were collected from model and DAPs groups of each genetic background mouse via eyeball extirpation, and centrifuged at 200×g for 30 min to obtain serum. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected following BMC intervention. The concentration of PGE2 in both mouse serum and CM were measured using an ELISA kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Isolation, identification and intervention of mouse DRG neuron

Lumbar DRGs (L1-L3) collected from 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice were digested for 1 h at 37 °C in an enzyme solution containing 5 mg/mL collagenase type I (GIBCO, USA) and 5 mg/mL dispase type II (Yuanye Biotechnology, CN). The cell suspension was filtered through a 200 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 200×g for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1 μM 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine, then plated on fibronectin-coated dishes for culture. After 5-7 days of culture, purified mouse DRG neurons were obtained [12]. Neurons were identified by immunofluorescence (IF) staining for β3-tubulin (neuronal cytoskeleton) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, to exclude astrocytes). The DRG neurons were then treated for 24 h with CM derived from BMCs exposed to either middle-concentration DAPs-containing or DAPs-free rat serum.

Quantitative gene expression analysis on fracture callus and DRG neurons

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on mouse fracture callus and DRG neurons using standard procedures [39]. Fracture callus samples from DAPs-treated mice or model mice at days 14 post-fracture was homogenized using a tissue homogenizer (Scientz, CN). Total RNA was extracted from DRG neurons or homogenized callus using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). After quantification, 500 ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Real-time PCR was performed with the cDNA template, Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II FAST (Takara, Japan), and gene-specific primers (listed in Table 1) on a QuantStudio™ 5 system. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and IF assay

Tissue sections of tibia, DRG, and hypothalamus were subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 60 °C for 4 h, then blocked with 3% H2O2 for 20 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ALP (1:200, Arigo, CN), Leptin Receptor (1:300, Wanleibio, CN), COX-2 (1:200, Abcam, UK), EP4 (1:150, Abcam, UK), CGRP (1:100, Abcam, UK), p-CREB (1:100, Abcam, UK), OCN (1:200, Takara, CN), and TH (1:500, Abcam, UK). For IHC, sections were incubated with goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG-HRP (1:1000, ZSGB-BIO, CN) and developed with DAB followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. For IF, sections were incubated with goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488, 1:500, Abcam, UK) and counterstained with DAPI.

Primary DRG neurons cultured on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocked with 3% BSA (30 min each), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against β3-tubulin (1:300, Wanleibio, CN), GFAP (1:200, Wanleibio, CN), EP4 (1:150, Abcam, UK), and CGRP (1:100, Abcam, UK). After washing, coverslips were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI for IF staining. Quantitative analysis of positive staining was performed using Image-Pro Plus software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were analyzed using independent samples Student's t-tests, while comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test. These statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software, and the value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

The serum obtained from rats treated with DAPs or pure water via oral gavage for three consecutive days was subjected to metabolomic analysis. The PCA score plot revealed a distinct separation in metabolic profiles between DAPs-containing serum and DAPs-free serum (Fig. 1D), and the information of 32 differential metabolites are listed in Fig. 1E. Based on chemical taxonomy, the differential metabolites can be categorized into 13 classes, including fatty acyls, glycerophospholipids, carboxylic acids and derivatives, indoles and derivatives, glycerolipids, steroids and steroid derivatives, macrolides and analogues, diazines, organic disulfides, organooxygen compounds, benzene and substituted derivatives, prenol lipids, and organonitrogen compounds (Fig. 1F). DAPs accelerate mouse tibial fracture healing through enhancing osteogenesis

To investigate the therapeutic effects of DAPs on bone fracture healing, a tibial transverse fracture model was established in 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, followed by administration of DAPs via oral gavage. Fractured tibiae, serum samples, DRG and hypothalamus tissues were collected at 4, 7, 14 and 21 days post-fracture for subsequent analyses (Fig. 2A). The results of gait analysis showed increased stride length, swing duration and paw area of the fractured limb in DAPs-treated mice compared to the Model group at 21 days post-fracture (Fig. 2B), indicating improved functional recovery of the fractured limb after DAPs treatment. Gross observation revealed no discernible fracture gap in the DAPs-treated mice at 21 days post-fracture versus the Model group (Fig. 2C). μCT images further demonstrated a larger fracture callus at 7 and 14 days post-fracture, and complete healing of the fracture gap at 21 days post-fracture in the DAPs-treated mice compared to the Model group (Fig. 2D and E). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly increased BV and BV/TV in the fracture callus at 7 and 14 days post-fracture, and a significant decrease in these two parameters at 21 days post-fracture in the DAPs-treated group compared to the Model group (Fig. 2F). Subsequently, a three-point bending test was performed to assess the biomechanical properties of the fractured tibiae. The results showed significant improvements in maximum loading and elastic modulus in the DAPs-treated group compared to the Model group (Fig. 2G). ABH/Orange G staining combined with histomorphometric analysis showed an increased cartilage area in the central region of fracture callus at 4 and 7 days post-fracture, and an increased woven bone area in the rim of fracture callus at 7 and 14 days post-fracture in the DAPs-treated mice compared to the Model group (Fig. 3A–C). DAPs activate neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-creb axis to downregulate sympathetic tone for osteogenesis

Sensory nerves not only transmit bone pain signals but also critically regulate osteogenesis [40]. PGE2, an inflammatory mediator synthesized by COX-2, is abundantly produced in response to bone injury [41,42]. Recent findings demonstrate that PGE2 exerts osteogenic effects through binding to EP4 receptors on sensory nerves, leading to hypothalamic CREB phosphorylation and subsequent downregulation of sympathetic tone [18,19]. Here, to determine whether DAPs induced osteogenesis by activating neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis to downregulate sympathetic tone, in vivo studies using sensory nerve-specific fluorescent reporter (TomatoAvil) mice and SSPCs-specific fluorescent reporter (TomatoGli1ER) mice, and in vitro experiments employing primary BMCs and DRG neurons were conducted. The results of IHC staining and ELISA assay showed that COX-2 protein in the fracture hematoma and callus, and PGE2 levels in the serum, bone marrow and fracture callus were significantly increased in the DAPs-treated mice at 4, 7 and 14 days post-fracture (Fig. 4A and B). It is well known that BMCs are composed of heterogeneous cell populations including SSPCs, osteoblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages and others, and contribute greatly to the formation of fracture hematoma/callus [43]. Therefore, we investigated whether DAPs could promote PGE2 synthesis in BMCs. Primary BMCs were isolated and treated for 24 h with FBS (blank), DAPs-free, low-, middle-, or high-concentration DAPs-containing rat serum (Fig. 4C). No significant morphological differences were observed among these groups (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis revealed a significant up-regulation of COX-2 in BMCs treated with DAPs-containing serum compared to blank and DAPs-free controls (Fig. 4E and F). ELISA quantification revealed elevated PGE2 levels in the culture medium of BMCs following treatment with DAPs-containing rat serum (Fig. 4H). Collectively, in vivo and in vitro data indicate that DAPs activate COX-2/PGE2 signaling in BMCs, which may serve as a critical source of PGE2 during fracture healing. Further, we analyzed histomorphological changes and COX-2 expression in the liver and kidney of DAPs-treated mice at 14 days post-fracture. No hepatorenal toxicity including cell necrosis, structural disorder, was observed following oral administration of DAPs (Fig. S1A). IHC staining showed increased COX-2 protein levels in both liver and kidney of DAPs-treated mice compared with the model mice (Fig. S1B and S1C), suggesting that the regulatory effects of DAPs on the COX-2/PGE2 signaling may be systemic rather than specific to bone. To further evaluate the direct role of DAPs on osteogenesis, we analyzed the expression of Runx2 in BMCs and C3H10T1/2 cells after treatment with DAPs-containing serum. The results showed no significant changes of Runx2 protein levels in either cell type following the intervention (Fig. 4G–S2A). Additionally, ALP staining reveled no increase in the ALP-stained area in C3H10T1/2 cells after treatment with DAPs-containing serum (Fig. S2B). Discussion

Despite of various treatments including surgery, physiotherapy and biologics, approximately 5∼10% of fractures still progress to delayed healing or non-union [2]. Thus, developing more efficient therapeutic approaches is urgently needed. Deer antlers are the only mammalian organ capable of complete regeneration, enabling them to form a new functional antler through production of bone, cartilage, blood vessels, nerves and skin within a few months [25]. As a valuable animal medicine, deer antler contains multiple bioactive components, among which peptides exhibit the most extensive biological effects [49]. In the present study, a multi-enzyme hydrolysis method was used to extract DAPs, and their peptide compositions and metabonomics were further analyzed by LC-MS/MS. We identified a total of 48,607 peptides, but only 303 could be annotated as functional peptides due to the limited scope of available peptide databases. Several of these confirmed peptides exhibited multifunctional properties. Furthermore, metabolomic analysis revealed 27 up-regulated and 6 down-regulated metabolites, which were categorized into 13 chemical classes. Among these, organonitrogen compounds, indoles and derivatives, carboxylic acids and derivatives, benzene and substituted derivatives, and organic disulfides are considered direct metabolites of DAPs. The remaining classes including fatty acyls, organooxygen compounds, and others, indicate that DAPs' metabolites are involved in the synthesis of structural components, energy metabolism, and signal regulation. Similar to other methods for polypeptide extraction, such as organic solvent extraction, ultrasonic-assisted extraction and high-voltage pulsed electric field extraction, enzymatic hydrolysis also affects the activity of polypeptides to varying degrees [50]. Therefore, improving extraction methods to minimize biological activity loss and enhance extraction efficiency remains one of the key challenges for the future commercial application of DAPs.

Currently, various biological functions of DAPs have been reported. Notably, DAPs significantly enhanced fracture healing by counteracting high glucose-induced suppression of osteogenesis in diabetic mice [26]. The present study aimed to investigate their effects on normal fracture healing and the underlying mechanisms. Gross observation and μCT analysis demonstrated that DAPs accelerated tibial fracture healing in mice. Meanwhile, we found improved mechanical properties and functional recovery of the fractured limb following DAPs treatment. Bone formation is essential to successful fracture healing. The stronger woven bone callus, increased mineralized bone formation rate, and up-regulated osteogenic gene expression (Alp, Runx2, Col1a1) in DAPs-treated mice revealed that enhanced osteogenesis is a key mechanism underlying DAPs-induced acceleration of fracture healing. Once fracture occurs, SSPCs are mobilize and undergo osteogenic differentiation for bone formation [9]. Furthermore, we analyzed the role of DAPs in SSPCs differentiation during fracture healing using SSPCs-specific fluorescent reporter (TomatoGli1ER) mice, and observed an increased trans-differentiation of Gli1-labeled cells into osteoblasts. Therefore, it can be concluded that DAPs accelerate fracture healing by promoting the osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs.

PGE2, a common inflammatory mediator synthesized by COX-2, is produced in response to fracture and plays a pivotal role in fracture healing [13,42,51]. Studies have reported that exogenous administration of PGE2 promotes osteogenesis and accelerates fracture healing [15,16]. Here, KEGG enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites between DAPs-containing and DAPs-free serum identified five significant metabolic pathways. Among these, linoleic acid metabolism pathway and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathway can supply direct precursors for arachidonic acid synthesis. Since PGE2 is produced from arachidonic acid catalyzed by COX-2, our findings indicated that DAPs metabolites contribute to the production of PGE2. Furthermore, we found that DAPs significantly elevated PGE2 levels and COX-2 expression within the mouse hematoma/callus. It is well known that BMCs are major cellular contributors to the formation of fracture hematoma/callus. In vitro experiments demonstrated that DAPs could stimulate substantial secretion of PGE2 from BMCs by activating COX-2, revealing that BMCs serve as a critical source of PGE2 within the hematoma/callus following DAPs treatment. It has been reported that oral administration of DAPs exerts systemic effects including alleviating liver injury [31] and cerebral ischemic injury [52], improving late-onset hypogonadism [53], and modulating immune responses [54] et al. Our data showed an significant increase in COX-2 protein in the liver and kidney of fractured mice after oral administration of DAPs, indicating that the regulatory effects of DAPs on COX-2/PGE2 signaling are not specific to bone, and that PGE2 produced from other tissues may also contribute to fracture healing. To our knowledge, no study has yet reported on the regulation of PGE2 by DAPs, while their modulatory effects on other inflammatory mediator including TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6 and IL-10, have been demonstrated in various animal models [52,55].

Sensory nerves transmit bone-derived signals to the central nervous system to initiate physiological responses [11,56,57]. Recent studies reported that PGE2 can bind to the EP4 receptor on sensory nerves to activate p-CREB signaling in the hypothalamus for osteogenesis [18,19]. Our present study provided evidence that DAPs activated the EP4 receptor on sensory nerves, as shown by an increased EP4+CGRP+/CGRP+ area within the fracture callus of C57BL/6 mice and an elevated EP4+Avil+/Avil + area in TomatoAvil mice after DAPs treatment. DRG is composed of neuronal cell bodies, and serves as a relay station between the bone microenvironment and the central nervous system through sensory nerves [58]. Furthermore, we demonstrated that DAPs activated the EP4 receptor in the DRG through detecting increased EP4 expression in both DAPs-treated TomatoAvil mice and primary DRG neurons cultured with CM derived from BMCs exposed to DAPs-containing rat serum. CGRP is a neuropeptide mainly produced by DRG neurons in response to stimulation of peripheral sensory nerves, and its release is regulated by EP4 receptor activity [59,60]. Consistently, the expression of CGRP was significantly upregulated in both DRG tissues and cultured DRG neurons following administration with DAPs or CM derived from BMCs exposed to DAPs-containing rat serum. Subsequently, p-CREB signaling in the VMH was activated by DAPs. However, DAPs failed to upregulate CGRP in the DRG or p-CREB in the VMH of EP4Avil mice compared with EP4fx/fx mice and untreated EP4Avil mice. The blockade of PGE2/EP4/p-CREB signal transduction abolished the DAPs-induced promotion of osteogenesis and acceleration of fracture healing. Furthermore, DAPs-containing serum failed to significantly upregulate Runx2 protein levels in BMCs or C3H10T1/2 cells, nor did it increase the ALP-stained area of C3H10T1/2 cells, demonstrating that DAPs could not directly promote osteogenesis. Overall, These findings demonstrate that the acceleration of fracture healing by DAPs depends on activation of the neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis.

The skeleton is richly innervated by sensory and sympathetic nerves, which converge and functionally interact in the central nervous system [61]. Sympathetic tone is known to negatively regulate bone metabolism, such as impairing bone healing [62,63], promoting bone loss [18,64] and osteonecrosis [65]. Circulating NE concentration serves as a key indicator of sympathetic activity, with its biosynthesis tightly controlled by TH [66]. Recent studies have revealed that elevated NE release driven by sympathetic tone suppresses the osteogenic commitment of mesenchymal stem cells [19]. These catabolic effects of sympathetic tone on bone are mediated by p-CREB signaling in the hypothalamus [19,65,67]. Here, down-regulation of TH in the hypothalamic PVN, DRG, fracture callus, decreased serum NE level and osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs were observed in DAPs-treated mice during fracture healing. However, these DAPs-induced changes were abolished after the blockade of PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis in EP4Avil mice. Thus, we concluded that DAPs enhance osteogenic differentiation of SSPCs for fracture healing by activating neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis to suppress sympathetic tone.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, we did not examine the osteogenic effects of DAPs on BMCs or SSPCs in vitro to exclude their direct actions on bone fracture. Secondly, EP4Avil mice exhibit reduced basal bone mass compared to Cre-nagative littermates [19,57]. Thus, the decreased bone formation within the fracture callus in EP4Avil mice compared to EP4fx/fx mice following DAPs treatment may be partially caused by the loss function of EP4 receptor in sensory nerves. Nevertheless, by analyzing the absence of changes in osteogenesis in EP4Avil mice treated with or without DAPs, it can be concluded that the acceleration of fracture healing by DAPs depends on activation of the neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis. Lastly, in vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that DAPs up-regulated CGRP expression in DRG neurons, and it has been reported that CGRP exerts positive effects on osteogenesis [68]. However, its specific role in DAPs-mediated fracture healing acceleration has not been thoroughly investigated.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identified 303 functional peptides and 32 differential metabolites of DAPs using LC-MS/MS. Through in vivo and in vitro experiments, we revealed that DPAs accelerated mouse fracture healing by activating neurosensory PGE2/EP4/p-CREB axis to downregulate sympathetic tone.
