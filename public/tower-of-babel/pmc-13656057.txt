Routine intraoperative frozen section assessment of lymph nodes in clinical stage IA non-small cell lung cancer: towards a risk-stratified approach
Authors: Andréanne Gagné
Published: 2026

Lung cancer screening programs are currently being implemented in multiple countries (1), leading to an expected increase in the number of patients with clinical early-stage IA non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), including a significant proportion harboring multiple synchronous and metachronous tumor foci (2). As curative-intent surgery is the standard of care in the treatment of these early-stage tumors (3), there is a renewed interest in assessing whether parenchyma-sparing sublobar resections are acceptable oncologic resections to preserve both pulmonary function and lung tissue. Two landmark clinical trials, JCOG0802 (4) and CALGB 140503 (5), recently showed the non-inferiority of sublobar resections compared to lobectomy for survival outcomes in NSCLC patients with peripheral clinical stage IA measuring 2 cm or less. The JCOG0802 trial, which evaluated segmentectomy specifically, even found an overall survival advantage for this approach, while CALGB 140503, which also included wedge resection, showed comparable overall survival between surgical techniques. These trials are certainly contributing to accelerate the shift in the current surgical practice towards performing more sublobar resections (6), raising new questions regarding peri-operative mediastinal staging.

According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, pre-treatment pathologic mediastinal staging remains optional in patients with peripheral NSCLC measuring less than 3 cm and negative nodes on chest computed tomography (CT) and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET)/CT scans, deferring nodal assessment to the time of surgery (3). Moreover, patient eligibility in the CALGB 140503 trial required confirmation of negative nodal status as determined on frozen section (FS) prior to randomization, while JCOG0802 mandated FS assessment of all macroscopically suspicious lymph nodes found during surgery. In this context, establishing the diagnostic performance of FS for the detection of occult nodal metastases becomes important for the safe implementation of sublobar resections in routine clinical practice.

I read with great interest the work by Ortiz and colleagues (7) that intended to answer this question. They aimed to assess the performance of the routine use of FS to determine nodal status during the resection of clinical stage IA NSCLC in an academic center over a 5-year period. A review of all intra-operative and final pathology reports was performed, and nodal status was compared between reports. Their retrospective cohort included 909 patients, among which 90% underwent preoperative FDG-PET/CT staging and 27% received an invasive pathological mediastinal staging. Occult nodal metastases were found in 46 patients, including pN1+ disease in 31 patients and pN2+ in 15 patients, and a total of 7, 016 lymph nodes were assessed among which 95 harbored a metastasis. At the patient level, FS demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy, with a near-perfect specificity of 99.9%, and a negative predictive value of 99.0%, though sensitivity reached only 80.4%. Performance was similar when examining the data at the lymph node level, including in N1 and N2 stations individually. Due to the potential implications of finding an occult nodal metastasis in patients undergoing sublobar resections, the authors reviewed their findings specifically in this subgroup. Among 565 patients with intention to receive a sublobar resection, occult pN+ disease was identified in 14, and FS allowed intra-operative identification of 9 of them. Interestingly, the authors noted that none underwent a completion lobectomy due to the FS finding.

To my knowledge, this work represents the first dedicated assessment of the diagnostic performance of intraoperative FS for lymph node assessment in patients with clinical stage IA NSCLC. It benefits from a large cohort of consecutive cases originating from a single institution with rigorous selection criteria. The retrospective design allows for a capture of the performance of FS in a real-world setting, outside of the constraints of a clinical trial. This work also addresses an important gap in quality-of-care assessment in thoracic pathology, as there is a lack of recommendations regarding the use of FS in the surgical management of oncologic lung resections.

However, the present study reports a sensitivity of around 80% to detect nodal metastases on FS. It would have been interesting to obtain further details about the causes of discrepancies, to understand whether this is caused by artifacts, micrometastases, sampling errors, interpretation errors, and whether the FS technique used by the authors played a role. This last element could also potentially limit the generalizability of the results. Indeed, the FS technique used by the authors is unique to their institution and involves freezing lymph nodes in water followed by cutting a 10-μm section subsequently stained with toluidine blue. This technique allows for a fast FS assessment, but the stain differs from traditional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as it highlights cells with high content of nucleic acids, such as lymphocytes. This could theoretically make it harder to distinguish small metastatic foci from the dense lymphoid background, potentially impacting the detection of smaller metastases.

Few other studies assessed the performance of FS to detect nodal metastases specifically in lung cancer. A recent prospective multicenter trial evaluating a selective mediastinal lymph node dissection strategy in clinical T1N0 NSCLCs reported a similar sensitivity of FS for hilar node metastasis of 76.9% (3 missed nodal metastases among 13 positive cases), with a specificity of 100% and an overall accuracy of 99.6% (8). However, unlike the present study, this was assessed within a clinical trial in which the primary objective was not to assess the diagnostic performance of FS, whereas Ortiz et al. offers a real-world assessment of FS across all sampled lymph nodes. In contrast, older data reported sensitivities ranging from 94% to 99%, but these have been obtained in the context of mediastinoscopies performed across various NSCLC stages (9). While these studies did not discuss the causes of FS discrepancies, it is likely that most nodal metastases in clinical stage IA NSCLC with negative preoperative imaging are smaller deposits or micrometastases, which are inherently harder to identify on FS than larger nodal metastases that are more likely to be encountered in older mediastinoscopy series. In addition, it is worth mentioning that given the small absolute number of occult metastases in this population, even a handful of missed cases on FS translates into a substantially lower sensitivity. Overall, there remains a need to better understand why FS fails to identify positive nodal disease in certain cases, both to guide quality improvement in pathology and to inform its appropriate use in this population.

Nonetheless, the surgical impact of missing occult nodal metastases on FS in this cohort of patients, even in low absolute numbers, needs to be discussed. Current NCCN guidelines do not provide formal recommendations regarding the surgical management of patients undergoing sublobar resection for whom nodal metastases are identified during surgery (3). In response to this question, a growing body of literature has investigated whether patients with clinical stage IA NSCLC and occult pN1 or pN2 metastases should undergo completion lobectomy, consistently demonstrating no survival difference between sublobar resections and lobectomies across multiple institutions (10-16). This suggests that patient outcomes are mostly driven by tumor biology and tumor stage rather than the extent of the resection. Furthermore, the authors investigated the impact of identifying occult nodal metastases on FS in patients undergoing a sublobar resection on intraoperative management and interestingly found that it did not result in conversion lobectomy in any patient. The reasons cited by the authors for not proceeding with lobectomy included multifocal disease, limited pulmonary function, lack of patient consent, and N2 disease that was considered to dictate prognosis regardless of the extent of the resection. This illustrates that a tailored approach for each patient ultimately guided surgical management. In light of these elements, the added value of routine FS for every lymph node in this population is worth reconsidering.

Addressing this first requires understanding how many patients will have an occult nodal metastasis in this population. In the present study, occult nodal disease was found in 5.1% of patients (pN1 in 3.4% and pN2 in 1.7%), a rate consistent with prior literature. Indeed, in the clinical trials, N1 or N2 disease was found intraoperatively in 6.4% of patients in CALGB 140503 and in 6.0% of cases in JCOG0802. Other studies report slightly wider ranges of occult nodal disease on final pathology, varying from 4% to 12.6% of cases (15-19), with N1 involvement generally reported to be more frequent than N2 involvement (11,13,16-19). Overall, these figures remain relatively low, but the risk is not evenly distributed across all patients with clinical stage IA NSCLC. While the present study does not explore this question in depth, the authors report significantly higher rates of nodal metastases in higher-stage tumors, with a trend toward association with histological type and tumor location. Several clinical and radiological factors have been associated with a higher likelihood of occult nodal metastases, including an elevated consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR) (8,18-21), larger tumor size (10,17,19), and higher standardized uptake value (SUV) on PET scan (17,22). In contrast, a recent phase III trial demonstrated that the risk of nodal metastases in ground-glass dominant adenocarcinomas is null, even proposing to omit lymph node dissection in this group of patients (23).

Routine FS of every lymph node also carries a practical cost. It can prolong operative time, add workload to the pathology laboratory, particularly in high-volume practices, and increase costs. In the present study, only 1.4% of assessed FS lymph nodes demonstrated a metastasis. The authors calculated that the number needed to test (NNT) with FS nodal assessment to identify one patient with a lymph node metastasis was 24. Among patients with an intention to undergo a sublobar resection, the NNT to intraoperatively identify one case of occult pN+ disease was 63 (565 patients/9 patients whose nodal disease was detected by FS). These numbers illustrate well the practical cost of a universal approach for intra-operative nodal assessment in clinical stage IA NSCLC.

Taken together, the previously discussed elements suggest that a risk-stratified approach to selecting patients who would most benefit from nodal assessment by FS could be reasonable and feasible. However, addressing this question requires closing a fundamental gap in thoracic pathology practice, which is the lack of standardization and recommendations in how FS should be performed during lung cancer resections. Indeed, no formal guideline specifies which and how many lymph nodes should be sent for FS, in which patients, what sampling protocol should be followed, or which freezing and staining technique should be used, resulting in various practices among different centers. As discussed above, the FS technique used in the present study differs substantially from the standard optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT)-embedding and H&E staining used in most academic institutions and its potential impact on diagnostic performance remains unknown. All these elements certainly warrant additional research to identify the best FS methods and quality metrics, as well as the patients and specimens that should undergo FS assessment, in order to standardize practice in pathology laboratories across institutions.

Overall, the work by Ortiz et al. represents an important effort toward a more rigorous evaluation of the use of FS for nodal status assessment. As discussed, its imperfect sensitivity, combined with the limited clinical impact on outcomes when nodal disease is missed and the low number of patients with nodal disease, suggests that routine FS of every lymph node in this population deserves reconsideration and calls for a more structured approach. Identifying patients at higher risk of occult nodal metastases, based on CTR, tumor size, location, or SUV, could help target the use of FS rather than applying it universally, but dedicated studies are needed to validate this approach in this population. Pathologists can play a central leadership role in these efforts, as previously demonstrated by our group through the development and implementation of grossing criteria for FS in surgical margin assessment in lung cancer (24,25). Ultimately, the value of FS nodal assessment also depends on whether and how the result will impact surgical management, underscoring the importance of a clear communication between surgeons and pathologists.
