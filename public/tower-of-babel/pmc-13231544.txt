Overcoming the curse of knowledge: guidelines for reporting system-specific details of host infection and migration dynamics
Authors: Sandra A. Binning, Allison K. Shaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Literature syntheses link knowledge across study systems and regions. However, specific details on the biological systems of the included publications may not always be explicitly outlined because of communication gaps (a.k.a. The Curse of Knowledge) and/or knowledge gaps. These gaps can lead to inappropriate comparisons across studies if researchers unfamiliar with the systems make inaccurate assumptions about aspects of study design or natural history. In the context of movement ecology, a growing number of publications explore how host infection and host migration intersect, work that is critical for predicting how global change will impact both animal movement and parasite transmission. Given the complexity of host and parasite life histories, these studies may be particularly vulnerable to knowledge and communication gaps. To minimize communication gaps and highlight knowledge gaps, we developed a checklist with 15 specific elements separated into three categories about migration, parasites, and sampling that should be addressed within a publication to provide a more complete picture of the study system for a non-specialist. We validate our checklist in three ways. First, we use two worked examples to show the information each element reveals. Second, we apply our checklist to a database of 36 publications on migrating infected hosts to quantify which details are typically missing from the published literature. Third, we create a try-it-yourself example based on a fictional study system to illustrate the challenges associated with extracting information on an unknown system. All but one of the publications assessed were missing information on at least one checklist element and 11 were missing more than half. Our checklist is therefore a tool that researchers can use to broaden the impact of their studies and facilitate cross-species comparisons and syntheses. We hope this work stimulates interest in the value of reporting guidelines and encourages researchers to apply this approach to other aspects of animal ecology that might similarly suffer from the Curse of Knowledge.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-026-00665-7.

Introduction

In an era of big data and literature syntheses, researchers are increasingly exposed to systems and species to which they may not be familiar. This lack of system-specific knowledge puts them at risk of misunderstandings, false assumptions and erroneous conclusions. A contributing factor to these problems is a lack of detail on species natural history in studies of animal ecology, driven by the authors’ assumptions that readers are more familiar with their system than they actually are, combined with space constraints when publishing. Indeed, the Curse of Knowledge is a well-established cognitive bias whereby a person who possesses specialized knowledge assumes that others share the same level of knowledge, leading to communication break-downs and ineffective knowledge-transfer [1]. In other words, it can be hard for us to imagine not knowing something we consider fundamental, and thus it may seem trivial to state such things explicitly. In reality, details of a given species life-history are often unknown to non-specialists, and not reporting seemingly mundane details can lead to inappropriate comparisons across study systems and inaccurate testing of frameworks.

One way to mitigate misunderstandings is to ensure that information is clearly communicated in scientific publications. Reporting guidelines and checklists can help accomplish this goal in several ways such as improving the quality of reporting and transparency of research results, standardizing methodologies across different settings and systems, reducing bias in data collection, and helping researchers avoid common pitfalls associated with applying a new approach. For example, the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) statement, first developed in 1996 as a checklist to improve the reporting of randomized controlled trials, is widely implemented in the medical sciences and updated regularly to reflect advances in the field [2]. Several studies have found that the reporting quality of randomized clinical trials in publications has improved since the CONSORT statement has become commonly used [3–5]. Reporting guidelines can also facilitate comparisons across taxa by providing species-specific information that may or may not apply broadly to all systems, allowing researchers to adapt their inferences accordingly. This information is especially useful to researchers conducting systematic reviews or meta-analyses where a lack of detail on study or sampling design can mean an otherwise relevant publication is omitted from the meta-analysis [6]. As a result, reporting guidelines can help make research in a given discipline more transparent, thus addressing a common problem in ecology and evolutionary biology [7].

Within movement ecology, a modern resurgence to understand the drivers and barriers facing migrating animals has occurred, as technological advances make the study of large-scale animal movements more logistically feasible [8, 9]. The shift in how we study animal migrations is coupled with a change in how we perceive the importance of movements across landscapes in terms of parasite and pathogen (hereafter parasites) risk and transmission. Indeed, numerous studies have linked animal migrations to disease outbreaks and spillover events [10–12]. Yet, migrating animals can also benefit from seasonal movement to reduce exposure or to recover from parasites [8, 13–15]. Frameworks aiming to explain and predict patterns of migration and infection dynamics across populations and species have helped advance our understanding of the complex interplay between host movement and parasites and serve as a foundation for the design of empirical studies to test assumptions, explain diverse migration patterns, and anticipate how altered migration might disrupt disease dynamics in nature ([8]; Table 1).

Frameworks that describe host migration in the context of parasite infection

Developing frameworks for studying the intersection of host migration and host infection is especially critical when thinking about the knock-on effects of anthropogenic changes on both animal movement and disease dynamics [27, 28]. A growing number of empirical studies combining metrics of migration and infection across diverse hosts and parasites sets the stage for assessing the generality of patterns observed (reviewed in Shaw et al., [29]). As momentum for designing and conducting these types of empirical studies grows, so too does the need to establish guidelines for what essential information should be reported in a publication so that a study can be interpreted and understood by a non-specialist hoping to draw inferences from across taxonomic groups. In addition to the CONSORT guidelines, reporting guidelines and checklists have been successfully developed for experimental protocols in physiological ecology [30–32], in environmental sciences [33], and in ecology and evolutionary biology [6, 7, 34].

In the context of studies reporting infection in migratory animals, details regarding the natural history of hosts (e.g., when and where mating and births occur, migration timing), parasites (e.g., how infections are transmitted, where parasites are acquired) as well as where and when infection metrics are sampled, are all critical in order to apply conceptual frameworks appropriately to a system. Unfortunately, these details are often lacking in published research, perhaps because authors assume readers have prior knowledge of the natural history of the hosts and parasites involved (i.e., communication gaps / The Curse of Knowledge), or because key elements of the natural history of the system are lacking (i.e., knowledge gaps). There may be patterns to the types of gaps, reflecting the baseline knowledge of a particular study system. For instance, studies on well-studied, common species may report less natural history data due to perceived familiarity within the broader public. For example, due to the popularity of birdwatching as a hobby, the migration cycles of many bird species are colloquially known and may not be explicitly stated in a publication. We might then expect communication gaps to be more common in relatively well-studied species. Alternatively, natural history details of many species are not fully understood by science. For instance, the exact migration routes of some aquatic megafaunas are not well documented. Thus, we might expect knowledge gaps to be more common when species are less well studied or have complex life-histories. Distinguishing between gaps in knowledge versus communication is important for identifying areas in need of further research effort. Reporting gaps may also depend on the scope or discipline of the journal where the article is published. Host-focused journals, for example, that only publish work on birds or insects, may assume greater knowledge of certain species than a general-ecology journal, and thus suffer more from communication gaps. Similarly, a disease-focused journal may assume more knowledge of parasite life history and not mention these details explicitly. Thus, we might expect to find differences in reporting depending on journal focus and breadth.

Here, we use research on infected migratory animals as a lens through which to highlight the value of reporting checklists and the extent to which knowledge and communication gaps exist in the published literature in movement ecology. The objectives of this paper are three-fold. First, we develop a reporting checklist aimed at helping authors studying infected migratory hosts to document key details of parasite and host natural histories, as well as sampling design, in order to improve the interpretation of studies and facilitate comparisons among systems. Incorporating this checklist into publications will help researchers interpret their results in the context of previously developed frameworks aimed at understanding and predicting host infection dynamics and migration. Second, we apply our checklist to a recently compiled database of 36 publications on migratory species with parasite infections. This database focused on parasite intensity (a key component of infection cost) and included studies that quantified parasite intensity at least once along the migratory route. Using it, we: (1) assess the practicality of including such details in a manuscript; (2) quantify the degree to which this information is typically included in publications and (3) identify gaps in reporting related to study attributes such as the taxa studied or journal scope. Third, we provide a fictional example of a novel host-parasite system discovered on an exo-planet that readers can use to test the checklist. This exercise attempts to recreate the frustration that can arise when attempting to extract relevant information in a system one has no familiarity with. Overall, our goal is to encourage researchers to think about how they can avoid the Curse of Knowledge in their writing while providing an example for how movement ecology research should be reported to maximize effective knowledge transfer.

Here we walk through the checklist that we developed with 15 reporting elements to consider in studies exploring infection in migratory animals (Table 2) and make an argument for including each one. We discuss how each element in the checklist provides information needed to assess the frameworks presented in Table 1. We recognize that addressing all these points is a lot of detail to include in the main text of publications that are already constrained in length. Thus, we provide a template in supplement 1 that authors can use as part of their supplementary material to report the checklist elements. If information for an element is unknown, this should be indicated.

Checklist of elements describing host-parasite system characteristics to include in a publication reporting infection of migratory hosts. This checklist could be filled out (see supplement 1) and included as a publication supplement

When are offspring born? (Mig_born)

Knowing when offspring are born with respect to the host migration cycle can help interpret sharp changes in prevalence – for example, whether a change is due to an influx of newborn susceptible individuals versus sudden change in infected individuals (e.g., mortality of infected individuals via migratory culling, or recovery of infected individuals via migratory recovery). Having this specific information (as opposed to the more generic ‘breeding’ period that is often used) would also help distinguish between transmission events that occur because of mating versus birthing.

What habitats are migrants moving through or between? (Mig_habitat)

Knowing what habitat(s) migrants move through (and how these relate to habitat at the end points of migration) can help determine whether individuals might escape parasites by changing environments (environmental migratory escape), avoid particular sites/routes with high numbers of parasites (migratory avoidance), recover from infection in a different habitat (migratory recovery), or increase exposure to parasites (migratory exposure).

What host activities are taking place in each habitat? (Mig_activity)

Knowing what activities take place at the host’s non-breeding sites can help determine both whether parasite transmission is possible there (if the transmission form is known), and what the selective pressures favoring migration might be. For example, this information can help distinguish between the two types of migratory escape (environmental vs. social).

What proportion of the year is spent in each location and in transit? (Mig_time)

Knowing how long hosts usually spend on each part of migration can help identify cases of migratory stalling (where infected individuals travel more slowly during migration) or migratory separation (where infected individuals delay the start of their migration compared to uninfected ones).

What is the migration strategy (partial migration, differential migration, over-summering, etc.)? (Mig_strategy)

Knowing whether the population has partial migration and/or differential migration would determine whether future work could compare infection in migratory versus sedentary individuals to explore whether infected hosts abandon migration (migratory dropout), whether migratory behaviour varies across ontogeny (migratory allopatry) and/or whether migrants move different distances (short versus long-distance) to explore migratory separation or migratory stalling.

What is the variation across migration strategies in the population in terms of who migrates, where they migrate, timing, route etc? (Mig_variation)

Knowing whether there is variation in which individuals migrate would determine whether mechanisms like social migratory escape to reduce population density and the transmission of directly-transmitted infections or migratory allopatry should be explored. Knowing if there is variation in migration route and timing would indicate whether migratory avoidance, migratory separation, or migratory stalling might occur.

What is the parasite life cycle (e.g., direct, indirect transmission)? (Par_life)

Knowing whether parasites are transmitted directly between hosts versus indirectly through the environment or via other intermediate hosts can help determine whether or not a migration between different habitats might be leading to environmental migratory escape.

How is the parasite acquired? (Par_acquired)

Knowing how the parasite is acquired and transmitted (e.g. through ingestion while foraging, through skin to skin contact while mating, through the environment), in combination with information on habitat and on other hosts of the parasite, can help determine whether mechanisms of migratory exposure, environmental or social migratory escape, or migratory allopatry could be occurring.

When is the parasite acquired? (Par_time)

Knowing when in the host migration cycle the host encounters the parasite would help distinguish between processes where migration comes with infection costs (e.g., migratory exposure) versus where migration comes with infection benefits (e.g., environmental migratory escape, migratory recovery).

What other (relevant) hosts does the parasite have? (Par_host)

Knowing whether parasites can infect other hosts would help determine whether migration routes that take individuals through the other host species’ habitat could then lead to migratory exposure.

What cost(s) does parasite infection have? (Par_cost)

Knowing what form of costs the parasites inflict on hosts (e.g.,energetic, nutritional, locomotor, reproductive,) would help determine whether infection costs might be exacerbated by migrating (e.g., migratory culling, migratory relapse, migratory stalling).

What impact does migration have on transmission/prevalence? (Par_migration)

Knowing which mechanism describes how host infection and migration intersect is needed to predict whether parasite infection prevalence will increase or decrease under different circumstances, for example, due to changing habitat availability as a result of human activity. If a specific mechanism is known (or is expected), it should be mentioned explicitly.

Which hosts were sampled? (Samp_which)

Knowing whether infection metrics differ among individuals of different classes (e.g. life stages - juveniles, sub-adults, adults) would help determine if spatial separation during migration of different life stages has any parasite-related advantages (migratory allopatry).

When during the migration cycle were the data collected? (Samp_when)

If migratory individuals have different parasite infection metrics (in terms of prevalence, intensity, etc.) than sedentary individuals, knowing whether parasite infection data were collected before hosts depart on their migration (pre-migration data) versus after they return from their migration (post-migration data) is critical for distinguishing whether migration varies with infection status (which could indicate migratory allopatry, migratory separation, migratory stalling, migratory dropout) versus whether migration leads to differential outcomes for individuals with different infection statuses (which could indicate migratory recovery, migratory culling, environmental and social migratory escape, migratory exposure, or migratory relapse of latent infections).

Where in the life cycle were the data collected? (Samp_where)

If parasite infection prevalence is measured in both the breeding and non-breeding grounds, knowing whether the infection data in the breeding grounds was collected before or after reproduction is needed to appropriately assess changes in prevalence across migration that could indicate migratory culling, migratory recovery, migratory exposure, or migratory relapse.

Worked example

We work through the checklist using two publications selected from the existing literature (Table S1 in supplement 2); the first is a study of Red deer (Cervus elaphus) infested with Ixodes ricinus by Mysterud et al. [35] and the second is a study of Dark-eyed junco (Junco hyemalis) infected with Haemosporidian blood parasites by Slowinski et al. [36]. These publications each describe a single host-parasite species set, were distinct from each other, represent taxa from the two most common host groups where data were available (birds, mammals), as well as two of the most common parasite types (ticks, blood parasites), and were relatively complete in terms of information. The authors separately scored both publications and then discussed our scoring to standardize discrepancies in our classification system.

Our aim was to illustrate that without a priori consideration of each element in the checklist, finding the relevant information can sometimes be difficult. We provide our scoring/coding of the publication for each element along with quotes taken from the publication where the information was found (Table S1 in supplement 2). While in some cases very explicit information is provided, in others it is ambiguous. Our goal here is not to call out these two studies in particular (both provide more information than average), but rather to demonstrate how challenging it can be to extract information from a publication even if it is present, a challenge that is exacerbated if the reader is not familiar with the study system.

When describing migration, Mysterud et al. [35] scored full marks (present in all 6 elements in the migration category). In some cases, the information provided was very explicit. For example, when describing migration timing (Mig_time), the authors provide both the median arrival date from the spring migration (8 May) as well as the average number of days spent by Red deer in the summer range (122 days). However, in other cases, the information was more difficult to assess. The migration activity element aims to assess whether behaviours such as foraging, hibernating, breeding etc. are occurring in a given habitat. Mysterud provides information about snowpack and forage availability in high altitude summer habitats that a reader can use to deduce that Red deer foraging activities are occurring, but this information relies on the reader’s interpretation of the text. In contrast, Slowinski et al. [36] scored partial marks for migration details (4 of the 6 elements). They describe clearly that one population was migratory and the other sedentary. However, for migratory timing, the authors state that the migratory and sedentary populations overlapped from October to April but did not provide details about the length of migration itself. Similarly, little detail was given on the other elements (migratory activities and habitat).

This pattern was reversed for details on the parasites. Slowinski et al. [36] scored full marks here (6 of 6 elements in the parasite category). As above, some answers were very specific and others were vague. For example, they clearly described the parasite life cycle and form of transmission (transmitted by insect vectors). In contrast, it was clear that Haemosporidians infect other birds generally, but no specific alternative hosts were mentioned. Mysterud et al. [35] scored partial marks (4 of the 6 elements) for parasite information. Although they provide some details on life stages and alternative host species, information on the life cycle and transmission were omitted. In other cases, information was vague. For example, the authors refer to the tick questing season as being in the summer. Understanding that this refers to the time of the year when deer hosts are exposed to ticks (Par_time) requires some knowledge of transmission terminology (i.e., questing referring to host-seeking behaviour in ticks). Interestingly, Mysterud et al. [35] were explicit about gaps in the knowledge of infection-related costs. While they clearly state that ticks suck blood and likely impose a cost to Red deer, they also acknowledge that limited data exists quantifying these costs. As the goal of our checklist is to communicate what is known regarding the natural history of a host-parasite system, we scored this as a 1 despite not quantifying the cost.

Both studies scored full points for checklist elements in the sampling category. Sampling information is very specific to a given study design and therefore forms an essential part of the methods. Authors would not necessarily assume that readers have prior knowledge of this information. Full details are typically available in archived data as well (e.g., capture date, location) as part of standard sampling. However, given that this information relies on a lot of background context, the value of this information depends on how explicitly previous sections of the checklist were filled out.

We note that there is inherently some subjectivity in how we have scored these papers. Each reader of a publication brings their own knowledge and experience and we, as authors, are no different. Information in the checklist that we assessed as present might be harder for other readers to infer, or information that we could not find might be found easily by other readers. In a sense, this underscores the need for researchers to be as explicit as possible about what information is known and unknown about their study system.

How much information from the checklist can be found in existing literature?

Next, we examined a subset of the literature on host infection in migratory species to assess how often each of the checklist elements was reported. We used a published literature review dataset of 36 papers, compiled by Shaw et al. [29], that measured parasite intensity in migratory animals (see Fig. 1 in [29]). Of the 36 publications, one [37] did not collect new data on parasites and migration and so was excluded from further analysis.

For each of the 35 publications included, we extracted the following information by reading the main article text plus any supplemental material provided. We recorded the host and parasite species studied (identified to the lowest taxonomic level reported). For publications that studied several species (hosts or parasites), we included each focal species set of host and parasite separately. We ignored species sets that were mentioned in the publication but were not its focus; for example, Sándor et al. [38] collected all ticks on birds at a stopover site, finding 4 tick species on 10 bird species, but 92% of the ticks were Ixodes ricinus so we only included information on that one parasite species for each of the 10 bird species. For publications that reported data on several subspecies or populations, we included these as a single species. For each species set within each publication, we recorded whether (1) or not (0) information about each checklist element was present in the publication. Typically, we scored as a present (1) any checklist element where some explicit information was included, even if incomplete. Hosts that were sedentary (non-migratory) were coded as a 1 for all migration elements of the checklist as well as the Samp_when element. We also recorded the host taxa type (Bird, Other (which includes Fish and Insect), Mammal) and the journal type, in terms of the expected readership of each journal (General-ecology, Disease-focused, Host-focused) (Table S2 in supplement 2). Overall, this left us with 248 species sets (i.e., host-parasite combinations; Table S3, dataset in supplement 3).

Reporting completeness was quantified at the study level by calculating the proportion of checklist elements satisfied for each host species evaluated within a publication. For publications assessing multiple host species, completeness scores were averaged to generate a single reporting completeness estimate per paper, thereby avoiding pseudoreplication arising from multiple host assessments within the same study. To account for differences in the amount of information contributed by each publication, analyses were weighted by the number of host species evaluated per paper. The effects of host taxa type, publication type and their interaction on reporting completeness were tested using generalized linear models with a quasibinomial error distribution and logit link function using the statistical software R (Version 4.5.2). We also ran a generalized linear model with a quasibinomial error distribution to test for a correlation between the proportion completeness and the number of host species sets assessed in a given publication. Model assumptions were evaluated using simulation-based residual diagnostics, which confirmed appropriate model fit and variance structure.

We found that most species sets were missing most information from the checklist (Fig. 1). The median (and mode) number of checklist elements included in a species set was 6 out of 15; less than half. Eleven (of 35) papers were missing more than half of the checklist elements for at least one of the species they reported. Notably, we had one publication that included all 15 elements for two species sets. Thus, it is realistic to think that this information could be included in future publications, especially if prompted by our checklist. There was substantial variation in how often each element was included (Figure S1, S2 in supplement 2). Some checklist elements (like Samp_when and Par_host were effectively always reported, while others (Par_life) were rarely reported.

There was no statistically significant interaction between journal type and host taxa type in terms of the predicted proportion of completeness in the reporting elements (GLM: F = 0.1711, P = 0.682, df = 31), nor was there a difference in the checklist elements reported by host taxa (F = 3.29, P = 0.07, df = 33; Fig. 2) despite studies on birds tending to have the lowest number of checklist elements across all journal types (less than 50% completeness, Figure S2 in supplement 2). This trend is likely driven by the high number of bird studies containing information on more than one bird species: Within our dataset, 218 of 248 species sets were for birds (Table S3 in supplement 2) and all the publications with 10 or more species sets came from birds (Figure S3 in supplement 2). One could imagine that publications that report on more species could be more likely to be less complete in reporting information (suggesting communication gaps). Indeed, there was a negative correlation between the number of species sets in a publication and the average proportion of elements reported in that publication (GLM: β = -0.027 ± 0.009 SE, P = 0.005, df = 34; Figure S4, S5 in supplement 2).

There was no statistically significant difference between element reporting by the type of journal that articles were published in (GLM: F = 0.0021, P = 0.96, df = 32). Our initial assumption was that journals with a specific focus on hosts or disease would score lower in most checklist elements than journals with a general ecology focus as the readership of specialty journals may be more familiar with the organisms studied leading to communication gaps, but this prediction was not supported by our analyses. However, there was a pattern to which checklist elements tended to be reported by journal type (Figure S6); publications in General-ecology journals (like Canadian Journal of Zoology, Oecologia) had the highest reporting for checklist elements about migration, but the same was not true for checklist elements about parasites (Figure S6). Teasing apart how communication gaps vary with both the information type and journal type could be a direction for future work.

Try it yourself

To illustrate the challenge of trying to understand a system without any knowledge of its natural history, we have generated a fictional host-parasite system. Thus, all readers should be equally lacking in knowledge of its natural history. We encourage readers to think through the example below and see how many elements from the checklist (Table 2 and supplement 1) can be answered from the information provided. We also encourage readers to reflect on which checklist elements are easier vs. more challenging to answer without more natural history knowledge of the system.

Readers could approach the example below from one of three lenses. First, as a theorist trying to generalize beyond a given system and/or interested in developing theory about the broad mechanisms acting in this system. Second, as an empiricist looking for a new system to study host infection and migration and who wants to figure out how to design a sampling protocol in this system in order to test specific mechanisms. Third, as an empiricist working with a different host-parasite system and wanting to draw comparisons between this system and their own. Each of these lenses require thinking about specific mechanisms, so readers should aim to identify which mechanism(s) from Table 1 are most likely acting in this system, and are thus worth pursuing in their own research.

During your research, you come across literature on a host-parasite system that is unfamiliar to you: bush thrux, which are infected with parasitic squiggle-gumps (Fig. 3 ). Bush thrux are six-limbed creatures that use hind limbs for rock-hopping, medial limbs for gliding, and fore limbs for foraging. A publication by Wang et al. 2065 conducted on the exoplanet Janus mentions that concern about the impact of squiggle-gumps on thrux communities (tawny thrux and common thrux as well as bush thrux) has been growing. Human residents have noticed sick individuals near new human developments on Janus raising questions about the impact of urbanization on thrux-squiggle-gump infection dynamics. Although research on the impact of squiggle-gumps on adult bush thrux is lacking, research on juvenile bush thrux suggests that infected thrux forage less and have reduced rock-hopping behavior. Wang et al. 2065 sampled adult bush thrux at rocky plateaus as they arrived following migration from their soft-silt breeding sites. Thrux were sampled once every orbital period for 3 sequential periods to assess prevalence of squiggle-gump infection. The authors conclude that prevalence of squiggle-gump infection seems to be increasing as a result of increased human urbanization on rocky plateaus on Janus.

Next, we walk through the checklist ourselves for this system showing what natural history information was missing that would have been helpful to know to better understand host infection and migration dynamics in this new system (see summary in Table S4 in supplement 2). The first part of the checklist deals with migration. From the information provided above, we know where bush thrux offspring are born and that thrux migrate between different habitats (Mig_born, Mig_habitat). This information suggests that certain environmental mechanisms could act (migratory escape, recovery, exposure, avoidance) and are worth studying in future work. Further information would be needed about within-year infection dynamics to tease these mechanisms apart. However, no information is provided about migration timing, strategy, or variation (Mig_time, Mig_strategy, Mig_variation), which means that none of the mechanisms that lead to differences across individuals can be assessed (migratory stalling, allopatry, dropout, separation). No information is provided about activities in the rocky plateau sites (other than rock hopping) (Mig_activity) making it challenging to know why migration is happening. Thus, we cannot evaluate how migration might be shaped by parasite infection or what the consequences of migration collapse would have on the bush thrux population. The second part of the checklist deals with parasites. We know other possible hosts of the parasite (Par_host), and if we knew something about the distribution of these other species, we could determine whether to study migratory exposure/avoidance. We know that parasites reduce foraging (Par_cost) and thus presumably energy but also knowing if reduced rock hopping behavior shapes the ability of thrux to migrate or not would be helpful for studying mechanisms like stalling, culling, relapse. No information is provided about transmission (Par_life, Par_acquired, Par_time); thus we do not know if parasite transmission requires direct contact with other individual bush thrux, other thrux species, or if it occurs through the environment or an intermediate host or vector, which also means we do not know if/how some of the mechanisms (e.g. migratory recovery) might happen (Par_migration). The final part of the checklist refers to sampling details. We know which individuals were sampled (Samp_which), when during migration sampling occurred (Samp_when) and where in the life cycle (Samp_where). However, having samples from throughout the annual cycle would let us know whether prevalence changes throughout the year and whether this is due to population dynamics (births, deaths) vs. infection dynamics (infection, recovery). Based on the information provided above, this study would receive a score of 6/15 in our checklist, which was the median score of the species sets assessed from the published literature.

Conclusion

Writing up results from scientific research can be challenging, and authors must make decisions about what information to convey to their readers while respecting journal word limits and ensuring the readability of the text. Often, this leads to background information on species’ natural history being left out. This omission is problematic, especially for readers who are non-specialists in the particular host-parasite system being studied: it can be difficult to know if missing details on host or parasite ecology stem from a communication gap (i.e., assumed general knowledge that is not made explicit), or a knowledge gap (i.e., little or no knowledge of a phenomenon in the literature). A checklist, such as the one we have provided here, can help reveal the knowledge gaps in a system explicitly, making it easier for researchers to generalize across systems, design a new study to build on previous work or fill a knowledge gap, and/or develop new models and frameworks to test predictions. As the push towards more open, transparent and interoperable science grows, tools that enhance comparability across publications, such as checklists, may continue to grow in popularity across the biological sciences.

Our checklist itself may be incomplete due to both knowledge gaps and communication gaps. Migrations are complicated and challenging to study, and we are still learning the details of some migrations – e.g., routes in open ocean or high altitudes, variation among individuals [8, 29]. Any new conceptual frameworks exploring how host migration is shaped by infection may well rely on information that is not included in our checklist. Often little is known about parasites as well. For example, costs of parasitism are challenging to quantify as they can vary widely with infection intensity, environmental context and other factors. Even less is known about how host migration influences parasite ecology and evolution, and the feedback loops among the species involved [39]. Indeed, the current conceptual frameworks predicting patterns of migration and infection dynamics are host-centric and focus less on the reciprocal question of how parasite-centric traits are likely to be shaped by host migratory behaviours. Our checklist reflects this bias in that many details of parasite ecology are lacking as they would not help a reader distinguish between the existing host-focused frameworks. Thus, there appears to be a need for new conceptual frameworks that generate predictions regarding the role of host migration in defining traits such as parasite transmission dynamics, virulence, host manipulation, and other mechanisms of pathogen evolution based on existing empirical evidence (e.g. [40–42]). In terms of communication gaps, we have built the checklist to reflect the information we see as important from the perspective of evaluating mechanisms linking host infection and migration. Thus, the elements we included are filtered through a particular lens. Moving forward, researchers incorporating this checklist as supplementary material in their publications should not be afraid to explicitly acknowledge which information is missing due to knowledge gaps, as this will help guide future research.

As the field of animal migration and parasite infection moves forward and more synthesis work is published, our checklist will serve three central purposes. First, it will give readers unfamiliar with the study system enough natural history information to be able to make links across systems or imagine frameworks of host migration in the context of infection operating in different ways. In our survey of the existing literature, we found that publications reporting on more species included fewer checklist elements (Figure S4 in supplement 2). This trend is a sign that space constraints in journal articles are real but is also a warning that as people publish more cross-species syntheses, the communication gaps will likely increase as less natural history is explicitly included. Thus, synthesis work–meant to bridge across species might increase the siloing of researchers by study systems. Second, our checklist will help all readers (regardless of familiarity with the system) to identify knowledge gaps to address with future research. Despite the bias towards positive results in science, often the most important results to communicate to unfamiliar readers are what is NOT known about a system. Including the checklist (supplement 1) as supplementary material will help authors do this more explicitly. Lastly, we hope our checklist will serve as inspiration for researchers interested in developing reporting guidelines tailored to movement ecology and related fields.
