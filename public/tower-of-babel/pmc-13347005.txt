Association between frailty and fall risk in elderly patients with knee osteoarthritis in community: a propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Deyu Jiang, Heting Liang, Yamin Peng, Qingyun Mao, Yumeng Zhang, Zhixia Jiang, Xiaoli Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo analyze the prevalence of frailty in community-dwelling older adults with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) and explore its independent association with fall risk. A cross-sectional study was conducted among older adults with KOA in Zunyi, China, from October 2022 to September 2023 using convenience sampling. Data were collected on sociodemographic characteristics, instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), depression, anxiety, sleep quality, frailty, and fall risk. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to match frail and non-frail participants at a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.05, based on sociodemographic and lifestyle variables. Conditional logistic regression analysis was then employed to adjust for other potential confounders and determine the strength of the association between frailty and fall risk. A total of 986 community-dwelling elderly patients with KOA were enrolled. The prevalence of frailty was 25.25%, and the proportion of individuals at high fall risk was 51.72%. After PSM, 470 matched participants were obtained (235 in each group). Conditional logistic regression analysis showed that frailty was significantly associated with high fall risk (P < 0.001), with frail individuals having a 5.21-fold higher likelihood of being at high fall risk than non-frail individuals (OR = 5.21, 95%CI 2.66–10.17). Additionally, poor self-rated health (OR = 7.38, 95%CI 1.20–45.46, P = 0.031) and IADL impairment (OR = 4.74, 95%CI 1.37–16.43, P = 0.014) were also significantly associated with increased fall risk. Frailty is independently associated with an increased fall risk in community-dwelling older adults with KOA. Routine frailty screening could be integrated into primary care for this population to support fall risk monitoring and stratified assessment.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis (KOA), Frailty, Fall risk, Elderly, Propensity score matching (PSM)Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

KOA, a common age-related degenerative condition, is characterized by recurrent joint pain, swelling, and progressive deterioration, potentially causing deformity and mobility impairment1. The World Health Organization reports 528 million global osteoarthritis cases in 2019 (~ 73% aged > 55 years), with the knee most affected (365 million)2; China has the largest population (132.81 million)2. KOA impairs older adults’ daily function and quality of life, increasing fall risk, fractures, hospitalization, and healthcare burden3,4. Joint degeneration may elevate frailty risk via altered body composition, inflammation, and hormonal imbalance5, with a Korean study confirming higher frailty risk in KOA versus non-KOA individuals6.

Frailty is a geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve, heightened vulnerability, and impaired homeostasis7, affecting 4.0–59.1% of adults aged 65 years and older8,9. In China, its prevalence is 10% (aged > 60 years), 15% (75–84 years), and 25% (≥ 85 years)7. Frailty associates with adverse outcomes, impaired quality of life, and heavy healthcare costs, severely frail individuals incur nearly six‑fold higher three‑month costs compared with non‑frail counterparts10. Falls are the second leading global cause of accidental death, accounting for approximately 684,000 annual fatalities, predominantly among adults aged ≥ 60 years11. Community-dwelling older adults face elevated fall risk, with injuries reducing independence and increasing societal burden. Frail older adults have higher fall risk12, as do KOA patients ≥ 65 years, due to impaired lower limb function, pain, and altered movement patterns13,14.

In conclusion, previous studies have established that both frailty and KOA are independently associated with an increased risk of falls in older adults, and that KOA may contribute to frailty through mechanisms such as chronic inflammation and physical functional impairment. However, in community-dwelling older adults with KOA, the specific strength of the association between frailty and fall risk remains poorly characterized. Notably, data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) demonstrate that the prevalence of KOA in China is 8.1% nationally, with the highest prevalence observed in southwest China (13.7%), significantly exceeding the national average15. Zunyi, a typical mountainous city in southwest China, is characterized by steep terrain. Such environmental features impose additional physical demands on daily mobility. For elderly individuals with KOA, such terrain may exacerbate biomechanical stress and activity limitations, potentially amplifying the association between frailty and fall risk. These characteristics make Zunyi a suitable setting to examine the frailty-fall association in this population. Therefore, this study aims to employ PSM to mitigate confounding and to explore the association between frailty and fall risk in community-dwelling elderly patients with KOA in Zunyi, China, thereby providing evidence for fall risk assessment in this population.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among community-dwelling older adults in Zunyi city, located in southwest China, from October 2022 to September 2023, using convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria were: 1) aged ≥ 60 years; 2) self-reported KOA history, which was confirmed by physicians according to clinical symptoms combined with X-ray imaging results; 3) ability to communicate; (4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria included severe mental illness or terminal disease.

With the significance level (α) set at 0.05 and the statistical power (1 − β) at 0.8. Based on previous study16,17, P1 = 33.75%, P2 = 17.36%. The two groups were matched and analyzed at a ratio of 1:1. It was found that n≈111.2, meaning that at least 112 samples were required for each of the frail group and the non-frail group, and the total sample size after matching was at least 224. Considering that some subjects might fail to be successfully matched with controls due to factors such as caliper value setting and covariate imbalance during the PSM process, resulting in sample loss, we preset a matching success rate of approximately 70%. Therefore, before matching, this study planned to include at least 160 frail elderly. Meanwhile, to ensure a sufficient control pool for 1:1 matching, the number of non-frail elderly to be included was approximately three times that of the frail group, which is 480. In summary, the total sample size before matching in this study was approximately 640. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (No. KLL2022-814).

Assessment tools

Sociodemographic characteristics

Self-designed by researchers, it includes gender, age, educational level, marital status, place of residence, living arrangement, per capita monthly household income, alcohol consumption, smoking status, participation in social activities, exercise habits, and self-rated health status.

Instrumental activities of daily living (IADL)

IADL was measured by Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale, which developed by Lawton et al.18, the scale comprises 8 items: using the telephone, shopping, meal preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transportation, medication management, and financial management. Each item is scored 1 (independent completion) or 0 (dependence), with a total score ranging from 0 to 8. A full score indicates independent instrumental self-care ability; lower scores reflect impaired ability. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.908.

Depression

Depression was measured by Short Form Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), which derived from the 30-item original version by Sheikh and Yesavage19 in 1986, this abbreviated scale has been widely used internationally. Its Chinese version demonstrated good reliability and validity among urban and rural elderly in China, as validated by Tang et al.20 in 2013, making it suitable for assessing depressive symptoms in Chinese older adults. The 15-item scale uses “Yes/No” responses, scored 1/0 respectively, with total scores ranging 0–15 (higher scores indicating more severe depression). A total score > 4 indicates depression, while a score ≤ 4 indicates no depression. In this study, its Cronbach’s α was 0.788.

Anxiety

Anxiety was measured by Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), which developed by Prof. W.K. Zung21, the SAS consists of 20 items scored 1–4. The raw score (sum of items) is multiplied by 1.25 and rounded to an integer. A total score < 50 indicates normal, while a total score ≥ 50 indicates anxiety. In this study, its Cronbach’s α was 0.841.

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was measured by Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which developed by Dr. Buysse et al.22 from the University of Pittsburgh in 1989, the PSQI comprises 18 items grouped into 7 components. The total score (sum of components) ranges 0–21. Scores were categorized as: 0–5 indicates good sleep quality; 6–21 indicates poor sleep quality. In this study, its Cronbach’s α was 0.765.

Frailty

Frailty was measured by the frail scale, which was recommended in Chinese 2017 Frailty Guidelines23 for assessing geriatric frailty, this scale includes 5 items: fatigue (frequent tiredness), stair climbing (ability to climb 1 flight unassisted), walking (ability to walk 100 m unassisted), comorbidities (≥ 5 chronic diseases), and weight loss (≥ 5% body weight loss in the past year). Each item scores 1 point (total 0–5). Frailty is classified as: none (0), pre-frailty (1–2), and frailty (≥ 3). In this study, the state of frailty was classified into frail and non-frail (including no frailty and pre-frailty).

Fall risk

Fall risk was measured by Chinese version of the self-related fall risk questionnaire, which originally developed by Rubenstein et al.24 and cross-culturally adapted into Chinese by Su Qingqing et al.25, this 12-item scale uses binary scoring: “Yes” earns points, “No” scores 0. Items 1 (“Fallen in the past year”) and 2 (“Uses/was advised to use a cane/walker for safety”) score 2 points each; others score 1 point. Total scores range 0–14, with ≥ 4 indicating fall risk (higher scores = greater risk). In this study, its Cronbach’s α was 0.846.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2). Categorical variables were described as n (%) and compared using the Chi-squared test. To minimize the confounding effects caused by baseline differences between the frail and non-frail groups, 1:1 nearest neighbor PSM was performed, the caliper value set at 0.05 and a random seed of 1357. The exposure variable was frailty status (frail vs. non-frail), and the covariates included in the propensity score calculation were sex, age, educational level, marital status, place of residence, living arrangement, monthly per capita household income, alcohol consumption, smoking status, social activity participation, and physical exercise. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate the balance of all included covariates before and after PSM, with an SMD < 0.10 indicating a well-balanced baseline, an SMD > 0.2 indicates a significant difference between groups and an imbalance.

After PSM, conditional logistic regression analysis was further conducted to quantify the independent association between frailty and fall risk. In this model, the adjusted covariates included health status variables closely related to frailty and fall risk (IADL impairment, self-rated health, depression, anxiety, sleep quality). The odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated to reflect the strength of the independent association between frailty and fall risk. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

PSM results between frail and non-frail groups

Before PSM, significant intergroup differences were observed between the frail and non-frail groups in age, place of residence, alcohol consumption, social activity participation, and physical exercise (all P < 0.001), indicating an unbalanced baseline distribution of these core sociodemographic and health lifestyle variables. In contrast, no significant differences were found in gender, educational level, marital status, living arrangement, and per capita monthly household income between the two groups before matching (all P > 0.05).

After performing 1:1 nearest neighbor PSM, a total of 470 matched participants were obtained, including 235 in the frail group and 235 in the non-frail group. For all the variables included in the matching model, the results of chi-square test showed no significant differences between the two groups (all P > 0.05). The standardized mean difference (SMD) of all variables was less than 0.15 after matching, most variables are below 0.1 in addition to marital status (SMD = 0.130) and living arrangement (SMD = 0.118), suggesting that the baseline characteristics of the frail and non-frail groups were well balanced. Conditional logistic regression analysis of the association between frailty and fall risk

Conditional logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore the independent association between frailty and fall risk, with fall risk as the dependent variable and frailty status as the independent variable. IADL impairment, self-rated health, depression, anxiety, and sleep quality were included as covariates in the model for adjustment.

After adjusting for all aforementioned covariates, frailty was significantly associated with fall risk (P < 0.001), with frail participants showing a 5.21-fold higher likelihood of high fall risk than non-frail individuals (OR = 5.21, 95%CI = 2.66–10.17).

Discussion

This study focuses on the specific group of elderly individuals with KOA in the community, as there is a relative scarcity of research on the current status of frailty and its association with fall risk in this population. Through investigation, it was found that the incidence rates of frailty and fall risk among community-dwelling elderly with KOA are relatively high. Meanwhile, using 1:1 nearest-neighbor PSM to balance sociodemographic characteristics and health-related lifestyle factors, this study further adopted conditional logistic regression analysis to adjust for potential residual confounding from health status variables, and explored the relationship between frailty status and fall risk in community-dwelling elderly with KOA. The results revealed that frailty was independently associated with a higher risk of falls in this population. Specifically, frail older adults had a high-risk fall assessment score 5.21 times that of non-frail individuals (95% CI 2.66–10.17, P < 0.001). Additionally, this study found that IADL impairment and poor self-rated health were also significantly associated with increased fall risk in community-dwelling elderly KOA patients.

Frailty status in community-dwelling elderly with KOA

In this study, frailty incidence was 25.25% and pre-frailty 31.14%. This aligns with Nicola Veronese et al.’s meta-analysis26 of 57 studies using multidimensional indices, which reported 26.8% frailty and 36.4% pre-frailty. However, it differs from Rónán O’Caoimh et al.’s27 pooled analysis of 240 studies across 62 regions, showing lower frailty (12%) but higher pre-frailty (46%). Discrepancies may stem from differing assessment tools (multidimensional indices only vs. all measurable tools) and populations (inpatients/nursing home residents vs. community-dwellers aged ≥ 50). Studies indicate disease-specific frailty rates in hospitalized elderly, with respiratory disease patients having the highest (59.6%) and cardiovascular patients the lowest (12.4%). Osteoarthritis patients exhibit elevated IL-6 and CRP, potentially inducing frailty via chronic inflammation-driven muscle catabolism28. A Chinese study29 reported higher frailty (50.9%) and pre-frailty (38.8%) in elderly arthritis inpatients, possibly due to acute disease phases. Overall, the elderly have high frailty rates, and KOA patients show relatively high frailty regardless of acute episodes, highlighting critical challenges in their health management and disease prevention.

High fall incidence in community-dwelling elderly with KOA

KOA patients face increased fall risk due to pain, joint stiffness, muscle weakness, and long-term medication30. In this study, 51.72% of elderly KOA patients were at fall risk. A study on Chinese elderly aged 60–79 reported a much lower high-risk rate of 10.5%—possibly because it excluded those ≥ 80 and included healthy elderly with better self-care and motor skills, unlike our KOA subjects. A retrospective study31 reported a 27.77% fall incidence and 9.88% recurrent fall rate among elderly patients with osteoarthritis, indicating a remarkably high and non-negligible risk in this population, which is consistent with our findings.

Frailty was an independent factor associated with high risk of falls among older adults with KOA

Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between frailty and fall risk in older adults32–35, however, the reported strength of this association varies considerably across studies, likely due to differences in study populations, frailty assessment tools, and analytical approaches. A study on Chinese community elderly36 found an 8.52-fold higher risk in frail individuals, possibly because it failed to eliminate baseline group differences despite stratified logistic regression adjustment. In contrast, our study adopted 1:1 nearest-neighbor PSM to balance sociodemographic and health-related lifestyle factors between groups, with all matched variables showing SMD < 0.15 (a clinically and statistically acceptable balance threshold) and no significant intergroup differences (P > 0.05). This PSM design, which simulates randomized controlled trials, effectively eliminated baseline confounding bias. Furthermore, we used conditional logistic regression to further adjust for residual confounding from IADL impairment, self-rated health and other health status variables, thus maximizing the control of potential confounders and significantly enhancing the reliability of the observed independent association and its clinical relevance. Teng L et al.36 noted higher fall risk in frail elderly with hypertension vs. without. Frailty correlates with higher fall risk in elderly type 2 diabetes patients37. Our study found that frailty is significantly associated with fall risk in elderly patients with KOA, indicating that the association between frailty and falls varies considerably across chronic disease conditions.

IADL impairment, and poor self-rated health are also associated with increased fall risk in community-dwelling elderly with KOA

For IADL impairment, the risk of having a high-risk fall assessment score in KOA elderly patients with IADL impairment was 4.74 times that of those without (OR = 4.74, 95%CI 1.37–16.43, P = 0.014). As a sensitive indicator of functional decline in the elderly, IADL impairment can reflect the decline of comprehensive abilities such as daily living operation, mobility and balance in advance compared with basic ADL. For elderly KOA patients, joint pain, stiffness and motor dysfunction caused by the disease itself will lead to the decline of IADL, and long-term inactivity and reduced exercise due to functional decline will further aggravate muscle atrophy and balance disorder, significantly increase the risk of falls38,39.

The risk of having a high-risk fall assessment score in KOA elderly patients with poor self-rated health was 7.38 times that of those with good self-rated health (OR = 7.38, 95%CI 1.20–45.46, P = 0.031). Poor self-rated health may capture a state of low psychological resilience in patients with KOA. Individuals with low psychological resilience are more prone to fear of falling and activity avoidance behaviors, which can lead to muscle atrophy and further functional decline, ultimately substantially increases the risk of falls40. Furthermore, the longitudinal study by Papp et al.41 demonstrated that older women with low levels of physical activity experienced a significant decline in balance performance over an 8.5 years follow-up period, and their self-rated health scores remained consistently lower than those of their highly active counterparts. These findings suggest that KOA patients with poor self-rated health may reduce their daily physical activity due to pain and fare, subsequently leading to decreased lower limb muscle strength, impaired balance function, and ultimately an elevated risk of falling.

Notably, this study found no statistically significant association between depression, anxiety, sleep quality and fall risk in KOA elderly patients (P > 0.05). This may be attributable to the rigorous PSM and conditional logistic regression adjustments employed, which potentially mitigated the confounding effects of other variables, thereby offsetting the otherwise weak associations between these psychological and sleep-related factors and fall risk.

Limitations

This study still has certain limitations. Firstly, since acute inflammation and pain may lead to frailty and increased fall risk by reducing activity ability and muscle regulation capacity, this study did not distinguish whether the KOA in the elderly was in the acute exacerbation stage during data collection and analysis. Future studies could explore whether acute episodes of KOA exacerbate the association between frailty and fall risk in the elderly. Secondly, the FRAIL scale is a brief screening tool that does not capture the multidimensional nature of frailty, and both KOA status and fall risk were based on self-report, which may introduce reporting bias. Additionally, despite the use of PSM to adjust for observed confounders, residual confounding and unmeasured confounding factors may still exist. Future studies should consider using standardized diagnostic criteria for KOA, comprehensive frailty assessment tools, objective fall measurements, and more comprehensive covariate collection. Thirdly, this study adopted convenient sampling, and all the participants were elderly people from communities in Zunyi City, China. Future multi-center studies across different regions can be conducted to verify the universality of the observed relationships.​

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of frailty and elevated fall risk among community-dwelling elderly patients with KOA. After adjusting for confounding by PSM and further controlling for health-related covariates using conditional logistic regression, frailty was independently and significantly associated with a higher fall risk in this population. These findings support the integration of routine frailty screening into the regular health management of elderly patients with KOA in primary care settings. Frailty is independently associated with higher fall risk in this population, which can provide an evidence‑based reference for fall risk assessment and stratified management of community‑dwelling older adults with KOA.
