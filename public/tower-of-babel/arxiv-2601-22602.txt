A spectral approach for online covariance change point detection
Zhigang Bao, Kha Man Cheong, Yuji Li, Jiaxin Qiu

Abstract
Change point detection in covariance structures is a fundamental and crucial problem for sequential data. Under the high-dimensional setting, most of the existing research has focused on identifying change points in historical data. However, there is a significant lack of studies on the practically relevant online change point problem, which means promptly detecting change points as they occur. In this paper, applying the limiting theory of linear spectral statistics for random matrices, we propose a class of spectrum based CUSUM-type statistic. We first construct a martingale from the difference of linear spectral statistics of sequential sample Fisher matrices, which converges to a Brownian motion. Our CUSUM-type statistic is then defined as the maximum of a variant of this process. Finally, we develop our detection procedure based on the invariance principle. Simulation results show that our detection method is highly sensitive to the occurrence of change point and is able to identify it shortly after they arise, outperforming the existing approaches.

Introduction

Online change point detection plays a pivotal role in statistical process control and time series analysis.
The field has a rich history, rooted in the classical works on Page’s cumulative sum (CUSUM) procedure
[
46
]
and Shiryaev-Roberts procedure
[
50
;
48
]
.
For a detailed exposition of the methodology and a survey of recent developments, we refer readers to
3
and the references therein.
While classical theories are well-established, monitoring the covariance structure in high-dimensional settings remains a critical yet challenging task, as it captures the dynamic dependencies among a large number of variables.
Motivated by this, we consider the problem of online change point detection for the covariance structure of high-dimensional random vectors.
Specifically, we observe a sequence of
p
-dimensional random vectors
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, modeled as
y_{i}=\Sigma_{i}^{1/2}x_{i}
.
Here,
\Sigma_{i}
is a deterministic
p\times p
symmetric positive definite matrix with uniformly bounded spectral norms, and the entries of
x_{i}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with zero mean and unit variance. More generally, we can potentially consider the model
y_{i}=T_{i}x_{i}
, where
T_{i}
satisfies
\Sigma_{i}=T_{i}T_{i}^{*}
. But for the sake of technical conciseness, we work with the mentioned setting.
Our objective is to sequentially monitor the stream and detect whether the covariance structure
\Sigma_{i}
changes at some unknown time instant.
Formally, we test the following hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0}:\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{0}\quad\forall i\geq 1\quad\text{vs}\quad H_{1}:\exists k^{\star}\text{ s.t. }\begin{cases}\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{0},&1\leq i\leq k^{\star},\\
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{1}\neq\Sigma_{0},&i>k^{\star}.\end{cases}

Here,
k^{\star}
denotes the change point location.
Our goal is to detect the change as promptly after the change point
k^{\star}
while controlling the false alarm rate under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
.

The problem of detecting structural changes in covariance matrices has been extensively investigated, primarily in the offline setting where the complete data sequence is available for analysis. In the classical fixed-dimensional regime, a multitude of methods have been developed based on likelihood ratios and CUSUM; see, for example,
15
,
38
,
30
,
1
,
61
,
24
,
For a comprehensive overview of these classical approaches, we refer readers to
20
,
16
,
2
and the references therein.
More recently, there has been a surge of literature addressing high-dimensional offline covariance change-point detection from various perspectives, including projection based technique
[
52
]
, binary segmentation algorithm
[
54
;
40
]
,
U
-statistics based method
[
42
;
21
]
, graph-based method
[
14
;
18
]
, dimension reduction technique
[
22
]
, self-normalization principle
[
13
;
58
]
, and spectral method
[
49
;
27
;
29

.

In contrast, online monitoring for high-dimensional covariance structures is still relatively limited in the literature.
Unlike offline methods that utilize the full data set to estimate the change point, online procedures must make decisions in real-time with limited historical data and no knowledge of the future.
This constraint requires statistics that are recursive, computationally efficient, and able to control false alarm rates.

4
and
5
investigated the problem of change point detection for high-dimensional precision and covariance matrices within a multiple-testing framework.
Their procedure constructs a collection of local window-based statistics based on
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, with critical values determined via high-dimensional Gaussian approximation and bootstrap techniques.
Addressing a specific structural change,
60
focused on sequentially detecting deviations from an identity matrix toward an unknown low-rank (spiked) covariance model.
To this end, they proposed two online detection procedures: a Largest-Eigenvalue Shewhart chart and a Subspace-CUSUM procedure.

39
studied the online change point detection of high-dimensional covariance matrices utilizing a window-based
U
-statistic coupled with a sequential stopping rule.
A key advantage of their method is that it admits explicit control of the Average Run Length (ARL) while accommodating non-Gaussian data with spatial and temporal dependence.
Most recently,
31
proposed a framework for the online detection of changes in the correlation structure of high-dimensional streaming data.
Their approach leverages
\ell_{2}
- and
\ell_{\infty}
-norm based statistics, employing a sign-flip permutation method to determine adaptive detection thresholds.

In this work, we take a different perspective rooted in Random Matrix Theory. Specifically, we construct a sequential monitoring procedure based on the linear spectral statistics of the Fisher matrix, which offers a powerful tool for distinguishing the null and alternative hypotheses in high-dimensional settings.
Suppose that at the current time
k
, we have observed the sequence
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
.
To detect potential changes, we compare the covariance structures of two adjacent segments partitioned by an index
k_{1}
, where
1<k_{1}<k
.
We define the reference sample covariance matrix
S_{1}
and the monitoring sample covariance matrix
S_{2,k}
as follows:

S_{1}=\frac{1}{k_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}y_{i}y_{i}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}},\qquad S_{2,k}=\frac{1}{k-k_{1}}\sum_{i=k_{1}+1}^{k}y_{i}y_{i}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}.

We then construct the multivariate
F
-matrix
(or
Fisher matrix
) defined as
F_{k}=S_{1}^{-1}S_{2,k}
,
and consider its
linear spectral statistic
(LSS)

\Tr f(F_{k})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{p}f\bigl(\lambda_{i}(F_{k})\bigr),

where
\lambda_{1}(F_{k})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}(F_{k})
denote the ordered eigenvalues of
F_{k}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a suitable test function.
The spectral properties of the
F
-matrix have been extensively investigated within the framework of random matrix theory.
The Limiting Spectral Distribution (LSD) was established in the foundational works of
6
and
7
, and subsequently extended by
51
and
65
.
Furthermore, the fluctuations of its LSS have been derived under various settings; see, for instance,
68
and
67
.
Moreover, the asymptotic behavior of the largest eigenvalue has attracted considerable attention. Relevant results include
33
;
32
and
56

37
;
59
;
35
;
57
and
36
.

The core of our methodology relies on monitoring the incremental differences of the LSS. To this end, we define the standardized increment

\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)\coloneqq\frac{\Tr f(F_{k})-\Tr f(F_{k-1})-\mu_{k}}{\sigma_{k}},

where
\mu_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
represent the mean and standard deviation of the one-step difference
\Tr f(F_{k})-\Tr f(F_{k-1})
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. The explicit forms of
\mu_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
will be provided in Proposition
2.1
.
Under
H_{0}
of no change point, the expectation of
\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)
is asymptotically negligible.
Once a structural change occurs at some unknown time
k^{\star}
, the expectation of
\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)
deviates systematically from zero.
To effectively accumulate the signal from such potentially subtle drifts, we further construct a normalized CUSUM statistic
T_{p}(n,i)
(see definition in (
2.6
)) based on the sequence
\{\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)\}
.
Under
H_{0}
, the process
\{T_{p}(n,i)\}
converges in distribution to a Brownian motion type process. Such convergence is based on the standardization in (
1
), which makes the increment behave as a normalized martingale difference; thus, the CUSUM statistic behaves as a random walk.
When a change point exists (
H_{1}
), the mean shift in the LSS increments will cause
T_{p}(n,i)
to deviate significantly from zero, thus triggering detection.
Accordingly, we define the
stopping rule

\widehat{k}^{\star}=\inf\{i:|T_{p}(n,i)|>c_{\alpha}\},

where the threshold
c_{\alpha}
is chosen to control the false alarm rate at a prescribed level
\alpha
. The value of
c_{\alpha}
is determined by the quantile of the limiting null distribution of the process
\{T_{p}(n,i)\}
.

2
Main Results

2.1
One-step LSS difference

In this section, we present our main theoretical results concerning the one-step LSS difference of the Fisher matrix.
These results form the foundation for our online change point detection procedure.
To facilitate the analysis, we first impose the following assumptions.

Assumption A

(Data generating model)
.

For any
i\geq 1
, let
y_{i}=\Sigma_{i}^{1/2}x_{i}
, where
\{\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are deterministic
p\times p
positive-definite symmetric matrix with uniformly bounded spectral norms. The vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d. with
x_{i}=(x_{ij})_{j=1}^{p}
, satisfying
\E x_{11}=0
,
\E x_{11}^{2}=1
, and
\E x_{11}^{4+\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta>0
.

Assumption B

(High-dimensional scaling)
.

The sample sizes
k_{1},k
and the dimension
p
satisfy

\frac{p}{k_{1}}\to c_{1}\in(0,1)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{p}{k-k_{1}}\to c_{2}\in(0,+\infty),

as
p,k,k_{1}\to\infty
.

Let
\{y_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of
p
-dimensional random vectors satisfying Assumptions
A
and
B
, with
\Sigma_{i}=I_{p}
for all
i\geq 1
under
H_{0}
.
We define the two sample covariance matrices as

S_{1}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}y_{i}y_{i}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}},\quad S_{2,k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k-k_{1}}\sum_{i=k_{1}+1}^{k}y_{i}y_{i}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}.

The corresponding
F
-matrix is given by

F_{k}\coloneqq S_{1}^{-1}S_{2,k}.

From
[
6
;
7
]
, the LSD of
F_{k}
is the distribution with the density function given by

f_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x)=\frac{(1-c_{1})\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}{2\pi x(c_{1}x+c_{2})},\quad a\leq x\leq b,

where

a\coloneqq\frac{(1-h)^{2}}{(1-c_{1})^{2}},\quad b\coloneqq\frac{(1+h)^{2}}{(1-c_{1})^{2}},\quad h\coloneqq\sqrt{c_{1}+c_{2}-c_{1}c_{2}}.

The Stieltjes transform of the LSD is

\displaystyle m(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-c_{2}}{zc_{2}}-\frac{c_{2}\{z(1-c_{1})+1-c_{2}\}+2zc_{1}-c_{2}(1-c_{1})\sqrt{(z-b)(z-a)}}{2zc_{2}(c_{2}+zc_{1})},

where
z
is any complex number with positive imaginary part.
The non-zero eigenvalues of
S_{1}^{-1}S_{2}
and its companion matrix
\mathscr{F}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k-k_{1}}X_{2}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}S_{1}^{-1}X_{2}
are the same, where
X_{2}=(x_{k_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{k})
is the
p\times(k-k_{1})
data matrix formed by the second sample. The Stieltjes transform of
\mathscr{F}_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\underline{m}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-c_{2}}{z}+c_{2}m(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{2}\{z(1-c_{1})+1-c_{2}\}+2zc_{1}-c_{2}(1-c_{1})\sqrt{(z-b)(z-a)}}{2z(c_{2}+zc_{1})}.

(2.1)

For any test function
f
, the
linear spectral statistic
(LSS) of
F_{k}
is defined as

\Tr f(F_{k})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i}(F_{k})).

Let
L_{k}(f)\coloneqq\Tr f(F_{k})-\Tr f(F_{k-1})
be the
one-step difference
of LSS.
By using the Cauchy integral formula, we have for analytic
f
,

\displaystyle L_{k}(f)=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)\underbrace{\bigl\{\Tr G_{k}(z)-\Tr G_{k-1}(z)\bigr\}}_{M_{k}(z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,

(2.2)

where
G_{k}(z)\coloneqq(F_{k}-z)^{-1}
is the
resolvent
(or
Green function
) of
F_{k}
, and the contour
\Gamma
is taken in the anticlockwise direction enclosing the support
[a,b]
of the LSD of
F_{k}
.

For simplicity, we denote
k_{2}\coloneqq k-1-k_{1}
in the following. The following proposition provides the explicit expressions of the mean and variance of the one-step difference
L_{k}(f)
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
A
and
B
hold.
Under
H_{0}
, for any analytic function
f
, the mean and variance functions of
L_{k}(f)
are given by

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(f)\coloneqq\E(L_{k}(f))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\biggl(f^{\prime}(z)z\underline{m}-f(z)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}}{\underline{m}}\biggr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z+\frac{\nu_{4}-3}{4p\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f^{\prime}(z)(1+z\underline{m})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2k_{2}\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\biggl(-\frac{\{zf(z)\}^{\prime\prime}z\underline{m}}{2}+zf^{\prime}(z)\frac{\underline{m}^{\prime}}{\underline{m}}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\underline{m}}\biggr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z+\mathfrak{o}\biggl(\frac{1}{p}\biggr),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}(f)\coloneqq\var(L_{k}(f))

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{4}-3}{k_{2}c_{2}}\biggl(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f^{\prime}(z)z\underline{m}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\biggr)^{2}+\frac{1}{k_{2}\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}\frac{\{f^{\prime}(z)\}^{2}}{\underline{m}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z+\mathfrak{o}\biggl(\frac{1}{p}\biggr).

(2.4)

The proof of Proposition
2.1
is given in Section
B.2
.
We refer to Section
3
for explicit expressions of the mean and variance of
L_{k}(f)
for some commonly used test functions.
Based on Proposition
2.1
, we first define a random walk type process. For a fixed
T>0
,

\displaystyle W_{n,t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{L}_{k}(f),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)\coloneqq\frac{L_{k}(f)-\mu_{k}(f)}{\sigma_{k}(f)},

(2.5)

and
\mu_{k}(f),\sigma_{k}(f)
are defined in Proposition
2.1
.
This process is used in defining our monitoring statistics in Section
2.2
. The following theorem establish the weak convergence of the above process under the null hypothesis.

Theorem 2.2

(Weak Convergence of
W_{n,t}
under
H_{0}
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
A
and
B
hold.
Under the null hypothesis,
\{W_{n,t},t\in[0,T]\}
converges in distribution to a standard Brownian motion
\{W(t),t\in[0,T]\}
, i.e., with covariance kernel

\cov(W(t),W(s))=t\wedge s.

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is given in Section
5
.

While the result above characterizes the dynamic behavior of the LLS-based monitoring process, the asymptotic theory of LSS was primarily developed for static hypothesis testing.
In the offline setting, the LSS of large-dimensional sample covariance matrices has served as a cornerstone for hypothesis testing
[
9
]
.
Based on this theory, an extensive literature has emerged, offering rigorous procedures for testing covariance structures.
These methodologies encompass one-sample tests for identity or sphericity
[
8
;
55
;
66
;
41
;
44
;
47
;
63
;
43
]
, as well as two-sample tests assessing the equality of covariance matrices
[
67
;
62
;
64
;
69
;
26
]
.
However, despite these results in static regimes, extending LSS-based inference to the sequential monitoring domain presents distinct challenges.
We remark here that a stochastic process constructed from the sequential linear spectral statistics of the sequential sample covariance matrices has been considered in
28
, for which the limiting process is a non-standard Gaussian process. Here, instead, we construct the process as a random walk with the normalized increments, so that the limiting process is the standard Brownian motion. A major advantage of such construction is that the local envelope of the Brownian motion is well-understood, which will be important for the construction of our testing statistics in the sequel.

2.2
Online change point detection procedure

In this section, we introduce our online change point detection procedure, which leverages the asymptotic results of the one-step LSS difference established in Proposition
2.1
.
We assume an initial historical dataset of size
n=k_{1}+k_{2}^{\circ}
is available.
Specifically, the first
k_{1}
observations are utilized to construct the baseline covariance matrix
S_{1}
, while the subsequent
k_{2}^{\circ}
observations form the initial covariance matrix
S_{2,n}
.
The initial sample size
k_{2}^{\circ}
is predetermined to ensure that Assumption
B
is satisfied at
k=n
.
Following this initialization, the procedure proceeds to sequentially monitor the incoming data stream
\{y_{n+i}\}_{i\geq 1}
.

Motivated by the online CUSUM procedure proposed in
17
, we construct a monitoring statistic at time
n+i
(
i\geq 1
) based on the cumulative sum of the standardized one-step LSS differences defined in (
2.5
):

T_{p}(n,i)=w(n,i)|\Psi(n,i)|,

(2.6)

where
w(n,i)
is the weight function satisfying Assumption
C
below, and
\Psi(n,i)
is a CUSUM-type statistic defined as

\displaystyle\Psi(n,i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}(f),\quad n=k_{1}+k_{2}^{\circ},\quad i\geq 1,

(2.7)

and
\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)
is defined in (
2.5
).
At each time point
n+i
(
i\geq 1
), we update
T_{p}(n,i)
by including the new sample
y_{n+i}
.
An alarm is triggered immediately if the monitoring statistic
T_{p}(n,i)
exceeds the critical threshold
c_{\alpha}
; otherwise, the monitoring process continues.
Hence, the stopping time
\widehat{k}^{\star}
of the procedure is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{k}^{\star}=\inf\bigl\{i\geq 1:T_{p}(n,i)>c_{\alpha}\bigr\}

(2.8)

with the convention that
\inf(\varnothing)=\infty
.
The implementation details of the proposed method are summarized in Algorithm
1
.

In our online detection procedure, we aim to control the asymptotic false alarm rate at a nominal level
\alpha\in(0,1)
while ensuring that the detection power converges to one.
Formally, this entails selecting a critical value
c_{\alpha}
such that

\displaystyle\Prob_{H_{0}}(\widehat{k}^{\star}<\infty)\to\alpha,\quad\text{and}\quad\Prob_{H_{1}}(\widehat{k}^{\star}<\infty)\to 1,

where
\Prob_{H_{0}}
and
\Prob_{H_{1}}
denote the probability under the null and alternative hypothesis, respectively.
To achieve this goal, we need to impose the following regularity conditions on the weight function
w(n,i)
:

Assumption C

(Weight function)
.

Let the weight function satisfy

(i)

w(n,i)=\rho(i/n)\mathds{1}\{i>\ell_{n}\}
with
\ell_{n}/n\to 0
, where
\rho:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is nonnegative and continuous if restricted to
(0,e_{\rho}]\cap\mathbb{R}
,
e_{\rho}=\sup\{t>0:\rho(t)>0\}
;

(ii)

\lim_{t\to 0}t^{\gamma}\rho(t)<\infty
for some
0\leq\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
;

(iii)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\rho(t)<\infty
.

Condition
C(i)
mandates a burn-in period (determined by
\ell_{n}
) prior to the start of monitoring.
This delay is designed to mitigate false alarms during the early stages,
where the monitoring statistic is prone to instability due to the limited sample size.
Conditions
C(ii)
and
C(iii)
characterize the weight function’s asymptotic behavior to balance sensitivity and robustness: the former restricts the growth rate at
t\to 0
to mitigate excessive false alarms during the initial monitoring phase, while the latter prevents slow decay at infinity to ensure effective long-term monitoring.

In this paper, we set the burn-in period as
\ell_{n}=\log(n)
.
To evaluate the detection performance under different weighting schemes, we employ the following two weight functions:

\displaystyle\rho_{1,\gamma}(t)=(1+t)^{\gamma-1}t^{-\gamma},\quad 0\leq\gamma<\frac{1}{2},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(t)=(1+t)^{-1/2}\bigl\{-2\log\alpha+\log(1+t)\bigr\}^{-1/2}.

(2.10)

Both the weight family
\rho_{1,\gamma}
and the logarithmic weight
\rho_{2}
are adopted from
17
.
To visually demonstrate the behavior of the standardized LSS difference process
\Psi(n,\lfloor nt\rfloor)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)
and the corresponding detection boundaries implied by these weight functions, we present a simulation example in Figure
1
.
Under
H_{0}

Remark 2.4

Assumption D

(Change point)

The change point

\displaystyle I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f^{\prime}(z)\log\bigl\{\tau_{1}-(1-\tau_{1})z\underline{m}(z)\bigr\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,

Remark 2.6
.

We examine whether the contour integrals
I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})
and
I_{2}(f)

vanish or not in general.
Let

L_{1}(z)\coloneqq\log\bigl\{\tau_{1}-(1-\tau_{1})z\underline{m}(z)\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{2}(z)\coloneqq\log(z\underline{m})+z\underline{m}.

According to the Residue Theorem, these integrals are non-zero if the sum of the residues of the integrands at all poles in the region exterior to
\Gamma
is non-zero.
We consider two kinds of test functions based on the analytic structure of its derivative
f^{\prime}(z)
in the domain exterior to
\Gamma
:

•

Case A: polynomial functions.
Assume
f(z)
is a polynomial of degree
k
:
f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}c_{j}z^{j}
, where
k
is a finite integer.
Its derivative is a polynomial of degree
k-1
:

f^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}jc_{j}z^{j-1}

Since
f^{\prime}(z)
is entire, there are no finite poles in the exterior region. The integral is determined solely by the residue at infinity. To compute this, we consider the Laurent expansion of
L_{1}(z)
at infinity:

L_{1}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}}{z^{m}}=A_{0}+\frac{A_{1}}{z}+\frac{A_{2}}{z^{2}}+\dots+\frac{A_{k}}{z^{k}}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-(k+1)}).

The integrand is the product:

f^{\prime}(z)L_{1}(z)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}jc_{j}z^{j-1}\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}}{z^{m}}\right).

According to the Residue Theorem, we have

I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})=-\mathrm{Res}(f^{\prime}(z)L(z),\infty)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}jc_{j}A_{j}.

The integral is non-zero provided that the linear combination

\sum_{j=1}^{k}jc_{j}A_{j}\neq 0.

Specifically, if
f(z)=z
, we need
A_{1}\neq 0
.
If
f(z)=z^{2}
, we need
2A_{2}\neq 0
.
Note that

A_{1}=\frac{(1-\tau_{1})c_{2}}{1-c_{1}}
and

A_{2}=\frac{c_{2}(1-\tau_{1})\{2+c_{2}(1-c_{1})(1+\tau_{1})\}}{2(1-c_{1})^{3}}
.
Both cases hold under the condition
\tau_{1}\neq 1
.
Hence,
f(x)=x
and
x^{2}
are valid choice to detect the change point when
\tau_{1}\neq 1
.

Similarly, for the case
\tau_{1}=1
, we have the Laurent expansion of
L_{2}(z)
at infinity:

L_{2}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}}{z^{m}}=B_{0}+\frac{B_{1}}{z}+\frac{B_{2}}{z^{2}}+\dots+\frac{B_{k}}{z^{k}}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-(k+1)}).

The integral
I_{2}(f)
is non-zero provided that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}jc_{j}B_{j}\neq 0.

Note that
B_{1}=0
and
B_{2}=-\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{2(1-c_{1})^{2}}
.
Thus,
f(x)
is not a valid choice to detect the change point when
\tau_{1}=1
.
However,
f(x)=x^{2}
is a valid choice since
2B_{2}\neq 0
.

•

Case B: function with singularities.
Assume
f(z)
possesses singularities (e.g., logarithmic functions like
\log(1+z)
). In this scenario, the derivative
f^{\prime}(z)
may exhibit isolated poles
z_{k}
in the region exterior to
\Gamma
. The integral is non-zero if the sum of residues at these poles is non-zero.

For both integrals, we consider the specific example
f(z)=\log(1+z)
. Using the Residue Theorem, we have

I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})=-\log\{\tau_{1}+(1-\tau_{1})m(-1)\}.

This integral is non-zero as long as
\underline{m}(-1)\neq 1
. It follows from that
\underline{m}(-1)=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{x+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}F(\lambda)<1
, where
F(x)
is the LSD of the companion matrix
\mathscr{F}_{k}
. Hence,
f(z)=\log(1+z)
is a valid choice to detect the change point when
\tau_{1}\neq 1
.

Similarly, for the case
\tau_{1}=1
, we have

I_{2}(f)=-\bigl\{\log\{\underline{m}(-1)\}-m(-1)+1\bigr\}.

This integral is non-zero as long as
\underline{m}(-1)\neq 1
, which is verified above.
Hence,
f(z)=\log(1+z)
is still a valid choice to detect the change point when
\tau_{1}=1
.

\displaystyle\text{Empirical Size/Power}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{k}_{\ell}^{\star}<\infty\}}{M},\qquad\text{EDD}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\widehat{k}_{\ell}^{\star}-k^{\star})_{+}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{k}_{\ell}^{\star}\geq k^{\star}\}},

where
\widehat{k}_{\ell}^{\star}
is the stopping time (see (
2.8
)) in the
\ell
-th replication.
The former measures the probability of successful detection within the monitoring horizon,
while the latter quantifies the average number of observations required after the true change point to trigger an alarm.

Table
2
reports the empirical sizes under different combinations of data distributions, weight functions, and test functions at the nominal level
\alpha=0.05
.
The results indicate that the choice of weight and test function impacts the empirical size control.
Specifically, the weight functions
\rho_{1,\gamma}
with larger values of
\gamma
tend to produce slightly inflated sizes, particularly when paired with the
square
test function.
In contrast, the weight
\rho_{2}
leads to conservative behavior with empirical sizes frequently below the nominal level.
Regarding test functions, the
log
,
linear
and
mix
generally provide more reliable size control than the
square
function, which leads to over-rejection in many settings.
Overall, the combination of the
\rho_{1,0}
weight with the
log
,
linear
, or
mix
test functions consistently achieves empirical sizes close to the nominal level across all distributions.
The empirical size behavior reported in Table
2
can be partly explained by the boundary geometry illustrated in Figure
1
.
For the
\rho_{1,\gamma}
family, increasing
\gamma
causes the boundary
c_{\alpha}\rho_{1,\gamma}^{-1}
to narrow around the monitoring process trajectories in the initial phase, making false alarms more probable and leading to slight size inflation.
Conversely, the logarithmic weight
\rho_{2}
generates a significantly wider boundary that stays well clear of the trajectories, which decreases spurious detections and yields conservative empirical sizes.

A Real Data Example

In this section, we demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed online covariance change point detection method through an analysis of the S&P 500 stock data.
Specifically, the covariance structure characterizes the dynamic comovement and risk integration among assets; detecting its abrupt changes is crucial for identifying market regime shifts and adjusting risk management strategies accordingly.
The dataset consists of historical daily adjusted closing prices for the constituents of the S&P 500 index, obtained from Yahoo Finance
2
2

2

https://finance.yahoo.com/
.
We utilized adjusted closing prices to account for corporate actions such as dividends and stock splits.
To ensure data quality, we excluded stocks with a missing data rate exceeding
5\%
and imputed minor gaps in the price series using the forward-filling method.
After preprocessing, we obtain daily adjusted closing prices for
496
stocks spanning
756
trading days.
Daily log-returns are computed as
y_{k}=\log(P_{k}/P_{k-1})
, where
P_{k}
denotes the adjusted closing price on day
k
.
To mitigate the impact of extreme outliers or data errors, we applied a Winsorization technique to the computed log-returns, clamping values exceeding 5 standard deviations from the mean.
To capture the most significant market fluctuations, we select a subset of the top
30
stocks with the highest volatility (defined as the standard deviation of daily log-returns) from the pool of
497
stocks.

We apply the proposed online monitoring procedure (Algorithm
1
) to the log-return series, utilizing the
\log(1+x)
test function and the
\rho_{1,0}
weight function.
The historical window sizes are set to
k_{1}=k_{2}^{\circ}=40
, and the monitoring phase commences at
k=81
(corresponding to December 26, 2019).
The nominal significance level is set to
\alpha=0.05
.
The procedure detects a change point on February 10, 2020.
This detection serves as a early warning signal preceding the major COVID-19 market crash.
It captures the initial structural change in the covariance matrix as investors began pricing in the systemic risk of the coronavirus outbreak, notably weeks before the widespread panic selling occurred in late February.
Figure
6
compares the sample covariance matrices before and after this detected change.
It is evident that the covariance structure undergoes a drastic shift, with the post-change period characterized by universally larger entries corresponding to the onset of widespread market stress.

Figure 6
:
Heatmaps of the sample covariance matrices for the top 30 S&P 500 stocks. The left panel represents the covariance structure before the detected change point (
\widehat{k}^{\star}=
February 10, 2020), while the right panel corresponds to the post-change period. The increased color intensity in the right panel illustrates the surge in volatility and correlation triggered by the onset of the COVID-19 market crash.

Proof of Theorem
2.2

To prove the weak convergence of the process
\{W_{n,t},t\in[0,T]\}
, we only need to show to the
finite dimensional distribution convergence
and the
tightness
of
\{W_{n,t},t\in[0,T]\}
(see e.g.
12
). Therefore, Theorem
2.2
follows immediately from the following two lemmas.

Lemma 5.1

(Finite-dimensional distribution)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
A
and
B
hold.
Under the null hypothesis, for any fixed
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{r}\in[0,T]
, the random vector
(W_{n,t_{1}},\ldots,W_{n,t_{r}})
converges in distribution to a
r
-dimensional Gaussian distribution with mean zero and covariance matrix
\Sigma=(\sigma_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
, where
\sigma_{ij}=t_{i}\wedge t_{j}
.

Lemma 5.2

(Tightness)
.

The sequence
\{W_{n,t},t\in[0,T]\}
is asymptotically tight in the space
\ell^{\infty}([0,T])
.

The proof of Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
are given in Section
5.1
and Section
5.2
respectively.

5.1
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Recall that

\displaystyle W_{n,t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{L}_{k}(f),\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

By the Cramér-Wold device, it suffices to show that, for any fixed
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle a_{1}W_{n,t_{1}}+a_{2}W_{n,t_{2}}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}N(0,\tau^{2}_{1,2}),

where
\tau_{1,2}^{2}\coloneqq a_{1}^{2}t_{1}+a_{2}^{2}t_{2}+2a_{1}a_{2}(t_{1}\wedge t_{2})
, and the extension to more points is straightforward.
WLOG, we assume that
T\geq t_{1}>t_{2}\geq 0
. Note that

\displaystyle a_{1}W_{n,t_{1}}+a_{2}W_{n,t_{2}}=\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}b_{k}(L_{k}-\mu_{k}),

where

b_{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{k}},&n+1\leq k\leq n+\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor,\\
\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}\sigma_{k}},&n+\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor+1\leq k\leq n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor.\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{n+k}
.
Denote
\E_{k}\coloneqq\E(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{k})
.
From the definition of
L_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{k}-\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)(1-\E)Y_{k}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z+\mathcal{O}(1/p),

where

Y_{k}(z)\coloneqq(\E_{k}-\E_{k-1})\frac{x_{k}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}A_{k}(z)x_{k}}{k_{2}}

forms a martingale difference sequence, and
A_{k}(z)
is defined as

\displaystyle A_{k}(z)\coloneqq\alpha(z)G^{2}S_{1}^{-1}+\alpha^{\prime}(z)GS_{1}^{-1},\quad\alpha(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}\Tr(GS_{1}^{-1})}.

The detailed calculation about the mean structure
L_{k}
can be found in Section
B.4
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle a_{1}W_{n,t_{1}}+a_{2}W_{n,t_{2}}=\frac{1-\E}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,\qquad S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}b_{k}Y_{k}(z).

By functional CLT and Lemma
B.1
, it suffices to show that
\{S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\}
converges weakly to a Gaussian process
S(z)
with covariance function

\displaystyle\cov(S(z_{1}),S(z_{2}))=\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}b_{k}^{2}\biggl\{\frac{\nu_{4}-3}{k_{2}c_{2}}\partial_{z_{1}}\bigl(z_{1}\underline{m}_{1}^{\prime}\bigr)\partial_{z_{2}}\bigl(z_{2}\underline{m}_{2}^{\prime}\bigr)-\frac{2}{k_{2}}\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{2}}\biggl(\frac{1/\underline{m}_{1}-1/\underline{m}_{2}}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\biggr)\biggr\}.

(5.1)

Using this formula, we conclude that
a_{1}W_{n,t_{1}}+a_{2}W_{n,t_{2}}
converges in distribution to a centered normal distribution with variance

\displaystyle\var(a_{1}W_{n,t_{1}}+a_{2}W_{n,t_{2}})=\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor}\frac{(a_{1}+a_{2})^{2}}{m}+\sum_{k=n+\lfloor nt_{2}\rfloor+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{m}=\tau_{1,2}^{2}.

Now, prove the weak convergence of
\{S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\}
.
First, we derive the finite-dimensional distribution of
S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)
.
From the martingale CLT it suffices to verify that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}\E\bigl\{b_{k}^{2}Y_{k}(z_{1})Y_{k}(z_{2})|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\bigr\}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{p}}\eqref{eq:cov_Sz1_Sz2},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}\E\bigl\{b_{k}^{2}Y_{k}^{2}(z)\mathds{1}_{\{|b_{k}Y_{k}(z)|>\varepsilon\}}\bigr\}\to 0,\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

The first condition can be verified by using Lemmas
B.1
and
B.2
.
We consider the second condition.
For any small
\delta>0
, using Lemma B.26 in
10
, we have the following bound

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}\E\bigl\{b_{k}^{2}Y_{k}^{2}(z)\mathds{1}_{\{|b_{k}Y_{k}(z)|>\varepsilon\}}\bigr\}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\delta/2}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}b_{k}^{2+\delta/2}\E|Y_{k}|^{2+\delta/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\;\frac{\max_{k}b_{k}^{2+\delta/2}}{k_{2}^{2+\delta/2}\varepsilon^{\delta/2}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}\Bigl[\E\bigl\{\nu_{4}\Tr\bigl(A_{k}(z)A_{k}(\bar{z})\bigr)\bigr\}^{1+\delta/4}+\nu_{4+\delta}\E\Tr\bigl\{A_{k}(z)A_{k}(\bar{z})\bigr\}^{1+\delta/4}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{k^{2+\delta/2}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}p^{1+\delta/4}=\mathcal{O}(p^{-\delta/4}).

where
\nu_{\ell}\coloneqq\E x_{11}^{\ell}
, and in the last step we used the fact that
\max_{k}b_{k}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and Lemma
B.2
.

Then, we show the tightness (in
z
) of the process
\{S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\}
.
It is sufficient to verify the moment condition (see
12
(12.51) for instance), i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in\Gamma}\frac{\E|S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{1})-S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{2})|^{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}<\infty.

Using the definition of
S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)
, for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\E|S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{1})-S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{2})|^{2}=\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt_{1}\rfloor}b_{k}^{2}\E|Y_{k}(z_{1})-Y_{k}(z_{2})|^{2}.

For any
k
,
Y_{k}(z)
is analytic in
z
. Thus, we have

Y_{k}(z_{1})-Y_{k}(z_{2})=(z_{1}-z_{2})\int_{0}^{1}Y_{k}^{\prime}\bigl(z_{2}+t(z_{1}-z_{2})\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

then

\E|Y_{k}(z_{1})-Y_{k}(z_{2})|^{2}\leq|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\E|Y_{k}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}.

Plugging into the required moment condition, we have

\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in\Gamma}\frac{\E|S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{1})-S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z_{2})|^{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}\leq\biggl(\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}\biggr)\sup_{k}\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\E|Y_{k}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}.

From the definition of
b_{k}
, it is easy to see that
\sum_{k}b_{k}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(p)
. It remains to show that

\displaystyle\sup_{k}\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\E|Y_{k}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1/p).

(5.2)

From the definition of
Y_{k}(z)
, we have

\E|Y_{k}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}=\frac{1}{k_{2}^{2}}\E\Big|x_{k}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}A_{k}^{\prime}(z)x_{k}-\Tr A_{k}^{\prime}(z)\Big|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{p^{2}}\E\Tr\bigl\{A_{k}^{\prime}(z)A_{k}^{\prime}(\bar{z})\bigr\}\leq\frac{1}{p}\E\|A^{\prime}(z)\|_{2}^{2},

and the derivative of
A_{k}(z)
is given by

A_{k}^{\prime}(z)=\Bigl\{\alpha^{\prime}(z)G_{k-1}^{2}(z)+2\alpha(z)G_{k-1}^{3}(z)+\alpha^{\prime\prime}(z)G(z)+\alpha^{\prime}(z)G_{k-1}^{2}(z)\Bigr\}S_{1}^{-1}.

Since
y_{1}<1
,
\|S_{1}^{-1}\|_{2}
is bounded with high probability. By Lemma
B.2
and the fact that
\|G_{k-1}(z)\|_{2}\leq\eta^{-1}
with
\eta=\Im z
, we have

\|A^{\prime}(z)\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

The above estimates imply (
5.2
), which completes the proof of tightness of
\{S_{m,t_{1},t_{2}}(z)\}
.

5.2
Proof of Lemma
5.2

Without loss of generality, we further assume
t\in[0,1]
.
Consider a partition
[0,1]=\cup_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}
, where

K_{j}=\left[\frac{j-1}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right],\quad n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}

From
[
53
, Theorem 1.5.6]
, it suffices to show that for any
\lambda>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\sup_{t_{1},t_{2}\in K_{j}}|W_{n,t_{1}}-W_{n,t_{2}}|>\lambda\right)=0.

(5.3)

Note that
\{\widetilde{L}_{k}(f)\}_{k\in\llbracket n+1,2n\rrbracket}
forms a martingale difference sequence. According to Lemma
A.2
, for some small
\varepsilon>0
, we have the following bound on the increment of
W_{n,t}
:

\E|W_{n,t}-W_{n,s}|^{2+\varepsilon}\lesssim(mt-ms)^{1+\varepsilon/2}\frac{\E|\widetilde{L}_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon}}{m^{1+\varepsilon/2}}=(t-s)^{1+\varepsilon/2}\E|\widetilde{L}_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon}.

From the definition of
\widetilde{L}_{k}
, the mean structure of
M_{k}(z)
given in (
B.9
), and Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\E|\widetilde{L}_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\asymp p^{1+\varepsilon/2}\E\biggl|\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)(1-\E_{k})M_{k}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\biggr|^{2+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\lesssim p^{1+\varepsilon/2}\int_{\Gamma}|f(z)|^{2+\varepsilon}\E\bigl|(1-\E_{k})M_{k}(z)\bigr|^{2+\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}z|

\displaystyle\lesssim p^{-1-\varepsilon/2}\E\bigl|x_{k}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}A_{k}(z)x_{k}-\Tr A_{k}(z)\bigr|^{2+\varepsilon}.

By Assumption
A
, we have

\E\bigl|x_{k}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}A_{k}x_{k}-\Tr A_{k}\bigr|^{2+\varepsilon}\lesssim\E|x_{11}|^{4+2\varepsilon}\Tr\{(A_{k}A_{k}^{*})^{1+\varepsilon/2}\}\lesssim p.

Using Markov’s inequality, it holds for all
\lambda>0
that

\Prob(|W_{n,t}-W_{n,s}|>\lambda)\lesssim(t-s)^{1+\varepsilon/2}p^{-\varepsilon/2}.

(5.4)

Similarly, we have for
0\leq r\leq s\leq t\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\;\Prob\bigl(\min\{|W_{n,s}-W_{n,t}|,|W_{n,s}-W_{n,r}|\}>\lambda\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\;\mathbb{P}(|W_{n,s}-W_{n,t}|>\lambda)+\mathbb{P}(|W_{n,s}-W_{n,r}|>\lambda)

\displaystyle\lesssim\;(t-r)^{1+\varepsilon/2}p^{-\varepsilon/2}.

(5.5)

Combining (
5.4
) and (
5.2
) with Lemma
A.3
, we get for any interval
K_{j}
and any
\lambda>0
,

\Prob\biggl(\sup_{t_{1},t_{2}\in K_{j}}|W_{n,t_{1}}-W_{n,t_{2}}|>\lambda\biggr)\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{1+\varepsilon/2}},

which implies (
5.3
) and thus completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
2.3

We start with the proof for the closed-end situation with
\rho(t)=0
for all
t>T
.
From Theorem
2.2
, for any
0<\xi<T
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi n\leq i\leq nT}T_{p}(n,i)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi n<i\leq nT}\rho\biggl(\frac{i}{n}\biggr)\Biggl|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi<t\leq T}\rho\biggl(\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}{n}\biggr)\Biggl|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\lfloor nt\rfloor}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}\sup_{\xi<t\leq T}\rho(t)|W(t)|.

(6.1)

Let
t=i/n\in(0,\xi)
, and
w(n,i)=\rho(i/n)=\rho(t)
. For any fixed
\widetilde{\gamma}>\gamma
, we have

\rho(t)=t^{-\widetilde{\gamma}}\cdot t^{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(t)\leq t^{-\widetilde{\gamma}}\sup_{0<x<\xi}x^{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(x)=\frac{n^{\widetilde{\gamma}}}{i^{\widetilde{\gamma}}}\sup_{0<x<\xi}x^{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(x).

Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i<\xi n}T_{p}(n,i)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<x<\xi}x^{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(x)\cdot\Biggl(\sup_{1\leq i<n}\frac{1}{n^{1/2-\widetilde{\gamma}}i^{\widetilde{\gamma}}}\Biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|\Biggr).

Under Assumption
C(ii)
, we have

\sup_{0<x<\xi}x^{\widetilde{\gamma}}\rho(x)=\sup_{0<x<\xi}x^{\widetilde{\gamma}-\gamma}x^{\gamma}\rho(x)\lesssim\xi^{\widetilde{\gamma}-\gamma}=\mathfrak{o}(1)\quad\text{as }\xi\to 0.

From the Hájek-Rényi inequality (Lemma
A.1
), we have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\Prob\Biggl(\sup_{1\leq i<n}\frac{1}{n^{1/2-\widetilde{\gamma}}i^{\widetilde{\gamma}}}\Biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|\geq\varepsilon\Biggr)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{1-2\widetilde{\gamma}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i^{-2\widetilde{\gamma}}\asymp\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},

and thus

\sup_{1\leq i<n}\frac{1}{n^{1/2-\widetilde{\gamma}}i^{\widetilde{\gamma}}}\Biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

From the above results, it holds for
\xi\to 0
that

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i<\xi n}T_{p}(n,i)=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

(6.2)

Recall that a standard Brownian motion satisfies
|W(t)|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{t})
as
t\to 0
. By Assumption
C(ii)
, we have
\rho(t)=\mathcal{O}(t^{-\gamma})
as
t\to 0
for some
0\leq\gamma<1/2
.
Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<\xi}\rho(t)|W(t)|=\sup_{0<t<\xi}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(t^{1/2-\gamma})=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\quad\text{as }\xi\to 0.

(6.3)

Combining (
6.1
), (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), we conclude that for any
T>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq i\leq nT}T_{p}(n,i)\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}\sup_{0<t\leq T}\rho(t)|W(t)|.

(6.4)

Now, we consider the open-end situation with
\rho(t)>0
for all
t>0
.
Using the similar arguments above, for any
T>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{i\geq 1}\rho\biggl(\frac{i}{n}\biggr)\biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\min(i,nT)}\widetilde{L}_{k}\biggr|\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}\sup_{t>0}\rho(t)\bigl|W(\min(t,T))\bigr|.

(6.5)

For any
i>nT
, we let
t=i/n\geq T
and get
\rho(i/n)\leq\frac{m}{i}\sup_{x\geq N}x\rho(x)
.
This, together with the triangular inequality, yields that

\displaystyle\;\Biggl|\sup_{i\geq 1}T_{p}(n,i)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{i\geq 1}\rho(i/n)\biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\min(i,nT)}\widetilde{L}_{k}\biggr|\Biggr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{i\geq nT}\rho(i/n)\Biggl|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}-\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+\min(i,nT)}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\;\sup_{t\geq T}t\rho(t)\cdot\sup_{i\geq nT}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{i}\Biggl|\sum_{k=n+nT+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Biggr|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(T^{-1/2})\cdot\sup_{t\geq T}t\rho(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(T^{-1/2}),\qquad(\text{Assumption \ref{asm:w_lim_inf}})

(6.6)

where the
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(T^{-1/2})
term follows from
that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Prob\biggl(\sup_{i\geq nT}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{i}\biggl|\sum_{k=n+nT+1}^{n+i}\widetilde{L}_{k}\biggr|\geq\varepsilon\biggr)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=nT}^{\infty}\frac{n}{i^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}T}.

This bound is obtained by applying the Hájek-Rényi inequality.
From Assumption
C(iii)
and the property of Brownian motion, we have

\displaystyle\biggl|\,\sup_{t>0}\rho(t)\bigl|W(\min(t,T))\bigr|-\sup_{t>0}\rho(t)\bigl|W(t)\bigr|\,\biggr|\leq\sup_{t>T}\rho(t)\bigl|W(T)-W(t)\bigr|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(T^{-1/2}).

(6.7)

From (
6.4
), (
6.5
), (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), we conclude that

\sup_{i\geq 1}T_{p}(n,i)\xrightarrow{\mathtt{d}}\sup_{t>0}\rho(t)|W(t)|.

Proof of Theorem
2.5

Let
\ell\coloneqq n+i-k^{\star}
be the number of post-change samples included in the current monitoring procedure at time
n+i
,
and assume that
\ell=\mathfrak{o}(n)
.
Define
d_{k}\coloneqq L_{k}(f)-\mu_{k}
, and let

\Phi_{n}(\ell):=C_{\sigma}\Bigl|\rho_{1,\gamma}(i/n)\sum_{k=k^{\star}+1}^{k^{\star}+\ell}\E d_{k}\Bigr|,\qquad C_{\sigma}:=\min_{k^{\star}+1\leq k\leq k^{\star}+\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\,\sigma_{k}},

R_{n}(\ell):=\Bigl|\frac{\rho_{1,\gamma}(i/n)}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=n+1}^{k^{\star}+\ell}\widetilde{L}_{k}\Bigr|.

From Proposition
2.1
, we have
C_{\sigma}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
By the triangle inequality,

T_{p}(n,i)\geqslant\Phi_{n}(\ell)-R_{n}(\ell).

We claim that (to be proven later), for
\ell=o(n)
, the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k^{\star}+1}^{k^{\star}+\ell}\E d_{k}=\biggl(I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})\ell+\frac{(\tau_{2}-1)I_{2}(f)}{k_{2}^{\star}}\ell^{2}\biggr)\bigl(1+\mathfrak{o}(1)\bigr),

(7.1)

where
k_{2}^{\star}\coloneqq k^{\star}-k_{1}
.
It then follows that

\displaystyle T_{p}(n,i)

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{\sigma}\rho_{1,\gamma}(i/n)\biggl|I_{1}(f,\tau_{1})\ell+\frac{(\tau_{2}-1)I_{2}(f)}{k_{2}^{\star}}\ell^{2}\biggr|\bigl(1+\mathfrak{o}(1)\bigr)-R_{n}(\ell).

Since
\{\widetilde{L}_{k},n+1\leq k\leq k^{\star}+\ell\}
is a martingale difference sequence,

R_{n}(\ell)=(k^{\star}-n+\ell)^{-\gamma}n^{\gamma-1/2}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(\max\{\sqrt{\ell},\sqrt{k^{\star}-n}\}).

We first consider the late-change case, where
k^{\star}-n\asymp n
.
In this case,

\rho_{1,\gamma}\biggl(\frac{k^{\star}-n+\ell}{n}\biggr)=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad R_{n}(\ell)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists constants
K_{\varepsilon}>0
and
N_{\varepsilon}
such that for all
n>N_{\varepsilon}
,

\Prob\bigl(R_{n}(\ell)\leq K_{\varepsilon}\bigr)>1-\varepsilon.

Choose

j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\end{subarray}}d_{j_{2}}d_{i_{1}}\E\left(O_{i_{1}j_{1}}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{j_{1}i_{2}}O_{j_{2}i_{2}}O_{j_{2}i_{3}}\mathcal{G}_{i_{3}j_{3}}O_{i_{1}j_{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\end{subarray}}d_{j_{2}}d_{i_{1}}\E\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{j_{1}i_{2}}\mathcal{G}_{i_{3}j_{3}}\bigr)\E\bigl(O_{i_{1}j_{1}}O_{i_{1}j_{3}}O_{j_{2}i_{2}}O_{j_{2}i_{3}}\bigr).

Using the method of
Weingarten calculus on orthogonal group

[see e.g.
11
, Lemma 6.2]
we have

\displaystyle\E\left(O_{i_{1}j_{1}}O_{i_{1}j_{3}}O_{j_{2}i_{2}}O_{j_{2}i_{3}}\right)=\frac{3}{p(p+2)}\mathds{1}_{\{i_{1}=j_{2}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=j_{3}=i_{2}=i_{3}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{p(p+2)}\mathds{1}_{\{i_{1}=j_{2}\}}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=j_{3}\neq i_{2}=i_{3}\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=i_{2}\neq j_{3}=i_{3}\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=i_{3}\neq i_{2}=j_{3}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{p+1}{p(p-1)(p+2)}\mathds{1}_{\{i_{1}\neq j_{2}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=j_{3}\neq i_{2}=i_{3}\}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{p(p-1)(p+2)}\mathds{1}_{\{i_{1}\neq j_{2}\}}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=i_{2}\neq j_{3}=i_{3}\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{j_{1}=i_{3}\neq i_{2}=j_{3}\}}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle\;\E\Tr\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}\Sigma_{1}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{3\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})}{p(p+2)}\sum_{a}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{aa})+\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})}{p(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{bb}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}\mathcal{G}_{ba}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}\mathcal{G}_{ab})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(p+1)\{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}-\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})\}}{p(p-1)(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}\mathcal{G}_{ba})

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}-\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})\}}{p(p-1)(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{bb}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}\mathcal{G}_{ab})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{3\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})}{p(p+2)}\sum_{a}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{aa})+\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})}{p(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{bb})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}}{p(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}\mathcal{G}_{ba})-\frac{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}}{p(p-1)(p+2)}\sum_{a\neq b}\E(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{aa}\mathcal{G}_{bb})+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\Tr(\Sigma_{1}^{2})}{p^{2}}\E\Tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})\Tr(\mathcal{G})+\frac{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}}{p^{2}}\E\Tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{G})-\frac{(\Tr\Sigma_{1})^{2}}{p^{3}}\E\Tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})\Tr(\mathcal{G})+\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}^{2}}{p}\E\Tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})\Tr(\mathcal{G})+\tau_{1}^{2}\E\Tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{G})+\mathcal{O}(1).

8.2
Green function comparison

The goal of this section is to show the following equation using the Green function comparison method:

\displaystyle\E\Tr\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}\Sigma_{1}\bigr)-\E\Tr\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{g}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}^{g}\Sigma_{1}\bigr)=\mathcal{O}(1).

(8.1)

This equation allows us to extend the Weingarten calculus result to the general distribution setting.

We assume that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are independent of each other and independent of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.
We construct a continuous interpolation of between the original data matrices and their Gaussian counterparts.
For
i=1,2
, define

\displaystyle X_{i}^{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{t}Z_{i}+\sqrt{1-t}X_{i},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Let
S_{i}^{t}
the corresponding sample covariance matrices formed by interpolated matrix
X_{i}^{t}
, and
\mathcal{G}^{t}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}
be the resolvent matrices defined similarly as
\mathcal{G}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
but with
S_{i}
replaced by
S_{i}^{t}
.

The LHS of (
8.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\E\Tr\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{1}\E\Tr\Bigl\{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}\bigl(\dot{S}_{2}^{t}-z_{1}\dot{S}_{1}^{t}\bigr)\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}^{t}\Sigma_{1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}^{t}\bigl(\dot{S}_{2}^{t}-z_{2}\dot{S}_{1}^{t}\bigr)\mathcal{G}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\Bigr\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{1}^{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{1}^{t}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{k_{1}}\Bigl\{Z_{1}Z_{1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}-X_{1}X_{1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}+c(t)\bigl(X_{1}Z_{1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}+Z_{1}X_{1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\bigr)\Bigr\},\quad c(t)\coloneqq\frac{1-2t}{2\sqrt{t(1-t)}},

and
\dot{S_{2}^{t}}
is defined similarly.
It suffices to estimate the order of each term in the integrand. All terms can be handled in a similar way, we only focus on the term

\E\Tr\bigl(\dot{S_{1}^{t}}H_{t}\bigr),\quad\text{where}\quad H_{t}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{G}^{t}\Sigma_{1}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{t}.

We expand the trace term as

\displaystyle\E\Tr\bigl(\dot{S}_{1}^{t}H_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{1}}\E\sum_{\ell=1}^{k_{1}}\Bigl\{z_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}z_{\ell}-x_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}x_{\ell}+2c(t)x_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}z_{\ell}\Bigr\},

(8.2)

where
x_{\ell}=(x_{\ell,1},\ldots,x_{\ell,p})^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
and
z_{\ell}=(z_{\ell,1},\ldots,z_{\ell,p})^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
are the
\ell
-th columns of
X_{1}
and
Z_{1}
, respectively.
We denote
\partial_{x}M\coloneqq\partial M/\partial x
.
Using the chain rule, it is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{\ell,a}}H_{t,ij}=\sqrt{t}\partial_{x_{\ell,a}^{t}}H_{t,ij},\qquad\partial_{x_{\ell,a}}H_{t,ij}=\sqrt{1-t}\partial_{x_{\ell,a}^{t}}H_{t,ij}.

Using Lemma
A.4
, we have

\displaystyle\E x_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}x_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\E\sum_{i,j}H_{t,ij}x_{\ell,i}x_{\ell,j}=\sum_{i,j}\E\partial_{x_{\ell,i}}\bigl(H_{t,ij}x_{\ell,j}\bigr)+\mathsf{Rem}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}\E\bigl(\partial_{x_{\ell,j}}\partial_{x_{\ell,i}}H_{t,ij}\bigr)+\E\Tr(H_{t})+\mathsf{Rem}_{1}+\mathsf{Rem}_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-t)\sum_{i\neq j}\E\bigl(\partial_{x_{\ell,j}^{t}}\partial_{x_{\ell,i}^{t}}H_{t,ij}\bigr)+\E\Tr(H_{t})+\mathsf{Rem}_{1}+\mathsf{Rem}_{2},

where the remainder terms are bounded as

|\mathsf{Rem}_{1}|\lesssim\sum_{i\neq j}\E|\partial_{x_{\ell,i}}^{2}H_{t}|=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad|\mathsf{Rem}_{2}|\lesssim\sum_{i\neq j}\E|\partial_{x_{\ell,i}}^{2}\partial_{x_{\ell,j}}H_{t}|=\mathcal{O}(1/p).

Similarly, we obtain that

\displaystyle\E z_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}z_{\ell}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i\neq j}\E\bigl(\partial_{x_{\ell,j}^{t}}\partial_{x_{\ell,i}^{t}}H_{t,ij}\bigr)+\E\Tr(H_{t}),

\displaystyle\E x_{\ell}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}H_{t}z_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{t(1-t)}\sum_{i\neq j}\E\bigl(\partial_{x_{\ell,j}^{t}}\partial_{x_{\ell,i}^{t}}H_{t,ij}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}(1).

These estimates and (
8.2
) yield

\E\Tr\bigl(\dot{S}_{1}^{t}H_{t}\bigr)=\mathcal{O}(1).

Similarly, we can show that the other term in the integrand is also of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
. This completes the proof.

Lemma A.1

.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}-\frac{1}{zk_{2}}\E\Tr(\mathcal{G}S_{2}\mathcal{G})-\frac{1}{zk_{2}}\E\{\Tr(\mathcal{G}S_{2})\Tr\mathcal{G}\}-\frac{p}{z}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p}).

Using the identity
\mathcal{G}S_{2}=I+zG
and the following covariance estimate

\displaystyle\E\{\Tr G\Tr\mathcal{G}\}-\E(\Tr G)\E(\Tr\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{O}(1),

(B.12)

and (
B.1
),
we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\biggl(1-\frac{z\E\Tr G+p}{k_{2}}\biggr)^{-1}\frac{z\E\Tr G+p}{k_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(p^{-1/2})=-\frac{z\underline{m}+1}{z\underline{m}}+\mathcal{O}(p^{-1/2}).

This, together with the variance estimate

\displaystyle\var(\Tr\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{O}(1),

(B.13)

completes the proof of (
B.2
).

Now we prove (
B.12
) and (
B.13
).
We denote

\displaystyle S_{2}^{(j)}\coloneqq S_{2}-\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}},\quad\mathcal{G}^{(j)}\coloneqq(S_{2}^{(j)}-zS_{1})^{-1},\quad j\in\llbracket k_{2}\rrbracket.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{j}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{j}\}
, and denote
\E_{j}\coloneqq\E(\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{j})
, then we have

\displaystyle\var(\Tr\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{2}}\E\bigl\{(\E_{j}-\E_{j-1})\bigl(\Tr\mathcal{G}-\Tr\mathcal{G}^{(j)}\bigr)\bigr\}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{2}}\E\biggl\{(\E_{j}-\E_{j-1})\frac{\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}}{1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}^{(j)}x_{j}}\biggr\}^{2}.

(B.14)

Let
\Delta_{j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k_{2}}\Tr\mathcal{G}^{(j)}-\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}^{(j)}
. From the Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}}{1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}^{(j)}x_{j}}=\frac{\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}}{1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}^{(j)}}-\frac{\frac{1}{k_{2}}x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}}{(1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}}\Delta_{j}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{j}^{2}).

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\E\biggl|\frac{1}{k_{2}}(\E_{j}-\E_{j-1})x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}\biggr|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{p^{2}}\E\Bigl|x_{j}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}x_{j}-\Tr(\mathcal{G}^{(j)})^{2}\Bigr|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1/p).

Similarly, we can show that the expectations of the second and third terms are also
\mathcal{O}(1/p)
. These estimates and (
B.14
) yield (
B.13
).

Similarly, we can verify that

\displaystyle\var(\Tr G)=\var(\Tr(\mathcal{G}S_{1}))=\mathcal{O}(1).

(B.15)

These estimates, together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, imply (
B.12
).

Proof of (
B.3
)

In the following, we denote
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\mathcal{G}(z_{i})
and
G_{i}=G(z_{i})
for
i=1,2
.
From the identity (
B.11
), we have

G_{2}G_{1}S_{1}^{-1}=\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\mathcal{G}_{2}XX^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}-\frac{1}{z_{1}}\mathcal{G}_{2},

and thus

\displaystyle\E\Tr(G_{2}G_{1}S_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\E\Tr(XX^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})-\frac{1}{z_{1}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}=\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\sum_{i,j}\E\bigl\{x_{ij}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ji}\bigr\}-\frac{1}{z_{1}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}.

For brevity, we denote

g_{ij}(X)=(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ji}.

By using the cumulant expansion, we have

\displaystyle\E\{x_{ij}g_{ij}(X)\}=\E\{\partial_{ij}g_{ij}(X)\}+R_{2,ij},

where the remainder term
R_{2,ij}
satisfies that

R_{2,ij}\lesssim\E\|\partial_{ij}^{2}g_{ij}(X)\|_{\infty}.

The first derivative of
g_{ij}
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}g_{ij}(X)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ii}-\frac{1}{k_{2}}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1})_{ji}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ji}-\frac{1}{k_{2}}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}X)_{jj}(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ii}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{k_{2}}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ji}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ji}-\frac{1}{k_{2}}(X^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}X)_{jj}(\mathcal{G}_{2})_{ii}.

The same argument as before shows that the remainder term
R_{2,ij}=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{p}).

Combining the above estimates, we have

\displaystyle\;\E\Tr(G_{2}G_{1}S_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\E\bigl\{(k_{2}-p-z_{1}\Tr G_{1})\Tr(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})\bigr\}-\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\E\biggl\{\biggl(\frac{z_{2}\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}-\frac{z_{1}\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}\biggr)\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}\biggr\}-\frac{1}{z_{1}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\bigl(k_{2}-p-z_{1}\E\Tr G_{1}\bigr)\E\Tr(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})-\frac{1}{z_{1}k_{2}}\biggl(\frac{z_{2}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}-\frac{z_{1}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}\biggr)\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}-\frac{1}{z_{1}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p}),

where the second equality following from (
B.13
), (
B.15
), and

\displaystyle\var\bigl(\Tr(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})\bigr)=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\cov\bigl(\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1},\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}\bigr)=\mathcal{O}(1).

(B.16)

The proof of these two estimates is similar to that of (
B.13
) and is thus omitted.
Combining the above estimate with the identity
G_{1}G_{2}=(G_{1}-G_{2})/(z_{1}-z_{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{z_{2}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}-z_{1}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}\biggr)=\biggl(1-\frac{p}{k_{2}}-\frac{z_{1}}{k_{2}}\E\Tr G_{1}\biggr)\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p}).

By symmetry, we also have

\displaystyle\frac{z_{2}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}-z_{1}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}}{z_{2}-z_{1}}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}\biggr)=\biggl(1-\frac{p}{k_{2}}-\frac{z_{2}}{k_{2}}\E\Tr G_{2}\biggr)\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{p}).

Combining the above two identities yields that

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{k_{2}}\E\Tr(\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2})=\frac{\bigl(z_{2}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}-z_{1}\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}\bigr)\bigl(\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{2}-\E\Tr\mathcal{G}_{1}\bigr)}{k_{2}(z_{2}-z_{1})\bigl(z_{2}\E\Tr G_{2}-z_{1}\E\Tr G_{1}\bigr)}+\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{p}).

This, together with (
B.1
), (
B.2
), and (
B.16
), completes the proof of (
B.3
).
