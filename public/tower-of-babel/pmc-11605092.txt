Social stimuli impact behavioral responses to caffeine in the zebrafish
Authors: Leah C. Wilson, Megan Lyttle, Aya Kanan, Alissa Le
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Caffeine is a widely consumed stimulant with dose-dependent effects on behavior. Across species, lower doses tend be anxiolytic and increase activity, while higher doses tend to be anxiogenic and decrease activity. Given the importance of the social environment on stress responses, we investigated how social stimuli modulate behavioral responses to caffeine. We exposed adult zebrafish to low (25 mg/L) or moderate (60 mg/L) caffeine either in isolation or within view of a stimulus group. Zebrafish are highly social, and social stimuli are known to buffer responses to environmental stressors. As predicted, we found that isolated fish were more sensitive to caffeine’s anxiogenic effects and less sensitive to caffeine’s stimulant effects. Among socially-exposed individuals, caffeine decreased social approach. Our data illustrate the importance of social context. Understanding how social cues shape pharmacological responses is important for understanding the external validity of studies that investigate drug effects in social species.

Introduction

Caffeine is a widely consumed stimulant with dose-dependent effects on mood, memory, and wakefulness1. Across vertebrates, lower doses tend to increase activity and attention, while higher doses tend to decrease activity and increase anxiety-like behavior2. Although the effects of caffeine on social behavior have not received a lot of attention, there appear to be both dose and species-specific effects on social responses2. Caffeine is a non-selective adenosine receptor antagonist, with a high affinity for A1 and A2A adenosine receptors3. Caffeine’s positive effects on mood and activity are associated with A2A antagonism4, and both A1 and A2A antagonism may play a role in the modulation of anxiety-like behavior4. In the current study, we explored how social cues modulate caffeine’s dose-dependent effects on behavior in the zebrafish. The zebrafish is a popular and powerful system in which to explore drug discovery5, toxicology6, and behavioral neuroscience7. The adenosine system is similar across species, and zebrafish exhibit a similar expression pattern of A1 and A2A receptors as rodents8,9.

Despite an appreciation that the social environment profoundly shapes responses to environmental challenges10, behavioral assays are often conducted in isolated animals11. While testing animals in isolation is vital to understanding effects in a controlled environment, social cues impact physiological and behavior responses to a variety of pharmacological compounds12. Social stimuli increase the physiological and behavioral sensitivity to stimulants in mice13 and humans12. In zebrafish, responses to caffeine differ between isolated and grouping individuals14. Understanding the impact of social context on pharmacological responses is important to understanding the external validity of studies that explore drug effects in social species.

To investigate how social stimuli shape behavioral responses to caffeine, we exposed adult zebrafish to a low or moderate dose of caffeine either in isolation or within view of a stimulus group. We measured anxiety-like behaviors, activity, and social approach. Given that visual social stimuli buffer stress responses in zebrafish15, we predicted that the presence of the stimulus group would reduce the sensitivity to caffeine’s anxiogenic effects. Based on previous work showing that caffeine-treated zebrafish tested in a group do not show the dose-dependent reduction in activity that isolated fish exhibit14, we predicted that social stimuli would decrease sensitivity to caffeine’s activity-suppressing effects.

Methods

Animals and housing

Adult wildtype zebrafish were purchased from a commercial supplier (Carolina Biological Supply, North Carolina, USA). Fish were housed in mixed-sex groups of 25 in 35 L tanks enriched with three 20 cm plastic aquarium plants. Structural enrichment improves welfare (e.g., reduces aggression and physiological and behavioral markers of anxiety)16. Fish were kept on a 14L:10D photoperiod, and tanks were maintained at 26 ± 1 °C, pH 7.0, < 0.25 ppm nitrites, < 10 ppm nitrates, and < 0.5 ppm ammonia. Fish were fed twice daily with commercial food flakes. Fish were acclimated to the laboratory for a minimum of six weeks prior to the experiment. This study followed the ARRIVE guidelines17; procedures were performed in compliance with federal and institutional guidelines and were approved by the Muhlenberg College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Social stimuli and caffeine exposure

To investigate how social stimuli impact behavioral responses to caffeine, fish were exposed to low (25 mg/L) or moderate (60 mg/L) caffeine either in isolation or within view of a stimulus group (Fig. 1). There were four treatment conditions: “Isolated + Low dose” (n = 6 F, 6 M); “Isolated + Moderate dose” (n = 6 F, 6 M); “Social + Low dose” (n = 6 F, 6 M); “Social + Moderate dose” (n = 7 F, 5 M). A focal fish was transferred to a test tank positioned next to a stimulus tank. The test and stimulus tanks lacked structural enrichment and each measured 20 cm x 29.5 cm (water height: 10 cm). The side and back walls of the tanks were covered with opaque white paper; one side of the test tank was left uncovered to allow for video recording. The test and stimulus tanks had heaters to maintain water temperature at 26 ± 1 °C.

Depending on the social treatment, the stimulus tank was either empty or contained 4 stimulus fish (2 F, 2 M). The stimulus fish were familiar with each other but were unfamiliar to the focal fish. Visual access to a group of four fish is sufficient to induce the social buffering of stress responses15,18. After habitation and baseline observation, we added caffeine (Sigma-Aldrich, C0750) dissolved in 50mL of test tank water to achieve a final exposure of either 25 mg/L or 60 mg/L. Our two doses were selected to capture a range of behavioral responses to caffeine. When zebrafish are tested in isolation, lower doses (10-25 mg/L) tend to increase activity and decrease anxiety-like behavior19,20, while higher doses (50-200 mg/L) tend to decrease activity and increase anxiety-like behavior19,21. Tanks and heaters were washed thoroughly with hot tap water and rinsed with DI water between every trail.

Behavior

We quantified behavior during a 10-minute “Baseline” period and a 10-minute period “Caffeine” period (Fig. 1). The “Baseline” period occurred after a 25-minute habitation. Isolated zebrafish show decreased freezing and increased exploratory behavior following a 6- or 30-minute habituation to a novel tank22,23. We used a longer habituation period to allow fish in the social condition to both habituate to the tank and to interact with the social stimulus group. Social stimuli buffer behavioral responses to environmental stressors following both short (e.g., 10-minute15) and long (e.g., overnight18) habituations. The “Caffeine” period began 15 min after caffeine was added; 15 min is sufficient to induce physiological and behavioral responses to caffeine21,24.

We analyzed the videos of the “Baseline” and “Caffeine” periods for bottom-dwelling, freezing, distance traveled, and time within 3 cm of the stimulus tank. We defined bottom-dwelling as time in the bottom third of the tank and freezing as no movement except for the eyes and gills25. We used ezTrack’s Location Tracking Module to quantify the amount of time a fish spent in a specific location (i.e., bottom of tank, within 3 cm of stimulus tank) and distance traveled. To calculate average speed, we divided “distance traveled” by time (600 s). We used ezTrack’s Freeze Analysis Module to quantify the proportion of time a fish was immobile26,27. We set the threshold for immobility to allow for movement of the eyes and gills, but not for larger displacements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on GraphPad Prism (v10). For each behavioral variable, we performed unpaired t-tests to look for sex differences in difference scores (“Caffeine” – “Baseline”); in the absence of statistically significant sex differences, we combined males and females for subsequent analysis. To determine if behavior varied with treatment condition, we performed three-way ANOVAs, with time included as a repeated variable. We assessed normality of the difference scores (“Caffeine” – “Baseline”) of each variable with the Shapiro-Wilk test28; all data passed the normality test (⍺=0.05). When there were statistically significant interaction effects, we performed post hoc Šídák’s multiple comparisons tests29.

Results

As predicted, we found that social cues modulated dose-dependent behavioral responses to caffeine. Compared to socially-exposed fish, isolated fish appeared to be more sensitive to anxiogenic effects and less sensitive to stimulant effects. Males and females did not differ in terms of change in bottom-dwelling (t(46) = 1.395 p = 0.1696), freezing (t(46) = 1.610, p = 0.1142), speed (t(46) = 0.103, p = 0.9184), or time in the social zone (t(46) = 0.6261, p = 0.5343). Sexes were combined for analysis.

Anxiety-like behavior

Isolation was associated with higher average baseline anxiety-like behavior, as assessed by bottom-dwelling and freezing. Caffeine had dose-dependent effects on anxiety in isolated, but not socially-exposed, fish. Three-way ANOVAs, with time included as a repeated measure, were performed to analyze the effect of social condition, caffeine, and dose on average bottom-dwelling and freezing. There were main effects of social condition (p < 0.0001) and dose (p = 0.0023) on bottom-dwelling (Table 1; Fig. 2A-B) and main effects of social condition (p = 0.0002) and dose (p = 0.0126) on freezing (Table 1; Fig. 2C-D). There were three-way interactions of social condition x caffeine x dose on bottom-dwelling (p = 0.0227) and freezing (p = 0.0266). In isolated fish, the moderate dose was associated an increase in both bottom-dwelling (p = 0.0271) and freezing (p = 0.0008), while the low dose was associated with non-significant decreases in bottom-dwelling (0.0792) and freezing (p = 0.1995). In socially-exposed fish, neither dose affected bottom-dwelling (low dose: p = 0.6494; moderate dose: p = 0.9482) or freezing (low dose: p = 0.4366; moderate dose: p > 0.9999).

Average speed

Caffeine’s effects on activity depended on both dose and social context. In isolated fish, only the low dose increased speed during the 10-minute observation period; in socially-exposed fish, both doses increased speed. A three-way ANOVA, with time included as a repeated measure, was performed to analyze the effect of social condition, caffeine, and dose on average speed. There were main effects of social condition (p = 0.0066), caffeine (p < 0.0001), and dose (p = 0.0002) on speed (Table 1; Fig. 3). There was a three-way interaction of social condition x caffeine x dose (p = 0.0006). In isolated fish, the low dose increased speed (p < 0.0001), while the moderate dose was associated with a non-significant decrease in speed (p = 0.2288). In socially-exposed fish, both doses increased speed (low dose: p < 0.0001; moderate dose: p < 0.0001).

Social Zone

As expected, fish spent more time in the social zone when stimulus fish were present. A three-way ANOVA, with time included as a repeated measure, was performed to analyze the effect of social condition, caffeine, and dose on time in the social zone. There were main effects of social condition (p < 0.0001) and caffeine (p = 0.0083) on average time in the social zone (Table 1; Fig. 4). There was a two-way interaction of social condition x caffeine (p = 0.0449). In isolated fish, caffeine did not impact time in the social zone (p = 0.85). In socially exposed fish, caffeine decreased time in the social zone (p = 0.002).

Discussion

In support of the hypothesis that social context impacts behavioral responses to caffeine, we found that isolated zebrafish were more sensitive to caffeine’s anxiogenic effects and less sensitive to caffeine’s stimulant effects.

Consistent with previous work19,21, we found that caffeine had dose-dependent effects on anxiety-like behavior in isolated zebrafish. Similar dose-dependent effects on anxiety have been described in rodents30 and humans31. Caffeine may increase anxiety though multiple adenosine-dependent mechanisms. In rodents, both A1 and A2A receptor knockouts show elevated anxiety-like behavior4. In the zebrafish, A1, but not A2A, receptor antagonism increases anxiety-like behavior32. A1 and A2A receptors modulate a variety of neurotransmitter systems, including glutamate, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), acetylcholine, serotonin, and dopamine33. In rodents and zebrafish, caffeine increases serotonin signaling and decreases GABA signaling2, two neurochemicals that modulate anxiety-like behavior34,35. Especially at higher caffeine doses, antagonism of hypothalamic and/or hippocampal adenosine receptors may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and stimulate glucocorticoid release36. In zebrafish, caffeine doses of 50-200 mg/L significantly increase whole-body cortisol21. Similar patterns have been described in rats36, and habitual caffeine use in humans is associated with elevated HPA axis reactivity37. HPA axis activity is associated with anxiety-like behavior across taxa38. Our data add to the consensus that caffeine can have dose-dependent effects on anxiety-like behavior.

Our finding that social stimuli reduced anxiogenic responses to caffeine is more novel: when fish were exposed to caffeine within view of conspecifics, neither dose impacted anxiety-like behavior. To our knowledge, only one other study has examined the effects of caffeine in non-isolated zebrafish; Neri et al. found that the effects of caffeine were diminished when treated fish could interact freely with untreated conspecifics. When tested alone, 70 mg/L increased freezing, but when tested in an arena with four untreated fish, 70 mg/L did not affect freezing14. Across species, social stimuli buffer behavioral, hormonal, and neural responses to environmental stressors39,40. Social cues could buffer the anxiogenic response to caffeine through multiple mechanisms. In zebrafish, social stimuli modulate dopamine and serotonin signaling41,42, neurotransmitters that are sensitive to caffeine and known to impact anxiety-like behavior. Social stimuli can dampen the activity of the HPA axis40,43, potentially buffering the neuroendocrine response to caffeine. While additional work is needed to clarify the mechanisms by which social stimuli modulate caffeine’s anxiogenic effects, our data support the hypothesis that social stimuli reduce the expression anxiety-like behavior in response to caffeine.

In addition to impacting anxiety-like responses, we found that social stimuli modulated caffeine’s effects on activity. When fish were isolated, only the lower dose increased average speed; when fish had access to social stimuli, both doses increased average speed. Our findings in isolated fish are consistent with bi-phasic activity responses in fish and rodents2. Our findings in socially-exposed fish suggest that social cues could increase the sensitivity to caffeine’s stimulant effects. While social context has not received much attention, there is some evidence that social stimuli increase sensitivity to sympathomimetic stimulants in mice13 and humans12. In zebrafish, Neri et al. found that social stimuli prevented the activity-suppressing effects seen at higher caffeine doses, but that social stimuli did not increase sensitivity to caffeine’s stimulant effects14. In the Neri et al. study, treated fish swam freely with untreated conspecifics; in our study, treated fish viewed untreated conspecifics through a transparent barrier. When treated fish can interact fully with conspecifics it is possible that treated individuals match the speed of the untreated individuals, obscuring any caffeine-mediated increase in activity. Future work should evaluate caffeine’s effects on activity in groups where the ratio of treated to untreated individuals is varied. In zebrafish, anxiety-like behavior is negatively associated with some measures of activity11. The reduced speed observed at higher caffeine doses could be a consequence of elevated anxiety-like behavior14. Our data are consistent with this hypothesis: the only group to show a decrease in activity (isolated fish exposed to moderate caffeine) was the only group to exhibit an increase in anxiety-like behavior. When fish have access to social stimuli, the social buffering of caffeine’s anxiogenic effects could be responsible for the increased sensitivity to caffeine’s stimulant effects.

Our analysis of social approach suggests that caffeine may impact social behavior. As expected for a social species, we found that fish spent more time in the social zone when a stimulus group was present. However, following caffeine exposure social fish spent less time in the social zone. Zebrafish social behavior can be assessed via group dynamics (e.g., interindividual distance, polarity) or with a social preference test where a focal fish may approach a stimulus group44. Previous work suggests that caffeine may impact social behavior in zebrafish, but the effects are not consistent. When tested in a group, a range of caffeine doses (25 mg/L-70 mg/L) increases interindividual distances between treated and untreated fish14. A similar range of caffeine doses (25 mg/L-50 mg/L) increases sensitivity to the movement of a robotic stimulus, but does not influence proximity to the stimulus45. Chronic exposure to very low concentrations of caffeine (0.0005 mg/L-0.3 mg/L) does not impact social approach, but does decrease distance traveled near a stimulus group46. While group dynamics and social preference are unique aspects of social behavior with distinct neural underpinnings, these studies suggest that caffeine may alter the response to social stimuli in context specific ways. In our study, the observed decrease in social approach could be due to caffeine’s anxiolytic effects. Grouping in zebrafish is sensitive to environmental context47 and pharmacological manipulation48. A variety of stressors (e.g., predator cues49, alarm substance50, novelty51, isolation52) increase grouping behavior, and anxiolytic compounds (e.g., nicotine, ethanol) decrease grouping behavior53. In isolated zebrafish, caffeine has anxiolytic effects at or below 25mg/L19; in the current study, we observed a near-significant decrease in freezing in isolated fish exposed to the low dose of caffeine (p = 0.08). When fish have access to socially-buffering stimuli, anxiolytic effects may extend above 25 mg/L. In our study, low baseline anxiety-like behavior in the social condition may have prevented us from detecting a caffeine-related decrease in our two measures of anxiety-like behavior. Future work should examine a wider variety of social and anxiety-like responses across a range of caffeine doses. If our hypothesis about social approach is correct, we would expect to see a caffeine-related increase in social approach at higher (i.e., anxiogenic) doses.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. Our data suggest that social cues decrease the anxiogenic response to caffeine. However, the presence of the stimulus group may have directly impacted behavior. Zebrafish are highly social and the salience of the social stimuli could have decreased the expression of anxiety-like behavior irrespective of anxiogenesis. To distinguish between the impact of social stimuli on behavior and the impact of social stimuli on the anxiogenic response to caffeine, future studies should measure behavior in isolated individuals following a “social” caffeine-exposure period. Our interpretation of the data predicts that higher doses of caffeine (> 60 mg/L) would increase anxiety-like behavior in individuals with access to social cues. We recommend that future studies include a range of doses to evaluate hypotheses about the impact of social context. Lastly, freezing and bottom-dwelling are part of suite of zebrafish anti-predator behaviors54 and are well established markers of both fear and anxiety-like behavior24. “Defensive distance”, the perceived immediacy of a threat, has been suggested as a framework for distinguishing between fear and anxiety-like behavior11. Fear can be considered a direct response to an immediate threat, while anxiety can be considered a more generalized state, often triggered by the anticipation of a future threat55. Given the robust relationship between caffeine and anxiety56, we have discussed freezing and bottom-dwelling as measures of anxiety-like behavior. While novel tank tests are usually interpreted within an anxiety framework11, we may be capturing both fear and anxiety-like responses in our analyses. Future studies could use an established anxiolytic (e.g., clonazepam57) to distinguish between anxiety-like responses to caffeine and anxiety-like/fear responses to isolation.

In conclusion, our data illustrate the importance of considering social context when evaluating pharmacological effects. We found that social stimuli altered dose-dependent behavioral responses to caffeine: isolated animals appeared to be more sensitive to anxiogenic effects and less sensitive to stimulant effects. Understanding how social cues shape pharmacological responses is important for understanding the external validity of studies performed in social species. Particularly in social species, dose-dependent responses may be sensitive to social context.
