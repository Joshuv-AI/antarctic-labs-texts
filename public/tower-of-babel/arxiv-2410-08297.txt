Matrix-free stochastic calculation of operator norms without using adjoints
Jonas Bresch, Dirk A. Lorenz, Felix Schneppe, Maximilian Winkler

Abstract
This paper considers the problem of computing the operator norm of a linear map between finite dimensional Hilbert spaces when only evaluations of the linear map are available and under restrictive storage assumptions. We propose a stochastic method of random search type to maximize the Rayleigh quotient and employ an exact line search in the random search directions. Moreover, we show that the proposed algorithm converges to the global maximum (the operator norm) almost surely and a sublinear convergence behavior for the corresponding eigenvector and eigenvalue equation. Finally, we illustrate the performance of the method with numerical experiments.

Introduction

In this paper, we revisit the problem of the computation of the operator norm
\|A\|
of a linear map
A
between Hilbert spaces of dimension
d
and
m
, respectively.
We reconsider this problem under restrictive conditions for which we could not find a satisfactory solution in the literature and which wwe encountered in our analysis of primal-dual algorithms with mismatched adjoint in the context of computerized tomography.
First, we assume that the map
A
is only available through a black-box oracle that returns
Av
for a given input
v
,
e.g. there is precompiled code available which evaluates a linear map.
Moreover, we consider that it is not possible to store a large number of vectors (of either the input or the output dimension),
which means that we assume that the method has a storage requirement of
\mathcal{O}(\max(m,d))
.
Finally, we are interested in a method that is able to approximate the value
\|A\|
to any accuracy and also is able to make further incremental improvements if the output at some stage is not good enough.
We describe, why we are interested in this scenario in the next section.

Related work

Our restrictive assumptions rule out existing methods:
The storage requirement rules out the brute force approach of building a full matrix representation for the linear map by evaluating
Ae_{i}
for all
i
and then calculating the singular value decomposition of this representation as this requires storagy

In

Another popular method for the computation of the spectral norm is the power iteration

which, in its plain form, works for maps for which the input and output dimensions are equal.
To calculate the norm of a map with different input and output dimensions, one could in principle apply the power method to the map

If the evaluation of

Moreover, it is known that the power method converges slowly if the spectral gap (i.e. the distance between the two largest eigenvalues) is small.

1.3
Contribution

The contribution of this work is:

•

We propose a stochastic algorithm that produces a sequence that converges to the operator norm

\displaystyle\max_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|^{2}

almost surely only using evaluations of the operator
A
, and calculation of norms and inner products.

•

Our method works for any linear map between finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, i.e. we do not need any assumptions on the dimension of the spaces or on the structure of the linear map.

•

The method has the minimal possible storage requirement
\mathcal{O}(\max(m,d))
, i.e., we only need to store two vectors of the input dimension
d
and two vectors of the output dimension
m
during the iteration.

We focus on the case where
A
maps
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and we work with the standard Euclidean inner product and norm. However, what we develop can be generalized to more general finite dimensional Hilbert spaces as well as to spaces over the complex numbers.

Additionally, our method produces an approximation of a right singular vector for the largest singular value and can be extended to compute further right singular vectors. Curiously, the method also allows us to check if a map
A
is orthogonal in the sense that
A^{*}A=cI
for some constant
c\geq 0
, where
I
denotes the identity on the input space of
A
.

We propose a stochastic method to approximate the operator norm

Remark 2.1

(Normalization of
v^{k}

)
.

By design we always have that

Remark 2.2

(Critical points)
.

By Rayleigh’s principle, the critical points of the problem
\max_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|
are exactly the right singular vectors of
A
and the values at these points are exactly the singular values.
More precisely, the intersection of the eigenspace of the largest (or smallest) singular value gives exactly the global maxima (or minima). All other eigenspaces lead to saddle points.
We will show in the course of the paper that the method we propose will (almost surely) avoid all saddle points and will converge (almost surely) to the global maximum.

A particularity of this method is that the search direction is quite arbitrary,
but the stepsize is determined exactly (and note that it is allowed to be negative).
This is different from many other stochastic methods which estimate update directions and then take a step according to a fixed stepsize schedule.

In the following, we will show that this method is simple to execute (and especially discuss how (
5
) is solved in practice) and indeed uses only evaluations of
A
.
We will also show that the method will produce a sequence
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|Av^{k}\|
converges to
\|A\|
almost surely, and that the distance of
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to the right singular space of
A
corresponding to the leading singular value converges to zero.

Remark 2.3

(Distribution of the

)
.

The random vector

is distributed as follows:
Since an orthogonal projection of normally distributed random vectors is again normally distributed, the projection

Proposition 2.4
.

For

iii)

The local extrema of

\displaystyle\tau_{\pm}\coloneqq\tfrac{b_{k}}{2a_{k}}\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{b_{k}^{2}}{4a_{k}^{2}}+1}

and one can check that

Remark 2.5

(Convergence of the sequence
\|Av^{k}\|
)
.

As long as
a_{k}\neq 0
, the algorithm leads to a sequence
\|Av^{k}\|
which is strictly increasing. Since we have
\|Av^{k}\|\leq\|A\|
, the sequence
\|Av^{k}\|
is convergent as long as we have
a_{k}\neq 0
throughout the iteration.

Remark 2.6

(Algorithm
1
is a stochastic projected gradient method)
.

Although we sample
x^{k}
uniformly from the unit directions orthogonal to
v^{k}
, the method is still a stochastic gradient method in some sense. This is because the stepsize
\tau_{k}
is positive for
a_{k}=\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle>0
and negative for
a_{k}<0
, and thus, we effectively move in direction
\sign(a_{k})x^{k}
. This means that the expected direction in which the algorithm moves is uniformly distributed on a half-sphere in the space orthogonal to
v^{k}
.
This half-sphere is defined by the inequality
a_{k}>0
which is
0<\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{A^{*}Av^{k}},{x^{k}}\right\rangle
. Due to the symmetry of the distribution, the expected direction
\mathbb{E}(\sign(a_{k})x^{k})
is a positive multiple of the projection of
A^{*}Av^{k}
on the space orthogonal to
v^{k}
. We conclude that Algorithm
1
is a stochastic ascent algorithm in the sense that the expected direction is a multiple of the projection on the gradient
2A^{*}Av^{k}
of the objective
\|Av\|^{2}
.

Remark 2.7

(Markov chain provided by

The random variables

Here,

forming a probability space

More precisely,

The sampling space

and

.
The probability measure

is then defined via the Markov chain rule using the defined Markov kernel equipped with the update (

and closed formula for

from Proposition

.

Lemma 2.8

and to

, this means that the vector

is in a subspace of dimension at most

By the previous lemma,
we know that

only happens (a.s.)
if

is a right singular vector.
Now we show that if
v^{k}
is not a right singular vector,

.

We start with the following observation regarding the eigenspaces of
A^{*}A
for some eigenvalue

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
\sigma^{2}
be an eigenvalue of
A^{*}A
with multiplicity
r<d
,
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
be no eigenvector for the eigenvalue
\sigma^{2}
. Then it holds that the set of vectors
x\in\{v\}^{\bot}
for which
v+x\in E_{\sigma}
holds is an affine subspace of dimension
r
or
r-1
, or is empty.

Proof.

Since
\dim(E_{\sigma})=r
we can write
E_{\sigma}=\left\{x\mid Cx=0\right\}
with
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d-r\times d}
having rank
d-r
. We assume without loss of generality (by applying a suitable rotation) that
v=e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{*}
, hence
x\in\{v\}^{\bot}
means
x=(0,x^{\prime})
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. We have that
v+x\in E_{\sigma}
exactly if
C(v+x)=0
which is equivalent to
Cx=-Cv
. Denoting
C=\left[c_{1},C^{\prime}\right]
with
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-r}
and
C^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-r\times d-1}
, i.e.
c_{1}
is the first column of
C
and
C^{\prime}
the matrix consisting of the remaining columns, we can rewrite the latter system of equations as
C^{\prime}x^{\prime}=-c_{1}
. The matrix
C^{\prime}
has rank either
d-r
or
d-r-1
as cutting the first column might reduce the dimension of the span of those. Hence, this system has an affine solution space of dimension either
d-1-(d-r)=r-1
or
d-1-(d-r-1)=r
, or might have no solution at all.
∎

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
d>2
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and assume that all eigenvalues of

have multiplicities less than

. Then it holds that if
v^{k}
is not a right singular vector of
A
, then
v^{k+1}
is almost surely also not a right singular vector of
A
.

Consequently, we have

(almost surely) throughout the iteration of Algorithm
1
if

is not a right singular vector of
A
and the sequence
\|Av^{k}\|
is increasing and convergent (a.s.).

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a singular value of
A
with multiplicity

and

the respective eigenspace.

If

is in

, then (by Lemma

)

is from an affine subspace

of dimension at most

Hence, the set of directions

that may result in

is a subset of the set
\left\{x/\|x\|\mid x\in L\right\}
. Since

and

are not parallel, this is a set of probability zero (with respect to the Haar measure on
\{v^{k}\}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, according to which

is sampled due to Remark

), if

(which then also implies

). The latter (restrictive case from Lemma

The monotonicity and the convergence of the sequence

is a consequence of Remark

∎

2.2
Almost sure convergence

Now we set out to prove that Algorithm
1
will almost surely give a sequence
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|Av^{k}\|\to\|A\|
.
The first step is to calculate the ascent in each iteration.
Secondly,
we discuss the convergence of
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Afterwards,
we can prove the convergence of the sequence
(\|Av^{k}\|^{2})
to a singular value
and that at least one subsequence
(v^{k_{j}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges into the corresponding singular space (almost surely).
Finally,
the almost sure convergence towards the maximal singular value for
(\|Av^{k}\|)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

is proven by considering a technical lemma,
which allows us to uniformly bound the probability of finding optimal search directions
in all tangential hyperplanes.

Lemma 2.12
.

For the random variables
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

and
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
from Algorithm
1
it holds

\displaystyle\|Av^{k+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}=\tau_{k}a_{k},\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\textrm{a.s.}

Proof.

We calculate the ascent in the objective:

\displaystyle\|Av^{k+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\|Av^{k}+\tau_{k}Ax^{k}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\left[\|Av^{k}\|^{2}+2\tau_{k}\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle+\tau_{k}^{2}\|Ax^{k}\|^{2}\right]-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle+\tfrac{\tau_{k}^{2}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\|Ax^{k}\|^{2}+\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}-1\right)\|Av^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle+\tfrac{\tau_{k}^{2}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\left(\|Ax^{k}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}a_{k}+\tfrac{\tau_{k}^{2}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}b_{k}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}\left(a_{k}+(a_{k}+b_{k}\tau_{k})\right).

Since we know that
\tau_{k}
solves
-a_{k}\tau_{k}^{2}+b_{k}\tau_{k}+a_{k}=0
we replace
a_{k}+b_{k}\tau_{k}=a_{k}\tau_{k}^{2}
and get

\|Av^{k+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}=\tfrac{\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}}(a_{k}+\tau_{k}^{2}a_{k})=\tau_{k}a_{k}.\qed

Now we show that the sequence
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to zero almost surely.

Lemma 2.13
.

For the random variables
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}},(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

and
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
1

it holds that
a_{k}=\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle\to 0
almost surely as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

First observe that since
\|Av^{k}\|^{2}
is convergent by Corollary
2.10
, Lemma
2.12
proves that
\tau_{k}a_{k}\to 0
.
Second, we have by definition that

a_{k}=a_{k}\tau_{k}^{2}-b_{k}\tau_{k}=\tau_{k}(a_{k}\tau_{k}-b_{k})
.
We multiply by
a_{k}

and get

\displaystyle a_{k}\tau_{k}(a_{k}\tau_{k}-b_{k})=a_{k}^{2},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\textrm{a.s.}

(9)

Since
|b_{k}|\leq\max\{\|Av^{k}\|,\|Ax^{k}\|\}\leq\|A\|
is bounded,
the left-hand side vanishes as

, which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}\big]=\frac{1}{d-1}\big\|\big(I_{d}-v^{k}(v^{k})^{*}\big)A^{*}Av^{k}\big\|^{2}.

Proof.

By symmetry arguments (see also Remark
2.3
) we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x^{k}(x^{k})^{*}\mid v^{k}]=\frac{1}{d-1}\big(I_{d}-v^{k}(v^{k})^{*}\big).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle^{2}\mid v^{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(v^{k})^{*}A^{*}Ax^{k}(x^{k})^{*}A^{*}Av^{k}\mid v^{k}\big]

\displaystyle=(v^{k})^{*}A^{*}A\cdot\mathbb{E}[x^{k}(x^{k})^{*}\mid v^{k}]\cdot A^{*}Av^{k}

and the assertion follows,
since

∎

Next, we show the existence of a subsequence of the random variables
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

generated by Algorithm

converging into a singular space, almost surely.
Crucially,

Therefore,
we define the distance of a set

Recall that the infimum is attained by some

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of random variables

.
Then,

(v^{k_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

By Lemma

for

As

.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d-1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\big(I_{d}-v^{k}(v^{k})^{*}\big)A^{*}Av^{k}\|^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}\big]\Big]=\mathbb{E}\big[a_{k}^{2}\big]\to 0,

for

Theorem 2.19
.

Let
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of random variables

It holds

Proof.

We consider the set

Lemma

Now we show that there is some

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|Av^{k}\|\not\to\sigma_{1})=\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{k}\Big)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k})=0.

We prove (

By

of

with a direction

By

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\cap\{v\}^{\perp}}\mathbbm{1}_{U(v,x)\not\in B}\ K_{x\mid v}(v,\ \mathrm{d}x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{v^{k}}(v)

As

with this

The update of the algorithm maximizes

over the stepsize and effectively maximizes the value of

for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\setminus B}\;\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\cap\{v\}^{\perp}}\mathbbm{1}_{U(v,x)\notin C_{\theta}}\ K_{x\mid v}(v,\ \mathrm{d}x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{v^{k}}(v)

Now assume that the singular space to

Remark 2.20

(Stopping criteria)
.

In Proposition
2.14
we have seen that

\mathbb{E}[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}]=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|A^{*}Av^{k}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}v^{k}\|^{2}
, i.e. a small expectation of
a_{k}^{2}
shows that
v^{k}
is close to a singular vector.
Furthermore,
Lemma
2.8
tells us
that
a_{k}=0
only happens at right singular vectors.
This motivates to try to use the expectation of
a_{k}^{2}
as stopping criterion,
i.e. stop if
(d-1)\mathbb{E}[a_{k}^{2}]\leq\varepsilon^{2}
as a stopping criterion.
It turns out to be practical to start approximating the expectation when we observe that
a_{k}^{2}
is small:
If
(d-1)a_{k}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}
,
resample
x^{k}
several times and stop if the observed empirical mean obeys the same bound.

Remark 2.21

(Handling the case of singular values of multiplicity
d-1
)
.

The assertion of Lemma
2.16
(ii) also holds in the case of a singular value of multiplicity
d-1
, implying that Algorithm
1
will also converge in this case:
Under this assumption, there are only two distinct singular values, and we distinguish two cases: If the smaller singular value has multiplicity
d-1
, then a uniform random initialization
v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
will almost surely give
\|Av^{0}\|>\sigma_{2}
as the corresponding eigenspace has measure zero. Hence, we have
a^{0}\neq 0
and can perform a step. Then either we are done (which is almost surely not the case) or we have
\sigma_{2}<\|Av^{k}\|<\sigma_{1}
and we obtain
\|Av^{k}\|\to\|A\|
from Lemma
2.16
.
If the larger singular value has multiplicity
d-1
we argue as follows:
First note that
\sigma_{1}\geq\|Av^{0}\|\geq\sigma_{2}
for any
v^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Second, if we initialize
v^{0}
uniformly on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and since the eigenspace corresponding to
\sigma_{1}
is one dimensional, having equality in the lower bound means the
v^{0}
is a right singular vector corresponding to
\sigma_{2}
, where the eigenspace is measure zero such that
\|Av^{0}\|>\sigma_{2}
almost surely.
Moreover, by Lemma
2.8
we obtain that
a_{0}\neq 0
holds (a.s.). By optimality of the stepsize
\tau_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain a direct projection onto the space of normalized maximal singular vectors, which is in this case an equator of the
(d-1)
-sphere. We end up with convergence after one iteration, i.e.
\|Av^{1}\|=\sigma_{1}
.

Remark 2.22

(Handling the case of singular values of multiplicity
d
—detecting orthonormal columns)
.

A curious fact about Algorithm
1
is as follows:
If we sample
v^{0}
uniformly on the unit sphere and observe
a_{0}=0
, then we almost surely have that the operator
A
has orthogonal columns and all have the same norm, i.e. that

\displaystyle A^{*}A=cI_{d}

(15)

holds for some
c>0
.
To see this,
we argue by contradiction:
If (
15
) does not hold,
then
A^{*}A
has at least two distinct eigenvalues
and therewith at least two distinct proper spaces of eigenvectors.
Therefore
v^{0}
is,
as uniformly sampled vector from the
(d-1)
-sphere,
almost surely not a right singular vector of
A
.
Hence,
we obtain from Lemma
2.8

that
a_{0}=0
happens with probability zero.
Equivalently,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(a_{0}=0)>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad A^{*}A=cI_{d},

which yields the assertion.

Remark 2.23

(Assumption
2.11
can be dropped)
.

We started the convergence analysis with Assumption
2.11
, i.e. that
A
has no singular value of multiplicity
d-1
or
d
. This was used to ensure that if we have

, we will have

However, now we see that this assumption can be dropped: Remark

Remark 2.24

(Calculating leading right singular vectors)
.

One can extend Algorithm
1
to compute a number of leading singular values and corresponding right singular vectors (if they are all distinct). Once
\sigma_{1}
and
v_{1}
have been found, we can restrict Algorithm

to the space

using Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization for

and find

and

in this way. Although this procedure gets more computationally heavy for each new singular value, we may still be able to compute the first few leading right singular vectors.

2.3
Convergence rates

In this subsection
we derive two convergence results:
one for the sequence
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
1
and the second
for the corresponding singular vector and singular value equation,
i.e.

\|A^{*}Av^{k}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}v^{k}\|^{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\text{where}\quad v^{k}\;\text{is from Algorithm~\ref{alg:mafno-orth-exact}}.

The following proposition
shows the convergence of the
a_{k}^{2}=\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle^{2}

from Algorithm
1
towards zero
in a sublinear rate.

Proposition 2.25
.

Let
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of random variables
generated by Algorithm
1
.
It holds almost surely

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}a_{k}^{2}\leq\tfrac{2\|A\|^{4}}{n+1}.

Proof.

From (
9
) we have
a_{k}\tau_{k}(a_{k}\tau_{k}-b_{k})=a_{k}^{2}
and
from Lemma
2.12
we get that
a_{k}\tau_{k}=\|Av^{k+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}\geq 0
and
a_{k}\tau_{k}\leq\|A\|^{2}
. We also have
|b_{k}|\leq\|A\|^{2}
, so we get

\displaystyle a_{k}^{2}=a_{k}\tau_{k}(a_{k}\tau_{k}-b_{k})\leq 2\|A\|^{2}a_{k}\tau_{k}=2\|A^{2}\|\left(\|Av^{k+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}\right)

We sum this equation from
k=0,\dots,n
and arrive at

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\leq 2\|A\|^{2}\|Av^{n+1}\|^{2}\leq 2\|A\|^{4}.

(16)

Finally, estimating
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\geq(n+1)\min_{0\leq k\leq n}a_{k}^{2}
proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2.26

(Convergence rate for
a_{k}\tau_{k}
)
.

Summing up the equation from Lemma
2.12

for
k=0,...,n
we have
(n+1)\min_{0\leq k\leq n}a_{k}\tau_{k}\leq\sum_{0\leq k\leq n}a_{k}\tau_{k}=\|Av^{n+1}\|^{2}-\|Av^{0}\|^{2}\leq\|A\|^{2}
.
Hence we also have
\min_{0\leq k\leq n}a_{k}\tau_{k}\leq\tfrac{\|A\|^{2}}{n+1}
(a.s.).

We can turn the above proposition into a convergence rate for the error in
the eigenvector equation for
A^{*}A
with approximative eigenvalue
\|Av^{k}\|

in the
k
-th iteration.

Theorem 2.27
.

Let
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of random variables
generated by Algorithm
1
.
It holds almost surely

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\big\|A^{*}Av^{k}-\|Av^{k}\|^{2}v^{k}\big\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Bigr)\leq\tfrac{2(d-1)\|A\|^{4}}{(n+1)\varepsilon}.

Proof.

First,
from Lemma
2.14
,
we get

\mathbb{E}[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}]=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|\|Av^{k}\|^{2}v^{k}-A^{*}Av^{k}\|^{2}.

(17)

From (
16
) we get

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}[a_{k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigr[\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\Bigl]\leq 2\|A\|^{4}.

Using the law of total expectation and (
16
)
we get

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[a_{k}^{2}\mid v^{k}\bigr]\Bigr]=\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[a_{k}^{2}\bigr]\leq\tfrac{2\|A\|^{4}}{n+1}.

Inserting (
17
) yields

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\big\|\|Av^{k}\|^{2}v^{k}-A^{*}Av^{k}\big\|^{2}\Bigr]\leq\tfrac{2(d-1)\|A\|^{4}}{n+1}.

Now, let
k^{*}(n)
be the index for which the latter minimum is attained,
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
holds by Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\big\|\|Av^{k^{*}(n)}\|^{2}v^{k^{*}(n)}-A^{*}Av^{k^{*}(n)}\big\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\big\|\|Av^{k^{*}(n)}\|^{2}v^{k^{*}(n)}-A^{*}Av^{k^{*}(n)}\big\|^{2}\bigr]}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(d-1)\|A\|^{4}}{(n+1)\varepsilon}.\qed

By Theorem
2.27
, we can substitute
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{n+1}
,
which implies an error bound of
1/\sqrt{n+1}
with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
.
In other words, with probability at least
1-p
, the error is
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
for any
p\in(0,1)
.

4
Conclusion and outlook

Algorithm
1
is a stochastic method for computing the operator norm of a linear map
A
between real Hilbert spaces and it only uses applications of the operator and computations of norms and inner products of vectors.
We show almost sure convergence of the value
\|Av^{k}\|
to the operator norm.

Our examples show that the method works in practice but also show that it may be slow (while it is indeed faster than the power iteration in some special cases).
The question of the convergence speed regarding
\|A\|-\|Av^{k}\|
is still open;
however, we answered the question of the convergence speed for the corresponding eigenvector and eigenvalue equation.

We only treated real Hilbert spaces here but the case of complex spaces can be treated similarly and only the definition of
a_{k}
should be changed to
\operatorname{Re}(\left\langle{Av^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle)
.

Another question that we did not answer here is the following. How can we calculate
\|A-V\|
if we only can do evaluations of
A
and
V^{*}
(and no evaluations of
A^{*}
and
V
are possible)? This question is important in the case of adjoint mismatch as it happens, for example, in computerized tomography
[
26
,
25
,
9
,
33
,
10
,
36
]
. The idea we presented here can be generalized to this case if we start from the formulation

\displaystyle\|A-V\|=\max_{\|u\|=1,\|v\|=1}\left\langle{u},{(A-V)v}\right\rangle=\max_{\|u\|=1,\|v\|=1}\left(\left\langle{u},{Av}\right\rangle-\left\langle{V^{*}u},{v}\right\rangle\right)

which we worked out in
[
5
]
.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that Algorithm
1
can be adapted to calculate the
smallest
singular value of
A
.
To that end, one only has to replace the
\argmax
in the calculation of the stepsize with an
\argmin
which can also be computed exactly. The practicality of this method shall be investigated in upcoming work.

Appendix

Recall that the regularized incomplete Beta function is

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{x}(a,b)\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{B}(x;a,b)}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)},\quad a,b>0,\quad x\in[0,1],

with the incomplete Beta function
[
31
, Eq. (2), III. § 4]

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(x;a,b)\coloneqq\int\limits_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{B}(a,b)\coloneqq\mathrm{B}(1;a,b).

Lemma A.1
.

For
0\leq x\leq 1
and
d\geq 3
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{x}(\tfrac{d-2}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi d}}\sqrt{x}^{d-2}.

Proof.

We first estimate (using that
(1-t)^{-1/2}\geq 1
for
0\leq t\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(x;a,\tfrac{1}{2})\geq\int\limits_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{x^{a}}{a}.

(19)

Furthermore it holds that
[
31
, Eq. (3), III. § 4]

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\tfrac{d-2}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})=\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d-2}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\right)}.

We use
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
and the Gautschi’s inequality
[
12
, Thm. 3]

\displaystyle x^{1-s}<(x+\tfrac{s}{2})^{1-s}<\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+s)}<(x+1)^{1-s}

(valid for
0<s<1
) with
x+1=(d-1)/2
and
s=1/2
to estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(\tfrac{d-2}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d-2}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{d-3}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}.

Combining this with (
19
) with
a=(d-2)/2
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{x}(\tfrac{d-2}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\geq\frac{\sqrt{x}^{d-2}}{d-2}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{d-3}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}

For
d\geq 3>\tfrac{20}{7}
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d-2}>\frac{1}{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\frac{d-3}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{1/2}>\frac{\sqrt{d}}{4}

gives the claimed bound.
∎
