Etalanetug (E2814) in dominantly inherited Alzheimer’s disease: an open-label phase 1b/2 study to assess safety and target engagement in participants with mild to moderate cognitive impairment

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEtalanetug (E2814) is designed to delay the clinical progression of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) by binding to the microtubule binding region (MTBR) of tau implicated in seeding and spreading of tau pathology. Dominantly inherited Alzheimer’s disease (DIAD) is a rare form of the disease (< 1%), having similar changes in the tau distribution and pathology to sporadic AD. Herein, we report the safety, pharmacology and biomarker results of the etalanetug Study 103 in DIAD patients.MethodsStudy 103 enrolled participants with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment due to DIAD who received etalanetug intravenously every 4 weeks escalating from 750 mg, 1500 mg, 3000 mg, to 4500 mg. After ascending to 4500 mg, patients received 4500 mg for up to 108 weeks. Tau pathology biomarkers, ptau217 and MTBR-tau243 were measured in CSF. Additional assessments included tau PET ([18F]MK-6240) and MRI. Pharmacodynamic effects of etalanetug on biomarkers were evaluated. Untreated participants from the DIAN Observational and DIAN-TU studies trial served as natural history controls.ResultsOverall, 8 participants enrolled in Study 103. Etalanetug reduced concentrations of ptau217 30.4% after 12 weeks (n = 7), 48.6% at 36 weeks (n = 5), and 57.9% at 108 weeks (n = 2). Etalanetug treatment reduced concentrations of MTBR-tau243 by 50.6% in DIAD participants (n = 7) after 12 weeks of treatment. Maximal reduction in MTBR-tau243 levels (71.6%) was observed at week 36 (n = 4) and was sustained to 108 weeks (n = 2). In healthy volunteers who lack tau pathology, etalanetug had no effect on MTBR-tau243 or ptau217 after 12 weeks of treatment. Three DIAD patients had tau PET acquired at week 60 and week 108. The data indicate that the tau PET SUVr signal remains stable overall, with a trend towards decrease over time. At 108 weeks, no tau accumulation was observed via tau PET in any of the 3 patients. Three participants experienced treatment-related adverse events (AEs) with the 3000 mg dose; additionally, 5 serious AEs total were reported in 3 participants.DiscussionEtalanetug treatment in these symptomatic participants with DIAD was tolerated across dose levels, and immunogenicity was found to be minimal. Etalanetug demonstrated effects on both hyperphosphorylated tau and tau tangle pathology. Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder defined neuropathologically by the presence of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles (NFT) [1, 2]. Dominantly inherited Alzheimer’s disease (DIAD) is a rare form of the disease, representing less than 1% of all AD cases [3, 4], caused by known mutations in the amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin-1 (PSEN1), or presenilin-2 (PSEN2) gene [5]. Changes in the distribution and type of tau pathology in DIAD are similar to sporadic AD, suggesting that a common pathophysiology exists between DIAD and sporadic AD [3, 6–9]. For example, aggregated tau pathology measured by tau PET increases at symptom onset in both sporadic and DIAD [10] and increases throughout the symptomatic phase of the disease.

While there are two disease modifying anti-amyloid therapies fully approved in several countries to treat symptomatic AD (i.e., mild cognitive impairment and mild AD) [11, 12], these therapies are effective in slowing but not completely arresting the NFT spread that is most strongly correlated to cognitive decline in early AD [13, 14]. This remaining NFT spread that anti-amyloid therapies do not fully arrest may therefore be responsive to an anti-tau therapy that targets tau directly.

Physiologically, tau is predominantly a neuronal microtubule-associated protein that plays a fundamental role in the stabilization of microtubules. Under pathological conditions, however, short motifs in the microtubule binding region (MTBR) domains of tau adopt a beta-sheet conformation, inducing self-assembly with other tau molecules that lead to the formation of NFTs [15, 16]. In AD, evidence from pathology suggests a direct correlation between the number of NFTs found in the brain, and the degree of dementia observed in participants with AD at the time of death [17, 18]. It has also been reported that tau can form seeds, which spread from cell to cell extracellularly to cause the initiation and propagation of intracellular tau aggregation [19].

The MTBR found in pathological tau species is required for tau seeding [15, 20–23]. Also, antibodies against the MTBR have demonstrated the ability to significantly impede tau aggregation under laboratory conditions [24]. Therefore, the MTBR is considered a promising drug target, wherein an antibody to this region of tau theoretically has the potential to disrupt tau aggregation and slow or potentially arrest neurodegeneration in AD.

Etalanetug (E2814) is a novel immunoglobulin G subclass 1a (IgG1a) monoclonal antibody (mAb) that binds with high affinity to 2 identical epitopes contained within the second (R2) and fourth (R4) domains of the tau MTBR [22]. It is hypothesized that high affinity binding to these epitopes will significantly reduce tau seeding and spread. In vitro, etalanetug demonstrates the ability to inhibit tau aggregation as well as reduce the intracellular levels of exogenously applied tau seeds to cultured neurons. In addition, the murine form of etalanetug (7G6) reduces the transmission of tau species in vivo [22]. The proposed mechanism of action in humans is that etalanetug will bind to extracellular, seed-competent MTBR-tau, reducing its levels within neurons and accelerating clearance from the interstitial space through processes such as mAb-mediated uptake and phagocytosis by microglia. This in turn will slow or stop the progressive accumulation of NFTs and its associated cognitive decline.

In a previously reported study of healthy participants, etalanetug presented an adequate safety and immunogenicity profile. Target engagement (TE) was demonstrated following single and multiple dose administrations of etalanetug, and CSF concentrations ranging between 100 and 200 ng/mL appear to saturate binding to MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354, the binding regions for etalanetug [25].

Herein, we present the results of an open-label phase 1b/2 study of etalanetug in DIAD participants with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment. This study was designed to evaluate the safety of etalanetug in DIAD and to further define its pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) profile. This was the first investigation of etalanetug in an AD population. Data from untreated DIAD participants in the Dominantly inherited Alzheimer’s Network Observational Study (DIAN-OBS) and Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer’s Network Trials Unit (DIAN-TU) study were used as a natural history comparator group to support interpretation of the results (DIAN ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT00869817; DIAN-TU trial ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01760005) [6].

Methods

Trial ethics

This study was approved by relevant institutional review boards (IRB)/ethics committees (Washington University IRB and others) and conducted in accordance with International Conference on Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) and all applicable local Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent.

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network Trials Unit (DIAN-TU). The DIAN-TU was launched in 2011 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Randall J. Bateman, MD. The primary goal of the DIANTU is to implement effective, safe and efficient clinical trials that have the highest likelihood of success in advancing overall treatments as well as scientific understanding of dominantly inherited Alzheimer’s disease. For up-to-date information, see https://dian.wustl.edu/.

Trial design and eligibility criteria

This was an open-label phase 1b/2 study of etalanetug in participants with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment due to DIAD (NCT04971733). The aim of the study was to evaluate the safety and target engagement (TE) of different doses of etalanetug in participants with DIAD having mutations in amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin 1 (PSEN1), or presenilin 2 (PSEN2) genes.

The study included 2 cohorts (A and B). Cohort A contained both phase 1b and phase 2 components; Cohort B contained only phase 2. In the phase 1b component, the treatment regimen was etalanetug 750 mg administered as a 1-hour IV infusion every 4 weeks (Q4W) for 12 weeks. This rolled into the phase 2 component, where individuals were given escalating doses of etalanetug at 1500 mg over 1-hour Q4W for 12 weeks, followed by 3000 mg over 1-hour Q4W for 12 weeks, then 4500 mg over 2 h Q4W for remaining time (up to 72 weeks). Treatment for Cohort B included 3000 mg etalanetug over 1-hour Q4W for up to 52 weeks.

Patients eligible for this study included male or female participants between the ages of 18 and 80 years, confirmed to be mutation-positive for PSEN1, PSEN2, or APP genes (Morris 2012), confirmed to have a CDR-sum of boxes (CDR-SB) score of 5 to 12 at screening, and a study partner who was able to provide accurate information as to the subject’s cognitive and functional abilities at relevant study visits. A full list of inclusion/exclusion criteria for this study are provided in the Supplemental Appendix A.

Untreated comparison groups

In the absence of an untreated control arm in this study, untreated participants from the DIAN-OBS study and DIAN-TU-001 double-blind placebo control arm were used as a comparison group against participants treated with etalanetug, representing the natural disease progression of DIAD (DIAN ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT00869817; DIAN-TU trial ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01760005) [6, 26]. DIAN-OBS is an international collaborative longitudinal study initiated in 2008 to obtain comprehensive and uniform data on brain biology and function in individuals at risk for DIAD (e.g. has a mutation in the APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2 gene) [27, 28]. Data and specimens collected are derived from full clinical assessments, including neurologic and physical examinations, extensive cognitive batteries, structural and functional neuroimaging, amyloid and tau pathological measures using PET, CSF and blood collection (plasma, serum, and whole blood), extensive genetic and multiomic analyses, and brain donation upon death [29]. Participants from the DIAN-OBS and DIAN-TU studies that were used as a natural history comparison group were selected based on having a similar range of baseline CDR-SB scores and proportion of mutation type. A subset of healthy participants from an etalanetug phase 1 study [25] were also used to compare effects of etalanetug on fluid biomarkers in healthy vs. DIAD participants.

Collection and analysis of fluid samples for pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

For pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) assessments, blood samples were collected for Cohort A predose, immediately after infusion, and at 4, 8, and 24 h after infusion on days 1 and 85. Additional predose samples were taken on days 15, 29, 57, and 169, and every 12 weeks thereafter. For patients receiving the 4500 mg dose, samples were collected predose and immediately after infusion of first dose and then predose every 12 weeks. For Cohort B, blood samples were collected predose and immediately after infusion on day 1 and predose at weeks 12, 24, 36, and 52.

CSF samples were used to assess etalanetug drug concentrations for PK and to assess PD. CSF samples for Cohort A were collected predose via lumbar puncture on day 1, day 84, and every 12 weeks after each dose escalation (i.e., day 169, day 253, and 12 weeks after initiation of the 4500 mg dose). For Cohort B, CSF samples were collected predose on day 1, week 24, and week 52.

PK (etalanetug concentrations) and immunogenicity were measured in serum and CSF by validated electrochemiluminescence as previously described [25]. TE was evaluated in CSF using an LC-MS/MS assay that quantitates bound and free forms of MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354 tryptic peptides as described in Rawal et al. [25] and was calculated as the ratio of etalanetug-bound MTBR-tau to total (free plus bound) MTBR-tau expressed and reported as a percent.

Measurement of CSF MTBR-tau243, phosphorylated tau (ptau) and nonphosphorylated tau (nptau) species were performed at Washington University using the previously reported methods with IP–MS [21, 30, 31]. A subset of CSF samples from healthy participants treated with etalanetug [25] were analyzed in the same batch alongside samples from DIAD participants treated with etalanetug. CSF samples from untreated participants from DIAN-OBS and DIAN-TU studies were analyzed separately.

Image acquisition and processing procedures

Structural MRI and tau PET using [¹⁸F]MK-6240 were performed at baseline, week 60, and week 108. Structural imaging was acquired at 3T and included 3D T1-weighted MRI acquisitions for anatomical reference and segmentation. Tau PET imaging was performed using a target dose of 5 mCi (185 MBq) of [¹⁸F]MK-6240 (± 10%). Each PET scan consisted of a 30-minute dynamic acquisition (6 × 5-minute frames), initiated approximately 90 min (± 5 min) post-injection [29].

All PET scans underwent motion correction to mitigate the effects of patient movement and preserve image integrity. The scans were then spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard anatomical space to enable consistent comparisons across participants. Following normalization, images were resampled to match the resolution and dimensions of the MNI template. SUVr images were generated using the pons as the reference region, chosen for its stable uptake and minimal off-target signal [32]. Global tau burden was quantified using the CenTauR Universal region of interest, as defined by Villemagne, which captures key cortical regions associated with tau deposition [33]. For participants with longitudinal tau PET data (i.e., baseline, week 60, and/or week 108), absolute changes in [¹⁸F]MK-6240 SUVr from baseline were plotted across visits. Additionally, partial volume correction (PVC) was performed using the Müller-Gärtner method [34] to assess the extent to which observed SUVr values could be attributed to brain atrophy.

For the untreated DIAD participants from the DIAN-TU study only that represent natural history progression in this population, a different tau PET tracer (flortaucipir), imaging acquisition method, and image analysis method were used as previously described [29]. In short, structural T1-weighted MR images were acquired at 3T and segmented into regions of interest (ROIs) using FreeSurfer (v5.3-HCP, Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Charlestown, MA). Tau PET was acquired using 6.5 mCi (240 MBq) of [18F]Flortaucipir (± 10%) and quantified over the 80–100 min post-injection time window (4 × 5 min frames) using the FreeSurfer-based PET Unified Pipeline (PUP; https://github.com/ysu001/PUP), which includes scanner resolution harmonization to a full-width half-max (FWHM) of 8 mm, inter-frame motion correction, affine registration of motion corrected PET to the structural T1, time activity curve extraction for each FreeSurfer ROI, and SUVR quantification using cerebellar grey as the reference region. A cortical ROI SUVR was defined as the arithmetic average of the non-partial volume corrected SUVRs for the bilateral precuneus, the superior and middle temporal, rostral middle and superior frontal, and the lateral and medial orbitofrontal.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints for this study were (1) the incidence of adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs), laboratory parameters, vital signs, and ECGs and (2) the change from baseline in CSF free and bound MTBR-tau and total MTBR-tau at 12 weeks (Cohort A only). Secondary endpoints included serum PK parameters following dosing on days 1 and 85, CSF etalanetug concentrations, serum anti-etalanetug antibody (ADA) concentrations, and change from baseline in CSF and/or plasma biomarkers including total tau and phosphorylated tau biomarkers.

Tau biomarkers included CSF MTBR-tau243 and CSF ptau species of interest (including individual analytes, as well as ptau/nptau for 153, 181, 199, 202, 205, 208, 217, and 231). Absolute change from baseline in tau PET SUVr was also assessed as a secondary endpoint.

Exploratory endpoints in this study included efficacy markers such as change from baseline in global CDR and CDR-SB.

Statistical methods

For this phase 1b/2 study, no formal sample size calculations were performed. Up to 8 participants in Cohort A and up to 5 participants in Cohort B were considered adequate to evaluate the initial safety and PK in symptomatic participants with DIAD and to establish evidence of target engagement (TE).

Adverse events (AEs) were summarized by dose. The incidence of AEs was reported as the number and percentage of participants with AEs by system organ class (SOC) and preferred term (PT).

Incidence of anti-etalanetug and neutralizing antibodies in serum was summarized by dose.

Laboratory results were summarized using Système International units. For all quantitative laboratory parameters, the actual value and the change from baseline to each postbaseline visit and to the end of treatment (defined as the last on-treatment value) were summarized by visit using descriptive statistics.

Descriptive statistics for demographic and other baseline characteristics percentages were derived.

Serum PK parameters were derived by noncompartmental analysis using Phoenix WinNonlin software (Version 8.2.2) for the 750 mg and 1500 mg dose levels. Summary statistics for the PK parameters were calculated for these dose groups.

Percentage change from baseline over time was presented graphically for CSF MTBR-tau243 and for several CSF ptau biomarkers.

CDR-SB over time was presented graphically for both the Study 103 participants and the untreated DIAD participants.

PK/PD modelling was used to assess the relationship between observed CSF etalanetug concentrations and percent bound MTBR-tau299 or MTBR-tau354. This model is described in the PK/PD modelling section above.

All analyses were descriptive with no statistical testing.

There were no participants who experienced treatment-related AEs while receiving etalanetug 750, 1500, or 4500 mg.

A total of 5 SAEs were reported in 3 participants. One participant, while receiving etalanetug 3000 mg, reported an SAE of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, assessed as treatment related, and an SAE of vaginal bleeding in the context of a suspected gynecologic malignancy. This subject was a 49-year-old white female with history of hypertension, cerebellar infarction, and DIAD due to a PSEN1 mutation and was on donepezil and memantine at study entry. Etalanetug was initiated at 750 mg and escalated to 1500 and 3000 mg. After the first 3000 mg dose (day 282), she was hospitalized with headache, lethargy, vomiting, and gait instability. Imaging confirmed acute dural venous sinus thrombosis. She was treated with aspirin and atorvastatin and etalanetug was discontinued. The subsequent vaginal hemorrhage event and workup identified a suspected gynecologic malignancy potentially related to both events.

One subject, while also receiving etalanetug 3000 mg, reported SAEs of chest infection and seizure, unrelated to treatment. Seizure was considered secondary to the chest infection. One subject, while receiving etalanetug 4500 mg, reported an SAE of arthritis, unrelated to treatment.

Three participants had AEs leading to discontinuation of study drug (cerebral venous sinus thrombosis [3000 mg]; vasculitis [3000 mg], and recurrent CLL [4500 mg]).

Serum and CSF PK

Serum etalanetug exposures within the IV dose range of 750 mg to 4500 mg increased in a dose-related manner (Table 3). Cmax was generally observed at the end of etalanetug infusion and the geometric mean serum Cmax increased from 41.7 µg/mL at the 750 mg dose to 101 µg/mL at the 1500 mg dose; inter-subject variability was large, ranging from 47% to 64%. As with serum, CSF concentrations increased with increasing etalanetug doses; the CSF-to-serum concentration ratio based on trough concentrations at week 12 was approximately 1% (Table S1).

Immunogenicity

Treatment-emergent ADA positivity was low across doses, with only 2 out of 7 (29%) participants reporting low titers. No neutralizing antibody-positive results in serum were observed during this study (Table S2 and Table S3).

Target engagement

Intravenous administration of etalanetug resulted in dose- and concentration-related increases in bound MTBR-tau299 and bound MTBR-tau354 at 750 mg and 1500 mg doses, confirming TE. Minimal further increase in bound MTBR-tau299 and bound MTBR-tau354 was observed with higher doses of 3000 mg and 4500 mg. TE was estimated to reach Emax at 85% for etalanetug bound MTBR-tau299 and at 78% for etalanetug bound MTBR-tau354. The EC50 was reached at etalanetug CSF concentrations of 159 ng/mL and 194 ng/mL for MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354, respectively, corresponding to the 750 mg dose (Fig. 2).

Pharmacodynamic (PD) effects demonstrating proof of mechanism

CSF tau pathology biomarkers

After treatment with etalanetug, CSF MTBR-tau243 concentrations decreased over time with a mean 51% reduction at 750 mg, a mean 69% reduction at 1500 mg, and a mean 72% reduction at 3000 mg after ~ 1 year of treatment. This contrasted with the natural longitudinal trajectory observed in untreated, matched DIAD participants, which showed that CSF MTBR-tau243 decreased by 3.7% per year (Fig. 3A). Treatment with etalanetug also decreased concentrations of CSF ptau217 by 35% at the 750 mg dose and continued reducing ptau217 concentration to a mean 55% reduction from baseline at the 4500 mg dose (Fig. 3B). The ratio of ptau217/nptau212-221 also demonstrated a significant reduction over time (Figure S1, panel 7 C). This contrasts with the natural progression of CSF ptau217 concentrations in the DIAD control group (Fig. 3) and the ptau217/nptau212-221 ratio that is generally stable over time (Figure S1, panel 7 C). Concentrations of other ptau species (ptau153, ptau199, ptau205, ptau208, and ptau231) were also reduced after treatment with etalanetug by ~ 25%, and concentration of nptau was reduced on peptides ranging from amino acids 151–221. When compared with the untreated DIAD group, concentration of nptau was reduced on peptides ranging from amino acids 151–221. When compared with the untreated DIAD group, there was no effect on ptau202 or nptau226. The ratio of nptau202/ptau195-210 showed an upward trend vs. natural progression, with ratios for the other sites remaining stable or showing a trend towards decline. (Figure S1). There was no effect on ptau202 or nptau226. Tau PET

[18F]MK-6240 tau PET SUVr images were superimposed onto each participant’s MRI, showing changes in tau PET signal from baseline in treated DIAD participants at week 60, and week 108 (Fig. 4A). These changes were also illustrated by the absolute difference in [18F]MK-6240 tau PET SUVr from baseline, plotted across each visit (Fig. 4B) and in the CenTauR Universal Region of Interest (Fig. 4C). The data indicate that the tau PET SUVr signal remains stable overall, with a trend towards decrease over time. A partial volume correction (PVC) analysis, based on the Müller-Gärtner method [34], indicated that approximately 60% of the observed SUVr reduction over time could be attributed to atrophy (data not shown). In untreated individuals, tau PET([18F]flortaucipir) signal increases over time around the time of onset and following the onset of clinical symptoms. Notably, the slope of global cortical tau SUVR accumulation is relatively consistent across subjects, suggesting a broadly similar rate of tau progression despite differences in clinical stage at study exit (Fig. 4D).

Exploratory assessments of clinical efficacy

The mean (SD) baseline global CDR score across all 8 participants was 1.6 (0.52), indicating mild-to-moderate level of cognitive impairment. The mean (SD) change in global CDR score from baseline to day 337 was 0.0 (0.0) (n = 3), and from baseline to day 673 was 0.7 (0.58) ranging from 0 to 1 (n = 3). Two of these 3 participants had progressed in their global CDR score (1 subject from 2 to 3, and 1 from 1 to 2); the third subject with a baseline global CDR score of 1 remained stable by day 673.

The mean (SD) baseline CDR-SB score across all 8 participants was 9.5 (2.20), indicating a moderate level of cognitive impairment. The mean (SD) change in CDR-SB score from baseline to day 337 was 1.7 (0.58) ranging from 1 to 2 (n = 3), and from baseline to day 673 was 4.7 (2.31) ranging from 2 to 6 (n = 3). An increase in CDR-SB score from baseline was observed for each subject, indicating cognitive decline.

Figure S4 shows the change in CDR-SB over time in DIAD participants treated with etalanetug compared to untreated DIAD participants (natural history comparator). The subset (n = 12, CDR-SB = 7.67 ± 2.68, range 5–12) of the untreated DIAD participants with baseline CDR-SB > 4.5 used as a natural history comparator group had a mean (SD) change in CDR-SB score from baseline to 12 months was 3.8 (1.96) ranging from 0 to 6 (n = 10), and from baseline to 24 months was 2.3 (2.52) ranging from 0 to 5 (n = 3).

Discussion

Presented here are the primary results from an open-label phase 1b/2 study for etalanetug treatment designed to provide initial safety and pharmacokinetic data on etalanetug and demonstrate target engagement in participants with DIAD with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment. This is the first investigation of etalanetug in an AD population. This study had a unique design of within-subject dose escalation following at least 3 doses of the preceding dose level. Robust PK sampling was conducted following the first 750 mg dose and first 1500 mg dose to enable PK characterization. Within-subject dose escalation allowed for robust characterization of target engagement for a wide range of etalanetug doses in a small number of participants based on binding of etalanetug to MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354 in CSF. Overall, our results demonstrated target engagement of etalanetug and a tolerable safety and immunogenicity profile in these participants with DIAD with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment. The data presented here expands upon the evidence presented in the first-in-human study (Rawal 2025). The observed PK and target engagement profiles (MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354) in DIAD were similar to those in healthy participants [25].

When compared with the natural trajectory of CSF tau pathology biomarkers in mild-to-moderate DIAD patients, etalanetug reduced hyperphosphorylated tau species such as ptau217 (both concentrations and percent ptau/nptau) and the tangle-specific biomarker, MTBR-tau243. Interestingly, etalanetug had differential effects on the phospho-sites. Hyperphosphorylated sites known to cluster with ptau217 like ptau153, 181 and 231 showed similar reductions after treatment, however, etalanetug had no effect on ptau202, which has been shown to be hypophosphorylated in AD CSF [31]. The etalanetug pharmacodynamic effect on CSF ptau217 and MTBR-tau243 may be disease-specific, as no pharmacodynamic effect was observed in healthy participants (Figure S2) while there was a clear pharmacodynamic effect observed in DIAD treated with etalanetug after 3 months of treatment (Fig. 3).

The effects of etalanetug treatment on CSF tau abnormal phosphorylation and tau pathology biomarkers in DIAD participants may be predictive of the stabilization in tau PET signal observed in these participants. As discussed, etalanetug treatment resulted in a sustained reduction of both CSF MTBR-tau243 (a tau pathology biomarker that has been shown to have high correlation with newly formed tau tangles and high correlation with tau PET) as well as CSF ptau217 and CSF ptau217/nptau (a hyperphosphorylated tau pathology biomarker that emerges at the time of amyloid PET pathology and is also associated with early tau PET Braak stages) [36]. This supports the lack of accumulation in tau pathology, as measured by tau PET in 3 participants (Fig. 4). After 60 weeks of treatment; there is a clear stabilization of tau PET signal across the cortex over time. This stabilization in tau PET SUVR in DIAD treated participants distinctly differs from the rapid increase in tau PET SUVR observed in untreated DIAD participants (natural history comparator) after symptom onset (Fig. 4D). The demographics of the subjects in Fig. 4D are represented in Table S4. Although it is theoretically possible that the etalanetug antibody could interfere with tau PET tracer binding to neurofibrillary tangles and thereby give rise to the appearance of non‑progression, the tau PET tracer [¹⁸F]MK‑6240 binds preferentially to insoluble, intracellular tau aggregates, whereas etalanetug is designed to bind soluble MTBR‑tau species in the extracellular space implicated in inter‑neuronal propagation, making direct competition or epitope masking unlikely.

Etalanetug was found to be safe and tolerable in healthy volunteers [25]. In these DIAD participants, etalanetug was also shown to be well tolerated across dose levels, with a few notable adverse events observed in the study. A serious seizure and lower respiratory infection occurred in a patient with no history of seizure after 11 doses of etalanetug. While the cause of seizure was not identified, it is known that patients with DIAD have a markedly increased risk of seizure [37]. One study found that among 94 patients with DIAD due to PSEN1 mutations, the incidence of seizures was 19.1% over 5 years [38]. These data suggest that progression may have been the cause or a contributing factor to the seizure event. A serious cerebral venous sinus thrombosis deemed related to etalanetug was observed in a patient after receiving 11 doses of etalanetug. A suspected gynecologic malignancy may have been a contributing factor, as the subject had a concurrent vaginal hemorrhage. In addition, another patient developed lymphocytic vasculitis after two doses of etalanetug. Study drug was discontinued, and the event was resolving at the time of discontinuation.

Regarding clinical progression, these 8 participants with mild-to-moderate cognitive impairment at baseline exhibited an increase in mean global CDR and CDR-SB scores from baseline during the 2-year study. These changes indicate a decline in cognitive function that is consistent with natural disease progression in untreated DIAD participants (Figure S3). At day 673, the mean (SD) change in global CDR score from baseline was 0.7 (0.58) (ranging from 0 to 1; n = 3); 2 of these 3 participants had progressed in their global CDR score (1 subject from 2 to 3, and 1 from 1 to 2). The other subject with a baseline global CDR score of 1 remained stable by day 673. Given the small sample size and lack of placebo control, the treatment effect of etalanetug cannot be conclusively assessed.

Based on PK/PD modeling from healthy adults (Study 001) and DIAD participants, the increases in bound MTBR-tau299 and MTBR-tau354 plateaued at higher doses (3000 mg and 4500 mg), indicating limited additional target engagement beyond this range. Modeling projections show that 500 mg and 3000 mg achieve CSF etalanetug concentrations approximating the EC80 and EC90, respectively. These findings suggest that these 500–3000 mg doses are pharmacologically meaningful for follow-up evaluation.

Limitations of this study include the small sample size, lack of a concurrent placebo arm, and utilization of the DIAN-OBS and DIAN-TU placebo arm study participants to represent the natural progression of DIAD. Additional limitations include variation in the total number of doses for each subject prior to dose escalation as well as a high study discontinuation rate because participants were in a more advanced stage of disease. The safety assessment is limited by the small sample size, open‑label design, and the mild to moderate disease stage of enrolled DIAD participants. The low study completion was primarily related to disease progression, which affected participants’ ability to adhere to study procedures or maintain home‑based care. Recruitment for Cohort B was similarly constrained, with only one participant enrolled during the recruitment period. These factors restrict the generalizability of the safety results, and further evaluation in a larger, double‑blind, placebo-controlled trial is warranted. In summary, our results demonstrate that etalanetug was tolerated across dose levels in these participants with symptomatic DIAD and immunogenicity was found to be minimal. Etalanetug demonstrated effects on both hyperphosphorylated tau (i.e., CSF ptau217) and tau tangle pathology biomarkers (i.e., CSF MTBR-tau243 and tau PET) in mild-to-moderate DIAD participants, with a robust reduction of CSF MTBR-tau243 for all participants and stabilization of tau PET in 3 participants that had tau PET scans out to approximately 2 years. However, given the small Tau PET sample size and the absence of formal benchmarking against longitudinal MK-6240 variability in comparable autosomal dominant AD cohorts at comparable disease stages, these findings should be confirmed with future studies. Robust target engagement was demonstrated in CSF, confirming the potential of etalanetug to inhibit the propagation of pathological tau species by binding to the MTBR, a domain important to the spreading and seeding of tau pathology in AD. Taken together, the data support continued evaluation of etalanetug as a disease-modifying therapy in DIAD (NCT01760005) and sporadic AD (NCT06602258) populations.
