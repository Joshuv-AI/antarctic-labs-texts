Regularization methods for solving hierarchical variational inequalities with complexity guarantees
Daniel Cortild, Meggie Marschner, Mathias Staudigl

Abstract
We consider hierarchical variational inequality problems, or more generally, variational inequalities defined over the set of zeros of a monotone operator. This framework includes convex optimization over equilibrium constraints and equilibrium selection problems. In a real Hilbert space setting, we combine a Tikhonov regularization and a proximal penalization to develop a flexible double-loop method for which we prove asymptotic convergence and provide rate statements in terms of gap functions. Our method is flexible, and effectively accommodates a large class of structured operator splitting formulations for which fixed-point encodings are available. Finally, we validate our findings numerically on various examples.

Introduction

Bi-level optimization problems consist of two nested optimization formulations, referred to as the inner and the outer problem. Solutions to the inner problem determine the feasible set over which a minimizer of an outer objective function is searched for. This class of problems is an extremely active area of research recently, driven by important practical applications in engineering, economics and machine learning. For recent comprehensive reviews, see
[
18
,
27
]
. A particularly appealing class of bi-level optimization problems are those in which only a single decision variable is involved at both layers of the problem. These special cases are often called simple bi-level optimization problems
[
17
]
. In this special instance, the problem is given by the convex constrained minimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}g(x),\quad\text{where}~\mathcal{S}_{0}=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\{f(x)+r(x)\},

\mathcal{S}_{0}
is the set of minimizers of a the inner level problem, where
f
and
g
are convex and continuously differentiable and
r
is an extended-valued proper closed convex function. This problem received significant attention over the last years, with important advances made in
[
8
,
38
,
33
,
5
,
40
]
and
[
43
]
. Moving beyond the convex optimization formulation of the inner and the outer level problem, recent papers consider a more general problem formulation by replacing them by variational inequalities. While this is a classical formulation in inverse problems and signal processing
[
19
,
30
,
42
]
, only a few papers obtain rates of convergence in terms of merit functions. Motivated by narrowing this gap, we consider the problem of solving a class of constrained variational inequality problems

(P)

\VI(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{S}_{0})\colon\text{ Find $u\in\mathcal{S}_{0}$ s.t. }\langle\mathsf{G}u,v-u\rangle\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{S}_{0}=\zer(\mathsf{M}),

where
\mathsf{G}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator, and
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\zer(\mathsf{M})
is the (non-empty) set of zeros of another maximally monotone operator
\mathsf{M}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
, all defined on a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norm
\|{\cdot}\|
. Besides the simple bi-level optimization problem mentioned above, the family of problems (
P
) contains many specific instances heavily studied in the literature.

Example 1.1 (Hierarchical Variational Inequality).

Given a mapping
\mathsf{F}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and a closed convex set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, the variational inequality problem
\VI(\mathsf{F},\mathcal{K})
, is the problem of finding a point
u\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\langle\mathsf{F}(u),v-u\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{K}.

The solution set of
\VI(\mathsf{F},\mathcal{K})
can be expressed as
\zer(\mathsf{F}+\NC_{\mathcal{K}})
, involving the normal cone operator

\NC_{\mathcal{K}}(v)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varnothing&\text{if }v\notin\mathcal{K}\\
\{p\in\mathcal{H}|\langle p,u-v\rangle\leq 0,\;\forall u\in\mathcal{K}\}&\text{ else.}\end{array}\right.

Hence, if we set the operator
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{F}+\NC_{\mathcal{K}}
, Problem (
P
) specializes to a hierarchical variational inequality problem given by

\VI(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{S}_{0}),\quad\text{where $\mathcal{S}_{0}$ is the solution set of $\VI(\mathsf{F},\mathcal{K})$}.

For recent studies of such problems in finite-dimensional euclidean spaces, we refer to
[
4
]
and
[
39
]
.

Example 1.2 (Equilibrium Selection).

A mathematical program with equilibrium constraints
[
28
]
optimizes a real-valued function over a feasible set described as the solution set of an equilibrium problem. As such, one can select amongst lower-level equilibria via a convex design criterion
g\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
by writing

\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{g(u)~~\colon~~u\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\right\}.

If
g
is convex and Fréchet differentiable, this becomes a hierarchical variational inequality with
\mathsf{G}=\nabla g
, hence a particular instance of Problem (
P
). This viewpoint covers a broad range of models encountered in, e.g., engineering, economics, inverse problems and control (see
[
23
,
hintermuller2015bi-level
,
22
,
35
,
de2023bi-level
]
).

1.1
Contributions

Numerical schemes with complexity statements for resolving Problem (
P
) have been recently published in the papers
[
24
,
39
]
and
[
4
]
.
[
24
]
extend the simple bi-level setting to a lower level problem defined in terms of a variational inequality while keeping the upper level problem as a convex optimization problem. This is a special instance of Problem (
P
), which is related to mathematical programming with equilibrium constraints
[
28
]
, and to the equilibrium selection problem in game theory
[
9
,
10
]
. The recent papers
[
39
,
4
]
go one step further and study the special instance of Problem (
P
) in which the upper and lower level problem are defined in terms of a variational inequality problem.

In this paper we generalize the aforementioned existing results by considering a more general class of hierarchical equilibrium problems admitting the formulation (
P
). We prove rates on both levels of the equilibrium problem via a very flexible numerical scheme, which can easily be modulated with respect to problem structure (specifically of the lower-level inclusion problem). Our main contributions are threefold:

•

Hilbert space setting.
We extend analyses that are typically confined to finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces to possibly infinite-dimensional real Hilbert spaces.

•

Monotone-inclusion lower level and modular oracle.
Rather than considering a variational inequality at the lower level, we allow the feasible set to be defined as the solution set of an abstract monotone inclusion. This formulation allows to draw from the rich theory of monotone operators and enables a very general algorithmic design based on a monotone-inclusion oracle obtained through a fixed-point encoding, which can be tailored to the structure of the operators involved.

•

Quantitative guarantees via gap functions.
While much of the existing literature focuses on weak convergence results without explicit rate statements, we introduce gap functions for both the lower- and upper-level problems and establish quantitative convergence guarantees for these measures. Our results are formulated within a specific geometric setting in which we assume that the feasibility gap function is assumed to satisfy an Hölderian error bound condition such as
[
13
]
in the context of hierarchical optimization and more recently
[
39
]
and
[
11
]
.

We achieve these results via the careful design of an algorithmic template drawing inspiration from classical diagonal methods involving iterative Tikhonov and proximal regularization via anchor terms. By constructing a family of auxiliary combined equilibrium problems, our method constructs a sequence in an inner loop method, which is designed to track a sequence of temporal solutions. This procedure is essentially an inertial Krasnoselskii-Mann iteration and thus only relies on the availability of a ready-to-compute fixed-point encoding map for the lower-level solution set
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\zer(\mathsf{M})
. An outer-loop procedure updates the Tikhonov parameter and the anchor term to steer the trajectory of the numerical algorithm towards a solution of the entire Problem (
P
).

Diagonal schemes for solving convex programs are a classical topic
[
2
]
. The idea of extending this approach to hierarchical equilibrium problems is taken from
[
21
]
and
[
25
]
. In particular,
[
25
]
also proposes a kind of double-loop scheme with a forward-backward iteration acting in the inner loop. In this paper, we consider a more general class of splitting problems, with explicit rate guarantees at both levels of the problem.

Specifically, in the case where
\mathsf{G}
is merely monotone (opposed to strongly monotone), we obtain weak convergence of the averaged sequence of iterates
(\overline{w}_{N})
, with rates of the form, for arbitrary
b\in(0,1)
,

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-b}}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\min(b,1-b)}}\right),

for suitably defined optimality and feasibility gaps (see Section
3.2
for precise definition). In this setting,
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
disregards logarithmic factors, which are natural consequences of the double-loop structure of our algorithm. Ignoring these logarithmic factors, which previous works do not obtain due to single-loop structures, our work is on par with established benchmarks:

•

In
[
24
, Corollary 3.5]
, the authors study optimization problems with Cartesian variational inequality constraints, where the objective is convex and the constraint set is a Cartesian product associated with a monotone mapping. Their algorithm resembles a forward-backward method, cast into a single-loop scheme. For the deterministic variant of their method, they obtain rates of
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{0.5-b})
for the optimality gap and
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{b})
for the feasibility gap, for
b\in(0,0.5)
. Therefore, ignoring the logarithmic factors, the rates achieved in the present work are more favorable.

•

In
[
39
, Theorem 3.6]
, the authors study a regularized extragradient method, and establish rates of
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{1-b})
for the optimality gap and
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{b})
for the feasibility gap. In the
b\in(0,0.5]
regime our rates match with theirs, and we improve upon their rates in the
b\in(0.5,1)
regime. We do again recall that our rates include logarithmic terms that their avoids. We note that
[
4
, Theorem 3.8]
studies an inertial version of the same method, but does not improve upon the rates.

•

The recent work
[
31
, Theorem 9]
analyzes a special case of our general algorithmic template, focusing on the forward-backward splitting only. The analysis provided is weaker, in particular, no rate statements are contained in that work.

When
\mathsf{G}
is strongly monotone, our rates improve to

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

These rates are comparable to those in
[
39
, Theorem 4.6]
, where a regularized extragradient method is analyzed. To the best of our knowledge, no other works address hierarchical programming problems in the strongly monotone setting in a manner that allows a direct comparison.

1.2
Notation and Definitions

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norm
\|{\cdot}\|
. A set-valued operator
\mathsf{A}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is monotone if
\langle u-v,b-a\rangle\geq 0
for all
(u,b),(v,a)\in\gr(\mathsf{A})\triangleq\{(v,a)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}|a\in\mathsf{A}(v)\}
. The operator
\mathsf{A}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is maximally monotone if it is monotone and there exists no other monotone operator whose graph properly contains
\gr(\mathsf{A})
. The resolvent of an operator
\mathsf{A}
is defined as
\resolvent_{\mathsf{A}}=(\Id+\mathsf{A})^{-1}
. If
\mathsf{A}
is maximally monotone, then the resolvent is a nonexpansive operator and thus single-valued. A mapping
\mathsf{T}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
quasi-contractive
if
\|Tx-p\|\leq q\|x-p\|
for all
p\in\text{Fix}(T)
and some
q\in(0,1)
. We refer to
[
7
]
for more details on operator theory.

For two sequences
(a_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
and
(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
, we say that
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for
n
sufficiently large. We say that
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}{b_{n}}/{a_{n}}=0
. Finally, we say that
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}{b_{n}}/{a_{n}}=C
.

2
Outline of the Algorithm

In this section, we first formulate the main assumptions imposed on Problem (
P
).

Assumption 1.

We assume the following on Problem (
P
):

1.

\mathsf{G}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a
\mu
-strongly monotone, with
\mu\geq 0
, and
L_{\mathsf{G}}
-Lipschitz continuous operator;

2.

\mathsf{M}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is maximally monotone with bounded domain;

3.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is nonempty.

Remark 2.1.

Assumption
1
guarantees that the overall Problem (
P
) has a solution. In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is a convex and compact subset of
\mathcal{H}

[
7
]
.

Remark 2.2.

We emphasize that our assumption includes the case
\mu=0
, in which
\mathsf{G}
is only Lipschitz and monotone.

For given parameters
(\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\infty)^{2}
and an anchor point
w\in\mathcal{H}
, we define the associated auxiliary problem as finding

(Aux)

\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M}+\beta\mathsf{G}+\alpha(\Id-w)).

Here,
\alpha>0
is a proximal parameter enforcing strong monotonicity, ensuring that the point
\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)
is the unique zero of
\mathsf{M}+\beta\mathsf{G}+\alpha(\Id-w)
. The parameter
\beta>0
is a Tikhonov parameter that balances the importance of the lower- and upper-level problems.

Our numerical scheme consists of two main procedures. The inner procedure is an inertial Krasnoselskii-Mann (KM) iteration, whose last iterate is designed to lie in a sufficiently small ball around the temporal solution
\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)
, whose radius is part of the parameters of the procedure. We use this last iterate as a new anchor point, and restart the KM iteration with fresh parameters. To this end, we assume access to a family of parameterized fixed-point mappings
\mathsf{T}_{k}\equiv\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, which satisfy the following condition.

Assumption 2.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
(w,\beta)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}
, the operator
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}
is a quasi-contraction with parameter
q_{k}\in(0,\overline{q})
for some
\overline{q}\in(0,1)
, sharing a common fixed point
p(w,\beta)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
\mathsf{T}_{k}^{(w,\beta)}
is related to Problem (
Aux
) via a nonexpansive fixed-point transportation map
\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that
\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)=\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}(p(w,\beta))
.

Concrete examples of such operators are provided in Section
4
. Generally, the operators may vary over the inner loop as a result of a varying step-size in the selected method.

Remark 2.3.

The proximal regularization parameter
\alpha>0
is treated in our approach as an external input to the algorithm. Hence, its concrete numerical value used in the computation affects the central funnel
\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)
, as well as the fixed-point encoding map
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}
and eventually also the fixed-point transportation map
\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}
. However, since it is a fixed parameter, we simplify the notation and omit an explicit dependence from the involved mappings and operators on the value of
\alpha
.

2.1
Inner Loop

The inner loop of our tracking algorithm employs the procedure
\mathtt{KM}(v,\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet},(\tau_{k})_{k},(\theta_{k})_{k},\varepsilon)
, see Algorithm
1
, which is an inertial Krasnoselskii-Mann iteration with user-provided parameters embodied in terms of the initial point
v\in\mathcal{H}
, fixed-point encoding map
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet}
satisfying Assumption
2
, as well as momentum parameters
(\tau_{k})_{k}
and relaxation parameters
(\theta_{k})_{k}
. This fixed-point iteration is designed to return an approximation of the central funnel fixed-point
\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)
via the fixed-point transportation map
\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}
.

Algorithm 1

Function
\mathtt{KM}(v,\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet},(\tau_{k})_{k},(\theta_{k})_{k},\varepsilon)

v_{1}=v_{0}:=v

Set
k=1

while

\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|>\varepsilon

do

k\leftarrow k+1

z_{k}\leftarrow v_{k}+\tau_{k}(v_{k}-v_{k-1})

v_{k+1}\leftarrow(1-\theta_{k})z_{k}+\theta_{k}\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}(z_{k})

end

while

return

v_{k+1}

Algorithm
1
employs the stopping time

(Stop)

\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)\triangleq\inf\{k\geq 1|\;\|{v_{k+1}-z_{k}}\|\leq\varepsilon\}.

This stopping criterion is motivated by the following approximation result.

Lemma 2.1.

Let
(\tau_{k})_{k}\subset[0,1],(\theta_{k})_{k}\subset[0,\overline{\theta}]
for
\overline{\theta}\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
(\mathsf{T}_{k})_{k}
be a sequence of operators satisfying Assumption
2
. Denote by
p
the common fixed-point of
(\mathsf{T}_{k})_{k}
. If
\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)
is finite and
v_{\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)+1}
is the result of Algorithm
1
, then

(2.1)

\|{v_{\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)+1}-p}\|\leq\mathtt{e}\triangleq\frac{\varepsilon}{\overline{\theta}}\left({1-\overline{\theta}}+\frac{\overline{q}}{1-\overline{q}}\right).

Proof.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, denote
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)
. By definition of
v_{k+1}
and quasi-contractiveness of
\mathsf{T}_{k}
, we get

\displaystyle\|{z_{k}-p}\|

\displaystyle=\|{z_{k}-\mathsf{T}_{k}(z_{k})+\mathsf{T}_{k}(z_{k})-p}\|\leq\|{\frac{v_{k+1}-z_{k}}{\theta_{k}}}\|+\|{\mathsf{T}_{k}(z_{k})-p}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta_{k}}+\overline{q}\cdot\|{z_{k}-p}\|.

Whence
\|{z_{k}-p}\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta_{k}(1-\overline{q})}
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\|{v_{\mathbb{K}+1}-p}\|

\displaystyle=\|{v_{\mathbb{K}+1}-\mathsf{T}_{k}(z{K})+\mathsf{T}_{k}(z{K})-p}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\frac{(1-\theta{K})(z{K}-v_{\mathbb{K}+1})}{\theta{K}}}\|+\|{\mathsf{T}_{k}(z{K})-p}\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\frac{1-\theta_{\mathbb{K}}}{\theta{K}}+\overline{q}\cdot\|{z{K}-p}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta{K}}\left({1-\theta_{\mathbb{K}}}+\frac{\overline{q}}{1-\overline{q}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\overline{\theta}}\left({1-\overline{\theta}}+\frac{\overline{q}}{1-\overline{q}}\right),

as wanted.

\blacksquare

Lemma
2.1
has important consequences for the tracking properties of the anchor points
w
which we recursively generate with the restarting procedure explained next. To give an outlook, consider the sequence
(v_{k})_{k}
produced by the function
\mathtt{KM}(v,\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet},(\tau_{k})_{k},(\theta_{k})_{k},\varepsilon)
, and let
w^{+}\triangleq\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}(v_{\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)+1})
. Lemma
2.1
guarantees that this point satisfies the tracking guarantee

(2.2)

\|{w^{+}-\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)}\|\leq\mathtt{e}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\overline{\theta}}\left({1-\overline{\theta}}+\frac{\overline{q}}{1-\overline{q}}\right),

by nonexpansiveness of
\mathsf{Z}^{(w,\beta)}
. The upper bound is thus of order
O(\varepsilon)
, and thus controlled by the user-defined targeted precision.

2.2
Restarting Procedure

Given an anchor point
w
, Algorithm
1
produces a sequence of points
(v_{k})
whose last iterate corresponds to the output of the function
\mathtt{KM}(v,\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet},(\tau_{k})_{k},(\theta_{k})_{k},\varepsilon)
and which provides a good candidate for a new anchor point, given the tracking property (
2.2
). Repeating this over time for a fixed number of iterations is the key updating step constituting our outer loop procedure. The mechanics of this procedure gives rise to an diagonal equilibrium tracking method (
DANTE
), formally described in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

DANTE
(DiAgoNal equilibrium Tracking mEthod)

w_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
(\tau_{0,k}),(\theta_{0,k})_{k}
given.

Set
\lambda_{0}=1
and
S_{0}=0
.

Set
\overline{w}_{0}=w_{0}
.

for

n=0,\ldots,N-1

do

Set
v=\mathtt{KM}(w_{n},\mathsf{T}^{(w_{n},\beta_{n})}_{n,\bullet},(\tau_{n,k})_{k},(\theta_{n,k})_{k},\varepsilon_{n})
.

Let
w_{n+1}=\mathsf{Z}^{(w_{n},\beta_{n})}(v)
denote the next anchor point.

Update
\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_{n}(1+\frac{2\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha})
and
S_{n+1}=S_{n}+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}

Update

\overline{w}_{n+1}=\frac{S_{n}\overline{w}_{n}+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}w_{n+1}}{S_{n+1}}.

Compute
\mathtt{e}_{n}
based on Equation (
2.1
).

Update
\beta_{n+1},(\tau_{n+1,k})_{k},(\theta_{n+1,k})_{k},\varepsilon_{n+1}
.

end

for

return

\overline{w}_{N}

Note that

is a method rather than an algorithm as we do not specify how the parameters required by the fixed-point iteration procedure
\mathtt{KM}(v,\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\bullet},(\tau_{k})_{k},(\theta_{k})_{k},\varepsilon)
are set. This is a part of the user-defined input and should be decided based on the eventually known problem structure.

Convergence Analysis

3.1
Convergence Analysis of Inner Loop

Before proving that Algorithm
1
terminates in finite time, we recall the following assumption from
[
15
]
. Note that the conditions for Lemma
2.1
are verified under Assumptions
2
and
3
, when considering a single outer iteration.

Assumption 3.

[
15
, Hypothesis 2.1]

Assume
(\theta_{k})_{k}\subset(0,\overline{\theta}]
and that
(\tau_{k})_{k}\subset[0,\overline{\tau}]
is monotonically non-decreasing, where
\overline{\theta},\overline{\tau}\in(0,1)
. Moreover, assume that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.1)

Q_{k}\tau_{k}(1+\tau_{k})+(\theta_{k}^{-1}-1)\tau_{k}(1-\tau_{k})-Q_{k}(\theta_{k-1}^{-1}-1)(1-\tau_{k-1})<0,

where
Q_{k}=1-\theta_{k}(1-q_{k}^{2})
, and that
\overline{Q}\triangleq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}Q_{k}<1
.

Under the above assumption, we can guarantee strong convergence of the iterates.

Theorem 3.1.

[
15
, Theorem 2.2]

Assume Assumptions
2
and
3
hold.
If
(z_{k},v_{k})_{k}
is generated by Algorithm
1
without stopping criterion, then
(z_{k},v_{k})_{k}
converges strongly to
(p,p)=(\textnormal{Fix}(\mathsf{T}_{k}),\textnormal{Fix}(\mathsf{T}_{k}))
. Moreover

\sum_{k\geq 1}\|{v_{k}-p}\|^{2}<\infty
, and specifically

\|{v_{k}-p}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\overline{Q}^{k}}{(1-\overline{\tau})(1-\overline{\theta})}\cdot\|{v_{0}-p}\|^{2}

for all
k\geq 1
.

An immediate corollary is that the stopping criterion in Algorithm
1
allows to conclude finite-time termination.

Corollary 3.2.

Assume Assumptions
2
and
3
hold. It holds that
\sum_{k\geq 1}\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|^{2}<\infty
.
Specifically,
\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|\to 0
, and hence Algorithm
1
terminates in finite time. Moreover, it holds that

\|{v_{k+1}-z_{k}}\|^{2}\leq\overline{Q}^{k-1}\cdot\frac{4(1+\overline{\tau})^{2}}{(1-\overline{\tau})(1-\overline{\theta})}\cdot\|{v_{0}-p}\|^{2}

for all
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{k+1}-p\|+\|z_{k}-p\|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{k+1}-p\|+(1+\tau_{k})\|v_{k}-p\|+\tau_{k}\|v_{k-1}-p\|

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\overline{\tau})\max(\|v_{k+1}-p\|,\|v_{k}-p\|,\|v^{k-1}-p\|).

The result follows by the square-summability of
(v_{k}-p)
, and the rate provided in Theorem
3.1
.

\blacksquare

The rate in Corollary
3.2
does not provide a user-friendly quantitative result unless one can control the quantity
\|v_{0}-p\|
. We present an additional set of assumptions under which this can be done.

Assumption 4.

We assume that
\dom(\mathsf{T}_{k})\subset\dom(\mathsf{M})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 3.1.

Assumption
4
might seem restrictive, but in fact, they are satisfied by many operators. For instance, if
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{F}+\NC_{\mathcal{K}}
, where
\NC_{\mathcal{K}}
is the normal cone of
{\mathcal{K}}=\dom(\mathsf{M})
and
\mathsf{F}
is a Lipschitz monotone operator, the forward-backward operator (
FB
) satisfies the desired conditions, as the resolvent of
\NC_{\mathcal{K}}
reduces to a projection onto
{\mathcal{K}}
, such that
\mathsf{T}(x)\in{\mathcal{K}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
. The forward-backward operator and other examples are discussed in Section
4
.

Under these assumptions, we can achieve a user-friendly rate, as presented in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3.

Assume Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
4
hold. Then

\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|\leq C\cdot\overline{Q}^{k/2},

for all
k\geq 1
, where

(Def-
C
)

C\triangleq\frac{2(1+\overline{\tau})}{\sqrt{\overline{Q}(1-\overline{\tau})(1-\overline{\theta})}}\cdot\diam(\dom(\mathsf{M})).

Specifically,

\mathbb{K}(\varepsilon)\leq\overline{K}_{\varepsilon}\triangleq\left\lceil 2\log(C/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\bar Q)\right\rceil.

Proof.

The conditions guarantee that all iterates are in
\dom(\mathsf{M})
, thus justifying the bounds. Moreover, since
\overline{Q}\in(0,1)
,
C\cdot\overline{Q}^{k/2}\leq\varepsilon
implies the bound on
k
.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.2.

By
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
, we also know that weak convergence of the iterates
(v_{k})
and the velocities
z_{k}
is guaranteed when
\mathsf{T}_{k}
is just nonexpansive. Moreover, if
\mathsf{T}_{k}
is a fixed-point encoding of Problem (
Aux
), which has a unique solution
p
by construction, we know that the sequence convergences weakly to this unique fixed-point
p
. Specifically, in the finite-dimensional setting, we know that
\|v_{k}-p\|\to 0
and
\|v_{k+1}-z_{k}\|\to 0
, meaning that Algorithm
2
still can be applied. It is however important to note that Lemma
2.1
no longer holds, and that we do not have an appropriate stopping criterion to guarantee
\|w^{+}-\overline{u}_{\beta}(w)\|\leq\mathtt{e}
. Within our final numerical implementation in Section
5.3
, we allow for nonexpansive operators, corresponding to
q=1
, heavily noting that the theoretical guarantees are weakened in this setting.

Remark 3.3.

Rather than assuming exact access to the operators
\mathsf{T}_{k}
, we could assume access to
\delta_{k}
-perturbations
\tilde{\mathsf{T}}_{k}
, with bounded inexactness model
0\leq\delta_{k}\leq\delta_{\max}
. Such an error structure is motivated by the fact that the exact map might in general not be available, or its computation may be very demanding. This occurs for instance when applying proximal methods to image deblurring with total variation
[
14
]
, or to structured sparsity regularization problems in machine learning and inverse problems
[
44
]
. In those cases, the proximity operator is usually computed using ad hoc algorithms, and therefore inexactly. The implications of inexact implementations are the following: Lemma
2.1
would still hold, by replacing
\varepsilon
by
\max(\varepsilon,\delta_{\max})
, provided
\delta_{\max}
is finite. Additionally, Theorem
3.1
would still hold
[
15
]
, provided
(\theta_{k}\delta_{k})\in\ell^{2}
, but without an explicit rate. As such, Corollary
3.2
still holds, but again without rate guarantees. Specifically, we have no apriori upper bound on the required number of iterations required for Algorithm
1
to terminate, but we know it will terminate.

3.2
Convergence Analysis of Restarting Procedure

In the following, we assume that the operator
\mathsf{G}
is
\mu
-strongly monotone with
\mu\geq 0
. The case in which
\mathsf{G}
is merely monotone is recovered by setting
\mu=0
throughout the subsequent analysis.

We introduce two gap functions, one associated with optimality and one with feasibility.

The
optimality gap
is defined as

(
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}
)

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(u)\triangleq\sup_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}\langle\mathsf{G}v,u-v\rangle.

We observe that if
u
is feasible, namely if
u\in\mathcal{S}_{0}=\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
, then the condition
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(u)\leq 0
is equivalent to
u\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{G}+\NC_{\mathcal{S}_{0}})
, such that
u
solves Problem (
P
). It is important to note, however, that
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(u)\leq 0
provides limited information when
u\notin\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
. It is thus important to obtain a lower bound on the optimality gap.

The
feasibility gap
is motivated by the observation that, by maximal monotonicity of
\mathsf{M}
, for any
x\in\mathcal{H}
,

x\in\mathcal{S}_{0}=\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})\Leftrightarrow\inf_{y\in\dom(\mathsf{M})}\inf_{v\in\mathsf{M}y}\langle 0-v,x-y\rangle\geq 0.

As such, we define the feasibility gap
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}
)

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(x)=\sup_{y\in\dom(\mathsf{M}),v\in\mathsf{M}y}\langle v,x-y\rangle.

We note that
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(x)\leq 0
implies
x\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
, and that
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(x)\geq 0
holds for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
since, by assumption, the problem admits at least one solution; in particular,
\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})\neq\varnothing
. Since
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}
and
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}
are pointwise suprema of affine functions, both are weakly lower semicontinuous.

Our convergence rates in terms of the optimality gap require a lower bound on the a priori signless optimality gap function
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}
. We derive such a lower bound within a specific geometric setting, imposing a Hölderian error bound on the feasibility gap.

Definition 3.4.

We say that the zero set
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
is weakly sharp with constant
\kappa>0
and order
\rho\geq 1
if, for all
u\in\dom(\mathsf{M})
,

(3.2)

\kappa\cdot\dist(u,\zer(\mathsf{M}))^{\rho}\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(u).

The notion of weak sharpness of zero sets is closely related to the classical concept of weak sharpness for variational inequalities
[
29
]
. Geometric conditions ensuring the error-bound characterization (
3.2
) can be found in
[
1
]
. The recent papers
[
39
]
and
[
3
]
rely also heavily on this assumption. An important class of examples arises when
\mathsf{M}
is single-valued and
\rho=1
, notably in monotone linear complementarity problems in finite dimensions under nondegeneracy conditions
[
12
,
36
]
. In the case where the lower-level problem is determined by a convex optimization problem, weak-sharpness implies an Hölderian errror bound, an assumption already imposed by
[
13
]
in the context of hierarchical minimization. Specifically, let us assume that
\mathsf{M}=\partial f
for a proper convex and lower semi-continuous function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Then,

\sup_{v\in\partial f(y)}\langle v,u-y\rangle=f^{\prime}(y;u-y)\leq f(u)-f(y),

so that
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(u)\leq f(u)-\min f
. It follows that Inequality (
3.2
) implies

\kappa\dist(u,\argmin f)^{\rho}\leq f(u)-\min f\qquad\forall u\in\mathcal{H}.

If
\rho=1
, this is the weak-sharpness condition of
[
12
]
. The case
\rho=2
corresponds to the "quadratic growth" condition of
[
20
]
. Rates of convergence under this Hölderian error bound assumption have recently been derived in
[
11
]
.

We next define the following quantities, which are finite because
\dom(\mathsf{M})
is assumed to be bounded:

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{M}}=\diam(\dom(\mathsf{M})),\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{M}}=\sup_{x\in\dom(\mathsf{M})}\sup_{v\in\mathsf{M}x}\|v\|,\quad

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{G}}=\sup_{x\in\dom(\mathsf{M})}\mathsf{G}x.

We also give more explicit expressions for the averaging coefficients
(\lambda_{n})_{n}
and the corresponding
averaged iterates
which are produced by
DANTE
. They read as

\lambda_{0}=1,\text{ and for all }n\geq 1,\;\lambda_{n}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(1+\frac{2\mu\beta_{i}}{\alpha}\right),\;\overline{w}_{N}=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}w_{n+1}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}.

In order to guarantee our convergence results, we must make some weak assumptions on the sequence of parameters.

Assumption 5.

We assume the following on the parameter sequences:

1.

\beta_{n}\in[0,\beta_{\max}]
is non-increasing and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}=0
.

2.

The sequence
(\lambda_{n}\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies
\sum_{n\geq 0}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}=\infty
, and

(3.3)

\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta^{2}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}\to 0\text{ as }N\to\infty.

3.

The sequence
(\mathtt{e}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies
\mathtt{e}_{n}=o(\beta_{n})
. We define
\mathtt{e}_{\max}=\max_{n\geq 1}\mathtt{e}_{n}
.

Note that since
\mathtt{e}_{n}=O(\varepsilon_{n})
, Assumption
5
implies
\varepsilon_{n}/\beta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty.
This means that when designing concrete parameters for implementing our algorithm, we can tailor the inner-loop tolerance
\varepsilon_{n}
to the evolution of the Tikhonov parameter
\beta_{n}\to 0
.

We are now ready to state the proposition that is central to our analysis.

Proposition 3.5.

For any
x\in\dom(\mathsf{M})
and
v\in\mathsf{M}x
, it holds that

(3.4)

\lambda_{n}\langle v,w_{n+1}-x\rangle+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-x\rangle\leq-\frac{\alpha\lambda_{n+1}}{2}\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\lambda_{n}}{2}\|w_{n}-x\|^{2}+C_{1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n},

where

(3.5)

C_{1}\triangleq 2\alpha\left(1+\frac{\mu\beta_{\max}}{\alpha}\right)(\mathtt{e}_{\max}+D_{\mathsf{M}})+\frac{C_{\mathsf{M}}}{\alpha}+\frac{\beta_{\max}C_{\mathsf{G}}}{\alpha}.

Proof.

First, expand

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n+1}-w_{n}\|^{2}+\langle w_{n+1}-w_{n},w_{n+1}-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle w_{n}-\overline{u}_{n+1},x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+\langle w_{n+1}-w_{n},w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\overline{u}_{n+1}-w_{n+1},x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle.

For the last two terms, we obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\langle w_{n+1}-w_{n},w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|{w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}}\|+\|{\overline{u}_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n}}\|+\|{\overline{u}_{n}-w_{n}}\|\right)\cdot\|{w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}}\|

\displaystyle\leq(\mathtt{e}_{n}+D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{n-1})\mathtt{e}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(D_{\mathsf{M}}+2\mathtt{e}_{max})\mathtt{e}_{n},

and

\langle\overline{u}_{n+1}-w_{n+1},\overline{u}_{n+1}-x\rangle\leq\|{\overline{u}_{n+1}-w_{n+1}}\|\cdot\|{\overline{u}_{n+1}-x}\|\leq D_{\mathsf{M}}\mathtt{e}_{n}.

Combining these inequalities yields

\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n}-x\|^{2}\leq\langle w_{n}-\overline{u}_{n+1},x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+2(D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{\max})\mathtt{e}_{n}.

As
-\beta_{n}\mathsf{G}\overline{u}_{n+1}-\alpha(\overline{u}_{n+1}-w_{n})\in\mathsf{M}\overline{u}_{n+1}
and
v\in\mathsf{M}x
, it follows by monotonicity of
\mathsf{M}
and by
\mu
-monotonicity of
\mathsf{G}
that

\displaystyle\langle w_{n}-\overline{u}_{n+1},x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle v,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\langle\mathsf{G}\overline{u}_{n+1},x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle v,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle-\frac{\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\|x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|^{2}.

Now we note that

\displaystyle\|x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x-w_{n+1}\|^{2}+\|w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|^{2}+2\langle x-w_{n+1},w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|x-w_{n+1}\|^{2}-2(\|x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|+\|\overline{u}_{n+1}-w_{n+1}\|)\cdot\|w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\geq\|x-w_{n+1}\|^{2}-2(D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{\max})\mathtt{e}_{n}.

Combining the previous three relationships yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle v,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle-\frac{\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\|{x-w_{n+1}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\frac{\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha}(D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{max})\mathtt{e}_{n}+2(D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{\max})\mathtt{e}_{n}.

Rearranging the terms and bounding
\beta_{n}\leq\beta_{\max}
gives

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\right)\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{n}-x\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\langle v,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle+2\left(1+\frac{\mu\beta_{\max}}{\alpha}\right)(D_{\mathsf{M}}+\mathtt{e}_{\max})\mathtt{e}_{n}.

After noting that

\displaystyle\langle v,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle=\langle v,x-w_{n+1}\rangle+\langle v,w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle\leq\langle v,x-w_{n+1}\rangle+C_{\mathsf{M}}\mathtt{e}_{n},

and

\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle=\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-w_{n+1}\rangle+\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\rangle\leq\langle\mathsf{G}x,x-w_{n+1}\rangle+C_{\mathsf{G}}\mathtt{e}_{n},

and multiplying both sides by
\lambda_{n}\alpha
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\langle v,w_{n+1}-x\rangle

\displaystyle+\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu\beta_{n}}{\alpha}\right)\lambda_{n}\alpha\|w_{n+1}-x\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{n}\alpha}{2}\|{w_{n}-x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(C_{\mathsf{M}}+\beta_{max}C_{\mathsf{G}}+2\left(1+\frac{\mu\beta_{\max}}{\alpha}\right)(\mathtt{e}_{\max}+D_{\mathsf{M}})\alpha\right)\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}.

The results follow by recalling the definition of
\lambda_{n}
.

\blacksquare

The lower bound in the preceding proposition enables us to deduce bounds on both the optimality and feasibility gaps, provided the remaining term can be controlled. The next two lemmas accomplish precisely this.

Lemma 3.6 (Optimality).

For all
N\geq 1
, it holds that

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq C_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}},

where
C_{1}
is defined in eq. (
3.5
) and

(3.6)

C_{2}\triangleq\alpha(\mathtt{e}_{0}^{2}+D_{\mathsf{M}}^{2}).

In particular, if Assumption
5
holds, all weak accumulation points
\tilde{w}
of
(\overline{w}_{n})
satisfy
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\tilde{w})\leq 0
.

Proof.

Select
x\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
and
v=0
in (
3.4
), and sum the result for
n=0,\ldots,N-1
to obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-\alpha\lambda_{N}}{2}\|w_{N}-x\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\lambda_{0}}{2}\|w_{0}-x\|^{2}+C_{1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq{\alpha\lambda_{0}}(\|w_{0}-\overline{u}_{0}\|^{2}+\|\overline{u}_{0}-x\|^{2})+C_{1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq{\alpha}(\mathtt{e}_{0}^{2}+D_{\mathsf{M}}^{2})+C_{1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}.

After normalization, this yields

\langle\mathsf{G}x,\overline{w}_{N}-x\rangle\leq C_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}.

The inequality follows by taking the supremum over
x\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
. The convergence conclusion follows by weak sequential lower-semicontinuity of
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 3.7 (Feasibility).

Assume
(\beta_{n})
non-increasing. For all
N\geq 1
, it holds that

0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq C_{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{4}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}},

where

(3.7)

C_{3}=\beta_{0}C_{2},\quad C_{4}\triangleq C_{\mathsf{G}}(\mathtt{e}_{\max}+D_{\mathsf{M}})

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are defined in Equations (
3.5
) and (
3.6
).
In particular, if Assumption
5
holds, all weak cluster point
\tilde{w}
of
(\overline{w}_{n})
satisfy
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\tilde{w})=0
, such that
\tilde{w}\in\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
.

Proof.

Multiply both sides of (
3.4
) by
\beta_{n}
, and recall that
\beta_{n+1}\leq\beta_{n}
. Moreover, notice that

\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-x\rangle\geq-\|{\mathsf{G}x}\|\cdot\|{w_{n+1}-x}\|\geq-\|\mathsf{G}x\|\cdot(\|w_{n+1}-\overline{u}_{n+1}\|+\|\overline{u}_{n+1}-x\|)\geq-C_{\mathsf{G}}(\mathtt{e}_{n}+D_{\mathsf{M}}).

It then follows

-\langle\mathsf{G}x,w_{n+1}-x\rangle\leq C_{\mathsf{G}}(\mathtt{e}_{\max}+D_{\mathsf{M}})
. Summing for
n=0,\ldots,N-1
then yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\langle v,w_{n+1}-x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\alpha\lambda_{N}\beta_{N}}{2}\|w_{N}-x\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha\lambda_{0}\beta_{0}}{2}\|w_{0}-x\|^{2}+C_{1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\mathsf{G}}(\mathtt{e}_{\max}+D_{\mathsf{M}})\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}+C_{1}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}+C_{4}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}.

By dividing by
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}
and taking the supremum over points
x\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
on both sides, we obtain the claimed inequality. The convergence conclusion again follows by weak sequential lower semicontinuity of
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}
.

\blacksquare

We may now combine the two previous lemmas to state our main theorem.

Theorem 3.8 (Main Theorem).

Let
(w_{n})_{n=0}^{N-1}
be generated through Algorithm
2
. Assume
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is non-increasing. Then the following statements hold true:

(a)

For all
N\geq 1
, the optimality gap is bounded as

(3.8)

-C_{\mathsf{G}}\cdot\dist(\overline{w}_{N},\mathcal{S}_{0})\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq C_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are problem-specific constants defined in (
3.5
) and (
3.6
).

(b)

For all
N\geq 1
, the feasibility gap is bounded as

(3.9)

0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq C_{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{4}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}},

where
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
are constants defined in (
3.7
).

(c)

With Assumption
5
in place, all weak accumulation points of
(\overline{w}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are solutions to (
P
).

(d)

If, moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is weakly sharp with constant
\kappa
or order
\rho\geq 1
, then it also holds that

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\geq-C_{\mathsf{G}}\kappa^{-1/\rho}\cdot\left(C_{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{4}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}\right)^{1/\rho}.

Proof.

(a)

The upper bound is just Lemma
3.6
. For the lower bound, we invoke the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\langle\mathsf{G}x,\overline{w}_{N}-x\rangle\geq\|{\mathsf{G}x}\|\cdot\|{\overline{w}_{N}-x}\|
, implying

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\geq-C_{\mathsf{G}}\dist(\overline{w}_{N},\mathcal{S}_{0}).

This proves the assertion.

(b)

This is just Lemma
3.7
.

(c)

Combine Lemma
3.6
with Lemma
3.7
.

(d)

If
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is weakly sharp with constant
\kappa
of order
\rho\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})

\displaystyle\geq-C_{\mathsf{G}}\dist(\overline{w}_{N},\mathcal{S}_{0})

\displaystyle\geq-C_{\mathsf{G}}\left[\frac{1}{\kappa}\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{n})\right]^{1/\rho}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{C_{\mathsf{G}}}{\kappa^{1/\rho}}\left(C_{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{4}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}+C_{1}\cdot\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}\mathtt{e}_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}}\right)^{1/\rho}.

\blacksquare

By selecting specific regularization parameters, we may obtain explicit rates on the convergent quantities, allowing direct comparisons to prior works. The choice of parameters differs between the solely monotone setting, detailed below, and the strongly monotone setting, detailed in Corollary
3.10
.

Corollary 3.9.

Let
(w_{n})
be generated through Algorithm
2
when
\mathsf{G}
is merely monotone (i.e.
\mu=0
). Let
\beta_{n}=(n+1)^{-b}
for
b\in(0,1)
and choose
\varepsilon_{n}
such that
\mathtt{e}_{n}=o(\beta_{n})
. Then, for all
N\geq 2^{1/(1-b)}
, it holds that

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-b}}\right),

as well

0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{b}}\right)&\text{if $b\in(0,0.5)$}\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln(N)}{N^{1/2}}\right)&\text{if $b=0.5$}\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-b}}\right)&\text{if $b\in(0.5,1)$}.\end{cases}

If, moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is weakly sharp with constant
\kappa>0
of order
\rho\geq 1
, then it also holds that

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\geq\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})^{1/\rho}).

Proof.

By standard integral bounds, one can show that for
c\in(0,1)
and
N\geq 2^{1/(1-b)}
,

\frac{N^{1-c}}{2(1-c)}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(n+1)^{-c}\leq\frac{N^{1-c}}{1-c}.

Specifically,
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta_{n}=\Theta(N^{1-b})
, and

\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta_{n}^{2}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta_{n}}=\begin{cases}\Theta(N^{-b})&\text{if $b\in(0,0.5)$}\\
\Theta(\ln(N)N^{-1/2})&\text{if $b=0.5$}\\
\Theta(N^{b-1})&\text{if $b\in(0.5,1)$}\end{cases}.

As
\mathtt{e}_{n}=o(\beta_{n})
, the conclusions follow by Theorem
3.8
.

\blacksquare

Corollary 3.10.

Let
(w_{n})_{n=0}^{N}
be generated through Algorithm
2
when
\mathsf{G}
is strongly monotone (i.e.
\mu>0
). Let
\beta_{n}=\frac{\alpha}{2\mu n+\xi}
for
\xi>0
and let
\mathtt{e}_{n}=o(\beta_{n})
. For all
N
sufficiently large, it holds that

\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

and that

0\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(N)}{N}\right).

If, moreover,
\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{M})
is weakly sharp with constant
\kappa
of order
\rho\geq 1
, then it also holds that
\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{opt}}(\overline{w}_{N})\geq\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Gap}_{\operatorname{feas}}(\overline{w}_{N})^{1/\rho}).

Proof.

Under the stated hypothesis, one easily computes that
\lambda_{n}=\frac{2\mu n+\xi}{\xi}
. As such,

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}=\frac{N\alpha}{\xi},

and

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}\beta_{n}^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\xi}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{2\mu n+\xi}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\xi}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\xi}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\log\frac{2\mu N+\xi}{\xi}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\log(N)).

The conclusion follows by applying these estimates to Theorem
3.8
.

\blacksquare

3.3
Combined Complexity Analysis

We now finally perform the complete complexity analysis of our scheme
DANTE
by combining our bounds derived for Algorithms
1
and
2
.

Before stating the final convergence result, we make one final assumption.

Assumption 6.

We assume
\mathsf{Z}_{n}
is domain-forward on
\dom(\mathsf{M})
, namely that
\mathsf{Z}_{n}(\dom(\mathsf{M}))\subset\dom(\mathsf{M})
. Finally, we assume that
\theta_{n,k}
is uniformly lower bounded by a positive quantity, such that
\overline{Q}_{n}\leq\overline{Q}<1
.

Remark 3.4.

The domain-forwardness assumption may appear restrictive on the first glance. However, for all relevant fixed-point mappings from Section
4
, the fixed-point transportation map will take the form of a resolvent operator for which the requirement is naturally fulfilled due to maximal monotonicity of the operator.

Theorem 3.11.

Assume the setting of Corollary
3.3
and that Assumptions
2
,
3
,
4
and
6
hold. Algorithm
2
(
DANTE
) reaches a point
\overline{w}_{N}
satisfying the inequalities in Theorem
3.8
after at most

K=\frac{N(2\log(C)+\log(\bar Q))+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log(1/\varepsilon_n)}{\log(1/\bar Q)}

inner iterations. If
\varepsilon_{n}=(n+1)^{-\eta}
where
\eta\geq b
, we reach the desired point
\overline{w}_{N}
after at most
{\mathcal{O}}(N\log N)
inner iterations.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3
, each outer loop
n\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
runs at most

\overline{K}_{n}=\left\lceil\frac{2\log(C)+2\log(1/\varepsilon_n)}{\log(1/\bar Q)}\right\rceil\leq\frac{2\log(C)+2\log(1/\varepsilon_n)}{\log(1/\bar Q)}+1

inner iterations. Specifically, the inequalities of Theorem
3.8
are satisfied after at most

K=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\overline{K}_{n}\leq\frac{2N\log(C)+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log(1/\varepsilon_n)}{\log(1/\bar Q)}+N=\mathcal{O}\left(N+\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\log(1/\varepsilon_n)\right)

By setting
\varepsilon_{n}=(n+1)^{-\eta}
, we obtain that
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\log(1/\varepsilon_n)\leq\eta N\log(N)
, as wanted.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.5.

Theorem
3.11
shows that accounting for the total number of inner iterations, rather than only the number of outer iterations, introduces merely a logarithmic factor. This is expected, as double-loop schemes inherently incur such a term. Consequently, the complexities stated in Corollaries
3.9
and
3.10
are preserved, up to logarithmic factors, when measured in terms of total inner iterations.

4
Relevant Splitting Methods

Depending on the structure of
\mathsf{M}
in Problem (
P
), the auxiliary problem (
Aux
) inherits different structural assumptions. The fixed-point encodings
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}
must be readily available for the numerical computations, and hence should exploit known problem structure. In Section
4.1
, we analyze the case where
\mathsf{M}
may be written as the sum of two operators, and in Section
4.2
, we consider a three-operator splitting scheme.

Remark 4.1.

With the following examples it will become clear that the fixed-point encoding
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}
not only depends on the problem parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
but also on the step size
\gamma_{k}
. Therefore, we treat the step size policy is treated in our approach as an ingredient of the fixed-point encoding strategy and consequently incorporated in the map
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{k}
, rather than being a parameter of the inner loop presented in Algorithm
1
.

4.1
Two-Operator Splitting Schemes

In this section, we focus on problems of the form (
P
) with
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{F}
, namely

(P-2-Split)

\VI(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{S}_{0}),\quad\text{where }\mathcal{S}_{0}\triangleq\zer(\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{F}).

We refine the problem assumptions through the following assumption:

Assumption 7.

We assume the following on Problem (
P-2-Split
):

1.

The lower level solution set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is nonempty;

2.

\mathsf{G}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is monotone and
L_{\mathsf{G}}
-Lipschitz continuous;

3.

\mathsf{F}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is monotone and
L_{\mathsf{F}}
-Lipschitz continuous;

4.

\mathsf{A}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
is maximally monotone with bounded domain.

These assumptions imply that
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{F}
is maximally monotone with
\dom(\mathsf{M})=\dom(\mathsf{A})
.

Given the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
we define the function
\Phi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
by

\Phi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(v)\triangleq\mathsf{F}(v)+\beta\mathsf{G}(v)+\alpha(v-w),

which is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{\alpha,\beta}\triangleq L_{\mathsf{F}}+\beta L_{\mathsf{G}}+\alpha
and
\alpha
-strongly monotone. Adopting this notation, we can rewrite the auxiliary problem (
Aux
) as determining the unique element of
\zer(\mathsf{A}+\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})
.

Forward-backward splitting.

The Forward-Backward splitting
[
26
,
37
]
is a very popular numerical scheme, defined in terms of the fixed-point encoding map
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
given by

(FB)

\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}=\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}\circ(\Id-\gamma\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w}).

We note that
\operatorname{Fix}(\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma})=\operatorname{Zer}(A+\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})
, so
\mathsf{Z}=\Id
is the fixed-point transportation map. This is the setting studied in
[
31
]
.

Lemma 4.1.

Assume Assumption
7
holds and let
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
be defined as (
FB
).
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is
q
-Lipschitz where

q\triangleq\sqrt{1-\gamma(2\alpha-\gamma L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})},

uniformly over
w\in\mathcal{H}
. Hence,
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is a contraction when
\gamma\in(0,2\alpha/L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})
.

Proof.

Pick
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
. By nonexpansiveness of
\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}
, and
L_{\alpha,\beta}
-Lipschitz continuity and
\alpha
-strong monotonicity of
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}
,

\displaystyle\|{T(z_{1})-T(z_{2})}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}(z_{1}-\gamma\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},w))-\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}(z_{2}-\gamma\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{2},w))}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{z_{1}-z_{2}+\gamma(\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{2},w)-\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{2},w))}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\|^{2}+2\langle z_{1}-z_{2},\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{2},w)-\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},w)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma^{2}\|{\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},w)-\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{2},w)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\|^{2}-2\gamma\alpha\|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\|^{2}+L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma^{2}\|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-2\gamma\alpha+L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma^{2})\cdot\|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\|^{2}.

The contraction property follows by choosing
\gamma
so that
\gamma(2\alpha-\gamma L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})\in(0,1)
.

\blacksquare

Backward-forward splitting.

In a similar way, we can employ the Backward-Forward splitting
[
6
]
for the auxiliary problem. Consider the operator

(BF)

\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}=(\Id-\gamma\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})\circ\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}.

Note that
\zer(\mathsf{A}+\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})=\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}(\text{Fix}(\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}))
, which means that the last iterate of the inner loop scheme needs one more evaluation of the resolvent of the operator
\mathsf{A}
in order to get close to the unique zero of
\mathsf{A}+\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w}
. Specifically, the fixed-point transportation map is
\mathsf{Z}=\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}
.

Lemma 4.2.

Assume Assumption
7
holds and let
\mathsf{T}_{\gamma}
be the fixed-point encoding defined in (
BF
). It holds that
\mathsf{T}_{\gamma}
is
q
-Lipschitz where

q=\sqrt{1-\gamma(2\alpha-\gamma L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})},

uniformly over
w\in\mathcal{H}
. In particular,
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is a contraction when
\gamma\in(0,2\alpha/L^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Lemma
4.1
.

\blacksquare

Douglas-Rachford splitting.

Analogously, we can also consider the Douglas-Rachford splitting
[
26
]
, whose operator
\mathsf{T}
is given by

(DR)

\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}=\frac{1}{2}(\Id+R_{\Phi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}}\circ R_{\mathsf{A}}).

where
R_{\bullet}
is the reflected resolvent given by
R_{\bullet}=2\resolvent_{\bullet}-\Id
. Note that
\resolvent_{\mathsf{A}}(\operatorname{Fix}(\mathsf{T}))=\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{A}+\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})
, such that
\mathsf{Z}=\resolvent_{\mathsf{A}}
takes over the role of the fixed-point transportation map.

Lemma 4.3.

[
34
, Corollary 3.1]

Assume Assumption
7
holds and let
\mathsf{T}
be the fixed-point encoding defined in (
DR
). It holds that
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}
is
q
-Lipschitz where

q=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1-2\alpha+L_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}}{1+2\alpha+L_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

uniformly over
w\in\mathcal{H}
. In particular,
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}
is a contraction.

4.2
Three-Operator Splitting Scheme

In this section we focus on problems of the form (
P
) with
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{F}+\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}
, namely

(P-3-Split)

\VI(\mathsf{G},\mathcal{S}_{0}),\quad\text{where }\mathcal{S}_{0}\triangleq\zer(\mathsf{F}+\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}).

We refine the problem assumptions through the following assumption:

Assumption 8.

We assume the following on Problem (
P-3-Split
):

1.

the set
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is nonempty;

2.

\mathsf{G}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is monotone and
L_{\mathsf{G}}
-Lipschitz continuous;

3.

\mathsf{F}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
L_{\mathsf{F}}
-Lipschitz continuous;

4.

\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
are maximally monotone such that
\mathsf{A}
has bounded domain satisfying
\dom(\mathsf{A})\subset\dom(\mathsf{B})
.

Under these assumptions,
\dom(\mathsf{M})=\dom(\mathsf{A})
. We note that in most practical examples,
\dom(\mathsf{B})=\mathcal{H}
, such that
\dom(\mathsf{A})\subset\dom(\mathsf{B})
holds naturally.

We consider the auxiliary function
\Psi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
given by

\Psi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(v):=\mathsf{F}(v)+\beta\mathsf{G}(v)+\alpha(v-w),

which is
\alpha
-strongly monotone and
L_{\alpha,\beta}\triangleq L_{\mathsf{F}}+\beta L_{\mathsf{G}}+\alpha
-Lipschitz continuous, and hence
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}=\alpha/L_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}
-cocoercive. The auxiliary Problem (
Aux
) reads as
\zer(\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\Psi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta})
, and may be solved through a three-operator splitting scheme
[
16
]
, namely through the fixed-point encoding map
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
depending on a step-size
\gamma
given by

(TOS)

\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}=\Id-\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{B}}+\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}\circ(2\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{B}}-\Id-\gamma\Psi^{w}_{\alpha,\beta}\circ\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{B}}).

We know that
\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}(\operatorname{Fix}(\mathsf{T}))=\operatorname{Zer}(\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}+\Psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{w})
, such that
\mathsf{Z}=\resolvent_{\gamma\mathsf{A}}
is the fixed-point transportation map. The operator
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is non-expansive under the provided assumptions, and a contraction for well-chosen
\gamma
.

Lemma 4.4.

[
16
, Theorem D.6]

Let
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
be the fixed-point encoding defined in (
TOS
). It holds that
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is non-expansive. Moreover, if
\mathsf{B}
is
L_{\mathsf{B}}
-Lipschitz continuous and
\gamma<\eta\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
, then, uniformly over
w\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is
\sqrt{1-q}
-Lipschitz with constant

q=\frac{2\gamma\alpha(1-\eta)}{(1+\gamma L_{B})^{2}},

where
\eta\in[0,1]
is arbitrary. In particular,
\mathsf{T}^{(w,\beta)}_{\gamma}
is a contraction when

\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{2\alpha(1-\eta)-2L_{\mathsf{B}}+\sqrt{4\alpha^{2}(1-\eta^{2})-8\alpha(1-\eta)L_{\mathsf{B}}}}{2L_{\mathsf{B}}^{2}}\right).

Remark 4.2.

Although we require
\mathsf{B}
to be Lipschitz continuous for Assumption
2
to be satisfied, we shall allow it not to be in our implementations, as per Remark
3.2
. This is specifically the case in Section
5.3
.

Equilibrium selection

We consider the two-player zero-sum game from

\begin{cases}&\min_{x_{1}}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\triangleq 20-0.1x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}\\

(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*})\in X\triangleq X_{1}\times X_{2}

f(x_{1},x_{2})\triangleq 20-0.1x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}

x_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\

5.2
Least-Norm Least-Squares

Inspired by
[
41
]
, for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times Q}
and a vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
, we consider the bilevel optimization problem given by

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}~\colon~u\in\argmin_{v\in X}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|{Av-b}\|^{2}\right\}\right\},

where
X=[-1000,1000]^{Q}
. We can reformulate this as a hierarchical variational inequality problem of the form (
P-2-Split
) with
\mathsf{G}(u)=u
,
\mathsf{A}=\partial\delta_{X}=\NC_{X}
, and
\mathsf{F}(v)=2A^{\top}(Av-b)
. We will make use of the Forward-Backward operator for the inner loop iterations.

Problem Setup.

We test the algorithm on four randomly generated instances with different dimensions given by

(P,Q)\in\{(70,100),(100,200),(100,500),(300,500)\}
. For each instance, the matrix
A
is generated as a low-rank (and sparse) matrix via
A=U_{1}\cdot U_{2}
,
where
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times R}
and
U_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times Q}
are chosen such that each component follows a standard normal law, with
R=50
to induce a low-rank structure. Moreover, to ensure the eigenvalues are not too large such that the step-size is not too small, we clip each singular value to the interval
[0,10]
. A vector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{Q}
is randomly generated with
20
non-zero entries according to a uniform law on
[0,10]
, and the observation vector
b
is constructed as
b=As+\nu
, where
\nu
is a normal random vector with small entries, namely a standard normal vector scaled by a factor
0.1
. During the random generation we ensure that
z=A^{\dagger}b\in X
, such that the analytical solution is known to be
z
, where
A^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse.

For the inner loop, we set

5.3
Image Inpainting

Finally, we consider the image inpainting problem, as done in
[
32
,
15
]
. We represent a grayscale image
Y
of dimension
(P,Q)
by a matrix in
X\triangleq[0,1]^{P\times Q}\subset\mathcal{H}\triangleq\mathbb{R}^{P\times Q}
. We denote by
\Omega\in\{0,1\}^{P\times Q}
a mask such that
\Omega_{i,j}=0
indicates that the pixel at position
(i,j)
has been damaged. We denote by
\mathcal{R}
the linear operator that maps an image to an image whose elements in
\Omega
have been corrupted, namely
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},Y\mapsto\tilde{Y}
with
\tilde{Y}_{i,j}=\Omega_{i,j}\cdot Y_{i,j}
. We note that
\mathcal{R}
is a self-adjoint bounded linear operator with operator norm
1
. We define the corrupt image by
Y_{\text{corrupt}}\triangleq\mathcal{R}(Y)
. The aim of the image inpainting problem is to recover the original image
Y
from the corrupt image
Y_{\text{corrupt}}
and the corruption map
\mathcal{R}
. Mathematically, we consider the problem

\min_{Y\in X}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{R}(Y)-Y_{\text{corrupt}}\|^{2}+\sigma\|Y\|_{*}\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
denotes the nuclear norm, which prones smoothness within the image, and
\sigma
is a regularization parameter. Specifically, we seek the least-norm solution to the above problem, given by

\min_{Y\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|Y\|^{2}~\colon~Y\in\argmin_{Y\in X}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{R}(Y)-Y_{\text{corrupt}}\|^{2}+\sigma\|Y\|_{*}\right\}\right\}.

This matches Problem (
P-3-Split
) with
\mathsf{G}=\Id
,
\mathsf{A}=\sigma\partial\|\cdot\|_{*}
,
\mathsf{B}=\NC_{X}
, and
\mathsf{F}(Y)=\mathcal{R}^{*}(\mathcal{R}(Y)-Y_{\text{corrupt}})
. We note that Assumption
8
is satisfied, and that the operator
\mathsf{T}
is nonexpansive per Lemma
4.4
. Although this setting does not strictly conform to our theoretical findings, its convergence properties are expected to be analogous due to the finite-dimensional nature of the problem, as detailed in Remark
4.2
.

\theta_{k}\equiv\theta=0.75
and we consider

to be constantly the largest value satisfying Equation (
3.1
) across every outer iteration. We set
\beta_{n}=(n+1)^{-\eta}
for
\eta=0.55
and
\varepsilon_{n}=\overline{\varepsilon}\cdot(n+1)^{-2}
for
\overline{\varepsilon}=2
. We run a total of
5000
iterations, with an initial point
w_{0}=Y_{\text{corrupt}}
. We set the regularization parameter to be
\sigma=50
, and generate
\Omega
randomly such that
20\%
of the image is corrupted.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a double loop path following method for solving a hierarchical variational inequalities. The inner loop employs a Krasnoselskii-Mann type iteration for solving a suitable auxiliary problem. The outer loop takes the form of a restarting procedure, giving rise to a diagonal equilibrium tracking method (
DANTE
). The obtained convergence rates are comparable with the state of the art results, obtained for
specific
numerical schemes. Our analysis is very flexible with respect to the chosen fixed-point encoding strategy of the lower level solution. Importantly, our rates are independent of the specific fixed-point encoding, as long as the stated assumptions are satisfied. We belief that our work opens up the possibilities for various interesting directions for future research. First, it would be very interesting to device a fully adaptive scheme without restarts. Second, an important future extension is the inclusion of stochastic data. All these directions are the subject of ongoing research.
