Ultra‐Wideline 2D Correlations Among Low‐γ Species in Solid‐State NMR via the Progressive Saturation of a Common Proton Reservoir
Authors: Rihards Aleksis, Lucio Frydman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUltra‐wideline nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra arising from large chemical shift anisotropy and/or quadrupolar broadening can span several MHz, rendering two‐dimensional (2D) correlations between low‐γ nuclei subject to these interactions exceptionally challenging. The limited pulse excitation bandwidth, poor sensitivity, and weak internuclear couplings make correlations between such spins nearly impossible in practice. To deal with such cases, this study introduces the PROgressive Saturation of the Protons Enhanced Correlation Technique (PROSPECT) experiment. PROSPECT correlates the powder spectra of low‐γ nuclei through their independent dipolar interactions with a common 1H reservoir, without requiring direct coupling between the correlated spins. 2D homonuclear and heteronuclear correlations are demonstrated experimentally using this method between 195Pt and 14N spins, dealing with resonances that span over 1 MHz. Spin dynamic numerical simulations reproduce very well the experimental 2D line shapes, and support our physical description of the method. In this way, PROSPECT extends multidimensional solid‐state NMR to regimes previously considered inaccessible, enabling correlations between ultra‐wideline anisotropic interactions.Keywords: 2D correlations, dipolar order, NMR crystallography, solid‐state NMR, ultra‐wideline NMR

Introduction

Solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) provides detailed insights into the local structure, bonding, and dynamics of crystalline and amorphous materials [1]. NMR can provide all this information even when solids lack long‐range order, thereby opening unique opportunities to study systems such as glasses [2], polymers [3], minerals [4, 5], and biomaterials [6, 7]. In many cases, however, the characterization of these environments proves highly challenging, as the targeted nuclei are often subject to very large anisotropic interactions. These include heavy metal spin‐1/2 species subject to a large chemical shift anisotropy (CSA), or nuclei with I>1/2 subject to quadrupolar interactions. Although the ensuing anisotropic broadenings are highly informative about structure and dynamics [8], their resulting ultra‐wideline (UW) NMR spectra can span several MHz. This substantial broadening reduces sensitivity by distributing the signal over a continuum of frequencies. For static powders, this sensitivity loss can be improved in part by employing Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) acquisitions [9, 10]. Still, conventional radiofrequency (RF) pulses find serious instrumental limitations when applying CPMG sequences on such wide resonances. Coupling CPMG with wideband, uniform rate, and smooth truncation (WURST) pulses [11] improves the excitation and refocusing bandwidth [12, 13]. Additional sensitivity gains can be achieved through polarization transfer from abundant 1H spins to unreceptive nuclei using the broadband adiabatic inversion cross‐polarization (BRAIN‐CP) sequence [14, 15]. Still, anisotropic interactions of quadrupolar and/or heavy nuclei often exceed the RF bandwidth of WURST‐CPMG or BRAIN‐CP pulses—as limited by the NMR probe hardware and by relaxation losses—calling for the repeated acquisition of the experiments at multiple frequency offsets as well as for repeated probe retunings.

While excitation problems and decreased sensitivities raise challenges when acquiring 1D UW NMR spectra, these complications are compounded even further when attempting to establish two‐dimensional (2D) correlations among such species. In either homonuclear or heteronuclear correlations, these additional challenges will then arise from: (1) The requirement to simultaneously act on the full spectral line shapes of two nuclides using either “hard” or “matched” RF fields, which becomes challenging due to hardware limitations when resonances span over a MHz. (2) The intrinsically weak couplings between low‐γ nuclei, which in turn will call for long polarization transfer periods to establish correlations, and lead to severe spin‐relaxation losses, decreasing even further the overall sensitivity of an already challenging acquisition. When considered in unison, these factors readily explain the paucity of UW/UW 2D correlation experiments in the NMR literature.

Recently, the possibility of relying on the PROgressive Saturation of the Proton Reservoir (PROSPR) was put forward, as an alternative to facilitate the acquisition of UW NMR powder spectra [16, 17]. PROSPR delivers low‐γ wideline X‐nucleus spectra with good sensitivity and without the need to subject the spectrometer to repeated retunings, by indirectly detecting the depletion that the X spins impart on created 1H dipolar‐order (DO). This DO can be generated in protonated solids by adiabatic demagnetization/remagnetization in the rotating frame pulses (ADRF/ARRF [18, 19]), or by the Jeener–Broeckart experiment [20]. In PROSPR, this bulk 1H DO is subject to recurrent cross‐polarization (CP) X‐pulses (Figure 1a), suitably matched to 1H‐1H dipolar coupling strengths in the range of a few kHz. Such CP pulses will transform part of the 1H DO into an X‐spin magnetization, which is then allowed to decay. Throughout the CP pulse and the subsequent period τ d, 1H–1H spin‐diffusion (SD) distributes this locally depleted DO among the entire homonuclear dipolar network. Looping such a process N‐times will thus deplete the 1H DO in a bulk, magnified fashion. By repeating this sensitive but narrow‐banded procedure as a function of X transmitter offset Ω, a full UW X‐spin spectrum can end up imprinted onto the 1H spin magnetization; that is, on the 1H spectrum arising following a final ARRF conversion. The conditions needed to implement such an experiment have been discussed for both static and spinning solids [16, 17, 21], as were variants that enable its use for measuring challenging systems [22], or relaxation times [23]. The latter procedure can in turn be used to separate multiple UW NMR signals arising from different local environments in a pseudo‐2D fashion: using relaxation‐assisted Laplace‐transform separations leveraging the spins’ distinct relaxation rates [24, 25]. The present study introduces an extension capable of providing more conventional 2D correlations—both homo‐ and heteronuclear ones—between low‐γ species. These correlations can arise even when both species have UW resonances, and even if their inter‐nuclear dipolar couplings are too weak to enable the practical execution of polarization or coherence transfers among them. Execution of these PROgressive Saturation of the Protons Enhanced‐Correlation Technique (PROSPECT) experiments (Figure 1b,c) only requires the targeted nuclear species to be dipole‐coupled to the same 1H dipolar network, and long‐lived 1H DO as in conventional PROSPR. This then enables establishing correlations between two powder patterns even if the intervening dipolar couplings are weak or null. The following section expands on the principles of the 2D PROSPECT approach and evaluates its performance through numerical spin dynamics simulations and experiments carried out at 14.1 T on 195Pt and on 14N species, showing heteronuclear and homonuclear 2D anisotropic tensor correlations among these resonances despite exhibiting breadths of over 1 MHz.

Results

To visualize how 2D PROSPECT can correlate the powder patterns of neighboring X and Y species sharing heteronuclear dipolar couplings to a common 1H reservoir, it is convenient to review how 1D PROSPR works for detecting the individual spectra of the X or Y species. As mentioned, this experiment relies on depleting the 1H DO by relatively weak CP pulses (Figure 1a), which, in unison with 1H–1H SD, will redistribute this loss throughout the homonuclear dipolar network. Repeating this CP‐based depletion while letting any generated X magnetization decay and the DO re‐equilibrate via 1H–1H SD magnifies the ensuing depolarization, leading to an effect that plateaus in a robust manner—in the sense of not needing probe retuning or a fine‐tuning pulse sequence parameter to proceed to completion (Figure 1d). This redistribution will deplete DO beyond the protons that were directly coupled to the low‐γ nucleus, even if still confined to the nanometer scale. The ensuing depletion of 1H DO is then probed by transforming it into 1H observable transverse magnetization with an ARRF pulse. The full UW X‐nucleus spectrum is then obtained by repeating the narrowband CP procedure in a frequency‐by‐frequency fashion and subtracting the ensuing 1H signals from the original observable 1H signal obtained in an identical experiment but in the absence of CP.

Consider now the PROSPECT pulse sequences depicted in Figure 1b,c, which represent homo‐ and heteronuclear 2D extensions of PROSPR, where an additional set of looped CP pulses has been incorporated into the sequence. Although divisible into homo‐ and heteronuclear versions, and while allowing simultaneous or subsequent CP pulses, the differences among all these are conceptually minor: PROSPR's CP depolarizations are, in any case, narrow‐banded, and two different offsets belonging to the same X‐channel can thus also be considered as a “heteronuclear” realization; hence, we focus only on this latter case. In the same fashion as 1D PROSPR varied its CP offset Ω over the full relevant bandwidth, these 2D realizations will now step two CP frequencies Ω1 and Ω2, independently of each other, over the full X and Y relevant spectral ranges. If both fall outside the spectral line shapes, they will incur no DO depolarization, and hence lead to a null once subtracting from their outcome the reference (no CP) 1H signal. If the offset of one of these pulses is within the addressed line shape but the other is not, the result will amount to a single‐species 1D PROSPR acquisition (Figure 1e). If, however, both irradiation frequencies fall within their respective powder line shapes, there are now two potential possibilities: that by virtue of their values, Ω1 and Ω2 address different crystallites within the powder (Figure 1f), or that they happen to address 1Hs within the same crystallite (Figure 1g). In the first case, the ensuing DO depolarization effects will be additive: each CP pulse addresses its own crystallite, and the overall 1H signal arising after ARRF will end up reflecting the sum of the two individual depletions. If, however, the pulses happen to address the same set of protons, the total DO depletion will be smaller than their overall individual sums. In principle, given the depolarization efficiency of PROSPR's looped CP procedure, there will be no contrast added upon relying on a second spin to deplete an already depleted local DO. Subtracting 1D PROSPR spectra originating from the off‐resonance cases for each nucleus along the corresponding dimension will therefore result in different patterns depending on whether the X/Y Ω2/Ω1 frequencies have addressed spins that were in the same nanoscopic environment, or in different ones. In other words, it will provide a pattern that correlates the anisotropic line shapes of the X and Y spins. Notice that, by contrast to traditional separate‐local‐field experiments designed to establish anisotropic correlations via direct dipolar couplings [26], the internuclear links highlighted by such PROSPECT scheme will not proceed via couplings between the correlated spins. Rather, they will reflect the fact that the sites showing mutual correlations are dipole‐coupled to common proton spins. This would be a similar proximity‐based information, yet alleviating the various weak coupling/transfer losses bottlenecks described above.

With these considerations as background, Figure 2 illustrates the application of 2D PROSPECT to the homonuclear correlation of the 195Pt in cisplatin at 14.1 T. In this field, this spin‐1/2 nucleus exhibits a ca. 1 MHz powder pattern, whose width is given by a nearly axially symmetric chemical shift anisotropy spanning ca. 9000 ppm. Figure 2a shows the raw series of 1H 1D spectra obtained when applying the pseudo‐2D scheme introduced in Figure 1b. As highlighted in the zoomed inset, the 1H resonances trace an inverted 1D 195Pt CSA pattern as expected from the 1D PROSPR experiment, nested inside another similar powder pattern. Integration of the 1H signal intensities followed by rearrangement according to the CP irradiation frequencies Ω1 and Ω2 (or δ 1 and δ 2 in ppm) leads to the diagram shown in Figure 2b (top). Subtracting the off‐resonance slices (corresponding to the 1D PROSPR response) along both dimensions leads to the 2D pattern shown in Figure 2b (bottom; see Supporting Information for details of the data processing). If the chemical shift tensor relative orientations of the two 195Pt sites are identical, the ensuing spectrum will display intensity solely along the diagonal, overlapping with the self‐correlation signal. On the other hand, an off‐diagonal signal will emerge when correlating magnetically non‐equivalent neighboring 195Pt environments.

For cisplatin, α and β polymorphs have been identified as the two predominant forms coexisting at room temperature [29]. In the α‐cisplatin crystal structure, the unit cell contains two cisplatin molecules related by inversion symmetry. As reported previously, the δ zz component of the 195Pt CSA tensor is perpendicular to the ligand plane [30], the relative orientation of the chemical shift tensors is described by a set of Euler angles (180°, 0°, 0°). Due to the symmetry of the resulting rotation matrix, the 195Pt chemical shifts in these orientations will be equivalent, and so effectively these will be related by Euler angles (0°, 0°, 0°). Figure 2c presents the 2D 195Pt homonuclear PROSPECT spectrum expected for this case. In the β‐polymorph, two symmetry‐independent molecules are in the unit cell, with each of these paired with an inversion‐related counterpart. The relative orientations of 195Pt chemical shift tensors for the environments in the asymmetric unit are given by Euler angles (9°, 8°, 16°). In the PROSPECT spectrum, this gives rise to a slightly off‐diagonal signal; the effect, however, is modest, given the small value of the β Euler angle (which has the largest effect on the eventual spectral line shape). The overall 195Pt PROSPECT spectrum of β‐cisplatin is thus expected to be a combination of the simulated spectra shown in Figure 2c,d. Hints of off‐diagonal signal potentially arising from the β‐polymorph are indeed observed experimentally (Figure 2b); however, this cannot be distinguished unambiguously from the PROSPR saturation broadening. Nevertheless, the results still highlight the potential of the method to differentiate between distinct polymorphs.

Figure 3 illustrates a similar homonuclear PROSPECT experiment, targeting now the 14N species of cisplatin. With quadrupolar parameters of C Q = 990 kHz and η Q = 0.12 [16], this resonance also spans over 1 MHz. The 2D PROSPECT spectra for this species will, because of the spin‐1 nature of 14N, be inherently more complex than for its spin‐1/2 195Pt counterpart. Indeed, when sufficiently separated in frequency, the two single‐quantum transitions of a single 14N will behave as two independent sources of depolarization that address the same 1H DO. The consequence of this is evident in the simulation of two coaxial (0°, 0°, 0°) electric‐field gradient (EFG) tensors shown in Figure 3b, which, by contrast to the spin‐1/2 case introduced in Figure 2c, displays strong diagonal and anti‐diagonal components. Cisplatin will be even more complex: In the α‐form, the unit cell contains four 14N nuclei, giving rise to six potential correlation pairs. Within each molecule, the two 14N EFG tensors are oriented relative to one another by Euler angles (0°, 90°, 0°), as the V zz component is approximately aligned with the N─Pt bond [30]. Four additional 14N pairs arise between the two inversion‐related molecules; by symmetry, two of these are effectively described by (0°, 0°, 0°) Euler angles, and two by (0°, 90°, 0°) (See Figure S1, for simulated PROSPECT spectra). We expect the resulting 2D 14N‐14N PROSPECT spectrum to be reflected by a sum of the spectra from all these different 14N pairs, as shown in Figure 3c. In the β‐polymorph, there are eight 14N spins with 28 different correlation pairs. The EFG tensor for four of these is oriented to each other as (0°, 0°, 0°) and eight as (0°, 90°, 0°), originating from nuclei within the same molecule or in inversion‐related molecules. The remaining 16 pairs are between 14N spins in the symmetry‐independent cisplatin molecules, from which 8 pairs are related by Euler angles (18°, 26°, 164°) and the other 8 pairs by (173°, 65°, 4°). The resulting spectrum for β‐cisplatin can be approximated by the sum of all these and is provided in Figure 3d. The rich ensuing 2D correlations (Figure 3c,d) recapitulate several of the experimental 2D 14N PROSPECT features; however, due to the complexity of the spectrum and foremost of the sample (large number of different environments and polymorphism), a quantitative interpretation becomes of limited value.

To conclude this experimental survey, Figure 4 presents a heteronuclear PROSPECT experiment correlating the 195Pt and 14N species in cisplatin. While, to the best of our knowledge, such an experiment, correlating two MHz‐UW powder patterns, has not hitherto been reported, the aforementioned strategy allows its recording in 11 h. The resulting 2D line shape (Figure 4a) is well structured and clearly deviates from a coaxial tensor scenario (Figure 4b). As mentioned above, the δ zz component of the 195Pt chemical shift tensor is perpendicular to the ligand (N─Pt─N) plane, whereas V zz component of the 14N EFG tensor lies within this plane and is approximately parallel to the N─Pt bond. The relative tensor orientations within a single cisplatin molecule are therefore described by Euler angles (90°, 90°, 19°) and (90°, 90°, 109°). In α‐cisplatin, intermolecular 195Pt chemical shift and 14N EFG tensor orientations will be the same as the intramolecular ones owing to inversion symmetry. In the β polymorph, additional correlation pairs arise, with Euler angles between the tensors defined by (94°, 97°, 44°) and (83°, 94°, 134°). The simulated individual 2D PROSPECT spectra for each case are provided in Figure S2. Notice that the 14N line shape in the projection of several 2D simulations is not consistent with the corresponding 1D spectrum. The deviation arises from the use of only two 1H spins in the simulations, which results in certain crystallite orientations experiencing reduced 1H DO depletion. Although such effects are expected to average out in systems with large 1H dipolar networks —for example, in the crystals of most organic compounds—we employed a small spin system here to minimize computational time. The summed simulated spectra for all spin pairs are shown in Figure 4c,d for each polymorph. Both predicted spectra reproduce the main features of the experimental data, precluding a clear differentiation between the two crystal forms. Nevertheless, the agreement between the experiment and simulations supports our description of the principles governing the PROSPECT method. This is also encouraging as it suggests that applications aimed at extracting relative tensor orientations in systems for which crystal structures are unknown can also rely on PROSPECT, utilizing approaches based on either SIMPSON [31, 32] or MRSimulator [33] for fitting such 2D line shapes.

Discussion and Conclusions

The present study introduced an approach for performing 2D UW NMR correlation experiments in which two nuclei are correlated through their independent interactions with a third, reporting nucleus. Indirect detection of low‐γ nuclei resonances via the observation of a high abundance, high‐γ nucleus is, of course, widespread in 2D NMR [34, 35, 36]. Likewise, the characterization of two low‐γ nuclei spectra via 1H‐based acquisitions is common in ≥3D biomolecular NMR [37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Such experiments, however, typically rely on direct interactions—J‐coupling in solution, dipolar coupling in solids—between the low‐γ species being correlated, which are encoded by incrementing the accrued chemical shift evolution phase in a point‐by‐point, nested manner on the proton signal. By contrast, although PROSPECT can be viewed as a pseudo‐3D experiment, the approach introduced here differs from previous propositions in that it does not rely on a direct coupling between the species being correlated; it only requires that the two nuclei or transitions possess couplings to common protons. For low‐γ species, this represents a substantial advantage, as low‐γ/low‐γ internuclear couplings tend to be weak and therefore, demand long polarization transfer times, which may be incompatible with practical T2 or T1ρ lifetimes. On the other hand, the PROSPECT approach is limited to interactions capable of efficiently depolarizing the reporting reservoir. For instance, J‐couplings are likely to be too weak to be employed in such schemes, hence motivating the reliance in this study on dipolar couplings to establish connectivities. Likewise, the T1D lifetime of the 1H DO places an upper bound on how weak the correlations being sought can be; it is conceivable that other non‐equilibrium but long‐lived 1H states, for instance those associated with the MIRROR experiment [43], can be used for establishing similar heteronuclear correlations. Interesting features may also arise when considering correlations within a dynamic system, which the PROSPR experiment has been shown to be able to reveal, even if involving rapidly‐relaxing quadrupolar heteronuclei [17]; dynamics are also expected to reflect in the CP efficiencies, and T 1D DO lifetimes. Also remaining to be further explored is the field‐dependent sensitivity of the method. This is expected to be relatively weak as PROSPECT relies on rather sensitive 1H observations and on dipolar‐based depletions; sensitivity is thus equally likely to depend on spectrometer and magnet stability, as on the actual field involved.

PROSPECT shares several conceptual similarities with the chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) experiment [44]. These include a step‐by‐step probing of insensitive spectra by porting information onto a readily observable reporting signal, and using narrow‐banded off‐resonance frequency‐domain manipulations for achieving this [45, 46, 47, 48]. In CEST, RF pulses are typically applied to the same nucleus that is observed, whereas in the present case, heteronuclear CP enabled irradiation of low‐γ species while detection was performed on highly sensitive protons. Employing 1H DO as the reservoir for saturation minimizes direct perturbations of the reporting protons and thereby reduces potential instrumental instabilities affecting the experimental sensitivity; however, more conventional magnetization transfer schemes could have been used in the solid‐state case [49]. Following this logic, the establishment of correlations might also be envisioned in the absence of direct internuclear couplings, by exploiting chemical exchange between various species and a common reservoir. Continuing with these CEST parallels, pulsed time‐domain experiments could also be conceived for the solid NMR correlations, which will convey information analogous to the present frequency‐based manipulations approach [49].

Another correspondence between the two experiments, and central to the formation of the 2D PROSPECT spectrum, stems from the way both CEST and PROSPR depolarize their reporting reservoirs—in the former, z‐magnetization, and in the latter, 1H DO. In both cases, the extent of depolarization depends on the spin–spin transfer rates as well as on the lifetime of the reporting spins' state: T1 for CEST, 1H T1D for PROSPR. Importantly, for a set of fixed rates and lifetimes, the resulting drop in signal will plateau in both cases to a value that reflects the number of 1H spins affected by the depolarization; it does not decay to zero. In the solid‐state case, assuming uniform rates and lifetimes across all crystallites and a sufficiently large number of depolarizing cycles to achieve maximal DO depletion, the ensuing signal provides a faithful spectral representation of the X/Y‐spins responsible for the depletion. These conditions enable PROSPR to imprint the 1D powder pattern of the irradiated species onto the 1H signal, and likewise allow PROSPECT to map 2D correlated spectra. The fact that, once a common 1H reservoir has been maximally depleted by one nucleus, irradiation of a second spin no longer decreases the 1H DO was critical for establishing correlations presented in Figures 2, 3, 4. Additional implications of these effects, and the opportunities it opens in other magnetic resonance areas, are currently under exploration.
