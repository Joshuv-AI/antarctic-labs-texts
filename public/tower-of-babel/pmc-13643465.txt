Modulating Secondary Inflammation After Spinal Cord Injury Affects Pathological Bladder Remodeling
Authors: Michael Kleindorfer, Karin Roider, Katia Monastyrskaia, Akshay Akshay, Patrick Heimel, David Hercher, Evelyn Beyerer, Sophina Bauer, Martha Georgina Bradtner, Lukas Lusuardi, Ludwig Aigner, Elena Esra Keller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNeurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (nLUTD) develops in nearly all patients after spinal cord injury (SCI). Evolving neuroinflammation is considered a key driver of secondary injury and is associated with worsening outcomes. However, few studies have examined treatments for nLUTD after SCI that specifically target these processes. This study investigated whether early montelukast therapy targeting proinflammatory leukotrienes improves long-term urological function and reduces pathological bladder remodeling.MethodsFemale Lewis rats received a permanently implanted urinary catheter and underwent contusion SCI (Th8/Th9) or sham surgery. Daily montelukast treatment began 1 day after injury and continued throughout the 2- or 6-month follow-up period. Injury severity and regeneration were evaluated using the Basso-Beattie-Bresnahan locomotor score and microcomputed tomography of the spinal cord. Bladder function was assessed through weekly urodynamic recordings in awake animals, and bladder structure was assessed by histological measurement of wall thickness. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were used to characterize the molecular processes underlying bladder remodeling.ResultsSpinal cord tissue damage and functional impairment were comparable across animals, regardless of treatment. Montelukast did not improve urodynamic outcomes and was associated with transiently poorer functional scores during early recovery. Bladder wall thickness increased after SCI and was greatest in the treated groups, suggesting ongoing remodeling. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses demonstrated marked activation of immune and contractility-related pathways in treated animals at 2 months. Animals followed for the longer period showed intensified immune signaling, indicating sustained inflammatory activity.ConclusionsEarly montelukast treatment did not improve functional or structural urological outcomes after contusion SCI. The unfavorable functional trends and intensified inflammatory and injury-related pathways suggested adverse effects rather than therapeutic benefit under the conditions studied. These findings may indicate the importance of injury model-specific mechanisms and of dose and timing when developing anti-inflammatory strategies for SCI-induced nLUTD.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Neurogenic bladder, Lower urinary tract dysfunction, Animal model, Preclinical study

• HIGHLIGHTS

- Early montelukast therapy did not improve urological outcomes after contusion spinal cord injury in female rats, despite comparable locomotor recovery and spinal damage across groups.

- Awake urodynamic recordings showed transiently poorer bladder function and increased bladder wall thickness in treated animals, indicating unattenuated pathological remodeling.

- Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling revealed amplified, persistent immune and contractility-related signaling in treated animals, suggesting adverse rather than therapeutic molecular effects.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal cord injury (SCI) can cause irreversible autonomic dysfunction affecting multiple organs and leading to specific pathophysiological changes [1]. The location and neural regulation of the lower urinary tract (LUT) make it particularly susceptible to injury-related changes and cumulative damage [2, 3]. Consequently, nearly all patients experience neurogenic lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) [4]. Current treatment strategies aim to protect the upper urinary tract and relieve LUTS [2, 5, 6], but cannot restore preinjury LUT function or reverse accumulated tissue damage. After SCI, bladder function is among patients’ functional priorities [7] and has a substantial impact on their quality of life (QoL) [8, 9].

Suppressing excessive (neuro)inflammation after SCI is considered a promising strategy for limiting the consequences of secondary damage as it develops [10, 11]. Early intervention could better preserve neuronal signaling pathways, prevent pathological remodeling, and provide a better basis for regeneration. Treatments targeting neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (nLUTD) after SCI with anti-inflammatory molecules, such as S-nitrosoglutathione [12, 13], simvastatin [14, 15], dantrolene [16, 17], and quercetin [18, 19], have shown some beneficial effects. However, further research appears necessary to pursue these findings or identify alternative candidates.

Leukotrienes are small fatty acids derived from the arachidonic acid pathway [20] that can transmit proinflammatory signals and have been found at elevated levels after SCI [21-23]. Montelukast, a cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 1 antagonist approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [24] and used mainly for asthma, has shown benefits well beyond its conventional therapeutic use. It could improve memory in healthy aged rats [25] and in a mouse model of dementia [26]. A single intraperitoneal injection of montelukast helped preserve bladder tissue integrity after SCI in rats [27]. In a previous pilot study, we also observed improved bladder function with early oral montelukast treatment at 4 weeks after transection SCI in rats [28].

This study followed up on those findings by investigating the potential of montelukast to treat nLUTD after contusion SCI in rats over 2 and 6 months.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal Groups and Follow-up Periods

On arrival, 3-month-old female Lewis rats (n=26) were randomly assigned to 5 groups: untreated sham, 2 months (n=5); treated sham, 2 months (n=5); untreated SCI, 2 months (n=5); treated SCI, 2 months (n=5); and treated SCI, 6 months (n=6). After 2 weeks of acclimatization and handling, all animals (n=26) received a permanently implanted urinary catheter. Two weeks later, 16 rats underwent SCI, whereas the 10 sham animals underwent laminectomy alone. All sham animals (n=10) and 10 of the 16 animals with SCI were assigned to the shorter, 2-month follow-up period. Due to the actual Infinite Horizon Impactor hit screening (actual force and displacement) as well as intactness of dura of sham animals, one animal per group had to be excluded. The final number of animals per group for all further in vivo and ex vivo assessments and analyses were as follows: untreated sham, 2 months (n=4); treated sham, 2 months (n=4); untreated SCI, 2 months (n=4); treated SCI, 2 months (n=4); and treated SCI, 6 months (n=5). Beginning 1 day after injury, half of the animals in each 2-month surgical group (n=4 per treatment group) received daily oral montelukast (10-mg/kg body weight) as a gel under blinded, placebo-controlled conditions. The remaining 5 animals with SCI formed a pilot group in which montelukast treatment continued for 6 months after SCI.

Permanent Bladder Catheter Implantation and Contusion SCI

A polyethylene bladder catheter (PE-50 tubing) was permanently implanted in each animal (n=26), as previously described [29]. Animals assigned to the SCI groups received a contusion injury at Th8/Th9 using an Infinite Horizon impactor delivering 250 kilodynes of force [30], whereas sham animals underwent laminectomy alone at the same spinal level. Animal care and health management followed our established protocols and refinements [31].

Awake Urodynamics

Bladder function was assessed weekly in awake, restrained rats using a Catamount cystometry station [31, 32]. Water was withheld during measurements, and saline was infused through the urinary catheter at a constant rate of 120 μL/min. Infusion continued until at least 3 micturition cycles had been recorded or, if no micturition occurred, until the estimated maximum bladder capacity was reached; this estimate was based on the highest residual volumes recorded during the preceding week. Bladder function was rated using a 4-point scoring system [31]. A score of 4 indicates normal bladder function. A score of 3 indicates an active micturition reflex with prevalent nonvoiding contractions. A score of 2 indicates the absence of an active voiding reflex and the presence of urine leakage. A score of 1 indicates a fully dysfunctional bladder, with no measurable detrusor activity and urine leakage only.

Locomotion Scoring

Two experimenters assessed hindlimb function using the 21-point Basso-Beattie-Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor scale [33] at 1 and 7 days postinjury (DPI) and at 1, 1.5, and 2 months after injury. Animals in the 6-month group were also assessed monthly at 3, 4, 5, and 6 months.

Microcomputed Tomography of the Spinal Cord

For microcomputed tomography (μCT), fixed spinal cords were incubated for 48 hours in Accupaque-350 (GE Healthcare, Germany) diluted 1:2 in phosphate-buffered saline. Scans were acquired using a SCANCO μCT 50 at 70 kVp and 85 μA with a 0.5-mm aluminum filter. A total of 3,400 projections/180° were integrated 4 times for 650 ms and averaged. Images were reconstructed at an isotropic resolution of 6 μm over approximately 21 mm centered on the lesion epicenter. Images were evaluated using Fiji (ImageJ v1.53a) [34], following the procedure described by Romanelli et al. [35]. The spinal cords were aligned to the lesion epicenter, and the mean cross-sectional areas of the spinal cord, damaged tissue, and cyst were measured for each 60-μm segment along the cord. Damaged regions were identified by changes in texture or increased signal intensity.

Bladder Wall Thickness

Bladder samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 hour, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 7-μm-thick cross sections for histological analysis. Tissue sections were visualized using standard Masson-Goldner trichrome staining. To measure bladder wall thickness at representative sites, images were acquired at 20-fold magnification with an Olympus VS120 slide scanner and analyzed using Olympus OlyVIA (ver. 2.9).

Transcriptomic and Proteomic Analyses of the Bladder

Messenger RNA sequencing and alignment

Total RNA was isolated using the mirVana kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) from bladder samples preserved in RNAlater (Sigma, USA), as previously described [36]. RNA was treated with DNase (DNA-free kit, Ambion, USA), assessed for quality using a BioAnalyzer, and processed for library preparation and next-generation sequencing, as described in our previous study [37]. Messenger RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 using total RNA preparations. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Bioconductor packages DESeq2 (ver. 1.30.1) and edgeR (ver. 3.32.1). Genes with an adjusted P-value <0.1 were classified as DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was used to interpret the biological significance of the DEGs. The cluster-Profiler package (ver. 3.18.1) in R was used to perform GOORA and GO gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of biological process terms associated with the DEGs. Results with P-values <0.1 were considered statistically significant. Word clouds were created using Wordle.net and the word cloud R package to condense and visualize gene enrichment data from the pathway analysis dataset. The font size of each gene name (tag) reflected its frequency in the pathway analysis dataset. To visualize the GO-ORA results, 6 major biological function terms were selected, their occurrences were counted for each group comparison, and the counts were plotted on a radar graph.

Protein sample preparation and liquid chromatography– tandem mass spectrometry

The remaining bladder tissue was snap-frozen, and acidified protein digests were extracted and analyzed by liquid chromatography– tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS; Dionex Ultimate 3000 nano-ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion LUMOS, Thermo Fisher, Germany). Protein digests were loaded onto a precolumn (PepMap C18, 5 μm, 100 Å, 300 μm×5 mm) at 10 μL/min using a loading solvent of 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid in water/acetonitrile (98:2). After loading, peptides were eluted in backflush mode onto the analytical nanocolumn (Waters CSH C18, 1.9 μm, 100 Å, 0.075-mm internal diameter×200 mm) with an acetonitrile gradient of 5%–40% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in water/acetonitrile 4,9:95) over 90 minutes at 250 nL/min. Column effluent was introduced directly into the mass spectrometer through a Nanoflex electrospray source (Thermo Fisher). Precursor ion scans were recorded with the Orbitrap detector at a resolution of 120,000 (at m/z=250), a maximum injection time (mIT) of 50 ms, and an automatic gain control (AGC) setting of 400,000. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragment spectra were acquired in the linear ion trap in parallel with the full scan, using a top-speed method that acquired fragment spectra from the most intense precursor ions over a 3-second cycle. Acquisition used an mIT of 35 ms, an AGC setting of 10,000, exclusion from further fragmentation for 30 s, and a relative HCD energy of 30%.

LC-MS/MS data were processed with MaxQuant (ver. 1.6.14.0) using the default Orbitrap peak-detection settings and strict trypsin cleavage, with up to 3 missed cleavages allowed. Variable modifications were methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation; cysteine carbamidomethylation was specified as a fixed modification, and the match-between-runs option was disabled. Fragment spectra were interpreted using the UniProtKB Rattus norvegicus protein sequence database, release 2022_01, together with potential contaminant proteins. Protein identifications were accepted only when at least 2 razor peptides were identified at a 1% false discovery rate cutoff at the peptide-spectrum match, peptide, and protein levels. Potential contaminants and protein groups identified only by site were removed before further analysis. Missing label-free quantification values were imputed as follows: when a replicate group contained at least 2 detections, missing values were drawn randomly from a Gaussian distribution with a width of 0.3× the sample standard deviation and centered at the sample distribution mean minus 2.5× the sample standard deviation; remaining missing values were imputed by maximum likelihood estimation. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were analyzed using the Ingenuity Canonical Pathway tool in Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; QIAGEN, USA). For each comparison, pathways representing 19 biological functions in the IPA database were counted and plotted on a radar graph.

RESULTS

Confirmation of SCI

Quantified tissue damage and hindlimb function scores indicated consistent injury severity across animals. After SCI, BBB scores gradually improved from paraplegia to consistent plantar stepping by approximately 29 DPI. No animal regained coordinated walking (Fig. 1A). The smallest intact cross-sectional area of the thoracic spinal cord (minimal healthy tissue) clearly distinguished sham animals, with approximately 4.86 mm², from animals with SCI, with approximately 0.19 mm². Damage volumes and cyst formation varied little within groups (Fig. 1B and C).

Urodynamics

Urodynamic scores fell sharply soon after SCI, from a mean of 3.75 to 2.5 in untreated animals, 3.6 to 1.6 in the treated 2-month group, and 3.4 to 1.4 in the treated 6-month group (Table 1). These changes reflected a transition from initially healthy bladder function to dysfunction. In the untreated group, functional loss was characterized by nonvoiding contractions and a mostly absent micturition reflex. All animals in the treated groups lost the micturition reflex, and several had no measurable detrusor activity. During the first 4 weeks postinjury (WPI), bladder function improved in all groups toward recovery of the micturition reflex, although the reflex remained absent more often in treated animals. Thereafter, bladder function did not improve further and remained unstable, with marked fluctuations between measurements (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Histological Analysis of Bladder Wall Thickness

Masson-Goldner trichrome-stained bladder sections showed slight bladder wall thickening after SCI (Fig. 2). The increase was most pronounced and consistent in the montelukast-treated 2-month group (2.44 mm, P=0.0341); the treated 6-month group had the second-highest mean thickness (2.01 mm, P=0.3249).

Transcriptomic and Proteomic Analyses of the Bladder

The treated and untreated SCI groups were compared at 2 months, and the treated SCI groups were compared at 2 and 6 months after SCI. The number of DEGs differed between comparisons: 2,417 were identified in the pairwise comparison of treated and untreated animals at 2 months (supplementary file deg_SCI_A2_B2), and 1,512 were identified in the comparison of treated animals at 2 and 6 months (supplementary file deg_ SCI_A2_A6). The comparison of treated and untreated animals at 2 months after SCI (Fig. 3) identified a significant number of pathways regulating muscle contractility, cell proliferation, and immune responses (Fig. 3A). The ridge plots showed that most genes involved in contractile processes and immune response regulation were upregulated in the bladders of treated animals (group A2) (Fig. 3B and C). The word cloud of major contractility signaling hubs showed predominantly increased expression in the treated group (Fig. 3D), with even more pronounced increases in immune response signaling (Fig. 3E). These data suggest that, at 2 months after SCI, montelukast increased contractility and enhanced immune cell proliferation and immune responses compared with those in untreated animals.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to attenuate the excessive neuroinflammation that develops after SCI and contributes to secondary injury, thereby preserving the structural and functional integrity of the LUT.

Functional deficits assessed by BBB scores and tissue damage quantified by spinal cord μCT confirmed high within-group consistency. Montelukast had no apparent treatment effect. However, the BBB scale may detect only substantial functional improvements, which were not expected in this study: its coarse grading does not always reflect linear functional progression, and ceiling effects may occur at specific scores [38].

Like BBB scores, urodynamic measurements in awake animals showed a rapid loss and gradual recovery of function after SCI. However, function fluctuated markedly as the condition entered the chronic phase, as previously reported by our group [31]. Montelukast neither improved nor stabilized bladder function. During the first 4 WPI, the opposite effect appeared possible: treated animals had lower scores, more frequent ab-sence of the micturition reflex, and, in some cases, no measurable detrusor activity. This unfavorable functional pattern was accompanied by slight bladder wall thickening in tissue-based measurements, which may indicate remodeling driven by sustained intravesical pressure, increased contractility, and repetitive strain. However, although urinary catheter implantation appears to have no significant effect on bladder function assessed by urodynamics [32], it is known to affect bladder tissue and markedly increase bladder wall thickness, muscular hypertrophy, and urothelial hyperplasia [39]. All animals in the 2-month groups, both sham and SCI, were exposed to this mechanical irritation for the same period, so catheter-related effects may have introduced a uniform rather than differential bias. In the 6-month pilot group, however, structural differences from the 2-month groups may reflect prolonged mechanical irritation as well as progression to chronic pathology or extended treatment. These duration-dependent catheter effects cannot be distinguished from pathological changes or montelukast effects; the structural findings in this group may therefore remain exploratory. Given the aim of obtaining comprehensive urological data from each animal while minimizing animal use in accordance with the principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement [40], the overall influence of the implanted catheter may be considered an acceptable limitation of the study design.

Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses confirmed remodeling and ongoing inflammation in the bladders of all injured animals. A more permissive significance threshold of P<0.1 was used to identify DEGs, providing a broader view of the processes underlying bladder remodeling in the subsequent ORA and GSEA. This approach also allowed subtle but important biological trends to be captured. Treatment increased immune responses and cellular stress and activated more injury-related pathways. At 2 months after SCI, treated animals had higher expression of contractility markers, greater immune cell proliferation, and enhanced immune responses compared with untreated animals.

At 6 months after SCI, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses showed decreased contractility and less proliferation of contractile cells, and further intensification of the immune response already elevated at 2 months in treated animals. However, the absence of an untreated SCI control group at 6 months is a notable limitation: the treated 6-month pilot group could be compared only with the treated 2-month SCI group. Treatment effects therefore cannot be separated from time- and catheterrelated influences, and the 6-month findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

Although our pilot study found beneficial effects on bladder function [28], the present results suggest that neither 2 nor 6 months of montelukast treatment improved bladder regeneration after SCI; both treatment durations instead appear to have produced adverse effects. Treated animals showed no functional benefit and had transiently poorer urodynamic scores during early recovery, together with signs of greater structural remodeling and increased inflammatory signaling in the bladder.

This marked discrepancy may reflect the different injury methods. Transection SCI is typically induced by a precise blade incision, producing predictable injury parameters and tissue damage largely confined to the site of acute axonal injury [41]. In contusion injuries, however, surrounding tissues absorb varying amounts of the applied force, potentially extending the injury proximally, distally, ventrally, dorsally, or laterally through the spinal cord parenchyma [41]. Although contusions are generally considered less severe, the resulting neuroinflammation may exceed that observed in transection models. Under these conditions, and given the rapid decline in plasma montelukast levels after oral administration in rats [28], the dose of 10-mg/kg body weight may be insufficient to counteract neuroinflammation and may instead trigger processes affecting its resolution. Although the present study also used a severe SCI model and the same treatment regimen, switching from transection to contusion produced conflicting outcomes. These findings may indicate the importance of injury model-specific mechanisms and of dose and timing when developing anti-inflammatory strategies for SCI-induced nLUTD. They may also suggest that tailoring drug treatment to the specific injury parameters could yield beneficial effects.

Although no effective therapy for SCI is available to date, reducing the inflammatory effects of secondary injury remains one of the most promising approaches to facilitating recovery [10, 11]. Most patients experience bladder dysfunction [4] which is considered a major contributor to reduced QoL [8, 9], creating a strong need for therapies that go beyond symptomatic relief. However, few studies have investigated SCI-induced nLUTD in controlled preclinical settings, and even fewer have addressed its chronic consequences [42, 43].

This study may therefore contribute to the field by providing a comprehensive urological dataset on chronic SCI in animals with a clinically translatable injury type and highly consistent lesion parameters. Function was assessed by repeated urodynamic measurements in conscious animals, structural changes were identified by histology, and the molecular mechanisms underlying pathological bladder remodeling were examined by transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Because these extensive data were generated from the same animals rather than assembled from different cohorts, they offer a uniquely comprehensive, integrated view of multiple aspects of SCI-induced nLUTD and associated pathological bladder remodeling.
