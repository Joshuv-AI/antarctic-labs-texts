Analysis of Fahliyan Reservoir Fractures by combining conventional petrophysical logs and FMI image log in an oil field in Southwest Iran
Authors: Seyed Abouzar Mohsenipour, Babak Samani, Behnaz Shooli, Ali Faghih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the fractures of the Fahliyan limestone formation, the third hydrocarbon-bearing reservoir in southwestern Iran, were investigated. Despite previous studies on the Fahliyan Formation, a comprehensive workflow integrating conventional petrophysical logs, FMI image analysis, and velocity deviation logs for systematic fracture characterization has not yet been fully developed. In the first step, petrophysical parameters, including water and hydrocarbon saturation and the overall lithology of the reservoir (limestone with minor shale content), were evaluated using conventional petrophysical logs, such as gamma ray (GR), density (RHOB), neutron (NPHI), photoelectric factor (PEF), and resistivity logs (MSFL, LLS, LLD). In the second step, processing of the Formation Micro Scanner (FMI) image logs led to the identification of bedding with dips less than 30° toward the northeast and fractures, which mainly extended with dips of 75° in the directions S65E/N65W and S25W. A more detailed analysis of the fracture patterns and their relationship with bedding was made possible by combining manual and automated methods. Analysis of the in-situ stresses indicated that borehole breakouts had mirror-dip orientations of N40W–S40E, and induced fractures had mirror-dip orientations of N70E–S70W, which is consistent with the Zagros trend. In the third step, the velocity deviation log (VDL) results showed negative values in the fractured upper part and zero to positive values in the lower part of the Fahliyan formation. This study, through integration of conventional petrophysical logs and FMI images, enabled the quantification and classification of fracture systems in the Fahliyan formation and demonstrated their direct relationship with the velocity deviation logs and in-situ stress orientations. This methodology facilitates the prediction of high-permeability fractured zones and optimization of reservoir development.Keywords: FMI imager log, Fahliyan reservoir, Fracture, Petrophysical logs, Velocity deviation log (VDL)Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Global oil and gas reserves are predominantly hosted in two types of rocks: carbonate and clastic. Carbonate rocks alone account for more than 60% of the world’s oil reserves and approximately 40% of its gas reserves1,2. In the Middle East, which holds the largest hydrocarbon reserves globally, around 65% of hydrocarbon reserves are located in carbonate reservoirs. In Iran, 90% of large hydrocarbon reservoirs are hosted in carbonate rocks, while sandstone reservoirs constitute only about 10% of these reserves3–5. Many studies have been conducted on the geomechanical, petrophysical, and seismic properties of Iranian carbonate reservoirs, including6–12.

Reservoir quality in these formations is controlled by a combination of sedimentary textures, diagenetic alterations, and fracture networks13. In such reservoirs, the presence of fractures significantly influences heterogeneity and reservoir behavior, affecting key petrophysical properties such as porosity, permeability, and compaction14–16. Carbonate reservoirs inherently exhibit very low matrix permeability and can only produce economically significant hydrocarbons through secondary processes, particularly fracture development. Consequently, fractures play a critical role in primary depletion, secondary recovery, and ultimately tertiary recovery of carbonate reservoirs17.

To date, numerous researchers, including1,18–28, have conducted extensive studies to understand the mechanisms of fractures and their effects on the behavior of hydrocarbon reservoirs. Fractures strongly influence the behavior of oil and gas reservoirs, especially carbonate reservoirs, by affecting rock porosity and permeability and facilitating fluid flow. The physical characteristics of fractures depend on their origin, the mechanical properties of the host rock, and its diagenetic history. In carbonate rocks, fractures may develop due to processes such as dissolution, collapse, recrystallization, and other mechanisms. Assessing fracture properties, including permeability, dip, orientation, aperture, and continuity, is critical for hydrocarbon production. Rock formations often contain multiple fracture sets with varying orientations, collectively referred to as fracture systems. Fractures are typically classified as shear, tensile, or extensional systems29. Therefore, studying fractures and understanding their spatial distribution in reservoirs can help determine optimal drilling locations, increase reservoir life and productivity, reduce costs, and enhance our overall understanding of fracture systems in hydrocarbon fields.

Fracture analysis is performed using direct methods, such as detailed core studies, and indirect methods, such as well logs. While core data provide the most reliable observations of fractures, their acquisition is limited by high costs, time requirements, and low recovery in fractured reservoirs30,31. Consequently, researchers have sought faster and more cost-effective methods.

Well logs are among the key sources of subsurface information in oil and gas wells32. These tools can measure geological and petrophysical properties, including mineralogy, sedimentary structures, porosity, and permeability, both directly and indirectly. Advanced image logs, such as Formation Micro Scanner (FMI), provide high-resolution identification of geological and geomechanical features, including fractures. In many cases, image logs, due to their low cost and ability to capture large portions of the borehole wall, can reduce the need for coring.

Despite the importance of the Fahliyan carbonate formation as Iran’s third-largest hydrocarbon reservoir, limited studies have been conducted on it, most of which focused on sedimentological aspects, with less attention given to quantitative and comprehensive fracture characterization.

The main objective of this study is to provide an integrated description of the subsurface fracture systems in the Fahliyan formation by combining conventional petrophysical logs with image logs. Specifically, the study aims to: (1) identify and classify fractures, (2) perform statistical analysis of fracture dip, orientation, and density, (3) investigate the relationship between the existing stress regime and fracture sets, and (4) use velocity deviation logs derived from density, neutron, and sonic logs to assess fracture-induced heterogeneity in the reservoir. The results of this study provide a valuable tool for better understanding the fracture-controlled behavior of the Fahliyan reservoir and offer a practical approach for fracture characterization in structurally complex reservoirs.

Geology of the region

The studied field is located in southwestern Iran, approximately 80 km from Ahvaz city in the Abadan Plain. The main trend of the field follows the north–south orientation of the Abadan Plain33. The Abadan Plain is a structural zone situated at the southwestern end of the Zagros. Its northern and northeastern borders are limited to the front of the Zagros Mountains (the southern edge of the Susangerd, Abteymour, and Mansouri anticlines), and it extends southward from the Reg-Sefid field into the Persian Gulf. Geologically, the Abadan Plain is part of the Mesopotamian Basin (Bein-e-Nahrin) and represents the northern limit of the Arabian Platform (Fig. 1a). Due to the presence of young alluvial deposits, geological knowledge is mainly limited to petroleum drilling results and geophysical studies17,34,35.

(a) Structural position of the Abadan plate (modified from49. (b) Schematic stratigraphy of the Cretaceous-Tertiary Abadan Plain (modified from50).

The Fahliyan reservoir in the studied field contains oil and is recognized as the second most productive oil reservoir after the Sarvak Formation (Fig. 1b).

The Fahliyan limestone formation, of Neocomian–Aptian age within the Khami Group, is recognized as the third hydrocarbon-bearing reservoir in southwestern Iran. This formation consists of 360–365 m of laminated to massive oolitic limestones, brownish-gray in color with a coarse texture, and represents one of the reservoir rocks of the Khami Group. Locally, at the base of this formation, a dissolution layer may exist, potentially equivalent to the Hith Formation. The lower boundary of the Fahliyan Formation may correspond to either the Surmeh Formation (Upper Jurassic) or the anhydritic Hith Formation. Where the Fahliyan overlies the Hith (coastal Fars), identifying the boundary is straightforward. In areas where the Hith is absent (particularly north of Shiraz), distinguishing between Fahliyan and Surmeh is more difficult35,36.

In southwestern Iran, the Fahliyan Formation is hydrocarbon-bearing due to its limestone and dolomite units, which have been confirmed through production tests37–39. Numerous sedimentological and geological studies have been conducted on the Fahliyan Formation, including those by40–47. Based on chronostratigraphic characteristics, the Fahliyan Formation in the Abadan Plain is informally divided into lower and upper Sects. 48.

Upper Fahliyan section

This section begins with a clean limestone layer, white to gray, followed by dark gray to greenish shale layers. Gray marly deposits also appear interbedded with limestone and shale. Cream to light brown limestones and whitish-gray calcareous shales are present among the upper Fahliyan sedimentary layers. This upper section can be considered laterally equivalent to the Ratawi Formation in Iraq17. Generally, the upper section has low reservoir quality and, together with the Geduan Formation, acts as a cap rock47.

Lower Fahliyan section

The lower section of the Fahliyan Formation consists of a hard-brown limestone layer containing traces of oil, accompanied by grayish-white and cream semi-hard calcareous sediments with oil staining. These relatively clean limestone layers are interbedded with dark gray calcareous shales and dark gray shale layers17. This section possesses good reservoir quality and contains economic hydrocarbons47.

Methodology

In this study, to identify and classify fractures in the Fahliyan carbonate reservoir of the studied field, a combination of data from conventional logs and the FMI (Formation Micro Scanner) image log from a single well was used. The flowchart of the interpretation steps for traditional logs and the FMI image log is shown in Fig. 2.

Fracture identification flowchart in the studied interval.

Initially, a quality control process was applied to the petrophysical logs, and zones with poor-quality or outlier data were removed. In the petrophysical study, conventional logs such as gamma ray (GR), sonic (DT), neutron porosity (NPHI), density (RHOB), and caliper logs were used. After loading the data, petrophysical parameters, including porosity and water saturation, were calculated. To evaluate these parameters, a software program that provides advanced responses for reservoir characterization was employed. This program is based on a probabilistic or optimization approach for modeling wireline log data and reservoir rock properties.

The MULTIMIN method provides a simultaneous solution for determining mineralogy, porosity, and fluid content. It is an optimization tool that statistically determines the characteristics and volumes of minerals and fluids from petrophysical data, including logs, cores, XRD data, and petrographic studies.

For lithology determination using petrophysical logs, the cross-plot method is commonly applied. The applicability of this method depends on the rock's complexity and the number of porosity logs used. This technique can also be applied to determine reservoir porosity and the presence of gas. In this study, the reservoir lithology was obtained from the neutron-density cross-plot and then selected as the dominant minerals in the MULTIMIN petrophysical model. Standard log responses for each mineral were adjusted and used in the petrophysical interpretation. These petrophysical logs, together with the FMI image logs, were used to improve the understanding of geological features observed in the image logs.

In 1991, Schlumberger introduced the Formation Micro Imager (FMI) tool. Compared to older tools such as FMS, FMI provided more exhaustive coverage, higher resolution (vertical resolution of 0.2 in., investigation depth of 1 in., and the ability to resolve details down to 50 microns), and employed an electrical measurement basis. The FMI tool consists of four arms, each equipped with pads and flaps, with 12 sensors per pad/flap, for a total of 192 sensors.

In this study, the raw data were initially quality-controlled using accelerometer and magnetometer measurements. The results confirmed the data’s reliability: The X–Y cross-plot of the magnetometer indicated a circular pattern around the origin, reflecting proper tool operation, and the Z-axis accelerometer recorded a value of 0.98 (Fig. 3). Additionally, the wellbore was free of washouts or constrictions (Fig. 4).

The velocity deviation log (VDL) was generated by combining porosity logs. The main objective of calculating the velocity deviation log is to create a synthetic sonic log using information from three petrophysical logs: sonic transit time (DT), neutron porosity (NPHI), and bulk density (RHOB). This approach allows the identification of different types of porosity and assists in characterizing sedimentary and diagenetic environments. The VDL provides a useful tool for obtaining information about the dominant porosity type in carbonates and for predicting permeability trends within the well.

In this study, porosity and velocity values from sonic and neutron logs were calculated using Wyllie’s equation (Wyllie et al., 1956), and after constructing the synthetic sonic log, it was compared with the actual sonic velocity. Results

One of the most critical aspects of studying an oil reservoir is the investigation and characterization of its fractures. Fractures can be used for various purposes, including reservoir heterogeneity assessment, identification of production zones, geomechanical studies, and other applications. Open fractures are the most critical fracture type in reservoirs, as they can significantly influence permeability in carbonate reservoirs54,55.

In this study, fractures in the Fahliyan reservoir, located in one of the oil fields of southwest Iran, were identified and classified through the integration of conventional well logs and image logs. The workflow for data interpretation and identifying geological features and fractures is described in the following steps.

Petrophysical evaluation

Petrophysical evaluation is the science of interpreting well-logging data to determine reservoir properties such as porosity, permeability, water saturation, and hydrocarbon content. In this study, petrophysical evaluation of the Fahliyan carbonate reservoir was carried out using the Geolog software based on a probabilistic approach, which indicates a predominantly limestone lithology with a minor shale content.

In the neutron–density cross-plot, the data points are mainly clustered along the limestone line, while shale data, shown in green, are positioned below the main trend (Fig. 7). The evaluated logs indicate that the shale volume in the upper part of the reservoir is higher than in the lower part. From a petrophysical standpoint, the upper reservoir interval contains producible hydrocarbons, whereas the lower part of the reservoir is predominantly water-bearing (Fig. 8).

Determination of lithology by neutron-density cross plot.

Evaluation results of petrophysical logs in Fahliyan carbonate reservoir using Paradigm Geolog software.

Processing the imager log

Checking the condition of the well wall

To identify and distinguish drilling-induced borehole breakouts, the difference between the caliper logs and the bit size log was used. The results indicate an excellent borehole wall condition, and except for the depth intervals of 4100–4150 m, 4200–4220 m, and 4280–4300 m, no significant breakouts were observed in other sections. The limited extent of unstable zones in the image indicates that mechanical effects during drilling had a minimal impact on the quality of the image log data, and that most of the variations observed in the image log can be attributed to genuine geological features.

In addition, the borehole wall is completely cylindrical, and no irregularities affecting the quality of the image log are present (Fig. 9). This reflects the high accuracy of image acquisition and reduces the likelihood of other phenomena such as induced fractures. Therefore, the image logs in the studied well are of sufficient quality to be reliably used for geological interpretation as well as for fracture and bedding analysis.

In the image log, bedding planes are observed as abrupt changes in electrical resistivity and, consequently, variations in color intensity in the statically normalized image31. In the interpreted image log, 241 bedding features were identified. The bedding dips are less than 30°, mainly ranging between 5° and 25°, dipping toward the northeast, with strikes oriented northwest–southeast.

This regular bedding pattern suggests deposition in a low-energy environment, lacking sudden energy fluctuations or syn-depositional structural activity. Moreover, no significant changes in bedding dip direction or magnitude indicative of faulting were observed in this well (Figs. 12 and 13). This structural characteristic results in transverse anisotropy of weak to moderate intensity induced by bedding, which therefore does not play a significant role in controlling the reservoir behavior of the formation.

An example of the borders of the bedding observed in the analyzed imager log.

Histogram and stereo-net related to bedding slope and trend.

The walkout plot (Fig. 14) for the studied interval illustrates a uniform bedding trend. The bedding shows an almost constant and regular pattern, dipping toward the northeast, which is well aligned with the regional Zagros trend. This pattern indicates that the bedding geometry was formed by regional structural controls before the development of the main fracture systems in the area. Comparison between the orientations of the identified fractures and the bedding planes reveals that the fractures generally have steep dips and are oriented nearly perpendicular to the bedding. This relationship suggests a post-depositional origin for the fractures, developed under the influence of later tectonic stresses, whereas the bedding reflects the primary depositional and early diagenetic conditions of the reservoir.

Walkout Plot diagram in the studied interval.

Therefore, it can be concluded that although bedding alone does not significantly enhance permeability, it plays a vital role in controlling the reservoir quality of the Fahliyan Formation by creating background anisotropy and influencing the spatial distribution of fractures.

In evaluating reservoir heterogeneity, the frequency of geological features can play an essential role as an indicator of both the intensity and concentration of geological processes. The image log in the studied interval shows that the frequency of geological features is not uniform, with a higher concentration of features in the upper part of the Fahliyan Formation compared to its lower part. This pattern may reflect factors such as the degree of diagenesis and the stress sensitivity conditions within the studied sequence.

Among the various geological features, bedding exhibits the highest frequency, whereas deformed layers show the lowest frequency (Fig. 15). The low abundance of deformed layers suggests that the intensity of tectonic deformation within the studied interval has been limited.

The analysis of geological features using stereonets indicates that these features (bedding, solution seams, and dense streaks) predominantly dip toward the northeast. This suggests control by a common structural framework, which is likely related to the overall Zagros trend and has influenced the development of these features. In contrast, the steep dips toward the southwest and orientations approximately perpendicular to bedding may indicate a post-depositional, tectonic origin.

The stereo net representation of each feature (Fig. 17) shows that although the general dip directions are similar, the dispersion of data associated with the identified fractures is greater than that of bedding. This indicates that fractures are more sensitive than bedding to local stress variations. Overall, the structural characteristics of the identified geological features reflect an orderly depositional framework for the Fahliyan Formation. This framework is crosscut by fractures with specific orientations, which can play a significant role in controlling reservoir behavior and permeability distribution.

Identification of fractures in the imager log

In general, any kind of rupture in the rock that the plates around it do not show displacement is called a fracture56. In the studied well, two types of open (continuous and discontinuous) and filled (closed) fractures were separated by two methods of automatic identification and manual identification. The following describes how to identify fractures.

Automatic method

Gradient filters are used to identify fractures in the FMI log automatically. This filter can detect the edge in an image. This filter identifies high-contrast boundaries in the texture of an imager log and separates them from other phenomena in the imager log based on the characteristics of natural fractures56.

In other words, Auto fracture analysis identifies fractures from wellbore images. There are two methods used in auto fracture analysis in Geolog. Edge Detection Method and Binarization Method.

In the Edge Detection Method for fracture detection uses an edge-following algorithm to remove all horizontal features and enhance all non-horizontal edges on the image (vertical edge enhancement). Then the image is normalized to enhance the visibility of fractures. An edge-following algorithm attempts to find the optimum path of the edge across the pad image. A fracture is then detected according to length, visibility, and linearity criteria.

The edge detection method consists of three stages: Image Flattening, Preprocessing and Auto fracture Detection.

In the Image Flattening step, the image is flattened using the tilt data. Consequently, dips should have either been automatically computed or manually picked beforehand.

Dip removal is required, for example, to avoid bed boundary effects when performing calculations across the whole image (e.g., Image Statistics, Histogram Analysis, Threshold Analysis, Dynamic Threshold Analysis, Image Binarization, Image Rescale, Image Porosity). Also, in the detection Fracture workflow, bedding dips need to be flattened so that they are not confused with fractures in later processing stages57. In other words, at this stage, the horizontal phenomena, which are mostly layering boundaries, are entirely removed from the image (Fig. 18).

Image Flattening step, removal of bedding effects from the FMI image log image texture.

In Preprocessing step, the process of strengthening the remaining steep borders is done, and the whole image must be normalized to highlight the fractures. In other words, the normalization process should be applied to the entire image58.

This part removes horizontal and enhances non-horizontal features on the input images. It also normalizes the image, so that the data have a normal distribution which is centered around a mean of close to zero57 (Fig. 19).

Preprocessing step in the automatic identification of fractures from the FMI imager log.

At Auto fracture Detection stage, by identifying the edges of the detected fractures and connecting them, the fractures are continuous. The user must control the process in all stages of automatic detection of fractures and apply changes if required.

In this part, potential fractures appear as dark and/or light linear features on the primary image, thus representing relatively high positive and negative image values57.

In this section, a threshold limit is defined for the detection of fractures, values higher than which are detected as fractures (Fig. 20).

The results of this method indicated that fractures are identified based on the frequency of the color spectrum. Only if there are large fractures in the reservoir, this method is able to accurately identify the fractures; otherwise, there is no accurate separation of different types of fractures from each other, especially vuggy and discontinuous ones. Also, the amount of closed fractures is calculated much higher than the actual value.

Binarization Method

This fracture detection approach, using binarization and shape manipulation filters, can be used for data sets where fractures show a relatively high contrast to the surrounding matrix (e.g., relatively high or low resistive fractures). The program uses histogramming to create a binarized image and then applies shape manipulation operators to isolate those features that appear to be fractures, which results in horizontal, non-linear, and very short features being removed from the binarized image57.

Because this method has a better performance in identifying cavities. It was not used in this study.

Examination of horizontal stresses

Analysis of the stresses around the well wall, which is an essential part of geological studies, is performed using stress-induced features on the imager log. The most important of these complications is borehole breakouts and drilling-induced fractures.

Borehole breakouts are created in the direction of the lowest horizontal stress on the well wall. In the interpretation of the studied interval, the borehole breakouts have a mirror slope direction of N40W-S40E, which has a trend against the general trend of Zagros (in the direction of the lowest stress) (Fig. 25). Induced Fractures are created in the direction of maximum horizontal stress on the well wall. In the studied interval, with the N70E-S70W mirror slope direction (Fig. 26), which is consistent with the general trend of the maximum horizontal stress on the well wall in Zagros. According to the stress studies (Fig. 27), it can be said that the fractures identified in the studied well are the result of tectonic pressures caused by the collision of the Arabian plate with the Iranian plate.

Velocity deviation log (VDL)

VDL is a synthetic log made from the combination of three petrophysical logs: neutron porosity, acoustic, and density. The porosities in carbonate rocks are identified and classified using this log, and it is used in a very special way to identify fractured zones.

Laboratory investigations on different carbonate samples by51 showed that the speed of sound in sediments and carbonate rocks is a function of the type of porosity prevailing in them. Types of porosity, such as intraparticle or mold porosity, have a higher speed compared to porosity such as interparticle porosity or micro porosity. In the VDL log, positive values indicate areas where the velocity value is high, such as areas with mold and intraparticle porosity. Zero velocities indicate intervals in which interparticle porosity or micro porosity is dominant. Negative velocities indicate regions where the velocity is low, for example, holes in the well wall, fractures, or the presence of gas, which cause negative velocities on the VDL. Also, since the types of porosity affect the rock permeability51, this log is also used to determine the permeability paths.

Upper Fahliyan

In the studied interval, the upper part of the Fahliyan Formation generally has a higher fracture density than the lower part. The velocity deviation log made in this section has an almost perfect match with the detected fractures and shows negative values in the areas with fractures. Most of the identified fractures can be seen in two depth ranges of 4120 to 4160 and 4220 to 4280. The results may indicate that hydrocarbon production in this well, if it occurs, is likely associated with the presence of fractures. Furthermore, in this reservoir, hydrocarbon production is expected to take place predominantly from the upper part of the Fahliyan Formation (Fig. 28).

Matching velocity deviation log with identified fractures.

Lower Fahliyan

The lower part of the Fahliyan Formation has no open fractures, and no other fractures were observed except for one case of a closed fracture. This part of the reservoir has water and does not contain any type of hydrocarbon. The VDL made in this section is almost in the positive limits, which indicates the absence of fractures (Fig. 28). Therefore, the likelihood of hydrocarbon production in this section is low.

Discussion

The integrated analysis of conventional petrophysical logs and the velocity deviation (VDL) logs, along with image logs, indicated that the most critical factor controlling reservoir heterogeneity in the Fahliyan Formation was the presence of fractures. In this study, interpretation of the FMI image logs identified 312 geological features (241 bedding planes, 51 solution seams, 18 dense streaks, and 2 deformed layers), indicating the dominance of depositional features over structural features. The bedding planes had a low dip (5–25°) toward the northeast, reflecting a stable depositional framework that did not directly affect reservoir permeability.

However, the fractures, with dips ranging from 40° to 80° and a NW–SE trend consistent with the Zagros fold-thrust belt, exhibited structural behavior completely different from bedding. The fracture patterns indicate a tectonic origin and are controlled by regional compressive stresses, suggesting that the fracture distribution is not random but structurally controlled.

In the studied well, fractures were mainly concentrated in two intervals: 4140–4150 m and 4190–4300 m. These intervals exhibited negative values in the VDL log, indicating a good correlation between the presence of open fractures and sonic velocity reduction. In the lower section of the Fahliyan Formation, VDL values were near zero, consistent with the low fracture density in that interval. Effective porosity was higher in the upper section and lower in the lower section of the formation, demonstrating good agreement between image logs and conventional logs. Therefore, in areas with limited image logs or core data, the VDL log can serve as a reliable indicator for identifying fractured zones.

The analysis of the dip orientation of bedding and fractures, along with the absence of sudden changes in dip magnitude and direction, suggests that no significant fault was present in the studied interval, and the fractures primarily resulted from brittle deformation due to tectonic stresses.

Overall, the results of this study highlight the high importance of integrating conventional petrophysical and image logs for identifying productive zones in fractured carbonate reservoirs and demonstrate their utility for reservoir quality assessment.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of fractures in the Fahliyan carbonate reservoir in southwest Iran through integration of conventional petrophysical logs, FMI image logs, and velocity deviation logs (VDL). The results demonstrated that the combination of these methods enables accurate identification, classification, and quantification of fracture systems, providing essential insights for reservoir characterization and development.

The main findings of this study are as follows:

Fracture Identification and Classification: A total of 76 fractures, including both open and filled types, were identified in the studied well. The manual interpretation method achieved higher accuracy compared to the automatic method, confirming the importance of expert oversight in fracture identification. Open fractures, primarily located in the upper Fahliyan formation, exhibited slopes ranging from 40° to 80° with NW–SE trends, consistent with the regional Zagros structural orientation. Only one closed fracture was detected in the lower part, indicating minimal impact on production.
