Childhood maltreatment and psychopathology during collective trauma: the longitudinal role of betrayal-related moral injury
Authors: Yasmin Friedler, Racheli Cohen, Reut Zabag, Maja Bergman, Shilat Haim-Nachum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood maltreatment (CM) is a risk factor for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety, yet the mechanisms linking early adversity to later vulnerability during traumatic events remain unclear. Betrayal-related moral injury (MI-betrayal) – the perception that trusted others or institutions have violated core moral expectations – has been proposed as one such mechanism. Since CM often involves betrayal, it may heighten sensitivity to later violations of trust, particularly during collective trauma in the context of war, when large-scale security breakdowns may be perceived as institutional failure to protect civilians.Objective: This study tested whether MI-betrayal may function as a longitudinal process linking CM with symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety among civilians in the aftermath of the events of 7 October 2023.Methods: A total of 1052 individuals (aged 18-40) participated in the study. CM was measured with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire; MI-betrayal with the Moral Injury Events Scale; and psychopathology with the PC-PTSD-5, PHQ-9, and GAD-7. Cross-lagged panel models were used to examine bidirectional longitudinal associations between betrayal-related appraisals and psychological symptoms, controlling for their stability over time.Results: CM was associated with higher MI-betrayal, PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms at baseline. Both MI-betrayal and symptoms showed strong stability over time. Psychological symptoms more consistently predicted subsequent betrayal than the reverse.Conclusions: CM may confer heightened vulnerability to moral-injury appraisals during collective trauma. Distress more consistently predicted later betrayal-related appraisals, suggesting a dynamic, bidirectional pattern rather than a single unidirectional pathway. Future research should refine developmental models of MI.

Introduction

Childhood maltreatment (CM) refers to experiences of abuse or neglect inflicted by primary caregivers or attachment figures during childhood, involving violations of basic expectations of care, protection, and safety (Manly, 2005). CM is a well-established risk factor for several psychopathologies including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety (Hogg et al., 2023; McLaughlin et al., 2020). However, despite robust associations between CM and adverse mental health outcomes, substantial heterogeneity exists: many individuals exposed to CM do not develop persistent psychopathology (VanMeter et al., 2021). This variability highlights the critical need to identify mechanisms that shape vulnerability following early adversity, particularly in the context of subsequent stress exposure.

One mechanism receiving increasing attention is moral injury (MI), defined as psychological distress arising from experiences perceived as violations of deeply held moral expectations by oneself or others (Litz et al., 2009). MI has been linked to disruptions in meaning, identity, trust, and social connectedness, and to elevated PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms (Griffin et al., 2019; Litz, 2024). A central dimension of MI is betrayal, which occurs when trusted individuals or institutions fail to uphold moral responsibilities such as protection, care, or accountability (Shay, 1994, 2014). Betrayal-related MI has been documented in both interpersonal contexts – most notably within caregiving relationships – and institutional contexts involving governments, military forces, or other authorities (Fani et al., 2021; Kidwell & Kerig, 2023; Smith & Freyd, 2014). Such violations may be especially injurious in contexts characterized by dependence and vulnerability, shaping enduring moral appraisals of others as unreliable or unjust.

From a developmental perspective, CM can be conceptualized as an early form of betrayal-related MI. Maltreatment involves harm perpetrated by caregivers who bear primary moral responsibility for a child’s safety and well-being, constituting violations of trust rather than harm alone (Fani et al., 2021; Kidwell & Kerig, 2023). Betrayal trauma theory suggests that such early violations undermine trust in protective systems and shape moral and relational schemas across development (Delker et al., 2018; Freyd, 1998). Consequently, individuals with CM histories may enter adulthood with heightened sensitivity to later experiences involving perceived failures of moral responsibility.

Betrayal-related MI has been increasingly observed following large-scale collective traumas such as wars, natural disasters, and pandemics, and has been associated with elevated PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms (Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Fisch, et al., 2025; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025). Collective trauma threatens not only physical safety but also shared moral and social frameworks, including trust in institutions and expectations of protection (Hirschberger, 2018). When authorities fail to meet these expectations, such failures may be appraised as institutional betrayal, contributing to psychological distress and moral disorientation (Smith & Freyd, 2014).

The 7 October 2023 attacks involved coordinated incursions into Israel, with widespread and varied exposure to violence, displacement, and ongoing threat. This study focuses on civilians with direct and substantial exposure. Within this context, the events provide a salient setting for examining betrayal-related MI, particularly in light of perceived failures in institutional protection and links to adverse mental health outcomes (Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Lazarov, et al., 2025; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025; Levi-Belz, Blank, et al., 2024; 2024).

Despite robust evidence linking CM to adverse mental health outcomes, including PTSD, depression, and anxiety, the psychological mechanisms through which early maltreatment contributes to later psychopathology – particularly in contexts of collective trauma – remain insufficiently understood. To address this gap, the present study examined the longitudinal associations between CM, betrayal-related MI, and symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety among Israeli civilians exposed to the 7 October 2023 events and ensuing war. We hypothesized that higher levels of CM would be associated with elevated levels of betrayal-related MI and psychological distress during collective trauma.

Method

Participants

Between February and May 2024, we recruited individuals who had resided in northern and southern Israel prior to 7 October 2023 – regions heavily affected by the war and characterized by widespread displacement, ongoing threat, and disruption of institutional protection (Amsalem et al., 2026; Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Lazarov, et al., 2025). This population was therefore particularly relevant for examining betrayal-related moral injury. Prior research in comparable populations has demonstrated associations between direct exposure to threat or loss and heightened perceptions of injustice, underscoring the relevance of this sample for examining moral distress following acute trauma (Mor-Elzas et al., 2026).

Based on evidence of higher prevalence rates of PTSD, depression, and anxiety in young adulthood, the sample was restricted to individuals aged 18–40 years (Amsalem et al., 2023; Cameron-Maldonado et al., 2023; Kujawa et al., 2020; Villaume et al., 2023). This age range was selected because it represents a developmental period characterized by ongoing consolidation of moral identity and institutional trust, as well as increased engagement with institutions such as the military, workplaces, and civic systems, which may heighten vulnerability to MI and institutional betrayal (Lapsley & Hardy, 2017; Wycoff et al., 2024).

The study focused on the Israeli population to examine betrayal-related MI in relation to Israeli institutions and authorities, including the government, military, and local leadership. Palestinians have also been deeply affected by the conflict and may experience MI in distinct ways that warrant dedicated investigation in future research (Buheji, 2025).

Participants were recruited via Panel4All, an online platform of approximately 100,000 Israeli panelists, enabling panel-based sampling. The platform supports demographic diversity, screens for fraudulent responses, and employs automated tools to detect non-authentic entries. Individuals failing to complete the questionnaire or flagged by ReCAPTCHA as bots were excluded. The baseline sample included 1052 civilians; 681 completed all follow-ups (M age = 31.3, SD = 5.6), of whom 56% were women. Most participants identified as Jewish (93%), with 5% Arab and 2% other ethnicities. They were compensated with gift vouchers valued at approximately $4. A subset of participants (14.3%) reported being in reserve service at the time of the 7 October events, indicating that while the sample was primarily civilian, some individuals had concurrent military involvement.

Procedure

Participants provided informed consent and completed baseline questionnaires online via Qualtrics in February 2024 (baseline; four months after the 7 October 2023), followed by assessments at 30-day (March 2024), 90-day (May 2024), and 365-day (February 2025) follow-ups, during an ongoing and evolving phase of the war. This period extended from the acute aftermath of the attacks into a more prolonged phase of the conflict, including ongoing security threats, frequent air-raid sirens, disruptions to daily routines and schooling, and displacement of communities, during which civilians continued to face varying levels of threat, uncertainty, and disruption to daily life, alongside changing perceptions of institutional responsibility and protection; such dynamics may have shaped both psychological symptoms and betrayal-related appraisals over time (Amsalem et al., 2026; Levi-Belz, Blank, et al., 2024).

Consent procedures emphasized confidentiality, anonymity, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time. Surveys included demographic information, war-related exposure, CM, betrayal-related MI, and symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety. Data were collected as part of a larger longitudinal project examining psychological responses to the 7 October 2023 events and their aftermath (Haim-Nachum et al., 2025). Data quality was ensured through automated verification procedures. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tel Aviv University (Approval No. 0007877-1).

Instruments

Childhood maltreatment

CM was assessed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF; Bernstein et al., 1994). The instrument covers five forms of maltreatment: emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, and emotional and physical neglect. Respondents indicated the frequency of each experience during childhood on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘never true’ to 5 = ‘very often true’). Each subscale provides a score ranging from 5 to 25, with established cut-offs (9, 8, 6, 10, and 8, respectively) that can be used to identify probable exposure of clinical relevance (Bernstein et al., 2003; Paivio & Cramer, 2004; Spinhoven et al., 2014). CTQ data were available for 546 baseline participants, 17.4% exceeded the clinical cut-off (Bernstein & Fink, 1998) for emotional abuse, 10.6% for physical abuse, 20.6% for sexual abuse, 25.5% for physical neglect, and 33.7% for emotional neglect. Internal consistency in the present sample was α = .79. The CTQ was administered at a single time point and was not reassessed across waves, as it reflects retrospective reports of childhood experiences assumed to be stable over time.

Moral injury

Betrayal-related MI was assessed using the three-item betrayal subscale of the Moral Injury Events Scale (MIES; Bryan et al., 2016), consistent with prior civilian applications of the measure (Fani et al., 2021; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025). In line with prior work and the aims of the study (Thomas et al., 2024), we focused specifically on betrayal-related appraisals (e.g. ‘I feel betrayed by leaders whom I once trusted’), as this dimension captures perceived failures of protection by trusted leaders and institutions, which are particularly relevant in civilian contexts of collective trauma. Other MIES domains (self – or other-transgressions) are more characteristic of military contexts and were therefore not aligned with the aims of the present study. All items were administered with explicit reference to the events of 7 October 2023 and the ensuing war, ensuring that responses were anchored to this specific context.

Recent civilian studies support the validity of using the betrayal subscale independently of the full MIES (Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Fisch, et al., 2025, 2025; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025; Levi-Belz, Blank, et al., 2024). Items were rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = ‘strongly disagree’ to 6 = ‘strongly agree’) and analysed as dimensional indices of MI, with higher scores indicating greater betrayal-related MI (Thomas et al., 2024). Internal consistency across the four assessment waves ranged from α = .72 to .77.

PTSD

Probable PTSD symptoms were captured with the Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5 (PC-PTSD-5; Prins et al., 2016). This five-item tool is designed to identify the core features of PTSD. Items were adapted to reference the 7 October 2023 event. Higher scores indicate greater symptom burden, with scores ≥ 3 indicate probable PTSD. The scale has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, including strong factor structure and high internal consistency (Spoont et al., 2015; Williamson et al., 2022). Internal consistency coefficients across the four measurement points ranged from α = .75 to α = .78.

Depression

Probable depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001). This scale includes nine items reflecting DSM criteria for depression, rated from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’). Total scores indicate the severity of symptoms. Symptom severity was interpreted using standard clinical cutoffs (5–9 mild; 10–14 moderate). The PHQ-9 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across clinical and community populations, including sensitivity to change (Löwe et al., 2004). Internal consistency coefficients across the four waves ranged from α = .91 to α = .92.

Anxiety

Probable anxiety symptoms were measured with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006), a seven-item measure covering common anxiety symptoms during the previous two weeks. Items are rated on a 4-point scale (0 = ‘not at all’ to 3 = ‘nearly every day’), with total scores reflecting symptom severity. Symptom severity was interpreted using established clinical cutoffs (5–9 = mild; 10–14 = moderate). Prior research has shown strong psychometric properties for this scale (Hinz et al., 2017; Löwe et al., 2008). Internal consistency coefficients across the four waves ranged from α = .93 to α = .94.

All questionnaires were presented in Hebrew. These translated versions have been validated in prior research and demonstrated robust psychometric properties (Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Fisch, et al., 2025; Levi-Belz & Zerach, 2018; Matalon et al., 2021; Serur et al., 2022; Talmon & Ginzburg, 2017; Weisman et al., 2022). Measurement invariance across waves was assumed.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS and AMOS. An a priori sample size estimation using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) was performed in line with prior longitudinal studies employing cross-lagged panel designs (e.g. Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Fisch, et al., 2025, 2025; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025). Cross-lagged panel models were estimated to examine bidirectional longitudinal associations between betrayal-related moral injury and psychological symptoms across four waves, with CM entered as a baseline predictor. Autoregressive and cross-lagged paths were specified for all constructs, and within-wave residual covariances were freely estimated. Age and gender were included as covariates. Missing data were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood. Model interpretation focused on autoregressive stability and cross-lagged pathways.

Cross-lagged panel models

Three cross-lagged panel models were estimated to examine longitudinal associations between CM (CTQ), betrayal-related MI, and symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety across four waves. Although global fit indices are reported for completeness, interpretation focuses on autoregressive stability and directional cross-lagged effects, which constitute the core hypotheses of the study. Model fit was evaluated using standard SEM criteria, and indices fell below conventional thresholds, likely reflecting the high temporal stability of constructs and known limitations of cross-lagged panel models in capturing complex longitudinal dynamics. The results were interpreted cautiously given suboptimal model fit.

Model 1: cross-lagged model for PTSD

The model (N = 1052) showed global fit indices below conventional thresholds, χ²(18) = 249.78, p < .001, CFI = .885, TLI = .713, RMSEA = .110 [.098, .123] (see Figure 1). Higher CM predicted higher baseline Betrayal (β = .17, p < .001) and higher baseline PTSD symptoms (β = .24, p < .001). Both Betrayal and PTSD showed substantial temporal stability across measurement waves (PTSD autoregressive paths: β = .62, .66, .56; all p < .001; Betrayal autoregressive paths: β = .46, .49, .42; all p < .001). Reverse effects (PHQ-9 → Betrayal) were consistently significant across all waves (β = .16, .13, .13; all p < .001). Model 3: cross-lagged model for anxiety

The anxiety model (N = 1052) also yielded suboptimal fit indices, χ²(18) = 295.47, p < .001, CFI = .890, TLI = .725, RMSEA = .121 [.109, .133]. CM predicted higher baseline Betrayal (β = .17, p < .001) and higher baseline anxiety (β = .31, p < .001). Temporal stability was strong (GAD-7 autoregressive paths: β = .71, .69, .67; all p < .001; Betrayal autoregressive paths: β = .43, .49, .42; all p < .001).

As in the other models, reverse effects (GAD-7 to Betrayal) were stronger and consistently significant (β = .18, .11, .13; all p ≤ .003). Thus, support for a consistent directional pathway was limited, while the reciprocal pathway (anxiety to betrayal) appeared more robust (See Figure 3). Discussion

The present study examined longitudinal associations between CM, betrayal-related MI, and psychological distress during a period of collective trauma. Using four-wave cross-lagged panel models, findings supported a dynamic, bidirectional process: CM was associated with heightened betrayal-related MI and psychological distress, while psychological symptoms more consistently predicted levels of perceived betrayal over time.

PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms demonstrated substantial temporal stability, indicating persistent symptom levels. Although mean levels were generally within the mild range, they may underestimate clinically meaningful distress, as a substantial proportion of individuals exceeded clinical thresholds. At baseline, 44.2% of participants screened positive for probable PTSD, and approximately one-third reported at least moderate depressive and anxiety symptoms, with similar patterns observed across follow-up assessments. Consistent with prior research in collective trauma, clinical significance is better reflected in threshold-based prevalence, particularly in heterogeneous civilian samples (Amsalem, Haim-Nachum, Fisch, et al., 2025, 2026; Haim-Nachum et al., 2025).

Across models, CM predicted higher baseline levels of betrayal-related MI and elevated PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms. These findings align with developmental models suggesting that early maltreatment shapes enduring vulnerabilities in emotion regulation, threat sensitivity, and trust, which influence how later adversities are appraised (Kidwell & Kerig, 2023; McLaughlin et al., 2020). Within this framework, CM may sensitize individuals to moral threat by shaping expectations regarding protection and responsibility.

Betrayal-related MI showed modest, time-limited prospective associations with psychological symptoms, predicting increases in PTSD, depressive, and anxiety symptoms during the acute post-trauma period but not across later intervals. This pattern suggests that betrayal-related appraisals may amplify distress primarily in the early aftermath of collective trauma, consistent with theories linking violated moral expectations to heightened vigilance, anger, hopelessness, and mistrust (Griffin et al., 2019; Litz et al., 2009). At the same time, betrayal-related processes may attenuate over time through mechanisms such as emotional numbing or disengagement, reducing their capacity to drive prolonged symptom escalation (Delker et al., 2018).

In contrast, psychological distress consistently predicted subsequent levels of betrayal-related MI across waves. Elevated distress may heighten sensitivity to cues of instability and perceived failures of protection, particularly during prolonged collective crises. This finding aligns with emerging longitudinal evidence indicating that distress precedes increases in injustice and betrayal appraisals following the 7 October 2023 events (Mor-Elzas et al., 2026). Together, these results challenge unidirectional models of MI and support a formulation in which moral appraisals are shaped, in part, by individuals’ evolving psychological states. Importantly, betrayal-related appraisals are not assumed to reflect distortion, but may represent meaningful interpretations of moral violations (Litz et al., 2009). They may also involve agency and, in some cases, reflect heightened sensitivity to moral violations rather than solely passive distress (Fleming, 2023).

These findings extend prior research by applying a multi-wave longitudinal design to examine MI processes in civilians. By positioning CM as a developmental vulnerability factor, the study highlights how early relational betrayal may shape moral appraisals during later collective trauma. Moreover, the consistent influence of distress on betrayal-related MI underscores that MI processes cannot be understood independently of broader psychological functioning.

Clinical implications

Clinically, distress and betrayal-related appraisals appear closely intertwined during collective trauma. Interventions targeting both current distress and developmental vulnerabilities may support processing of morally injurious experiences. At the same time, responses may extend beyond the clinical domain to include meaning-making, social engagement, or collective action. At the community level, fostering social connectedness and institutional accountability may support resilience during prolonged crises (Guo et al., 2020; Pazderka et al., 2021).

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. The sample included only Israeli civilians, limiting generalizability to other populations. Palestinian populations profoundly impacted by the same war were not represented in the sampling frame used for recruitment (Buheji, 2025). Given documented differences between Israeli and Palestinian populations in patterns of war exposure, sociocultural context, and trust in state and institutional systems, the moral meanings ascribed to betrayal and protection may differ substantially and warrant separate, context-specific investigation (Mayer et al., 2024). The study was conducted in Hebrew and relied on an online panel of primarily Hebrew-speaking respondents, likely contributing to the underrepresentation of Arab participants. This limits generalizability and highlights the need for more linguistically and culturally diverse samples in future research.

Furthermore, all constructs were assessed via self-report, and CM was measured retrospectively, which may introduce recall and response biases (Brewin et al., 1993; Hardt & Rutter, 2004). Notably, the MIES also does not include established clinical cutoffs, and scores are interpreted dimensionally, which may limit interpretability (Thomas et al., 2024). Attrition across waves may further limit generalizability. Methodologically, reliance on cross-lagged panel models and the inability to estimate bias-corrected indirect effects with FIML require cautious interpretation of directional pathways.

Future research should clarify how early relational adversity shapes moral and psychological responses across development and whether betrayal-related appraisals reflect transient distress reactions or more stable patterns rooted in early experiences. Examining these processes across a broader age range will be important, as developmental differences in reliance on institutions may shape betrayal-related distress.

In addition, distinguishing interpersonal from institutional betrayal and examining contextual moderators such as exposure severity, trust in authorities, and community cohesion may further clarify when and for whom betrayal-related appraisals most strongly influence adjustment during collective trauma. Integrating developmental, contextual, and longitudinal perspectives may advance a more nuanced understanding of MI processes. Lastly, military involvement was assessed only at baseline. As the war evolved, military status may have changed, limiting our ability to capture time-varying exposure to morally salient experiences.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CM is associated with heightened betrayal-related MI appraisals and psychological distress during collective trauma. Importantly, the findings challenge unidirectional MI models: while betrayal-related appraisals showed time-limited associations with symptoms, psychological distress more consistently predicted subsequent levels of perceived betrayal. Together, these findings highlight a dynamic process in which early adversity confers vulnerability to moral threat, while ongoing distress shapes civilians’ perception of failures of protection during collective crises. These insights underscore the value of interventions that address both developmental vulnerabilities and current distress, alongside efforts to strengthen trust during prolonged collective trauma.
