Abstract
. We prove a generalization of the topological Tverberg theorem. One special instance of our general theorem is the following: Let Δ \Delta denote the 8-dimensional simplex viewed as an abstract simplicial complex, and suppose that its vertices are arranged in a 3 × 3 3\times 3 array. Then for any continuous map f : Δ → ℝ 3 f:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}^{3} it is possible to partition the rows or the columns of the vertex array into two parts, such that the disjoint faces σ \sigma and τ \tau induced by the two parts satisfy f ⁡ ( σ ) ∩ f ⁡ ( τ ) ≠ ∅ f(\sigma)\cap f(\tau)\neq\emptyset . Our result also has consequences for geometric transversals and topological Helly theorems.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Tverberg’s theorem
[
12
]
is a famous result of combinatorial geometry asserting that any set of
(d+1)(p-1)+1
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be partitioned into
p
parts such that their convex hulls have a point in common.
This theorem has been extremely influential, with a number of applications and generalizations, and was the starting point of several research directions within geometric and topological combinatorics. See e.g.
[
4
]
for a recent survey.

Here is a standard reformulation of Tverberg’s theorem. Let
X
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\Delta(n)
denote the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex with vertex set
V
.
(Note that here, somewhat unconventionally,
n
denotes the number of vertices and
not
the dimension.)
The point set
X
“induces” a unique
affine
map
f:\Delta(n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
f(V)=X
, which maps a face of
\Delta
to the convex hull of the corresponding points in
X
. From this point of view, Tverberg’s theorem states that
for any affine map

f:\Delta(n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

with
n=(d+1)(p-1)+1
, there exists
pairwise disjoint
faces
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{p}
of
\Delta(n)
such that

f(\sigma_{1})\cap f(\sigma_{2})\cap\cdots\cap f(\sigma_{p})\neq\emptyset.

Bárány, Shlosman, and Szücs
[
2
]
gave a topological generalization of Tverberg’s theorem by showing that the statement above, in the case when
p
is prime, is in fact true for any
continuous map

f
. Their result was further extended by Volovikov
[
13
]
who showed that the theorem also holds when
p
is a prime power. It was a long-standing open problem whether or not the topological Tverberg theorem holds for arbitrary integers
p
, which was eventually disproved by Frick
[
7
]
.

The goal of this note is to introduce a new type of generalization of the topological Tverberg theorem.

1.2.
Main result

We start by fixing some notation.
For positive integers
n
and
m
,
let
\Delta^{m}(n)
denote the
(n^{m}-1)
-dimensional simplex on the vertex set

V=[n]\times[n]\times\cdots\times[n]=[n]^{m}.

Note that
\Delta^{1}(n)=\Delta(n)
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex.
For
i\in[m]
and a subset
A\subset[n]
we define the vertex subset

V^{(i)}{(A)}=\big\{\>(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[n]^{m}:x_{i}\in A\>\big\}\subset V.

In particular, we have
V^{(i)}{(\emptyset)}=\emptyset
.
Observe that a fixed
i\in[m]
and partition
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
, induces a partition of the vertices of
\Delta^{m}(n)

V^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\cup V^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\cup\cdots\cup V^{(i)}{(A_{p})}=V.

Finally, for
i\in[m]
and a subset
A\subset[n]
let
\sigma^{(i)}{(A)}
denote the face of
\Delta^{m}(n)
induced by the vertices
V^{(i)}{(A)}
.
We are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d
,
m
, and
p
be positive integers, with
p
a prime power,
and let
n=\lceil(\frac{d}{m}+1)(p-1)+\frac{1}{m}\rceil
. For any continuous map
f:\Delta^{m}(n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

there exists an integer
i\in[m]
and a partition
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
such that

f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\big)\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\big)\cap\cdots\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big)\neq\emptyset.

Observe that for
m=1
we recover the usual topological Tverberg theorem. The proof of Theorem
1
when
p
is prime will be given in the following section. In Section 3 we explain how to extend the proof to prime powers.
Before getting to the proof we make a few remarks and point out some consequences of Theorem
1
.

1.3.
Remarks

Theorem
1
is a topological generalization of a recent result by Dobbins, Lee, and the first author
[
5
]
. Their result was motivated by the colorful Helly theorem and was formulated in terms of Tverberg partitions for families of convex sets that satisfy the “colorful Helly hypothesis”, but is easily seen to be equivalent to the
affine
case of Theorem
1
. While their proof also uses methods from equivariant topology, and therefore requires
p
to be prime (power), convexity also plays a crucial role in their proof (in particular the use the hyperplane separation theorem and Sarkaria’s tensor method) and is not adaptable to deal with the general continuous case. Let us also note that they showed that when
d
is divisible by
m
, then the value of
n
is optimal (even in the affine case)
[
5
, Remark 3]
.

The affine case is interesting in its own right due to the following connection to geometric transversal theory.
A theorem of Montejano
[
9
,
11
]
states that if we take three red convex sets
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,
R_{3}
and three blue convex sets
B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
where
R_{i}\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset
for all
i
and
j
, then there is a
line transversal
to the red sets or to the blue sets.
To see how this result follows from (the affine case of) Theorem
1
, choose a point
p_{i,j}\in R_{i}\cap B_{j}
for every
i
and
j
.
Thus the points
p_{i,j}
are naturally indexed by the vertices of
\Delta^{2}(3)
, and there is an affine map
f:\Delta^{2}(3)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

such that the triangle
f\big(\sigma^{(1)}{(i)}\big)
is contained in
R_{i}
and the triangle
f\big(\sigma^{(2)}{(j)}\big)
is contained in
B_{j}
for every
i
and
j
. (This uses the fact that the sets are convex.)
By Theorem
1
there is a partition of the red triangles or the blue triangles into two parts such that their convex hulls intersect. But it is easily seen that such a partition implies that the triangles can be intersected by a single line. (Again this uses the convexity of the sets.)
Since these triangles are contained in distinct sets of the same color, the result follows. For other values of
d
,
m
, and
p
one can obtain similar types of results on higher dimensional transversals. (See
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
for further details.)

Another interesting instance of Theorem
1
is when
m=d+1
. In the case when
f
is an affine map we get families
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
\dots
,
F_{d+1}
of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where

F_{i}=\Big\{f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(j)}\big)\Big\}_{j=1}^{n}
.
Since

(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{d+1})\in\sigma^{(1)}{(\{j_{1}\})}\cap\sigma^{(2)}{(\{j_{2}\})}\cap\cdots\cap\sigma^{(d+1)}{(\{j_{d+1}\})}

for every
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{d+1})\in[n]^{d+1}
, it follows that
the families
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
\dots
,
F_{d+1}
satisfy the hypothesis of the colorful Helly theorem
[
3
]
. Therefore there exists a point that intersects every member of one of the families
F_{i}
. In other words, there exists an
i\in[d+1]
such that

\textstyle\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(\{j\})}\big)\neq\emptyset.

We do
not
claim that this is a consequence of Theorem
1
, however,
when
f
is an arbitrary continuous map we obtain a “weak” colorful topological Helly theorem stated below. Note that this differs from the colorful topological Helly theorem of Kalai and Meshulam
[
8
]
as it does not require that the sets form a good cover, but the conclusion we get is also weaker.

Corollary 2
.

For any continuous map
f:\Delta^{d+1}(n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists an integer
i\in[d+1]
and a subset
S\subset[n]
with
|S|\geq\frac{1}{2d+1}n-o(n)
such that

\textstyle\bigcap_{j\in S}f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(\{j\})}\big)\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Suppose first that
n=\big\lfloor\frac{2d+1}{d+1}p\big\rfloor
for some prime (power)
p
. Since
n\geq\frac{2d+1}{d+1}(p-1)+\frac{1}{d+1}
it follows from Theorem
1

that there is an integer
i\in[d+1]
and a partition
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
such that

f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\big)\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\big)\cap\cdots\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big)\neq\emptyset.

Since the average size of a part
A_{i}
is at most
\frac{2d+1}{d+1}<2
there are at least
\frac{1}{d+1}p\geq\frac{1}{2d+1}n
of the parts
A_{i}
that have size 1, which is what we wanted to show. The result for general
n
follows from the fact that the interval
[n,n+n^{0.525}]
contains a prime for all sufficiently large
n
(see
[
1
]
).
∎

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

2.1.
Notation

We use standard notions and terminology from topological combinatorics, in particular the
configuration space / test map
scheme presented for instance in
[
10
]
. One crucial detail to keep in mind is the distinction between partitions and disjoint unions. Recall that

A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{k}=[n]

is a
partition
if all the sets
A_{i}
are nonempty and pairwise disjoint. When we say
disjoint union
, on the other hand, we allow for some of the
A_{i}
to be empty. For partitions or disjoint unions, the order of the parts does not matter, but in the case when the order of parts plays a role we use the notation

A_{1}\uplus A_{2}\uplus\cdots\uplus A_{k}.

2.2.
Proof outline

Fix integers
m\geq 1
,
n\geq 2
, and
p\geq 2
, with
p
prime. We define a simplicial complex
K=K(n,m,p)
on a vertex set consisting of an ordered union of
p
disjoint copies of
[n]^{m}
. That is,

V(K)=\underset{\text{part 1}}{[n]^{m}}\uplus\underset{\text{part 2}}{[n]^{m}}\uplus\cdots\uplus\underset{\text{part $p$}}{[n]^{m}}.

The facets of
K
are given by

\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}

for all
1\leq i\leq m
and for all disjoint unions
A_{1}\uplus A_{2}\uplus\cdots\uplus A_{p}=[n]
. Here it should be understood that
\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{j})}
belongs to the
j
th part of the vertex set. Observe that the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
acts on
K
by cyclically shifting the parts of the union. Thus
K
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplex of
\big(\Delta^{m}(n)\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}
, the
p
-fold pairwise deleted join of
\Delta^{m}(n)
. (The complex
K
is the so-called “configuration space” for our problem.)

There are two main steps to the proof of Theorem
1
. The first step is to show that if the conclusion of the theorem fails for some continuous map
f:\Delta^{m}(n)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we can construct a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map (the so-called “test map”)

K\to S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}

The second step is to construct a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-complex
T
together with a

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
T\to K
. The
complex
T
will be a subcomplex of the nerve complex of the facets of
K
. We will show that when
n
satisfies the bound in the hypothesis of Theorem
1
, then the complex
T
is
\big((d+1)(p-1)-1\big)
-connected.
Therefore, a counter-example to Theorem
1
would give us
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
T\to S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}
, contradicting Dold’s Theorem.

Theorem 3

(Dold
[
6
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite, nontrivial group,
X
an
n
-connected
G
-space, and
Y
a paracompact topological space with a free
G
-action and dimension at most
n
. Then there does not exist a
G
-map
X\rightarrow Y
.

2.3.
Proof

Since our configuration space
K
is a subcomplex of
\big(\Delta^{m}(n)\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}
, the points of
K
may be represented as
formal convex combinations

t_{1}x_{1}\oplus t_{2}x_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{p}x_{p}

where

t_{1}+\cdots+t_{p}=1
and
t_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
,

each
x_{i}
is in the simplex spanned by the
i
th part of
V(K)
, and

\textrm{supp}(x_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\textrm{supp}(x_{p})
is a face of
K
, i.e., there is some
i
and a disjoint union
A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
such that
\textrm{supp}(x_{j})\subset\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{j})}
for all
1\leq j\leq p
.

Note that there is a natural action of
S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}
, the symmetric group on
p
elements, on
K
given by

\pi\>(t_{1}x_{1}\oplus t_{2}x_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{p}x_{p})=\\
t_{\pi^{-1}(1)}x_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\oplus t_{\pi^{-1}(2)}x_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{\pi^{-1}(p)}x_{\pi^{-1}(p)}.

Furthermore, the subgroup of
S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}
generated by the permutation
(1\,2\,\cdots\,p)
(written in cycle notation) induces a free
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-action on
K
.

Recall that
\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}
, the
p
-fold (
p
-wise) deleted join of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subset of the
p
-fold join of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is given by

\textstyle\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}=\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}\setminus\left\{\frac{1}{p}x\oplus\frac{1}{p}x\oplus\cdots\oplus\frac{1}{p}x:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\},

Here
S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}
acts on
\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}
in a natural way by permuting the parts, and in particular,
\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}
has a free
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-action when
p
is prime.

Let
f:\Delta^{m}(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a continuous map as in Theorem
1
, and assume for contradiction that

f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\big)\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\big)\cap\cdots\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big)=\emptyset

for every
i\in[m]
and every partition
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
.
This assumption allows us to define the test map
\tilde{f}:K\rightarrow\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}
by

t_{1}x_{1}\oplus t_{2}x_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{p}x_{p}\mapsto t_{1}f(x_{1})\oplus t_{2}f(x_{2})\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{p}f(x_{p}),

which is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map. Since there exists a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta}\rightarrow S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}
where
S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}
is equipped with a free
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-action (see e.g.
[
10
, Proposition 6.3.2]
), we obtain the following.

Lemma 4
.

If
f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\big)\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\big)\cap\cdots\cap f\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big)=\emptyset
for every
i\in[m]
and every partition
A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}=[n]
, then there exists a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
K\to S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}
.

We now want to show that if
n\geq\lceil(\frac{d}{m}+1)(p-1)+\frac{1}{m}\rceil
, then there exists a
((d+1)(p-1)-1)
-connected
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-complex
T
and a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map

T\to K
, which gives us the desired contradiction to Dold’s theorem. The construction of
T
and the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map requires several steps and takes up the remainder of this section.

Let
F
denote the set of facets of
K
, and let
N
be the
nerve
of
F
, that is, the simplicial complex with vertex set
F
whose faces are the subsets
S\subset F
such that
\bigcap_{\tau\in S}\tau\neq\emptyset
.
Then
S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}
acts on
N
by

\pi\>\big(\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big)=\\
\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{\pi^{-1}(1)})}\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{\pi^{-1}(2)})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{\pi^{-1}(p)})}.

Note that the action of the subgroup
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
on
K
is free, which gives us a free

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-action on
N
.

Lemma 5
.

There is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
N\to K
.

Proof.

Recall that for a simplicial complex
L
, its barycentric subdivision
\mathrm{sd}(L)
is the simplicial complex whose vertices are the nonempty faces of
L
and simplices are the chains of nonempty faces of
L
ordered by inclusion. Note that if
L
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-complex, then the group action on
L
induces a group action on
\mathrm{sd}(L)
, and for this induced group action there are
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-maps
\mathrm{sd}(L)\to L
and
L\to\mathrm{sd}(L)
.
Therefore it suffices to exhibit a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map
\mathrm{sd}(N)\to\mathrm{sd}(K)
.

Let
\tau=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}\}
be a nonempty face of
N
.
Observe that
\tau
is a vertex of
\mathrm{sd}(N)
and
\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}
is a vertex of
\mathrm{sd}(K)
.
Moreover, for the chain

\{\tau_{1}\}\subset\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\}\subset\cdots\subset\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{k}\},

we have

\tau_{1}\supset(\tau_{1}\cap\tau_{2})\supset\cdots\supset(\tau_{1}\cap\tau_{2}\cap\cdots\cap\tau_{k})

and therefore
\tau\mapsto\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}
defines a simplicial map
\mathrm{sd}(N)\to\mathrm{sd}(K)
. Since
\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\tau=\{\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\tau_{1},\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\tau_{2},\dots,\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\tau_{k}\}
and
\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{i}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\pi\hskip 0.86108pt\tau_{i}
, this gives us the desired
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-map.
∎

We now define a family of vertex disjoint,
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplexes of
N
. Our goal is to show that their join gives us a highly connected subcomplex of
N
.

Let
M
be the
induced subcomplex
of
N
on the vertex set

\big\{\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{1})}\uplus\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{p})}\big\}

taken over all

A_{1}\uplus A_{2}\uplus\cdots\uplus A_{p}=[n]
. Clearly
M
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplex of
N
. We also observe that
M
has precisely
n\cdot p
facets. Indeed for every
j\in[n]
and

we get a unique facet induced by the vertices of

for which
j\in A_{k}
.

Lemma 6
.

The complex
M
is
(n-2)
-connected.

Proof.

Recall that the
p
-fold pairwise deleted join of the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplex
\big(\Delta^{n-1}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}
is an
(n-2)
-connected space (see e.g.
[

Let
B
denote the nerve complex of the set of facets of
\big(\Delta^{n-1}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}
. By the nerve theorem

\big(\Delta^{n-1}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}

(n-2)

By viewing
\Delta^{n-1}
as the simplex on the vertex set
[n]
, a facet of
\big(\Delta^{n-1}\big)^{*p}_{\Delta(2)}
(i.e. a vertex of

A_{1}\uplus A_{2}\uplus\cdots\uplus A_{p}=[n].

To complete the proof we simply observe that the map

A_{1}\uplus A_{2}\uplus\cdots\uplus A_{p}\mapsto\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{1})}\uplus\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(1)}{(A_{p})}

is a simplicial isomorphism between

.
∎

For each
2\leq i\leq m
, define a subcomplex of

on the set of vertices

\big\{\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{1})}\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{p})}\big\}

. The faces of

\big\{\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{j,1})}\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{j,2})}\uplus\cdots\uplus\sigma^{(i)}{(A_{j,p})}\big\}_{j=1}^{k}

where
\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}A_{j,\ell}\neq\emptyset
for every

.

Clearly

is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplex of
N
. We also observe that
L_{i}
has precisely
\frac{n!}{(n-p)!}
facets.
Indeed, for every ordered
p
-tuple
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{p})
of
[n]
we get a unique facet induced by the vertices of
L_{i}
for which
j_{1}\in A_{1}
,
j_{2}\in A_{2}
, …,
j_{p}\in A_{p}
.

Lemma 7
.

For all
2\leq i\leq m
, the complex
L_{i}
is
(n-p-1)
-connected.

Proof.

Let
C_{n,p}
be the simplicial complex whose vertices are the ordered partitions of
[n]
into
p
non-empty parts
(A_{1},\dots,A_{p})
with faces

\{(A_{j,1},A_{j,2},\dots,A_{j,p})\}_{j=1}^{k}

where
\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}A_{j,\ell}\neq\emptyset
for all
1\leq\ell\leq p
. It is straightforward to show that
C_{n,p}
and
L_{i}
are isomorphic, and it was shown in
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
that
C_{n,p}
is

(n-p-1)
-connected.
∎

Recall that the vertices of
N
are the facets of
K
, and it follows from the definition that a facet of
K
is a vertex in at most one of the complexes
M,L_{2},\dots,L_{m}
(but not every vertex of
N
appears in one of these subcomplexes). Since the complexes
M,L_{2},\dots,L_{m}
are vertex disjoint we may take their join and define

T=M*L_{2}*\cdots*L_{m}.

Lemma 8
.

The complex
T
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplex of
N
.

Proof.

We first show that
T\subset N
. The
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariance follows from the fact that
M,L_{2},\dots,L_{p}
are all
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-invariant subcomplexes of
N
.

Let
\tau=\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\tau_{m}
be a facet of
T
, where
\tau_{1}
is a facet of
M
and
\tau_{i}
is a facet of
L_{i}
for all
2\leq i\leq m
.
Since each
\tau_{i}
is a collection of facets of
K
, it follows that
\tau
is a face of
N
if and only if there exists a vertex

v\in\underset{\text{part 1}}{[n]^{m}}\uplus\underset{\text{part 2}}{[n]^{m}}\uplus\cdots\uplus\underset{\text{part }p}{[n]^{m}}=V(K)

which belongs to every facet (of
K
) in

\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\tau_{m}
.

To see why this is true we first observe that for every
2\leq i\leq p
there is an ordered
p
-tuple
(j_{i,1},j_{i,2},\dots,j_{i,p})
of
[n]
such that every facet (of
K
) in
\tau_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\tau_{m}
contains

\begin{array}[]{cccccccl}[n]&\uplus&\{j_{2,1}\}&\uplus&\cdots&\uplus&\{j_{m,1}\}&\text{ in part 1,}\\
[n]&\uplus&\{j_{2,2}\}&\uplus&\cdots&\uplus&\{j_{m,2}\}&\text{ in part 2,}\\
&&&&\vdots&&&\\
[n]&\uplus&\{j_{2,p}\}&\uplus&\cdots&\uplus&\{j_{m,p}\}&\text{ in part $p$.}\end{array}

Next, observe that there exists integers
j\in[n]
and
k\in[p]
such that every facet (of
K
) in
\tau_{1}
contains

\begin{array}[]{cccccccl}\{j\}&\uplus&[n]&\uplus&\cdots&\uplus&[n]&\text{ in part $k$.}\\
\end{array}

It follows that the vertex
v=(j,j_{2,k},\dots,j_{m,k})
in the
k
th part of
V(K)
belongs to every facet (of
K
) in
\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}\cup\cdots\cup\tau_{m}
.
∎

In summary we have the following
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-maps

T\underset{\text{Lemma }\ref{lem:T}}{\hookrightarrow}N\underset{\text{Lemma }\ref{lem:NerveMap}}{\to}K\underset{\text{Lemma }\ref{lem:test}}{\to}S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}.

To complete the proof of Theorem
1
, observe that for
n\geq\lceil(\frac{d}{m}+1)(p-1)+\frac{1}{m}\rceil
we have

n-2+(m-1)(n-p+1)\geq(d+1)(p-1)-1.

By the connectivity of the join (see e.g.
[
10
, Proposition 4.4.3]
) together with Lemmas
6
and
7
it follows that
T
is
\big((d+1)(p-1)-1\big)
-connected, which gives us the desired contradiction to Dold’s theorem. ∎

3.
Final remarks

The proof of Theorem
1
in the case when
p=q^{r}
is a prime power is more or less identical to the prime case. The set up of the configuration space and the test map is the same, and even though the symmetric group
S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}

does not act freely, it does give a
fixed-point free
action on
K
and on the target space
S^{(d+1)(p-1)-1}
. Therefore the action of the subgroup
\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\subset S\hskip-1.72218pt_{p}
is also fixed-point free, which gives us an equivariant test-map that contradicts the following theorem of Volovikov
[
13
]
.

Theorem

(Volovikov)
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
be the product of finitely many copies with
q
prime. Let
X
and
Y
be fixed point free
G
-spaces, where
X
is
n
-connected and
Y
is finite-dimensional and homotopy equivalent to
S^{n}
. Then there is no
G
-equivariant map
X\to Y
.

Regarding Corollary
2
, we do not have any reason to believe that the constant
\frac{1}{2d+1}
is optimal, but it seems that our proof method can not lead to further improvements. For
d\geq 2
we do not even have an example that shows that the constant is strictly less than 1. (For
d=1
it is easily seen that the constant equals 1.)
We leave it as an open problem to determine the optimal constant.
