Neglected sleep quality problems in women with gestational diabetes mellitus: a multi-center cross-sectional study
Authors: Jingya Yu, Zhixiang Chen, Yanhan Chen, Xingyu Wang, Zhongchen Luo, Jili Fan, Lingyao Chen, Qinghua Zhao, Jiao Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is the most common complication of pregnancy, affecting an estimated 16.7% of live births worldwide, which translates to approximately 21 million cases annually. Although poor sleep quality is a prevalent concern in this population, most existing studies predominantly focus on the impact of sleep disorders on GDM, rather than directly assessing sleep quality in women diagnosed with GDM. This study aimed to investigate sleep quality and its associated factors specifically among women with GDM.MethodsWe originally aimed to include 287 women with GDM; ultimately, 618 were invited, of whom 393 participated in the survey through a convenience sampling method at the obstetric clinics of four general tertiary hospitals in Chongqing, China, from June to August 2022. Data were collected utilizing sociodemographic characteristics, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Sleep Hygiene Scale, and the Pregnancy-related Anxiety Scale (PrAS). We employed linear regression analysis to identify factors associated with sleep quality.ResultsThe PSQI score of participants was (5.83 ± 3.41). Notably, 43.77% of participants reported an actual sleep duration of less than 8 h per night, whereas 45.55% experienced poor sleep quality. Worrying during the day about your inability to fall asleep (β = 0.204, P < 0.01), body image concern (β = 0.159, P < 0.01), going to bed thirsty (β = 0.160, P < 0.01), educational attainment (β=-0.150, P < 0.01), avoidance (β = 0.124, P < 0.01), sleep disturbed by bed partner’s sleep (β = 0.126, P < 0.01), and worrying as you prepare for bed about your inability to fall asleep (β = 0.137, P < 0.05) significantly affected the sleep quality of participants.ConclusionsThe study revealed a high prevalence of insufficient sleep duration and poor sleep quality among women with GDM, which was significantly influenced by inadequate sleep hygiene habits and pregnancy-related anxiety. These findings enhance our understanding of the factors affecting sleep quality in this population and underscore the necessity for healthcare professionals to timely identify sleep disorders. Implementing appropriate interventions, such as the development of educational programs focused on sleep hygiene and body image acceptance through cognitive behavioral therapy, could enhance the quality of care for women with GDM.Keywords: Sleep quality, Sleep hygiene, Anxiety, Gestational diabetes mellitus, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), the most prevalent complication of pregnancy, is defined as an intolerance to glucose during this period [1]. As reported by the International Diabetes Federation, the global prevalence of GDM is estimated to affect 16.7% of live births, which equates to approximately 21 million cases annually complicated by GDM [2]. Furthermore, around one in six neonates is affected by gestational diabetes-related complications at birth [3]. GDM significantly impacts both maternal and fetal health. It increases the likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preeclampsia and cesarean delivery), and has long-term health implications. Mothers with GDM face an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease later in life. Furthermore, their offspring are predisposed to a higher incidence of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome in adulthood [4, 5].

Sleep is a complex physiological process that constitutes one-third of an individual’s life and is essential for energy restoration and proper functioning [6, 7]. Sleep quality refers to an individual’s satisfaction with various aspects of sleep, including efficiency, latency, duration, and wakefulness after sleep onset [8]. Good sleep quality is typically defined by the ability to fall asleep within 30 min, maintain uninterrupted sleep throughout the majority of the night with minimal awakenings, and achieve a total sleep duration of 7–9 h in adults [9]. Disruptions in sleep can negatively impact the endocrine, cardiovascular, immune, and neurological systems, leading to long-term health consequences [10–12].

Many studies have explored the role of sleep disorders in the etiology of GDM [13–16]and reported that insufficient sleep during pregnancy is related to an increased risk of developing hyperglycemia [14, 15]. Specifically, inadequate sleep induces metabolic and neuroendocrine changes, particularly those affecting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. These alterations may significantly contribute to the development of glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, and GDM [14, 16, 17]. Improving sleep quality may be a valuable approach for preventing GDM among pregnant women. A cohort study conducted in Singapore revealed that poor sleep quality during pregnancy was an independent predictor of GDM [13], and sleep-disordered breathing is associated with a 55% greater risk of gestational diabetes [18].

Despite the existing evidence, data regarding the prevalence of sleep disturbances among women with GDM remain limited. One study indicated that up to 43.1% of women with GDM report poor sleep quality [13]. However, researchers have largely overlooked the sleep problems faced by pregnant women with GDM. These women often experience physical discomfort due to hyperglycemia, including increased urination, excessive thirst, and restless leg syndrome, all of which can disrupt sleep patterns. Moreover, the constant need for self-monitoring of blood glucose levels and adherence to strict dietary adjustments often heightens anxiety and stress, thereby exacerbating sleep disturbances [19]. Poor sleep quality among women with GDM is associated with compromised glucose metabolism and increased insulin resistance, leading to a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus. A previous study recommended that pregnant women get a minimum of 8 h of sleep per night [20]. For women with GDM, inadequate sleep can lead to an increase of 2-6 mg/dL in glucose per hour [21]. This can fail to achieve glycemic control, and cause 10–20% of GDM-complicated pregnancies [22], which is related to a greater rate of adverse pregnancy outcomes [23].

In summary, further research is needed to fully comprehend the complex relationship between sleep quality and GDM. It is particularly important to address the issue of sleep disturbances experienced by women with GDM. However, there is a scarcity of data available to determine the magnitude and associations of sleep quality among this population. This study endeavors to bridge the current research gap by assessing the prevalence of sleep quality and the associated factors among women with GDM. These associated factors may include demographic variables such as employment status, and educational attainment, as well as psychological factors like anxiety, all of which are hypothesized to significantly affect sleep quality. By examining these variables, this study seeks to enhance the understanding of sleep quality in women with GDM, thereby enabling healthcare professionals to develop more targeted and effective strategies for managing sleep disturbances in this population.

Methods

Participants

This multi-center cross-sectional study was conducted in Chongqing, a city located in Southeast China, from June to August 2022. The study was carried out at four major hospitals in Chongqing, chosen for their substantial patient base and extensive medical resources. These hospitals are recognized as significant healthcare institutions in the region, offering access to an adequate number of patient records for our research.

The study included participants according to the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of GDM [24], (2) age ≥ 18 years, (3) singleton pregnancy, and (4) provision of informed consent. The following were the exclusion criteria: (1) presence of severe internal and surgical diseases (e.g., dysfunction of the heart, brain, kidneys, or other organs, and severe trauma), (2) being in a state of obstetrical emergency (e.g., eclampsia, placental abruption, or placenta previa hemorrhage), and (3) having comorbid conditions (e.g., pregestational diabetes mellitus). Participants were identified through the electronic medical records system during routine prenatal check-ups. Healthcare professionals, trained in the study protocol, reviewed the participants’ records to confirm their diagnosis of GDM.

G*Power version 3.1.9.7 was employed to guarantee a sufficient sample size for the linear multiple regression analysis [25]. We selected the linear multiple regression: random model in G*Power, considering an effect size of 0.10, 13 independent variables associated with sleep quality based on the relevant published studies [26–28], a significant level (P) of 0.05, and 95% power, the sample size was calculated to be 287. Ultimately, 618 GDM women were recruited from four hospitals in Chongqing. 393 contributed comprehensive data available for analysis.

Data collection

Data were collected using Questionnaire Star, a reputable online survey tool developed by Changsha Ranxing Information Technology. In China, 217 million questionnaires were distributed on this platform, and 17.41 billion responses were retrieved. The data collection procedure was as follows.

E-questionnaire setting

The questionnaire was edited on the platform and a link was created enabling access through WeChat (China’s most popular mobile messaging app). On the front page of the E-questionnaire, the informed consent form was provided to the participants (e.g., they are free to choose whether or not to participate in this survey and quit the survey at any time without suffering any consequences) and consent was obtained. Then, coming to the second page to evaluate the inclusion and exclusion requirements. Healthcare professionals verified each participant’s eligibility through their electronic medical records, ensuring that only those meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were invited to participate. Finally, participants who meet the inclusion criteria proceed to complete the E-questionnaire. Otherwise, exit the link. The participants needed approximately 15 min to complete the questionnaire.

E-questionnaire distribution

Healthcare professionals in the obstetric outpatient department were invited and encouraged to share the E-questionnaire link with potential participants through personal WeChat or WeChat groups. To motivate participation, assistants who helped distribute the questionnaires received a monetary incentive. Moreover, participants were informed that there would be a reward for completing the questionnaire; however, the specific nature of this reward, such as the random digital money via WeChat, was not disclosed in advance. This approach aimed to emphasize voluntary participation rather than the incentive itself. Consequently, the link was effectively disseminated to a broad network. The questionnaire was easily accessible to participants through the provided link, and they were able to complete it by following the online instructions.

Measures

Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a widely utilized tool for assessing sleep quality and disturbances over a one-month period, was selected for its comprehensive evaluation of sleep patterns, duration, and disturbances, essential for exploring the relationship between sleep quality and GDM. The PSQI is a self-report questionnaire consisting of 19 items designed to measure seven dimensions of sleep: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each dimension of the PSQI is rated on a scale from 0 to 3, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 21. The higher the score is, the inferior one is to sleep in the previous month [29]. Specifically, a score of 5 or higher signifies inadequate sleep and poor sleep quality [30]. The Cronbach’s alpha for pregnant women is 0.73. While identifying good and bad sleepers were identofied, the diagnostic sensitivity was 90%, and the specificity was 87% for a total score exceeding 5 [31].

Sleep hygiene scale

The Sleep Hygiene Scale was included to evaluate the impact of sleep-related behaviors on sleep quality, specifically assessing whether modifiable habits contribute to poor sleep among women with GDM. Developed by Lacks and Rotert [32], this 19-item instrument is designed to objectively measure the effects of daily activities and habits, including eating and drinking, on sleep. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 (never) to 7 (very often) with a higher total score indicating poorer sleep hygiene practices. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.60, indicating moderate reliability [33].

Pregnancy-related anxiety scale (PrAS)

Pregnancy-related anxiety disrupts sleep quality and metabolic regulation, potentially contributing to GDM. The PrAS was chosen to assess this specific anxiety. The PrAS was developed by Brunton [34]and consistes of 32-item self-reported items, categorized into eight subscales: childbirth concerns, body image concerns, attitudes toward childbirth, worry about motherhood, acceptance of pregnancy, anxiety indicators, attitude towards medical staff, avoidance, and baby concern. Each item of the PrAS is measured using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very often), with higher scores indicate greater anxiety. Notably, eleven items of the PrAS are inversely scored. A total score ≥ 75.5 suggested that an increased anxiety level was associated with pregnancy. The PrAS intercoder reliability ranged from 0.77 to 0.95, which is considered excellent [35].

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics datasheet

Participant’s general socio-demographic data were collected using a self-designed questionnaire, including age, marital status, educational attainment, body mass index (BMI), and employment status. Clinical characteristics included current gestational weeks, birth history, problematic pregnancies, and pregnancy symptoms. The socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were gathered to account for potential factors influencing sleep quality. These variables serve as critical indicators for identifying individuals at an increased risk of experiencing poor sleep quality.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Science (SPSS) software version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. Descriptive analyses were performed to describe the characteristics of the participants by frequency and percentage, as well as mean and standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests were used to compare sleep quality between binary subgroups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess differences in sleep quality across categorical variables with more than two levels. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to explore associations between continuous variables. Variables with P < 0.05 were included in a multiple-liner regression model to identify factors associated with sleep quality.

Ethical considerations

The study followed the guidelines established in the 1995 Helsinki Declaration (as revised in Edinburgh 2000). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (No.2022174). Additionally, we secured permission from the hospitals to access participants electronic medical records. Prior to data collection, participants were provided with informed consent through an online survey tool. To safeguard participants’ information throughout the study, we implemented data privacy measures, including the anonymization of all collected data to protect participant identities.

A total of 304 participants (77.40%) had an education background of a college degree or above degree, and 237 participants (60.30%) were employed during pregnancy. Associated factors of poor sleep quality

The univariate analysis revealed that several factors were significanly associated with sleep quality: pregnancy-related anxiety(F = 0.36, P < 0.01), sleep hygiene(F = 0.33, P < 0.01), college degree or above (F = 7.05, P < 0.01), and symptoms of discomfort (t = 3.22, P < 0.01) (Table 3). The multiple linear regression model included the above variable with P < 0.05. Furthermore, the best-fit multiple linear regression model included seven variables. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this cross-sectional study is the first to explore sleep quality and its associated factors among women with GDM in China. Our findings demonstrate that poor sleep quality is a significant concern in this population, with over 45.55% of participants reporting insufficient sleep. This high prevalence highlights the critical need to address sleep disturbances in GDM women as an integral component of prenatal care throughout pregnancy. Although it is widely recognized that sleep disturbances are prevalent among women with GDM, there has been a lack of comprehensive research in this area, which complicates glycemic management during pregnancy. This research gap presents considerable challenges for optimizing glycemic control and emphasizes the necessity of integrating sleep disturbance management into clinical practice. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of poor sleep quality and identify its key associated factors among women with GDM.

In this study, 45.55% of women reported poor sleep quality during pregnancy, and many failed to meet the recommended sleep time of 8 h [36]. This finding aligns with our hypothesis that sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among women with GDM. This high prevalence of sleep disturbances may contribute to suboptimal glycemic control, thereby complicating pregnancy outcomes for women with GDM. Poor sleep quality among women with GDM might cause a series of pathophysiological responses, including impaired judgment and psychomotor skills, even aggravating insulin resistance [37, 38]. Moreover, a cohort study involving 6714 pregnant women conducted in Shanghai demonstrated that poor sleep quality among women with GDM was associated with adverse health outcomes for their offspring, such as overweight or obesity and unhealthy lifestyles [39]. Therefore, healthcare workers are advised to actively identify and monitor sleep-related issues in women with GDM, particularly during the first and third trimesters when physiological and hormonal changes are most pronounced [40].

The findings of our study are in agreement with existing research, indicating that individuals with poor sleep hygiene practices (e.g., daytime napping) tend to have poorer sleep quality. Research has shown that individuals with inadequate sleep hygiene often develop unhealthy sleep behaviors such as delayed bedtime and irregular routines, which can lead to emotional disorders [41, 42]. Improving sleep hygiene practices represents a low-cost and low-risk strategy that is crucial to public health efforts aimed at addressing sleep disorders [43]. Thus, healthcare workers are encouraged to pursue straightforward and economical measures to enhance sleep hygiene and prevent sleep-related issues. Initiatives such as sleep education programs and interventions, including establishing a consistent wake-up time, promoting healthy sleep habits, and managing daytime napping, are necessary to raise awareness among women with GDM and to foster healthy sleep behaviors.

The study revealed a significant association between pregnancy-related anxiety and poor sleep quality. Our findings, similar to previous studies [44], found that a higher level of pregnancy-related anxiety, particularly concerning body image, predicted poorer sleep quality [45]. Body image refers to an individual’s internal representation of their physical appearance [46]. During pregnancy, rapid and extensive physical changes can make women feel disconnected from their bodies. One study reported that pregnant women would experience anxiety if they concentrate excessively on their weight gain or fail to meet the weight gain expectations set by others [47]. Therefore, healthcare professionals can offer clinical communication care to women with GDM to help them cope with body image concerns and develop strategies to promote acceptance or reduce dissatisfaction. Moreover, web and computer-based cognitive behavioral therapy has proven effective in preventing pregnancy-related anxiety.

Our findings demonstrated a significant association between sleep quality and educational attainment among women with GDM. Individuals with a bachelor’s or master’s degree reported poorer sleep quality. This phenomenon may be attributed to the tendency of highly educated women to focus more on glycemic management than less educated women do. In addition, highly educated women with GDM may acquire knowledge about pregnancy and childbirth through a variety of sources [48]. However, it is easy for highly educated women to fall into a one-sided perspective. Therefore, when developing a control strategy, it is critical to consider sociodemographic characteristics. Furthermore, substantial attention is needed for populations with higher educational attainment to prevent them from receiving nonprofessional knowledge, which may cause anxiety. For example, medical staff can provide women with brochures for health education and facilitate online health education through mobile phones [49].

The findings revealed a significant association between sleep quality and employment status during pregnancy, indicating that unemployed pregnant women with GDM reported poorer sleep quality than their employed counterparts. This relationship was in accordance with the results of a previous study conducted in Taiwan, which likewise revealed that unemployed women experienced poorer sleep quality than those who were employed full-time [28]. The underlying reason for this disparity may be attributed to the lack of daily routine activities among unemployed individuals, as suggested by a recent study [50]. In addition, unemployment is related to sleep problems, potentially due to the anxiety that accompanies unemployment, particularly for those who are economically inactive [51]. These findings underscore the crucial role of economic status in influencing sleep quality among women with GDM. Therefore, it is advisable for the government to provide financial support or free medical services to this population, as this approach could serve as an effective strategy to enhance sleep quality for women with GDM.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between sleep quality and associated variables, permitting only for the identification of associations. Future longitudinal studies are needed to gain a deeper understand sleep quality before, during, and after pregnancy. Additionally, the relatively small sample size and the focus on Chongqing, China, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions. Conducting larger, more diverse samples across multiple regions would improve the robustness and applicability of the results. Further research should also explore factors such as comorbidities and lifestyle behaviors, and assess intervention strategies, including behavioral therapies and psychological counseling, to improve sleep quality and health outcomes in women with GDM.

Conclusion

This study revealed that nearly half of the participants experienced poor sleep quality, underscoring the urgent need for public health interventions for wome with GDM. Poor sleep quality was associated with inadequate sleep hygiene, elevated pregnancy-related anxiety, and higher educational attainment. Promoting effective sleep habits, managing anxiety, and providing tailored sleep education are crucial for these women. Healthcare providers should implement comprehensive sleep hygiene programs and anxiety-reduction strategies to improve sleep quality and potentially enhance glycemic control. Policymakers must prioritize sleep education in prenatal care guidelines throughout pregnancy to effectively support women with GDM. These findings deepen our understanding of the factors affecting sleep quality in this population, laying the groundwork for targeted interventions to improve maternal and fetal health outcomes. Addressing these issues will contribute to better health outcomes for this vulnerable population, highlighting the importance of prioritizing sleep health in GDM management.
