Improving IoT security through an explainable hybrid CNN-transformer model and federated learning
Authors: Aymen M. Al-Hejri, Riyadh M. Al-Tam, Archana Harsing Sable, Sultan S. Alshamrani, Kaled M. Alshmrany, Abdullah Alshehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has intensified cybersecurity threats, exposing critical infrastructure to sophisticated intrusion attacks. Existing intrusion detection systems (IDS) typically rely on centralized architectures that compromise data privacy, or employ single-architecture models that fail to capture both local spatial patterns and long-range temporal dependencies in network traffic simultaneously. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a novel explainable hybrid CNN-Transformer model integrated with federated learning (FL) for privacy-preserving intrusion detection in IoT environments. The proposed framework uniquely combines four key components not previously integrated in this context: a dual-block CNN-Transformer architecture, federated learning with FedAvg aggregation, multi-class attack classification, and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) for decision transparency. Evaluated on the IoT-23 dataset across both federated and non-federated scenarios, the proposed model achieves 94.89% accuracy in federated binary classification and 92.17% in federated multi-class classification, outperforming standalone CNN and ensemble baselines by significant margins. Generalizability is further validated through an ablation study on the CIC IoT-DIAD 2024 dataset. The integration of LIME provides actionable feature-level explanations that support real-time decision-making for network security analysts, advancing both the interpretability and trustworthiness of automated IoT intrusion detection systems.

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) zone has seen great development characterized by a large number of packages, thereby producing an increasing array of enterprise potentialities in recent years. As an essential element of the approaching era of the net, IoT is widely applied across numerous contexts. However, it is no longer limited to smart cities, clever homes, and industrial IoT to facilitate statistics-driven decision-making and sensible automation through profound engagement with the physical environment1,2. Despite its super achievements, the total capability of the IoT has yet to be found out, especially because of its insecurities. The demanding security situations of IoT are derived from numerous elements, including the growing amount of data, the growing range of devices, and the embedded nature of IoT devices3,4. These elements heighten the heterogeneity, the limited computation capability, and the confined energy, storage, and bandwidth of many IoT devices5. Threats like Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) assaults, botnets, and data breaches are increasingly targeting IoT networks, underscoring the need for a strong IDS able to correctly identify and mitigate these threats6,7. These demanding situations are leading to further safety dangers and capability vulnerabilities and are affecting many fields, which include privacy troubles, infrastructure protection, and the security of diverse embedded software and system offerings.

Over the past decade, numerous contributions have been made regarding the protection against security issues and the efficient detection of malicious attacks. To improve the performance of the attack detection process, the most traditional centralized mechanism is to gather the data from an IoT network environment and then transmit it to an external server for the detection of malicious activities by AI-powered models locally8.

The AI-powered models have exhibited strong capabilities in detecting malicious activities, and their training processes have a strong demand for large-scale and diverse datasets9. However, IoT devices are often equipped with low-capacity hardware, computing, storage, and power resources, which may limit their contribution to AI model training10. Moreover, considering the problem of privacy preservation, the communication cost needed to transmit large amounts of data may be intolerable, especially when the data are sensitive and require effective protection. Advances in machine learning and deep learning have the potential to improve security for IoT11. However, traditional methods that rely on centralized data storage and processing raise privacy concerns and are not easily scalable across distributed IoT networks. Federated learning is a method of machine learning in which a model is trained using separate local models from multiple decentralized clients. Only model updates from the clients are exchanged between clients and aggregations to the global server, and no raw data is exchanged across the systems12. FL is particularly useful in privacy-sensitive device data settings, where user data is not allowed to leave the user’s device13. Despite these benefits, challenges with accuracy and understanding complex IoT datasets still need to be addressed. In parallel, hybrid models, specifically those combining CNN and transformer architectures, have shown potential in addressing the unique data characteristics of IoT environments. CNNs excel in capturing spatial patterns within network data, while transformers effectively model temporal dependencies and long-range interactions14. By merging these strengths, a transformer-CNN hybrid model can significantly improve the accuracy of intrusion detection systems, particularly in multi-class classification tasks involving various types of IoT-based attacks.

Despite these advancements, existing approaches often focus solely on centralised learning or lack effective hybrid models for capturing complex patterns in IoT data. Moreover, many models fail to incorporate explainability, which is crucial for gaining user trust and ensuring transparency in cybersecurity decisions.

To address the gaps in detection accuracy and transparency, this paper proposes an explainable hybrid CNN transformer-based model within a non-federated and federated learning framework scenario for IoT attack detection using the IoT-23 dataset. The model is designed to improve attack classification accuracy while incorporating explainability through LIME, allowing users to understand model predictions and build trust in the system’s decision-making process. This study evaluates the model’s performance in both non-federated and federated learning settings, demonstrating its effectiveness in accurately detecting diverse IoT attacks while preserving data privacy. Literature review

In this section, we will be conducting a comprehensive and extensive comparison between the previously conducted studies, diligently examining and thoroughly exploring the various facets of how machine learning models are efficiently deployed in IoT attack detection and put to use in both non-federated and federated learning scenarios.

IoT security and attack detection in a non-federated learning approach

The security of Internet of Things (IoT) systems has been a topic of extensive research, as there is a growing worry about the risks and dangers that interconnected devices face. The IoT and ML have seen significant growth in sectors such as healthcare, manufacturing, agriculture, and transportation, with applications ranging from sales prediction to device monitoring and security improvement15. In this section, we examine the current body of literature that focuses on IoT attack datasets in scenarios involving both non-federated learning with central data training and federated learning. For example, the studies16,17 have put forward a deep learning methodology for identifying irregularities in IoT network traffic, which has demonstrated remarkable precision in detecting malicious behaviors. In the same way18, used machine learning methods to forecast and stop vulnerabilities in IoT devices by examining how they operate and recognizing possible patterns for attacks. Umair et al.19 explored IoT security using the N-BaIoT dataset, revealing the potential of spiking neural networks for malware classification on IoT devices. Similarly, Okur et al.20 highlighted the critical role of robust intrusion detection systems in defending against DDoS attacks, underscoring the urgent need for effective detection techniques to counter these significant threats.

The IoT-23 dataset was employed by the authors to explore the diverse range of threats present in IoT networks. For example, the authors in this study emphasize the importance of uniform features, providing a fundamental basis for future studies on detecting intrusions21. IoT security and attack detection with hybrid, ensemble, and transformer models in the federated learning approach

The studies mentioned in the previous section have consistently applied threat testing to machine learning for its effectiveness in identifying and mitigating potential attacks. However, one common challenge in these studies is their application to centralized structures, which often require the collaborative training necessary to gather comprehensive threat intelligence27. As a result, there has been growing interest in leveraging blockchain technology as an emerging solution for federated management of threat intelligence statistics31. The decentralized and immutable nature of blockchain technology allows for overcoming the limitations of traditional methods, providing a platform for improved collaboration, data sharing, and consensus in threat intelligence efforts32.

An alternative approach to improving detection accuracy was used: hybrid models that combine multiple recognition techniques, such as CNN-RNN, to improve test accuracy33. This approach leveraged spatial and temporal statistical patterns in attacks on the IoT dataset 23. This hybrid model demonstrated promising results, achieving an accuracy of approximately 93% for binary classes and 90% for multi-class classes. However, due to their complexity and resource requirements, these hybrid models may be less suitable for deployment on IoT devices with limited assistance.

In recent years, federated learning has emerged as a method for protecting privacy in IoT security. The federated learning model achieved strong performance across all phases of evaluation. Confusion matrices illustrated high accuracy with minimal misclassifications for various intrusion types, such as DDoS, backdoor, injection, and reconnaissance34. This highlights the model’s effectiveness in distinguishing between different types of attacks. Study35, utilized federated learning with a mix of CNN and LSTM models on the IoT-23 dataset, producing strong results in a decentralized learning scenario. Their method upheld privacy by training the models on edge devices without centralizing data, which is important for IoT setups. However, the accuracy was a little lower, approximately 88–90%, compared to centralized models due to the challenges of distributed learning. Sorour et al. (2025) proposed an LSTM-JSO framework for privacy-preserving, adaptive intrusion detection in federated IoT networks36. They introduced a Joint Strategy Optimization (JSO) algorithm to optimize hyperparameters dynamically during training, significantly improving model adaptability and performance. Experimental results on multiple real-world datasets (including TON_IoT) demonstrated that the proposed LSTM-JSO model achieved superior accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score compared to existing methods. Furthermore, due to increasing privacy issues, several research studies have explored the use of federated learning along with ensemble models for enhancing security in IoT attack detection37. A hybrid ensemble model that combines CNN, LSTM, and XGBoost was introduced in a federated learning framework38. The model was trained on IoT devices locally and combined globally, ensuring privacy while delivering high performance. This method demonstrates potential, especially in situations where centralizing data is not feasible.

Transformer models, initially created for processing natural language, have now been utilized in the field of network security because of their capacity to comprehend intricate connections39. By incorporating self-attention mechanisms40, it can effectively capture the complex interdependencies of sequential network data features, resulting in the successful identification of IoT attacks. Despite this achievement, it was discovered that using transformers alone was demanding on resources and could lead to overfitting when working with smaller datasets. As a solution, a hybrid approach was developed that combines CNNs for the initial extraction of features with transformers for sequence modeling. In addition, the integration of CNNs and transformers has proven to be an effective method for improving the detection of attacks in IoT systems. One such example is a hybrid model proposed by researchers in41, which combines CNN layers to extract spatial features from network data with transformer layers to capture temporal dependencies. This model demonstrated enhanced accuracy in both binary and multi-class classification when compared to using CNN or Transformer models alone. The hybrid architecture effectively balances the efficiency of CNNs with the contextual understanding of transformers, making it particularly suitable for complex multi-class classification in IoT-23 scenarios. Recent efforts have aimed to make explainable AI models more interpretable due to the perception of these models as black boxes42. However, there is a lack of research exploring explainable AI techniques on the IoT-23 dataset. For instance, the integration of explainable AI methods such as SHAP or LIME with AI models has been used to elucidate feature contributions in identifying certain attacks43. This is essential for allowing cybersecurity experts to comprehend model decisions and have confidence in their results, but it still requires additional research and improvement. As evidenced by the comparative analysis in Tables 1 and 2, despite significant progress in applying deep learning and federated learning to IoT intrusion detection, four critical gaps remain unaddressed in the literature. First, most existing hybrid models, including CNN-Transformer architectures such as Ullah et al. and Yao et al. have not been integrated with federated learning, leaving data privacy a persistent concern in distributed IoT deployments. Second, model transparency is largely overlooked; very few works incorporate XAI techniques such as LIME within FL-based IDS frameworks, limiting the trustworthiness of automated detection decisions for security analysts. Third, many solutions focus exclusively on binary classification and show significantly degraded performance under multi-class attack scenarios, as demonstrated by Roy18, who reported only 72% multi-class accuracy on the IoT-23 dataset. Fourth, generalizability is rarely validated across multiple independent datasets, raising questions about real-world applicability beyond the training distribution. The proposed framework directly addresses all four gaps by integrating a dual-block CNN-Transformer architecture with federated learning using FedAvg, LIME-based explainability, and comprehensive multi-class classification, validated on both the IoT-23 and CIC IoT-DIAD 2024 datasets.

To address these gaps, this study makes four distinct contributions not previously integrated in the IoT intrusion detection literature. First, a novel dual-block CNN-Transformer architecture is proposed that simultaneously captures local spatial features via convolutional layers and long-range temporal dependencies via multi-head self-attention, a combination not previously evaluated on IoT-23 under both binary and multi-class classification within a federated learning context. Second, the proposed hybrid model is integrated within a four-client federated learning framework using FedAvg aggregation, enabling privacy-preserving decentralised training without exposing raw IoT traffic data. Third, LIME-based explainability is incorporated to generate per-prediction feature attributions, enabling network security analysts to identify which traffic features drive attack classification decisions, representing the first integration of this XAI-FL combination evaluated on the IoT-23 dataset. Fourth, model generalizability is validated through a comprehensive ablation study on the CIC IoT-DIAD 2024 dataset, confirming robust performance across diverse IoT attack scenarios beyond the primary training distribution.

The power of CNNs lies in their ability to identify spatial patterns, making the detection of malicious IoT traffic more accurate. The self-aware mechanisms in Transformer models detect long-term relationships and temporal correlations in ordered data, improving our understanding of how attack behaviors change over time. When we combine feature extraction in CNNs with chronological modeling in Transformers, we make detection even more precise, especially when multiple IoT attacks occur simultaneously.

Methodology

Following this, the process involves dataset preprocessing, feature selection, and the division of the dataset into training, validation, and testing subsets. Subsequently, various machine learning models are trained, including ensemble models, a CNN model, and a newly proposed explainable hybrid CNN transformer-based model that combines CNN and transformer architectures with a non-federated learning scenario in the first experiments. Additionally, a federated learning approach is applied to improve the performance of the proposed model and keep the privacy of data by training it with four different clients, as shown in Fig. 1. Finally, evaluation measures are used to gauge the effectiveness of the models that have been introduced.

Dataset description

To establish the validity of the proposed Transformer-CNN model, we used a dataset that is one of the most popular datasets for IoT attack detection and contains 23 different IoT devices. The dataset contains 21 benign and 23 malicious device classes, originating from multiple sources. Data was collected over 4 months and contains benign and attack traffic, such as DDoS, port scanning, spam, etc. The dataset contains over 20,000 attack samples, over 2 million benign samples, and over 320 PCAP files44. The dataset comes with 21 features, including labels grouped into 12 distinct categories. In this paper, we use most of the IoT-23 dataset except for four attacks out of twelve because of the lack of rows, including C&C-Mirai, C&C-HeartBeat-FileDownload, FileDownload, and Okiru, for both experiments, without federated and with federated learning. The IoT-23 dataset is unique in that it is specifically tailored for IoT environments, capturing realistic traffic from various smart devices infected with malware such as Mirai and Gafgyt, as well as benign IoT traffic.neering.

Dataset preprocessing

The processing of IoT-23 data unfolds in a structured, three-step manner. First, the task involves converting Packet Capture (PCAP) files into CSV format, allowing for easier handling and analysis. The combination of the pyshark, pandas, and openpyxl libraries were used to read PCAP files and export the data to excel format45. Subsequently, the focus shifts to addressing any missing values within the dataset, alongside the transformation of categorical data into numerical representations using the OrdinalEncoder library46. Upon obtaining the PCAP files, various tools are employed to serve as metadata and features, facilitating the extraction of essential metadata features from the data using the MinMaxScaler method47.

Feature selection

Selecting relevant features from large datasets is a key step in reducing machine learning processing time and cost48. IoT-generated data is huge and complex and needs to be processed quickly. It also contains redundant and less reliable features49.

Feature selection from the available IoT-23 dataset is most important to improve the accuracy and efficiency of future machine learning algorithms in classifying whether traffic is from a botnet50. Our proposed method uses a Random Forest (RF) model and removes less important features from the dataset, resulting in better performance of future machine learning algorithms51. In Fig. 2, a collection of notable features is displayed, each assessed against specific levels of significance. Any features that fell below the established threshold of 0.050 were excluded from consideration.

Non-federated learning scenario

Non-federated, or centralized, experiments in machine learning rely on one centralized server for data collection, storage, and processing. Data is stored and processed on a single machine, making non-federated methods inherently centralized. This allows easy access to stable, private, and homogenously distributed datasets52. In this experiment, the dataset was used exclusively on one server for all training purposes. We used recent machine learning classification techniques, such as ensemble models, CNN, and compared the results with the proposed model. The hybrid CNN transformer-based model combines the advantages of both CNN and transformer-based techniques. The following sections provide a detailed description of each technique.

Machine learning models and CNN

In the context of non-federated learning, we utilized XGBoost53, Catboost54, Random Forest55, Voting Ensemble56, and a CNN model. All machine learning models were fine-tuned with n_estimators set to 160 and random_state to 14. Additionally, the ensemble model was created using a soft voting classifier, which combined the top three accuracy models: XGBoost, Catboost, and RF. The hybrid CNN transformer-based model produced improved results and outperformed the previous models in terms of accuracy.

CNNs are deep learning algorithms commonly used in cybersecurity. They are effective for feature extraction in visual data, making them valuable in various security systems57. CNNs have been applied in malware detection, deception honeypots, and network intrusion detection. Intrusion detection is a rapidly developing field for CNN applications. By identifying abnormal network traffic, CNNs can provide accurate results while minimizing computational costs and bandwidth usage58. CNNs can offer better feature extraction capabilities than traditional classification methods.

Federated learning scenario

Federated learning is a concept that does not depend on the central collection of training data64. It is an extension of distributed learning or collaborative learning, yet it focuses on distributing the model training process to multiple nodes without the need to collect the data from one of the nodes65. For instance, federated learning can help reduce the power consumed by an IoT device if its limited data is used repeatedly in transmission66. Additional example can be found in a medical-related application, where the local node whose data is used cannot transfer its data outside of the hospital for the patient’s privacy protection, but it can use the other hospital’s data through the concept of federated learning67.

In the context of this study, FL can provide a reasonable solution to the security of IoT devices. In our current implementation, the client data split is assumed to be Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) for simplicity, given that the IoT-23 dataset used in our study contains data from various devices that share a similar distribution of data across all clients. However, we acknowledge that this is a simplifying assumption that may not reflect real-world IoT deployments, where data distributions across devices are typically heterogeneous and non-IID due to differences in device types, locations, and traffic patterns. Under non-IID conditions, the FedAvg algorithm is known to suffer from client drift, where local models diverge significantly from the global optimum, potentially degrading global model accuracy by 5–15% compared to IID settings, as reported in the literature. To mitigate this in future work, personalised federated learning strategies such as FedProx, which introduces a proximal term to limit local model divergence, or per-FedAvg, which incorporates model-agnostic meta-learning, could be adopted to improve convergence under non-IID data distributions. Explanation AI model

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a response to the black-box nature of many machine learning models. Evaluation

The models were evaluated again using the test data and taking into account specific detection criteria as used in our previous work73. Accuracy is a measure of the percentage of all accurate predictions. It demonstrates the overall performance of the model for both positive and negative classes, and is especially beneficial when the classes are evenly distributed. It is determined by the following formula,5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accurac{\mathrm{~}}\left( {ACC} \right)=\frac{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)+True~Negative~\left( {TN} \right)}}{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)+True~Negative\left( {TN} \right)+False~Positive{\mathrm{~}}\left( {FP} \right)+False~Negative~\left( {FN} \right)}}$$\end{document}

Precision evaluates the accuracy of positive predictions by measuring the proportion of correctly predicted positive results out of all instances predicted as positive. This metric is particularly beneficial for minimizing the occurrence of false positives, as it emphasizes the correctness of positive predictions rather than capturing all actual positive outcomes. It can be calculated as follows,

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Precision~\left( {PRE} \right)=\frac{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)}}{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)+False~Positive~\left( {FP} \right)}}$$\end{document}

Recall, also referred to as sensitivity or the True Positive Rate, calculates the percentage of actual positive results that the model accurately identifies. Mathematically, it is expressed as,7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Recal~\left( {REC} \right)=\frac{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)}}{{True~Positive~\left( {TP} \right)+False~Negative~\left( {FN} \right)}}$$\end{document}

The F1-score is a metric that measures the ratio between Precision and Recall by calculating their Harmonic Mean.8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F1 - Score=\frac{{2Precision \times Recall}}{{Precision+Recall}}$$\end{document}

The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is a metric that evaluates a classifier’s ability to differentiate between classes using the ROC curve. A high AUC suggests that the model can accurately differentiate between positive and negative samples. The ROC curve illustrates the balance between false positives and true positives, offering important insights for assessing the classifier. We can measure the occurrence of false positives by comparing the misclassified negative samples with the total number of negative samples.

Results

In this section, we presented and thoroughly examined the outcomes of the IoT attack detection utilizing the IoT-23 dataset in two distinct scenarios: one involving non-federated learning and the other involving federated learning. We delved into each scenario, exploring both binary classifications and multi-class classification approaches. These approaches were implemented using machine learning ensemble models, CNN, and the groundbreaking hybrid CNN transformer-based model that we propose. The CNN model and the suggested model have been tested once more in the federated scenario, demonstrating the durability of anomaly detection for IoT networks in a federated learning scenario. In federated learning, frequent exchange of model parameters between clients and the central server can lead to significant bandwidth consumption, especially in resource-constrained IoT networks. This can be a limiting factor for deploying real-time intrusion detection systems, where timely model updates are crucial. However, to minimize the impact of communication overhead, we employed a limited number of communication rounds (10 rounds), balanced with local training (50 epochs per round) to reduce the frequency of transmissions while maintaining model performance. Additionally, using the FedAvg algorithm helps by transmitting only model weights, rather than raw data, which conserves bandwidth and enhances privacy.

For enhancing security on large-scale systems, implement adaptive client selection, secure aggregation protocols, and differential privacy. In addition, the potential of asynchronous FL frameworks to improve scalability and reduce synchronization delays in large and heterogeneous IoT environments.

Discussion

The study’s experimental findings show how well different ML and DL models, including the suggested hybrid CNN-Transformer model, perform in a multi-class classification framework when subjected to various attack types. But there are noticeable differences in performance between classes, which point to areas that need work. In particular, the suggested CNN-Transformer model has a significant F1 score of 87.97% and an overall accuracy of 89.11%. This suggests a robust general ability to categorise network traffic, with the hybrid model showing exceptional performance in detecting DDoS attacks (90.41% F1 score) and PartOfAHorizontalPortScan (99.79% accuracy). These findings imply that the model works well for identifying high-priority threats in Internet of Things systems, which are frequently distinguished by distinct attack patterns. On the other hand, the model performs poorly against C&C and C&C-HeartBeat attacks; its accuracy (14.73%) and F1 score (25.67%) are much lower for C&C. Potential class imbalance may be the cause of this performance discrepancy, as the training data for these classes might not have been accurately representative of actual attack scenarios. Additionally, even though the precision was high (94.63%), the misclassification of benign traffic implies a trade-off between preventing false positives and detecting attacks.

Limitations and future works

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, regarding class imbalance: the IoT-23 dataset exhibits severe class imbalance, with the majority class (PartOfAHorizontalPortScan) containing 753,565 instances compared to only 30 instances in the smallest class (C&C-Torii). Although SMOTE was applied, it produced no measurable improvement due to this extreme disparity. Future work should explore more effective strategies such as: (1) class-weighted loss functions (e.g., focal loss), which penalise misclassification of minority classes more heavily during training; (2) threshold-moving techniques that adjust decision boundaries per class; and (3) hybrid resampling combining SMOTE with Tomek Links to remove majority-class borderline samples. Second, the FL implementation assumes IID data distribution across clients, which may overestimate performance in heterogeneous real-world IoT environments, as discussed in Sect. 3.6. Third, the FL framework faces inherent security risks including model poisoning, where malicious clients deliberately submit manipulated model updates to degrade global performance or introduce backdoors, and inference attacks that may expose private training data. To address these threats, future work will explore Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC) within blockchain consensus mechanisms. Fourth, communication overhead between clients and the central server remains a practical constraint in bandwidth-limited IoT networks; future work will investigate adaptive client selection and gradient compression to reduce transmission costs. Future work is to include more diverse datasets from various domains and multimodal forms, such as MBB-IoT76 with transfer ensemble models77,78 and transfer ViT models79,80, and SMPC and work of the stage in consensus mechanism blockchain environments81.

Communication Cost.

Additionally, communication overhead represents a practical constraint in bandwidth-limited IoT deployments. The estimated communication overhead per federated round is approximately 12 MB, calculated based on the model’s approximately 3 million trainable parameters at 4 bytes (float32) per parameter, multiplied across 4 clients. Over 10 communication rounds, the cumulative total communication cost is approximately 480 MB. While this overhead is feasible for IoT gateway devices with standard network connectivity, it represents a practical constraint for severely bandwidth-limited edge nodes. Future work will investigate gradient compression and adaptive client selection strategies to reduce per-round communication cost by an estimated 10–100× without significant accuracy degradation, as demonstrated in the federated learning compression literature.

Conclusion

This study developed a CNN-transformer-based model capable of elucidating its methodology for detecting IoT attacks in both federated and non-federated learning environments. The integration of transformers and CNNs has demonstrated significant potential in identifying intricate IoT attack patterns, achieving higher accuracy while requiring more computational resources related to the base CNN. The federated learning framework enables model training across several clients without compromising privacy, rendering it suitable for extensive IoT networks. Comprehensive testing has demonstrated the model’s accuracy in identifying various types of attacks. The model outperforms others, achieving 92.10% accuracy in non-federated binary classification and 94.89% accuracy in federated binary classification. It achieves 89.11% accuracy in non-federated classification and 92.17% accuracy in federated learning for multi-class classification, respectively. The integrated explainability feature fosters trust by allowing users to understand the model’s decision-making process. The findings indicate that this strategy significantly enhances the security of IoT ecosystems by providing precise, scalable attack detection tools that safeguard privacy. Furthermore, our use of explainability enables stakeholders to scrutinize the rationale behind identified abnormalities, hence enhancing the transparency and integrity of security solutions for IoT environments.
