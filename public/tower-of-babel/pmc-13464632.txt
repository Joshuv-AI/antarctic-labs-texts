Breaking solubility of metal-organic cages via polymer carrier for concentrated type II porous liquid to extract n-alkanes
Authors: Shuaibo Yang, Yu Han, Mifen Cui, Jihai Tang, Xu Qiao, Zhuxiu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type II porous liquids that exhibit host-guest properties derived from molecular cages are of potential utility in liquid-liquid separations; however, those with metal-organic cages (MOCs) as pore hosts are extremely rare due to the limited solubility of MOCs in cavity-excluded solvents. Here, we report the use of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) to deliver polyoxovanadate-organic hybrid cages (HD) in polyethylene glycol (PEG) for a concentrated type II porous liquid (HDPVP/PEG). Structural characterizations and modeling studies collectively confirm that the PVP-PEG compatibility and the HD-PVP interaction enable the high HD concentration in PEG. The minimal loading of PVP coupled with the steric hindrance of PVP and PEG ensure the permanent porosity and room-temperature fluidity. HDPVP/PEG exhibits high uptake and selectivity toward C5–C7 n-alkanes over their branched isomers due to the host-guest interactions between HD and n-alkanes. The design strategy of HDPVP/PEG was successfully extended to other typical MOCs and commonly-used cavity-excluded solvents.

INTRODUCTION

The permanent porosity and fluidity of porous liquids afford them the opportunity to impact separation and purification technologies that entail size/shape-selective recognition and mass transport under flow conditions (1–5). An important feature of porous liquids is the diversity of scale and composition as exemplified by the judicious selection of molecular cages as pore hosts that enable the control over intrinsic porosity in terms of pore dimension and pore chemistry (6–10). Type II porous liquids can thereby exist if molecular cages are dissolved in solvents that are cavity excluded from entering the cage cavities (11–13). Most of type II porous liquids are based upon porous organic cages (POCs) (14–19). The “POC-solvent” compatibility derived from their adjacent functional groups serves as the critical role for the solvation of POCs (20). The high-throughput robotic workflow and computational methods have been well-developed to identify the optimal POC-solvent pairs with high POC solubility (21–24). Molecular cages are known for their supramolecular recognition properties, and POCs at elevated solubility achieve both optimal uptake and selectivity during the separation of chemicals (25, 26). There is no strict solubility threshold for POC-based type II porous liquids, and the minimum mass of POC per unit volume of cavity-excluded solvent is normally within the range of 30 to 50 mg/ml at the room temperature. The solubility larger than 100 mg/ml is particularly favorable for POC-based type II porous liquids (21). To intuitively reflect the number of molecular cages in cavity-excluded solvents, solubility can be expressed in molar concentration, although this value is closely related to the molecular weight of the cage. Most POCs have molecular weights below 1000 g/mol [e.g., the molecular weight of CC13 (27) is 853.13 g/mol]. If the molecular weight of 1000 g/mol was used as a reference for estimation, then a POC mass concentration of 100 mg/ml corresponds to a molar concentration of 100 mmol/liter.

Unlike POC-based type II porous liquids, the development of metal-organic cage (MOC)–based type II porous liquids has been limited by the dearth of solvents with high MOC solubility. The MOC’s amenability to crystal engineering (28, 29) should, in principle, offer broad compositional and functional variability of the corresponding type II porous liquids, but until very recently, there are four typical solvents to dissolve specific MOCs: (i) 15-crown-5 for the dodecyloxy Cu-based nanoball (30); (ii) ionic liquid [P6,6,6,14][Cl] for the V-based hydoughnut and Zr-based tetrahedron (8); (iii) ionic liquid [PEG(BIm)2][NTf2]2 for the azobenzyl Cu-based nanoball (31, 32), the Zr-based tetrahedron (33) and the Pd-based small-rhombihexahedron (34); (iv) deep eutectic solvent for the Zr-based tetrahedron (35). The solubility tests reveal that, while charged MOCs dissolve in [P6,6,6,14][Cl] with 23 to 90 mg/ml (9.8 to 19.7 mmol/liter), these values are still relatively low by the standards of POC-based type II porous liquids. The solubilities of neutral MOCs (e.g., the prototypal nanoball and its derivatives) in either [P6,6,6,14][Cl] or [PEG(BIm)2][NTf2]2 are even lower than 10 mg/ml (equivalent to 2.7 mmol/liter in terms of prototypal nanoball). The [TEA][3PO] is a nonionic solvent, but the solubility of cationic Zr-based MOCs in [TEA][3PO] is only 22 mg/ml (6.8 mmol/liter). Turning to the 15-crown-5, the poor solubility of prototypal nanoball can be mitigated through the ligand functionalization of the dodecyloxy groups, leading to a high solubility of 480 mg/ml (84.1 mmol/liter) at 60°C, but it quickly drops to 58 mg/ml (10.2 mmol/liter) at the room temperature. Until very recently, Zhang et al. (36) achieved the solubility up to 300 mg/ml (52.6 mmol/liter) for dodecyloxy Cu-based nanoball by using host-guest interactions between the cavity of liquid pillar[5]arene and the dodecyloxy group. Although this strategy is currently limited to MOCs functionalized with alkyl chains and macrocyclic solvents, it underscores the crucial role of intermolecular MOC-solvent interactions in enhancing the MOC solubility. In this contribution, we introduce a new approach that employs a minor amount of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as an additive to a previously known polyoxovanadate-organic hybrid doughnut (HD)–like nanostructure (37). The resulting nanocomposite (HDPVP) is readily dissolved in polyethylene glycol (PEG) to form a new type II porous liquid (HDPVP/PEG) because PVP serves as the hydrophilic carrier to deliver HD into the PEG phase via HD-PVP interactions. Although both PVP and PEG are cavity-excluded polymers, the minimal PVP content (∼1 mol % relative to PEG) has negligible impact on the PEG volume. We believe that HDPVP/PEG represents a rare example of the MOC-based type II porous liquid that meets the solubility standard of POC-based type II porous liquids.

Current researches on porous liquids have predominantly focused on their gas separation properties (38–41), but their potential for the selective separation of specific molecules from liquid-phase remains relatively unexplored (42–45). In particular, reports on type II porous liquids for liquid-liquid separation systems are scarcely documented in the literature (8, 18, 42). For MOC-based type II porous liquids, only our group has recently reported the molecular recognition property of HD/[P6,6,6,14][Cl] for the separation of l-tryptophan and d-ribose from the aqueous phase (8) with high uptakes. Apart from biomolecule separation, the separation of linear alkanes (C5–C7) from branched isomers, which is critical for the petrochemical industry, is another potential application scenario for MOC-based type II porous liquids (46). Recently, Wang et al. (47) combined the benefit of low-temperature extraction with the molecular sieving effect of molecular cages to realize the sequential extraction of n-alkanes (C5–C9) over an aqueous solution of MOC at the room temperature. Although these MOC solutions lack permanent porosity due to water occupation in their cavities, this extraction-adsorption process is compatible with MOC-based type II porous liquid. It is suggested that HD-based type II porous liquid can serve as the porous extraction media for the isolation and purification of n-alkanes from light naphtha.

In this work, we report the use of the type II porous liquid (HDPVP/PEG) for the selective extraction of linear alkanes from their branched isomers. HDPVP/PEG was characterized by various techniques to assess its key properties such as porosity and rheological behavior. The cage solubility in HDPVP/PEG ranks among the highest in all type II porous liquids. HDPVP/PEG exhibits a remarkable extraction selectivity of linear C5–C7 alkanes due to the high concentration of HD that provide binding affinity based on pore dimension and intermolecular interactions revealed by computational studies. The synthetic approach of HDPVP/PEG can be extended to a broad family of MOCs and a diverse scope of cavity-excluded solvents.

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of HDPVP/PEG

The synthetic process of the HDPVP/PEG is shown in Fig. 1A. According to a reported procedure, the solvothermal reaction of 5-methoxyisophthalic acid with VCl3 in a mixed solvent of N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) and H2O (10:1) was carried out at 100°C over 3 days followed by slow evaporation of the resulting solution at 100°C. The slow evaporation enables the crystallization of the anionic organic-inorganic HD-like nanostructure with dimethylammonium (DMA) cations to afford single crystals of HD_DMA4 (37). Alternatively, gram-scale preparation of HD_DMA4 powder can be achieved by rapidly evaporating the solvothermal reaction solution under reduced pressure at 100°C. The bulk purity of HD_DMA4 crystalline powder was verified by the agreement between its experimental powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern and the PXRD pattern calculated from the reported single-crystal structure (Fig. 1B). Each HD anion in HD_DMA4 is charge-balanced by four DMA cations with an HD mass fraction of 90 wt %. A mixture of 80 g of HD_DMA4 and 200 g of PVP [weight-average molecular weight (Mw) = 8000] was dissolved in 2.2 liters of a DMF/water mixed solution (1:10, v/v), followed by spray drying under the conditions of a liquid flow rate of 100 ml/min, a gas flow rate of 150 m3/hour, and an inlet temperature of 170°C, yielding ∼274 g of a green powder denoted as 30% HDPVP. This nomenclature indicates that HD_DMA4 accounts for 30 wt % (corresponding to an HD content of 27 wt %), and PVP constitutes 70 wt % in the 30% HDPVP composite. The minor DMA component is omitted in this nomenclature for clarity. Then, 4 g of 30% HDPVP was added to 10 g of pEG-400 [number-average molecular weight (Mn) = 400] as a cavity-excluded solvent. The mixture was stirred at 80°C for 0.5 hours and then cooled to room temperature to yield a homogeneous green liquid (HDPVP/PEG). This HDPVP/PEG system achieved a maximum HD concentration of 123.3 mg/ml (40.8 mmol/liter, corresponding to HD_DMA4 solubility of 137.0 mg/ml) while maintaining good transparency and fluidity, whereas HD_DMA4 is insoluble in both pure PEG and PVP-containing PEG solutions. Unless stated otherwise, all structural characterizations of HDPVP/PEG in this section are for a HD concentration of 40.0 mmol/liter.

To confirm the presence of HD in HDPVP/PEG via mass spectrometry, the HDPVP/PEG was diluted with acetonitrile. The quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrum of this solution reveals a peak at an mass/charge ratio (m/z) of 755.2461, corresponding to the molecular formula of [(V4O8Cl)4(C9H6O5)8]4− (Fig. 1C). The experimental isotopic distribution patterns are in good agreement with the calculated isotopic distribution patterns based on the chemical composition of HD. The observed m/z range of 357.8644 to 480.9654 was assigned to PEG that had an Mn of 400, while the Mn of PVP (Mn = 8000) exceeds the detection limit of the mass spectrum system (Mw < 1500). The cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) provides additional visual evidence for the presence of HD in HDPVP/PEG (Fig. 1D). The liquid-state HDPVP/PEG sample was rapidly vitrified in a liquid nitrogen environment, followed by sectioning into microscale thin films for cryo-EM imaging. The cryo-EM image reveals quite a few highly dispersed particles with a size of ca. 2 nm, which is consistent with the dimensions of a single HD cage. This observation indicates that no notable aggregation of HD occurred within HDPVP/PEG. The rheological properties of HDPVP/PEG were then studied via a strain sweep test and a frequency sweep test, with the frequency sweeps set in a linear viscoelastic region between 0.1 and 1% (Fig. 1E). When a 1% strain within the linear viscoelastic region was used, HDPVP/PEG exhibited a typical liquid-like behavior (G″ > G′) across the angular frequency range of 0.1 to 100 rad/s (Fig. 1F), and there is no convergence of G″ and G′ values. HDPVP/PEG was classified as a Newtonian fluid through a linear dependence between the shear rate and the shear stress (fig. S1). The viscosity of HDPVP/PEG thereby remains as a relative constant of 982 mPa·s with increasing shear rate at 25°C (fig. S2), and it decreases to 73 mPa·s at 80°C (Fig. 1G).

Intrinsic porosity in HDPVP/PEG

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed on a bulk system containing 1 HD anion, 4 DMA cations, 1 PVP molecule, and 72 PEG molecules. The simulation was conducted in the NPT ensemble at 1 bar and 298 K with an integration time step of 2 fs. The potential of mean force (PMF) was calculated to evaluate the energy barriers for the translocation of DMA cations, PVP, and PEG molecules across the HD anion. The results indicate that as PVP and PEG approach the HD anion, their PMF values increase significantly from 0 to 192.1 kJ/mol and 162.8 kJ/mol, respectively, leading to minimum approaching distances of PVP and PEG, respectively, be 0.39 and 0.45 nm (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the HD anion and DMA cations can approach as close as ∼0.28 nm, which is primarily attributed to the reported charge-assisted hydrogen bonds between the DMA cations and polyoxovanadate of HD anions. Overall, the simulation results demonstrate that at any given time, the DMA cations, PVP, and PEG molecules are located outside the HD cage, indicating the presence of sufficient molecular-scale cavities within the HDPVP/PEG.

The permanent porosity of HDPVP/PEG was then investigated through high-pressure methane absorption measurement at the room temperature, which provided direct evidence of accessible void spaces within HDPVP/PEG under operational conditions (Fig. 2B). To elucidate the contribution of HD to the permanent porosity, HDPVP/PEG with the HD_DMA4 solubility of 60, 100, and 130 mg/ml were selected to evaluate methane uptake, while pure PEG and the PEG solutions of PVP (PVP/PEG) are the reference samples. Three PEG solutions of PVP were prepared (PVP/PEG-a, PVP/PEG-b, and PVP/PEG-c), with PVP concentrations of 140, 233, and 303 mg/ml, corresponding to the PVP concentrations in HDPVP/PEG with HD_DMA4 solubility of 60, 100, and 130 mg/ml, respectively. At a pressure of 20 bar, the gravimetric methane uptake of PVP/PEG-c is only 0.013 mmol/g. Reducing the PVP content leads to a further decrease in methane uptake with PVP/PEG-a and PVP/PEG-b, but they are still higher than that of pure PEG (0.002 mmol/g). A positive correlation was observed between the methane uptake and the HD concentration, demonstrating that HD cages are the main accommodation sites for methane molecules. For HDPVP/PEG with the HD_DMA4 solubility of 130 mg/ml, the methane uptake is 0.041 mmol/g at 20 bar, which corresponds to 1.1 methane molecules per HD cage.

Based on high-pressure methane absorption experimental data, we then carried out an MD simulation of the 1:4:1:72 mixture of HD, DMA, PVP, and PEG combined with 100 methane molecules at 20 bars and 298 K. The 100 methane molecules were randomly placed in the simulation box without any atomic overlaps as the starting configuration. Figure S3 shows the temporal evolution of the number of methane molecules within the HD cavity that can be occupied by 0, 1, or 2 methane molecules at different simulation time points. The simulation snapshots at certain simulation times show one HD cavity encapsulating exactly one or two methane molecules (Fig. 2, C and D). On average, each HD cavity accommodates 1.3 methane molecules, which is consistent with the experimental result from the methane uptake. The MD simulations and the adsorption experiments further confirm the intrinsic porosity in the HDPVP/PEG.

Role of PVP during HDPVP dissolution

The liquid PEG is a good solvent for PVP (48), whereas the as-synthesized HD_DMA4 is not readily soluble in either neat PEG or a PEG solution of PVP. To investigate the role of PVP in the HD_DMA4 dissolution within PEG, we additionally prepared two HDPVP samples with HD_DMA4 loadings of 10 and 50% using the same method as for 3% HDPVP, designating them as 10% HDPVP and 50% HDPVP, respectively. The actual HD contents in 10% HDPVP and 50% HDPVP were determined to be 9 and 45 wt %, respectively. All HDPVP samples were soaked in a certain volume of PEG and stirred for half an hour. The release content of HD and PVP are similar (ca. 92 to 96%) for 10% HDPVP and 30% HDPVP, but only PVP is completely released from 50% HDPVP. The HD in 50% HDPVP exhibits a reduced release of only 2%, which was comparable to that of pure HD in PEG. Figure 3 (A to C) presents scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of HDPVP with varying HD loadings before and after soaking in PEG. The surface morphology of 10% HDPVP and 30% HDPVP remains relatively uniform after 10 min of soaking in PEG, whereas the surface of 50% HDPVP become pitted and uneven. In light of the difference on component release and surface morphology, we proposed that 10% HDPVP and 30% HDPVP might have comparable nanostructures, which accounts for their analogous dissolution behavior.

To provide insight into the molecular structure of HDPVP, all-atom molecular dynamic (MD) simulations were then performed for all three HDPVP systems. The model of all three HDPVP systems was built using the simulated annealing method to replicate the temperature variations of the experimental spray-drying process. The quantitative ratio of HD:DMA:PVP was set to match the molar ratio used in the experimental synthesis. The convergence of all MD systems begins from 40 to 50 ns, and the associated root mean square deviation (RMSD) values remain relatively stable until the simulation endpoint at 100 ns (fig. S4). From the simulation snapshots, HD aggregation occurs exclusively in the 50% HDPVP system, while 10 and 30% HDPVP exhibits dispersed HD in PVP matrices (fig. S5, A to C). This observation was corroborated by PXRD patterns. The 10 and 30% HDPVP samples shows no diffraction peaks, but 50% HDPVP sample exhibits several diffraction peaks within the 2θ range of 5 to 15°. Some of these diffraction peaks matching well with those of pure HD-DMA4 crystals while others showed certain shifts. In addition, broad peaks appeared in the PXRD pattern of 50% HDPVP, suggesting the presence of poorly crystalline phases. Collectively, these results indicate that HD adopts diverse packing modes in the 50% HDPVP sample encompassing both crystalline ordered packing and amorphous random packing (Fig. 3D). Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis from the MD simulations reveals characteristic peaks at 3.0 Å between the NH2 groups of DMA cations and V═O groups of HD anions in all three systems (fig. S6), confirming the presence of charge-assisted N─H···O hydrogen bonds as previously reported in the literature (37). Broader RDF peaks emerge for the N atoms of PVP and the V═O groups of HD (0.5 ∼ 2.6 nm), with g(r) values increasing significantly with the higher PVP amount (fig. S7A). A similar trend was observed for the RDFs of entire PVP molecules and the V═O groups of HD (fig. S7B). These results collectively indicate that both PVP molecules and DMA cations reside in the external space of the HD cages without entering the cage cavities.

There is a correlation between the HD dispersion in PVP and their mutual interactions as reflected by the stronger HD-PVP binding energy at higher PVP content (fig. S8). According to the detailed energy component analysis, the MD simulation system of 10% HDPVP, respectively, shows the average van der Waals and electrostatic interaction energies of −4111.2 and −4389.1 kJ/mol during the last 20 ns of simulation (fig. S9). Combined with the RDF results, these electrostatic interactions can thereby occur between negatively charged V═O groups of HD and the N atoms of PVP. The 30% HDPVP system shows comparable average van der Waals interaction energy (−3782.3 kJ/mol) to 10% HDPVP, indicating similar molecular contact and intermolecular distances (fig. S10). However, because of the reduced PVP content, its average electrostatic interaction energy decreased significantly to −2062.7 kJ/mol. When the PVP content was further reduced in 50% HDPVP, HD aggregation led to substantial decreases in both van der Waals and electrostatic interaction energies (fig. S11). These results systematically reveal the correlation between PVP content and HD-PVP interactions, which in turn affects the dispersion state of HD within the PVP matrix. Since electrostatic interactions are closely related to the electronic structure of specific atoms, x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to examine the binding energies of C, N, and O atoms (Fig. 3, E to G). Compared with pure PVP, the 30% HDPVP exhibited a 1.0-eV blue shift for the C 1s peak of the carbonyl carbon (from 287.5 to 288.5 eV) and a 1.7-eV blue shift for the N 1s peak (from 399.2 to 400.9 eV). These shifts indicate reduced electron densities around both the carbonyl carbon and the nitrogen atom of PVP in the composite. Meanwhile, 30% HDPVP showed a 0.6 eV red shift for the O 1s peak of the terminal oxygen from the polyoxovanadate (from 531.2 to 530.6 eV) compared with pure HD. This red shift reflects increased electron density around the oxygen atoms of the polyoxovanadate in 30% HDPVP. These observations can be attributed to the ion-dipole interaction between the tertiary amide group in the PVP structure and the V═O moiety of HD. Such an interaction is well recognized in systems containing polyoxometalates and tertiary amides (49, 50). To further elucidate this charge redistribution mechanism at the molecular level, we analyzed the electrostatic potential (ESP) distributions of the N-methylpyrrolidone unit of PVP, the polyoxovanadate anion of HD, and their binary model complex (Fig. 3H). The results showed that the N-methylpyrrolidone unit exhibits a notable dipole moment owing to the higher electronegativity of the oxygen atom compared with the carbon atom. The most negative ESP is concentrated on the oxygen atom (−45.01 kcal/mol), whereas relatively positive regions exist near the nitrogen and carbon atoms (+9.38 and +12.91 kcal/mol, respectively). In the polyoxovanadate anion, negative ESP values ranging from −20.67 to −19.71 kcal/mol were observed around the terminal oxygen atoms. In the N-methylpyrrolidone–polyoxovanadate complex model, the oxygen atom (from −45.01 to −23.09 kcal/mol), the nitrogen atom (from +9.38 to +22.86 kcal/mol), and the carbon atom (from +12.91 to +24.93 kcal/mol) of N-methylpyrrolidone all exhibited notable blue shifts. Meanwhile, several oxygen atoms in the polyoxovanadate anion, particularly the terminal oxygen atom adjacent to the nitrogen atom of N-methylpyrrolidone, showed a red shift (from −20.66 to −30.22 kcal/mol). The ESP isosurface images of the relevant atoms also reflect the changes in charge density. The relevant O···C═O angles approach 90°, which is consistent with the characteristic of orthogonal polar contacts widely observed in proteins and small organic molecules (51). These changes in ESP values clearly indicate electron transfer from the N-methylpyrrolidone unit to the polyoxovanadate anion. The ESP calculation result confirms the presence of an anion-dipole interaction, which is in agreement with the XPS results.

Given the structural similarities between 10% HDPVP and 30% HDPVP, we selected 30% HDPVP and 50% HDPVP for the molecular-level investigation of their dissolution behavior. Considering the substantial spatiotemporal scales involved in actual dissolution processes, a dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) method was used instead of conventional all-atom MD simulation. The HD, PVP, and PEG molecules were divided into coarse grain sites that each represents a group of connected atoms of comparable volume to enable the study of HD dissolution over a relatively long-time scale (fig. S12). Both 30% HDPVP/PEG and 50% HDPVP/PEG system were constructed by randomly distributing molecules in cubic cells with periodic boundary conditions while carefully avoiding excessive overlap of DPD sites. The dissolution simulations were carried out in the canonical ensemble for 2 μs at 298 K. In the 30% HDPVP/PEG system, dynamic aggregates composed of HD, PVP, and DMA forms rapidly during the DPD simulation (Fig. 4, A to C, and movie S1), while the 50% HDPVP/PEG system only exhibits aggregates of HD and DMA (Fig. 4, D to F, and movie S2). The radius of gyration (Rg) was used to quantitively describe the spatial extent of an aggregate. The aggregates in the 30% HDPVP/PEG system are highly dynamic, as indicated by the large variation in Rg values (Rg = 4.75 ± 0.33 nm; Fig. 4G) and substantial Rg fluctuations. In contrast, the 50% HDPVP/PEG system formed a more compact HD-DMA core (Rg = 3.01 ± 0.15 nm), with PVP chains extending into the PEG solution phase and undergoing rapid conformational changes. The SD of Rg values furtherly confirms that the dynamic stability of aggregates in 30% HDPVP/PEG is significantly lower than in 50% HDPVP/PEG. The differences on the DPD simulation configurations and Rg values are consistent with the dissolution experimental results, where 50% HDPVP exhibits poor solubility in PEG with an HD release rate of only about 2%. The compact HD-DMA core in the 50% HDPVP/PEG simulation system aligns with the PXRD diffraction peaks observed from the undissolved solid in the dissolution test of 50% HDPVP, which can be attributed to the dense packing of HD cages with DMA cations (fig. S13). In contrast, the highly dynamic nature of the aggregates in the 30% HDPVP/PEG system enables complete dissolution of HDPVP without any crystalline or amorphous precipitations.

The coordination number calculation (Fig. 4I) based on RDF analysis (Fig. 4H) reveals that the PEG coordination number per HD cage in 30% HDPVP/PEG (6.3 ± 0.36) marginally exceeds that in 50% HDPVP/PEG (5.5 ± 0.25), but the coordination number of PVP per HD cage in 30% HDPVP/PEG (7.5 ± 1.1) is much higher than that in 50% HDPVP/PEG (5.1 ± 3.9). Notably, while both 30% HDPVP/PEG and 50% HDPVP/PEG exhibit the comparable SD of PEG coordination numbers per HD cage, the 50% HDPVP/PEG system shows a 3.5-fold higher SD of PVP coordination number than 30% HDPVP/PEG, indicating a competition between HD-PVP contacts and self-stacking of HD cages in 50% HDPVP/PEG. These key findings in turn demonstrate that HD-PVP contacts are essential for suppressing the HD aggregation during the dissolution of 30% HDPVP in PEG. These intermolecular contacts between HD and PVP derived from DPD simulation is also consistent with the HD-PVP interaction characteristics obtained from all-atom MD simulations of HDPVP. In other words, it is the HD-PVP interactions that enable PVP to effectively deliver and disperse HD into the PEG phase. Given that both 10% HDPVP and 30% HDPVP systems exhibit highly dispersed HD cages and similar HD-PVP contacts, the DPD simulation results of 30% HDPVP/PEG can be directly extrapolated to the dissolution process of 10% HDPVP in PEG.

Based on of simulation results and experimental data, we elucidate the molecular mechanism by which PVP content governs the dissolution behavior of HD in PEG. When PVP is present in sufficient amounts, stable intermolecular interactions between HD and PVP effectively suppress HD self-aggregation, promoting the formation of dynamic and loosely structured aggregates in PEG and enabling complete dissolution. In contrast, at lower PVP content, weakened intermolecular interactions lead to the formation of compact HD-based structures, accompanied by partial exclusion of PVP into the PEG phase, which significantly diminishes HD dissolution. We have established a clear structure-property relationship spanning intermolecular interactions, aggregate dynamics, and macroscopic dissolution behavior, providing a critical theoretical foundation for the rational design of high-loading HD-based type II porous liquid materials.

Selective extraction of C5–C7 n-alkanes

To evaluate the extraction property of 30% HDPVP/PEG, C6 alkanes were selected for the single-component extraction experiments in which 1 ml of 30% HDPVP/PEG was mixed with 0.1 ml of either linear or branched C6 alkanes at 25°C for 2 hours (phase ratio = 1:0.1). Although the 1:0.1 phase ratio appears high, the feed phase is the pure C6 alkane rather than a dilute C6 alkane solution. Therefore, the amount of alkane added was intentionally controlled to enable effective analysis of compositional changes in the feed phase. Figure S14 shows a marked increase in the extraction rate before equilibrium as reflected by the rising concentration of n-hexane (nHEX) in the 30% HDPVP/PEG phase over the 0- to 80-min period. The final nHEX uptake in 30% HDPVP/PEG is 188.6 ± 0.4 mmol/liter (Fig. 5A). In comparison, hydrophilic PEG exhibits a nHEX uptake of only 39.0 ± 0.2 mmol/liter throughout the extraction process (Fig. 5A), forming a typical PEG/alkane biphasic system (52, 53). Although PVP contains hydrocarbon structures, it only exhibits a slightly increased nHEX uptake of PVP/PEG to 41.9 ± 0.2 mmol/liter over the whole extraction time range (Fig. 5A). For the branched C6 alkanes, both PEG and PVP/PEG absorbs 2-methylpentane (2MP) and 2,2-dimethylbutane (22DMB) (33 to 36 mmol/liter), which is consistent with their nHEX uptake levels. The 30% HDPVP/PEG system shows absorption capacities of 38.7 ± 0.9 and 37.4 ± 0.3 mmol/liter for 2MP and 22DMB, respectively. Although these values are lower than its nHEX uptake, they were still 4.9 and 4.4% higher than those of the PVP/PEG.

In single-component extraction experiments with C5 and C7 alkanes, a phase ratio of 1:0.1 between 30% HDPVP/PEG and alkane was maintained. All experimental procedures including the extraction time range and temperature were identical to those used in the single-component extraction experiments with C6 alkane (Fig. 5A). The results shows that 30% HDPVP/PEG absorbs n-pentane (nPEN; 175.8 ± 2.0 mmol/liter) but only its branched isomer 2-methylbutane (2 MB; 69.6 ± 1.5 mmol/liter). Both PVP/PEG and PEG exhibits absorptions of 37 to 61 mmol/liter for nPEN and 2 MB, while the uptake of 2 MB by 30% HDPVP/PEG was ∼70% higher than that of PVP/PEG. For C7 alkane extraction, 30% HDPVP/PEG absorbs n-heptane (nHEP; 152.5 ± 1.3 mmol/liter), but its uptake of 2-methylhexane (2MH) and 2,3-dimethylpentane (23DMP) was, respectively, 14.9 ± 1.0 and 17.1 ± 0.3 mmol/liter, representing a reduction of about 60% compared to the PVP/PEG system. These results demonstrate that 30% HDPVP/PEG has strong selectivity for linear C5–C7 alkanes, which is closely related to the HD component. To further validate the role of HD, the extraction performance for C6 alkanes was evaluated at varying HD concentrations. A decrease in HD concentration led to a notable reduction in nHEX uptake, confirming HD’s affinity for linear alkanes (Fig. 5B). Moreover, HD displayed stronger exclusion toward branched C7 alkanes than toward branched C5 and C6 alkanes, likely due to differences in molecular size and interaction forces, which will be further investigated in subsequent studies. The reusability of 30% HDPVP/PEG was evaluated through five consecutive cycles of nHEX extraction. The regeneration of 30% HDPVP/PEG between each cycle was achieved by vacuum treatment at 60°C to remove absorbed nHEX. As shown in fig. S15, the nHEX uptake remained relatively stable throughout five cycles, with only an 8.4% decrease after the fifth cycle compared to the first cycle. The used 30% HDPVP/PEG maintained its dark green color without precipitation (fig. S16). The methane sorption isotherm of the used 30% HDPVP/PEG is nearly identical to that of the as-synthesized 30% HDPVP/PEG, confirming the integrity of the intrinsic porosity during the repeated extraction tests (fig. S17).

The notable difference in the single-component extraction of linear or branched C5–C7 alkanes by 30% HDPVP/PEG has prompted us to study the binary-component separation capability for C5–C7 n-alkanes over their respective isomers (Fig. 5C). The molar ratio of n-alkane to branched alkane was maintained at 1:1. When the phase ratio of HDPVP/PEG to the mixed alkanes is 1:0.1 (same as in the single-component extraction experiments), the extraction efficiency of nPEN is 38.4% and that of 2 MB was 5.3% (Fig. 5C), and the separation factor was calculated as 11.2 (Fig. 5D). For nHEX and its corresponding branched isomers (2MP and 22DMB), the extraction efficiency of nHEX is 44.4% in the nHEX/2MP system and 43.6% in the nHEX/22DMB system, while the extraction efficiencies of both 2MP and 22DMB are below 6%, resulting in separation factors of 14.2 and 12.9, respectively. For nHEP with 2MH and 23DMP, the separation factors are 12.1 and 12.4, corresponding to nHEP extraction efficiencies of 47.9 and 48.4%, respectively, while the extraction efficiencies of 2MH and 23DMP were 7.2 and 7.1%. When the feed amount of the binary-component alkanes was increased, such that the phase ratio of HDPVP/PEG to the mixed alkanes became 1:0.2, both the extraction efficiencies and the corresponding separation factors for all binary-component systems decreased. When the phase ratio was further adjusted to 1:0.5, the separation factors for nHEX/2MP, nHEX/22DMB, nPEN/2 MB, nHEP/2MH, and nHEP/23DMP even decreases to 9.2, 8.4, 8.3, 7.6, and 7.3, respectively. In conventional water-oil extraction systems, the extracted species are dissolved in water as a diluted state. In contrast, in the HDPVP/PEG-alkane extraction system, the extracted phase is composed solely of neat alkanes without any solvent. Therefore, the extraction capacity of HDPVP/PEG can be satisfied with a relatively low alkane input, whereas excessive alkane input exceeds the capacity of HDPVP/PEG to accommodate alkane molecules, leading to reduced extraction efficiencies and consequently lower separation factors. Conversely, when the phase ratio of HDPVP/PEG to the mixed alkanes was set to 1:0.05, the separation factors for nHEX/2MP, nHEX/22DMB, nPEN/2 MB, nHEP/2MH, and nHEP/23DMP increases to 57.4, 53.3, 27.9, 175.1, and 186.5, respectively. The extraction efficiency of nHEP exceeds 90%, slightly higher than those of nHEX and nPEN.

To elucidate the high selectivity of the 30% HDPVP/PEG system toward n-alkanes, we performed 20-ns MD simulations under isothermal-isobaric ensemble conditions (1 bar, 298 K) for systems containing 30% HDPVP/PEG and C6 alkane isomers (nHEX, 2MP, or 22DMB; fig. S18). Binding energy calculations based on equilibrium configurations reveals that all C6 alkanes exhibit similar binding affinities with PVP, whereas notable differences emerge in their interactions with the HD cage (fig. S21). nHEX shows a 49.1 kJ/mol stronger binding energy to HD than to PVP, whereas 2MP and 22DMB show substantially weaker HD binding energy. Because the porous liquid is a homogeneous system, the reduced binding energy reflects expulsion tendency to branched 2MP/22DMB from the 30% HDPVP/PEG phase.

The noncovalent interactions between C6 alkanes and HD were investigated based on the equilibrium configurations from MD simulation via the independent gradient model (IGM) method (54). The two-dimensional scatter plots and sign(λ2)ρ-colored δginter isosurface maps reveal distinct interaction patterns among different C6 alkane isomers (Fig. 6A and fig. S22, B and C). For nHEX encapsulated in the HD cage, van der Waals and C─H···π interactions (dC···X = 3.6 ∼ 4.4 Å, ∠C-H-X = 119.7 ∼ 150.8°, dHp···X = 0.3 ∼ 1.1 Å) were identified, as visualized by dispersed green isosurfaces between nHEX and the phenyl groups of the HD cage (Fig. 6A). In addition, a short C─H···O contact exists between the terminal methyl group of nHEX and the methoxy oxygen of the HD cage (dC···O = 3.5 Å, ∠C–H–O = 112.7°), with a geometry approaching the geometric limit of a C─H···O hydrogen bond (dC···O < 4.0 Å, ∠C-H-O > 110°) (55). Given the extremely weak acidity of the terminal methyl group of nHEX and the relatively high electronegativity of the methoxy oxygen of the HD cage, this short contact can be considered a van der Waals interaction involving Debye and London forces. This interaction further induces a slight torsional deformation of the carboxyl groups of the HD cage, thereby facilitating the encapsulation of nHEX within the cage cavity. In contrast, the interaction of external nHEX and HD cage (dC···O = 4.1 to 4.4 Å, dC···X = 4.6 to 4.9 Å) falls beyond conventional interaction cutoffs for the C─H···O hydrogen bond and the C─H···π interaction (dC···X < 4.5 Å, ∠C-H-X > 120°, dHp···X < 1.2 Å) (56).

Weak interaction between HD cage and C5–C7 linear alkanes.

For the branched C6 isomers, one 2MP molecule forms several short C─H···O contacts with the polyoxovanadate moiety of the HD cage, with C···O distances approaching 4.0 Å (fig. S23B), while the other 2MP molecule exhibits a nonideal C─H···π geometry with the phenyl group of the HD cage (dC···X = 4.1 Å, ∠C–H–X = 114°, dHp···X = 1.7 Å; fig. S23C), so there only van der Waals interactions between 2MP and HD. 22DMB shows the C─H···π interaction (dC···X = 3.9 Å, ∠C-H-X = 128.1°, dHp···X = 0.6 Å; fig. S24B) that positions itself near the polyoxovanadate of the HD cage, which in turn forms several weak C─H···O contacts (dC···O = 3.6 ∼ 3.8 Å, ∠C-H-O = 125.0° ∼ 175.9°; fig. S24, C and D). The aforementioned energy analysis and intermolecular interaction study collectively present the nHEX recognition ability of the HD cage. Geometrically, encapsulated nHEX exhibits optimal alignment for C─H···π interaction with the HD cage, in contrast to the less favorable binding geometries of 22DMB or 2 MB. This nHEX selectivity arises not only from these intermolecular interactions but also from the HD cage’s structural constraints: The narrow window aperture and the gating effect imposed by the ligand flexibility synergistically facilitate the size/shape-selective molecular discrimination.

Following the same modeling approach, MD simulations were performed for 20 ns under isothermal-isobaric ensemble conditions (1 bar, 298 K) to obtain equilibrium configurations for systems containing 30% HDPVP/PEG with C5 and C7 alkanes (n-alkanes: nPEN and nHEP; Fig. 6, B and C; branched alkanes: 2MB, 2MH, and 23DMP; figs. S19, S20, and S22, A and D and E). The energy analysis reveals that the binding energies of nPEN and nHEP with PVP are, respectively, −121.7 and −148.5 kJ/mol, which are comparable to their branched counterparts with PVP (2MB: −159.1 kJ/mol; 2MH: −170.2 kJ/mol; 23DMP: −163.7 kJ/mol; fig. S21). However, the binding energies of nPEN and nHEP with the HD cage are not only higher than those with PVP but also significantly exceeded those of the corresponding branched isomers with the HD cage. These different binding energies of C5/C7 alkanes with key components of 30% HDPVP/PEG follow the same trend as observed for C6 alkanes. Moreover, although the binding energy of 2MB with HD is lower than that with PVP, it is significantly higher than the binding energies of 2MH or 23DMP with HD, suggesting a relatively stronger affinity of 2MB to HD among C5–C7 branched alkanes. Furthermore, the binding energy of 2MH with HD is only 30% of that of 2MH with PVP, whereas the binding energy of 2MB with HD reaches nearly 80% of that with PVP. This reflects that HD in HDPVP/PEG exerts a certain degree of repulsive effect toward 2MH, and the affinity of PVP for 2MH is insufficient to counteract the repulsive effect of HD, resulting in a lower extraction amount of 2MH in HDPVP/PEG than in PVP/PEG. A similar trend is also observed for 23DMP.

In addition to the binding energy analysis, the noncovalent interactions between C5/C7 alkanes and the HD cage were also examined using the IGM method. The encapsulated nPEN and nHEP respectively formed multiple C─H···π interactions with the phenyl groups of the HD cage (dC···X = 3.6 ∼ 4.4 Å, ∠C-H-X = 101.9 ∼ 143.4°, dHp···X = 0.7 ∼ 1.2 Å; Fig. 6, B and C). Although a few angles at the hydrogen atom (∠C-H-X) slightly below the cutoff value of 120° (Fig. 6, B and C), these deviations were compensated by other well-aligned C–H···π interactions (Fig. 6, B and C). In addition, the cavity of the HD cage that imposes conformational constraints on nPEN and nHEP may also induce the reduction of their ∠C-H-X angles. This reflects a common phenomenon observed in spatially confined systems as exemplified by the similar angle reductions occurred in protein binding sites (57) and macrocyclic host cavities (58). For nPEN located outside the HD cage, two C─H⋯O short contacts are observed between the methyl groups of nPEN and the polyoxovanadate of the HD cage (dC···O = 3.4 Å and 3.4 Å, ∠C-H-O = 110.9° and 134.1°; Fig. 6B). Although the external nPEN is in close proximity to the phenyl group of the HD (dC···X = 4.0 Å and 4.2 Å), the bond angles at the hydrogen atoms in the methyl and methylene groups (∠C-H-X = 111.1° and 112.6°) are below the threshold for C─H···π interactions (∠C-H-X > 120°). For nHEP located outside the HD cage, a C─H···O short contacts occur between methyl groups of nHEP and polyoxovanadate of the HD cage (dC···O = 3.5 Å, ∠C-H-O = 138.1°; Fig. 6C). A C─H···π interaction is identified between the methylene group of nHEP and the phenyl group of the HD (dC···X = 3.8 Å, ∠C-H-X = 121.1°, dHp···X = 1.2 Å). Although another methylene group of nHEP shows a C···X distance of 4.2 Å to the centroid of the phenyl group of the HD cage, the bond angle at the hydrogen atom in this methylene group (∠C-H-X = 98.6°) is significantly below the threshold for C─H···π interactions. The interaction between this methylene group of nHEP and the phenyl group of the HD cage is thereby classified as the van der Waals interaction rather than the C─H···π interaction. In contrast, branched C5/C7 alkanes only exhibits van der Waals interactions with the polyoxovanadate of the HD cage (figs. S25 to S27), among which short C─H···O contacts exist between 2MB and the polyoxovanadate of HD with a C···O distance of 3.6 Å. The maximum C─H···O short contact distance between 2MH and the polyoxovanadate of the HD cage is 4.7 Å, and some ∠C-H-O angles even approach 90°. Therefore, the van der Waals interaction between 2MH and HD is weaker than that between 2 MB and HD, which is consistent with the binding energy analysis of 2MH/2 MB with HD. Similarly, the van der Waals interaction between 23DMP and HD is also weaker than that between 2MB and HD. Overall, the intrinsic pores of HD encapsulate C5–C7 n-alkanes through C─H···π interactions and van der Waals interaction. Although several C─H···O short contacts exist between the exterior of the HD cage and the C5–C7 alkanes, their dC···O and/or ∠C-H-O values are either close to or do not meet the geometric cutoff criteria for C─H···O hydrogen bonds. It is thereby more appropriate to classify C─H···O short contacts as van der Waals interactions involving Debye and London forces, which in turn indicates the weak recognition ability of the external surface of the HD cage toward C5–C7 alkanes. These comprehensive computational results demonstrate the molecular recognition capability of the intrinsic pores of the HD cage in 30% HDPVP/PEG for linear C5–C7 alkanes, which is consistent with the high separation factors observed in the biphasic extraction experiments.

Scope of MOCs and cavity-excluded solvents

We evaluated the solubility of 30% HDPVP in eight cavity-excluded solvents commonly used for type II porous liquids. The HD cage demonstrated a minimum 51-fold solubility enhancement across all tested solvents, with PEG showing the highest solubility (fig. S28). Based on these findings, the use of PVP for solubility enhancement in PEG was extended to six vanadium-based MOCs and three zirconium-based MOCs. These MOCs are classified as either edge-directed or face-directed, depending on whether the organic ligands form the edges or faces of the MOC’s topological polyhedron, according to the widely accepted classification system introduced by Stang (59). Although these MOCs show negligible solubility in PEG, their corresponding PVP composites exhibit remarkable solubility improvements (table S4). Specifically, H-HD and Br-HD, which are derivatives of HD with its methoxy group respectively substituted by hydrogen and bromine group, retain the edge-directed doughnut-like structure and similar pore size to HD. Despite being virtually insoluble in PEG alone, both H-HD and Br-HD form deep green homogeneous solutions upon precomplexation with PVP, achieving solubilities of 30.5 and 36.6 mmol/liter, respectively. VMOP-1, an edge-directed vanadium-based octahedral MOC, reaches a solubility of 28.0 mmol/liter in PEG upon complexation with PVP. VMOP-4, featuring an edge-directed vanadium-based triangular structure, forms a yellow-green homogeneous solution in PEG after complexation, with the color change likely attributable to the presence of thiophene-based ligands in its structure. Face-directed tetrahedral VMOP-14 and octahedral Hyball-3, respectively, achieve solubilities of 41.2 and 27.8 mmol/liter in PEG after PVP complexation. Notably, the PVP-mediated solubilization strategy is effective not only for anionic vanadium-based MOCs but also for cationic zirconium-oxo cluster-based MOCs. The edge-directed zirconium-based tetrahedron (ZrT-1) and cube (HCCF-1) respectively achieve solubilities of 41.5 and 19.9 mmol/liter after PVP complexation, while the face-directed zirconium-based tetrahedron (ZrT-2) also shows an increased solubility of 41.7 mmol/liter via the same approach. All prepared solutions maintain good fluidity, and their distinct color changes indicate the successful dissolution of the MOCs in PEG. These broad applicability is within our expectation, as most MOCs contain terminal and/or bridging oxygens capable of forming intermolecular interactions with PVP that play a pivotal role in the dissolution of MOCs in PEG.

We then compared the volumetric methane uptake among these nine MOC-based type II porous liquids expressed as moles of absorbed methane per unit volume of porous liquid (mmol/ml), where the volume refers to that of the PEG (fig. S29). The slight volume changes due to the dissolution of the composite of MOC and PVP was neglected. All components were weighed during preparation, giving methane uptake as mass of absorbed methane per gram of porous liquid. This can be easily converted to volumetric uptake (mmol/ml) using the known masses of all components, the PEG density and the molecular weight of methane. We analyzed how the cavity volume, the pore window size, the molecular weight, and the concentration of MOCs affect the methane uptake of corresponding type II porous liquids. The pore window size is determined by measuring the distance between specific atoms lining the pore window in the crystal structure, followed by subtraction of their van der Waals radii. HD-H and HD are both edge-directed doughnut-like nanostructures with similar cavity volumes. The pore window size (5.2 Å) of HD-H exceed the kinetic diameter of methane (3.8 Å), allowing the ingress of methane molecules to the HD-H cavity. Although the concentration of HD-H in HD-HPVP/PEG (30.5 mmol/liter) is lower than that of HD in HDPVP/PEG (40 mmol/liter), its smaller molecular weight (2781.78 g/mol) compensates for the reduced HD quantity and corresponding total pore volume, resulting in a comparable methane uptake between HD-HPVP/PEG and HDPVP/PEG. Similarly, ZrT-1 is an edge-directed tetrahedron with a sufficiently large pore window size of 4.2 Å. Although its cavity volume (300 Å3) is smaller than that of HD (480 Å3), ZrT-1 exhibits a higher concentration (41.5 mmol/liter) than HD (40 mmol/liter), so the methane uptake of ZrT-1PVP/PEG is 5.5% higher than that of HDPVP/PEG. In contrast, HD-Br has a molecular weight (3412.94 g/mol) 13% higher than that of HD, resulting in an ∼9% reduction in its molar concentration (36.6 mmol/liter). This leads to a decreased number of HD-Br molecules in the HD-BrPVP/PEG material and a corresponding reduction in methane uptake. HCCF-1, an edge-directed cube, has a pore window matching methane’s size (3.8 Å), but its high molecular weight (6666.36 g/mol) and moderate concentration (19.9 mmol/liter) lead to a low uptake (0.02 mmol/ml at 20 bar)—about half that of HD-HPVP/PEG. VMOP-1 is an edge-directed octahedron. Its concentration in VMOP-1PVP/PEG (28.0 mmol/liter) is 30% lower than that of HD in HDPVP/PEG (40 mmol/liter). Despite a higher molecular weight (4574.31 g/mol), its larger cavity volume (500 Å3) and pore window size (4.1 Å) provides an adequate total pore volume, leading to a higher methane uptake at 20 bar (0.06 mmol/ml), corresponding to approximately two methane molecules per VMOP-1 cage. Conversely, for face-directed octahedral Hyball-3, the small pore window size (0.9 Å) prevents the ingress of methane, so the reduced methane uptake comes mainly from PVP and PEG in Hyball-3PVP/PEG. Similarly, the type II porous liquids based on VMOP-4 (pore window size of 3.6 Å), VMOP-14 (completely blocked aperture), and ZrT-2 (pore window size of 1.2 Å), respectively, exhibit very limited methane uptake. In summary, by comparing the methane absorption behaviors of various high-concentration type II MOC-based porous liquids, we find that the methane uptake is closely related to the accessible pore volume. Low molecular weight, high concentration, and an edge-directed structure of MOCs are likely to provide sufficient accessible pore volume for reside of methane molecules in MOC-based type II porous liquids. Given that polymer carriers can increase MOC concentration in cavity-excluded solvents, the design and synthesis of edge-directed MOCs from lighter metals and simpler ligands is a promising strategy for developing type II MOC-based porous liquids with higher methane uptake.

We have realized the kilogram-scale synthesis of HDPVP/PEG, and the readily accessible MOC, PVP, and PEG in conjunction with well-established unit operations (e.g., dissolution and spray-drying) suggests the potential for the large-scale production of MOC-based porous liquids. On the basis of effectiveness of PVP as a polymer carrier in enhancing the solubility of MOCs in cavity-excluded solvents, this study preliminarily explored the possibility of replacing PVP with other potential polymers as the carrier. PEG-3000 (Mw = 3000 g/mol) was chosen because its molecular structure contains ether bonds and terminal hydroxyl groups, which are absent in PVP, and this polymer is widely used in the chemical, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries. The prototypical Cu-nanoball (NB) (60, 61) and its hydroxylated (OH-NB) (62) and methoxylated (MeO-NB) (63) derivatives were selected as model compounds to systematically investigate their dissolution behavior. The NB series were selected due to its overall electrical neutrality which distinguishes it from the previously studied V/Zr-based ionic MOCs. Since the discovery of these faceted polyhedra (also known as the small rhombihexahedron) in the early 21st century, they have had a substantial impact on the development of supramolecular chemistry and coordination polymers (64, 65). Following the HDPVP synthetic method, NB, OH-NB and MeO-NB were separately dissolved with PEG-3000 in a DMF/water mixed solvent (volume ratio 1:10), and the corresponding composites NBPEG, OH-NBPEG, and MeO-NBPEG were obtained via spray drying treatment. All three composites exhibited good solubility in typical sterically hindered solvents such as PEG-400 and 15-crown-5. Taking 15-crown-5 as an example, when pure NB series compounds were added, the solution remained colorless and transparent, without showing the characteristic blue-green color of NB series compounds. After complexation with PEG, the solubility of NB, OH-NB and MeO-NB increased significantly to 112.7 mg/ml (19.4 mmol/liter), 101.3 mg/ml (16.5 mmol/liter), and 108.5 mg/ml (16.9 mmol/liter), respectively (table S5). These results further validate the generality and feasibility of using polymers as carriers to enhance the solubility of MOCs in cavity-excluded solvents.

DISCUSSION

In summary, HDPVP/PEG currently represents a rare example of MOC-based type II porous liquid with high MOC solubility reported to date. HDPVP/PEG is permanently porous and maintains comparable viscosity with other reported porous liquids even at high HD concentrations. The dissolution behavior, structure characterizations, and modeling studies provide the insight to concurrent dissolution of PVP and HD. Our findings reveal that PVP plays a dual role: serving as both a delivery carrier for HD dissolution in PEG and a stabilizing agent that prevents HD aggregation. We have demonstrated how the highly-concentrated HD cages, coupled with their host-guest properties, endow HDPVP/PEG with high selectivity for n-alkane separation. Perhaps most importantly, the approach taken here to improve the HD solubility is broadly applicable, as it exploits variants of previously reported MOCs, extensively used polymer and cavity-excluded solvents, to address what were hitherto infeasible pore hosts for MOC-based type II porous liquids. The implications are even broader as MOC structure can be custom-designed and fine-tuned to enable the on-demand fabrication of the corresponding type II porous liquids for the targeted molecular separation.

While using PVP or other polymers as a transport carrier to enhance the solubility of MOCs in cavity excluded solvents presents a certain method, it also comes with limitations. Before dissolution, a homogeneous solution of polymer carrier and MOC must first be prepared and then dried to obtain the composite, which imposes requirements on the solubility of MOCs in common solvents. A typical example is that H-HD with DMA as the cation is extremely difficult to dissolve in common solvents and water. It requires ion exchange to replace DMA with diethylammonium before it can be used for preparing porous liquids. Another limitation lies in the lack of structural dynamic changes when porous liquids are applied to liquid-phase separation. Although this paper elaborates on these structural dynamics through MD simulations, this remains a rare case. In contrast, the structural dynamic changes of solid-state metal-organic materials under different environmental conditions have been proven to offer unparalleled advantages in various substance separation systems.

To advance the findings of this paper toward further applications, the first issue to address is the cost of the materials. Although there are already some cases of large-scale preparation of metal-organic materials, the prices of the required raw materials cannot be reduced. If the price of MOCs can be lowered to a level comparable to that of common polymers such as PVP and PEG, it will promote the further application of porous liquids. Another issue to resolve is the need to develop separation processes and specialized equipment compatible with this new type of porous liquid material, enabling continuous and controllable substance separation scenarios.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Material synthesis

HD_DMA4 crystal

The synthesis of HD_DMA4 was carried out according to a previously reported procedure with minor changes. A mixture of 5-methoxy-1,3-bdc (12.0 mg), VCl3 (20.0 mg), and 5 ml of mixture solvent (DMF:H2O = 10:1) was put in a 20-ml scintillation vials at 100°C for 3 days. The resulting solution was slow evaporated at 100°C to obtain dark green block crystals. The obtained crystals were washed with methanol give pure HD_DMA4 of 12.6 mg.

HD_DMA4 crystalline powder

A mixture of 5-methoxyisophthalic acid (25.0 g) and VCl3 (50.0 g) in DMF (900 ml) and water (90 ml) was stirred in a 2-liter stirred tank reactor at 100°C for 12 hours to afford a homogeneous dark green solution. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure at 90°C, and the resulting solid was washed with methanol to yield 21.9 g of HD_DMA4 as a crystalline powder.

30% HDPVP

A solution of HD_DMA4 (80 g) and PVP (Mn = 8000, 200 g) in 2200 ml of a mixture of DMF and water (1:10) was spray-dried in a OUMENG Spray Dryer OM-QPG-3-B at a feed rate of 100 ml/min, a flow rate of 150 m3/hour, and an inlet temperature of 170°C. About 274 g of green powders was collected.

HDPVP/peg

30% HDPVP (4 g) was added to PEG-400 (Mn = 400, 10 g). The mixture was vigorously stirred at 80°C for 0.5 hours and then cooled to room temperature to yield a homogeneous green liquid with an HD_DMA4 concentration of 137 mg/ml. This method can be directly scaled up to achieve the kilogram-scale synthesis. Specifically, 400 g of 30% HDPVP was added to 1 kg of PEG and stirred for 1 hour in a 3-liter reactor, yielding 1.4 kg of porous liquid.

PVP/PEG

After adding 7 g of PVP to 50 ml of PEG, the mixture was vigorously stirred at 80°C for 0.5 hours and then cooled to room temperature, yielding a colorless and transparent solution designated as PVP/PEG-a, with a PVP mass concentration of 140 mg/ml. By keeping all other conditions unchanged and varying the amount of PVP added to 11.65 or 15.15 g, PVP/PEG-b or PVP/PEG-c were obtained with PVP mass concentrations of 233 and 303 mg/ml, respectively.

Instrumental analysis

PXRD patterns were collected at 40 kV and 15 mA on a Rigaku Miniflex600 diffractometer with Cu Kα (λ = 1.5416 Å) radiation with the scan speed of 10°/min and the 2θ step size of 0.02°. XPS measurements were carried out on a Thermo Scientific Nexsa x-ray photoelectron spectrometer system with an energy of 15 kV and a beam current of 10 mA. Cryo–transmission electron microscope images (cryo-TEM) were carried out using a Thermo Scientific Krios G3i cryo-transmission electron microscope. For sample preparation, the cryogenic tools (foam box, copper cup, four-post holder, foam ring, etc.) was assembled in sequence. Liquid nitrogen was poured into the foam box and copper cup for thorough cooling, and then ethane was introduced until a solid-liquid coexistence state is achieved. The HDPVP/PEG sample was carefully placed onto the grid which was then secured with specialized tweezers. The grid was fixed using a sliding plate before inserting into the designated slot. After cryo-freezing the sample via the programmed foot pedal operation, the grid was swiftly transferred from liquid ethane into a liquid nitrogen-soaked storage box. Cryo-TEM imaging was performed using a K2 Summit direct electron detector equipped with a GIF Quantum energy filter in super-resolution counting mode at 300 kV. SEM images were collected using a Hitachi S4800N field electron microscope. The microscopic morphology and elemental distribution were analyzed using a JEOL JEM-2100F operated at 200 kV. Mass spectrum was obtained using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class/XEVO G2-XS QTOF-MS in negative-ion detection mode. The concentration of HD and PVP in PEG after the dissolution of HDPVP were determined using a Shimadzu Nexera SCL-40 gel permeation chromatography (GPC) system equipped with an Shodex GF-310 HQ (7.5 mm by 300 mm, 5 μm) column and an SPD-40 UV-Vis detector. The centrifugation supernatant of the dissolution slurry was diluted to an appropriate concentration with DMF before injection to the GPC system with the mobile phase of DMF (0.6 ml/min) and the detection wavelength of 254 nm. High-pressure CH4 absorption data were collected using an ISOSORP gravimetric sorption analyzers from Rubotherm-TA instrument at 25°C and a pressure range of 0 to 20 bar. Strain-dependent and frequency-dependent rheology measurements were test by equipping CP25-2-SN27479 with a gap of 0.104 mm at room temperature. For temperature-dependent viscosity and stress versus shear rate, measurements were test by equipping PP25-SN27504 with a gap of 0.100 mm under rotation mode.

Extraction experiments

Extraction experiments were performed by mixing 1 ml of 30% HDPVP/PEG with 0.1 ml of alkane under vigorous stirring at 25°C for 2 hours. All extraction experiments were conducted in sealed containers, and the boiling points of the alkanes to be extracted range from 28°C (2 MB) to 99°C (nHEP), which exceed the extraction temperature of 25°C. The occasional mass loss induced by evaporation is thereby precluded. After extraction experiment finished, the remaining alkane were analyzed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector and an HP-PLOT/Q capillary column (30 m by 0.535 mm by 40 μm). The n-octane was selected as the internal standard. The gas chromatography analysis was performed under the following conditions: The initial column temperature was set at 60°C and held for 5 min, followed by a programmed temperature increase at a rate of 10°C/min up to 220°C, which was then maintained for 8 min. The injector and detector temperatures were set at 250° and 280°C, respectively. The gas flow rates were maintained at 30 ml/min for H2, 400 ml/min for air, and 25 ml/min for makeup gas (N2). N2 was used as the carrier gas with a constant flow rate of 3 ml/min.
