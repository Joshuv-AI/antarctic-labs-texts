Deubiquitinase MYSM1 promotes doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity by mediating TRIM21-ferroptosis axis in cardiomyocytes
Authors: Xiaowen Shi, Jianjiang Xu, Xin Zhong, Yuanyuan Qian, Liming Lin, Zimin Fang, Bozhi Ye, Yiting Lyu, Ran Zhang, Zhanxiong Zheng, Jibo Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracycline antitumor drug doxorubicin (DOX) induces severe cardiotoxicity. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are crucial for protein stability and function and play a significant role in cardiac pathophysiology. By comparing RNA sequencing datasets and conducting functional screening, we determined that Myb-like, SWIRM, and MPN domains 1 (MYSM1) is a key regulator of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. In this study, we aimed to explore the function and regulatory mechanisms of MYSM1 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Genetic knockdown of MYSM1 significantly mitigated DOX-induced cardiomyopathy. Correspondingly, cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 by AAV9 protected the heart from DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Gain- and loss-of-function analysis verified that MYSM1 mediated DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury in vitro. Through a Co-IP combined with LC-MS/MS analysis, we discovered that MYSM1 directly interacted with tripartite motif-containing protein 21 (TRIM21). Mechanistic investigations revealed that MYSM1 regulates the deubiquitination and the stability of TRIM21 via its MPN domain. Furthermore, MYSM1 exacerbated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by enhancing ferroptosis. This study identified MYSM1 as a potential therapeutic target for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and illustrated a MYSM1-TRIM21-ferroptosis axis in regulating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01955-6.Keywords: Doxorubicin, Cardiotoxicity, Deubiquitinating enzymes, MYSM1, TRIM21, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX) is an anthracycline antitumor drug known for its high efficacy against various types of cancer [1]. However, the major clinical limitation of DOX, like other anthracyclines, is cardiotoxicity [2]. Clinically, DOX-induced cardiotoxicity manifests as arrhythmia, impaired left ventricular function, congestive heart failure, and even refractory delayed cardiomyopathy [2]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and identifying potential therapeutic targets are critical to addressing this urgent medical need. Given the limited regenerative capacity of cardiomyocytes [3], it is of great importance to maintain the stability of key intracellular proteins.

Ubiquitination is a reversible post-translational modification of proteins that is regulated by both E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) [4]. It has been found that DUBs are vital for protein stability and function, playing a significant role in various biological processes and influencing the development and advancement of diverse diseases [4]. JAB1/MPN/Mov34 metalloenzymes (JAMMs) are the only zinc metalloprotease family of DUBs, while other DUB families are cysteine peptidases [5]. The distinctive catalytic mechanism of JAMMs family, which involves zinc-induced cleavage of peptide bonds, makes JAMMs attractive drug candidates for disease research [5]. However, the roles of JAMMs family in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases remain unclear. In this research, through a comparison of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) databases and functional screening of the JAMMs, we identified Myb-like, SWIRM, and MPN domains 1 (MYSM1) as a potential regulator of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. MYSM1 contains three main domains: SANT, SWIRM, and MPN domain [6]. Current reports have highlighted MYSM1 as an essential regulator of hematopoiesis, immunity, and other aspects of mammalian physiology by interacting with specific substrates [7, 8]. Up to now, the potential role and mechanism of MYSM1 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity have not yet been revealed.

The purpose of this study was to clarify MYSM1’s regulatory function and mechanism in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Here, we showed that MYSM1 was positively correlated with DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, and MYSM1 knockdown in cardiomyocytes significantly protected hearts from this condition. Through a Co-IP combined LC-MS/MS analysis, we found that MYSM1 directly interacted with tripartite motif-containing protein 21 (TRIM21). Detailed mechanistic experiments revealed that MYSM1 regulates the K63-linked deubiquitination and the stability of TRIM21 through its MPN domain. Furthermore, MYSM1 exacerbated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by increasing ferroptosis signaling pathway activity. This study identified MYSM1 as a potential therapeutic target for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and illustrated a MYSM1-TRIM21-ferroptosis axis in regulating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Methods

Animal experiments

Animal procedures complied with the guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (US National Institutes of Health) and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University (Approval No. JUMC2023-102). Whole-body Mysm1-knockdown mice (Mysm1−/+, KD, Strain No. T058305) with a C57BL/6J background and littermate wild-type (WT) mice were provided by Gempharmatech. All mice were kept under the following housing conditions: 12-h dark and 12-h light cycles, 20–24 °C and 30–70% humidity.

To specifically inhibit cardiomyocyte MYSM1 in vivo, an adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV-9) vector was engineered by Shanghai Genechem Co. The AAV-9 vector carried a cTNT promoter driving short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Mysm1 (CAGTGATGAGAACAGAGCTATCATT), as well as negative control shRNA (TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT). The AAV9-cTNT-shMYSM1 (total dose 2 × 1011vg/mouse) or negative control (shNC) were administered to mice via tail vein injection. After 4 weeks, the DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model began to be constructed.

For the DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model, DOX was administered to mice at a dosage of 15 mg/kg/week (three times per week for 2 weeks), while 0.9% saline was administered to control mice. Four weeks after the first injection of DOX, the cardiac function was evaluated noninvasively by transthoracic echocardiography (Fujifilm VisualSonics). Following sacrifice, cardiac tissues and serum were collected for further analysis.

Serum biochemical analysis

Lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) were used as standards for detecting myocardial DOX injury. LDH concentrations (A020-2-2) were determined using LDH kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Serum CK-MB (E-EL-M0355c) and cTnT (E-EL-M1801c) levels were detected using their respective ELISA kits from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Histological analysis

Cardiac tissues were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, followed by dehydrated, transparent, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin Sect. (5 μm) of cardiac tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, G1080, Solarbio), wheat germ agglutinin (WGA, I3300, Solarbio), Sirius Red (G1472, Solarbio), and TNUEL stain (C1098, Beyotime) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The samples were observed using a light microscope (Zeiss), and semi-quantitative analysis was determined using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining

Five-micrometer frozen sections were used for staining of MYSM1, α-actinin, vimentin, and F4/80. Sections were fixed in cold methanol and permeabilized using 0.25% Triton X-100 (T8200, Solarbio). Then sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A8020, Solarbio) for 30 min and incubated with the primary antibody for MYSM1 (abs136708, Absin, 1:200) and α-actinin (69758, CST, 1:200), vimentin (EM0401, Huabio, 1:200), or F4/80 (sc-377009, Santa, 1:200) overnight at 4˚C. After washing, sections were incubated with Alex 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (8889, CST, 1:200) or Alex 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse (4408, CST, 1:200) secondary antibodies for 1 h and counterstained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio) for 10 min. The stained sections were observed by the fluorescence microscope (Leica).

The cellular localization of MYSM1 and TRIM21 was detected by immunofluorescence assay. HL-1 cells were transfected with Flag-MYSM1 plasmid (green) before being treated with DOX (1 µM) for 24 h. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 20 min on an orbital shaker. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-TRIM21 primary antibody (12108-1-AP, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. At room temperature, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with Alex 594-conjugated (red) goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (8889, CST) for 2 h. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscopy (Leica).

RNA-seq

The RNA-seq datasets (GSE224157 and GSE133054) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). The RNA-Seq data were downloaded in FASTQ format and subsequently mapped to the mouse genome mm10. Read counts for each gene were determined using HTseq analysis. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). In the heat map, ratios are represented with a gradient transitioning from blue (indicating the lowest ratios) through white to red (indicating the highest ratios).

Cell culture and treatment

Neonatal rat primary cardiomyocytes (NRPCs) were obtained from SD rats’ hearts as previously reported [9]. Adult ventricular cardiomyocytes (AVCMs) were isolated from the ventricles of mice in accordance with previously described procedures [10]. HL-1 and NIH/3T3 cells were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology. DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 mg/mL) was used for cell culture at a 37 °C under 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. DOX was purchased from Sigma (D1515) and a concentration of 1 µM DOX was selected for in vitro experiments to induce the model of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Upregulation of MYSM1, TRIM21, or other proteins in HL-1 or NIH/3T3 cells were achieved using plasmids (Flag-MYSM1-WT (mouse), Flag-MYSM1-△MPN (mouse), Flag-MYSM1-△SWIRM (mouse), Flag-MYSM1-△SANT (mouse), Myc-TRIM21-WT (mouse), HA-Ub (mouse), HA-K48 (mouse), and HA-K63 (mouse)). Transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen).

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting MYSM1 (siMYSM1) were designed, synthesized, and purified by Gene Pharma. MYSM1 was silenced in NRPCs through siMYSM1. The target sequences were as follows: 5’-GCCAGACAAUACUUCAGAATT-3’. All transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).

The knockdown of MYSM1 was accomplished by lentiviral-based specific short-hairpin RNA, which were designed and purified by the Hanbio Tech (Table S1). The lentivirus was transfected into HL-1 cells for 48 h, followed by screening with puromycin (10 µg/mL) for 2 weeks. Subsequently, single stable cells were generated.

After different treatments, we used CCK-8 assay (CA1210, Solarbio) and LDH cytotoxicity assay (C0016, Beyotime) to assess cell viability and cytotoxicity, respectively. The experiments were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) combined LC-MS/MS analysis

To investigate MYSM1-binding proteins in cells, HL-1 cells were transfected with either Flag-MYSM1 or Flag- empty vector (EV) plasmid before DOX treatment. Cells were harvested using lysis buffer and incubated with anti-Flag-beads (B26102, Bimake) at 4℃ overnight. After five washes, the precipitated protein mixtures were enzymatically hydrolyzed in SDT lysate (4% SDS, 100mM DTT, 100mM TrisHCl) and then desalted using C18 StageTip. Subsequently, the samples underwent LC-MS/MS analysis conducted by BIOPROFILE (Shanghai, China).

Western blotting and Co-IP assay

Total proteins were extracted from cardiac tissues or cells using lysis buffer (AR0101/AR0103, Boster) and quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime). Proteins (20 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Then the membranes were blocked with 3% BSA for 2 h at room temperature; following this, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: MYSM1 (abs136708, Absin, 1:1000), TRIM21 (12108-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), γ-H2AX (ab81299, Abcam, 1:1000), SLC7A11 (26864-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), GPX4 (67763-1-lg, Proteintech, 1:1000), Nrf2 (12721, CST, 1:1000), Histone H3 (68345-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000), TUBULIN (2128, CST, 1:1000), and GAPDH (5174, CST, 1:1000). Afterwards, the membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (7074, CST, 1:5000) or goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (7076, CST, 1:5000). Following three washes with TBST, images were obtained using ECL (WBKLS0500, Merck Millipore). Densitometric analysis of each band was measured by ImageJ software for quantification.

Protein complexes were evaluated by Co-IP. Proteins were isolated from cardiac tissues or cells with lysis buffer. A portion of the lysis solution was retained as the input sample. The remaining lysis solution was incubated with corresponding primary antibody or the anti-Flag-beads (B26102, Bimake) for MYSM1, as well as anti-Myc-beads (B26102, Bimake) for TRIM21(overnight, 4 °C). Next, protein A + G agarose beads were added to the protein lysis solution (4 h, 4 °C). Protein-bead mixtures were washed three times with IP lysis buffer and used for subsequent western blotting experiments.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cardiac tissues or cells using Trizol reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen), followed by reverse transcription to cDNA using the Prime Script RT Master Mix reagent (RR036A, Takara). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed using SYBR Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (RR820A, Takara). PCR quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The mRNA expression levels of target genes were normalized to β-actin. The PCR primers used are listed in Table S2.

Propidium iodide (PI) staining

PI fluorescent staining was applied to determine DOX-induced cardiomyocyte death. After treatment, cells were added with Hoechst and PI solution respectively, which were incubated on ice for 30 min. The cells were then examined under the fluorescence microscope (Leica).

Determination of reactive oxygen species generation (ROS) in cells

We constructed MYSM1-knockdown (KD) HL-1 cells and overexpressed MYSM1 by transfecting Flag-MYSM1 (oeMYSM1) in HL-1 cells, followed by treatment with DOX (1 µM). To analyze ROS generation, the superoxide subtype of ROS was detected using Dihydroethidium (DHE, S0063, Beyotime). After the incubation period, the cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope (Leica), and the intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the ImageJ software. Additionally, superoxide dismutase (SOD, S0101, Beyotime), glutathione (GSH, S0053, Beyotime), and malondialdehyde (MDA, S0131, Beyotime) assays were used to measure intracellular ROS levels according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

JC-1 staining assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential changes were measured with JC-1 (C2006, Beyotime), a fluorescence dye that aggregates in normal mitochondria and disaggregates upon loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. We constructed MYSM1-KD HL-1 cells and overexpressed MYSM1 by transfecting Flag-MYSM1 (oeMYSM1) in HL-1 cells, followed by treatment with DOX (1 µM). Later, cells were incubated in JC-1 working solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark and washed with PBS. JC-1 fluorescence images were captured using the fluorescence microscopy (Leica).

Measurement of ferrous iron

Using a Ferrous Iron Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K881-M, Elabscience), ferrous iron levels in cells were determined. All calculations and procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from both in vitro and in vivo experiments were expressed as mean ± SEM. The Student’s t-test was utilized to compare two independent samples (GraphPad Pro Prism 8.0). The ANOVA with Bonferroni’s Tukey post hoc analysis was utilized to compare multiple samples (GraphPad Pro Prism 8.0). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of MYSM1 as a regulator of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity

To determine the functions of the JAMMs family in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, we initially analyzed the expression profiles of the JAMMs family genes by using RNA-seq of mouse heart tissues treated with DOX (GSE224157, Fig. 1A). In this dataset, the mRNA expression of MYSM1 and PRPF8 were elevated in the DOX group compared to the WT group. Similarly, we observed that the mRNA expression of MYSM1, EIF3F, STAMBP, and BRCC3 were elevated in heart failure (HF) group compared to the control normal heart (NHF) group (GSE133054, Fig. 1B). Next, we overexpressed JAMMs family genes in HL-1 cells. The results showed that MYSM1 or EIF3H overexpression decreased cell viability, while PSMD14 overexpression increased cell viability under DOX treatment (Fig. 1C). Through a comparison of RNA-seq datasets and functional screening of JAMMs family, we concluded that MYSM1 may be a key regulator of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (Fig. 1D).

Notably, significantly increased Mysm1 mRNA levels were observed in DOX-treated HL-1 cells and mouse heart tissues (Fig. 1E-F). Similarly, the protein expression of MYSM1 was upregulated in DOX-treated HL-1 cells and mouse heart tissues (Fig. 1G-H). We further analyzed the expression and cellular distribution of MYSM1 in heart sections through double-immunofluorescence staining. The results revealed that the increase in MYSM1 was primarily localized to and elevated in cardiomyocytes (marked with α-actinin), but not in primary fibroblasts (marked with vimentin) or macrophages (marked with F4/80), following DOX treatment (Fig. 1I and Fig. S1A). This finding was confirmed by examining the MYSM1 expression in cardiomyocytes isolated from DOX-treated mice (Fig. S1B). RT-qPCR further showed that DOX significantly increased the mRNA levels of Mysm1 in cardiomyocytes rather than non-cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1J). These results indicated that cardiomyocyte MYSM1 may serve as a potential regulator in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Knockdown of MYSM1 alleviates DOX-induced cardiotoxicity

To investigate the role of MYSM1 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, we generated Mysm1-deficient mice (Fig. S2A). Whole-body MYSM1 knockout mice have been reported to exhibit partial embryonic lethality, complex hematopoietic immune phenotypes, growth retardation, reduced lifespan, and abnormalities in tail morphology [11, 12]. We also observed these phenomena during the generation of Mysm1−/− mice (Fig. S2B). However, heterozygous Mysm1−/+ mice (Fig. S2C) did not display phenotypic abnormalities, such as growth retardation, tail morphology (Fig. S2B), or abnormal blood parameters (Fig. S2D-G). Therefore, we selected Mysm1−/+ mice to validate the function of MYSM1 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. The DOX dose for WT and Mysm1−/+ mice was 15 mg/kg/week (three times per week for 2 weeks) (Fig. 2A). Four weeks after the first injection of DOX, the cardiac function was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2B-D, serum cardiac injury markers LDH, CK-MB, and cTnT were significantly decreased in Mysm1−/+ mice compared to WT mice after DOX treatment. Non-invasive transthoracic echocardiography revealed that Mysm1−/+mice significantly alleviated DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction, indicated by ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%) (Fig. 2E-G). Meanwhile, Mysm1−/+mice significantly resisted DOX-induced reductions in whole heart size (Fig. 2H-K) and cardiomyocyte size (Fig. 2L-M). H&E staining, Sirius Red staining and TUNEL staining demonstrated that myocardial damage caused by DOX was significantly reduced in Mysm1−/+ mice, including myofiber disorganization (Fig. 2N), myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 2O-P) and cell death (Fig. 2Q-R). These results suggested that knockdown of MYSM1 alleviated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 protects against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity

Due to the embryonic lethality observed in mice with global deletion of MYSM1 [11], we prepared a recombinant AAV-9 vector containing a cTNT promoter to drive shMYSM1, specifically blocking cardiomyocyte MYSM1 expression. The AAV9-cTNT-shMYSM1 or negative control (shNC) was administered to mice via tail vein injection. Four weeks later, the DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model began to be constructed (Fig. 3A). The successful cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 was verified by western blotting in isolated cardiomyocytes of AAV9-shMYSM1 mice (Fig. S3A). Similar to Mysm1−/+ mice, injection of AAV9-shMYSM1 did not affect the hematopoietic immune phenotype of mice (Fig. S3B-E). We observed that the serum level of cardiac injury markers, such as LDH, CK-MB and cTnT, were significantly decrease in AAV9-shMYSM1 mice compared to AAV9-shNC mice after treated with DOX (Fig. 3B-D). Notably, DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction was less severe in AAV9-shMYSM1 mice, as reflected by EF% and FS% (Fig. 3E-G). Likewise, DOX-treated AAV9-shNC mice exhibited remarked reductions in whole heart size (Fig. 3H-K) and cardiomyocyte size (Fig. 3L-M), whereas these adverse effects were less pronounced in AAV9-shMYSM1 mice (Fig. 3H-M). H&E staining, Sirius Red staining and TUNEL staining demonstrated that myocardial damage caused by DOX was significantly reduced in AAV9-shMYSM1 mice, including myofiber disorganization (Fig. 3N), myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 3O-P) and cell death (Fig. 3Q-R). Taken together, these results indicated that cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 protect against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

MYSM1 mediates DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury in vitro

To further explore the role of MYSM1 in Dox-induced cardiomyocyte injury, the MYSM1-knockdown (KD) HL-1 cells were constructed (Fig. S4A). We found that MYSM1 knockdown dramatically increased cell viability (Fig. 4A) and suppressed the LDH release (Fig. 4B) after DOX treatment. In addition, MYSM1 knockdown led to a decrease in the quantity of PI-positive nuclei following DOX treatment (Fig. 4C-D).

MYSM1 alleviates DNA damage and inhibits cellular senescence [11, 13], which is associated with DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [14]. The gamma-H2A histone family member X (𝛾 -H2AX) protein, a marker of DNA damage, is initially triggered by DOX treatment and suppressed by MYSM1 in lymphocytes [13]. Therefore, we investigated whether MYSM1 mediates DOX-induced DNA damage in cardiomyocytes by detecting 𝛾 -H2AX. As shown in Fig. S4B, 𝛾 -H2AX expression was increased in DOX-induced cardiomyocytes, which is in accordance with the previous study [14]. However, MYSM1 knockdown did not influence DOX-induced 𝛾 -H2AX expression (Fig. S4C), suggesting that DNA damage might not be one of the pathological conditions in MYSM1-mediated cardiotoxicity.

Analogous to the loss-of-function assessment of MYSM1, the gain-of-function of MYSM1 was evaluated by transfecting HL-1 cells with Flag-MYSM1 plasmid (oeMYSM1, Fig. S4D). Conversely, overexpression of MYSM1 led to a reduction in cell viability (Fig. 4E), an increase in LDH release (Fig. 4F), and a higher quantity of PI-positive nuclei after DOX treatment (Fig. 4G-H).

Subsequently, we performed experiments on NRPCs, where MYSM1 was silenced by siRNA transfection (siMYSM1, Fig. S4E). As expected, siMYSM1 markedly increased cell viability (Fig. 4I) and suppressed the LDH release (Fig. 4J) after DOX treatment. PI staining further confirmed that MYSM1 silencing led to a decrease in the quantity of PI-positive nuclei after DOX treatment (Fig. 4K-L). Collectively, these results suggested that MYSM1 mediated DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury in vitro.

MYSM1 interacts with TRIM21 to maintain the stability of TRIM21 protein

MYSM1, as a deubiquitylating enzyme, controls its proprietary biological functions by hydrolyzing specific substrates [6]. To explore the potential substrates of MYSM1, we transfected Flag-MYSM1 or Flag-EV plasmid into HL-1 cells before DOX treatment and then screened for specific proteins interacting with MYSM1 through Co-IP combined with LC-MS/MS (Fig. 5A). The Co-IP of Flag-MYSM1 was verified by immunoblotting (Fig. 5B). A total of 47 potential target proteins of MYSM1 were identified (Fig. 5C). Among them, TRIM21 exhibited the highest-ranking score (Fig. 5C-D, Table S3). TRIM21 has been reported to be involved in the regulation of several cardiac diseases, including DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [15]. Therefore, we speculated that TRIM21 may act as a candidate substrate of MYSM1 in cardiomyocytes to mediate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. We conducted preliminary validation of the LC-MS/MS results and found that the interaction between endogenous MYSM1 and TRIM21 was observed in DOX-treated cardiac tissues (Fig. 5E) and HL-1 cells (Fig. 5F). Then Flag-MYSM1 and Myc-TRIM21 plasmids were co-transfected into NIH/3T3 cells, confirming the combination of exogenous MYSM1 and TRIM21 (Fig. 5G). Colocalization analysis further demonstrated a significant proportional colocalization of MYSM1 and TRIM21 in DOX-treated HL-1 cells (Fig. 5H). These findings confirmed the existence of a direct interaction between MYSM1 and TRIM21.

Next, we investigated whether MYSM1 regulates the stability of TRIM21. We transfected NIH/3T3 cells with oeMYSM1 and observed an increase in TRIM21 protein levels following overexpression of MYSM1 (Fig. 5I). Importantly, this increase was not attributed to altered transcription of TRIM21 (Fig. 5J). Subsequently, we treated NIH/3T3 cells with a protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) and measured the degradation of TRIM21. It was noted that the degradation rate of TRIM21 was reduced in Flag-MYSM1-overexpressing NIH/3T3 cells treated with CHX (Fig. 5K-L). Furthermore, we explored the regulation of MYSM1 on TRIM21 ubiquitination. As shown in Fig. 5M and Fig. S5A, oeMYSM1 promoted the deubiquitination of TRIM21. Previous studies have indicated that MYSM1 removes K63-linked ubiquitination and stabilizes substrate proteins [16]. Consistently, MYSM1 reduced the K63-linked ubiquitination of TRIM21 but not K48-linkage. These findings supported the notion that MYSM1 deubiquitinates and regulates the stability of TRIM21 protein.

MYSM1 binds to TRIM21 via the SWIRM domain and deubiquitinates TRIM21 in an MPN domain-dependent manner

Structurally, MYSM1 comprises three main domains, including SANT, SWIRM, and MPN domains [6]. To further clarify the domain required for MYSM1-TRIM21 interaction. NIH/3T3 cells were co-transfected with Myc-TRIM21, Flag-MYSM1 (MYSM1-WT) or its mutants lacking SANT domain (△SANT, aa 113–164), SWIRM domain (△SWIRM, aa 363–464), or MPN domain (△MPN aa 563–673) (Fig. 6A). As depicted in Fig. 6B, it was revealed that the SWIRM domain, but not the SANT or MPN domains, was involved in the interaction between MYSM1 and TRIM21. The catalytic domain of MYSM1 is the MPN metalloprotease domain [6]. Correspondingly, MYSM1 mutant (△MPN) had a reduced ability to maintain TRIM21 protein stability (Fig. 6C-D). Furthermore, MYSM1 lacking MPN domain failed to remove ubiquitin molecules from TRIM21 (Fig. 6E and Fig. S5B). These data indicated that MYSM1 binds to TRIM21 via the SWIRM domain and deubiquitinates TRIM21 in an MPN domain-dependent manner (Fig. 6F).

MYSM1 mediates ferroptosis in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity

Ferroptosis works as a vital pathological process in TRIM21 mediated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [15]. Consequently, we explored the effects of MYSM1 on ferroptosis in DOX-induced cardiomyocytes.

Ferroptosis has three main biochemical characteristics, including ROS dependence, accumulation of ferrous iron (Fe2+), and lipid peroxidation [17]. As presented in Fig. 7A, MYSM1 knockdown led to a decrease in Fe2+ content in DOX injury. Additionally, MYSM1 knockdown diminished the elevated MDA levels induced by DOX (Fig. 7B) and augmented both GSH levels and SOD activity (Fig. 7C-D). Conversely, the upregulation of MYSM1 further augmented DOX- induced the increase in Fe2+ content (Fig. 7E), elevated the MDA levels (Fig. 7F), and diminished the GSH levels and SOD activity (Fig. 7G-H). Similarly, MYSM1 knockdown alleviated DOX-induced upregulation of DHE fluorescence, whereas this altered profile was further aggravated by MYSM1 overexpression (Fig. 7I). Reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) also serves as a marker of ferroptosis [18]. JC-1 fluorescent probes demonstrated that MYSM1 knockdown elevated MMP while MYSM1 overexpression reduced MMP (Fig. 7J).

Nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)‐like2 (Nrf2) could mitigate ferroptosis by stimulating the expression of various antioxidant genes and enzymes to resist oxidative stress [19]. Knockdown of MYSM1 induced an increase in Nrf2 translocation from the cytosol to the nucleus (Fig. 7K), whereas overexpression of MYSM1 reversed this alteration (Fig. 7L), suggesting that MYSM1 may mediate ferroptosis through Nrf2. Additionally, MYSM1 knockdown led to a significant increase in the level of ferroptosis-related inhibition proteins solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), while upregulation of MYSM1 resulted in a decrease level of these proteins (Fig. 7M-N).

To further verify the involvement of ferroptosis in MYSM1-mediated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was administered to HL-1 cells before DOX treatment. MYSM1 overexpression markedly decreased cell viability (Fig. 7O) and promoted the LDH release (Fig. 7P) in DOX-treated HL-1 cells, while the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 significantly inhibited these effects (Fig. 7O-P). PI staining confirmed that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 alleviated the cardiotoxicity induced by MYSM1 overexpression (Fig. 7Q-R). Collectively, these findings suggested that MYSM1-ferroptosis axis plays a crucial role in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Discussion

In this study, we found a positive correlation between MYSM1 and DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Knockdown of MYSM1 significantly alleviated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, including cardiac dysfunction, myocardial interstitial fibrosis, and cardiomyocyte death. Accordingly, cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 via AAV9 had a therapeutic effect on DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Subsequently, we identified TRIM21 as a direct substrate protein of MYSM1 by Co-IP with LC-MS/MS. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that MYSM1 regulates the K63-linked deubiquitination and the stability of TRIM21 through its MPN domain. Furthermore, MYSM1 mediated ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes to promote DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

A single zinc metalloprotease family is represented by the JAMMs family, whereas the remaining DUB families belong to the cysteine peptidases family [4]. The distinctive catalytic mechanism of the JAMMs family makes them attractive candidates for drug development. However, the regulatory function of JAMMs family in cardiomyocytes remains unknown. Through a comparison of RNA-seq data and functional screening of JAMMs, we identified a JAMMs family member, MYSM1, as a potential regulator of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Whole-body MYSM1 knockout (homozygote) exhibit growth retardation and abnormalities in hematopoietic immune phenotypes, whereas MYSM1 knockdown (heterozygote) does not show these symptoms. Our findings revealed that Mysm1−/+ mice were protected from myocardial DOX injury due to MYSM1 knockdown in vivo. To specifically knockdown cardiomyocyte MYSM1, we used an AAV-9 vector carrying a cTNT promoter to drive shMYSM1 and found that cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 protected against myocardial DOX injury. Loss- and gain- of function analysis further confirmed that MYSM1 promotes DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury in vitro. Our study indicated that MYSM1 can be used as a pharmacological target for heart disease. Current studies have suggested that MYSM1 plays a protective role in the physiological growth and development of mammals [20–22], the function of hematopoietic stem cells [23], blood cell production [7, 24], as well as immune response [8, 25]. However, we noted that MYSM1 is a detrimental DUB in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Therefore, cardiomyocyte specificity should be considered in the development of MYSM1-targeting inhibitors. It is recommended that MYSM1 should be used in the treatment of other diseases to avoid toxic side effects on the heart.

MYSM1, as a deubiquitylating enzyme, exerts its proprietary ubiquitin biological function by hydrolyzing specific substrates [26]. Hence, the function of MYSM1 is closely related to its substrate proteins [6]. The substrate and function of MYSM1 vary in different physiological and pathological processes [6]. Here, we performed an IP combined with LC-MS/MS analysis and identified TRIM21 as a direct substrate protein of MYSM1 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. TRIM21 has been identified as a vital factor in various forms of myocardial damage, including atrial inflammation and remodeling post-myocardial infarction [27], acute myocardial infarction [28], viral myocarditis [29], and Dox-induced cardiotoxicity [15]. We systematically revealed the process of MYSM1 deubiquitinating TRIM21. As reported, MYSM1 removes K63-linked ubiquitination and maintains the protein stability of substrate [16]. Consistent with the findings of the present study, MYSM1 reduced the K63-linked ubiquitination of TRIM21 but not K48-linkage. MYSM1 comprises three main domains: SANT, SWIRM, and MPN [6]. The SANT domain of MYSM1 binds to DNA and is crucial for MYSM1’s binding to histones [6]. The SWIRM domain of MYSM1 is essential for the interaction between MYSM1 and substrate proteins [6]. Consistently, our data indicated that MYSM1 binds to TRIM21 in a SWIRM-dependent manner. Moreover, the MPN metalloprotease domain is the key catalytic domain of MYSM1 [6]. Our study revealed that MYSM1 deubiquitinates and stabilizes TRIM21 through its MPN metalloprotease domain.

Another important conclusion of this study was that TRIM21 is the potential substrate of MYSM1 in DOX injury. Hou K et al. firstly revealed the role of TRIM21 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, demonstrating that TRIM21 knockout mice exhibited resistance to heart failure and mortality in a DOX-induced cardiotoxicity model [15]. Furthermore, knockout of TRIM21 alleviates cardiotoxicity by inhibiting DOX-induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes [15]. Ferroptosis, a type of programmed cell death triggered by iron-mediated lipid peroxidation of the cellular membrane, plays an important role in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [30]. Mechanics of ferroptosis involves the lipid repair enzyme GPX4 and the depletion of GSH, as well as the production of lipid radicals facilitated by iron [17]. Inhibition of ferroptosis can effectively alleviate the cardiotoxicity induced by DOX [31]. In our study, we also found that MYSM1 regulates TRIM21 function via ferroptosis in DOX-induced cardiomyocytes, indicating that MYSM1-ferroptosis axis plays a crucial role in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. On the other hand, no specific inhibitors for TRIM21 has been reported. It might pose challenges in designing small-molecule drugs targeting the protein structure of TRIM21. Consequently, we propose that MYSM1 may serve as a more viable therapeutic target than TRIM21 for addressing DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

In conclusion, our results showed that MYSM1 plays a deubiquitination role in regulating TRIM21 stability and mediating DOX-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. This is the first study to identify the role of MYSM1 as a detrimental factor to promote myocardial DOX injury. These findings extend our understanding of the role of DUBs in Dox-induced cardiotoxicity. We also demonstrated the pathogenic role of specific MYSM1-TRIM21-ferroptosis axis in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, suggesting that cardiomyocyte-specific knockdown of MYSM1 may be an attractive therapeutic strategy for this condition.
