Abstract
. Recommender systems have become increasingly ubiquitous in daily life. While traditional recommendation approaches primarily rely on ID-based representations or item-side content features, they often fall short in capturing the underlying semantics aligned with user preferences ( e.g. , recommendation reasons for items), leading to a semantic-collaborative gap. Recently emerged LLM-based feature extraction approaches also face a key challenge: how to ensure that LLMs possess recommendation-aligned reasoning capabilities and can generate accurate, personalized reasons to mitigate the semantic-collaborative gap. To address these issues, we propose a novel Content Understanding from a Collaborative Perspective framework (CURec), which generates collaborative-aligned content features for more comprehensive recommendations. CURec first aligns the LLM with recommendation objectives through pretraining, equipping it with instruction-following and chain-of-thought reasoning capabilities. Next, we design a reward model inspired by traditional recommendation architectures to evaluate the quality of the recommendation reasons generated by the LLM. Finally, using the reward signals, CURec fine-tunes the LLM through RL and corrects the generated reasons to ensure their accuracy. The corrected reasons are then integrated into a downstream recommender model to enhance comprehensibility and recommendation performance. Extensive experiments on public benchmarks demonstrate the superiority of CURec over existing methods.



1.
Introduction

Recommender systems have become increasingly ubiquitous in daily life, powering applications such as e-commerce
(
Chai et al. 2025
)
, short-video
(
Luo et al. 2025
)
, or social media
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
platforms.
Traditional recommendation algorithms
(
He et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
primarily rely on ID embedding features to represent users and items. While these approaches have demonstrated strong recommendation performance, they are inherently limited by their memorization-based nature
(
Cheng et al. 2016
)
: the models tend to recall historical interactions while overlooking the semantic content information of items. To enrich the content representations of users and items, several methods
(
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Hou et al. 2022
)
incorporate additional item attributes, such as category labels or textual descriptions, into the recommendation process to enhance performance. Emerging approaches
(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2024
)
further leverage LLMs to generate features for recommendation, utilizing their reasoning capabilities to infer user behavior patterns
(
Xi et al. 2024
)
and their extensive world knowledge to uncover richer item information
(
Jia et al. 2024
)
.

Enabling content understanding from the perspective of recommendation is critical for recommender systems
(
Jia et al. 2024
)
, as it facilitates comprehensible recommendations, improves the efficiency of collaborative filtering, and enhances overall user experience. While incorporating various modalities of item information can alleviate the issue of limited content awareness, such approaches typically focus solely on the item’s semantic information. They focus on introducing more precise descriptive information about items, rather than generating representations tailored to the recommendation objective. This neglect of the gap between semantic and collaborative perspectives results in a misalignment, preventing existing methods from achieving optimal recommendation performance.

As illustrated in Fig.

, different user interaction sequences reflect distinct interest patterns, which in turn lead to diverse reasons for liking the same movie.
These reasons from collaborative perspectives cannot be adequately described by semantic text information. However, such reasons are beneficial for generating comprehensible recommendations, as they provide deeper insights into user preferences and decision-making processes.

The rich world knowledge and powerful reasoning capabilities of LLMs
(
Xi et al. 2024
;
Xia et al. 2025
)
offer a promising solution for generating collaborative-perspective reasons by leveraging users’ historical interaction sequences and the semantic descriptions of items. However, despite their impressive capabilities, directly applying LLMs to recommendation reason generation may yield suboptimal results
(
Bismay et al. 2024
)
, such as incorrect, generic, or uninformative explanations. This is largely due to the misalignment between the training objectives of LLMs and the reasoning goals of recommender systems. Therefore, ensuring that LLMs are equipped with a recommendation-oriented chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning process, as well as the ability to generate accurate and meaningful recommendation reasons, remains a significant challenge.

To this end, we propose a novel approach, Content Understanding from Collaborative Perspective framework (CURec), which leverages LLMs to generate and refine recommendation-perspective content features and efficiently integrates them into recommender systems to enable content-aware, comprehensible recommendations. Specifically, CURec consists of three key components: (1) recommendation alignment pretraining, (2) a reward model with recommendation signal, and (3) chronological CoT correction.

Given the fundamental mismatch between the training objective of LLMs and recommender system models, to equip LLMs with the ability to follow structured instructions and understand the recommendation process through reasoning, we design the recommendation alignment pretraining for LLMs. The pretraining is aimed at guiding the LLM to acquire the capability to leverage world knowledge and engage in chain-of-thought reasoning to complete recommendations for users.
However, due to the hallucination problem inherent in LLMs
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
, directly applying the pretrained model to recommendation reason generation can lead to inaccurate or generic outputs. Introducing such flawed reasons into the recommender system may inject noise into item features or result in overly generalized features that hinder personalized recommendation. To address this, we design a recommendation reasons reward model and the corresponding chronological CoT correction process.
First, to evaluate the accuracy of generated reasons, we construct a reward model inspired by traditional recommendation architectures. This model takes as input the user’s interest pattern along with a list of recommendation reasons for an item and outputs a predicted score between the user and the item. This score serves as an indicator of reason quality.
Next, we use the trained reward model to guide the LLM through reinforcement learning, alternately correcting the LLM and the recommendation reasons in a chronological order. This enables the LLM to produce reasons that are both accurate and personalized. Finally, since the reward model itself functions as a recommender model, it can directly utilize the corrected reasons in downstream recommendation tasks, thereby achieving enhanced recommendation performance.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a chain-of-thought correction framework based on reinforcement learning, enabling LLMs to leverage world knowledge and perform recommendation-oriented reasoning, thereby generating accurate and personalized recommendation reasons.

•

We introduce a model that effectively utilizes the generated user interest patterns and item recommendation reasons, achieving comprehensible and high-performance recommendation.

•

Extensive experiments on public benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed CURec framework.

2.
Related Works

In this section, we discuss research relevant to this work, including traditional collaborative filtering, content-based recommendation, and LLM for recommendation.

2.1.
Collaborative Filtering

Collaborative filtering (CF)
(
Koren et al. 2009
)
algorithms represent a milestone in recommendation systems, focusing on using historical user-item interaction data to predict potential future interactions. The main idea of CF is to generate representations for users and items and use their similarity to make recommendations
(
He et al. 2017
)
. In recent years, emerging approaches have gained prominence using GNNs
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
He et al. 2020
;
Su et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Xia et al. 2022
;
Xia et al. 2023
)
, aiming to treat user-item interactions as a bipartite graph, propagating information between users and items through the graph’s links and optimizing the performance of similar nodes based on their interaction histories. Another common collaborative filtering approach models user representations based on their historical behavior sequences
(
Sun et al. 2019
;
Kang and McAuley 2018
;
Tang and Wang 2018
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Qiu et al. 2022
)
, with many methods integrating GNNs for enhanced modeling
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Ye et al. 2023
)
. However, they rely solely on ID embeddings while ignoring rich content information, which limits their overall performance.

2.2.
Content-based Recommendation

Content-based recommendation aims to alleviate the generalization limitations of recommender systems and enhance recommendation efficiency by incorporating side information about users and items. Early works, such as VBPR
(
He and McAuley 2016
)
, optimize recommendation by combining visual features of items with ID embeddings. More recent approaches like M3CSR
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
and MAKE
(
Sheng et al. 2024
)
further improve the utilization of content information by introducing modality-specific encoders. Inspired by the BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
encoding model, many studies
(
Ding et al. 2021
;
Hou et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
)
integrate textual modalities to generate more informative item representations.
Although these methods effectively incorporate content features, they still fall short in enabling deep content understanding through reasoning. Moreover, they overlook the gap between the item-perspective and recommendation-perspective, resulting in suboptimal utilization of content information.

2.3.
LLM for Recommendation

LLM-based recommendation methods can be broadly categorized into two types: using LLMs as recommender systems and leveraging LLMs to enhance recommendations. Approaches that employ LLMs directly as recommender systems
(
Bao et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Bismay et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
)
typically involve fine-tuning the language model using recommendation data or ID information.
However, using LLMs as recommenders faces practical limitations due to their high inference latency. Additionally, the mismatch between the training objectives of LLMs and recommender systems further hinders their ability to achieve optimal performance.
On the other hand, LLM-assisted recommendation
(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Jia et al. 2024
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
Xia et al. 2025
)
focuses on using LLMs to generate informative features.
Despite effectively incorporating external knowledge and reasoning through LLMs, they have yet to address the challenge of content understanding from the recommendation perspective.

4.
Methods

4.1.
Overview

In this section, we will provide a detailed description of our proposed approach CURec.

Recommendation Alignment Pretraining.
First, we perform RL-based pretraining of the LLM on recommendation tasks, enabling it to follow instructions in a specified format and conduct chain-of-thought reasoning. The pretraining also encourages the LLM to leverage world knowledge to perform reasoning aligned with recommendation objectives. Through this pretraining, the LLM gains a deeper understanding of the items in the system and the recommendation reasons behind why users might prefer them.

Reward Model with Recommendation Signal.
Next, we design and train a reward model to evaluate the accuracy of the generated recommendation reasons. Specifically, we utilize the pretrained LLM to generate interest patterns for each user and the recommendation reason for each user-item interaction. Following the architecture of traditional recommender systems, we compute the relevance score using a user’s interest pattern and a list of item reasons with an attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
mechanism, and train the model on recommendation data. In this way, when generating new reasons, they can be appended to the item’s reason list and evaluated by the reward model, allowing us to assess the quality of the generated explanations in a principled and recommendation-aware manner.

Chronological CoT Correction.
After obtaining the reward model, we perform a correction process of the LLM and the content features in a chronological order. Specifically, for a given interaction sample, the new recommendation reason generated by LLM is combined with the item’s existing reason list and passed into the reward model to assess the quality of the generated reason. The reward score is then utilized to optimize the LLM via reinforcement learning. The updated LLM subsequently updates the user interest pattern and the item reason list based on the interaction. Through this correction process, we obtain an LLM capable of generating accurate, personalized reasons, as well as corrected reasons.

Since the reward model is inherently designed as a recommendation model, it can be directly applied to downstream recommendation tasks without requiring additional training.

4.2.
Recommendation Alignment Pretraining

Since LLMs are primarily trained with objectives centered around dialogue completion and various domain-specific tasks (
e.g.
, code, mathematics), directly applying them to recommendation tasks often yields suboptimal performance, despite their rich world knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities. Moreover, to effectively activate the reasoning abilities of LLMs, it is essential to guide them to produce outputs in a structured chain-of-thought format.

To bridge the gap between the training objectives of LLMs and recommender systems, we conduct recommendation alignment pretraining. Specifically, we construct prompts based on recommendation data, prompting the LLM to perform chain-of-thought reasoning to complete the recommendation task. The generated completions are then evaluated via reward signals, which are used to fine-tune the LLM through reinforcement learning.

To address this, we design a
Recommendation Alignment Prompt
instruction template, denoted as
prompt_{\text{rec}}
, to guide the LLM in performing chain-of-thought reasoning, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. For each interaction sample
((u,\mathcal{S}_{u}),(i,\mathcal{S}_{i}))\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
,
prompt_{\text{rec}}
instructs the model to analyze the descriptions of items in the user’s interaction history
\mathcal{S}_{u}
, infer the user’s interest pattern, and identify the most relevant item from a candidate list
\mathcal{N}_{ui}
. The candidate list
\mathcal{N}_{ui}
consists of the target item
i
from the interaction sample along with several negative items that do not appear in the user’s interaction history
i^{-},i\not\in\mathcal{S}_{u}
. We obtain the LLM’s recommendation output by feeding it the constructed
prompt_{\text{rec}}
:

(2)

o_{\text{rec}}=LLM(prompt_{\text{rec}},\mathcal{S}_{u},\mathcal{N}_{ui})

To evaluate the outputs of the LLM, we design a rule-based reward
r_{\text{pre}}
to guide the RL training. The reward consists of three components:
(1) Format Reward: This checks whether the output follows the required structure, specifically whether the reasoning process is enclosed within <think> </think> tags and the answer within <answer> </answer> tags.
(2) Legal Reward: This checks whether the predicted item appears within the provided candidate list.
(3) Correctness Reward: This evaluates whether the predicted item matches the ground truth item from the interaction sample.

We train the LLM using GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
based on the designed reward signals. For each
prompt_{\text{rec}}
, GRPO samples a group of responses
\{{o_{\text{rec}}}_{1},{o_{\text{rec}}}_{2},\cdots,{o_{\text{rec}}}_{G}\}
and optimizes the policy model according to the following objective:

(3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}{[\{{o_{\text{rec}}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{G}\sim LLM_{\theta_{old}}(O|prompt_{\text{rec}},\mathcal{S}_{u},\mathcal{N}_{ui})]}\\
&\frac{1}{G}\sum_{n=1}^{G}\left(\rho_{n}-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}\left(LLM_{\theta}||LLM_{ref}\right)\right),\\
\rho_{n}&=\min\left[\delta_{n}A_{n},\text{clip}\left(\delta_{n},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A_{n}\right],\\
\delta_{n}&=\frac{LLM_{\theta}({o_{\text{rec}}}_{n}|prompt_{\text{rec}},\mathcal{S}_{u},\mathcal{N}_{ui})}{LLM_{\theta_{old}}({o_{\text{rec}}}_{n}|prompt_{\text{rec}},\mathcal{S}_{u},\mathcal{N}_{ui})}\end{split}

where
\epsilon
and
\beta
are hyper-parameters, and
A_{i}
is the advantage, computed using a group of rewards
\{{r_{\text{pre}}}_{1},{r_{\text{pre}}}_{2},\ldots,{r_{\text{pre}}}_{G}\}
corresponding to the outputs within each group:

(4)

A_{n}=\frac{{r_{\text{pre}}}_{n}-{\mathrm{mean}(\{{r_{\text{pre}}}_{1},{r_{\text{pre}}}_{2},\cdots,{r_{\text{pre}}}_{G}\})}}{{\mathrm{std}(\{{r_{\text{pre}}}_{1},{r_{\text{pre}}}_{2},\cdots,{r_{\text{pre}}}_{G}\})}}

Through the above RL-based training, we achieve an alignment between the LLM and the recommendation task. This enables the LLM to acquire both the reasoning ability required for recommendation objectives and the capability to follow structured output instructions. The pretrained LLM, denoted as
LLM_{\text{pre}}
, becomes capable of effectively inferring user interest patterns and generating recommendation reasons in subsequent tasks.

4.3.1.
Pattern and Reason Generation

To extract user interest patterns and item recommendation reasons from interaction data using the pretrained LLM, we design two prompt templates:
Pattern Generation Prompt
and
Recommendation Reason Generation Prompt
, denoted as
prompt_{\text{pattern}}
and
prompt_{\text{reason}}
, which guide the LLM to produce outputs through reasoning, as shown in Fig.
2
.

Pattern Generation Prompt
takes as input the descriptions of items from the user’s interaction sequence and prompts the LLM to analyze the user’s interests from multiple perspectives. For example, in the MovieLens dataset,
prompt_{\text{pattern}}
directs the LLM to consider aspects including but not limited to genre, category, and director. For each user
u
, we use their interaction sequence
\mathcal{S}_{u}
from the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
as input and apply
prompt_{\text{pattern}}
to obtain a summary of their personalized interest pattern:

(5)

p_{u}=LLM_{\text{pre}}(prompt_{\text{pattern}},\mathcal{S}_{u})

where
p_{u}
represents the generated pattern of the user.

Recommendation Reason Generation Prompt
additionally takes the description of the target item as input.
prompt_{\text{reason}}
guides the LLM to infer why the user might favor the target item by identifying the commonalities between the user’s interest pattern and the item’s content description. The resulting reason is not a simple multi-perspective description of the item. Instead, it represents a recommendation-perspective content feature that integrates the LLM’s rich world knowledge, the user’s evolving interests, and the underlying reasons for user-item matching.
For each sample
((u,\mathcal{S}_{u}),(i,\mathcal{S}_{i}))
in the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
, we use it as input to this prompt, allowing the LLM to generate a personalized reason explaining why the item is recommended to the user:

(6)

q_{ui}=LLM_{\text{pre}}(prompt_{\text{reason}},\mathcal{S}_{u},i)

where
q_{ui}
denotes the generated recommendation reason. For each item, we aggregate all previously generated recommendation reasons into a reason list
Q_{i}=\{q_{ui}\}_{u\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}
, which serves as the item’s content representation. This list provides a rich, recommendation-oriented understanding of the item from multiple angles. By doing so, we move beyond generic semantic descriptions and construct a content representation that reflects how different users perceive and relate to the item in the context of personalized recommendation.

4.3.2.
Reason Evaluation Model

For each user-item interaction sample, both the user interest pattern and the item recommendation reason are represented in a language semantic space. We encode them into embeddings using the LLM:

(7)

\boldsymbol{e}_{p_{u}}=\mathrm{Encoder}(p_{u}),\boldsymbol{E}_{Q_{i}}=\{\mathrm{Encoder}(q)\}_{q\in Q_{i}}

To compute the user-item matching representation, we apply a multi-head attention mechanism
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, where the user embedding serves as the query, and the list of item reason embeddings serves as the keys and values:

(8)

\boldsymbol{s}_{ui}=\mathrm{MultiHeadAttention}(\boldsymbol{e}_{p_{u}},\boldsymbol{E}_{Q_{i}},\boldsymbol{E}_{Q_{i}})

where
\boldsymbol{s}_{ui}
represents the matching representation embedding.

This attention-based interaction allows the model to assess which recommendation reasons are most aligned with the user’s interests. Through this approach, we can effectively evaluate whether specific aspects of the item resonate with the user’s preferences, thereby enhancing the comprehensibility of the recommendation process.

To effectively train the attention parameters and derive user-item matching scores that serve as reward signals, we integrate the matching representation into a traditional recommendation model and train it using recommendation data. Following common practices in sequential recommendation
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
, we encode the user’s interaction history as a sequence of item embeddings and process it through a Transformer
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
to obtain a user embedding:

(9)

\boldsymbol{e}_{u}=\boldsymbol{h}_{1},\{\boldsymbol{h}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{u}}=\mathrm{Transformer}\left(\{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{u}}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the item ID embedding. Next, we compute the prediction score by combining the user and item embedding, and the matching representation using the Deep Interest Network (DIN)
(
Zhou et al. 2018
)
:

(10)

\hat{y}_{ui}=\mathrm{DIN}([\boldsymbol{e}_{u},\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\boldsymbol{s}_{ui}])

where
\hat{y}_{ui}
is the prediction score. Finally, we train the model using the negative log-likelihood loss:

(11)

\mathcal{L}=-\log\frac{\exp(\hat{y}_{ui})}{\sum_{i^{-}\in\mathcal{I}}\exp(\hat{y}_{ui^{-}})}

4.4.
Chronological CoT Correction

After designing and training the reward model, we are able to evaluate the quality of the recommendation reasons generated by the LLM and fine-tune the model accordingly. However, since both user interests and item reason lists evolve over time, how to update these features dynamically during fine-tuning the LLM remains a problem. To address this, we propose an alternating correction framework, as illustrated in Fig.
4
.
Specifically, we perform one-step fine-tuning of the LLM using the current user interest patterns and item reason lists. The finetuned LLM then updates user patterns and item reason lists. This process is repeated in an alternating manner chronologically.

4.4.1.
LLM Finetuning

Similar to the pretraining stage, we fine-tune the LLM using a reinforcement learning approach. We adopt the
prompt_{\text{reason}}
described in Sec.
4.3.1
to guide the LLM in reasoning and generating a new recommendation reason
q_{ui}^{\prime}
, following the formulation in Equ. (
6
). The newly generated reason is appended to the item’s reason list and evaluated using the reward model:

(12)

r=\hat{y}_{ui}=f(p_{u},Q_{i}\cup\{q_{ui}^{\prime}\},\mathcal{S}_{u},i)

where
f
represent the reward model process described in Sec.
4.3.2
.

We adopt the GRPO training framework. For each interaction, we sample a group of recommendation reasons and compute the corresponding reward signals. Based on Equ. (
3
-
4
), we then calculate the loss and perform a one-step fine-tuning update on the LLM:

(13)

\theta_{new}=\theta_{old}-\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}

where
\theta
refer to the parameter of LLM, and
\alpha
is the learning rate.

4.4.2.
Updating Pattern and Reasons

After fine-tuning, the LLM gains an improved ability to generate high-quality recommendation reasons. Since the training process is chronological, we use the fine-tuned LLM to update user interest patterns and the item reason lists, to ensure that both of them remain up-to-date.
Specifically, we design a
Pattern Update Prompt
(
prompt_{\text{update}}
), similar to the combination of
Pattern Generation Prompt
and
Recommendation Reason Generation Prompt
described in Sec.
4.3.1
.
prompt_{\text{update}}
takes as input the user’s current pattern along with the target item, and guides the LLM to generate an updated user pattern and the recommendation reason:

(14)

p_{u}^{\prime},q_{ui}^{\prime}=LLM_{new}(prompt_{\text{update}},p_{u},i)

where
p_{u}^{\prime}
,
q_{ui}^{\prime}
denotes the updated pattern and newly generated reason. The item’s recommendation reason list is updated by appending
q_{ui}^{\prime}
to the existing list
Q_{i}
:

(15)

Q_{i}^{\prime}=Q_{i}\cup\{q_{ui}^{\prime}\}

Through the chronological correction process, we obtain a fine-tuned LLM capable of generating high-quality reasons, along with more accurate and up-to-date patterns and reason lists. Since the reward model introduced in Sec.
4.3.2
is itself a recommendation model, it can directly utilize the corrected patterns and reason lists for recommendation without requiring additional training.

5.1.3.
Evaluation Metrics

In our setup, we utilize the widely adopted Recall and NDCG
(
Wang et al. 2013
)
metrics to evaluate the performance of various recommendation models. Following prior work, we adopt the commonly used leave-one-out
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
evaluation strategy. Specifically, for each user, we hold out the most recent interaction as the test instance and use the remaining interactions for training. During evaluation, we perform a full ranking over the entire item set, rather than relying on negative sampling. Finally, we report the average values of the metrics across all samples in the test set.

5.1.4.
Implementation Details

For the RL fine-tuning of the LLM during both the pretraining and chronological correction phases, we use Qwen2.5
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
as the base model. The training is conducted using the AdamW
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
)
optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-7, and we apply a cosine learning rate scheduler. We train the model for 2 epochs on each dataset to ensure effective alignment without overfitting.
During the training process of the reward model, we utilize Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
as the optimizer for gradient descent with a learning rate set to 1e-3. The batch size is set to 128, and the embedding dimension is 64 for all types.

Overall Performance (RQ1)

Tab.
2
presents the top-K evaluation results of various methods on the dataset, where K is set to 1, 5, 10, and 20. Based on the experimental results, we make the following observations:

Our proposed CURec achieves significant improvements compared to all baseline methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of leveraging LLM-generated, collaboration-perspective recommendation reasons as content features to enhance traditional recommendation models. Notably, on movie datasets, content-based models generally outperform ID-based models, which can be attributed to the richer and more informative content features available in movies compared to games, where such features are relatively sparse. We also observe that trainable ID-based features remain crucial for traditional recommendation models, relying solely on frozen content features (UniSRec, LEARN) often leads to suboptimal performance.

Compared to other approaches that utilize LLMs for content feature generation, CURec offers two key advantages:
(i)
it leverages chain-of-thought reasoning to generate recommendation-aligned features, and
(ii)
it employs LLM correction through reinforcement learning to produce more accurate and personalized features. These enhancements lead to improved recommendation performance.

5.3.1.
Effect of Introducing Reasons.

In this section, we analyze the impact of introducing collaborative-perspective recommendation reasons. When reasons are entirely excluded and replaced with raw item textual descriptions, encoded and used alongside user patterns as features within a traditional recommender (variant Text), the model performs similarly to other LLM-assisted baseline methods. This result highlights a key insight: simply providing more detailed descriptions of item content is insufficient for optimal performance. Instead, incorporating content understanding from a collaborative perspective is more effective in supporting and enhancing recommendation comprehensibility.

5.3.2.
Effect of Chronological Correction

In this section, we analyze the impact of the chronological CoT correction. The results show that omitting the correction process (
w/o
Corr.) leads to a significant performance drop, indicating that the correction process effectively enhances the LLM’s ability to generate accurate recommendation reasons and updates content features accordingly.
Moreover, if we finetune the LLM without updating features (
w/o
Update), the performance also degrades, highlighting the necessity of alternating updates between the LLM and reasons.
From another perspective, even without correction, the model still outperforms most baseline methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of generating collaborative-perspective content features from an LLM to enhance recommendation performance.

5.3.3.
Effect of Reasons Utilization

In this section, we analyze the impact of different strategies for utilizing recommendation reasons. We compare three integration methods for the item reason list: average pooling (AP), MLP, and self-attention (SA). In each case, the fused reason representation is concatenated with the user interest pattern and replaces the attention-based matching representation in CURec. The results show that applying an attention mechanism to integrate the user interest pattern with the item reason list yields the best performance. This approach not only enhances recommendation performance but also improves comprehensibility, as it explicitly models the alignment between user interests and specific recommendation reasons.

6.
Conclusion

We propose CURec, a novel framework that incorporates fine-tuned LLMs to generate collaborative-perspective content features and integrates them into recommendation, to enhance both performance and comprehensibility of recommendation.
CURec first fine-tunes the LLM through recommendation-aligned pretraining. By introducing a reward model based on recommendation signals, CURec effectively evaluates the quality of the generated reasons and applies them in a chronological CoT correction process to refine more accurate and personalized reasons. These features can be directly utilized in downstream recommendation tasks.
Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of CURec over existing baselines, highlighting its ability to bridge the semantic-collaborative gap for content understanding comprehensive recommendation.
