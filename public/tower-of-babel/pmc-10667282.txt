Preventing diabetes in the southeast Asia region
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia
Published: 2023

The prevalence of diabetes has been increasing globally, with most people with diabetes living in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). In 2021, around 75 million people in India (aged 20–79 years) were reported to be living with diabetes compared with 19 million people in Indonesia. These large numbers could be attributed to their large populations, as the age-adjusted prevalence of diabetes was highest in Bangladesh (14.2%) followed by Sri Lanka, Indonesia, and Bhutan. Earlier, diabetes was believed to mostly affect the urban population, but inflation and consumption of processed foods have led to increased prevalence of diabetes among the rural population too. Considering that the rural population constitutes a major part of the population in the southeast Asia region, prevention of diabetes is particularly important in rural areas.

In 2019, the largest number of cases and deaths related to diabetes in children were reported from India. Childhood obesity is a risk factor for development of diabetes during adolescence and adulthood. Academic pressure, increasing screen time, and reduced playtime have also contributed to the increasing burden of childhood obesity. Lifestyle modifications at an early age are hence essential. India has introduced physical activity programmes in all schools, but their implementation is questionable. Most countries have initiated laws and guidelines to promote food labelling, restrict advertisements of unhealthy foods during hours in which children watch television, regulate foods available in schools, and create more awareness through extracurricular activities. Thailand has implemented front-of-pack nutrition labelling, but customers may find it difficult to interpret. In addition to detailed labelling, simpler labelling would help in easy interpretation by customers with low education. However, ensuring availability of healthy choices at affordable prices remains a major challenge.

Governments must ensure there is increased public awareness about complications associated with diabetes among people with diabetes, including children. For treatment adherence, awareness programmes must be more engaging and prevent misinformation, such as the idea that insulin is addictive and can harm other organs. There would be a better response during emergency cases if caregivers understood the increased predisposition to stroke and other comorbidities in people with diabetes. A study by Sunuwar and colleagues in this issue showed that a dietician-led dietary approach can help in the management of type 2 diabetes in Nepal. The study participants were provided a nutritional counselling session as well as individualised diet plan education, which led to reduction in glycated haemoglobin. In other LMICs, low-resource settings pose a major challenge in using such interventions. However, incorporating digital health solutions at primary health centres would be a good option—for example, consultations with remote diabetologists.

People with diabetes were affected by lockdown during the COVID-19 pandemic. Restrictions led to mental stress, reduced physical activity, and lower access to medications. As hyperglycaemia is a risk factor for severity of COVID-19, mortality was higher among people with diabetes. Also, development of diabetes has been reported in many COVID-19 survivors, indicating an association between long COVID and development of diabetes. More studies are required to understand how the COVID-19 pandemic affected diabetes services in the southeast Asia region so that countries are better able to prepare themselves for another pandemic.

Diabetes is a global epidemic, but a healthy diet and physical activity can prevent and control diabetes. Walking for 15 min after meals helps in better glycaemic control. Health authorities would be able to protect their citizens from diabetes by implementation of stricter nutrition guidelines. Encouraging constructive criticism by citizens and reducing lobbying by food companies would play a major role in the success of such an initiative. Better public awareness, early detection of prediabetes, and changing to a healthier lifestyle would help delay or prevent the onset of diabetes.
