Adsorptive removal of azithromycin from aqueous solution using flake-like Co-ZIF coated melamine sponge in batch and continuous systems
Authors: Amir Adashi, Golshan Mazloom, Milad Ghani, Farhad Banisharif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shape engineering of the efficient adsorbents such as metal-organic-frameworks (MOFs) into hierarchically porous structure allows to be used in various situations, because the powder MOFs suffer from low stability in aqueous solution, reusing, and recovery. In this work, low cost and commercially available melamine sponge (MS) was coated successfully via facile one-step in-situ growth of flake-like Co-ZIF without using any additives/treatment, denoted as ZIF/MS. The performance of ZIF/MS was assessed in the azithromycin (AZ) adsorption in both batch and continuous modes. ZIF/MS exhibited excellent performance with a high theoretical adsorption capacity of 1428.57 mg/g. This was attributed to this fact that interconnected microporous structure of MS prevented the aggregation of ZIF particles, causing improvement in the accessibility of the numerous active sites of ZIF. Moreover, easy recovering makes ZIF/MS stand out as an effective adsorbent with superior stability. The breakthrough curves obtained in fixed bed were evaluated by Thomas model. In the column experiments, lower feed flow rate, higher influent concentration, and larger bed depth were in favor of more uptake capacity. Introduction

Pharmaceutical products play an important role in saving the lives from dangerous diseases and improving the life span. In recent years, pharmaceutical products have been globally high utilized by animals and humans1. One of the negative effects of this growing trend is releasing the pharmaceutical compounds into the ecological environment2. The problem becomes more acute when it has been considered that less than 10% of the pharmaceuticals is metabolized by the body and the remaining 90% are excreted to the environment3,4. Antibiotics as the organic material have been employed widely by the animals, humans, and agricultural products for treatment of the bacterial infections5. The steady releasing of antibiotics from hospitals, households, and industries to the aquatic environment causes the great concern about the emerging hazards to humans6. Azithromycin (AZ) is a type of antibiotics, which is prescribed for treatment of bacterial infections, including respiratory, skin, and sexually-transmitted bacterial infections7,8. However, the concentration of AZ in the environment is in the range of nano to micrograms, but its accumulation in the bodies leads to the different problems. Furthermore, AZ cannot be treated completely in the natural ecosystem, as it can regularly detected in the ground-, surface-, and drinking-water.

So far, various techniques have been utilized for elimination of pharmaceutical materials, namely photo/sono degradation9–11, ozone oxidation12, reverse osmosis13, nanofiltration14,15, electrochemical treatment16, and adsorption using different adsorbents17,18. Among these different technologies, the adsorption is the most widely used methodology for elimination of organic materials19. Other methods suffer from some drawbacks. For example, coagulation produces huge amounts of sludge. Reverse osmosis processes require high pressure. The photo/sono catalytic processes consume lots of energy and usually produce secondary pollutants20. These disadvantages make the adsorption method superior to other methodologies. Adsorption process possesses many advantages, such as low cost, straight forward design and operation, low energy consumption, excellent efficiency, and various effective adsorbents. The main concern in the adsorption processes is the design of the effective adsorbent, which is eco-friendly, recyclable, cost-effective, with high adsorption capacity. In this regard, different materials such as carbon-based materials21, zeolites22, and metal-organic-frameworks (MOFs)23,24 have examined as adsorbent.

Zeolitic imidazolate framework-67 (ZIF-67) is the subclass of MOF, which contains Co2+ as the metal cation and 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM) as the organic linker25. ZIF-67 with the structure similar to the zeolite exhibits outstanding features of both zeolites and MOFs, including high crystallinity, high porosity, high surface area, and high stability in aqueous solution. Moreover, ZIF-67 can be produced by a simple method without any templates at room temperature, in contrast to the zeolites. Hence, in recent years, ZIFs have been attracted much interest as adsorbent for removing of different organic materials24. For example, Liu et al.26 have synthesized a novel ZIF/zeolite via sono-crystallization method. Based on their results, the composite represented the high adsorption capacity of 131 mg/g with excellent recyclability in 10 cycles for AZ removal. Zhang et al.27 have prepared ZIF-67/C3N4 composite for adsorption of Tetracycline. Their composite represented the adsorption capacity of 206 mg/g, which was much higher than those obtained for pristine ZIF-67 (116 mg/g) and C3N4 (19 mg/g). Saghir et al.28 have employed Ag@ZIF-67 for adsorptive removal of minocycline and methyl orange. Ag@ZIF-67 was found to be an effective adsorbent with ultra-high adsorption capacity of 938.4 mg/g and 994.6 mg/g for minocycline and methyl orange, respectively.

However, ZIF-67 suffers from some disadvantages, which limits its applications in water treatment processes. Generally, ZIF-67 synthesizes in the powder form that brings difficulties for separation and recovery. Moreover, in industries, the processes are generally operated in continuous type, and the adsorbents in powder form are not suitable for practical large-scale applications. The addition of magnetic materials is the most employed method for facilitating the separation of powders from liquid phase. But magnetic particles have a tendency to be aggregated, which affect the performance. In addition, dispersion of ZIF in the aqueous solution is difficult due to its hydrophobicity29. To overcome these shortcomings, a suitable solution is anchoring ZIF over a well-structured three-dimensional network as a substrate. In this way, the adsorbent can be collected without using external magnetic force. For example, Chen et al.29 have prepared a novel adsorbent with in-situ immobilization of ZIF-67 over the wood aerogel for adsorptive removal of tetracycline. In other work, a hydrogel bead was prepared by Yang et al.30 via loading ZIF-67 over reduced graphene oxide and chitosan network, which exhibited ultra-high adsorption capacity of 1685.26 mg/g and 1890.3 mg/g for tetracycline and norfloxacin, respectively. Abdi et al. have synthesized the polymeric nanocomposite beads based on the poly ether sulfone/ZIF-8/ZIF-67 via phase inversion method and employed in malachite green adsorption in both batch and continuous systems31.

It seems that melamine sponge (MS) is an appropriate substrate for loading ZIF-67. MS composes wires of formaldehyde melamine sodium bisulfite co-polymer, and possesses outstanding features including high porosity, excellent mechanical properties, low density, low cost and eco-friendly feature32. The modification of MS with different MOFs is well studied before. For example, Jiang et al.32 have employed different MOFs for preparation MOF/polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)/MS for adsorptive removal of methyl orange, in which PVDF were used as the crosslinking agent. ZIF-8/MS was prepared by Lei et al.33 in the presence of poly dopamine, which serves as the linker. The prepared ZIF-8/MS showed excellent oil/water separation performance with oil absorption capacity up to 3800 wt%. Guo et al.34 have prepared MS@ZIF via facile deep coating method in which ZIF-L was loaded over MS under ultrasonic homogenizer and PVDF as the linker, and employed for heavy metal ions adsorption. They have reported that the structure and morphology of ZIF can be modulated by variation of the ultrasonication time. As reported by Cao et al.35, Co-ZIF-L@MS was synthesized using in-situ growth of Co-ZIF. The modified MS showed a good performance in oil/water separation. Interestingly, in the preparation of Co-ZIF-L@MS, no other additives were employed as linkers.

So far, some efforts have been performed for the immobilization of ZIFs over MS. But generally, these researches have focused on the evaluation of ZIF/MS in the oil/water separation, and the investigation of its performance in the adsorptive removal of pharmaceutical compounds such as AZ especially in continuous flow system is scarce.

Herein, we employed the facile in-situ growth method for loading flake-like Co-ZIF (ZIF-F) over pristine MS without using other additives or surfactants, and construct a monolithic adsorbent for elimination of AZ from aqueous solution in both batch and continuous systems. The performance of ZIF/MS under various adsorbent dosages, pH, initial AZ concentrations, and contact time was evaluated in batch mode. The obtained experimental data was examined by kinetic and isotherm modeling. Moreover, in the continuous flow system, the influence of different feed flow rates, influent AZ concentrations, and bed heights was studied. The breakthrough curves were analyzed and discussed using Thomas model. The modeling of the fixed bed adsorption is a useful method for prediction of the process parameters, which are essential for practical applications. This study provides supportive evidences for efficiency of ZIF/MS for removal of pharmaceutical contaminants. Since MS is flexible, it can be easily shaped to different forms, such as filter, foam, or membrane, which reveals its outstanding potential for application in different situations and specific requirements. In addition, the facile synthesized method employed in this work without using any additives or surfactants at room temperature is scalable.

Materials and methods

Materials

2-methyl imidazole (C4H6N2, 2-MIM, 98%), cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2.6H2O, 98%), and Azithromycin (AZ) were purchased from Merck Co. (Germany). Methanol (CH3OH) and melamine sponge (MS) were supplied from local provider.

Preparation of ZIF/MS

First, the MS was cut to the pieces with the size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\times\:2\times\:2$$\end{document} cm3 and washed with a mixture of methanol and deionized water, under sonication several times. After drying the MS in the oven at 60 °C for 12 h, ZIF-F was loaded by in-situ growth method. In this way: the pristine MS was immersed for 5 h in the cobalt nitrate solution (19.4 mg/mL), which was prepared by dissolving 0.7 g cobalt nitrate in 36 mL deionized water. Then, another solution was prepared by dissolving 1.6 g 2-MIM in 36 mL deionized water, and this solution was added to the mixture of cobalt nitrate and MS, and kept for another 5 h over the magnetic stirrer. Eventually, the ZIF-F/MS was drawn from the solution, washed with deionized water and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. The prepared adsorbent was nominated as ZIF/MS. The preparation method is schematically presented in Fig. S1.

Characterization techniques

The crystalline structure of the adsorbents was studied by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using PHILIPS PW1730 instrument. The morphology of the adsorbents was identified by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) analysis using TESCAN MIRA instrument, which was equipped by energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to assess the elemental composition. The functional groups of the adsorbents were investigated using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis using Thermo Avatar instrument.

Batch experiments

The performance of ZIF/MS was evaluated in the typical batch experiments. The aqueous solution of AZ with specified concentration and volume was prepared as the model wastewater. Then, the ZIF/MS was immersed into the solution to adsorb AZ. During different time intervals, the samples were taken out and the remaining AZ concentration was measured using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, waters) with a C18 column (Length: 250 mm, particle size: 10 μm) and UV-visible detector (Perkin-Elmer). The performance of ZIF/MS was evaluated under different operating conditions, namely various adsorbent dosages (0.05–0.2 g/L), contact time (0–60 min), pH (2–11), and initial AZ concentration (50–150 ppm). The pH of the solution was adjusted using 0.1 M solution of HCl or NaOH. Continuous experiments

The experiments of dynamic adsorption were carried out in a Pyrex column with 2 cm diameter and 10 cm height. The column was packed with ZIF/MS until the desired height was obtained. The glass wools were placed in the lower and upper parts of the ZIF/MS in the column for avoiding the washing out. The column was fed continuously in the upward direction with AZ aqueous solution. The feed flow rate was controlled using a syringe pump. The treated solution was eluted and collected from the top end of the column, and the remained AZ concentration was measured using HPLC.

The effects of different parameters, namely influent AZ concentration (30, 40, and 55 ppm), feed flow rate (2, 2.7, and 4 mL/min), and bed depth (2, 4, and 6 cm) on the adsorptive performance of ZIF/MS were examined. In addition, the breakthrough curves were plotted in different operating conditions.

Modeling of continuous experiments

As known, the modeling of the experimental data in the fixed bed adsorption is a useful tool to provide critical information, which is necessary for practical applications. In this work, Thomas model36 as the most employed kinetic model was employed for correlation of the obtained experimental data in the column. This model follows the assumptions of Langmuir model for equilibrium condition and pseudo-second-order (PSO) model for reaction kinetic. Results and discussion

Characterization

The appearance view of the pure MS and ZIF/MS is shown in Fig. S1. It can be seen that after ZIF loading, the white color of the pure MS turned to the light purple. The obtained ZIF/MS was not brittle and still maintained its outstanding mechanical properties.

The XRD diagram of the pristine MS, ZIF/MS are displayed in Fig. 1. The XRD pattern of MS indicates one broad peak at about 2θ = 20°, reflecting the amorphous nature of the melamine sponge37. The ZIF/MS diagram exhibited the characteristic peak of melamine sponge. This observation reveals that the structure of MS was preserved by in-situ growth of ZIF without any damaging. Meanwhile, the weak diffraction peaks at 2θ = 10.4° and 11° also presented in the XRD diagram of ZIF/MS, which may be corresponded to ZIF type materials. The low intensity of these peaks may be related to the relatively low mass content of the ZIF particles with respect to the melamine sponge. In addition, the broad and big amorphous peak of the melamine matrix may mask and lead to the disappearance of the weak ZIF peaks. These results confirm that ZIF particles were successfully produced and anchored on the sponge structure.

The SEM images of pristine MS and ZIF/MS at various scales are shown in Fig. 2. The pure MS was made of interwoven melamine wires formed a very porous structure. In addition, its surface was smooth and clean. In comparison for ZIF/MS sample, MS surface was covered with two-dimensional flake-shaped ZIF particles, providing a rougher surface. This flake-like morphology is reported before38, when high 2-MIM/Co2+ ratio used during synthesis, which promotes anisotropic crystal growth in aqueous media. Interestingly, ZIF-F particles were stuck to each other due to the hydrogen bonding interactions and exhibited the flower like structure39, leading to increasing the efficient adsorption sites. SEM images confirm that the ZIF/MS can be synthesized directly via in-situ growth of ZIF-F over the MS surface without using any linkers or other additives. When MS submerged in the Co2+ solution. Co2+ was anchored over the melamine wires through coordination and electrostatic interactions with –NH and –OH groups of the melamine skeleton. After addition of 2-MIM solution, the adsorbed Co2+ acts as the active sites for nucleation and growth of ZIF-F. Therefore, the ZIF-F layer was directly formed and strongly bonded on the sponge surface without the need for any additives or linking agents. Cao et al.35 have also reported the modification of MS with Co-ZIF without using any surfactants or additives, employing in the water/oil separation.

Batch adsorption studies

Effect of adsorbent dose

Figure 5A indicates the effects of different adsorbent doses from 0.05 to 0.2 g/L on AZ removal and adsorption capacity. With increasing adsorbent doses from 0.05 to 0.1 g/L, more active sites became available for AZ and hence, removal efficiencies increased from 76.1 to 97.2%. However, with further increase in the adsorbent dose, the removal efficiency increased no longer. In return, increasing adsorbent doses caused decreasing in the adsorption capacity. This is because of the declining in the concentration gradient between AZ adsorbed over the surface and AZ molecules in the liquid phase17. Therefore, lower amounts of AZ can adsorb over the unit mass of adsorbent, leading to decreasing qe. Therefore, 0.1 g/L was chosen as the optimum value for other experiments.

To clarify the contribution of each component, additional adsorption tests were carried out using pristine MS, ZIF-F powder and ZIF/MS under identical conditions. The obtained results were discussed in supplementary data as Fig. S2.

Effect of solution pH

Solution pH is a vital parameter affecting the adsorption process. Because the type of the adsorbate species and adsorbent surface state change with the solution pH, which can play the key role in the interaction between adsorbate and adsorbent9.

Figure 5B indicates that the effects of different pH of 2 to 11 on the adsorptive removal of AZ using ZIF/MS. It was shown that AZ exhibits three forms48: electropositive form for the case of pH lower than 3.5, neutral form, and electronegative form, whereas the solution pH is higher than 9.7. In addition, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of leaf-like ZIF/MS have been measured at 10.3 by Lv et al.49. This outcome reflects that the adsorbent surface was positively charged for pH < 10.3, and negatively charged, when the pH increased further than 10.3. As seen in Fig. 5B, the adsorbent performance was weak at pH of 2. This phenomenon was explained by the electrostatic repulsion, which prevents the adsorption of AZ+ over the positively charged of ZIF/MS. In addition, the lower adsorption performance of ZIF/MS at highly acidic condition might also be assigned to the instability and partial decomposition of ZIF structure. As the pH increased from 4 to 7, AZ molecules were mainly existed in zwitterion forms, but the increasing trend was observed for adsorptive removal over positively charged surface of ZIF/MS. This indicates that different forces such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pi\:-\pi\:$$\end{document} interaction and H-bonding are involved in the adsorption rather than electrostatic interaction. When the solution pH exceeded from pHpzc of adsorbent to 10 and 11, the adsorption efficiency declined, due to the repulsion between anionic form of AZ and negatively charged of ZIF/MS. The highest performance of the adsorbent was achieved at pH of 7.

Effect of time and kinetic modeling

In order to study the kinetic modeling of AZ adsorption over ZIF/MS, the impacts of contacting time on the amounts of the adsorbed AZ molecules were examined. The obtained removal efficiencies at different adsorbent doses versus time are plotted in Fig. S3A. As seen, the adsorption rates were rapid at the initial step. As the contact time proceeded, the rate of adsorption slowed down steadily until achieving the equilibrium stage during 60 min. This observation confirms that the adsorption is influenced by the diffusion. The high adsorbate concentration at the initial step induced high driving forces to overcome the mass transfer resistance between the aqueous phase and adsorbent phase. In addition, at initial step substantial active sites existed50. As the contact time prolonged, the quantity of active sites as well as AZ concentration was lowered, resulted in decreasing the AZ uptake rate51. As shown in Fig. S3A, the adsorption reached equilibrium after approximately 40 min. The relatively short equilibrium time can be attributed to the highly open and interconnected porous structure of the melamine sponge, which facilitates rapid diffusion of AZ to the active sites.

This stage is related to the external diffusion in which AZ molecules diffuse from the aqueous solution towards the adsorbent surface. The second stage is assigned to the intraparticle diffusion which represents the diffusion of AZ into the pores of the adsorbent, and eventually, the final stage is regarded as the equilibrium stage. The kinetic parameters of these 3 stages are listed in Table S1. As seen, the values of C were not zero. In other words, the obtained lines did not cross the origin, confirming the intraparticle diffusion was not the only step which controls the adsorption rate56. Moreover, the values of intercepts (C), which indicates the boundary layer thickness, increased from 906.66 to 1241.20 in the equilibrium stage, as the adsorbent dose decreased from 0.1 to 0.05 g/L. This trend indicates that decreasing the adsorbent dose increases the external mass transfer resistance, which lead to the thickening boundary layer. Interestingly, increasing in the C values led to increasing in the adsorption capacity, implying that a greater boundary layer effect is favor of greater concentration gradient, and therefore, promoting AZ uptake. This multi-linear fitting behavior in IPD model is typical for hierarchical porous materials, where both macro-/mesoporous channels and micropores coexist58. In ZIF/MS sample, the macropores of the melamine sponge provided rapid external diffusion region, and the micro/mesopores of ZIF-F dominated the slower intraparticle diffusion area.

According to the obtained results, it can be concluded that AZ adsorption over ZIF/MS was dominated by the chemisorption and the adsorption process includes bulk diffusion as well as intraparticle diffusion.

Effect of initial AZ concentration and isotherm modeling

Figure S4A shows the R and qe at different initial AZ concentrations of 50, 100, 125, and 150 ppm using ZIF/MS adsorbent. An opposite relation was seen between R and initial concentration. As the AZ concentration increased from 50 to 150 ppm, removal efficiency decreased from 99.9 to 90.2, possibly due to the saturation and occupation of active sites. However, an increasing trend in the obtained equilibrium adsorption capacity appeared. This outcome was explained with enhancing adsorbate concentration gradient in the liquid phase59. Therefore, more AZ molecules diffuse towards the adsorbent, resulting in more adsorption capacity.

The relevance between adsorbate molecules in the liquid bulk and solid phase in the equilibrium state at constant temperature can be examined by isotherm modeling60, which is helpful to study the adsorption mechanism. Significant information can be deduced about the relationship between adsorbent and adsorbate by isotherm models. In addition, isotherm modeling represents an opinion about the adsorption capacity of the adsorbent61.

In this study, the experimental data obtained at different initial AZ concentrations using ZIF/MS were employed for fitting with two useful classical isotherm models, namely Langmuir model62 and Freundlich model63.

Langmuir model assumes that the adsorption process occurs in monolayer over the active sites, which are homogenous. While, based on the Freundlich model, the adsorption behavior is multilayer over the heterogenous active sites. The linear forms of Langmuir and Freundlich models are given as Eqs. Reusability of ZIF/MS

Recyclability is a crucial feature for an adsorbent from a commercial point of view. The stability and reusability of ZIF/MS was evaluated. In this regard, after each experiment at the operating conditions of adsorbent dosage of 0.1 g/L, pH of 7, 60 min, and initial AZ concentration of 100 ppm, the used ZIF/MS was separated, and washed with deionized water several times, followed by drying. The experiments were performed for 3 consecutive cycles and the obtained removal efficiency is plotted in Fig. 6A. As can be seen, the removal efficiency decreased from 97.2 to 92.0% during 3 cycles, indicating the appropriate stability of the proposed ZIF/MS.

The stability of the proposed adsorbent in this work was evaluated further with FTIR analysis. The FTIR spectra of the fresh and used ZIF/MS are compared in Fig. 6B, revealing that the structure of the adsorbent was retained after 3 reuses. Adsorption mechanism

The FTIR spectra of ZIF/MS before and after adsorption indicates that after adsorption of AZ, the peaks appeared in the range of 3100–3600 cm− 1, which are associated to -NH and -OH vibration bonds became much stronger. This fact was resulted from AZ adsorption caused by H-bonding interaction65,66. In addition, there are aromatic rings in the structure of adsorbent, which act as electron-rich adsorption sites, forming \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pi\:-\pi\:$$\end{document} interaction with aromatic rings of AZ50,67. Another important force contributed in the adsorption process is electrostatic interaction49. The presence of positively charged Co2+ centers in the ZIF structure and negatively charged functional groups (–NH2, –OH) on the sponge surface facilitated electrostatic attraction with the oppositely charged sites on AZ molecule. Furthermore, the synergistic structure of the ZIF-coated sponge further enhanced the accessibility of these active sites and reduced diffusion resistance. Conclusion

In this work, flake-like Co-ZIF was coated over melamine MS via facile in-situ growth without using any pretreatment/additives. The loading of Co-ZIF-F over MS (named as ZIF/MS) was successfully showed by XRD, SEM, and FTIR analysis. The performance of ZIF/MS in the adsorptive removal of AZ was evaluated in batch and fixed bed experiments. The effects of different operating conditions of adsorbent dosage, pH, initial AZ concentration, and contact time were studied and discussed. The prepared ZIF/MS exhibited promising adsorption performance with maximum adsorption capacity of 1428.57 mg/g, which was stable during 3 successive cycles. The integration of the advantages of both MS (macroporous structure) and Co-ZIF-F (numerous active sites) led to the excellent performance. In addition, the recovery and handling facilitated by its three-dimensional skeleton structure. In the dynamic experiments, the effects of feed flow rate, influent AZ concentration, and bed height were examined. The obtained breakthrough curves were correlated by Thomas model. This work provides a facile strategy that may improve the practical applicability of MOF-based adsorbents in water treatment processes. Nevertheless, there are still some limitations to the present work. The long-term stability of ZIF/MS under highly acidic conditions and in real wastewater containing competing contaminants has not been thoroughly evaluated. Furthermore, additional studies are needed to assess its large-scale applicability and long-term continuous operation.
