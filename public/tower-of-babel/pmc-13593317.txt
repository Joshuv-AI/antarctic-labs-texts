Age- and sex-specific associations between thyroid function and diabetes in Korean adults
Authors: Ji-Young Choi, Young-Mo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thyroid dysfunction and diabetes mellitus (DM) are two common endocrine disorders that share overlapping metabolic pathways. While several studies have explored the association between thyroid function and DM, findings have been inconsistent. Specifically, both elevated and suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) have been variably reported as risk or protective factors for diabetes, and the contribution of thyroid autoimmunity (thyroid peroxides antibody, TPOAb) to diabetes risk remains particularly contested across study populations. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between thyroid function markers—TSH, free thyroxine (free T4), and TPOAb – and the presence of diabetes in Korean adults. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from 4910 adults aged 19 years or older who participated in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES VI) from 2013 to 2015. Serum levels of TSH, free T4, and TPOAb were measured, and DM was defined based on fasting glucose levels, use of antidiabetic medication, or self-reported diagnosis. As KNHANES does not classify diabetes by etiological subtype, the DM group predominantly reflects type 2 diabetes, consistent with its overwhelming predominance among Korean adults; type 1 diabetes could not be separately identified. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for diabetes across thyroid function categories, with subgroup analyses by age and sex. High TSH levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes (OR = 2.82; 95% CI: 1.26–6.32), especially in men under 65 years (OR = 5.10; 95% CI: 1.65–15.77). Low TSH levels were inversely associated with diabetes risk (OR = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.15–0.86). Low free T4 levels were also linked to an increased risk (OR = 2.08; 95% CI: 1.16–3.75), particularly in younger men. TPOAb positivity showed no significant overall association with diabetes, although a potential protective trend was observed in older men. Altered thyroid function, especially high TSH and low free T4 levels, is associated with increased diabetes risk in Korean adults. These findings suggest the clinical utility of thyroid function screening in diabetes risk assessment and highlight the need for further longitudinal research.

1. Introduction

The thyroid gland plays a crucial role in regulating the body’s metabolism by releasing hormones, primarily thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3).[1] The secretion of these hormones is regulated by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), while thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels indicate the presence of autoimmune thyroid disease.[2] Together, these indicators – TSH, free T4, and TPOAb – provide important insights into thyroid function and health.[2] Thyroid dysfunction most commonly arises from autoimmune conditions – Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, which leads to hypothyroidism, and Graves’ disease, which leads to hyperthyroidism – both of which can also disrupt glucose metabolism through their effects on circulating thyroid hormone levels. The thyroid’s substantial influence on metabolic processes makes it a key regulator of glucose homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and energy balance, thereby establishing a physiological basis for its potential interaction with diabetes mellitus.[3–6]

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic condition characterized by elevated blood glucose levels, represents a major global public health concern.[7] It is generally classified into type 1 and type 2 diabetes, with type 2 diabetes comprising the majority of cases.[8] In Korea specifically, recent nationwide surveillance data indicate that the prevalence of diabetes among adults aged ≥ 30 years reached 15.5% in 2021–2022, rising to 29.3% among those aged ≥ 65 years, underscoring the growing burden of this condition in the aging Korean population.[9] This condition is linked to a wide array of serious complications, including cardiovascular disease, renal failure, and neuropathy.[8,10,11] The diverse complications associated with DM highlight the urgent need to identify all possible risk factors and therapeutic targets that may guide prevention and management strategies.

The intersection of thyroid dysfunction and DM represents a compelling area for investigation, as both conditions influence overlapping metabolic pathways and are increasingly prevalent in aging populations. Emerging evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between thyroid dysfunction and DM, with each condition potentially affecting the onset and progression of the other.[3,12–15] From a mechanistic standpoint, thyroid hormones directly regulate glucose metabolism by modulating gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and insulin sensitivity.[16–18] Conversely, diabetes-related metabolic disturbances may impair thyroid hormone synthesis, transport, and peripheral action, creating a complex interplay that warrants systematic investigation.[15,19] The clinical relevance of this relationship becomes clear when considering that thyroid dysfunction affects approximately 5–10% of adults globally, while the prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, particularly in developed countries.[1,20,21] The potential for these conditions to co-occur and exacerbate one another’s effects has important implications for patient management, treatment outcomes, and healthcare resource planning.

Despite the biological plausibility and clinical importance of thyroid–diabetes associations, the existing literature presents a complex and often contradictory picture. Studies investigating associations between specific thyroid function markers – such as TSH, free T4, and TPOAb – and DM risk have produced inconsistent findings.[22–30] While some studies suggest an association, others report conflicting results, particularly regarding the role of TPOAb and its potential contribution to diabetes risk.[22,23,27,31] These inconsistencies underscore the need for further research to clarify how variations in these markers may relate to diabetes, either as contributing factors or as consequences of the disease.

Part of this inconsistency may stem from differences in the type of diabetes examined across studies. Thyroid autoimmune disorders, such as Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and Graves’ disease, share genetic susceptibility loci and immune mechanisms (e.g., HLA, CTLA-4, and CD40 signaling) with type 1 diabetes, and the two conditions frequently co-occur within the same individuals and families.[32,33] In contrast, the relationship between thyroid function and type 2 diabetes is thought to be driven primarily by shared metabolic rather than autoimmune pathways.[34] This distinction may partly explain why associations between TPOAb positivity and diabetes risk are particularly inconsistent across the literature, as clinic-based studies of patients already diagnosed with diabetes, nationwide twin-registry analyses of autoimmune clustering, and population-based surveys of euthyroid adults differ considerably in the mix of diabetes subtypes and thyroid autoimmune status captured.[12,35] Because the present study relies on the KNHANES definition of DM, which does not distinguish diabetes subtype, its findings are most directly comparable to studies of type 2 diabetes; potential differences in the thyroid-diabetes relationship by diabetes type could not be examined directly and are discussed further as a limitation.

This study aimed to address these gaps by evaluating the relationship between thyroid function indicators – TSH, free T4, and TPOAb levels – and the presence of DM in a large, nationally representative sample of Korean adults. By examining these specific markers, the study explored whether particular thyroid function indicators could serve as predictive markers for DM or help identify individuals at increased risk. In doing so, this research sought to contribute to a clearer understanding of the potential role thyroid function may play in the development and management of DM.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

This study utilized data from the sixth Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES VI), conducted between 2013 and 2015 by the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC). The KNHANES employed a stratified, multi-stage probability sampling design to select 3840 individuals annually from 192 regions across Korea. It comprises a health interview, a health examination, and a nutrition survey. Data were collected through household interviews, and standardized physical examinations were performed at mobile examination centers. Thyroid function tests, including TSH, free T4, and TPOAb measurements, were conducted on a randomly selected subsample of KNHANES participants as part of the standardized health examination protocol. The selection process followed the survey’s probability sampling framework and was not influenced by clinical indicators such as thyroid disease history or symptoms, thereby minimizing potential selection bias. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the KCDC, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. Only individuals aged ≥ 19 years with available data on thyroid function and diabetes status were included. No additional clinical exclusion criteria (e.g., known thyroid disease, pregnancy, or thyroid medication use) were applied beyond these two requirements, consistent with the population-based screening design of KNHANES. Because KNHANES classifies diabetes status by fasting glucose, antidiabetic medication use, and self-reported diagnosis without distinguishing etiological subtype, stratified or subgroup analyses by diabetes type (type 1 vs type 2) were not feasible in the present dataset; this limitation is addressed further in the Discussion. Consequently, 4910 out of 22,948 subjects were eligible for inclusion in this study (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of selecting study subjects from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2013–2015.

2.2. Thyroid function measurements

Serum levels of TSH, free T4, and TPOAb were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) with a Cobas 8000 E-602 analyzer (Roche, Germany) and the corresponding E-TSH, E-Free T4, and E-Anti-TPO kits (Roche, Germany). TSH levels were classified as low (<0.59 mIU/L), normal (0.59–7.03 mIU/L), and high (>7.03 mIU/L). Free T4 levels were categorized as low (<0.92 ng/dL), normal (0.92–1.60 ng/dL), and high (>1.60 ng/dL). TPOAb levels were considered negative if < 34.0 IU/mL and positive if ≥ 34.0 IU/mL.[36] Anti-TSH receptor antibody (TRAb) was not measured in KNHANES VI. Therefore, our assessment of autoimmune thyroid disease was limited to TPOAb, which is considered the most sensitive single marker for autoimmune thyroid disorders in population-based studies.

2.3. Definitions of glucose metabolism status, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL, and hypertension

Serum glucose, triglyceride, and HDL cholesterol levels were measured after a minimum 8-hour overnight fast. A normal glucose level was defined as fasting glucose < 100 mg/dL; impaired fasting glucose (prediabetes) as fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL; and DM as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL.[37] Subjects previously diagnosed with DM and treated with antiglycemic agents and/or insulin, regardless of fasting glucose level, were also classified in the DM group. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a fasting triglyceride level ≥ 200 mg/dL, indicating classification into the high or very high category according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III guidelines.[38] A Low HDL cholesterol level was defined as HDL < 40 mg/dL for men and HDL < 50 mg/dL for women.[38]

Blood pressure (BP) was measured on the right arm of participants who had been seated for at least 5 minutes, using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer. Three measurements were taken at 5-minute intervals, and the average of the second and third readings was used in the analysis. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, or current use of antihypertensive medication regardless of BP.[39]

2.4. Body mass index and waist circumference

Body weight and height were measured to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, respectively, while participants wore light indoor clothing without shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared (kg/m2). BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 was defined as underweight; BMI between ≥ 18.5 kg/m2 and < 25.0 kg/m2 as normal weight, and BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2 as overweight or obese.[40] Waist circumference (WC) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm in a horizontal plane at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lower rib. High WC was defined as ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women, according to the criteria for abdominal obesity set by the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity.[41]

2.5. Hs-CRP measurement

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured using immunoturbidimetry with a Cobas analyzer (Roche, Germany) and a Cardiac C-Reactive Protein High Sensitivity reagent (Roche, Germany). According to the American Heart Association and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, hs-CRP levels were classified as follows: ≥3.0 mg/L as “high risk,” 1.0–2.9 mg/L as “average risk,” and < 1.0 mg/L as “low risk.”[42] In this study, these criteria were used to categorize hs-CRP levels accordingly.

2.6. Serum creatinine measurement

Blood samples were collected from participants following a fasting period of at least 8 hours. Samples were properly processed, immediately refrigerated, and transported under cold storage conditions to the central laboratory within 24 hours. Serum creatinine levels were measured using the Hitachi Automatic Analyzer 7600–210 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

2.7. Other variables

Participants completed a self-reported questionnaire covering age, gender, socioeconomic variables (i.e., household income and educational level), and lifestyle variables (i.e., smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity). Household income was categorized into quartiles based on monthly average family equivalent income: low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high. Educational level was stratified into 4 groups: elementary school graduation or lower, middle school graduation, high school graduation, and college graduation or higher.

Smoking status was classified into 3 groups. nonsmokers were defined as individuals who had never smoked in their lifetime, while past smokers were those who had smoked previously but were not smoking at the time of the survey. Current smokers were defined as those who continued to smoke daily or frequently at the time of the survey. Alcohol consumption was grouped into 4 categories: never, <1 time per month, 1–4 times per month, and ≥ 5 times per month. Physical activity during work, transportation, and leisure time was assessed to evaluate the total time and intensity of physical activity per week. To meet health-related physical activity recommendations, participants were required to engage in at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity physical activity, 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of both, achieving a total of ≥ 600 MET-minutes per week.

2.8. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), applying the survey procedures to account for the complex sampling design and sampling weights of KNHANES in order to generate nationally representative estimates. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Participants included in the analysis were grouped according to TSH, free T4, and TPOAb levels. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age and gender. Age stratification at 65 years was based on conventional epidemiological practice distinguishing working-age from elderly populations. To address residual age-related confounding within strata, age was included as a continuous covariate in adjusted models. The chi-square test was used to summarize categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, while the independent t-test was used to summarize continuous variables as means and standard errors. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between thyroid function (using normal TSH, normal free T4, and negative TPOAb as reference groups) and the presence of DM by age (<65 years and ≥ 65 years) and gender subgroups. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

3. Results

3.1. Study population characteristics

This study analyzed 4910 participants, including 432 individuals with DM and 4478 nondiabetic controls. The prevalence of diabetes was 8.8%, with significant demographic differences between the groups (Table 1). Diabetic participants were older, with a mean age of 53.44 years compared to 39.96 years for non-diabetics (P < .0001). The proportion of individuals aged 65 or older was higher in the diabetic group (18.36%) than in the nondiabetic group (5.37%). Men made up 65.03% of diabetics, compared to 55.05% of non-diabetics (P = .0002).

Socioeconomic and lifestyle factors showed disparities (Table 1). Diabetics were more likely to be in the lowest income quartile (33.69% vs 25.35%, P = .0016) and had lower educational attainment (elementary school or lower: 26.09% vs 8.81%, P < .0001). Physical inactivity was also more common among diabetics (54.20% vs 41.42%, P = .0003).

3.2. Characteristics of thyroid function and metabolic indicators by DM Status

Thyroid function indicators revealed distinct patterns (Table 1). High TSH levels were more frequent among diabetics (13.91% vs 4.99%, P = .0006), while low TSH levels were less common (4.24% vs 12.16%, P = .0006). Mean free T4 levels were slightly lower in diabetics (1.23 ng/dL vs 1.26 ng/dL, P = .0272), with low free T4 levels (<0.92 ng/dL) being more prevalent (4.43% vs 2.15%, P = .0235). TPOAb positivity rates were similar between groups (6.43% vs 7.02%, P = .6723).

Metabolic indicators demonstrated significant differences (Table 1). Diabetics had a higher mean BMI (25.86 kg/m2 vs 23.65 kg/m2, P < .0001), more frequent overweight or obesity (53.14% vs 31.00%, P < .0001), and a greater prevalence of high waist circumference (45.06% vs 20.98%, P < .0001). Hypertension (47.72% vs 16.84%, P < .0001), hypertriglyceridemia (34.51% vs 13.92%, P < .0001), and low HDL cholesterol (51.25% vs 28.49%, P < .0001) were also more common in diabetics. Elevated hs-CRP levels were observed in diabetics (mean: 2.74 mg/L vs 1.10 mg/L, P = .0011).

Age-stratified analysis revealed important differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between younger (<65 years) and older (≥65 years) participants (Table 2). Among participants under 65 years, diabetics showed more pronounced differences in gender distribution, with 67.37% being men compared to 55.27% of non-diabetics (P < .0001). Educational disparities were particularly evident in the younger age group, while these differences were less pronounced among older participants.

3.3. Associations between thyroid function and DM

High TSH levels were strongly linked to diabetes risk (OR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.26–6.32, P < .05) (Table 3). The association remained significant across multiple adjustment models, with the fully adjusted model showing an OR of 7.02 (95% CI: 2.49–19.81). Subgroup analyses revealed that the association was particularly significant in individuals under 65 years (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.48–7.93) and among men (OR = 4.65, 95% CI: 1.60–13.48) (Table 4). Among men under 65 years, high TSH levels were even more strongly associated with diabetes (OR = 5.10, 95% CI: 1.65–15.77). Conversely, low TSH levels appeared to offer a protective effect, with an OR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.15–0.86) in the unadjusted model, though this association weakened in fully adjusted models (Table 3).

4. Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that thyroid dysfunction is significantly associated with diabetes risk, particularly through alterations in serum TSH and free T4 levels. Elevated TSH and reduced free T4 levels were each associated with an increased likelihood of diabetes, with more pronounced effects observed among men under 65 years of age. In contrast, suppressed TSH levels appeared to confer a protective effect. TPOAb positivity, commonly considered a marker of autoimmune thyroid disease, was not significantly associated with diabetes in the overall population, but a potential protective trend was observed among older men. These findings suggest a complex interplay between thyroid hormone regulation, immune function, and glucose metabolism, indicating that thyroid dysfunction may play a more active role in metabolic health than previously recognized.

The observed association between elevated TSH levels and increased diabetes risk is consistent with prior research suggesting that hypothyroidism may impair glucose metabolism through multiple mechanisms.[32,43–47] Elevated TSH levels, particularly in subclinical or overt hypothyroidism, have been linked to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and systemic inflammation—all of which contribute to the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes. Our findings indicate that this relationship is especially strong among younger men, which may be attributable to differences in hormonal milieu, body composition, or health-seeking behavior across sex and age groups. This observation aligns with earlier studies identifying male sex and younger age as modifiers of the metabolic impact of thyroid dysfunction.[32,45,46]

Conversely, the protective association between suppressed TSH levels and diabetes risk may reflect the effects of mild hyperthyroidism or a relatively elevated thyroid hormone state.[43,44,48] Thyroid hormones are known to enhance basal metabolic rate, increase glucose uptake by cells, and improve insulin sensitivity under certain physiological conditions. However, these effects are highly dose-dependent. Excessive thyroid hormone levels can exert catabolic effects and promote oxidative stress, muscle breakdown, and cardiovascular strain, indicating that both hypo- and hyperthyroid states may be metabolically detrimental depending on the context.[32,49,50] The observed protective effect in our study is likely limited to cases of mild or subclinical hyperthyroidism and should be interpreted with caution in clinical settings.

Free T4, the biologically active form of thyroxine, also demonstrated a significant association with diabetes risk. Specifically, reduced free T4 levels were associated with increased odds of diabetes, particularly among younger men. Given free T4’s role in modulating energy homeostasis and glucose metabolism, its deficiency may impair cellular energy production, reduce insulin sensitivity, and promote adipose tissue accumulation.[43,51–53] This finding supports the hypothesis that not only TSH but also circulating thyroid hormone levels are independently relevant to metabolic outcomes. Interestingly, elevated free T4 levels did not show consistent associations with diabetes risk, suggesting a non or U-shaped relationship that warrants further investigation[32,45,54]

The role of thyroid autoimmunity, as measured by TPOAb positivity, was less straightforward. Although TPOAb is a common marker of autoimmune thyroid disease, its presence was not significantly associated with diabetes risk in the overall population. However, in subgroup analyses, older men with TPOAb positivity appeared to have a reduced risk of diabetes, suggesting a potential protective effect in specific populations.[43,52,55,56] This counterintuitive finding may be influenced by several factors, including differences in immune response, duration of autoimmune disease, or coexisting hormonal adaptations. Prior studies have reported mixed results regarding the metabolic implications of thyroid autoimmunity, with some suggesting increased diabetes risk and others showing no association – or even a protective effect – depending on the population studied.[43,49,50]

The ambiguous role of TPOAb observed here should be interpreted in light of the specific type of diabetes under study. Because thyroid autoimmunity shares genetic and immunological pathways with type 1 diabetes, studies enriched for type 1 diabetes or conducted in clinical thyroid-autoimmunity cohorts have generally reported stronger, more consistent associations between TPOAb positivity and diabetes than population-based studies such as ours, which predominantly capture type 2 diabetes.[32,33] For example, a genome-wide association study of patients with co-occurring autoimmune thyroiditis and type 1 diabetes identified shared susceptibility loci (including GPR103, PTPN22, and HLA-region variants) and immune pathways such as CD40 and CTLA-4 signaling underlying this specific comorbidity, a mechanism not expected to operate in isolated type 2 diabetes.[33] Similarly, a population-based twin study found substantial shared genetic liability between type 1 diabetes and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, further supporting an autoimmune-specific link that would not be captured in a general adult population screened for type 2 diabetes.[34] Clinic-based studies of diabetic patients have similarly reported that thyroid dysfunction, including autoimmune subtypes, is common among individuals already diagnosed with diabetes and is associated with poorer glycemic and cardiovascular outcomes, although such clinic-based samples differ substantially from the nationally representative, population-based sample used here in both selection and disease severity.[12,32] Notably, a study of Korean adolescents using the same national survey platform reported sex-specific associations between thyroid function and insulin resistance indices even among euthyroid, nondiabetic youth, suggesting that the thyroid-glucose relationship observed in our adult cohort may have antecedents earlier in life and may be similarly modified by sex across the lifespan.[35] Taken together, these comparisons suggest that differences in diabetes subtype, autoimmune status, and study population (clinic-based vs population-based) are important contributors to the heterogeneity of findings across the thyroid-diabetes literature and should be considered when interpreting and generalizing the present results.

Taken together, these findings carry several important clinical implications. First, assessment of thyroid function – including TSH and free T4 – may serve as a valuable tool for identifying individuals at increased risk of diabetes, particularly among younger men who may not yet present traditional risk factors such as obesity or family history.[32,44,46,51] Given the growing global burden of both thyroid disorders and diabetes, incorporating thyroid screening into routine metabolic evaluations could enhance early detection and intervention efforts.

Second, the potential protective association of suppressed TSH levels with diabetes suggests that the traditional reference range for thyroid function may warrant reevaluation in the context of metabolic health. While overt hyperthyroidism remains a clinical concern, mild increases in thyroid hormone activity may exert favorable effects on glucose metabolism in select patients.[32,48,52] Third, the ambiguous role of thyroid autoimmunity in diabetes risk underscores the need for individualized risk assessment. Although TPOAb positivity alone may not reliably predict diabetes risk, its interaction with age, sex, and comorbid conditions may support more personalized care strategies.[43,52,55,56] Clinicians should consider the full thyroid profile – including antibody status – when evaluating patients with metabolic disorders, particularly those belonging to higher-risk subgroups.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; it remains unclear whether thyroid dysfunction precedes the development of diabetes or arises as a consequence of altered metabolism. Longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the temporal direction of these associations. Second, thyroid and metabolic markers were measured at a single time point, which may not fully reflect their dynamic interactions over time. Third, the study lacked data on other relevant thyroid hormones, such as T3 and TRAb, as well as on medication use, dietary intake, and physical activity – all of which could influence both thyroid function and diabetes risk. Information on thyroid hormone replacement therapy (e.g., levothyroxine) was not available in the dataset, which may have affected thyroid function parameters, particularly in elderly participants. Dietary iodine intake, which can significantly influence thyroid function in iodine-replete populations such as Korea, was not assessed. While Korea is generally iodine-sufficient, individual variations in seaweed consumption and other iodine-rich foods could not be controlled. Our assessment of autoimmune thyroid disease was limited to TPOAb, as TRAb measurements were not available; this limits comprehensive evaluation of conditions such as Graves’ disease. Fourth, while we stratified analyses by age (<65 vs ≥65 years) and adjusted for continuous age in all models, more granular age stratification could potentially reveal additional age-specific patterns, though at the cost of reduced statistical power in smaller subgroups. Relatedly, because KNHANES defines diabetes status without distinguishing type 1 from type 2 diabetes, we were unable to stratify or separately examine the thyroid-diabetes association by diabetes type. Given that thyroid autoimmunity shares genetic and mechanistic links with type 1 diabetes that are not expected to operate in type 2 diabetes, this is a meaningful limitation; however, since the DM group in this cohort predominantly comprises older, overweight adults consistent with type 2 diabetes, our findings should be interpreted primarily in that context, and future studies with diabetes subtype classification are needed to clarify whether the associations reported here differ by diabetes type. The fifth limitation is the possibility of residual confounding, despite extensive covariate adjustment. Factors such as socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and comorbid conditions may differentially affect thyroid function and metabolic health, and not all of these variables could be fully controlled. Finally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited to populations with similar demographic and clinical characteristics; replication in other ethnic and geographic cohorts is needed to confirm the observed associations.

Nevertheless, this study possesses several notable strengths. The use of a large-scale, population-based dataset enhances both statistical power and external validity. Careful adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical covariates strengthens the internal validity of the findings. In particular, the inclusion of subgroup analyses by age and sex provides valuable insight into high-risk populations, revealing patterns that may not be evident in analyses of the overall sample.

Future research should address these limitations by employing prospective cohort designs to elucidate causal pathways and clarify the temporal relationship between thyroid dysfunction and diabetes. In addition to monitoring thyroid function and glucose metabolism over time, future studies should incorporate mechanistic investigations – such as analyses of hormone receptor signaling, genetic susceptibility, and inflammatory markers – to improve understanding of the biological underpinnings of the observed associations.[43,44,50,51] Moreover, examining the interaction between thyroid function and modifiable lifestyle factors – such as diet, physical activity, smoking, and psychological stress – may offer a more holistic view of metabolic risk.[32,45,47,55] It may also be valuable to explore whether normalization of thyroid function – through pharmacological or lifestyle interventions – can improve glycemic outcomes. The integration of novel technologies, including continuous glucose monitoring, thyroid imaging, and -omics approaches, could enhance the precision of future analyses and inform the development of personalized strategies for prevention and treatment.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that thyroid dysfunction – specifically elevated TSH and reduced free T4 levels – is significantly associated with increased diabetes risk, particularly among younger men. While TPOAb positivity showed no overall association, a potential protective effect in specific subgroups warrants further investigation. These findings suggest that thyroid function assessment may assist in identifying individuals at higher metabolic risk and reinforce the need for personalized strategies in diabetes prevention and management.
