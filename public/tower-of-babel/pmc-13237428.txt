T cells are elevated in abdominal and femoral adipose tissue in females with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Kerri Z Delaney, Muhammad Ilyas Nadeem, Claire Plissonneau, Natalie Khor, Jacob Delaney, José A Morais, Michael A Tsoukas, Radu Pescarus, Pierre Y Garneau, Sylvia Santosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipose tissue (AT) immune cells are implicated in the pathology of type 2 diabetes (T2D), and their content likely differs between abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (abSAT), femoral SAT (fmSAT), and visceral adipose tissue (VAT). Therefore, the objective of the current study was to develop an understanding of how immune cell presence differs between abSAT, fmSAT, and VAT depots in females who are lean (NO), have obesity (OB), or have OB + T2D. Premenopausal females were recruited from the community (NO, n = 14) or bariatric surgery clinics (OB, n = 10; OB + T2D, n = 8) in Montreal, Quebec, Canada. abSAT and fmSAT were obtained by needle aspiration biopsy in all participants, while VAT was excised from the OB and OB + T2D groups during surgery. AT immune cells were isolated from AT biopsies and stained for quantification via flow cytometry. OB + T2D fmSAT had a greater number of T cells (CD3+) than the NO group, with no difference between the NO and OB groups. We further found that abSAT CD3+, CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+, and CD68+CD206+ cells were greater in the OB + T2D group, followed by the OB group and then NO participants. There were no regional differences in T cells, macrophages, natural killer cells, or B cells between individuals with OB and OB + T2D. Our findings indicate that the importance of fmSAT and abSAT T cells in T2D pathogenesis should be further investigated.Keywords: regulatory T cells, macrophages, human adipose tissue, type 2 diabetes, female

Obesity is the leading risk factor for developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1]. Therefore, examining and identifying physiological changes that occur in obesity that contribute to the development and progression of T2D would provide essential knowledge to prevent T2D progression. Not only does an overall increase in adiposity affect the risk of developing T2D, but also where excess adipose tissue is stored further mitigates T2D risk. Though adipose tissue can be found in several regions, the 3 main adipose tissue depots in the human body are abdominal and femoral subcutaneous adipose tissue (abSAT and fmSAT, respectively) and visceral adipose tissue (VAT). In obesity, adipose tissue located centrally is believed to be more detrimental to health, as central adiposity has a greater association with insulin resistance than adipose tissue located peripherally, which is often considered protective [2, 3]. As each region has a different association with T2D risk, it is conceivable that the cellular characteristics of each depot also differ.

Inflammation appears to play a major role in the pathophysiology of T2D [4]. Immune cells, including T cells [5], adipose tissue macrophages (ATM) [6], natural killer (NK) cells [7], and B cells [8], secrete cytokines that contribute toward inflammation and have been found within obese adipose tissue. The 2 main subtypes of T cells in adipose tissue are T helper cells and cytotoxic T cells. Within adipose tissue, T cells function by secreting an array of inflammatory cytokines, which act locally to kill targeted cells and polarize ATMs [5]. ATMs exist on a continuum of polarizations, with M1-like macrophage phenotypes being proinflammatory and M2-like macrophage phenotypes being anti-inflammatory. However, ATMs can express both M1-like and M2-like characteristics at the same time. ATMs function to clear dead and necrotic adipocytes while also secreting an array of cytokines. NK cells also regulate ATMs through the secretion of cytokines [9]. Within adipose tissue, B cells appear to modulate inflammation through activating T cells and macrophages, producing antibodies, and secreting cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 [10, 11].

The role of adipose tissue immune cells in metabolic disease risk remains to be elucidated. A first step toward understanding the importance of these cells in metabolic pathology is to characterize their presence within adipose tissue and to understand how immune cell profiles shift under different metabolic conditions. Thus, the objective of this study is to determine how regional adipose tissue immune cell profiles differ between individuals who are lean (NO) and healthy, have obesity (OB) but do not have T2D, and have OB and T2D (OB + T2D).

Methods

Participants

Participants were nonsmoking, premenopausal females who were not pregnant or lactating. All participants were weight stable (±2 kg) within the 3 months before testing. Weight stability was ensured by cross-referencing intake weights to the bariatric surgery clinic with intake weights in our study. Individuals living with obesity (n = 10) or obesity and T2D (n = 8) were recruited from the bariatric surgery clinic at Hôpital du Sacré-Cœur de Montreal, Montreal, Quebec, Canada. Participants with T2D were included if they were taking Metformin and/or Invokana. No other antidiabetic medications were permitted. Individuals without diagnosed metabolic conditions, with the exception of some participants with overweight body mass index (BMI; recruitment BMI < 27.0 kg/m2), were recruited from Montreal, Quebec, Canada, through poster advertising (n = 14).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Comité d’ethique de la recherche du CIUSSS du Nord-de-l’Ile-de-Montreal, Hôpital du Sacré-Coeur de Montreal and Comité central d’éthique de la recherche du ministre de la Santé et des Services sociaux. All participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation.

Protocol

All study visits were conducted at the School of Health at Concordia University. Participants arrived after fasting for at least 8 hours. They underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and computed tomography (CT) scans to measure body composition. Adipose tissue biopsies were taken from the abSAT and fmSAT regions by needle aspiration. VAT biopsies were taken from the greater omentum (OB and OB + T2D only) via excision at the start of the participant's bariatric surgery.

Body composition

Total and regional subcutaneous adipose tissue body composition was quantified using DXA (Lunar Prodigy Advance; GE Healthcare; Madison, WI, USA) with Encore software (version 14.10; GE Healthcare; Madison, WI, USA). A single slice (10 mm) CT scan was obtained at L2 and L3 (Revolution Evo, GE Medical System, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with automated body composition analysis using CT image segmentation software, as previously described [12]. CT scans were combined with DXA scans to quantify abSAT and VAT, as previously described [13].

Adipose tissue biopsies

Approximately 1 to 2 g of abSAT and fmSAT were obtained from OB and OB + T2D groups, whereas 0.5 to 1 g of adipose tissue was obtained from NO participants. Biopsies were obtained by needle aspiration, as previously described [14]. Briefly, a lidocaine/ringer lactate solution was superficially injected into the biopsy sites. Adipose tissue was then aspirated with a 9-gauge trieye needle. Fasted subcutaneous adipose tissue biopsies were collected 1 to 3 months before bariatric surgery, prior to the presurgical weight loss regimen. Approximately 1 to 2 g of the greater omentum (VAT) was excised at the start of each bariatric surgery in OB and OB + T2D participants. VAT from NO patients was not collected since NO participants were not recruited from a surgical population.

Adipose tissue analyses

Briefly, adipose tissue samples were digested in a digestion buffer solution containing collagenase II (Sigma Aldrich, cat no. 10103578001). After digestion, the sample was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The stromovascular fraction was then isolated, and flow cytometry was used to quantify immune cells as previously described [14]. Due to the difficulties of obtaining adipose tissue from NO participants, antibody staining was done on 1 panel (Table 1, Panel 3) [15]. A greater amount of adipose tissue was obtained from OB and OB + T2D groups, which allowed for antibody staining on all 3 panels (Table 1) [15].

Statistics

Statistics were run using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.0.0 (Armonk, NY, USA), and data are presented as mean ± SEM. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilks test, and data that were not normally distributed were logarithmically transformed to be normally distributed. Outliers were treated according to Aguinis et al [16] and were logarithmically transformed to achieve a normal distribution. Univariate ANOVAs with a Tukey post hoc test were used to compare adipose tissue regions between NO, OB, and OB + T2D groups. When only the OB and OB + T2D groups were sampled, independent samples t tests were used to compare each adipose tissue region. A P-value ≤ .05 was considered statistically significant.

Between-group differences in abSAT immune cells

In abSAT, we observed that collectively, T cells (CD3+ cells/g initial tissue) were 10.5 and 9.3 times greater (P < .01, Cohen's d = 2.50 and P < .01, Cohen's d = 2.24, respectively) in OB + T2D and OB groups, respectively, compared to NO participants (Fig. 1). CD3+CD4+ (T helper) and CD3+CD8+ (cytotoxic T) cell composition followed a similar pattern and were also greatest in the OB + T2D and lowest in the NO groups (P < .001, Cohen's d = 2.50 and P = .01, Cohen's d = 2.02, respectively). When looking specifically at CD3+CD4+CD45RA+ (naïve T helper cells, cells/g initial tissue), we noted that they were approximately 40 times greater (P = .04, Cohen's d = 1.45) in the OB + T2D than the NO group. There were no differences in CD3+CD4+CD45RA+ (cells/g initial tissue) between OB and NO groups (P = .36, Cohen's d = 1.88) (Fig. 1).

Between-group differences in fmSAT immune cells

In fmSAT, CD3+ cells/g adipose tissue were approximately 3 times greater in the OB + T2D group compared with the NO group (P = .03, Cohen's d = 1.15) (Fig. 1). There was no difference in the number of CD3+ cells/g adipose tissue between the NO and OB groups (P = .61, Cohen's d = 0.40). A trend (P = .06) occurred when examining the differences in CD3+CD4+ cells/g adipose tissue between the 3 groups, where CD3+CD4+ cells/g adipose tissue were greater (P = .06, Cohen's d = 1.00) in the OB + T2D than in the NO group (Fig. 1). CD3+CD4+ cells/g adipose tissue were not different between the NO and OB groups (P = .43, Cohen's d = 0.50). There were no differences in any macrophage markers in fmSAT between the NO, OB, and OB + T2D groups CD68+ (NO and OB: P = .61, Cohen's d = 0.43; NO and OB + T2D: P = .99, Cohen's d = −0.08; and OB and OB + T2D: P = .59, Cohen's d = 0.43, respectively) and CD68 + CD206+ (NO and OB: P = .24, Cohen's d = 0.71; NO and OB + T2D: P = .57, Cohen's d = 0.41; and OB and OB + T2D: P = .87, Cohen's d = 0.29) (Fig. 2). Similarly, there were no differences in CD16+ (P = .88, Cohen's d = 0.67), CD56+ (P = .98, Cohen's d = 0.13) (NK cells), CD19+ (P = .853, Cohen's d = 0.10) (B cells), or T-cell receptor Vα24-Jα18+ (P = .55, Cohen's d = 0.33 (iNKT cells) between the OB and OB + T2D groups (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The results from this study provide an important first step toward understanding how, in females, regional adipose tissue immune cell profiles differ by region in a “normal” state compared with varying states of metabolic disease (OB and OB + T2D). We show for the first time that T cell numbers (CD3+, CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD4+CD45RA+, CD3+CD8+) are at least 3 times greater in those with OB + T2D than in females who were NO. Moreover, we found that only females with T2D had elevated T cells (CD3+), particularly T helper (CD3+, CD3+CD4+), cells in fmSAT. Interestingly, though M2-like ATM (CD68+CD206+) was higher than NO groups in both groups with obesity, ATM (CD68+) was only elevated in females with obesity and not in those with T2D.

The abundance of T cells found in abSAT in OB and OB + T2D groups is particularly interesting, as T cells, especially T helper cells, have been implicated in the development of T2D. In mice, in vivo depletion models found that reducing CD3+CD4+ T helper cells improved glucose tolerance [17]. In humans, individuals with OB + T2D have a greater absolute number of peripheral leukocytes, mainly due to increased T helper cells (CD3+CD4+) than individuals with OB [18, 19]. While these results are at the whole-body level, we found 1 study that examined abSAT T cells. Similar to our findings, Fabbrini et al observed that abSAT from OB + T2D participants had 3 and 7 times greater T helper cell counts than OB and NO participants, respectively [20]. These T helper cells secrete IL-17 and IL-22, cytokines, which are implicated in glucose homeostasis. IL-17 secreted from adipose tissue T helper cells negatively impacts glucose homeostasis by suppressing adipocyte differentiation, inhibiting the expression of genes coding for glucose metabolism, and reducing adipocyte glucose uptake [21]. In adipose tissue, IL-22 secreted from T helper cells has been shown to impair glucose metabolism by amplifying IL-1ß release [22]. While secretions were not measured in our study, these findings point to a need for future work to examine not only the presence of adipose tissue immune cells but also their function to assess causality.

Though previous studies have focused on investigating T cell concentrations in abdominal fat depots, our study is the first to examine how T cell profiles vary under different metabolic conditions in the fmSAT depot. In doing so, we discovered that in females, the pan T cell marker (CD3+) was significantly greater in fmSAT of those with T2D. This elevation in T cell numbers is likely primarily driven by greater T helper cells (CD3+CD4+) observed in this region. Our previous work suggested that femoral T cell infiltration may be an early event during weight accumulation [23]. Currently, fmSAT from individuals of various metabolic states remains greatly understudied, and compelling evidence suggests that T helper cells may be associated with fmSAT dysfunction. Future research is needed to further elucidate the role of fmSAT in T2D pathogenesis.

Past studies have suggested ATMs are at the forefront of T2D pathogenesis [24-30]. ATMs surround dying adipocytes and secrete a myriad of cytokines that propagate the inflammatory response. ATM-derived cytokines also activate proinflammatory kinases, such as c-Jun N-terminal kinase, which inhibit insulin action [31]. Our findings that CD68+ and CD68+CD206+ cells do not differ between OB and OB + T2D participants in all 3 adipose tissue depots contrast these ideas. Instead, our results align with those of Espinosa et al [32], who not only find no difference between OB and OB + T2D abSAT and fmSAT ATMs but also find that a 10% weight loss improved adipose tissue insulin sensitivity and did not reduce ATM content [32]. Additional work has found that ATMs do not predict homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance or systemic inflammation, nor does ATM not correlate with insulin-stimulated glucose uptake [26, 29].

Based on our results, we propose that differences in ATM occur when comparing populations who are living with obesity to those who are lean. Here, we find that abSAT CD68+ cells are evaluated in OB compared with NO participants and that CD68+CD206+ cells are elevated in both OB and OB + T2D participants compared with the NO group. Therefore, ATMs may peak with the accumulation of adipose tissue but do not appear to be involved in the development of T2D.

These cumulative findings provide evidence that potentially contradicts a past body of literature, which suggests ATMs are involved with obesity-induced insulin resistance. Although our findings are based on a small sample size and, therefore, should be interpreted with caution, there remain several limitations to the past body of literature that has found ATM is associated with T2D. Of primary concern is that these studies are largely conducted in rodents; therefore, the human relevance must be interpreted with caution. We found 1 coculture study in humans where CD11c+ conditioned media was exposed to adipocytes, and a decrease in glucose uptake was recorded [27]. However, the in vivo relevance of such a hyperconcentrated situation remains to be determined. Methodological limitations exist as well. The quantification of adipose tissue immune cells is best done through flow cytometry, which directly quantifies immune cell numbers. Several studies that find a relationship between ATM and T2D use PCR, which indirectly measures immune cell content [28-30]. Moreover, how ATM data is present alters the association with insulin resistance [32]. When ATM data are presented as per 100 adipocytes, there are more positive associations with insulin resistance [25-27] than when data are presented as per gram of adipose tissue [26, 32]. However, caution must be taken when presenting data as per 100 adipocytes, as this methodology is confounded by adipocyte size and body composition [26].

This is also the first study to examine NK cells (CD16+, CD56+) in the context of T2D in humans. Rodent models have previously found that CD56+ depletion was associated with a modest improvement in insulin sensitivity [33]. Thus, we hypothesized that adipose tissue from the T2D group would have a greater number of NK cells than the OB group. However, we find no difference in abSAT, fmSAT, or VAT CD16+ and CD56+ cells between individuals with OB and OB + T2D.

iNKT+-deficient mice on a regular diet displayed signs of insulin resistance without signs of adipose tissue inflammation, indicating that iNKT+ cells may be protective against the development of insulin resistance [34]. Although individuals with OB + T2D had a greater number of iNKT cells than OB participants in all 3 groups, these differences did not reach a level of significance. These results indicate that further studies on iNKT+ cells in human adipose tissue are required.

Animal studies suggest that B cells (CD19+) have a role in glucose homeostasis; however, the role of adipose tissue B cells in humans remains unclear. Murine studies consistently show that CD19+ cell depletion during obesity is associated with improved glucose tolerance, primarily through T-cell regulation [35-37]. However, we observed no differences in CD19+ cells between OB and OB + T2D groups in any region. Our results are aligned with the only other human study that could be found, which shows that there were no differences in circulating B cells (CD19+CD38+ cells) between OB and OB + T2D groups [18]. The regulation of adipose tissue T cells may be more significant to T2D pathogenesis than B cells. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and some limitations. A major strength of the study is the use of a reliable and reproducible gold standard flow cytometry protocol for the direct quantification of adipose tissue immune cells [14]. Unfortunately, a limitation of our study is that adipose tissue immune cell function was not assessed. Regardless of whether there is a difference in the quantity of immune cells, it is possible that the secretory profiles of these immune cells are altered in T2D. Understanding differences in secretory function would provide greater metabolic insight than merely quantifying differences in the absolute numbers of immune cells; therefore, conclusions about causation should not be drawn from our analyses. However, in this study, we sought to first quantify the immune cells in the main adipose tissue depots to determine how they varied with metabolic disease. By using flow cytometry, a wide array of adipose tissue immune cells can be examined, including immune cells that are rarely examined. Future studies should build on these results to determine whether there are differences in adipose tissue immune cell function and whether structural differences in immune cell localization in crown-like, vascular, and nerve structures occur in adipose tissue samples. The unique ascertainment of immune cells in lean participants provided an important reference point for the quantities of these cells in healthy tissue. Due to practical limitations, we did not have enough tissue to quantify certain immune cells and could not collect VAT from lean individuals. We did have enough tissue for a more comprehensive quantification of regional immune cells in the OB and OB + T2D groups, including from VAT. This quantification was the first of its kind to allow for the identification of cells of higher potential relevance to T2D. Our study only consists of 32 individuals. The challenges of recruiting a sample population with such strict inclusion/exclusion criteria limited our overall sample size. However, while our sample size might have been small, there were strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in more uniform characteristics between groups. When groups are well matched, the lower variability means greater power to detect differences where differences exist. Lastly, the known anti-inflammatory effects of the diabetic medications included in this study should be noted as potentially mitigating the differences we observed, mainly between the OB and OB + T2D groups. Overall, this study provides valuable insight into our understanding of how an array of immune cells differ between NO, OB, and OB + T2D individuals.

Conclusion

Our study indicates an association between fmSAT immune cells in T2D pathogenesis. Furthermore, fmSAT T cells may be more strongly linked with T2D than ATM or other lymphocytes. Future studies should investigate the role of T cells in T2D pathogenesis to identify a direct causal link to T2D. As the present study included only females and is limited by a small sample size, the immune cell profiles of regional adipose tissue in T2D in females and males should be further investigated.
