Transition experiences of female youth leaving youth shelters: a descriptive phenomenological study
Authors: Goun Kim, Eunyeong Kim, Jinyoung Park, Dabok Noh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouths living in alternative care facilities in Korea must leave the facilities by the age of 18 or 25 years at the latest. They often face significant financial and psychological difficulties in securing stable housing and employment as they transition from out-of-home care to independence. This study aimed to explore the transition experiences of female youth after leaving youth shelters.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological approach was used, and in-depth interviews were conducted with 10 female youth who had left youth shelters. The analysis yielded 18 formulated meanings, six theme clusters, and three overarching themes.ResultsParticipants experienced significant financial and psychological challenges after leaving the shelters. Over time, however, they gradually overcame these difficulties by actively seeking support from organizations, accepting their circumstances, and developing improved impulse control and self-care. Ultimately, they expressed aspirations for a stable life, a normal family, and personal growth.ConclusionsThe findings suggest the need for targeted programs focused on vocational training, financial education, and phased settlement allowances, with an emphasis on job placement services and financial literacy. Transitional housing should be located near previous shelters to preserve support networks. Gender-responsive policies are needed to address safety concerns by improving access to secure living environments. Aftercare services, including case management, mentoring, and long-term psychological support, are essential to address ongoing needs and past traumas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04536-6.Keywords: Independence, Out-of-home care, Female youth, Youth shelters, Descriptive phenomenology

Background

Youth shelters are a key component of South Korea’s youth welfare system, providing protection and support for adolescents who cannot safely remain at home due to family conflict, abuse, or other crises. As of March 2025, 137 youth shelters were operating nationwide [1]. The most recent available administrative data indicate that between 20,401 and 32,402 adolescents were admitted to youth shelters annually between 2019 and 2022 [2, 3]. These figures illustrate the sustained demand for youth shelters and highlight their important role in supporting adolescents in crisis.

Youths residing in youth shelters represent one of the most vulnerable groups among adolescents in out-of-home care, showing higher levels of mental health problems and lower preparedness for independence compared to those in group homes, foster care, or childcare facilities [4, 5]. This heightened vulnerability is largely due to the short-term, crisis-driven nature of youth shelters [6, 7]. Many of these youths typically enter shelters after acute crises, such as family conflict or disintegration, leading to short-term stays and limited opportunities for support, which can undermine both emotional stability and readiness for independent living [5]. In contrast, youths in group homes, foster care, or childcare facilities typically enter care at a younger age and benefit from stable, long-term environments that provide consistent support and opportunities to develop life skills for independence [8, 9].

Although the Korean government provides youths leaving out-of-home care with support such as housing assistance, settlement allowances, monthly independence allowances, and counseling services [10], concerns regarding the challenges faced by this population have intensified. Recent statistics in South Korea showed that 4.5% experienced precarious housing situations—including tiny single-room accommodation; non-residential multi-use facilities; spaces in workplaces, motels, or inns; and street homelessness—a rate five times higher than that of the general youth population [11]. Alarmingly, 18.3% of these youths have seriously contemplated suicide, and nearly half (46.5%) have considered it at least once in their lifetime [11]. Additionally, 10.6% experienced social withdrawal, indicating severe risks of isolation [11].

Despite differences in national welfare systems, an international systematic review suggests that care leavers experience disproportionately adverse outcomes across various contexts [12]. Prior research, primarily focused on Western contexts, shows that care leavers face elevated risks of unemployment and housing instability and are two to four times more likely to experience mental health disorders compared to youths in the general population [13]. Furthermore, a previous study using longitudinal data from England and Wales highlighted the long-term risks, reporting that adults with a history of out-of-home care have a 1.62-fold higher mortality rate, primarily due to suicide, accidents, or mental health issues [14].

When youths in out-of-home care turn 18—or 25 at the latest—they must leave their care facilities and transition to independence [15]. This transition period coincides with ‘emerging adulthood,’ a critical developmental shift from adolescence to young adulthood [16]. This period is characterized by major transitions in education, employment, and social relationships, often accompanied by instability and uncertainty as young people explore their identity and navigate new roles and responsibilities [16, 17]. While such instability is normative during the late teens and twenties, youth leaving out-of-home care often face these developmental tasks with fewer family-based safety nets and less time to prepare, intensifying both practical burdens and psychological distress during early independent living [18]. Specifically, they often struggle to secure stable employment and housing and to meet basic living expenses such as rent, utilities, and groceries, which compounds their psychological distress [19, 20]. Furthermore, the transition is frequently accompanied by loneliness, anxiety, and lingering feelings of resentment or hurt related to their families of origin [19–22].

Beyond these age-related developmental struggles, gender has been recognized as an important factor shaping the transition experiences of youth leaving out-of-home care. Previous studies have reported gender differences in problem behaviors and resilience among vulnerable adolescents and youths transitioning from out-of-home care [23, 24]. In addition, research focusing on girls leaving out-of-home care highlights that young women may face distinct challenges during the transition to independent living [25]. Recent research suggests that young women leaving care may face increased vulnerability to safety concerns, gender-based violence, and a higher likelihood of early pregnancy [26]. Despite these gender-related concerns, female care leavers have received relatively limited empirical attention in the leaving-care literature [18]. Therefore, a more in-depth understanding of female youths’ transition experiences is needed.

Although substantial research exists on youths leaving group homes [20, 27, 28], foster care [22, 29], and childcare facilities [19, 30], there is a notable lack of studies examining the experiences of youths after leaving shelters. This gap can be attributed to several reasons. First, youth shelters provide short-term care [5], making it difficult to research youths transitioning out of these shelters. Additionally, youths who enter shelters after running away from home are often experiencing acute crises—such as family conflict or violence [4, 5]—which adds complexity and variability that make research more challenging. Furthermore, youth shelters were institutionalized relatively later than other out-of-home care systems to address increasingly complex issues such as runaway situations and family conflict. By focusing on this underrepresented group, this study fills a critical gap in the literature and provides insights to inform future research and practice, specially exploring the experiences of female youths transitioning to independence after leaving youth shelters.

Methods

Design

This study used the descriptive phenomenological approach of Colaizzi [31] to understand and describe the meaning and essence of the transition experiences of female youths after leaving youth shelters and moving toward independence. Colaizzi’s method was chosen for its systematic approach to identifying common themes across participants’ experiences, making it well-suited for understanding the collective experiences of youths. Unlike Giorgi’s method, which emphasizes individual uniqueness by providing detailed situational descriptions, or Van Kaam’s method, which involves quantitative features such as tracking the frequency of meaningful statements, Colaizzi’s approach stays closer to the participants’ language, ensuring a deeper and more accurate reflection of their experiences [32].

Sampling and recruitment

The participants were ten female youths aged 18–24, living independently after leaving youth shelters in Seongnam City, Korea. The criteria for selecting participants were as follows. First, participants who had experienced at least 3 months of independent living after leaving youth shelters were recruited. The minimum period of independent living was informed by previous research on youth leaving care that explored the transition experiences of young people leaving out-of-home care [33]. Second, participants aged 18 to 24 were recruited to capture the transition from adolescence to adulthood. This study focused on the experiences of individuals transitioning from out-of-home care to independence and from adolescence to young adulthood. Third, only female participants were recruited to explore gender-specific experiences from their own perspectives.

A purposive sampling method was used to select informants who had transition experiences from out-of-home care to independence after leaving youth shelters and were willing to share their experiences. Since shelter staff were more familiar with potential participants, a researcher requested recommendations for individuals who could express their experiences. To minimize selection bias, the researcher asked the shelter staff to avoid recommending participants based on specific experiences, perspectives, or convenience. Instead, they were asked to consider a diverse range of individuals in terms of age, occupation, education, duration of independent living, and current residence type. Although shelter staff suggested participants, these individuals were no longer under the authority or power of the shelter, as they had already left shelters. Additionally, ethical safeguards were in place to ensure participant autonomy. Shelter staff were asked not to exert pressure or influence on potential participants to be included in the study.

Before the researcher contacted the recommended individuals, shelter staff first asked potential participants whether they consented to being contacted by the research team. Contact information was shared with the researcher only for individuals who consented. The researcher then contacted the recommended individuals directly, explained the study, and arranged meetings with those who voluntarily agreed. To further mitigate potential bias, the researcher cross-checked participant characteristics, ensuring that no particular demographic was over- or underrepresented. Importantly, participants were recruited based on their independence, not solely those with positive experiences during their journey to independence.

Data collection

Individual in-depth interviews were conducted with each participating female youth between December 2022 and May 2023, lasting 60–90 min. The interviews took place in a quiet space to ensure privacy and comfort for participants and the interviewer.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by two researchers to explore participants’ experiences of transitioning to independence after leaving youth shelters. Each interview began with a broad open-ended question, such as: “Tell me about the process of becoming independent after leaving the youth shelter.” Additional open-ended questions were used to explore participants’ experiences in greater depth, including their experiences after leaving the shelter, challenges encountered during the transition to independent living, thoughts and feelings related to these challenges, coping strategies, sources of support, and reflections on their journey toward independence. Clarifying and probing questions were asked as needed to further understand participants’ experiences. The semi-structured interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

All interviews were transcribed after data collection, and the two researchers independently analyzed the transcribed data. When discrepancies between the researchers’ analyses arose, the causes, such as differences in interpretation and data ambiguity, were examined, and mutual discussions were held. Throughout these discussions, the researchers shared their perspectives and collaborated to reach a consensus to ensure consistent interpretation. After resolving any discrepancies, the data was re-examined, and analyses were adjusted as needed to preserve the context and original meaning of the data. Furthermore, discussions with other researchers offered additional perspectives on the data, and any changes in interpretation were thoroughly documented. Two participants reviewed the results and provided feedback on whether the interpretations aligned with their experiences. These procedures effectively addressed analysis discrepancies and ensured the reliability of the research findings.

Interviews were conducted until no new information was generated. We determined data saturation through ongoing thematic coding and discussions with qualitative researchers and the shelter director. Saturation was reached when no new themes emerged, and the content became repetitive across interviews [34]. For example, by the eighth and ninth interviews, participants repeatedly described similar financial and psychological challenges they faced after leaving youth shelters. Additionally, their efforts to overcome these challenges became increasingly redundant. By the tenth interview, expressions of hope for the future mirrored those of earlier participants. At this point, no new themes or perspectives emerged, confirming that further data collection would not yield additional insights.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the shelter directors and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Eulji University (No. EU-22-81). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Potential participants were approached with an information sheet clearly explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, expected benefits, risks, anonymity, confidentiality, the option to withdraw consent, audio recording, and compensation. The researcher avoided using technical jargon when explaining the study to participants and employed simple language to facilitate understanding. After providing sufficient information, written consent was obtained, allowing participants time to consider their decision without pressure. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Given that participants had experienced adversity, trauma-sensitive interviewing techniques were employed to minimize emotional distress. The researcher established a supportive and non-judgmental environment by using open-ended questions, avoiding leading or intrusive inquiries, and allowing participants to share their experiences at their own pace. If a participant showed signs of distress, the researcher paused the interview, offered reassurance, and allowed them to decide whether to continue or take a break. Additionally, participants were informed in advance that they could decline to answer any question without explanation. If participants experienced emotional discomfort or fatigue from recalling difficult experiences, they could stop the interview immediately without any negative consequences, and the researcher would provide immediate individual support and counseling as needed. Participants could verbally withdraw their consent at any time during the interview, even after it had started, without facing any negative consequences.

Participants received a gift card of approximately $33 as a token of appreciation for their time spent in individual interviews. The amount was carefully considered based on the level of participation and duration of the interviews and was approved by the IRB. The gift card was provided immediately upon consent, allowing participants to receive it even if they chose to withdraw their consent during the process, and the compensation was not contingent upon completing the interview. The collected data were kept anonymous, stored in encrypted files for security, and accessible only to the researchers. Upon completion of the study, the data will be retained for 3 years. After this period, all related data would be permanently and irretrievably deleted and destroyed.

Data analysis

This study used Colaizzi’s [31] phenomenological research methods for analysis. Colaizzi’s method consists of seven steps for data analysis. Firstly, the interview transcripts were read repeatedly to obtain an overall understanding of the participants’ experiences. Secondly, significant statements were identified by selecting meaningful sentences or phrases related to the phenomenon. These statements were extracted for each participant and coded with the transcript page number and line number. Thirdly, significant statements were formulated in the researcher’s language with more general meanings. Fourthly, the researchers organized the formulated meanings into clusters of themes and integrated them into emergent themes. Fifthly, the phenomenon was exhaustively described by synthesizing all theme clusters and emergent themes and formulated meanings into a detailed description. Sixthly, the fundamental structure of the phenomenon was described by eliminating repetitions and providing a clear and concise description. Lastly, the study findings were validated by the participants through member checking.

Rigor

Following Lincoln and Guba’s [35] frameworks for ensuring research rigor, we focused on this study’s credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. The interviewer had diverse experiences as a youth professional and received training in qualitative research methods. We ensured that the interviewer had the required knowledge and research skills to perform her role and maintain a neutral and non-judgmental stance during interviews. The interviewer used semi-structured and open-ended questions to mitigate the potential impact of the researcher’s intentions or biases on the interview content. We conducted structured peer debriefings with other researchers at multiple stages of the analysis process to critically examine emerging themes, challenge potential biases, and refine interpretations.

To enhance credibility, two participants reviewed the research findings to ensure that the results aligned with their lived experiences rather than being solely researcher-driven. Although this process helped validate the results, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of self-reporting, as it can lead to recall bias—resulting in the under- or overestimation of past events due to memory constraints and reluctance to disclose sensitive experiences [36]. Given these limitations, we employed checking with a shelter director and a staff member to verify the alignment of the results with their observations and experiences, along with peer debriefings and an external expert review.

To improve transferability, the cooperator at the local youth shelters recommended selecting participants who could provide detailed statements about their experiences with independent living. We held regular meetings to discuss the analysis process and determined that data saturation was reached when no new information emerged. To ensure dependability, we prepared a detailed data collection process. Researchers carefully recorded all processes and adhered strictly to the procedures. The results were prepared by quoting the participants’ words verbatim to enhance conformability. The researchers manually coded the data independently, followed by cross-validation and discussions to compare results, resolve discrepancies between researchers, and ensure that the themes consistently and faithfully represented the participants’ experiences.

Additionally, we sought feedback from an external expert in qualitative research to review our findings. The expert helped identify potential biases and areas where our interpretations might have been influenced by researcher assumptions, ensuring a more objective analysis.

Results

The age range of the participants was 18 to 24 years old. There were two participants aged 18, two aged 20, one aged 21, two aged 22, one aged 23, and two aged 24, resulting in an average age of 21.2 years. Regarding their occupations, five participants had jobs, three were preparing for employment, and two were college students. The participants’ educational backgrounds included two high school dropouts, five high school graduates, two currently enrolled in college, and one college graduate. The duration of independent living ranged from 4 months to 5 years, with three participants having lived independently for less than a year, four for 1 to < 3 years, and three for 3 to 5 years. Six participants lived alone, two with friends, and two with partners. The details of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Theme 1. Challenges that emerged after leaving youth shelters

Financial challenges

Participants experienced challenges securing stable employment due to their young age and lack of educational qualifications. As a result, they initially engaged in part-time jobs; as Participant 2 explained, "At that time, since I did not have a high school diploma, I could only work part-time jobs like serving or working in cafes."

Some participants also lacked the savings and financial resources needed for independent living. Without financial security, they struggled to afford necessities such as rent, food, and transportation. Participant 6 described this precariousness, stating, "When I had financial trouble, I felt like I was pushed over the edge. After paying the rent, I had no money left to live on." For many, the transition from the shelter was particularly difficult because, while "the shelter had provided me with food and a place to sleep," they now faced the "biggest stress" of paying for those same basic needs.

To address these needs, they had to rely on a relentless cycle of part-time work and loans, which caused immense stress. Participant 6, who was called "the king of part-time jobs" by friends, expressed the exhaustion of this lifestyle: "I kept working part-time jobs in many places. I am tired of working part-time jobs... but money drains away like pouring water through a sieve. I had to take out a loan."

Despite government assistance, participants experienced difficulties finding affordable and safe housing due to rising rental prices, with rent often exceeding the support amount. They expressed a desire to live in a safe building with security, but as Participant 2 noted, "the rent keeps increasing, and the support amount is too small to keep up with the increasing rental costs." Consequently, even with assistance from the Korea Land and Housing Corporation (LH) Lease Project—where LH signs a lease contract with the homeowner and then sublets the property to the tenant at an affordable price—it remained "hard to find a house that's both affordable and safe." due to its limited availability.

Psychological instability

Participants shared their lives with peers and shelter staff while residing in the youth shelters. However, in stark contrast, after leaving the shelter, they experienced feelings of loneliness and isolation, particularly during the initial stage of independence. Participant 5 reflected on this painful transition, stating, “The hardest time for me was when I first started living alone. I was crying every day because the feeling of loneliness was too much to bear.” While they had once longed for privacy in the shelter, the reality of being alone proved difficult to manage. As Participant 10 shared, “After becoming independent, it’s strange, too quiet, and I can’t adapt to it… It seems like I am all alone in the world.” This isolation was often exacerbated by financial constraints that limited their social activities, as Participant 4 noted: “Once I go out, I need to spend money, so I usually stay at home, and then I feel trapped.”

Participants also felt overwhelmed when handling practical situations independently, particularly when managing home repairs or other household issues. The added discomfort of interacting with male strangers, such as repairmen, made these situations even more stressful and overwhelming for female participants. Participant 1 described this feeling of being overwhelmed, explaining, " When something breaks down in my apartment, I get really stressed out and feel overwhelmed. Since I live alone as a woman, I don’t feel comfortable having a repairman come into my personal space… I usually try my best to fix things myself, and if I can’t, I just live with it.”

Furthermore, decision-making became difficult without guidance or advice from trusted adults, leading to persistent uncertainty and anxiety regarding their choices and future. Participant 8 stated, “When I have issues that are difficult for me to decide on, I need advice from a grown-up. I have no one.” Participant 6 also shared, “Living alone means I have no one to give me feedback, so I often feel uncertain and anxious about whether I’m living my life correctly. I worry about issues like romantic relationships and employment alone.” This anxiety was compounded by a reluctance to seek help from others who might not fully understand her background; as Participant 6 noted, “I hesitate to ask for advice from others because that would require revealing my personal story.”

Participants also faced hurt and betrayal in relationships with family and peers, as well as demands for money from them. These experiences led to decreased trust in others and significant interpersonal confusion. Participant 5, reflecting on a recent betrayal, shared a painful realization: “I realized that relationships could derail my life… I painfully learned that I should not easily trust anyone.”

Theme 2. Efforts to overcome challenges

Efforts to overcome financial challenges

Participants sought various forms of financial assistance when they left youth shelters. They received a settlement allowance for independence and monthly independence allowances from the city. Some participants received deposit and rent assistance for housing and educational expenses from Youth Independence Support Centers, as Participant 5 shared: “The Youth Independence Support Center helped me with my housing deposit, rent, and tuition for a private institute to obtain my certification.” Participant 2 noted, “I applied to be a recipient of basic living benefits at the Community Service Center, and the borough office gave me a list of job openings and helped me work in the Self-Help Center.”

Additionally, participants recognized the critical need for money to live independently and made significant efforts to save and manage their finances. Participant 3 described this effort, noting that “While staying at the youth shelter, I did not have any fixed expenses, which allowed me to save money from my job. However, after becoming independent, it has been more challenging to save money. Nevertheless, I prioritize saving half of my salary… I rarely spend money, avoid buying clothes, and only purchase essential items.”

Efforts to overcome psychological instability

Participants engaged in various efforts to overcome psychological instability, including maintaining positive social relationships, accepting their situation, self-regulation, self-care, and seeking psychological counseling.

Some participants maintained contact and familiar relationships with peers or shelter staff even after leaving, while others utilized case management services. Participant 5 highlighted how regular meetings with a case manager provided significant emotional support, stating: “I receive case management from a case manager at the Youth Independence Support Center. Regular meetings with her are extremely helpful… She is the person who worries for me for me the most and helps me when I’m sick, so having someone like that in my life is a great source of support.”

Additionally, participants gradually accepted that living alone would naturally be lonely and challenging, and they made a conscious decision to overcome hardships. As Participant 5 shared: “I accepted the situation and made up my mind. Living on my own is not easy. With firm determination, I live my life fiercely. Having hit rock bottom and made it this far, I’m proud of myself for holding on and persevering.”

They also managed their impulse control differently than when they lived in shelters. Participant 3 reflected on this shift toward self-regulation, stating: “When I was in the shelter, I lived my life as impulsively as I liked… Now, I always come home to sleep even though it is late.” This control extended to social boundaries, with Participant 2 noting that they no longer associated with those who might negatively influence their new lifestyle: “I don’t meet my old friends with whom I used to hang out and wander when I was young… because meeting them seems pathetic and unhelpful.”

Furthermore, participants recognized the importance of self-care by improving their physical and mental health. Participant 5 added that prioritizing self-care became essential, stating: “I did not pay attention to myself while I was only working, but I realized that my physical and mental health had worsened… I now make it a priority to go to the gym regularly and control my diet.”

Finally, some sought psychological counseling and subsequently received psychiatric care when experiencing psychological difficulties. Participant 6 described the process of seeking help: “I thought, ‘I can’t stay like this,’ so I went to the counseling center at my school first. I had three counseling sessions and then went to a psychiatrist. I was diagnosed with depression; therefore, I received psychological therapy and medication. I am still taking my medication.”

Theme 3. Hope for the future

Desire for stability and normalcy

Participants commonly used the terms “stability” and “normalcy,” expressing a strong desire for a stable life and a normal family. They prioritized securing jobs with steady incomes, obtaining certifications, and preparing for long-term employment. Participant 3 reflected this pragmatic approach to stability, stating: “The reason I got my license as a nurse aide was… because I wanted a stable life. For that, I need a license. I want to buy a house. I think I will be more stable once I own a house.”

Furthermore, participants expressed concerns about marriage and starting a family, stemming from the dissolution of their original families. Participant 6 shared these concerns, noting the perceived disadvantages of lacking parental support: “I don’t even have my parents. I think that marriage without my parents would be disadvantageous to me. I have worried about whether I might have to live as a single person for the rest of my life.” Despite these worries, they remained hopeful that they could achieve an ordinary life with a spouse and children once they attained financial stability. As Participant 5 expressed: “If I become financially stable, I want to get married, have a baby, and live an ordinary life.”

Dreaming and striving for personal growth

Participants dreamed of and strived for personal growth by setting up future goals for what they wanted to achieve. This process often involved deep self-reflection on their interests and capabilities. Participant 2 described this journey of discovery, noting: “I wondered what I liked the most and what I did best. Most of all, I realized that I liked animals… I decided to learn about becoming a companion dog stylist.” For others, the shelter environment played a role in building the confidence necessary to set ambitious goals. Participant 7 shared: “I think I had a clear goal. I wanted to go to college… the shelter made me feel like I was capable. So, I think that’s what encouraged me to go to college.”

Beyond setting goals, participants were actively moving toward these goals by obtaining high school diplomas, pursuing college entrance, and acquiring various certifications. Participant 2 highlighted this persistent effort, explaining: “Since I had not even completed my high school education at that time, I made up my mind to prepare and take the general equivalency diploma (GED)… I am studying to get a dog stylist license. I am preparing for the written exam myself.” Similarly, Participant 7 emphasized the link between professional preparation and personal stability: “My current situation is unstable. I need to get a job to achieve stability. I want to work on product planning… so I am currently preparing for the Service Management Ability Test.”

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of female youths transitioning to independence after leaving youth shelters. The findings illustrate that the transition to independence is a complex and multifaceted process characterized by significant financial and psychological challenges, ongoing efforts to cope with these difficulties, and aspirations for a more stable future. Rather than representing a linear process, participants’ experiences reflected a dynamic process in which challenges, coping strategies, and hopes for the future were closely interconnected.

With respect to the theme cluster of financial challenges, we found that participants struggled to secure stable employment after leaving youth shelters. Due to their younger age than typical employment age in Korea and their lack of educational qualifications or certificates, they primarily relied on part-time jobs characterized by low wages and job insecurity. Additionally, several participants experienced financial difficulties, particularly in the early stages of independence, due to limited savings and financial resources. One participant highlighted the severity of their situation: “After paying the rent, I had no money left to live on. I had to take out a loan and work part-time jobs.” Another participant described feeling pushed to the limit by these financial difficulties.

These financial challenges reflect a broader experience observed during emerging adulthood, a developmental stage in which young people transition toward financial independence. Research on emerging adults in the United States indicates that a substantial proportion experience financial precarity or are at risk of financial difficulties [37]. However, such challenges tend to be more pronounced among youths leaving out-of-home care, who transition to independence with limited financial resources and inadequate preparation for managing expenses [27, 29].

Unlike many emerging adults who can rely on financial support from their families during this transitional period, participants in this study had no access to parental assistance, which further intensified their financial vulnerability. This contrast is particularly notable in the Korean context, where young adults commonly continue to receive financial support from their parents even after completing higher education or entering the workforce [38]. A national survey reported that approximately 86% of Korean parents believe they should financially support their children until college graduation [39]. In contrast, youths leaving out-of-home care must navigate early independence without such family support, placing them in a structurally more vulnerable position during the transition to adulthood.

Although the Korean government provides support for youths leaving out-of-home care—including a settlement allowance of approximately 10 million KRW (approximately 6,751 USD) and a monthly independence allowance of 400,000 KRW (approximately 270 USD)—many participants in this study continued to experience financial hardship. This suggests that the issue is not solely the amount of financial support but also reflects challenges related to limited financial management skills and restricted access to information about available benefits [11]. To address these challenges, programs focusing on vocational training, job placement services, and financial literacy education may help equip youths leaving out-of-home care with the practical skills and knowledge necessary to achieve sustainable financial independence during the transition to adulthood.

We found that participants experienced psychological instability in the transition from youth shelters to independence. Consistent with previous studies [19, 28], participants reported feelings of loneliness and isolation in the early stages of independence. The contrast between the communal environment of the shelter and the solitude of independent living intensifies these emotions. One participant described their experience by stating, “I feel trapped. It’s just kind of frustrating and annoying.” This sense of isolation can be understood through Maslow’s hierarchy of needs [40], as the lack of stable social connections after leaving the shelter disrupts their sense of belonging, leading to psychological distress. From an attachment theory perspective [41, 42], the abrupt loss of supportive relationships with shelter staff can contribute to emotional insecurity, further intensifying the feeling of loneliness.

Previous research indicates that living alone and limited social contact during early adulthood are strongly associated with heightened loneliness [43], while intimacy in interpersonal relationships helps alleviate loneliness [44]. Therefore, to address the anticipated psychological challenges in the early stage of independence, it is essential for youths to maintain close relationships with shelter staff even after leaving the shelters to receive psychological support. One participant expressed their distress, stating, “I feel so sad and lonely. It seems like I am all alone in the world.” This highlights the importance of structured support systems that can provide continuous emotional reassurance during the transition to independence. Based on the findings and existing research, we recommend expanding policies supporting transitional housing programs that provide stable temporary accommodations near the shelters where youths previously resided. These housing options can help bridge the gap between institutional care and independence. Currently, only six mixed-type Youth Independence Support Centers in Korea, overseen by the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family, operate transitional housing including internal living quarters and shared houses [1, 45]. Expanding these initiatives would not only provide more youths with safe temporary housing but also help them maintain essential social connections and receive psychological support. By ensuring a stable transition period, these housing options can play a crucial role in fostering long-term stability for youths as they move from out-of-home care to independent living.

Safety was a significant concern for female participants, particularly as they navigated new environments and began living alone. They described feeling overwhelmed by potential threats in unsafe neighborhoods, as well as discomfort when interacting with male strangers—such as repairmen—in their homes. Previous research indicates that women perceive urban environments as significantly less safe than men, showing a 40% gender disparity in perceived safety [46]. Another study found that women prioritize crime prevention when choosing their place of residence and often spend more on housing to ensure their safety [47]. Taken together with the findings of this study, concerns about safety among women are not merely personal anxieties but are influenced by gender norms and cultural narratives. Women are often framed as vulnerable, and the emphasis on self-protective behavior reinforces heightened vigilance, leading to the internalization of societal expectations that place the responsibility for safety on women themselves [47]. These findings highlight the unique challenges faced by female participants and the need for comprehensive support systems that address both physical and psychological safety for female youths transitioning to independence. To address these challenges, gender-responsive policies should be implemented, such as strengthening housing support policies to enable young women to access safer living environments and improving community safety infrastructure.

This study found that participants experienced uncertainty and anxiety regarding their choices and future. These emotions were largely related to a lack of guidance or advice, leaving them unsure about their direction. Such experiences align with the broader developmental context of emerging adulthood, a life stage characterized by ongoing transitions in romantic relationships, education, and career pathways [17]. These transitions often involve uncertain outcomes and can contribute to increased anxiety [48, 49]. However, youths transitioning out of out-of-home care may experience these developmental challenges under more constrained circumstances. Without supportive parents who can provide reassurance, feedback, and guidance, they may face greater uncertainty when making important life decisions, which may further intensify their anxiety.

These findings highlight the importance of structured support systems that provide consistent guidance during the transition to independent living. In Korea, Youth Independence Support Centers offer programs such as independence plan monitoring and motivational support [45]. However, their effectiveness and level of youth engagement remain uncertain [11]. To address these gaps, mentorship programs should be developed for youth transitioning from out-of-home care. Such programs could facilitate the development of decision-making skills, reduce uncertainty and anxiety, and promote a successfully transition to independent living by providing personalized support and guidance. To maximize these outcomes, it is imperative that mentors undergo training to understand the unique vulnerabilities and challenges faced by this population.

Participants experienced significant challenges with a lack of trust in others and confusion in their interpersonal relationships. Previous studies indicated that most of the youth living in out-of-home care have experienced childhood abuse, parental divorce, abandonment, and running away from home [50], all of which are associated with lower trust in others [51]. Additionally, another previous study found an association between recalling parental rejection and having low trust in others [52]. The study revealed that participants often felt betrayed by their family or friends who, aware of their independence settlement allowance, frequently asked to borrow money. These post-independence experiences may further exacerbate their already low levels of trust, rooted in childhood traumas.

In this study, there were cases of overcoming financial challenges through financial assistance from the government and local authorities and saving money while living in the shelter. To support financial independence, continuous case management, including financial planning counseling and budgeting workshops, is essential before and after leaving out-of-home care. Establishing referral systems that streamline access to resources such as settlement allowances, rental assistance, and educational expenses is also crucial. Additionally, connecting youths with stable sources of income can be achieved through partnerships with local businesses and employment agencies that offer job placement services. Partnering with local financial institutions to offer financial literacy programs and microloans could address immediate needs and achieve long-term financial stability.

Participants’ efforts to maintain positive social relationships with peers, shelter staff, and case managers emerged as a crucial factor in mitigating psychological instability. This finding suggests that for youth leaving shelters, as with other populations in out-of-home care, maintaining connections with supportive figures may play a significant role in promoting psychological stability. Previous studies indicate that close relationships can alleviate feelings of loneliness and isolation [43], and social support from one’s surroundings can affect how individuals in emerging adulthood cope with life challenges, thereby reducing anxiety about the future [53]. Additionally, tailored coaching that offers direction, goal-setting, encouragement, and skill-building assistance can be effective for individuals in emerging adulthood facing life crises due to future uncertainties [54]. Given that youth leaving out-of-home care often exhibit low levels of trust and a reluctance to share their personal stories, it is recommended to strengthen case management at Youth Independence Support Centers and connect them with reliable mentors who can provide concrete coaching and psychosocial support.

Participants gradually learned to accept their realities rather than merely complaining about their difficulties. This finding aligns with a previous study indicating that youths transitioning out of foster care often develop the mental strength to embrace their realities despite significant environmental changes [22]. This phenomenon can be understood as resilience, defined as the ability to cope and adapt successfully in the face of adversity [55], where the acceptance of harsh realities serves as a core attribute [56]. In addition, this study extracted the formulated meanings of impulse control and self-care. These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that impulse control is a significant predictor of resilience [57], and that self-care strategies for physical and mental health enhance resilience [58]. Consequently, these results suggest that participants demonstrated a degree of resilience in overcoming the challenges and sudden environmental changes associated with leaving out-of-home care.

Participants in this study sought psychological counseling to address their mental health difficulties. Their experiences align with prior research on youth in out-of-home care, which indicates that these individuals are more prone to developing issues such as depression and alcohol-related problems compared to emerging adults in the general population [18, 21]. Additionally, as noted in a study of youth transitioning out of foster care, the need for psychological interventions often arises from the confusion, hurt, and loss stemming from past relationships with biological parents [29]. The results of this study show that participants may experience psychological difficulties due to childhood trauma and stress arising from sudden environmental changes. Therefore, tailored psychological support should be provided as a key component of the transition plan, considering their psychological instability, complex past traumas, and current life circumstances.

Participants in this study expressed a desire to establish what they perceived as a “normal” family that differed from their families of origin, and some appeared concerned that the absence of parents might be perceived as a disadvantage when forming their own families through marriage. This finding contrasts with broader trends among emerging adults in South Korea, where many individuals in their twenties increasingly prioritize personal development, autonomy, and flexible lifestyles rather than marriage or family formation [59, 60]. However, the participants in this study seemed to place greater importance on forming their own families. From the perspective of emerging adulthood, this aspiration may reflect efforts to attain emotional stability and belonging that were limited by earlier experiences of family instability. A previous study similarly suggested that individuals from single-parent households, economically disadvantaged backgrounds, and those with lower levels of education tend to feel a greater need to form families due to unmet emotional needs [59]. Additionally, another previous study indicated that female youths who lack positive maternal relationships may be more likely to enter cohabitation or unprepared marriages [61], suggesting that early family experiences may significantly influence perspectives on family formation. Taken together, these findings highlight that youth transitioning from out-of-home care may experience emerging adulthood differently from their peers and may benefit from sustained support—such as parenting education and programs promoting financial stability—to build stable, fulfilling lives and families.

Consistent with previous studies [27, 28], participants planned and worked toward their goals with dreams of personal growth. However, previous studies indicated that youths leaving out-of-home care are more likely to experience insecurity and exclusion in education and employment [18, 62]. Cases of individuals dropping out of school or seeking illegal part-time jobs due to financial constraints have been reported in previous studies examining youth leaving out-of-home care [21, 27], and a survey on crisis youths in Korea revealed that 38.4% of them have experienced dropping out of school [63]. A study in Japan found youths in out-of-home care exhibited a ten times higher risk of high school dropout [64]. Given these systemic vulnerabilities, a comprehensive support strategy should focus on making education more accessible by addressing financial constraints. This includes expanding targeted scholarships, vocational training, and paid internships, as well as forming partnerships with non-governmental or private organizations to fund certification programs. Addressing financial barriers to education may provide stable educational pathways for young people leaving out-of-home care, reducing reliance on precarious employment and improving long-term career prospects.

This study has some limitations that warrant acknowledgement. First, we purposively sampled participants who could provide rich, detailed descriptions of the transition experience after leaving youth shelters. To support this process, we sought recommendations from shelter staff, as they were well acquainted with the youths and could identify individuals who were more likely to articulate their experiences in depth. However, because these recommendations were limited to youth with known contact information, those who had lost all connection with shelter staff may not have been represented. As a result, the study may not fully capture the experiences of youths facing extreme instability, such as chronic homelessness or severe mental health crises. Future research should recruit these high-risk youths to better understand the factors contributing to adverse outcomes—including homelessness, financial insecurity, and mental health struggles—and to identify interventions that could support their stability. Second, this study did not analyze how various factors, such as socioeconomic status and geographic location, intersect to influence participants’ experiences. Differences between rural and urban environments, as well as between youths from different socioeconomic backgrounds, may significantly shape their experiences and needs. Therefore, further research is need to explore these socio-cultural variables and their influence on the transition process.

Conclusions

Although this study primarily focused on the initial transition experiences after leaving the shelter, it is essential to consider the long-term impact of these early experiences on youths’ lives. Participants in this study had varying lengths of independent living, ranging from 4 months to 5 years, and most continued to face significant challenges, including securing stable employment, accessing affordable and safe housing, and managing psychological instability. These early unstable experiences may contribute to long-term financial insecurity, which may make it more difficult for some youth to save for future goals, pursue further education, and advance in their careers. Additionally, these challenges may also affect the development of resilience, making it harder for youth to cope effectively with ongoing difficulties. Taken together, the findings suggest the importance of gradual and supported transitions to independence through coordinated welfare systems. Such support may include vocational training, financial education, transitional housing, gender-responsive safety measures, and sustained aftercare services such as case management, mentoring, and psychological support to promote more stable transitions to adulthood.
