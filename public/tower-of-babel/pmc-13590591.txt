Identification of DNA methylation signatures of smoking in women and associations with cardiovascular disease mortality
Authors: Dennis Khodasevich, Aladdin H. Shadyab, Adam X. Maihofer, Caroline M. Nievergelt, Robert Wallace, Lisa W. Martin, Anne K. Bozack, Rosemarie de la Rosa, Marcia L. Stefanick, Nora Franceschini, Andres Cardenas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tobacco smoking is critical risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality, with epigenetics implicated as a key biological mechanism. Smoking disproportionately impacts CVD risk in women; however, limited research has examined smoking-associated DNA methylation (DNAm) changes in women. We measure whole blood DNAm (EPICv2) and self-reported smoking (three-category, pack-years, and years since cessation) via questionnaires in 6,005 Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study participants. We perform epigenome-wide association studies and develop epigenetic predictors of each smoking phenotype, and compare DNAm and self-reported pack-years for CVD-related and all-cause mortality prediction. Here we find 20,388 FDR-significant sites related to current smoking, 2149 for former smoking, 14,709 for pack-years, and 753 for smoking cessation. The epigenetic predictors of smoking activity are highly correlated with their respective phenotypes and DNAm pack-years outperforms self-reported pack-years in mortality prediction. We report robust validation of previous findings, novel smoking associations in women, and introduce epigenetic predictors of smoking activity.

Introduction

Cigarette smoke contributes to a wide range of adverse health outcomes, including coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and several forms of cancer1–3. Cigarette smoke is a complex mixture consisting of upwards of 7300 chemical compounds with extensive biological mechanisms of action, including induction of oxidative stress, inflammation, DNA damage, and apoptosis4,5. Despite these well-documented health impacts, smoking remains one of the leading preventable causes of death in the United States6. Epigenetic mechanisms involved in the regulation of gene expression without directly altering the underlying DNA sequence have emerged as a major biomarker through which cigarette smoke impacts human health7. DNA methylation (DNAm), the methylation of cytosine to 5-methylcytosine, is a key epigenetic modification involved in the regulation of gene expression8. DNAm is responsive to environmental exposures and can serve as both stable indicators of past exposure as well as potential mediators in the associations between environmental exposures and downstream effects on health9,10. Tobacco smoking has emerged as one of the strongest environmental influences on DNAm, with several large studies reporting extensive associations of tobacco smoking with DNAm throughout the genome in adults11–13, as well as in newborns in response to maternal smoking during pregnancy14. Altered methylation at specific CpG sites can be highly reflective of past smoking exposure and several DNAm-based predictors of smoking activity, including both categorical classifications and estimates of pack-years, have been developed15,16.

Smoking is a major modifiable contributor towards cardiovascular disease (CVD)17. Previous research has demonstrated that smoking-associated changes in DNAm are highly predictive of CVD, with DNAm within the Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor Repressor (AHRR) gene being associated with cardiovascular outcomes, including prevalent CVD and carotid plaque scores18–21. DNAm-based proxies of smoking may provide more informative measures of cumulative exposure and biological impact of smoking compared to self-reported smoking activity, even outperforming self-reported smoking activity in disease risk prediction in certain scenarios22. Previous research has also suggested differential associations of smoking with CVD risk by sex, with worsened CVD outcomes related to smoking activity among women23. However, few studies have sought to develop epigenetic biomarkers of smoking activity tailored for female populations and examine the ability of these DNAm smoking biomarkers to predict CVD and mortality among women24,25. Furthermore, most previous studies examining the associations of smoking with DNAm have been run on the Illumina 450K and EPICv1 microarrays, which feature more limited coverage of the epigenome compared to the newer EPICv2 array.

We performed a multistage analysis of smoking, DNAm, and CVD mortality risk among 6005 participants from the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS). First, we examined associations of self-reported three-category smoking status, smoking pack-years, and years since smoking cessation with DNAm across the epigenome, identifying differentially methylated sites and enrichment of biological pathways. Second, we developed novel DNAm-based predictors of self-reported three-category smoking status, smoking pack-years, and years since smoking cessation within WHIMS, then further validated these predictors in two independent datasets, which included male participants. Third, we compared DNAm-based smoking pack-years and self-reported smoking pack-years in the prediction of CVD-related mortality, as well as all-cause and cancer-related mortality.

Results

Study sample

The study sample consisted of 6005 WHIMS participants with DNAm data available at baseline (Table 1). Participants had an average age of 70.03 years (SD = 3.81 years, range: 63–81 years), with 53.3% of the study sample reporting never smoking, 39.8% reporting formerly smoking, and 6.9% reporting current smoking. Among former smokers, participants quit smoking at an average age of 44.67 years (SD = 12.83). Participants most commonly reported attaining an education level of some college to college graduate (36.2%), and most commonly reported a household income ranging from $20,000 to $74,999 (65.6%). Most participants self-identified as White (89.2%) and Non-Hispanic/Latino (97.1%). Most participants reported being current alcohol drinkers (63.8%), did not have diabetes at the time of enrollment (93.7%), had an average BMI of 28.54 (SD = 5.68), and 81.6% of participants reported no history of CVD events at baseline. EWAS of self-reported smoking

Current smoking status was associated with a total of 20,388 sites with an false discovery rate (FDR) value < 0.05, with 3501 sites reaching Bonferroni significance (Supplementary Data 1). Among these associations, 8379 were novel associations not previously reported among either the 65,857 and 18,760 FDR significant sites identified in relation to current smoking in Hoang et al.12 and Joehanes et al.11, respectively. Effect estimates for overlapping sites with these two meta-analyses featured high agreement with effect estimates in WHI (Supplementary Fig. 3). Former smoking was associated with 2149 sites with an FDR value < 0.05, with 461 sites reaching Bonferroni significance (Supplementary Data 2). Of the 2149 FDR-significant associations related to former smokers, 1804 (83.95%) were also identified in relation to current smoking. Smoking pack-years was associated with 14,709 sites with an FDR value < 0.05, with 2555 sites reaching Bonferroni significance (Supplementary Data 3). Of these sites, 9201 (62.55%) sites overlapped with sites identified in relation to current smoking, indicating a large portion of current smoking sites also exhibit linear associations with smoking activity dosage (Fig. 1B).

Years since smoking cessation was associated with a total of 753 sites with an FDR value < 0.05, with 256 sites reaching Bonferroni significance (Supplementary Data 4). Of the 753 associations, 673 sites (89.38%) were also found to be associated with current smoking status, with effect estimates from these associations exhibiting largely opposite direction of effects (correlation coefficient for effect estimates: −0.78) (Fig. 1C). Linear trends in DNAm by years since cessation for the top 15 CpG sites identified in the cessation EWAS indicated that some sites exhibit rapid changes after cessation while other sites exhibit gradual changes in DNAm over time (Supplementary Fig. 4). Manhattan plots for each epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) model series are provided in the supplement (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Of the associations identified in our analyses, a substantial portion were sites added in the new EPICv2 array (Current smoking: 3854; Former smoking: 356; Pack-years: 2592, Cessation: 138). The EPICv2 array also features several internal replicate probes capturing DNA methylation at the same CpG sites. In the current smoking EWAS, there were 322 FDR-significant associations in replicate probes mapped to 153 distinct CpG sites, including 84 Bonferroni-significant associations mapped to 46 unique CpG sites. There was generally high agreement between effect estimates from significant replicate probes mapped to the same CpG sites (e.g., cg20496616_BC22: beta = −0.053, p = 4.00e-36; cg20496616_BC21: beta = −0.049, p = 4.95e-26), with a median difference in effect estimates of 1.04e-03 (range: 1.38e-05, 1.21e-02) and a median difference in p-values of 2.91e-05 (range: 4.95e-26, 9.32e-04). Results were similar for the other EWAS: the former smoking EWAS findings included 35 FDR-significant associations in replicate probes mapped to 17 distinct CpG sites, the pack-years EWAS findings included 235 FDR-significant associations in replicate probes mapped to 110 distinct CpG sites, and the years since cessation EWAS included 18 FDR-significant associations in replicate probes mapped to 8 distinct CpG sites. Additionally, we discovered significant associations on the X chromosome in each EWAS model series (Current smoking: 69; Former smoking: 6; Pack-years: 75; Cessation: 4), which may be overlooked in analyses conducted in mixed sex samples with filtering of sex chromosomes. In particular, multiple CpG sites annotated to the WWC3 gene on the X chromosome were represented among the Bonferroni-significant associations from each EWAS model series. All associations mapped to the X chromosome are displayed in Supplementary Data 5.

Biological pathway and functional enrichment

We utilized KEGG pathway enrichment using sites found to be Bonferroni-significant in each EWAS model as input to characterize the biological functions of our findings. Several KEGG pathways were found to be enriched in relation to years since smoking cessation after FDR-correction, including pathways in cancer, melanoma, chemokine signaling pathway, small cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, and breast cancer (Supplementary Data 6). Pathways in cancer were significantly enriched in the former smoking models. Pathways in cancer and small cell lung cancer were among the top-enriched pathways for the current smoking models, although these enriched pathways were not significant after FDR-adjustment. Similarly, pancreatic cancer and pathways in cancer were among the top-enriched pathways identified in relation to smoking pack-years, although these enriched pathways were not significant after FDR-adjustment.

We additionally sought to identify enrichment of known disease-gene associations represented among our results based on DisGeNET, finding high overlap in identified associations across all EWAS model series. Considering the current smoking EWAS model results, blood basophil count (lab test), serum total cholesterol measurement, vital capacity, and acute myeloid leukemia (AML-M2) were among the top enriched disease states (Supplementary Fig. 6). Considering the former smoking EWAS model results, physical activity measurement, smoking, mixed lineage leukemia, and venous thromboembolism were among the top enriched disease states (Supplementary Fig. 7). Considering the smoking pack-years EWAS model results, acute myeloid leukemia (AML-M2), acute myeloid leukemia (M1), physical activity measurement, and meningioma (benign, no ICD-O subtype) were among the top enriched disease states (Supplementary Fig. 8). Only physical activity measurement was significantly enriched in the cessation models, which was also significantly enriched across all other EWAS model series. (Supplementary Fig. 9) All disease pathways significant after multiple testing adjustment for each EWAS series are presented in Supplementary Data 7.

Epigenetic predictors

The epigenetic predictor of smoking pack-years consisted of 649 CpG sites, achieving a correlation (MAE) of 0.801 (8.213) and 0.641 (9.765) with reported pack-years in the training and testing samples, respectively (Fig. 2A, B) (Supplementary Data 8). The GrimAge2 DNAm pack-years estimator was also highly correlated with self-reported pack-years within the WHI testing sample (0.607), and the GrimAge2 and WHI pack-years predictors exhibited high correlation with each other (correlation = 0.897), despite only sharing 15 common CpG sites (Supplementary Fig. 10).Fig. 2Epigenetic predictor performance summary.Correlation and MAE between actual and DNAm-estimated smoking pack-years in the training (A) and testing (B) samples, and correlation and MAE between actual and DNAm-estimated years since smoking cessation in the training (C) and testing (D) samples.

Epigenetic predictor performance summary.

Correlation and MAE between actual and DNAm-estimated smoking pack-years in the training (A) and testing (B) samples, and correlation and MAE between actual and DNAm-estimated years since smoking cessation in the training (C) and testing (D) samples.

The epigenetic predictor of years since smoking cessation consisted of 195 CpG sites, achieving a correlation (MAE) of 0.71 (7.798) and 0.57 (8.603) with reported years since cessation in the training and testing samples, respectively (Fig. 2C, D) (Supplementary Data 9). When applied to current and never smokers, the years since smoking cessation predictor tended to assign current smokers low values of years since cessation, with a large portion of participants being assigned negative values, while never smokers were assigned higher values resembling the distribution of values assigned to those with >40 years since cessation (Supplementary Fig. 11). Among all former smokers, predicted years since cessation was modestly negatively correlated with reported pack-years (r = −0.54) and strongly negatively correlated with DNAm pack-years (r = −0.85). Similarly, among current smokers, predicted years since cessation was modestly negatively correlated with reported pack-years (r = −0.31) and strongly negatively correlated with DNAm pack-years (r = −0.91), suggesting that a large portion of observed variation in predicted years since cessation is related to the extent of smoking activity.

Considering the categorical bins of years since smoking cessation, all groups had significantly higher predicted years since cessation compared to current smokers (Supplementary Fig. 12). In contrast, when never smokers were used as the reference group, participants with >50 years since smoking cessation did not exhibit significantly different predicted years since cessation from never smokers (p = 0.055), and participants with 40–50 years since cessation exhibited only marginally lower predicted years since cessation compared to never smokers (p = 0.041), suggesting that the DNAm profiles of former smokers with >40 years since cessation are difficult to distinguish from never smokers.

The WHI epigenetic predictor of three-category smoking consisted of 501 CpG sites (Supplementary Data 10) and achieved an accuracy measure, calculated as the proportion of true predictions over the total number of predictions, of 0.8284 and 0.7675 in the training and testing samples, respectively (Table 2). The highest weighted CpG sites in the predictor included several sites often found to be strongly associated with smoking, including cg05575921_BC11 in AHRR and cg19859270_BC21 in GPR15. Within the WHI testing sample, we also found that DNAm at cg05575921_BC11 alone was also effective at distinguishing smoking status (accuracy = 0.6347), particularly for distinguishing between current and non-current smokers (Supplementary Fig. 13). We further sought to examine the performance of each novel epigenetic predictor of smoking activity in two independent datasets. The GSE50660 dataset (N = 464) consisted of 327 male and 137 female European participants, and contained 179 never smokers (38.6%), 263 former smokers (56.7%), and 22 current smokers (4.7%). The three-category smoking predictor achieved a high accuracy of 0.7026, outperformed the EpiSmoker predictor (accuracy: 0.4655), and both the pack-years and years since cessation predictors exhibited the expected trends among never, former, and current smokers (Supplementary Fig. 14) (Supplementary Table 1). The Chile dataset consisted of 238 male and 123 female participants ranging in age from 34 to 89 years, and contained 137 never smokers, 145 former smokers, and 79 current smokers. The three-category smoking predictor achieved a modest accuracy of 0.5845, outperforming the EpiSmoker predictor (accuracy: 0.4709) (Supplementary Table 2), and the pack-years predictor achieved a correlation of 0.549 with self-reported pack-years in the Chile dataset (Supplementary Fig. 15). When stratifying both the Chile and GSE50660 datasets by sex, there was no notable drop-off in performance among male participants compared to female participants (Supplementary Table 3). Accuracy measures for all predictor testing scenarios are presented in Supplementary Fig. 16.

Prediction of mortality

Of the 5110 study participants with smoking and complete covariate data available, 3224 participants died during the study period, with a mean time of 17.24 years from enrollment to death (range: 0.40 to 28.60 years). The most common causes of death included other cardiovascular (N = 413), cerebrovascular (N = 294), known other cause (N = 281), dementia NOS (N = 241), possible CHD (N = 230), definite CHD (N = 230), and Alzheimer’s (N = 187). A total of 1182 participants died of a CVD-related cause (Definite CHD, Cerebrovascular, Pulmonary Embolism, Possible CHD, Other Cardiovascular, and Unknown Cardiovascular). A total of 597 participants died of a cancer-related cause, with lung cancer (N = 148), pancreatic cancer (N = 59), colon cancer (N = 55), and breast cancer (N = 41) the most common specific forms of cancer.

The base Cox model predicting time to CVD-related death had a concordance of 0.692. Adding self-reported pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.168, 95% CI = 1.102, 1.237) increased the concordance to 0.699, with the likelihood ratio test (LRT) indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p = 7.34e-07) (Fig. 3). Adding DNAm pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.251, 95% CI = 1.177, 1.330) increased the concordance to a modestly higher value of 0.704, with the LRT indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p = 7.39e-12).Fig. 3Time-to-event analyses of smoking.Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for a 1-SD increase in self-reported or WHI DNAm pack-years in predicting CVD-related mortality, all-cause mortality, and cancer-related mortality.

Time-to-event analyses of smoking.

Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for a 1-SD increase in self-reported or WHI DNAm pack-years in predicting CVD-related mortality, all-cause mortality, and cancer-related mortality.

Results were similar considering all-cause mortality. The base Cox model predicting time to overall death had a concordance of 0.636. Adding self-reported pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.276, 95% CI = 1.236, 1.316) increased the concordance to 0.659, with the LRT indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p < 2.2e-16). Adding DNAm pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.371, 95% CI = 1.325, 1.418) increased the concordance to a modestly higher value of 0.668, with the LRT indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 3).

Lastly, results were similar considering cancer-related mortality. The base Cox model predicting time to cancer-related had a concordance of 0.595. Adding self-reported pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.386, 95% CI = 1.305, 1.473) increased the concordance to 0.648, with the LRT indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p < 2.2e-16). Adding DNAm pack-years to the base model (HR = 1.551, 95% CI = 1.451, 1.659) increased the concordance to a modestly higher value of 0.661, with the LRT indicating significantly improved model fit compared to the base model (p < 2.2e-16). For all predictions, the WHI DNAm-based pack-years predictor exhibited a higher magnitude hazard ratio and higher concordance compared to self-reported pack-years (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We identified evidence for extensive changes in DNAm across the epigenome in response to smoking in the WHI, including 20,388 FDR-significant sites related to current smoking status and 2149 sites related to former smoking status. Additionally, we identified 14,709 sites associated with smoking pack-years and 753 sites associated with years since smoking cessation, further contributing to our understanding of sites that exhibit linear relationships with smoking activity and cessation beyond three-category smoking activity. We developed epigenetic predictors of three-category smoking, pack-years, and years since cessation, which were highly correlated with their respective smoking phenotypes across the training, testing, and validation datasets. Furthermore, we found that a novel epigenetic proxy of pack-years may outperform self-reported pack-years in the prediction of CVD-related, all-cause, and cancer-related mortality.

We identified several associations related to smoking in women that have not been reported in previous meta-analyses, including differential methylation of CpG sites located on the X chromosome. For example, we found that smoking was associated with lower methylation of several CpG sites within the WWC3 gene, which has been implicated as a tumor suppressor and inhibitor of cell proliferation26,27. Differential methylation of sites in WWC3 has similarly been found in previous smaller studies, including the X chromosome28,29. These findings highlight the importance of considering DNAm on the sex chromosomes, which are often excluded in analyses of mixed-sex samples, although researchers should note some additional processing considerations for sex chromosomes in mixed-sex samples30. Even among sites found to be significant in previous meta-analyses, we found some evidence for higher magnitude associations among top hits within WHI. For example, current smoking was associated with lower methylation at cg05575921 within AHRR, with the highest magnitude association within WHI (WHI effect estimate: −0.236, Hoang effect estimate: −0.138, Joehanes effect estimate: −0.180). This observation of higher magnitude effect estimates among top smoking-associated CpG sites within WHI may also provide some mechanistic explanations for observations of greater magnitude of associations of smoking with CVD among women23. However, more research is necessary to explore whether these findings are related to the biological impact of smoking, or differences in smoking behaviors, or technical factors between studies. Furthermore, sites added in the new EPICv2 array made up a large portion of novel results, highlighting the importance of re-examining associations as coverage of the epigenome on DNAm microarrays continues to increase. Although there was some evidence for agreement in effect estimates from replicate probes mapped to the same CpG sites, there was still notable heterogeneity in the magnitude and significance of effect estimates from replicate probes, even among significant probes. These findings suggest that although the replicate probes are generally picking up related signals, but there is still notable variation between replicate probes, which may have important implications for processing pipelines which involve the averaging of methylation values across replicate probes. We additionally were able to identify a large number of sites related to former smoking status and years since cessation that were generally low-magnitude associations relative to the high-magnitude associations related to current smoking, and these findings have important implications for power calculations in analyses seeking to examine the epigenetic impacts of former smoking.

In addition to the novel results, we further validated a large number of previous smoking-associated CpG sites from mixed sex meta-analysis efforts. We found that current smoking was associated with 10,874 sites previously identified by Hoang et al. and 4888 sites previously identified by Joehanes et al., as well as high agreement in effect estimates for sites identified in these studies, suggesting substantial commonality with previous large meta-analyses of smoking11,12. Despite extensive validation of previous associations, we did not validate a portion of findings from these previous meta-analyses which may be related to differences in smoking behaviors, population characteristics, array type differences, or sample size. Additionally, biological pathway enrichment of our findings revealed that several pathways related to cancer were enriched in response to smoking, supporting extensive previous research linking smoking to cancer development. In particular, KEGG pathways related to small cell lung cancer, a cancer subtype with strong associations with smoking, appeared to be enriched across most smoking measures in WHI31.

We developed epigenetic predictors of three-category smoking, pack-years, and years since cessation within the WHI population, each of which was highly correlated with their respective smoking phenotype. These predictors may offer a particular advantage in female study populations, although we demonstrate that each predictor performs well in samples containing male participants. Our novel predictor of years since smoking cessation may confer a particular benefit for characterizing former smokers and performs well across current and never smokers as well. In particular, we found that our years since cessation predictor readily differentiated current smokers from former smokers regardless of the length of time since cessation, while former smokers who quit >40 years ago were difficult to distinguish from never smokers. These findings support previous research on the immediate impact of smoking cessation on DNAm, while also suggesting that some of the impacts of smoking on DNAm can still be detected even decades after cessation32,33.

We found that our DNAm-based predictors of smoking pack-years may be more strongly associated with CVD-related, all-cause, and cancer-related mortality compared to self-reported smoking pack-years, which may be explained by DNAm better capturing individual smoke exposure compared to self-report34. Ample previous research has noted that DNAm changes in smoking-responsive genes, including AHRR and F2RL3, are predictive of CVD risk and mortality21,35. Similarly, previous studies have suggested DNAm of the AHRR gene may provide improved identification of patient cancer mortality risk compared to self-reported smoking36. Other studies have also reported that DNAm-based biomarkers of biological aging, also known as epigenetic clocks, may mediate associations between smoking and health outcomes, with a study of 2345 NHANES participants finding that DNAm PhenoAge partially mediates the relationship between early-life smoking and increased CVD risk37, and another study of 2978 HRS participants finding that GrimAge, PhenoAge, and DunedinPoAm all partially mediated associations between smoking pack-years and heart disease38. Previous studies have also reported that an epigenetic inflammation index can outperform measured circulating C-reactive protein (CRP) in association with health-traits39,40. Taken together, our results complement these previous findings and suggest that DNAm-based predictors of smoking may offer advantages for risk prediction and identification of high-risk patients relative to self-reported smoking measures, both for CVD-related mortality as well as all-cause and cancer-related mortality.

Our study features several strengths. First, we were able to examine several facets of smoking behavior in our analyses including three-category smoking status, smoking pack-years, and years since smoking cessation. Second, our study featured a large sample size, allowing for the identification of low-magnitude associations, particularly in relation to former smoking and years since cessation. Third, we developed three novel DNAm-based biomarkers of smoking activity and provided public implementations to enable their application in novel datasets. Our study also features some limitations. First, self-reported smoking activity may be influenced by recall bias, and epigenetic signatures of smoking may also be impacted by environmental tobacco smoke exposure. Secondly, we did not have access to more objective measures of smoking activity, such as cotinine, to complement the self-reported measures. Third, although we controlled for a wide range of technical, genetic, behavioral, and sociodemographic traits in our analysis, we cannot rule out the potential of other factors (e.g., dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and other preexisting health conditions) in influencing our results. Additionally, we validated the performance of our novel epigenetic predictors in samples with varying array types and processing methodologies, both of which may influence performance. Lastly, the improvement in risk prediction for our novel DNAm pack-years measure compared to self-reported pack-years was relatively small in magnitude (0.5–1.3%), and more research is necessary to determine the clinical relevance of these small improvements in predictive performance.

In conclusion, smoking was strongly related to DNAm in older women, with 20,388 FDR-significant sites related to current smoking status, 2149 for former smoking status, 14,709 for smoking pack-years, and 753 for years since smoking cessation, and pathway analysis implicating several cancer-related biological pathways enriched among these associations. We additionally introduce novel epigenetic predictors of three-category smoking, pack-years, and years since cessation, which were highly correlated with their respective smoking phenotypes, and may outperform self-reported pack-years in the prediction of CVD-related mortality, as well as cancer-related and all-cause mortality.

Methods

Study population

The study population consisted of women from the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS) who underwent epigenetic profiling as part of a study of cognitive function. WHIMS enrolled women who were age 65 years and older at baseline and enrolled in the WHI randomized controlled trials of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), and the study design of WHIMS has been described in detail previously41,42. Informed consent was obtained from all study subjects and the WHI project was reviewed and approved by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center Institutional Review Board. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Among 7479 initial WHIMS participants, we excluded 240 with only one WHIMS cognitive assessment, 519 who did not consent to DNA sharing, and 304 with no baseline DNA available, leaving 6416 participants whose DNA was extracted from blood samples collected at the baseline visit (1996–1999) and shipped to the University of Minnesota Genomics Center for DNA methylation (DNAm) measurement. Demographic and baseline health information on participants was collected from survey questionnaires at the WHI study baseline. Variables included chronological age at screening, educational attainment, household income, diabetes at screening (Do you have sugar diabetes or high blood sugar now? Yes, No), BMI (continuous), alcohol consumption (Do you still drink alcohol? Yes, No), history of CVD (defined as reporting ever CVD, cardiac arrest, congestive heart failure, stroke, cardiac catheterization, coronary bypass surgery, angioplasty of coronary arteries, carotid endarterectomy/angioplasty, atrial fibrillation, aortic aneurysm, angina, or CABG/PTCA at screening), self-reported race (classified as American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian/Other PI, Black, White, More than one race, or Unknown/Not reported), self-reported ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino, Not Hispanic/Latino, or Unknown/Not reported), MHT trial arm (E-alone intervention, E-alone control, E+P intervention, or E+P control), hypertension ever (Did a doctor ever say that you had hypertension or high blood pressure? Yes, No), and reporting ever requiring pills to treat high cholesterol (Yes, No). Race and Ethnicity are both socio-political constructs utilized here as proxies for both historical and ongoing differences in advantages arising from social, economic, environmental, and structural factors. Education was grouped together into categories of Didn’t go to school - Some high school, High school diploma/GED - Vocational school, Some college - College graduate, and Some post-graduate or professional - Doctoral degree. Adjudicated outcomes data including time from enrollment to death or end of follow-up and cause of death were acquired for all participants through to the end of the study period (February 15, 2025). Briefly, WHI has standardized protocols for the ascertainment and classification of CHD, heart failure and subtypes, as well as overall and cause-specific mortality43. Mortality was ascertained from death certificates and interviews of next of kin and adjudicated by expert physicians. Definite CHD, Cerebrovascular, Pulmonary Embolism, Possible CHD, Other Cardiovascular, and Unknown Cardiovascular causes of death were grouped together as general CVD-related mortality. Additionally, all causes of death linked to a form of cancer were grouped together as a general cancer-related mortality category.

Smoking exposure

We classified three-category smoking status (never, former, current) based on questions inquiring whether respondents had ever smoked at least 100 cigarettes and smoke cigarettes at the time of the baseline survey. We computed pack-years of smoking activity, accounting for reported years of smoking and the average number of cigarettes smoked per day. Age at smoking cessation (How old were you when you quit smoking regularly?) was reported in categorical bins of 5-year periods. For analysis, the categorical bins were recoded as the numeric value of the midpoint of the bin (e.g., 22 years was used for the 20–24 years bin). Participants reporting quitting smoking at less than 15 years or greater than 60 years of age were coded as 12 years and 62 years, respectively. Age at smoking initiation (How old were you when you first started smoking cigarettes regularly?) was classified into categorical bins of 5-year periods and recoded as the numeric value of the midpoint of the bin, while those reporting initiating smoking at less than 15 years or greater than 50 years were coded as 12 years and 52 years, respectively.

Genotype Data

Genotyping of single-nucleotide polymorphisms was conducted as previously described44. Kinship was determined from genotype data using KING45. For each pair of participants with estimated 3rd degree or closer relatedness, one was excluded, with the preference to retain cases, and, if case status matched, one of the pair was removed at random. To determine global ancestry principal components of each subject, genotype data were put through a standardized SNPweights pipeline, including up to 10,000 ancestry informative markers genotyped in a reference panel of 2911 subjects from six continental groups46,47. Concordance between samples was measured using the SNP fingerprinting probes built into the array. Illumina SNP probes were converted to genotype data. These genotypes were assessed for pairwise identity by descent (IBD) in PLINK. If a sample had a 100% match with an unexpected sample, the pair was compared against genotype array data. The sample from the pair that did not match the array genotype was excluded under the assumption that the array genotypes represented the “truth” dataset.

DNA methylation

Genomic DNA (1 μg) from blood samples was treated with sodium bisulfite using the EZ-96 DNAm kit (Zymo Research, CA) and quantified using the Illumina MethylationEPICv2.0 BeadChip Kit (EPICv2) run on an Illumina iScan System according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol (Illumina, CA, USA). Samples were removed if they did not meet Illumina’s recommended thresholds for each of the 17 control metrics. Sex was determined by clustering samples on the average intensity values of CpG sites on the X and Y chromosomes, and samples outside of the female cluster or exhibiting a mismatch between reported and detected sex were excluded48. Samples with mean bisulfite intensity values < 4000 were excluded (N = 1). Detection p-values were calculated using out-of-band (OOB) probes49. CpG measurements were set to missing if detection p > 0.05 or ≤3 detection beads (66,532 probes), and CpG sites were removed if >5% of samples were missing data. Samples were excluded if >5% of CpG sites had missing methylation values. In the remaining samples, OOB background correction, RELIC dye bias correction, and RCP probe type bias correction were applied using the R ENmix package50. Estimated blood cell proportions were generated using the IDOL algorithm and the FlowSorted.Blood.EPIC R package51,52. Participants failing any of the DNAm QC measures (N = 43), failing fingerprinting of SNP probes (N = 14), or exhibiting estimated 3rd degree or closer relatedness to another participant (N = 27) were further excluded, leaving 6005 participants available for analysis. EPICv2 probes mapped to the Y, M, or 0 chromosome, as well as probes recommended for masking were also removed, leaving 841,062 probes available for analysis53. Gene annotation for individual sites was obtained from a recent re-annotation of the EPICv2 manifest54.

Statistics and reproducibility

Epigenome-wide associations between each smoking exposure (three-category smoking status, smoking-pack-years, and years since smoking cessation) and DNAm beta values at each probe were assessed using multivariable linear regression models. Models were adjusted for age, educational attainment, household income, race, ethnicity, baseline alcohol consumption, baseline diabetes status, BMI, CVD history, estimated cell proportions (CD8T, CD4T, B cell, NK cell, Monocyte, Neutrophil), array plate, array row, and the first 5 genetic ancestry principal components (PCs). Genetic ancestry PCs were generated using the SNPweights pipeline46,47. Default analyses were conducted using complete cases, leaving an effective analytical sample size of N = 5311 for the three-category self-reported smoking analysis, N = 5200 for the smoking pack-years analysis, and N = 1984 former smokers for the smoking cessation EWAS. Test statistics were corrected for inflation using the Bacon procedure55. QQ plots and inflation factors before and after bacon-adjustment for all EWAS models are provided in the supplemental materials. (Supplementary Fig. 1) Bacon-corrected p-values < 9e-08 were considered significant (referred to as Bonferroni-significant throughout)56. FDR-corrected p-values were additionally calculated for comparison to previous studies reporting FDR-significant results.

To assess enrichment for biological pathways represented in the Bonferroni-significant results, we performed KEGG pathway analysis utilizing all CpG sites with bacon-corrected p-values < 9e-08 as inputs using gometh to account for bias introduced by uneven representation of genes on methylation arrays57. We additionally performed an enrichment analysis of disease-gene associations based on DisGeNET using the enrichDGN function from the DOSE R package, using all associations with p-values < 9e-08 as inputs58,59.

We used elastic net regression with tenfold cross-validation to generate DNAm-based predictors of each smoking phenotype using the R glmnet package60. To ensure applicability across several generations of the Illumina DNAm array, we subset to the 352,392 sites present in the processed DNAm dataset and found across the Illumina 450K, EPICv1, and EPICv2 arrays. Participants were randomly split into 80:20 training:testing sample split to develop a predictor of pack-years (Training N = 4629, Testing N = 1158), years since smoking cessation (Training N = 1748, Testing N = 438), and three-category smoking status (Training N = 4732, Testing N = 1183).

We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients and mean absolute errors (MAE) to assess the performance of the smoking pack-years and years since smoking cessation predictors in the entire training sample and testing sample. We used confusion matrices to assess the accuracy of the three-category smoking predictor using the R caret package61. To further assess the potential of using DNAm at a single CpG site for identifying smoking status, we utilized the pROC R package to identify optimal cutpoints of methylation beta values at cg05575921 (labeled as cg05575921_BC11 on the EPICv2 array) to distinguish current smokers from non-current smokers (0.7875276) and to distinguish never smokers from ever smokers (0.8719632) within the three-category smoking predictor training sample, then assessed accuracy within the testing sample62. Furthermore, we generated predicted smoking status using the EpiSmoker R package and smoking pack-years from GrimAge2 for comparison15,63.

We utilized Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate whether the DNAm-based biomarkers of smoking improve the prediction of CVD-related mortality relative to basic demographic and health variables among 5110 participants with smoking pack-years and complete covariate data available. Both reported and DNAm smoking pack-years were centered and scaled prior to analysis to allow interpretation of hazard ratios in terms of a 1 standard deviation increase in each measure. We grouped together CVD-related causes of death including Definite CHD, Cerebrovascular, Pulmonary Embolism, Possible CHD, Other Cardiovascular, and Unknown Cardiovascular. The base nested model included adjustment for age, educational attainment, household income, baseline alcohol consumption, baseline diabetes status, history of CVD at baseline, BMI, the first 5 genetic ancestry PCs, MHT arm, history of hypertension at baseline, and history of medication use for high cholesterol. Reported race and ethnicity were dropped as covariates due to small sample sizes within certain categories. We then examined change in model concordance when adding self-reported pack-years or DNAm pack-years to the base model, assessing significance of improvement in model fit relative to the base model with the LRT. Additionally, as a sensitivity analysis, we further examined the prediction of all-cause mortality and cancer-related mortality.

We further utilized two independent datasets to validate the novel smoking predictors. Firstly, we acquired processed 450K DNAm data consisting of 464 adults participants from the public GSE50660 dataset and tested each of our novel epigenetic predictors64. Second, we validated the WHI smoking predictors in a sample of 361 Chilean adults sampled from a subset of controls from a previous study65. Briefly, EPICv2 IDATs were processed using the recommended sesame pipeline, including masking of non-uniquely mapped probes, Infinium-I probe channel inference, non-linear dye bias correction, detection p-value masking using OOB, and background subtraction using noob. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients and MAE to assess performance of the smoking pack-years predictor and confusion matrices to assess performance of the three-category smoking status predictor.

Inclusion and ethics statement

The WHI project was reviewed and approved by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center (Fred Hutch) IRB in accordance with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services regulations at 45 CFR 46 (approval number: IR# 3467-EXT). Participants provided written informed consent to participate. Additional consent to review medical records was obtained through signed written consent. Fred Hutch has an approved FWA on file with the Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP) under assurance number 0001920. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki.
