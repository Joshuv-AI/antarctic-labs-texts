Transient epigenetic changes promote cardiomyocyte differentiation of the subcutaneous adipose stromal vascular fraction
Authors: Yusuke Shinjo, Shinichiro Takashima, Chiaki Goten, Oto Inoue, Daiki Hashimuko, Kosei Yamaguchi, Yusuke Takeda, Kenji Sakata, Masayuki Takamura, Soichiro Usui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe subcutaneous adipose-tissue-derived stromal vascular fraction (Sa-SVF) contains stem/progenitor cells that rarely transdifferentiate into beating cardiomyocytes. Previously, we developed a culture protocol in which adult murine inguinal Sa-SVF cells reproducibly transdifferentiate into beating cardiomyocyte-like cells (beating CMs) without any specific induction in the primary culture. However, the mechanism underlying the differentiation of the Sa-SVF toward the cardiac lineage is unclear.MethodsTo identify a key regulator of cardiac differentiation, we investigated sequential changes in global gene expression profiles of Sa-SVF cells during primary culture. Sa-SVF cells were isolated from adult murine inguinal subcutaneous fat pads and cultured using our beating CM induction method. At six time points during primary culture, total RNA was extracted and subjected to RNA-sequencing and quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis.ResultsBeating CMs appeared on day 14. Of 14,574 prefiltered genes, 749 showed significant expression changes (153 upregulated and 596 downregulated, > 2-fold) between days 7 and 14, coinciding with the appearance of beating CMs. Gene ontology analysis highlighted histone deacetylase (HDAC)-associated genes, expression of which was transiently suppressed during the period when cardiac sarcomere-related genes and the transcription factor Mef2c were upregulated. Treatment with an HDAC inhibitor increased cardiac troponin T expression on day 28. Furthermore, treatment of Mef2c-transduced Sa-SVF cells with an HDAC inhibitor markedly augmented cardiac troponin T expression (18-fold vs. control).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that transient epigenetic modulation, particularly involving class II HDACs, is associated with and may facilitate cardiomyocyte-like differentiation of Sa-SVF cells. This study provides a mechanistic framework for combining epigenetic modulation with transcriptional programming to enhance SVF-based cardiac regenerative strategies.

Introduction

Cardiomyocyte regeneration in adult mammals is limited [1]. The subcutaneous adipose-tissue-derived stromal vascular fraction (Sa-SVF) contains multipotent stem/progenitor cells [2,3]. Compared with induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [4,5], Sa-SVF cells have several advantages, including ease of large-scale harvest, low immunogenicity, low tumorigenicity, and reduced cost [6,7]. The Sa-SVF has been tested for regeneration therapy in patients with multiple diseases, including acute myocardial infarction [8] and chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy [9]. The primary mechanism of action of the Sa-SVF is paracrine [10], and transdifferentiation into beating cardiomyocytes is limited. Consequently, application of the Sa-SVF to treat advanced heart failure, in which many cardiomyocytes are lost, remains challenging [11]. We recently developed a culture protocol in which murine Sa-SVF cells reproducibly transdifferentiate into beating ventricular-type cardiomyocytes without induction in primary culture [12]. Furthermore, myocyte-specific enhancer factor 2c (Mef2c) triggered transdifferentiation of Sa-SVF cells into beating cardiomyocytes. Transduction of Sa-SVF cells with Mef2c using a lentivirus vector increased cardiomyocyte differentiation [12]. However, the yield of cardiomyocytes was small, and the precise mechanisms of transdifferentiation remained unknown. In this study, we investigated the temporal changes in the global transcriptome of Sa-SVF cells in primary culture, in which beating cardiomyocytes appeared, and found critical molecular functions involved in the transdifferentiation of the Sa-SVF into cardiomyocytes.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J female mice (age 8–12 weeks) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All experiments were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee at the Institute for Experimental Animals, Kanazawa University, and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

Isolation of mouse Sa-SVF cells

Bilateral inguinal subcutaneous adipose tissues without lymph nodes, weighing approximately 500 mg, were obtained from female C57BL/6J mice (age 8–12 weeks). The Sa-SVF cells were isolated as described previously [12]. Briefly, the tissues were digested using a Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in serum-free Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM)/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C using gentleMACS (Miltenyi Biotec). The digested tissue was thoroughly pipetted approximately 10 times in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (F7524, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), filtered through a 100-μm nylon mesh, and then centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min. After washing with DMEM/F-12 with FBS and centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in BD Pharm Lyse Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and incubated for 5 min to lyse red blood cells. Cell culture

Sa-SVF cells were counted using a TC20 automated cell counter (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and seeded on a standard uncoated tissue culture plate (Nunc) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in minimal growth medium (GM) consisting of Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Medium (IMDM; I3390, Sigma-Aldrich), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mmol/L l-glutamine (Wako, Osaka, Japan), 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 0.1 mmol/L 2-mercaptoethanol (StemSure) (Wako) at 5000 live cells/cm2, and then cultured under standard conditions (37 °C with 5% CO2). They were treated with the indicated concentration of pracinostat (SB939) (S1515; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA), a pan-histone deacetylase inhibitor.

Intracellular calcium ion imaging

The beating Sa-SVF cells were washed with PBS twice and loaded with 4 μM Fluo-8AM ester (AAT Bioquest, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) in Hank's buffer with 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HHBS, AAT Bioquest) for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were washed twice with HHBS, and fluorescence images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-8000, Keyence, Tokyo, Japan) operating in video-recording mode (30 frames/s).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA (15 ng) was extracted from Sa-SVF cells using an ISOSPIN Cell & Tissue RNA Kit (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan) or NucleoSpin RNA XS (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) for small sample volumes. The quality of the RNA was verified (RNA integrity number > 8) using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent 6000 Nano Assay, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Total RNA was amplified to produce cDNA, and cDNA libraries for RNA-Seq were constructed using a SMART-Seq HT PLUS Kit (R400749; Takara Bio USA, CA, USA). The libraries were sequenced to generate an average of 26.7 million paired-end 150-bp reads using the NovaSeq6000 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The RNA-Seq fragment count was normalized relative to the fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM).

The RNA-seq data were analyzed using Subio Platform ver. 1.24 and the Subio Basic Plug-in (Subio Inc. Aichi, Japan). The raw signal intensity (FPKM value) was log2-transformed and normalized via 90th percentile normalization. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups, Welch's t-test was applied. Genes with a fold change > 2 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Pathway analysis of the altered genes was performed using MetaCore software (Clarivate, London, UK).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA (100 ng) was used to generate cDNA with reverse transcription reactions using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), qPCR MasterMix Plus Low ROX (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium) was used on a QuantStudio 12K Flex (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The relative expression was normalized to the endogenous control; beta-actin (Mouse ACTB, 4352341E) was assessed using the comparative CT method, correcting for the amplification efficiency of the primers, and each sample was analyzed in duplicate.

Transfection of Sa-SVF cells

Endogenous HDAC isoform levels in Sa-SVF cells were knocked down by transfection with the specific Hdac siRNAs (Hdac 4 Silencer Select: s101932, Hdac 7 Silencer Select: s80225, Hdac 8 Silencer Select: s88696, or negative control: 4390843; all Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfection was performed at ∼50% confluence on day 7 after seeding at 5000/cm2 on 6-well culture plates, followed by incubation for 24 h according to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium.

Transduction of Sa-SVF cells

Sa-SVF cells were transduced with Mouse LentiORF Particles (pLenti-C-mGFP-P2A-Puro; Origene, Rockville, MD, USA): Mef2c (NM_025282, MR226865L4V) for overexpression, or LentiORF Control Particles (pLenti-C-mGFP-P2APuro, PS100093V, Origene) as an empty vector control. Cells at ∼50% confluence on days 5–7 after seeding SVF at 5000/cm2 on 12-well culture plates were incubated for 24 h in 500 μL of minimal medium at a multiplicity of infection of 10. Subsequently, the lentivirus-containing medium was replaced with fresh medium.

Statistics

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error. Groups were compared using Student's t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc test, as appropriate, in GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Time course change in the global transcriptome in primary culture of Sa-SVF cells

Sa-SVF cells, isolated and cultured according to the established protocol (Fig. 1a; see Methods), reproducibly yielded spontaneously beating cell clusters in primary culture. At around day 14, round cells appeared (Fig. 1b, Supplementary video S1), began beating, and elongated into tube-shaped beating cells that aggregated into clusters (Fig. 1c, Supplementary video S2). The number of tube-shaped colonies and the size of the beating cell clusters increased until day 21 (Fig. 1d and e). The round cells showed an increased intracellular calcium concentration, which began to oscillate, as the cells elongated and formed a cluster. By day 28, multiple cells in these clusters began to synchronize, contracting and relaxing (Supplementary videos S3 and S4). These findings suggested that the beating cells observed in the Sa-SVF cells were functionally united.

To examine temporal changes in gene expression, we performed RNA-Seq analysis of Sa-SVF cell groups, from which total RNA was collected at days 0, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 of primary culture (Fig. 1f). After excluding genes expressed below the level of sensitivity in all groups, unsupervised clustering was performed on 14,574 prefiltered genes. The heatmap image of the global transcriptome showed that each group was aligned in temporal order (Fig. 1g). Transcriptome changes were most pronounced at days 7–14. Expression of Tnnt2, encoding cardiac troponin T, began to increase on day 7, while that of Tnnt1 and Tnnt3, coding slow and fast skeletal troponin T, respectively, did not change throughout culture (Fig. 1h). Myl2, coding myosin light chain (MLC) ventricular type, increased on day 21, whereas Myl7, coding MLC atrial type, and Myh6, coding alpha myosin heavy chain (MHC), did not change. qPCR analysis confirmed that the expression of Tnnt2 and Myl2 was increased on day 28 (Fig. 1i).

Transcription-associated gene expression changed between days 7 and 14 in Sa-SVF primary culture

Because the profile of global gene expression on day 14 was very different from that on day 7, during which cardiac differentiation of the Sa-SVF occurred, we hypothesized that the expression of the key regulators responsible for Sa-SVF cardiac differentiation changed between days 7 and 14. Therefore, we next examined the difference in gene ontology between days 7 and 14. For 14,574 prefiltered genes, class comparison analysis (>2-fold, P < 0.05) identified 596 downregulated and 153 upregulated genes on day 14 compared to day 7 (Fig. 2a). Gene ontology analysis revealed that the downregulated genes were associated with transcription coactivator binding, transcription factor binding, transcription coregulator binding, and histone modifying activity (Fig. 2b). The upregulated genes were associated with RNA polymerase II sequence-specific DNA binding transcription factor recruiting activity and the extracellular matrix structural constituent (Fig. 2c). The pathway analysis demonstrated that a set of downregulated genes, which reportedly function with other genes in the group, included histone deacetylase (HDAC) and O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (Fig. 2d). HDAC isoforms were transiently suppressed in Sa-SVF primary culture

In RNA-Seq analysis, Hdac 4, 5, and 7, but not Hdac 8 or 9, were expressed at higher levels relative to the mean of all genes in the Sa-SVF on day 0 before primary culture. Expression of Hdac 4, 5, and 7 decreased on days 7, 3, and 14, respectively (Fig. 3a). In the qPCR analysis showing expression levels relative to beta-actin, those of Hdac 4, 5, and 7 were 3–10-fold higher than those of Hdac 8 and 9. HDAC inhibitor augmented cardiac differentiation

We next investigated the impact of HDACs on cardiomyocyte differentiation in a primary culture of Sa-SVF cells. Among the isoforms, the knockdown of both HDAC4 and HDAC7 using small interfering RNA (siRNA) increased cardiac troponin T (Tnnt2) expression, but not the knockdown of either or HDAC8 silencing (Fig. S1). Supplementation of Sa-SVF cells with pracinostat, a pan-HDAC inhibitor (HDACi) (150 nM), from days 3 to 10 resulted in a 10-fold increase in Tnnt2 expression (Fig. 4a). By contrast, HDACi treatment tended to decrease Pparg (Fig. 4b) and Col2a1 (Fig. 4c) expression. As reported previously, transduction of Sa-SVF cells with Mef2c resulted in a five-fold increase in Tnnt2 expression. Discussion

We investigated the mechanism by which murine Sa-SVF cells transdifferentiate into cardiomyocytes in primary culture. Temporal transcriptome analysis revealed a marked shift in global gene expression between days 7 and 14, coinciding with the appearance of beating cardiomyocytes. RNA-Seq and gene ontology analysis revealed that the specific isoforms of HDACs expressed in Sa-SVF cells were transiently suppressed, and inhibition of HDACs augmented cardiomyocyte differentiation.

The stromal vascular fraction harvested from adult subcutaneous adipose tissue consists of heterogeneous cell populations [13,14]. A subset of Sa-SVF cells that was seeded on non-coated plastic dishes for cell culture reproducibly formed round progenitors by day 14, which elongated into beating cardiomyocyte-like cells without induction by day 28, as reported previously [12]. These progenitors appeared randomly on the plate to form a cluster, and other cells simultaneously differentiated into different cell types, including adipocytes, chondrocytes, and skeletal myocytes [12]. However, the factors regulating cardiomyocyte transdifferentiation remained unclear. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct global changes between days 0 and 3 and between days 7 and 14. Importantly, the expression of 749 genes changed significantly between days 7 and 14, the critical window in which beating progenitors and cardiomyocytes emerged. Gene ontology analysis identified the suppression of HDACs and O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GluNAc) transferase (OGT)-associated genes and induction of Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4)-associated genes as key features of this stage.

Histone acetylation is a fundamental epigenetic mechanism that regulates transcription [[15], [16], [17]]. Acetylation of histone lysine residues neutralizes their positive charges, leading to chromatin relaxation and enhanced transcriptional accessibility, in which deacetylation promotes chromatin compaction and transcription repression. The balance between HDACs and histone acetyltransferases (HATs) controls developmental processes and influences disease. The HDACs consist of a molecular family of 11 isoforms and sirtuins, composing class I HDACs (HDAC1, 2, 3, and 8); class II HDACs (HDAC4, 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10); class III HDACs (Sirt1–7); and class IV HDAC (HDAC11) [18]. Our RNA-Seq analysis showed that the specific HDAC isoforms were transiently suppressed from days 7 to 14, coinciding with the appearance of cardiomyocytes. qPCR analysis confirmed that HDAC4 and HDAC7 were highly expressed in freshly isolated Sa-SVF cells and decreased during early culture, and then partially recovered by day 28. Both HDAC4 and HDAC7 are classified as class II HDACs and are preferentially expressed in cardiac tissue [19,20], suggesting that their transient suppression facilitated cardiac differentiation. Consistent with this, knocking down both HDAC4 and HDAC7 augmented Tnnt2 expression, whereas silencing either HDAC4 or HDAC7 individually, or silencing class I HDAC8, did not. These findings suggest that the knockdown of multiple class II HDACs, rather than individual knockdowns, are required to promote Sa-SVF transdifferentiation into cardiomyocytes.

HDACs regulate transcription not only through histone modification but also independently of histones [21]. We previously identified Mef2c as a key transcription factor driving Sa-SVF cells transdifferentiation into cardiomyocytes [12]. HDAC class II represses gene expression by directly binding Mef2c and recruiting transcriptional repressors and epigenetic regulators to DNA promoter regions [18]. Class II HDAC–Mef2c binding is regulated by mechanisms such as phosphorylation and oxidation. Specifically, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase 2 (CaMKII) phosphorylates class II HDAC, allowing a chaperone protein to bind and sequester class II HDAC from Mef2c, after which it is transported to the cytoplasm. Reactive oxygen species can also drive class II HDAC out of the nucleus [18,22]. Blocking or knocking down the catalytic domain of class II HDAC [23], or disrupting its association with Mef2c [24], may help recruit HATs and enhance Mef2c transcription [18,25]. In this study, combining HDAC inhibition with Mef2c transduction synergistically enhanced cardiomyocyte differentiation. This finding implies that the HDAC inhibitor may have increased histone accessibility and modified the associated non-histone proteins, thereby boosting Mef2c transcription efficiency, which is critical for cardiomyocyte differentiation. Dual modulation of Mef2c and HDAC activity is a promising strategy for enhancing cardiac regenerative programming.

Nevertheless, several questions remain. HDAC inhibition promoted cardiac differentiation, while suppressing adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation. The regulation of HDACs is complex, as inhibition can both activate and repress gene expression depending on the context [17,26]. Additional studies using isoform-specific knockdown or genetic modification are required to clarify the contributions of individual HDACs, and an optimal combination for promoting Sa-SVF transdifferentiation into cardiomyocytes. The precise mechanisms underlying the uncoupling of HDACs with Mef2c in Sa-SVF cells also remain to be investigated. Furthermore, in our data, OGT and KLF4 emerged as transcriptional regulators associated with cardiomyocyte differentiation, and their mechanistic roles warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that dynamic epigenetic regulation, particularly transient suppression of HDAC activity, is associated with the acquisition of cardiomyocyte-like properties in Sa-SVF cells. Our findings suggest that modulation of HDAC signaling creates a permissive transcriptional environment for Mef2c-driven cardiac gene expression. Although additional functional and mechanistic studies are necessary to fully establish cardiomyocyte identity and define causal pathways, the present results provide a conceptual basis for integrating epigenetic modulation with gene-based approaches to improve SVF-mediated cardiac regenerative strategies.
