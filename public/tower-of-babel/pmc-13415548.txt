Impacts of structural and soil parameters on the seismic response of neighbouring buildings: a numerical investigation
Authors: Mohammed A. Abdulaziz, Mohammed J. Hamood, Mohammed Y. Fattah, Mo’men Ayasrah, Venkataramana Guntreddi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a numerical investigation of structure-soil-structure interaction (SSSI) effects on the seismic performance of adjacent buildings. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on small-scale experimental models or limited parametric ranges, the present work introduces three novel aspects: (i) evaluation of SSSI effects at farther normalized building distances (d/a = 4 and 5) beyond those previously tested, (ii) quantification of seismic response changes due to asymmetric building orientations, and (iii) comparison of box versus equivalent pipe column cross-sections under identical soil and seismic excitation conditions. A finite element model was developed and validated against existing experimental data (Ali Al-Gharbi earthquake, PGA = 0.1 g), showing acceptable convergence with a PGA difference of approximately 13.4%. Subsequently, a parametric study was conducted using the Koyna earthquake record (PGA = 0.33 g) on large-scale, three-story adjacent steel buildings with one embedded story. The major quantified findings justify the following conclusions: (1) The peak ground acceleration (PGA) and roof displacement are maximized at a normalized distance of d/a = 4 (1.37 g and 151.5 mm, respectively), representing increases of 33% and 1.4% compared to d/a = 2, indicating a critical distance threshold for seismic design. (2) Asymmetric building orientation increases PGA by 14.6% but reduces frictional shear stress by 5.2%, revealing a trade-off that can be exploited in dense urban planning. (3) Replacing box columns with equivalent pipe columns increases PGA by 20.4%, roof displacement by 2.25%, and frictional shear stress by 3.0%, demonstrating the superior seismic performance of box sections. These findings provide directly actionable design guidance for minimum separation gaps, orientation strategies, and column section selection in earthquake-prone urban environments, thereby advancing both the scientific understanding and practical application of SSSI in seismic engineering.

Introduction

Earthquake is one of the most damaging catastrophes that the world may experience. During the past years and till the current day, many parts of the globe has undergone from several brutal earthquakes, such as those occurred in Japan, Syria, Morocco, and Turkey. Therefore, the topic of earthquake engineering has become one of the most significant fields of study that attracts researchers. For the sake of realizing this field related to the specific part of our study, there are some significant aspects that should be taken into account considered for further inspection such as the soil-structure interaction (SSI) and the structure-soil-structure interaction (SSSI). The term SSI is known as the association of structure, foundation, and soil. While the term SSSI is defined as the link between neighboring structures with the soil. Previously, these aspects have not been considered in investigating the seismic behavior or the design of structures lying in seismic zones. So, the participation of soil in the analytical or experimental approaches related to studying the seismic responses of buildings was ignored. It is noticed that the neglection of soil led to undesired consequences including severe damages in human lives and structures. The SSI comprising the structure, the foundation, and the soil, has to be fully comprehended for replicating the structure’s actual behavior during the tremor activity. This interaction can affect the structural responses by magnifying or diminishing them depending on many factors like the attributes of the structure, and the characteristics of the soil. The SSI may also have the capability to make the seismic demand on a structure larger than the case of the fixed base situation1,2.

Regarding the experimental investigations, Hosseinzadeh and Nateghi3 performed shaking table examinations to inspect the influences that the SSI can make on the dynamic response of two nearby models of steel structure. Their results exhibited that the largest impact was caused by the inertial interaction, while the kinematic interaction had the least effect. The emergence of SSI in low-rise buildings had been resulted by the foundation’s horizontal and rocking motions. There was another reason of the occurrence of SSI emergence attributed to the ground rocking movement caused by the high-rise buildings. A two-phase test was conducted by Aldaikh et al.4 via a shaking table system to inspect the seismic behavior of a steel-aluminum structure model, and how can it be influenced by SSSI. The first phase was performed on two adjacent structures, while the second phase was performed on three adjacent structures. The findings showed that the effect of height had a clear impact on the peak responses of acceleration and seismic power. This impact can have appreciative or unfavorable consequences on a structure in the presence of close buildings, compared to the case when the structure is alone.

In order to inspect the dynamic SSSI of two neighboring sway structures, Jabary and Madabhushi5 executed geotechnical centrifuge experiments taking into account duplicating domestic buildings having near distances in an urban area prone to seismic activity. The test had been conducted on structures attached to outer instruments of damping utilizing tuned mass damper (TMD) setups via two situations. The first situation was represented by the presence of the damping instrument, while the second situation was represented by the absence of this instrument. The outcomes illustrated that there was a significant impact on the ultimate building roof acceleration response with inter-story drift for a nearby structure resulted from the TMD considering the SSSI, and this technique was also productive through earthquakes of low-intensity nature. On the other hand, the TMD could damage adjacent buildings. Kirkwood and Dashti6 realized the critical effects of the building distance and the properties of ground motion on the behavior of the close structures founded on a liquefiable and mounded soil profile. The outcomes indicated that the utilization of suitable layouts with the standard techniques of mitigating the seismic impacts subjected to the superstructure could enhance the settlement-rotation response of the close buildings. The main goal of Barrios et al.7 was to examine the dynamic response of single degree of freedom adjacent models standing on sand contained in a laminar box. These models had been subjected to synthetic ground motions and impulse loads. This investigation involved inspecting several fundamental frequencies with slenderness ratios. The results displayed that the presence of a building had remarkable effects on the seismic behavior of the neighboring building compared to the case when this neighboring building was alone, like affecting the building displacement, and the maximum accelerations.

Ngo et al.8 concentrated on examining the influences of SSSI on the response of nearby buildings. For the purpose of achieving this goal, they conducted dynamic geotechnical centrifuge tests on aluminum frame models. The findings revealed that the effect of SSSI is evident, especially on smaller structures. Furthermore, the structures’ seismic reaction can be affected via some factors related to SSSI like distance between structures, height, and mass ratio. Based on the branch mode approach, He and Jiang9 applied seismic excitation during implementing a substructure shake table test (SSTT) to find out the principles and system of equipment-adjacent structure-soil interaction (EASSI). The EASSI system had been classified into three substructures including the soil, the adjacent structure, and the equipment-single structure. Their study also contained inspection of some variables such as the position of the key structure, mass ratio of the equipment, and frequency ratio. The results exhibited the significant role of soil in raising the flexibility and the damping response of the system, which led to diminishing the frequency of the system.

Abdulaziz et al.10 performed seismic investigations using a shaking table to give panoramic information about the SSSI. Their study considered checking how far could the building height affect the seismic behavior of two neighboring structure models made from steel standing on sand. The seismic process of their study involved assessing the responses of a three-story building next to buildings with different heights (three, four, and five stories). The metrics used to represent these responses were building acceleration, building roof displacement, soil pressure, and foundation rotation. The outcomes clearly demonstrated that the highest building had maximized almost all the responses of the inspected adjacent building, while the shortest building with the identical height to the examined model offered the ultimate soil pressure behavior.

Abdulaziz et al.11 conducted a sequence of seismic examinations using a shaking table system to determine the SSSI behavior of adjacent structures and provide helpful knowledge on significant factors that should have been considered previously in seismic analysis and design. They examined the impact of structure orientation and distance between adjacent structures on their dynamic response. Test results illustrated that the diversity of buildings’ orientation with distances significantly affects the SSSI behavior of the neighboring buildings. Furthermore, the orientation perpendicular to the direction of the earthquake wave offered the maximum impact on the dynamic responses at the far distance, while the parallel orientation gave the ultimate effect at the medium distance.

Abdulaziz et al.12 intended to give a comprehensive awareness of some substantial factors, including the influences of the embedment level of a building and the presence of dead loading, which affect the structure-soil-structure interaction (SSSI) of nearby buildings that needed to be investigated thoroughly in the preceding studies related to seismic analysis, design, and earthquake hazard assessment. The first test includes examining the effect of several levels of embedment of one of the adjacent buildings, and the second test includes exploring the impact of dead loading in different situations. Test results indicated that these factors have critical implications for the SSSI response. Additionally, the increase in embedment level led to mitigating the impact of the earthquake wave on the examined structure’s seismic response. At the same time, the dead loading conditions had some positive and negative aspects on the seismic responses of the tested structure.

Referring to the theoretical studies, the object of the study of Behnamfar and Sugimura13 was to examine the seismic responses of real-life paired nearby buildings experienced earthquake activities. The tested buildings belong to Tohoku University which lies in Sendai, Japan. In order to collect the structures’ responses during the seismic activity, they equipped them with appropriate devices. They utilized recorded data to check the analytical approach. Their investigation also contained evaluating the roof and base acceleration spectrum. For the purpose of modeling these buildings, they executed a process of two-dimensional boundary element. The results displayed that the SSSI analysis could be significantly helpful for engineering objectives.

To examine the impact of SSI on seismic reaction of neighboring buildings, Yahyai et al.14 investigated two close 32-story buildings considering displacements, base shear, and time period. In order to complete their study, they had also taken into account inspecting some factors like the influence of soil type, and diverse distances between the buildings. The findings revealed the critical impact of the association between structure and soil on the seismic response of buildings, especially when increasing the period of displacement and base shear. ANSYS 5.4 was utilized to simulate the buildings via two-dimensional frame. Naserkhaki and Pourmohammad15 conducted a numerical study of the consequences of SSI and SSSI on identical buildings’ behavior throughout earthquake events. The buildings had been modeled as shear buildings, while the soil had been modeled by a discrete model symbolizing a viscoelastic half-space. The soil here was exposed to seismic acceleration. Their study also involved forging building motion equations by analytical methods considering SSSI, SSI, and Fixed-Based (FB). Based on the study results, they concluded that there was a major role of the soil in affecting the structures’ seismic reaction. Moreover, neglecting the interaction between the soil and structure could have unfavorable consequences on the structures due to the unbalanced arrangement of their responses.

Farghaly16 focused on examining the structural behavior of three-dimensional two close structures with various heights. These structures were affixed by viscous dampers arranged in a unique formation in the plane and grounded on distinctive sorts of soil. For the sake of giving a realistic representation of the response of the nearby structures, the author utilized a three-dimensional Winkler model. He utilized SAP2000n to simulate the system. The results showed that the soil type had an evident effect on altering the behavior of the whole structural system with the viscous dampers.

A numerical inspection was implemented by Wang17 to study the dynamic connection through the soil between a structure supported by piles and a subterranean station on soil of viscous-elastic type via subjecting shear wave. The author executed the test with frequency domain by ANSYS software. The test outcomes illustrated that orientation of the shaking in addition to the structures’ arrangement had a substantial impact on amplifying or mitigating the seismic responses of the structures. The results also indicated that if the building has a fundamental frequency identical to the frequency of the free field, this will significantly influence the seismic behavior of the nearby underground structure.

Using two-dimensional ambient, Bybordiani and Arici18 concentrated on investigating the SSSI behavior between close structures. This experiment considered creating groups of buildings on the viscoelastic half-space. These groups had been produced by the finite element modeling with varied heights including 5, 15, and 30 stories. They concluded that the interaction between similar buildings of lower heights had slight effects on the seismic response.

In order to examine how can the SSSI affect neighboring buildings’ behavior, Ada and Ayvaz19 took into consideration the impact of the soil on this behavior. Their study involved utilizing the direct approach of the finite element method during seismic activity to investigate the resulted seismic effects caused by some factors such as soil stiffness, distance, building height, and structures formation. The tested structures were of diverse heights (3, 6, and 12 stories). They also conducted a comparison study between two cases: the case of soil base, and the case of fixed base. and the number of stories in the structure. The authors found out that there were a possibility of occurring favorable or adverse consequences as a result of the existence of nearby buildings in modifying the responses of structures based on the dynamic features of the system.

To inspect the impacts of dynamic SSSI, Gan et al.20 performed a numerical investigation of three close buildings supported by foundations of a pile-raft type grounded on a viscoelastic half-space. The buildings were positioned in a direction parallel to the orientation of the seismic activity. In order to reflect the soil role, they executed the method of Davidenkov model. The study also considered some aspects like the structures’ first natural periods, height, structure kind, and stretch between the buildings. The investigation results revealed the possibility of these aspect of adjusting the SSSI effects which were capable of magnifying or diminishing the seismic response of the structures.

Abdel Raheem et al.21 performed a mathematical modeling to evaluate the changes of the seismic design demands of symmetric neighboring buildings resulted from the impacts of eccentric pounding. Their investigation also contained inspecting some factors such as the effects of building’s stories number, and buildings arrangement. The results showed that the properties of the vibration of the buildings and input of the ground motion, the buildings arrangement, and the earthquake activity orientation played a critical role in controlling the dangerous results of the seismic pounding.

The main goal of Düzgün et al.22 was to discover the optimal situations that lead to minimize the base shear and roof displacement throughout a seismic excitation taking into account the SSI for neighboring structures. So, they tested two neighboring structures linked by viscous dampers of fluid type using two-dimensional finite element technique via the Taguchi method. It was found that the soil sort had the most critical effect on the response of the system, while the slightest response had been got when using just single damper.

Khatami et al.23 inspected the periods of two adjacent linear and nonlinear buildings through an innovative equation to check the least distance between them for preventing the hazards could be caused from pounding between them during earthquakes. The findings showed that the introduced equation can be helpful to prevent possible seismic threats of pounding on stiff buildings via assuring a suitable spacing between the buildings.

Chen and Wu24 conducted an examination to observe the seismic response of two nigh buildings attached by viscous dampers, considering various positions of the dampers. Their examination involved creating analytical models for studying different dynamic structural characteristics. The results illustrated the significance of dampers in magnifying the behavior of the floors that were not prepared with dampers.

Favvata25 investigated the association between the confined distance separating the nearby structures which could be of an important participation resisting pounding of structures, in addition to its role in improving the seismic response of a real reinforced concrete structure at several stages of seismic demand. The experiment included evaluating the problem situation of the pounding of inter-story for nine stages of seismic demand by 14 seismic motivations for each stage. Investigation outcomes exhibited that the distance between structures was smaller than that required by Eurocode 8 terms.

For the sake of observing the impacts of pounding and SSSI during earthquake activity on the behavior of close buildings supported by shallow foundation and standing on soft soil, Raheem et al.26 created finite element models for assessing the influences of specific aspects like soil circumstances, distance, and pounding on the structures’ properties of vibration and seismic behavior.

The main focus of Kandemir and Jankowski27 was to examine the impact of pounding between adjacent buildings of various number of stories on their seismic response considering SSI. Their study also encompassed inspecting the changes in the frequency of the pounding force between the cases of flexible and fixed bases of the structures using the Wavelet transform. For the purpose of ensuring secure application of the experiments, they equipped the close floors of the buildings with Linear Viscous Dampers (LVDs) to protect them from pounding risks. According to the test results, they concluded that SSI can have an important role in mitigating the pounding effects during earthquake activities.

Raheem et al.28 performed a numerical investigation to estimate the likely threats that could occur as a result of seismic pounding between nigh buildings to offer adequate knowledge required for code modifying. The authors took into consideration inspecting different factors like distance, height, structures, and structures’ position. The findings revealed that the harshness of the pounding influences could be affected by specific aspects like earthquake activity orientation, dynamic features of the buildings.

Regarding the aforementioned relevant studies and based on the comprehensive review by Abdulaziz et al.29, a clear research gap exists in the understanding of structure-soil-structure interaction (SSSI) effects under several critical but previously unexamined conditions. Specifically, no previous study has systematically investigated: (i) the seismic response of adjacent buildings at normalized distances beyond d/a = 3, particularly at d/a = 4 and d/a = 5, where the interaction may diminish or exhibit non-linear trends; (ii) the effect of asymmetric building orientation (different arrangement) compared to the conventional symmetrical arrangement under identical soil and excitation conditions; and (iii) the influence of column cross-section type (box vs. equivalent pipe section) on SSSI responses, including acceleration, roof displacement, and foundation-soil frictional shear stress.

Therefore, the novelty of the present study is threefold:

Extended distance analysis: This study quantifies SSSI effects at farther normalized distances (d/a = 4 and 5) than previously tested experimentally (maximum d/a = 3 in Abdulaziz et al.11, revealing that peak seismic responses occur at d/a = 4 rather than at the closest distance—a critical threshold not documented before.Asymmetric building orientation: Unlike all prior experimental works that used identical (symmetrical) building arrangements, this study compares identical vs. different arrangements of adjacent buildings, demonstrating that orientation asymmetry can increase PGA by 14.6% but reduce frictional shear stress by 5.2%.Column cross-section comparison: For the first time, this study compares box columns with equivalent pipe columns under SSSI conditions, showing that box sections provide superior seismic resistance (20.4% lower PGA and 3.0% lower shear stress) despite having similar mass and dimensions.

The key objective of this paper is to examine the effects of these three novel factors and their possible consequences—whether beneficial or unfavorable—during earthquake activity, thereby bridging the identified gap in SSSI knowledge. To accomplish this goal, a numerical modeling approach was adopted in two steps: first, validation against existing experimental work10 to establish modeling accuracy; second, a parametric study investigating the three novelty aspects mentioned above. This research focuses on presenting helpful, quantified insights into SSSI for earthquake engineering practice, covering positive and negative effects that could be of significant importance for seismic analysis, design, and hazard assessment in dense urban environments.

Boundary conditions and load steps

Boundary conditions The lateral boundaries of the soil domain are fixed against horizontal displacements perpendicular to the shaking direction but are allowed to move in the shaking direction (x-axis) to simulate free-field motion. The base of the soil domain is fixed in all directions. This simplified boundary condition is acceptable because the seismic input is applied as a base acceleration history, and the primary interest is in the differential response between adjacent buildings rather than absolute free-field motion.

Load steps A two-step analysis is performed.

Step 1 (Static): Gravity loads (self-weight of all components) are applied linearly. The Full Newton (Newton-Raphson) method is used to achieve equilibrium under static conditions.Step 2 (Dynamic): The earthquake acceleration history is applied as a base motion in the x-direction (horizontal shaking). The implicit direct integration method (Hilber-Hughes-Taylor algorithm with default parameters) is used, which is unconditionally stable for linear problems and provides robust convergence for the nonlinear contact interactions present.

Step unit

In this unit, the analysis steps of the seismic test are identified. Furthermore, the requested output can be specified directly from this step. Our test for all the cases contained two steps: the first step was a static analysis represented by the gravity load of the model, and the second step was a dynamic analysis represented by the time acceleration record of a past earthquake chosen for this test. This program uses the Full Newton Technique (Newton-Raphson Method) to solve complicated nonlinear problems. The solving process contains a mixture of incremental and iterative methods, defining convergence, determining the loads depending on their relation with time, and choosing the appropriate time increments. Interaction unit

This unit handles the different types of interactions, such as mechanical and thermal interactions, in addition to the constraints between various areas of the model. Regarding this study for all the cases, the models contained constraints of the tie type between the columns’ bases and the foundation, the beams and the steel slabs, and the steel slabs and the concrete slabs. Experimental work validation

In order to investigate the accuracy of modeling SSSI using ABAQUS software, the case of two three-story symmetrical adjacent buildings conducted by Abdulaziz et al.10 was selected, whose details are displayed in Fig. 1 with a clear distance between them taken from d/a = 1 as shown in Fig. 9. Figures 10, 11, 12, and 13 illustrate the dynamic behavior of one of these symmetrical buildings resulting from the neighboring building. Referring to Figs. 10 and 11, it is found that the waves’ shapes of acceleration and roof displacement responses for the experimental and numerical works are very close throughout the seismic activity time. The peak ground acceleration PGA values are 0.172 g, and 0.149 for the experimental and numerical work, respectively. The peak values of the roof displacement are − 34.4 mm and 31.9 mm for the experimental work and numerical work, respectively.

On the other hand, according to Figs. 12 and 13, it is noted that the waves’ shapes of the foundation rotation and soil pressure responses for the experimental and numerical works are different, with some convergence at certain points. This response contrast may be attributed to the complicated nature of the finite element analysis, especially for the soil. Furthermore, the numerical software might handle the problems with almost optimal situations without considering the actual boundary conditions of the soil container with the possible constraints that can be faced during the seismic excitation, the accuracy of the sensors, the noise of the system, accuracy of signal transmitting from the input motion device to the shaking table, or any blotch that could occur during the experimental work as explained in the study of Brandis et al.30 who stated that any differences existing between the numerical and experimental works could be attributed to the limitations of the experimental system that were not taken into consideration in the numerical modeling. The ultimate values of the foundation rotation are 0.59° and 0.47° for the experimental work and numerical work, respectively. The topmost values of the soil pressure response are 8.94 kPa and 6.74 kPa for the experimental and numerical work, respectively.

Generally, the results of the simulation process illustrated previously reveal an acceptable convergence between the experimental and numerical results. This provides an indicator that the adopted way of simulating the problem via the numerical software is accurate and effective. Furthermore, the software is precise and reliable for simulating these types of problems.

Effect of distance

According to this effect, and in order to offer a complete awareness of it, three situations of different distances are investigated: d/a = 2 (standard situation), d/a = 4, and d/a = 5, as exhibited in Fig. 14. Figures 15, 16, and 17 illustrate the dynamic behavior of one of these symmetrical buildings resulting from the neighboring building. According to the acceleration response, it is noticed that the maximum PGA occurred at d/a = 4 (middle distance) with a value of 1.37 g, and when the distance increased to d/a = 5 (far distance), the acceleration response began to decrease, with a PGA = 1.25 g. Regarding the roof displacement response, and just like the acceleration response, the maximum roof displacement occurred at d/a = 4, with a PGA = 151.5 mm, and this response also decreased at d/a = 5, with a PGA = 144.6 mm. The frictional shear stress had a different trend here because the ultimate response occurred at d/a = 2 (close distance) with a value of 625 kPa. So, whenever the distance increases, the frictional shear stress response diminishes. This result may be attributed to the close distance that may lead the neighboring building to generate severe friction shear stress amplified via the soil to the other building. However, this result is compatible with the one obtained by Ngo et al.8, which indicated that the most intense impacts could result between adjacent buildings spaced at a distance lower than the foundation width.

Generally, these significant findings are similar to that of the experimental work, which indicated that whenever the distance increased, the responses increased either, except that with the buildings of real dimensions, the limit of distance was larger with the ultimate response occurring at d/a = 4 rather than d/a = 2 for the experimental work of Abdulaziz et al.11. This fact may be attributed to some factors, like the difference in the boundary conditions between the experimental work and the numerical work, in addition to the difference in damping and friction effects between the small-scale and large-scale models.

Effect of building orientation

According to this effect, two different situations are investigated: two symmetrical adjacent buildings with identical arrangement (standard situation) and two symmetrical adjacent buildings with different arrangement, as exhibited in Fig. 18. The distance between the buildings was constant for all cases (d/a = 2).

Figures 19, 20, and 21 illustrate the dynamic behavior of one of these symmetrical buildings resulting from the neighboring building. Referring to the acceleration response, the PGA for the situation of identical arrangement was 1.03 g. For the second situation, when the arrangement of one of the adjacent buildings is changed, the PGA is raised to 1.18 g. According to the roof displacement response, and just like the acceleration response, the maximum roof displacement has just increased from 149.34 mm for the symmetrical arrangement situation to 149.98 mm for the diverse arrangement. On the other hand, the frictional shear stress decreased from 625 kPa to 592.34 kPa when the arrangement of the buildings was different.

Generally, this effect had positive and unfavorable sides that could affect the seismic responses of the adjacent buildings. The positive side was represented by mitigating the effect of frictional shear stress between the close buildings that could cause serious problems such as structural damages, foundation issues, or magnifying the vibrations. The unfavorable side was represented by the increase in the building roof displacement, which may cause pounding between the adjacent buildings. These results are compatible with the results of Wang17, who stated that the arrangement and orientation of the seismic shaking are very significant issues, and because of them, the seismic behavior of the structures’ behavior can have different trends, either magnifying or diminishing depending on the distance or the dynamic characteristics of the whole system.

Effect of column cross-section

According to this effect, two different situations were investigated: two symmetrical adjacent buildings having box cross-section columns (standard situation) with dimensions of 400 × 400 mm2 and 25 mm thickness, and two symmetrical adjacent buildings having equivalent pipe cross-section columns with dimensions of 250 mm radius and 30 mm thickness as exhibited in Fig. 22. Figures 23, 24 and 25 illustrate the dynamic behavior of one of these symmetrical buildings resulting from the neighboring building. Referring to the acceleration response, the PGA for the situation of box cross-section columns was 1.03 g. For the second situation, when the column cross-section of the adjacent buildings was changed to an equivalent pipe cross-section, the PGA was raised to − 1.24 g. According to the roof displacement response, and just like the acceleration response, the maximum roof displacement has just increased from 149.34 mm for the box cross-section columns situation to 152.7 mm for the equivalent pipe cross-section columns. Similarly, the frictional shear stress has also raised from 625 kPa to 643.6 kPa when the column cross-section was changed from a box type to an equivalent pipe type.

Generally, these results illustrate that the box cross-section columns are better at resisting all the seismic effects introduced in this study due to their higher moment of inertia, although the pipe cross-section had a slightly higher inertia force due to its heavier mass. These outcomes are consistent to some degree with the findings of Ismail et al.31, who indicated that the raft size with column stiffness can have a major influence on the seismic response of the building.

Innovation and practical utility of the dynamic analysis results

The dynamic analysis results presented in this study are not only novel but also carry significant practical utility for earthquake-resistant design in urban environments. Innovation lies in the systematic quantification of structure-soil-structure interaction (SSSI) effects under previously unexamined combinations of parameters—specifically, farther normalized distances (d/a = 4 and 5), asymmetric building orientations, and equivalent column cross-section substitutions (box vs. pipe) in large-scale models. Unlike prior experimental studies limited to small-scale or symmetric configurations, this numerical investigation provides new, validated insights into how SSSI can either amplify or mitigate seismic responses depending on the parameter set32.

From a practical utility perspective, the quantified findings offer direct design guidance:

The observed peak in PGA and roof displacement at d/a = 4 (1.37 g and 151.5 mm, respectively) identifies a critical distance range where seismic demands on adjacent buildings are maximized—information that can inform building codes regarding minimum separation gaps.The reduction in frictional shear stress (by 5.2%) when using asymmetric building orientations suggests a potential passive strategy to reduce foundation-soil distress in dense urban clusters.The superior performance of box columns over equivalent pipe columns (20.4% lower PGA and 3% lower frictional shear stress) provides clear, actionable recommendations for structural engineers selecting column cross-sections in seismic zones.Thus, the dynamic analyses not only advance the scientific understanding of SSSI but also deliver directly applicable thresholds and design preferences that can improve the safety, resilience, and cost-effectiveness of adjacent buildings in earthquake-prone areas33.

Limitations of study

Boundary condition limitations

As detailed in Sect. 2.4, this study uses simplified boundary conditions without absorbing elements. Wave reflections from the lateral and base boundaries may corrupt the dynamic responses, particularly for absolute PGA and displacement values. The convergence study (Sect. 2.4.1) indicates that absolute values have an uncertainty of approximately ± 6% for the baseline configuration. Relative comparisons between parametric cases (e.g., d/a = 2 vs. d/a = 4) are less affected by systematic reflection artifacts because the same boundary conditions apply to all cases. Nevertheless, future studies should implement perfectly matched layers (PML) or viscous boundaries to eliminate this limitation. Readers should interpret absolute numerical values with appropriate caution.

Frictional shear stress as a response metric

The “frictional shear stress” reported in this study is computed from the penalty contact formulation (τ_contact = µ × p_contact) and is not checked against the soil’s Mohr-Coulomb yield criterion. For all configurations, τ_contact exceeds the soil’s shear strength (τ_max = σ’_v·tan φ ≈ 25–70 kPa) by factors of 6–25. This occurs because:

The contact algorithm operates independently of the soil element plasticity.The soil elements adjacent to the foundation have yielded, but the contact algorithm does not reduce τ_contact accordingly.The reported values represent elastic demand, not actual stresses.Therefore, the absolute τ_contact values should not be used for design. Only relative comparisons between configurations (e.g., τ decreases with increasing d/a) are meaningful. Future studies should implement a more sophisticated contact formulation that couples with soil plasticity or use a different interface element that enforces a shear strength limit.

Domain size justification

The soil domain dimensions (150 m × 30 m × 30 m) represent a compromise between computational cost and reflection management. The distance from the structures to the nearest lateral boundary is 71.5 m (half-length minus half-building width). Given the shear wave velocity $V_s = 150$ m/s, the travel time to the boundary is approximately 0.48 s. With absorbing lateral boundaries, outgoing waves are largely absorbed at first incidence, and reflected energy is reduced by approximately 70–80%. A convergence study (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Material) confirmed that increasing domain size to 200 m × 40 m × 40 m changed PGA predictions by less than 3%, indicating that the 150 m domain with absorbing boundaries is sufficient for engineering accuracy.
