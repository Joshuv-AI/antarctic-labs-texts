Safety of nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture as a novel food pursuant to regulation (EU) 2015/2283
Authors: EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA), Dominique Turck, Torsten Bohn, Montaña Cámara, Jacqueline Castenmiller, Stefaan De Henauw, Ángeles Jos, Alexandre Maciuk, Inge Mangelsdorf, Breige McNulty, Androniki Naska, Kristina Pentieva, Alfonso Siani, Frank Thies, Margarita Aguilera‐Gómez, Thomas Frenzel, Francesca Marcon, Harry J McArdle, Peter Moldeus, Monika Neuhäuser‐Berthold, Alexandros Siskos, Marcello Laganaro, Irene Nuin Garciarena, Francesco Suriano, Areti Favata, Anna‐Maria Pieger, Francesca Seminati, Karen Ildico Hirsch‐Ernst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, the EFSA Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA) was asked to deliver an opinion on a nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture as a novel food (NF) pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283. The NF is a mixture composed of a 1:1 combination of nonapeptide and pentapeptide. These two peptides are independently produced by solid‐phase chemical synthesis and have a defined amino acid sequence. The information provided on the production process, composition and specifications of the NF does not raise safety concerns. The NF is proposed to be used as a food supplement intended for adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women, at a maximum dose of 30 mg NF/day. Effects on the liver were observed in the two subchronic toxicity studies performed in rats and dogs. The lowest no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) was identified in a 90‐day study in dogs (40 mg NF/kg body weight (bw) per day). Applying the standard uncertainty factor of 200, the resulting safe dose is 0.2 mg/kg bw per day. This corresponds to a safe level of 14 mg NF/day for 70‐kg adults. Regarding the human intervention study provided by the applicant, uncertainties related to whether or not the test substance used corresponds to the NF do not allow to draw conclusions regarding the safety of the NF. The Panel concludes that the NF is safe when consumed at intake levels up to 14 mg NF/day by adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women.Keywords: chemical synthesis, food supplement, nonapeptide, novel foods, pentapeptide, peptides

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

On 18 March 2023, the applicant ‘Caregen Co., Ltd’ submitted a request to the European Commission in accordance with Article 10 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 1 to authorise the placing on the market of ‘nonapeptide‐pentapeptide mixture’ as a novel food.

The application requests to authorise the use of nonapeptide‐pentapeptide mixture in food supplements as defined in Directive 2002/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council 2 intended for the general population, excluding infants and young children. 3

The applicant has requested data protection under Article 26 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 for data in support of this request.

In accordance with Article 10(3) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, the European Commission asks the European Food Safety Authority to provide a scientific opinion on the safety of nonapeptide‐pentapeptide mixture as a novel food.

In this opinion on nonapeptide‐pentapeptide mixture as a novel food, the European Food Safety Authority should also document whether and to what extent the requirements of Article 26(2)(c) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 are fulfilled regarding the data for which the applicant is requesting data protection.

Methodologies

The assessment follows the methodology set out in the EFSA guidance on NF applications (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021) and the principles described in the relevant existing guidance documents from the EFSA Scientific Committee. The legal provisions for the assessment are laid down in Article 11 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 and in Article 7 of Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2469.

In 2025, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) published the Guideline on the Development and Manufacture of Synthetic Peptides (EMA, 2025), which addresses specific aspects regarding the manufacturing process, characterisation, specifications and analytical control for synthetic peptides. The relevant aspects for the safety assessment of synthetic peptides have been taken into consideration in the current assessment of the NF.

This assessment concerns only the risks that might be associated with the consumption of the NF under the proposed conditions of use and is not an assessment of the efficacy of the NF with regard to any claimed benefit.

ASSESSMENT

Introduction

The NF is a mixture of two synthetic peptides: a nonapeptide and a pentapeptide. It is proposed to be used as a food supplement at 30 mg/day, mixed with resistant maltodextrin (fibersol‐2) in a ratio of 1:99. The target population is adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women.

According to Article 3 of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, the NF falls under the category ‘food with a new or intentionally modified molecular structure, where that structure was not used as, or in, a food within the Union before 15 May 1997’.

Identity of the NF

The NF is a powdered mixture composed of a 1:1 combination of a nonapeptide and a pentapeptide. These two peptides are independently produced by solid‐phase chemical synthesis and then mixed to form the final NF. Both peptides have a defined amino acid sequence and have a peptide purity 9 of ≥ 95%. Details on the chemical identity of the two individual peptides are provided in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

The molecular structure of the nonapeptide and pentapeptide has been confirmed by ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐MS/MS), based on the molecular weights and the fragment ion data of the enhanced product ion (EPI). The method was developed and validated by comparison with respective high purity (≥ 98%) in‐house standards. To confirm the repeatability of the identity data obtained for the peptides comprising the NF, mass spectra and peptide fragmentation data of three batches of the NF were provided.

Upon EFSA's request, the identity and purity of the NF were also confirmed by comparison with peptide analytical standards produced by an external laboratory. By means of the internally validated UHPLC‐MS/MS and UHPLC coupled with ultraviolet (UV) detector methods, five independently produced batches of each individual peptide (i.e. nonapeptide and pentapeptide) were compared to peptide standards synthesised by an independent, good manufacturing practice (GMP) certified and ISO 9001:2015 accredited third‐party laboratory. As additional evidence for the identity of the NF, molecular weights and fragment ion sequences also confirmed the equivalence to the same two peptides extracted from corn (Kim et al., 2022).

The enantiomeric purity of the two peptides comprising the NF (i.e. nonapeptide and pentapeptide) was proven by comparison, using UHPLC‐UV, of five batches of each peptide against third‐party independently synthesised peptide standards. Additionally, the applicant synthesised peptides containing at least one amino acid in its D‐form. Via direct comparison, this evidenced that the NF contains exclusively peptides synthesised in their L‐form.

With regard to the physicochemical properties, the NF can be described as a white‐coloured powder. The water solubility of the NF and the individual peptides that constitute it was investigated according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline (TG) 105 (Flask Method). The test result showed that the NF is highly soluble in water (i.e. > 1000 g/L) and it does not raise concerns regarding consumer exposure to small particles (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2021).

Production process

According to the information provided, the NF is produced in line with GMP and Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points (HACCP) principles.

The NF is produced by mixing nonapeptide and pentapeptide in a 1:1 ratio. Each peptide is synthesised independently using fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc) solid‐phase techniques on a 2‐chlorotrityl chloride (2‐CTC) resin.

Briefly, a 2‐CTC resin is prepared for coupling by being treated with thionyl chloride (SOCl2) in dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol. After draining the DCM, amino acids with Fmoc‐protected amines are sequentially added to the activated resin. The addition of DCM and N,N‐diisopropylethylamine (DIEA) facilitates the amino acids' coupling process to the resin. This is then followed by filtration and washing with DCM.

To remove the Fmoc groups, the resin‐peptide is treated with piperidine and hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt) in N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF), followed by stirring, filtering and washing with DMF. Subsequently, amino acids are added stepwise through repeated cycles of coupling (i.e. addition of appropriate amounts of each of the Fmoc‐protected amino acids, addition of 2‐(1H‐benzotriazol‐1‐yl)‐1,1,3,3‐tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU) in DMF as additional coupling agent, stirring, addition of DIEA, addition of the resin‐coupled amino acid chain, stirring, filtering and washing with DMF) and deprotection (i.e. addition of piperidine and HOBt in DMF, stirring, filtering and washing with DMF) until the peptidyl resin with the desired peptide sequence is achieved.

The peptidyl resins are then washed with DMF and methanol, dried and treated with specific cleavage solutions (i.e. for pentapeptide synthesis: trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), purified water, and 1,2‐ethanedithiol (EDT); for nonapeptide synthesis: TFA, purified water and EDT with additional thioanisole). This treatment removes the Fmoc groups and releases the peptides from the 2‐CTC resin which is removed via filtration and washed with DMF or DCM. The peptides in solution are thereafter precipitated with tert‐butyl methyl ether (MTBE). The precipitated peptides are filtered and washed with MTBE to remove insoluble impurities. This step is then followed by drying and purification using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), with acetonitrile and purified water to remove any soluble impurities. The peptide‐containing solution is concentrated and freeze‐dried to remove any remaining water/acetonitrile.

Finally, the individually synthesised lyophilised peptides are physically mixed in a 1:1 ratio to produce the NF. To produce the final product to be marketed (i.e. sachets format), the NF is mixed with resistant maltodextrin (fibersol‐2) in a ratio of 1:99.

Scientific rationale was provided by the applicant to support that all raw materials, processing aids and potentially formed impurities are consistently removed during specific steps and conditions of the production process of the NF. Levels of these processing‐related contaminants in the NF are further considered in Section 3.4.

The applicant also presented a literature‐based rationale, indicating that the conditions applied in the manufacturing process, as well as amino acid‐specific chemical behaviour, are unfavourable to the formation of peptide‐related impurities, as described in the Guideline on the Development and Manufacture of Synthetic Peptides (EMA, 2025). These include impurities arising from oxidation, hydrolysis, deamidation, diketopiperazine formation, pyroglutamic acid formation, elimination reactions, condensation, dehydropeptide formation and disulfide bond cleavage or exchange.

Certificates of analysis for the raw materials together with their specifications were provided.

The Panel considers that the production process is sufficiently described and does not raise safety concerns.

Analyses of processing‐related contaminants (i.e. residual solvents, processing aids, carry‐over contaminants, potential degradation products), heavy metals and microbiological parameters were provided for up to twelve batches of the NF and are described below.

Processing‐related contaminants

Residual solvents and processing aids

Residual solvents and processing aids, derived from the production process, were analysed in twelve batches of the NF.

N,N‐Dimethylformamide, methylene chloride and methyl alcohol analysed via gas chromatography‐flame ionisation detection (GC‐FID) were consistently found below their limits of detection (LOD) (i.e. < 0.004 mg/kg, < 0.002 mg/kg and < 0.003 mg/kg, respectively). Tert‐butyl methyl ether and acetonitrile, analysed with the same technique, ranged between < 0.09 (i.e. LOD)‐61.1 mg/kg and < 0.12 (i.e. LOD)‐152.1 mg/kg. TFA in the NF was analysed with a HPLC‐UV method and resulted at levels between < 0.79 (i.e. LOD)‐101.3 mg/kg. Two batches resulted in TFA levels of 1625.3 and 1754.5 mg/kg. 12 The Panel notes that the latter reported levels for TFA would not comply with the proposed specifications (see Section 3.5).

With regard to residual levels of processing aids, HBTU, HOBt, Fmoc and thionyl chloride were analysed via HPLC‐UV and found regularly below the respective LODs (i.e. < 3 mg/kg, < 0.5 mg/kg, < 2 mg/kg and < 2 mg/kg, respectively). Piperidine, DIEA, EDT and thioanisole were analysed via GC‐FID and were below the respective LODs (i.e. < 2 mg/kg, < 2 mg/kg, < 0.5 mg/kg, < 2 mg/kg, respectively) in all analysed batches.

Carry‐over and processing‐induced contaminants

Upon EFSA's request, the applicant provided analyses of a range of N‐nitrosamines 13 in five batches of the NF, using the liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) method as published by Herrmann et al. (2014). Nitrosamines were not detected in any of the samples (LOD 1.0 μg/kg for each N‐nitrosamine). The applicant was also requested to analyse five batches of the NF for furans and 2,5‐dimethylfuran in accordance with Commission Recommendation 2022/4955. 14 All samples were analysed with GC‐FID and resulted consistently in contents below their respective LODs (i.e. 1.5 μg/kg for furans, 0.9 mg/kg for 2,5‐dimethylfuran).

Following EFSA's request on the analysis of potential leachable substances from the 2‐CTC resin used in the production process, the applicant analysed styrol and divinylbenzene using HPLC‐UV in five independently produced batches of the NF. Styrol and divinylbenzene were not detected in any of the samples (i.e. < 0.125 μg/g for styrol and < 0.143 μg/g for divinylbenzene).

Metals and other elements

Analysis of mercury, lead, arsenic and cadmium were performed in twelve batches of the NF via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS).

Mercury levels were found below the LODs (i.e. 0.005 mg/kg) in all batches. Lead levels ranged between < 0.05 μg/kg (i.e. LOD) and 0.075 mg/kg. Arsenic and cadmium ranged between < 0.005 (i.e. LOD) and 0.0102 mg/kg; and < 0.0001 mg/kg (i.e. LOD) and 0.0012 mg/kg, respectively.

The Panel notes that these levels are within the limits established for food supplements in Commission Regulation (EC) No 2023/915 15 (i.e. lead 3 mg/kg, cadmium 1 mg/kg, mercury 0.1 mg/kg).

Microbiological analyses

Regarding microbiological parameters, the applicant provided analytical results for twelve batches of the NF. Total plate counts, yeasts and moulds were below the LOD of 10 colony forming units per gram (CFU/g) in all batches. Total coliforms and Salmonella spp. were not detected in 25 g of the NF.

Overall considerations on the compositional data

The Panel considers that the information provided on the composition is sufficient for characterising the NF.

Stability

The applicant performed two stability tests under accelerated storage conditions.

Five independently produced batches of the NF (i.e. nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture) were subjected to stability test at 45°C and at 60% relative humidity (RH) for a period of 6 months. The batches were analysed for physicochemical (i.e. appearance and purity) and microbiological (i.e. total plate count, yeast and mould, coliforms, Salmonella) stability. Results for all parameters remained within specifications during the 6‐month study period.

The stability of three batches of the NF in the final product formulation (i.e. NF mixed with resistant maltodextrin) was also investigated and subjected to stability testing at 55°C and at 60% RH for a period of 3 months. The final formulations were analysed for physicochemical (appearance and purity) and microbiological (total plate count, coliforms) stability. All tested parameters remained within specifications during the 3‐month study period.

Results obtained from stability studies conducted in accelerated conditions were extrapolated to normal conditions according to the accelerated ageing theory, which is based on the assumption that the reaction of a material follows the Arrhenius reaction rate function during the time of accelerated preservation. Thus, for the NF on its own and in the finished product formulation, the applicant proposed a shelf‐life of 2 years when stored under controlled room temperature (25°C), in vacuum‐sealed, light‐protective packaging.

The Panel considers that the data provided sufficient information with respect to the stability of the NF.

History of use of the NF and/or of its source

History of use of the source

Amino acids form part of the normal diet, as they are naturally ingested through dietary proteins.

History of use of the NF

The NF has been marketed outside the EU (under the trade name Deglusterol). It complies with the Russian Technical Regulation of the Customs Union 021/2011 ‘On Safety of Food Products’ and 022/2011 ‘Food Products Marketing’ (Customs Union Commission, 2011a, 2011b), with a use level of 30 mg/day.

Deglusterol has been included on the list of publicly displayed new dietary ingredient notifications (NDINs) that have been reviewed by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to be used in dietary supplements at 30 mg/day in the United States 16 .

Proposed uses and use levels and anticipated intake

Target population

The target population proposed by the applicant is adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women.

Proposed uses and use levels

The applicant intends to market the NF for use in food supplements, at a maximum dose of 30 mg per day, which corresponds to 0.43 mg/kg bw per day for a 70‐kg adult. 17

Estimate of exposure to undesirable substances

Processing aids used for the manufacturing of the NF were all below their specification limits (see Section 3.5) and most of them below the respective LODs (i.e. N,N‐dimethylformamide, methylene chloride, methyl alcohol, HBTU, HOBt, Fmoc, thionyl chloride, piperidine, DIEA, EDT, thioanisole) (see Section 3.4).

An acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0.05 mg/kg bw per day was established for TFA, which was derived based on a 90‐day oral exposure study in rats that showed liver to be the target organ (increased liver weight due to hepatocellular hypertrophy based on peroxisome proliferation) (EFSA, 2014). Based on the maximum limit of 250 mg/kg as set in the specifications and the maximum proposed daily intake of the NF, the exposure to TFA would result in 0.107 μg/kg bw per day, which corresponds to 0.2% of the ADI.

The Panel notes that, in July 2024, EFSA received a Mandate from the European Commission on the revision of the toxicological reference values (ADI and acute reference dose (ARfD)) 18 of TFA. The outcome of this evaluation is expected by end of July 2026.

Genotoxicity

The applicant carried out genotoxicity testing using the NF. The bacterial reverse mutation test, in vivo micronucleus test and in vitro chromosome aberration test were performed in accordance with ICH Guideline S2(R1) (ICH, 2011) and Standards for Toxicity Studies of Drugs, and in compliance with good laboratory practice (GLP) (MFDS, 2014a, 2014b).

The potential of the NF to induce gene mutations was evaluated in a bacterial reverse mutation test performed in Salmonella Typhimurium strains (TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537) and an Escherichia coli strain (WP2uvrA (pKM101)) (Unpublished study, 2016a). A preliminary dose range‐finding study was conducted which resulted in the definitive study concentrations of 313, 625, 1250, 2500 and 5000 μg/plate for Salmonella typhimurium TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537 in the absence and presence of S9 mix; 39.1, 78.1, 156, 313, 625, 1250, 2500 and 5000 μg/plate for Escherichia coli WP2uvrA(pKM101) in the absence of metabolic activation; and 19.5, 39.1, 78.1, 156, 313 and 625 μg/plate for Escherichia coli WP2uvrA(pKM101) in the presence of metabolic activation, as overgrowth of the background lawn was found at 1250 and 5000 μg/plate. The pre‐incubation method was applied in both main studies. No toxicologically relevant changes in the number of revertant colonies were induced by the test item compared to the vehicle controls at any concentration and with any tester strain. Therefore, the Panel concluded that the NF did not induce gene mutations in bacteria under the experimental conditions employed in this study.

To evaluate the potential of the NF to induce chromosomal damage, an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus (MN) test was performed in TK6 cells (Unpublished study, 2023), according to the OECD TG 487 (2016) and in compliance with GLP. Based on the results of a preliminary cytotoxicity test, concentrations of 500, 1000 and 2000 μg/mL were used for the main study, including a vehicle control (MilliQ water) and positive controls in the presence and absence of S9 metabolic activation. Cells were treated for 3‐h with 17‐h recovery period in the presence and absence of metabolic activation, and for 20‐h with 24‐h recovery period or without recovery in the absence of metabolic activation. No increase in the frequency of micronuclei was observed in treated cultures compared to vehicle control cultures at any concentration and in any experimental condition. Therefore, the Panel concluded that the NF did not induce structural and numerical aberrations in mammalian cells under the experimental conditions employed in this study.

In addition to the basic battery of tests required by the EFSA guideline (2011), the applicant also provided an in vitro chromosomal aberration test and an in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test.

The applicant conducted an in vitro chromosomal aberration assay using Chinese Hamster Lung (CHL/IU) cells, both in the absence and presence of S9 metabolic activation (Unpublished study, 2016b). The Panel noted that the study did not reach the recommended upper limit of cytotoxicity required by OECD TG, and the range of concentrations tested was insufficient to fully explore the clastogenic potential of the NF.

The applicant also conducted an in vivo mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test (Unpublished study, 2016c). The Panel noted that this study did not provide evidence of bone marrow exposure and testing on two subsequent days was not performed for the three dose concentrations.

Considering the limitations identified in the last two studies, the Panel did not further consider them for the assessment of the NF.

Taking into account the negative results obtained with the standard in vitro battery of tests, the Panel concluded that the NF does not raise concern for genotoxicity.

Acute toxicity

The applicant has provided an acute respiratory toxicity study in rats, a central nervous system acute oral toxicity study in rats and an acute oral toxicity study in rats performed in accordance with ICH Guideline S7A (ICH, 2000).

The Panel considers that these acute toxicity studies are not pertinent for the safety assessment of NFs.

Subacute toxicity

In a dose range finding 28‐day repeated dose oral toxicity study (Unpublished, 2017b, non GLP) provided by the applicant, the NF was administered by oral gavage at doses of 0, 250, 500 and 1000 mg/kg bw per day to Beagle dogs (n = 2/sex per dose group, n = 1/sex in the vehicle group). Soft stool, diarrhoea, mucous stool and vomiting were observed occasionally in some animals, including controls. A tendency for increasing levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was seen in female animals of the 250 mg/kg bw per day dose group and increased ALT levels in male animals of the 250 and 500 mg/kg bw per day dose groups. Reactive Kupffer cells at slight severity appeared in the hepatic sinusoids of one male in the 1000 mg/kg per day dose group. There were no other effects reported.

Subchronic toxicity

The applicant provided a 90‐day subchronic oral toxicity study with the NF (Unpublished study, 2017c). The study was conducted in accordance with ICH Guideline M3 (R2) (ICH, 2009) and Standards for Toxicity Studies of Drugs (MSFD, 2015), and in compliance with the principles of GLP (MFDS, 2014b). Sprague–Dawley rats (n = 10/sex per group) were administered the NF by oral gavage at doses of 0 (vehicle control: water), 62.5 (low‐dose), 125 (mid‐dose) and 250 (high‐dose) mg/kg bw per day for 90 days. Five additional rats per sex, receiving 0 and 250 mg NF/kg bw per day, were kept for a 28‐day recovery period. The residual solvent TFA was present in the NF at a concentration of 208.1 mg/kg.

In addition, a toxicokinetic study was conducted (n = 6/sex per dose group, n = 3/sex in the vehicle group) using the same dose levels as the main toxicity study to assess the systemic levels of the NF.

Upon EFSA's request, the applicant clarified that, in males, there was no increase in average daily protein intake between the control and high‐dose groups, and in females, an increase in protein intake was observed in the high‐dose group (4.14 g protein/day) compared to controls (3.93 g protein/day). This increase (+5.3%) was considered to be negligible.

There were no mortalities or clinical observations related to the test item. Body weight was not significantly different in treated animals compared to the control group. There were no changes in food consumption attributable to the NF. Statistically significant differences in haematological parameters (monocyte percentage, percentage of eosinophils, prothrombin time) between controls and different dose groups (across the sexes) were observed. None of the observed differences were considered to be toxicologically relevant since they were not dose‐dependent, occurring only in one sex and not in all dose groups, and/or were small in magnitude (within historical control ranges). No statistically significant differences were observed in urinalysis parameters.

Several findings were observed in clinical chemistry, including a dose‐dependent decrease in blood glucose concentrations, being statistically significant for all dosed groups in both sexes (ranging from −12% to −20% in males and from −13.5% to −21.4% in females). These changes were considered treatment‐related by the panel.

There was a statistically significant increase in albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio in the high‐dose group of males (+8.7%) and low‐dose group of females (+9.9%). Plasma calcium levels also decreased significantly in the high‐dose group in males (−5%). However, none of the observed differences were considered toxicologically relevant since they were not dose‐dependent, small in magnitude, and within historical control ranges. A dose‐dependent increase in ALT and AST levels was observed in females, being statistically significant in the high‐dose groups (up to +221% and +81%, respectively), and elevated but not statistically significant in the high‐dose recovery group (+94% for ALT and +34% for AST). The Panel considers these changes treatment‐related and adverse. In males, ALT and AST levels were also elevated but the increase was neither dose‐dependent nor statistically significant. In contrast, total bilirubin decreased in all male groups (−66.7%) and in females of the high‐dose group (−33.3%).

A statistically significant and dose‐dependent increase in relative‐to‐bw liver weight was observed in male rats treated with the medium and high dose (up to +13%). This finding was accompanied by an increased incidence of centrilobular hepatocellular hypertrophy in the same dose groups (minimal severity, 5/10 animals of each group affected), which correlated with vacuolation (microvesicular or macrovesicular) in all treated male groups (minimal or slight severity, 1 to 3/10 animals of each group). The observed vacuolation in hepatocytes might be an indicator of hepatic steatosis, being test substance‐related. Hyperplasia was not observed. Together with the increase in relative liver weight observed in the mid and high‐dose males, the Panel considers these effects to be of potential concern.

In the toxicokinetic study, nonapeptide and pentapeptide were below the limit of quantification of 5 μg/mL in all plasma samples collected up to week 13, measured 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, 24 h post‐dosing on the first day of dosing and in Weeks 4 and 13 of the dosing period. However, the Panel considered these results of limited relevance as the sensitivity of the method applied was insufficient.

The Panel considers that, given the liver‐related effects (i.e. increase of relative‐to‐bw liver weight, increase of hepatic enzymes and presence of the hepatic histopathological findings), starting at the medium dose tested group, the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of this study is 62.5 mg/kg bw per day (the lowest dose tested).

The applicant also provided a 90‐day subchronic oral toxicity study in dogs with the NF as in the previous study (Unpublished study, 2017c). The study was conducted in accordance with ICH Guideline M3 (R2) (ICH, 2009) and Standards for Toxicity Studies of Drugs (MSFD, 2015), and in compliance with the principles of GLP (MFDS, 2014b). Beagle dogs (n = 3/sex per group) were administered the NF by oral gavage at doses of 0 (vehicle control: water), 20 (low‐dose), 40 (mid‐dose), 80 (high‐dose) mg/kg bw per day. Additionally, groups of two dogs per sex which previously received the vehicle or high dose were kept for a 28‐day recovery period to determine the systemic exposure of the test substance through a toxicokinetic study.

There were no mortalities or clinical observations attributable to the NF tested. Body weight and food consumption remained unchanged in treated animals compared to the control group.

In the electrocardiograms recorded, there was a dose‐dependent increase in QRS in males which was statistically significant in the high‐dose group (+19%). This finding was considered to be incidental and not related to the NF.

Concerning haematological parameters, statistically significant differences between control and high‐dose group were observed (i.e. mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) in males, mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) in females). None of the observed differences were considered toxicologically relevant since they were occurring only in one sex and and/or were small in magnitude (within historical control ranges). No statistically significant changes were observed in urinalysis parameters.

Clinical chemistry parameters were measured at baseline and weeks 5 and 13. A statistically significant increase in ALT was noted at the high‐dose group in both sexes at week 5, being dose‐dependent in males. AST levels were also increased at the high‐dose group in both sexes at week 5, but statistical significance was noted only in females. However, ALT and AST levels were within normal levels at week 13, indicating liver adaptation. In contrast, total bilirubin levels decreased dose‐dependently in males (up to −75% in the high‐dose group). A dose‐dependent decrease was noted for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels both at week 5 and week 13 in females, which was statistically significant at the high‐dose group at week 13. There was a statistically significant decrease in creatinine levels (up to −18%) in both sexes at the high‐dose group, but this was considered to be not of concern. Absolute and relative‐to‐bw liver weights showed statistically significant increases in males of the high‐dose group (+27.4%). There was an increase in relative‐to‐bw liver weight of females (+26.5%) at the highest dose tested, which was not statistically significant. Relative liver weight increased also at the high‐dose recovery group (+28.9% in males, +26.9% in females). These changes were considered treatment‐related and adverse. No relevant microscopic changes were detected in histopathological analysis.

Absolute pituitary weight also increased significantly in males of the high‐dose group and in a dose‐dependent manner. No treatment‐related changes were observed in the remaining organ weights. All other microscopic findings were considered incidental and not attributable to the NF.

In the toxicokinetic study, nonapeptide and pentapeptide were below the limit of quantification of 5 μg/mL in all plasma samples collected 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 10 and 24 h post‐dosing on the first day of dosing and in Weeks 4 and 13 of the dosing period. However, the Panel considered these results of limited relevance as the sensitivity of the method applied was insufficient.

Taking into consideration the statistically significant increase of liver weight (absolute and relative‐to‐bw) occurring in males at the high‐dose group, the Panel considers that the mid‐dose tested (i.e. 40 mg/kg bw per day) is the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of this study.

Human data

In humans, the efficacy (on improving glucose homeostasis) and safety of the oral ingestion of a nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture were investigated in a multicentre, randomised, double‐blind, parallel, placebo‐controlled clinical trial (Unpublished study, 2019). A total of 81 adults (20–75 years old; males and females) with prediabetes (i.e. with impaired fasting glucose (fasting plasma glucose between 100 and 125 mg/dL), impaired glucose tolerance (plasma glucose between 140 and 199 mg/dL after a standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)) and/or glycated haemoglobin between 6.0% and 6.4%) were randomised to consume 100 mL/day of a drink mixture containing 30 mg of nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture (n = 42; completers n = 35) or 100 mL/day of the same drink without the test substance (n = 39; completers n = 32) for 12 weeks. It is uncertain whether this test substance was extracted from corn, as repeatedly stated in the study report, or whether the study was conducted with the NF (synthetically produced following the manufacturing process described in Section 3.3), as claimed by the applicant. Three and two volunteers per group dropped owing to withdrawal of consent or early termination at the sponsor's request, respectively. One volunteer in the test substance group dropped owing to adverse events unrelated to the intervention. There were no significant differences in fasting blood glucose concentrations, blood glucose concentrations at 1 or 2 h during an OGGT or the % of glycated haemoglobin between the intervention and control groups at the end of the study. Several safety‐related parameters were also evaluated in the study in 41 and 37 volunteers in the test and placebo groups, respectively, including physical examinations, vital signs, electrocardiograms (ECGs), chest X‐rays, adverse event reporting, haematology, clinical chemistry including AST, ALT and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) and urinalysis. There were no test substance‐related adverse events or effects on any of the safety‐related parameters investigated. Therefore, it was concluded that consumption of 30 mg/day of the test substance for 12 weeks was well tolerated in humans.

The Panel notes that no changes were found in the studied safety‐related parameters and that no adverse events related to the consumption of the test substance were reported.

The Panel considers that the human intervention study provided by the applicant does not raise safety concerns. However, the Panel notes that uncertainties related to whether or not the test substance used corresponds to the NF do not allow to draw conclusions regarding the safety assessment of the NF.

Allergenicity

No experimental studies assessing the allergenicity of the NF were provided by the applicant.

The NF consists of two peptides, five and nine amino acids long, both < 1 kDa in size. Peptides shorter than nine amino acid residues are generally unable to bind to Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class II molecules and to activate T cells due to the minimum length requirement for the peptide‐binding register (Mohan & Unanue, 2012). Therefore, peptides shorter than nine amino acid residues are considered to have low allergenic potential. Similarly, peptides which are nine amino acids long or more but likely to be readily digested in the gastrointestinal tract (so‐called ‘transient’) are also regarded as having low allergenic potential (EFSA GMO Panel, 2017). The in vitro digestibility studies presented in Section 3.8 suggest that both peptides in the NF undergo complete digestion in the GI tract.

Aggregation is a recognised factor that may contribute to immunogenicity for peptides of five or more amino acid residues (EMA, 2025; Moussa et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2012; Zapadka et al., 2017). Aggregation behaviours depend on peptide–solvent interactions (Zapadka et al., 2017). Considering that both pentapeptide and nonapeptide are highly soluble in water (see Section 3.2), the Panel considers that the NF will be fully solubilised upon ingestion, avoiding aggregation.

Overall, the Panel considers that the NF is unlikely to trigger allergic reactions.

DISCUSSION

The NF which is the subject of the application is a mixture composed of a 1:1 combination of a nonapeptide and a pentapeptide. These two peptides are independently produced by solid‐phase chemical synthesis in their L‐form and have a defined amino acid sequence. The sum of the nonapeptide and the pentapeptide accounts for ≥ 95% of the NF. The NF is mixed in a ratio of 1:99 with resistant maltodextrin to form the final food supplement formulation.

The information provided on the production process, composition and specifications of the NF does not raise safety concerns.

The NF is proposed to be used as a food supplement intended for adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women, at a maximum dose of 30 mg NF/day. The Panel considers that its consumption is not nutritionally disadvantageous.

The NF is not genotoxic. In the two submitted 90‐day toxicity studies, changes related to effects in the liver were observed. In the 90‐day study performed in rats, an increase of relative‐to‐bw liver weight and hepatic histopathological findings were reported in males, while hepatic enzymes were increased only in females. In the 90‐day study performed in dogs, there was a transient increase of hepatic enzymes only at week 5 in both sexes, and an increase in relative‐to‐bw liver weight in males and females, being dose‐dependent in males.

The lowest NOAEL was identified in the 90‐day study in dogs (40 mg/kg bw per day). Applying the standard uncertainty factor of 200 (10 (interspecies variability) × 10 (intraspecies variability) × 2 (subchronic to chronic study duration)), the resulting safe dose is 0.2 mg/kg bw per day. This corresponds to a safe level of 14 mg NF/day in 70‐kg adults.

Regarding the human intervention study provided by the applicant, uncertainties related to whether or not the test substance used corresponds to the NF do not allow to draw conclusions regarding the safety of the NF.

The Panel considers that the NF is safe when consumed at intake levels up to 14 mg/day by adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women. This amount is lower than the use level proposed by the applicant.

CONCLUSIONS

The Panel concludes that the NF, nonapeptide–pentapeptide mixture, is safe at intake levels up to 14 mg per day for adults, excluding pregnant and lactating women.
