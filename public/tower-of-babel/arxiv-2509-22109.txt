Generalized Thue–Morse measures: spectral and fractal analysis
Philipp Gohlke, Marc Kesseböhmer, Tanja I. Schindler

Abstract
. We investigate a family of Riesz products and show that they can be regarded as diffraction measures of generalized Thue–Morse sequences, possibly over an infinite alphabet. These measures are closely related to the dynamical system arising from the doubling map together with an observable exhibiting a logarithmic singularity. For this system, we develop a generalized thermodynamic formalism beyond the standard setting, which yields explicit formulas for Birkhoff and dimension spectra. A further novel aspect is the identification of a precise connection between these spectra and the L q L^{q} -spectrum of the underlying Riesz product. This new link allows us to determine, explicitly, the Fourier and quantization dimension, and to describe the spectral asymptotics of the associated Kreĭn–Feller operator, providing new insights into the interplay between diffraction, fractal geometry, and spectral theory in the Thue–Morse context.

1.
Introduction

Both the thermodynamic formalism and the concept of multifractal analysis provide powerful tools to analyze in detail the statistical properties of a given dynamical system and corresponding invariant measures. In the most classical setup, this provides a good understanding of the Birkhoff averages of Hölder continuous functions that are sampled along orbits of expanding (compact) dynamical systems and the scaling properties of associated equilibrium measures; see for example
[
9
,
14
,
54
]
for comprehensive introductions to the topic.
The last few decades have seen generalizations of this classical situation into many different directions, relaxing many of the strong regularity assumptions. This includes the consideration of non-uniformly hyperbolic and parabolic dynamical systems (see for example
[
11
,
28
,
32
,
43
,
52
]
) as well as progress under less restrictive assumptions on the continuity of the sampling function (referred to as a potential in the following)
[
35
,
20
,
53
,
8
,
29
]
. We also refer to
[
10
]
for an overview.

In this work we want to contribute to the understanding of unbounded potentials, while maintaining the most simple setup of an expanding dynamical system (the doubling map). The multifractal study of unbounded potentials appears quite naturally, for example in the context of number theory, in an attempt to get a detailed understanding of the statistics of the coefficients in continued fraction expansions
[
16
]
. Other instances include continuous potentials over non-compact expanding dynamical systems like shift-spaces over countable alphabets. In this setup, a systematic framework for the thermodynamic formalism was provided by Sarig
[
56
]
and results about the multifractal analysis of (unbounded) continuous functions can be found for example in
[
27
]
.
Moreover, Baladi and Castorrini provide results concerning the thermodynamic formalism for unbounded potentials over piecewise expanding interval maps under some condition on the behaviour of the potential at the boundary of the partition the interval map is generating
[
7
]
. Kautzsch et al. showed in
[
35
]
that for intermittent maps and unbounded potentials with one singularity, the convergence of the associated transfer operator depends on the structure of the
\omega
-limit set of the singularity.
Countable alphabet shifts can also be used via an appropriate encoding in the context of singular potentials over the doubling map, as was shown in
[
44
]
at the example of the so-called Saint-Petersburg potential.

Despite the aforementioned progress, the understanding of singular potentials is far from what can be considered complete, even over such a simple system as the doubling map and under the restriction of having only one (one-sided) singularity.
Fan, Schmeling and Shen recently studied the (rescaled) singular potential
\psi^{0}(x)=2\log|\cos(\pi x)|
over the doubling map on the torus
\mathbb{T}
and a family of rigid translations
\psi^{c}(x)=\psi^{0}(x-c)
and showed that the appearance of certain pathological features in the multifractal spectrum depends critically (and in a discontinuous manner) on the translation parameter
c\in\mathbb{T}

[
18
,
17
]
. We will take this family as the starting point for our analysis.
The Birkhoff averages of the potential
\psi^{1/2}
are also of independent interest in aperiodic order because they are closely related to the scaling properties of the classic Thue–Morse measure, which can be interpreted as the corresponding equilibrium measure
[
3
,
23
]
.
In fact, each element of the family
(\psi^{c})_{c\in\mathbb{T}}
can be related to a generalized Thue–Morse sequence
t^{c}
as defined later in (
5
)
[
18
]
. Indeed, as we will establish below, the equilibrium measure corresponding to
\psi^{c}
is given by the diffraction measure
\mu_{c}
of the sequence
t^{c}
. Here, we rely on the fact that
t^{c}
emerges as a limiting object of a substitution procedure over a compact alphabet.
While the theory of substitutions on finite alphabets is well-established
[
4
,
55
]
, their counterparts on infinite alphabets have been studied much more sporadically
[
13
,
21
]
.
A systematic study of substitution systems over compact alphabets was recently undertaken in
[
49
,
50
]
and we will make use of this framework for an appropriate interpretation of the diffraction measures.
Hence, the present work can be seen both as a case study of Birkhoff averages of singular potentials and as an in-depth analysis of spectral measures that arise naturally in the realm of aperiodic order.
In fact, we will see that both frameworks complement each other in this study and that each of them offers additional insight.

With regards to the thermodynamic formalism, we show that the classical variational principle for the pressure function
p_{c}
(with
p_{c}(t)
denoting the topological pressure of
t\psi^{c}
) extends to the present setting using an appropriate notion of topological pressure. Also, we verify that the pressure function coincides (up to a multiplicative constant) with the
L^{q}
-spectrum of the associated equilibrium measure. Combining this with the multifractal analysis performed in
[
18
]
, this yields several ways to compute the dimension spectrum of both the Birkhoff averages of
\psi^{c}
and the local dimensions of
\mu_{c}
, at least some of them numerically accessible
[
3
]
.
The equality between
L^{q}
-spectrum and pressure function also allows us to further address the question about the regularity of the parameter dependence. As was shown in
[
18
]
, the dependence of the pressure function
p_{c}(t)
on
c
is highly discontinuous for negative
t
and there is recent work indicating that it is in fact continuous for positive
t

[
24
]
.
Here, we show that
p_{c}(2)
is an analytic function in
c
. In fact, we prove that the exponential of
p_{c}(2)
coincides with the spectral radius of an explicit matrix
M_{c}
whose entries are analytic in
c
. This observation relies on the interpretation of
p_{c}(2)
as a Fourier dimension and makes decisive use of the autocorrelation structure of the generalized Thue–Morse sequence
t^{c}
.
This extends previous work on the Thue–Morse measure
[
59
]
, using the characterization of Fourier dimension as provided in
[
58
]
.
Continuing along the path of dimensional analysis as for the Fourier dimension, we use the relation between the pressure function and the
L^{q}
-spectrum of
\mu_{c}
and prove that this spectrum has crucial regularity properties, which in turn affect further regularity properties of
\mu_{c}
.
In particular, the spectral dimension for Kreĭn–Feller operators associated to the measure
\mu_{c}
and the quantization dimension for
\mu_{c}
of order
r\geqslant 0
both exist and can be read off entirely from the
L^{q}
-spectrum.

2.
Notation and main results

The base dynamics throughout this work is given by the doubling map
(\mathbb{T},T)
, with
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
the one-dimensional torus, and

T\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T},\quad x\mapsto 2x\mod 1.

It is often useful to think of this action in terms of a binary representation of
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}2^{-i}
, where each
x_{i}\in\Sigma=\{0,1\}
. The representation
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is unique for all
x
except for the countable set of dyadic points, and the doubling map corresponds to the shift action

S\colon\Sigma^{{\mathbb{N}}}\to\Sigma^{{\mathbb{N}}},\quad(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mapsto(x_{i+1})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}.

More precisely, if
\tilde{x}
is a binary representation of
x
, then
S(\tilde{x})
is a binary representation of
T(x)
.
Stated formally,
\pi\colon(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}2^{-i}
is a factor map from the dynamical system
(\Sigma^{{\mathbb{N}}},S)
to
(\mathbb{T},T)
, which is invertible on the complement of a countable set. Indeed, equipping
\Sigma
with the discrete topology and
\Sigma^{{\mathbb{N}}}
with the corresponding product topology, it is straightforward to verify that
\pi
is continuous. With some abuse of notation, we sometimes identify
x\in\mathbb{T}
with (any of) its binary representation(s)
(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
\Sigma^{{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Staying on the symbolical side for a moment, we refer to the elements of
\Sigma^{n}
as
words of length
n
(for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) and define for
\omega=\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
the corresponding
cylinder set
by

\langle\omega\rangle\coloneqq\left\{(x_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}:x_{1}\cdots x_{n}=\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{n}\right\},

in particular, if
\omega\in\Sigma^{0}
, we have the empty word and
\langle\omega\rangle=\Sigma^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The image of
\langle\omega\rangle
under
\pi
with
\omega\in\Sigma^{n}
is a closed interval of length
2^{-n}
in
\mathbb{T}
, specified by the first
n
digits in the binary representation and with the same abuse of notation as above, we also denote this interval by
\langle\omega\rangle
.

2.1.
A family of singular potentials

Our main interest is centered around the family
(\psi^{c})_{c\in\mathbb{T}}
with

\psi^{0}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T},\quad x\mapsto 2\log|\cos(\pi x)|,

and
\psi^{c}(x)=\psi^{0}(x-c)
for all
x,c\in\mathbb{T}
. Note that the function
\psi^{c}
obtains its maximal value
0
at
x=c
and has a singularity at the position
\breve{c}:=c+1/2
.
Sampling the function
\psi^{c}
along orbits of the doubling map, we obtain the corresponding
Birkhoff sums
, given by

\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi^{c}(T^{k}x),

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\in\mathbb{T}
. The
Birkhoff average
is given by

\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\psi^{c}_{n}(x),

whenever the limit exists. A classical object of interest is the corresponding
dimension spectrum

b_{c}(\alpha)\,\coloneqq\,\dim_{H}\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)=\alpha\right\},

where
\dim_{H}
denotes Hausdorff dimension.
As it is standard, we always take
\dim_{H}(\varnothing)=0
.

For each
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the
variational pressure
of
t\psi
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}}h\left(\nu\right)+\nu\left(t\psi^{c}\right),

where
\mathcal{M}_{T}
is the set of all
T
-invariant Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{T}
, we denote
\nu\left(t\psi^{c}\right)=\int t\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
and we call
\nu
an
equilibrium measure
of
t\psi^{c}
if it achieves the above supremum.
A variant of this notion that was considered in
[
18
]
is the restricted variational pressure, defined as

p_{c}(t)\,\coloneqq\,\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var},c}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T,c}}h\left(\nu\right)+\nu\left(t\psi^{c}\right),

where
\mathcal{M}_{T,c}\coloneqq\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\colon\breve{c}\notin\mathrm{supp}\left(\nu\right)\}
and
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
denotes the topological support of
\nu
.
The interest in this quantity is partly justified by the fact that it is intimately linked to the
Birkhoff spectrum

b_{c}(\alpha)
via its Legendre transform, given by

p_{c}^{*}(a)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{q\in{\mathbb{R}}}\bigl(q\hskip 0.5pta-p_{c}(q)\bigr),

for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By construction, both
p_{c}
and
p_{c}^{\ast}
are convex functions from
{\mathbb{R}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
. The domain of
p_{c}^{\ast}
is
\operatorname{dom}(p_{c}^{\ast})=\left\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}:p_{c}^{\ast}(\alpha)<\infty\right\}
, and the interior of this domain is always given by an open interval
(\underline{\alpha}_{c},\overline{\alpha}_{c})
. In the present setting,
\underline{\alpha}_{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}

[
18
]
. With this notation the statement reads as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
18
, Thm. B]
)
.

For every
c\neq 0
and
\alpha\in(\underline{\alpha}_{c},\overline{\alpha}_{c})
, the Birkhoff spectrum of the function
\psi^{c}
is given by

b_{c}(\alpha)\,=\,\frac{-p_{c}^{*}\hskip 0.5pt(\alpha)}{\log 2},

and
b_{c}(\alpha)=0
if
\alpha<\underline{\alpha}_{c}
or
\alpha\geqslant\overline{\alpha}_{c}
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is worth pointing out that the statement of Theorem
2.1
fails in the special case
c=0
; see
[
18
, Thm. 5.1]
for details. We will come back to this particular case at a later point.

As we will see below, the distinction between restricted and unrestricted pressure is unnecessary on the positive halfline. In fact, both expressions can be related to the
topological pressure
. We define the
upped
and
lower topological pressure
as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c})\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\exp\bigl(t\hskip 0.5pt\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,\,\begin{cases}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\bigl(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\bigr)&t\geqslant 0\\
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\bigl(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\bigr)&t<0,\end{cases}

(1)

and

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\exp\bigl(t\hskip 0.5pt\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\bigr).

We talk about the
topological pressure

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}
if
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}=\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}
.
Our first main result is that a version of the classical variational principle persists in the present setting.

Theorem 2.3
.

Fix
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var},c}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)\eqqcolon\mathcal{P}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)

(2)

and for
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}\left(t\psi^{c}\right).

(3)

Moreover, the pressure function
t\mapsto\mathcal{P}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)
is convex on
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
.

In fact, the topological pressure is helpful to obtain numerical calculations of the pressure itself or the dimension and the Birkhoff spectrum as was illustrated for the case
c=1/2
in
[
3
]
.

2.2.
Equilibrium measures via diffraction

Each of the functions
\psi^{c}
has a very particular structure as it is the logarithm of a
g
-function
g^{c}
in the sense of Keane
[
36
]
. More precisely, we call
g\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
a
g
-function if

\sum_{y\in T^{-1}x}g(y)=1,

for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
. This relation is easily checked for the function
g^{0}(x)=\cos^{2}(\pi x)
and naturally extends to all of its rigid translations
g^{c}(x)=g^{0}(x-c)
. Due to a well-known result by Ledrappier, the logarithm of a
g
-function has vanishing pressure and admits an equilibrium measure
[
48
]
. Each such equilibrium measure is called a corresponding
g
-measure (or Doeblin measure).
That is,

0=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}(\psi^{c})=h_{\mu_{c}}+\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{c},

for some unique choice of
\mu_{c}\in\mathcal{M}_{T}
.
If – as in our case – the
g
-function has at most one zero, then additionally the equilibrium measure is also unique,
[
36
]
.
Since
g^{c}
is continuous and has a single
0
for each
c
the following product representation is a direct corollary of a result by Keane
[
36
]
(compare Subsection
7.1
for details)

\mu_{c}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}2g^{c}(T^{n}x)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigl(1+\cos(2\pi(2^{n}x-c))\bigr),

(4)

where the right hand side is understood as the weak limit of densities with respect to Lebesgue measure. It should be noted that
\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}
coincides with the point mass at the origin and that
\mu_{c}
is purely singular continuous with full topological support, for all
c\neq 0

[
2
]
.

In the special case
c=1/2
, it is well established that the Riesz product given in (
4
) describes the diffraction of the Thue–Morse sequence. In order to extend this relation to arbitrary values of
c\in\mathbb{T}
,
we consider a
generalized Thue–Morse sequence

t^{c}=(t_{n}^{c})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, taking values in the unit circle
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\left\{\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}x}\mid x\in[0,1)\right\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
More precisely, we set

t^{c}_{n}\,\coloneqq\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}c\mathsf{S}_{2}(n)},

(5)

where
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
\mathsf{S}_{2}(n)
denotes the sum of digits in the binary expansion of
n
(e.g.
\mathsf{S}_{2}(7)=3
). A connection to the study of
\psi^{c}
was already noted in
[
18
]
. Here, we extend
t^{c}
arbitrarily to the left, yielding a two-sided sequence
\widetilde{t^{c}}
in
\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
(equipped with the product topology), and let
X_{c}
be the set of all accumulation points of
(S^{n}\widetilde{t^{c}})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
. As before, we denote by
S
the left shift on
X_{c}
, defined by
S(x_{n})\coloneqq x_{n+1}
. Since
SX_{c}=X_{c}
, this gives a well-defined dynamical system
(X_{c},S)
. In fact, we will see in Section
4
that
(X_{c},S)
is strictly ergodic.

To each
x=(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in X_{c}
, we can assign a diffraction measure in the following manner.
First, (denoting complex conjugation with a bar) we define the autocorrelation coefficients via

\eta^{c}_{n}\,\coloneqq\,\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{x_{m}}x_{m+n},

(6)

which is well-defined and independent of
x\in X_{c}
for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
due to unique ergodicity. We therefore have a well-defined autocorrelation measure

\gamma_{c}\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta^{c}_{n}\delta_{n},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure supported on
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The diffraction measure associated to
(X_{c},S)
is given by
\widehat{\gamma}_{c}
as the Fourier transformation of
\gamma_{c}
. The Fourier transform
\widehat{\gamma}_{c}
is a well-defined positive measure because
\gamma_{c}
is a positive definite measure.
We refer to
[
4
]
and Section
3
for general background on autocorrelation measures and diffraction.
The following result establishes the desired link to our earlier discussion.

Proposition 2.4
.

For every
c\in\mathbb{T}
, we have
\mu_{c}=\widehat{\gamma}_{c}|_{[0,1)}
.

In order to describe the local structure of a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{T}
, we use the notion of a local dimension, given by

d_{\nu}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\lim_{r\searrow 0}\frac{\log(\nu(B_{r}(x)))}{\log r},

if the limit exists, and where we denote by
B_{r}(x)
the closed ball around
x
with radius
r
with respect to the Euclidean distance
\rho
.
Similarly to the investigation of Birkhoff averages, we approach this quantity for
\mu_{c}
via the corresponding dimension spectrum

f_{c}(\alpha)\,\coloneqq\,\dim_{H}\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:d_{\mu_{c}}(x)=\alpha\right\}.

Making use of the analysis in
[
18
]
we obtain the following.

Theorem 2.5
.

For each
c\neq 0
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\left\{-\underline{\alpha}_{c}/\log 2\right\}
, we have
f_{c}(\alpha)=b_{c}(-\alpha\log 2)
.

Hence, the dimension spectrum can also be expressed in terms of the Legendre transform of the pressure via Theorem
2.1
.

Another convenient way to study the structure of a (fully supported) measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{T}
is via its
upper
and
lower

L^{q}
-
spectrum
, given for all
q\in{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{\nu}(q)\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\log 2}\log\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}{\nu}(\langle\omega\rangle)^{q}\right),

(7)

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{\nu}(q)\,

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\log 2}\log\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}{\nu}(\langle\omega\rangle)^{q}\right).

Moreover, in case that the limit in (
7
) exists, we simply write
\beta_{\nu}(q)
and denote it as the
L^{q}
-
spectrum
.
In the present setup, this is closely related to the pressure function as the following theorem shows.

Theorem 2.6
.

For each
c\neq 0
, we have

p_{c}=\log(2)\,\,\beta_{\mu_{c}}.

This result can be regarded as a generalization of the corresponding statement for the Gibbs measures of Hölder continuous potentials. Indeed, although
\mu_{c}
is not a (weak) Gibbs measure, our proof relies on a Gibbs type relation for
\mu_{c}
given in Proposition
7.7
.

We remark here that the existence of the
L^{q}
-spectrum also has further implications concerning Kreĭn–Feller operators and the quantization dimension of the measure
\mu_{c}
. In particular, we have that the spectral dimension of the Kreĭn–Feller operator and the quantization dimension exist.
This connection – including the necessary notations and concepts – will be provided in detail in Section
8
.

A quantity that is particularly accessible is the Fourier dimension of the measure
\mu_{c}
given by
\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)
. Using the autocorrelation structure of the generalized Thue–Morse sequences, we will show the following.

Theorem 2.7
.

The function
c\mapsto\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)
is real-analytic on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

In fact, we will express
\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)
in terms of a
correlation exponent

D_{2}^{c}
, see (
14
), via the relation
\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)=1-D_{2}^{c}
(Corollary
8.2
). The correlation exponent in turn can be written as the logarithm of the spectral radius of an explicit matrix (Theorem
5.4
); see Figure
2
for an illustration.
This also shows that
p_{c}(2)
depends analytically on
c
. The same is trivially true for
p_{c}(0)=1
and
p_{c}(1)=0
, due to a common normalization, and certainly is wrong for
p_{c}(t)
if
t<0

[
18
]
. For general
t>0
, continuity of
p_{c}(t)
in
c
holds
[
24
]
, but stronger notions of regularity remain open in general.

2.3.
The special case
\bm{c=0}

The point
c=0
is the unique fixed point of the doubling map and as such, special properties may be expected for the potential
\psi^{0}
that has its maximum at this position. In fact, this explains why
\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}
is the only
g
-measure in our family that is given by a pure point measure
[
12
]
. Let us emphasize that
\psi^{0}
is a coboundary, as was already noted in
[
18
]
. More precisely,

\psi^{0}(x)=2\log|\sin(2\pi x)|-2\log|\sin(\pi x)|-2\log 2,

(8)

for all
x\neq 0
. Following
[
18
]
, this can be used to work out an explicit expression for the (variational) pressure function, given by

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var},{0}}(t\psi^{0})=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}(t\psi^{0})=\max\{1-2t,0\}.

(9)

We will show explicitly that this expression coincides with the topological pressure, thus solving the case
c=0
in Theorem
2.3
.
On the other hand, the Birkhoff average
\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)
can only assume three possible values, namely
0
(for
x=0
),
-\infty
(for dyadic rationals) and
-\log 2

3.
Autocorrelation and diffraction

In this section we recall some basic facts about mathematical diffraction in the context of compact alphabets. We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for background information and further details.
For convenience, we will simply write
measure
for a (complex) Radon measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and given
g\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
(the compactly supported continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
), we write
\nu(g)
for the integral of
g
with respect to
\nu
.
As before, we let
\mathcal{A}
be the complex unit circle and
S
the shift action on the compact sequence space
\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Let
X\subset\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
be a subshift, that is, a closed and shift invariant subset of
\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Let us assume that
(X,S)
is uniquely ergodic.
As in the previous section, we associate to a sequence
x\in X
an
autocorrelation measure
. This is a complex (Radon) measure, given by

\gamma_{x}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\eta_{n}(x)\delta_{n},\quad\eta_{n}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{x_{m}}x_{m+n}.

Note that the autocorrelation coefficient
\eta_{n}(x)
is the Birkhoff average of the continuous function
f_{n}\colon x\mapsto\overline{x_{0}}x_{n}
. Hence, the unique ergodicity of
(X,S)
guarantees that
\eta_{n}=\eta_{n}(x)
exists as a limit and is independent of
x\in X
. Similarly,
\gamma=\gamma_{x}
is the same measure for all
x\in X
.

Alternatively, the autocorrelation
\gamma
is often introduced in terms of an Eberlein convolution. Given a sequence
x\in X
, we assign a weighted Dirac comb

\omega=\omega(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}x_{k}\delta_{k}.

In the context of diffraction theory, this models an atomic configuration of equally spaced scatterers of different types.
In the following, let
\nu_{1}\ast\nu_{2}
denote the convolution of two (finite) measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
. Further, for a general measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}

\widetilde{\nu}
be the measure determined by
\widetilde{\nu}(g)=\overline{\nu(\widetilde{g})}
, where
\widetilde{g}(x)=\overline{g(-x)}
, for every continuous function of compact support
g\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\omega_{n}
be the restriction of
\omega
to the interval

. Then, using that
\widetilde{\alpha\delta_{k}}=\overline{\alpha}\delta_{-k}

\gamma=\omega\circledast\widetilde{\omega}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\omega_{n}\ast\widetilde{\omega_{n}}}{n},

(10)

to be understood as a vague limit of measures.
By construction, the measure
\gamma
is
positive definite
in the sense that
\gamma(g\ast\widetilde{g})\geqslant 0
for every
g\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
.

. A systematic framework for substitutions on

alphabets was more recently given in
[
50
]
.

It is a matter of straightforward calculation to verify that the sequence
t^{c}
is invariant under
the following substitution on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
,

\vartheta_{c}\colon z\mapsto z\,R_{c}(z),

(11)

where
z\,R_{c}(z)
is supposed to be understood as a concatenation of
z
and
R_{c}(z)
.

Extend
t^{c}
arbitrarily to a sequence
\widetilde{t^{c}}

in
\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
and let
X_{c}
be the set of all accumulation points of
(S^{n}\widetilde{t^{c}})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, where, as before,
S
is the left shift on
X_{c}
.
Then,
(X_{c},S)
is a uniquely ergodic subshift of
(\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}},S)
, compare
[
55
, Cor. 12.2, Ex. 12.1]
. As the following remark shows,
(X_{c},S)
is minimal and hence, in fact, strictly ergodic.

Remark 4.1
.

If
c
is rational, all entries of
t^{c}
are contained in the finite alphabet
\mathcal{B}=\left\{R_{c}^{n}(1):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
. In this case,
t^{c}
is the unique fixed point of the primitive substitution
\vartheta_{c}
on
\mathcal{B}
and it follows from the classical theory that
(X_{c},S)
is strictly ergodic
[
4
]
.
If
c
is irrational,
\vartheta_{c}
is a substitution on a compact alphabet, and we can associate a subshift
(X_{\vartheta_{c}},S)
that is generated from the language of the substitution, as defined in
[
50
]
. In their notation,
\vartheta_{c}
is a
primitive
substitution and hence the minimality of
(X_{\vartheta_{c}},S)
follows from
[
50
, Prop. 33]
. It is worth noticing that
X_{c}
and
X_{\vartheta_{c}}
coincide. Indeed,
X_{c}\subset X_{\vartheta_{c}}
is verified from the definition and equality follows from the fact that
X_{\vartheta_{c}}
is minimal and
X_{c}
is a closed invariant subspace of
X_{\vartheta_{c}}
.

Note that
t^{c}_{[0,2^{n}-1]}=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We extend the circle rotation
R_{c}
to words as a homomorphism, that is
R_{c}(v_{1}\cdots v_{m})=R_{c}(v_{1})\cdots R_{c}(v_{m})\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
. We observe
R_{c}\circ\vartheta_{c}=\vartheta_{c}\circ R_{c}
. For
u_{c}^{n}=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)
we easily obtain the recursion

u_{c}^{n+1}=\vartheta_{c}^{n+1}(1)=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)\vartheta_{c}^{n}(R_{c}(1))=u_{c}^{n}\,R_{c}(u_{c}^{n}).

(12)

Remark 4.2
.

If
c
is irrational, there is an uncountable family
\left\{t^{c}(\alpha)\right\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
of points in
X_{c}
that restrict to
t^{c}
on the non-negative integers, but none of these is a fixed point for
\vartheta_{c}
(or any power of
\vartheta_{c}
).
These points can be constructed as follows. Given
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, define
\alpha_{n}=R_{c}^{-n}(\alpha)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Note that all two-letter words in
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
are legal for
\vartheta_{c}
(for a formal definition of legal words see
[
50
]
). Hence, all of the words

\vartheta_{c}^{n}(\alpha_{n})\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)

are legal. Note that
\vartheta_{c}^{n+1}(1)=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)R_{c}\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)
has
\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)
as a prefix. Similarly,

\vartheta_{c}^{n+1}(\alpha_{n+1})=R_{c}^{-1}(\vartheta_{c}^{n+1}(\alpha_{n}))=R_{c}^{-1}(\vartheta_{c}^{n}(\alpha_{n}))\,\vartheta_{c}^{n}(\alpha_{n})

has
\vartheta_{c}^{n}(\alpha_{n})
as a suffix for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence, the point
t^{c}(\alpha)
with

t^{c}(\alpha)_{[-2^{n},2^{n}-1]}=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(\alpha_{n})\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is well defined and contains only legal words.
Here and in the following
u_{[j,k]}
denotes the finite word of the
j
th digit to the
k
th digit of the (possibly infinite) word
u
.
Note that
t^{c}(\alpha)_{-1}=\alpha
, that is,
t^{c}(\alpha)
is parametrized by its first entry to the left of the origin.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
, the diffraction measure associated to
(X_{c},S)
is given by the Riesz product

\widehat{\gamma}_{c}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1+\cos(2\pi(2^{n}x-c))),

which converges in the vague topology. In particular,
\widehat{\gamma}_{c}=\mu_{c}\ast\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
\mu_{c}
as in (
4
).

Proof.

Let
t^{c}=t^{c}(\alpha)
be any of the sequences in
X_{c}
that coincides with
\widetilde{t}^{c}
on all non-negative indices
and
\omega^{c}
the corresponding weighted Dirac comb. By Corollary
3.2
, we can write the diffraction measure as

\widehat{\gamma}_{c}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\widehat{\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}}|^{2}}{2^{n}}\mathrm{Leb},

where

\widehat{\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}t_{k}^{c}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}kx}.

Recall that the first
2^{n}
entries of
t^{c}
are given by the word
u_{c}^{n}=\vartheta_{c}^{n}(1)
.
On the level of Dirac combs, (
12
) implies

\omega_{2^{n+1}}^{c}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}t_{k}^{c}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}R_{c}(t_{k}^{c})\delta_{2^{n}+k}=\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}+\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}c}\,\delta_{2^{n}}\ast\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}.

Hence, we find for
h_{n}=|\widehat{\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}}|^{2}/2^{n}
the recursion

\displaystyle h_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\Big|\big(1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi{\mathrm{i}}(2^{n}x-c)}\big)\,\widehat{\omega_{2^{n}}^{c}}(x)\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Big|1+\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi{\mathrm{i}}(2^{n}x-c)}\Big|^{2}h_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(1+\cos(2\pi(2^{n}x-c)))h_{n}(x).

Since
h_{0}(x)=1
, the diffraction measure becomes

\widehat{\gamma}_{c}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1+\cos(2\pi(2^{n}x-c))),

to be understood as a vague limit of (Lebesgue) density functions.
∎

5.
Autocorrelation coefficients and correlation exponent

For the Thue–Morse sequence (corresponding to the case
c=1/2
) it is well known that the autocorrelation coefficients given in (
6
) satisfy the recursive relations

\eta_{2n}^{1/2}=\eta_{n}^{1/2},\quad\eta_{2n+1}^{1/2}=-\frac{\eta_{n}^{1/2}+\eta_{n+1}^{1/2}}{2},

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, see
[
4
, Ch. 10]
.
This recursive structure is very useful to study both the autocorrelation and the diffraction measure of the Thue–Morse sequence. For example, it can be used to show that the Thue–Morse sequence is singular continuous. It has also been used in
[
5
]
to obtain a detailed understanding of the scaling behaviour of the Thue–Morse measure at the origin.
For general
c
, it follows already from
(
6
) that
\eta_{-n}^{c}=\overline{\eta_{n}^{c}}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We will use the following generalization of the recursion relations of the Thue–Morse sequence to obtain an expression for the Fourier dimension of the diffraction measure in the spirit of Zaks et al. in
[
59
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
\phi_{c}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}c}
. Then, the autocorrelation coefficients of the sequence
(t_{n}^{c})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
satisfy the relations

\displaystyle\eta_{2n}^{c}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}^{c},

\displaystyle\eta_{2n+1}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\phi_{c}\eta_{n}^{c}+\overline{\phi_{c}}\eta_{n+1}^{c}),

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Further,
\eta_{0}^{c}=1
and

\eta_{1}^{c}=\frac{\phi_{c}}{2-\overline{\phi_{c}}}.

Proof.

Following the same steps as in
[
4
, Ch. 10]
, this follows in a straightforward manner from the relations

t_{2n}^{c}=t_{n}^{c},\quad t_{2n+1}^{c}=\phi_{c}t_{n}^{c},

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, which can be verified from the fact that the sequence is a fixed point of the substitution
\vartheta_{c}
as defined in (
11
). Indeed, observe that for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{2k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{m}}t_{m+2n}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{2m}}t_{2m+2n}^{c}+\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{2m+1}}t_{2m+2n+1}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{m}}t^{c}_{m+n}+|\phi_{c}|^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{m}}t^{c}_{m+n}=2\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\overline{t^{c}_{m}}t^{c}_{m+n}.

Dividing by
2k
and sending
k\to\infty
yields the first relation
\eta_{2n}^{c}=\eta_{n}^{c}
. The second relation is obtained in a similar manner, again regrouping a finite sum according to odd and even indices. The statement
\eta_{0}^{c}=1
follows from the definition, noting that each element
t_{n}^{c}
of the sequence is an element of the unit circle. The expression for
\eta_{1}^{c}
follows by applying the second recursion relation in the case
n=0
.
∎

Recall that
\widehat{\nu}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}},x\mapsto\int\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi ix\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(\xi)
denotes the
Fourier transform
of the Borel measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{T}}
. Then the
upper Fourier dimension
of a measure
\nu
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{F}(\nu)\coloneqq\limsup_{R\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(R^{-1}\int_{-R}^{R}|\widehat{\nu}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)}{\log R}

(13)

and we talk about the
Fourier dimension

\dim_{F}(\nu)
in case the
\limsup
in (
13
) is a limit.
The Fourier dimension of
\widehat{\gamma}_{c}
is related to the growth behavior of the sequence
(\Theta_{n}^{c})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, with

\Theta_{n}^{c}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\eta_{m}^{c}|^{2}.

This growth property is captured in the
correlation exponent
given by

D_{2}^{c}\coloneqq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Theta_{n}^{c}}{\log n}.

(14)

Since
\Theta_{n}^{c}
is non-decreasing in
n
, a straightforward interpolation argument shows that
D_{2}^{c}
may alternatively be defined along the subsequence
(2^{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, i.e.

D_{2}^{c}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Theta_{2^{n}}^{c}}{n\log 2}.

We will see in Section
8.1
that this characteristic number is related to the Fourier dimension of
\widehat{\gamma}_{c}
and to the corresponding pressure function evaluated in
2
. Similarly to the proof of Proposition
5.1
, we would like to establish a recursive structure for
\Theta_{n}^{c}
by splitting the sum according to odd and even indices and using Proposition
5.1

for
\eta_{2m}^{c}
and
\eta_{2m+1}^{c}
. Since this creates several “cross-terms”, we introduce some auxiliary sequences, defined via

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}|\eta_{m}^{c}|^{2},

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{c}^{2}}{2}\,\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\eta_{m}^{c}\overline{\eta_{m+1}^{c}},

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For every
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
v_{n}=v_{n}^{c}=(Z_{n}^{c},\Pi_{n}^{c},\overline{\Pi^{c}_{n}})^{T}
. Then, we have
v_{2n}=M_{c}v_{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where

M_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}3&1&1\\
\phi_{c}&2\phi_{c}&0\\
\overline{\phi_{c}}&0&2\overline{\phi_{c}}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We prove the matrix relation line by line. Recall,
\phi_{c}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}c}
. First, note that

\displaystyle Z^{c}_{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left|\eta^{c}_{2m}\right|^{2}+\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left|\eta^{c}_{2m+1}\right|^{2}=\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left|\eta^{c}_{m}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left|\phi_{c}\eta^{c}_{m}+\overline{\phi_{c}}\eta^{c}_{m+1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=Z^{c}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}Z^{c}_{n}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left(\phi_{c}^{2}\eta^{c}_{m}\overline{\eta^{c}_{m+1}}+\overline{\phi_{c}}^{2}\,\overline{\eta^{c}_{m}}\eta^{c}_{m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}Z^{c}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Pi^{c}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\Pi^{c}_{n}},

which gives the first line of the matrix relation. The second line is found in a similar manner via

\displaystyle\Pi^{c}_{2n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{c}^{2}}{2}\biggl(\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\eta^{c}_{2m}\overline{\eta^{c}_{2m+1}}+\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\eta^{c}_{2m+1}\overline{\eta^{c}_{2m+2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{c}^{2}}{4}\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left(\overline{\phi_{c}}|\eta^{c}_{m}|^{2}+\phi_{c}\eta^{c}_{m}\overline{\eta^{c}_{m+1}}\right)+\frac{\phi_{c}^{2}}{4}\sum_{m=n}^{2n-1}\left(\phi_{c}\eta^{c}_{m}\overline{\eta^{c}_{m+1}}+\overline{\phi_{c}}|\eta^{c}_{m+1}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{c}}{2}Z^{c}_{n}+\phi_{c}\Pi^{c}_{n},

and the last line follows by taking the complex conjugate of the second line.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
, there is a unique eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{c}
of
M_{c}
with modulus strictly greater than
1
. This eigenvalue is real and depends real-analytically on
c
. An eigenvalue lies on the unit circle if and only if
c\in\{0,1/2\}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we may restrict our attention to
c\in[0,1/2]
.
It is easily verified that a value
\lambda^{c}
is an eigenvalue of
M_{c}
if and only if the same holds for its complex conjugate
\overline{\lambda^{c}}
. Hence, for each
c
, either all eigenvalues are real, or one of them is real and the other two form a complex conjugate pair.
Since all matrix entries of
M_{c}
depend analytically on
c
, it follows by standard perturbation theory that there is an (unordered) tuple
\lambda_{r}^{c},\lambda_{s}^{c},\lambda_{t}^{c}
of eigenvalues that depends analytically on
c

[
34
]
. Comparing the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
M_{c}
and its Jordan normal form yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}^{c}+\lambda_{s}^{c}+\lambda_{t}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}+2\cos(2\pi c),

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}^{c}\lambda_{s}^{c}+\lambda_{r}^{c}\lambda_{t}^{c}+\lambda_{s}^{c}\lambda_{t}^{c}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{5}{2}\cos(2\pi c),

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}^{c}\lambda_{s}^{c}\lambda_{t}^{c}

\displaystyle=1.

Assuming
\lambda^{c}=\lambda_{s}^{c}=\lambda_{t}^{c}
(implying
\lambda_{r}^{c}=(\lambda^{c})^{-2}
) leads to the system

\displaystyle 4(\lambda^{c})^{3}-(4+10\cos(2\pi c))\lambda^{c}+8

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4(\lambda^{c})^{3}-(3+4\cos(2\pi c))(\lambda^{c})^{2}+2

\displaystyle=0.

This can be verified to have precisely two pairs of solutions
(c,\lambda^{c})
with
c\in[0,1/2]
. In both cases
|\lambda^{c}|<1
.
Further, an explicit calculation shows that
\lambda^{c}=1
is an eigenvalue if and only if
c=0
and
\lambda^{c}=-1
is an eigenvalue if and only if
c=1/2
. In both cases there is a unique third eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{c}>1
.
Without loss of generality, for
c=0
, assume that the indices
r,s,t
are assigned in such a way that
\lambda_{1}^{c}=\lambda_{r}^{c}
,
\lambda_{s}^{c}=1
and
\lambda_{t}^{c}<1
.

We argue that
\lambda_{r}^{c}>1
for all
c\in[0,1/2]
. Indeed, by continuity, it cannot decrease below
1
along the real line because there is no eigenvalue
\lambda^{c}=1
for
c\in(0,1/2]
. Also,
\lambda_{r}^{c}
cannot split into a complex conjugate pair because eigenvalues
\lambda^{c}
of non-trivial multiplicity occur only for
|\lambda^{c}|<1
.
Conversely, for some
c\in(0,1/2)
we obtain that
\lambda_{s}^{c}=\lambda_{t}^{c}
is contained in the unit disc. Both
\lambda_{s}^{c}
and
\lambda_{t}^{c}
cannot leave the unit disc for any
c\in(0,1/2)
. Indeed, this is impossible as a complex conjugate pair because it would contradict
\lambda_{r}^{c}>1
and
\lambda_{r}^{c}\lambda_{s}^{c}\lambda_{t}^{c}=1
. Also, along the real line there are no eigenvalue solutions for
|\lambda^{c}|=1
if
c\in(0,1/2)
.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

The correlation exponent is well defined and given by

D_{2}^{c}=\frac{\log\lambda_{1}^{c}}{\log 2},

for all
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
. In particular,
c\mapsto D_{2}^{c}
is a real-analytic function on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Figure 2.
Illustration of
D_{2}^{c}
.

Proof.

If
c=0
, the observation
\eta_{n}^{0}=1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
easily implies the desired relation

D_{2}^{0}=\frac{\log 2}{\log 2}=1.

We hence assume
c\in(0,1)
in the following. Let
\lambda_{1}^{c},\lambda_{2}^{c},\lambda_{3}^{c}
be the eigenvalues of
M_{c}
, ordered via
|\lambda_{1}^{c}|\geqslant|\lambda_{2}^{c}|\geqslant|\lambda_{3}^{c}|
.
For the sake of establishing a contradiction, assume that
v_{1}^{c}=({Z}_{1}^{c},\Pi_{1}^{c},\overline{\Pi_{1}^{c}})^{T}
is contained in the two-dimensional subspace that is spanned by the (generalized) eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{2}^{c}
and
\lambda_{3}^{c}
.
We distinguish two cases.
If
c\neq 1/2
, it follows by Lemma
5.3
that
|\lambda_{2}^{c}|,|\lambda_{3}^{c}|<1
. We obtain that
v_{2^{n}}^{c}=M_{c}^{n}v_{1}^{c}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, in contradiction to the observation that
Z_{2n}^{c}\geqslant Z_{n}^{c}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

Z_{1}^{c}=|\eta_{1}^{c}|^{2}=\frac{1}{|2-\phi_{c}|^{2}}\neq 0.

If
c=1/2
, we have
\lambda_{2}^{c}=-1
,
|\lambda_{3}^{c}|<1
, again in contradiction to
Z_{2n}^{c}\geqslant Z_{n}^{c}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
It follows that
v_{1}^{c}
has a non-trivial component in the direction of the eigenvector
u^{c}
of
\lambda_{1}^{c}
.
Hence, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{1}^{c})^{-n}v_{2^{n}}^{c}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{1}^{c})^{-n}M^{n}_{c}v_{1}^{c}=\alpha u^{c},

(15)

for some
\alpha>0
. It is straightforward to see that
u_{1}^{c}\neq 0
. Indeed assuming
u^{c}=(0,u_{2}^{c},u_{3}^{c})
would lead to

\lambda_{1}^{c}u^{c}=M_{c}u^{c}=\begin{pmatrix}(u_{2}^{c}+u_{3}^{c})/2\\
\phi_{c}u_{2}^{c}\\
\overline{\phi_{c}}u_{3}^{c}\end{pmatrix},

which gives rise to the contradiction
1<|\lambda_{1}^{c}|=|\phi_{c}|=1
. The first component in (
15
) therefore gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{1}^{c})^{-n}Z_{2^{n}}^{c}=\alpha u_{1}^{c}>0.

Since we may write

\Theta_{2^{n}}^{c}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}Z_{2^{k}}^{c},

the fact that
Z_{2^{k}}^{c}
grows like a constant multiple of
(\lambda_{1}^{c})^{k}

immediately implies

D_{2}^{c}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Theta_{2^{n}}^{c}}{n\log 2}=\frac{\log\lambda_{1}^{c}}{\log 2}.

Since
\lambda_{1}^{c}
is bounded away from
0
, it follows that
D_{2}^{c}
is an analytic function of
\lambda_{1}^{c}
and hence of
c
. Since both
c
and
D_{2}^{c}
take only real values,
D_{2}^{c}
is in fact real-analytic.
∎

6.
Properties regarding the topological and variational pressure

In this section we will prove Theorem
2.3
. We start with the case
c=0
, proving (
9
). As pointed out in Section
2.3
, this case is substantially different and different methods of proof are necessary.

Proposition 6.1
.

We have
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{0})=\max\{(1-2t)\log 2,0\}
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

From the coboundary relation (
8
), we easily get the explicit expression

\psi^{0}_{n}(x)=-2n\log 2+2\log|\sin(2^{n}\pi x)|-2\log|\sin(\pi x)|,

for
x\neq 0
and fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By symmetry of
\psi^{0}
, we can restrict our attention to
x\in(0,1/2]
.
Let
0\leqslant m<2^{n-1}
and
w^{n,m}
be the word corresponding to the
m
th interval of length
2^{-n}
, that is, assume
\langle w^{n,m}\rangle=2^{-n}[m,m+1]
. Let
x_{m}=2^{-n}(m+1/2)
be the middle point of
\langle w^{n,m}\rangle
.
At these points, the expression
|\sin(2^{n}\pi x)|
assumes its maximum value
1
on
\langle w^{n,m}\rangle
, and we have

\psi_{n}^{0}(x_{m})=-2n\log 2-2\log|\sin(\pi x_{m})|.

On the other hand, since
2x\leqslant|\sin(\pi x)|<\pi x
on
(0,1/2]
we get for
x\in\langle w^{n,m}\rangle
and
m\geqslant 1
,

-2n\log 2-2\log|\sin(\pi x)|\in(-2\log(\pi(m+1)),-2\log(2m)].

Hence, up to an additive constant,
\sup_{x\in\langle w^{n,m}\rangle}\psi^{0}_{n}(x)
evaluates to
-2\log m
. For
m=0
, we get
\sup_{x\in\langle w^{n,0}\rangle}\psi_{n}^{0}(x)=\psi_{n}^{0}(0)=0
. Using these relations, we obtain for the upper topological pressure

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{0})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{m=1}^{2^{n-1}}m^{-2t}.

(16)

Using an integral estimate, we get for
t=1/2
,

\sum_{m=1}^{2^{n-1}}m^{-1}\sim\int_{[1,2^{n-1}]}x^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=(n-1)\log 2,

where we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=1
.
Hence,
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(\psi^{0}/2)=0
. For
t\neq 1/2
, we get

\sum_{m=1}^{2^{n-1}}m^{-2t}\sim\int_{[1,2^{n-1}]}x^{-2t}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{1-2t}\left(2^{(1-2t)(n-1)}-1\right).

Plugging this into (
16
), and distinguishing the cases for
t<1/2
and
t>1/2
, we note that the limsup in (
16
) is indeed a limit and we finally get

Remark 6.2
.

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
2.3
a comment regarding the definition of the topological pressure is in place. While for Hölder continuous potentials the value in (
1
) and the line below doesn’t change if we consider the supremum or the infimum of the sum
\psi_{n}^{c}
, here the use of the supremum (infimum) for
t>0
(
t<0
respectively) is paramount as can be seen as follows. Since
\psi^{c}
is unbounded on the negative side, there exists
x\in\Sigma^{n}
such that
\inf_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)=-\infty
making the definition trivial. However, as shown in
[
18
]
the set of points
c
for which the restricted variational pressure as given in (
17

-\infty
is a set of zero Hausdorff dimension. Hence, to obtain the statement of Theorem
2.3
, indeed the choice of the infimum and supremum matters.

The next step in proving Theorem
2.3
is to associate the restricted variational pressure with a restricted topological pressure.
For a closed subshift
{\mathbb{X}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathbb{X}}
that is invariant under the
left shift, let us define the set of admissible
n

\Sigma^{\prime n}=\left\{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}\colon\langle\omega\rangle\cap{\mathbb{X}}^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\right\}
and thus define the restricted topological pressure by

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c}\hskip 0.5pt|\hskip 0.5pt{\mathbb{X}}^{\prime}\right)\,:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{\prime n}}\exp\Bigl(t\hskip 0.5pt\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle\cap{\mathbb{X}}^{\prime}}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\Bigr).

By definition,

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\hskip 0.5pt\psi^{c}\hskip 0.5pt|\hskip 0.5pt{\mathbb{X}}\right)
(assuming the limit exits, otherwise analogous statements hold for the upper and lower (restricted) pressure).

Furthermore, we use the definition from
[
18
]
for the restricted variational pressure

\displaystyle p_{\operatorname{var}}(t;\delta)\coloneqq\sup\left\{h_{\nu}+t\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\colon\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\left(\delta\right)\right\},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{T}\left(\delta\right)=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\colon\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset K_{\delta}\right\}

K_{\delta}:=K^{c}_{\delta}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}:\rho(T^{n}x,\breve{c})\geqslant\delta,\,\forall n\geqslant 0\right\},

(18)

for

For the following let
\breve{c}^{\ast}
to be the alternative representation of
\breve{c}
in case

is a dyadic point and has thus two dyadic representations. Otherwise, formally, we simply identify
\breve{c}
and

.

Our aim is to show that for

we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$t\hskip 0.5pt\sup_{x\in\langle\omega_{n}\rangle\cap{\mathbb{X}}_{m}}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}t\left(-\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$m+1$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$2$}}+\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$m^{2}$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\right)=\infty.

In particular, the limit of this expression as
m\to\infty
exists. Moreover, as
\psi^{c}
is Hölder continuous on
{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}
, we can directly argue with
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}
not needing to consider the upper or lower pressure.
On the other hand, using again
\psi^{c}(x)\leqslant 2\log(\pi\rho(x,\breve{c}))
we have for
\omega_{n}=0^{n}
that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\langle\omega_{n}\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log(\pi 2^{-k})=n(2\log\pi-(n+1)\log 2)

and thus

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)\geqslant\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$t\hskip 0.5pt\sup_{x\in\langle\omega_{n}\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)$}}{\raisebox{0.5pt}{$n$}}\geqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}t(2\log\pi-(n+1)\log 2)=\infty,

proving the proposition for
c=1/2
.

Next, we prove the general case
c\notin\{0,1/2\}
. For the following we set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}^{n}=\Sigma_{m}^{n}(c)=\left\{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}\colon\nexists\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}\colon\omega_{[\ell,\ell+m]}=\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}\text{ or }\omega_{[\ell,\ell+m]}=\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,m+1]}\right\}.

(20)

In words,
\Sigma_{m}^{n}
denotes the set of
n
-letter words which do not have an
m+1
-letter subword coinciding with
\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}
(or
\breve{c}_{[1,m+1}^{\ast}
) and thus
\Sigma_{m}^{n}=\{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}\colon\langle\omega\rangle\cap{\mathbb{X}}_{m}\neq\varnothing\}
. For
m\geqslant n
we understand
\Sigma_{m}^{n}
to be
\Sigma^{n}
.

Furthermore, we set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{n}=\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{n}(c)=\left\{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}\colon\right.

\displaystyle\nexists\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n-m\}\colon\omega_{[\ell,\ell+m]}\in\big\{\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]},\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,m+1]}\big\},

\displaystyle\hskip-2.27621pt\left.\nexists i\in\{1,\ldots,m+1\}\colon\omega_{[n-i+1,n]}\in\big\{\breve{c}_{[1,i]},\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,i]}\big\}\right\}.

Here we have additionally excluded words
u\in\Sigma^{n}_{m}
such that a suffix of
u
coincides with a prefix of the forbidden word(s). This construction ensures that the concatenation of words in
\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n_{1}}_{m}
and
\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n_{2}}_{m}
still does not contain a forbidden word. More precisely,

\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n_{1}}_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n_{2}}_{m}\subset\widetilde{\Sigma}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}_{m},

(21)

for all
n_{1},n_{2},m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This property will be important for establishing monotonicity in a suitable approximation of
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})
if
t<0
.

We start by showing an alternative expression for
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})
, i.e. we first show that for
large enough
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t<0
we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma_{m}^{n}}\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma_{m}^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right).

(22)

We will use a similar argumentation as in
[
3
, Proof of Lem. 4.1]
and first note that
there exists
K>0
such that on
[0,1]\setminus C_{m}(\breve{c})
we have
|\psi^{\prime}|\leqslant K/2
.
Then
for all
\omega\in\Sigma_{m}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)-\inf_{y\in\langle\omega\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\psi_{n}^{c}(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\sup_{x\in\langle S^{k-1}\omega\rangle}\psi^{c}(x)-\inf_{y\in\langle S^{k-1}\omega\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\psi^{c}(y)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}K2^{-(n-k)-1}\leqslant K.

This follows from the fact that the supremum over
\langle S^{k-1}\omega\rangle
is always attained outside
C_{m}(\breve{c})
.
Thus, for
t<0
we obtain (
22
).
Since this expression is smaller than the (lower) topological pressure of
t\psi^{c}
, we obtain that

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})\leqslant\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})

for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and hence that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})\leqslant\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}).

(23)

For
t<0
we define the functions
\widetilde{a},a\colon\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\right)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}\left(n,m\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)

and its acceleration
a(n,m)=\widetilde{a}(2^{n},m)
.
For
m=\infty
we understand the above term as

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n}=\left\{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}\colon\nexists i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\colon\omega_{[n-i+1,n]}\in\big\{\breve{c}_{[1,i]},\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,i]}\big\}\right\}.

We notice that
a(n,\cdot)
is monotonically increasing since for
m
increasing we just add more summands.
Moreover,
n\cdot\widetilde{a}(n,m)
is superadditive in
n
– this can be derived in a straightforward manner, using (
21
).
Thus,

2^{n+1}a(n+1,m)=2^{n+1}\widetilde{a}(2^{n+1},m)\geqslant 2^{n}(\widetilde{a}(2^{n},m)+\widetilde{a}(2^{n},m))=2^{n+1}a(n,m)

and thus the function
a(\cdot,m)
is monotonically increasing and hence,
a(\infty,m)
is well-defined by taking the limit
n\to\infty
.

Hence, for
t<0
the function
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is monotonically increasing in both variables. This allows us to conclude

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})

\displaystyle\geqslant\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}a(n,m)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}a(n,m)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{2^{n}}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{2^{n}}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right),

(24)

where the last limit exists by superadditivity.
It remains to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right).

In order to proceed we will give the following lemma. Its proof will be postponed to Section
9
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Assume
c\notin\{0,1/2\}
. Then, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists an
\ell_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. For all
v\in\Sigma^{n}
, there is
w=vu\in\Sigma^{n+\ell_{n}}
such that
w_{[k,|w|]}
is not a prefix of
\breve{c}
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant|w|
. For large enough
n
, we can choose
\ell_{n}\leqslant 5\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n
.

By Lemma
6.5

for each
\omega\in\Sigma^{n}
there exists a word
u
with

|u|=\ell_{n}
and
\ell_{n}\leqslant\lfloor 5\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n\rfloor

such that no suffix of
wu
is a prefix of
\breve{c}
. For the following we denote such a word by
u_{\omega}
. In case there are more than one such word, we choose one.
Since
\psi^{c}\leqslant 0
and thus
t\psi^{c}\geqslant 0
if
t<0
, we have by (
24
)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega u_{\omega}\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega u_{\omega}\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n+\ell_{n}}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n+\ell_{n}}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n+\ell_{n}}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n+\ell_{n}}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n+\ell_{n}}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n+\ell_{n}}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\log\sum_{\omega\in\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\infty}^{n}}\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}).

Combining this with (
23
), we obtain

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})\leqslant\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})\leqslant\limsup_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})\leqslant\underline{\mathcal{P}}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}),

proving the statement of the proposition.
∎

In the next steps we will prove an analog to Proposition
6.4
for
t\geqslant 0
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
c\neq 0
. For all
t\geqslant 0
, we have that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}^{c}_{m})=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}).

We note that here, the existence of
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}
already follows by subadditivity.
An important part of the proof of Proposition
6.6
is the following Proposition
6.7
. Its proof is rather technical and we postpone it to Subsection
9.3
after we have collected all the necessary results in Section
9
.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
\breve{c}\neq 1/2
and
\varepsilon>0
and let
\Sigma^{+}
be the set of finite letter words with letters in
\Sigma
and let
\Sigma^{n}_{m}
be defined as in (
20
). There are infinitely many
m
such that there is a map
\theta_{m}\colon\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}
with the following properties for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
sufficiently large,

(1)

\theta_{m}(\Sigma^{n})\subset\Sigma^{n}_{m}
;

(2)

for all
\omega\in\Sigma^{n}_{m}
we have
\#\theta_{m}^{-1}(w)\leqslant 2^{\varepsilon n}
;

(3)

if
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and
w^{\prime}=\theta_{m}(w)
, we have

\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\leqslant\sup_{y\in\langle w^{\prime}\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\psi^{c}_{n}(y)+\varepsilon n.

Proof of Proposition
6.6
.

Let
t\geqslant 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, the sequence
(p_{m}(t))_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
p_{m}(t)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}^{c}_{m})
is monotonously increasing in
m
, and hence

p(t):=\lim_{m\to\infty}p_{m}(t)\leqslant\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})

certainly exists. Choose
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\theta_{m}
as in Proposition
6.7
and
n
sufficiently large. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\exp(t\psi^{c}_{n}(x))

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{t\varepsilon n}\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{y\in\langle\theta_{m}(w)\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\exp(t\psi^{c}_{n}(y))

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{(t+\log 2)\varepsilon n}\sum_{w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{n}_{m}}\sup_{y\in\langle w^{\prime}\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\exp(t\psi^{c}_{n}(y)).

Taking logarithms, dividing by
n
and taking limits, yields

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})\leqslant p_{m}(t)+(t+\log 2)\varepsilon,

for infinitely many
m
and hence
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})\leqslant p(t)+(t+\log 2)\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Finally, we are in the position to give a proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

For
c=0
, the equality of topological pressure and (restricted) variational pressure follows from the explicit expressions in Proposition
6.1
and (
9
). In the following, we assume
c\neq 0
. If we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)\coloneqq\sup\left\{h_{\nu}+t\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\colon\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)\right\},

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{T}\left({\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}\colon\mathrm{supp}(\nu)\subset{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right\},

then we have

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)
as
\psi^{c}
is Hölder continuous on
{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}
.

Furthermore, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\langle\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle\supset B_{\delta}(\breve{c})

(or
(\langle\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle\cup\langle\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle)\supset B_{\delta}(\breve{c})
in case of a dyadic representation of
\breve{c}
) and on the other hand for all
\delta>0
there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{\delta}(\breve{c})\supset\langle\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle
(or
B_{\delta}(\breve{c})\supset\langle\breve{c}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle\cup\langle\breve{c}^{\ast}_{[1,m+1]}\rangle
) in case of a dyadic representation of
\breve{c}
).
Hence, by Propositions
6.3
,
6.4
and
6.6
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var},c}(t\psi^{c})=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}p_{\operatorname{var}}(t;\delta)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\lvert{\mathbb{X}}_{m}^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)

giving (
2
).

In order to prove (
3
) we follow the proof of
[
9
, Prop. 1.21]
.
Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}
. Then we have

\int t\psi_{n}^{c}/n\,\mathrm{d}\nu=t\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
. Hence, we obtain for
t\geqslant 0
that

\displaystyle h_{\nu}+t\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\nu(\langle w\rangle)(-\log\nu(\langle w\rangle))+t\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\exp(t\psi_{n}^{c}(x))=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}),

where the last inequality follows from elementary calculus, compare
[
9
, Lem. 1.1]
.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}(t\psi^{c})=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}}h_{\nu}+t\int\psi^{c}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\leqslant\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})

implying (
3
).

Finally, we note that
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)
is a supremum over affine functions and hence convex.
∎

Two comments regarding the just concluded proof are in order.

Remark 6.8
.

By
[
37
, Thm. 4.4.11]
, for
t\geqslant 0
we already have that
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{var}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}\left(t\psi^{c}\right)
.

Remark 6.9
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is given in
[
18
, Sec. 6]
. One central part of this proof is
[
18
, Prop. 6.4]
stating

\displaystyle p^{*}(t)=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}p^{*}(t;\delta).

(25)

Our results provide an alternative method to show this statement by noting that
the proof of Theorem
2.3
implies that for each

sufficiently small we can find
m,m^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}

).

7.
Measure decay of the generalized Thue–Morse measure

In this section for
\omega\in\Sigma^{n}
we will first find ways to estimate
\mu_{c}(\langle\omega\rangle)
comparing it with
\psi^{c}_{n}(x)
,
x\in\langle\omega\rangle
. These results will help us to prove Theorem
2.5
and Theorem
2.6
.

7.1.
Gibbs type properties

It is immediately clear that
\mu_{c}
can not be a (weak) Gibbs measure (see
[
38
]
for a definition of weak Gibbs measures) for any
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
as for any
\omega\in\Sigma^{n}
we have
\inf_{x\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp(\psi_{n}^{c}(x))=0
.
However, in this section we collect a number of statements presenting weak (partly one-sided) Gibbs properties which will be helpful in the following.

We start by recalling a few basic facts about
g
-measures. To a given continuous
g
-function
g\colon{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{T}}
, satisfying
\sum_{y\in T^{-1}x}g(y)\equiv 1
, we assign a transfer operator
\varphi_{g}
on the space
C({\mathbb{T}})
of continuous functions from
{\mathbb{T}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
via

\varphi_{g}(f)\colon x\mapsto\sum_{y\in T^{-1}x}g(y)f(y).

We define its dual action on the set of Borel probability measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{T}}
via the relation
(\varphi^{*}_{g}\nu)(f)=\nu(\varphi_{g}f)
, where as before
\nu(f)=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\nu
. Following Keane
[
36
]
, we call
\nu
a
g
-measure
(with respect to
g
), if it is invariant under
\varphi^{*}_{g}
. Due to a result by Ledrappier
[
48
]
, this is the case precisely if
\nu
is an equilibrium measure with respect to the potential
\psi_{g}=\log g
. While the existence of
g
-measures is automatic for continuous
g
, uniqueness is more subtle in general and depends on the set of pre-images of one

g^{-1}(\{1\})
and the regularity of
g
. For convenience, we restrict ourselves to Hölder continuous functions
g
in the following. As long as there is at most one position
x_{1}
with
g(x_{1})=1
, uniqueness of the
g
-measure
\nu_{g}
is guaranteed, and the corresponding
g
-measure is continuous, unless the position
x_{1}=0
is the unique fixed point of
T
(in which case
\nu_{g}=\delta_{0}
)
[
12
]
. If
x_{1}\neq 0
, the measure
\nu_{g}
has full topological support
[
2
, Thm. 4.1]
and
\nu_{g}
is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure unless
g\equiv 1/2
.
For our family of
g
-functions
(g^{c})_{c\in{\mathbb{T}}}
, this yields the following (for notational convenience, let
\varphi_{c}=\varphi_{g^{c}}
).

Corollary 7.1
.

For each
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
, the measure
\mu_{c}
is the unique
g
-measure with
\varphi_{c}^{*}\mu_{c}=\mu_{c}
. We have
\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}
, and for all
c\neq 0
the measure
\mu_{c}
is singular continuous and has full topological support on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

In fact,

\mu_{c}
is strongly mixing with respect to the dynamical system
(\mathbb{T},T)
and is attractive in the sense that
(\varphi_{c}^{*})^{n}\nu
converges weakly to
\mu_{c}
for every
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{T}

[
36
]
. Choosing
\nu
to be the Lebesgue measure, we obtain that
(\varphi_{c}^{*})^{n}\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, with density function

\prod_{m=0}^{n-1}2g^{c}(T^{m}x),

compare
[
15
, Prop. 1]
.
For
n\to\infty
, this gives the product representation of
\mu_{c}
in (
4
). Using the transfer operator
\varphi_{c}^{*}
will also be helpful to prove the following self-consistency relation.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
I\subset\mathbb{T}
an interval of length at most
2^{-n}
. If
|I|=2^{-n}
assume that at least one of the boundary points is not contained in
I
. Then, the restriction
T_{I}^{n}\colon I\to T^{n}I
is bijective and, writing
T_{I}^{-n}
for its inverse,

\mu_{c}(I)=\int_{T^{n}I}\exp(\psi_{n}^{c}(T_{I}^{-n}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{c}(x).

In particular,

\log\mu_{c}(T^{n}I)+\inf_{x\in I}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\leqslant\log\mu_{c}(I)\leqslant\log\mu_{c}(T^{n}I)+\sup_{x\in I}\psi_{n}^{c}(x).

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mu_{c}
is a
g
-measure for the
g
-function
g(x)=\exp(\psi^{c}(x))
, the proof is similar to
[
22
, Prop. 6.1.14]
. In fact,
\mu_{c}
being invariant under the transfer operator
\varphi_{c}
, we have

\mu_{c}(I)=\mu_{c}(\mathds{1}_{I})=\mu_{c}(\varphi_{c}^{n}\mathds{1}_{I}),

where
\mathds{1}_{I}
is the indicator function on
I
,
and, writing
g_{n}^{c}(x):=\exp(\psi_{n}^{c}(x))
,

(\varphi_{c}^{n}\mathds{1}_{I})(x)=\sum_{y\in T^{-n}x}g_{n}^{c}(y)\mathds{1}_{I}(y)=g_{n}^{c}(T_{I}^{-n}x)\mathds{1}_{T^{n}I}(x),

using that
y\in I
requires
x=T^{n}y\in T^{n}I
and, in this case,
y=T_{I}^{-n}x
because the restriction of
T^{n}
to
I
is bijective. This shows the first claim. The second claim follows by estimating the function

with its infimum (or supremum) and taking logarithms.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

If

, the restriction in Lemma

that

may not be closed if

is not essential. In this case, the claim remains true if we choose

assigns no mass to the boundary points.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and
v\in\Sigma^{m}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

. Then, for all

\log\mu_{c}(\langle v\rangle)+\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\leqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle wv\rangle)\leqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle v\rangle)+\sup_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)

and for

\inf_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\leqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\leqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x).

There is a global lower bound for the decay of the measure on cylinder sets, as long as the measure
\mu_{c}
is non-atomic.

Proposition 7.5
.

Assume that
c\neq 0
. Then,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\inf_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant-1.

Remark 7.6
.

For the case of arbitrary
c\neq 0
, the bound
-1
in the preceding lemma is sharp. Indeed, for the Thue–Morse measure, the limit exists and equals
-1
. Of course, for particular choices of

The proof of this as well as of all the following propositions in this subsection are postponed to a later point in Subsection
9.4
.

The following proposition gives a two-sided bound for those systems where
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})<\infty
.
It will play a central role in the proof of Theorem
2.6
.
Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})<\infty

, and
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})=\infty

Proposition 7.7
.

Let

. If
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c})<\infty

, we have

\sup_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\Bigl|\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)-\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\Bigr|\in o(n).

Remark 7.8
.

This result shows that if

and

, the measure

is in fact very close to a (weak) Gibbs measure. More precisely, we obtain that there is a sequence

such that for all

the set of points

Finally, if we restrict ourselves to intervals that intersect the

.
We also note that

is closed and hence compact.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let

and

. Then there exists a number

Connection to

-spectrum – proof of Theorem

The results collected in Section
6
and the last Subsection

enable us to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

By the last line of Corollary
7.4
we have for
t\geqslant 0
that

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{\mu_{c}}(t)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\sup_{y\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\,\psi^{c}_{n}(y)\right)\right)}{n\log 2}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c})}{\log 2}.

On the other hand, by Lemma
7.9
and Proposition
6.3
we have for
t\geqslant 0
and all

sufficiently large that

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{\mu_{c}}(t)

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log R+\log\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}_{m}}\sup_{y\in\langle\omega\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}}\exp\left(t\,\psi^{c}_{n}(y)\right)\right)}{n\log 2}\geqslant\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c}|\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c})}{\log 2}

and by Proposition
6.4
we have
\underline{\beta}_{\mu_{c}}(t)\geqslant\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c})/\log 2
.

In the next steps we consider the case
t<0
. By Corollary
7.4
we have

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{\mu_{c}}(t)

\displaystyle\geqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(\sum_{\omega\in\Sigma^{n}}\inf_{y\in\langle\omega\rangle}\exp\left(t\,\psi^{c}_{n}(y)\right)\right)}{n\log 2}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c})}{\log 2}.

To show the upper bound for
t<0
we notice that if

\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c})=\infty
, there is nothing to show. Otherwise, we can control the size of
\mu_{c}
on cylinder sets via Proposition

. In particular, there is a sequence
(\iota_{n})
with

such that

\log\mu(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-\iota_{n},

for all

. It follows that

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{\mu_{c}}(t)\,\log 2

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\exp(t\,\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle))\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\exp\Bigl(t\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\Bigr)\right)-\frac{t\iota_{n}}{n}=\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\,\psi^{c}),

completing the proof.
∎

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.5

It is well-known that the dimension spectrum of a measure can be bounded above by the Legendre transform of its
L^{q}
-spectrum, see for example
[
47
]
- a fact that we will need to prove Theorem
2.5
. Since definitions and formulations of the result differ slightly in the literature, we give a self-contained proof.

Lemma 7.10
.

Let
\nu
be a (fully-supported) measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
f_{\nu}
its dimension spectrum. Assume
\beta_{\nu}(q)
exists for all
q\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then,
f_{\nu}(\alpha)\leqslant\max\{-\beta_{\nu}^{\ast}(-\alpha),0\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First, fix
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and some
\varepsilon>0
, and let us set
r_{n}=2^{-n}
. Then,
d_{\nu}(x)=\alpha
implies that
r_{n}^{\alpha+\varepsilon}\leqslant\nu(B_{r_{n}}(x))\leqslant r_{n}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}
for all large enough
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For each such
n
, we have
\langle x_{1}\ldots x_{n+1}\rangle\subset B_{r_{n}}(x)
. That is,
x\in\langle v\rangle
for some
v\in\Sigma^{n+1}
with

\nu(\langle v\rangle)\leqslant\nu(B_{r_{n}}(x))\leqslant r_{n}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}.

If we set
\Gamma_{n}^{-}=\{v\in\Sigma^{n}:\nu(\langle v\rangle)\leqslant r_{n-1}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\}
, we therefore get for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)=\{x\in\mathbb{T}:d_{\nu}(x)=\alpha\}\subset\bigcup_{n\geqslant m}\bigcup_{v\in\Gamma_{n}^{-}}\langle v\rangle.

(26)

Similarly,
x\in\langle w\rangle
for words
w,w^{L},w^{R}\in\Sigma^{n}
such that
B_{r_{n}}(x)\subset\langle w^{L}\rangle\cup\langle w\rangle\cup\langle w^{R}\rangle
. Here
w^{L}
and
w^{R}
are the left and right neighbors of
w
in (cyclic) lexicographic order. The sets

\Gamma_{n}^{+}=\{w\in\Sigma^{n}:\nu(\langle w^{L}\rangle)+\nu(\langle w\rangle)+\nu(\langle w^{R}\rangle)\geqslant r_{n}^{\alpha+\varepsilon}\}

therefore also provide a cover, for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, via

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)\subset\bigcup_{n\geqslant m}\bigcup_{w\in\Gamma_{n}^{+}}\langle w\rangle.

For
s>\max\{-\beta_{\nu}^{\ast}(-\alpha),0\}
, we use these covers to show that the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)
vanishes, implying the desired bound on the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)
. By definition of the Legendre transform, we can find
q\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

s>\alpha q+\beta_{\nu}(q).

We proceed by a distinction of cases. First assume that
q\leqslant 0
. The value of
\beta_{\nu}(q)
provides a bound on the cardinality of
\Gamma_{n}^{-}
because for each
\delta>0
, we get for large enough
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

r_{n}^{-\beta_{\nu}(q)-\delta}\geqslant\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\nu(\langle w\rangle)^{q}\geqslant\sum_{w\in\Gamma_{n}^{-}}\nu(\langle w\rangle)^{q}\geqslant r_{n-1}^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)q}\#\Gamma_{n}^{-}.

We use the cover in (
26
) to bound the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)
from above by

\sum_{n\geqslant m}\#\Gamma_{n}^{-}r_{n}^{s}\leqslant C\sum_{n\geqslant m}r_{n}^{s-\beta_{\nu}(q)-\delta-(\alpha-\varepsilon)q}\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}0,

provided that
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
are chosen small enough. This shows that
f_{\nu}(\alpha)>s
in the case
q\leqslant 0
.
If
q>0
, we estimate
\#\Gamma_{n}^{+}
via

\displaystyle 3^{q+1}\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\nu(\langle w\rangle)^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}3^{q}\left(\nu(\langle w^{L}\rangle)^{q}+\nu(\langle w\rangle)^{q}+\nu(\langle w^{R}\rangle)^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{w\in\Gamma_{n}^{+}}\left(\nu(\langle w^{L}\rangle)+\nu(\langle w\rangle)+\nu(\langle w^{R}\rangle)\right)^{q}\geqslant\#\Gamma_{n}^{+}r_{n}^{(\alpha+\varepsilon)q}.

The remainder of the proof proceeds just like in the case
q\leqslant 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We first note that

\displaystyle\{x\in{\mathbb{T}}\colon d_{\mu_{c}}(x)=\alpha\}\supseteq\left\{x\in K_{\delta}\colon d_{\mu_{c}}(x)=\alpha\right\},

for all
\delta>0
with
K_{\delta}
as in (
18
).
In the following we will prove

\displaystyle\left\{x\in K_{\delta}\colon d_{\mu_{c}}(x)=\alpha\right\}=\left\{x\in K_{\delta}\colon\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)=-\alpha\log 2\right\},

(27)

where
\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)
denotes the Birkhoff average
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)/n
.
We note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\dim_{H}\left\{x\in K_{\delta}\colon\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)=\alpha\right\}=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}-\frac{p_{c}^{*}(\alpha;\delta)}{\log 2}=-\frac{p_{c}^{*}(\alpha)}{\log 2}=\dim_{H}\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}\colon\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)=\alpha\right\},

where the first equality follows from the fact that
\psi^{c}
is Hölder continuous on
K_{\delta}
, the second equality is given in (
25
) and the third equality is Theorem
2.1
.
Hence, (
27
) establishes the lower bound of Theorem
2.5
.
Let
x\in K_{\delta}
. Applying Lemma
7.9
to
I=B_{2^{-n-1}}(x)
, we obtain, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

R^{-1}\leqslant\frac{\mu_{c}(B_{2^{-n-1}}(x))}{\exp(\psi^{c}_{n}(x))}\leqslant R.

This yields

d_{\mu_{c}}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu_{c}(B_{2^{-n}}(x))}{\log 2^{-n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\psi^{c}_{n}(x)}{-n\log 2}=-\frac{\overline{\psi}^{c}(x)}{\log 2},

whenever the limit exists.

To prove the upper bound, recall from Lemma
7.10
that
f_{c}(\alpha)\leqslant\max\{-\beta_{\mu_{c}}^{\ast}(-\alpha),0\}
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By Theorem
2.6
, we have
p_{c}=\beta_{\mu_{c}}\log 2
, which implies
\beta^{\ast}_{\mu_{c}}(\alpha)=p^{\ast}(\alpha\log 2)/\log 2
for all
\alpha
. Hence,

f_{c}(\alpha)\leqslant\max\left\{-\frac{p_{c}^{\ast}(-\alpha\log 2)}{\log 2},0\right\},

which is precisely the expression for
b_{c}(-\alpha\log 2)
in Theorem
2.1
, if
-\alpha\log 2\neq\underline{\alpha}_{c}
.
∎

8.
Applications of the
L^{q}
-spectrum to Fourier dimensions, Kreĭn–Feller operators, and quantization dimensions

In this section we demonstrate how special points of the
L^{q}
-spectrum
\beta_{\mu_{c}}
can be related to other analytic and fractal-geometric characteristics of
\mu_{c}
. A particularly prominent example is the
Fourier dimension

\dim_{F}(\mu_{c})
as defined in (
13
) which we consider next.

8.1.
The Fourier dimension

The following statement holds for arbitrary measures
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}
and gives the connection of the Fourier dimension and the
L^{q}
-spectrum, see for example
[
46
]
and
[
58
, Thm. 6.6]
. If the
L^{q}
-spectrum
\beta_{\nu}
exists as a limit, we have

\dim_{F}(\nu)=-\beta_{\nu}(2).

(28)

If
\nu
has compact support, regarding it as a measure on the torus yields a similar relation that employs the discrete Fourier coefficients instead of the Fourier transform on the real line. This can be understood from the fact that the Pontryagin dual of the torus is given by the integer lattice (whereas the real line is self-dual). The natural analogue of (
28
) reads as follows.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\nu
be a Borel probability measure on the torus
{\mathbb{T}}
and assume that
\beta_{\nu}(2)
exists. Then,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Bigl(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}\Bigr)}{\log n}=1-\beta_{\nu}(2).

(29)

In particular, the limit exists and coincides with
1+\dim_{F}(\nu)
.

Proof.

The last statement follows immediately from (
28
). For the proof of (
29
), we follow similar ideas as in
[
26
]
.
First, let us regard
\nu
as a measure on
[0,1]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, an alternative expression for
\beta_{\nu}(2)
is given by

\beta_{\nu}(2)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{\log r}\log\Bigl(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|\nu(B_{r}(x))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)-1,

see for example
[
58
, Thm. 6.2]
. Considering the ball to be defined on the torus instead of
{\mathbb{R}}

gives a negligible contribution since for
r<1/2
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|\nu(B_{r}(x))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\nu(B_{r}(x))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant 2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|\nu(B_{r}(x))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which leaves the exponential growth behaviour unchanged. We may hence work with an integration over the torus instead.
For convenience of notation, let us set

\Gamma(r)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\nu(B_{r}(x))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
\nu(B_{r}(x))
as a function of
x
can also be expressed as
\nu\ast\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}
. Using Parseval’s identity, we hence get

\Gamma(r)=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}}(m)|^{2},

where

\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}}(m)=2r\operatorname{sinc}(2r\pi m),\quad\operatorname{sinc}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\sin(x)}{x}.

Since
\operatorname{sinc}(x)
is continuous in
x
and
\operatorname{sinc}(0)=1
, it is easy to see that there is an interval
I=[-2\pi K,2\pi K]
with
K>0
such that
4\operatorname{sinc}^{2}(x)\geqslant\mathds{1}_{I}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence,

\Gamma(r)\geqslant r^{2}\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}\mathds{1}_{I}(2r\pi m)=r^{2}\sum_{|m|\leqslant Kr^{-1}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|.

Along the sequence
r_{n}=K/n
this yields

\Gamma(r_{n})=\frac{K^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{|m|\leqslant n}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}\geqslant\frac{K^{2}}{n^{2}}M_{n},

where
M_{n}=\sum_{m\leqslant n}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}
.

Therefore, we obtain the upper bound

\beta_{\nu}(2)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Gamma(r_{n})}{\log r_{n}}-1\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(n^{-2}M_{n})}{-\log n}-1=1-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\sum_{m\leqslant n}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}}{\log n}.

Showing the opposite bound is slightly more involved, because
\operatorname{sinc}(x)
cannot be dominated by the indicator function on a finite interval. We therefore proceed a bit more cautiously and replace the function
\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}
by a smoother function before taking the Fourier transform. More precisely, we choose a
C^{\infty}
-function
g\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geqslant 0}
with compact support and such that
g(x)=1
on
[-1,1]
.
In particular,
g
is in the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions and the same holds for
\widehat{g}
.
Setting
g_{r}(x)=g(x/r)
for all
r>0
, we obtain for small enough
r
that
g_{r}
is supported on the unit interval
[-1/2,1/2]
(identified with the torus
{\mathbb{T}}
in the following). In this case, we have
\mathds{1}_{B_{r}}\leqslant g_{r}
and therefore obtain

\Gamma(r)\leqslant\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}|\nu\ast g_{r}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Again, Parseval’s identity yields

\Gamma(r)\leqslant\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)\widehat{g_{r}}(m)|^{2}.

Note that

\widehat{g_{r}}(m)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}g_{r}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi{\mathrm{i}}mx}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}g_{r}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi{\mathrm{i}}mx}\,\mathrm{d}x=r\widehat{g}(rm).

Splitting the summation region into a core and a tail, we get for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
,

\Gamma(r)\leqslant r^{2}|\widehat{g}(0)|^{2}\sum_{|m|\leqslant r^{-(1+\varepsilon)}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}+r^{2}\sum_{|m|>r^{-(1+\varepsilon)}}|\widehat{g}(rm)|^{2}.

Without loss of generality assume that
|\widehat{g}(k)|
is symmetric for
k\mapsto-k
and monotonically decreasing for
k>0
.

We can then estimate the tail via

\sum_{|m|>r^{-(1+\varepsilon)}}|\widehat{g}(rm)|^{2}\leqslant 2\int_{r^{-(1+\varepsilon)}/2}^{\infty}|\widehat{g}(rx)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2}{r}\int_{r^{-\varepsilon}/2}^{\infty}|\widehat{g}(y)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y,

which in the limit
r\to 0
decays faster than any polynomial in
r
because
\widehat{g}
is rapidly decreasing. Hence, for
r_{n}=1/n
and large enough
n
,

\Gamma(r_{n})\leqslant\frac{2|\widehat{g}(0)|^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{|m|\leqslant n^{1+\varepsilon}}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}\leqslant\frac{4|\widehat{g}(0)|^{2}}{n^{2}}M_{\lfloor n^{1+\varepsilon}\rfloor}.

Similarly as before, this yields,

\beta_{\nu}(2)\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(n^{-2}M_{\lfloor n^{1+\varepsilon}\rfloor})}{-\log n}-1=1-(1+\varepsilon)\,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\sum_{m\leqslant n}|\widehat{\nu}(m)|^{2}}{\log n}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

The next corollary is a refinement to Theorem
2.7
.

Corollary 8.2
.

For
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
, we have

\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)=1-{D_{2}^{c}}=1-\frac{\log\lambda_{1}^{c}}{\log 2},

where
\lambda_{1}^{c}
is the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
M_{c}
, as defined in Proposition
5.2
. In particular,
c\mapsto\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)
is a real-analytic.

Proof.

Recall that
\mu_{c}
coincides with the restriction of
\widehat{\gamma_{c}}
to the torus, represented by the unit interval. Hence, we get from Lemma
3.3
that
\widehat{\mu_{c}}(m)=\overline{\eta^{c}_{m}}
for all
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In particular,

\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\widehat{\mu_{c}}(m)|^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\eta_{m}^{c}|^{2}=\Theta_{n}^{c},

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and hence, via Proposition
8.1
,

1-\beta_{\mu_{c}}(2)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Theta_{n}^{c}}{\log n}=D_{2}^{c}.

The relation to
\lambda_{1}^{c}
was established in Proposition
5.4
.
∎

8.2.
Kreĭn–Feller operators

Classical Kreĭn–Feller operators have been introduced in

[
45
,
33
,
19
]
as the second order weak derivative of a function with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

We only give a brief introduction of Kreĭn–Feller operators with respect to measures with full support as details for the general case can e.g. be found in
[
39
, Sec. 2.1]
or
[
40
]
.
First, we define the formal Laplace operator
\Delta_{\nu}
with respect to a finite Borel measure
\nu
.
To ease notation we always assume that our underlying interval is
[0,1]=\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
.
Let
H^{1,2}
denote the usual real Sobolev space of weakly differentiable functions
f
on
[0,1]
with square-integrable derivatives
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]})
.
We define the Laplacian (here only with Neumann boundary conditions) via a Dirichlet form on
H^{1,2}\times H^{1,2}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{[0,1]}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Leb}.

For each finite Borel measure
\nu
with
\mathrm{supp}(\nu)=[0,1]
we have that the natural embedding of
H^{1,2}
into
L^{2}(\nu)
is continuous, injective and dense.
Then
\Delta_{\nu}
is the non-negative self-adjoint operator with the following properties:

f\in H^{1,2}
lies in the domain
\operatorname{dom}(\Delta_{\nu})
of the Laplacian
\Delta_{\nu}
, if and only if there exists
h\in L^{2}(\nu)
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(g,f)=\langle g,h\rangle_{\nu},\quad\text{ for all }g\in H^{1,2}

and we set
\Delta_{\nu}f:=h
.

In this section we will investigate the asymptotic spectral properties of
\Delta_{\nu}
.
An element
f\in H^{1,2}\backslash\{0\}
is called
eigenfunction
of
\Delta_{\nu}
with
eigenvalue

\kappa
, if for all
g\in H^{1,2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(g,f)=\kappa\langle g,f\rangle_{\nu}.

Moreover, we introduce the eigenvalue counting function
N_{\nu}
as

N_{\nu}(x):=\#\{\kappa\leqslant x:\kappa\text{ is eigenvalue of }\Delta_{\nu}\}

with which we are able to introduce the
upper
and
lower spectral dimension
given by

8.3.
Quantization dimension

As a good reference on quantization theory we refer the reader to
[
25
]
. The main object is the

\displaystyle\mathfrak{e}_{n,r}(\nu)=\begin{cases}\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}(\int\rho(x,\alpha)^{r}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x))^{1/r}&r>0\\
\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\exp(\int\log\rho(x,\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x))&r=0,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{\alpha\subset\mathbb{R}:1\leqslant\operatorname{card}(\alpha)\leqslant n\}

and

.
It is known that for

[
25

In order to study this behaviour in more detail

and

of order

\displaystyle\overline{\mathsf{D}}_{r}(\nu):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log n}{\log\mathfrak{e}_{n,r}(\nu)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\underline{\mathsf{D}}_{r}(\nu):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{\log n}{\log\mathfrak{e}_{n,r}(\nu)}.

9.
Estimates on Birkhoff sums and combinatorial results

In general, we are concerned with expressions of the form
\psi_{n}^{c}(x)
. On cylinders
\langle w\rangle
with
|w|=r\geqslant n
, this takes the form

\psi^{c}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi^{c}(x_{k}),

with
x_{k}\in\langle w_{[k,r]}\rangle
for all
k
. Both the size and the variation of
\psi^{c}
on
\langle w_{[k,r]}\rangle
depend on how close this cylinder is to the singularity
\breve{c}
. This problem is not independent for different values of
k
since being close to
\breve{c}
determines a certain prefix of
w_{[k,r]}
. We are therefore led to investigate the combinatorial structure of
\breve{c}
and words
w
. For some applications, we wish to find a modification of
w
that allows a certain control of
\psi_{n}^{c}
on
\langle w\rangle
. This falls basically into two classes: First, we try to find words
v
that restrict the length of prefixes of
\breve{c}
that appear in
wv
(possibly starting in
w
). Another goal will be to concatenate words
w
and
w^{\prime}
by relatively short words
v
such that
u=wvw^{\prime}
has the property that
\langle u_{[k,|u|]}\rangle
stays sufficiently far away from the singularity for
k\leqslant|wv|
.
We split this section into two parts. In the first part, we work purely combinatorially, and in the second part we spell out the implications for the corresponding Birkhoff sums.

9.1.
Combinatorics of words

For convenience of notation, we may think of a word
w
as a dyadic representation of an integer such that adding or subtracting a number is well-defined, as long as the resulting number is non-negative. For the moment, let us assume that the singularity position
\breve{c}
has a unique expansion in base
2
.
Again, we denote by

the finite word of the

th digit to the

.
We introduce

together with the conventions

That is,

consists of the length-

, along with its right and left neighbor. By this definition,

\displaystyle x_{[1,n]}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}

\displaystyle\implies\rho(x,\breve{c})\leqslant 2^{-n+1},

\displaystyle x_{[1,n]}\notin\mathcal{G}_{n}

It is straightforward to see that, given

without altering any of the decisive properties above.

for all

For later use, we record that a large number of hitting times (relative to the word length) requires the number of independent hitting times to be small.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and let
\delta\in(0,1)
be such that
\delta n=\#\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(w)-s
, with
s
as in Lemma
9.6
. Then we have
s\leqslant 2+\log_{3/2}(2\delta^{-1})
.

Proof.

In the notation of Lemma
9.6
, let
m_{i}=|u_{i}|
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
. Then, for every
k
, the largest word in
\mathcal{W}_{k}
has length
\ell_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_{i}m_{i}
. Furthermore,
m_{i+1}\geqslant\ell_{i}/2
, and thus

\ell_{i+1}=N_{i+1}m_{i+1}+\ell_{i}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(N_{i+1}+2)\ell_{i},

for all
1\leqslant i<s
. Iterating this relation, and using
\ell_{1}=N_{1}m_{1}\geqslant N_{1}
yields

\ell_{s}\geqslant 2^{-s+1}N_{1}\prod_{i=2}^{s}(N_{i}+2).

(33)

Note that for
a_{1},a_{2}\geqslant 0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

(a_{1}+k)(a_{2}+k)\geqslant k(a_{1}+a_{2}+k)

and inductively

\prod_{i=1}^{r}(a_{i}+k)\geqslant k^{r-1}\biggl(k+\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\biggr),

given
a_{i}\geqslant 0
.
Applying this with
a_{i}=N_{i+1}-1
and
k=3
gives

\prod_{i=2}^{s}(N_{i}+2)\geqslant 3^{s-2}\biggl(3+\sum_{i=2}^{s}(N_{i}-1)\biggr).

Moreover,
from (
33
), the fact that
aN_{1}\geq a+N_{1}-1
for all
a\geq 1
and
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\#\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}N_{i}
we get

2^{s-1}\ell_{s}\geqslant N_{1}\prod_{i=2}^{s}(N_{i}+2)\geqslant 3^{s-2}\biggl(3+\sum_{i=2}^{s}(N_{i}-1)\biggr)N_{1}\geqslant 3^{s-2}\biggl(3+\sum_{i=1}^{s}(N_{i}-1)\biggr)\geqslant 3^{s-2}\delta n.

Since the length
\ell_{s}
of the largest word in
\mathcal{W}(w)
is bounded by
n
, we therefore conclude that

2n\geqslant(3/2)^{s-2}\delta n,

which gives the desired bound for
s
.
∎

9.2.
Variation of
\psi_{n}^{c}
on cylinders

It will be important to assess the distance of cylinder sets to the singularity point
\breve{c}
.
This is because
\psi^{c}
and the variations of
\psi^{c}
can be estimated in terms of the Euclidean distance
\rho
to
\breve{c}
.

Lemma 9.11
.

For every
x
, we have

2\log(2\rho(x,\breve{c}))\leqslant\psi^{c}(x)\leqslant 2\log(\pi\rho(x,\breve{c})).

Furthermore, if
x,y\in\mathbb{T}\setminus B_{r}(\breve{c})
for some
r>0
, we have the Lipschitz estimate

|\psi^{c}(x)-\psi^{c}(y)|\leqslant\frac{2}{r}\rho(x,y).

Proof.

Recall that
\psi^{c}(x)=2\log|\cos(\pi(x-c))|
.
Regarding
y=x-\breve{c}=x-c+1/2
as an element on the torus, we observe that

2\rho(y,0)\leqslant|\sin(\pi y)|\leqslant\pi\rho(y,0).

This is straightforward to verify for
y\leqslant 1/2
and the general case follows by symmetry. Since
\rho(y,0)=\rho(x,\breve{c})
,
the first claim follows. The second claim follows easily from the mean value theorem; compare
[
3
, Lem. 4.1]
for details.
∎

We use these bounds to obtain estimates on cylinders that correspond to
j
-hitting times.

Lemma 9.12
.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{n}
and
k\in\mathcal{H}_{j}(w)
for some
j<n
. If
x,y\in\langle w_{[k,n]}\rangle
, we have

(k-j-1)\,2\log 2\leqslant\psi^{c}(x)\leqslant(k-j+2)\,2\log 2

and

|\psi^{c}(x)-\psi^{c}(y)|\leqslant 2^{j-n+2}.

The lower bound on
\psi^{c}(x)
also holds for
j=n
.

Proof.

By assumption, we have
w_{[k,j]}=x_{[1,j-k+1]}
and
w_{[k,j+1]}=x_{[1,j-k+2]}
. Hence,
k\in\mathcal{H}_{j}(w)
(together with the properties of
\mathcal{G}
) implies that

2^{-(j-k+2)}\leqslant\rho(x,\breve{c})\leqslant 2^{-(j-k)},

and likewise for
y
, with the upper bound holding also for
j=n
. Using furthermore that
\rho(x,y)\leqslant 2^{-(n-k+1)}
, all estimates follow readily from the bounds in Lemma
9.11
.
∎

It is remarkable that the variation bound in the preceding lemma does
not
depend on the choice of the

-hitting time

.

For every word
w
let
\kappa(w)=\#\mathcal{H}(w)
be the cardinality of the set of (full) hitting times in
w
,
and let
\kappa_{n}=\max_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\kappa(w)
. It is straightforward to verify that
\kappa_{n}
is increasing in
n
. Indeed, if
v\in\Sigma^{m}
is a suffix of
w\in\Sigma^{n}
, we obtain that
\kappa(v)\leqslant\kappa(w)
. Since for
m\leqslant n
, every word
v\in\Sigma^{m}
is a suffix of some

, we obtain

\kappa_{m}=\max_{v\in\Sigma^{m}}\kappa(v)\leqslant\max_{v\in\Sigma^{m}}\kappa(w(v))\leqslant\max_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\kappa(w)=\kappa_{n}.

Lemma 9.13
.

If the pressure function
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{\operatorname{top}}}(t\psi^{c})
is finite for some

, it follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{n}<\infty.

In particular, the sequence
(\kappa_{n}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded in this case.

Proof.

For a proof via contradiction, assume
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{n}^{2}/n=\infty
and
t<0
. We estimate
\psi^{c}_{n}
on
\langle w\rangle
with
w\in\Sigma^{n}
. First, note that

\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sup_{x\in\langle w_{[k,n]}\rangle}\psi^{c}(x)\leqslant\sum_{k\in\mathcal{H}(w)}\sup_{x\in\langle w_{[k,n]}\rangle}\psi^{c}(x).

By Lemma
9.12
, we have

\psi^{c}(x)\leqslant(k-n+2)\log 4
for

and hence

\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\leqslant\sum_{k\in\mathcal{H}(w)}(k-n+2)\log 4\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\kappa(w)}(3-j)\log 4.

For each

let
w^{n}
be a word such that
\kappa_{n}=\kappa(w^{n})
, yielding

provided

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\exp\biggl(t\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\biggr)\right)\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}t\sup_{x\in\langle w^{n}\rangle}\psi_{n}^{c}(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}(-t)\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{2n}=\infty

and we have reached the desired contradiction.
∎

.

(34)

w_{q}\in\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}}
, any word of length
m^{\prime}
starting in
p_{q}
is not in
\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}}
, as well as
|u^{\prime}_{q}|=r(w_{q})
, for all
1\leqslant q\leqslant J
, and such that
|s|\geqslant\ell
and
J
is maximal with this property. Further, for each
1\leqslant q\leqslant J
let
v_{q}
be the word of length
\ell
that follows
u_{q}^{\prime}
in the splitting above.
For all
q
, we choose
u_{q}=u(v_{q},w_{q})
with properties as in Proposition
9.16
and define
\theta_{m}(w)
by replacing each
u_{q}^{\prime}
by
u_{q}
in the splitting above, that is,

\theta_{m}(w)=p_{1}w_{1}u_{1}p_{2}w_{2}u_{2}\cdots p_{J}w_{J}u_{J}s.

We inherit from the structure of
w
that every word of length
m^{\prime}
starting in
p_{q}
in the above splitting of
\theta_{m}(w)
is not contained in
\mathcal{G}
. Additionally, if a word of length
m
starts in some

or

it certainly also completely contains

, and therefore it is not in

. Finally, there is no word from
\mathcal{G}_{m}
in
s

. It follows that
\theta_{m}(w)
contains no prefix of
\breve{c}
of length
m
, proving the first claim.

If
\breve{c}
is periodic, we define
\theta_{m}
in the same manner up to a slight modification. Instead of
w_{1}
being the first instance of a word in
\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}}
, we define it as the prefix of length
m^{\prime}
of the first word in
\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}+2}
in
w
(such that
|w_{1}s_{1}|\geqslant m
). Corresponding modifications are applied to the definition of
w_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant J
. Formally, we obtain the same kind of splitting of
w
as before and again we define
\theta_{m}
as the map replacing
u_{i}^{\prime}
by the word
u_{i}
from Proposition
9.16
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant J
. The observation that
\theta_{m}(w)
contains no word from
\mathcal{G}_{m}
follows in a similar manner as before. The only point where we need to adapt the argument concerns words of length
m^{\prime}+2
that start one letter before a word of type
w_{i}
. For large enough
m^{\prime}
, this word and
w_{i}
can only be simultaneously in
\mathcal{G}
if
\breve{c}=0
, the word
w_{i}
is constantly equal to one letter
a
and the letter preceding
w_{i}
is also
a
. But this contradicts the minimality of the position in the definition of
w_{i}
. Hence, such words cannot be in
\mathcal{G}
and we conclude as before.

In order to estimate the cardinality of preimages of a word
w^{\prime}=\theta_{m}(w)
, we first identify the positions that might have been affected by the application of
\theta_{m}
. Let us call an index
i
a
suspect
in
w^{\prime}
if
w^{\prime}_{[i+1,i+m^{\prime}]}\in\mathcal{G}
. We call a suspect
i

essential
if there is no suspect
j
with
i+r_{\max}+\ell\leqslant j\leqslant i+m^{\prime}
. This notion is relevant because if
i+1
is the starting position of some
w_{q}
in the splitting of
w
, then

is an essential suspect in
\theta_{m}(w)
.
Indeed, since
w_{q}
is not affected by
\theta_{m}
, we obtain that
(\theta_{m}(w))_{[i+1,i+m^{\prime}]}=w_{q}\in\mathcal{G}
. On the other hand, if
i+r_{\max}+\ell\leqslant j\leqslant i+m^{\prime}
, we conclude that
(\theta_{m}(w))_{[j+1,j+m^{\prime}]}
starts with

(w_{q}u_{q}v_{q})_{[j-i+1,|w_{q}u_{q}v_{q}|]}\notin\mathcal{G}
, implying that
j
is not a suspect.
Due to the repellent nature in the definition of essential suspects, they come in groups
(\mathcal{E}_{q})_{q=1}^{N}
of diameter at most
r_{\max}+\ell
, separated by gaps of size at least
m^{\prime}-r_{\max}-\ell
. That is,
the number of groups is bounded by

N\leqslant\lfloor n/(m^{\prime}-r_{\max}-\ell)\rfloor+1\leqslant\lfloor n/((1-2\varepsilon)m^{\prime})\rfloor,

(43)

assuming that
m^{\prime}
is large enough to ensure
\ell<\varepsilon m^{\prime}
and
n
sufficiently larger than
m^{\prime}
. Note that
w
and
\theta_{m}(w)
only differ on intervals of the form

D_{i}=[i+m^{\prime}+1,i+m^{\prime}+r]\subset i+m^{\prime}+[1,r_{\max}]

with
i+1
the starting position of some
w_{q}
, implying that
i\in\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{q=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{q}
is an essential suspect. We conclude that all changes between
w
and

appear in

Due to the fact that the elements in
\mathcal{E}_{q}
are no more than
r_{\max}+\ell
apart we obtain that each
D^{(q)}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{q}}D_{i}
is contained in an interval of length at most
2r_{\max}+\ell\leqslant 3\varepsilon m^{\prime}
for large enough

. Combining this with (
43
), we get that the cardinality of

is bounded by

\#D\leqslant 3\varepsilon m^{\prime}N\leqslant\frac{3\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}n.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we can also achieve
\#D\leqslant\varepsilon n
for a fixed given
\varepsilon>0
and since all words
w\in\theta_{m}^{-1}(w^{\prime})
can differ only on

, their total cardinality is bounded by

\#\theta_{m}^{-1}(w^{\prime})\leqslant 2^{\#D}\leqslant 2^{\varepsilon n},

proving the second claim of the proposition.

Finally, we aim to estimate the difference
\psi_{n}^{c}(x)
and
\psi_{n}^{c}(y)
, where
x\in\langle w\rangle
,
y\in\langle\theta_{m}(w)\rangle\cap\mathbb{X}_{m}^{c}
are chosen such that they maximise the corresponding expression.
Recall the decompositions of
w
and
\theta_{m}(w)
given above.
For each
1\leqslant q\leqslant J
let
j_{q}+1
be the starting index of
p_{q}
in these decompositions and set
x_{q}=S^{j_{q}}x
as well as
y_{q}=S^{j_{q}}y
. Similarly, we set
x_{q}^{\prime}=S^{i_{q}}x
and
y_{q}^{\prime}=S^{i_{q}}y
, where
i_{q}+1
is the starting index of
w_{q}
. And finally, let
j+1
be the starting index of
s
and set
\widetilde{x}=S^{j}x
and
\widetilde{y}=S^{j}y
.
We can split up the difference

\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-\psi^{c}_{n}(y)=\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{x})-\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{y})+\sum_{q=1}^{J}\bigl(\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(x_{q})-\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(y_{q})\bigr)+\sum_{q=1}^{J}\bigl(\psi^{c}_{|w_{q}u_{q}|}(x_{q}^{\prime})-\psi^{c}_{|w_{q}u_{q}|}(y_{q}^{\prime})\bigr).

First, we rewrite

\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{x})-\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{y})\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{|s|}\psi^{c}(\widetilde{x}_{k})-\psi^{c}(\widetilde{y}_{k}),

where both
\widetilde{y}_{k}=S^{k-1}\widetilde{y}
and
\widetilde{x}_{k}
start with
s_{[k,|s|]}
and
\widetilde{x}_{k}
is chosen to maximise the distance to
\breve{c}
with this property.
Note that for
k
with
1\leqslant k\leqslant|s|-m
, the length of the words
s_{[k,|s|]}
is at least
m+1
and hence
s_{[k,k+m]}\notin\mathcal{G}
. In particular,
k\in\mathcal{H}_{j}
for some
j<k+m
and we obtain from Lemma
9.12
that

\psi^{c}(\widetilde{x}_{k})-\psi^{c}(\widetilde{y}_{k})\leqslant 2^{k+m+1-|s|}.

For
|s|-m<k\leqslant|s|
, it follows from
\widetilde{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{X}^{c}_{m}
and the maximising property of
\widetilde{x}_{k}
that both points have a distance of at least
2^{-m-1}
to the singularity and at the same time they share a common prefix of length
|s|-k+1
implying a bound on their distance given by

\rho(\widetilde{x}_{k},\widetilde{y}_{k})\leqslant 2^{k-|s|-1}.

Using Lemma
9.11
once again,

\psi^{c}(\widetilde{x}_{k})-\psi^{c}(\widetilde{y}_{k})\leqslant 2^{k+m+1-|s|},

so this bound holds for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant|s|
and we get

\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{x})-\psi^{c}_{|s|}(\widetilde{y})\leqslant 2^{m+2}.

For the next estimate, note that
x_{q}
and
y_{q}
both have
p_{q}w_{q}
as a common prefix and we estimate

\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(x_{q})-\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(y_{q})=\sum_{k=1}^{|p_{q}|}\psi^{c}(S^{k-1}x_{q})-\psi^{c}(S^{k-1}y_{q})\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{|p_{q}|}\psi^{c}(x_{q,k})-\psi^{c}(y_{q,k}),

where both
y_{q,k}=S^{k-1}y_{q}
and
x_{q,k}=S^{k-1}x_{q}
start with the word
(p_{q}w_{q})_{[k,|p_{q}w_{q}|]}
. By the defining property of

, the prefix of length
m^{\prime}+2
of this word is not in
\mathcal{G}
(length

if
k=|p_{1}|
), and hence the hitting depth is at most
k+m^{\prime}
(respectively
k+m^{\prime}-1
if
k=|p_{1}|
). Hence, by Lemma
9.12
and recalling
|w_{q}|=m^{\prime}
,

\psi^{c}(x_{q,k})-\psi^{c}(y_{q,k})\leqslant 2^{k-|p_{q}|+2},

and summing up over all

yields

\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(x_{q})-\psi^{c}_{|p_{q}|}(y_{q})\leqslant 8.

Finally, we recall that due to the choice of

we have for all
1\leqslant q\leqslant J
that

\psi^{c}_{|w_{q}u_{q}|}(x_{q}^{\prime})-\psi^{c}_{|w_{q}u_{q}|}(y_{q}^{\prime})\leqslant\varepsilon m^{\prime},

due to the fact that
x_{q}^{\prime}
starts with
w_{q}u_{q}^{\prime}v_{q}
and
y_{q}^{\prime}
starts with
w_{q}u_{q}v_{q}
(and
w_{q}(u^{\prime}_{q})_{[1,2]}\in\mathcal{G}
if
\breve{c}
is periodic), compare (
42
).
Combining all the previous estimates, we obtain,

\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-\psi^{c}_{n}(y)\leqslant 2^{m+2}{+8J}+J\varepsilon m^{\prime}\leqslant 2^{m+2}+2\varepsilon n,

for sufficiently large

,
using

in the last step. Provided that
n
is sufficiently larger than
m
,
this expression can be bound by
3\varepsilon n
. Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, this yields the claim.
∎

9.4.
Proofs of the Gibbs type properties

Proof of Proposition
7.5
.

The assumption
c\neq 0
or
\breve{c}\neq 1/2
is clearly necessary since
\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}
for
c=0
. On the other hand, it follows from Corollary
7.1
that
\mu_{c}
has full support if
c\neq 0
.
Let
w\in\Sigma^{n}
with
n\geqslant n_{0}
and
v
as in Lemma
9.7
. Due to Corollary
7.4
, we have

\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle wv\rangle)\geqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle v\rangle)+\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x).

We can estimate

\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\inf_{x\in\langle w_{[k,n]}v\rangle}\psi^{c}(x).

Let
m_{n}=|wv|=n+|v|
.
By the choice of
v
, we have that
w_{[k,n]}v\notin\mathcal{G}
and hence for
x\in\langle w_{[k,n]}v\rangle
,

\rho(x,\breve{c})\geqslant 2^{-(m_{n}-k+1)},

which, by Lemma
9.11
implies

\psi^{c}(x)\geqslant 2\log(2^{k-m_{n}})=(k-m_{n})2\log 2.

Hence,

\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k-m_{n})2\log 2=n\log 2(n+1-2m_{n})\geqslant-(n^{2}+2n|v|)\log 2.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
|v|\leqslant 2\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n=:h(n)
. For large enough
n
we get

n^{2}+2n|v|\leqslant n^{2}+4n\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}

and therefore

\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\geqslant-(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}\log 2.

We thereby get the recursion

\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant-(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}\log 2+\log\mu_{c}(\langle v\rangle).

If
|v|>n_{0}
, we can iterate the relation and get

\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant-(1+\varepsilon)n^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)(h(n))^{2}+\log\mu_{c}(\langle v^{\prime}\rangle),

for some
v^{\prime}
with
|v^{\prime}|\leqslant h^{2}(n)=h(h(n))
. The iteration stops after
r_{n}
steps, where
r_{n}
is the minimal integer such that
h^{r_{n}}(n)\leqslant n_{0}
. Since the iterates of
h
are decaying very fast, it is straightforward to see that
r_{n}<n
. We obtain

\inf_{w\in\Sigma^{n}}\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant-(1+\varepsilon)(n^{2}+n(h(n))^{2})+\inf_{u\in\Sigma^{n_{0}}}\log\mu_{c}(\langle u\rangle).

Note that the last term is finite because
\mu_{c}
has full support.
Dividing by
n^{2}
, taking the liminf and sending
\varepsilon\to 0
yields the assertion.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.7
.

The upper bound

\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\leqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)

holds in full generally.
For the lower bound, let
w\in\Sigma^{n}
, with
n\geqslant n_{0}
and
v
as in Lemma
9.7
. By Corollary
7.4
and Proposition
7.5
, we have

\displaystyle\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)

\displaystyle\geqslant\log\mu_{c}(\langle v\rangle)+\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\geqslant-2|v|^{2}+\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\geqslant-8(\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n)^{2}+\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x),

provided
n
is large enough. Due to Lemma
9.14
, we have the estimate

\inf_{x\in\langle wv\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)\geqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-2^{|v|+2}\kappa_{n+|v|}\geqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-4\kappa_{2n}(\log_{3/2}n)^{2}.

Overall, this yields

\log\mu_{c}(\langle w\rangle)\geqslant\sup_{x\in\langle w\rangle}\psi^{c}_{n}(x)-\iota_{n}

with

\iota_{n}=8(\log_{2}\log_{3/2}n)^{2}+4\kappa_{2n}(\log_{3/2}n)^{2}.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{top}}(t\psi^{c})<\infty
, we know from Lemma
9.13
that
\kappa_{n}\in O(\sqrt{n})
and hence
\iota_{n}\in o(n)
. The claim follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.9
.

We start with the upper bound. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have by Lemma
7.2
that

\mu_{c}(I)\leqslant\exp\Big(\sup_{y\in I}\psi_{n}^{c}(y)\Big).

We define a regularized version of
\psi^{c}
via

\psi^{c,\delta}(y)=\begin{cases}\psi^{c}(y),&\mbox{if }\rho(y,\breve{c})\geqslant\delta,\\
\psi^{c}(\breve{c}-\delta),&\mbox{if }\rho(y,\breve{c})<\delta.\end{cases}

Since
\psi^{c}(\breve{c}-\delta)=\psi^{c}(\breve{c}+\delta)
this function is continuous and, in fact, Hölder continuous. Also note that
\psi^{c}(y)\leqslant\psi^{c,\delta}(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{T}
.
For
x\in I\cap K_{\delta}
we have
\psi^{c}(T^{k}x)=\psi^{c,\delta}(T^{k}x)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. For every
y\in I
, we obtain

\psi^{c}_{n}(y)-\psi^{c}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi^{c}(T^{k}y)-\psi^{c}(T^{k}x)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi^{c,\delta}(T^{k}y)-\psi^{c,\delta}(T^{k}x)\leqslant\log(R),

for an appropriate number
R>1
. This yields the upper bound.

For the lower bound, let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and fix some large number
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
to be determined later. Decompose
I
into
N
intervals
I_{i}
with
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, each occupying a fraction
r=1/N
of the original length
2^{-n}
. By Lemma
7.2
, we have

\mu_{c}(I_{i})\geqslant\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i})\,\exp\left(\inf_{y\in I_{i}}\psi_{n}^{c}(y)\right).

(44)

We define a subset of indices with good variation control

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{c,\delta,r}=\left\{1\leqslant i\leqslant N:\rho(\breve{c},T^{k}I_{i})\geqslant r\delta,\,\forall 0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1\right\},

and its complement
\mathcal{B}=([1,N]\cap{\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{V}
. Recall
x\in I\cap K_{\delta}
. If
i\in\mathcal{V}
, we get for all
y\in I_{i}
that both
T^{k}y
and
T^{k}x
are bounded away from
\breve{c}
by at least
r\delta
for all
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
. Hence, we can use the Hölder continuity of
\psi^{c}
restricted to the complement of
B_{r\delta}(\breve{c})
to conclude that

\psi_{n}^{c}(x)-\psi_{n}^{c}(y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi^{c}(T^{k}x)-\psi^{c}(T^{k}y)\leqslant\log(R^{\prime})

for some
R^{\prime}=R^{\prime}(c,\delta,r)>1
.
Combining this with the estimate in (
44
), we obtain for
i\in\mathcal{V}
,

\mu_{c}(I_{i})\geqslant\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i})\,\frac{1}{R^{\prime}}\,\exp\left(\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right).

Hence,

\mu_{c}(I)\geqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\mu_{c}(I_{i})\geqslant\frac{1}{R^{\prime}}\exp\left(\psi_{n}^{c}(x)\right)\,\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i}).

(45)

Note that the union over all
T^{n}I_{i}
with
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
is given by
T^{n}I=\mathbb{T}
, implying

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i})=1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i}).

(46)

We argue that the cardinality of
\mathcal{B}
cannot be too large. By definition
i\in\mathcal{B}
requires that
i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
for some
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, where

\mathcal{B}_{k}=\left\{1\leqslant i\leqslant N:\rho(\breve{c},T^{k}I_{i})<r\delta\right\}.

Let
y\in I_{i}
for some
i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
and recall
x\in I\cap K_{\delta}
.
Since
\rho(x,y)\leqslant 2^{-n}
, we have, if
2^{k-n}\leqslant\delta/2
,

\rho(T^{k}y,\breve{c})\geqslant\rho(T^{k}x,\breve{c})-\rho(T^{k}x,T^{k}y)\geqslant\delta-2^{k-n}\geqslant\delta/2\geqslant r\delta.

That is,
\mathcal{B}_{k}=\varnothing
as long as
k\leqslant n-1+\lceil\log_{2}\delta\rceil
.
It therefore suffices to consider
k=n-m
with
m=1,\ldots,-\lceil\log_{2}\delta\rceil
. For such
k=n-m
, each interval
T^{k}I_{i}
has length

|T^{k}I_{i}|=2^{n-m}|I_{i}|=r2^{-m}\geqslant r\delta,

and therefore there can be at most two indices
i
such that
\rho(\breve{c},T^{k}I_{i})<r\delta
, implying that
\#\mathcal{B}_{k}\leqslant 2
for such
k
. Overall, we obtain

\#\mathcal{B}\leqslant\sum_{k=n+\lfloor\log_{2}\delta\rfloor}^{n-1}\#\mathcal{B}_{k}\leqslant-2\lfloor\log_{2}\delta\rfloor=:C.

In particular, this bound is independent of
r
. Note that each interval
T^{n}I_{i}
has length
r
. Since
\mu_{c}
is a continuous measure for
c\neq 0
, we can find an
r=1/N
such that each interval
J
of length
r
is bounded in measure by
\mu_{c}(J)\leqslant 1/(2C)
. With this choice of
r=r(c,\delta)
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{c}(T^{n}I_{i})\leqslant\frac{C}{2C}=\frac{1}{2}

and, combining this with (
45
) and (
46
), we get the required lower bound with
R=2R^{\prime}
.
∎
