Breastfeeding type is associated with urinary metabolic changes in early life: influence of sex and early weight gain in healthy term infants
Authors: Pere Bibiloni, Jean-Charles Martin, Pilar Cobo, María De Lucas, María Victoria Jiménez-Cabanillas, Catherine Tardivel, Catalina Picó, Francisca Serra, Juana Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerinatal factors are key modulators of neonatal metabolism and may contribute to long-term health trajectories. However, the metabolic adaptations underlying these early-life influences remain poorly characterized. In this study, we comprehensively characterized neonatal urinary metabolic profiles associated with multiple perinatal factors in full-term, healthy, breastfed infants at one month of age.MethodsNinety-six urine samples from full-term, healthy, breastfed infants at one month of age were analyzed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). Exploratory multivariate analyses identified the maternal and infant factors most strongly associated with urinary metabolic variation, followed by multiblock integration to characterize the biological functions associated with the identified metabolic signatures.ResultsBreastfeeding type (exclusive, n = 79; mixed, n = 17) emerged as the factor most associated with urinary metabolic variability and was associated with differences in metabolites related to carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, gut microbiota, oxidative status, and vitamin handling, among others. Infant sex was also associated with metabolic differences, particularly affecting steroid metabolism, amino acid profiles, and mitochondrial-related compounds. In addition, weight gain during the first month of life was associated with variation in metabolites related to amino acid metabolism, gut-microbiota and antioxidant pathways.ConclusionsUrinary metabolomics revealed distinct metabolic signatures associated with early-life exposures. Among the factors studied, breastfeeding type (mixed vs. exclusive) was associated with the most pronounced differences in the urinary metabolome. These findings highlight the potential of urinary metabolomics for investigating early-life metabolic adaptations and provide a basis for future studies exploring the relationship between infant feeding and metabolic programming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40348-026-00258-9.

Background

Perinatal factors may alter neonatal metabolism and influence health later in life. This phenomenon is known as the developmental origins of health and disease [31]. A substantial number of metabolic disorders have been linked to adverse conditions during early-life development, including obesity, cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus, fatty liver disease [33], as well as neurodevelopmental alterations [34]. Growing evidence in the field points to epigenetic modifications as the main mechanisms driving these long-lasting effects [63].

Multiple challenges have been demonstrated to cause this metabolic misprogramming. First, maternal conditions during gestation, such as obesity, overnutrition, poor diet quality or diabetes [70]. Next, neonatal characteristics, like preterm birth [52], high birth weight [18] or a low birth weight accompanied by an accelerated growth [71]. Finally, lactation conditions may also play a role in this scenario, including maternal food habits, maternal BMI, type of feeding (breastfeeding or formula) and its duration [54]. Despite this stratification, all factors might interact and influence each other. Furthermore, sex may influence these processes, making it essential to account for potential sex-related biases in studies of metabolic programming [17].

A deeper understanding of the molecular adaptations underpinning these long-lasting effects could ease the design of preventive strategies. The discovery of early metabolic biomarkers is of special interest, given their potential for monitoring child development and the effectiveness of health strategies. Urine is a powerful source of biomarkers, as it presents multiple advantages—it is easily obtainable, mostly sterile, free of interferences and with an intricate chemical composition [53]. In this context, metabolomic techniques represent a powerful tool for characterizing the urine metabolome and evaluating the impact of perinatal factors.

We have previously described a significant impact of preterm birth on the urinary metabolome of infants compared to individuals born at term [8]. The changes observed suggest a profound metabolic adaptation of several functions, which could speculatively be linked to future health outcomes associated with prematurity. In the present study, we further explored the effects of perinatal factors that may influence metabolic programming in the subcohort of full-term breastfed newborns. To this end, we analyzed the urinary metabolome of term breastfed neonates using two complementary techniques – liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). An exploratory analysis was conducted to identify maternal or neonatal features driving significant changes in urinary composition. Finally, we characterized these variations to assess their potential links with future health outcomes.

Methods

The present research is a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional, observational, single-center study aimed to compare the metabolic profiles of preterm and term newborns at one month of age. Herein, we analyzed in detail the urinary metabolome of the full-term subcohort to decipher the influence of perinatal factors. Cohort recruitment

For the present study, individuals were recruited from the cohorts miARN-milk/miARN-baby (local ethical committee codes—CEI-IB IB3464/17 and CEI-IB 3716/18, respectively). Enrollment took place in the neonatal unit of the Hospital Universitari Son Espases (HUSE; Palma, Spain) from July 2018 to March 2020. All participants signed the informed consent.

One hundred and two baby-mother pairs were included at birth. The inclusion criteria were the following ones: 1) term birth (approximately week 40 of pregnancy); 2) mother age between 18 and 45 years old; 3) absence of pre-existing maternal diseases; 4) no recent changes in the regular diet as self-reported by the study participants; 5) absence of drug consumption (except nutritional supplementation); 6) no history of drugs or alcohol abuse; and 7) exclusive breastfeeding initiation. A total of 96 mother-infant dyads were included in the study, representing those from whom urine samples were successfully collected.

Data and specimens’ collection

Mothers’ anthropometric data were obtained at one month post-partum. Weight, height, and body composition were measured using a body composition scale (OMRON BF-511, OMRON Healthcare). Pre-gestational data were collected from clinical records and self-reported questionnaires.

Regarding newborns, somatometric information was obtained at birth and at one month of age. This included body weight, body length, and head circumference, both in absolute terms and as sex- and gestational age-adjusted percentiles based on national reference databases [12, 13]. At one month of age, 96 urine samples were collected applying Temapar adhesive pediatric collection bags. Specimens were immediately transferred to cryovials and stored at −80ºC until analysis.

Metabolomic analysis

Urine composition of the 96 collected samples was analyzed through both LC–MS and NMR, as previously described [8].

LC–MS was carried out in the Biomet platform, Aix-Marseille Université. Briefly, 50 μL of filtered urine was studied along with their respective blanks (consisting of distilled water and filtered distilled water) and quality control (QC, a pool of all samples). Signal contributions from blank samples were subtracted from those of the study samples prior to data processing to remove background noise and contaminants. No internal standards were added to the samples. Chromatography was performed in two different columns – C18 and HILIC – and in both positive and negative ionization. Additionally, MS/MS experiments were conducted in QC samples. Data was extracted, merged, and processed by MzMine 3 [65], while the W4M platform [30] was utilized to annotate the metabolites according to an in-house database of ~ 1300 metabolite standards. A final manual inspection resulted in the quantification of 521 metabolites, which were classified according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) identification confidence levels [72].

1H-NMR technique was conducted at the University of the Balearic Islands. Concisely, 350 μL of urine were added to 150 μL of distilled water and 50 μL of phosphate buffer containing the internal standard tetramethylsilylpropanoic acid (TSP). Blanks (containing only distilled water and the buffer) and samples were processed in a Bruker Avance III spectrometer at 298 K. Blank subtraction was performed to correct for background signals prior to spectral profiling. Both NOESY and DIPSI pulse sequences were accumulated. Preprocessing was performed manually, whereas profiling and quantification were conducted automatically using the open-source tool rDolphin [11], resulting in the identification of 74 regions of interest (ROIs) classified according to the MSI guidelines [72].

Data analysis

All statistical procedures below were carried out utilizing R version 4.3.1 [58]. These were applied to both LC–MS and NMR datasets independently and then interpreted jointly. To explore potential sex-related differences, the cohort was stratified by infant sex, and analyses were performed in the overall cohort as well as in male and female subgroups. In total, six data subsets were considered: LC–MS all, LC–MS male, LC–MS female, NMR all, NMR male, and NMR female. Given the limited sample size of the sex-stratified subgroups, these analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

Pre-processing

Firstly, metabolomic data were imputed, normalized, and autoscaled through MetaboanalystR package [53] to correct for missing values and inter-sample technical variation. Imputation was implemented using the half-minimum method, resulting in 14.6% values for the LC–MS dataset and 7.4% for the NMR dataset. Probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) was selected as an appropriate normalization method in the context of urine samples to diminish the impact of dilution among specimens [21] since insufficient volume prevent using other normalization methods such as specific gravimetry. The median of the samples was used as reference. Normalization and autoscaling were applied to every subset out of the six constructed.

Identification of variables associated with the urinary metabolome

An exploratory analysis was conducted to identify which variables were associated with the urinary metabolome. A partial least squares (PLS) model was created for each subset, including all metabolites identified and all variables of study (36 in total) and retaining two latent components. Variables with the highest overall relevance (top 10 according to their variable importance in projection (VIP) score) were selected for individual analysis. Infant sex was always included to assess potentially relevant sex biases. As PCA of the whole dataset did not display any grouping distribution, individual variable effects were examined through supervised multivariate analyses (MVA) – PLS for numerical variables and PLS discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) for categorical variables – retaining two latent components in all cases. Model validity was assessed by R2Y and Q2Y values (obtained from a sevenfold cross-validation) and permutation tests (200 permutations, pQ2 and pRY2 both ≤ 0.05). Package ropls was utilized for this purpose [74]. From this analysis, type of breastfeeding, infant sex and weight gain were selected for further characterization.

Identification of potential confounding factors

To identify clinical variables that could confound the associations between the selected variables and the urinary metabolome, their relationships with other participant characteristics were examined. For continuous variables (weight gain), four multiple linear regression models were built with the feature of interest as the response variable, including all maternal variables (A), all infant variables at birth (B), all infant variables at one month of age (C), or the change between both timepoints (D) as predictors. In all four models, infant sex was included as a covariate. For the categorical variables (breastfeeding type and infant sex), between-group comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and either Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables depending on data normality (assessed using Lilliefors test). Effect sizes were estimated using Cramer’s V statistic or Cohen’s d statistic, respectively. Variables displaying a significant association with the factors of interest were considered potential confounders and included in the subsequent analyses.

Confounders and imbalance-bias analyses

To assess the robustness of the identified associations and evaluate the contribution of potential confounders, the PLS and PLS-DA models were re-evaluated using a residualization-based confounding adjustment strategy adapted from [80]. A linear regression model was fitted for each metabolite with the corresponding confounder(s) as independent variable(s), and the resulting residuals were used as the adjusted feature matrix. For categorical (class) outcome variables, sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), and accuracy (Acc) were obtained from a sevenfold cross-validation, in which residualization was performed independently within each training fold. R2Y, Q2Y and permutation-based p-values for both adjusted and non-adjusted models were obtained from models built on the full dataset, with residualization performed on the complete feature matrix prior to model fitting. Additionally, to assess potential bias induced by class imbalance in PLS-DA models, a balanced cross-validation was performed, in which the majority class was undersampled in each training fold to match the size of the minority class. Adjusted and non-adjusted models were compared in terms of R2Y, Q2Y, permutation p-values, and (where applicable) Sens, Spec, and Acc, to evaluate the extent to which the observed metabolic variation could be attributed to the confounding variables considered. Packages ropls [74] and caret [39] were utilized for these purposes.

Identification of discriminant metabolites and biological pathway analysis

To characterize the metabolites driving the observed associations and provide biological interpretation, firstly Univariate analysis were performed for all identified metabolites: Mann–Whitney U test with FDR correction for breastfeeding type and infant sex, and Spearman’s rank correlation for weight gain (Supplementary data). Additionally, discriminant metabolites were identified based on VIP scores from the PLS models, using a Q-Q plot-based threshold above which scores deviated from a normal distribution. Finally, a hierarchical multiblock model was constructed to facilitate the biological explanation of the outcomes, following Wold et al. guidelines [82]. Summarily, each metabolite was attributed to up to three biological functions (blocks) depending on an in-house database, with a minimum of three metabolites per group. An OPLS-DA was built for each block to assess the concrete impact of the variable of study on that biological function. Each metabolite block was summarized by the predictive component (t1), while orthogonal variation unrelated to the variable of interest was captured by the orthogonal component (o1). Scores of the component t1 from the previous models were subsequently weighted to reduce the influence of the number of metabolites per block (n), dividing each value by a factor F, defined as 1 + 0.5 log10(n) [82]. In this way, we obtained a dataset of scores representing the impact on a particular biological function for each individual in the cohort or the respective subgroup. The weighted scores were also utilized to create a new PLS-DA model (two latent components) to assess potential data distortion introduced during the processing. Impact of the feature of interest in the blocks was calculated using a random forest test [42]. Number of trees were set at 500, the number of variables randomly samples as candidates at each split were set to the square root of the number of variables for classification models (breastfeeding type, infant sex) or to one-third for regression models (weight gain). No class-balancing procedures were applied, and models were trained using the original class distribution. The out-of-bag error estimate was used to check the error estimate. VIPs of the latter (mean decrease in Gini) were again plotted in a normality plot, and those with higher scores and who deviated the most from the distribution were considered the most different between groups.

In the preparation of this manuscript, ChatGPT-4.0 (OpenAI) was used to support code development for data analysis and to enhance the clarity and linguistic quality of the text. All outputs generated with this tool were carefully reviewed and revised by the authors, who assume full responsibility for the final published content.

Results

Cohort description

All features of the participants are summarized in Table S1. Mothers had a mean age of 35.4 years (median of 36.0 years) and delivered at a mean gestational age of 39.6 weeks (median of 39.9 weeks), thus classifying the pregnancies as full-term. The cohort consisted predominantly of Caucasian women (76%), with minor representation of other ethnicities. On average, participants were classified as normal weight before pregnancy (BMI = 24.1 kg/m2) but shifted to the overweight category at one month postpartum (BMI = 25.3 kg/m2). Body composition revealed relatively high adiposity (BFP = 37.3%), consistent with the postpartum period. The mean number of previous pregnancies was 1.82, and nearly half of the women (47%) had breastfed in the past. Only one multiple pregnancy was recorded, and 76% of deliveries were vaginal. In line with the inclusion criteria, all infants received breast milk: 82.3% were exclusively breastfed, while the remainder were partially breastfed with varying amounts of formula (mixed feeding). Sporadic cases of tobacco or alcohol consumption were reported, whereas no participants reported drug use.

Infants were balanced in terms of sex distribution (55% male, 45% female). Anthropometric measures at birth were within the normal range, with percentiles close to P50. All parameters increased during the first month, confirming a positive growth trajectory, with head circumference showing the most pronounced increment.

Exploratory analysis

All metabolites identified in urine were utilized to assess the influence of perinatal factors. LC–MS quantified 521 metabolites and NMR annotated 74 ROIs (62 identified metabolites and 12 unknown regions). Table S2 lists the compounds with their HMDB ID, highlighting the 38 detected by both techniques.

The global PLS model including all 36 variables of study identified the type of breastfeeding (exclusive or mixed) as the factor explaining the greatest proportion of variance in the urinary metabolome, as revealed by both LC–MS and NMR analyses (Supplementary data 2, Fig. 2A, B). Based on their VIP scores, the top 10 contributors mainly included anthropometric parameters from both newborns (body weight and length at birth and at one month) and their mothers (BMI and body composition), alongside with tobacco consumption in the LC–MS dataset and gestational diseases in the NMR dataset. When the analysis was stratified by infant sex (Fig. 2C-F), neonatal somatometric parameters remained as the main contributors to VIP scores in combination with maternal body composition. The type of breastfeeding persisted as the top variable only in the NMR dataset of baby females. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the complete metabolite dataset did not reveal clear sample clustering, suggesting that within-class variation was comparable to between-class variation and that overall metabolic profiles were broadly similar across individuals (Figure S1). No clear class separation was observed according to the representative variables under study, with only a subtle trend related to breastfeeding type identified in the LC–MS dataset (Figure S1A). To further explore these subtle differences, supervised models were subsequently constructed.

A single supervised MVA model was built for each of these three sets of features to study its impact on the overall metabolite profile, PLS-DA for categorical variables and PLS for those continuous (Table S3). To determine which variables exerted a significant effect, R2Y and Q2Y values were considered (both positive, with values closer to 1 indicating a stronger impact), as well as pR2Y and pQ2 values (both < 0.05). The type of breastfeeding remained the factor explaining the greatest proportion of variance in both LC–MS and NMR datasets. Considering infant characteristics, weight gain during the first month of life showed a statistically significant association with the urine metabolome using both techniques. LC–MS data also distinguished the influence of the weight percentile at one month of age and of the infant sex. In contrast, neither tobacco consumption, gestational diseases, nor any of the maternal variables exerted a significant effect in urine metabolome when analyzed individually in any of the datasets.

Considering the importance of discerning potential sex biases, particularly in perinatal studies, the analyses were repeated after stratifying the data by infant sex (Tables S4 and S5 for males and females, respectively). Given the reduced sample sizes resulting from this stratification, these analyses are considered exploratory. The influence of breastfeeding type on urine metabolome was consistently observed in both sex-stratified subcohorts. In addition, weight gain was significantly associated with the NMR dataset only in male infants.

Covariable analysis

We examined the relationships between the selected variables and other clinical characteristics (Table S1). Comparisons according to type of breastfeeding showed significant differences in maternal anthropometric measurements. Specifically, mothers in the mixed-feeding group had a higher pre-pregnancy BMI and a higher BMI at one month postpartum, with mean values within the overweight range at both timepoints. They also showed greater adiposity at one month postpartum (higher body fat percentage (BFP) and visceral fat index (VF)), together with lower lean mass. Effect sizes were large for all the anthropometric variables, with the largest difference observed for visceral fat percentage. Additionally, delivery mode was also evaluated because it has been reported to be associated with maternal overweight and obesity [90]. Delivery mode showed a trend towards an association with higher pre-gestational BMI (p = 0.057) but no significant association was observed with breastfeeding type (Figure S2A). Based on these results, maternal anthropometric variables and delivery mode were considered potential confounding factors for the effects of breastfeeding type and were therefore included in the subsequent analyses.

Comparisons between male and female infants revealed that only somatometric parameters differed according to infant sex. Specifically, male infants had higher length and head circumference at birth, as well as higher weight, length, head circumference, and weight gain at one month of age. However, no differences were observed in growth percentiles during the first month of life. Multiple linear regression analyses of weight gain showed no significant associations with maternal features, whereas several infant characteristics were significantly associated with this variable. As expected, weight gain correlated with body weight at one month of age and with other measurements of early growth. In light of these results, infant sex was considered a potential confounding factor and was included in the subsequent analyses of weight gain.

Metabolic profiles associated with the variables of interest

The variables of interest identified in the exploratory analysis (breastfeeding type, infant sex, and weight gain) were further characterized at the metabolomic level by identifying the metabolites with the highest discriminant power and the functional metabolic blocks most associated with each perinatal factor.

Type of breastfeeding

All infants in the study received breast milk; however, 17.7% (9 males and 8 females) were mixed-fed and received formula supplementation. The estimated proportion of formula feeds relative to the total daily feed (assuming eight feeds/day as the reference) ranged from 12.5% to 75% among mixed-fed infants. Specifically, a formula proportion of 12.5% (1 feed/day) was estimated for four infants, 25% (2 feeds/day) for four infants, 37.5% (3 feeds/day) for one infant, 50% (4 feeds/day) for seven infants, and 75% (6 feeds/day) for one infant. Formula composition was not standardized and varied across individuals.

The urinary metabolome reflected the presence of formula feeding in both LC–MS and NMR datasets, as exclusive- and mixed-breastfeeding groups were clearly separated in the respective PLS-DA models (Fig. 3A, C). This effect was relatively notable in the LC–MS model and explained approximately 78% of variability and 60% of predictive ability, while it was more discrete from NMR data, around 69% and 35%, respectively. In both cases, these values were statistically significant (pR2Y = 0.005, pQ2 = 0.005). In terms of classification performance, the LC–MS model achieved an overall accuracy of 96%, with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 97%. The NMR model showed slightly lower performance, with 93% accuracy, 76% sensitivity, and 99% specificity. To assess the robustness of the association between breastfeeding type and the urinary metabolome, PLS-DA models were constructed after adjusting for potential confounding factors, individually and in combination (Figure S2B). Model validity was preserved across most conditions; however, model performance was notably reduced upon adjustment, particularly in Q2 and sensitivity values and in the NMR dataset. Delivery mode had minimal influence, while maternal VF, BFP, post-partum BMI and the combination of all factors introduced the greatest reductions in performance, suggesting that a portion of the metabolic variability initially attributed to breastfeeding type was partially explained by these covariates. Nevertheless, significant group-related metabolic differences persisted after adjustment, particularly in the LC–MS dataset.

Metabolite selection in the LC–MS and NMR datasets was based on their VIP scores, resulting in 95 and 10 compounds, respectively (Figures S3.1A, B; S3.2A; Figs. 3B, D). Most metabolites displayed higher levels in the mixed-breastfeeding group compared with exclusive breastfeeding. These involved metabolic intermediates (as succinic and oxoglutaric acids), derivatives of vitamins (riboflavin, lumichrome, and 4-pyridoxate) or microbiota metabolites (N-6-carboxymethyllysine, N-2-phenylacetyl glycine, 3-indoxylsulfate, and acetic acid), among others.

Metabolites were assigned to specific biological functions (Table S6), and a new PLS-DA model was generated with a multiblock analysis (Figs. 3E, G). Random forest of the weighted score values permitted the identification of 3 biological functions of interest from NMR multiblock data and 13 blocks from LC–MS data (Figs. 3F, H). Three common urine metabolome functional blocks reflected the type of breastfeeding –carbohydrate metabolism, xenobiotics/exogenous compounds, and microbiota metabolism. LC–MS data outlined ten additional metabolic pathways showing a relevant influence on: antioxidants, metabolic regulation, cell growth and proliferation, carbohydrate metabolism and vitamin metabolism.

The same workflow was applied to male and female subgroups (Figure S3.1, S3.2). Overall, the metabolic differences associated with breastfeeding type were consistent with those observed in the whole cohort at both the metabolite and functional level. Most of the key metabolites and enriched blocks were shared across all subsets – total, male, and female infants – particularly among the top discriminant features. To some extent, the effect seemed more pronounced in the female subset, as indicated by higher-performing PLS-DA models and a greater number of significantly changed metabolites. Although the unbalanced group size within the sex-stratified cohorts limits the interpretability of these findings, they suggest that the urinary metabolic associations with the type of breastfeeding are largely sex-independent, with a slightly greater association in females.

Infant sex

A distinction in the urinary metabolome was found between male and female infants, particularly in the dataset from LC–MS (Fig. 4A). Even though the PLS-DA model was statistically significant (pR2Y = 0.04, pQ2 = 0.005), its predictive capacity was modest, with a Q2 value of approximately 25%. The top 56 metabolites driving differences among sex groups (VIP scores > 1.44, Fig. 4B) included several amino acids and derivatives (glutamine, hexanoylglycine, N-acetylaspartic acid, serine, …), derivative of steroid hormones (androsterone glucuronide), vitamin derivatives (pyridoxine, nicotinamide mononucleotide, flavin mononucleotide, pyridoxamine), nucleotides (deoxyguanosine, uracil, 5-methylthioribose, cyclic adenosine monophosphate [cAMP], N2-methylguanosine), neurotransmitters (serotonine, aspartic acid), or compounds with a role in metabolic regulation (phenylethanolamine or 5-methylthioribose, among others) (Fig. 4C).

This approach was followed by multiblock analysis. Sample distribution was still very similar to the previous model constructed with individual metabolite data (Fig. 4D), and the model was statistically significant, confirming that the multiblock approach did not introduce any relevant distortion in the data. Then, a random forest analysis revealed which of these biological functions were the most different between male and female newborns (Fig. 4E). Fourteen blocks were highlighted in this comparison, with the top five being metabolic regulation, xenobiotics, antioxidants, microbiota metabolism, and amino acid metabolism.

Metabolite selection according to VIP scores led to the identification of 71 compounds from LC–MS and 16 metabolites from NMR (Figures S4 & 5B, D). Many of the selected metabolites were related to amino acid metabolism (e.g., sarcosine, leucine, and tyrosine, …). In addition, multiblock analysis identified four biological functions – three from LC–MS data and one from NMR data (Fig. 5E-H), mainly related to xenobiotics, microbiota metabolism and metabolic regulation.

The exploratory analysis dichotomizing sex-specific subgroups displayed a significant model in the male subset of the NMR dataset, with even higher performance values. In the LC–MS dataset, similar metrics were obtained, but the model did not meet the predefined validity criteria (pQ2 = 0.18). No valid models were obtained for the female subgroup. Accordingly, these sex-stratified results should be considered exploratory.

Discussion

Urine integrates end-products of multiple metabolic pathways, including endogenous compounds, microbial-derived metabolites, and exogenous compounds from diet and environmental exposure. The capacity of the kidneys to concentrate metabolites at levels often exceeding those detectable in blood, combined with the non-invasive nature of its collection, makes urine particularly well-suited for metabolomic studies in vulnerable populations such as neonates [9]. Previous metabolomic studies have shown that neonatal urine provides valuable insight into metabolic adaptations associated with perinatal conditions, particularly in studies comparing preterm and term neonates [8, 45]. Our findings extend this approach to healthy full-term infants, showing that breastfeeding type (exclusive vs. mixed), infant sex, and early weight gain were the perinatal variables most strongly associated with variation in the urinary metabolome, with breastfeeding type emerging as the predominant contributor to metabolic variability.

Indirect effects of maternal BMI and body composition

Although maternal BMI and body composition were not directly associated with the variation in the urinary metabolome, indirect associations with infant metabolic profiles were observed. Mothers who practiced mixed breastfeeding had higher pre-pregnancy and one-month postpartum BMI together with greater total and visceral adiposity at one month postpartum than those who exclusively breastfed. Adjustment for maternal anthropometric variables partially accounted for the metabolic differences associated with breastfeeding type, consistent with their contribution to the initial exploratory models.

Maternal obesity is known to negatively affect breastfeeding by hindering initiation, shortening duration and reducing milk supply [76]. While psychosocial factors contribute, excess adiposity itself may also induce hormonal imbalances that impair milk production or supply [7]. Indeed, in our cohort, visceral fat showed the largest difference between feeding groups, further supporting the link between greater maternal adiposity and reduced breastfeeding.

Altogether, these results suggest that the association between breastfeeding type and the infant urinary metabolome is partially explained by maternal BMI and body composition. This raises the possibility that maternal BMI may influence neonatal metabolism not only through direct biological mechanisms, but also indirectly through its association with breastfeeding practices. Importantly, these findings emphasize the importance of supporting breastfeeding among mothers with overweight or obesity. Even though policies promoting exclusive breastfeeding exist, research addressing this subpopulation remains limited [25].

Metabolomic differences associated with breastfeeding type

Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended as the optimal feeding pattern for the first six months of life [84] and has been associated with lower risks of childhood morbidity and later metabolic complications, particularly obesity and type 2 diabetes [35]. Even partial formula supplementation has been associated with greater weight gain during the first week of life [26] and a higher risk of overweight at 12 months [27]. These effects have been shown to be long-lasting, as exclusive breastfeeding is also linked to lower adiposity in children and adolescents aged 7 and 15 years (in both sexes) when compared to mixed feeding [51]. In the present study, however, feeding pattern was not associated with differences in infant growth during the first month of life, consistent with findings from other cohorts [23, 29].

Despite the absence of neonatal anthropometric changes, a metabolic urinary imprinting associated with mixed feeding was found. The impact of mixed lactation on the neonatal urinary metabolome had been previously hypothesized, as the introduction of less than 40% infant formula was sufficient to shift the neonatal urinary metabolome from that of the respective maternal milk, which shows a strong overlap in exclusively breastfed neonates [19]. In agreement with these hypotheses, most of the top discriminant metabolites identified in our study were present at higher levels in mixed-fed infants, suggesting that infant formula may substantially contribute to these compounds.

Notably, maternal BMI and adiposity showed a significant confounding effect on the association between breastfeeding type and the urinary metabolome. Adjustment for these variables partially attenuated the metabolic differences initially associated with mixed feeding, suggesting that maternal metabolic status may also contribute to variation in the neonatal urinary metabolome. This is biologically plausible, as in-utero exposure to maternal obesity has been linked to persistent alterations in the urinary metabolome of offspring later in childhood [28]. Additionally, maternal obesity has been associated with alterations in the composition of breast milk [56], meaning that mixed-fed infants may be exposed to two concurrent nutritional influences: obesity-related alterations in breast milk composition and the inherently distinct composition of infant formula. Therefore, the observed confounding effect of maternal adiposity may reflect both prenatal metabolic programming and postnatal differences in nutrient and bioactive exposure.

Several metabolic pathways appeared strongly associated with mixed breastfeeding, likely because of the slightly different composition of formula milk from that of breastmilk [4]. It has been suggested that the higher carbohydrate intake from formula stimulates glucose utilization as the primary energy source [10], a pattern consistent with the reduced levels of glucose and maltose and elevated glycerol-3-phosphate observed here under a mixed feeding regime. Interestingly, 4-deoxythreonic acid (4-DEA) was also increased in mixed-fed infants (Table 1), supporting the idea of a specific adaptation to carbohydrate metabolism. Regarding lipids, the key outcomes involved mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, as the main metabolites affected were carnitines, such as octanoyl-carnitine, suggesting a differential reliance on β-oxidation pathways depending on the feeding mode. Together, these findings point to a metabolic shift from fatty acid- to glucose-based energy utilization in mixed-fed infants, consistent with the greater reliance on fat oxidation previously reported in exclusively breastfed infants and putatively linked to the distinct lipid composition of human milk versus infant formula [32]. Cholesterol metabolism was also significantly affected, possibly due to a different supply of cholesterol through lactation. Infant formulas typically contain lower amounts of cholesterol, or none in plant-based formulations, and may also include phytosterols, which can reduce intestinal cholesterol absorption. Consequently, formula feeding has been associated with increased endogenous cholesterol production and bile acid reabsorption [3, 47], a pattern that was also observed in mixed-fed infants in our cohort (Table 1). Our results also showed changes in steroid hormones, including corticosterone. Interestingly, hair cortisol levels in eight-year-old children have been reported to negatively correlate with breastfeeding duration [43]. Vitamin metabolism was also associated with breastfeeding type, with mixed-fed infants showing elevated levels of riboflavin and B6 vitamers — including pyridoxine, pyridoxal, and 4-pyridoxate — likely reflecting the higher vitamin content of standard infant formulas [92]. Multiple xenobiotic-related metabolites were also associated with breastfeeding type (Table 1). As many of these compounds are plant- derived, their presence likely reflects maternal diet and/or the composition of infant formula, which often contains plant-based ingredients. For example, soy-based formulas have been associated with higher urinary polyphenol levels in infants [62]. Most of these compounds are bioactive rather than inert and may exert antioxidant effects, potentially influencing neonatal health. However, the absence of detailed information on formula composition in this cohort precludes confirmation of this hypothesis. Contrarily, mixed-fed infants showed lower levels of certain other antioxidants, consistent with previous studies showing elevated urinary oxidative stress markers in formula-fed compared with breastfed neonates [67]. Thus, the overall oxidative status of the mixed-fed infants in this cohort was unclear. Similarly, microbiota-derived metabolites were also influenced by breastfeeding type. Supporting the latter, a recent study reported different microbiota profiles in mixed-fed infants [41]. Mixed-fed infants showed elevated levels of gut microbiota-derived toxins, such as 3-indoxyl sulfate, p-cresol sulfate, trimethylamine oxide (TMAO), or phenylacetylglycine, suggesting a protective role of exclusive breastfeeding in shaping early microbiota health. These compounds are end-products of bacterial proteolytic fermentation of aromatic amino acids — tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine — and their elevated levels in mixed-fed infants may reflect both the higher protein load associated with infant formula [94] and differences in microbiota composition. Indeed, human oligosaccharides present in breast milk promote the dominance of Bifidobacterium species, which preferentially channel aromatic amino acids towards aromatic lactic acids [40] rather than toxic sulfated metabolites. The impact of infant formula on microbiota metabolism and its potential implications for adult health outcomes have been previously proposed [4]. Notably, several biomarkers associated with cardiovascular health were also affected by mixed breastfeeding, e.g. N-6-carboxymethyllysine (CML), N-acetylmethionine, and TMAO. These findings may reflect differences in formula composition, including the generation of CML during thermal processing [57], its higher protein content, and the higher choline content reported in some infant formulas [46], a precursor of TMAO.

Although the overall associations between breastfeeding type and the urinary metabolome were consistent across sexes, the associations appeared slightly stronger in females. While this observation should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory and underpowered nature of the sex-stratified analyses, it is consistent with previous studies suggesting greater metabolic sensitivity to formula supplementation in female neonates [16].

Metabolic differences associated with infant sex

Infant sex was also associated with variation in the urinary metabolome. Previous studies have reported sex-specific differences in early metabolic development, which may contribute to long-term metabolic programming [17]. One potential mechanism underlying this dimorphism is variation in steroid hormone levels, consistent with our observations in steroid metabolism regarding androsterone glucuronide. The metabolite block exhibiting the greatest change was primarily linked to processes governing metabolic regulation, many of which include biomarkers of inborn errors of metabolism (Table 1). Although our cohort consisted of healthy infants, these findings highlight the potential influence of biological sex on metabolic processes. Notable variations were also observed in the amino acid metabolism (Table 1), in agreement with previous studies. Tryptophan metabolism was particularly associated with infant sex, a pathway implicated in the developmental programming of cardiovascular and renal systems [36], suggesting potential consequences for sex-biased health outcomes. Sex-related differences were also observed in neurotransmitter metabolism, e.g. aspartate and serotonin, hypothetically reflecting early divergences in nervous system development [17]. Mitochondrial metabolism also showed sex-specific differences (Table 1), consistent with previously reported molecular divergences between males and females [78]. Mitochondria metabolism is intrinsically related to cell redox status. In this context, females typically exhibit higher enzymatic antioxidant capacity [78]. Interestingly, in our cohort, males exhibited elevated levels of several antioxidants, such as pyridoxin and gallic acid. Finally, microbiota-derived metabolites also varied according to sex, including 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid and 2,6-diaminoheptanedionate, consistent with the evidence that biological sex strongly shapes intestinal microbiota in early life, particularly between one and six months of age [44].

Metabolic differences associated with weight gain

In healthy term infants, weight gain occurs in parallel with gains in length and head circumference, reflecting coordinated somatic and brain growth during early postnatal life, as documented in the WHO growth standards [85]. Consistent with these normative data, weight gain in the first month of life was significantly correlated with length gain in our cohort, supporting its use as an indicator of overall early growth.

Early life growth trajectories have a lasting impact on health in adulthood. Adequate weight gain has been associated with a lower risk of developing obesity, insulin resistance, and other cardiometabolic alterations later in life [50]. Specifically, weight gain during the first month of life correlates with BMI at three years of age [73], making it a useful measure for studies of metabolic programming related to obesity. In our cohort, weight gain was associated with differences in the urinary metabolome.

A general function of metabolic regulation emerged as the block most associated with weight gain. Among the discriminant metabolites, 3-carboxy-4-methyl-5-propyl-2-furanpropanoic acid (CMPF) emerged as the most discriminant. Circulating CMPF levels during pregnancy have been linked to a western dietary pattern and to neurodevelopmental disorders in the progeny [34]; however, these associations were reported in pathological contexts and their relevance to healthy term infants such as those in the present cohort is unclear. In terms of glucose metabolism, weight gain correlated positively with both 1,5-anhydro-sorbitol and glucose. This finding supports the use of 1,5-anhydro-sorbitol as a marker of glycemic status and suggests a potential link between higher weight gain and increased glycemia. Weight gain was also correlated with several microbiota-related metabolites (Table 1). Accordingly, microbiota composition is associated with weight gain during the first month of life [29]. Furthermore, higher weight gain was associated with reduced levels of exogenous antioxidant metabolites, such as 4-methylcatechol and abscisic acid. This could suggest higher oxidative stress in newborns with greater iBWG, a proposed mechanism linking rapid early growth to later obesity [87].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it employs non-invasive urine sampling to explore metabolic profiles in neonates, offering a practical and easily applicable method for studying early-life biological processes. Second, the study design included an exploratory analysis, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of the perinatal environment rather than focusing solely on predefined targets. Third, all newborns included were healthy, allowing the observed variations to be interpreted as metabolic responses within a physiological context, increasing their potential predictive value. Finally, the use of two analytical techniques provided broader metabolite coverage, reinforcing the robustness and depth of the results.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Pediatric urine collection bags are prone to bacterial contamination from the perineal area, which may interfere with the detection of urinary metabolites (including glucose and several amino acids) and may also introduce plastic-related contaminants or additives. The single sampling timepoint, together with the absence of a pre-analytical normalization strategy maybe more appropriate than our signal-correction strategy used (PQN method), may further introduce intra-individual variability not accounted for, such as differences in hydration state or time since the last feeding. Additionally, detailed information on the type and composition of infant formula was not available, limiting our ability to assess the influence of this factor. The inherent uncertainty associated with some metabolite annotation should also be acknowledged. Regarding the study design and population, the relatively small sample size, unbalanced analysis groups, potential confounding factors, and the homogeneity of the maternal population (predominantly from the same region and ethnicity) may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings, despite the use of several statistical approaches to evaluate their potential influence. Furthermore, the cross-sectional, observational design limits our ability to infer long-term consequences of the observed metabolic shifts. While interpretations have been grounded in current knowledge about metabolite function, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating: longitudinal studies, replication in validation cohorts, and targeted analyses focusing on metabolites of biological interest herein identified would be required to establish causal links between these early metabolic profiles and future health outcomes. Nonetheless, these findings provide novel insights into how perinatal factors are associated with early metabolic profiles and highlight the potential of urine-based metabolomics in studying neonatal physiology.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings suggest that the urinary metabolome of healthy full-term, breastfed infants is broadly similar across individuals but displays subtle differences associated with several perinatal factors. Breastfeeding type (mixed vs. exclusive) was the factor most strongly associated with metabolic variation, with infants receiving mixed breastfeeding exhibiting distinct metabolic signatures compared with exclusively breastfed infants. Infant sex and weight gain during the first month of age were also associated with urinary metabolic variation, although to a lesser extent. Together, these findings point to differences in metabolic pathways related to metabolic regulation, microbiota-related metabolism, and antioxidant capacity (Fig. 6).
