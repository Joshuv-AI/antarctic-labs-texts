Accuracy of Discretely Sampled Stochastic Policies in Continuous-time Reinforcement Learning
Yanwei Jia 1, Du Ouyang 2, Yufei Zhang 3

Abstract
. Stochastic policies are widely used in continuous-time reinforcement learning algorithms. However, executing a stochastic policy and evaluating its performance in a continuous-time environment remain open challenges. This work introduces and rigorously analyzes a policy execution framework that samples actions from a stochastic policy at discrete time points and implements them as piecewise constant controls. We prove that as the sampling mesh size tends to zero, the controlled state process converges weakly to the dynamics with coefficients aggregated according to the stochastic policy. We explicitly quantify the convergence rate based on the regularity of the coefficients and establish an optimal first-order convergence rate for sufficiently regular coefficients. Additionally, we show that the same convergence rates hold with high probability concerning the sampling noise, and further establish a 1 / 2 1/2 -order almost sure convergence when the volatility is not controlled. Building on these results, we analyze the bias and variance of various policy evaluation and policy gradient estimators based on discrete-time observations. Our results provide theoretical justification for the exploratory stochastic control framework in [H. Wang, T. Zariphopoulou, and X.Y. Zhou, J. Mach. Learn. Res., 21 (2020), pp. 1-34].



1.
Introduction

Continuous-time RL

Reinforcement learning (RL) has catalyzed significant changes across various domains by attacking complex sequential decision-making challenges in a data-driven way. The theoretical framework for analyzing RL problems and designing RL algorithms has been predominantly based on the discrete-time Markov decision processes
[
5
,
30
]
. However, many practical applications require real-time interaction with environments where time progresses continuously, such as autonomous driving, stock trading, and vehicle routing. Recent studies
[
33
,
38
,
20
,
12
]
have shown that certain algorithms originally designed for discrete-time RL can become unstable when applied in high-frequency settings. This highlights the need for a new theoretical framework for designing RL algorithms that are robust to time discretization.

To address this issue, many suggest formulating the problem in a continuous-time framework from the outset and only introducing time discretization at the final implementation stage. Here, time discretization refers to the frequency at which data is collected and actions are updated, while the underlying system itself evolves continuously according to its intrinsic dynamics. Early studies on continuous-time RL have primarily focused on deterministic systems, where state variables follow ordinary differential equations; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
9
,
33
,
26
,
22
]
.

Exploratory control and
stochastic policy

Recently,
[
36
]
proposed a novel exploratory control formulation
with stochastic policies
for continuous-time RL problems, which has served as the foundation for designing various RL algorithms; see, e.g.,
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
15
,
8
,
11
,
37
,
12
,
29
,
32
]
.

This control formulation in
[
36
]
has three key features: (1) The agent incorporates
stochastic policies
into the control problem, which models exploration in RL. These policies are mappings from the state space to distributions over the action space, aligning with the notion of “relaxed control” in classical control theory. (2) The agent
continuously
samples actions from a stochastic policy, and the state process evolves according to a new diffusion process, with its coefficients integrated with respect to the sampling distribution specified by the stochastic policy. This auxiliary state dynamic is often referred to as the
exploratory
or
aggregated
dynamics.
(3) The optimal policy remains stochastic due to the inclusion of an additional entropy regularizer in the cost objective, in contrast to the deterministic/strict policies typically found in standard control problems.

Execution of stochastic policies

However, despite its success in algorithm design, the use of stochastic policies in continuous-time RL problems introduces theoretical and practical gaps. Specifically, the connection between exploratory dynamics with aggregated coefficients and state dynamics controlled by frequent sampling from a stochastic policy remains unjustified.
In fact, interacting with a diffusion process by continuously sampling from a stochastic policy requires generating uncountably many independent random variables, which raises measure-theoretical issues and renders the resulting controlled state dynamics ill-defined (see the end of Section
2
for more details).

To address this issue,
[
32
]
proposes approximating exploratory dynamics by sampling actions from a stochastic policy only at discrete time points. This approach naturally aligns with the practical implementation of stochastic policies. However, for algorithms designed based on aggregated dynamics, executing a stochastic policy in discrete time introduces a bias that depends on the sampling frequency.
This leads to the following natural question:

What is the precise impact of sampling frequency on the performance of a stochastic policy and the corresponding learning algorithms?

To the best of our knowledge, this question has only been addressed in the context of linear-quadratic (LQ) RL problems with Gaussian policies
[
12
,
32
]
. In this special setting, due to the LQ structure, the analysis is simplified by examining the discrepancy between the first and second moments of the aggregated dynamics and the controlled dynamics with sampled actions. Unfortunately, these techniques developed specifically for LQ RL problems are clearly not applicable to general RL problems. In particular, it is essential to quantify the difference between the entire distributions of two state processes.

Our contributions

This paper analyzes the accuracy of the discrete-time execution of stochastic policies for general diffusion processes and quantifies its impact on the associated learning algorithms.

•

We show that when actions are sampled from a stochastic policy at a discrete time grid

\mathscr{G}

and executed as a piecewise constant control process, the resulting state process, referred to as the
sampled dynamics
, converges weakly to the aggregated dynamics as the time stepsize approaches zero.
In particular, under sufficient regular coefficients, we show that the convergence rate is of first order
O(|\mathscr{G}|)
, with
|\mathscr{G}|
being the time stepsize
(Theorem
4.1
).
This matches the optimal convergence rate for standard piecewise constant control approximations
[
16
]
.
We further quantify the precise weak convergence rate for cases with less regular coefficients
(Theorem
4.2
).

The convergence results rigorously justify the exploratory control framework proposed in
[
36
]
(see Section
2
). The weak convergence rate will be used to analyze the performance of learning algorithms based on observations from the sampled dynamics.

•

We prove that the sampled dynamics, in fact, converges
almost surely
with respect to the randomness in policy execution.
Specifically, we show that the weak convergence order to the aggregated dynamics holds with high probability with respect to the randomness used to sample actions (Theorem
4.3
).
We further show that when the volatility is uncontrolled, the sampled dynamics converges almost surely to the aggregated dynamics with an order of
\mathcal{O}(|\mathscr{G}|^{1/2})
,
for each fixed realization of the Brownian motion (Theorem
4.4
).
The analysis leverages concentration inequalities for bounded and sub-exponential martingale difference sequences to control the concentration behavior of the sampling errors in terms of the sampling frequency (see the discussions below Theorems
4.3
and
4.4
).

The almost sure convergence result indicates that the convergence of the sampled dynamics to the aggregated dynamics can be interpreted as
a law of large numbers in time
, i.e., the convergence can be achieved by refining the sampling frequency while fixing the realization of the system noise.
Note that such an almost sure convergence fails when the volatility is controlled, as shown in Example
4.6
.

•

We apply the weak convergence rate to quantify the error incurred when implementing continuous-time RL algorithms based on observations from sampled dynamics. In particular, we show that evaluating the reward involving aggregated dynamics by discrete sampling and piecewise constant execution has a bias of the order
O(|\mathscr{G}|)
.
Calculating the martingale orthogonality conditions proposed in
[
18
]
using discretely sampled stochastic policies also introduces a bias of the same order,
O(|\mathscr{G}|)
. The policy gradient representation developed in
[
19
]
is an informal integration that involves the realized actions and the state variables. We rigorously show that using the forward Euler scheme to calculate such terms with discretely sampled stochastic policies is a biased estimator for the policy gradient, and the bias is
O(|\mathscr{G}|)
and variance is bounded. The q-learning with quadratic variation penalty for the risk-sensitive RL proposed in
[
17
]
is also shown to have a similar order of discretization error.

Our approaches and most related works

This paper quantifies the accuracy of the discrete-time execution of a stochastic policy and its impact on continuous-time RL algorithms. The accuracy of policy execution has been studied in
[
14
,
32
]
for LQ problems with Gaussian policies. However, as mentioned earlier, the techniques developed in
[
14
,
32
]
are not applicable to RL problems with general state dynamics and stochastic policies considered in this paper.

For general state dynamics, the weak convergence of sampled dynamics has recently been studied by
[
4
,
3
]
. They adopt a different definition of sampled dynamics, treating it as being driven by a random measure, and establish the weak convergence of the state process without quantifying the convergence order.
In contrast to
the time-varying control considered in
[
4
,
3
]
,
we analyze sampled dynamics controlled by a piecewise constant control process. Moreover, we take a completely different approach and establish the weak convergence rate under various regularity conditions by leveraging the solution regularity of the associated partial differential equations (PDEs).
Beyond weak convergence, we also establish convergence in the almost sure sense with respect to the policy execution noise. These strong convergence results require a careful analysis of the concentration properties of the state dynamics concerning the policy execution noise.

In addition to discrete sampling error, we also analyze the error incurred when approximating continuous-time RL algorithms using discrete-time observations.
[
18
]

analyzes a similar error but assumes that observations are generated from the
aggregated dynamics
.
[
2
]
analyzes both errors for LQ problems with uncontrolled volatility.

Our analysis of the policy execution error is fundamentally different from classical numerical analysis of time discretizations for stochastic differential equations (SDEs). In particular, numerical analysis of SDEs focuses on the error caused by discretizing the system noise, such as Brownian motion, while both the SDE and its time-discretized version have the same coefficients.
In our setting, we treat the controlled SDE as a black-box environment evolving continuously and focus on the error introduced by the piecewise constant execution of a stochastic policy. As a result, the environment noise is not discretized, but the limiting SDE (the aggregated dynamics) and the sampled dynamics have
different coefficients
. The former involves aggregated coefficients, while the latter has random coefficients determined by the specific procedure used to sample the policy.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
briefly reviews the
use of stochastic policies in continuous-time RL. Section
3
presents the problem setup and introduces the sampled dynamics rigorously. Section
4
presents the main convergence results
of the sampled dynamics,
and Section
5

applies them to analyze continuous-time RL algorithms. Finally, we conclude in Section
6
. The proofs of all statements are included in Section
7
.

Preliminary

In this section, we review the exploratory control formulation and the use of stochastic policies for continuous-time RL algorithms.

2.1.
Classical stochastic control

Recall that in a classical stochastic control problem, an agent aims to control the state process and optimize an objective functional through an adapted control process taking values in a Polish space
A
.
Specifically, given an admissible control process
\{a_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, the corresponding state process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is governed by the following dynamics:

1

1

To simplify the presentation, we assume that the state processes have a deterministic initial condition. However, our analysis and results extend naturally to cases with a random initial condition, provided it is independent of the Brownian motion
W
and satisfies appropriate integrability conditions. We also assume that the Brownian motion’s dimension equals the state variable’s dimension; otherwise, we can introduce more Brownian motions or state variables to make them equal.

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t},a_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t},a_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\in[0,T];\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

where

x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a given initial state,

and

W
is an
d

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
The agent’s objective is to maximize the following objective

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}r(t,X_{t},a_{t})\mathrm{d}t+h(X_{T})\right]

over all admissible controls
\{a_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where
r:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times A\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a given instantaneous reward function, and

is a given terminal reward function.

It is known that under suitable conditions (see

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t},\bm{u}(t,X_{t}))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t},\bm{u}(t,X_{t}))\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\in[0,T];\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

The function can be determined using the coefficients

2.2.
RL, stochastic policy and exploratory control

In the RL problem, the agent seeks to optimize the objective (
2.2
) by directly interacting with the dynamics (
2.1
),
experimenting with different actions, and refining their strategies based on the observed responses. Unlike traditional control settings, the agent treats (
2.1
) as a black-box environment evolving in continuous time and learns an optimal policy solely from observations, without direct access to the coefficients
b,\sigma,r,
and
h
.

To avoid getting trapped in local optima, a key feature of RL is explicitly exploring the environment through
randomized
actions. A common approach is to use
stochastic policies

\bm{\pi}:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\mathcal{P}(A)
, which maps the current state to a probability distribution over the action space. Given a stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}
, the agent interacts with the environment by sampling noisy actions from
\bm{\pi}
and observing the resulting system states. Over time, the agent refines the stochastic policy to optimize the objective.

To describe the performance of a stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}
,
[
36
]
suggests that the associated state process
\tilde{X}
evolves according to the following dynamics (called the
exploratory dynamics
therein):

(2.4)

{\mathrm{d}}X_{t}=\tilde{b}^{\bm{\pi}}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{\sigma}^{\bm{\pi}}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\in[0,T];\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

with the coefficients
\tilde{b}^{\bm{\pi}}:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\tilde{\sigma}^{\bm{\pi}}:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}^{\bm{\pi}}(t,x)\coloneqq\int_{A}b(t,x,a)\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}a|t,x),\quad\tilde{\sigma}^{\bm{\pi}}(t,x)\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{A}(\sigma\sigma^{\top})(t,x,a)\bm{\pi}({\mathrm{d}}a|t,x)},

where
(\cdot)^{1/2}

is the matrix square root of positive semidefinite matrices, and
(\cdot)^{\top}
is the matrix transpose.
Moreover, the associated objective function is given by:

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{r}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+h(\widetilde{X}_{T})\right],\quad\textnormal{with
$\tilde{r}(t,x)\coloneqq\int_{A}r(t,x,a)\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}a|t,x)$.
}

The dynamics (
2.4
) and the objective (
2.6
) are derived heuristically in
[
36
]
under the assumption that the agent interacts with the system (
2.1
) by
continuously
sampling

independent

random actions from the policy
\bm{\pi}
. As a result of these random actions, a “law-of-large-numbers” heuristic suggests that the infinitesimal change in the state at time
t
is determined by the coefficients in (
2.5
), aggregated over the sampling distribution defined by
\bm{\pi}
. Likewise, the instantaneous reward is also aggregated with respect to this sampling distribution.
In the sequel,
we refer to (
2.4
) as the
aggregated dynamics
to highlight this aggregating effect.

However, the rigorous justification of the connection between (
2.4
) and the state dynamics resulting from the adoption of a stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}
in (
2.1
), as well as the relationship between (
2.6
) and the original reward functional (
2.2
), remains to be established.
Indeed, interacting with
the SDE (
2.1
) by
continuously

sampling
from a stochastic policy creates measure-theoretical issues.
As already pointed out in
[
32
, Remark 2.1]
,
it is impossible to construct
a family of non-constant random variables
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

such that
(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}

is
(essentially) pairwise independent
and
t\mapsto\xi_{t}
is Lebesgue measurable.
This implies that if one controls (
2.1
) by continuously generating independent actions, the resulting coefficients are not progressively measurable, rendering the conventional stochastic integral ill-defined;
see
[
3
]
for more discussion.
Moreover, it is practically infeasible to generate these independent samples continuously.

Therefore, both from theoretical and practical perspectives, evaluating the performance of a stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}
requires discretely sampling actions from the policy and applying them to (
2.1
), as suggested by
[
32
]
. This work quantifies the accuracy of such a discrete execution of a stochastic policy and its impact on the corresponding RL algorithms.

5.1.

5.2.
Accuracy of Temporal-Difference learning

The
temporal difference
(TD) plays a crucial role in many RL algorithms as the policy evaluation methods. In contrast to the Monte-Carlo evaluation, TD learning often aims to find an approximation of the value function associated with a given policy
\bm{\pi}
, defined as

(5.8)

\tilde{J}(t,x;\bm{\pi})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\tilde{r}(s,\widetilde{X}_{s})\mathrm{d}s+h(\widetilde{X}_{T})\Big|\widetilde{X}_{t}=x\right],

within a suitable parametric family of functions, and it makes use of the increment of the value function process.

In particular, suppose
V^{\theta}
is a family of parameterized functions of
(t,x)
with
\theta
a finite-dimensional parameter. TD learning concerns the increment in the form “
\mathrm{d}V^{\theta}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})
” and the instantaneous reward “
r(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t
”. In the continuous-time framework, these two increments have different natures. “
\mathrm{d}V^{\theta}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})
” is merely an informal notation that shall be combined with a suitable integrand as in the definition of Itô’s integral, and the latter “
r(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t
” is the usual Lebesgue integral with respect to time. In theory,
[
19
]
identify the continuous-time counterpart of TD learning as solving the martingale orthogonality condition. More precisely, if
\theta^{*}
is the root to

(5.9)

\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\int_{0}^{T}S(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\left[\mathrm{d}V^{\theta}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})+\tilde{r}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right]\right]=0,

for all admissible test functions
S(t,x)
, then the
V^{\theta^{*}}(t,x)=\tilde{J}(t,x;\bm{\pi})
is the desired value function associated with a given policy
\bm{\pi}
. Therefore, various RL algorithms are based on using stochastic approximation (cf.
[
7
]
) to find the root
\theta^{*}
to the martingale orthogonality condition (
5.9
). The most popular choice is the so-called “TD(0)” algorithm, which corresponds to taking
S(t,x)=\frac{\partial V^{\theta}}{\partial\theta}(t,x)
.

For a given test function
S
and parameter
\theta
, applying the stochastic approximation algorithm means approximating this expectation with random samples, i.e., an estimator of this expectation. The typical convergence conditions of the stochastic approximation algorithm are threefold
5
5

5

Besides these conditions on the accuracy, the convergence also depends on the landscape of (
5.9
) as a function of
\theta
. However, this is jointly determined by the parametrization
\theta\mapsto V^{\theta}
and the properties of the aggregated dynamics, which is not affected by time discretization.
: first, the estimator has diminishing bias; second, the estimator has non-exploding variances; third, the learning rates should be suitably chosen according to the bias and variance of the estimator. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the magnitude of bias and variance and how they depend on the grid size.

In continuous time, however, due to the discretely sampled actions and the nature of the stochastic integral inside (
5.9
), the agent only has access to the sampled dynamics and uses the discrete sum (we drop the superscript
\theta
in the following to illustrate the results for a generic function

with the observed accumulated reward

R_{t_{i}}^{\mathscr{G}}=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}r(s,X^{\mathscr{G}}_{s},a_{t_{i}})\mathrm{d}s

as an approximation for the expectation in (
5.9

5.3.
Accuracy of policy gradient

The policy gradient is one of the most commonly used methods in policy optimization in RL. The well-celebrated (model-free) policy gradient representation (e.g.,
[
31
]
) gives an unbiased estimator for the gradient of the value function with respect to certain parameters in the policy. For example, the stochastic policy
\bm{\pi}^{\psi}
is parameterized by
\psi
, a finite-dimensional parameter, and it admits a density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We denote the density function by
H^{\psi}(t,x,a)=\frac{\bm{\pi}^{\psi}(\mathrm{d}a|t,x)}{\mu(\mathrm{d}a)}
. Then we know the value function under the policy
\bm{\pi}^{\psi}
(defined in (
5.8
)) will depend on
\psi
implicitly, denoted by
\tilde{J}(t,x;\bm{\pi}^{\psi})
. The policy gradient concerns about the derivative of
\tilde{J}(t,x;\bm{\pi}^{\psi})
with respect to
\psi
, i.e.,
\frac{\partial\tilde{J}(t,x;\bm{\pi}^{\psi})}{\partial\psi}

A heuristic derivation in
[
19
]
shows that

\frac{\partial\tilde{J}(\bm{\pi}^{\psi})}{\partial\psi}

can be represented by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\partial\log H^{\psi}}{\partial\psi}(a_{t}|t,X_{t})\left[\mathrm{d}\tilde{J}(t,X_{t};\bm{\pi}^{\psi})+r(t,X_{t},a_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right]\right]

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{A}\frac{\partial\log H^{\psi}}{\partial\psi}(a|t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathcal{L}^{a}\tilde{J}(t,\widetilde{X}_{t};\bm{\pi}^{\psi})\bm{\pi}(\mathrm{d}a|t,\widetilde{X}_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right].

The expectation (

6.
Conclusion

This paper addresses a fundamental problem in the continuous-time RL framework proposed in
[
36
]
by answering the relation between the aggregated dynamics (referred to as the “exploratory dynamics” therein) and the actual sampling process. In terms of SDE theory, we provide a rigorous treatment of the “law-of-large-numbers” heuristics used in
[
36
]
to demonstrate how the original dynamics lead to the aggregated dynamics. Our analysis is particularly crucial for understanding how discrete sampling relates to the continuous-time RL problems. We establish the convergence and convergence rate of discretely sampled stochastic policies, i.e., the sampled dynamics, to the aggregated dynamics in different senses. Furthermore, we illustrate the bias and variance of the estimate for several important quantities in analyzing continuous-time RL algorithms when using sampled dynamics, including values, temporal differences, policy gradients, and quadratic variations. These bias and variance analyses are critical for the convergence of stochastic approximation algorithms, and they provide guidance on properly tuning the interaction frequency to foster faster convergence of RL algorithms.

The results of this paper also stimulate more interesting open questions for future research. For example, other sampling methods that could achieve higher accuracy in the RL context, any exact simulation methods for controlled SDEs under stochastic policies if the coefficients are all known/unknown, and a full account for the convergence of the gradient-based continuous-time RL algorithm.
