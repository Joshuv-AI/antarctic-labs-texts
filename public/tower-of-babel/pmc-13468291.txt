Non-Traumatic Fat Embolism Syndrome in Sickle Cell Disease Complicated by Bone Marrow Necrosis Successfully Treated with Therapeutic Plasma Exchange: A Case Report
Authors: Lojain Abdullah Alsahman II, Bashair Abdullah Alnemer, Sarah Ahmed A Al Dawood, Mona Ali Alzaher, Ayat Hussain Ali Al-Mutawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-traumatic fat embolism syndrome (NT-FES) is a rare but frequently fatal complication of sickle cell disease (SCD) that is often associated with bone marrow necrosis. A delayed diagnosis or lack of standard criteria leads to a higher mortality rate. Firstline therapy in NT-FES is red cell exchange transfusion (RCE); however, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) is considered to be an adjunct or potential alternative in recent times.Case PresentationWe report a case of a middle-aged male with HbSS sickle cell disease and α-thalassemia trait who presented with a vaso-occlusive crisis, which led to severe multiorgan failure, including acute respiratory failure, seizures, acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy, acute hepatic injury, and cardiac ischemia. The laboratory reports showed remarkable hemolysis, hyperferritinemia and cytopenia. The clinical symptoms were consistent with NT-FES secondary to bone marrow necrosis.InterventionThe patient went through three sessions of therapeutic plasma exchange along with supportive therapy due to alloimmunization and unavailability of compatible RCE.OutcomeThe patient showed remarkable clinical improvement, which included recovery of consciousness, resolution of seizures, stabilization of hemolysis markers, and normalization of renal and hepatic parameters.ConclusionThis case demonstrates NT-FES as a critical and underrecognized complication of sickle cell disease. Immediate intervention is necessary to avoid mortality; TPE therapy presents itself as a potential life‑saving intervention in cases with immuno-incompatibility and delayed RCE.

Introduction

Fat embolism (FE) and fat embolism syndrome (FES) refer to conditions in which fat droplets enter the bloodstream and circulate throughout the body. Once these fat particles lodge within the microvasculature, they interfere with capillary flow and trigger a widespread inflammatory response.1–4 The resulting clinical features often involve respiratory compromise, neuro-cognitive system, and petechial rash.5 Although FES is most associated with orthopedic injuries, it can also arise in non-traumatic situations, including pancreatitis, bone marrow transplantation, and cosmetic procedures such as liposuction.5 Trauma-related cases represent the majority, while non-traumatic FES is uncommon and has been documented only rarely in disorders like sickle cell disease (SCD) and thalassemia.5 Interestingly, FES disproportionately affects patients with non-SS genotypes.6 Non-traumatic FES in individuals with SCD carries a high risk of serious illness and death.7 This life-threatening condition develops when bone marrow undergoes necrosis (BMN), releasing fat into the circulation, which can then trigger FES and progress to multi-organ failure.7 During vaso-occlusive crises (VOC) the damaged marrow can release larger amounts of fat that may embolize to the lungs and contribute to acute chest syndrome (ACS).8 When BMN becomes more widespread, substantial quantities of fat can enter the systemic circulation, resulting in FES and the typical pattern of multi-organ involvement.9 The harmful effects of fat emboli stem from both mechanical obstruction and biochemical injury driven by inflammatory cytokines.10,11 Diagnosis of FES remains challenging due to the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria and the heterogeneity of clinical presentations. In our case, in the absence of petechiae, the patient did not exhibit the complete classical triad of fat embolism syndrome, rendering the diagnosis more challenging. Therefore, it is crucial to detect these changes, as a prompt early exchange of red blood cells (RCE) can potentially reduce mortality.7 Moreover, recent literature evidently demonstrated that therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) also warrants more than benefits, particularly in cases with refraction or RCE therapy is not feasible.7

This report presents a case of an individual with Non-traumatic fat embolism syndrome (NT-FES) secondary to BMN with HbSS sickle cell disease and α-thalassemia trait. Also, the complications were introduced due to multiorgan failure, which was recovered through therapeutic plasma exchange. A written informed consent was obtained from the patient before presenting the case to the community.

Case Presentation

A middle‑aged Saudi male with sickle cell disease (HbSS) and thalassemia trait was presented to the emergency department with a complaint of severe generalized bone pain that was consistent with a vaso-occlusive crisis. The initial investigation revealed that the patient was receiving long-term hydroxyurea therapy. Medical history showed hypertension and seizure disorder as comorbidities that were managed by amlodipine and levetiracetam, respectively. Furthermore, patient was admitted to the ICU in the past due to bilateral hip avascular necrosis and multiple prior episodes of acute chest syndrome. Surgical history showed the patient had undergone laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

The therapy required an urgent blood transfusion, but due to multiple red-cell alloantibodies and lack of immediately compatible units, the blood transfusion was delayed. The kidney condition progressed to anuria, with persisting hyperkalemia despite medical therapy and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated. The repeated lab showed a further decline of hemoglobin level to 3.7 g/dL with an indication of continuous hemolysis.

The neurological examination showed that the patient has suffered from reduced consciousness and focal myoclonic seizures. The CT and MRI examination of the brain revealed no intracranial abnormality or no acute parenchymal lesions, respectively. Given concern for systemic inflammation with an association of the nervous system, the patient was managed with increased antimicrobial therapy and initiation of higher doses of intravenous corticosteroid. The cardiovascular assessment showed sinus bradycardia with an ischemic ECG and higher levels of cardiac biomarkers that were consistent with type 2 myocardial infarction. Moreover, the patient developed marked jaundice and acute hepatocellular injury with higher levels of hepatic markers (transaminases). The abdominal ultrasound revealed no abnormality, and a sample for viral hepatitis serology was tested positive.

In the view of these conditions, ie hemolysis, cytopenias, and multiorgan failure involving the lungs, brain, kidneys, heart, and liver, the clinical diagnosis was most consistent with bone marrow necrosis complicated by non-traumatic fat embolism syndrome in the setting of sickle cell disease.

After multidisciplinary discussion, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) was considered as rescue therapy. However, the initiation was postponed due to profound anemia, hemodynamic instability, and limited availability of plasma. The intravenous therapy with dexamethasone and immunoglobulin was given for three consecutive days. Compatible packed red blood cells were finally arranged through directed family donations, and two units were transfused.

After the first transfusion, the patient underwent three TPE sessions (Table 2) using fresh frozen plasma as the replacement fluid along with calcium supplementation. After the completion of TPE, the patient’s clinical and biochemical indicators and markers improved with a recovery of consciousness, stopping of seizures, hemolysis resolved, and renal function gradually recovered, which allowed the discontinuation of renal replacement therapy. Liver enzymes normalized, and blood counts returned to baseline.

Lastly, the hepatitis screening showed a positive hepatitis C antibody, and PCR confirmed viremia (3,450,000 IU/mL). The patient was referred to gastroenterology for antiviral management. Later, he was discharged in stable condition after an observation period, with residual mild hearing loss for which outpatient ENT follow-up was arranged.

Discussion

NT-FES is a rare, underdiagnosed, and frequently fatal complication, particularly in patients with sickle cell disease and other hemoglobinopathies.12 While FES classically occurs following traumatic long-bone fractures, NT-FES has been reported in non-traumatic settings such as pancreatitis and hematological disorders, including SCD.13–15

The pathogenesis of NT-FES in SCD is incompletely understood but is strongly linked to bone marrow necrosis. Ischemic marrow injury leads to the release of lipid globules into the circulation, which embolize to the pulmonary and systemic vasculature.12,13 Lehman’s biochemical theory, proposed in 1927, provides an explanation for both NT-FES and the delayed onset of symptoms typically observed 24–72 hours after the inciting event.16 According to this theory, neutral fat globules are hydrolyzed into toxic intermediates, including free fatty acids, which induce endothelial injury, increase vascular permeability, inhibit pulmonary surfactant, and trigger a systemic inflammatory response mediated by cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1, and interleukin-6.13–16 Hulman later suggested that elevated C-reactive protein may promote aggregation of chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins, facilitating fat globule formation and embolization.17,18 These combined mechanical and biochemical mechanisms account for the multisystem involvement characteristic of FES.

FES is primarily a clinical diagnosis, established characteristic triad of respiratory compromise, neurological abnormalities, and a petechial rash that occurs in an appropriate clinical context and when alternative etiologies have been excluded.13,15,19 The diagnosis of NT-FES/BMN was supported by the Gurd and Wilson criteria, with the patient fulfilling two major criteria (respiratory insufficiency and neurological manifestations) and two minor criteria (thrombocytopenia and anemia). Despite the absence of petechiae, the constellation of clinical, laboratory, and radiological findings strongly supported the diagnosis of fat embolism syndrome.20

The earliest case report of FEC in SCD was recorded during a post-mortem examination dated back to 1941.20,21 After that, multiple cases were recorded and reported, showing the high mortality rate associated with delayed diagnosis.11,12,14,15

A 2019 systematic review published that patients with milder forms of SCD represented the highest-risk group for developing FES. The majority of affected individuals had HbSC or HbSβ⁺ genotypes, whereas those with HbSS accounted for approximately 15% of cases.22

Untreated FES carries a mortality rate of up to 91%. Early recognition and urgent red cell exchange transfusion are considered the cornerstone of management.22,24 Despite increased use of RCE, mortality remains high, and many survivors suffer permanent neurological impairment.22

In a 2020 case series by Tsitsikas et al, seven of eight patients with non-SS variants of SCD who received RCE demonstrated favorable outcomes, with 63% achieving either near-complete or long-term complete recovery.6 It also reported a marked elevation in ferritin levels, with increases of up to 100-folds.6 In our case, the patient had HbSS disease and, upon presentation with the FES, his serum ferritin level was markedly elevated more than 4000 pmol/L (reference range 48–708 pmol/L).

Even with the increased use of RCE, the reduction of mortality by 33% remains unsatisfactory, with no change in the number of patients who regained full recovery with no severe neurological impairment. (22) An additional therapeutic measurements to be considered that could potentially add benefit as therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE).6,22 A 2023 case report described a patient with HbSS SCD and FES who was refractory to RCE but achieved complete recovery following the addition of TPE.23

TPE has emerged as a potential adjunctive therapy, particularly in refractory cases or when RCE is not feasible. Recent case series have demonstrated favorable outcomes with the addition of TPE, including complete neurological recovery.6,7 The proposed mechanism involves removal of circulating fat globules, free fatty acids, inflammatory mediators, and pro-thrombotic factors, thereby mitigating ongoing endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis.22 Although HCV infection may have contributed to the elevated liver enzyme levels, it does not fully explain the overall clinical presentation, which was more consistent with non-traumatic fat embolism syndrome (NT-FES) associated with bone marrow necrosis (BMN).

In the present case, RCE was not immediately possible due to extensive alloimmunization. Due to these circumstances, the plasma exchange was used as a primary intervention, which led to sudden sustained recovery of the patient. The current case reports therefore add to the current literature which provide evidence regarding the supporting role of TPE in the management of NT-FES in sickle cell disease. This report is inherently limited by its single-case design, precluding any inference of causality or generalizability. Furthermore, the patient received multiple concurrent therapeutic interventions, including intensive care support, corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), transfusion therapy, and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), thereby confounding the assessment of the independent therapeutic effect attributable to TPE. Additionally, the diagnosis of non-traumatic fat embolism syndrome (NT-FES) was established on clinical and radiological grounds without histopathological confirmation, introducing the possibility of diagnostic uncertainty.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this case study. Firstly, it is based on a single case and cannot be generalized to other patients. This patient was treated with several therapies while being managed, making it impossible to determine the extent to which any one intervention influenced the clinical response. Moreover, histopathological testing was not done, thus making diagnostic accuracy questionable.

Conclusion

Non-traumatic fat embolism syndrome is a rare but life-threatening complication of sickle cell disease. Therefore, it requires high level of detection, particularly in patients with mild phenotypes or non-SS genotypes who develop multiorgan failure and symptoms. Even though red cell exchange transfusion is the main therapy, therapeutic plasma exchange presents itself as a potential alternative or adjunct therapy in specific cases, such as those in which RCE is incompatible or delayed. Therefore, it is important for physicians to be more aware and ready to provide early intervention to the patient to increase survival rates and reduce neurological damage.
