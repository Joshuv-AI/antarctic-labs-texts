Abstract
. We introduce an optimal transport based approach for comparing undirected graphs with non-negative edge weights and general vertex labels, and we study connections between the resulting linear program and the graph isomorphism problem. Our approach is based on the notion of a joining of two graphs G G and H H , which is a product graph that preserves their marginal structure. Given G G and H H and a vertex-based cost function c c , the optimal graph joining (OGJ) problem finds a joining of G G and H H minimizing degree weighted cost. The OGJ problem can be written as a linear program with a convex polyhedral solution set. We establish several basic properties of the OGJ problem, and present theoretical results connecting the OGJ problem to the graph isomorphism problem. In particular, we examine a variety of conditions on graph families that are sufficient to ensure that for every pair of graphs G G and H H in the family (i) G G and H H are isomorphic if and only if their optimal joining cost is zero, and (ii) if G G and H H are isomorphic, the the extreme points of the solution set of the OGJ problem are deterministic joinings corresponding to the isomorphisms from G G to H H .



1.
Introduction

Optimal transport provides a flexible and powerful approach to comparing probability measures that has found application in a variety of disciplines.
This paper introduces an optimal transport-based framework
for comparing graphs and investigates
its application to the problem of detecting and identifying graph
isomorphisms. We focus on undirected graphs with
non-negative edge weights, including graphs that possess
vertex labels.
Our investigation is based on the notion of a graph joining,
which is analogous to the notion of a coupling in the standard optimal transport problem.
Loosely speaking, a joining of graphs
G
and
H

with vertex sets
U
and
V
is a graph
K
on
U\times V
from which

G
and
H
can be recovered via coordinate projections.
A precise definition
of graph joining is given in Section
2.2
.
As discussed in Section
2.2.1
, the definition of graph joining is
based on probabilistic ideas, specifically reversible Markovian couplings
of random walks on the given graphs
G
and
H
.

The family of joinings of two graphs
G
and
H
provides a means of
quantifying and studying their potential interactions. As in the
setting of optimal transport, one may discriminate among graph joinings
of
G
and
H
on the basis of a cost function

c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times V\to[0,\infty)
relating their vertices.
The optimal graph joining (OGJ) problem minimizes the degree-weighted average
of
c(u,v)
over all possible graph joinings of
G
and
H
, where the degree of
(u,v)
is the sum of the graph joining weights on the edges incident to
(u,v)
.
When graphs and their joinings are represented in terms of edge weight functions, the OGJ problem takes the form of a linear program whose solution set is a (non-empty) convex polytope.

While the OGJ problem is based on probabilistic principles, we show
that it has close connections with the detection and identification
of graph isomorphisms. Our analysis builds on the elementary fact that
any isomorphism
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
between graphs
G
and
H
corresponds to a
“bijective” graph joining whose degree function is supported on
pairs
(u,f(u))
, that is, the graph of
f
.
In more detail, let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of graphs together
with a scheme that assigns labels to the vertices of each graph
in
\mathcal{G}
. For
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}
, let
\rho(G,H)
be the
minimizing value of the OGJ problem for the pair
(G,H)
under the
0
-
1
label-agreement cost

c(u,v)=\mathbb{I}(\mbox{label}(u)\neq\mbox{label}(v))
.
We say that OGJ
detects
isomorphism for
\mathcal{G}
if
for all
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}
, we have
\rho(G,H)=0
if and only if
G
and
H
are isomorphic.
Assuming that OGJ detects isomorphism for
\mathcal{G}
,
we say that OGJ
identifies
isomorphism if for all isomorphic graphs
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}
,
the extreme points of the OGJ solution set are bijective graph joinings corresponding to the isomorphisms from
G

to
H
.
Identification is similar to the property of
convex exactness
, which arises in convex relaxations of the graph isomorphism problem
(see Section
5
for further discussion).

The primary theoretical results of the paper provide sufficient
conditions on graph families
\mathcal{G}
and their labeling schemes
under which OGJ detects and identifies isomorphism.
A brief summary of these results follows; more detailed
statements can be found in Section
4
.
Initially we establish that OGJ detects and identifies isomorphism
if for each graph in
\mathcal{G}
its vertex labels
are invariant under isomorphism, the labels assigned to the neighbors
of every vertex are distinct, and at least one vertex has a unique label.
We then show that positivity of the OGJ cost between two labeled graphs is
unaffected if for both graphs the label at each vertex
is replaced by the label process of the standard random walk
beginning at that vertex.
As a corollary, OGJ can detect
and identify isomorphism for families of graphs with suitable vertex landmarks.

The sufficient conditions of the detection and identification results above imply that each graph
in
\mathcal{G}
is asymmetric (i.e., has trivial automorphism group).
Asymmetry of graphs is assumed in many detection results
in the literature; see Section
5
.
We next consider structured graph families whose elements may be symmetric (i.e., have nontrivial automorphism groups).
In particular we show that OGJ can detect and identify isomorphism
for the family of weighted trees with vertex labels.
We then establish an extension principle showing that
if OGJ can detect and identify isomorphism for a family
\mathcal{G}
, then it can detect and identify isomorphisms for
families obtained by gluing together graphs in
\mathcal{G}
in an
appropriate manner.
As an application of the extension principle, we show that OGJ can detect and identify isomorphism for forests and certain graphs with loops. Finally, we show that OGJ has at least as much discriminating power as the classical Weisfeiler-Leman test.

The notion of graph joining and the optimal graph joining problem introduced here extend ideas from earlier work concerning optimal transport for Markov chains and directed graphs
[
31
,
39
]
.
Existing approaches to detection and identification of graph isomorphism
may be roughly categorized
into three groups: those employing algebraic techniques from group theory, those employing combinatorial techniques, and those based on convex relaxations of graph isomorphism (see Section
5
for further discussion of related work).
By contrast, our approach to graph isomorphism through optimal graph joinings is based on ideas from optimal transport and stochastic processes.
The OGJ approach automatically integrates local information captured
by the vertex-based cost function (the objective function of the
OGJ problem) with global information captured by the family of graph joinings
(the feasible set of the OGJ problem).
The OGJ approach is conceptually straightforward and flexible. It applies without modification to graphs with non-negative edge weights, and to graphs with vertex labels.
Our theoretical results guarantee that OGJ can detect and identify isomorphism
for a variety of graph families, including families with highly symmetric graphs,
for which existing detection and identification methods may not have theoretical guarantees.

2.
Graph joinings and the optimal graph joining problem

This section introduces graph joinings and the optimal graph joining (OGJ) problem. We begin with preliminary definitions and notation.

2.1.
Notation and graphs of interest

Throughout this paper we study finite undirected graphs whose edges may be weighted, and whose vertices may be labeled.
Graphs may contain self-loops, but multi-edges are excluded. In what follows the support of a function

f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S\to[0,\infty)
is defined by
\spp(f)=\{s\in S\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f(s)>0\}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
U
be a finite set. A function
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times U\to\mathbb{R}
is a
weight function
on
U
if it satisfies the
following three properties:

(1)

(Non-negativity)
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})\geq 0
, for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
;

(2)

(Symmetry)
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=\alpha(u^{\prime},u)
, for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
;

(3)

(Normalization)
\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=1
.

Definition 2.2
.

A weight function
\alpha
on
U
has
marginal function

p\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
p(u)=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})
.

Note that any marginal function
p
is non-negative and satisfies
\sum_{u\in U}p(u)=1
and
p(u)=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U}\alpha(u^{\prime},u)
. In particular,
p
is a probability
mass function on
U
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
U
be a finite vertex set and
\mathcal{L}
be a (possibly infinite) label set. A weighted undirected labeled graph is
defined by a triple
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
, where
\alpha
is a weight function on
U

and
\phi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathcal{L}
is referred to as a vertex label function.

Under the definition, the edges in
G
are described by ordered pairs: the pair
(u,u^{\prime})
represents an edge from
u
to
u^{\prime}
,
which is assigned weight
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})
. Symmetry of
\alpha

ensures that the weight assigned to
(u,u^{\prime})
is the same as the weight assigned to
(u^{\prime},u)
,
and in this sense the graph
G
is undirected. The edge set
E(G)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mbox{supp}(\alpha)
of
G
consists of
all edges with positive weight. For a vertex
u\in U
, the neighborhood of
u
is the set
N_{G}(u)=\{u^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})>0\}
, and any element of
N_{G}(u)
is referred to as a neighbor of
u
. The marginal function
p
of
\alpha
coincides with the weighted
degree function of
G
; in particular
p(u)
is the sum of the weights of all edges attached to the vertex
u
.
Here and throughout we say that a graph
G
is
fully supported
if its marginal function is strictly positive.
We also recall that a graph
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
is
connected
if for each pair of distinct vertices
u
and
u^{\prime}
in
U
, there exists
u_{0},\dots,u_{n}\in U
such that
u_{0}=u
,
u_{n}=u^{\prime}
, and
\alpha(u_{k},u_{k+1})>0
for each
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

The map
\phi_{G}
appearing in the specification of
G
will be called the
primary label function
of
G
.
Whereas the weight function
\alpha
specifies the connectivity between vertices, the primary label function may capture
features of the vertices.
Unlabeled graphs are a special case of labeled graphs in which the label space
\mathcal{L}
may be taken to be a singleton.
An unweighted graph
can be represented as a graph with constant edge weights.
The set
\mathcal{L}
will be referred to as the label space of

2.2.
Weight joinings and graph joinings

In this section we introduce graph joinings and state some of their basic properties.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\alpha
be a weight function on a finite set
U
with marginal
p
, and let
\beta
be a weight function on a finite set
V
with marginal
q
.
A weight function
\gamma
on
U\times V
with marginal function
r_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times V\to[0,1]
defined by

r_{\gamma}(u,v)=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U,v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

is a
weight joining
of
\alpha
and
\beta
if it satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

(Marginal coupling condition) The marginal function
r_{\gamma}
is a coupling of
p
and
q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}r_{\gamma}(u,v)

\displaystyle=p(u),\mbox{ for all $u\in U$},\;\text{and}

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}r_{\gamma}(u,v)

\displaystyle=q(v),\mbox{ for all $v\in V$}.

(2)

(Transition coupling condition) For all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
and
v,v^{\prime}\in V
,

\displaystyle p(u)\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{v}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},\tilde{v}))

\displaystyle=\alpha(u,u^{\prime})\,r_{\gamma}(u,v),\;\text{and}

\displaystyle q(v)\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{u}\in U}\gamma((u,v),(\tilde{u},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\beta(v,v^{\prime})\,r_{\gamma}(u,v).

In what follows we will denote the set of all weight joinings of
\alpha
and
\beta
by
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
.

The marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions ensure that the weight functions
\alpha
and
\beta
are, in an appropriate sense, marginals of
\gamma

(see Proposition
6.3
).
The definition of weight joining is based on reversible Markovian couplings of random walks on
G
and
H
; see Section
2.2.1
below for further discussion.
The family
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
of weight joinings is non-empty, as it contains the product weight joining
\gamma=\alpha\otimes\beta
,
defined for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
and
v,v^{\prime}\in V
by

\displaystyle\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\alpha(u,u^{\prime})\beta(v,v^{\prime}).

If
|U|=m
and
|V|=n
, then a weight joining
\gamma
can be written as an
mn\times mn
real matrix.
The following result, whose proof is in Section
6
, is straightforward.
Recall that a convex polyhedron is a subset of a Euclidean space that can be written as an intersection of a finite number of closed half-spaces. The proof of this proposition appears in Section
6
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The set
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is a nonempty, compact, convex polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
.

We now turn to the notion of graph joinings.

Definition 2.6
.

A
graph joining

Informally, a graph joining of

Graph joinings preserve edges in the sense that if

(u,u^{\prime})
is an edge in

(v,v^{\prime})

is an edge in

.

2.2.1.
Connections with Markov chains and ergodic theory

Weighted undirected graphs are intimately connected with
reversible Markov chains.
Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be weight functions on finite sets
U
and
V
with marginals
p
and
q
, respectively.
Together
\alpha
and
p
determine a unique
stationary Markov chain
X=X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
with state space
U
, transition probability matrix

\displaystyle P(u^{\prime}\mid u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}&\text{ if }p(u)>0,\\[7.22743pt]
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and stationary distribution
p
. The Markov chain
X
describes
the standard random walk on
G
, which moves from each vertex
u
to a neighboring vertex
u^{\prime}
with probability proportional
to
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})
.
The symmetry of
\alpha
ensures that the process
X
is reversible.
Let
Y=Y_{0},Y_{1},\ldots
be the reversible Markov
chain on
V
determined by
\beta
and
q
,
with transition probability matrix
Q(v^{\prime}\mid v)
.
In the same fashion, a joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
with marginal
r_{\gamma}
determines a reversible Markov chain

(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})=(\widetilde{X}_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0}),(\widetilde{X}_{1},\widetilde{Y}_{1}),\ldots
taking values in
U\times V
with transition probability matrix

\displaystyle R((u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\mid(u,v))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}&\text{ if }r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0,\\[7.22743pt]
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and stationary distribution
r_{\gamma}
.
The marginal coupling condition ensures that the stationary distribution of
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is a coupling of the stationary distributions of
X
and
Y
.
Furthermore, the transition coupling condition ensures that if

r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
, then the transition distribution

R(\cdot\mid(u,v))
is a coupling of
P(\cdot\mid u)
and

Q(\cdot\mid v)
.
In the language of Markov chains
[
25
]
,

(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is a reversible Markovian coupling of
X
and
Y
. In graph terms, the processes
X
,
Y
, and
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
are
the standard random walks on the graphs
(U,\alpha)
,
(V,\beta)
, and
(U\times V,\gamma)
, respectively (and we note that vertex labels do not play a role).

The term joining was introduced by Furstenberg
[
16
]
to refer to a stationary coupling of stationary processes. Joinings have subsequently played an important
role in ergodic theory; we refer the interested reader to

[
12
,
17
]
for more details.
While the random walk associated with a graph joining
K
of graphs
G
and
H
is a stationary coupling of the random walks on
G
and
H
, our definition of graph joining imposes additional constraints. In particular not every stationary coupling of the random walks on
G
and
H
will correspond to
a graph joining in the sense defined here (since it need not be a reversible Markovian coupling).

2.3.
Optimal graph joining

Given a vertex-based cost function, one may seek to minimize the expected value of the cost over the space of graph joinings.
Given two functions
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a finite set
A
, let
\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{x\in A}f(x)g(x)
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be graphs, and let

c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a cost function relating the
vertices of
G
and
H
.
The
optimal graph joining (OGJ) problem
for
G
and
H
is

\displaystyle\text{minimize }\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta),

where
r_{\gamma}
denotes the marginal function of
\gamma
.
Denote the minimum transport cost by

\rho(G,H)=\min_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)}\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle,

and the set of optimal solutions by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)=\argminunder_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)}\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle.

The minimum transport cost
\rho(G,H)
may be viewed as a measure of the difference between
G
and
H
with respect to the cost function
c
.
Any optimal weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
provides a transport plan from
G
to
H
that minimizes the
expected cost over all such plans.
As the objective function
\gamma\mapsto\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle
is linear, Proposition
2.5
gives the following result. The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
7
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The OGJ problem is a linear program and always has at least one solution. As such, a solution of the OGJ problem can be found in polynomial time.

As graph joinings can be interpreted in terms of random walks (as in Section
2.2.1
), the OGJ problem can be viewed as a constrained optimal transport problem for stochastic processes.
Indeed, if
X
and
Y
are the reversible Markov chains associated
with the graphs
G
and
H
, respectively, then
the OGJ problem is to minimize
\mathbb{E}c(\widetilde{X}_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0})
over
all reversible Markovian couplings
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
of

X
and
Y
. In the special case that the cost function takes the form
c(u,v)=\mathbb{I}(\psi_{G}(u)\neq\psi_{H}(v))
for some labeling functions
\psi_{G}
and
\psi_{H}
, the expected cost
\mathbb{E}c(\widetilde{X}_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0})
can be interepreted as the long-run average amount of time that the coupled chains spend in states with different labels.

We note that the OGJ problem described above differs from the problem of finding an optimal transition coupling of
directed
graphs, as studied in
[
31
,
39
]
.
Indeed, the symmetry requirement on weight joinings places nontrivial constraints on the set of feasible couplings, with a substantial impact on the associated optimization problem.

3.
Connections with graph isomorphism: Background and preliminary results

In this section we provide some background and preliminary results connecting the OGJ problem with the problems of detecting and identifying graph isomorphism.
To begin, we recall the definition of isomorphism for labeled graphs.

Definition 3.1
.

Labeled graphs
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
are
isomorphic
,
written
G\cong H
, if there is function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
, called a graph isomorphism, such that

(1)

f
is bijective,

(2)

for each pair
(u,u^{\prime})\in U\times U
, we have
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=\beta(f(u),f(u^{\prime}))
, and

(3)

for each
u\in U
, we have
\phi_{G}(u)=\phi_{H}(f(u))
.

In words,
G
and
H
are isomorphic if there is a bijective correspondence between their vertices that preserves
both edge weights and vertex labels.

3.1.
Labeling schemes

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of labeled graphs. A
labeling scheme
for
\mathcal{G}
is an indexed family of functions

\Upsilon=\{\eta_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}G\in\mathcal{G}\}
such that
\eta_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathcal{A}
, where
U
is the vertex set of
G
,
and
\mathcal{A}
is a label set that is the same for every
G
.
The
primary labeling scheme
of
\mathcal{G}
is the family
\Phi=\{\phi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}G\in\mathcal{G}\}

of primary label functions associated with graphs in
\mathcal{G}
, with label space
\mathcal{L}
. In studying connections between optimal graph joinings and graph isomorphisms, we will also allow a secondary label
function
\phi_{G}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
for each graph

G\in\mathcal{G}
, giving rise to a secondary labeling scheme

\Phi^{\prime}=\{\phi_{G}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}G\in\mathcal{G}\}
with label space
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
In general,
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
may be different from
\mathcal{L}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of graphs.
Below are some examples of labeling schemes on
\mathcal{G}
.

(1)

(Identity labeling scheme) Suppose that for each
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})\in\mathcal{G}
, the vertex set
U

\mathbb{N}
. Then the restriction of the identity function to

\Id|_{U}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathbb{N}

identity labeling scheme
.

(2)

(Discrete degree labeling scheme) For a graph
G
with vertex set
U
, for each
u\in U
, let
\deg(u)
denote the discrete degree of
u
, i.e., the number of edges incident to
u
. For each
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})\in\mathcal{G}
, define a label function
\phi_{G}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to\mathbb{N}
by setting
\phi_{G}^{\prime}(u)=\deg(u)

3.2.
Detection and identification of graph isomorphisms

Here we define what is meant by detection and identification of isomorphism for a graph family
\mathcal{G}

with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
. We begin by describing the cost function induced by the augmented labeling scheme.
For graphs
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
in
\mathcal{G}
with augmented label functions
\psi_{G}
and
\psi_{H}
,
we define the
label-based binary cost function
by

c_{\Psi}(u,v)=\mathbb{I}(\psi_{G}(u)\neq\psi_{H}(v))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{ if }\,\psi_{G}(u)\neq\psi_{H}(v)\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

In particular,
c_{\Psi}(u,v)=0
if and only if

u
and
v
have identical primary and secondary labels.
Let
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)
denote the OGJ transport cost between
G
and
H
with cost function
c_{\Psi}
,
and let
\mathcal{J}_{\Psi}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
be the associated family of optimal weight joinings. The proof of this proposition can be found in Section
9
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph family with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
. If
\Psi
is
isomorphism-invariant, then for any
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}
,

G\cong H\Longrightarrow\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0.

In general, the reverse implication does not hold.
For example, consider two unlabeled, unweighted graphs that are
not isomorphic but have the property that the vertices of both
graphs all have the same
discrete degree. If the discrete degree labeling function is used as the secondary labeling scheme, then
the cost function
c_{\Psi}
will be identically zero, and the
OGJ transport cost will also be zero, despite the fact that the graphs are not isomorphic.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph family with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
. We say that OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}

detects isomorphisms
for
\mathcal{G}
if for all
G,H\in\mathcal{G}
,

\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\iff\hskip 10.00002ptG\cong H.

Our study of graph isomorphism begins with the study of bijective weight joinings.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be weight functions on
U
and
V
respectively.
A joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
with marginal
r_{\gamma}
is
bijective
if (i) for
each
u\in U
, there is a unique
v\in V
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
,
and (ii) for each
v\in V
, there is a unique
u\in U
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
.

If
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is bijective, then one may define a bijection
f_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
by letting
f_{\gamma}(u)

be the unique
v\in V
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
.
We refer to
f_{\gamma}
as the map induced by
\gamma
. Note that
f_{\gamma}
preserves the weights of all edges.
Furthermore, if
\gamma
has transport cost
\sum c_{\Phi}(u,v)r_{\gamma}(u,v)=0
,
then
f_{\gamma}
necessarily also preserves the labels of all nodes. This is summarized in the following proposition. The proof of this proposition appears in Section
8
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be two fully supported graphs.
If
\rho_{\Phi}(G,H)=0
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Phi}(\alpha,\beta)
is a bijective weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
, then the induced map
f_{\gamma}
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
H
.

Conversely, if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a graph isomorphism from
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
to
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
,
then there is a unique bijective weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
f=f_{\gamma}
, and
\gamma
has zero cost under
c_{\Phi}
; see Section
8
.
Thus zero-cost bijective weight joinings are in one-to-one correspondence with graph isomorphisms.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph family with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
. We say that OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}

identifies isomorphism
for
\mathcal{G}
if for every pair of isomorphic graphs
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
in
\mathcal{G}
, the extreme points of the OGJ solution set
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi}(\alpha,\beta)
coincide with the zero-cost bijective weight joinings of
\alpha
and
\beta
.

This property is closely related to the notion of
convex exactness
in the study of convex relations of the graph isomorphism problem
[
14
]
; see Section
5
for further discussion.

Connections with graph isomorphism: Main results

In this section we present our main results connecting the OGJ problem and the problems of detecting and identifying graph isomorphisms. The proofs of all results in this section appear in Section

.

4.1.
Detection and identification with informative labeling schemes

We say that a labeling scheme
\Psi=\{\psi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}G\in\mathcal{G}\}
is
injective
if for every
G\in\mathcal{G}
the label function
\psi_{G}
is injective, that is,
\psi_{G}
distinguishes the vertices of
G
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of connected graphs with an isomorphism-invariant augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
. If
\Psi
is injective, then OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}
detects and identifies isomorphism for
\mathcal{G}
.

We next investigate weaker conditions on
\Psi
under which the same conclusions hold.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
G
be graph with vertex set
U
and an augmented label function
\psi_{G}
.

(1)

\psi_{G}
is
locally injective
if for each
u\in U
, the restriction of
\psi_{G}
to the neighborhood
N_{G}(u)
is injective.

(2)

\psi_{G}
has a
magic symbol
if there is a vertex
u\in U
with a unique label not shared by any other
vertex, that is,
|\psi_{G}^{-1}(\psi_{G}(u))|=1
. In this case,
\psi_{G}(u)
is called a
magic symbol
.

An augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
is locally injective for a family
\mathcal{G}
if
\psi_{G}
is locally injective for every

G\in\mathcal{G}
. An augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
has magic
symbols if
\psi_{G}
has a magic symbol for every

G\in\mathcal{G}
.

We note that the term
magic symbol
appears in a related context in the field of symbolic dynamics; see
[
26
]
.
Now we are ready to state our next result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of connected graphs with an isomorphism-invariant augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
.
If
\Psi
is locally injective and has magic symbols, then OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}

detects and identifies isomorphism for
\mathcal{G}
.

4.2.
Results with process-level labeling schemes

The results of the previous section rely on the power of the augmented labeling scheme to discriminate between vertices in some fashion.
In this section we change our perspective and consider the OGJ problem from the point of view of random walks on graphs and Markovian couplings (as in Seccion
2.2.1
).
Using this perspective, we show that OGJ inherently takes into account information about the random walks at all times, despite the fact that the OGJ problem only explicitly refers to an expectation at time
0
. Combining the results of this section and the previous section, we obtain that OGJ can detect and identify isomorphisms for graphs with landmarks.

We begin with some definitions.
Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
be a graph with marginal function
p
and any
label function
\psi_{G}
.
Let
P(u^{\prime}|u)=\alpha(u,u^{\prime})/p(u)

(see Section

).
For each vertex

. Finally, let

be the function that

\psi^{*}_{G}(u)\ =\ \text{Law}(\psi_{G}(X^{u}_{0}),\psi_{G}(X^{u}_{1}),\dots).

In other words,

is the distribution of the process
of labels of

If
\Psi
is a labeling scheme for

, then we let

It is clear that the process-level scheme
\Psi^{*}
contains
at least as much information as the original scheme

,
as the time-zero label of

is equal to

Corollary 4.7

Let
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}

4.3.
Isomorphism detection and identification for trees

Recall that an automorphism of a graph
G
is an isomorphism from
G
to itself. Furthermore, the set of automorphisms together with the composition operation form the automorphism group of
G
. A graph is said to be asymmetric if its automorphism group is trivial, i.e., the group contains only one element (the identity automorphism). When the automorphism group is nontrivial, the graph is said to be symmetric. Asymmetry is a common assumption in existing work on isomorphism detection and identification; see Section
5
for details.
In this vein, we note that the hypotheses of Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.3
imply that the graphs under consideration are asymmetric. However, our optimal transport-based approach is flexible enough to address certain families of symmetric graphs. As a first example, we consider the family of weighted trees.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the family of finite, labeled, weighted trees with at least two
vertices. Let
\Psi
be the augmented labeling scheme obtained from the primary
labels in conjunction with the multiweight labeling. Then OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}
detects and identifies isomorphism for
\mathcal{G}
.

We note that there are several algorithms that have been shown to detect isomorphism among the family of (unweighted) trees, including the classical Weisfeiler-Leman test
[
22
]
and the AHU algorithm
[
2
]
, which can be modified to handle the weighted case. However, we are not aware of prior work addressing the identification of isomorphism (in the sense of Definition
3.10
) or convex exactness for the family of trees.

The proof of Theorem
4.8
, which appears in Section
9
, proceeds by induction on the size of the trees involved. In the crucial inductive step, we show that if
G
and
H
are trees with
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0
, then any optimal graph joining must transport the leaves of
G
to those of
H
(and vice versa). Then we argue that we can remove the leaves from both graphs and apply our induction hypothesis to the resulting smaller trees and get an isomorphism of these subgraphs. Finally, we rely on Birkhoff’s Theorem
[
6
]
to extend the isomorphism of the subgraphs to an isomorphism of the leaves.

4.4.
An extension principle via magic decompositions

Suppose that OGJ detects and identifies isomorphism for a graph family
\mathcal{G}
.
Here we establish an extension principle showing that if the graphs in a family
\mathcal{F}
can be created by “gluing together” graphs from
\mathcal{G}
in an
appropriate fashion, then OGJ detects and identifies isomorphism for
\mathcal{F}
(see Theorem
4.9
below). Prior to making this statement, we require several technical definitions that give a precise description of how the graphs in
\mathcal{G}
may be glued together. The main idea is that one may take a disjoint union of graphs in
\mathcal{G}
and then glue some of their leaves together to create a new graph in
\mathcal{F}
. However, the leaves cannot be glued together arbitrarily, as any graph could be obtained by gluing together finitely many graphs with only one edge. Thus, we also require that the labels of the new graph in
\mathcal{F}
contain enough information that one may recover the graphs in
\mathcal{G}
and their gluing data. Let us now state these conditions precisely.

We call a vertex
u
of a graph
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
a
leaf
if there is exactly one vertex
u^{\prime}\in U
such that
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})>0
, and moreover
u^{\prime}\neq u
. In this case we refer to
u^{\prime}
as the
base
of the leaf
u
and denote it by
b(u)
. Let
L(G)
denote the leaves of
G
.

Now let
G_{i}=(U_{i},\alpha_{i},\phi_{G_{i}})
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, be a finite collection of connected graphs and let
M
be a finite set. Let us first describe a gluing of the graphs
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
along the set
M
.
We suppose that
M
and
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are all pairwise disjoint.
Further, suppose that for each
i=1,\ldots,k
we have identified a (possibly empty) subset
L_{i}\subset L(G_{i})
of the leaves of
G_{i}
such that
U_{i}\setminus L_{i}\neq\varnothing
, and additionally we have a map
f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}L_{i}\to M
such that if
u\neq v\in L_{i}
and
b(u)=b(v)
, then
f_{i}(u)\neq f_{i}(v)
; that is,
distinct leaves with the same base must map to distinct elements of
M
.
Let
U^{\prime}_{i}=U_{i}\setminus L_{i}
(which we assume is nonempty), and let
U=M\cup U^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup U^{\prime}_{k}
.
The set
U
will serve as the vertex set of the new graph.
For each
i=1,\ldots,k
define the modified weight function
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times U\to[0,\infty)
by setting

\alpha^{\prime}_{i}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{i}(u,v),&\text{ if }u,v\in U^{\prime}_{i}\\
\alpha_{i}(u,v^{\prime}),&\text{ if }u=b(v^{\prime})\text{ and }f_{i}(v^{\prime})=v\\
\alpha_{i}(u^{\prime},v),&\text{ if }v=b(u^{\prime})\text{ and }f_{i}(u^{\prime})=u\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
is well-defined by our restriction on the map
f_{i}
. Informally, the weight function
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
is just the extension of
\alpha_{i}
to the larger vertex set
U
, where we have replaced each leaf
u^{\prime}
in
L_{i}
with its image
f_{i}(u^{\prime})
in
M
.

A graph
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
is a
gluing
of the graphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
along the set
M
if the following conditions hold.

(1)

There are sets
L_{i}\subset L(G_{i})
and functions
f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}L_{i}\to M
satisfying the assumptions given above.

(2)

The vertex set of
G
is
U=M\cup U^{\prime}_{1}\cup\dots\cup U^{\prime}_{k}
.

(3)

There are constants
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}>0
and a function
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times U\to[0,\infty)
such that
\spp(\tau)\subset M\times M
and

\alpha=\tau+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
.

In short, to construct the gluing
G
, we take a weighted disjoint union of
G_{1},\dots,G_{k}
, replace the leaves
u^{\prime}
in
L_{i}
by their images
f_{i}(u^{\prime})
in
M
(which may amount to gluing some of these leaves together), and then we may add in edges between pairs of vertices in
M
(with weights given by
\tau
). See Figure

for an example. We note that if any
L_{i}
is empty, then the resulting gluing is disconnected.

Here we describe the specific conditions that the label functions must satisfy in order for our extension theorem to apply. These conditions guarantee that the labels associated with a graph

(1)

for all

\eta^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}}\times\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}}\to\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{G}}

5.
Related Work

Optimal transport (OT) provides a mathematical framework for comparing probability distributions.
Driven in part by advances in computing, ideas and techniques from OT have found use in a variety of fields, including
computer vision
[
28
,
20
]
, oceanography
[
21
]
, and cell biology
[
36
,
10
,
9
]
.
Recently, a number of authors have explored OT-based methods for comparing graphs
[
11
,
13
,
29
,
30
,
38
,
39
]
.
One branch of work in this direction makes use of the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distance and extensions for the purpose of graph comparison and alignment
[
30
,
33
,
38
]
. These distances treat the graphs as metric spaces and aim to couple distributions over graph vertices so as to minimize the metric distortion.
The paper
[
34
]
establishes limit laws for the empirical GW distances across various settings and applies them to a problem involving hypothesis testing for isomorphisms in the context of random graph models.

Another branch of related work uses OT in the context of Markov chains to compare and align graphs based on their dynamics
[
8
,
11
,
31
,
39
]
.
As described above in Section
2.2.1
, a graph
G
with positive edge weights is naturally associated with a random walk on its vertices.
For directed graphs the resulting Markov chain may not be reversible, in contrast to the situation studied here.
In
[
8
,
11
]
the authors develop graph distance measures related to the classical Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) isomorphism test
[
18
]
.
These distance measures are based on consideration of the OT problem for the
k
-step distributions of the Markov chains on the given graphs;
connections with the present work are discussed in Section
5.1
below.
In
[
31
,
39
]
the authors propose and develop a method
called NetOTC for comparing and aligning graphs by computing an optimal transition coupling of their random walks using a vertex-based cost function. Like OGJ, NetOTC is based on graph couplings derived from random walks. However,
NetOTC considers general directed graphs, i.e., no symmetry constraints
are placed on the weight functions of the given graphs or their
transition couplings. Absent symmetry constraints, the
NetOTC problem is not a linear program, and general connections
between NetOTC and graph isomorphism are not known at the present time.

The graph isomorphism (GI) problem is to determine whether two given
graphs are isomorphic, which in this work corresponds to the
detection
problem. It is not known whether the GI problem is NP-complete or solvable in polynomial time; the best existing results establish
that the GI problem can be solved in quasipolynomial time
[
4
]
.
While much of the recent work on the GI problem is algebraic, involving the automorphism group of the graphs in question
[
4
,
18
]
,
there is a substantial body of work that is more combinatorial in nature, with the AHU algorithm
[
2
]
and the WL test (also known as the color refinement algorithm) being classical examples.
The WL test is known to detect isomorphisms for certain families of graphs, including trees and forests
[
22
]
, while the AHU algorithm applies only to trees and forests
[
2
,
27
]
. For detailed surveys on combinatorial approaches to the GI problem,
see
[
18
,
19
]
.

The GI problem can be formulated as a combinatorial optimization problem
involving the adjacency matrices
A
and
B
of the given graphs.
The goal of the optimization problem is to minimize a cost, often the Frobenius norm, between
PA
and
BP
over all permutation matrices
P
.
A common approach to the GI problem is to formulate and study convex
relaxations in which the permutation matrices
P
are replaced with doubly stochastic matrices or
Markov kernels (as in
[
32
]
).
Recent work
[
1
,
14
,
15
]

provides theoretical guarantees establishing
sufficient conditions under which the relaxed isomorphism problem can detect and identify graph isomorphism for certain graph families.
As these results are similar in spirit to the identification results presented here, we describe them in further detail below.

In
[
1
]
the authors show that the relaxed GI problem
provides a method for detection and identification of isomorphism for the class of ‘friendly graphs.’
A graph is friendly if its adjacency matrix has distinct eigenvalues and none of its eigenvectors
is orthogonal to the all-ones vector. Note that friendly graphs are
necessarily asymmetric (meaning that they have trivial automorphism groups).
Subsequent work has extended these results to some non-friendly yet still asymmetric graphs
[
15
]
.
Other work based on convex relaxation
establishes identification results for families that may contain
symmetric graphs
[
14
]
.
Given two isomorphic graphs, the relaxed problem is said to be
convex exact

if the set of optimal solutions is equal to the convex hull of the permutation matrices corresponding to the common automorphism group of the graphs.
Given a fixed set of vertices
U
and a fixed group
\Gamma
of permutations of
U
,
[
14
]
considers the set
\mathcal{W}(U,\Gamma)
of adjacency matrices of weighted undirected graphs with vertex set
U
and
automorphism group
\Gamma
.
They establish that if all elements of
\Gamma
are involutions and the action of
\Gamma
on
U
has an orbit of size
|\Gamma|
, then convex exactness holds for almost every adjacency matrix in
\mathcal{W}(U,\Gamma)
(with respect to a modified version of Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{W}(U,\Gamma)
).

In more recent work
[
24
]
, the authors study the set of solutions to the relaxed problem considered in
[
1
,
15
,
14
]
and show in particular that if the adjacency matrices have repeated eigenvalues, then the dimension of the solution space increases, thereby complicating graph isomorphism testing.
In
[
35
]
, rather than using the Frobenius norm for the cost function as in
[
1
,
15
,
14
]
, the authors consider the problem of minimizing a linear cost over the set of doubly stochastic matrices
P
satisfying
PA=BP
. It is shown that the resulting program can detect and uniquely identify isomorphism for friendly graphs.

5.1.
Relation with Weisfeiler-Lehman and NetOTC

In this section we discuss connections between OGJ,
the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) test, the WL distance,
and NetOTC. The proofs of the results in this section can be found in Section
9
.

The classical WL test (see
[
18
]
)
is an algorithm that takes two graphs as input and returns one of two possible outputs: either the graphs are not isomorphic, or the algorithm cannot determine whether the graphs are isomorphic. The more recent WL distance
[
8
,
11
]
is a (generally non-metric) distance function

d_{\WL}(G,H)
on directed
graphs that is defined via optimal non-stationary Markovian couplings
of the random walks on
G
and
H
using a vertex-based cost.
The NetOTC method of
[
39
]
gives rise to a (generally non-metric)
distance function
d_{\OTC}(G,H)
on directed graphs via optimal stationary Markovian
couplings of the random walks on
G
and
H
using a vertex-based cost.
It is shown in
[
8
]
that
the WL distance is upper bounded by the NetOTC distance. Since reversible Markovian couplings are necessarily stationary, it is straightforward
to see that the NetOTC distance is upper bounded by the OGJ cost.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be undirected graphs. For any cost function
c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\WL}(G,H)\leq d_{\OTC}(G,H)\leq\rho_{c}(G,H).

As the name suggests, the WL distance is closely related to the WL test.
Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi)
be an unweighted labeled graph, and let
\phi^{\prime}(u)=(\deg(u),|U|)
define a secondary labeling.
Fix
\delta\in(1/2,1)
, and let
\hat{G}_{\delta}
be the weighted graph corresponding to the
\delta
-lazy random walk on
G
with augmented label function
\psi=(\phi,\phi^{\prime})
.
It is shown in
[
11
, Proposition 3.3]
that the WL test distinguishes unweighted labeled graphs
G
and
H
(i.e., determines correctly that they are not isomorphic) if and only if the WL distance between
\hat{G}_{\delta}
and
\hat{H}_{\delta}
with the label-based cost function is positive.
In this sense, the WL distance has the same discriminating power as the WL test.
Combining this fact with Proposition
5.1
, we obtain a similar result for OGJ.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
G
and
H
be unweighted labeled graphs. If the WL test determines that
G
and
H
are not isomorphic, then
\rho_{\Psi}(\hat{G}_{\delta},\hat{H}_{\delta})>0
.

In this sense, OGJ has at least as much discriminating power as the WL
test when restricted to the family of unweighted labeled graphs.
The WL test can distinguish a random unweighted, unlabeled,
and undirected graph on
n
vertices from every other such graph on
n

vertices with probability tending to one as
n
tends to infinity
[
3
]
. Thus the same conclusion holds for OGJ.

6.
Properties of weight joinings and graph joinings

In this section, we describe the basic theoretical properties of weight joinings and graph joinings, beginning with properties of the set of weight joinings and some consequences of the marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions. In Section
6.3
, we show that any weight joining of disconnected graphs can be decomposed into connected components corresponding to weight joinings of pairs of the connected components of the marginal graphs. Then in Section
6.4
we characterize the extreme points of weight joinings in terms of their support sets.

6.1.
The set of weight joinings

Here we describe some basic properties of the set of weight joinings
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
for a given pair of weight functions
\alpha
and
\beta
. Recall that a convex polyhedron is a subset of a Euclidean space that can be written as an intersection of a finite number of closed half-spaces. In the following result, suppose
\alpha
and
\beta
are weight functions on
U
and
V
, respectively, where
|U|=m
and
|V|=n
. We fix enumerations
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
and
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and view each weight joining
\gamma
as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
.
See
2.5

Proof.

Let
\gamma^{*}
be the product weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
, i.e.,
\gamma^{*}=\alpha\otimes\beta
. It is straightforward to verify that
\gamma^{*}
is a weight function that satisfies both the marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions. Hence,
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is nonempty. Because
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is characterized by finitely many linear constraints over
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
, it is a convex polyhedron and is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
. Finally, for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, the non-negativity and normalization conditions for weight functions imply that
\gamma
is a probability vector with index set
(U\times V)^{2}
, and therefore
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is bounded.
∎

6.2.
The marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions

To simplify notation, we use
G=(U,\alpha)
to denote graphs when we do not need to refer to the label functions. The following proposition states that for connected graphs, the marginal coupling condition in the definition of weight joining is redundant.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
are two connected graphs. Then any weight function
\gamma
on
U\times V
that satisfies the transition coupling condition also satisfies the marginal coupling condition.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a weight function that satisfies the transition coupling condition. Let
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
be its associated reversible Markov chain. Let
X
and
Y
be the reversible Markov chains associated with
G
and
H
, respectively. When
G
and
H
are connected, the reversible Markov chains
X
and
Y
are irreducible. As
\gamma
satisfies the transition coupling condition,
[
31
, Proposition 4]
gives that
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is a coupling of
X
and
Y
. By considering the first marginals of these chains, we then see that
r_{\gamma}
is a coupling of
p
and
q
, and hence the marginal coupling condition is satisfied.
∎

If two graphs are fully supported, then the following restatement of the transition coupling condition is useful.

Remark 6.2
.

Suppose
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
are fully supported. Then
p(u)>0
for all
u\in U
and
q(v)>0
for all
v\in V
, and therefore the transition coupling conditions are equivalent to the following equations: for any
u,u^{\prime}\in U
and
v,v^{\prime}\in V
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{v}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},\tilde{v}))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}\,r_{\gamma}(u,v),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{u}\in U}\gamma((u,v),(\tilde{u},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\beta(v,v^{\prime})}{q(v)}\,r_{\gamma}(u,v).

The marginal coupling condition and the transition coupling condition together imply an
edge coupling condition
, described in the following proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be weight functions defined on
U
and
V
, respectively. Suppose
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. Then we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,{v}),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\alpha(u,u^{\prime}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }u,u^{\prime}\in U,

(6.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{{u},{u^{\prime}}\in U}\gamma(({u},v),({u^{\prime}},v^{\prime}))=\beta(v,v^{\prime}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }v,v^{\prime}\in V.

Consequently, if
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
, then
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})>0
and
\beta(v,v^{\prime})>0
. We call this the edge preservation property.

Proof.

First suppose that
p(u)=0
. Then by definition of
p
we must have
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=0
for all
u^{\prime}\in U
, and by the marginal coupling condition, we have
r_{\gamma}(u,v)=0
for all
v\in V
. Thus both sides of (
6.1
) are zero. Now suppose
p(u)>0
. Then the transition coupling condition can be rewritten as in Remark
6.2
. Hence summing over
v\in V
and using the maringal coupling condition gives

\displaystyle\sum_{{v},{v^{\prime}}\in V}\gamma((u,{v}),(u^{\prime},{v^{\prime}}))=\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}\sum_{{v}\in V}r_{\gamma}(u,{v})=\alpha(u,u^{\prime}).

We have established Equation (
6.1
). Equation (
6.2
) is obtained analogously.
∎

6.3.
Decomposition of weight joinings over disjoint unions

We now consider disconnected graphs and study the structure of their weight joinings. Any disconnected graph can be expressed as a disjoint union of its connected components. Let us introduce some additional notation to describe disjoint unions of graphs. We assume that all graphs in this section are fully supported. We let
G=(U,\alpha)
denote the graph
G
with vertex set
U
and weight function
\alpha
when vertex labels are not taken into account. Suppose a graph
G=(U,\alpha)
has
k
connected components, and denote the vertex sets of these connected components by
U_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. For each component, we define a weighted undirected graph
G_{i}=(U_{i},\alpha_{i})
as follows. Let
S_{G_{i}}=\sum_{u\in U_{i}}p(u)
. For all
u,u^{\prime}\in U_{i}
, define
{\alpha}_{i}(u,u^{\prime})=(S_{G_{i}})^{-1}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})
. Since
U_{i}
is a connected component of
G
, it is straightforward to verify that
\alpha_{i}
is a weight function on
U_{i}
. We therefore write
G_{i}=(U_{i},\alpha_{i})
, and note that
G_{i}
is a connected graph. Furthermore, observe that
\nu_{G}=(S_{{G}_{1}},\ldots,S_{{G}_{k}})
is a probability distribution on the set
\{1,\dots,k\}
. Now, if
G
has
k
connected components, then we write
G=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
.

Suppose we have two disconnected graphs
G=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
and
H=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{l}H_{j}
, where
G_{i}=(U_{i},{\alpha}_{i})
and
H_{j}=(V_{j},{\beta}_{j})
are the connected component graphs as described above. For
G_{i}=(U_{i},{\alpha}_{i})
, we use
p_{i}
to denote its marginal function, defined as
p_{i}(u)=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{i}}{\alpha}_{i}(u,u^{\prime})
, for
u\in U_{i}
.
Similarly, for
H_{j}=(V_{j},{\beta}_{j})
, we let
q_{j}
denote its marginal function.

The following proposition provides a decomposition of any weight joining of a pair of disconnected graphs. In particular, it states that for two disconnected graphs
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
, we can express a weight joining
\gamma
of
\alpha
and
\beta
as a linear combination of weight joinings of pairs of connected components. Moreover, the coefficients of this linear combination form a coupling
\pi
of the weight distributions of the connected components of
G
and
H
, denoted by
\nu_{G}
and
\nu_{H}
(as above).

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
be two fully supported graphs that can be disconnected. Suppose
G=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
and
H=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{l}H_{j}
, with notation as above.
Then for any weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, there is a coupling
\pi
of
\nu_{G}
and
\nu_{H}
such that

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\pi_{ij}\cdot\gamma_{ij},

where each
\gamma_{ij}
is a weight function on
U\times V
such that
\spp(\gamma_{ij})\subset(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}
and
\gamma_{ij}|_{(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}}
is a weight joining of
{\alpha}_{i}
and
{\beta}_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
. For
i=1,\ldots,k
and
j=1,\ldots,l
, let

\displaystyle\pi_{ij}=\sum_{(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{i}\times V_{j}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})).

If
\pi_{ij}=0
, then let

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\begin{cases}\alpha_{i}(u,u^{\prime})\beta_{j}(v,v^{\prime})&\text{if }(u,v),\ (u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{i}\times V_{j},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Hence
\gamma_{ij}
is a weight function and
\gamma_{ij}|_{(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}}=\alpha_{i}\otimes\beta_{j}
, which is a weight joining of
{\alpha}_{i}
and
{\beta}_{j}
.
Now suppose
\pi_{ij}>0
.
For all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
and
v,v^{\prime}\in V
, let

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\begin{cases}(\pi_{ij})^{-1}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))&\text{if }(u,v),\ (u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{i}\times V_{j},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The non-negativity
\gamma_{ij}
is clear, and the normalization property follows immediately from the definition of the scaling factor
(\pi_{ij})^{-1}
. The symmetry of
\gamma_{ij}
is inherited from
\gamma
. Hence
\gamma_{ij}
is a weight function. It is also clear from the definition that
\spp(\gamma_{ij})\subset(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}
. Let us now verify that
\gamma_{ij}|_{(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}}
is a weight joining of
{\alpha}_{i}
and
{\beta}_{j}
. Since
G_{i}
and
H_{j}
are connected, it remains only to show that
\gamma_{ij}
satisfies the transition coupling conditions (by Proposition
6.1
). To this end, we first observe that for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U_{i}
, we have
p(u)>0
and
S_{G_{i}}>0
since
G
is fully supported, and then

(6.3)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}=\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})/S_{G_{i}}}{p(u)/S_{G_{i}}}=\dfrac{\alpha_{i}(u,u^{\prime})}{p_{i}(u)}.

Let
r_{ij}
be the marginal function of
\gamma_{ij}
. For any
(u,v)\in U_{i}\times V_{j}
, the definition of
\gamma_{ij}
and the fact that
U_{i}
and
V_{j}
are connected components yields that

(6.4)

r_{ij}(u,v)=(\pi_{ij})^{-1}r_{\gamma}(u,v).

By fact that
V_{j}
is a connected component, the transition coupling condition for
\gamma
, and Equations (
6.3
) and (
6.4
), for
u,u^{\prime}\in U_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V_{j}}\gamma_{ij}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(\pi_{ij})^{-1}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=(\pi_{ij})^{-1}\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}r_{\gamma}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{{\alpha}_{i}(u,u^{\prime})}{{p}_{i}(u)}r_{ij}(u,v).

The remaining transition coupling condition relating
\gamma_{ij}
and
\beta_{j}
is established by an analogous argument.
Hence
\gamma_{ij}|_{(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}}
is a weight joining of
{\alpha}_{i}
and
{\beta}_{j}
.
By the definition of
\gamma_{ij}
for all
i
and
j
, we observe that

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\pi_{ij}\gamma_{ij}.

To prove that
\pi=(\pi_{ij})
is a coupling of
\nu_{G}
and
\nu_{H}
, note that for each
i=1,\ldots,k
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}\pi_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in U_{i}\times V_{j}\\
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{i}\times V_{j}\end{subarray}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U_{i}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=S_{G_{i}},

where the second equality is a consequence of Proposition
6.3
.
Thus
\nu_{G}
is the first marginal of
\pi
. Similarly,
\nu_{H}
is the second marginal of
\pi
, which completes the proof.
∎

We also note that if one has weight joinings for each pair of connected component graphs, then one can construct a weight joining of
G
and

.

Remark 6.5
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
6.4
, let
{\gamma}_{ij}\in\mathcal{J}({\alpha_{i}},{\beta_{j}})
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
and
j=1,\ldots,l
.
For any coupling
\pi
of
\nu_{G}
and
\nu_{H}
, define the function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times V)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting

\gamma|_{(U_{i}\times V_{j})^{2}}=\pi_{ij}\gamma_{ij}

for all

,

, and

elsewhere. Then
\gamma
is a weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
. We omit the straightforward proof of this fact.

6.4.
Extreme points of weight joinings

Recall that
\gamma
is an extreme point of the convex set
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
if the following condition holds: whenever
\gamma=t\gamma_{1}+(1-t)\gamma_{2}
for some
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
and some
t\in(0,1)
, it necessarily follows that
\gamma=\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
. In the next proposition below we establish an equivalence between the extreme points of the set of weight joinings and those weight joinings with minimal support.

Definition 6.6
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be weight functions on
U
and
V
, respectively. We say a set
F\subset(U\times V)^{2}

supports a weight joining
if there exists a weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\spp(\gamma)=F
. Given a set
F
that supports a weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we say
F
is
minimal
if no set
F^{\prime}
supporting a weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
is a proper subset of
F
.

The following proposition is essentially a restatement of standard results in linear optimization applied to our setting. Nonetheless, we provide a proof for completeness.

Proposition 6.7
.

A weight joining
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
if and only if
\spp(\gamma)
is minimal. Moreover, if
\gamma
is extremal and
\spp(\gamma)=\spp(\gamma^{\prime})
for some
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, then
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
.

Before proving Proposition
6.7
, we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be weight functions. Let
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
with
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subset\spp(\gamma)
. Then there exists a sufficiently small
t>0
such that
\tilde{\gamma}=(1+t)\gamma-t\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle t=\min_{((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\in\spp(\gamma^{\prime})}\dfrac{\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}{\gamma^{\prime}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}.

Since
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subset\spp(\gamma)
, we have that
t
is well-defined and positive. If
((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\in\spp(\gamma^{\prime})
, then by the minimality of
t
, we have

\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))-t\gamma^{\prime}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\geq 0.

If
((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\notin\spp(\gamma^{\prime})
, then

\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))-t\gamma^{\prime}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\geq 0.

Thus
\tilde{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(1+t)\gamma-t\gamma^{\prime}=(\gamma-t\gamma^{\prime})+t\gamma\geq 0
. Since the rest of the weight joining constraints (in Definition
2.4
) are linear and
\tilde{\gamma}
is a linear combination of the weight joinings
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, it is straightforward to verify that
\tilde{\gamma}
is a weight joining.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.7
.

Let
\gamma
be an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. Suppose for contradiction that
\spp(\gamma)
is not minimal. Then there exists
{\gamma^{\prime}}
such that
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subsetneq\spp(\gamma)
. Note that
\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma
.
Then by Lemma
6.8
, for a sufficiently small
t>0
, we have that
\tilde{\gamma}=(1+t)\gamma-t\gamma^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. Consequently,
\gamma
can be expressed as
\gamma=\frac{1}{1+t}\tilde{\gamma}+\frac{t}{1+t}\gamma^{\prime},
which contradicts the assumption that
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
(since
\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma)
. Note that by this same argument, if
\spp(\gamma)=\spp(\gamma^{\prime})
, then we must have
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
, which establishes the second conclusion of the proposition.

Now suppose
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is a weight joining such that
\spp(\gamma)
is minimal.
Since
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is a compact, convex polytope, it has finitely many extreme points. By the Krein-Milman theorem,
there exist extreme points
\gamma^{(1)},\dots,\gamma^{(k)}
of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}>0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=1
such that

\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\gamma^{(i)}.

By the non-negativity of each
\gamma^{(j)}
, the support of each
\gamma^{(j)}
must be contained in that of
\gamma
. Specifically, for any
((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\in\spp(\gamma^{(j)})
, it follows that
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
, and hence

\spp(\gamma^{(j)})\subseteq\spp(\gamma)\text{ for all }j=1,\cdots,k.

Then by the minimality of
\spp(\gamma)
, we must have
\spp(\gamma^{(j)})=\spp(\gamma)
for all
j
.

Now suppose for contradiction that
\gamma^{(j)}\neq\gamma
for some
j
. By Lemma
6.8
, there exists a sufficiently small
\theta>0
such that
\gamma^{*}=(1+\theta)\gamma^{(j)}-\theta\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
.
Then
\gamma^{(j)}
can be written as a nontrivial convex combination
\gamma^{(j)}=\frac{1}{1+\theta}\gamma^{*}+\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}\gamma
, with
\gamma\neq\gamma^{(j)}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\gamma^{(j)}
is an extreme point. Hence,
\gamma=\gamma^{(i)}
for some
i
, and we conclude that
\gamma
is an extreme point.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, our next result states that an extremal graph joining of connected graphs must also be connected. Note that we only consider the part of the graph joining where each vertex has positive marginal degree.

Proposition 6.9
.

If
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
are connected graphs and
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is extremal, then the graph
K=(\spp(r_{\gamma}),\gamma)
is also connected.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that the graph
K=(\spp(r_{\gamma}),\gamma)
is not connected.
Then there exists two nonempty subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subset U\times V
such that

S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset
,
S_{1}\cup S_{2}=U\times V
,
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=0
for all
(u,v)\in S_{1},(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{2}
, and there exist pairs
(u,v)\in S_{1}
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{2}
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
and
r_{\gamma}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})>0
. Define the normalized restriction of
\gamma
to
S_{1}
by

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\begin{cases}(C^{-1})\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))&\text{if }(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{1},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where the normalization constant is given by

\displaystyle C=\sum_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S_{1}}\gamma((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))>0.

We now verify that
\gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. For
(u,v)\in S_{1}
,

\displaystyle r_{\gamma_{1}}(u,v)=\frac{\sum_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S_{1}}\gamma((u,v),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))}{C}=\frac{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}{C},

since
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=0
whenever
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{2}
. For
(u,v)\in S_{2}
, we have
\gamma_{1}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=0
for all
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, and thus
r_{\gamma_{1}}(u,v)=0
.
We now verify the transition coupling condition. First suppose
(u,v)\in S_{1}
and
u^{\prime}\in U
. If
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=0
, then the transition coupling condition is trivially satisfied. So suppose that
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})>0
. Then the transition coupling condition for
\gamma
gives that there exists
v^{\prime}\in V
such that
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
, and by the definition of
S_{1}
, any such
v^{\prime}
must satisfy
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{1}
. Then by the definition of
\gamma_{1}
, the previous sentence, the transition coupling condition for
\gamma
, and the previous display, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma_{1}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{1}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}{C}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}{C}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}\frac{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}{C}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}r_{\gamma_{1}}(u,v),

which verifies the transition coupling condition for
\gamma_{1}
for
(u,v)
and
u^{\prime}
.
If
(u,v)\in S_{2}
, then both sides of the transition coupling condition equal zero, so the equality trivially holds.
The other transition coupling condition can be verified analogously.
By Proposition
6.1
, since
G
and
H
are connected, the marginal coupling condition is implied by the transition coupling condition.
Thus,
\gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
.

By assumption,
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, and by Proposition
6.7
, its support is minimal.
However, by construction,
\spp(\gamma_{1})\subsetneq\spp(\gamma)
, contradicting the minimality of
\spp(\gamma)
.
Therefore, we conclude that
K
must be connected.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

Note that when
\gamma
is not extremal,
K=(\spp(r_{\gamma}),\gamma)
may be disconnected.

7.
Properties of the optimal graph joining problem

In this section, we first establish basic properties of the OGJ problem, including the fact it is a linear program. We then define the OGJ distance on graphs with a common vertex set and verify that it satisfies the properties of a metric when the vertex cost
c
does. Finally, we establish a stability result for OGJ to conclude this section.

7.1.
OGJ as a linear program

As mentioned in Section
2
, the OGJ problem is indeed a linear program and always has solutions. The following proposition is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.5
.

See
2.8

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is a convex polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
and the objective function is linear, the OGJ problem is a linear program. Because
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
is nonempty and compact, the OGJ problem always has solutions.
∎

The optimal solution set
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is also a convex set by the convexity of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
and the linearity of the objective function. The following remark about the extreme points of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is a direct consequence of Theorem 2.7 in
[
5
]
.

Remark 7.1
.

If
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
, then
\gamma
is also an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
. Conversely, if
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
, then
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta).

7.2.
The OGJ distance

Our development is compatible with the metric theory for optimal transport. That is, one can define a Wasserstein-style distance on undirected graphs with a common vertex set using the OGJ problem. We call this distance the
\kappa
-
\OGJ
distance.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
c\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times U\to\mathbb{R}
be a metric on
U
. For
\kappa\geq 1
, the
\kappa
-
\OGJ
distance between two graphs
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(U,\beta)
is defined by

\rho_{\kappa}(G,H)=\min_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)}\left(\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in U\times U}c(u,u^{\prime})^{\kappa}\,r_{\gamma}(u,u^{\prime})\right)^{1/\kappa}.

In the special case when
\kappa=1
, we note that the
1
-OGJ distance is equal to the OGJ transport cost. Next we prove that the
\kappa
-
\OGJ
distance is indeed a metric on the family of graphs with the same vertex set
U
when
c
is a metric on
U
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the family of graphs on vertex set
U
. If the cost function
c
is a metric on
U
, then the
\kappa
-
\OGJ
distance
\rho_{\kappa}(\cdot,\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is a metric on
\mathcal{G}
.

In the proof of the metric property of the OGJ distance, which is given below, we follow the standard outline from optimal transport theory by first establishing a Gluing Lemma and then using it to establish the triangle inequality. In the rest of this subsection, let
\mathcal{G}
be the family of graphs on vertex set
U
. Consider three graphs
G_{1}=(U,\alpha_{1})
,
G_{2}=(U,\alpha_{2})
, and
G_{3}=(U,\alpha_{3})
in
\mathcal{G}
. Let
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
, and
p_{3}
denote their marginal functions, respectively. We use
\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
to denote the set of three-way weight joinings of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
, which are defined as weight functions on
(U\times U\times U)^{2}
satisfying the three-way marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions (as in Definition
2.4
). For example, for a weight function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times U\times U)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the marginal coupling and transition coupling conditions with respect to
\alpha_{1}
are

\displaystyle\sum_{y,z\in U}r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=p_{1}(x),\text{ and }

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)\sum_{y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}(x,x^{\prime})\,r_{\gamma}(x,y,z),

where
r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)=\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))
.
The other constraints for
\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{3}
are analogous. For two arbitrary weight joinings
\gamma_{12}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and
\gamma_{23}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
, let
r_{12}
and
r_{23}
denote their marginal functions, respectively.

Lemma 7.4

(Gluing Lemma)
.

For weight joinings
\gamma_{12}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and
\gamma_{23}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
, there exists a three-way weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
such that
\gamma_{12}
and
\gamma_{23}
are its marginals, i.e,

\displaystyle\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\gamma_{12}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})),

\displaystyle\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\gamma_{23}((y,z),(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})).

Proof.

Define a function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times U\times U)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by setting

\displaystyle\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{\gamma_{12}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\,\gamma_{23}((y,z),(y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))}{\alpha_{2}(y,y^{\prime})}&\text{ if }\alpha_{2}(y,y^{\prime})\neq 0\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then it is straightforward to show that
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
. It remains to prove that
\gamma
admits
\gamma_{12}
and
\gamma_{23}
as marginals. Indeed, if
\alpha_{2}(y,y^{\prime})>0
, then because
\gamma_{23}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
, Proposition
6.3
yields that

\displaystyle\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\gamma_{12}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in U}\dfrac{\gamma_{23}((y,z),(y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))}{\alpha_{2}(y,y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{12}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})).

If
\alpha_{2}(y,y^{\prime})=0
, then both sides of the coupling condition are zeros.
Thus
\gamma_{12}
is a marginal of
\gamma
. The fact that
\gamma_{23}
is also a marginal of
\gamma
is proved similarly.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.3
.

One may easily show that
\rho_{\kappa}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a nonnegative function and is symmetric on
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
. Next, let us prove that
\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})=0
if and only if
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
. Suppose
\alpha_{1}
is identical to
\alpha_{2}
on
U\times U
. Consider the following function
\gamma^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times U)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\alpha_{1}(x,x^{\prime})&\text{if}\;x=y,\text{ }x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Then for all
x,y\in U
, we have

\displaystyle r_{\gamma^{*}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in U}\alpha(x,x^{\prime})&\text{if }x=y,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that
\gamma^{*}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
.
Since
c
is a metric, we have
c(x,y)=0
whenever
x=y
. Thus
\langle c,r_{\gamma^{*}}\rangle=0
, which implies
\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})=0
.

Conversely, suppose
\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})=0
. Let
\gamma
be an optimal weight joining. Consider
u,u^{\prime}\in U
such that
\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime})>0
. By Proposition
6.3
, there are some
v,v^{\prime}\in U
such that
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
. Hence,
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
. Since
\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})=0
, we must have
c(u,v)=0
. Hence
u=v
, since
c
is a metric. By a similar argument for
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, we obtain that
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
. Thus
v,v^{\prime}
are the unique vertices of
U
such that
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
. Then by Proposition
6.3
, we have that

\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime}).

Likewise, we get

\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\alpha_{2}(v,v^{\prime}).

Since
u=v
and
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
, we obtain

\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime})=\alpha_{2}(v,v^{\prime})=\alpha_{2}(u,u^{\prime}).

Now consider
u,u^{\prime}\in U
such that
\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime})=0
. If
\alpha_{2}(u,u^{\prime})>0
, then applying the same argument, we obtain
\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime})>0
, a contradiction. In conclusion,
\alpha_{1}(u,u^{\prime})=\alpha_{2}(u,u^{\prime})
for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
.

Finally, let us show that
\rho_{\kappa}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the triangle inequality, i.e.,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{3})\leq\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})+\rho_{\kappa}(G_{2},G_{3}).

Indeed, let
\gamma_{12}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
and
\gamma_{23}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
be optimal solutions of the OGJ problems on the right-hand side of (
7.1
). By Lemma
7.4
, there exists
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})
such that it admits
\gamma_{12}
and
\gamma_{23}
as marginals. Now, define
\gamma_{13}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times U)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by letting

\displaystyle\gamma_{13}((x,z),(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\sum_{y,y^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})).

It is straightforward to show that
\gamma_{13}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3})
.
The corresponding marginal function
r_{13}
of
\gamma_{13}
is given by

\displaystyle r_{13}(x,z)=\sum_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in U}\sum_{y,y^{\prime}\in U}\gamma((x,y,z),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\sum_{y\in U}r_{\gamma}(x,y,z),

for all
x,z\in U
. Hence
r_{\gamma}
has
r_{13}
as a marginal. Using this fact, the triangle inequality for
c
, Minkowski’s inequality, the fact
\gamma
has
\gamma_{12}
and
\gamma_{23}
as marginals, and the optimality of
\gamma_{12}
and
\gamma_{23}
, we see that

\displaystyle\rho_{\kappa}^{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{3})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,z\in U}c(x,z)^{\kappa}\,r_{13}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,z\in U}c(x,z)^{\kappa}\,r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y,z\in U}(c(x,y)+c(y,z))^{\kappa}\,r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\Big(\sum_{x,y,z\in U}c(x,y)^{\kappa}\,r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)\Big)^{1/\kappa}+\Big(\sum_{x,y,z\in U}c(y,z)^{\kappa}\,r_{\gamma}(x,y,z)\Big)^{1/\kappa}\Big]^{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\Big[\Big(\sum_{x,y\in U}c(x,y)^{\kappa}\,r_{12}(x,y)\Big)^{1/\kappa}+\Big(\sum_{y,z\in U}c(y,z)^{\kappa}\,r_{23}(y,z)\Big)^{1/\kappa}\Big]^{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\Big[\rho_{\kappa}(G_{1},G_{2})+\rho_{\kappa}(G_{2},G_{3})\Big]^{\kappa}.

Raising both sides of this inequality to the power
1/\kappa
gives the result. ∎

7.3.
Stability of OGJ

We now explore the stability of the OGJ problem. Before stating this property, we recall that for two complete metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, a set
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{Y}
is a neighborhood of a set
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{Y}
if
\mathcal{W}\subset\inte(\mathcal{V})
. Neighborhoods in
\mathcal{X}
are defined analogously. A multifunction
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
is upper semicontinuous at a point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
if for every neighborhood
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{Y}}
of
F(x_{0})
, there is some neighborhood
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}
of
x_{0}
such that
F(\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}})\subset\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. The function
F
is upper semicontinuous if it is upper semicontinuous at every point.

The following proposition ensures that the optimal cost function of OGJ is continuous and the optimal solution function of OGJ is upper semicontinuous. The result is based on Theorem 13 in
[
31
]
.
Consider two vertex sets
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
and
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. Let
\mathcal{W}(U)
and
\mathcal{W}(V)
be the set of all weight functions on
U
and
V
, respectively. We endow
\mathcal{W}(U)
with the subspace topology inherited by viewing
\mathcal{W}(U)
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, and similarly we endow
\mathcal{W}(V)
with the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Let
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{W}(U)\times\mathcal{W}(V)
, and let

\mathcal{S}=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{W}(U\times V)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta),\text{ for some }(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{Z}\}.

We endow
\mathcal{S}
with the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
c
be a fixed cost function defined on
U\times V
. Then the following statements are true:

(1)

The optimal cost function
\rho_{c}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

\rho_{c}(\alpha,\beta)=\min\limits_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)}\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle,

is continuous.

(2)

The optimal solution (multi)function
J^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{Z}\to 2^{\mathcal{S}}
, defined by

J^{*}(\alpha,\beta)=\argminunder_{\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)}\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle,

is upper semicontinuous.

Our proof of this proposition relies on a general stability result for optimization problems (Proposition 4.4 in
[
7
]
). We first consider this general framework and recall some preliminaries. Consider the following optimization problem with the set of possible solutions
\mathcal{Y}
and the set of parameters
\mathcal{X}
:

(7.2)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\text{minimize }f(y,x)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }y\in F(x),

where
f(\cdot,x)
is the objective function and
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
is the constraint function. Problem (
7.2
) is a parameterized optimization problem, where each point
x\in\mathcal{X}
serves as a parameter. In what follows we suppose that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are two complete metric spaces. The multifunction
F
is closed at a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
if the following condition holds: for all sequences
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{X}
and
\{y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{Y}
and for all points
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, if
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
x
,
\{y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
y
, and
y_{k}\in F(x_{k})
for all
k
, then
y\in F(x)
. If
F
is closed at every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then it is closed.

Proof of Proposition
7.5
.

Let us first reformulate the OGJ problem as an instance of Problem (
7.2
) and then show that the hypotheses of Proposition 4.4 in
[
7
]
are satisfied. Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}
, and let
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{S}
.
Then we claim that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact spaces in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
, respectively. Indeed, since
\mathcal{X}
is a product of two compact sets, it is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The boundedness of
\mathcal{Y}
follows directly from the normalization property of weight functions, so we only need to check that it is closed. Let
\{\gamma_{k}\}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{Y}
that converges to
\gamma
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\gamma_{k}\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
, for some
(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\in\mathcal{X}
. As
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are the images of
\gamma_{k}
under the coordinate projections, which are continuous, we obtain that the sequences
\{\alpha_{k}\}
and
\{\beta_{k}\}
converge. Moreover, since
\mathcal{X}
is closed (with the product topology),
\{(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\}
converges to some
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{X}
. By passing to the limit in the linear constraints in the definition of weight joinings (Definition
2.4
), we see that
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{Y}
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn}
.
The objective function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
is chosen as the map
(\gamma,(\alpha,\beta))\mapsto\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle
. Additionally, we let the multifunction
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{X}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
be the constraint function for the problem; that is, we let
F(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{X}
.

Now we show that the OGJ problem satisfies the hypotheses (i)-(iv) of Proposition 4.4 in
[
7
]
. Indeed, let
x_{0}=(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathcal{X}
. First, observe that

(7.3)

\displaystyle f(\cdot,\cdot)\leq|f(\cdot,\cdot)|\leq\|c\|_{\infty}.

Due to the continuity of the inner product,
f
is continuous. Next, suppose there are two sequences
\{(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\{\gamma_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{Y}
and a point
\gamma\in\mathcal{Y}
such that the sequence
\{(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})\}
converges to
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{X}
, the sequence
\{\gamma_{k}\}
converges to
\gamma
, and for each
k
, we have
\gamma_{k}\in F(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k})
.
By an argument similar to the one in the previous paragraph (showing that
\mathcal{Y}
is closed),
we see that
\gamma\in F(\alpha,\beta)
. Therefore
F
is closed, and (ii) holds. To show (iii), from the observation (
7.3
), we can choose
M=\|c\|_{\infty}
. Then the level set
\{y\in F(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}f(y,x)\leq M\}
is always nonempty. In addition, the set
\mathcal{Y}
is compact, and the level set above is a subset of
\mathcal{Y}
. Lastly, let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{Y}}
be a neighborhood of
\argmin_{y\in F(x_{0})}f(y,x_{0})
. Choose

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\exists\gamma\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{Y}},\,\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)\}.

Then, by construction,
x_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}
, and
F(x)\cap\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{Y}}\neq\emptyset
for all

. We have shown that all conditions of Proposition 4.4 in

]
are satisfied. Hence,
\rho_{c}
is continuous, and
J^{*}
is upper semicontinuous at every point
(\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

8.
Deterministic and bijective weight joinings

As in standard optimal transport, a weight joining may split mass from one vertex in
G
and transport it to multiple vertices in
H
. In this section, we first characterize those weight joinings that do
not
split mass in this manner. We then relate such deterministic weight joinings to graph isomorphisms. Finally, we establish results concerning the relationship between bijective weight joinings and extreme points of weight joinings.

8.1.
Relations with graph isomorphisms

We first introduce weight joinings that do not split mass in one direction.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be weight functions on
U
and
V
, respectively. A weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
with marginal function
r_{\gamma}
is
deterministic
from
U
to
V
if for each
u\in U
there is a unique
v\in V
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
.

In other words, a weight joining
\gamma
is deterministic if the mass of each vertex in the source graph is sent to only one vertex in the target graph. However, a vertex in the target graph may receive mass from multiple source vertices.
Bijective weight joinings (as defined in Section
4
) are deterministic in both directions.

If
\gamma
is a deterministic weight joining from
G
to
H
, then we let
f_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
denote the map induced by
\gamma
, which is the map such that
f_{\gamma}(u)
is the unique
v\in V
satisfying
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
. Note that
f_{\gamma}
is well-defined by the property of deterministic weight joinings. We refer to
f_{\gamma}
as the map induced by the deterministic weight joining
\gamma
.

Any map induced by a deterministic weight joining satisfies a characteristic property, which was first introduced in
[
39
]
for weighted directed graphs. Here we provide the analogous definition in the setting of weighted undirected graphs.

Definition 8.2
.

Given two weighted undirected graphs
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
, a map
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a
factor map
if it is surjective and for all
v,v^{\prime}\in V
and
u\in f^{-1}(v)
, we have

\displaystyle q(v)\sum_{u^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(v^{\prime})}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=p(u)\,\beta(v,v^{\prime}).

If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a factor map, then we define a function
\gamma_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(U\times V)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by letting

\displaystyle\gamma_{f}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})&\text{ if }v=f(u),\text{and }v^{\prime}=f(u^{\prime})\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The following proposition makes precise connections between deterministic weight joinings and factor maps.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha)
and
H=(V,\beta)
be two fully supported graphs.

(1)

If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a factor map, then the function
\gamma_{f}
is a deterministic weight joining from
U
to
V
.

(2)

If
\gamma
is a deterministic weight joining of
\alpha
and
\beta
, then
f_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a factor map.

As this proposition is a special case of Theorem 16 in
[
39
]
, we omit its proof.

It turns out that graph isomorphisms are closely related to factor maps. Specifically, if a factor map is injective, then it is a bijection that preserves the edge weights. However, additional considerations are required to ensure label preservation (and obtain an isomorphism of labeled graphs).
Recall that a labeled graph is given by
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
, where
\phi_{G}
is a vertex label function. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of labeled graphs. Then
\Phi=\{\phi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}G\in\mathcal{G}\}
is the primary labeling scheme of
\mathcal{G}
. The primary label-based cost function is defined by

c_{\Phi}(u,v)=\mathbb{I}(\phi_{G}(u)\neq\phi_{H}(v)).

We also recall that
\rho_{\Phi}(G,H)
and
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Phi}(\alpha,\beta)
are the OGJ transport cost and the set of optimal weight joinings of
G
and
H
with the cost function
c_{\Phi}
, respectively.
See
3.9

Proof.

Let
f=f_{\gamma}
be the map induced by
\gamma
. First, since
\gamma
is bijective, for each
v\in V
, there exists a unique
u\in U
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
, and therefore the induced map
f
is injective. Also, since
\gamma
is bijective, it is deterministic from
U
to
V
. Hence
f
is a factor map by Proposition
8.3
. Then
f
is surjective, making it a bijection, and for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
, we have

q(f(u))\,\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=p(u)\,\beta(f(u),f(u^{\prime})).

Furthermore, since
\gamma
is bijective and
r_{\gamma}
is a coupling of
p
and
q
, we obtain that
p(u)=r_{\gamma}(u,f(u))=q(f(u))
for all
u\in U
.
Since
G
is fully supported,
p(u)>0
for all
u\in U
. Thus, by the previous display, we have
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=\beta(f(u),f(u^{\prime}))
for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
, i.e.,
f
preserves weights. To show that
f
preserves the primary labels (given by
\Phi
), first observe that for every
u\in U
, since
G
is fully supported, we have
p(u)>0
. Hence, by the marginal coupling condition, we have
r_{\gamma}(u,f(u))>0
. Because
\rho_{\Phi}(G,H)=0
, we must have
c_{\Phi}(u,f(u))=0
, and thus
\phi_{G}(u)=\phi_{H}(f(u))
(by definition of the cost function
c_{\Phi}
). In conclusion,
f
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
H
.
∎

The following proposition provides the converse: if
f
is a graph isomorphism, then there is a unique bijective weight joining such that its induced map coincides with
f
, and it has zero OGJ transport cost. Combining this result and Proposition
3.9
, these zero-cost bijective weight joinings are in one-to-one correspondence with graph isomorphisms.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be fully supported graphs. If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
H
, then there is a unique bijective weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
f=f_{\gamma}
, and
\gamma
has zero cost under
c_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

Since
f
is a graph isomorphism,
f
is a factor map. Let
\gamma=\gamma_{f}
. Hence,
\gamma
is a (deterministic) weight joining from
U
to
V
by Proposition
8.3
. Next, let us show that
\gamma
is bijective. First, by the definition of
\gamma_{f}
, we observe that the marginal function of
\gamma
must take the following form:

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}p(u)&\text{ if }v=f(u)\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Using this expression for
r_{\gamma}
, the fact that
f
is a bijection, and the fact that
G
and
H
are fully supported, we obtain that
\gamma
is bijective and
f_{\gamma}=f
. Since
f
preserves labels with
\Phi
, we obtain
\langle c_{\Phi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
. Therefore, the OGJ transport cost of
\gamma
is zero. Finally, note that any bijective weight joining
\gamma^{\prime}
corresponding to the same
f
must have the following form:

\gamma^{\prime}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\begin{cases}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})&\text{ if }v=f(u),v^{\prime}=f(u^{\prime}),\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Hence,
\gamma
is the unique bijective weight joining with these properties.
∎

These results suggest that the OGJ problem is closely related to the graph isomorphism problem. See Section
9
for further statements in this direction.

8.2.
Bijective weight joinings and extreme points

In this subsection, we explore the connection between bijective weight joinings and extreme points. Later we use these results to establish that OGJ identifies isomorphism for some graph families.

The following proposition states that any bijective weight joining
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
must be an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
. Since a bijective weight joining corresponds to a graph isomorphism, this insight enables us to identify graph isomorphisms by examining the extreme points of the feasible set
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
. Note that there is no reference to the labeling scheme in the next two propositions, so these results hold for a general vertex cost function.

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be two fully supported graphs. If
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is a bijective weight joining, then
\gamma
must be an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta).

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
be bijective, and suppose that there exists
\gamma^{\prime},\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
and
t\in(0,1)
such that

\gamma=t\gamma^{\prime}+(1-t)\tilde{\gamma}.

Let
f_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\to V
denote the bijective map induced by
\gamma
. Also, let
r_{\gamma},r_{\gamma^{\prime}},r_{\tilde{\gamma}}
denote the respective marginal functions, and note that
r_{\gamma}=tr_{\gamma^{\prime}}+(1-t)r_{\tilde{\gamma}}
. Since
\gamma
is bijective with induced map
f_{\gamma}
, we have
r_{\gamma}(u,v)=0
for all
v\neq f_{\gamma}(u)
. Thus we see that
r_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u,v)=0
for all
v\neq f_{\gamma}(u)
. Moreover, since
G
is fully supported, we must have

\sum_{v\in V}r_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u,v)=p(u)>0,

and hence
r_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u,f_{\gamma}(u))=p(u)>0
. Therefore
\gamma^{\prime}
is also a bijective weight joining with the same induced map
f_{\gamma}
.
As
\gamma
is the unique bijective weight joining with induced map
f_{\gamma}
(by Proposition
8.4
), we must have
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma
.
An analogous argument shows that
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
.
Thus we conclude that
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

It is natural to ask about the converse direction. We provide a characterization of when an extreme point of the optimal weight joinings is bijective.

Proposition 8.6
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two weight functions. Suppose that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
, we have
\langle c,r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
.
Then the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

For every weight joining
\gamma
in
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
, there exists a bijective weight joining
\gamma^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subset\spp(\gamma)
.

(ii)

Every extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is bijective.

Proof.

Suppose statement (i) holds, and let
\gamma
be an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
. By assumption, there exists a bijective weight joining
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subseteq\spp(\gamma)
. Since
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
, by Remark
7.1
,
\gamma
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
. By Proposition
6.7
,
\spp(\gamma)
is minimal. Thus, we conclude that
\spp(\gamma)=\spp(\gamma^{\prime})
.
Let
f
be the bijective map associated with
\gamma^{\prime}
. Then

\gamma^{\prime}\left((u,v),\left(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\right)\right)=\begin{cases}\alpha\left(u,u^{\prime}\right)&\text{ if }v=f_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u),\text{ and }v^{\prime}=f_{\gamma^{\prime}}\left(u^{\prime}\right)\\
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Thus,

\spp(\gamma)=\spp\left(\gamma^{\prime}\right)=\Big\{\big((u,f_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u)),(u^{\prime},f_{\gamma^{\prime}}(u^{\prime}))\big)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\alpha\left(u,u^{\prime}\right)>0\Big\}.

Using this expression for the support of
\gamma
, one may readily check that
\gamma
is bijective, which establishes statement (ii).

Now suppose statement (ii) holds, i.e., every extreme point of
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is bijective. Since
\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
is a convex polytope, any
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
can be expressed as a convex combination of bijective weight joinings. Therefore there exists at least one bijective weight joining
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\spp(\gamma^{\prime})\subset\spp(\gamma)
.
∎

Optimal graph joining and graph isomorphism

This section is dedicated to the proofs of results connecting OGJ to graph isomorphism detection and identification.

Labeling schemes

Proof.

The implication (2) implies (1) is immediate from the definitions.

is a (finite) path in

is in

. Given two graphs

(u,v)\in U\times V
. The projection map
\pr_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\times V\to V

Finally, recall that for a vertex

N_{G}(u)=\{u^{\prime}\in U\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})>0\}

in

.

Definition 9.2
.

Consider two graphs
G
and
H
in
(\mathcal{G},\Psi)
, and let

be a graph joining of

and

(resp.

) is

, the restriction of

(resp.
\pr_{H}
) to

is an injection.

The next lemma provides a connection between local injectivity of augmented label functions and projection maps.

Lemma 9.3
.

Consider two graphs
G
and
H
in
(\mathcal{G},\Psi)
. Suppose
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0
and the labeling scheme
\Psi
is locally injective. Then for any optimal graph joining
K
, the maps
\pr_{G}
and
\pr_{H}
are locally injective on
K
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be the optimal weight joining associated with
K
, with marginal function
r_{\gamma}
. Suppose for contradiction that
\pr_{G}
is not locally injective. Then there exists some
(u_{0},v_{0})
in
\spp(r_{\gamma})
with
(u_{1},v_{1}),(u_{1},v^{\prime}_{1})\in N_{K}(u_{0},v_{0})
and
v_{1}\neq v^{\prime}_{1}
.
Since
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0
and we have both
r_{\gamma}(u_{1},v_{1})>0
and
r_{\gamma}(u_{1},v^{\prime}_{1})>0
, Lemma
9.1
gives that
\psi_{H}(v_{1})=\psi_{G}(u_{1})=\psi_{H}(v^{\prime}_{1})
. In addition, by the transition coupling condition, we have that both
v_{1}
and
v^{\prime}_{1}
are in

. Combining these two observations, we obtain a contradiction to the local injectivity assumption of
\psi_{H}
. Hence,
\pr_{G}
is locally injective. Interchanging the roles of
G
and
H
gives the conclusion for
\pr_{H}
.
∎

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a family of connected graphs with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
.
Suppose
\Psi
is locally injective and has magic symbols.
For any two graphs
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}
, if
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0
, then
G\cong H
, and furthermore there is a unique optimal weight joining, which is bijective.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be an optimal weight joining and
K
be its corresponding graph joining.
Let
v_{*}\in V_{j}
be a magic vertex in
V
, i.e., there is a label
w\in\mathcal{L}
such that
v_{*}\in V
is the unique vertex in
V
such that
\psi_{H}(v_{*})=w
.
Since
H
is fully supported, we have
q(v_{*})>0
, and then since
r_{\gamma}
is a coupling of
p
and

, there is some
u_{*}\in U
satisfying

.
Hence, by Lemma

, we have
\psi_{G}(u_{*})=\psi_{H}(v_{*})=w
.

Now let us show that
\gamma
is bijective. Let
u\in U
be arbitrary, and suppose
(u,v),(u,v^{\prime})\in\spp(r_{\gamma})
.
Our aim is to show
v=v^{\prime}
. Indeed, because
G
is connected, there exists a path
u_{0}\dots u_{k}
in
G
that starts at
u_{0}=u
and ends at

. Since

, by the transition coupling condition, there exists a path

in
H

v_{0}=v
, and
\Gamma=(u_{0},v_{0})(u_{1},v_{1})\ldots(u_{k},v_{k})

.
Similarly, there is a path

\Gamma^{\prime}=(u_{0},v^{\prime}_{0})(u_{1},v^{\prime}_{1})\ldots(u_{k},v^{\prime}_{k})

K
. Since
u_{k}=u_{*}
, we have
r_{\gamma}(u_{*},v_{k})>0
and
r_{\gamma}(u_{*},v^{\prime}_{k})>0
. By Lemma
9.1
, we obtain
\psi_{H}(v_{k})=\psi_{G}(u_{*})=\psi_{H}(v^{\prime}_{k})

Since
v_{*}
is the unique vertex in

whose image is

, we must have
v_{k}=v_{*}=v^{\prime}_{k}
. Thus,
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are two paths that end at the same vertex

in

.

Next, note that
(u_{k-1},v_{k-1})(u_{*},v_{*})

By assumption,

\pr_{G}

.
Since

. Since

By Proposition
3.9
, there is a graph isomorphism

Since

is convex and consists solely of optimal weight joinings, it follows that all elements of

are extreme points. Since

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

By Proposition

The converse direction is given by Proposition

∎

9.4.
Results with process-level labeling schemes

In this subsection we prove the results regarding process-level labeling schemes. Recall from Section
4.2
that
\Psi^{*}
denotes the process-level labeling scheme.
See
4.4

Before proving this theorem, we first establish the following proposition.

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be fully supported weighted graphs with augmented labeling scheme
\Psi
.
For
\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
, we have
\langle c_{\Psi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
if and only if
\langle c_{\Psi^{*}},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
.

Proof.

Suppose
\langle c_{\Psi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
. Let
\gamma
be the optimal solution of this OGJ problem, and
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
be its associated reversible Markov chain as in Section
2.2.1
. Since
\langle c_{\Psi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
and
r_{\gamma}
is the distribution of
(\widetilde{X}_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0})
, we get that
c_{\Psi}(\widetilde{X}_{0},\widetilde{Y}_{0})=0
a.s. Then, since
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is stationary, for all
k\geq 0
, we see that
c_{\Psi}(\widetilde{X}_{k},\widetilde{Y}_{k})=0
a.s. Thus, for all
k\geq 0
, we have
\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X}_{k})=\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y}_{k})
a.s., which means
\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X})=\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y})
a.s. Now let
(u,v)\in U\times V
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
. Since
\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X})=\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y})
a.s., we get

\displaystyle\sum_{(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U\times V}\mathbb{P}(\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X})\neq\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y})\mid\widetilde{X}_{0}=u^{\prime},\widetilde{Y}_{0}=v^{\prime})r_{\gamma}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=0.

As the right-hand side is zero and
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
, we must have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X})\neq\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y})\mid\widetilde{X}_{0}=u,\widetilde{Y}_{0}=v)=0.

But this means
\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X}^{u})=\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y}^{v})
a.s. On the other hand, since
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is a coupling of
X
and
Y
, we have
\widetilde{X}^{u}\overset{d}{=}X^{u}
and
\widetilde{Y}^{v}\overset{d}{=}Y^{v}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(X^{u})\overset{d}{=}\psi_{G}(\widetilde{X}^{u})\overset{d}{=}\psi_{H}(\widetilde{Y}^{v})\overset{d}{=}\psi_{H}(Y^{v}),

which means
\psi_{G}^{*}(u)=\psi_{H}^{*}(v)
. This gives that
c_{\Psi^{*}}(u,v)=0
. Hence, for any
(u,v)\in U\times V
satisfying
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
, we have
c_{\Psi^{*}}(u,v)=0
. Therefore,
\langle c_{\Psi^{*}},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
.

Conversely, assume that
\langle c_{\Psi^{*}},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
. Let
\gamma
be the optimal solution of this OGJ problem.
Let
(u,v)\in U\times V
such that
r_{\gamma}(u,v)>0
. Since
\langle c_{\Psi^{*}},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
, we see that
\psi_{G}(X^{u})
is equal in distribution to
\psi_{H}(Y^{v})
, and hence
\psi_{G}(X_{0}^{u})
is equal in distribution to
\psi_{H}(Y_{0}^{v})
. As
X_{0}^{u}=u
and
Y_{0}^{v}=v
,
we get
\psi_{G}(u)=\psi_{H}(v)
. Thus, we obtain
\langle c_{\Psi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

For any
\gamma\in J(\alpha,\beta)
, by Proposition
9.5
, we have
\langle c_{\Psi},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
if and only if
\langle c_{\Psi^{*}},r_{\gamma}\rangle=0
. Thus,
\rho_{\Psi}(G,H)=0
if and only if
\rho_{\Psi^{*}}(G,H)=0
, which proves the first sentence of the theorem, as well as fact that OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi}
detects isomorphism if and only if OGJ with cost
c_{\Psi^{*}}
detects isomorphism. Furthermore, when these costs are zero, Proposition
9.5
gives that
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi^{*}}(\alpha,\beta)
. Hence the extreme points of
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi}(\alpha,\beta)
coincide with the extreme points of
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi^{*}}(\alpha,\beta)
. Thus, the extreme points of
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi}(\alpha,\beta)
are bijective weight joinings with
c_{\Psi}
-zero cost if and only if the extreme points of
\mathcal{J}^{*}_{\Psi^{*}}(\alpha,\beta)
are bijective weight joinings with
c_{\Psi^{*}}
-zero cost. This proves the identification part of the theorem.
∎

Next, we show that OGJ can detect and identify isomorphism for graphs with complete sets of landmarks.
Recall that the definitions for graphs with landmarks are given in Definition
4.6
.
See
4.7

Proof.

Let
\Phi^{*}
be the process-level labeling scheme. First we show that
\Phi^{*}
is injective. To that end, let

, and let

be in

. Since

has a complete set of landmarks, there is label

such that

.
Without loss of generality, assume that

and let

. If

is the random walk on

Since

is injective, by Proposition

, OGJ with cost

.
∎

Before proving this theorem, we require some preliminary definitions and results.

Definition 9.6
.

Let
\alpha
be a weight function on

with marginal function

. Suppose

. First, we define

Z_{\alpha}(U_{0})=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime}),

If

Remark 9.7

The restriction
\alpha_{0}

U_{0}
.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
G=(U,\alpha,\phi_{G})
and
H=(V,\beta,\phi_{H})
be fully supported graphs, and let
U_{0}\subset U
and
V_{0}\subset V
be nonempty. Let
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
be the restrictions of
\alpha
and
\beta
to
U_{0}
and
V_{0}

\gamma\in\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\beta)
satisfies
\spp(r_{\gamma})\subset(U_{0}\times V_{0})\cup((U\setminus U_{0})\times(V\setminus V_{0}))
. Then
Z_{\alpha}(U_{0})=Z_{\beta}(V_{0})=Z_{\gamma}(U_{0}\times V_{0})
and the restriction of
\gamma
to

\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose the hypotheses of the lemma. Let us first show that

To begin, we note that by the assumption on
\spp(r_{\gamma})
and the edge coupling property (
6.1
), we have

\displaystyle Z_{\gamma}(U_{0}\times V_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=Z_{\alpha}(U_{0}).

Similarly, we have
Z_{\gamma}(U_{0}\times V_{0})=Z_{\beta}(V_{0})
.

Now let
Z
be the common value of
Z_{\gamma}(U_{0},V_{0})
,
Z_{\alpha}(U_{0})
, and
Z_{\beta}(V_{0})
. Note that
Z>0
, since
U_{0}
and
V_{0}
are nonempty and
G
and
H
are fully supported.
For notation, let
\gamma_{0}
denote the restriction of
\gamma
to
U_{0}\times V_{0}
(which is well-defined, since
Z>0
), and let
r_{0}
be its associated marginal function.
Let
p_{0}
and
q_{0}
denote the marginal functions for the restrictions
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
, respectively.
Recall from Definition
9.6
that for
u\in U_{0}
and
v\in V_{0}
, we have the notation

\displaystyle Z_{\gamma}((u,v),U_{0}\times V_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{0}\times V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}(u,U_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}(v,V_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\beta(v,v^{\prime}).

It is immediate from these definitions that we have
r_{0}(u,v)=Z_{\gamma}((u,v),U_{0}\times V_{0})/Z
,
p_{0}(u)=Z_{\alpha}(u,U_{0})/Z
, and

. Additionally, if

and the transition coupling condition, we have

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\gamma}((u,v),U_{0}\times V_{0})}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}\sum_{(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{0}\times V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\frac{1}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\frac{1}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\frac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{p(u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\alpha}(u,U_{0})}{p(u)},

Similarly, we get

\frac{Z_{\gamma}((u,v),U_{0}\times V_{0})}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}=\frac{Z_{\beta}(v,V_{0})}{q(v)}.

By Remark

,
\gamma_{0}

. Next let us show that

satisfies the marginal coupling condition and the transition coupling condition. For the marginal coupling condition, for any

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{0}}r_{0}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{v\in V_{0}}\sum_{(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in U_{0}\times V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in U_{0}}\alpha(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=p_{0}(u).

This establishes the marginal coupling condition for

with respect to

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{0}(u,v)}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\gamma_{0}((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}{r_{0}(u,v)}\cdot\frac{1}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V_{0}}\frac{\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))}{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}{Z_{\gamma}((u,v),U_{0}\times V_{0})/Z}\cdot\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\frac{1}{r_{\gamma}(u,v)}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(u,u^{\prime})}{Z_{\alpha}(u,U_{0})}

. Now consider case (ii), and let

. Since
u^{\prime}\in M_{G}
, there is some
u^{\prime\prime}\in L_{i}
such that the gluing map
f_{i}
satisfies

. Let
v^{\prime\prime}=f_{ij}(u^{\prime\prime})

\gamma_{ij}
, we have
\gamma((u,f(u)),(u^{\prime},g_{j}(v^{\prime\prime})))=\pi_{ij}\gamma_{ij}((u,f(u)),(u^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}))
. Since
f(u)=f_{ij}(u)
,
v^{\prime\prime}=f_{ij}(u^{\prime\prime})
, and
f_{ij}
satisfies
\gamma_{ij}((u,f_{ij}(u)),(u^{\prime\prime},f_{ij}(u^{\prime\prime})))>0
, we obtain
\gamma((u,f(u)),(u^{\prime},g_{j}(v^{\prime\prime})))>0
. Since
u^{\prime}\in M_{G}
and
\gamma
is bijective from
M_{G}
to
M_{H}
, we conclude that
g_{j}(v^{\prime\prime})=g(u^{\prime})=f(u^{\prime})
. Furthermore, by property (5) of magic decomposition and the fact
f_{ij}
is a graph isomorphism, we obtain that
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=\beta(f(u),f(u^{\prime}))
. Finally, consider case (iii). By Proposition
6.3
, there exists
(v,v^{\prime})\in V\times V
such that
\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))>0
. Since
\gamma
is bijective from
M_{G}
to
M_{H}
, we conclude that
v=f(u)
and
v^{\prime}=f(u^{\prime})
. Again using Proposition
6.3
, we must have that
\alpha(u,u^{\prime})=\gamma((u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime}))=\beta(v,v^{\prime})=\beta(f(u),f(u^{\prime}))
.
This concludes the proof of the claim.
∎

9.12

Relation to Weisfeiler-Leman test

Lastly, we establish the relationship between OGJ and the Weisfeiler-Leman test.
Recall that
d_{\OTC}
and
d_{\WL}
are the NetOTC distance and the WL distance introduced in Section
5.1
, respectively. Recall the statement of the following proposition.
See
5.1

Proof.

Let
X
and
Y
be the associated reversible Markov chains on
G
and
H
, respectively. Let
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
be any reversible Markov chain associated with a graph joining of
G

(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})
is also a transition coupling (see Definition 3 in

31

8
