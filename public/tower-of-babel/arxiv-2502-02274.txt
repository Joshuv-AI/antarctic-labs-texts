Hyperpolygonal arrangements
Lorenzo Giordani, Paul Mücksch, Gerhard Röhrle, Johannes Schmitt

Abstract
. In [ BCS+24 ] , a particular family of real hyperplane arrangements stemming from hyperpolygonal spaces associated with certain quiver varieties was introduced which we thus call hyperpolygonal arrangements ℋ n {\mathscr{H}}_{n} . In this note we study these arrangements and investigate their properties systematically. Remarkably the arrangements ℋ n {\mathscr{H}}_{n} discriminate between essentially all local properties of arrangements. In addition we show that hyperpolygonal arrangements are projectively unique and combinatorially formal. We note that the arrangement ℋ 5 {\mathscr{H}}_{5} is the famous counterexample of Edelman and Reiner [ ER93 ] of Orlik’s conjecture that the restriction of a free arrangement is again free.

Primary 52C35, 14N20, 32S25, 32S22.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

In
[
BCS+24
]
,
Bellamy et al
introduced a particular family of real hyperplane arrangements in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
stemming from
hyperpolygon spaces realized as certain
quiver varieties which we call

hyperpolygonal arrangements

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
, see Definition
1.1
.
In this note we study these
hyperpolygonal arrangements and
investigate their properties in a systematic manner.
It turns out that the arrangements

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
differentiate essentially between all local properties of arrangements, see Theorem
1.2
.
In addition we show that hyperpolygonal arrangements are projectively unique, see Theorem
1.5
, and combinatorially formal, see Theorem
1.6
.

We briefly indicate how the arrangements
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
arise in
[
BCS+24
]
and show their connection to birational geometry, see
[
BCS+24
]
for details and references.
The arrangement
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
characterizes stability conditions on the parameter
\theta
of the hyperpolygon space
X_{n}(\theta)
.
Namely,
X_{n}(\theta)
is smooth if and only if
\theta
does not lie on any hyperplane in
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
.
As explained in
[
BCS+24
]
, the varieties
X_{n}(0)
are
conical symplectic varieties
and the map
X_{n}(\theta)\to X_{n}(0)
is a crepant projective resolution (hence a symplectic projective resolution), if
\theta
does not lie in
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
.
Furthermore, two such resolutions
X_{n}(\theta_{1})\to X_{n}(0)
and
X_{n}(\theta_{2})\to X_{n}(0)
are isomorphic if
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
lie in the same region of the complement of
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
This construction works for all conical symplectic varieties by Namikawa
[
Nam15
]
:
For a conical symplectic variety
Y
, there is a certain “parameter space” containing a hyperplane arrangement that characterizes the isomorphism classes of crepant projective resolutions (or, in general, the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-factorial terminalizations) of
Y
.
Very little is known about the hyperplane arrangements arising in this way, although they are essential in understanding the birational geometry of
Y
, see also
[
Bel16
,
BST18
]
.
Special cases of conical symplectic varieties are
symplectic quotient singularities

V/G
, where
V
is a symplectic vector space over
{\mathbb{C}}
and
G\leq\operatorname{Sp}(V)
is a finite group maintaining the symplectic form.
By
[
BCS+24
]
, the hyperpolygon space
X_{n}(0)
is not a quotient singularity for
n>5
.
In contrast,
X_{4}(0)
is the Kleinian singularity of type
D_{4}
, that is, the quotient of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
by the quaternion group
Q_{8}
. Further,

X_{5}(0)
is the quotient of
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
by a symplectic reflection group of order 32.
This quotient was extensively studied in
[
BS13
]
and
[
DW17
]
.

In this note we study the hyperpolygonal arrangements
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
in a systematic manner.
We note that
{\mathscr{H}}_{5}

is the famous counterexample of Edelman and Reiner
[
ER93
]
of Orlik’s conjecture that the restriction of a free arrangement is again free.

We first recall the definition from

[
BCS+24
]
.
Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
V={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be the dual basis in
V^{*}
of the standard
{\mathbb{R}}
-basis of
V
.
For
I\subseteq[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define the hyperplane

H_{I}:=\ker\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus I}x_{j}\right)

in
V
.

Definition 1.1
.

With the notation as above, the
hyperpolygonal arrangement

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
in
V
is defined as

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}:=\{\ker x_{i}\mid i\in[n]\}\cup\{H_{I}\mid\varnothing\neq I\subseteq[n]\}.

For the various notions used in our main theorem, we refer the reader to Section
2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then we have

(i)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is supersolvable if and only if
n\leq 2
;

(ii)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is inductively factored if and only if
n\leq 3
;

(iii)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is inductively free if and only if
n\leq 4
;

(iv)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is free if and only if
n\leq 5
;

(v)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is simplicial if and only if
n\leq 4
;

(vi)

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is not
K(\pi,1)
if
n\geq 6
.

It follows from Theorem
1.2
(v) and Remark
2.21
(i) that
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is
K(\pi,1)
for
n\leq 4
and
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
fails to be
K(\pi,1)
for
n\geq 6
by part (vi). It is not known whether
{\mathscr{H}}_{5}
is
K(\pi,1)
.

In general,
K(\pi,1)
arrangements need not be free, e.g. see
[
OT92
, Fig. 5.4]
. However, for hyperpolygonal arrangements, this does seem to be the case.
While
{\mathscr{H}}_{5}
is free, it is not known whether
{\mathscr{H}}_{5}
is
K(\pi,1)
. We can thus formulate

Corollary 1.3
.

With the possible exception when
n=5
,

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is
K(\pi,1)
if and only if
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is free.

Thus with the possible exception of
{\mathscr{H}}_{5}
, the hyperpolygonal arrangements

{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
satisfy Saito’s Conjecture that for a complexified arrangement freeness implies
K(\pi,1)
. Of course, the latter is know to be false in general
[
ER95
]
.

Theorem
1.5
(ii) and (v) imply the following which
is in support of a conjecture due to Falk and Randell namely that every (complex) factored arrangement is
K(\pi,1)
,
[
FR00
, Probl. 3.12]
.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is factored, then
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is
K(\pi,1)
.

A property for arrangements is said to be
combinatorial
if it only depends on the intersection lattice of the underlying arrangement.
In this context our next theorem shows that the class of hyperpolygonal arrangements is
very special in the sense that essentially every property
we may formulate for members of this class is
combinatorial. This is formally captured by the notion of
projective uniqueness
due to Ziegler
[
Z90
]
, see Definition
4.1
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is projectively unique.

Theorem
1.5
implies that
for the class of all real arrangements whose underlying matroid admits a realization over
{\mathbb{R}}
as a hyperpolygonal arrangement
freeness is combinatorial. In particular, Terao’s conjecture over
{\mathbb{R}}
is valid within this class, cf.
[
Z90
, Prop. 2.3]
. Likewise, asphericity is combinatorial within this class. Whether both these properties are combinatorial in general are longstanding and wide open problems, see
[
OT92
, Conj. 4.138]
and
[
FR00
, Prob. 3.8]
.

A hyperplane arrangement is called
formal
provided all linear dependencies among the defining forms of the hyperplanes are generated by ones corresponding to intersections of codimension two.
The significance of this notion stems from the fact that
complex arrangements with aspherical complements are formal,
[
FR87
, Thm. 4.2]
.
In addition, free arrangements are known to be formal,

[
Y93
, Cor. 2.5]
,
and factored arrangements are formal,
[
MMR24
, Thm. 1.1]
.
Thus all the properties studied in Theorem
1.2
entail formality.
In our next result we show that indeed
all hyperpolygonal arrangements are
combinatorially formal
, see Definition
5.1
.

Theorem 1.6
.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is combinatorially formal.

Theorem
1.6
is proved in §
5
, based on results from
[
MMR24
]
.

We end in §
6
with a brief discussion of the rank generating functions of the poset of regions of the free hyperpolygonal arrangements
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
.

For general information about arrangements
we refer the reader to

[
OT92
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperplane arrangements

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be a field and let

V={\mathbb{K}}^{n}

be an
n
-dimensional
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space.
A
hyperplane arrangement
is a pair

({\mathscr{A}},V)
, where
{\mathscr{A}}
is a finite collection of hyperplanes in
V
.
Usually, we simply write
{\mathscr{A}}
in place of
({\mathscr{A}},V)
.

The
lattice

L({\mathscr{A}})
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is the set of subspaces of
V
of
the form
H_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H_{i}
where
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{i}\}
is a subset
of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
For
X\in L({\mathscr{A}})
, we have two associated arrangements,
firstly

{\mathscr{A}}_{X}:=\{H\in{\mathscr{A}}\mid X\subseteq H\}\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}
,
the
localization of
{\mathscr{A}}
at
X
,
and secondly,
the
restriction of
{\mathscr{A}}
to
X
,
({\mathscr{A}}^{X},X)
, where

{\mathscr{A}}^{X}:=\{X\cap H\mid H\in{\mathscr{A}}\setminus{\mathscr{A}}_{X}\}
.
The lattice
L({\mathscr{A}})
is a partially ordered set by reverse inclusion:

X\leq Y
provided
Y\subseteq X
for
X,Y\in L({\mathscr{A}})
.

Throughout, we only consider arrangements
{\mathscr{A}}

such that
0\in H
for each
H
in
{\mathscr{A}}
.
These are called
central
.
In that case the
center

T({\mathscr{A}}):=\cap_{H\in{\mathscr{A}}}H
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is the unique
maximal element in
L({\mathscr{A}})
with respect
to the partial order.
A
rank
function on
L({\mathscr{A}})

is given by
r(X):=\operatorname{codim}_{V}(X)
.
The
rank
of
{\mathscr{A}}

is defined as
r({\mathscr{A}}):=r(T({\mathscr{A}}))
.

The
Poincaré polynomial

\pi({\mathscr{A}},t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is defined by

\pi({\mathscr{A}},t):=\sum_{X\in L({\mathscr{A}})}\mu(X)(-t)^{r(X)},

and the
characteristic polynomial

\chi({\mathscr{A}},t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]

of
{\mathscr{A}}
is defined by

\chi({\mathscr{A}},t):=t^{\ell}\pi({\mathscr{A}},-t^{-1})=\sum_{X\in L({\mathscr{A}})}\mu(X)t^{\dim X},

where
\mu
is the Möbius function of
L({\mathscr{A}})
,
see
[
OT92
, Def. 2.48, Def. 2.52]
.

We recall the concept of a generic arrangement from
[
OT92
, Def. 5.22]
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
\ell
-arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}
with
\operatorname{rank}({\mathscr{A}})=r
is called
generic
if
every subarrangement
{\mathscr{B}}
of
{\mathscr{A}}
of cardinality
\ell
is linearly independent,
[
OT92
, Def. 5.22]
.

2.2.
Supersolvable Arrangements

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be an arrangement.
Following
[
OT92
, §2]
, we say
that
X\in L({\mathscr{A}})
is
modular

provided
X+Y\in L({\mathscr{A}})
for every
Y\in L({\mathscr{A}})
,
cf.
[
OT92
, Def. 2.32, Cor. 2.26]
.
The following notion is due to Stanley
[
Sta72
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be a central (and essential)
\ell
-arrangement.
We say that
{\mathscr{A}}
is
supersolvable

provided there is a maximal chain

V=X_{0}<X_{1}<\ldots<X_{\ell-1}<X_{\ell}=\{0\}

of modular elements
X_{i}
in
L({\mathscr{A}})
.

Remark 2.3
.

(i).
By
[
OT92
, Ex. 2.28]
,

V
,
\{0\}
and the members in
{\mathscr{A}}

are always modular in
L({\mathscr{A}})
.
It follows that all
0
-
1
-, and
2
-arrangements are supersolvable.

(ii). Supersolvability is a local property
[
Sta72
, Prop. 3.2]
.

2.3.
Free arrangements

Free arrangements play a crucial role in the theory of arrangements;
see
[
OT92
, §4]
for the definition and
basic properties. If
{\mathscr{A}}
is free, then
we can associate with
{\mathscr{A}}
the multiset of its
exponents
,
denoted
\exp{\mathscr{A}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

(i).
Generic arrangements are not free, e.g., see
[
RT91
, §4.4]
.

(ii). Freeness is a local property
[
OT92
, Thm. 4.37]
.

Terao’s
Factorization Theorem

[
Ter81
]
shows
that the Poincaré polynomial
of a free arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}

factors into linear terms
given by the exponents of
{\mathscr{A}}

(cf.
[
OT92
, Thm. 4.137]
):

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that

{\mathscr{A}}
is free with
\exp{\mathscr{A}}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}\}
.
Then

\pi({\mathscr{A}},t)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1+b_{i}t).

Terao’s celebrated
Addition-Deletion Theorem

[
Ter80
]
plays a
fundamental role in the study of free arrangements,

[
OT92
, Thm. 4.51]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose that
{\mathscr{A}}\neq\Phi_{\ell}
.
Let
({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime})
be a triple of arrangements. Then any
two of the following statements imply the third:

(i)

{\mathscr{A}}
is free with
\exp{\mathscr{A}}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell-1},b_{\ell}\}
;

(ii)

{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
is free with
\exp{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell-1},b_{\ell}-1\}
;

(iii)

{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}
is free with
\exp{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell-1}\}
.

Theorem
2.6
motivates the notion of an

inductively free
arrangement,

[
OT92
, Def. 4.53]
.

Definition 2.7
.

The class
{\mathcal{IF}}
of
inductively free
arrangements
is the smallest class of arrangements subject to

(i)

\Phi_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{IF}}
for each
\ell\geq 0
;

(ii)

if there exists a hyperplane
H_{0}\in{\mathscr{A}}
such that both

{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}
belong to
{\mathcal{IF}}
, and
\exp{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\exp{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
,
then
{\mathscr{A}}
also belongs to
{\mathcal{IF}}
.

Remark 2.8
.

(i).
Inductively free arrangements are free. However, the latter class properly contains the former, cf.
[
OT92
, Ex. 4.59]
.

(ii). Inductive freeness is also a local property, thanks to
[
HRS17
, Thm 1.1]
.

(iii). Supersolvable arrangements
are inductively free,

[
OT92
, Thm. 4.58]
.

2.4.
Nice arrangements

The notion of a
nice
or
factored

arrangement goes back to Terao
[
Ter92
]
.
It generalizes the concept of a supersolvable arrangement.
We recall the relevant notions and results from
[
Ter92
]

(cf.
[
OT92
, §2.3]
).

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{s})
be a partition of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
Then
\pi
is called
independent
, provided
for any choice
H_{i}\in\pi_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq s
,
the resulting
s
hyperplanes are linearly independent, i.e.
r(H_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H_{s})=s
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{s})
be a partition of
{\mathscr{A}}

and let
X\in L({\mathscr{A}})
.
The
induced partition

\pi_{X}
of
{\mathscr{A}}_{X}
is given by the non-empty
blocks of the form
\pi_{i}\cap{\mathscr{A}}_{X}
.

Definition 2.11
.

The partition

\pi
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is

nice
for
{\mathscr{A}}
or a
factorization
of
{\mathscr{A}}
provided

(i)

\pi
is independent, and

(ii)

for each
X\in L({\mathscr{A}})\setminus\{V\}
, the induced partition
\pi_{X}
admits a block
which is a singleton.

If
{\mathscr{A}}
admits a factorization, then we also say that
{\mathscr{A}}
is
factored
or
nice
.

Remark 2.12
.

The class of nice arrangements is closed under taking localizations;
cf. the proof of
[
Ter92
, Cor. 2.11]
.

In
[
Ter92
, Thm. 2.8]
,
Terao proved that a partition
\pi
of
{\mathscr{A}}
gives
rise to a tensor factorization of the Orlik-Solomon algebra of
{\mathscr{A}}

if and only if
\pi
is
nice for

{\mathscr{A}}
, see
[
OT92
, Thm. 3.87]
.
We record a consequence of this fact for our purposes.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{s})
be a factorization of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
Then the following hold:

(i)

s=r=r({\mathscr{A}})
and

\pi({\mathscr{A}},t)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+|\pi_{i}|t);

(ii)

the multiset
\{|\pi_{1}|,\ldots,|\pi_{r}|\}
only depends on
{\mathscr{A}}
;

(iii)

for any
X\in L({\mathscr{A}})
, we have

r(X)=|\{i\mid\pi_{i}\cap{\mathscr{A}}_{X}\neq\varnothing\}|.

Remark 2.14
.

It follows from
Corollary
2.13
that
the question whether
{\mathscr{A}}
is factored is a purely combinatorial
property and only depends on the lattice
L({\mathscr{A}})
.

Moreover, the following is immediate from
Corollary
2.13
and Theorem
2.5
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
({\mathscr{A}},\pi)
be a factored arrangement which is also free.
Then
\exp{{\mathscr{A}}}=\{|\pi_{1}|,\ldots,|\pi_{\ell}|\}
.

2.5.
Inductively factored arrangements

Following Jambu and Paris

[
JP95
]
and
[
HR16a
]
,
we introduce further notation.
Suppose that
{\mathscr{A}}
is non-empty and
let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{s})
be a partition of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
Let
H_{0}\in\pi_{1}
and let

({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime})
be the triple associated with
H_{0}
.
We have the
induced partition

\pi^{\prime}
of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}

consisting of the non-empty parts
\pi_{i}^{\prime}:=\pi_{i}\cap{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
.
Further, we have the
restriction map

{\varrho}={\varrho}_{\pi,H_{0}}:{\mathscr{A}}\setminus\pi_{1}\to{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}
given by

H\mapsto H\cap H_{0}
, depending on
\pi
and
H_{0}
.
Let
\pi_{i}^{\prime\prime}:={\varrho}(\pi_{i})
for
i=2,\ldots,s
.
Clearly, imposing that

\pi^{\prime\prime}=(\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2},\ldots,\pi^{\prime\prime}_{s})
is
again a partition of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}
entails that

{\varrho}
is onto.

Here is the analogue for nice arrangements of Terao’s
Addition-Deletion Theorem (cf. Theorem
2.6
) for free arrangements from

[
HR16a
]
.

Theorem 2.16
.

Suppose

\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{s})
is a partition of
{\mathscr{A}}\neq\Phi_{\ell}
.
Let
({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime})
be the triple associated with
H_{0}\in\pi_{1}
.
Then any two of the following statements imply the third:

(i)

\pi
is nice for
{\mathscr{A}}
;

(ii)

\pi^{\prime}
is nice for
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
;

(iii)

{\varrho}:{\mathscr{A}}\setminus\pi_{1}\to{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}

is bijective and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
is nice for
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}
.

The Addition-Deletion Theorem
2.16

for nice arrangements motivates
the following stronger notion of factorization,
cf.
[
JP95
]
.

Definition 2.17

(
[
HR16a
, Def. 3.8]
)
.

The class
{\mathcal{IF\!AC}}
of
inductively factored
arrangements
is the smallest class of pairs
({\mathscr{A}},\pi)
of
arrangements
{\mathscr{A}}
along with a partition
\pi

subject to

(i)

(\Phi_{\ell},(\varnothing))\in{\mathcal{IF\!AC}}
for each
\ell\geq 0
;

(ii)

if there exists a partition
\pi
of
{\mathscr{A}}

and a hyperplane
H_{0}\in\pi_{1}
such that
for the triple
({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime},{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime})
associated with
H_{0}

the restriction map
{\varrho}={\varrho}_{\pi,H_{0}}:{\mathscr{A}}\setminus\pi_{1}\to{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}

is bijective and for the induced partitions
\pi^{\prime}
of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
and

\pi^{\prime\prime}
of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime}

both
({\mathscr{A}}^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})
and
({\mathscr{A}}^{\prime\prime},\pi^{\prime\prime})
belong to
{\mathcal{IF\!AC}}
,
then
({\mathscr{A}},\pi)
also belongs to
{\mathcal{IF\!AC}}
.

If
({\mathscr{A}},\pi)
is in
{\mathcal{IF\!AC}}
, then we say that

{\mathscr{A}}
is
inductively factored with respect to
\pi
, or else
that
\pi
is an
inductive factorization
of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
Usually, we say
{\mathscr{A}}
is
inductively factored
without
reference to a specific inductive factorization of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Remark 2.18
.

(i). If
{\mathscr{A}}
is inductively factored, then
{\mathscr{A}}
is inductively free,
by
[
HR16a
, Prop. 3.14]
.

(ii).
Thanks to
[
MR17
, Thm. 1.1]
,
inductive factoredness is preserved under localizations.

(iii). If
{\mathscr{A}}
is supersolvable, then it is inductively factored, see
[
JP95
]
or
[
HR16a
, Prop. 3.11]
.

2.6.
Simplicial arrangements

A real arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}
is
simplicial
provided each chamber of the complement of
{\mathscr{A}}
is an open simplicial cone in the ambient space.
Simpliciality is a combinatorial property. For, thanks to
[
CG15
, Cor. 2.4]
, a central essential real
\ell
-arrangement

{\mathscr{A}}
is simplicial if and only if

(2.19)

\ell\cdot\chi({\mathscr{A}},-1)+2\sum_{H\in{\mathscr{A}}}\chi({\mathscr{A}}^{H},-1)=0.

Remark 2.20
.

Simpliciality is preserved under localizations, see
[
CM19
, Lem. 2.17(1)]
.

2.7.
K(\pi,1)
-arrangements

A complex
\ell
-arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}
is called
aspherical
, or a

K(\pi,1)
-arrangement
(or that
{\mathscr{A}}
is
K(\pi,1)
for short), provided
the complement
M({\mathscr{A}})
of the union of the hyperplanes in

{\mathscr{A}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{\ell}
is aspherical, i.e. is a

K(\pi,1)
-space. That is, the universal covering space of
M({\mathscr{A}})

is contractible and the fundamental group

\pi_{1}(M({\mathscr{A}}))
of
M({\mathscr{A}})
is isomorphic to the group
\pi
.
This is an important
topological property, for
the cohomology ring
H^{*}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
of a
K(\pi,1)
-space
X

coincides
with the group cohomology
H^{*}(\pi,{\mathbb{Z}})
of
\pi
.
The crucial point here is that the intersections of codimension
2

determine the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(M({\mathscr{A}}))
of
M({\mathscr{A}})
.

Remark 2.21
.

(i).
By Deligne’s seminal work
[
Del72
]
, complexified simplicial arrangements are
K(\pi,1)
.
Likewise for complex supersolvable arrangements, cf.
[
FR85
]
and
[
Ter86
]
(cf.
[
OT92
, Prop. 5.12, Thm. 5.113]
).

(ii).
Thanks to an observation by Oka,
asphericity is preserved under localizations,
e.g., see
[
Pa93
, Lem. 1.1]
.

(iii).
By work of Hattori,
generic arrangements are not
K(\pi,1)
,
[
OT92
, Cor. 5.23]
.

4.
Projective Uniqueness: Proof of Theorem
1.5

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
,
{\mathscr{B}}
be two arrangements in a finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
V
.

(i)

{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
are
linearly isomorphic
if there is a
\varphi\in\operatorname{GL}(V)

such that
{\mathscr{B}}=\{\varphi(H)\mid H\in{\mathscr{A}}\}
; denoted by
{\mathscr{A}}\cong{\mathscr{B}}
.

(ii)

{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
are
L
-equivalent
if
L({\mathscr{A}})
and
L({\mathscr{B}})
are isomorphic as posets;
denoted by
{\mathscr{A}}\cong_{L}{\mathscr{B}}
.

(iii)

{\mathscr{A}}
is
projectively unique
if for any arrangement
{\mathscr{C}}
in
V
we have:

{\mathscr{C}}\cong_{L}{\mathscr{A}}
implies
{\mathscr{C}}\cong{\mathscr{A}}
.

We recall some results from
[
GMMR25
]
.
Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be an
{\mathbb{R}}
-arrangement.
We specify what we mean by a subarrangement of
{\mathscr{A}}
being generated by a subarrangement of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\varnothing\neq S\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}
. Set
\operatorname{Gen}_{0}({\mathscr{A}},S):=S
and inductively

\operatorname{Gen}_{i+1}({\mathscr{A}},S):=\left\{H\in{\mathscr{A}}\mid\exists\ J\subseteq L(\operatorname{Gen}_{i}({\mathscr{A}},S)):H=\sum_{X\in J}X\right\}

for
i\geq 0
.
Then we say that

\langle S\rangle_{\mathscr{A}}:=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\operatorname{Gen}_{i}({\mathscr{A}},S)\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}

is the subarrangement of
{\mathscr{A}}

generated by
S
. If
\langle S\rangle_{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}
, then we say that
S
generates
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
GMMR25
, Lem. 3.5]
)
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
be two arrangements in
V
.
Suppose
\varnothing\neq S\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}
,
\varnothing\neq T\subseteq{\mathscr{B}}

such that
\langle S\rangle_{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}
and
\langle T\rangle_{\mathscr{B}}={\mathscr{B}}
, i.e.
S
generates
{\mathscr{A}}

and
T
generates
{\mathscr{B}}
.
If
{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathscr{B}}
are
L
-equivalent via a poset isomorphism
\psi:L({\mathscr{A}})\to L({\mathscr{B}})
and
\varphi\in\operatorname{GL}(V)

such that

\psi(S)=\varphi(S)=T
and
\psi(H)=\varphi(H)
for all
H\in S
,
then
\varphi
extends to a linear isomorphism between the whole arrangements, i.e.
\varphi({\mathscr{A}})={\mathscr{B}}
.

The following is a consequence of Lemma
4.3
.

Proposition 4.4

(
[
GMMR25
, Prop. 3.6]
)
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be an essential and irreducible arrangement in
V\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
.
Suppose there is a subset
S
of
{\mathscr{A}}
such that
\langle S\rangle_{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}
and
|S|=\ell+1
.
Then
{\mathscr{A}}
is projectively unique.

Finally, the following result gives
Theorem
1.5
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
let

S:=\{\ker x_{1},\ldots,\ker x_{n-1},\ker(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}),\ker(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n-1}-x_{n})\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}_{n}.

Then

\langle S\rangle_{{\mathscr{H}}_{n}}={\mathscr{H}}_{n}
.
In particular,
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is projectively unique over
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Let
\varnothing\neq I\subseteq[n]
. Without loss, we may assume that
n\notin I
. For
i\in[n-1]
, set
H_{i}:=\ker x_{i}
, and
for
\alpha:=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}
and
\beta:=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n-1}-x_{n}
, set
H_{\alpha}:=\ker\alpha
and
H_{\beta}:=\ker\beta
.
Then

H_{I}=\ker\left(\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}-\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus I}x_{j}\right)=\ker\gamma
, where

(4.6)

\gamma=-\alpha+2\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}=\beta-2\sum_{j\in[n-1]\setminus I}x_{j}.

It follows from (
4.6
) that

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle:=H_{\alpha}\cap\bigcap_{i\in I}H_{i}\subseteq H_{I}\text{ and }

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=H_{\beta}\cap\bigcap_{j\in[n-1]\setminus I}H_{j}\subseteq H_{I}.

Since
\dim X=n-|I|-1
,
\dim Y=|I|
, and
\dim(X\cap Y)=0
, we have
H_{I}=X+Y
.
Since both
X
and
Y
belong to the lattice of intersections of the subarrangement
S
of
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
, we infer
H_{I}=X+Y\in\langle S\rangle_{{\mathscr{H}}_{n}}
.
Consequently, the result follows thanks to Proposition
4.4
.
∎

5.
Combinatorial Formality: Proof of Theorem
1.6

A property for arrangements is said to be
combinatorial
if it only depends on the intersection lattice of the underlying arrangement.
Yuzvinsky
[
Y93
, Ex. 2.2]
demonstrated that formality is not combinatorial, answering a question raised by Falk and Randell
[
FR87
]
in the negative.
Yuzvinsky’s insight motivates the following notion from
[
MMR24
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
{\mathscr{A}}
is a formal arrangement. We say
{\mathscr{A}}
is
combinatorially formal
if every arrangement with
an intersection lattice isomorphic to the one of

{\mathscr{A}}
is also formal.

The following definitions, which are originally due to Falk for matroids
[
F02
]
, were adapted for arrangements in
[
MMR24
, §2.4]
.
Let
{\mathscr{B}}\subset{\mathscr{A}}
be a subset of hyperplanes.
We say
{\mathscr{B}}
is
closed
if
{\mathscr{B}}={\mathscr{A}}_{Y}
for
Y=\bigcap\limits_{H\in{\mathscr{B}}}H
.
We call
{\mathscr{B}}

line-closed

if for every pair
H,H^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{B}}
of hyperplanes, we have
{\mathscr{A}}_{H\cap H^{\prime}}\subset{\mathscr{B}}
.
The
line-closure

{\operatorname{lc}}({\mathscr{B}})
of
{\mathscr{B}}
is defined
as the intersection of all line-closed subsets of
{\mathscr{A}}
containing
{\mathscr{B}}
.
The arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}
is called
line-closed

if every line-closed subset of
{\mathscr{A}}
is closed.
With these notions, we have the following criterion for combinatorial formality, see
[
F02
, Cor. 3.8]
,
[
MMR24
, Prop. 3.2]
:

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be an arrangement of rank
r
. Suppose
{\mathscr{B}}\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}
consists of
r
hyperplanes such that
r({\mathscr{B}})=r
and
{\operatorname{lc}}({\mathscr{B}})={\mathscr{A}}
. Then
{\mathscr{A}}
is combinatorially formal.

A subset
{\mathscr{B}}\subseteq{\mathscr{A}}
as in Proposition
5.2
is called an
lc-basis
of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
{\mathscr{B}}=\{\ker x_{1},\ldots,\ker x_{n-1},\ker(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n})\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
.
Then it is easy to see that successively all
H_{I}
for
\varnothing\neq I\subseteq[n]
belong to the line-closure
{\operatorname{lc}}({\mathscr{B}})
of
{\mathscr{B}}
, as follows.
For
i\in[n]
, set
H_{i}:=\ker x_{i}
, and
for
\alpha:=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}
set
H_{\alpha}:=\ker\alpha
.
Then for
I=[n]\setminus\{i\}
, we have

H_{I}=\ker\left(\alpha-2x_{i}\right)\supset H_{\alpha}\cap H_{i}
. Thus for each
I
of cardinality
n-1
,
H_{I}
belongs to
{\operatorname{lc}}({\mathscr{B}})
. Now iterate the argument above for
I
of successively smaller cardinality.
Consequently,
{\operatorname{lc}}({\mathscr{B}})={\mathscr{H}}_{n}
. Since

\operatorname{rk}({\mathscr{H}}_{n})=n=|{\mathscr{B}}|=\operatorname{rk}({\mathscr{B}})
, it follows from Proposition
5.2
that
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is combinatorially formal.
∎

There is a stronger notion of formality for an arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}
, that of
k
-formality
for
1\leq k\leq\operatorname{rk}({\mathscr{A}})
due to Brandt and Terao
[
BT94
]
.
In view of Theorem
1.6
and in view of the fact that all free arrangements are not just formal but are
k
-formal for all
k
, by
[
BT94
, Thm. 4.15]
, one might ask for this stronger notion of
k
-formality among hyperpolygonal arrangements.
Computational evidence for further non-free hyperpolygonal arrangements suggests the following.

Conjecture 5.3
.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathscr{H}}_{n}
is
k
-formal for any
k
.

6.
Rank-generating functions of the poset of regions

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be a
hyperplane arrangement in the real vector space
V={\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
.
A
region
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is a connected component of the
complement
M({\mathscr{A}}):=V\setminus\cup_{H\in{\mathscr{A}}}H
of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
Let
{\mathscr{R}}:={\mathscr{R}}({\mathscr{A}})
be the set of regions of
{\mathscr{A}}
.
For
R,R^{\prime}\in{\mathscr{R}}
, we let
{\mathcal{S}}(R,R^{\prime})
denote the
set of hyperplanes in
{\mathscr{A}}
separating
R
and
R^{\prime}
.
Then with respect to a choice of a fixed
base region
B
in
{\mathscr{R}}
, we can partially order

{\mathscr{R}}
as follows:

R\leq R^{\prime}\quad\text{ if }\quad{\mathcal{S}}(B,R)\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}(B,R^{\prime}).

Endowed with this partial order, we call
{\mathscr{R}}
the

poset of regions of
{\mathscr{A}}
(with respect to
B
)
and denote it by

P({\mathscr{A}},B)
. This is a ranked poset of finite rank,
where
\operatorname{rk}(R):=|{\mathcal{S}}(B,R)|
, for
R
a region of
{\mathscr{A}}
,

[
Ed84
, Prop. 1.1]
.
The
rank-generating function
of
P({\mathscr{A}},B)
is
defined to be the following polynomial in

{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}[t]

\zeta(P({\mathscr{A}},B);t):=\sum_{R\in{\mathscr{R}}}t^{\operatorname{rk}(R)}.

This poset along with its rank-generating function
was introduced by Edelman

[
Ed84
]
.

Thanks to work of Björner, Edelman, and Ziegler

[
BEZ90
, Thm. 4.4]

(see also Paris
[
Pa95
]
), respectively
Jambu and Paris
[
JP95
, Prop. 3.4, Thm. 6.1]
,
in case of a real arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}

which is supersolvable,
respectively inductively factored,
there always exists a suitable base region
B
so that

\zeta(P({\mathscr{A}},B);t)

admits a multiplicative decomposition which
is determined by the exponents of
{\mathscr{A}}
, i.e.

(6.1)

\zeta(P({\mathscr{A}},B);t)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1+t+\ldots+t^{e_{i}}),

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}=\exp{\mathscr{A}}
is the
set of exponents of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Quite remarkably
many classical real arrangements do satisfy the
factorization identity (
6.1
),
the most prominent ones being Coxeter arrangements.

Let
W=(W,S)
be a Coxeter group with associated reflection arrangement

{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}(W)
which consists of the reflecting hyperplanes of
the reflections in
W
in the real space
V={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
|S|=n
.
The

Poincaré polynomial

W(t)
of
the Coxeter group
W
is the polynomial in
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
defined by

(6.2)

W(t):=\sum_{w\in W}t^{\ell(w)},

where
\ell
is the length function
of
W
with respect to
S
.
Then
W(t)

coincides with the rank-generating function of the poset of regions
\zeta(P({\mathscr{A}},B);t)
of
the underlying reflection arrangement

{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}(W)
with respect to
B
being the dominant Weyl chamber of
W
in
V
;
see
[
BEZ90
]
or
[
JP95
]
.

The following factorization of

W(t)
is due to Solomon
[
Sol66
]
:

(6.3)

W(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+t+\ldots+t^{e_{i}}),

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is the
set of exponents of
W
, i.e., the set of exponents of
{\mathscr{A}}(W)
; see also
[
Mac72
]
. So by the comments above, (
6.3
) coincides with the factorization in (
6.1
).

Let
W
be a Coxeter group again with reflection arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}={\mathscr{A}}(W)
,
let
X
be a member of the intersection lattice
L({\mathscr{A}})
, and consider the restricted reflection arrangement
{\mathscr{A}}^{X}
. In general,
{\mathscr{A}}^{X}
is no longer a reflection arrangement.
It was shown in
[
MR19
, Thm. 1.3]

that there always exists a suitable base region
B
of
{\mathscr{A}}^{X}
in
X
so that also

\zeta(P({\mathscr{A}}^{X},B);t)
satisfies (
6.1
), with the exception of only three instances when
W
is of type
E_{8}
