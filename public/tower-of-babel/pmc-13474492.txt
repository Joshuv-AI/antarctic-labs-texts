Intrapartum cardiac telemetry monitoring in patients with cardiovascular disease
Authors: Madeline E. Duncan, Anne Marie Valente, Giulia Ensing, Sarah Rae Easter, Kimberlee Gauvreau, Jean Marie Carabuena, Nancy Barker, Katherine E. Economy, Fred M. Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are no guidelines for cardiac telemetry monitoring during labor and delivery in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD). We sought to identify factors influencing provider recommendations for intrapartum telemetry monitoring and associated outcomes.MethodsThis is a single-center, cohort study comparing cardiovascular outcomes during the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods between those who received telemetry and those who did not. Clinically significant arrhythmic events (CSAEs) were assessed in all patients.ResultsAmong 717 pregnancies in patients with CVD, 94 (13.1%) had antepartum recommendations for telemetry. Type of CVD differed between those with and without telemetry recommendations (P < 0.001), with a higher proportion of individuals with arrhythmias in the recommended telemetry group. These pregnancies were also more likely to present with New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class \ge 2 at enrollment (12.8 vs. 5.3%, P = 0.008), new antepartum arrhythmia (26.6 vs. 2.1%, P < 0.001), and greater use of beta blockers (55.3 vs. 27.9%, P < 0.001), anti-arrhythmic medications (16.0 vs. 2.1%, P < 0.001), or calcium channel blockers (8.5 vs. 2.1%, P = 0.002). Telemetry was used in 77 vaginal deliveries (10.7%) and all 261 cesarean deliveries. Only one patient monitored with telemetry experienced an intrapartum CSAE (0.3%). There were no CSAEs in patients not monitored on telemetry.ConclusionsCardiac telemetry monitoring has a limited impact on the intrapartum management of patients with CVD. Further studies are needed to identify which patients may benefit most from intrapartum telemetry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09368-8.

Background

The intrapartum and immediate postpartum stages of labor are considered high risk for persons with cardiovascular disease (CVD) due to the associated hemodynamic changes that accompany labor and delivery [1, 2]. Arrhythmias are the most common cardiac event in the peripartum period which may prompt clinicians to recommend telemetry during the intrapartum and postpartum course for people with CVD [3]. Contemporary guidelines range from recommendations for telemetry for any person with a clinical history of arrhythmia to recommended use only for those with ventricular tachycardiac and comorbid structural heart disease [3–5]. While expert opinion differs regarding the optimal intrapartum telemetry population, the recommendation to continue telemetry use for 24 h after delivery appears to be universal and is predicated on an uncomplicated birth [4, 5].

Guidelines on intrapartum telemetry highlight its role in the early detection of decompensation, allowing for timely intervention. Yet such recommendations imply that obstetric units providing continuous ECG monitoring should also be prepared to respond to abnormalities, which may warrant a different level of care prompting transfers between units, hospitals, or healthcare systems for delivery [4, 5]. Conversely, the absence of a recommendation for telemetry (and a team prepared to respond appropriately) may imply an absence of risk, leading people with CVD to deliver at hospitals lacking the resources or personnel to address potential intrapartum needs [4]. The low incidence of intrapartum arrhythmias, wide range of CVD encountered in pregnancy, and potential for obstetric events to complicate delivery [6, 7] may indefinitely preclude the development of evidence-based guidelines. Regardless, the growing experience of cardio-obstetrics teams in the management of CVD during labor and birth may still be used to inform and align best practices for care.

Given the conflicting recommendations for telemetry during labor and delivery, we sought to characterize expert opinion surrounding intrapartum telemetry and to highlight its role in the management of people with CVD. Our primary aim for this paper was to identify clinical risk factors associated with the recommendation for intrapartum cardiac telemetry by an experienced multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team. Our secondary aim was to describe outcomes associated with the receipt of intrapartum cardiac telemetry in a diverse contemporary cohort of patients with CVD.

Methods

Study design

This is a single-center, retrospective cohort study of all subjects who were prospectively enrolled in the Standardized Outcomes in Reproductive Cardiovascular Care (STORCC) initiative. The methodology and inclusion criteria for STORCC have been previously described [8]. For this study, we included all pregnancies of subjects with congenital heart disease (CHD), cardiomyopathy, connective tissue disease (including aortopathy without a specific connective tissue disease diagnosis), arrhythmia, valvular disease, vascular disease (Kawasaki disease, Takayasu arteritis, pulmonary embolism), and ischemic heart disease who had enrolled in STORCC from its launch on September 14, 2011, through September 1, 2023. All deliveries occurred between October 2011 and September 2023. Pregnancies resulting in a miscarriage, abortion, or delivery at an outside institution were excluded. All patients provided informed consent, and this protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Boston Children’s Hospital.

For our primary aim of identifying patient characteristics that were associated with a recommendation for telemetry at the time of delivery, we used an intention-to-treat strategy to classify patients by the plan for telemetry established as a part of delivery planning by the pregnancy heart team [7] (Fig. 1). At our center, these plans are developed and reviewed at our monthly multidisciplinary conference, which includes healthcare professionals specializing in maternal–fetal medicine, cardiology, obstetric anesthesia, and critical care obstetric nursing. The plan for telemetry along with the timing of initiation and plan for discontinuation postpartum were included as a part of the comprehensive birth plan documented in the electronic medical record. For patients with multiple pregnancies, decisions pertaining to intrapartum telemetry for each pregnancy were made independent of whether telemetry had been used during previous deliveries. This plan was reviewed by the clinical care team on admission to labor and delivery at our institution by the obstetrician, anesthesiologist, cardiologist, and nurse caring for the patient.Fig. 1Inclusion criteria diagram

Inclusion criteria diagram

Our high-volume regional referral center utilizes continuous 5-lead ECG telemetry. On labor and delivery, telemetry is directly observed and interpreted by nurses cross-trained in obstetrics and critical care. Concerns identified on telemetry are reviewed in real time at the bedside by the nurse, obstetric care team, obstetric anesthesia team, and cardiology. Management plans are reviewed by the multidisciplinary team and enacted on the labor floor. In most cases, our obstetrical unit has the ability to initiate and titrate continuous IV cardiac medications (e.g. antiarrhythmics, inotropes, vasopressors, antihypertensives), but if a higher level of intervention is warranted, patients are transferred to the operating room or an intensive care unit setting.

All patients undergoing cesarean delivery receive intraoperative 5-lead ECG telemetry and pulse oximetry monitoring continued until two hours postpartum as routine perioperative care.

Baseline demographics, type of CVD, comorbid conditions, and medications were collected at the patient’s first visit. Detailed cardiac (including antepartum arrhythmia), obstetric, and fetal data were collected prospectively at each clinic visit and during inpatient admissions leading up to delivery. Antepartum arrhythmias were recorded if they were sustained for > 30 s, were symptomatic, or were associated with positive Holter monitor findings. Positive Holter findings were defined as > 5% burden of either supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) or ventricular ectopic beats, non-sustained SVT or ventricular tachycardia (VT) (≥ 3 beats at > 100 bpm) and/or sustained SVT or VT (> 30 s), sick sinus syndrome (symptomatic sinus bradycardia < 60 bpm or symptomatic sinus pause > 3 s), symptomatic Mobitz Type 1 second-degree atrioventricular (AV) block, symptomatic or asymptomatic Mobitz Type 2 second-degree AV block, or complete heart block.

For our secondary aim of evaluating associations between the receipt of intrapartum telemetry and cardiovascular outcomes of interest, we compared cardiovascular outcomes between those receiving intrapartum telemetry and those receiving routine care, stratified by mode of delivery (Fig. 1). Mode of delivery was categorized as vaginal delivery, planned cesarean delivery, or unplanned cesarean delivery. Unplanned cesarean delivery included patients who were initially planned for a vaginal birth but required a cesarean delivery due to obstetric or neonatal complications during labor. Cardiovascular outcomes of interest were recorded in the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. Antepartum events occurring prior to enrollment in the STORCC registry were included in our analysis. Postpartum was defined as beginning after delivery of the placenta following childbirth. Cardiovascular outcomes included congestive heart failure (CHF) diagnosed by physical exam and requiring diuresis, cerebrovascular event, new significant valvular dysfunction, cardiac arrest, endocarditis, aortic dissection, and clinically significant arrhythmic events (CSAEs). CSAEs were defined as arrhythmias requiring medical intervention or implantable cardioverter defibrillator therapy and were identified through review of the antepartum medical record and intra- and postpartum telemetry monitoring notes by a single attending cardiologist from the cardiology team (F.M.W.) who was not blinded to telemetry status since this was evident from chart review.

The research reported in this paper adhered to STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as frequency (%) and continuous variables as median [25th, 75th percentile]. Comparisons of clinical covariates between patients with and without a recommendation for intrapartum telemetry were performed using logistic regression analysis, with standard errors accounting for the correlation among multiple pregnancies within the same patient. Similar analyses were used to compare cardiovascular outcomes for patients with vaginal deliveries with and without telemetry, and for those with vaginal delivery and telemetry versus cesarean delivery. Analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas).

Comparing pregnancies with and without recommended telemetry, there was no significant difference in maternal age at delivery (32.7 [28.6, 36.1] years vs. 32.9 [29.6, 36.1] years, P = 0.86), gestational age at delivery (38.6 [37.1, 39.1] weeks vs. 38.9 [37.4, 39.3] weeks, P = 0.75), nulliparity (47.9 vs. 49.8%, P = 0.73) or type of delivery (P = 0.16). Type of CVD differed between the two groups (P < 0.001), with the recommended telemetry group having a higher proportion of subjects with arrhythmia (Supplemental Table 2) and a lower proportion of subjects with CHD and connective tissue disorders.

Recommendation for telemetry was made more often in pregnancies in which an arrhythmia occurred during the prenatal period (26.6 vs. 2.1%, P < 0.001) and in patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class \ge 2 at enrollment (12.8 vs. 5.3%, P = 0.008). These patients were more likely to be taking cardiac medications, specifically beta blockers (55.3 vs. 27.9%, P < 0.001), anti-arrhythmic medications (16.0 vs. 2.1%, P < 0.001), diuretics (6.4 vs. 2.2%, P = 0.051), calcium channel blockers (8.5 vs. 2.1%, P = 0.002), or heparin (9.6 vs. 4.2%, P = 0.037) (Table 1).

Telemetry recommendations were based on each patient’s clinical presentation at the time of delivery planning, rather than on prior telemetry use. Among 142 subjects with two or more pregnancies included in the dataset, 16 were recommended for telemetry during the first pregnancy, and of these, 50% received a telemetry recommendation in their second pregnancy. Among the 126 subjects without a telemetry recommendation in their first pregnancy, 6.4% were recommended for telemetry in their second pregnancy.

Seventy-seven (10.7%) patients ultimately underwent vaginal delivery with intrapartum telemetry monitoring. The most common reason for telemetry was high-risk anatomy or physiology (31.2%), followed by arrhythmia prior to pregnancy (27.3%) and arrhythmia within pregnancy (22.1%). Other indications included cardiac symptoms (9.1%), provider discomfort (9.1%), and other cardiac events during pregnancy (1.3%). Of these 77 patients, 68 had a prenatal plan for telemetry. Unplanned telemetry was initiated during labor and delivery admission in the remaining nine pregnancies. Reasons for adding telemetry in these cases included provider discomfort (66.7%), patient-reported cardiac symptoms (22.2%), and high-risk anatomy or physiology (11.1%) (Table 2). Across all pregnancies where telemetry was applied, including vaginal and cesarean deliveries, only one patient (0.3%) had a CSAE detected by telemetry. The CSAE consisted of 2 runs of SVT occurring in the postpartum period, once within 10 min of delivery and again 4 h after delivery. The patient had a history of CHD, including an atrial septal defect and prior atrioventricular canal and mitral valve repairs during infancy. Due to a history of recurrent SVT, there was a prenatal plan for intrapartum telemetry and neuraxial analgesia, and they had been maintained on a beta blocker and an anti-arrhythmic medication throughout pregnancy. Telemetry was initially planned for six hours postpartum but was extended to 24 h following the SVT episodes, both of which were successfully managed with IV metoprolol and vagal maneuvers. The patient was asymptomatic and hemodynamically stable throughout their intrapartum and postpartum course.

Discussion

Current recommendations for intrapartum cardiac telemetry use in patients with CVD are nonspecific. It is generally advised that patients with complex CVD and those with a history of tachyarrhythmias should be monitored with five-lead electrocardiographic telemetry during labor due to the risk of peripartum arrhythmias [3, 11–13]. In our cohort of 717 pregnancies in patients with CVD, telemetry was recommended in just 13.1% of pregnancies. This proportion is substantially lower than at other academic centers where rates of intrapartum telemetry use have been reported as high as 54.8% [14]. At our institution, telemetry was typically reserved for patients with previously diagnosed arrhythmias, pregnancies complicated by new or symptomatic prenatal arrhythmias, pregnancies experiencing cardiac symptoms or functional limitation (NYHA Class ≥ 2), and individuals prescribed cardiac medications. However, even among patients meeting these criteria, telemetry was not universally applied. Telemetry was recommended for only 28% of those with preexisting arrhythmia and 26.7% of those who were NYHA Class ≥ 2. This finding suggests that providers at our institution have adopted a more selective approach to telemetry, reserving it for a narrower subset of patients than all of those with guideline-specified risk factors.

Adverse maternal cardiovascular complications occurred in 7.3% of pregnancies, with the majority of complications arising in the antepartum (55.6%) and postpartum (42.6%) periods of pregnancy. Complication rates were low across all delivery types. Therefore, we were unable to detect significant associations between use of intrapartum telemetry and antepartum, intrapartum, or postpartum cardiovascular outcomes. However, higher rates of CHF and CSAE were observed in patients who had vaginal delivery with telemetry compared to those without telemetry. These differences may reflect underlying patient risk profiles that influenced provider decisions to initiate monitoring. Intrapartum cardiovascular events were rare, with only one event (0.4%) identified in a patient with CHF undergoing cesarean delivery. Additionally, only one CSAE was detected by telemetry during the intrapartum or immediate postpartum periods, representing just 0.3% of all telemetry-monitored pregnancies.

The low yield of telemetry-detected intrapartum arrhythmias observed in our study is consistent with findings from a recent retrospective analysis which reported a similarly low (0.8%) rate of significant arrhythmias detected by continuous ECG monitoring during the intrapartum period [14]. Our findings also correlate with emerging evidence suggesting that the intrapartum period may carry less risk than previously believed. A recent study examined 1,315 pregnancies in individuals with heart disease and found the rate of serious arrhythmia or cardiac arrest during the intrapartum and postpartum periods to be only 0.2% (0.5% including myocardial infarction) [7]. Similarly, the CARPREG II study, which analyzed 1,983 pregnancies in mothers with CVD, found that only 4% of serious cardiac events occurred during labor and delivery [6]. These collective findings highlight the rarity of significant intrapartum cardiac events even among patients with pre-existing CVD and suggest that broad application of routine telemetry has limited value for detecting or altering the management of cardiovascular complications during labor and delivery.

Consideration of telemetry use in this population should balance clinical benefit with healthcare resource utilization and cost. A single-center study of patients receiving telemetry in noncritical care units found that only 24% of telemetry days met practice standards, with telemetry days costing $30.28 more than non-telemetry days [15]. Similarly, a multicenter study found that 35% of telemetry days in noncritical care units lacked clinical indications, with only 3.1 arrhythmia events observed for every 100 days of non-indicated monitoring; all of these events were clinically insignificant. This study estimated that eliminating non-indicated telemetry could save at least $53 per patient and up to $250,000 annually per hospital [16]. While these results are highly variable and do not consider the unique needs of mothers with CVD, they highlight a widespread over-reliance on cardiac telemetry in hospitals. Indeed, in our cohort, we identified four pregnancies where telemetry was initiated during labor without clinical indication or clear rationale, suggesting that application may have been driven more by provider comfort than by clinical indication.

Furthermore, unwarranted telemetry use may reduce access to necessary high-risk care. In patients with cardiac disease, obstetric complications such as hemorrhage and infection pose a substantially greater risk than adverse cardiac events during labor [17–19]. In many institutions, the lack of telemetry capabilities on labor and delivery floors necessitates transferring obstetric patients to intensive care units (ICU), where staff may have limited experience managing obstetric complications [20, 21]. This shift can strain ICU resources and place an additional burden on critical care services [21]. Broad recommendations for telemetry in patients with CVD may also place an unnecessary strain on subspecialty care centers, such as Level III and IV facilities designated by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Society for Maternal–Fetal Medicine, which are equipped for continuous cardiac monitoring but may become overburdened by managing pregnancies that do not meet high-risk criteria [20].

Our data suggest that intrapartum cardiac telemetry may be overused in pregnant patients with CVD who are at lower risk given the low number of telemetry-identified CSAEs that occurred in our study population. While telemetry remains an important tool for pregnant patients with high-risk CVD, particularly in the immediate postpartum period when risk is increased due to rapid hemodynamic shifts, and did identify instances of premature ventricular complexes, ventricular bigeminy, and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia which can trigger additional evaluation, medication adjustment, or cardiology follow-up, serious arrhythmic events were rare in this cohort. Reserving intrapartum telemetry for such patients with active arrhythmias and complex CVD may help reduce healthcare spending and increase patient comfort during delivery. Alternative heart rate monitoring could be considered for patients with non-critical disease or without active arrhythmias. In our practice, pulse oximetry is relied upon heavily as a low-cost, minimally invasive option to monitor cardiac status during labor and delivery [22, 23]. Future studies are needed to better identify which patients are at the highest risk for intrapartum arrhythmias and to refine criteria for when telemetry is necessary in this population.

Limitations

There are several limitations to the current study. First, telemetry monitoring was not uniformly applied to all patients. At our institution, telemetry is typically continued during active labor and for six hours postpartum. However, telemetry monitoring was discontinued in some patients after active labor or before the full six-hour postpartum period if no abnormal rhythm was detected. This inconsistency may have led to undetected asymptomatic arrhythmias due to shorter monitoring durations. Additionally, although the STORCC initiative was prospective, some of our results relied on retrospective collection of telemetry monitoring notes from the medical record. It is possible that some arrhythmias were not recorded but very unlikely that an arrhythmia meeting our definition for a CSAE would not be documented. Subjects included in the STORCC initiative were closely monitored during pregnancy, which may have reduced the number of adverse outcomes at our center. Due to the low number of cardiovascular outcome events in our cohort, statistical power was not sufficient to detect small differences in outcome rates.

With only 6.3% of patients having NYHA Class ≥ 2 at enrollment, the applicability of our findings to patients with more severe cardiac symptoms during delivery may be limited. Finally, since subjects were recruited from a tertiary care center with multidisciplinary teams available, there is a potential that our study population is skewed, though it would most likely be skewed toward more complex patients.

Conclusions

Pregnancies in patients with a preexisting diagnosis of isolated arrhythmia, new or symptomatic arrhythmias during pregnancy, NYHA class \ge 2, and use of cardiac medications are more likely to be recommended for cardiac telemetry during labor and delivery. However, intrapartum cardiac complications were rare in our study cohort compared to antepartum and postpartum complications. Of all telemetry-monitored deliveries, only one CSAE (0.3%) was detected in the immediate postpartum period. These findings indicate an opportunity to refine criteria for prescribing perioperative monitoring for individuals with CVD who undergo pregnancy. Further studies are needed to determine which patients would benefit most from cardiac telemetry and the optimal timing for its initiation.
