The impact of pulmonary artery invasion on bronchial artery embolization in treating hemoptysis in lung cancer
Authors: Yang Zi, Baoqi Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effect of pulmonary artery invasion on bronchial artery embolization (BAE) for the treatment of hemoptysis caused by lung cancer. A single-center retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 58 lung cancer patients who underwent BAE treatment for hemoptysis between July 2019 and July 2023. Based on preoperative CT angiography results, patients were divided into two groups: the pulmonary artery invasion group (n = 40) and the non-pulmonary artery invasion group (n = 18). The clinical characteristics, technical success rate, clinical success rate, complication rate, hemoptysis recurrence rate, and other factors were compared between the two groups. The prognostic factors for hemoptysis-free survival were analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards model. The results showed that, compared with the non-pulmonary artery invasion group, there was no significant difference in age (65.6 ± 8.2 vs. 67.2 ± 9.0, P = 0.52), gender (P = 0.67), Maximal tumor size (59.7 ± 26.0 vs. 51.6 ± 21.1, P = 0.25), the clinical success rate (85.0 % vs. 100.0 %, P = 0.16), the postoperative complication rate (15.0 % vs. 22.2 %) and so on between the two groups. However, the recurrence rate of hemoptysis one month after BAE was significantly higher in the pulmonary artery invasion group (60.0 % vs. 11.1 %, P < 0.01). The median hemoptysis-free survival time in the pulmonary artery invasion group was 28.5 days (IQR 54.3 days), which was significantly lower than 420.0 days (IQR 637.5 days) in the non-pulmonary artery invasion group (P < 0.01). Multivariate analysis showed that pulmonary artery invasion was an independent risk factor for hemoptysis recurrence after BAE (OR=18.46, 95 % CI: 6.37–53.49, P < 0.01). The study concluded that pulmonary artery invasion has a significant impact on hemoptysis recurrence after BAE in lung cancer.

Introduction

Hemoptysis that can be life-threatening is one of the most common and severe complication of lung cancer [1]. Bronchial artery embolization (BAE) is a minimally invasive intervention that has become the preferred method for controlling hemoptysis in lung cancer patients [2]. However, the clinical efficacy of BAE in treating tumor-related hemoptysis is relatively low, or the postoperative recurrence rate of hemoptysis is relatively high. Current studies suggest that this phenomenon is mainly caused by improper selection of embolic materials or incomplete embolization. However, based on our clinical experience, pulmonary artery invasion is a factor contributing to the low clinical efficacy of BAE for tumors and the high recurrence rate of hemoptysis.

Currently, there is limited research on the effect of pulmonary artery invasion on BAE in treating hemoptysis caused by lung cancer, and the conclusions remain unclear. Some researchers believe that pulmonary artery invasion may increase the risk of hemoptysis recurrence after BAE [3], [4], while other studies suggest that for patients with pulmonary artery invasion, a combination of BAE and pulmonary artery embolization (PAE) may be needed [5]. Therefore, exploring the effect of pulmonary artery invasion on the treatment of hemoptysis in lung cancer patients using BAE is of significant clinical importance. This study aims to evaluate the effect of pulmonary artery invasion on the efficacy and safety of BAE for treating lung cancer-related hemoptysis through a retrospective analysis, providing a reference for clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was a single-center retrospective study, including patients who underwent bronchial artery embolization (BAE) for the first time due to "tumor-related hemoptysis" in our hospital from July 2019 to July 2023. All cases were consecutive patients who presented during this period, met the inclusion criteria, with no selective exclusion or omission. And this study was approved by the ethics investigation committee.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Pathologically diagnosed as primary lung cancer; (2) Presented with hemoptysis of varying degrees; (3) Received BAE treatment; (4) Completed enhanced chest computed tomography(CT) examination before the operation. Exclusion criteria: (1) Loss to follow-up; (2) Incomplete clinical data; (3) Patients without hemoptysis; (4) Patients who did not undergo embolization due to no angiographic abnormalities. After screening, a total of 58 eligible patients were included (50 males and 8 females, with an average age of 66.1 years ± 8.4 years [standard deviation]).

Preoperative phase

Preoperative enhanced chest CT images of each patient were assessed by two radiologists to determine whether the tumor had invaded the wall or lumen of the pulmonary artery (as shown in Fig. 1 and 2). According to the results of the CT images, the patients were divided into the pulmonary artery traversing group (PAI，n = 40) and non-pulmonary artery traversing group(NPAI，n = 18). The clinical data of the patients were collected, including age, gender, pathology, stage, amount of hemoptysis, previous history of hemoptysis, tumor characteristics, etc.Fig. 1CT Image: Pulmonary Artery Invasion. BAE procedure

All patients underwent BAE treatment using the Seldinger technique via the femoral artery. The right femoral artery was punctured and a vascular sheath (Terumo) was inserted. A 5-F pigtail catheter (COOK) was advanced through a 0.035-inch hydrophilic guidewire (COOK) into the ascending aorta to obtain diagnostic angiography of the aortic arch and descending aorta (injecting 30 ml of contrast agent at a rate of 15 ml per second) to identify the bronchial arteries and other systemic collateral vessels. Selective angiography of the bronchial arteries was performed using a shaped 5-F angiographic catheter (Cobra, left gastric artery catheter; COOK). If no abnormal bronchial arteries were found, the non-bronchial systemic arteries were carefully examined according to the location of the primary lung cancer. Embolization treatment was targeted at the following angiographic findings: tumor staining, abnormal thickening and tortuosity of the arteries, arteriovenous fistulas, contrast agent extravasation, and pseudoaneurysms. With the coaxial catheter technique, a 2.7 F microcatheter (Terumo) was introduced distally. The choice of embolic agent was determined by the surgeon performing the procedure. The embolic materials used for BAE were 350–550 μm or 550–750 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles (Hengrui), 350–560 μm or 560–700 μm gelatin sponge particles (Alicon), or microcoils (Cook, extended length: 4 cm, 5 cm, diameter: 2 mm). Embolic materials are based on 350–550 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles. During the procedure, gelatin sponge particles, coils, or larger polyvinyl alcohol particles may be added as appropriate according to the embolization status. If the slowdown of contrast medium flow rate is not significant after embolization with 350–550 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles, 550–750 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles can be added. Alternatively, if there is a risk of ectopic embolism due to reflux of embolic materials during the procedure, gelatin sponge particles may be added to the 350–550 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles to reduce the probability of ectopic embolism. Postoperative phase

After the operation, the technical success rate of the procedure, the clinical success rate of the procedure, the rate of hemoptysis control, the incidence of complications, the recurrence time of hemoptysis, and the survival period were evaluated during the follow-up.

Definitions and data analysis

The definition for technical success is that the embolic agent is precisely deposited in the target bronchial artery, and the blood flow of the target vessel is completely or nearly completely interrupted. The definition for clinical success is that the hemoptysis of preoperation completely disappear or significantly improve after operation immediately. Complications were classified as minor or major according to the guidelines of the Society of Interventional Radiology.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0 software. The normality of continuous data was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data with normal and non-normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median, interquartile range (IQR), respectively. The t-test was used for the comparison between groups of variables with normal distribution and homogeneity of variance. The natural logarithm was taken for data that did not conform to a normal distribution. If the data met the criteria of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance after statistical transformation, the t-test would be continued. The non-parametric rank sum test was performed for variables with non-normal distribution. Enumeration data were expressed as the number of cases and rates, and the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was used for the comparison between groups. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the overall survival rate and the survival rate without hemoptysis. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to analyze the prognostic factors associated with the survival time without hemoptysis. The factors included in the multivariate analysis were those that were significant or showed a significant trend (P < 0.1) in the univariate model. The results were expressed as hazard ratio (HR) and 95 % confidence interval (CI). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Malignant lung tumors can cause angiogenesis, vascular destruction, coagulation dysfunction, and tissue necrosis, leading to hemoptysis in about 30 % of lung cancer patients [6]. In severe cases, conservative treatment alone can result in a 50 % mortality rate [7], [8]. Hemoptysis caused by lung cancer is typically one of the most challenging clinical issues due to its complex pathogenesis, low remission rates, and high recurrence rates [6].

Although BAE has been considered one of the safest and most effective methods for treating lung cancer-related hemoptysis, the recurrence rate of hemoptysis after BAE is typically between 10 % and 30 % [9]. Related studies suggest that recurrence after BAE is primarily due to tumor progression, incomplete embolization, vascular recanalization, and the formation of collateral circulation [3], [4], [10]. Our clinical experience indicates that pulmonary artery invasion might affect hemoptysis recurrence after BAE. We retrieved relevant literature. There are relatively few studies on the recurrence of hemoptysis after BAE due to pulmonary artery invasion. The recent report by Hanotin et al. [4] suggested that recurrent hemoptysis in lung cancer is closely related to pulmonary artery invasion. Meanwhile, the study by Claudinot et al. [5] demonstrated the safety and effectiveness of pulmonary artery embolization (PA embolization) in treating hemoptysis caused by lung cancer. The results of this study indicate that pulmonary artery invasion significantly affects the efficacy of BAE in treating hemoptysis in lung cancer patients. The overall recurrence rate was 44.8 % one month after BAE, slightly higher than the 10 % - 30 % reported in previous studies. This is mainly related to the following factors: First, the proportion of patients with pulmonary artery invasion included in this study is relatively high (69.0 %). Pulmonary artery invasion increases the complexity of tumor-feeding vessels. Bronchial artery embolization (BAE) can only reduce bleeding from the systemic circulation, but cannot improve the invaded pulmonary arteries, resulting in low clinical success rate and high recurrence rate of hemoptysis after embolization. Second, the definition of hemoptysis recurrence in this study is stricter; for example, the presence of fresh blood in sputum is considered as a recurrence of hemoptysis. In addition, some literatures have strict requirements on the coagulation function of enrolled patients, while this study has no special requirements on coagulation. These factors together lead to a higher recurrence rate in this study compared with the literature. Specifically, the recurrence rate within one month was 60 % in the PAI group and 11.1 % in the NPAI group. Both the hemoptysis - free survival time and the median hemoptysis - free survival time in the PAI group were significantly lower than those in the NPAI group. Through univariate and multivariate analyses, we can also see that pulmonary artery invasion is an independent risk factor for recurrent hemoptysis in lung cancer after BAE.

We believe that the main reasons why pulmonary artery invasion affects BAE in treating hemoptysis in lung cancer are as follows: Firstly, although lung cancer is mainly supplied by the bronchial artery, for large, peripheral, and poorly differentiated tumors, the pulmonary artery can participate in blood supply [11]. Secondly, lung cancer cells may involve the branches of the pulmonary artery through local invasion or lymphatic spread. Even if imaging examinations (such as CTA) do not clearly show the involvement of the pulmonary artery, tiny blood vessels may have been invaded at the pathological level [12]. This occult invasion may lead to the continuous blood supply to the tumor through the branches of the pulmonary artery after BAE, thus triggering the recurrence of hemoptysis. On the other hand, due to the dual blood supply characteristics of lung cancer, we speculate that the sudden decrease in the pressure of the bronchial artery after BAE will correspondingly increase the pressure in the pulmonary artery [13]. Moreover, the end of the pulmonary artery is fragile due to invasion, resulting in the rupture of the pulmonary artery, rendering BAE ineffective in stopping bleeding and causing the recurrence of hemoptysis.

In this study, we also found that pleural invasion is significantly associated with the recurrence of hemoptysis after BAE. Pleural invasion may increase the risk of hemoptysis recurrence through the following mechanisms: Firstly, pleural invasion usually indicates that the tumor has advanced to an advanced stage. The tumor tissue may invade the subpleural vascular network, leading to damage to the vascular structure and an increased risk of bleeding [14]. Secondly, pleural invasion may be accompanied by an inflammatory response and neovascularization. These newly formed blood vessels have an incomplete structure and are prone to rupture and bleeding [15]. In addition, pleural invasion may also affect local hemodynamics and increase the formation of collateral circulation [16], thus providing a new source of blood supply for the recurrence of hemoptysis. Moreover, since the newly formed blood vessels are thin, it is difficult to detect the newly formed tumor - feeding blood vessels during angiography. In the other hand, tumor invasion of the pleura often involves the intercostal arteries, which are frequently communicating with the spinal arteries. During embolization, incomplete embolization may occur due to concerns that embolizing the spinal arteries could result in paraplegia. This finding suggests that in clinical practice, for lung cancer patients with pleural invasion, during BAE, it is necessary to fully search for the tumor - feeding blood vessels. When injecting contrast agents, the injection dose and flow rate should be appropriately increased to fully visualize the tiny tumor - feeding blood vessels. If intercostal arteries are found to be involved in tumor blood supply, on the one hand, superselective catheterization of the tumor-feeding vessels is required; on the other hand, embolic materials must be carefully selected to ensure complete embolization of the tumor vessels while avoiding ectopic embolization of the spinal arteries that could cause paraplegia. After BAE, follow - up and monitoring should be strengthened, especially for patients with pleural invasion. A more aggressive comprehensive treatment strategy, such as combination of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy, may be required to control tumor progression and reduce the risk of hemoptysis recurrence. Future research can further explore the specific mechanisms between pleural invasion and the recurrence of hemoptysis after BAE, and evaluate the potential effects of interventions targeting pleural invasion on reducing the recurrence rate of hemoptysis.

Globally, gelatin sponge has become a traditional embolic material for hemoptysis due to its low cost and ease of use. However, its solubility in human tissues results in a high recurrence rate of hemoptysis. In contrast, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and N-Butyl-2-Cyanoacrylate Glue (NBCA) have gradually replaced gelatin sponge [17]. Additionally, Miyano [18] proposed that embolization of bronchial arteries with coils, especially interlocking detachable coils (IDC), yields better outcomes, as it reduces not only time and economic costs but also the recurrence rate of hemoptysis. However, our study showed that the use of different embolic materials had no significant effect on the recurrence of hemoptysis. This may be attributed to the fact that in our study, polyvinyl alcohol embolic microspheres were used as the base embolic material, with gelatin sponge particles and coils serving as supplementary embolic materials.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, as a single - center retrospective study, there may be selection bias. Secondly, the sample size is relatively small, which may affect the stability of the results. Thirdly, The embolic materials used intraoperatively were not entirely the same. Although all were based on 300–500 μm polyvinyl alcohol particles, some were mixed with gelatin sponge particles. It remains unclear whether the self-dissolution of gelatin sponge particles contributes to the recurrence of postoperative hemoptysis. In addition, the follow - up time is short, and it is impossible to evaluate the long - term efficacy of pulmonary artery invasion on BAE. In the future, large - sample, multi - center, and prospective studies are needed to further verify the conclusions of this study and explore optimized treatment plans for patients with pulmonary artery invasion.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that pulmonary artery invasion may represent an independent risk factor influencing the treatment outcome. This finding suggests that in clinical practice, for lung cancer patients with hemoptysis who have pulmonary artery invasion, individualized treatment plans need to be developed. These may include combined pulmonary artery embolization or other alternative treatment methods. Future research should further explore the hemodynamic characteristics of pulmonary artery invasion and its potential impact on BAE treatment, so as to guide the formulation of individualized treatment strategies and improve treatment efficacy and safety.
