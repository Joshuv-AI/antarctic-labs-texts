Protocol for developing digital 3D zoological collections for research and academic training in a biodiversity global crisis scenario
Authors: Constanza Delgado, Amadis Lillo, Carolina Cuevas, Laura Tavera, Emmanuel Vega, Felipe Gamonal, Jeremías Norambuena, Cristián E. Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDigital biological collections are an important source of information for preserving natural heritage and serve as a scientific-pedagogical tool. Here, we present the first protocol for the 3D digitization of zoological collections in Chile. We describe the steps and challenges in scanning and modeling specimens from the UdeC Museum of Zoology, together with procedures for creating the digital repository of biological collections. This protocol contributes to greater accessibility of biological information, thereby supporting policy decision-making for biodiversity conservation.

Before you begin

3D digitization and the information associated with a physical specimen exponentially increase the value of the specimens in a collection1 and play a key role in the dissemination of natural heritage and research. Massive data on digital platforms also enhances access to biological collections, strengthens the role of museums in society and public service,2,3,4 and supports the education of future professionals.5 An example of the relevance of digitizing museum collections is the publication of entomological data from the Zoology Museum of Sapienza University of Rome on the GBIF platform.6 This dataset, from the order Odonata, comprises 2.406 specimens and includes georeferenced records, high resolution photographs of the specimens, and an interactive web platform that allows users to explore the collection, facilitating access to this information for researchers, students, and enthusiasts.

Open access to digitized information enhances research across areas such as taxonomy, ecology, evolution, and comparative anatomy, promoting the analysis of large datasets and2,7 the transfer of knowledge.4 This fosters the development of multiple academic fields, promotes the emergence of new researchers and scientists, and reduces the financial, social, institutional, and academic gaps that restrict access to scientific collections.1,8,9 Moreover, these digitized collections will contribute to a global understanding of rare species and taxa of critical importance for conservation biology, informing evidence-based decisions in the face of the global biodiversity crisis. This is urgent and necessary, given that global extinction rates are 100–1,000 times higher than the natural background rate,10 nearly one million species face extinction within decades,11 freshwater biodiversity has declined by more than 80% since 197012 and an alarming decline of 73% of wildlife between 1970 and 2020.13

In Chile, biological collections currently face certain limitations due to regulations and new bioethical guidelines that restrict or regulate the use and collection of biological material, especially considering endemic or endangered species. This limits the use of biological samples as a teaching tool for research and academic training in the fields of natural sciences and biology. Therefore, the development of this digitization protocol for zoological collections can help fill this gap by enhancing accessibility to biological specimens by displaying exact 3D replicas of the originals, reducing the need to handle rare or very fragile specimens.3,14 However, local biological collections from isolated and unique places on the planet generally do not have the financial resources to fund state-of-the-art equipment such as 3D scanners that digitize biological material, as costs can exceed $20,000 to access state-of-the-art equipment. Photogrammetry-based approaches have therefore been proposed as an accessible alternative for generating accurate three-dimensional models of biological specimens.15 In its place, free, easily accessible, and applicable applications such as Polycam and Agisoft Metashape, among others, promote and facilitate 3D digitization, creating high-quality models for display in digital repositories.16

Innovation

The biological collections of the Zoology Museum of the University of Concepción (MZUC-UCCC) constitute the largest repository of faunal diversity in Chile, housing 15,060 native and exotic species, as well as various endangered specimens that are difficult to observe and collect nowadays. Consequently, the use of zoological collection digitized according to this protocol provides an innovative educational tool that complements the knowledge acquired in theory and the study of current and past biodiversity. Direct access to these specimens from anywhere in the world with internet connection, through digital platforms, promotes the extraction and availability of diverse information (biological, ecological, physiological, and behavioral) from a single individual, for the development of taxonomic, ecological, evolutionary, and biogeographic studies, as well as the development of skills (motivation, collaborative work, critical thinking, and problem solving) and the training of future scientists through new communication and information technologies.17,18 Therefore, it is urgent and necessary to advance in the interaction with three-dimensional information from biological collections to enhance the educational and scientific functions of biological collections in Chile. And lay the foundations for a digital reference collection as a primary source of information for research and the training of new generations of scientists. This work focuses on developing a 3D digitization protocol for the bird, mammal, and insect collections of the MZUC-CCC, providing valuable and interesting information for other museums and researchers undertaking similar work in hidden local collections worldwide, which can help confront the current biodiversity crisis.

Programs and applications used for 3D scanning

The PolyCam application, available in web browsers and on iOS and Android devices, is an accessible and user-friendly tool due to its intuitive interface and efficient processing times.19,20 This application allows three-dimensional models to be generated using two main procedures: 1) photogrammetry and 2) LiDAR scanning.

Photogrammetry involves capturing multiple images of an object from different angles, which are then processed using algorithms to generate a model. To ensure accurate reconstruction, images should be captured with sufficient overlap between consecutive photographs (typically 60–80%), allowing the software to identify common features across images and correctly align them. Higher overlap percentages can improve the accuracy of the resulting 3D models,21 but they also increase the number of images required.

Unlike photogrammetry, LiDAR scanning uses laser light pulses that strike and reflect off an object. LiDAR-equipped devices can calculate the distance to the object using the speed of light to collect a point cloud, which is then transformed into a three-dimensional model.

In its free mode, Polycam allows you to generate up to 20 models from a maximum of 100 images each. In contrast, its subscription plans allow you to generate unlimited models using up to 2,000 images, which increases the resolution, accuracy, and level of detail in the results. Photogrammetry is chosen for this protocol because, in the context of digitizing biological collections, it offers higher-quality texture capture and color accuracy.

Another application that can be used for photogrammetric reconstruction, similar to Polycam, is Agisoft Metashape, a software available in standard and professional versions. However, this software requires unmodified images, preferably in RAW format, captured with high-resolution digital cameras. These images must be acquired using controlled camera settings, such as ISO, focal length, and aperture, ideally keeping these parameters constant throughout the image series.16 Additionally, before processing the images to generate the model, it is often necessary to apply masking to remove the background. Agisoft Metashape offers greater control over reconstruction parameters and supports larger image sets, which can improve the resolution, accuracy, and level of detail of the resulting models. However, this workflow requires more specialized equipment, controlled capture conditions, and greater computational resources, resulting in longer processing times. In contrast, Polycam provides a more simplified and automated workflow.

Due to the project timeline and the need to generate many models within a limited timeframe, Polycam was selected as the primary software for generating 3D models. Its simplified and automated workflow made it possible to generate 3D models of sufficient quality for the visualization and digital documentation of the specimens, while significantly reducing processing time compared to Agisoft Metashape.

3D digitization of specimens

Timing: Approximately 4 months (including the bird, insect, and mammal collections) 2.Bird Collection.a.Preparation and assembly:i.It is recommended to comb and align the plumage with fine-bristled combs, remove any traces of dust, and, if necessary to reduce excessive shine that may cause reflections, apply a light layer of cornstarch.ii.A total of ten bird samples is selected, ranging in size from 7 to 16 cm in length and 2 to 25 cm in width. Each selected sample is positioned on a 360° rotating stand (Figure 1).Figure 1Schematic diagram of the 3D scanning of bird specimens at the MZUC- CCCb.Use of background: a white background is used to provide consistent contrast, making the specimen’s colors more distinguishable and facilitating the interpretation of the three-dimensional structure and texture quality.c.Light adjustment: white light is used at a distance of 1 to 15 cm, ensuring that the light is directed at the specimen from the front or at an angle.d.Photography: a minimum of 100 photographs is taken of each specimen with 108 MP cellphone camera using photogrammetry, a technique that consists of taking multiple 2D photographs from all possible angles.22,23 These photographs are taken using a rotating base, which is rotated a total of three times, and a tripod that remains static during the rotation of the stand. Note: It is important to note that, depending on the individual's size, the distance from the device to the individual varies, with the entire individual being visible within the frame. 3.Insect Collection.a.Preparation and assembly:i.Once the specimen is removed from its insectarium, its labels are removed, and it is mounted vertically on a pedestal, where an entomological needle is inserted into the insect on the metal tip of a support wire, which is held in place with plasticine (Figure 2).Figure 2Graphic diagram of the 3D scanning of insect specimens from the MZUC- CCCii.A total of ten insects samples were selected, ranging in size from 2 to 10 cm in length and 1 to 8 cm in width.iii.Manipulation: the specimen is then manipulated with entomological tweezers so that all body structures (legs, antennae, wings) are visible and not twisted).b.Use of background: The background and base are monochromatic, either black or white. It will depend on the colors of the insects but always use a monochromatic background. For example, for dark beetles we use a monochromatic white background, and for butterflies we use a monochromatic black background.c.Light adjustment: external cold-light sources are added to eliminate visual noise, homogenize the lighting, and reduce shadows cast on the insect.d.Photography: photographs are taken with cell phones and a Canon EOS Rebel camera. A minimum of 60 photographs for ten small insects (below 20 mm) and 100 photographs for longer insects (above 20 mm). Covering the entire body volume, including lateral, dorsal and ventral views, and using a rotating base and a fixed pedestal with a mobile camera (Figure 2).Figure 3Graphic diagram of the 3D scanning of mammal specimens at the MZUC- CCC CRITICAL: For specimens with higher hairiness (e.g. Bombus dahlbomii), a minimum of 100 photographs is taken from all angles. For these specimens we suggest the combination of two capture modalities (rotating base and a fixed pedestal) 4.Mammal Collection.a.Preparation and assembly:i.When selecting the specimens, body size, posture, and state of preservation are considered, making it easier to take photographs for 3D modelling.ii.A total of ten mammal samples are selected, ranging in size from 21 to 40 cm in length and 3 to 11 cm in width. Each selected sample is positioned on a 360° rotating white stand (Figure 3) which is covered with white cloth to prevent it from being detected by the scanner.b.Use of background: A station is set up for photographing with a white background.c.Light adjustment: White light is used at a distance of 10 to 20 cm, ensuring that details such as hair and whiskers.Note: The environment must be well illuminated, avoiding shadows, reflections and any element that may alter the capture of the photographs.d.Photography: each specimen is placed on the rotating base, which rotates slowly, taking a minimum of 50 photographs using a 108 MP cell phone camera, from all possible angles. The cell phone is kept stationary with a tripod while the photographs are taken (Figure 3).Note: The photographs are taken with an emphasis on areas that are more difficult to capture (e.g. the abdomen, armpits, and tail).CRITICAL: For larger specimens, it is more optimal to photograph in sections, covering smaller body structures such as the head, legs, and tail, so that the Polycam algorithm can capture as much information as possible for the 3D model.

13.Use of visual references.

Use of visual references.

Both the 3D modelling and digital texturing processes used visual references such as photographs, taxonomic guides, and audiovisual records of each species. These sources are essential to ensuring the morphological and chromatic fidelity of each represented species, thereby maintaining a balance between scientific rigor and artistic aesthetics in the digital recreation.14.3D model development form zero.The three-dimensional modelling of the species is carried out using a Wacom Intuos Draw digitizer tablet and ZBrush software (version 2025.3.0), which specializes in high-resolution digital sculpture. This program allows users to work like clay modelling in a digital environment, offering sculpting, subdivision, and manipulation tools that facilitate the detailed representation of biological structures.a.Construction of a base mesh: the general shapes of the organism are defined and then progressed to sculpting proportions and main morphological features. Subsequently, fine anatomical details, such as folds, scales, feathers, and surface textures are incorporated using digital tools.b.Optimization of 3D models: the digitized specimen is optimized through a process called retopology, which reduces the polygon density without compromising detail fidelity and facilitates subsequent digital painting or texturing.15.Digital texturing.Texturing is developed using a Wacom Intuos Draw digitizing tablet and Substance Painter, a software designed for applying realistic materials and surfaces to three-dimensional models.a.The optimized mesh is imported from ZBrush.b.The UV coordinates and base materials are configured.c.Layers of color are applied to this structure to replicate the species’ natural coloring, along with roughness, gloss, and relief maps to give the surface greater realism (Data S1).Note: This procedure allows for the simulation of high-precision biological textures, resulting in a finish visually close to the original specimen.

Expected outcomes

This protocol is expected to be a useful tool for developing 3D digital collection repositories, such as that of the Museum of Zoology, hosted on the Sketchfab platform (sketchfab.com/MuseoZoologiaUdec) [Figure 10]. This is the first repository in Chile specifically dedicated to native fauna and species facing conservation issues, which does not require a large amount of financial resources and is relatively accessible to develop this type of digital resource for museums and local collections. In this way, digital biological collections promote the use of new communication and information technologies in education, serving as an interactive pedagogical tool for undergraduate programs in the natural sciences and biology. Limitations

Camera resolution: the cellphones or tablets must have a camera with at least 108 MP camera for the scanning process, ensuring that as much detail as possible is captured regarding the specimen’s morphological structure.

Light adjustment: depending on the taxonomic group, it is necessary to adjust the intensity, type, and distance of the light before digitization step to reduce shadows and ensure that details such as hair and whiskers are more visible.

Specimen size: the total length of each sample must have a minimum size of 4 cm; for specimens smaller than 4 cm, the Polycam application does not have the necessary resolution to generate the 3D model.

Morphological complexity: In the case of specimens with structures such as hairs, mouthparts, or detailed ornamentation, the Polycam application may not detect them. Therefore, it is recommended to perform the 3D scan using Metashape software.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

3D modelling of bird, insect and mammal specimens step: the Polycam application has certain limitations when interpreting some parts or structures of the object, due to the complexity or detail of the structure omitting them or modelling them incorrectly (e.g., hair, legs, wings, antennae or mouthparts).

Potential solution

It has been observed that taking more photographs from different angles can enhance the optical definition of three-dimensional depth and texture. Specifically for insects is recommended to use a LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) sensor. This is a measurement system based on scanning structures using a laser, where the emitter sends out a laser that bounces off the surface and is returned to the emitter, thus receiving distance information that is reflected in the depth and details of the 3D representations15,26.

Problem 2

Preparation and assembly step for the insect and mammal collections: The amount of light required depending on the specimen to be digitized, especially in brightly colored or iridescent insects and mammals might be critical.

Potential solution

It is advisable to use light moderately and not to place it directly on the specimen, but rather as an “umbrella” where the light bounces off light surfaces. In contrast, for mammal specimens, it is recommended to use good direct lighting.

Problem 3

Photography step: fur is complex to digitize, as hair scatters light, making it difficult to capture fine details and extremities such as tails and ears.

Potential solution

It is recommended to take at least 50 photos in these areas and to apply talcum powder to the surface to mattify the fur.
