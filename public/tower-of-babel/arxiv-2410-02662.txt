Axion-photon conversion down to the nonrelativistic regime
Clemente Smarra, Pierluca Carenza

Abstract
In the presence of a magnetic field, axions can convert into photons and vice versa. The phenomenology of the conversion is captured by a system of two coupled Klein-Gordon equations, which, assuming that the axion is relativistic, is usually recast into a pair of first-order Schrödinger-like equations. In such a limit, focusing on a constant magnetic field and plasma frequency, the equations admit an exact analytic solution. The relativistic limit significantly simplifies the calculations and, therefore, it is widely used in phenomenological applications. In this work, we discuss how to evaluate the axion-photon system evolution without relying on such relativistic approximation. In particular, we give an exact analytical solution, valid for any axion energy, in the case that both the magnetic field and plasma frequency are constant. Moreover, we devise an analytic perturbative expansion that allows for tracking the conversion probability in a slightly inhomogeneous magnetic field or plasma frequency, whose characteristic scale of variation is much larger than the typical axion-photon oscillation length. Finally, we discuss a case of resonant axion-photon conversion giving useful simplified formulae that might be directly applied to dark matter axions converting in neutron star magnetospheres.

27

\theta_{z}(z=0)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}\mathcal{O}^{-1}=-\theta_{z}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{O}^{-1}=-\theta_{zz}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}-\theta_{z}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\sim 0,

(35)

where
\theta_{zz}\equiv\partial_{z}^{2}\theta
and
\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{O}^{-1}\sim 0
, as it is higher order in the
\theta_{z}
expansion.
In total, Eq. (
32
) reads:

\begin{split}&\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}_{\text{diag}}\right]\Psi^{\prime}+2\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}\mathcal{O}^{-1}\partial_{z}\Psi^{\prime}+\\
&+\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{O}^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}=0\end{split}

(36)

or, using Eq. (
35
),

\begin{split}\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}_{\text{diag}}\right]\Psi^{\prime}+2\mathcal{O}\partial_{z}\mathcal{O}^{-1}\partial_{z}\Psi^{\prime}=0.\end{split}

(37)

This equation can be compared with Eq. (
6
), valid in the case of
\theta_{z}=0
. In the inhomogeneous setting, it is immediate to see that the components
A_{\parallel}^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
, encapsulated in the vector
\Psi^{\prime}
, will no longer be propagation eigenstates. In contrast to the constant-
\theta
case, they will mix, with a strength that will, in general, be proportional to
\theta_{z}
.
Writing the system in Eq. (
37
) explicitly, we have

\begin{split}&\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+Q_{\parallel}^{\prime}\right]A_{\parallel}^{\prime}-2\theta_{z}\partial_{z}a^{\prime}=0,\\
&\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+Q_{a}^{\prime}\right]a^{\prime}+2\theta_{z}\partial_{z}A^{\prime}=0,\end{split}

(38)

where we have understood the
z
dependence of
Q_{\parallel}^{\prime},Q_{a}^{\prime}
for clarity.
We can solve this set of equations perturbatively. In particular, we can take
Q_{\parallel}^{\prime}(z)\sim Q_{\parallel}^{\prime}(z=0)\equiv Q_{\parallel,0}^{\prime}
and
Q_{a}^{\prime}(z)\sim Q_{a}^{\prime}(z=0)\equiv Q_{a,0}^{\prime}
. At this point, we firstly solve the homogeneous system

\begin{split}&\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+Q_{\parallel,0}^{\prime}\right]A_{\parallel,h}^{\prime}=0,\\
&\left[\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+Q_{a,0}^{\prime}\right]a_{h}^{\prime}=0,\end{split}

(39)

which, using Eq. (
24
), gives

\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}(z)\\
a^{\prime}_{h}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\,e^{-i\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}z}\\
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}\,e^{-i\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}z}\end{pmatrix},

(40)

with
\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h},\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}
constant.
To be precise, we should have inserted a subscript “0” in
\Delta^{\prime}_{a,f}
and
\Delta^{\prime}_{\parallel,f}
to signal that they are computed at the initial point
z=0
with
Q_{a,0}^{\prime},Q_{\parallel,0}^{\prime}
. However, we omit this to avoid cluttering the notation.
Then we plug the solution of Eq. (
39
) in the
\theta_{z}
-dependent terms in Eq. (
38
). With this approximation, the system in Eq. (
38
) reduces to a well-known class of second-order differential equations:

y^{\prime\prime}(z)+c_{1}y^{\prime}(z)+c_{2}y(z)=g(z)

(41)

with
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
g(z)=Q(z)e^{\lambda z}
. The full solution of the differential equation in Eq. (
41
) can be expressed as the sum of the homogeneous and particular solutions, which, for Eq. (
38
), yields

\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(z)\\
a^{\prime}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\,e^{-i\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}z}+\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,p}e^{-i\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}z}\\
\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime}\,e^{-i\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}z}+\bar{a}_{p}^{\prime}\,e^{-i\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}z}\end{pmatrix},

(42)

with
\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h},\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,p},\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime}
and
\bar{a}_{p}^{\prime}
constant. By substitution of Eq. (
42
) into Eq. (
38
), we can find the coefficients
\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,p}
and
\bar{a}_{p}^{\prime}
, which read

\begin{split}&\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,p}=2i\theta_{z}\frac{\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}}{Q_{a,0}^{\prime}-Q_{\parallel,0}^{\prime}}\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime}\equiv M\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime},\\
&\bar{a}_{p}^{\prime}=2i\theta_{z}\frac{\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}}{Q_{a,0}^{\prime}-Q_{\parallel,0}^{\prime}}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\equiv N\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h},\end{split}

(43)

where we define some order-
\theta_{z}
auxiliary quantities
M,N
for later convenience.
Notice that the particular solution is of order
\theta_{z}
, which justifies a-posteriori the approach followed before. Finally, we can rewrite Eq. (
42
) as

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(z)\\
a^{\prime}(z)\end{pmatrix}=&\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}z}&Me^{-i\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}z}\\
Ne^{-i\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}z}&e^{-i\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}z}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\\
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}\end{pmatrix}\\
\equiv&\,S(z)\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\\
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(44)

As
\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}
are
z
-independent quantities, Eq. (
44
) can be seen as expressing the fields
A_{\parallel}^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
at the point
z
as a function of
z=0
quantities. However, initial conditions on photons (axions) are given on the full
A^{\prime}_{\parallel}
(
a^{\prime}
), not on
\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}
(
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}
). Therefore, we should incorporate this requirement in Eq. (
44
). In particular, evaluating Eq. (
42
) at
z=0
and using Eq. (
43
) yields

\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(0)\\
a^{\prime}(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}+M\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime}\\
\bar{a}_{h}^{\prime}+N\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\end{pmatrix},

(45)

which can be inverted to give

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{A}^{\prime}_{\parallel,h}\\
\bar{a}^{\prime}_{h}\end{pmatrix}=&\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(0)-Ma^{\prime}(0)\\
a^{\prime}(0)-NA^{\prime}_{\parallel}(0)\end{pmatrix}\\
\equiv&\,L\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(0)\\
a^{\prime}(0)\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(46)

valid up to first order in
\theta_{z}
.
Therefore, the solution is

\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(z)\\
a^{\prime}(z)\end{pmatrix}=\,S(z)\,L\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}_{\parallel}(0)\\
a^{\prime}(0)\end{pmatrix}

(47)

and rotating back to the initial fields we finally have

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}A_{\parallel}(z)\\
a(z)\end{pmatrix}&=\mathcal{O}^{-1}(z)\,S(z)\,L\,\mathcal{O}(0)\begin{pmatrix}A_{\parallel}(0)\\
a(0)\end{pmatrix}\equiv\\
&\equiv M_{\text{pert}}(z)\begin{pmatrix}A_{\parallel}(0)\\
a(0)\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(48)

The evolution matrix
M_{\text{pert}}
describes the dynamics of the axion-photon mixing when the plasma or magnetic field inhomogeneities can be treated as a small perturbation. Analogously to what is done in Eqs. (
20
) and (
27
), we can obtain an analytic form that well approximates the conversion probability. Indeed, at leading order, the conversion probability reads

\begin{split}P_{\text{pert}}(z)&=\cos(\theta_{0})^{2}\sin(\theta)^{2}+\cos(\theta)^{2}\sin(\theta_{0})^{2}\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\cos\left[\left(\Delta_{a,f}^{\prime}-\Delta_{\parallel,f}^{\prime}\right)z\right]\sin(2\theta)\sin(2\theta_{0})\,,\end{split}

(49)

where
\theta
is evaluated at
z
, while
\theta_{0}
is the initial value of the mixing angle, i.e.,
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta(z=0)
.

Using simple trigonometric identities, it is easy to see that Eq. (
49
) reduces to Eq. (
27
) in the homogeneous case (
\theta\rightarrow\theta_{0}
).
In Fig.
3
, we plot the conversion probability obtained from the numerical solution (solid red line) of the fully
z
-dependent Eq. (
5
), comparing it with the homogeneous [Eq. (
27
), in dotted blue] and the perturbative [Eq. (
49
), in dashed gray] solutions, obtained, respectively, when the magnetic field is homogenous or features small inhomogeneities.
To produce Fig.
3
, we consider a magnetic field varying on a length scale much smaller than
\Delta_{\text{osc}}^{\text{full}}
defined in Eq. (
28
). In particular, we select an axion with mass
m_{a}=10^{-9}~\text{eV}
, coupling
g_{a\gamma}=10^{-14}\,\text{GeV}^{-1}
and energy
\omega=1.1~m_{a}
, traveling in a magnetic field
B(z)\sim B_{0}(1+10^{-2}\Delta_{\text{osc}}^{\text{full}}z)
, with
B_{0}=10^{12}~\text{G}
. In principle, one could repeat the analysis for plasma frequency inhomogeneities as well.

In particular, Fig.
3
shows that the perturbative approach provides a first attempt to track the variation of the conversion amplitude, induced by a varying magnetic field.
Indeed, the change in the amplitude is mainly driven by the modification in
\theta
, which grows as
B(z)
becomes greater for increasing values of
z
.

As the perturbative solution requires constant
Q^{\prime}_{a,0}
and
Q^{\prime}_{\parallel,0}
values, the range of validity of the approximation in Eq.(
42
) can be found by demanding that, roughly,

\begin{split}&\text{min}\left[\left(k^{\prime}_{\parallel}(z)-k^{\prime}_{\parallel,0}\right),\left(k^{\prime}_{a}(z)-k^{\prime}_{a,0}\right)\right]\cdot z\ll 2\pi,\\
&\theta_{zz}\ll\theta_{z}\Delta^{\text{full}}_{\text{osc}}\,.\end{split}

(50)

In other words, we demand that the phase difference between the perturbative and the numerical solutions does not exceed
2\pi
. The perturbative approximation is broken at the distance
z
that saturates this condition.

Moreover, as we neglect higher-order terms in
\theta_{z}
, we should also ensure that the second condition in Eq. (
50
) is satisfied.
For the example in Fig.
3
, we find
z\lesssim 10^{6}~\text{m}
, which is confirmed numerically.

V
Resonant conversion

In this section, we discuss resonant axion-photon conversions happening when
Q_{a}=Q_{\parallel}
. We consider the case
B_{0}\ll B_{\rm crit}
, leading to the resonant condition being satisfied when the axion mass is equal to the plasma frequency. Resonant conversions are sensitive on the local properties of the plasma frequency and magnetic field at the resonance point. This feature is in contrast with nonresonant conversions, which depend on the properties of the plasma along the axion-photon beam path.
In particular, resonances are of primary importance in the case of DM axions converting into radio waves in a neutron star magnetosphere
[
27
]
and for conversions happening in the primordial magnetic field, which cause distortions in the cosmic microwave background blackbody spectrum
[
45
,
46
]
.
We can have an analytical understanding of resonant conversions focusing on a constant magnetic field and a linearly varying
\omega_{\rm pl}^{2}
. In this setting, we can express the conversion probability in terms of a generalized Landau-Zener (LZ) formula
[
34
]
(see also Refs.
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
).

In the following, we consider this scenario and compare the exact numerical solution of Eq. (
5
) with the analytical results in the relativistic axion case, as given by Eq. (3.17) in Ref.
[
34
]
. Our findings are shown in Fig.
4
, for a plasma frequency expressed as

Q_{\rm par}=-m_{a}^{2}\left(\frac{z}{z_{0}}\right)\,,

(51)

with a resonance, or level crossing, at
z=z_{0}\sim 2\times 10^{7}
m. To produce Fig.
4
, we consider an axion with mass
m_{a}=10^{-10}\,\text{eV}
, coupling
g_{a\gamma}=10^{-14}\,\text{GeV}^{-1}
and traveling in a constant magnetic field
B_{0}=2\times 10^{12}\,\text{G}
. The two panels display the conversion probability for a relativistic axion, with energy
\omega=6\,m_{a}
(upper panel), and an almost nonrelativistic one, with energy
\omega=1.2\,m_{a}
(lower panel). The exact solution is given by the thin black line, while the red line corresponds to the analytical relativistic approximation presented in Eq. (3.17) in Ref.
[
34
]
. In the first case, the two solutions show a good agreement, as expected; in the mildly relativistic case, instead, the relativistic approximation significantly underestimates the numerical result.
We can highlight this mismatch focusing on the behavior of the conversion probability at a large distance from the resonance point, as a function of the axion energy, as shown in Fig.
5
. Here, the numerical exact solution (violet squares) always overestimates the analytical relativistic formula (green rhombi), up to an order-one disagreement at the lowest plotted energy. These results suggest that one should be careful in applying the relativistic axion formulation to mildly to nonrelativistic axions, because there are important disagreements.
We can qualitatively understand this behavior as follows.
Firstly, we notice that, in the case of mildly relativistic axions, the oscillation length is smaller than what would have been predicted by a naive application of the relativistic approximation (see Fig.
2
). When applying the LZ formula, the conversion probability at a large distance from the resonance point
z_{0}
is
[
34
]

P_{a\gamma}(\infty)=1-e^{-\pi^{2}\left(\frac{L_{\rm res}}{L_{\rm osc}}\right)^{2}}\,,

(52)

where
L_{\rm res}=\sqrt{2\pi/|\partial_{z}\Delta_{\parallel}|}
, evaluated at
z_{0}
, is the resonance length, measuring how fast the plasma frequency varies, and
L_{\rm osc}=2\pi/\Delta_{\rm osc}\simeq\pi/\Delta_{a\gamma}
is the oscillation length related to the axion-photon mixing at the resonance point.
While Eq. (
52
) is strictly valid only in the relativistic regime, we claim that it can be effectively extended down to the nonrelativistic case, provided the oscillation length
L_{\text{osc}}
is computed by replacing
\Delta_{\rm osc}
with
\Delta_{\rm osc}^{\rm full}
[defined in Eq. (
28
)].
Indeed, following Eq. (
52
), a shorter oscillation length would correspond to a larger conversion probability, in agreement with our numerical results.

We remark that this constitutes a very naive explanation for the observed behavior, which anyway well captures the physical results also at a quantitative level. Correctly reproducing ultra and mildly relativistic axion conversions, the modified formula loses accuracy only deep down in the nonrelativistic regime, signaling that a more refined analysis should be carried out.
We show Eq. (
52
) and its modified counterpart, labeled as
P_{a\gamma}^{\text{full}}(\infty)
, in Fig.
5
. The relativistic version of Eq. (
52
) (dashed green line) correctly reproduces the analytical result presented in Eq. (3.17) in Ref.
[
34
]
, while the counterpart
P_{a\gamma}^{\text{full}}(\infty)
is in better agreement with numerical simulations, and captures the behavior at low energies. At high energies, the two formulae converge, as expected.
Thus, we conclude that, upon choosing the oscillation length appropriately, Eq. (
52
) accurately reproduces the numerical results of Eq. (
5
). With this example, we point out that adopting a relativistic treatment for mildly to nonrelativistic axions can lead to significant errors in the conversion probability estimates.

VI
Conclusions

In the presence of a magnetic field, axions and photons can convert into each other.
Assuming a constant magnetic field, we improved on the commonly assumed relativistic limit for the axion, and we found expressions for the conversion probability that held on general grounds. We noticed that the amplitude of the probability was correctly retrieved by the relativistic approach; however, in the nonrelativistic limit, the conversion length scale was generally different than what the relativistic limit predicted. This feature may be relevant for axion DM conversion in the neutron star magnetosphere, where the axion could be mildly to nonrelativistic
[
32
,
31
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
, although the formalism should be extended to account for the full phenomenology of the conversion in such extreme environments
[
35
,
52
]
.
In Eq. (
27
), we provided a ready-to-use analytical formula describing the conversion probability, in full generality, for a constant magnetic field and plasma frequency.

Moreover, we devised a perturbative expansion to be used when the magnetic field or the plasma featured inhomogeneities whose characteristic scale was larger than the typical axion-photon conversion length, given by the inverse of Eq. (
28
).
We provided a leading-order accurate analytical formula in Eq. (
49
), and identified the range of validity of the expansion in Eq. (
50
). When such a condition is not satisfied, a full numerical analysis should be implemented to determine the correct conversion probability.
Finally, we considered resonant axion-photon conversions, specializing to the case of a varying plasma frequency and a constant magnetic field. In this setting, we demonstrated how using a naive relativistic formulation can lead to significant underestimations of the conversion probability for mildly to nonrelativistic axions, and we provided a heuristic modification of the usual LZ formula that correctly reproduced the numerical results.
In conclusion, we provided, for the first time, an analytical understanding of the axion-photon mixing valid for all the axion energies, providing simple formulae that can be readily applied for estimates.

Appendix: Comparison with previous literature

The axion-photon conversion in the presence of a background magnetic field has been studied in different contexts, and different ways to tackle the problem have been developed. In this appendix, we review different methods to solve the coupled system, showing how they connect to our results. As discussed in Sec.
II
, the axion-photon system is described by the following coupled equations:

\left(-\partial_{t}^{2}+\nabla^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}\right)\phi=0,

(53)

where
\phi=(A_{\parallel},a)^{T}
and the mass matrix is represented by
\mathcal{M}^{2}
. In the following, we will refer to the diagonal entries of the mass matrix as
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}
, while the symmetric off-diagonal terms will be labeled by
\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}
. The entries of the mass matrix will not be specified further, but they should be thought in analogy to Eq. (
3
). At this point, we can generically expand the field
\phi
in a superposition of plane waves with time- and momentum-dependent coefficients

\int\frac{dp}{2\pi}\tilde{\phi}(p,t)e^{ipz},

(54)

where we focus on a 1D propagation along the
z
-axis. Then, Eq. (
53
) can be written in momentum space, where it becomes:

\left(-\partial_{t}^{2}-p^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}\right)\tilde{\phi}(p,t)=0.

(55)

Choosing
\tilde{\phi}(p,t)=\tilde{\phi}(p)e^{-i\omega t}
, we have, for each
p

\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{2}-p^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}&\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}\\
\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}&\omega^{2}-p^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{A}_{\parallel}(p)\\
\tilde{a}(p)\end{pmatrix}=0.

(56)

The system admits a solution only when the determinant is zero. Assuming a static magnetic field, the energy in the conversion process is conserved, and this condition enforces the momentum to be

p^{2}_{\pm}=\omega^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}.

(57)

This is exactly what we find, in our language, in Eq. (
24
). It tells us that the most general solution we can look for is

\begin{pmatrix}A_{\parallel}\\
a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1}e^{-ip_{+}z}+\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{2}e^{-ip_{-}z}\\
\tilde{a}^{1}e^{-ip_{+}z}+\tilde{a}^{2}e^{-ip_{-}z}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\omega t},

(58)

which is exactly what we find in Eq. (
25
). This formalism has been employed in several works, see, e.g., Ref.
[
23
]
. The difference with Ref.
[
23
]
is that they consider a fixed
p
and varying
\omega
and they focus on the effects on light propagation. Instead, for us,
\omega
is fixed, and
p
varies. To draw a connection with our formalism, we will show that this leads to the same conversion probability that we find in Eq. (
27
). To do so, we fix the initial conditions

\displaystyle A_{\parallel}(t=0,z=0)=1\Longrightarrow\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{2}=1-\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1}

(59)

\displaystyle a(t=0,z=0)=0\Longrightarrow\tilde{a}^{2}=-\tilde{a}^{1};

(60)

in other words, our initial state has one photon and no axion.
Then, we have

\left(\omega^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}+\mathcal{M}^{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1}e^{-ip_{+}z}+(1-\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1})e^{-ip_{-}z}\\
\tilde{a}^{1}(e^{-ip_{+}z}-e^{-ip_{-}z})\end{pmatrix}=0.

(61)

Solving the coupled system yields

\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1}=\frac{\omega^{2}-p_{-}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}}{p_{+}^{2}-p_{-}^{2}}\qquad\tilde{a}^{1}=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}}.

(62)

At this point, we have

\begin{pmatrix}A_{\parallel}\\
a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\omega^{2}-p_{-}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}}{p_{+}^{2}-p_{-}^{2}}e^{-ip_{+}z}-\frac{\omega^{2}-p_{+}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}}{p_{+}^{2}-p_{-}^{2}}e^{-ip_{-}z}\\
\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}}\left(e^{-ip_{+}z}-e^{-ip_{-}z}\right)\end{pmatrix}e^{i\omega t},

(63)

from which the conversion probability follows very easily,

P_{a\gamma}=\left|a\right|^{2}=\frac{4\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}+4\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{+}-p_{-}\right)z\right),

(64)

and perfectly agrees with the probability we found in Eq. (
27
). Again, we see that the axion and the photon cannot be considered as plane waves with a definite value of the momentum, rather, they are described by the specific superposition of plane waves in Eq. (
58
). As our analysis highlights, the underlying reason is that axions and photons are not propagation eigenstates, and, therefore, we do not expect them to propagate following a simple plane wave behavior.

As the strength of the mixing term incorporating the magnetic field decreases, however, we expect the plane wave description to be more reliable. Indeed, in the limit of zero magnetic field, axions and photons are decoupled, and so they can rightfully be described by plane waves with fixed values of momentum. Therefore, let us assume that
\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}\ll|\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}|
. Then, we can expand Eq. (
57
) as

p^{2}_{\pm}=\omega^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)\pm\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\right)\pm\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}},

(65)

or, explicitly,

\displaystyle p^{2}_{+}=\omega^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}+\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}}

(66)

\displaystyle p^{2}_{-}=\omega^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}-\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}},

(67)

where we assumed
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}>0
, without loss of generality.
Substituting this into the coefficients of the first row of Eq. (
63
) gives

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{1}=\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2})^{2}+\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2})^{2}+2\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}},

(68)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{\parallel}^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}{(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2})^{2}+2\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{4}}.

(69)

Therefore, in the
\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}\ll|\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}-\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}|
limit, the plane wave ansatz represents a good approximation. However, when the magnetic field is strong or, alternatively, when we consider resonance, as in Sec.
V
, the plane wave ansatz fails, even if the plasma is homogeneous and the magnetic field is constant.
In other words, the plane wave ansatz works only in the limit
\theta\ll 1
, where
\theta
is defined by Eq. (
9
), while our formalism works even when extrapolated to large
\theta
, as it happens close to resonances.

To gain a deeper understanding, let us consider an ultrarelativistic axion, with energy
\omega
so large, compared to its mass, that we can effectively set
m_{a}\sim 0
. For simplicity, let us relabel the entries of the mass matrix as

\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}\rightarrow Q_{\parallel},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}\rightarrow Q_{a},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\times}^{2}\rightarrow Q_{a\gamma},

(70)

in accordance with the analysis in the main text.
Then, let us assume that also
Q_{\parallel}\sim 0
. Under these conditions, the relativistic prescription for the oscillation length dictates

\Delta_{\text{osc}}\sim\frac{Q_{a\gamma}}{\omega},

(71)

where we used Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
). The complete approach developed in Sec.
III
, instead, yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{osc}}^{\text{full}}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\omega^{2}+Q_{a\gamma}}-\sqrt{\omega^{2}-Q_{a\gamma}}\sim

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\frac{Q_{a\gamma}}{\omega}\qquad&\text{if}\quad\omega\gg g_{a\gamma}B_{0}\\
\newline
\sqrt{2}g_{a\gamma}B_{0}\qquad&\text{if}\quad\omega=g_{a\gamma}B_{0}\end{cases}

(72)

substituting in Eq. (
28
) and using Eq. (
4
) for
Q_{a\gamma}
. Therefore, even if we focus on an ultrarelativistic axion, with
\omega\gg m_{a}
, the relativistic approach can produce inaccurate results if
\omega\sim g_{a\gamma}B_{0}
. Indeed, in Sec.
III
we clearly specified that the relativistic approximation is really the statement
\omega\gg M
, where
M
is a placeholder for
|Q^{\prime}_{\parallel}|^{1/2},|Q_{a}^{\prime}|^{1/2}
. In this case, when
\omega\sim g_{a\gamma}B_{0}
, the relativistic condition is not satisfied.

With this discussion at hand, we can also understand the differences with other papers, where the axion-photon conversion is analyzed from a quantum field theory perspective, in terms of Feynman diagrams
[
24
,
25
,
53
]
. In particular, they have to calculate the matrix element

\left.\langle\gamma(p)|\frac{1}{4}g_{a\gamma}aF_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}|a(q)\rangle\right|_{B_{\text{0}}},

(73)

where
\gamma(p)
and
a(q)
are the coefficients of the plane wave expansion of the incoming axion and the outgoing photon, with specific momentum
q
and
p
, respectively. The computation of this matrix element assumes that the axion and photon behave as plane waves with a specific momentum, which is only true perturbatively. In other words, saying that axions and photons have a definite momentum is only strictly true when the magnetic field is not present, for the system (
53
) becomes decoupled and Eq. (
57
) truly defines the momenta of the axion and the photon. In light of this observation, it would be worth reviewing the resonant axion-photon conversion in neutron star magnetospheres, exploring the regime of validity of the commonly assumed plane wave ansatz and Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin approximation
[
31
,
37
]
. We leave this analysis to future work.
