Community pharmacy transformation under universal health coverage: Implementation barriers, cultural determinants, and cross-national lessons from Japan's ongoing transition – A narrative review
Authors: Ryuta Urakawa, Kazumi Kubota, Risa Marikawa Urakawa, Rika Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity pharmacies worldwide are shifting from product-centered dispensing toward patient-centered clinical services. Japan achieved a prescribing–dispensing separation rate exceeding 80%, yet faces persistent challenges in delivering patient-centered services. This review examines why Japan's transformation has progressed slowly despite favorable structural conditions, and what lessons it offers other universal health coverage (UHC) systems.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review using PubMed/MEDLINE and Ichushi-Web, covering literature primarily from 2016 to 2026. Six targeted searches addressed Japan's context, three tiers of comparator countries, and implementation science frameworks. From 2388 unique records, 87 publications were included. The updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR 2.0) served as the analytical framework.ResultsTier 1 countries demonstrated that successful transformation requires aligned policy incentives, expanded scopes of practice, and robust outcome evidence. Tier 2 countries revealed shared cultural barriers, including physician-dominant hierarchies and dispensing-centered patient expectations. Japan's barriers spanned all five CFIR 2.0 domains: misaligned reimbursement, workforce shortages and absent pharmacy technicians, low pharmacist assertiveness, high service complexity, and fragmented implementation. Cultural factors—particularly high uncertainty avoidance and collectivism—interacted with institutional structures. Japan's family pharmacist system was associated with emerging evidence of benefit, including approximately 9% lower mortality risk, but limited adoption.ConclusionsStructural separation alone is insufficient without concurrent investment in workforce capability, practice authority, payment reform, and cultural adaptation. CFIR 2.0 effectively captures the multi-level nature of implementation barriers, supporting performance-based remuneration, scope expansion, and culturally sensitive strategies.

Background

Universal health coverage systems face a common paradox in community pharmacy reform: the infrastructure for dispensing is well established, yet the transition to patient-centered clinical services remains incomplete in many countries. This gap between structural capacity and functional transformation represents one of the most persistent challenges in primary healthcare reform globally.1, 2.

Community pharmacists are the most accessible healthcare professionals in most health systems, with the potential to contribute substantially to chronic disease management, medication optimization, preventive care, and public health delivery.3 The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated recognition of this potential, as pharmacies worldwide rapidly adapted to provide vaccination, screening, and emergency medication supply services.4 A review of the pandemic's impact on pharmacy practice documented expanded pharmacist roles across numerous countries, yet also noted that many of these expansions remained temporary or unevenly implemented.4

Japan presents an instructive case for understanding the barriers to community pharmacy transformation within UHC systems. Following decades of deliberate policy intervention, Japan achieved a prescribing–dispensing separation rate exceeding 80%, structurally positioning community pharmacies as independent healthcare providers rather than extensions of physician clinics.5, 6., 7 The 2015 “Pharmacy Vision for Patients” articulated a national policy direction toward patient-centered pharmaceutical services,8 subsequently reinforced by the 2019 amendment to the Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act, which established legal categories for “community collaborative pharmacies” and “specialized pharmacies”.9 Despite these structural and legislative foundations, the actual delivery of patient-centered services has not yet reached its intended scale. National surveys reveal that medication follow-up consultations are conducted for only 0.84% of eligible patients,10 pharmacist-led deprescribing occurs at a rate of 0.08%,11 and public awareness of the Health Support Pharmacy designation remains at 12.2%.12

This implementation gap is not unique to Japan. Across 16 European nations, pharmaceutical care provision showed slow and uneven progress despite decades of advocacy.13 A comparative survey of 12 countries demonstrated varying levels of clinical pharmacy service development, with considerable disparity between educational preparation and actual practice delivery.3 These global patterns suggest that the barriers to community pharmacy transformation are systemic rather than unique to individual countries, yet the specific configurations of institutional, cultural, and individual factors differ significantly across national contexts.

The World Health Organization's health system building blocks framework provides a structural lens for understanding how financing, workforce, governance, and service delivery interact within UHC systems,1 while Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory offers a complementary perspective on how national cultural characteristics—including power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism–collectivism, and long-term orientation—shape healthcare performance and professional behavior.14, 15 A 35-country analysis demonstrated significant associations between cultural dimensions and health system outcomes, suggesting that culture may function as more than a contextual backdrop and could serve as a meaningful factor in implementation success.15

This review addresses three interconnected questions. First, why has Japan struggled to achieve meaningful patient-centered pharmacy practice despite having achieved structural prescribing–dispensing separation? Second, what institutional and cultural factors distinguish successful community pharmacy transformations in comparator countries? Third, what cross-national lessons can inform implementation strategies for Japan and other UHC systems facing similar challenges? To address these questions, this review employs the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR 2.0)16 as an analytical framework capable of capturing the multi-level, culturally embedded nature of implementation determinants. Methods

This study employed a narrative review methodology, following established guidance and methodological rationale for narrative-style literature reviews.17, 18. We deliberately chose a narrative rather than a systematic or scoping review because the research questions required interpretive synthesis across diverse evidence types—including policy analyses, implementation studies, qualitative research, randomized controlled trials, and grey literature—spanning multiple countries with distinct healthcare systems and cultural contexts. This breadth and heterogeneity of evidence precluded the protocol-driven aggregation and meta-analysis that characterize systematic reviews, as well as the evidence-mapping aims of scoping reviews, while the interpretive, theory-driven nature of our inquiry aligned with the strengths of narrative synthesis.19 The structured search documentation and the adapted flow diagram (Fig. 2) are provided solely to enhance transparency and reproducibility and do not imply adherence to systematic review methodology.Fig. 2Flow diagram of the literature selection process, adapted from the PRISMA 2020 template.Note: This is a narrative review. Literature searches were conducted across two electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE and Ichushi-Web (Japan Medical Abstracts Society) (Supplementary File 1). Six separate searches were performed targeting: (1) Japan's community pharmacy context; (2) Tier 1 comparator countries (United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada) with established community pharmacy clinical service frameworks under UHC; (3) Tier 2 comparator countries (Republic of Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore) sharing cultural and institutional similarities with Japan; (4) studies applying the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to pharmacy settings; (5) United States medication therapy management implementation; and (6) Nordic countries' integrated care and digital pharmacy models. Search strategies combined Medical Subject Headings terms and free-text keywords related to community pharmacy services, pharmacist roles, pharmaceutical care, and country-specific terms. For most searches, the period covered January 2016 to February 2026. The start date of January 2016 was chosen as the year following Japan's 2015 “Pharmacy Vision for Patients,” to capture the most recent decade of reform literature most directly relevant to the current policy context. For the CFIR, Asian, and Nordic searches, the period was extended back to January 2011 because the foundational literature in these areas emerged earlier: CFIR–pharmacy applications began around 2013, key Asian prescribing–dispensing separation studies were published in the early 2010s, and Nordic deregulation literature included important pre-2016 analyses. The Japan and Tier 1 searches did not require this extension, as their most policy-relevant literature is concentrated within the post-2016 period. Searches were limited to English- and Japanese-language publications. The non-inclusion of Embase is acknowledged as a limitation. The complete search strings, Boolean operators, database-specific strategies, and record counts for each of the six searches are provided in Supplementary File 1 to support transparency and reproducibility.

The initial search yielded 2496 records. After removal of 108 duplicates (90 duplicate sets), 2388 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Inclusion criteria prioritized reviews and policy analyses providing comprehensive overviews of national community pharmacy systems, studies applying CFIR or other implementation science frameworks to pharmacy practice, research directly addressing Japan's community pharmacy reforms, multi-country comparative studies, and evaluations of key institutional innovations. Exclusion criteria comprised single-site small-scale studies with limited generalizability, disease-specific pharmacotherapy comparisons without institutional context, basic pharmacological research, studies focused exclusively on hospital pharmacists, pharmacy education research, and purely descriptive patient satisfaction surveys. An evidence-driven depth approach was adopted, whereby the breadth and quality of available literature for each country naturally determined the level of analytical detail. From 2388 unique records, 74 publications were selected through title and abstract screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Thirteen additional supplementary sources were identified through hand-searching and reference tracking: these comprised government reports and legislative research documents essential to the analysis of Japan's pharmacy reform trajectory,6., 7, 8 the primary analytical and supplementary framework references 1, 2., 14, 16, 20, methodological literature supporting the narrative review design,17, 18., 19 a historical analysis of the Japanese pharmacist's role during the COVID-19 pandemic,21 and a reporting guideline adopted to enhance transparency of the literature selection process.22 In total, 87 publications were included in the analysis. While this study employs a narrative review methodology, a flow diagram adapted from the PRISMA 2020 template22 is provided to enhance transparency of the literature selection process (Fig. 2).

The updated CFIR (CFIR 2.0)16 served as the primary analytical framework, supplemented by the CFIR Outcomes Addendum.20 CFIR 2.0 was selected for its comprehensive coverage of implementation determinants across five domains—Innovation, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Individuals, and Implementation Process—and its explicit accommodation of cultural and contextual factors through constructs such as Local Attitudes, Critical Incidents, and Recipient-Centeredness. The adaptation of the original CFIR to community pharmacy settings has been previously validated,23 supporting the applicability of its updated version to this context. The WHO Health System Building Blocks framework1 was used for background contextualization, and Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory14 informed cross-national cultural analysis.

Selected literature was organized by mapping findings to CFIR 2.0 domains, with comparative analysis structured across the three-tier country framework. Mapping of findings to CFIR 2.0 constructs was conducted by the lead author (RU) through iterative close reading of each included source, with each relevant finding assigned to the most salient domain and construct. To reduce interpretive bias, provisional mappings were reviewed and discussed with the co-authors (KK, RMU, RT); disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and ambiguous cases were assigned to the domain judged most consistent with the CFIR 2.0 construct definitions. As in any narrative synthesis, this mapping involved interpretive judgment; the resulting structure is intended as an analytical lens rather than a validated coding scheme.

The three-tier structure was defined by analytical purpose rather than by a ranking of overall system performance. Tier 1 (United Kingdom, Australia, Canada) comprised UHC systems with nationally contracted, remunerated community pharmacy clinical services and a substantial peer-reviewed implementation literature, selected as models from which transferable lessons could be drawn. Tier 2 (Republic of Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) comprised Asian UHC systems sharing cultural and institutional features with Japan, selected to control partially for cultural context. Tier 3 (United States, Nordic countries) comprised systems included as functional reference points for specific innovations (medication therapy management; market deregulation and digital models) rather than as comprehensive transformation models. Other UHC systems with notable pharmacy reforms, such as the Netherlands, Germany, France, and New Zealand, could offer additional lessons; they were not included as primary comparators in order to keep the comparison analytically tractable and to prioritize systems either sharing Japan's cultural and institutional context or offering the most extensively documented contractual clinical-service models. The tiered structure is therefore a heuristic analytical device rather than a rigid or exhaustive classification. The label “successful transformation models” for Tier 1 denotes relative progress in establishing remunerated clinical services, not the absence of implementation barriers; as documented in this review, tensions and unresolved challenges persist even in these systems. Supplementary sources, including framework documents and theoretical works, were identified through reference tracking where they provided essential contextual information.

Tier 1: Successful transformation models

United Kingdom

The United Kingdom's National Health Service provides one of the most extensively documented models of community pharmacy clinical service integration within a UHC system. The NHS community pharmacy contractual framework has evolved through successive five-year agreements, progressively shifting remuneration from dispensing volume toward clinical service delivery.27 The 2024 Pharmacy First services represent the culmination of this trajectory, enabling community pharmacists to assess, treat, and supply medications for specified clinical conditions without prior physician consultation. To support safe and effective integration, a comprehensive CFIR-informed study protocol has been developed for evaluating the ongoing implementation and its health system impact.28 Independent prescribing by pharmacists has been progressively expanded, with a qualitative systematic review identifying organizational support, interprofessional relationships, and regulatory clarity as key facilitators for implementation in primary care settings.29 The New Medicine Service, evaluated through both a randomized controlled trial demonstrating approximately 10% improvement in medication adherence30 and a CFIR-based organizational ethnography revealing the practical challenges of workflow integration,31 illustrates how evidence generation and implementation analysis can proceed in parallel. The Pharmacy Quality Scheme introduced pay-for-performance elements, providing financial incentives for quality indicators rather than dispensing volume.32 However, research on pharmacist and general practitioner perspectives reveals persistent tensions regarding professional boundaries and collaborative relationships, with solidarity and dissonance coexisting within interprofessional dynamics.33 Organizational factors including pharmacy ownership type, skill mix configuration, and workplace culture have been shown to significantly influence clinical productivity, suggesting that structural reforms alone are insufficient without attention to organizational context.34

Australia

Australia's community pharmacy system operates through the Community Pharmacy Agreement (CPA), a five-yearly negotiated agreement between the government and the Pharmacy Guild of Australia that structures both remuneration and service delivery expectations.35 Policy analysis has characterized the CPA as occupying an inherent tension between industrial policy protecting pharmacy business viability and health policy promoting clinical service expansion.36 Within this framework, professional pharmacy services have been implemented through iterative cycles of development and evaluation. A CFIR-informed qualitative study applying the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy identified a six-stage implementation process for professional services, emphasizing the importance of tailored facilitation strategies.37 A semi-structured survey of pharmacists using CFIR constructs found that Inner Setting factors—particularly workforce availability and workflow infrastructure—were rated as the most critical implementation priorities, outweighing external policy factors in day-to-day practice.38 A systematic review of clinical medication review services in Australian community pharmacies documented positive impacts on medication-related problems but noted inconsistent implementation across practice settings.39 Multi-level analysis of factors influencing community pharmacy service delivery identified barriers operating simultaneously at micro (individual pharmacist), meso (organizational), and macro (system) levels, with policy-level factors shaping the boundaries of what was achievable at practice level.40

Canada

Canada's community pharmacy system is characterized by provincial jurisdiction over pharmacy regulation, creating what has been described as a “patchwork” of varying scopes of practice, reimbursement mechanisms, and service delivery models across the country.41 This decentralized structure has, paradoxically, enabled innovation by allowing individual provinces to serve as natural experiments in pharmacy reform. Alberta's compensation plan, developed through stakeholder consultation and implemented progressively, demonstrated that physician-equivalent remuneration for clinical services could drive a dramatic increase in service delivery, from approximately 30,000 to 170,000 monthly clinical claims—a 5.7-fold increase.42 Qualitative research with family physicians revealed that pharmacist prescribing was accepted when supported by transparent communication and established trust, though concerns about fragmentation of care persisted.43 The landmark RxEACH trial demonstrated that pharmacist-led cardiovascular risk management reduced estimated cardiovascular risk by 21% compared with usual care, providing robust evidence for pharmacist prescribing effectiveness.44 Systematic reviews of pharmacist prescribing roles and competencies across multiple countries identified training models, collaborative practice frameworks, and regulatory clarity as common enablers.45, 46 An international comparison of pharmacy payment models across OECD jurisdictions documented the shift from fee-for-service dispensing reimbursement toward performance-based and capitation models, with payment reform consistently identified as a necessary precondition for service transformation.47 A randomized controlled study of practice facilitation in community pharmacy demonstrated that external facilitation support significantly improved the adoption of clinical services, suggesting that implementation support infrastructure is as important as service design.48 (Table 2).Table 2CFIR 2.0 domain mapping across Tier 1 countries.CFIR 2.0 domainConstructUKAustraliaCanadaOuter SettingFinancing(+) Progressive shift to service-based contractual framework27(+) CPA provides structured funding for professional services35; Tier 2: Asian comparators with shared cultural contexts

Republic of Korea

Korea's experience with prescribing–dispensing separation provides a direct comparator to Japan, as both countries implemented separation policies within Asian healthcare systems characterized by physician-dominant hierarchies. Analysis of pharmaceutical consumption in areas exempt from Korea's separation mandate revealed that separation alone does not guarantee rational medication use, underscoring the need for complementary clinical services.49 A survey of public acceptance toward a “Family Pharmacy” model—analogous to Japan's “family pharmacist” system—found moderate acceptance but significant gaps in public understanding of expanded pharmacist roles.50 Public perception research confirmed that while Koreans increasingly acknowledged the importance of community pharmacies, awareness of clinical service capabilities remained limited, particularly for chronic disease management.51 The government's Prescribing Analysis System generated notable physician resistance, with qualitative research revealing tensions between regulatory oversight and perceived clinical autonomy.52 Good Pharmacy Practice implementation faced barriers primarily attributed to institutional factors rather than individual pharmacist resistance.53

Taiwan

Taiwan's single-payer National Health Insurance system has enabled system-wide digital integration that offers instructive lessons for pharmacy informatics. The PharmaCloud system, evaluated using the RE-AIM framework, demonstrated that cloud-based medication record sharing across all pharmacies and hospitals could improve medication safety and reduce duplication.54 Quantitative analysis confirmed significant reductions in medication duplication following PharmaCloud implementation.55 Strategic interventions to promote chronic disease prescription dispensing at community pharmacies, analyzed through interrupted time series methods, showed that policy incentives could gradually shift prescriptions from hospital outpatient departments to community settings, paralleling Japan's prescribing–dispensing separation trajectory.56 A pharmacist home visit project targeting high-frequency healthcare utilizers reduced physician visits, providing evidence for pharmacist-led patient management within the NHI framework.57 During the COVID-19 pandemic, Taiwan's community pharmacy network served as the primary infrastructure for real-name mask distribution, demonstrating the public health utility of an integrated pharmacy network connected through the NHI system.58

Singapore

Singapore's healthcare system adopts a physician-led, pharmacist-enabled approach, as reflected in the Healthier SG policy framework and recent specialist pharmacy position papers advocating structured pharmacist roles under physician oversight.59 Qualitative research with general practitioners revealed limited enthusiasm for expanded pharmacist clinical roles, with business competition and concerns about patient loyalty identified as primary barriers to collaboration.60 Even within hospital settings, patient and caregiver understanding of pharmaceutical care remained limited, with expectations centered on dispensing accuracy and product availability rather than clinical consultation.61

Tier 3: Functional reference models

United States

Medication therapy management programs in the United States provide instructive examples of CFIR application to community pharmacy implementation. A CFIR-based evaluation of Tennessee's MTM pilot identified early-stage contextual factors influencing implementation, including organizational readiness, leadership engagement, and available resources.62 Analysis of barriers and facilitators for community pharmacist population health management highlighted the critical role of payment reform and provider status legislation in enabling expanded services.63 Notably, a Virginia statewide protocols study applying CFIR 2.0 specifically demonstrated the utility of the updated framework's constructs, including Local Attitudes and Recipient-Centeredness, for capturing the cultural dimensions of pharmacy practice transformation.64

Nordic countries

Sweden's experience following pharmacy market deregulation illustrates the tension between commercialization and clinical service development. The shift from a state pharmacy monopoly to a competitive market increased geographical access but raised concerns about whether commercial pressures would disincentivize time-intensive clinical services.65 Finland's policy roadmap for community pharmacy development, led by the pharmacy profession with government support, articulated a vision integrating digitalization, clinical service expansion, and regulatory modernization within a framework of regulated private pharmacy ownership.66

Japan's context and implementation barriers

Historical and policy context

Understanding Japan's current implementation challenges requires appreciating the historical trajectory of community pharmacy within the Japanese healthcare system. The 150-year history of Japanese pharmacy has been characterized by a gradual, contested evolution from a product-centered, physician-subordinate role toward an independent clinical profession.5, 6. The policy of prescribing–dispensing separation (iyaku bungyo), pursued through incremental reimbursement incentives over several decades, achieved its structural objective of establishing independent community pharmacies: the prescribing–dispensing separation rate rose from 11.3% in 1989 to 80.3% in 2023.7 Notably, reimbursement for patient-oriented services was first introduced in 1983 through the establishment of pharmacy management fees (yakugaku kanri-ryo),6 yet more than four decades later, the reimbursement structure continues to disproportionately reward dispensing volume over clinical service delivery.67 However, critical analyses have noted that the separation policy has not yet been accompanied by commensurate legal provisions for expanded pharmacist authority. It has been argued that recent policy revisions—such as changes to dispensing fees—may have prioritized healthcare cost containment over the legislative development needed to support broader professional autonomy.67

The 2019 amendment to the Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act established legal definitions for community collaborative pharmacies (chiiki-renkei yakkyoku) and specialized pharmacies (senmon-iryo-kikan renkei yakkyoku), creating institutional categories for pharmacies providing enhanced clinical and collaborative services.9 These legislative changes, building on the 2015 Pharmacy Vision,8 articulated a clear national policy direction. Yet a substantial gap persists between legislative intent and practice implementation. Outer Setting barriers: policy misalignment and cultural attitudes

Japan's reimbursement system exemplifies the Outer Setting construct of Financing within CFIR 2.0. Under the current fee schedule, the dispensing fee (chozai gijutsu-ryo) and the drug management and guidance fee (yakuzai kanri shido-ryo) form the remuneration core, while patient-oriented activities such as medication follow-up and deprescribing are compensated through comparatively small add-on fees rather than being integrated into the basic technical fees.67 Because the base fees remain tied largely to prescription volume, formulation complexity, and pharmacy location rather than to the clinical services actually provided, the marginal financial return on time-intensive clinical activities is low relative to high-throughput dispensing.67 This creates a structural disincentive for pharmacies to invest in clinical service capacity, particularly given the economic pressures facing community pharmacies operating in a competitive market. An international comparison of pharmacy payment models confirms that fee-for-service dispensing reimbursement consistently inhibits clinical service development across jurisdictions.47 Plausible reforms within the national health insurance framework include rebalancing the base fees toward patient-oriented services, converting selected clinical add-on fees into core technical fees to signal their essential nature, and introducing performance-linked components analogous to the UK Pharmacy Quality Scheme32; whether such reforms are politically and fiscally feasible within Japan's cost-containment environment remains an open question.

Cultural attitudes constitute a powerful and underexplored implementation determinant. A 35-country analysis of cultural dimensions and health system performance found that Japan scores notably high on uncertainty avoidance and mid-range individualism, lower than Western comparators but notably higher than other East Asian nations,15 characteristics that may be associated with risk-averse professional behavior and deference to hierarchical authority. In the pharmacy context, this manifests as reluctance among pharmacists to challenge physician prescribing decisions and low rates of proactive clinical intervention. A cross-sectional study of pharmacist assertiveness found significant associations between self-assertiveness scores and deprescribing experience, suggesting that individual behavioral characteristics mediate the relationship between structural opportunity and clinical action.68 Qualitative research using the Theory of Planned Behavior revealed that both physicians and pharmacists perceived deprescribing as professionally risky, with subjective norms favoring non-intervention over proactive medication optimization.69

Inner Setting barriers: workforce and organizational constraints

Japan is notable among developed nations for the absence of a formally recognized pharmacy technician role. An international survey of pharmacy support workforces across 67 countries documented Japan's position as an outlier in this regard.70 This structural gap means that pharmacists must perform all dispensing and administrative tasks themselves, substantially limiting the time available for clinical services. National survey data confirm that workforce shortages and excessive dispensing workload are the most frequently cited barriers to expanded clinical service delivery.71

The organizational culture of Japanese community pharmacies, analyzed through the CFIR 2.0 construct of Recipient-Centeredness, reflects broader cultural norms of service orientation (omotenashi) that paradoxically may impede clinical assertiveness. A qualitative study using the Donabedian framework identified that Health Support Pharmacy service quality was influenced by structural factors (staffing, space, equipment), process factors (consultation skills, documentation), and outcome factors (patient satisfaction), with structural limitations fundamentally constraining process improvements.72

Individuals: capability, opportunity, and motivation gaps

Analysis through the CFIR 2.0 Individuals domain, structured by COM-B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation), reveals multilayered challenges. Application of the FIP Global Competency Framework to Japanese pharmacists identified significant gaps in organizational management and professional development competencies relative to international benchmarks.73 A comparative study of Japanese and Irish pharmacists' attitudes toward diabetes management found that Japanese pharmacists reported lower confidence and narrower self-perceived scope of practice.74

Research on pharmacist behavior provides further insight into the gap between capability and action. A qualitative investigation of barriers experienced by pharmacists who were motivated to propose deprescribing but faced obstacles in doing so, identified fear of damaging the physician–pharmacist relationship, time constraints, and lack of institutional support as primary barriers.75 These findings align with national survey data showing that even trained pharmacists deliver medication follow-up at rates below 1%10 and pharmacist-led deprescribing at 0.08% of eligible encounters.11

Innovation characteristics: emerging evidence and adoption paradox

Despite these implementation challenges, emerging evidence indicates that Japan's pharmacy service innovations, when delivered, are associated with meaningful outcomes. A large-scale cohort study of the “family pharmacist” (kakaritsuke-yakuzaishi) system was associated with an approximately 9% lower mortality risk among patients with cardiovascular and endocrine conditions.76 The system was also associated with a lower incidence of therapeutic drug–drug interactions among underage individuals.77 Research examining factors associated with family pharmacist prescription modifications found that family pharmacists were 1.37 times more likely to achieve prescription changes compared with non-designated pharmacists.78 The “My Pharmacist/My Pharmacy” model demonstrated associations with improved service quality across multiple dimensions.79

Yet the gap between evidence and adoption is substantial. National survey data indicate that the family pharmacist consent rate stands at approximately 2.9%,80 Health Support Pharmacy awareness at 12.2%,12 and a broader patient awareness survey found that only 22.2% of respondents were aware of the family pharmacist system, though 40.7% expressed interest in utilizing it if better informed.81 Medication review national trends show a 3.5-fold increase between 2010 and 2015 with high physician acceptance rates, yet absolute implementation rates remain low.82 National collaborative pharmacy surveys in 2023 documented the economic benefits of pharmacist interventions including inquiry-based prescription modifications and home pharmaceutical care, but also highlighted the limited scale of these activities.80 (Table 3).Table 3Japan's implementation gap quantified.MetricJapanInternational comparator (illustrative; Cross-cutting educational dimension

The implementation barriers described above have an educational dimension that spans individual capability and system-level workforce development. A comparative study of community pharmacy education and practice between Japan and the United States identified structural differences in clinical training intensity, experiential learning opportunities, and patient interaction skills that contribute to the confidence and competency gaps observed among Japanese pharmacists.83

Implementation strategies

The comparative analysis and Japan-specific findings described above point toward a set of evidence-informed implementation strategies, organized here using CFIR 2.0 domains. In presenting these strategies, we distinguish three bases for each recommendation: direct evidence from studies conducted within Japan; extrapolation from comparator countries whose institutional and cultural context differs from Japan's; and the authors' interpretive synthesis. Because no Japanese trials have directly evaluated most of the structural reforms proposed here, the majority of recommendations rest on extrapolation and interpretation rather than on direct domestic evidence, and should be read as hypotheses to guide policy experimentation rather than as established solutions.

Regarding Outer Setting interventions, the most consistent finding across Tier 1 countries is that payment reform is a necessary precondition for clinical service transformation. Alberta's compensation plan demonstrated that remunerating clinical services at rates comparable to physician consultations generated dramatic increases in service delivery.42 The United Kingdom's progressive shift from dispensing-based to service-based contractual frameworks provides a model for incremental payment reform within a centralized UHC system.27 For Japan, this points to the need for restructuring the fee schedule to create meaningful financial incentives for medication follow-up, deprescribing, and collaborative care services, while simultaneously reducing the relative attractiveness of high-volume dispensing (extrapolated from Tier 1 evidence27., 42; no direct Japanese evidence).

Inner Setting strategies require addressing the workforce infrastructure gap. The establishment of a formal pharmacy technician role, which exists in virtually all Tier 1 and many Tier 2 comparator countries but is absent in Japan,70 would enable task redistribution and free pharmacist time for clinical activities (extrapolated from cross-national workforce comparison70; the proposed benefit for Japan is an interpretive inference). Practice facilitation, demonstrated through a Canadian randomized controlled study to improve clinical service adoption,48 offers an evidence-based approach to supporting individual pharmacies through the transition process. Australia's experience with CFIR-informed implementation priority setting38 and ERIC-based facilitation strategies37 provides practical methodologies for identifying and addressing practice-level barriers.

At the Individuals level, strategies must address the capability, opportunity, and motivation dimensions identified through COM-B analysis. Clinical competency development should be aligned with international frameworks, as the FIP Global Competency Framework gap analysis suggests.73 Assertiveness training and interprofessional communication skills development may help address the cultural determinants of low clinical intervention rates (interpretive inference informed by the association between assertiveness and deprescribing experience reported in Japan68). The contrast between Japan's pharmacist confidence levels and those of international counterparts74 suggests that experiential training models—such as those integrated into Canadian pharmacy education—warrant adaptation for the Japanese context.

Innovation-level strategies should focus on reducing complexity and demonstrating relative advantage. Systematic reviews have shown that intervention complexity is a significant determinant of implementation success and that adaptation strategies are essential.26 Taiwan's PharmaCloud system illustrates how digital infrastructure can reduce the information asymmetry that makes clinical interventions more complex in fragmented information environments54, 55 (extrapolated from Taiwan's single-payer context, which differs substantially from Japan's). The economic evidence base for community pharmacy services, synthesized through cost-utility analyses, provides important justification for investment in transformation.84

Implementation Process strategies should emphasize systematic needs assessment and adaptive evaluation. The fragmented nature of Japan's reform efforts—multiple simultaneous policy changes without coordinated implementation support—contrasts with the more systematic approaches observed in Tier 1 countries. The vaccination service implementation literature, systematically analyzed through CFIR, demonstrates the value of structured barrier and facilitator assessment prior to service rollout85 (extrapolated from international implementation studies). Discussion

This review reveals that Japan's community pharmacy transformation challenge is neither purely institutional nor purely cultural but rather emerges from the interaction between the two. The CFIR 2.0 framework proved particularly useful for capturing this interaction because its updated constructs—especially Local Attitudes, Recipient-Centeredness, and COM-B-based Characteristics—explicitly accommodate cultural factors that are often treated as external to implementation analysis.

The comparison across three tiers of countries shows several cross-cutting themes. First, structural reform without cultural and behavioral adaptation is insufficient. Japan's achievement of dispensing separation, like Korea's,49 demonstrates that structural change does not automatically translate into functional transformation. Second, payment reform is necessary but not sufficient. While Alberta's experience42 and the UK's contractual evolution27 confirm the importance of aligned financial incentives, an analysis of Australia's CPA suggests that remuneration agreements designed to support new roles may not effectively advance professional scope if their financial distribution and structural design continue to prioritize traditional dispensing models over clinical service development.36 Third, scope-of-practice expansion requires parallel investment in interprofessional relationships. The persistent tensions between pharmacists and physicians documented in the UK,33 Singapore,60 and Korea52 suggest that legislative authority must be accompanied by relational infrastructure.

The COVID-19 pandemic served as a natural experiment in pharmacy scope expansion, with multiple countries granting temporary or permanent extensions to pharmacist authority.86 Australia's policy analysis of COVID-related scope changes87 and Taiwan's demonstration of pharmacy network public health utility58 illustrate how critical incidents can catalyze reforms that were previously politically unfeasible. For Japan, the pandemic period did not result in comparable scope expansion. Japanese pharmacists were not included in the temporary legal authorizations to administer COVID-19 vaccines,21 in contrast to the expanded pandemic roles granted to community pharmacists in several UHC systems, most extensively documented in the United Kingdom.4, 86. This absence of comparable scope expansion in Japan reflects the institutional and regulatory constraints identified across CFIR 2.0 domains. Beyond these specific policy catalysts, deeper cultural and institutional factors shape the pace and direction of pharmacy transformation.

Cultural dimensions have received relatively little attention in implementation research and warrant further examination. Japan's cultural profile—characterized by high uncertainty avoidance, moderate power distance, and mid-range collectivism—may be one of several factors that, alongside reimbursement structures, legal authority, and workforce configuration, contribute to the persistence of existing practice patterns.15 The assertiveness findings,68 the deprescribing reluctance patterns,69, 75. and the low proactive intervention rates10, 11 are all consistent with this cultural interpretation. Culture, however, should not be treated as a fixed or deterministic barrier: Singapore's physician-led, pharmacist-enabled model59 and Korea's Family Pharmacy concept50 represent culturally adapted approaches that work within rather than against hierarchical norms. Cultural dimensions operate alongside—and are shaped by—policy, reimbursement, legal authority, workforce structure, and professional norms; attributing implementation gaps primarily to national culture would overstate the evidence and risk essentializing a dynamic and changeable set of professional behaviors.

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, the literature selection involved subjective judgment, and the analysis does not claim to be exhaustive; moreover, screening was conducted by a single reviewer (RU), which may introduce selection bias. Second, the non-inclusion of Embase and limited grey literature searching may have resulted in relevant publications being missed. Third, the breadth of the comparative scope necessarily limits the depth of analysis for individual countries. Fourth, many Japanese-language sources in Ichushi-Web lacked English abstracts, requiring reliance on title-based assessment for initial screening. Fifth, the evidence-driven depth approach means that countries with more extensive English-language research literature (particularly the United Kingdom and Canada) receive more detailed treatment than those with less accessible evidence bases. Sixth, CFIR 2.0 was applied as a post hoc analytical framework to organize findings from studies that were not originally designed using this framework; consequently, the mapping of findings to specific CFIR 2.0 constructs involved interpretive judgment by the authors.

Conclusions

Japan's experience with community pharmacy transformation under universal health coverage provides important lessons for health systems worldwide. The achievement of structural prescribing–dispensing separation, while necessary, is insufficient to drive the transition from product-centered to patient-centered pharmacy practice. We identified implementation barriers across all five CFIR 2.0 domains, with cultural factors appearing to interact with institutional structures in ways that slow the pace of transformation and limit the effectiveness of incremental, non-coordinated reform efforts.

The emerging outcome evidence for Japan's family pharmacist system suggests promising effectiveness when delivered, making the implementation gap—rather than the innovation design—the primary target for intervention. Cross-national comparison reveals that successful transformation requires simultaneous action on payment reform, scope-of-practice legislation, workforce restructuring, interprofessional relationship building, and culturally sensitive implementation support. The updated CFIR 2.0 framework, with its explicit attention to local attitudes, recipient-centeredness, and individual characteristics, provides a robust analytical tool for understanding and addressing the culturally embedded nature of pharmacy transformation barriers in UHC systems.
