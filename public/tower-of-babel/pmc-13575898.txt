Extracellular Vesicle Proteomics in Gastric Cancer: Moving Beyond Early Detection
Authors: Kwanjoo Lee, Jun-young Seo
Published: 2026

Gastric cancer remains a major clinical burden despite advances in endoscopic diagnosis and treatment.1 In countries with organized screening programs, endoscopy has enabled the detection of a substantial proportion of early gastric cancers. In many other regions, however, the disease is still identified at an advanced stage, when curative treatment is rarely feasible. Even where endoscopy is widely available, blood-based biomarkers retain clinical value for risk stratification, characterization of tumor biology, and adjunctive assessment alongside endoscopy. Conventional serum markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 are convenient to measure but lack sensitivity and specificity, especially in early-stage disease.2 The search for more informative, minimally invasive biomarkers therefore remains clinically relevant.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a promising source of such biomarkers.3 Released by virtually all cell types and detectable in blood and other body fluids, EVs carry proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites from their cells of origin. Their cargo can therefore reflect ongoing processes within the tumor microenvironment. The lipid bilayer protects intravesicular content from enzymatic degradation, an advantage for liquid biopsy applications. Among EV cargo, proteins are of particular interest because they mediate tumor invasion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and intercellular communication.3

In this issue of Gut and Liver, Joo et al.4 examined EV-associated proteomic biomarkers in gastric cancer. Proteomic profiling was first performed on EVs from one normal gastric cell line and two gastric cancer cell lines. Candidate proteins consistently altered in cancer cell line-derived EVs were then validated in plasma-derived EVs from gastric cancer patients and healthy controls. Two candidates emerged: vitronectin (VTN) and laminin subunit alpha-3 (LAMA3). In the clinical validation cohort, EV-associated LAMA3 was significantly higher in patients than in controls, with elevation apparent from stage II onward. EV-associated VTN did not differ significantly in the overall comparison but was elevated in stage III and IV disease. Both markers correlated positively with stage.4

This stage-dependent pattern also makes biological sense. VTN, an extracellular matrix glycoprotein involved in cell adhesion, migration, and integrin-mediated signaling, has been implicated in the progression of several malignancies.5-7 LAMA3, a component of laminin-332, contributes to cell–matrix interaction and has been associated with invasion, migration, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition.8,9 The enrichment of such matrix-related proteins in tumor-derived EVs is consistent with the notion that circulating vesicles capture features of tumor progression and stromal remodeling.

A cautious reading, however, is needed. Although liquid biopsy studies are often discussed in the context of early detection, the signal in this study was stronger in more advanced disease. Neither marker was significantly elevated in stage I cancer. LAMA3 became apparent from stage II, whereas VTN was mainly a feature of stage III–IV disease.4 VTN and LAMA3 may therefore serve better as indicators of tumor burden than as screening tools. This distinction matters because the analytical and clinical demands placed on a screening biomarker differ substantially from those placed on a prognostic or monitoring biomarker.

These findings should also be viewed in light of several limitations. The validation cohort was small, comprising 36 patients and 10 controls, and the two groups differed in age and sex distribution—variables that may affect circulating EV profiles.4 Different isolation methods were applied to cell line supernatants and plasma, reflecting a well-recognized but unresolved challenge in EV research.4,10 Western blotting is appropriate for exploratory validation but does not translate readily to standardized clinical measurement. Confirmation with quantitative assays in larger and independent cohorts will be required before VTN and LAMA3 can be considered for clinical use.

Future work should therefore be directed toward more clinically grounded validation. Cohorts enriched for stage I disease and premalignant gastric lesions are needed to test any role in early detection. Longitudinal sampling before and after surgery, chemotherapy, or recurrence would clarify whether EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 track tumor burden. The incremental value of these markers should be examined in combination with conventional tumor markers, imaging, endoscopic findings, and clinicopathologic variables. Without greater standardization of EV isolation, characterization, and protein quantification, promising discovery studies will remain difficult to reproduce across institutions.10

The study by Joo et al.4 adds a valuable piece to the evolving field of EV-based biomarker research in gastric cancer. At present, EV-associated VTN and LAMA3 appear to be markers of progression rather than early disease. With further validation, however, this line of research may help shift liquid biopsy studies from the simple question of whether cancer is present to the more clinically useful question of how that cancer is behaving.
