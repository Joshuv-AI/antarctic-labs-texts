Prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis following envenomation by a snake consistent with the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex: a case report
Authors: Nguyen Dang Duc, Nguyen Tien Dat, Lam Nguyen Hong Khanh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMembers of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex are rear-fanged natricine snakes distributed throughout Southeast Asia. Envenomation is characterized predominantly by venom-induced consumption coagulopathy (VICC), severe hypofibrinogenemia, and systemic bleeding. Although kidney dysfunction has been reported following Rhabdophis envenomation, prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis has been rarely described.Case presentationA 70-year-old Vietnamese man with no known history of chronic kidney disease was bitten on the right fourth finger by a snake that was subsequently killed and brought to the hospital. Based on a dorsal photograph and the geographic locality, an experienced herpetologist assessed the snake as being consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex; however, definitive species identification was not possible because molecular confirmation and comprehensive morphometric assessment were unavailable. Fourteen hours after the bite, the patient presented with gingival and persistent bite-site bleeding and severe VICC, characterized by prothrombin activity < 5%, international normalized ratio > 10, activated partial thromboplastin time > 180 s, fibrinogen < 0.10 g/L, thrombocytopenia, and markedly elevated D-dimer levels. Kidney dysfunction was already present on admission, with a serum creatinine concentration of 259 µmol/L. Because baseline renal measurements were unavailable, the magnitude and chronicity of the renal impairment could not be determined. Kidney dysfunction subsequently progressed to severe oliguric-anuric kidney dysfunction and was treated with at least 13 documented sessions of intermittent hemodialysis during hospitalization. Serum creatinine reached a maximum pre-dialysis concentration of 864 µmol/L at 459 h after envenomation. Although conventional coagulation parameters subsequently recovered, severe kidney dysfunction persisted. At discharge on hospital day 31, kidney dysfunction remained severe, with a serum creatinine concentration of 696 µmol/L, and ongoing intermittent hemodialysis was planned. At one-month follow-up after discharge, the patient and his daughter reported by telephone that intermittent hemodialysis was ongoing; however, the exact post-discharge dialysis frequency, most recent dialysis date, and contemporaneous renal measurements were unavailable.ConclusionSevere envenomation by a snake consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex was associated with profound VICC and prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis. In this patient, recovery of conventional coagulation parameters did not parallel recovery of kidney function. The mechanism of the renal dysfunction could not be established because baseline renal function was unavailable, renal biopsy was not performed, and thrombotic microangiopathy could neither be confirmed nor excluded. Prolonged renal monitoring may therefore be warranted after severe Rhabdophis envenomation, particularly when kidney dysfunction is already evident during the acute phase.

Introduction

Members of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex, commonly referred to as red-necked keelback snakes, are rear-fanged natricine snakes distributed throughout Southeast Asia. Recent taxonomic revision has shown that populations previously identified as Rhabdophis subminiatus in mainland Southeast Asia comprise several distinct species, including Rhabdophis helleri and Rhabdophis siamensis, whereas Rhabdophis subminiatus sensu stricto is largely restricted to Java and Sumatra [1]. Consequently, species identification in clinical reports requires caution. Envenomation by members of this species complex is characterized predominantly by venom-induced consumption coagulopathy (VICC), severe hypofibrinogenemia, prolonged clotting times, thrombocytopenia, and potentially life-threatening systemic bleeding [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. VICC is an important systemic complication of snake envenomation that can result in severe or life-threatening hemorrhage [6].

Experimental studies have demonstrated that venoms within the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex possess potent procoagulant activity involving multiple coagulation pathways, including activation of prothrombin and coagulation factors VII and X, resulting in rapid fibrinogen consumption and severe coagulopathy [7,8]. Although acute kidney dysfunction has occasionally been reported following envenomation by members of this species complex, detailed descriptions of prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis remain scarce [[3], [4], [5]]. The mechanisms underlying renal injury remain uncertain and may include venom-associated microvascular injury, coagulation disturbances, renal hypoperfusion, inflammatory responses, or thrombotic microangiopathy. However, the relative contribution of these mechanisms cannot usually be established clinically without appropriate laboratory or histopathological investigations. We describe a patient who developed severe VICC and progressive kidney dysfunction and was treated with at least 13 documented sessions of intermittent hemodialysis during hospitalization, with continued intermittent hemodialysis reported at one-month follow-up after discharge. The case highlights the temporal dissociation between recovery of conventional coagulation parameters and persistent severe kidney dysfunction.

Case Presentation

A 70-year-old Vietnamese man with no known history of chronic kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, or other major comorbidities was admitted to the Poison Control Center, Bach Mai Hospital, Hanoi, Vietnam, approximately 14 h after sustaining a snakebite.

The patient reported being bitten on the fourth finger of his right hand while collecting leaves in a mountainous rural area of northern Vietnam. The bite occurred in Nhan Nghia Commune, Lac Son District, Hoa Binh Province. The snake was killed immediately after the bite and subsequently brought to the hospital.

A dorsal photograph of the snake was reviewed by Dr. Nguyen Thien Tao, Institute of Ecology and Biological Resources, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology. The dead snake was brought to the hospital and photographed but was not retained; consequently, the herpetologist's subsequent assessment was based exclusively on the available dorsal photograph and geographic locality information. Molecular confirmation and comprehensive morphometric assessment were not performed, and additional close-up photographs of the head, ventral surface, and tail were unavailable. Therefore, the identification should be interpreted as a photographic assessment consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex rather than definitive species identification.

On admission, approximately 14 h after envenomation, the patient was fully conscious, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. His blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, heart rate was 67 beats/min, and oxygen saturation was 98% while breathing room air. No hypotension, syncope, altered mental status, or clinical evidence of circulatory shock was documented at presentation.

Physical examination demonstrated gingival bleeding and continuous oozing from two fang puncture wounds on the right fourth finger. No neurotoxicity or respiratory distress was observed.

Severe venom-induced consumption coagulopathy (VICC) was present on admission. Prothrombin activity was < 5%, international normalized ratio (INR) was > 10, activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was > 180 s, and fibrinogen was < 0.10 g/L. Coagulopathy subsequently progressed, with the platelet count reaching a nadir of 17 × 10⁹/L at approximately 53 h after envenomation. The patient developed progressive anemia, with hemoglobin decreasing from 132 g/L on admission to a nadir of 70 g/L. Coagulation parameters gradually improved during hospitalization. By discharge, prothrombin activity and INR had normalized, platelet count had recovered to the normal range, and fibrinogen concentration had substantially improved (Table 1).

Peripheral blood smear examination, lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, plasma-free hemoglobin, and ADAMTS13 activity were not performed. Therefore, snakebite-associated thrombotic microangiopathy could neither be confirmed nor excluded.

Kidney dysfunction was already present on admission, with a serum creatinine concentration of 259 µmol/L approximately 14 h after envenomation. Previous serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urinalysis measurements were unavailable; therefore, the magnitude of the acute change could not be determined and previously unrecognized chronic kidney disease could not be completely excluded. Kidney dysfunction subsequently deteriorated to severe oliguric-anuric kidney dysfunction despite gradual improvement in coagulation parameters. The available medical record did not document a specific conventional indication for initiation of hemodialysis at admission. Hemodialysis was first documented at approximately 14 h after envenomation, essentially at the time of hospital admission, and the corresponding IHD consent documentation was present in the electronic medical record. Severe oliguria was documented in the nursing monitoring records and incorporated into the patient's electronic medical record. Urine output before hospital admission was not prospectively quantified, however, and the duration of oliguria before the first dialysis session could not be established. The available medical record did not document a specific conventional indication for initiation of hemodialysis at admission. Accordingly, HD1 is reported as the first documented hemodialysis session at approximately 14 h after envenomation, without attributing initiation of hemodialysis to oliguria alone.

The wound was cleaned and disinfected. Supportive management consisted of wound cleansing, intravenous fluid therapy, intermittent hemodialysis, blood component transfusion, tranexamic acid, and intravenous clindamycin. Clindamycin was administered prophylactically according to the treating team's practice for a contaminated bite wound and was continued for 7 days. No clinical or microbiological evidence of wound infection was documented. No antivenom with demonstrated efficacy against the implicated species complex was available in Vietnam.

Tranexamic acid was administered intravenously on two occasions because of persistent gingival bleeding and continuous oozing from the bite site. Each dose consisted of 1 g, administered approximately 28 h apart (total cumulative dose, 2 g). Because the role of antifibrinolytic therapy in venom-induced consumption coagulopathy remains uncertain, its potential influence on the clinical course cannot be completely excluded.

Persistent severe hypofibrinogenemia associated with ongoing bleeding required three separate cryoprecipitate transfusions, each consisting of four locally defined units (approximately 400 mL). Fibrinogen concentrations increased transiently after each transfusion before declining again, with sustained recovery occurring later during hospitalization.

The patient developed progressive thrombocytopenia, reaching a nadir of 17 × 10⁹/L approximately 53 h after snakebite.

The patient developed progressive anemia and received five units of packed red blood cells in three transfusion episodes. Hemoglobin decreased from 132 g/L on admission to a nadir of 70 g/L. The dialysis chronology was reconstructed from a comprehensive review of the patient's electronic medical record. The dialysis count increased from eight sessions reported in an earlier version of the manuscript to 13 sessions after a more complete review of the inpatient medical records identified all documented intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) sessions. Each of the 13 documented IHD sessions had a corresponding dialysis record and IHD consent documentation in the electronic medical record. Pre-dialysis creatinine and urea values were extracted from the laboratory results associated with the documented sessions. Thus, the final count was based on primary inpatient dialysis documentation rather than retrospective estimation. Because the timing of the snakebite and admission was based on the available clinical history and was approximate, post-envenomation times should be interpreted as approximate rather than exact.

The documented intervals between some dialysis sessions were variable, including intervals of approximately 10 h between HD7 and HD8, 144 h between HD9 and HD10, and 93 h between HD12 and HD13. The available records did not permit reliable reconstruction of the clinical rationale for these intervals, and no additional undocumented dialysis session is assumed in the present analysis.

Urine output was monitored daily by nursing staff and recorded in the nursing monitoring records incorporated into the patient's electronic medical record. These records documented profound oliguria, generally approximately 50–100 mL/day, with a period of anuria during hospitalization. Urine output was not prospectively quantified specifically in relation to each individual hemodialysis session; therefore, these observations represent the broader inpatient course rather than session-specific measurements.

The principal renal complication was severe oliguric-anuric kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis. Kidney dysfunction was already present on admission, with a serum creatinine concentration of 259 µmol/L approximately 14 h after envenomation. Urine output subsequently declined to a period of anuria and then remained profoundly reduced, generally approximately 50–100 mL/day, despite supportive treatment and repeated intermittent hemodialysis. At least thirteen hemodialysis sessions were documented in the available inpatient records. HD13 at 552 h after envenomation was the final dialysis session identified in the available inpatient documentation. No additional inpatient dialysis sessions were identified in the records reviewed between HD13 and the discharge assessment at approximately 712 h; however, the available records did not permit complete reconstruction of the dialysis schedule during this interval. Accordingly, the 13 sessions should be regarded as at least 13 documented inpatient sessions rather than a definitive total number of sessions performed. At one-month follow-up after discharge, the patient and his daughter were contacted by telephone and reported that he was continuing intermittent hemodialysis. The exact post-discharge dialysis frequency, most recent dialysis session, and contemporaneous renal measurements were unavailable. Renal biopsy was not performed during the period of severe coagulopathy because of bleeding risk and was not subsequently pursued after coagulation parameters recovered; therefore, the underlying histopathological lesion could not be established.

Renal ultrasonography demonstrated bilaterally increased renal cortical echogenicity with preserved corticomedullary differentiation. No hydronephrosis or urinary tract obstruction was identified. Quantitative renal dimensions and cortical thickness were not available.

No sustained hypotension or clinically documented circulatory shock was recorded during hospitalization. However, continuous hemodynamic monitoring data were unavailable, and transient episodes of reduced renal perfusion cannot be completely excluded.

At hospital discharge on day 31 after envenomation, ongoing intermittent hemodialysis was planned. Continued intermittent hemodialysis was reported at one-month follow-up after discharge; the exact post-discharge dialysis frequency was unavailable.

Troponin T was markedly elevated, reaching a peak concentration of 1210 ng/L despite preserved left ventricular systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction, 73%) and no regional wall-motion abnormalities. No chest pain, arrhythmias, or electrocardiographic evidence of acute myocardial infarction was documented. Because advanced kidney dysfunction may contribute to impaired troponin clearance, the clinical significance of this elevation remains uncertain. In the absence of serial cardiac imaging, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, or coronary angiography, concomitant myocardial injury cannot be confirmed or excluded.

Coagulation abnormalities gradually improved during hospitalization. At their last available measurements before discharge, prothrombin activity and INR had normalized, platelet count had recovered to the normal range, and fibrinogen concentration had substantially improved (Table 1); these measurements were obtained at different time points and should not be interpreted as having been performed at discharge. Despite recovery of conventional coagulation parameters, kidney dysfunction remained severe, and ongoing intermittent hemodialysis was planned at discharge on day 31. Continued intermittent hemodialysis was reported at one-month follow-up after discharge.

Discussion

The present case describes severe envenomation following a bite from a snake assessed as consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex. The clinical course was characterized by severe venom-induced consumption coagulopathy, progressive kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis, and marked elevation of troponin T. The most clinically important feature was the temporal dissociation between recovery of conventional coagulation parameters and persistent severe kidney dysfunction. Although VICC is well recognized after Rhabdophis envenomation, prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis appears to be less frequently described [[3], [4], [5],[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Experimental studies have demonstrated potent procoagulant activity in venoms from members of this species complex, including activation of prothrombin and coagulation factors VII and X [7,8]. However, the present case does not establish a direct causal mechanism for the renal dysfunction, particularly because baseline renal function was unavailable and the underlying renal lesion was not determined. The principal clinical message is that recovery of coagulation parameters should not be interpreted as evidence of renal recovery, as clinically significant kidney dysfunction may persist long after apparent hematologic normalization; in the present case, at one-month follow-up after discharge, the patient and his daughter reported by telephone that intermittent hemodialysis was ongoing, although the exact dialysis frequency and contemporaneous renal measurements were unavailable.

Although hemorrhagic manifestations of Rhabdophis envenomation have been well described, severe kidney dysfunction has received considerably less attention. Early clinical reports established the severe coagulopathic and hemorrhagic potential of Rhabdophis subminiatus envenomation. Smeets et al. described severe coagulopathy with complete depletion of fibrinogen following R. subminiatus envenomation [9]. Hoffmann et al. subsequently characterized the haemostatic effects observed in a human envenomation, further supporting the profound coagulotoxic effects of this species [10]. Experimental work by Zotz et al. demonstrated potent prothrombin-activating activity in R. subminiatus venom gland extract, providing mechanistic support for the severe defibrinogenation observed clinically [11]. Seow et al. later reported clinically significant morbidity following a R. subminiatus bite in Southeast Asia, emphasizing that this species should not be regarded as harmless [12]. These historical reports established the clinical and mechanistic context for Rhabdophis envenomation, whereas more recent reports have continued to focus predominantly on coagulopathy and bleeding [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. In the present patient, kidney dysfunction was already evident approximately 14 h after envenomation, with a serum creatinine concentration of 259 µmol/L, and subsequently progressed to severe oliguric-anuric kidney dysfunction treated with at least 13 documented sessions of intermittent hemodialysis. Because baseline renal measurements were unavailable, the magnitude and temporal pattern of the renal decline could not be determined. Lu et al. described two patients with severe Rhabdophis subminiatus envenomation who developed profound coagulopathy and systemic bleeding, but prolonged dialysis-dependent kidney dysfunction was not reported [3]. Similarly, Nelwan et al. reported severe coagulopathy following Rhabdophis subminiatus envenomation with favorable recovery and without persistent renal dysfunction [4]. More recently, Tongpoo et al., in a retrospective study of Rhabdophis siamensis bites, reported VICC and bleeding as the predominant clinical manifestations, while prolonged dialysis-dependent renal dysfunction appeared to be uncommon [5]. Antivenom availability and evidence also differ among Rhabdophis species. In Japan, antivenom against R. tigrinus has been used clinically, and a retrospective study suggested that antivenom administration was associated with lower in-hospital mortality and a lower frequency of renal failure requiring hemodialysis, although the authors emphasized that further research was required to confirm its efficacy [13]. These findings cannot be directly extrapolated to the R. subminiatus species complex, for which an effective and locally available antivenom was not available in Vietnam.

The mechanism underlying the severe kidney dysfunction in this patient remains uncertain. Potential mechanisms of snakebite-associated renal injury include venom-associated endothelial or microvascular injury, coagulation disturbances, renal hypoperfusion, inflammatory responses, and thrombotic microangiopathy. In this patient, no sustained hypotension or clinically documented circulatory shock was observed, and creatine kinase elevation was modest, making prolonged shock or severe rhabdomyolysis less compelling as sole explanations. Nevertheless, continuous hemodynamic monitoring was unavailable, and transient episodes of reduced renal perfusion cannot be completely excluded. Renal ultrasonography showed increased cortical echogenicity without hydronephrosis or urinary tract obstruction, but these findings were nonspecific and did not establish a particular renal lesion.

Snakebite-associated thrombotic microangiopathy is another possible contributor to severe renal dysfunction following envenomation. However, peripheral blood smear examination for schistocytes and laboratory assessment of microangiopathic hemolysis, including lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, and plasma-free hemoglobin, were not performed. ADAMTS13 activity was also not measured; this test is primarily relevant when considering severe ADAMTS13-deficient thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura rather than being required to establish snakebite-associated thrombotic microangiopathy. Consequently, thrombotic microangiopathy could neither be confirmed nor excluded. The renal dysfunction should therefore be considered temporally associated with severe envenomation, while venom-associated microvascular injury and thrombotic microangiopathy remain possible but unconfirmed mechanisms [14,15].

Troponin T was also markedly elevated, peaking at 1210 ng/L despite preserved left ventricular systolic function and no documented evidence of acute myocardial infarction. Given the severe kidney dysfunction and absence of more definitive cardiac investigations, the clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain and is considered an associated finding rather than evidence of confirmed myocardial infarction.

The clinical course emphasizes that hematologic recovery may precede renal recovery after severe envenomation. Although coagulation abnormalities gradually improved following supportive treatment and blood component replacement, severe kidney dysfunction persisted and the patient continued to receive intermittent hemodialysis. Clinically, patients presenting with severe VICC accompanied by early kidney dysfunction may require prolonged renal monitoring even after conventional coagulation parameters normalize. Persistent oliguria or sustained elevation of serum creatinine should therefore prompt continued nephrological follow-up rather than assuming that hematologic recovery represents recovery of systemic organ function.

This report has several limitations. First, baseline serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urinalysis before envenomation were unavailable; therefore, previously unrecognized chronic kidney disease could not be completely excluded, and the magnitude of the change in kidney function could not be quantified. Second, renal biopsy was not performed because of the severe coagulopathy during the acute phase and was not subsequently pursued after coagulation parameters improved; consequently, the underlying histopathological renal lesion could not be established. Third, investigations required to evaluate snakebite-associated thrombotic microangiopathy, including peripheral blood smear examination for schistocytes and markers of microangiopathic hemolysis, were not performed; therefore, thrombotic microangiopathy could neither be confirmed nor excluded. Fourth, several clinical and dialysis-related variables requested for detailed characterization of the renal phenotype, including patient weight, urine output normalized to body weight, daily fluid balance, acid-base measurements, detailed volume status, admission and inpatient urinalysis and urine microscopy, proteinuria and haematuria, potential nephrotoxic or traditional-medicine exposures, and detailed dialysis parameters such as vascular access, session duration, ultrafiltration, anticoagulation, and access-site bleeding, were not available in the records reviewed. Fifth, snake identification was based on a single dorsal photograph and locality information, without molecular confirmation or comprehensive morphometric assessment; therefore, the snake should be regarded as consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex rather than definitively identified to species level. Sixth, tranexamic acid was administered for persistent bleeding, and its potential influence on the clinical course, including coagulation and renal outcomes, cannot be completely excluded. Finally, post-discharge follow-up was limited to one month. At that time, the patient and his daughter were contacted by telephone and reported that intermittent hemodialysis was ongoing; however, the exact post-discharge dialysis frequency, most recent dialysis session, contemporaneous renal measurements, and subsequent long-term renal recovery could not be determined from the available records.

Conclusion

Envenomation following a bite from a snake assessed as consistent with a member of the Rhabdophis subminiatus species complex was associated with severe venom-induced consumption coagulopathy and prolonged severe kidney dysfunction treated with intermittent hemodialysis. Conventional coagulation parameters showed substantial recovery during hospitalization, whereas severe kidney dysfunction persisted, with continued intermittent hemodialysis reported at one-month follow-up after discharge. The mechanism of the renal dysfunction remains uncertain because baseline renal function was unavailable, renal biopsy was not performed, and thrombotic microangiopathy could neither be confirmed nor excluded. This case highlights that recovery of conventional coagulation parameters does not necessarily parallel recovery of kidney function and supports prolonged renal monitoring after severe Rhabdophis envenomation.
