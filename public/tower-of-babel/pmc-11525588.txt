Inflammatory factors are associated with prognosis of non-small cell lung cancer patients receiving immunotherapy: a meta-analysis
Authors: Wenxian Tong, Huilin Xu, Jindan Tang, Nan Zhao, Dingjie Zhou, Chunzhou Chen, Dedong Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to explore the association between inflammation-based prognostic markers and outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients undergoing therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We conducted a comprehensive search of the Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane databases for studies that reported on the impact of inflammation-based prognostic factors, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), on the prognosis of NSCLC patients treated with ICIs. The primary outcomes of interest were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 14 software, with assessments of publication bias and heterogeneity conducted as necessary. Our meta-analysis included 27 studies encompassing 5,174 patients, evaluating factors such as CRP, the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), and the CRP-albumin ratio (CAR). The analysis revealed that elevated levels of CRP were significantly correlated with both reduced PFS (I2 = 0%, P = 0.72; HR = 1.50, 95%CI: 1.33–1.67, P < 0.01) and shorter OS (I2 = 0%, P = 0.55; HR = 1.90, 95%CI: 1.50–2.30, P < 0.01). Similarly, elevated levels of mGPS values were associated with worse PFS (I2 = 0%, P = 0.75; HR = 1.28, 95%CI: 1.10–1.46, P < 0.05) and OS (I2 = 0%, P = 0.94; HR = 1.56, 95%CI: 1.31–1.81, P < 0.05). However, the relationship between elevated levels of CAR and worse outcomes for PFS (I2 = 59.6%, P = 0.94; HR = 1.42, 95%CI: 0.74–2.11, P > 0.05) and OS (I2 = 45.3%, P = 0.16; HR = 1.41, 95%CI: 0.70–2.13, P > 0.05) was not statistically significant. Our findings suggest that CRP and mGPS may serve as potential prognostic markers in NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy. Nonetheless, further research with more homogeneous study populations is necessary to valid these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76052-2.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents approximately 75–80% of all lung cancer cases, with about 70% of NSCLC patients being diagnosed at an advanced stage1. For those with identifiable driver mutations, targeted therapies with multi-kinase inhibitors have demonstrated effective outcomes2. Conversely, for patients lacking specific driver mutations, the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionized the management of this lung cancer subtype3. Agents such as Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab, Tislelizumab, Camrelizumab, Sintilimab, and Toripalimab, which target PD-1, along with Durvalumab, Avelumab, Tremelizumab, and Atezolizumab targeting PD-L1, and Ipilimumab targeting CTLA-4, have exhibited substantial efficacy in treating various solid tumors, including NSCLC, whether used as monotherapy or in combination therapies4. Compared with traditional chemotherapy, immunotherapy has better efficacy and acceptable toxicity and side effects5. Several studies have demonstrated that ICIs alone or in combination with chemotherapy can significantly improve the long-term survival of patients with advanced NSCLC4.

The tumor microenvironment’s inflammatory condition is recognized for its critical role in tumorigenesis and progression6. The presence of inflammatory cells and their responses within the tumor can facilitate tumor growth, progression, and immune evasion, rather than eliciting effective anti-tumor activities7. Based on this, assessing inflammation levels in cancer patients has emerged as a viable approach for prognostic evaluation. This assessment can be conducted through markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), the C-reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR), and the modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS). CRP, an acute-phase protein, serves as a nonspecific biomarker for diagnosing both acute and chronic inflammatory states across various body systems8. Its prognostic significance has been documented in several malignancies, including esophageal and colorectal cancers9. However, the utility of CRP in prognosticating outcomes for advanced NSCLC patients undergoing ICI therapy remains to be fully elucidated. Similarly, the mGPS, which calculates prognostic scores based on serum CRP and albumin levels, offers valuable insights into a patient’s inflammatory response and nutritional status, primarily in post-surgical settings10. However, its application in predicting survival outcomes of NSCLC patients treated with ICIs is not well determined. CAR is a new inflammation-based prognostic score and it reflects the severity of inflammation11. Although CAR is a promising, inflammation based prognostic factor in acute diseases, its role in NSCLC patients with ICIs therapy remains uncertain.

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to elucidate the potential of inflammatory markers such as CRP, CAR, and mGPS in identifying NSCLC patients who might benefit from ICI therapy. Our aim was to assess the associations between these inflammatory factors and survival outcomes, thereby evaluating their prognostic relevance in NSCLC patients treated with ICIs.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients: Individuals diagnosed with NSCLC via pathology or cytology, with advanced stages confirmed through imaging examinations. For grouping patients, “elevated levels of CRP” refers to CRP levels above a specified threshold, while “elevated levels of CAR” refers to the CRP/albumin ratio exceeding a defined value.

Interventions: Receipt of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, either as monotherapy or in combination with other treatments.

Endpoints: Presentation of survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), quantified through hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Definitions of inflammation-based prognostic factors: Clear definitions provided for CRP, CAR, and mGPS, derived from serum CRP and albumin levels at initial treatment12. The scoring criteria for mGPS are as follows: if CRP > 10 mg/L and Alb < 35 g/L, the mGPS score is 2, indicating a poor prognosis; If CRP > 10 mg/L and Alb ≥ 35 g/L, the mGPS score is 1, indicating a moderate prognosis. If CRP ≤ 10 mg/L, the mGPS score is 0, indicating a better prognosis13. The cut-off values for CRP in our subgroup analyses were chosen based on their frequent use in existing studies and their clinical relevance. Specifically, a CRP cut-off value of 1 mg/dL was used to differentiate between low and high inflammation groups. This threshold is well-established in the included studies. Moreover, CRP was handled as both a continuous and categorical variable to provide a more nuanced analysis, as it was reported in both forms among the included studies.

Prognosis analysis: Analysis of survival outcomes based on CRP level, CAR score, or mGPS.

Exclusion criteria

Abstracts, meeting reports without outcome data, reviews, basic research articles, expert consensus, evaluations, and case reports. Clinical studies with fewer than 30 participants. Studies scored using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Scale (NOS) as low quality14.

Search strategy

This meta-analysis was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement15 (Supplemental Table 1). We searched Embase, PubMed, and the Cochrane Library for studies from their inception until April 2022. Key terms included “non-small cell lung cancer,” “C-reactive protein,” “CRP,” “CRP/albumin ratio,” “CAR,” “modified Glasgow prognostic score,” “mGPS,” “survival,” and “prognosis.” The search strategy for each database is detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by two researchers, with full-text evaluations conducted for articles meeting inclusion criteria.

Datra extraction

Analysis and data extraction were performed independently by two researchers, and the extracted data were cross-checked, and any disagreements were resolved after discussion by a third researcher. The following data were extracted from each study: first author’s name, year of publication, region, sample size, age, sex, disease characteristics, treatment, cut-off values ​​for CRP, CAR, and mGPS, survival time, and other indicators. The NOS was used to assess the quality of the included studies, which mainly assessed three parts of each study, including the selection of the study population, comparability between groups and measurement results14. The highest score is 9, and studies with a score of ≥ 7 were considered to be of good quality, and those with score less than 5 were excluded from meta-analysis. HRs and their 95% confidence intervals for survival data were obtained directly or indirectly from survival curves of the included studies.

Statistical analysis

STATA14 and GraphPad Prism 8 software were used for analysis. Subgroup analyses were performed based on baseline features. Heterogeneity analysis was performed according to the Handbook of the Cochrane Collaboration16. We introduced Q test to evaluate the heterogeneity of data from the included sutdies. And the risk of heterogeneity was presented using P value and I2. When P < 0.1 and I2 > 50%, it was considered that there was significant heterogeneity, and a random effect model was used for meta-analysis; when P ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, it was considered that there was no significant heterogeneity, and a fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis. We pooled and analyzed the HR values ​​of PFS and OS according to groups of CRP, CAR, and mGPS. The data from univariate analyses were used for the meta-analysis. Subgroup analysis was performed according to the CRP cut-off value, with a cut-off value of ≤ 1 mg/dl as the low CRP group, and > 1 mg/dl as the high CRP group.

In order to evaluate the source of study heterogeneity and the stability of the combined effect, we excluded the included studies one by one and performed the sensitivity analysis. If there was no significant change in the overall estimates, the stability of the included studies was considered as good. Otherwise, the stability was impaired, and the reasons for these different outcomes needed to be analyzed at the same time. The funnel plot was used to analyze the publication bias of the included studies. If the graph shaped symmetrical, it indicated that the risk of publication bias was low. If the graph was skewed, it indicated that there was a high possibility of publication bias, suggesting that the combined outcome was unreliable. The difference was considered to be statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Baseline characteristics

The participants in the selected studies were diagnosed with NSCLC according to TNM diagnostic criteria and were primarily at metastatic, relapsed, or advanced stages of the disease. All interventions were based on ICI therapy, administered as either first-line, second-line, or subsequent treatment. Among the studies included, 22 investigated the relationship between baseline levels of CRP and treatment efficacy and/or survival prognosis. Additionally, baseline CAR associations were explored in 4 studies, and the mGPS in another 4, in the context of efficacy and/or survival outcomes. The overall quality of the included studies was considered high based on the NOS assessments, detailed in Supplemental Table 3. Notably, the primary quality limitations identified were related to comparability between study groups and rates of loss to follow-up.

Meta-analysis results

Association between baseline CRP and PFS

Thirteen articles17,24–28,34–38,41,43 analyzed the relationship between PFS and baseline CRP (Fig. 2). According to the heterogeneity analysis, it was found that there was no significant heterogeneity among the studies (P = 0.72, I2 = 0%), so the fixed-effects model was used for analysis. The HRs from univariate analyses were extracted from the included studies. The results of meta-analysis showed that the PFS of the high CRP group was significantly shorter than that of the low CRP group (HR = 1.50, 95%CI: 1.33–1.67, P < 0.05). We performed subgroup analysis based on CRP cut-off value. The primary objective of performing subgroup analyses in our study was to investigate whether specific grouping factors could influence the outcome measures of PFS and OS. According to the CRP cut-off value, they were divided into a cut-off value ≤ 1 mg/dl group and a > 1 mg/dl group, followed by subgroup analysis (Fig. 2). The combined results showed that the PFS of patients in the high CRP group was still significantly shorter than that of the patients in the low CRP group (for ≤ 1 mg/dl group, HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.27–1.86, P < 0.05; for > 1 mg/dl group, HR = 1.47, 95%CI: 1.26–1.68, P < 0.05).Fig. 2Forest plots for the association between pretreatment CRP grouped by cutoff value and progression-free survival. Meanwhile, two studies29,39 used CRP as a continuous variable to analyze its relationship with PFS (Supplemental Fig. 1A). According to the heterogeneity analysis, it was found that there was significant heterogeneity among the studies (P < 0.01, I2 = 87.5%), so a random-effects model was used for meta-analysis. The results of meta-analysis showed that the risk of disease progression increased in patients with elevated levels of CRP (HR = 1.05, 95%CI: 1.00-1.10, P < 0.05).

Association between baseline CRP and OS

Eight articles23,28,32,34–36,38,43 reported the relationship between baseline CRP and OS (Fig. 3). Significant heterogeneity was not found among the studies (P = 0.55, I2 = 0%) by heterogeneity analysis. Considering possible clinical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used. The results showed that the OS of the low-level CRP group was significantly longer than that of the high-level CRP group (HR = 1.90, 95%CI: 1.50–2.30, P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis was performed according to the CRP cutoff value (Fig. 3). It was found that high levels of CRP were significantly associated with worse OS regardless of the CRP cutoff value (for ≤ 1 mg/dl group, HR = 2.11, 95%CI: 1.24–2.99, P < 0.05; for > 1 mg/dl group, HR = 1.85, 95%CI: 1.40–2.30, P < 0.05).Fig. 3Forest plots for the association between pretreatment CRP grouped by cutoff value and overall survival. Meta-analysis of high versus low CRP and OS by HRs from univariate analysis.

Forest plots for the association between pretreatment CRP grouped by cutoff value and overall survival. Meta-analysis of high versus low CRP and OS by HRs from univariate analysis.

A total of 5 studies20–22,29,39 used CRP as a continuous variable to explore the relationship between CRP and OS (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Heterogeneity between studies was significant (I2 = 81.8%, P < 0.01), and a random-effects model was used. The results showed that elevated CRP was significantly associated with increased risk of death (HR = 1.01, 95%CI: 1.01–1.02, P < 0.01).

Association between car and PFS

A total of 3 studies33,40,42 were included (Fig. 4A), with high risk of heterogeneity between included studies (I2 = 59.6%, P = 0.08). Association between car and OS

A total of 3 studies19,33,40 were included (Fig. 4B). Although there was no significant heterogeneity between these studies (I2 = 45.3%, p = 0.16), we consider the risk of heterogeneity was high and used the random-effects model. The results showed there was a significant increase in mortality in patients with high CAR (HR = 1.41, 95%CI: 0.70–2.13, P > 0.05).

Association between MGPS and PFS

There were three studies18,33,40 included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 4C).The heterogeneity among included studies (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.75) was low, therefore a fixed-effects model was applied. The results showed that elevated levels of CAR was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR = 1.28, 95%CI: 1.10–1.46, P < 0.05).

Association between MGPS and OS

There are four studies18,19,33,40 reporting the relationship between mGPS and OS (Fig. 4D). The heterogeneity analysis between the included studies was found to be low (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.94), so a fixed-effects model was used for pooling. The results of the meta-analysis showed that elevated levels of CAR was significantly associated with worse OS (HR = 1.56, 95%CI: 1.31–1.81, P < 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

The effect of each included study on the results was assessed using a sensitivity analysis of removing single study once a time. As illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 2, there were no significant influences on the original overall results when each study was removed.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using the data from meta-analyses of PFS and OS. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 3, the funnel plots of CRP and PFS, and CRP and OS were visually asymmetric, suggesting possible publication bias may exist. Publication bias was further assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests. The results of Egger’s test showed that there was no significant publication bias (P = 0.13 for PFS; P = 0.29 for OS), and the results of Begg’s test showed that there was no significant risk of publication bias (P = 0.11 for PFS; P = 0.37 for OS). These findings suggested that, despite the visual asymmetry in funnel plots, the statistical analyses di not corroborate a significant risk of publication bias in our meta-analyses of PFS and OS outcomes related to CRP levels in NSCLC patients undergoing ICI therapy.

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis to assess the prognostic significance of CRP, the CAR, and the mGPS in patients with advanced NSCLC. Our findings revealed that elevated baseline levels of CRP were significantly linked to both OS and PFS in advanced NSCLC patients undergoing ICIs. Importantly, this relationship remained positive regardless of the CRP cutoff values and the variable types used for CRP. Additionally, our analysis indicated that both CAR and mGPS were associated with worse prognoses, underscoring their potential as prognostic indicators in this patient population. Further validation through sensitivity analyses and tests for publication bias confirmed the robustness and reliability of our meta-analysis results.

Tumor-associated inflammatory responses play a pivotal role in cancer progression, mediating the secretion of inflammatory mediators and cytokines that foster tumor growth and metastasis. CRP, an acute-phase protein, has been consistently linked to the prognosis of patients with various solid malignancies. A meta-analysis of 35 studies summarizes the association of serum CRP and OS and PFS in breast cancer patients, and finds that high serum CRP is an indicator of poor prognosis44. Another meta-analysis assesses the value of pretreatment hematologic biomarkers for predicting response to pembrolizumab in metastatic urothelial carcinoma patients. The results show that metastatic urothelial carcinoma patients with elevated levels of pretreatment CRP have a worse survival despite pembrolizumab therapy45. Serum albumin, a key indicator of nutritional status, often declines in patients experiencing progressive weight loss and cachexia, further complicating cancer management46. Recent studies suggests that prognostic models incorporating serum CRP and albumin levels, such as the mGPS and the CAR, demonstrate significant prognostic utility across a spectrum of cancers9,10. Despite this, the implications of these markers in the context of NSCLC patients undergoing immunotherapy remain less explored. In this study, we reviewed and assessed their prognostic roles in these patients. Our findings suggested promising prognostic capabilities of these markers, potentially guiding clinical decision-making and improving patient management in the context of immunotherapy for NSCLC.

In the field of oncology, biomarkers can be broadly classified into predictive and prognostic markers. Predictive markers are used to identify the likelihood of response to a specific therapy, guiding personalized treatment plans. At present, a variety of predictors have been used to predict the efficacy of ICIs, such as PD-L1, TMB, and MSI-h/dMMR47. However, these factors are dependent on specific detection methods and sufficient tumor specimens, which limit the clinical application of these predictors. Prognostic markers, in contrast, provide information on the overall outcome of the disease, independent of the treatment. They are crucial for assessing the likely course and prognosis of the disease. This study focuses on inflammation-based prognostic markers, specifically CRP, CAR, and mGPS, to evaluate their association with survival outcomes in NSCLC patients treated with ICIs. CRP, CAR and mGPS have the features of invasion, simple detection and well repeatability, and show possible advantages in guiding the clinical application of ICIs. However, these factors also have limitations, such as the selection of the optimal cut-off value, which needs further validation.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, all included studies are retrospective design, which inherently contain a risk of bias. Additionally, there is variability among the studies in terms of the immunotherapy agents used, potentially impacting the aggregated estimates. Retrospective studies often lack the rigorous control of variables present in prospective studies, which may influence the findings. The included studies utilized various immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and treatment combinations, leading to heterogeneity in the treatment protocols. This variability might affect the observed associations between inflammatory markers and survival outcomes. Secondly, while some studies delineated the association between inflammatory markers and survival outcomes, they did not provide specific HRs, introducing a possible risk of selection bias. Thirdly, the analysis was based on data from only 27 studies examining the correlation between CRP levels and the effectiveness of immunotherapy in NSCLC, which might lead to an underestimation or overestimation of the actual effect. Further research, particularly large-scale cohort studies, is essential to clarify the relationship between inflammatory markers and the efficacy of ICIs in advanced NSCLC patients.

In summary, this study suggests that inflammatory markers such as CRP, CAR, and mGPS may serve as potential prognostic indicators for NSCLC patients undergoing immunotherapy. Nonetheless, the conclusions drawn from this study are constrained by its limitations. Future research, especially high-quality clinical trials featuring large sample sizes, multicentric collaboration, and extended follow-up periods, is imperative to validate these preliminary findings.
