Lighting the Way for BRIGHT: Reproducible Baselines with Anserini, Pyserini, and RankLLM
Yijun Ge, Sahel Sharifymoghaddam, Jimmy Lin

Abstract
The BRIGHT benchmark is a dataset consisting of reasoning-intensive queries over diverse domains. We explore retrieval results on BRIGHT using a range of retrieval techniques, including sparse, dense, and fusion methods, and establish reproducible baselines. We then apply listwise reranking with large language models (LLMs) to further investigate the impact of reranking on reasoning-intensive queries. These baselines are integrated into popular retrieval and reranking toolkits Anserini, Pyserini, and RankLLM, with two-click reproducibility that makes them easy to build upon and convenient for further development. While attempting to reproduce the results reported in the original BRIGHT paper, we find that the provided BM25 scores differ notably from those that we obtain using Anserini and Pyserini. We discover that this difference is due to BRIGHT’s implementation of BM25, which applies BM25 on the query rather than using the standard bag-of-words approach, as in Anserini, to construct query vectors. This difference has become increasingly relevant due to the rise of longer queries, with BRIGHT’s lengthy reasoning-intensive queries being a prime example, and further accentuated by the increasing usage of retrieval-augmented generation, where LLM prompts can grow to be much longer than “traditional” search engine queries. Our observation signifies that it may be time to reconsider BM25 approaches going forward in order to better accommodate emerging applications. To facilitate this, we integrate query-side BM25 into both Anserini and Pyserini.



1
Introduction

The growing use of large language models (LLMs) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
lewis2020retrieval
;
ram2023context
is transforming the role of information retrieval.
In RAG, LLMs use retrieved documents as context to produce more accurate and grounded responses.
The BRIGHT benchmark
bright
was developed to evaluate retrieval systems specifically for this application, focusing on their ability to handle the complex, reasoning-intensive queries often found in user prompts.
To facilitate further research with the BRIGHT benchmark, we have integrated reproducible baselines into two popular information retrieval (IR) toolkits, Anserini
anserini
;
anserini2
and Pyserini
pyserini
, for convenient first-stage retrieval.
The baselines we incorporate include BM25
bm25
, a classic lexical retrieval algorithm; BGE-large-en-v1.5
bge
, a representative model for dense retrieval; and SPLADE-v3
spladev3
, a representative model for learned sparse retrieval.

Additionally, we have incorporated BRIGHT into RankLLM
rankllm
, a widely-used Python package for second-stage reranking with LLMs.
This integration is particularly valuable given that LLM-based rerankers, such as those demonstrated in previous work
LRL
;
RankGPT
;
rankzephyr
;
first
, have shown to provide significant improvements in retrieval effectiveness.
We also discuss some of our findings about BRIGHT during this process.

In our efforts to establish these baselines, we find that the BM25 results reported in the original BRIGHT paper
bright
differ from what we obtained initially using Anserini and Pyserini.
This is because for BM25 retrieval, by default, Anserini uses bag-of-words to generate sparse query vectors, i.e., the weight of each token in the query vector is the number of times the token appears in the query, whereas the implementation used by the BRIGHT paper applies the BM25 scoring algorithm to each query token to obtain token weights.
In both cases, similarity scores are the inner product between the query and document vectors.
For simplicity, we refer to the former approach as “bag-of-words” and the latter as “query-side BM25”.
Traditionally, BM25 is not applied to the query
robertson1995Okapi
;
robertson1996okapi
;
bm25
, but it may be time to reconsider this as on average, query-side BM25 achieves better results than bag-of-words for BRIGHT.

To reflect the more detailed, reasoning-intensive nature of user prompts for LLMs, BRIGHT queries are significantly longer than the typical keyword-based queries found in traditional information retrieval benchmarks like BEIR
beir
and TREC 2019-2023 Deep Learning Tracks
dl19
;
dl20
;
dl21
;
dl22
;
dl23
.
This shift in query style reflects how users interact with LLMs, providing more context to get better responses, as opposed to the concise keyword queries used for search engines.

Our primary contribution is a set of solid and reproducible baselines for the BRIGHT benchmark, covering BM25 as well as sparse and dense retrieval techniques.
These baselines are integrated into popular IR toolkits, including Anserini, Pyserini, and RankLLM, making it easy for the research community to build upon our work.
We also implement and discuss the difference between bag-of-words and query-side BM25, providing insights into their effectiveness and the implications for retrieval.
To facilitate future research, we have added support for query-side BM25 in Anserini and Pyserini.
Finally, we share observations on BRIGHT intended to guide future improvements.

2
Background and Related Work

BRIGHT

is a benchmark designed to evaluate how well retrieval systems handle reasoning-intensive tasks, which remains a significant challenge for current state-of-the-art methods.
The difficulty of the benchmark is reflected in the leaderboard,
1
1

1

https://brightbenchmark.github.io/
where the highest nDCG@10 is only about 0.46 as of August 31, 2025.

A common way to improve effectiveness is to use LLMs, either to expand the query before retrieval or to rerank first-stage retrieval results.
This pattern is evident from the BRIGHT leaderboard, where the top-performing systems, ranked by nDCG@10, all incorporate some form of LLM involvement.
Notably, systems using LLM-based query expansion achieve nDCG@10 scores more than 0.1 points higher than those using the original queries
brightEntry
.
The BRIGHT benchmark facilitates this research by providing LLM-expanded versions of its queries.
Along with that, it also provides a series of first-stage retrieval results, which do poorly on their own compared to the systems with LLM involvement, but are nevertheless an important part of the pipeline for the retrieval goal
bright
.
We reproduce two of their first-stage retrieval results, with BM25 and BGE-large-en-v1.5.

BM25

is a widely used lexical matching algorithm that assigns a relevance score to documents based on several factors: the number of times a query term appears in a document, the document’s length, and the overall rarity of the term across the entire document collection.
The algorithm was originally developed by combining two earlier versions, BM11 and BM15, through the introduction of a new parameter,
b
allowing to allocate different weights to the document length penalty term depending on the workload
robertson1995Okapi
.

Several implementations of BM25 also account for the frequency of terms within the query itself
whichBM25
;
robertson1995Okapi
;
robertson1996okapi
.
While early versions of the algorithm included a saturation function for query term frequency (qtf), similar to how document term frequency is handled, later experiments suggested that this saturation effect was not significant
bm25
.
As a result, many modern implementations of BM25 treat query term frequency linearly, creating the query vector using bag-of-words.
This method effectively counts multiple occurrences of a term in a query as separate instances to boost its importance.

In contrast, applying the BM25 scoring function on the query side to generate query vectors has received little attention.
Prior work
bm25q
has found that query term frequency has minimal impact on retrieval scores when most terms appear only once.
However, for long queries—such as in document similarity tasks where the query is itself another document
lin2007pubmed
—query length normalization becomes important.
This is particularly relevant in BRIGHT, where queries are much longer than in traditional datasets, leading to repeated tokens.
The difference in query length is so substantial that it exposed a previously unnoticed bug in Anserini and required adjustments to RankLLM’s default 4k context-length limit.
Faced with such unprecedentedly long queries, we argue that the role of query token weights warrants re-examination.

Dense and Sparse Retrieval

methods have led the
recent advances in information retrieval to two major directions beyond traditional lexical methods.
Dense retrieval models
dpr
encode queries and documents into continuous embedding vectors and compute similarity through inner products.
Among these, the BGE
bge
family of models has become widely adopted for its strong performance across diverse benchmarks.
In particular, BRIGHT reports results with BGE-large-en-v1.5, a dense model that generates high-quality semantic representations for both queries and documents, enabling retrieval based on meaning rather than exact term matches.

In parallel, learned sparse retrieval models extend the bag-of-words paradigm by introducing neural weighting mechanisms
conceptualFramework
.
SPLADE-v3
spladev3
exemplifies this approach by analyzing query and document tokens and assigning importance weights that capture both lexical and semantic relevance.
Importantly, SPLADE-v3 can also expand the representation by introducing semantically related tokens that are not present in the original text.
This enables the model to capture richer relationships while retaining the interpretability and efficiency of sparse representations.
Together, learned dense and sparse retrievers represent two complementary strategies: dense models capture deep semantic similarity in continuous space, while sparse models refine token-level weighting in discrete space.
BRIGHT provides a valuable setting to compare these approaches, since its reasoning-intensive queries stress both semantic understanding and token-level precision.

Our experiments employ Anserini and Pyserini for first-stage retrieval, and RankLLM for second-stage reranking, ensuring full reproducibility.
We select these toolkits, and provide integrations with them, because they are widely adopted within the community and enable streamlined information retrieval research with two-click reproducibility.
Documentation and usage instructions are available in the respective GitHub repositories.

The goal of our experiments is to establish reliable, reproducible baselines for comparison, not to maximize benchmark scores.
To keep the baseline setup simple, we use one-shot retrieval with reranking, without query expansion, rewriting, or multi-step agent orchestration.
We rely on standard, widely used retrievers for the first-stage and off-the-shelf LLMs for listwise reranking.

3.1
First-Stage Retrieval

We study three first-stage retrieval methods: the lexical baseline BM25, the dense retriever BGE-large-en-v1.5, and the sparse retriever SPLADE-v3.
These diverse models give a good overview of the current widely used retrieval methods
conceptualFramework
.

BM25.

In Anserini, we explore four versions of BM25 by adjusting two settings, each with two options.
The four variations are Anserini bag-of-words, Anserini bag-of-words accurate, Anserini query-side BM25, and Anserini query-side BM25 accurate.
These different settings can be selected using simple flags in the indexing and retrieval commands.

The first setting we change is accuracy.
By default, Anserini uses Lucene’s BM25Similarity
anserini
, which speeds up scoring by converting document-length norms into a single byte.
It then uses a precomputed table with 256 values to quickly retrieve the BM25 length-normalization factor in constant time
lucene
.
To compare this with more precise results, Anserini includes its own version of BM25Similarity that avoids this approximation.
However, studies show that the difference in accuracy is minor and does not strongly favor one method over the other
whichBM25
.
To exactly match the results from the BRIGHT paper, we include this accurate version for reference, but we use the Lucene version as the default to stay consistent with other Anserini baselines.

The second setting is the query generator.
Anserini typically uses the bag-of-words method, where each token’s frequency in the query determines its weight in the vector used to calculate the final similarity score.
For our experiments, we introduce a new query generator to Anserini called BM25QueryGenerator.
This generator applies the BM25 scoring algorithm on each query token to obtain the weights for the tokens in the query vector.
Since the effectiveness difference between the two query generators is significant, we include both in Anserini baslines.

In summary, Anserini bag-of-words is the default setting and part of the standard BM25 baselines, while its accurate variant is included only for completeness.
Query-side BM25, now available in Anserini, is our recommended and reliable baseline, with its accurate version being the closest match to the version used in the BRIGHT paper.
BRIGHT provides an open-source repository
4
4

4

https://github.com/xlang-ai/BRIGHT/tree/main

that employs the Pyserini tokenizer together with the Gensim
gensim
package for BM25 indexing and retrieval.
By integrating BRIGHT into Anserini and Pyserini, we provide a unified pipeline that consolidates all components for easy first-stage retrieval.

BGE-large-en-v1.5.

Dense retrieval methods
dpr
generate embedding vectors that capture the semantic meaning of text and retrieve relevant documents by ranking their dot-product similarity with query vectors
conceptualFramework
.
The BRIGHT dataset, which requires complex reasoning, poses a significant challenge for these methods due to the limits of current semantic encoding techniques.

Both Anserini and Pyserini currently support the BGE-base-en-v1.5 model for several datasets.
Given that the BRIGHT benchmark reports results using BGE-large-en-v1.5, it is necessary to add support for this model on this dataset as well.
To address this, we provide document and query embeddings for BRIGHT using BGE-large-en-v1.5.
We also release prebuilt indexes: Faiss
faiss
for Pyserini, and Lucene flat for Anserini.

SPLADE-v3.

Just as BGE-large-en-v1.5 demonstrates the limits of dense semantic encoding on the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT dataset, SPLADE-v3 highlights how token weighting can be stretched by the same reasoning demands.
This makes BRIGHT a valuable testbed for understanding how sparse learned models behave under more complex semantic pressure.
To support research in this area, we provide SPLADE-v3–encoded document and query collections, along with prebuilt indexes for easy use.

3.2
Second-Stage Reranking

Given the reasoning-intensive nature of BRIGHT, integrating LLMs for reranking has been shown to significantly improve retrieval effectiveness.
As of August 31, 2025, all top-ten leaderboard entries report results obtained with a second-stage reranker.

For this stage, we integrate BRIGHT with RankLLM
rankllm
, a widely used Python package for LLM-based reranking that supports a broad range of models.
In our experiments, we evaluate two open-source models with strong reasoning capabilities: Qwen3-8b and gpt-oss-20b.
Since the default context length of 4k tokens in RankLLM is insufficient for BRIGHT’s long queries and documents, we extend the context length to 16k tokens.
We rerank the top 100 retrieved results using the default stride and window settings of 10 and 20, respectively.
All runs employ the RankZephyr
rankzephyr
ranking prompt.
For gpt-oss-20b, we set the reasoning effort to “medium”, and for both models, we allocate the maximum 16k output tokens as their reasoning budget.
Each reranking run over the full dataset was executed on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU, with average runtimes of approximately 6 hours 15 minutes for Qwen3-8b and 2 hours 20 minutes for gpt-oss-20b.
By setting the temperature to 0, we obtain identical results across repeated runs.
However, variations in the runtime environment can still lead to differences in output. To facilitate reproducibility, we list all dependency versions in

.

The first-stage retrieval results used for reranking are obtained by applying pairwise Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to the outputs of BM25, BGE-large-en-v1.5, and SPLADE-v3. These retrieval results are publicly available on Hugging Face,

Quantized vs. accurate length normalization.

Comparing columns (a) vs. (b) and (c) vs. (d) in
Table 1
, we find that, regardless of query representation, quantized and exact document-length normalization yield nearly identical results. On average, the quantized variant scores slightly higher by about 0.002–0.003 nDCG@10 points.

To better understand this effect across tasks, we examine the token–length statistics of documents in
Table 2
.
We use the Pyserini analyzer for tokenization, so our numbers differ from the original BRIGHT paper, which relies on the GPT-2 tokenizer
gpt2
.
While the “better” choice ultimately depends on the distribution of relevant document lengths relative to the corpus for each query, two general patterns emerge:

•

When document lengths are tightly clustered (e.g., Stack Overflow and TheoremQA-Questions in
Table 2
), the accurate per-document normalization performs slightly better.
This is because Lucene encodes each field’s length norm into a single byte (256 buckets), and thus quantization collapses nearby lengths, reducing discrimination.

•

When the collection exhibits a heavy long tail of very long documents (e.g., Biology, Earth Science, Economics, and Psychology in
Table 2
), quantization often helps by softening BM25’s length penalty (controlled by
b
), mitigating the tendency to over-penalize long documents.
In such cases, re-tuning
b
could improve the exact normalization results, but we keep
b
fixed here due to the lack of a dedicated development/validation split in BRIGHT tasks.

Overall, our findings support the conclusions of Kamphuis et al.
whichBM25
that quantization has negligible impact.
For consistency with Anserini’s defaults and the library’s regression tests settings, we report results using the quantized variant moving forward.

Bag-of-Words vs. BM25 for query vectors.

Comparing columns (a) and (c) in
Table 1
, BM25-weighted query vectors are more effective than plain bag-of-words vectors on
seven
of the twelve datasets, bag-of-words is better on

From the aggregate query-length statistics (

) we do not observe a direct correlation between a task’s query-length distribution and whether BM25 or bag-of-words is superior.
However, a per-query analysis (
Figure 1
) reveals three consistent patterns:

•

For
short
queries (
<\!16
tokens) the two methods are almost identical, with bag-of-words only rarely ahead—consistent with the traditional practice of
not
applying BM25 to query vectors
robertson1995Okapi
;
robertson1996okapi

•

As query length grows, both the
frequency

•

Either method can win depending on whether down-weighting frequent terms in favor of rarer ones helps or harms the query’s key terms. As BM25’s wins are both

4.3
BRIGHT Corpora

During our experiments, we observed two quality issues with the BRIGHT corpora:
(a)
Duplicate documents.
As shown in
Table 5
, up to 38% of documents are exact duplicates after trimming leading and trailing whitespace.
(b)
Very short documents.

Eight of the twelve tasks contain documents with fewer than five tokens (1.2%–26.6%); some are even empty.
These cases occur when a document is mostly whitespace that is removed during tokenization.

We recommend further cleaning of the scraped data before chunking it into shorter passages.
Most duplicates are low-quality fragments unrelated to any query and therefore have little effect on retrieval evaluation.
However, some relevant gold IDs are missing from the positive candidate lists, and adding them changes the results.
To estimate the impact of missing duplicate IDs, we adjusted the relevance labels (qrels) as follows: for each set of identical documents, if any ID was marked relevant for a given query, we marked all IDs in that set as relevant.

For first-stage retrieval, the differences between original and adjusted qrels are small: the largest gap in nDCG@10 is 0.008, with an average of 0.002.
For second-stage reranking, the differences increase slightly, with a maximum of 0.010 and an average of 0.003.
We expect even larger discrepancies for stronger retrievers and rerankers, which are more likely to be incorrectly penalized for retrieving missing gold IDs.
To mitigate this issue, we recommend removing duplicate documents from the dataset.
In the meantime, we release the adjusted qrels on Hugging Face.

5
Limitations and Future Work

Our work establishes a baseline for retrieval on BRIGHT, prioritizing clarity and reproducibility over leaderboard optimization.
Consequently, several limitations point to directions for future development.

First-stage retrieval.

Although the BRIGHT repository includes a wide range of first-stage retrieval methods, we restricted our experiments to BM25, BGE, SPLADE v3, and their fusions, which provide a minimal yet representative coverage of the current retrieval landscape.
However, new retrievers—particularly those optimized for reasoning-intensive workloads via reinforcement learning or other techniques beyond conventional sparse and dense approaches—are being developed at a rapid pace.
As these methods mature, we plan to integrate them into Anserini and Pyserini, provided they demonstrate robustness and generalizability beyond BRIGHT to other reasoning-intensive datasets
r2med
.

Reranking.

Reranking continues to be a crucial component for improving retrieval quality.
We demonstrated this with Qwen3-8b and gpt-oss-20b, but the rapid pace of model releases suggests that many other candidates are worth exploring.
Notable examples include Qwen QwQ
qwq
, already deployed as a reranker on the BRIGHT leaderboard, and Qwen3-Reranker
qwen3rerank
, a dedicated reranking model from the same series.
In addition, post-trained models via supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning have begun to gain traction on the leaderboard, and we plan to integrate such approaches into RankLLM once their effectiveness and generalizability beyond BRIGHT have been validated.
Finally, scaling to larger models remains a straightforward way to improve effectiveness, though this comes with significant cost and latency trade-offs.

Query expansion and rewriting.

Query-side interventions, particularly LLM-driven query expansion and rewriting, represent another promising avenue for improving retrieval.
On reasoning-intensive datasets such as BRIGHT, expanded queries substantially outperform the original inputs, as evidenced by leaderboard results where nDCG@10 gains exceed 0.1 points.
However, as with reranking, these improvements come at the cost of increased computation and latency, raising concerns about efficiency and the practicality of deployment in real-world settings.
An emerging variant of this approach is agentic multi-turn retrieval, which interleaves query rewriting with retrieval and reranking to iteratively refine results, and may offer additional gains over single-step expansion.

Generalization of query-side BM25.

Finally, we evaluated query-side BM25 only on BRIGHT, where we initially discovered its effectiveness.
Future work should examine its behavior on traditional benchmarks like BEIR, both to validate its robustness and to further explore its potential correlation with query length or structure.

6
Conclusion

BRIGHT is a powerful benchmark for evaluating retrieval systems on reasoning-intensive tasks.
By integrating diverse first-stage retrieval techniques and reporting results on BRIGHT within widely used toolkits such as Anserini, Pyserini, and RankLLM, we establish an open and reproducible baseline for the community to build upon.
While several findings from traditional retrieval datasets—such as the effectiveness of retrieval fusion and second-stage reranking—still hold on BRIGHT, other conventions merit reconsideration.
In particular, our discovery of the significance of query-side BM25 for long queries highlights the need to revisit established practices in light of evolving retrieval use cases.
Finally, our observations also suggest directions for improving BRIGHT itself, helping the benchmark become more reliable and robust.
We hope our contributions provide a foundation for further advances in retrieval methods and their evaluation on reasoning-oriented tasks.
