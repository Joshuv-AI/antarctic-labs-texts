Adherence to Obstetrician-/Gynecologist-Ordered Multi-target Stool DNA Test Screening and Follow-Up Colonoscopy: A National Multi-payer Study of US Women
Authors: Mallik Greene, Leobon Gameng, Peter Nowd, Travelle Ellis, Christine Molmenti, Jemel Bingham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionColorectal cancer (CRC) screening remains below national targets among US women. Primary care providers (PCPs) play an important role in promoting CRC screening adherence. Many US women see an obstetrician/gynecologist (OB/GYN) as their PCP. We evaluated adherence among US women to multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) testing and follow-up colonoscopy ordered by OB/GYNs.MethodsWe linked Exact Sciences Laboratories (ESL) orders with a national multi-payer claims database (2016–2022). Eligible participants were average-risk women aged 45–75 years with continuous health plan enrollment (− 180 to 0 days baseline; 0–365 days to assess mt-sDNA adherence; and, after a positive mt-sDNA result, 0–365 days to assess colonoscopy). Primary outcomes were mt-sDNA completion within 365 days of kit shipment and colonoscopy completion within 365 days of a positive mt-sDNA test. Logistic regression evaluated factors associated with mt-sDNA adherence.ResultsOf 160,068 women with OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA, 71.4% (n = 114,360/160,068) completed testing within 365 days. Test positivity was 8.6% (n = 9773/114,360). Among those with positive results, 71.3% (n = 6971/9773) completed colonoscopy within 365 days. Test adherence increased with age (45–49 years, 67.1%; 50–64 years, 71.8%; 65–75 years, 76.7%), was higher in non-metropolitan areas, and rose with higher ZIP code median household income. Test adherence was higher among individuals with traditional Medicare (85.2%) and Medicare Advantage (77.3%) than commercial insurance (71.3%) and Medicaid (61.7%).ConclusionIn routine practice, more than 70% of women completed OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA testing and, after a positive result, more than 70% completed colonoscopy within 1 year. OB/GYN clinicians may play a key role in improving CRC screening adherence, with opportunities to address gaps by payer, income, and race/ethnicity.Keywords: Colonoscopy, Colorectal neoplasms, Gynecologists, Mt-sDNA, Obstetricians, Real-world evidence, Treatment adherence and compliance

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Obstetricians/gynecologists (OB/GYNs) frequently deliver preventive care for US women; real-world adherence to OB/GYN-ordered multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) tests and follow-up colonoscopy is not well described.This study aimed to describe adherence patterns and identify factors associated with adherence to inform the development of future targeted approaches for improving the uptake of colorectal cancer (CRC) screening in US women. What was learned from this study? Among 160,068 US women with OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA, 71.4% returned the kit within 1 year; among those with positive results, 71.3% completed colonoscopy within 1 year.mt-sDNA adherence was higher among White women and women with traditional Medicare or Medicare Advantage insurance and lower among Black or African American women, Hispanic or Latina women, and younger women; colonoscopy adherence increased with higher ZIP-code median household income.Logistic regression analysis found that women who were > 50 years old, White, from a non-metropolitan area, from a ZIP code with median annual income ≥ US$50k, and insured by traditional Medicare were more likely to adhere to testing (compared with women who were 45–49 years old, Asian or Pacific Islander, from metropolitan areas, lived in a ZIP code with median income < $50k, or had commercial insurance, respectively).

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) was the third most common incident cancer and fourth most common cause of cancer death among women in the United States (US) in 2024, accounting for an estimated 71,270 new CRC cases and 24,310 CRC deaths in the female population [1]. Nevertheless, progress has been made in reducing CRC incidence and mortality among US women in recent decades [2]. Overall reductions in CRC mortality are partially explained by the widespread adoption of CRC screening [3].

CRC screening involves direct visualization methods, such as colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy, and stool-based tests, including fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) and multi-target stool DNA (mt-sDNA) testing [4]. Direct visualization modalities offer longer intervals between screens than stool-based modalities but are more resource-intensive to administer [4]. Stool-based tests require repeated use at shorter intervals, as well as a timely diagnostic colonoscopy after a positive result [4, 5]. To achieve their full clinical benefit, positive screening tests must be followed by appropriate interventions, often surgical resection for localized cancer [4]. Despite the availability of multiple effective screening modalities, CRC screening uptake in the US remains suboptimal [6]. As of 2023, 63.5% of US adults, and 64.7% of women, are adequately screened, below the national target of 80% [7].

Recommendations for CRC screening are frequently delivered in primary care settings during routine visits that include clinician-initiated discussions of preventive care [8]. Many women in the US receive routine gynecological care, and some view their obstetrician/gynecologist (OB/GYN) as their primary clinician for preventive services [9]. However, the proportion of screening-eligible women who rely exclusively on OB/GYN care is small, particularly at older ages when preventive care utilization shifts toward primary care; thus, OB/GYN-based CRC screening efforts are likely to reach a subset, rather than the majority of, age-eligible women [9, 10]. This context underscores that OB/GYN-delivered CRC screening represents a complementary, rather than replacement, pathway for preventive care. As of 2021, guidelines lowered the recommended age for average-risk CRC screening from 50 to 45 years [4, 11, 12]. With this update, a larger proportion of women engaged in routine OB/GYN care fall within the guideline-concordant screening age range, making OB/GYN encounters a potential opportunity to initiate or facilitate CRC screening for appropriate patients.

Physician recommendation is a key factor in facilitating patient participation in CRC screening [13]. However, adherence to prescribed screening remains variable in real-world settings. Real-world adherence to stool-based screening tests differs by modality, clinical setting, and patient population [14, 15]. Although mt-sDNA testing has demonstrated higher adherence compared with other stool-based modalities in several analyses, adherence remains influenced by demographic, socioeconomic, and provider-related factors [16–18]. Provider type has also been associated with differences in screening uptake and adherence patterns [16–18].

A growing body of literature has examined OB/GYN knowledge of CRC screening guidelines, attitudes toward screening, and reported screening practices [19]. However, substantially less is known about real-world patient adherence to CRC screening tests that are prescribed or ordered by OB/GYN clinicians. Understanding whether patients complete OB/GYN-ordered screening and whether they follow through with recommended diagnostic colonoscopy after a positive result is essential for evaluating the effectiveness of OB/GYN-based screening delivery. To address this gap, the current study used linked laboratory and administrative claims data to assess adherence to OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA testing and follow-up colonoscopy, and to characterize demographic and socioeconomic factors associated with adherence.

Methods

Data Sources

This real-world retrospective cohort study used data from two sources: (1) Exact Sciences Laboratories LLC (ESL), the exclusive national laboratory for mt-sDNA testing, and (2) a national multi-payer administrative claims database (Komodo Health, New York, NY, USA).

The ESL database contains laboratory order and result information for mt-sDNA tests, including patient age, sex, test shipment date, test return status, test result, and insurance type at the time of order. ESL data do not include complete longitudinal medical histories.

Komodo Health maintains a large, de-identified administrative claims database derived from commercial, Medicare Advantage, traditional Medicare, and Medicaid health plans across the US. The database includes medical and pharmacy claims, enrollment files, and associated demographic variables. Claims include diagnosis and procedure codes, allowing ascertainment of CRC risk factors and colonoscopy completion.

To construct the study cohort, ESL laboratory order records were linked to Komodo claims data using a privacy-preserving deterministic tokenization process performed by a third-party data integration vendor. Direct patient identifiers (e.g., name, address, and birth date) were converted into encrypted tokens by a third-party data integration vendor before linkage occurred and before the research dataset was created. The analytic dataset provided to the investigators contained only de-identified, tokenized records and was created after identifiers had been removed. Investigators did not have access to any identifiable patient information at any stage. No manual chart review of identifiable medical records was performed at any stage of the study.

Following linkage, all analyses were conducted using de-identified data consistent with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule (§164.514). The dataset qualified as de-identified because all direct identifiers were removed prior to investigator access.

Ethical Approval

Per 45 CFR §46.104(d)(4), this secondary analysis of existing de-identified data was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) oversight and did not require informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. ESL and Komodo Health provided permission for use of the data.

Study Population

The study population included women aged 45–75 years who were prescribed mt-sDNA testing by an OB/GYN provider between July 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022. This period captured real-world clinical use following the inclusion of mt-sDNA in national screening recommendations and enabled evaluation of adherence across varying stages of implementation in routine practice.

The analysis included only women who were at average risk for CRC and continuously enrolled in a health plan for the study’s duration, and excluded women who died before the end of the follow-up period or returned invalid test kits. Average risk of CRC was defined as the absence of any diagnosis indicating elevated risk (including CRC, inflammatory bowel disease, familial adenomatous polyposis, colonic adenomas, colonic polyps, or a documented family history of CRC) or prior colectomy during the 6-month baseline period.

CRC risk status was determined algorithmically using diagnosis and procedure codes captured in administrative claims during the 6-month baseline period preceding the index date (defined as the shipment date of the mt-sDNA kit; Fig. 1). Continuous health plan enrollment was required as follows: a 6-month baseline period prior to index date to assess baseline characteristics and eligibility; a 1-year period after index date to assess adherence to mt-sDNA screening; and a 1-year period after return of a valid test kit to assess colonoscopy completion among patients with a positive mt-sDNA test result.

Outcome Measures

The main study outcomes were adherence to mt-sDNA screening, defined as the return of a valid test kit within 365 days of the index date (kit shipment), and adherence to colonoscopy, defined as the completion of colonoscopy within 365 days of a positive mt-sDNA result. A multivariable analysis was also conducted to identify factors affecting adherence.

Statistical Analysis

We summarized baseline demographic characteristics using frequencies and percentages and compared outcomes across demographic strata using χ2 tests. We fit a multivariable logistic regression model with a binary dependent variable (mt-sDNA adherence: yes/no) adjusted for covariates significant in χ2 tests (age group, race/ethnicity, four-level rural–urban classification area, ZIP-code median household income, and insurance type). Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses used available data without imputation. All p values were two-sided with significance at p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 160,068 women aged 45–75 years had OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA tests and met eligibility criteria (Table 1). Most were aged 50–64 years (74.9%), resided in metropolitan areas (84.3%), and had commercial health insurance (89.3%). Overall, 71.4% (n = 114,360/160,068) were adherent to mt-sDNA testing. In χ2 tests, adherence increased with age (45–49 years, 67.1%; 50–64 years, 71.8%; 65–75 years, 76.7%) and with decreasing urban density (metropolitan, 70.9%; micropolitan, 73.4%; small town, 75.0%; rural, 75.9%). Adherence varied by race/ethnicity (White, 73.1%; Black or African American, 66.3%; Hispanic or Latina, 66.8%; Asian or Pacific Islander, 68.8%; Other/Unknown, 71.2%) and by ZIP-code median household income (< US$50k, 69.0%; $50–75k, 71.3%; $75–100k, 71.6%; $100–200k, 71.9%; > $200k, 73.1%). Adherence was higher with traditional Medicare (85.2%) and Medicare Advantage (77.3%) compared with commercial insurance (71.3%) and Medicaid (61.7%).

Among women who completed an mt-sDNA kit ordered by an OB/GYN, 9773 (8.6%) had a positive test result (Table 1). The proportion of women with a positive mt-sDNA result increased with age, peaking among those aged 65–75 years (15.2%). A positive mt-sDNA result was also most common among White women (9.8%) and least common among Asian/Pacific Islanders (4.9%). Metropolitan residence was associated with lower percentage of mt-sDNA positivity (8.4%) than other residential locations (range 9.3–9.7%). Positivity was also highest among women with traditional Medicare (13.1%) and lowest among those with commercial insurance (8.1%).

Colonoscopy was completed in 71.3% (n = 6971) of women with a positive mt-sDNA result (Table 1). As with mt-sDNA adherence, colonoscopy adherence varied significantly by demographic factors in the χ2 tests. Median household income and health insurance type were significant predictors of colonoscopy completion. Colonoscopy adherence increased with increasing median household income (< $50k, 67.4%; $50–75k, 67.6%; $75–100k, 72.2%; $100–200k, 76.4%; > $200k, 83.2%; p < 0.0001). Women with traditional Medicare (78.6%) and commercial health insurance (71.7%) were more likely to have follow-up colonoscopies after positive mt-sDNA tests than women with Medicare Advantage (68.7%) and Medicaid (64.7%). Follow-up colonoscopy adherence did not vary significantly by age group, race/ethnicity, or urban/rural classification.

The cumulative percentage of women completing mt-sDNA testing rose rapidly in the first months after prescription and thereafter accrued more slowly (cumulative completion: Day 60, 62.5%; Day 180, 69.1%; Fig. 2a). In contrast with cumulative percentage of mt-sDNA adherence, completion of colonoscopy was more gradual (cumulative completion: Day 60, 40.1%; Day 180, 66.8%; Fig. 2b).

In multivariable logistic regression, higher odds of mt-sDNA adherence were observed for women aged 50–64 years (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.18–1.25, p < 0.0001) and 65–75 years (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.17–1.31, p < 0.0001) when compared with women aged 45–49 years (Fig. 3). White women were more likely to return valid test kits than Asian or Pacific Islander women (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.09–1.25, p < 0.0001); Black or African American women (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.85–0.99, p = 0.034) and Hispanic or Latina women (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.99, p = 0.021) were less likely to return kits. Women from micropolitan areas (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.12–1.22, p < 0.0001), small towns (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.20–1.36, p < 0.0001), or rural areas (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.22–1.42, p < 0.0001) were more likely to adhere to testing than women from metropolitan areas. Return of mt-sDNA kits was more likely among women from ZIP codes with median annual income ≥ $50k compared with those from ZIP codes with median household income < $50k ($50–75k: OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.15, p < 0.0001; $75–100k: OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.11–1.22, p < 0.0001; $100–200k: OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.14–1.25, p < 0.0001; > $200k: OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.13–1.40, p < 0.0001). Women insured by traditional Medicare (OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.92–2.40, p < 0.0001) and Medicare Advantage (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.22–1.38, p < 0.0001) were more likely to adhere to mt-sDNA testing than women insured by commercial health insurance, while those insured by Medicaid were less likely to adhere to testing (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.64–0.71, p < 0.0001).

Discussion

In this national, multi-payer cohort of 160,068 women with OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA test kits, 71.4% completed testing within 365 days. Among those with a positive result, 71.3% completed a colonoscopy within 365 days. Adherence to mt-sDNA was higher at older ages, in non-metropolitan areas, at higher ZIP-code median household income levels, and among those with Medicare coverage; colonoscopy completion after a positive result also increased with higher ZIP-code median household incomes. These findings highlight the role of OB/GYN clinicians as an access point for CRC screening and provide insight into adherence patterns when screening is initiated in this care setting.

OB/GYN clinicians occupy a unique position within preventive care delivery. While most CRC screening is coordinated through primary care, OB/GYN visits represent an additional point of contact for eligible women, particularly those who may not routinely engage with internal medicine or family medicine providers. However, OB/GYN practices vary in their comfort and familiarity with CRC screening guidelines, and screening discussions may compete with reproductive and gynecologic priorities during clinical encounters [19]. Furthermore, women who rely on OB/GYN care may differ in sociodemographic characteristics, healthcare utilization patterns, and engagement with other preventive services compared with women managed primarily in traditional primary care settings. These contextual factors should be considered when interpreting adherence patterns observed in this cohort.

The relatively high adherence observed for both mt-sDNA completion and follow-up colonoscopy may reflect multiple factors. mt-sDNA testing includes structured patient outreach and navigation support to facilitate kit return, which may contribute to adherence. In contrast, follow-up colonoscopy requires coordination between the ordering clinician, patient, and endoscopy services. The similar magnitude of adherence observed for both outcomes suggests that follow-up processes in this cohort were generally effective. However, adherence likely reflects both provider- and patient-level factors. It is possible that women who engage in preventive gynecologic care represent a subset of patients receptive to screening recommendations, although we did not evaluate broader preventive health behaviors in this analysis.

Our finding of 71% overall mt-sDNA adherence aligns with prior analyses, in which adherence to OB/GYN-prescribed mt-sDNA testing was 71.2% among women aged 45–85 years in 2023 [20] and 73.1% among women aged 65–85 years in 2016 and 2017 [21]. A real-world analysis of CRC screening by modality reported higher adherence to OB/GYN-prescribed screening of 79.5% [22]. However, in contrast to our analysis, which included all eligible patients, the latter analysis included only patients with a PCP, a factor positively associated with adherence. Notably, our observed rate of 71% adherence is lower than the 81.6% [16] and 82.5% [23] adherence rates reported for mt-sDNA testing prescribed by gastroenterologist (GI) specialists. Comparisons across studies should be interpreted cautiously because populations, ordering patterns, and reimbursement policies of health insurers differ.

One possible explanation for this discrepancy is variation in specialty-specific communication patterns or in the emphasis placed on CRC screening discussions, as differences in clinician knowledge and comfort with screening guidelines have been reported [19, 24]. A 2018 survey showed that many OB/GYN clinicians have limited knowledge of CRC screening recommendations, such as CRC risk factors and age-based guidelines, which could hinder their communication with patients on this issue [19]. OB/GYNs also report patients’ low awareness of, and lack of willingness to undergo, CRC screening, which are key barriers to CRC screening [19, 24]. CRC screening may also receive less patient focus at OB/GYN visits, where care is predominantly related to reproductive health [25], than at GI specialist visits, where colorectal health receives more attention. Interestingly, one survey reported that women rated the importance of CRC screening significantly lower than men did, which highlights both the challenges and opportunities for OB/GYN practitioners to facilitate increased CRC screening among women [26].

We observed that women aged > 50 years were more likely to complete mt-sDNA testing than those aged 45–49 years, among whom adherence was 67.1%, which is lower than the adherence rate of 71.4% reported in a 2025 analysis of 45- to 49-year-olds prescribed mt-sDNA by an OB/GYN [27]. Our findings agree with previous studies reporting higher screening adherence in older age groups across screening modalities, including mt-sDNA testing and other contemporary CRC screening approaches [17, 18, 23, 28, 29]. Potential reasons for this age trend are multiple. Sociological data have shown that, compared with older adults, younger adults encounter more logistical barriers to screening related to work and childcare, and also have a lower knowledge of CRC screening methods [30]. Furthermore, behavioral changes to reduce CRC risk, including screening, may be hindered by the incorrect belief that genetics is the primary determinant of CRC, which is a common misconception among adults aged 45–49 years [26]. This misconception is particularly concerning as CRC incidence among average-risk US adults aged < 50 years has risen rapidly in recent decades [31, 32]. However, OB/GYNs may be well placed to educate patients about CRC screening, and particularly to reach women aged < 50 years who seek gynecological care more often than older women [25].

In this cohort, adherence to mt-sDNA testing differed across racial and ethnic groups. Compared with White women, lower adherence was observed among Black or African American women and Hispanic or Latina women in both unadjusted analyses and multivariable models. Differences in adherence by race and ethnicity observed in this study are consistent with prior literature examining disparities in mt-sDNA adherence [33], other CRC screening modalities [14, 15], CRC incidence and mortality outcomes [34], and screening adherence and outcomes in gynecologic cancers [35–37]. Multiple structural and social determinants of health have been associated with variation in cancer screening participation, including differential access to care, insurance coverage, language barriers, healthcare system navigation challenges, and trust in medical institutions [24, 37, 38]. Multicomponent and culturally tailored interventions have been proposed as strategies to improve screening equity [39]. For example, Spanish-language outreach initiatives have been associated with improved mt-sDNA adherence among Latina women in community health settings [40].

Insurance type was associated with adherence patterns in this cohort. Women insured by traditional Medicare or Medicare Advantage demonstrated higher adherence to mt-sDNA testing and follow-up colonoscopy than those insured by commercial plans or Medicaid. Although sample sizes for non-commercial payors were smaller, particularly for colonoscopy, lower screening adherence among Medicaid-insured populations has been described in prior studies [41, 42], including analyses of OB/GYN-prescribed screening [27].

In addition, we observed lower adherence in metropolitan areas compared with micropolitan and rural areas. Although access to OB/GYN care is generally lower in rural settings [43], prior studies have reported mixed findings regarding geographic differences in CRC screening adherence [44–46]. Lower screening participation in underserved urban populations may partially explain the findings for metropolitan areas in this analysis [47].

This study has several limitations. Eligibility required continuous enrollment, which may limit generalizability to individuals with unstable insurance coverage. Claims-based ascertainment may misclassify colonoscopy completion if services occurred outside observable settings or if coding inaccuracies were present, although standard procedural codes were used. Race and ethnicity included a sizable “Other/Unknown” category, and non-random missingness could bias disparity estimates. Residual confounding is possible because not all determinants of adherence, such as language preference, health literacy, navigation intensity, or patient beliefs, are captured in administrative data. The analysis focused specifically on OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA testing and did not evaluate the use of other stool-based modalities, such as FIT, ordered outside the ESL laboratory system. Similarly, prior CRC screening history before the index mt-sDNA order was not assessed, so we could not determine whether mt-sDNA represented initial or repeat screening or whether patients had previously been adherent to screening recommendations.

The study period included early and later phases of mt-sDNA implementation in clinical practice, and ordering patterns may have evolved as clinician familiarity and guideline recommendations changed over time. Although ordering volume increased during this period, the primary outcome evaluated adherence among women who were prescribed testing rather than population-level uptake. The relatively high adherence observed may reflect multiple factors, including structured outreach and navigation support associated with mt-sDNA testing, as well as provider- and patient-level influences. However, we did not directly measure navigation exposure or broader preventive health engagement. Finally, logistic regression yields ORs that may overstate relative differences when outcomes are common; therefore, we emphasize absolute percentages alongside adjusted associations. Because the analysis relied on de-identified administrative claims data linked to laboratory records through encrypted tokens, investigators did not have access to identifiable patient-level clinical charts.

Conclusions

In routine practice, more than 70% of women completed OB/GYN-ordered mt-sDNA, and more than 70% of those with positive results completed colonoscopy within 1 year. OB/GYN practices represent an important complementary setting for promoting CRC screening adherence, with targeted strategies needed to address gaps by insurance type, income, and race/ethnicity.
