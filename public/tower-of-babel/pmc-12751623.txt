Health terminators in working children: a critical ethnography
Authors: Haleh Jafari, Nahid Dehghan Nayeri, Serge Brand, Nematollah Fazeli, Alireza Nikbakht Nasrabadi, Fatemeh Khoshnavay Fomani, Shokoh Varaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple factors influence the health of working children, including cultural, behavioral, and environmental determinants. Identifying and understanding these factors is essential for developing effective interventions. This study aimed to identify and critically analyze the factors—termed “health terminators”—that undermine the physical and mental health of working children, considering cultural, behavioral, and environmental influences.MethodsThis study is the second part of a larger ethnographic research conducted in Tehran, employing a qualitative critical ethnographic design over four years. Data collection included prolonged participant observation in workplaces, homes, and schools, semi-structured interviews with children aged 10–18 and key informants, informal conversations, and analysis of documents and children’s drawings. Data were coded and analyzed following Carspecken’s framework, with triangulation, member checking, and peer debriefing used to ensure trustworthiness.ResultsAnalysis generated 3,057 low-level codes, with approximately one thousand assigned to the main theme “health terminators,” comprising five intermediate categories: cultural beliefs, self-harming behaviors, living in unhealthy environments, forgotten children, and social abandonment and vulnerability. Key issues identified included superstition, risky behaviors, neglect of preventive health measures, unsafe housing, educational and emotional neglect, and restricted access to health services.ConclusionsWorking children face multifaceted health risks driven by cultural, behavioral, and structural factors. Comprehensive, equity-oriented policies addressing physical needs, cultural beliefs, health behaviors, and environmental conditions are essential. Interventions such as culturally sensitive health education, improved living conditions, health insurance coverage, and social support can reduce vulnerability and promote equitable health outcomes, contributing to health justice for marginalized children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02707-9.

Background

Today, socioeconomic inequalities are evident across nearly all aspects of children’s physical and mental health worldwide. Children living in disadvantaged socioeconomic conditions tend to have poorer health outcomes compared to their peers [1], and child laborers represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup. The International Labour Organization (ILO) defines child labor as work that deprives children of their childhood, potential, and dignity, and is harmful to their physical and mental development. Such work is mentally, physically, emotionally, socially, or morally hazardous, interferes with schooling, and compromises the experience of a healthy childhood in any form [2]. As a result, these children often receive inadequate care, have limited educational opportunities, and face multiple challenges and adversities [3].

Due to their dependency and developmental immaturity, children are more vulnerable than adults to harmful working conditions [4]. Early employment exposes them to both immediate harms, such as injuries, occupational illnesses, and educational deprivation, and long-term consequences related to chronic physical and mental conditions. Malnutrition, limited health knowledge, and exposure to violence and abuse further exacerbate risks, leading to persistent mental disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety, phobias), chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular problems, immune dysfunction), and higher tendencies toward tobacco, alcohol, and substance use [3, 5, 6].

Health outcomes for individuals and communities are determined by a complex interplay of factors beyond biological characteristics. Socioeconomic status, access to adequate nutrition and housing, healthcare, culture, and individual and collective values and beliefs can shape health-related behaviors. Race and ethnicity, through discrimination and structural inequalities, influence educational, occupational, and healthcare opportunities, thereby widening health disparities. Social support from family, friends, and the community plays a crucial role in promoting mental health and coping with stress. In addition, the physical and social environment of neighborhoods, housing quality, safety, and environmental pollution directly affect physical and mental health. Education level, health literacy, and access to healthcare services are also key determinants of health. The interaction and overlap of these factors create the context for health inequalities, highlighting the importance of addressing them to improve population health, particularly among vulnerable groups [7]. In an ethnographic study by Navile, factors affecting health were identified as a combination of material, cultural, and spiritual elements. Parents perceived children’s health to be influenced by inadequate or unhygienic nutrition, poverty, nutrient deficiencies, and stunted growth. Traditional and spiritual beliefs, as well as supernatural forces, were also considered contributors to illness. Limited access to healthcare services, high costs, and long waiting times led many parents to avoid medical centers, relying instead on pharmacies or traditional and spiritual remedies [8].

Systematic reviews on child labor and health indicate that most studies in Iran and other developing countries have relied on cross-sectional designs, with very few longitudinal investigations. Moreover, prior research has largely focused on measurable health outcomes such as injuries, malnutrition, and toxic exposures, while differences related to the type of child work or the influence of environmental, cultural, and behavioral factors have been insufficiently explored. This is partly due to limitations in study design, lack of contextual data, and the predominance of quantitative approaches, which often fail to capture complex, multifaceted determinants of child health. These gaps highlight an incomplete understanding of the contextual and systemic factors affecting the health of child laborers, thereby underscoring the need for approaches capable of capturing these nuanced dimensions [9, 10].

In this context, anthropological approaches, such as ethnography, provide a framework for deeper and more comprehensive analyses of individual and group cultures. Ethnography elucidates the relationships between contexts, policies, structures, and human experiences, allowing researchers to examine complex, multidimensional interactions within work environments and health services. Key features include attention to local contexts, prolonged researcher engagement, collection of diverse data, and continuous reflexivity throughout the research process. This approach enables the identification of subtle details and unexpected relationships between health determinants and the experiences of children and service providers, which are often overlooked in cross-sectional or short-term studies [11]. Accordingly, this study aimed to critically explore the cultural, behavioral, and environmental factors undermining the health of child laborers through a critical ethnographic approach.

Methods

This article presents the second part of a larger ethnographic study exploring the culture of health among working children in Tehran, with a focus on identifying factors that harm their health. The first part of this research, which addressed general health impairments in this population, has been published in 2024 in BMC Public Health [12]. While that article examined the overall health impairments experienced by this population, the present study extends this inquiry by critically analyzing the cultural, behavioral, and environmental factors contributing to such impairments.

The present study is a qualitative critical ethnography conducted over four years, including more than two years of fieldwork. Following Carspecken’s approach, the study began with three preliminary steps: developing research questions, specifying study items, and reflecting on the researcher’s values. The main analytical process involved building a primary record through general and purposeful observations, conducting reconstructive analysis using low-level coding and horizon analysis (A method for exploring the different ways people understand or interpret a situation, helping the researcher see multiple perspectives in the data), generating data dialogically through interviews, describing systemic relationships (Comparing cultural content with other sites and social contexts, and discovering specific systemic relationships) by comparing cultural content across contexts, and explaining findings using theoretical and social models [13].

Before entering the field, research questions and specific study items were developed, and the researcher’s values and biases were examined through interviews with a knowledgeable team member to enhance reflexivity and reduce bias. Ethical approval and necessary permissions were obtained from Tehran University of Medical Sciences and the Association for Protection of Child Laborers.

Data were collected using multiple complementary methods. Extended participant observation was conducted across diverse settings, including workplaces, homes, and schools of working children in various districts of Tehran, with observations recorded daily immediately after each field session. To enrich understanding of children’s lived experiences, additional data sources were analyzed, including relevant documents such as medical records and children’s drawings, which were interpreted with the support of an expert in child psychology and drawing analysis.

Children were included in the study if they were aged 10–18 years, residing in Tehran province, working to support their family’s financial needs, and engaged in types of labor such as street vending, car window cleaning, or work in workshops or shops. Children who declined to participate in formal interviews were excluded from that part of the study. Additional participants, including family members, teachers, social workers, healthcare officials, and other professionals with at least two years of experience working with child laborers, were included to provide broader contextual insights. Overall, hundreds of working children were observed and assessed during the study. Informal, friendly conversations were established with over 50 children, while formal semi-structured interviews were conducted with six working children (2 girls and 4 boys). The interviews with working children began with questions such as “Can you describe a typical working day?”, “What does health mean to you and how does your work affect it?”, and “What do you usually do or where do you seek help when you feel sick or tired?” Additional probing and follow-up questions were asked during the interviews based on the children’s responses. Purposeful interviews were also conducted with 9 key informants, including the social deputy, the school principal, two psychologists, two social workers, a physician, a health officer at the Association for the Protection of Working Children, and the person responsible for the Children’s Rights Convention, as well as with 6 parents of the working children. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, with children providing their own consent for formal interviews in addition to parental or school official consent.

Data analysis followed Carspecken’s framework, beginning with low-level coding of transcripts and observational notes using MAXQDA software. These codes were then iteratively reviewed and grouped based on similarities and conceptual relevance, forming high-level codes, which were subsequently organized into subcategories and, through combining these subcategories, into overarching main categories. Findings were contextualized by comparing them with other social settings and explained using relevant social and theoretical models.

As an example of the coding process, the category of Cultural Beliefs was developed using low- and intermediate-level codes. Low-level codes included children’s preference for consulting spiritual healers instead of doctors, considering contraception sinful, beliefs about harmful effects of bathing at night, recommending healers for infertility, and avoiding discussion of lice. Other low-level codes reflected self-reliance, such as perceiving their bodies as strong and neglecting hygiene while relying on divine protection. Misconceptions and incorrect knowledge were also coded, including beliefs in the benefits of alcohol or lice, misbeliefs about menstruation, and lack of awareness about sexual assault prevention. Simplified or fantastical views of health (e.g., “I am healthy because my hands and feet aren’t broken”) and attitudes toward death (e.g., normalizing death in families and discussing serious illnesses openly) were also included. These low-level codes were grouped into intermediate categories such as Superstition, Self-Reliance, Misconceptions, Simplification of Health, and Attitudes toward Death, which together formed the overarching category of Cultural Beliefs, illustrating the cultural dimensions influencing child laborers’ health behaviors.

To ensure trustworthiness, several strategies were employed, including prolonged engagement with participants, triangulation of data sources and methods, and peer debriefing with colleagues to minimize bias. Member checking was conducted with two children, two parents, and four key informants to ensure the accuracy and credibility of the findings, and their feedback was incorporated into the analysis and interpretation. Continuous reflexivity was practiced throughout fieldwork, with the researcher systematically reflecting on personal assumptions, emotional responses, and potential biases. Special attention was paid to managing power dynamics between researcher and participants, encouraging open expression of self-perceptions, and critically reflecting on conflicts and interactions in the field, in accordance with Carspecken’s recommendations [13].

Findings

The findings of this study were derived from the analysis of observations in various settings, the researcher’s active participation as a street vendor of flowers and tissues, formal interviews with working children, their families, and individuals connected to child labor, informal and friendly conversations with working children and others, as well as the interpretation of 10 drawings by working children. A total of 3,057 low-level codes were generated, of which approximately one thousand codes were assigned to the main category of “health terminators”. This main category encompasses the set of factors, behaviors, and beliefs that contribute to the deterioration and decline of working children’s health. It consists of five intermediate categories: cultural beliefs, self-harming behaviors, living in unhealthy environments, forgotten children, and Social abandonment and vulnerability. The high-level codes of each intermediate category are presented in Table 1.

Cultural beliefs

The sub-category of Cultural Beliefs refers to superstitious views and misinformation commonly observed among working children and their families. The main findings shaping this sub-category include Superstition, Illusory Self-Reliance, Health Misinformation, Oversimplified Health Beliefs, and Emotional Detachment from Death.

Superstition

The lower-level codes forming this category highlight the presence of superstitious beliefs among working children and their families, which directly influence their health-related decisions.

Formal interview with school principal: “Clerics take money from children for their treatment, about 50 thousand Tomans per session. For example, the family of the girl you saw having a seizure in class once traveled outside Tehran to have the ‘jinn’ removed from her. Since medical visits such as neurology, psychology, or CT scans are so expensive, they thought it would cost less to see the exorcist. They said there was a jinn inside her.”

Illusory self-reliance

This category reflects the belief among working children that their bodies are extraordinarily resistant and that supernatural forces protect them from harm and illness. Although field observations confirm that these children may display a higher tolerance in difficult circumstances due to their living conditions, this does not equate to superhuman immunity.

Informal dialogue with a working girl: ‘They say there’s a cold that has killed some people.’ I said: ‘Yes, it’s called Corona. What do you do to avoid it?’ She replied: ‘Us? Nothing. We don’t get it. Our bodies are strong.”

Health misinformation

Beyond superstitions, a lack of accurate information or reliance on false knowledge also contributes to health risks in this group.

Informal dialogue with a working boy: “When you drink alcohol, your hands loosen up, and you feel heavy. It’s really good. They say alcohol washes your body clean.”

Oversimplified health beliefs

Interviews and observations revealed that working children often hold naive and simplistic views of health. Many perceive health as an abstract or distant concept, sometimes associating it only with affluent children, while neglecting their own well-being. Even when aware of the risks, they rarely engage in preventive measures.

Street interaction: “S(working boy) came and started smoking, and I was passively exposed as usual. I told him not to blow the smoke toward me. He joked: ‘No, the air is already polluted.’ It was true; schools had closed because of air pollution that day. He added: ‘But really, the air is polluted, you should wear a mask.’ I asked: ‘So why didn’t you wear one?’ He laughed and said: ‘Health? What’s that? Health isn’t for us. It’s for rich kids.”

Emotional detachment from death

Another striking observation was the children’s seemingly indifferent reactions to severe illness or even death within their families and communities. Although this cannot be fully reduced to neglect of health—since cultural and socioeconomic conditions also play a role—it markedly differs from the broader societal norms.

Formal interview with school principal: “We were talking about Sara, a girl who died from a throat infection a few months ago. We tried hard to inform her classmates gently, with the help of a psychologist. We started from philosophy and life, then told them Sara was sick, and finally pretended we received a message that she had passed away. Surprisingly, the children did not react as expected—no crying or deep sorrow. We decided to take them to the cemetery to process grief. A couple of them shed a few tears, but on the way back, they were so cheerful, singing songs, as if they were returning from a picnic. School principal said these children often report the death of relatives without showing much sadness, as though it were a normal event. It seems they have a high level of acceptance toward death.”

Self-harming behaviors

Self-harming behaviors constitute one of the subcategories under the main category of health disruptors. This subcategory does not merely focus on the internal beliefs and attitudes of working children but manifests more concretely through observable behaviors. The primary self-harming behaviors identified among these children, which endanger their health, include self-medication, poor adherence to health and treatment, substance and alcohol use, risky sexual behaviors, neglect of health and preventive behaviors, and risk-taking behaviors.

Self-medication

One prevalent behavior among many working children and their families is self-medication and avoidance of medical centers. Although multiple factors such as poverty, lack of insurance, and issues with identity documents (e.g., birth certificates) contribute to their reluctance to seek medical care, self-medication appears to be culturally normalized among working children and their families. Most children do not seek professional care except in life-threatening emergencies.

Formal interview with health officer: “Generally, until the pain becomes severe and unbearable, they prefer to get a drug from a pharmacy or use traditional methods they know themselves.”

Informal dialogue with a working girl: “I asked what you do at home if you get sick. He said my dad puts some oil on our stomach and wraps it with plastic until we get better, but if we get really, really sick, we go for an injection.”

Poor adherence to health and treatment

Another behavior potentially harmful to working children’s health is poor adherence to prescribed treatments. Often, medical instructions are ignored due to the daily challenges and workload these children face. Lack of knowledge and literacy among the children and their families leads to discontinuation of treatment, and overall, little importance is given to health and medical instructions.

Formal interview with social worker: “Maybe on the first day, they are eager to visit a volunteer doctor or receive free medication and follow up on treatment. But by the second, third, or fourth day, both the child and the family tend to forget. They rush to see the doctor and get the medicine, but they don’t pay attention to how to use it properly. For example, an antibiotic must be taken on time; even a half-hour delay can reduce its effectiveness.”

Substance and alcohol use

Despite prohibitions on alcohol and substance use in Iran and restrictions for individuals under 18 in many countries, a significant proportion of working children engage in early use of these substances. Influencing factors include peer group norms, accessibility of alcohol and drugs, coping with work-related and emotional pressures, and prolonged presence in unsupervised environments.

Formal interview with a working boy: “We smoke two or three packs a day. At first, we didn’t smoke. Then the other kids taught us, and we tried it, and eventually, we became smokers.”

Formal interview with a working boy: “I drank a lot of alcohol. I can also do gasoline. You know, you pour it in a can and shake it like this (gestures), and inhale it.”

Risky sexual behaviors

Many working children and adolescents have engaged in sexual relationships. These interactions may be exploitative, often in exchange for money, or consensual, usually with known peers to meet their needs. Preventive measures and hygiene are frequently neglected, resulting in health risks associated with risky sexual behaviors.

Formal interview with social deputy: “After a while, it becomes normal for the kids. They think if someone manipulates them to achieve a goal, like buying medicine, it’s just part of life.”

Field interaction: “Boys were around two girls, about 13–14 years old. One boy mentioned that sometimes boys have sexual interactions with them in alleyways. He admitted he had done it too. Money exchanges happen initially, but often they try to get the money back by force afterward. One boy had genital irritation afterward, and the others were scared.”

Neglect of health and preventive behaviors

Neglect of health and preventive behaviors is another striking pattern. Working children often disregard hygiene, safe food practices, and preventive health measures, prioritizing treatment only when illness becomes severe. Their attitudes sometimes trivialize serious risks.

Field interaction: “P (working boy) came carrying slices of sausage given to him by a driver and insisted I eat. I asked: ‘Are you sure it’s safe? What if it’s poisoned?’ He replied: ‘At worst, I’ll just die.”

Field interaction: “P (working boy) found a bottle on the street and drank from it. F (working boy) and R (working boy) finished the rest. When I asked if it was theirs, he said no. I warned: What if it’s contaminated? What if it’s unclean water?” He laughed and said: “So what? I’ve drunk plenty of same water before!”

Risk-taking behaviors

Finally, Risk-taking behaviors were widely observed in the daily activities of working children. Many of their games involved dangerous practices that exposed them to physical harm.

Field observation: “One night, R (working boy) was walking on the narrow edge of a wall about two and a half meters high near the subway, while P(working boy) kept throwing a ball at him. I was constantly worried that Reza might fall and get injured.”

Field observation: “P (working boy) picked up burning pieces of coal with his bare hands and put them into a censer. Soon after, F (working boy) joined in, not wanting to appear weaker, and held the red-hot coals in his hands, turning them over as if to demonstrate his courage.”

Living in unhealthy environments

Living in Unhealthy Environments is another subcategory of the overarching category Health Terminators. This subcategory highlights the impact of home and neighborhood conditions on the health of working children.

Unsafe housing

This concept refers to the issues that many working children face within their homes, which negatively affect their health. Examples include child abuse, domestic violence, a stressful family environment, and lack of a stable family nucleus. These conditions transform what is generally considered a safe haven into a space marked by insecurity, often driving children to seek escape from home.

Formal interview with social worker: “Given the difficult conditions at home, the only thing they can do is get married; Often at a young age. Most do not dislike this because of the harmful and stressful environment at home; they think it will bring them relief, but in most cases, it doesn’t, and their situation often worsens.”

Field interaction: “I was sitting on my usual bench on the sidewalk. Shortly after, S (working boy) came and sat next to me. He was upset and said, ‘People have become stingy, they don’t give money. I didn’t do anything tonight, and if I go home, my mother and brother will beat me again.”

Neighborhood as a pathway to misguidance

Field observations and social service case reviews showed that most working children and their families live in neighborhoods with poor socioeconomic conditions. Living and interacting in these neighborhoods exposes children to disorder, lawlessness, addiction, prostitution, aggression from residents, and poor environmental hygiene. These harmful behaviors act as a negative informal teacher, instilling misguided habits in children that gradually affect various aspects of their health.

Formal interview with social deputy: “Unfortunately, social harms like addiction are widespread here. Crime rates are high, and unfortunately, prostitution exists. It’s a kind of gathering place where anyone who isn’t welcome elsewhere can settle. People who don’t have money to rent a house can get a room here easily. Without documents or ID, they can still find a place to stay. This attracts a particular population and way of life to this area.”

Field observation: “Seeing a man, apparently addicted, sitting on a piece of cloth in the street, holding bread in one hand and soda in the other, asleep and bent over, was upsetting but not new.”

Health-neglecting lifestyle

Visits to homes, interactions with working children and their families, and discussions with informed officials led to the creation of the higher-level code Health-Neglecting Lifestyle. This emerged from conditions such as keeping birds indoors, shared and contaminated bathrooms and kitchens, lack of routine home cleaning, and no separation of sleeping areas for parents and children. These patterns are common across most families and create similar health risks for working children.

Home Visit: “The home environment was very different from what I considered a proper house. There was a yard with five rooms—or rather, five separate living spaces. The bathroom was about the size of a squat toilet with a single faucet, a plastic hose lying on the ground, and a broken sink outside that had apparently been unused for a long time. The kitchen was slightly elevated and almost next to the bathroom. The only thing making it a kitchen was an old, dirty stove. There was no sink, so dishes had to be washed in the yard—the same place they washed their hands. A few bird cages were in the kitchen, with droppings visible everywhere, and apparently there was no organized plan for general hygiene.”

Forgotten children

The intermediate category of Forgotten Children refers to the diminished role of many parents regarding their children and the disregard for working children. This parental inadequacy can manifest in forms such as neglect in upbringing, educational neglect, emotional neglect, and neglect of health, hygiene, and care, exposing children to numerous harms.

Neglect in upbringing

The family, as the fundamental unit responsible for child-rearing, is expected to attend to the educational and behavioral development of children. However, in the families of working children, issues such as lack of attention to parenting, neglecting the children’s behavioral problems, and failure to address inappropriate behaviors are noticeably common.

Informal dialogue with a working boy: “I asked, ‘What would your mom do if she found out you smoke?’ He said, ‘Nothing.’ He added, ‘A while ago, I was smoking in the bathroom. My sister smelled it and told my mom. I quickly put a piece of soap in my mouth so it wouldn’t smell like smoke.’ I said, ‘But your mom found out?’ He laughed, ‘Yeah, she knew. She didn’t care.”

Educational neglect

Families are expected to provide guidance on issues such as puberty, but unfortunately, many working children are deprived of this education by their parents. Moreover, some parents prevent their children from attending school, making educational dropout a significant issue in this group.

Observational participation: “I attended a class on puberty education. The class included about 15 girls, aged 12 to 14, who, due to their family circumstances, had been out of school and were attending early elementary grades. When asked if they had started menstruating, some looked puzzled and asked, ‘Menstruation?’ After explaining, it became clear that they knew nothing about it, and their mothers had not informed them.”

Emotional neglect

The study revealed that emotional neglect by parents, leading to emotional deprivation and a sense of not being loved by the family, is a prevalent issue. Factors such as viewing children as income-generating tools and weak emotional bonds within the family contribute to parental emotional neglect.

Formal interview with a social worker: “Sometimes parents just abandon them. What do they see? Only the income these children generate. They don’t care if the child gets sick, arrives home late, or leaves early—they just focus on that money.”

Neglect of health, hygiene, and care

Another form of neglect observed in parent-child relationships among the studied children relates to health and care. Examples include shirking responsibility for medical visits and treatment follow-up, unkempt hair, nails, and clothing, insufficient attention to children’s travel between work and home, inadequate nutrition, lack of care at home or workplace, parental awareness of substance use by the child without helping to stop it, all of which confirm the presence of neglect.

Field interaction: “It was very cold. M (working girl) stood beside me, talking, sometimes covering her mouth with her hands to warm it with her breath. She was wearing much less than I was. She had a thin pair of pants that offered no protection from the cold. She said she even wears them to school because she doesn’t have another pair. She had a jacket too, but it didn’t seem to protect her from the cold much. Her voice was hoarse. I asked, ‘Did you catch a cold?’ She said, ‘Yeah.’ I asked, ‘What did you do to get better?’ She said, ‘Nothing, school gave me medicine.’ I asked, ‘And at home? Did your mom do anything?’ She laughed, ‘No way, in our house everyone thinks about themselves.’”.

Formal interview with a volunteer physician: “It is very common to see children visiting doctors alone, and usually their families do not accompany the sick child during these visits.”

Social abandonment and vulnerability

Social abandonment and vulnerability emerged as another intermediate category in this study, representing a destructive factor affecting health. This intermediate category refers to issues that hinder individuals in preventing or treating illness. For instance, many working children, due to being immigrants, lack identification documents and insurance, making the costs of disease prevention and treatment significantly higher than for ordinary citizens. Furthermore, due to insufficient social support, many of these children are compelled at a young age to engage in harmful labor that jeopardizes their health. In addition, social and workplace harassment often acts as an additional burden on these children, causing multiple harms, while their rights are daily violated in various ways by different individuals.

Lack of insurance coverage

Lack of access to insurance coverage is a key factor affecting the health of working children and their families. Since many of these children are undocumented immigrants, they are usually deprived of services such as health insurance. This results in substantial healthcare costs, and because their families often lack sufficient financial resources, they tend to avoid seeking treatment.

Formal interview with a social worker: “Every doctor you visit, if you say you need a knee X-ray, or an endoscopy for your stomach, it all costs money for the family. And there’s absolutely no discount for immigrants.”

Limited capacity to obtain health

Poverty and lack of social support are among the most important factors affecting health and can influence various health dimensions. Our study showed that many health issues stem from inadequate diets lacking sufficient nutritional value. Unfortunately, many working children and their families cannot afford healthy and nutritious food, relying instead on minimal and insufficient meals. Limited financial capacity is not only evident in nutrition but also in minor health-related expenses such as masks and hygiene items, exposing them to health risks. Moreover, due to high treatment costs, this group often refrains from seeking modern medical care.

Formal interview with health officer: “When they see a treatment will cost them, they try to use something at home, a decoction or something they believe will help the child, or for fractures, since going to the hospital is expensive, they use local bone-setters.”

Formal interview with volunteer physician: “In our prescriptions, we cannot provide dietary instructions because the families cannot afford it.”

Forced engagement in harmful labor

Various life challenges and lack of societal and governmental support push some groups to engage in harmful labor for livelihood, often compelling their children to work for additional income. Childhood labor deprives children of their rights, including education and play, while exposing them to serious health risks. This study found that child labor often involves dangers such as street accidents, work-related illnesses in unsafe conditions, pollution, and multiple other health hazards.

Field observation: “During peak COVID days, I went to the streets. Nothing had changed; children and adolescents were everywhere—scavenging, standing in front of shops asking for money, cleaning windows at intersections, sitting with scales in corners—all without masks or gloves. A shopkeeper near Fatemi Metro said they come every day, like before. No masks, no gloves. A school principal said the children cannot stop working despite COVID.”

Social and workplace harassment

Harassment in society and workplaces is a visible issue experienced by many. Often, workplace and societal violence exert significant pressure on these children. Such violence manifests in various ways: stigmatizing child labor, undermining children’s dignity through neglect or pity, social exclusion, exploitation of children’s property, direct abuse, exploiting their poverty and needs, and making them invisible or ignored.

Participatory observation: “Tonight my migraine flared. If I were truly a flower seller, people would harass me constantly. Even now, I face harassment. ‘Do you want flowers?’—he yawns without answering, looks away, gestures ‘no’ while talking to someone beside him. I lower my mask slightly: ‘Do you want flowers?’ He stares into my eyes, then responds with a smile and calm tone: ‘No,’ raising his eyebrows (OC: a gaze seemingly meaningful, evoking a sense of lewdness).”

Formal interview with working girl: “One day I cleaned a woman’s car window, and she grabbed my hair, pulled me down, and yelled at me, asking why I did it. I cried a lot.”

Violated rights

Beyond harsh behaviors and societal harassment, working children either relinquish many of their rights due to circumstances or have them violated in various ways. Deprivation from play and education, yielding to parental demands, violations of child labor laws, scavenging under unsafe conditions, being overpowered by others, lack of protection against assault, or sexual exploitation are examples of violated rights of children.

Formal interview with psychologist: “Even though they are being treated unfairly, since they lack identity documents, they usually do not complain. Unfortunately, many employers hire immigrants because they can pay them less. Even if they don’t get paid, they dare not complain, and they are heavily exploited—unfortunately, there is nothing they can do.”

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that multiple factors, including cultural beliefs, self-harming behaviors, living in unhealthy environments, neglected children, and social vulnerability, each contribute to undermining the health of working children. Following Carspecken’s recommendation, the findings are interpreted within relevant sociological frameworks, with micro-level results analyzed in the context of broader theories. In the following section, we first present selected theoretical perspectives and then discuss relevant previous studies.

The findings of this study indicate that the health of working children is shaped by multiple interrelated factors, which can be understood through key sociological frameworks. From Habermas’s communicative action perspective, limited opportunities for open dialogue and mutual understanding distort health information and foster cultural beliefs such as superstition, illusory self-reliance, and oversimplified health perceptions, while the systemic dominance of political and economic structures undermines children’s access to care [14]. Oscar Lewis’s culture of poverty theory explains how socioeconomic deprivation and familial constraints contribute to self-harming behaviors, substance use, neglect of preventive health, risky sexual practices, and insufficient emotional and educational support. Social disorganization theory accounts for the impact of unsafe living environments and neighborhood contexts that increase exposure to health hazards and harmful labor [15]. Finally, Castells’ social exclusion framework illustrates how systemic discrimination, lack of insurance, and social or workplace harassment limit children’s participation in normative societal activities and heighten vulnerability. Together, these theories demonstrate that working children’s health outcomes are the result of complex interactions between cultural, familial, and structural factors [16].

In the following, the findings are interpreted in light of previous studies. Pearce and colleagues examined factors and pathways leading to health inequalities among children, highlighting the role of social, economic, and environmental factors, such as family income, parental education, access to health services, and living conditions, in shaping children’s physical and mental health [1].These results align with the present study regarding factors affecting working children’s health.

Cultural beliefs were found to influence health behaviors and access to care, often leading to misconceptions and health risks. Similar findings were reported by Navile, who noted the influence of material, cultural, and spiritual dimensions on children’s health, with traditional and spiritual beliefs contributing to illness and limited healthcare access [8]. Bewunetu Zewude et al. also emphasized the role of cultural beliefs in shaping preventive behaviors among working children [17]. These findings underscore the importance of considering cultural beliefs when designing health interventions to improve health outcomes.

Self-harming behaviors among working children, including substance use, risky practices, and neglect of preventive or treatment measures, pose serious threats to their physical and mental health. Bewunetu Zewude et al. also reported that street children are exposed to risks such as smoking, inhaling harmful substances, sharing personal items, and unprotected sex, all of which endanger their health [17]. Despite tendencies toward self-medication and reliance on traditional or spiritual remedies, many still seek modern healthcare when seriously ill. This is consistent with the present study, which found that working children and their families often use self-medication and traditional practices, turning to modern care mainly in life-threatening cases. Adam and Aigbokhaode showed that self-medication is the most common response to illness due to widespread access to traditional and alternative treatments [18]. Omeire noted that many people first prefer simple and inexpensive methods, resorting to modern care only if those fail [19]. Similarly, Eshita found that street children frequently use over-the-counter drugs for self-treatment [20]. These patterns reflect cultural, economic, and healthcare system differences. Limited access, financial hardship, and lack of information often drive working children toward unsafe practices and reliance on unscientific self-medication.

The study showed that living environments play a major role in the health risks of working children. Unsafe homes and neighborhoods with crime and poverty threaten their well-being. Woan et al. reported that poor shelters expose street children to infectious diseases, reproductive health issues, mental illness, and stunted growth [21]. Chowdhury et al. linked crowded and unhealthy settings to skin diseases and other infections [22]. Wallerich et al. emphasized overcrowding, pollution, and family and social support as key health determinants [23]. These results support the present study, confirming the strong impact of living conditions on working children’s health.

The study found that working children are often a neglected group, facing deficits in education, caregiving, emotional support, and health. Ahad et al. reported similar results in rural Bangladesh, where children suffered from inadequate care, poor education, lack of emotional support, and health neglect. Many experienced aggression, verbal abuse, rejection at home, insufficient care during illness, poor clothing, malnutrition, and lack of supervision. Economic hardship, parents’ own childhood maltreatment, and poor working conditions were identified as major risk factors [24]. These findings are consistent with the present study, reinforcing the notion that working children are often overlooked and deprived of necessary support.

In summary, integrating the study’s findings with relevant sociological theories highlights the broader implications of the factors affecting working children’s health and sets the stage for introducing the novel framework. The present study introduces the “health terminators” framework, which systematically categorizes the multiple social, cultural, behavioral, and environmental factors threatening the health of working children. Unlike previous studies that addressed these factors separately, this framework integrates them into a cohesive model, offering a comprehensive lens for understanding health risks. While developed based on data from Tehran, the framework’s principles may be adapted and tested in other contexts with vulnerable child populations, providing a useful tool for both research and policy design.

Conclusion

Working children face severe threats to their physical and mental health due to a combination of cultural beliefs, self-harming behaviors, unsafe living conditions, and social neglect. The present study demonstrates that these factors do not act in isolation but interact to produce complex health vulnerabilities, highlighting the need for comprehensive interventions. Based on these findings, specific policy recommendations can be made. First, expanding universal health insurance coverage for all children, including migrants and those in informal labor, is essential to ensure timely access to medical care. Second, culturally sensitive health education programs should be implemented to address misconceptions, superstitions, and risky behaviors, promoting preventive health practices. Third, improvements in housing, sanitation, and neighborhood safety are necessary to mitigate environmental health risks. Fourth, social support programs, including psychosocial counseling, educational assistance, and child protection services, should target marginalized and vulnerable groups to prevent neglect and exploitation. Finally, coordination between governmental agencies, civil society organizations, and local communities is critical to ensure that interventions are comprehensive, sustainable, and equitable. By integrating these measures, policymakers can effectively reduce health disparities, protect the rights of working children, and move toward greater health justice for this marginalized population.
