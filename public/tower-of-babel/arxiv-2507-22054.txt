Pitfalls when tackling the exponential concentration of parameterized quantum models
Reyhaneh Aghaei Saem, Behrang Tafreshi, Zoë Holmes, Supanut Thanasilp

Abstract
Identifying scalable circuit architectures remains a central challenge in variational quantum computing and quantum machine learning. Many approaches have been proposed to mitigate or avoid the barren plateau phenomenon or, more broadly, exponential concentration. However, due to the intricate interplay between quantum measurements and classical post-processing, we argue these techniques often fail to circumvent concentration effects in practice. Here, by analyzing concentration at the level of measurement outcome probabilities and leveraging tools from hypothesis testing, we develop a practical framework for diagnosing whether a parameterized quantum model is inhibited by exponential concentration. Applying this framework, we argue that several widely used methods (including quantum natural gradient, sample-based optimization, and certain neural-network-inspired initializations) do not overcome exponential concentration with finite measurement budgets, though they may still aid training in other ways.

II
Framework

Any parameterized quantum model, whether part of a variational or non-variational algorithm, consists of two essential components:

1.

Extracting information from a quantum device via measurements, and

2.

Classical post-processing of the resulting data.

To formalize this structure, we introduce a general procedure
\mathcal{P}
(sketched in Fig.
2
) that underlies a wide range of parameterized quantum models.
For variational quantum algorithms, which are adaptive in the sense that later measurements depend on earlier outputs, our procedure
\mathcal{P}
captures a single iteration of the algorithm. In contrast, for non-adaptive models such as quantum kernel methods
Schuld 2021
;
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Thanasilp et al. 2024
;
Kübler et al. 2021
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Gentinetta et al. 2022
;
Gan et al. 2023
or quantum reservoir computing
Xiong et al. 2025
;
Xiong et al. 2023
;
Fujii and Nakajima 2017
;
Nakajima et al. 2019
;
Mujal et al. 2021
;
Ghosh et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2023
,
\mathcal{P}
captures the entire algorithm.

Specifically, we consider a procedure
\mathcal{P}
that computes a set of variable-dependent physical quantities
\{\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}
where
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
is real valued and depends on some variables
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
. We suppose that the number of relevant quantities
N_{\ell}
scales at most polynomially with the system size i.e.,
N_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. We note here that each individual
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
is kept general for now. As will be seen in the examples below, it could represent either some variable evaluated at some specific values (e.g., trainable parameters evaluated at some specific point on the loss landscape), or a new
type
of variable (e.g., classical input data).

Each
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
is then estimated by performing a POVM measurement
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}=\{M^{(i)}_{k}\}_{k}
on a quantum state
\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
, where the
M^{(i)}_{k}
are positive operators satisfying
\sum_{k}M^{(i)}_{k}=\mathbb{1}
.
Thus, each
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
is associated with a specific POVM
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
. It follows that the total procedure is associated with a set of
i=1,\dots,N_{\ell}
different POVMs (i.e., a set of sets of POVM operators).

After performing the POVM measurement
N
times on the quantum state
\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
(by repeatedly preparing the state for each measurement), we obtain a set of outcomes
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\{\lambda_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
where the
j^{\rm th}
measurement outcome takes the label
\lambda^{(i)}_{j}
(chosen from a set of labels
\{m^{(i)}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|}

associated with POVM elements
\{M^{(i)}_{k}\}
) with the probability

\displaystyle p^{(i)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\Tr[\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})M^{(i)}_{k}]\;\;.

(1)

Then, by applying some post-processing map
\Phi_{i}(\cdot)
on the outcomes, we obtain the statistical estimate of
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\Phi_{i}\big(\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\big)\;.

(2)

Eventually, with all
N_{\ell}
estimates obtained, the procedure
\mathcal{P}
processes these with another map
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)
.

By specifying appropriate components of the procedure
\mathcal{P}
, this framework covers a wide range of schemes used in VQAs and QML. We provide a non-exhaustive list of examples:

Evaluating a standard linear loss.
Consider a loss of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
with a parametrized state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a unitary operator parametrized by the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\rho_{0}
is an initial state, and some observable
H=\sum_{i}c_{i}O_{i}
where
\{c_{i}\}_{i}
is a set of coefficients and
\{O_{i}\}_{i}
is a set of operators (commonly, some Pauli strings or projectors)
Cerezo et al. 2021a
;
Bharti et al. 2022
;
Abbas et al. 2023
;
McArdle et al. 2020
. In our framework, each quantity
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
corresponds to
\Tr[O_{i}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
, with
\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\theta}
)
;\;\forall i
. The associated POVM measurement is simply an eigenbasis measurement of
O_{i}
.

Given a set of samples
\mathcal{S}^{(i)}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
where each outcome label
\lambda^{(i)}_{j}
corresponds to one of the eigenvalues of
O_{i}
, a statistical estimate
\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be obtained from the empirical mean of
\mathcal{S}^{(i)}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. That is,

\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Phi_{\rm mean}\left(\mathcal{S}^{(i)}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda^{(i)}_{j}

(3)

The final post-processing map
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)
simply takes the linear combination of these estimates with the relevant
c_{i}
coefficient. That is, the procedure outputs

\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}c_{i}\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,,

(4)

which is an unbiased estimate of
\Tr[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
.

Evaluating other loss functions.

The evaluation of non-linear loss functions can be captured in this framework by modifying the final map in Eq. (
4
) to incorporate a non-linear function and also different initial states. For example, consider a supervised learning task with a training dataset
\{\rho_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}
such that an input quantum state
\rho_{i}
is associated with a label
y_{i}
. Further consider a parametrized circuit
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and a model of the form
\Tr[U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rho_{i}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})h]
where
h
is some Pauli operator. Again, in our framework we have that each
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
corresponds to the model prediction with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\theta}\;;\;\forall i
. The Mean Square Error (MSE) can be estimated using a post-processing map
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)
that implements

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)=\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}(y_{i}-\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{2}\;.

(5)

Gradient-based and non-gradient based training.

The general procedure can cover any training strategy which requires loss values at different points on the landscape. This generally includes gradient-based methods
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019
and gradient-free methods
Singh et al. 2023
;
Arrasmith et al. 2021
. The main idea here is that each loss value can be estimated with the approach described above, and the procedure map
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)
corresponds to processing these estimated losses and outputting the updated parameters according to the optimization method. For example, in the case of vanilla gradient descent we have that the final output
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)
is the vector which represents the difference between the updated parameters for the next training iteration and the current ones. In particular, for circuits obeying the parameter shift rule
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019
, the
k^{\rm th}
component of the output vector
[\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)]_{k}
with current parameter values
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(with the
k^{\rm th}
component
\theta_{k}
) is expressed as

\displaystyle\left[\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\big(\{\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\big)\right]_{k}=\theta_{k}-\frac{\eta}{2}

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\;\;\;\;-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)\right]\;,

(6)

where
\eta
is the learning rate. The term
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)
denotes the estimated loss in Eq. (
4
) evaluated at the shifted parameter values
\boldsymbol{\theta}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}
where
\hat{e}_{k}
is the unit vector in the direction of the
k^{\rm th}
parameter component. That is, in the simple case where the Hamiltonian is a single Pauli operator
O
and there are
N_{\rm p}
parameters (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{N_{p}})
),
we have that
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})O]
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i+N_{\rm p}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,N_{p}
such that
N_{\ell}=2N_{\rm p}
.

Quantum natural gradient.
This optimization method explicitly takes into account the local geometric structure of the parametrized state space encoded in Quantum Geometric Tensor (QGT)
Stokes et al. 2020
. If the parametrized circuit uses gates composed of Pauli gate generators, the QGT is block diagonal and the relevant components of QGT can be obtained by measuring and processing expectation values of some relevant Pauli operators. Hence, in addition to the loss values for estimating the typical gradients (as in Eq. (
II
)), these extra quantities correspond to additional POVM measurements in the procedure (see Appendix
C
for more details).

Sample-based training strategy.
We can also tackle an optimization strategy which does not explicitly construct an expectation value but relies directly on samples obtained from measurements. Essentially, in the optimization process, one has to process the samples in some sense (– even if this process does not correspond to expectation/loss values), this is equivalent to choosing an appropriate processing map in our setting. To be more concrete, consider CVaR optimization proposed in Ref.
Barkoutsos et al. 2020
to solve a binary optimization problem. For a given set of samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(sorted in non-decreasing order), the strategy involves only
\lceil{\gamma N}\rceil
samples with some hyperparameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
to construct a loss component of the form

\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Phi_{\rm CVaR}^{(\gamma)}\left(\mathcal{S}^{(i)}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)=\frac{1}{\lceil{\gamma N}\rceil}\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil{\gamma N}\rceil}\lambda^{(i)}_{j}\,.

(7)

Classical neural network-assisted initialization strategy.
This approach relies on initializing
circuit
parameters with a classical neural network
Friedrich and Maziero 2022
;
Miao et al. 2024
. Upon training, the weights and biases of the classical neural network are directly adjusted (instead of circuit parameters). Through the lens of our general procedure, we can cover this strategy by simply identifying the trainable weights and biases as
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Non-variational QML models.
The general procedure also encompasses different families of QML model. The core thread here is that any QML algorithm, simply by definition, requires the model to interact with quantum computers one way or the other. One can then appropriately specify the components in the procedure to fit the QML model of interest.
Consider quantum kernel methods as an example. Let
\boldsymbol{x}
denote a classical input,
U(\boldsymbol{x})
a data-embedding unitary and
\rho_{0}
an initial state chosen to be the all-zero state
\rho_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n}
. Then we have
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\Tr[|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n}U^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}})U(\boldsymbol{x})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x})U(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})]
for all
i
such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}\}
and
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=U^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}})U(\boldsymbol{x})\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x})U(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
. Using the overlap test which computes the fidelity by measuring the expectation value of the all-zero state for
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, the estimated fidelity kernel between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}
can be expressed as in Eq. (
3
) with the POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n},\mathbb{1}-|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n}\}
.

Polynomial POVMs in disguise.

A particularly observant reader might have noticed that in our specification of the procedure
\mathcal{P}
we consider only ‘polynomial’ POVMs. Namely, we require that each of the POVMs
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
contain at most polynomially (in
n
) many elements,
|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. It is then natural to wonder if standard procedures in the literature really do satisfy this constraint. After all, a computational basis measurement has exponentially many (
2^{n}
) different outcomes. Here we briefly address this potential point of confusion and explain how, while it is possible to consider parameterized quantum models that use POVMs with exponentially many outcomes (more on this in the Discussion), the standard ones currently used by the community in fact involve polynomial POVMs in disguise.

To illustrate this, consider the procedure of computing the standard VQA loss function
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
, where
H
is a diagonal Hamiltonian of the form
H=\sum_{i}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}
, with
Z_{i}
a single-qubit Pauli-Z operator on the
i^{\rm th}
qubit. From an initial brief look at the Hamiltonian, one might have thought that the relevant POVM elements are all the
n
-qubit computational basis states, hence scaling exponentially. However, this example does fall under the umbrella of our general procedure, as should be clear from looking back at the section on ‘evaluating a standard linear loss’ above. Namely, here the set of quantities to be estimated is
\{\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})Z_{i}Z_{i+1}]\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then, we can identify the associated POVM for the
i^{\rm th}
term
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}=\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle\langle 00|_{i,i+1}+|11\rangle\langle 11|_{i,i+1}),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle\langle 01|_{i,i+1}+|10\rangle\langle 10|_{i,i+1})\}
where these are projectors onto
+1
and
-1
eigenvalues subspaces. This POVM crucially contains only two elements and can be achieved by measuring in the two-qubit computational basis.

A further observation to make from the previous example is that while each of the
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
are different, they can all be simultaneously measured by measuring all the qubits in the computational basis. This is true more generally. Namely, the different
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})
generally require different POVMs, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}\neq\mathcal{M}^{(j)}
for
i\neq j
. However, some of the
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
may be measurable simultaneously.

III
Exponential concentration

Having distilled the information processing strategies used by standard parameterized quantum models into the general procedure outlined above, it becomes clear that the scalability of
\mathcal{P}
hinges on whether the measurement outcome probabilities, and thus the outcomes themselves, carry information about the underlying variables. This motivates a shift in focus: rather than analyzing exponential concentration at the level of the overall loss function (as is commonly done
McClean et al. 2018
;
Larocca et al. 2025
;
Marrero et al. 2021
;
Sharma et al. 2022
;
Patti et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Arrasmith et al. 2022
;
Larocca et al. 2022b
;
Holmes et al. 2022
;
Cerezo et al. 2021b
;
Khatri et al. 2019
;
Rudolph et al. 2024
;
Kieferova et al. 2021
;
Thanaslip et al. 2023
;
Holmes et al. 2021
;
Martín et al. 2023
;
Fontana et al. 2024
;
Ragone et al. 2024
;
Thanasilp et al. 2024
;
Letcher et al. 2024
;
Anschuetz 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2023
;
Crognaletti et al. 2024
;
Mao et al. 2024
;
Xiong et al. 2025
;
Suzuki and Li 2023
;
Cerezo et al. 2023
;
Arrasmith et al. 2021
), we instead consider concentration at the level of the POVM outcome probabilities associated with individual quantities.

For a given POVM
\mathcal{M}
, chosen from the set of POVMs
\{\mathcal{M}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{l}}
associated with a procedure, together with the state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, this alternative notion of exponential concentration can be formally defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Outcome probability concentration)
.

Consider a parametrized
n
-qubit state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{k}\}
. The POVM outcome probabilities concentrate with respect to the variable
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
if for all
M_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(|p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}|\geqslant\delta\right)\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\delta^{2}}\;\;;\;\;\beta\in\mathcal{O}\left(\exp(-n)\right)

(8)

where
p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})M_{k}]
is the probability of measuring an outcome associated with the element
M_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\mu_{k}
is some concentration point independent of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Crucially, we note that the underlying mechanisms that result in the concentration of the outcome probabilities are identical to those leading to the concentration of expectation values
Larocca et al. 2025
. This simply follows from the fact that the outcome probability can be expressed as the expectation of a POVM operator.

Nevertheless, Definition
1
allows us to pin down the practical consequence of the concentration and later provide the guidelines to determine the scalability of the given protocol (as presented in Section
II
).
That is,
for any
state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
the outcome probability distribution associated with the POVM
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\big(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),...,p_{|\mathcal{M}|}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\big)
, is close to the fixed
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-independent distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}=\big(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},...,\mu_{|\mathcal{M}|}\big)
. By using tools from hypothesis testing, we can rigorously show that if the support of these distributions scales at most polynomially in
n
, then with high probability these two probability distributions cannot be distinguished using only polynomial outcomes. Thus the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1

(Indistinguishability from probability concentration, informal)
.

Assume the exponential concentration of outcome probabilities as in Definition
1
on a POVM set
\mathcal{M}
with
|\mathcal{M}|\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
.
After polynomial measurement shots
N\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
, the obtained measurement samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
are with high probability
1-\delta
, such that
\delta\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, statistically indistinguishable from samples
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}
drawn from the fixed
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-independent distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
.

The direct consequence of Theorem
1
is stated below - namely, no classical post-processing removes this indistinguishability.

Corollary 1

(No post-processing, informal)
.

Post-processing
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
with any arbitrary map
\Phi^{\prime}(\cdot)
, with high probability
1-\delta^{\prime}
, such that
\delta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, leads to an estimate that is statistically indistinguishable from an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-independent random variable

\displaystyle\widehat{\ell}_{\rm fixed}=\Phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}})\;\;,

(9)

where each outcome in
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
.

We refer the readers to Appendix
B
for the formal statements, the proofs and the additional details.

Corollary
1
leaves the choice of post-processing arbitrary and hence applies generally to any procedure
\mathcal{P}
. To illustrate its practicality, we apply it to the training of a standard VQA loss with the BP landscape using a traditional gradient-based method.

Corollary 2

(Random walk via gradient descent, informal)
.

Consider a parametrized quantum state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
that depends on some trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and a loss function of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}c_{i}\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
where each
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})O_{i}]
with some parametrized state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, some Pauli operator
O_{i}
and
N_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. Further, consider a state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
that is generated from some parametrized circuit and suppose that the parameter shift rule is applied. Training the loss with vanilla gradient descent algorithm with a random initialization for polynomial training iterations using overall polynomial measurement shots results in a random walk with high probability
1-c
for some
c\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. That is, for a given iteration, the updated parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm new)}
for the next iteration follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(new)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}\;\;,

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm current)}
are parameter values for the current iteration, and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}
is a vector where each component is an instance of some parameter-independent random variable.

This is further supported by the numerical results shown in Fig.
3
, which illustrate training on a BP landscape under different shot budgets for a
15
-qubit system. The loss function is defined as the expectation value of a global Pauli-
Z
operator, and the ansatz consists of a single layer of single-qubit
X
rotations. This setup is known to suffer from the globality-induced barren plateau phenomenon that is observed for unstructured quantum circuits
Cerezo et al. 2021b
. Panel a) presents a two-dimensional projection of training trajectories obtained under various shot conditions (
150
shots,
2^{15}
shots, and infinite shots). The projection is computed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), as implemented in the
ORQVIZ
package
Rudolph et al. 2021
. We observe that trajectories with
2^{15}
shots and infinite shots converge toward a solution, while those with
150
shots exhibit a random wandering behavior over the landscape. Panels b) and c) show the scaling of the mean and variance of the parameter updates (averaged over multiple trajectories) as functions of training steps. Notably, both the mean and variance of the updates under the
150
-shot regime closely resemble those of a random walk.

We note that a similar conclusion is expected to hold for quantum natural gradient descent, albeit with some technical subtleties. While the estimated loss gradients remain indistinguishable, the situation is more subtle for the estimated QGT. In particular, certain elements of the QGT that capture the curvature of the quantum state generated by an
early
part of the circuit may still be estimated efficiently. However, extracting curvature information from the deeper parts of the circuit becomes increasingly difficult. As these are combined with the information-less estimated gradients, we do not expect the overall
estimated
quantum natural gradient to provide any meaningful direction on a featureless landscape. Nonetheless, the proof would be more complex.

IV
Practical step-by-step guidelines

We now outline a set of criteria to determine whether a given training or encoding procedure is vulnerable to scalability limitations due to exponential concentration.

1.

Given a procedure
\mathcal{P}
, identify the quantities
\{\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}
which require information to be extracted from quantum computers.

2.

For each quantity
\{\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})\}
, identify the corresponding
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
and check whether
|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|
, the number of POVM elements in
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
, scales at most polynomially with system size
n
.

Note that all barren plateau mitigation strategies we are aware of involve such polynomial-sized POVMs, even if some may initially appear to have exponentially many elements (see Section
II
above on ‘Polynomial POVMs in disguise’).

3.

Determine whether the outcome probabilities
p^{(i)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})=\Tr[\rho_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i})M^{(i)}_{k}]
exponentially concentrate with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
(as per Definition
1
).

If this is the case, the procedure
\mathcal{P}
suffers from the concentration in the sense that the measurement outcomes, with probability exponentially close to 1, contain no information about the variables
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
.

Based on the above guidelines, it follows that some proposals that were hoped to mitigate, or entirely avoid, the effects of exponential concentration may in fact still suffer from them. Namely, all of the examples discussed in Section
II
potentially fall under this category. These include certain forms of natural gradient descent
Stokes et al. 2020
, sample-based CVaR optimization
Barkoutsos et al. 2020
, agnostic classical neural network-assisted initialization
Friedrich and Maziero 2022
, and a rescaled gradient approach
Teo 2023
. In particular, if a given parameterized quantum circuit exhibits exponential concentration under standard gradient-based training, switching to a more sophisticated cost evaluation strategy, such as natural gradient descent or neural network-assisted training, will not resolve the problem, because it does not address the root cause: exponential concentration at the level of outcome probabilities.

In Fig.
4
, we further provide numerical simulations of the actual training on BP landscapes with these optimization methods using different shot budgets (polynomial shots of
10\times n
, exponential shots of
2^{n}
and infinite shot limit) on various system sizes (
n=9,11,13,15,17
). Similar to Fig.
3
, the numerical set-up is engineered such that the global-induced BP emerges (see further details in Appendix
C
). Here, one can clearly see the interplay with shot noise in the training process. While these methods indeed lead to successful optimization in the limit of
2^{n}
measurement shots, they fail to move in any meaningful direction with relatively small
10\times n
shots.

It is important to note that while our results highlight that some methods hoped to avoid exponential concentration still suffer the effects of exponential concentration, this does not mean that they cannot be used in any way to boost scalability. For example, quantum natural gradients methods are naturally employed in conjunction with identity initialization or warm starting strategies
Wierichs et al. 2020
;
Haug et al. 2021
. Such deployments can indeed avoid exponential concentration. However, this is in virtue of using a different initialization strategy that changes the concentration properties of the circuit, not in virtue of switching from vanilla gradients to natural gradients. More generally, these optimization strategies could have their own other strengths that aid training or scalability. Indeed, some of them were originally proposed independently of the scalability issue. By considering again the quantum natural gradient approach as an example, the method takes into account the local curvature of the state space, resulting in generally faster training convergence. More heuristically, neural network initialization strategies have been seen to lead to faster training
Friedrich and Maziero 2022
;
Miao et al. 2024
.

Lastly, although our primary focus in this section has been on variational quantum algorithms, we stress that these guidelines also apply to non-variational QML models. In particular, scalability issues in quantum kernel methods
Huang et al. 2021
;
Thanasilp et al. 2024
;
Kübler et al. 2021
;
Suzuki and Li 2023
;
Shaydulin and Wild 2022
and quantum reservoir approaches
Xiong et al. 2023
;
Xiong et al. 2025
, arising from concentration over the input data, can also be diagnosed using our core guidelines.

V
Discussion

Our work provides guidelines for assessing whether a given quantum model can avoid the effects of exponential concentration. These guidelines are grounded in a simple observation: any variational quantum procedure involves estimating certain quantities using quantum hardware followed by classical post-processing. As such, analysis should focus on concentration at the level of outcome probabilities rather than expectation values. Using tools from hypothesis testing, we show that for POVM measurements with a polynomial number of elements, exponential concentration of outcome probabilities implies that the measurement outcomes—together with any post-processing—contain no information about the variables (Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
).

We use these guidelines to critically re-examine several training strategies previously discussed as barren-plateau-free, including natural gradient descents
Stokes et al. 2020
, sample-based CVaR optimization
Barkoutsos et al. 2020
, agnostic classical neural network-assisted initialization
Friedrich and Maziero 2022
, and a rescaled gradient approach
Teo 2023
, with possible extensions to others such as
Rad et al. 2022
;
Faílde et al. 2023
;
Kashif and Al-kuwari 2023
. More concretely, we argue that while these methods may well have other benefits, they are still prone to the effects of exponential concentration in a similar manner to more standard methods
McClean et al. 2018
;
Arrasmith et al. 2021
;
Cerezo and Coles 2021
.

We now discuss two scenarios that are not captured by the general procedure and, consequently, fall outside the scope of the associated guidelines. The first scenario arises when the number of physical quantities scales exponentially with the system size, while the number of POVM elements in each POVM remains polynomial. Specifically, we consider the case where
N_{\ell}\in\Omega(\exp(n))
and
|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|\in\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
. A practical example of this setting is generative modeling with an explicit loss function, such as the Kullback–Leibler Divergence (KLD), where the objective is to train a parameterized model to reproduce a target distribution over computational basis bit-strings. Here the explicit loss consists of exponentially many terms; each comparing the probability assigned to an individual bit-string. The POVM associated with estimating each such probability consists of only two elements (i.e., for a bit-string
\boldsymbol{x}
, the POVM is
\{|\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{x}|,\;\mathbb{1}-|\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{x}|\}
). Moreover, all of these probabilities can, in principle, be estimated simultaneously via measurements in the computational basis. The subtlety, however, as studied in Ref.
Rudolph et al. 2024
, is that sampling a polynomial number of bit-strings only allows a polynomial number of terms in the loss to be assigned non-zero values. Since we cannot control which bit-strings are sampled, this results in a mismatch between the sampled loss terms and those present in the training distribution. Consequently, the estimated explicit loss fails to reliably assess the similarity between the model and the target distribution, ultimately inhibiting training.

Strategies that require POVM measurements with exponentially many elements also do not fall within the scope of our guidelines and could, in principle, offer a path toward avoiding the effects of exponential concentration. In such cases, the hypothesis testing analysis breaks down: although each individual outcome probability may be exponentially close to a concentration point, the total number of possible outcomes is also exponential. To highlight this explicitly, Appendix
D
presents a simple counterexample showing that two probability distributions with exponential support—whose individual components are exponentially close—can still be distinguishable, provided information about both distributions is available.
That said, we are not aware of any current proposals in variational quantum computing or quantum machine learning that make use of such exponential POVMs. As discussed in Section
II
, while some procedures may initially appear to require exponential POVM measurements, they are more accurately interpreted as involving multiple polynomial POVMs in disguise. Nonetheless, exploring this gap in our argument remains an intriguing direction for the hunt to vanquish barren plateaus.

Appendix A
Hypothesis Testing

Here, we provide preliminaries on binary hypothesis testing, a necessary tool for proving the statistical indistinguishability of measurement outcome probability distributions, as well as of post-processing applied to those outcomes. We present theoretical statements concerning the success of hypothesis testing in the single-sample case (Appendix
A.1
) and the multi-sample case (Appendix
A.2
). Finally, in Appendix
A.3
, we rigorously define the notion of statistical indistinguishability.

A.1
One sample

Lemma 1
.

Consider two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
over some finite set
\mathcal{I}
. Suppose we are given a single sample
\mathcal{S}
drawn from either
\mathbb{P}
or
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
with equal probability. We have the following two hypotheses:

•

Null Hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}
.

•

Alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
.

The probability of correctly deciding the true hypothesis is given by

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{4}\;\;,

(11)

where
\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}|p(s)-p^{\prime}(s)|
is the 1-norm between the two distributions.

Proof.

For two given distributions, there exists a region
\mathcal{A}
such that
p(s)>p^{\prime}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{A}
. The optimal test is to choose that the given sample
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}
(null hypothesis) if it falls in the region i.e.,
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{A}
and guess
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
(alternative hypothesis), otherwise. The probability of choosing the correct hypothesis can be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P})\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P})+\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\notin\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\prime})\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\prime})

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big[\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P})+\text{Pr}(\mathcal{S}\notin\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\prime})\big]

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}p(s)+\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{A}}p^{\prime}(s)\Bigg],

(14)

where the second equality is due to the sample being equally likely to be drawn from either
\mathbb{P}
or
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
. In the last equality, we use the fact that the given sample is from
\mathbb{P}
, the probability that this sample takes any value within the region
\mathcal{A}
is simple
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}p(s)
, and similarly for
s\notin\mathcal{A}
.

The 1-norm between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
is the 1-norm between the probability vectors, and it can be written as

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}|p(s)-p^{\prime}(s)|

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}\big(p(s)-p^{\prime}(s)\big)+\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{A}}\big(p^{\prime}(s)-p(s)\big)\;\;,

(16)

where the second equality is due to the definition of the region
\mathcal{A}
. Lastly, we notice that

\displaystyle\frac{2+\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}p(s)+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}p^{\prime}(s)+\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}\bigg)

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}p(s)+\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{A}}p^{\prime}(s)\;\;,

(18)

which upon substituting back to Eq. (
14
) completes the proof.
∎

Many samples

We begin with Lemma
2

26

Lemma 2
.

Consider the probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{i}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}
over some finite set
\mathcal{I}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}

\mathbb{P}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}

(19)

Proof.

The 1-norm between the two product distributions can be bounded as

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}

(20)

\displaystyle=\left\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=2}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]+\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=2}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]-[\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}]\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=3}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]+[\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}]\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=3}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]+\dots\right.

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\left.-\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right]\otimes\mathbb{P}_{N}+\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right]\otimes\mathbb{P}_{N}-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right\|_{1}

(21)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=2}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]\right\|_{1}+\left\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=2}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]-[\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}]\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=3}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]\right\|_{1}+

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\left\|[\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}]\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=3}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]-[\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{3}]\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=4}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right]\right\|_{1}+\dots+\left\|\mathbb{P}_{N}\otimes\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right]-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right\|_{1}\;,

(22)

where in the second equality, we have added and subtracted terms, the first inequality is simply the triangle inequality. We further proceed the bound as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\|_{1}\,\left\|\bigotimes_{i=2}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right\|_{1}+\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\|_{1}\,\|\mathbb{P}_{2}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}\|_{1}\,\left\|\bigotimes_{i=3}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{i}\right\|_{1}+\dots+\left\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\right\|_{1}\,\|\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{N}\|_{1}

(23)

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\|_{1}\,\prod_{i=2}^{N}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}\|_{1}+\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{1}\|_{1}\,\|\mathbb{P}_{2}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{2}\|_{1}\,\prod_{i=3}^{N}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}\|_{1}+\dots+\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{1}\,\|\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{N}\|_{1}

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbb{P}_{i}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{1}\;\;,

(25)

where we use the following properties that the 1-norm of each individual distribution sums up to
1
i.e.,
\|\mathbb{P}_{i}\|_{1}=\|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{1}=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 1
.

Consider the probability distributions
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\otimes N}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\prime\otimes N}
over the finite set
\mathcal{I}^{\otimes N}_{0}
. Suppose we are given
N
samples (denoted as
\mathcal{S}
) drawn from either
\mathbb{P}_{0}
or
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{0}
with equal probabilities. We have the following two hypotheses:

•

Null Hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}
.

•

Alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
.

The probability of correctly choosing the hypothesis is upper bounded as

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N\|\mathbb{P}_{0}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{0}\|_{1}}{4}.

(26)

Proof.

We use Lemma
1
to find the probability of making a correct hypothesis as

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}}{4}.

(27)

Then according to Lemma
19
, we have
\|\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leqslant N\|\mathbb{P}_{0}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{0}\|_{1}
. So we can find an upper bound for the probability of the right hypothesis as

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\|\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\otimes N}-\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\prime\otimes N}\|_{1}}{4}

(28)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N\|\mathbb{P}_{0}-\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{0}\|_{1}}{4}\;\;.

(29)

∎

A.3
Statistical indistinguishability

We define statistical indistinguishability based on the success probability of a binary hypothesis testing task. Furthermore, we introduce the notion of statistical indistinguishability at two levels. Namely, Definition
2
concerns the level of distributions or samples, while Definition
3
addresses the level of outputs.

Definition 2

(Statistical indistinguishability of distributions)
.

Two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
are statistically indistinguishable with
N
samples if a binary hypothesis test cannot be passed with probability at least
0.51
. That is, given a set of
N
samples
\mathcal{S}
drawn from either
\mathbb{P}
or
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
(with an equal probability), consider the following hypotheses

•

Null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}
,

•

Alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
:
\mathcal{S}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
,

where
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
are statistically indistinguishable (with
N
samples) if for any algorithm the probability of correctly identifying the correct hypothesis satisfies:

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}[``{\rm right\;decision\;between\,}\mathcal{H}_{0}\,{\rm and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}"]\leqslant 0.51\;.

(30)

Remark that the threshold
0.51
is arbitrarily chosen to be close to that of random guessing.

Definition 3

(Statistical indistinguishability of outputs)
.

Consider a map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
(with
M
being the dimension of the output) and two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
which are statistically indistinguishable under
N
samples according to Definition
2
. Draw
N
respective samples from
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
, which we respectively denote as
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{P}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{P}^{\prime}}
. We say that
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{P}})
and
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{P}^{\prime}})
are statistically indistinguishable outputs.

Appendix B
Indistinguishability from probability concentration

In this Appendix, we provide the formal statements of the theoretical results in the main text together with their detailed proofs. In particular, we have:

•

The formal version of Theorem
1
is presented in Theorem
2
.

•

The formal version of Corollary
1
is presented in Corollary
3
.

•

The formal version of Corollary
2
is presented in Corollary
4
.

We begin with general theoretical results demonstrating that the practical consequence of outcome probability concentration manifests as statistical indistinguishability—both at the level of distributions/samples, as shown in Theorem
1
, and at the level of outputs, as shown in Corollary
3
.

Theorem 2

(Formal version of Theorem
1
)
.

Consider a parametrized
n
-qubit state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and a POVM set
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}
with polynomial elements
|\mathcal{M}|\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. The associated outcome probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\{p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\cdots,p_{|M|}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\}\;,

(31)

with
p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\alpha})M_{k}]
as the probability of obtaining the measurement associated with the element
M_{k}
. Now, assume the exponential concentration of outcome probabilities as in Definition
1
, with some
\beta\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, for all possible outcomes with the fixed probability distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}=\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\dots,\mu_{|\mathcal{M}|}\}\;,

(32)

where
\mu_{k}
is the concentration point of
p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
. For any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, with high probability exponentially close to
1
, the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
are statistically indistinguishable with polynomial samples
N\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
according to Definition
2
.
That is, let
\mathcal{S}_{N}
be polynomial-sized samples drawn from either
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
or
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
(with equal probability) and consider the following hypotheses:

•

Null Hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

•

Alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
:
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
such that
\delta\in\mathcal{O}({\rm exp}(-n))
, the probability of correctly deciding the true hypothesis is given by

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\;,

(33)

where
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. More particularly, we have
\delta=|\mathcal{M}|\sqrt{\beta}
and
\varepsilon=\frac{N|\mathcal{M}|\beta^{\rm 1/4}}{4}
.

Proof.

Given an
N
-sized set of samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, the probability of successfully making the decision can be upper bounded with Proposition
26
as

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}}{4}.

(34)

To compute
\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}
we can use the definition of 1-norm between two probability distributions and write

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}|{p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}}|,

(35)

where
|\mathcal{M}|
is the number of POVM operators.

To further proceed, we use the definition of the exponential concentration of outcome probabilities as in Definition
1
. For each outcome probability, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(|p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}|\geqslant\delta^{\prime}\right)\leqslant\frac{\beta}{\delta^{\prime 2}}\;\;,\;\;\beta\in\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{b^{n}}\right)\;\;\;,

(36)

with some
b>1
and
\mu_{k}
is some concentration point independent of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
We then choose
\delta^{\prime}=\beta^{\frac{1}{4}}
and invert the inequality of Eq. (
36
), leading to

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(|p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}|\leqslant\beta^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\geqslant 1-\sqrt{\beta}\;,\;\;\beta\in\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{b^{n}}\right)\;.

(37)

That is, we have that each outcome probability is exponentially close to the concentration point with high probability, exponentially close to
1
.

We now show that this is sufficient to imply exponential vanishing of the one-norm. In particular, this can be shown by using the union bound over all outcome probabilities.
Let
E_{k}
be the event that
|p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}|\leqslant\beta^{\frac{1}{4}}
. From Eq. (
37
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(E_{k}\right)\geqslant 1-\sqrt{\beta}\;,\;\;\beta\in\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{b^{n}}\right)\;.

(38)

Now, the probability that all
E_{k}
occur can be bounded with the union bound as

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}E_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}\bar{E}_{k}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\bar{E}_{k}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-|\mathcal{M}|\sqrt{\beta}\;,

(41)

where
\bar{E}_{k}
is conjugate event of
E_{k}
, we use union bound in the second line and use
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left(\bar{E}_{k}\right)\leqslant\sqrt{\beta}
by reversing the inequality in Eq. (
37
). So, with probability at least
1-|\mathcal{M}|\sqrt{\beta}
over the parameters, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}|{p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{k}}|\leqslant|\mathcal{M}|\beta^{\frac{1}{4}},

(42)

By putting all together in Eq. (
34
), this leads to, with the probability at least
1-|\mathcal{M}|\sqrt{\beta}
, the probability of success is bounded as,

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N|\mathcal{M}|\beta^{\frac{1}{4}}}{4}=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\;,

(43)

where
\varepsilon=\frac{N|\mathcal{M}|\beta^{\frac{1}{4}}}{4}\in\mathcal{O}(c^{\prime-n})
for some
c^{\prime}>1
since
N,|\mathcal{M}|\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. In addition, by denoting
\delta=|\mathcal{M}|\sqrt{\beta}
, we also have
\delta\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
.
That is, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
are statistically indistinguishable with polynomial samples according to Definition
2
. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3

(No post-processing, formal)
.

Consider a map
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
(with
M
being the dimension of the output) on the set of measurement outcomes. Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
2
, consider two
N
-sized sets of samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}
where samples in
\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
(and
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}
) are drawn from
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
(and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
). With probability at least
1-\delta
such that
\delta\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, the outputs
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
and
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}})
are statistically indistinguishable as in Definition
3
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2
, the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
are statistically indistinguishable with probability at least
1-\delta
with
\delta\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. If the outputs from the processing map
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
and
\Phi(\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}})
were distinguishable, this would imply a strategy to reliable distinguish
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
. Hence, by contradiction, it must not be possible to distinguish the outputs from the processing map on the samples
\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}
.
∎

The implication of Corollary
3
is that post-processing the obtained samples cannot overcome the limitations imposed by exponential concentration. This conclusion holds for arbitrary procedures. To illustrate a concrete example, we show that training the loss on a featureless landscape using a standard gradient-based approach results in a random walk over the landscape.

Corollary 4

(Random walk via gradient descent, formal)
.

Consider a parametrized quantum state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
that depends on some trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and a loss function of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
where each
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})O_{i}]
with some parametrized state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, some Pauli operator
O_{i}
, and
N_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. Further, consider
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is generated from some parametrized circuit such that the parameter shift rule is applied. Training the loss with the standard gradient descent algorithm with a random initialization for polynomial training iterations using overall polynomial measurement shots results in a random walk with high probability
1-c
for some
c\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. That is, for a given iteration, the updated parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm new)}
for the next iteration follow

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(new)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}\;\;,

(44)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm current)}
are parameter values for the current iteration, and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}
is a vector where each component is an instance of some parameter-independent random variable. In particular, the
k^{\rm th}
component
[\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}]_{k}
is of the form

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}]_{k}=-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{z_{ijk}}{N}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{z^{\prime}_{ijk}}{N}\right)\right]\;,

(45)

where each individual
z_{ijk},z^{\prime}_{ijk}
is a random variable which takes a value
+1
or
-1
with equal probability.

Proof.

To prove that the optimization trajectory behaves as a random walk with high probability, we proceed in two main steps: (1) show that each individual optimization step exhibits random walk behavior; and (2) demonstrate that, over the course of training, the entire optimization trajectory remains consistent with a random walk. This can be established by repeatedly applying Corollary
3
to various relevant quantities and invoking the union bound.

(1). Show that each individual optimization step exhibits random walk behavior.

Consider a loss function of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}{c_{i}\ell_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})O_{i}]
where
N_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
and the set of POVMs
\{\mathcal{M}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{L}}
. We denote

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

(46)

as the empirical estimate of the full loss function at a given parameter setting
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. To estimate the full loss, we are required to estimate each individual term
\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
by performing POVM
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
resulting in a set of measurement outcomes
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
consisting of polynomial measurement shots i.e.,
N\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. Therefore, estimating the full loss function at a single parameter point generally requires
N_{L}
such sample sets, one for each POVM.

To estimate the loss gradients, we need to repeatedly estimate the loss at different points on the landscape. For gradient descent optimization with the parameter shift rule with
N_{p}
parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{N_{p}})
and learning rate
\eta
, the updated parameter given the current parameter values
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}
can be described by the general procedure (– similar to Eq. (
II
) in Section
II
) as:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\left\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\right\}_{i,j,k}\right)=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{p}}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}+\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}-\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)\right]\hat{e}_{k}\;,

(47)

where
\hat{e}_{k}
is the unit vector in the direction of the
k^{\rm th}
parameter component. Here,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}\right)
denotes the estimated loss in Eq. (
46
) evaluated at the shifted parameter values
\boldsymbol{\theta}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k}
. Furthermore, we have the collection
\left\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\right\}_{ijk}
, where each
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})
corresponds to the measurement outcomes used to estimate an individual term in the full loss function at a specific parameter setting. The total number of such measurement sets is
N_{\ell}=2N_{\rm p}N_{L}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
. Since the indexing notation may appear somewhat dense, let us unpack the meaning of each index:

•

The index
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{L}\}
labels the individual term
\ell_{i}
in the full loss function.

•

The index
k\in\{1,\dots,N_{\rm p}\}
denotes the component of the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
being shifted.

•

The index
j\in\{1,2\}
specifies the direction of the parameter shift:
j=1
corresponds to a
+
shift, and
j=2
corresponds to a
-
shift.

That is, we have
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i1k)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i1k})
corresponding to the estimation of
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k})
, and
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(i2k)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i2k})
for
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\pi}{2}\hat{e}_{k})
.

Now, we can further re-express Eq. (
47
) in terms of individual measurement outcomes as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\left\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\right\}_{i,j,k}\right)=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i1kq}}{N}-\sum_{q=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i2kq}}{N}\right]\hat{e}_{k}\;,

(48)

where
\lambda_{ijkq}
is the
q^{\rm th}
measurement outcome from
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})
, which takes a value
``+1"
with probability
p^{(i)}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})=(1+\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk}))/2
, and a value
``-1"
with probability
p^{(i)}_{-}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})=(1-\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk}))/2
.

Next, we consider that each individual POVM exponentially concentrate according to Definition
1
and hence each set of measurement outcomes
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})
is, with high probability exponentially close to
1
, statistically indistinguishable from another set
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}^{(ijk)}
where each individual outcome takes a value
``+1"
or
``-1"
with equal probability, according to Theorem
2
. Consequently, by invoking Corollary
3
, each estimate
\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})
is, with high probability exponentially close to
1
, indistinguishable from another
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk}
-independent random variable of the form

\displaystyle\ell_{\rm fixed}^{(ijk)}=\sum_{q}\frac{z_{ijkq}}{N}\;,

(49)

where each
z_{ijkq}
takes a value
``+1"
or
``-1"
with an equal probability.

Now, the key proof strategy is to show that when considering all outcome measurement sets
\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\}_{ijk}
of size
N_{\ell}=2N_{p}N_{L}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
, the probability of the updated parameters in Eq. (
48
) being statistically indistinguishable remains exponentially close to
1
. In order to do so, we can invoke the union bound. In particular, denote
A_{ijk}
as an event that
\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})
is statical indistinguishable from
\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}^{(ijk)}
which, from Theorem
2
, happens with the probability

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(A_{ijk})

\displaystyle\geq 1-|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|\sqrt{\beta^{(i)}}

(50)

\displaystyle=1-2\sqrt{\beta^{(i)}}\;,

(51)

where
\beta^{(i)}
is some exponentially vanishing value associated with the exponential concentration of the POVM
\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
i.e.,
\beta^{(i)}\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. Note that in our setting with Pauli operator measurements, each POVM has two elements, that is
|\mathcal{M}^{(i)}|=2
for all POVM measurements.

We are ready to bound the probability of all
\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\}_{ijk}
being indistinguishable using the union bound, leading to

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\left(\bigcap_{i,j,k}A_{ijk}\right)}

\displaystyle=1-\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\left(\bigcup_{i,j,k}\bar{A}_{ijk}\right)}

(52)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{i,j,k}\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\left(\bar{A}_{ijk}\right)}

(53)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{i,j,k}2\sqrt{\beta^{(i)}}

(54)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-2N_{\ell}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}\;\;,

(55)

where
\bar{A}_{ijk}
is the conjugate event of
A_{ijk}
, in the first inequality we use the union bound, in the second inequality we use
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\left(\bar{A}_{ijk}\right)}\leqslant|\mathcal{M}^{(ijk)}|\sqrt{\beta^{(ijk)}}
by reversing the inequality in Eq.
50
. To reach the last line, we denote
\beta^{*}
is the maximum value of
\{\beta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{L}}
. Since
N_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(n))
, we have that
\delta^{\prime}=2N_{l}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
. That is, with the probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
such that
\delta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, all
\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\}_{ijk}
are indistinguishable from all
\{\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}^{(ijk)}\}_{ijk}
. Following directly, with the same probability, the updated parameter
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\left\{\mathcal{S}_{N}^{(ijk)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{ijk})\right\}_{i,j,k}\right)
is statistically indistinguishable from

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\left\{\mathcal{S}_{N,{\rm fixed}}^{(ijk)}\right\}_{i,j,k}\right)=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rm(current)}-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{L}}c_{i}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{N}\frac{z_{i1kq}}{N}-\sum_{q=1}^{N}\frac{z_{i2kq}}{N}\right]\hat{e}_{k}\;\;,

(56)

which means that the update does not incorporate information about the current parameter values when determining the next step, effectively forming a random walk for a single training iteration. Note that the random walk
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}
introduced in Corollary
4
can be directly identified from Eq. (
56
).

(2). Demonstrate that, throughout training, the entire optimization trajectory remains consistent with a random walk.

We know that for each training step, the probability of a random walk is bounded as shown in Eq. (
55
). To demonstrate that the entire training trajectory consisting of
N_{\mathrm{step}}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
steps forms a random walk, we invoke the union bound. Denote
B_{k}
be the event that an
k^{\rm th}
training step resembles a random walk. We have that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(N_{\rm step})}}{\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{N_{\rm steps}}B_{k}\right)}

\displaystyle=1-\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(N_{\rm step})}}{\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{N_{\rm step}}\bar{B}_{k}\right)}

(57)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm step}}\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}{\left(\bar{B}_{k}\right)}

(58)

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\rm steps}}2N_{\ell}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}

(59)

\displaystyle=1-2N_{\rm step}N_{\ell}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}\;\;,

(60)

where we use union bound in the second line and use
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}{\left(\bar{B}_{k}\right)}\leqslant 2N_{\ell}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}
by reversing the inequality in Eq. (
55
). That is, with probability
1-\delta^{\prime\prime}
such that
\delta^{\prime\prime}=2N_{\rm step}N_{\ell}\sqrt{\beta^{*}}\in\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n))
, the whole training trajectory is statistically indistinguishable from a random walk. This completes the proof.

∎

Appendix C
Further details of numerical simulation

Here, we describe the numerical setup in more detail. To specify the setup, we detail the parametrized circuit, the measurement operator, the initialization of the circuit parameters, the estimation map used to evaluate the loss function, and the optimization strategy.

In all methods, the quantum circuit consists of a single layer of single-qubit Pauli-
X
rotations applied to all qubits,

U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{-i\theta_{i}X_{i}}\;,

(61)

where
X_{i}
denotes the Pauli-
X
operator acting on the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
qubit. In addition, although the exact form may vary slightly, the observables are all global, acting non-trivially on
k
qubits such that
k\in\Omega(n)
. This combination is well known to induce barren plateaus due to the global nature of the loss function
Cerezo et al. 2021b
.

Quantum natural gradient descent optimization.

Quantum natural gradient descent is a quantum analogue of natural gradient descent. In this method, the optimization direction in the loss landscape is determined with respect to the underlying quantum information geometry, which is characterized by the real part of the Quantum Geometric Tensor (QGT). As a result, this approach can yield more effective optimization steps than standard gradient descent. The parameters update rule, with learning rate
\eta
, is given by

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm new)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm current)}-\eta\,g^{+}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm current)})\,\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\rm current)})\;,

(62)

where
g^{+}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\mathrm{current})})
denotes the pseudo-inverse of the Fubini–Study metric tensor
g(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which can be expressed in terms of QGT
G(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and
\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\mathrm{current})})
represents the gradient of the loss function at that step.
To obtain the Quantum Geometric Tensor (QGT), let us be general and consider a parameterized quantum circuit composed of
L
layers of non-commuting parameterized unitaries (– we will simplify to the circuit in Eq. (
61
) later)

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{L}V_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,W_{i}\;,

(63)

where
W_{i}
are non-parametrised unitaries and
V_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are parametrised unitaries, with
n_{l}
parameters at
l^{\rm th}
layer, of the form

V_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{l}}e^{-i\theta^{(l)}_{i}\sigma_{i}^{(l)}}\;,

(64)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{{(L)}}\}
with
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(i)}=\{\theta^{(i)}_{1},\theta^{(i)}_{2},\dots,\theta^{(i)}_{n_{l}}\}
, and
\{\sigma_{i}^{(l)}\}_{l}
are a set of commuting generators such that
\left(\sigma_{i}^{(l)}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{1}
. Then, as shown in Ref.
Stokes et al. 2020
, the QGT becomes a block-diagonalized matrix with
L
blocks, and a
l^{\rm th}
block is an
n_{l}\times n_{l}
matrix whose elements are given by

G_{ij}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\sigma^{(l)}_{i}\,\sigma^{(l)}_{j}|\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle-\langle\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\sigma^{(l)}_{i}|\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle\,\langle\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\sigma^{(l)}_{j}|\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle\;,

(65)

where
|\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle
is the state of circuit at at
l^{\rm th}
layer, i.e.,

|\psi_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{l}V_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,W_{i}|\psi_{0}\rangle\;,

(66)

with an initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. At the end, the block-diagonal QGT leads to a block-diagonal Fubini-Study metric tensor,

g_{ij}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\text{Re}{[G_{ij}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]}\;.

(67)

In our numerical setup, the parameterized quantum circuit is simplified to the form given in Eq.
61
, and the measurement operator is taken to be a global Pauli-
Z
observable. In other words, in the loss function of the form
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[H\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
, the Hamiltonian is given by

H=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\;.

(68)

The circuit parameters are sampled from a uniform random distribution. The loss function is estimated using the empirical average as the post-processing map. For the optimization strategy, the pseudo-inverse of the Fubini–Study metric is computed to be
g^{+}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=4\,\mathbb{1}
, making the update rule equivalent to a gradient descent method with an effective learning rate of
4\eta
.

Sample-based CVaR optimization.

For this method, the measurement operator is a linear combination of four global Pauli-
Z
terms, and the Hamiltonian is given by

H=c_{1}\,\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\right]+c_{2}\,\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-1}Z_{i}\right]\otimes\mathbb{1}+c_{3}\,\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-2}Z_{i}\right]\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}+c_{4}\,\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-3}Z_{i}\right]\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\;,

(69)

where
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{4}
are some constant coefficients and
H
has
16
distinct eigenvalues
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{16}
.

The initial parameters of the circuit are sampled from a uniform random distribution, similar to the previous method. The loss function is estimated using the CVaR processing map:

\widehat{\ell}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Phi_{\rm CVaR}^{(\gamma)}\left(\mathcal{S}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)=\frac{1}{\lceil{\gamma N}\rceil}\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil{\gamma N}\rceil}\lambda_{j}\;,

(70)

where each
\lambda_{j}
is one of the eigenvalues
\{e_{i}\}_{i}
. For the optimization strategy, we use the gradient descent method with learning rate
\eta
.

Classical neural network assisted initialization.

In this method, the key difference lies in the initialization of the circuit parameters. Unlike the previous approaches, where the circuit parameters are sampled from random distributions, here the parameters are initialized using the output of a classical neural network. The output vector of this neural network is used to initialize the parameters of the quantum circuit.

The measurement operator is a global Pauli-
Z
measurement, and the loss function is estimated using the empirical average processing map. Finally, the optimization strategy involves applying gradient descent to train the parameters of the classical neural network, rather than those of the quantum circuit.

Re-scaled Parameter Shift (RPS) rule optimization.

In this method
Teo 2023
, it is claimed that using a scaled parameter-shift rule to compute gradients can improve the training of loss functions that suffer from barren plateaus. This corresponds to using the scaled parameter-shift rule with a scaling factor that minimizes the mean squared error in the estimation of both the loss function and its gradients.

In our numerical results, the measurement operator is a global Pauli-
Z
operator, the initial circuit parameters are sampled from the uniform distribution, and the loss function is estimated using the empirical average processing map. For the optimization strategy, we employ the scaled parameter-shift rule to compute gradients within the gradient descent algorithm, using learning rate
\eta
. The scaling factor is given by

\lambda=\frac{dN}{2d^{2}+Nd-2}\;,

(71)

where
N
is the number of measurement shots and
d=2^{n}
is the Hilbert space dimension.

Appendix D
Examples for a probability distribution with exponential support

D.1
Counter example

We provide a counterexample of a distribution with exponential support that exhibits exponential concentration according to Definition
1
, yet remains distinguishable from its fixed distribution.

Consider two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
over a finite set
\mathcal{X}
with size
M
such that
M\in\Omega(\exp(n))
. In other words, we have two distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\dots,p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{M})
where the categories are named as
\{1,2,\dots,M\}
, and the probability of each category
i
is given with
p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\mu_{i}
respectively.
We then perform a binary hypothesis test. That is, suppose we are given a set of
N
samples
\mathcal{S}
all drawn from either
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
or
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
with equal probabilities. We aim to distinguish between the following two hypotheses:

•

Null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

•

Alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
:
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}

According to Proposition
26
, the probability of correctly identifying the true hypothesis is upper bounded by

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}}{4}.

(72)

Now, let us assume that the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
takes the following form:

p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}+f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})&;\;i\in\text{odd}\\
\mu_{i}-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})&;\;i\in\text{even}\end{cases}

(73)

where
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]
.
The one-norm distance between
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
can be written as

\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}|p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\mu_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{M}|f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|=M|f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|.

(74)

Considering the exponential concentration of all outcome probabilities, we can derive the condition that must be satisfied by the function
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
. In particular, each outcome probability
p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
exhibits concentration if

{\rm Var}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]\leqslant\beta,

(75)

where
\beta\in\mathcal{O}(1/b^{n})
for some
b>1
. The variance of these probabilities can be expressed as a function of
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, and the corresponding condition on
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is given by

{\rm Var}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]={\rm Var}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[\mu_{i}\pm f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]={\rm Var}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[f^{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]\leqslant\beta.

(76)

To illustrate the counterexample, consider the specific setting where
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
is a uniform probability distribution, that is for all outcomes
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]=\frac{1}{M}
, and
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{M}
. In this case, the problem reduces to the hypothesis test between the following two probability distributions:
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\dots,p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
with

p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{M}&;\;i\in\text{odd}\\
0&;\;i\in\text{even}\;\;,\end{cases}

(77)

and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{M})
which is the uniform distribution with

\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\;\;,\;\;\forall m\;.

(78)

In this case, we have
\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}=1
. Using Eq. (
72
), the probability of correctly distinguishing between the two hypotheses is upper-bounded by

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant 1/2+N/4\;.

(79)

This is a loose bound and does not provide any meaningful constraint on the number of samples required to succeed in the hypothesis test.

However, we can provide a simple test for our hypotheses. Consider the test
\mathcal{T}
, which chooses the null hypothesis (
\mathcal{T}=0
) if all samples
s_{i}
are odd, and chooses the alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
otherwise (i.e., if at least one even number appears among the samples). The probability of error in this test can be written as

\displaystyle p_{\text{error}}

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}[\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\otimes N}]\,\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=1|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\otimes N}]+\text{Pr}[\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}^{\otimes N}]\,\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=0|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}^{\otimes N}]

(80)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=1|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\otimes N}]+\frac{1}{2}\,\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=0|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}^{\otimes N}]

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N+1}}\;\;,

(82)

where
\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=0|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}^{\otimes N}]
corresponds to the probability that all sampled values are odd (i.e., no even numbers appear) when the samples are drawn from the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}
. On the other hand,
\text{Pr}[\mathcal{T}=1|\mathcal{S}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\otimes N}]=0
in our setting, since
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
assigns zero probability to all even outcomes. Here, one can observe that the error probability decays exponentially with respect to
N
, allowing the two distributions to be distinguished with high confidence using a realistic number of samples.

D.2
Indistinguishable example

We also present another example where the distribution remains indistinguishable, despite having exponential support. To see this, consider the same setup, but now choose
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{M^{2}}
. In this case, the hypothesis testing problem is between the two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\dots,p_{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
with

p_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{M}+\frac{1}{M^{2}}&;\;i\in\text{odd}\\
\frac{1}{M}-\frac{1}{M^{2}}&;\;i\in\text{even}\end{cases}

(83)

and
\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{M})
which is the same uniform distribution with
\mu_{i}=1/M
for all. In this example the one-norm distance is
\|\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\mathbb{P}_{\rm fixed}\|_{1}=1/M
. Following Eq. (
72
), the probability of correctly identifying the true hypothesis is upper-bounded as follows:

\text{Pr}[``{\text{right decision between }\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\text{and}\,\mathcal{H}_{1}}"]\leqslant\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N}{4M}.

(84)

Here, the bound remains exponentially close to
\frac{1}{2}
as
\frac{N}{4M}\in\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)}
with polynomial number of measurements.
