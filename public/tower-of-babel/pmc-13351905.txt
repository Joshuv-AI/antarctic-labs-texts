Cost-effectiveness of three-level cervical disc arthroplasty vs. anterior discectomy and fusion: a comparative single-system analysis
Authors: Zachary T. Hoglund, Varun G. Kathawate, Christopher S. Sollenberger, Albert Q. Wu, Raphael Englander, Neha Rani, Jacob Saadoun, Elie Massaad, James M. Schuster, Paul J. Marcotte, Berje H. Shammassian, William C. Welch, David S. Casper, Ali K. Ozturk, Zarina S. Ali, Neil R. Malhotra, John H. Shin, Brendan F. Judy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical degenerative disc disease is a common indication for surgical intervention to stabilize vertebral segments and alleviate symptomatic radiculopathy or myelopathy. While anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has long been a standard procedure for these cases, more recently, cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) has offered a motion preserving alterative to potentially reduce adjacent segment degeneration (ASD). Although cost-effectiveness data exist for single- and two-level procedures, there remain no direct comparisons for three-level CDA constructs. This study retrospectively evaluates admission costs and 3-month clinical outcomes between three-level CDA and ACDF to evaluate each procedure’s relative cost-effectiveness.MethodsIn this single-system retrospective study, patients receiving elective three-level CDA (n=10) or ACDF (n=10) for degenerative cervical pathology were compared. Exclusion criteria included acute trauma, infection, or tumors. Data collected included patient demographics, comorbidities, symptoms, radiological findings, procedural details, postoperative complications, and 3-month clinical outcomes. Financial metrics, normalized to institutional cost units, included total admission costs and subcategories [e.g., equipment cost, operating room (OR) cost]. Statistical comparisons employed Kruskal-Wallis and chi-square tests (P<0.05 threshold).ResultsCohorts exhibited comparable baseline characteristics, including age (CDA: 52.3±9.6 years; ACDF: 58.7±7.4 years), comorbidities, symptoms, and diagnoses (P>0.05). Operative times trended longer for CDA (268.6±40.7 min) vs. ACDF (222.8±84.4 min; P=0.08). No intraoperative complications occurred in either cohort, and postoperative complications were not significantly higher for CDA vs. ACDF (30% vs. 10%; P=0.58). There were no significant differences in 3-month symptom resolution and readmission/reoperation rates between procedures (P>0.40). Total admission costs were markedly elevated for CDA (78.04 units) compared to ACDF (46.25 units; +68.75%, P<0.001), driven predominantly by equipment costs (+152.34%, P<0.001), with lesser increases in OR (+32.97%, P=0.006), anesthesia (+56.0%, P=0.008), and radiology costs (+74.56%, P=0.005).ConclusionsThree-level CDA incurs substantially higher admission costs than ACDF without short-term clinical superiority. These findings highlight equipment-driven cost differences amid comparable clinical efficacy, suggesting ACDF may be superior to CDA in three-level cases where motion preservation is not essential and cost is a significant consideration. However, further cost-utility analyses are necessary to evaluate how differences in long-term ASD rates and reoperations may compensate for the higher short-term admission costs of CDA.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Three-level cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) incurred 68.75% higher total admission costs than three-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) in a matched single-system retrospective cohort of 20 patients (CDA n=10, ACDF n=10; P<0.001).

• The cost differential was driven predominantly by equipment costs (+152%), with smaller but statistically significant increases in operating room, anesthesia, and radiology costs.

• Postoperative complications, hospital length of stay, readmissions, reoperations, and 3-month symptom resolution were comparable between three-level CDA and ACDF cohorts.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that, at single- and two-level constructs, CDA incurs higher upfront equipment costs than ACDF while potentially being cost-effective over the long term through reduced rates of adjacent segment degeneration and reoperation.

• It is known that three-level CDA is technically feasible and produces short- and mid-term clinical outcomes comparable to three-level ACDF.

• This is the first direct comparison of admission costs between three-level CDA and three-level ACDF, demonstrating that the upfront cost gap persists with the multiplicative effect of additional arthroplasty devices.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In multilevel cervical degenerative disc disease where motion preservation is not a clinical priority, three-level ACDF appears to be the more cost-effective in the immediate, short-term.

• Surgeons, hospital administrators, and payers should explicitly weigh the substantial perioperative cost premium of three-level CDA against its potential long-term benefits when planning interventions for cervical degenerative disc disease.

• Prospective studies with protocol-mandated radiographic and clinical follow-up are needed to determine whether reductions in adjacent segment degeneration and revision surgery with CDA may offset its higher upfront cost.

Introduction

Cervical degenerative disc disease is a highly prevalent condition which frequently manifests as radiculopathy or myelopathy that impairs patients’ quality of life through pain, motor deficits, and sensory disturbances. The goal of surgical intervention in these cases is to stabilize vertebral segments, decompress neural elements, and provide long-term symptom relief (1).

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) involving intervertebral disc removal and interbody fusion has become widely utilized for cervical degenerative disc disease since its inception in the late 1950s (2,3). This technique often serves as the reference surgical intervention in cervical degenerative disc disease, offering reliable decompression, high fusion success rates, and favorable patient outcomes (4). However, despite its efficacy, ACDF inherently eliminates segmental motion at treated levels, which is thought to accelerate degeneration at adjacent segments over time resulting in recurrent symptoms and need for retreatment (5-7). To address this limitation, cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) has emerged as a motion-preserving alternative (8). By maintaining physiological kinematics, CDA aims to mitigate the biomechanical drawbacks of fusion while achieving comparable neural decompression. For single-level and two-level disease, randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have generally shown CDA to be non-inferior or superior to ACDF with regard to clinical outcomes, while preserving range of motion and reducing adjacent segment degeneration (ASD) (9,10). However, some analyses have questioned the significance of CDAs’ benefits vs. ACDF (11-13).

With the increased utilization of CDA and evidence supporting its efficacy, examining the relative cost-effectiveness of this procedure has gained substantial interest to provide further guidance for surgical decision-making. In single-level cases, CDA has been shown to incur higher inpatient costs by approximately $4,694 compared to ACDF, largely attributable to device-related expenditure (14). Other time-driven activity-based costing (TDABC) analyses have found that supply costs dominate intraoperative expenses in CDA, often exceeding those in ACDF by up to 128% (15). However, long-term data for single-level CDA indicate sustained cost-effectiveness up to 7 years post-procedure due to reduced reoperation and improved long-term clinical outcomes relative to ACDF (16). In two-level applications, cost-utility analyses have similarly supported CDA’s value, with incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) typically falling below accepted willingness-to-pay thresholds when compared to ACDF over mid-term follow-up periods (17,18).

Promising outcomes with single- and two-level CDA procedures have increased interest in CDA constructs beyond two levels for more extensive cervical pathology. Retrospective analyses have shown that CDA at 3-levels is safe and demonstrates clinical outcomes comparable with ACDF while preserving segmental motion (19,20).

Despite the growing adoption of arthroplasty devices in these more complex cases and larger constructs, there remain no direct comparisons of procedure cost between three-level CDA and ACDF. The present study addresses this critical gap by conducting a retrospective comparison of admission costs and 3-month clinical outcomes between three-level CDA and ACDF in patients with degenerative cervical pathology. By integrating clinical and economic metrics, we aim to provide an initial comparison of treatment costs to inform future study into the cost-effectiveness of multilevel CDA and guide surgical decision making. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-1-251/rc) (21).

Methods

Study design & approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from the Penn Medicine IRB, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research.

Patient identification & selection criteria

We identified all elective cases of three-level CDA and three-level anterior discectomy and fusion. Patients were included if they underwent treatment for three-level cervical degenerative disc disease and if a three-level intervention was performed in a single procedure. No cases involving acute trauma, infection, or spine tumors were included.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data collected included demographics (age, sex, race), medical history [body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol use, diabetes, osteoporosis, prior myocardial infarction (MI), cancer history, hypertension, osteoarthritis], pre-intervention symptoms (pain, weakness, paresthesia, gait abnormalities, bowel/bladder dysfunction, symptom duration), diagnoses (radiculopathy, myelopathy), and pre-intervention radiology findings (disc herniation, osteophytes, facet arthropathy, ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament, uncovertebral hypertrophy). Procedure characteristics collected included surgical levels, CDA implant model, operative time, blood loss, and intraoperative complications. Additional admission data collected included post-operative complications, hospital stay length, and discharge destination. Clinical outcomes at 3-month included recurrent symptoms, readmissions, and retreatment.

Financial data acquisition

Financial data were obtained through our institutional electronic medical record system using unique case identifiers. Each patient included in the study was linked to detailed case-specific financial records, which were extracted by a designated member of the Department of Neurosurgery’s finance team. The data reflected direct costs incurred during the hospitalization and were derived from standardized internal cost accounting protocols used across the health system. Collected variables included total cost of care, operating room (OR) services, supplies, room and board, anesthesia and respiratory services, radiology, pharmacy, diagnostic and lab testing, and other therapeutic services (physical, occupational, speech therapy). Intensive care unit (ICU) and recovery room/post-anesthesia care unit costs were included in room and board cost. All financial data were normalized to internal cost “units” by dividing raw cost values by a random number to facilitate standardized comparison across cases while preserving confidentiality of protected health system financial information. Financial data normalization was performed solely for display purposes and was performed after statistical testing to ensure the accuracy of the calculated metrics.

Statistical analysis

Differences across the intervention groups’ baseline, procedural, and outcome statistics were determined using the Kruskal-Wallis one way analysis of variance test and chi-square test as appropriate in MATLAB R2024b. Cost differences were compared using two-sided Mann-Whitney tests in GraphPad Prism 10. Mean and error bar plots were constructed to visualize significant differences in cost variables. For all data, outliers were retained, and data were displayed without transformation, except for cost values, which were normalized. A significance threshold of P<0.05 was used for all comparisons.

Results

Baseline characteristics and comorbidities

The study included 10 patients in each cohort undergoing either three-level CDA or ACDF. Baseline demographics were comparable between groups, with no statistically significant differences in age (CDA: 52.3±9.6 years; ACDF: 58.7±7.4 years; P=0.14), BMI (CDA: 29.2±3.4 kg/m2; ACDF: 30.3±8.6 kg/m2; P=0.73), sex (CDA: 50% male; ACDF: 50% male; P>0.99), smoking status (CDA: 70% non-smokers; ACDF: 40% non-smokers; P=0.37), or alcohol use (CDA: 80%; ACDF: 70%; P>0.99) (Table 1). Comorbidities were also similar, including diabetes (CDA: 10%; ACDF: 20%; P>0.99), osteoporosis (CDA: 10%; ACDF: 0%; P>0.99), active cancer (CDA: 0%; ACDF: 10%; P>0.99), prior cancer (CDA: 0%; ACDF: 10%; P>0.99), and osteoarthritis (CDA: 10%; ACDF: 10%; P>0.99).

Procedural characteristics

In the CDA group, the most common construct was C4–7 in 6 patients, followed by C2–5 in 3 patients, and C3–6 in 1 patient. The ACDF group had a similar distribution (P>0.20) with 8 C4–7 constructs, no C2–5 constructs, and 2 C3–6 constructs (Table 2). All cases were performed using the same implant model for all levels of arthroplasty. Mean operative time was higher in the CDA group [268.6 min; standard deviation (SD): 40.7] vs. ACDF (222.80; SD: 84.43), although this difference was not significant (P=0.08).

Detailed 3-month clinical follow-up was available for all 10 CDA patients and 8 ACDF patients. Cost differences

Total admission costs were significantly higher in the CDA group relative to ACDF. In the CDA group, mean costs totaled 78.04 and 46.25 units in the ACDF group. This represents a 68.75% (P<0.001) increase in cost for three-level CDA vs. ACDF. Within total admission costs, the most significant contributors included equipment cost, OR cost, and anesthesia and respiratory care cost. Equipment cost totaled 42.35 units in the CDA group and 16.78 in the ACDF group, and was the sub-category with the largest difference between groups (+152.34% vs. ACDF; P<0.001). OR, anesthesia, and radiology costs were also significantly different between groups, albeit at lesser percent and absolute cost differences. CDA OR costs totaled 17.89 units vs. 13.45 for ACDF (+32.97% vs. ACDF; P=0.006) and anesthesia cost totaled 4.85 units in the CDA group vs. 3.11 in the ACDF group (P=0.008). Radiology costs were higher for the CDA group vs. ACDF at 0.65 vs. 0.37 units (P=0.005), respectively. Other categories were not significantly different between groups (P>0.05) (Figure 1, Table 4).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of 10 patients receiving three-level CDA and 10 patients receiving three-level ACDF for cervical degenerative disc disease, total admission costs were significantly higher for CDA patients (78.04 units) compared to ACDF (46.25 units), representing a 68.75% increase primarily driven by equipment costs (+152.34% in CDA). However, clinical outcomes were comparable between cohorts at 3-month follow-up, with no significant differences in symptoms, readmissions, or reoperations. These findings provide the first direct comparison of costs between three-level CDA and ACDF, addressing a critical gap in the literature amid growing adoption of multilevel arthroplasty constructs.

The elevated costs associated with three-level CDA align with prior reports on single- and two-level procedures, where arthroplasty implants contribute substantially to procedural expenses (14). Our results extend this observation to three-level constructs, where the multiplicative effect of additional artificial discs amplifies the cost differential. Equipment costs alone accounted for over half of the total disparity in our cohort, consistent with TDABC analyses of smaller constructs finding that CDA supply costs exceed those in ACDF by 128% (15). Additionally, more modest but significant increases in OR and anesthesia costs for CDA (33% and 56% higher vs. ACDF, respectively) may reflect longer operative times or procedural complexity for CDA, though our study did not detect statistically significant differences in operative time (P=0.08). Higher radiology cost for the CDA group (+74.56% vs. ACDF, P=0.005) may reflect more imaging (e.g., fluoroscopy) being necessary to achieve and confirm accurate implant placement.

Despite a higher CDA cost burden, the short-term clinical equivalence between three-level CDA and ACDF in our study mirrors findings from retrospective and prospective trials evaluating multilevel arthroplasty. Radiological and clinical outcomes, including pain relief, functional improvement, and complication rates, have been reported as similar between three-level CDA and ACDF over 2-year follow-ups, with CDA additionally preserving segmental motion (20). Long-term data from multilevel CDA series support sustained improvements in neck disability index, Veterans RAND 12-item health survey (VR-12), neck pain, and arm pain up to 7 years postoperatively, with patient satisfaction rates exceeding 88% and low secondary surgery rates (19).

While our study similarly noted comparable clinical efficacy at 3-month post-intervention, the CDA group demonstrated a trend toward higher readmission (20% vs. 0%, P=0.47) and reoperation rates (10% vs. 0%). In larger cohorts, these differences may gain statistical significance and possibly result in greater clinical efficacy being achieved with ACDF vs. CDA in 3-level constructs. Although, future studies with larger sample sizes would be necessary to determine significance in this regard.

Additionally, while only available for a limited subset of our patient cohorts, no re-operations at 1 year follow-up were noted in either cohort, apart from the single re-operation which occurred within 90 days post-intervention in the CDA group. This may suggest no significant difference in long-term reoperations between interventions, increasing the importance of considering initial procedural expenses. However, 1-year follow-up is still inadequate to fully rule out differences in long-term ASD or revision occurrence.

The clinical implications of our findings are multifaceted. For surgeons managing extensive cervical degenerative disease, three-level CDA appears safe and clinically noninferior to ACDF in the short term, supporting its use in select patients where motion preservation is prioritized, such as younger individuals or those with more mild facet arthropathy. However, the substantial cost increase associated with CDA may influence decision-making in resource-constrained settings or under value-based reimbursement models. Notably, outpatient vs. inpatient CDA has shown promise in reducing costs by 62% while maintaining safety, potentially mitigating some of the disparities observed in this study (22).

A key limitation in interpreting our findings is the potential for long-term cost savings with CDA due to lower rates of ASD and reoperations which were assessed in only a small subset of our cohort (CDA: 6 patients, ACDF: 4 patients). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found that CDA reduces radiographic ASD, symptomatic ASD, and adjacent-level reoperation rates compared to fusion, with these differences becoming more pronounced beyond 4 years (23-25). However, other studies have questioned CDA’s benefits for ASD reduction, including a recent analysis from Alsalek et al. (13,26). For two-level disease, a 5-year cost-utility analysis reported an ICER of $8,518 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) from the health system perspective and a negative ICER of −$165,103 per QALY from the societal perspective, reflecting cost savings due to reduced productivity loss and faster return to work with CDA (27). Although our study found no reoperations or noted ASD at 1 year post-operation, apart from one re-operation within 90-day post-intervention in the CDA group, the study’s small sample size limits statistical comparison necessary for definitive conclusions.

Additionally, while our analysis compared three-level CDA to ACDF, hybrid constructs integrating CDA and ACDF at different levels may offer a balanced alternative, potentially reducing costs relative to multilevel CDA while preserving range of motion and mitigating ASD. Hybrid approaches have been found to achieve similar improvements in Neck Disability Index and Visual Analog Scores as CDA and ACDF, but with superior motion preservation over ACDF (28,29). Although direct cost comparisons for multilevel hybrid constructs are scarce, the lower number of CDA implants needed implies potential cost savings. Given these attributes, there is a need for more extensive evaluation of outcomes and costs between hybrid constructs, multilevel CDA, and ACDF to inform optimal surgical decision-making in complex cervical degenerative disc disease.

Several other limitations in our study must be acknowledged. The small sample size (n=20 total) and retrospective design introduce risks of selection bias and underpowering for detecting differences in rare events such as complications. As a single-system study, generalizability may be limited by institutional practices, implant preferences, and cost accounting methods. In particular, cost differences observed in this study may not be fully generalizable across institutions, as contracted pricing agreements with device vendors vary and individual arthroplasty implant costs differ between manufacturers. Data on reimbursement, contribution margins, or net financial impact would be beneficial for value-based decision making, however, were unable to be analyzed in this study due to institutional financial confidentiality. Importantly, the 3-month clinical follow-up window reported here represents the primary postoperative assessment interval available in our electronic health record for the majority of this cohort, as most patients were lost to spine surgery follow-up after the initial 3-month post-operative visit. Consequently, we were unable to systematically evaluate longer-term radiographic or clinical endpoints such as fusion status, subsidence, heterotopic ossification, device-related complications, ASD, or delayed revision surgery for most patients, all of which would be expected to emerge across longer time courses. This limitation restricts the ability of the present study to detect longer-term differences between CDA and ACDF that could meaningfully alter the relative cost-effectiveness of each procedure, and should be addressed in future prospective studies with protocol-mandated radiographic and clinical follow-up. Future research may also prioritize prospective, multicenter trials with extended follow-up and formal cost-utility modeling for three-level CDA, incorporating societal perspectives and sensitivity analyses for varying willingness-to-pay thresholds.

Overall, while this study is limited by its small sample size, the large and statistically significant procedural cost differences between 3-level ACDF and CDA demonstrate ACDF as a more affordable intervention in the short-term. These data serve to generate hypotheses for future study which may evaluate the cost-effectiveness of these procedures in larger patient cohorts with longer follow-up, and which may consider additional comparisons such as hybrid 3-level constructs.

Conclusions

Three-level CDA incurs significantly higher admission costs than ACDF without short-term clinical superiority, supporting ACDF as the more cost-effective approach for groups where motion preservation is not essential. However, given the limited follow-up available in this cohort, these conclusions are restricted to the immediate perioperative period and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; with long-term radiographic and clinical follow-up being necessary to establish whether the short-term cost advantage of ACDF is sustained once differences in long-term complication and reoperation rates are accounted for.
