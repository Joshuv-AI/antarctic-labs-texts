Moving from mechanisms to clinical practice: non-invasive spinal cord stimulation for recovery of autonomic functions after spinal cord injury – a protocol for a pilot randomised controlled trial
Authors: Raza N Malik, Mohamed Gomaa Sobeeh, Andrea L Maharaj, Claire Shackleton, Ali Hosseinzadeh, Mujtaba Saloojee, Armin Khavandegar, Antonios Kontaxakis, Thomas Thordarson, Christopher Chang, Shane J T Balthazaar, Alison M M Williams, Tania Lam, Amandeep Ghuman, Katherine Anderson, Stacy L Elliott, Michael J Berger, Soshi Samejima, Rahul Sachdeva, Andrei V Krassioukov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSpinal cord injury (SCI) impairs autonomic functions, which are ranked among the highest priorities for recovery. The loss of autonomic control, including bowel, bladder, sexual and cardiovascular functions, interferes with rehabilitation and decreases health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Preliminary evidence indicates that non-invasive transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (TCSCS) has the potential to improve autonomic stability in people with SCI. However, the optimal stimulation site for improving autonomic responses remains to be determined. This pilot randomised clinical trial aims to explore the efficacy of non-invasive mid-thoracic and lumbosacral TCSCS (proof-of-concept) for blood pressure stability (orthostatic hypotension and autonomic dysreflexia burden) alongside end-organ autonomic functions (lower urinary tract, bowel and sexual function) and HRQoL.Methods30 participants with chronic (>1 year) motor-complete SCI (American Spinal Injuries Association Impairment Scale A and B) at or above T6 will be enrolled in this open-label, two-arm randomised pilot clinical trial. Participants will be block randomised into either the mid-thoracic or lumbosacral TCSCS group. Participants will then undergo 8 weeks of TCSCS (3 times per week for 60 min; 24 sessions total) while in a seated position. Post-treatment effects will be recorded following the 8-week intervention and follow-up effects will be recorded 8 weeks after the end of the intervention. Primary and secondary outcomes will assess resting blood pressure, autonomic dysreflexia, orthostatic hypotension and lower urinary tract, bowel and sexual functions as well as HRQoL.Ethics and disseminationThis study is approved by The University of British Columbia’s Clinical Research Ethics Board (UBC CREB H22-00365), and by Health Canada for Investigational Testing Authorisations (ITA) for Class II medical devices used in this trial (ITA#346875 TESCoN; ITA#381 154 SCONE). The findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conferences, seminars and SCI community outreach.Trial registration numberNCT05369520.Keywords: Clinical Trial, Electric Stimulation Therapy, Cardiovascular Disease, Bladder disorders, Functional bowel disorders, Sexual dysfunction

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) results in autonomic dysfunctions, including cardiovascular, lower urinary tract (LUT), bowel and sexual functions.1,4 Autonomic impairments are primary determinants of quality of life, health and survival post-SCI,5,7 and account for most long-term healthcare costs.6 8 9 Autonomic functions are among the top recovery priorities for individuals with SCI.5 10

Cardiovascular dysfunction affects the majority of individuals with SCI at or above T6, due to disrupted sympathetic control of the cardiovascular system. Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD, ie, abnormally high blood pressure (BP)) is often instigated by the pelvic organs such as with bladder, bowel and sexual activities. For example, bladder distension, routine bowel evacuation methods and genital stimulation are common triggers for episodes of AD.11,16 As AD is triggered by daily activities it reduces independence, interferes with rehabilitation, and can be life-threatening.17 Within the same day, individuals can alternate between episodes of AD and suffer from episodes of orthostatic hypotension (OH, ie, abnormally low BP) during postural stress.18 19 Low resting BP and frequent bouts of OH is associated with reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF), impaired cognition, increased fatigue, reduced participation in rehabilitation, elevated risk of falls and readmission to acute care.20 21 Considering these debilitating consequences, it is not surprising that cardiovascular dysfunction is among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality post-SCI.818 22,24

LUT dysfunction affects over 90% of people with SCI,25,27 resulting from loss of bulbospinal control and disrupted integration between somatic and autonomic pathways.4 Proper urine storage and voiding require coordinated control by cortical and spinal (thoracolumbar and sacral) centres.28 29 Following SCI, disruption of this control leads to LUT dysfunction, which substantially reduces health-related quality of life (HRQoL).1 Impaired urine storage, due to detrusor overactivity and reduced activation of the external urinary sphincter together with impaired voluntary voiding and detrusor sphincter dyssynergia results in elevated intravesical pressure and inefficient voiding with high post-void residual volumes.30 These abnormalities increase the risk of vesicoureteral reflux, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) and progressive renal dysfunction, contributing to significant disease burden.

Bowel dysfunction is common after SCI and includes colonic dysmotility, constipation and loss of volitional control with incontinence, increasing the risk of long-term complications.31 32 The lower gastrointestinal tract is regulated through integrated somatic and autonomic (sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric) control,33 with voluntary defecation dependent on supraspinal input.4 Disruption of this multilevel control in SCI, particularly in cervical and high thoracic injuries, results in neurogenic bowel dysfunction.4 34 Consequently, 98% of individuals with SCI report at least one bowel-related problem, with bowel management often requiring up to 2 hours35 and adversely affecting quality of life in over 60% of cases.36,38

Sexual dysfunction is highly prevalent after SCI, substantially reduces HRQoL and consistently ranks among the highest recovery priorities.5 39 Sexual function requires coordinated somatic, sympathetic (T10–L2) and parasympathetic (S2–S4) control.40 41 Disruption of supraspinal regulation following SCI impairs erection, ejaculation and vaginal lubrication.42 43 In individuals with cervical and high thoracic injuries, genital stimulation can trigger AD resulting in potentially life-threatening elevations in BP during orgasm or ejaculation,44 leading to avoidance or cessation of sexual activity.44 45 Systolic BP (SBP) has been reported to reach 229 mm Hg in women with C7 SCI during orgasm following self-stimulation.45 Our prior work demonstrates that penile vibratory stimulation frequently provokes severe prolonged AD,46 often accompanied by cardiac arrhythmias.15 47 48 Such episodes require acute medical intervention and substantially disrupt occupational participation, social relationships and sexual well-being.46

Current treatment options including pharmacological agents, catheterisation, bowel regimens and sexual aids, remain limited in scope and efficacy and often carry side effects.49,56 As a result, there is growing interest in spinal cord neuromodulation strategies that can improve function by engaging latent spinal cord circuitry below the level of injury.57,59 Epidural spinal cord stimulation has demonstrated improvements in cardiovascular, LUT and sexual function in individuals SCI.1360,64 However, epidural stimulation is invasive, costly and once implanted, stimulation parameters and electrode locations are difficult to modify in real time.65

Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (TCSCS) is a non-invasive alternative that activates similar afferent pathways to epidural stimulation and can generate comparable functional effects.66,74 Electrical stimulation delivered over the dorsal spinal cord primarily recruits large-diameter dorsal root afferents that modulate propriospinal interneurons and increase the excitability of spinal circuits.75 These effects may facilitate activation of sympathetic preganglionic neurons involved in cardiovascular regulation, thereby improving BP control, while also influencing somatic and autonomic pathways that contribute to pelvic organ function.75 Our team and others have shown that TCSCS improves cardiovascular and LUT function in small numbers of participants,76,80 however adequately powered studies that examine the multi-function effects of stimulation are lacking. Importantly, autonomic dysfunctions do not occur in isolation. Considering the proximity and intersegmental organisation of neural networks controlling sexual, bowel and bladder functions,41 it is anticipated that TCSCS targeted for one autonomic domain may indirectly benefit several others. Furthermore, because pelvic organ function involves both autonomic and somatic pathways within lumbosacral spinal circuits,4 41 neuromodulation may influence these systems simultaneously. Therefore, interventions targeting multiple systems concurrently are more likely to enhance therapeutic effectiveness and clinical uptake.

We propose to investigate the impact of TCSCS delivered at the mid-thoracic and lumbosacral spinal cord segments. Prior studies have demonstrated that stimulation delivered at both thoracic and lumbosacral spinal cord levels can improve autonomic functions, although the optimal stimulation site remains unclear.81,83 We hypothesise that mid-thoracic TCSCS will modulate sympathetic preganglionic neurons involved in autonomic control, thereby increasing resting BP and mitigating OH.80 Conversely, lumbosacral TCSCS may reduce AD by modulating afferent inputs arising from pelvic organ stimuli while also engaging spinal circuits involved in bowel, LUT and sexual function.13 76 The primary aim of this proof-of-concept pilot clinical trial is to explore the efficacy of mid-thoracic and lumbosacral TCSCS for improving BP instability (AD and OH burden) and pelvic organ function. Specifically, we will assess the effects of TCSCS delivered in real-time (within a single session), after long-term therapy (8 weeks of stimulation), and at follow-up (8 weeks after the end of therapy).

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This study is a pilot, open-label, two-arm randomised clinical trial designed to evaluate the efficacy of TCSCS in individuals with chronic SCI. The protocol was approved by The University of British Columbia’s Clinical Research Ethics Board (CREB; H22-00365, approved protocol included in the online supplemental material), and Health Canada for Investigational Testing Authorisations (ITA) for Class II medical devices used in the trial (ITA#346875 TESCoN; ITA#381 154 SCONE). The Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT)84 checklist can be found in the online supplemental material. Experiments and interventions will be conducted at the Blusson Spinal Cord Centre within the Vancouver General Hospital Complex or the Gastroenterology Clinic at St. Paul’s Hospital in Vancouver, Canada. Participants will be enrolled in the pilot clinical trial after provision of written informed consent (included in the online supplemental material) and screening. They will attend 42 visits over 30 weeks. The first visit will be TCSCS mapping for safety and to optimise the stimulation location by evaluating BP and lower limb and pelvic floor electromyography (EMG). Participants will then complete five visits of baseline assessments over 4 weeks. Baseline functional assessments will be performed in a randomised order to minimise order effects. Participants will then be randomly assigned to the mid-thoracic or lumbosacral TCSCS groups, using block randomisation,85 followed by 8 weeks of TCSCS, three times per week, for 60 min (total planned sessions=24). Post-treatment assessments (six visits over 4 weeks) will then be conducted after the end of the intervention. The persisting effects of TCSCS will be examined by repeating all assessments 8 weeks after the end of therapy over 4 weeks. Figure 3 illustrates an overview of the study design. To promote participant retention and complete follow-up, participants will receive flexible scheduling and regular contact with study personnel. Participants who discontinue the intervention will be encouraged to complete outcome assessments when possible, and all available outcome data will be retained for analysis.

Participants, power calculation and recruitment

This study aims to recruit 30 participants (age: 19–60 years) with chronic, (>1 year), motor-complete SCI (T6 or above) with documented autonomic dysfunction. Participants will attend 42 study visits over 30 weeks. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is outlined in table 1. A sample size calculation was performed using G*power software (V.3.1, Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) with power=0.80 and α=0.050.66 We conducted a power analysis on two studies,67 68 considering the effect of spinal cord stimulation on autonomic functions. One study67 assessed the effects of epidural SCS on BP control in people with cervical motor-complete SCI (mean ΔSBP (stim off vs stim on) = 16.2 mm Hg, effect size=1.29, n=4). Based on these data, a minimum sample of 11 participants per group is required. Power analysis based on another experiment68 in people with cervical or thoracic SCI, investigating the effect of transcutaneous SCS on LUT function (bladder volume stim-off=130±67 mL vs stim-on=200±56 mL, effect size=1.12), indicates a minimum sample size of 13 participants is required in each group. Given this is a pilot study and considering dropouts, we will recruit 15 individuals with motor-complete (American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) A or B) SCI in each group (n=15). Recruitment will be facilitated through advertisements in local SCI advocacy groups (eg, the Spinal Cord Injury BC’s Spin Magazine) and by contacting previous study participants who consented to be contacted for future research.

Randomisation

Randomisation will be conducted using permuted block randomisation, with fixed block sizes to ensure balanced allocation across intervention arms throughout recruitment.85 The randomisation sequence will be generated a priori using a web-based computer randomisation tool by a study team member who is not involved in participant recruitment, outcome assessment or intervention delivery. The allocation sequence will be stored by this study member and will not be accessible to investigators responsible for participant recruitment or enrolment until assignment. Following completion of baseline assessments, participants will be assigned to their intervention group according to the next sequential allocation (figure 1).

Given the open-label design and the nature of the neuromodulation intervention, participants and investigators delivering the intervention will not be blinded to group allocation. Outcome assessments, where feasible, will be conducted by qualified study personnel who are not involved in intervention delivery to minimise assessment bias.

Intervention: TCSCS

TCSCS will be delivered using a non-invasive central nervous system stimulator (TESCoN/SCONE, SpineX, California, USA). Two round self-adhesive cathode electrodes (ValuTrode, Axelgaard Manufacturing, Fallbrook, California, USA) will be placed midline on the posterior thorax, below the T7 and T9 spinous processes (midthoracic) or below the T11 and L1 spinous processes (lumbosacral), and two rectangular anode electrodes (ValuTrode, Axelgaard Manufacturing) will be placed bilaterally on the skin over the iliac crests. A skin temperature probe (Cadwell Industries, Kennewick, Washington, USA) will be positioned adjacent to the cathodes to monitor changes in skin temperature. TCSCS will be applied using charge-balanced monophasic 1 ms pulses at 30 Hz with a 10 kHz carrier frequency, and with current amplitudes ranging from 10 mA to 200 mA. Stimulation intensity will be determined through baseline mapping, assessing participants’ discomfort levels, cardiovascular responses and skin temperature. Stimulation intensity may be reduced or discontinued if participants experience discomfort, autonomic instability or skin irritation at the electrode site.

Neurological assessments

International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI): Neurological evaluations of participants with SCI will be performed using the ISNCSCI exam at baseline, postintervention and follow-up. The neurological level of injury and the severity (AIS) of damage to motor and sensory pathways will be determined by a trained physician using the standard ISNCSCI examination (2019 revision).69 Briefly, motor scores (muscle power graded as 0–5; for a total of 20 muscles in the four limbs) and sensory scores (light touch and pin prick across 28 dermatomes) will be recorded, along with assessment of anal tone and sensation. Sensory scores for each dermatome are assigned as 0=absent, 1=abnormal and 2=normal.

Autonomic Dysfunction following Spinal Cord Injury (ADFSCI): The ADFSCI will assess self-reported frequency and severity of autonomic-related symptoms. The AD part of the questionnaire includes 10 items, each using a 5-point scale to assess the frequency and severity of hypertensive symptoms, such as headache, goose bumps and confusion, under various circumstances.14

Outcomes and procedures

Primary and secondary outcomes are organised into mapping endpoints used to individualise parameters and safety, and domain-specific outcomes to assess cardiovascular stability, AD burden, LUT function, bowel function, sexual function and HRQoL.

Primary outcomes and procedures

Mapping

Haemodynamics: Beat-to-beat BP and HR responses to TCSCS will be used to establish safety and identify which TCSCS locations and parameters best modulate cardiovascular responses within the assigned electrode configuration. Cardiovascular monitoring (BP and HR) will be measured continuously (beat-by-beat) via finger photoplethysmography and ECG corrected to brachial pressure (Finapres NOVA, Finapres Medical Systems, Netherlands) as we test the effects of different stimulation frequencies (1 and 30 Hz), current amplitudes (10–200 mA) and locations (T7–L2 (vertebral levels)). Beat-by-beat BP and ECG signals will be digitised at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz using an analog-to-digital converter (PowerLab 16/35 System, LabChart, ADInstruments, Colorado Springs, Colorado, USA). Minute-by-minute BP and HR will also be measured (Dinamap Pro; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA).

Recordings of BP and heart rate (HR) can be used to detect and record potential adverse cardiovascular events, such as AD, during mapping and all functional assessments with and without TCSCS. Adverse events (incidence, type and severity graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) V.5.0)70 will include abnormal BP responses (eg, AD), cardiac responses (eg, tachycardia, bradycardia and arrhythmia), frequency and severity of skin irritation and any symptoms occurring during mapping, stimulation sessions and all assessments.71 Participants will be monitored for potential development of episodes of AD, which is defined according to the International Standards to document remaining Autonomic Function after SCI as an increase in SBP ≥20 mm Hg from baseline.41 AD will be resolved with the implementation of an established clinical management protocol in accordance with the Consortium of Spinal Cord Medicine Clinical practice guidelines (Clinical Practice guidelines provide recommendations for healthcare specialists based on evidence-based research conducted by the Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine).72

Surface EMG: Lower limb muscles and pelvic-floor EMG responses following a TCSCS pulse can be used to identify the activation of specific spinal cord segments involved in LUT, bowel and sexual control.78 Surface EMG electrodes (Bagnoli, Delsys, Natick, USA) will be used to record motor evoked potentials for different stimulation frequencies (1 and 30 Hz), current amplitudes (10–130 mA), and locations (T7−L2 (vertebral levels)). Potential motor targets and muscle recordings include: T10−12=lower abdominal (rectus abdominis), L1−2=hip flexor (rectus femoris), L3=knee extensors (vastus medialis and lateralis), L4=ankle dorsiflexors (tibialis anterior), L5=long toe extensor (hallucis longus), S1=ankle plantar flexors (gastrocnemius), S2−3=knee flexion (hamstring), S2−S4=pelvic floor muscles. EMG signals will be stored for offline analysis using custom MATLAB routines (MathWorks, Natick, USA).

Cardiovascular stability

OH during a 60° head-up tilt test (HUTT): The presence and severity of OH, quantified by a 20 mm Hg drop in SBP or a drop in diastolic BP of 10 mm Hg, will be evaluated using a 60° HUTT. on arrival to the centre, participants will be asked to empty their bladder to minimise the influence of reflex sympathetic activation on peripheral vascular tone (total duration: 25 min). In the supine position, participants will be instrumented with a five-lead ECG. Beat-to-beat SBP, diastolic, and mean BPs will be recorded continuously from the left hand (Finapres NOVA, Finapres Medical Systems, Netherlands), while discrete BPs will be taken every minute from the right brachial artery (Dinamap Pro; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA). Following instrumentation, baseline recordings will be made during a 10 min supine rest period. Data acquisition will be performed as detailed above. Participants will then be passively moved to 60° upright tilt position by the investigators using the tilt table. This position will be maintained for 15 min, followed by 5 min of recovery. Change in SBP during OH triggered by HUTT will be quantified.

AD during urodynamic studies (UDS): The burden of AD (severity and symptoms) will be recorded during UDS. The procedures for UDS are outlined below under LUT function. Changes in beat-by-beat BP between baseline and peak detrusor activity or at maximum infusion volume during UDS will be indexed.

AD during digital anorectal stimulation (DARS): DARS is a routine bowel evacuation technique in individuals with SCI.73 Participants will be asked to empty their bladder and perform a bowel routine within the preceding 24 hours of the DARS session. Participants will be positioned in the left lateral decubitus position while an experienced physician (AVK) will conduct the procedure using a gloved, lubricated index finger. Gentle pressure will be applied to the rectal wall for 30–60 s in line with clinical practice. Beat-by-beat haemodynamics will be recorded for cardiovascular safety and to index the severity of AD. Participants will also report any symptoms experienced (eg, feeling flushed, sweating, goose bumps).

AD during genital vibrostimulation (GVS; penile or clitoral): AD responses during genital stimulation will be conducted to assess the effect of TCSCS on sexual function. Beat-by-beat haemodynamics will be monitored continuously using finger plethysmography and ECG. GVS will be conducted by a sexual health physician (SLE) and will be applied by a commercially-available handheld vibrator applied to the glans penis (males) or the clitoral area (females) with a frequency of 70–100 Hz. Vibrostimulation will be stopped prior to ejaculation or orgasm, and AD symptoms during each GVS will be monitored and recorded. Before GVS participants will lie supine for 5 min to record baseline haemodynamics. This will be followed by GVS for a maximum of 3 min, followed by a pause of 1 min before the cycle is repeated. The burden of AD will be quantified by peak ΔSBP and subjective (AD severity symptom scale) assessments.

LUT function

Urodynamic studies (UDS) will be used to assess LUT function by assessing cystometric capacity and detrusor pressure. Before UDS, participants will be screened for the presence of an acute UTI by a trained clinician. Participants will then undergo standard clinical procedures for UDS,74 86 including cystometry with body-warm (37°C) isotonic saline solution and a filling rate of <30 mL per minute through a 6F double lumen catheter with the participants in the supine position. Abdominal pressure will be measured with a 10F intrarectal balloon catheter. Detrusor pressure will be calculated by subtracting intra-abdominal pressure from intravesical pressure.74 86 Pelvic floor EMG will be recorded during cystometry with a surface electrode placed on the perineum. Filling will be stopped when the participants report a sensation of fullness, at the moment of urine leakage, AD, a detrusor pressure of over 40 mm Hg, or per the request of the patient. Cystometric capacity will be quantified as the voided volume after permission to void plus the post-residual volume at the end of UDS.74 86 Post-residual volume will be quantified through a urinary bladder ultrasound (Cubescan BioCon-700, Roxon, Canada). After the procedure, participants will receive 500 mg prophylactic antibiotic (Ciprofloxacin) to reduce the risks of UTI, or another equivalent antibiotic in the event that the participant is allergic to Ciprofloxacin. UDS and follow-up will be performed by a trained clinician under the supervision of a urologist (KA).

Bowel function

Anorectal manometry (ARM): To evaluate anorectal function, high-resolution ARM will be performed using a balloon-tipped solid-state catheter with 16 circumferential regions and 256 sensors (Manoscan, Medtronic, USA), with participants in the left decubitus position. The procedure will be conducted by a colorectal surgeon (AG). Participants will be required to fast for at least 6 hours and undergo an enema before each ARM.

Prior to manometry, an anorectal exam will be conducted to inspect the perianal skin, anal canal and rectum for external haemorrhoids. The manometry probe will then be inserted, with the balloon positioned 12 cm from the anal verge in the distal rectum. Measurements include resting and squeeze pressures, the rectoanal inhibitory reflex (RAIR), and anorectal sensation.87 Resting pressure will be recorded twice for 20 s. Maximum squeeze pressure will be measured twice by instructing participants to contract their pelvic floor for up to 10 s. The change in pressure (delta) will be calculated by subtracting resting from squeeze pressure.

The recto-anal-inhibitory-reflex (RAIR) is a centrally and enterically mediated reflex essential for defecation88 89 that will be assessed by inflating the rectal balloon with 60 mL of air. RAIR is confirmed by a ≥20% drop in anal canal pressure, with reflex duration defined as the time for pressure to return to 80% of baseline.90 Amplitude reduction will be calculated based on the minimum pressure and percent change relative to baseline.

Sexual function

Semi-structured qualitative interview: Participants will be interviewed in person by a sexual medicine clinician (SLE) to ascertain qualitative information on sexual functioning. These interviews will be digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim and de-identified. The interview schedule will include questions pertaining to the following: participants’ sexual history and current relationship status, level of sexual drive, ability to orgasm, barriers to sexual activity, self-esteem, use of ergogenic aids and participants’ perceptions of the role of TCSCS on sexual functioning. Female participants will be asked about lubrication quality, and male participants will be asked about erection quality (with and without pharmaceutical or prosthetic assistance) and their ability to ejaculate. Participants will be advised during the consent process that the clinician will be acting as a researcher during this interview and is therefore prohibited from providing clinical care or direct medical advice during the study. Participants may decline to answer any questions they do not feel comfortable answering. A complete thematic analysis will be performed using NVivo software (V.9.0 or later, QSR, Southport, UK). The interview topics were compiled by a sexual medicine physician working with individuals with SCI (SLE). The expected duration of the interview will be approximately 30–45 min.

Secondary outcome and procedures

Cardiovascular stability

Transcranial doppler (TCD): CBF will be measured during HUTT with a TCD (PMD150 Bilateral Digital Transcranial Doppler, SPENTECH, DBA Spencer Technologies, Redmond, Washington, USA). TCD is a non-invasive ultrasound technique used to measure real-time CBF velocity. TCD emits ultrasound waves from a probe at a frequency of 2 MHz which penetrate through the skull and are reflected by moving red blood cells. Bilateral isonation of the middle (M1 segment) and posterior (P1 segment) cerebral arteries will be obtained through the temporal window using standardised depths and optimisation for maximal signal-to-noise ratio.91 Probes will be secured with a headframe fixation device to minimise motion artefact during HUTT. Outcomes will be the peak change in CBF during HUTT If an adequate transtemporal window cannot be obtained, TCD outcomes will be handled as missing.

Cognitive tests during HUTT: Cognitive function will be assessed using the Stroop (Colour-Word) Test and the Verbal Fluency Test. The Stroop Test is used to assess attention, concentration and psychomotor speed. The Stroop Test includes three sections in which the participant will be required to perform the cognitive tasks as quickly as possible.92 (1) Stroop Word: participants will be asked to read the randomised and repeated presentation of three words (red, green, blue) printed in black ink; (2) Stroop Colour: participants will be asked to name the colour of repeated presentation of an X printed in red, green and blue ink, and (3) Stroop Colour-Word: participants will be asked to name the colour of the ink in which colour names were printed while ignoring the word itself. The time to complete second list (P2), third list (P3), as well as the time difference between these two lists (P3–P2) will be recorded.93 This test measures attention, cognitive interference and processing speed.92 94 The verbal fluency test evaluates an individual’s executive functioning, testing the ability to retrieve specific information within restricted search parameters. We assessed letter fluency by asking participants to generate words beginning with a single letter (most commonly F, A and S). Individuals are given 60 s to name as many words as possible.94 95

24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (24 hours ABPM): 24-hour ABPM will be performed using the Meditech Card(X)plore (Meditech, Budapest, Hungary) and Schiller BR-102 plus (Schiller AG, Baar, Switzerland), a well-established clinical protocol.96 Compared with a single one-time clinic visit, 24 -hour ABPM has been found to provide a highly effective and comprehensive assessment of true BP and HR. Briefly, a standard adult-sized cuff (arm circumference of 24–32 cm) will be fixed on the non-dominant arm, with recordings taken every 15 min between 07:00–23:00 (daytime) and hourly from 23:00 to 07:00 (night-time). Mean daytime and night-time values will be determined from all valid measurements. Participants will be asked to complete an activity log to document bowel movements, transfers, meals, sleep position changes or perceived BP changes, and will manually measure these events on the device. Routine daily activities may continue, but exercise, excessive physical exertion and scheduled bowel routines (a common AD trigger) on the same day of wearing the device will be avoided. Prior to starting 24-hour ABPM, three consecutive baseline measurements (separated by 1 min) will be recorded (Dinamap Pro; GE Healthcare) with the participant seated in their own wheelchair, following 5 min of rest with the average used as the baseline values. AD will be identified as SBP ≥20 mm Hg above baseline, with severity calculated by subtracting the highest recorded SBP from the respective baseline. Night-time AD assessment will use a separate (supine during sleep) baseline and absence of nocturnal dip (BP is reduced by 10% during sleep). OH will be measured as a reduction of SBP by 20 mm Hg or diastolic BP by 10 mm Hg below the baseline.

LUT function

Questionnaires: Participants will complete two questionnaires related to LUT function at baseline, post-treatment and follow-up. (1) The Neurogenic Bladder Symptom Score: comprises 23 questions covering 3 domains, including incontinence, storage and voiding and specific consequences, as well as one question on QoL.97 (2) The Incontinence-QoL: comprises 10 questions covering 3 domains, including avoidance and limiting behaviour, psychosocial impacts and social embarrassment, which will be summarised as a total score. All scores, for each domain and a total, will be transformed into a continuous scale value.98

Bowel function

Whole gut transit time using dye: Participants will be asked to follow their regular diet and lifestyle routine. Participants will be asked to consume a standardised drink containing dye within a 10 min period. The standardised drink will consist of milk with the addition of royal blue dye (at least 0.15 g) or beetroot powder (usually 30 g), determined by the participant’s preference. This assessment is based on the whole gut transit time using blue dye99; in lieu of synthetic royal blue dye, participants may opt to take a natural food dye. If a person is lactose intolerant, a non-dairy alternative such as soy or almond milk will be substituted. Bowel transit time will begin as soon as the participant begins drinking. Participants will log the first appearance of coloured stool in the study log noting time and date. Transit time will be considered from the time of dye ingestion to the first visualisation of blue dye or beetroot powder within stool. Stool consistency will be monitored with the Bristol Stool Chart which has been used to define patients as constipated—type 1 to 3, normal—type 4 or 5 and diarrhoea—type 6 or 7.100

Questionnaires: Participants will complete two questionnaires related to bowel function at baseline, post-treatment and follow-up. (1) Neurogenic bowel dysfunction score (NBD Score). The NBD score is a measure of both constipation and faecal incontinence and was developed for and validated in the SCI population.101 This questionnaire comprises 10 questions focusing on defecation (ie, frequency, duration, and clinical symptoms), constipation (ie, use of aiding medication and digital stimulation), faecal incontinence (ie, frequency, aiding medication, and flatus; and peri-anal skin problems. The consequential NBD score relates to four different neurogenic bowel dysfunction severity levels.101 (2) The modified Wexner faecal incontinence score (WIS): The WIS scale includes items that take into account type, frequency and extent to which faecal incontinence alters an individual’s life, use of anti-diarrhoea medications and the ability to defer defecation for 15 min.102

Time needed for bowel management (TNFBM): During participants’ regular bowel routines at home, they will be asked to record the time from ‘suppository insertion’ to ‘when bowel evacuation is completed’. This time will represent the TNFBM.

Sexual function

Questionnaires: Both males and females will complete the Orgasm Rating Scale, which is a self-reported measure that evaluates the multidimensional subjective experience of orgasm using 25 adjectives answered on a 6-point Likert-type scale.103 Males will also complete The International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-15), which comprises 15 questions covering five domains, including erectile function, orgasmic function, intercourse satisfaction and overall satisfaction.104 Females will complete two additional questionnaires; (1) The Female Sexual Function Index, which comprises 19 questions covering six domains, including desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction and pain105 and (2) The Female Sexual Distress Scale, which is a 13-item scale for measuring sexually related distress in women, which has been found to be effective in discriminating between women with sexual dysfunctions and those without.106

Health related quality of life

Short Form (36) Health Survey (SF-36): The SF-36 is widely used to measure HRQOL in patients with SCI.107 The SF-36 consists of 8 domains pertaining to the respondents’ experiences in the last 4 weeks. Each of the 8 summed scores is linearly transformed onto a scale from 0 (negative health) to 100 (positive health) to provide a score for each subscale.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, SD, frequency distributions and counts of incidents) will be reported for each outcome and group. For outcomes exploring the efficacy of TCSCS on autonomic function, linear mixed-effects models will be used to account for repeated observations within participants and to allow inclusion of participants with incomplete (missing) observations across study visits. All randomised participants will be included in the analysis according to their assigned intervention group. Participants will be included as a random intercept to account for within-subject variability. Fixed effects will include stimulation site (mid-thoracic vs lumbosacral), single-session TCSCS (stimulation-on vs stimulation-off), time (baseline, postintervention, follow-up) and relevant interaction terms. Model fit will be evaluated using information criteria (Akaike Information Criterion [AIC]/Bayesian Information Criterion [BIC)], and model diagnostics will include inspection of residual distributions, assessment of homoscedasticity, and evaluation of influential observations. Effect estimates will be reported with 95% CIs. When significant interactions are observed, planned contrasts will be conducted using estimated marginal means, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. All analyses will be performed using R statistical software (V.4.3.2 or later), with statistical significance set at α=0.05.

Data management and safety

To protect participant anonymity, all data will be deidentified, and the code linking identities to study IDs will be securely stored and accessible only to authorised personnel. Study data will be stored on secure, password-protected institutional servers. Final deidentified research data will be deposited in a community-based repository for SCI research, such as the Open Data Commons for SCI (https://odc-sci.org/), following publication of the primary study findings and in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines. Statistical code and analysis protocols used in the study will also be made available through the same repository or on reasonable request. Signed consent forms will be retained for 25 years in accordance with Health Canada regulations. Any adverse events during the intervention will be immediately reported to the Data and Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC)—a panel of three independent physician scientists unaffiliated with the study or its funders—as well as the University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board. The DSMC will advise on trial continuation and data use for participants who withdraw. Study progress will be reviewed by the DSMC every 6 months. No formal interim efficacy analyses are planned given the pilot design; however, the DSMC may recommend modification or termination of the study if safety concerns arise.

Ethics and dissemination

This clinical study will follow the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and comply with ICH-GCP guidelines and relevant regulations. Protocol version 9.0 was approved by the UBC Clinical Research Ethics Board (UBC CREB H22-00365, NCT05369520) on 16 January 2026 (see online supplemental material for approved protocol); and the trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05369520) on 5 May 2022. Any significant protocol changes will be communicated to both the UBC CREB and the DSMC. Participants may receive feedback based on their functional outcomes to support adherence and potential benefit. Trial findings will be shared at national and international conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals, following CONSORT reporting standards. A summary will also be posted on the ICORD website and published in SCI-related magazines in participating provinces (eg, SCI BC). Data collected will be deidentified and shared through a public research repository (eg, Open Data Commons for SCI) or data will be made available on reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept pilot clinical trial will explore the efficacy of TCSCS for improving patient-centred autonomic outcomes following motor-complete SCI, which are a high priority for recovery in this population. By examining multiple autonomic domains, including cardiovascular stability, LUT, bowel and sexual function, the study will provide a comprehensive characterisation of autonomic dysfunction and generate effect size estimates to inform outcome selection and study design for future trials.

A strength of this study is the comprehensive evaluation of multiple autonomic domains using objective physiological assessments in addition to validated patient-reported outcome measures. This approach will allow for detailed characterisation of how neuromodulation influences different components of autonomic control. Furthermore, by comparing stimulation delivered at mid-thoracic and lumbosacral spinal levels, the study aims to explore whether stimulation site may differentially influence specific autonomic functions, which may help guide optimisation of stimulation strategies for future clinical applications.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this pilot trial. The absence of a sham stimulation condition limits the ability to control for potential placebo effects, and participants and investigators delivering the intervention will not be blinded. The study will also include a relatively small and heterogeneous sample of individuals with chronic motor-complete SCI, which may limit generalisability to individuals with incomplete injuries or lower-level lesions. Nevertheless, findings from this pilot study will inform the design of future adequately powered randomised controlled trials evaluating neuromodulation strategies for improving autonomic function following SCI.
