Sumsets, 3SUM, Subset Sum: Now for Real!
Nick Fischer

Abstract
We study a broad class of algorithmic problems with an “additive flavor” such as computing sumsets, 3SUM, Subset Sum and geometric pattern matching. Our starting point is that these problems can often be solved efficiently for integers , owed to the rich available tool set including bit-tricks, linear hashing, and the Fast Fourier Transform. However, for real numbers these tools are not available, leading to significant gaps in the best-known running times for integer inputs versus for real inputs. In this work our goal is to close this gap. As our key contribution we design a new technique for computing real sumsets. It is based on a surprising blend of algebraic ideas (like Prony’s method and coprime factorizations ) with combinatorial tricks. We then apply our new algorithm to the aforementioned problems and successfully obtain, in all cases, equally fast algorithms for real inputs. Specifically, we replicate the running times of the following landmark results by randomized algorithms in the standard real RAM model: • Sumsets: Given two sets A , B A,B , their sumset A + B = { a + b : a ∈ A , b ∈ B } A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\} can be computed in time O ~ ​ ( | A + B | ) \widetilde{O}(|A+B|) [Cole, Hariharan; STOC’02]. • Geometric pattern matching: Given two sets A , B A,B , we can test whether there is some shift such that A + s ⊆ B A+s\subseteq B in time O ~ ​ ( | A | + | B | ) \widetilde{O}(|A|+|B|) [Cardoze, Schulman; FOCS’98]. • 3SUM with preprocessing: We can preprocess three size- n n sets A , B , C A,B,C in time O ~ ​ ( n 2 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{2}) such that upon query of sets A ′ ⊆ A , B ′ ⊆ B , C ′ ⊆ C A^{\prime}\subseteq A,B^{\prime}\subseteq B,C^{\prime}\subseteq C , the 3SUM instance ( A ′ , B ′ , C ′ ) (A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}) can be decided in time O ~ ​ ( n 13 / 7 ) \widetilde{O}(n^{13/7}) [Chan, Lewenstein; STOC’15]. • Output-sensitive Subset Sum: Given a size- n n (multi-)set X X and a target t t , we can compute the set of subset sums { Σ ( X ′ ) : X ′ ⊆ X , Σ ( X ′ ) ≤ t } \{\Sigma(X^{\prime}):X^{\prime}\subseteq X,\Sigma(X^{\prime})\leq t\} in output-sensitive time O ~ ​ ( n + out 4 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(n+\OUT^{4/3}) [Bringmann, Nakos; STOC’20].

Introduction

We study a broad set of basic computational problems with an “additive component” such as 3SUM, Subset Sum, computing sumsets and geometric pattern matching. These problems are of great importance to several communities, notably within fine-grained algorithms and complexity, computational geometry, and, to a lesser degree, also in cryptography and computer algebra, and have received substantial attention in the literature. Especially in recent years this effort resulted in many beautiful algorithms, often powered by tools from additive combinatorics.

In this work we revisit these additive problems for
real

Can we adapt the landmark results for “additive problems” to real inputs?

This question is not only naturally fundamental, but is also driven by countless theoretical and practical applications, particularly in computational geometry, some of which we will encounter soon. It has been studied for many important algorithmic problems (see e.g. the exposition on 3SUM algorithms in the following paragraph), but was also asked in the context of fine-grained lower bounds; see

Integer-Specific Techniques

For many other additive problems, however, the gap between the fastest algorithms for integers versus for reals is still not closed—and for all problems considered in this paper the gap is more dramatic than “just” log-factors. The reason, in all these cases, is the routine use of integer-specific techniques, at least indirectly, and often in combination with each other. Specifically, we can almost exclusively trace back the issues to the following palette of problematic techniques:

1.

Bit-tricks, rounding, scaling:

The first integer-specific tool that comes to mind is
bit-tricks
. A typical use case is that the computation of some
L

L=O(\log n)
) can be reduced to the
L

For obvious reasons, this idea cannot work for real numbers that generally require infinitely many bits. In fact, removing this scaling trick is
the
obstacle in achieving strongly polynomial-time algorithms for a plethora of algorithms from combinatorial optimization and is throughout considered one of the most important questions in the area; see
[
31

for a recent breakthrough on single-source shortest paths without scaling.

2.

Linear hashing:

We call a hash function

3.

Fast Fourier Transform:

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is one of the most influential computational primitives with countless applications across various disciplines. A typical algorithmic consequence of the FFT is that the

Real Sumset.

There is a Las Vegas algorithm that, given

It turns out that the nontrivial computation of sumsets is exactly the primitive that is missing in many algorithms for additive-type problems. Most applications work in a “plug-and-play” fashion, while for others (such as for geometric pattern matching) we design new reductions to the sparse sumset problem.

1.2
The Real RAM Model and its Downsides

All of our algorithms assume the standard real RAM model supporting basic arithmetic operations (
{+},{-},{\cdot},{/}
) and comparisons (
{=},{\leq},{\geq},{<},{>}
) of
infinite-precision
real numbers. In particular, we do
not
presuppose stronger operations such as computing exponential or logarithm functions, or flooring (see e.g.
[
30
]
or
Section

2
for more details). In this sense, our algorithms qualify as satisfying
theoretical
solutions to the problems described before.

Nevertheless, the real RAM model often reflects the real world quite inaccurately, and also in our case we consider it unlikely that our algorithms have immediate
practical
impact. The reason is that our algorithms crucially rely on an
exact, infinite-precision
model. This can be realistic in rare cases, e.g. when a fast library for rational numbers is available (our algorithms work just as well over the rationals, assuming that arithmetic operations take unit time). But in the majority of cases this assumption is simply unrealistic. We leave it as future work to come up with algorithms in more realistic models, perhaps along the following lines:

•

A reasonable weaker model is the real RAM model restricted to
bounded-degree comparisons
as suggested in
[
23
]
. This could concretely mean that we are only allowed to compare two inputs (
a\leq b
), or compare sums of inputs (
a+b\leq c
), or testing whether a bounded-degree polynomial of the inputs is nonnegative.

Unfortunately, we immediately face a barrier when attempting to compute sumsets in this restricted model. It is a folklore assumption (related to the combinatorial Boolean matrix multiplication conjecture
[
70
]
) that sumsets (or equivalently, Boolean convolutions) cannot be computed in truly subquadratic time without using algebraic methods. Under this assumption, in a model where we restrict the power of multiplication any efficient sumset algorithm would have to first “identify” that the input consists of integers, then convert the input to integers and apply the FFT on the integer registers of the machine. This appears quite strange, and might indicate that the strong version of the real RAM is indeed necessary.

•

Another avenue is to explore algorithms that behave numerically stable. But it is unclear what this should even mean in our context, as already the definition of many our problems assumes exact arithmetic. Besides, for many approximate variants (such as if we tolerate additive
\pm\epsilon
errors) a practical approach would be to treat the instance as an integer instance obtained by rounding (see e.g.
[
19
]
), in which case the integer toolkit becomes available and we have stripped the problem of its real hardness.

Having clarified these restrictions, we emphasize that when restricted to
integer
inputs all of our algorithms can be implemented in the word RAM model in essentially the same running time (with only small modifications such as computing modulo an appropriate prime). We thereby also introduce an alternative technique for integer problems, which we believe has broader applicability beyond the problems discussed here.

1.3
Technical Overview

In this section we will summarize the key ideas leading to our new algorithm for computing real sumsets (
Theorem

1.1

1.3.1
An Algebraic Algorithm for Integer Sumsets

As a first step, let us reformulate the problem of computing the sumset
A+B
in terms of polynomials. Specifically, let
f(X)=\sum_{a\in A}X^{a}
and
g(X)=\sum_{b\in B}X^{b}
, and denote their product polynomial by
h:=fg
. It is easy to check that
A+B
is exactly the
support

\supp(h)
(i.e., the set of all
c
for which the monomial
X^{c}
has a nonzero coefficient). Let
t=|A+B|
.
5
5

5

Throughout this overview we will implicitly assume that
t
is known; this assumption can be removed, e.g. by exponential search.
Note that all three polynomials
f
,
g
and
h
are
t
-sparse
(i.e., have at most
t
nonzero coefficients). In order to compute
h
we follow an evaluation–interpolation approach:

1.

Evaluate
f(x_{0}),\dots,f(x_{2t-1})
and
g(x_{0}),\dots,g(x_{2t-1})
at some strategic points
x_{0},\dots,x_{2t-1}
.

2.

Multiply
h(x_{0})\leftarrow f(x_{0})\cdot g(x_{0}),\dots,h(x_{2t-1})\leftarrow f(x_{2t-1})\cdot g(x_{2t-1})
.

3.

Interpolate
h
from the evaluations
h(x_{0}),\dots,h(x_{2t-1})
.

Sparse Evaluation and Interpolation

How fast can we implement this algorithm? The second step runs in linear time
O(t)
. And while perhaps not a priori clear, the first step can also be efficiently implemented in time
\widetilde{O}(t)
with comparably little effort if we choose the evaluation points to be a geometric progression:

1.7
Sparse Evaluation and Interpolation.

Let
f
be a
t
-sparse polynomial over a field
\mathbb{F}
, and let
\omega\in\mathbb{F}
have multiplicative order at least
\deg(f)
.
6
6

6

The condition that
\omega
requires large multiplicative order is technical and we encourage the reader to not worry about this condition throughout.
Then:

•

Given
f
we can compute
f(\omega^{0}),\dots,f(\omega^{t-1})
in time
O(t\log^{2}t)
.

•

Given
f(\omega^{0}),\dots,f(\omega^{t-1})
and
\supp(f)
, we can uniquely interpolate
f
in time
O(t\log^{2}t)
.

This lemma is an immediate consequence of the fact that linear algebra operations (such as matrix-vector products, solving linear systems, computing determinants, etc.) involving transposed Vandermonde matrices can be implemented in time
O(t\log^{2}t)

[
44
,
17
,
51
,
59

. The second item in the lemma also helps in implementing step 3 if we can compute

We remark that this general recipe is not new and has already been used for computing sparse sumsets

by a scaling trick which cannot work for real numbers and is thus prohibitive for us. We will instead employ

Prony’s Method

Prony’s method
, named after its inventor Gaspard de Prony, is an old polynomial interpolation algorithm dating back to 1795

As we do not expect the target audience of this paper to be necessarily familiar with Prony’s method, we include a detailed overview. In this section our aim is to be more intuitive than formally precise. Prony’s method states that we can recover the support of a

-sparse polynomial

h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})

Definition 1.8

(Linear Recurrence)
.

A sequence
s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{t-1}
is
linearly recurrent with degree

\Lambda(X)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}X^{\ell}
such that each term in the sequence is determined by a linear combination of its
r
preceding terms, weighted with
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{r}
:

\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot s_{i+\ell}=0\quad\text{for all $0\leq i<t-r$.}

If
\Lambda
is the monic polynomial (i.e., with leading coefficient
\lambda_{r}=1
) with smallest-possible degree satisfying this condition then we call
\Lambda
the
minimal polynomial
of the sequence
s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{t-1}
.

At first it might seem odd why we view the
\lambda_{i}
’s as a polynomial, but this viewpoint hopefully soon starts to make sense. It turns out that the sequence of evaluations
h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})
is linearly recurrent with degree exactly
t
. And, more importantly, we can read of the support from the minimal polynomial of this recurrence as stated in the following lemma. This lemma constitutes the heart of Prony’s method:

1.9
Prony’s Method.

Let
h
be a polynomial which is exactly
t
-sparse, and let
\omega
have multiplicative order at least
\deg(h)
. Then the sequence
h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})
is linearly recurrent with degree
t
. Moreover, its minimal polynomial is

\Lambda(X)=\prod_{c\in\supp(h)}(X-\omega^{c}).

Proof.

We include a proof at this point as we believe it to be quite informative. Let
c_{0},\dots,c_{t-1}
denote the support of
h
and let
h(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}h_{j}\cdot X^{c_{j}}
. Assume that the sequence
h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})
is linearly recurrent with degree
r
for some polynomial
\Lambda(X)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}X^{\ell}
. We can therefore express the recurrence condition as follows, for any
i\geq 0
:

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot h(\omega^{i+\ell})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}h_{j}\cdot\omega^{(i+\ell)c_{j}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\omega^{ic_{j}}\cdot h_{j}\cdot\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot\omega^{\ell c_{j}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\omega^{ic_{j}}\cdot h_{j}\cdot\Lambda(\omega^{c_{j}}).

We see two consequences: First, picking the polynomial
\Lambda(X)=\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}(X-\omega^{c_{j}})
with roots at all powers
\omega^{c_{0}},\dots,\omega^{c_{t-1}}
satisfies this recurrence condition for all
i
. In particular, the recurrence has degree at most
t
.

Second, suppose that the minimal polynomial
\Lambda
has degree less than
t
. Then for at least one power
\omega^{c_{0}},\dots,\omega^{c_{t-1}}
,
\Lambda
does not have a root. Let
p(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}h_{j}\cdot\Lambda(\omega^{c_{j}})\cdot X^{c_{j}}
, and note that
p
is not identically zero. We can rewrite the recurrence from before as
p(\omega^{i})=0
for all
i\in[t]
. This yields a contradiction: On the one hand
p
is not identically zero, but on the other hand,
p
is
t
-sparse and therefore uniquely determined by its evaluations
p(\omega^{0}),\dots,p(\omega^{t-1})
all of which are zero (see
Lemma

1.7
).
∎

Moreover, it is well-know that we can compute the degree of a linear recurrence as well as its minimal polynomial. This can be achieved in quadratic-time by the well-known Berlekamp-Massey algorithm
[
10
,
53
]
, but also in near-linear time by an application of the Extended Euclidian Algorithm
[
44
,
69
]
:

1.10
Solving Linear Recurrences.

Let
s_{0},\dots,s_{t-1}\in\mathbb{F}
for some field
\mathbb{F}
. The minimal polynomial of this sequence can be computed in time
O(t\log^{2}t)
.

Putting the Pieces Together

Lemma

1.9
suggests the following three steps to fill in the missing implementation of step 3:

1.

Evaluate
f(\omega^{0}),\dots,f(\omega^{2t-1})
and
g(\omega^{0}),\dots,g(\omega^{2t-1})
(
Lemma

1.7
).

2.

Multiply
h(\omega^{0})\leftarrow f(\omega^{0})\cdot g(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})\leftarrow f(\omega^{2t-1})\cdot g(\omega^{2t-1})
.

3a.

Compute the minimal polynomial
\Lambda
of the sequence
h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})
(
Lemma

1.10
).

3b.

Compute the roots
\omega^{c_{0}},\dots,\omega^{c_{t-1}}
of
\Lambda
.

3c.

Compute the logarithms
c_{0},\dots,c_{t-1}
of the roots to the base
\omega
.

It turns out that we can indeed carry the computations of Steps 3b and 3c efficiently over some appropriate chosen
finite field
; see e.g.
[
43
]
. This involves several number-theoretic tricks which, inconveniently, cannot work for real numbers. Interestingly, with the new ideas we are about to present, even these tricks will not be necessary anymore.

1.3.2
Adapting the Algorithm to Real Sumsets

We emphasize that up until this point we have not invented anything new, but rather viewed the existing techniques in a different light. So let us finally get back to the original problem of computing sumsets of
real
numbers. Is it conceivable that this approach succeeds for real numbers? Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We similarly define functions
f,g,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(X)=\sum_{a\in A}X^{a}
,
g(X)=\sum_{b\in B}X^{b}
and
h(X)=f(X)\cdot g(X)
(though these are no longer polynomials). Apart from this difference in naming it can be checked that all steps are well-defined and reasonable. In particular, the sequence of evaluations
h(\omega^{0}),\dots,h(\omega^{2t-1})
(for some real number
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
) is still linearly recurrent with degree exactly
t=|A+B|
and its minimal polynomial is
\Lambda(X)=\prod_{c\in A+B}(X-\omega^{c})
. Thus, the same algorithm as before is correctly computing the set
A+B
.

Unfortunately, there are two major issues with this algorithm. First, Step 3b requires the computation of the real roots of the degree-
t
polynomial
\Lambda
. As discussed before, this step could only be made efficient by working over an appropriate finite field, and these tricks are fruitless for the reals. The second problem seems even more hopeless: The real RAM model simply does not support computing the exponentiations
\omega^{0},\dots,\omega^{2t-1}
in Step 1, and taking the logarithms in Step 3c. In the following two paragraphs we present fixes for these two problems.

Solution 1: Coprime Factorizations

While there are many specialized algorithms for computing the roots of polynomials over finite fields, the integers, or the rationals (see e.g.
[
69
]
), to our knowledge there is no general-purpose near-linear-time root finding algorithm for abstract fields. Another line of research has focused on
approximately
computing the real roots based on numerical methods (but of course even the smallest error is intolerable in our setup). All in all, we are not aware of any algorithm that exactly computes the real roots of a polynomial in near-linear time, leaving us in an unclear situation.

Our solution is to settle for a weaker notion of factorization—a so-called
coprime factorization
. The background is that factoring a polynomial or an integer into its irreducible factors (i.e., primes) is typically a hard problem. In contrast, it is a much simpler problem to factor several polynomials or integers into factors that are only required to be
pairwise coprime
.
7
7

7

In this setup a single polynomial or integer can be trivially be factored into the singleton set containing itself. In this set all factors are vacously pairwise coprime.
Bernstein
[
11
]
gave a near-linear-time algorithm to compute coprime factorizations over arbitrary abstract fields:

1.11
Coprime Factorization of Polynomials
[
11
]
.

Given a set
\mathcal{P}
of nonzero polynomials (over some field
\mathbb{F}
), in time
\widetilde{O}(\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\deg(P))
we can compute a set of polynomials
\mathcal{Q}
such that:

•

Each polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}
can be expressed as the product of polynomials in
\mathcal{Q}
(up to scalars), and each polynomial
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
appears as a factor of some polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

•

The polynomials in
\mathcal{Q}
are pairwise coprime (i.e.,
\gcd(Q,Q^{\prime})=1
for all distinct
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}
).

•

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\deg(Q)\leq\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\deg(P)
.

In fact, Bernstein presents this result in a broader algebraic context that not only applies to polynomials. Since Bernstein employs a nonstandard model of computation, we include a self-contained proof of
Theorem

1.11
in
Appendix

A
. We consider this a very interesting tool which, to our surprise, has only limited applications so far.

To apply this theorem we depart from the algebraic framework and attempt a more combinatorial solution. In summary, the previously outlined sumset algorithm allows us to compute in near-linear time a polynomial
\Lambda=\Lambda_{A,B}
defined by

\Lambda_{A,B}(X)=\prod_{c\in A+B}(X-\omega^{c}).

Instead of factoring this polynomial directly, our idea is to repeat this algorithm for a polylogarithmic number of times with sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\subseteq A
and
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\subseteq B
to compute the set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}:=\{\Lambda_{A_{1},B_{1}},\dots,\Lambda_{A_{m},B_{m}}\}
. On these polynomials we apply
Theorem

1.11
to compute a coprime factorization. Let us call two factors
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
and
X-\omega^{c_{2}}

separated
if there some polynomial
\Lambda_{A_{i},B_{i}}
that contains
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
as a factor but does not contain
X-\omega^{c_{2}}
as a factor. The key insight is that any separated pair
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
and
X-\omega^{c_{2}}
does not appear in the same factor in
\mathcal{Q}
. Indeed, suppose that both
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
and
X-\omega^{c_{2}}
appear in the same factor
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. By the coprime condition this is the only factor
Q
in which
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
appears. Therefore,
Q
must be a factor of
\Lambda_{A_{i},B_{i}}
. But this yields a contradiction as we assumed that
X-\omega^{c_{2}}
is not a factor of
\Lambda_{A_{i},B_{i}}
. In light of this insight, in order to obtain the complete factorization
\mathcal{Q}=\{X-\omega^{c}:c\in A+B\}
, it suffices to guarantee that all pairs of factors
X-\omega^{c_{1}}
,
X-\omega^{c_{2}}
are separated. Luckily, this can be achieved by the following lemma:

1.12
Random Restrictions.

There is a Monte Carlo algorithm that, given sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of size at most
n
, computes in time
O(nm)
subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\subseteq A,\,B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\subseteq B
such that:

•

For every pair
c_{1},c_{2}\in A+B
, there is some
i\in[m]
with
\lvert(A_{i}+B_{i})\cap\{c_{1},c_{2}\}\rvert=1
(with high probability).

•

m=O(\log^{2}n)
.

The insight behind
Lemma

1.12
is a rather simple subsampling procedure: By subsampling
A
with rates
2^{-0},2^{-1},\dots,2^{-\log|A|}
we obtain with good probability a subset
A^{\prime}
such that there is a unique witness
a\in A^{\prime}
of
\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
; that is, there exist
b_{1}\in B
such that
a+b_{1}=c_{1}
or there exists
b_{2}\in B
such that
a+b_{2}=c_{2}
. If only one of these events happens, then we have successfully separated
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. Otherwise, we subsample
B
with rate
\frac{1}{2}
to eliminate exactly one of the two events with constant probability. For more details see
Section

3.3
.

Solution 2: Prony’s Method for Other Operators

Recall that the second challenge is to eliminate the need to compute exponentiations and logarithms (as these operations are not supported by the real RAM model). It turns out that Prony’s interpolation method works for operators other than “polynomial evaluation”. That is, we can interpolate a polynomial
f
not only given the evaluations
f(\omega^{0}),\dots,f(\omega^{t-1})
(which is perhaps the most natural representation), but also from evaluations of its derivatives
\frac{d^{0}f}{dx^{0}}(1),\dots,\frac{d^{2t-1}f}{dx^{2t-1}}(1)

[
40
]
, or even from evaluations of more general operators
[
66
]
.

With these works in mind, let us rework some of the previous setup to better match the real RAM model. Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the indicator functions of
A
and
B
(satisfying that
f(a)=1
if
a\in A
and
f(a)=0
otherwise). We write
\supp(f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)\neq 0\}
. Let
h
denote the discrete convolution of
f
and
g
, defined by
h(c)=\sum_{a+b=c}f(a)\cdot g(b)
(which is well-defined since
f,g
have only finite support). For integers
i\geq 0
, we define the
sum operator

\Sigma^{i}(f)
as the quantity

\Sigma^{i}(f)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x^{i}\cdot f(x).

Note that
\Sigma^{i}(f)
can indeed be computed in the real RAM model as all powers
i
are integers and can be computed efficiently by repeated squaring. Moreover, analogously to before it turns out that for any function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with support size
t
, the sequence
\Sigma^{0}(h),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(h)
is linearly recurrent with degree
t
, and its minimal polynomial is

\Lambda(X)=\prod_{a\in\supp(f)}(X-a).

This suggests the following algorithm: We evaluate
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f)
and
\Sigma^{0}(g),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(g)
, somehow multiply these evaluations to obtain
\Sigma^{0}(h),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(h)
, and then apply the modified Prony’s method to compute and factor
\Lambda(X)=\prod_{a\in\supp(f)}(X-a)
. This alternative setup, while somewhat less intuitive, indeed avoids exponentiations and logarithms. It comes, however, with additional challenges such as how to efficiently evaluate
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f)
, and how to combine these evaluations to efficiently obtain
\Sigma^{0}(h),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(h)
. All of these challenges have natural algebraic solutions which we provide in the technical
Section

3
.

2
Preliminaries

We write
[n]=\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and typically index objects starting from
0
.

Machine Model

Throughout we work in the standard real RAM model. In this model we assume the standard word RAM model with word size
\Theta(\log n)
(where
n
is the input size of the problem) and equipped with special machine cells holding real numbers. The model supports basic arithmetic operations (
{+},{-},{\cdot},{/}
) and comparisons (
{=},{\leq},{\geq},{<},{>}
) on reals in constant time. Moreover, we can convert in constant time any integer (stored in a word cell) into a real (stored in a real cell). We refer to
[
30
]
for a more formal definition.

We frequently deal with sets of real numbers. As we cannot use hash maps in this setting (as there is no way to hash from reals to integer indices), we implicitly assume that these sets are stored either as sorted arrays or balanced binary trees. The log-factors incurred to the running times will never matter for us.

Randomization

We say that an event occurs with high probability if it occurs with probability at least
1-n^{-c}
, where
n
is the problem size and
c
is an arbitrarily chosen constant. We say that a problem is in Monte Carlo time
T
if it can be solved by a Monte Carlo algorithm running in time
T
and succeeding with high probability. Analogously, we say that it is in Las Vegas time
T
if there is a Las Vegas (i.e., zero-error) algorithm running in expected time
T
, and terminating in time
T
with high probability.

3
Real Sumset in Near-Linear Time

In this section we design a near-linear time algorithm to compute the sumset
A+B
for two sets of real numbers
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. In fact, we solve the more general problem of computing the discrete convolution of two real functions. Specifically, throughout this section we consider functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

with finite support
(i.e., for which
\supp(f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)\neq 0\}
is a finite set). Computationally, we represent functions
f
with finite support as a finite list of its nonzero entries
(x,f(x))
.

We define the
discrete convolution

f\star g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as the function defined by

(f\star g)(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}\\
x+y=z\end{subarray}}f(x)\cdot g(y).

Note that
f\star g
again has finite support. Convolutions are tightly connected to sumsets in the following way: For a finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, let
\One_{A}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the indicator function of
A
(which is
1
on
A
and
0
elsewhere). Then
A+B=\supp(\One_{A}\star\One_{B})
, as can easily be verified from the definition.

We structure this section as follows: In
Section

3.1
we tailor Prony’s method to our need in the context of real numbers up to some missing pieces. In
Section

3.2
we demonstrate how to compute the support size of the convolution, and in
Section

3.3
we show how to replace the polynomial factorization step.

3.1
A Modified Prony’s Method

We start with the definition of the “sum operator”, and show that it satisfies a “product rule”.

Definition 3.1

(Sum Operator)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(with finite support) and
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We define

\Sigma^{i}(f)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x^{i}\cdot f(x).

3.2
Product Rule.

Let
f,g\in\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(with finite support). Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\Sigma^{k}(f\star g)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\Sigma^{i}(f)\cdot\Sigma^{j}(g).

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward calculation involving the binomial theorem:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{k}(f\star g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{R}}z^{k}\cdot(f\star g)(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{R}}z^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}\\
x+y=z\end{subarray}}f(x)\cdot g(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}(x+y)^{k}\cdot f(x)\cdot g(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}x^{i}\cdot f(x)\cdot y^{j}\cdot g(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\Sigma^{i}(f)\cdot\Sigma^{j}(g).\qed

Next, we prove that, for any function
f
, we can quickly evaluate the sum operator and interpolate
f
from its evaluations (
Lemma

3.3
) and that we can maintain the evaluations of the sum operator under taking convolutions (
Lemma

3.4
).

3.3
Evaluation and Interpolation.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be
t
-sparse. Then there are deterministic algorithms in time
O(t\log^{2}t)
for the following two tasks:

•

Given
f
, we can evaluate
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(f)
.

•

Given
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(f)
and
\supp(f)
, we can (uniquely) interpolate
f
.

Proof.

Let
a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1}
denote the support of
f
, and consider the following obvious identity:

\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma^{0}(f)\\
\Sigma^{1}(f)\\
\vdots\\
\Sigma^{t-1}(f)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{0}^{t-1}&a_{1}^{t-1}&\cdots&a_{t-1}^{t-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f(a_{0})\\
f(a_{1})\\
\vdots\\
f(a_{t-1})\end{bmatrix}

Both statements follow from the fact that linear algebraic operations involving transposed Vandermonde matrices run in time
O(t\log^{2}t)

[
44
,
17
,
51
,
59
]
. For the first item we compute a matrix-vector product, and for the second item we solve a linear equation system with indeterminates
f(a_{0}),\dots,f(a_{t-1})
.
∎

3.4
Convolution of Sum Operators.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(with finite support). Given
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(f),\Sigma^{0}(g),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(g)
, we can compute
\Sigma^{0}(f\star g),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(f\star g)
in time
O(t\log t)
.

Proof.

The key idea is to use the product rule (
Lemma

3.2
):

\Sigma^{k}(f\star g)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\Sigma^{i}(f)\cdot\Sigma^{j}(g).

Using that
\binom{k}{i}=\frac{k!}{i!\,j!}
, this identity can be rewritten as

\frac{\Sigma^{k}(f\star g)}{k!}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\frac{\Sigma^{i}(f)}{i!}\cdot\frac{\Sigma^{j}(g)}{j!}.

We can compute the sequences
(\Sigma^{i}(f)/i!)_{i=0}^{t-1}
and
(\Sigma^{j}(g)/j!)_{j=0}^{t-1}
in time
O(t)
. Then we view these sequences as the coefficients of two degree-
t
polynomials over
\mathbb{R}
; note that
(\Sigma^{k}(f\star g)/k!)_{k=0}^{t-1}
corresponds exactly to the first coefficients the product polynomial. Thus, by computing the product of two degree-
t
polynomials over
\mathbb{R}
we can recover the desired values
(\Sigma^{k}(f\star g))_{k=0}^{t-1}
. This task can be solved in time
O(t\log t)
by the Fast Fourier Transform (even over general rings, see
[
45
,
18
]
).
∎

3.5
Prony’s Method for the Sum Operator.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be
t
-sparse. Then the sequence
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f)
is linearly recurrent with degree exactly
t
, and its minimal polynomial is

\Lambda(X)=\prod_{a\in\supp(f)}(X-a).

Proof.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}
denote the support of
f
. If the sequence
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f)
is linearly recurrent with some degree
r
for some polynomial
\Lambda(X)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot X^{\ell}
, then we can express the recurrence condition as follows, for any
0\leq i<2t-r
:

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot\Sigma^{i+\ell}(f)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}^{i+\ell}\cdot f(a_{j})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}^{i}\cdot f(a_{j})\cdot\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot a_{j}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}^{i}\cdot f(a_{j})\cdot\Lambda(a_{j}).

Observe the following two consequences: First, picking the polynomial
\Lambda(X)=\prod_{j=1}^{t}(X-a_{j})
with roots at all support elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}
satisfies this recurrence condition for all
i
. In particular, the recurrence has degree at most
t
.

Second, suppose that the minimal polynomial
\Lambda
has degree
r<t
. Then for at least one support element
a_{i}
,
\Lambda
does not have a root. Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function supported on
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{t}\}
defined by
g(a_{j})=f(a_{j})\cdot\Lambda(a_{j})
. Clearly
g
is at most
t
-sparse and not identically zero. Moreover, we can rewrite the previous recurrence relation as
\Sigma^{i}(g)=0
, for all
0\leq i<t
. This yields a contradiction: On the one hand
g
is not identically zero, but on the other hand,
g
is
t
-sparse and therefore uniquely determined by its evaluations
\Sigma^{0}(g),\dots,\Sigma^{t-1}(g)
, all of which are zero (see the second item in
Lemma

3.3
).
∎

The following statement combines the previously established lemmas:

3.6
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be finite sets and let
t\geq|A+B|
. There is a deterministic algorithm running in time
O(t\log^{2}t)
computing the polynomial

\Lambda_{A+B}(X)=\prod_{c\in A+B}(X-c).

Proof.

Let
f=\One_{A}
and
g=\One_{B}
. Using
Lemma

3.3
we evaluate the sequences
\Sigma^{0}(f),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f)
and
\Sigma^{0}(g),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(g)
. Then, using
Lemma

3.4
we compute
\Sigma^{0}(f\star g),\dots,\Sigma^{2t-1}(f\star g)
. Finally, we solve this linear recurrence (by
Lemma

1.10
) and compute its minimal polynomial, which, by
Lemma

3.2
Computing the Sumset Size

One shortcoming of

is that it requires as an input a good approximation of the size
|A+B|
. It is easy to approximate this size by exponential search and a “Schwartz-Zippel-like evaluation”, but it turns out that in our setting we can even compute
|A+B|

exactly
and
deterministically
. This algorithm is based on the following lemma which was first proposed to be useful in this context in
[

]
. Since the proof is short and insightful, we include it.

3.7
Sparsity Tester for Nonnegative Functions,
[
47
,
52
]
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(with finite support). For any
s\geq 0
,
f
has sparsity more than
s
if and only if

\det\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma^{0}(f)&\Sigma^{1}(f)&\cdots&\Sigma^{s-1}(f)\\
\Sigma^{1}(f)&\Sigma^{2}(f)&\cdots&\Sigma^{s}(f)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Sigma^{s-1}(f)&\Sigma^{s}(f)&\cdots&\Sigma^{2s-2}(f)\\
\end{bmatrix}>0.

Proof.

Let
M=[\Sigma^{i+j}(f)]_{i,j\in[s]}
denote the matrix in the statement. We first prove that
M
is a positive semi-definite matrix. To this end let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
be arbitrary; we prove that
\lambda^{T}M\lambda\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\lambda^{T}M\lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[s]}\lambda_{i}\cdot\lambda_{j}\cdot\Sigma^{i+j}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[s]}\lambda_{i}\cdot\lambda_{j}\cdot\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x^{i+j}\cdot f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{i\in[s]}\lambda_{i}x^{i}\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{j\in[s]}\lambda_{j}x^{j}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)\cdot\left\lparen\sum_{i\in[s]}\lambda_{i}x^{i}\right\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Having established that
M
is always positive semi-definite, we prove that it is positive definite if and only if
f
has sparsity more than
s
. For a vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
as before, let
\Lambda(X)=\sum_{i\in[s]}\lambda_{i}X^{i}
. The previous calculation reveals that
\lambda^{T}M\lambda=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)\cdot\Lambda(x)^{2}
. Thus, on the one hand, if
f
is at most
s
-sparse we can pick
\Lambda
to be any degree-
s
polynomial with roots at
\supp(f)
so that
\lambda^{T}M\lambda=0
. On the other hand, if
f
has sparsity more than
s
then any nonzero degree-
s
polynomial
\Lambda
must be nonzero at some support element
x\in\supp(f)
and thus
\lambda^{T}M\lambda>0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
∎

4.1
The Baur-Strassen Theorem

The Baur-Strassen theorem intuitively states that any algebraic algorithm computing some function

can compute additionally all partial derivatives of
f
, essentially for free. To make this precise, we first have to fix what we understand by an algebraic algorithm.

Definition 4.1

(Arithmetic Circuit)
.

An
arithmetic circuit
C
over the field
\mathbb{F}
and the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is a directed acyclic graph as follows. The nodes are called
gates
, and are of the following two types: Each gate either has in-degree
0
and is labeled with a variable
x_{i}
or a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
, and it has in-degree
2
and is labeled with an arithmetic operation (
{+},{-},{\times},{/}
).

We refer to the gates labeled by variables
X_{i}
as
input gates
, by constants as
constant gates
and by an operation
{\circ}\in\{{+},{-},{\times},{/}\}
as
\circ
-gates
. A gate with out-degree
0
is called an
output gate.
The
size
of an arithmetic circuit
C
, denoted by
|C|
, is the number of gates plus number of edges in
C
. Note that each gate in an arithmetic circuit computes a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
in a natural way: Input gates compute
x_{i}
, constant gates compute the constant polynomial
\alpha
, and each, say,
\times
-gate computes the product of the polynomials computed by its two incoming gates. We typically say that an arithmetic circuit computes polynomials

if there are
m

[

,

For any arithmetic circuit

for all

. The circuit

has size

and can be constructed in time
O(|C|)
.

Arithmetic circuits form a natural model of algebraic computation. It can easily be verified that most algorithms developed in the last section can similarly be implemented with arithmetic circuits. In particular, it is relevant that

In this section we revisit the following theorem due to Bernstein

See
1.11

Note that we can assume that all polynomials

1.11

is a

of some other set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}
if the polynomials
\mathcal{Q}
are pairwise coprime, and every polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{Q}
. We say that the coprime basis is

if every polynomial
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
appears as a factor of some polynomial

A.1
.

If
\mathcal{Q}

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{P}

can be factored into polynomials from
\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check that the set of polynomials that can be factored into polynomials from
\mathcal{Q}
is closed under taking greatest common divisors. To this end, consider any two polynomials expressible as
\prod_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q^{a_{Q}}
and
\prod_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q^{b_{Q}}
, respectively. Their gcd is
\prod_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q^{\min(a_{Q},b_{Q})}
, which clearly also factors into polynomials from
\mathcal{Q}
.

A.2
.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a reduced coprime basis of
\mathcal{P}

\deg(\mathcal{Q})\leq\deg(\mathcal{P})

Proof.

Consider the factorization of

\mathcal{Q}
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is pairwise coprime, this factorization is unique. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is further reduced, every polynomial

1.1
).

The correctness is immediate, but it remains to analyze the running time. Note that the recursion tree has depth
\log n
. Focus on any level
\ell\in[\log n]
. This level induces a partition into at most
2^{\ell}
submultisets
A_{1},\dots,A_{2^{\ell}}\subseteq A
. The running time of level
\ell
is dominated by computing the sets
\mathcal{S}(A_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{S}(A_{2^{\ell}})
near-linear output-sensitive time. By the previous lemma, this time can be bounded as follows:

\widetilde{O}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{2^{\ell}}|\mathcal{S}(A_{i})|\right\rparen\leq\widetilde{O}\lparen|\mathcal{S}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{S}(A_{2^{\ell}})|+2^{\ell}\rparen\leq\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A)|+n)=\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A)|).

Summing over all
\log n\leq\log|\mathcal{S}(A)|
levels yields the claimed time bound.
∎

Subset Sum with a Target

The more interesting problem is to compute all subsets sums below a prespecified target
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\mathcal{S}(A,t)=\mathcal{S}(A)\cap[0,t]
. We replicate Bringmann and Nakos’ result
[
15
]
that
\mathcal{S}(A,t)
can be computed in roughly time
|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3}
for the reals:

See
1.4

Having already established that (prefix-restricted) sumsets can be computed efficiently (
Theorems

1.1
and
1.5
), the remaining proof of
Theorem

1.4
does not differ much from Bringmann and Nakos’ original version. We will therefore keep the presentation concise. The only issue that arises is that, in contrast to the integer setting, for the reals we cannot assume to (approximately) know
|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|
in advance. We deal with this issue by adding another layer of recursion (similar to the scaling trick, but somewhat more involved, see the proof of
Theorem

1.4
).

The general idea is to use Bringmann’s two-level color-coding technique
[
12
]
, and to use prefix-restricted sumset computations to avoid computing subset sums above the threshold
t
. Throughout, we assume that
A
is preprocessed in such a way that
n\leq|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|
(i.e., we remove elements bigger than
t
or reduce their multiplicity appropriately).

D.4
Second Level.

There is a Monte Carlo algorithm that, given a multiset
A\subseteq[u,2u]
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, computes a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{S}(A,t)
in time
\widetilde{O}(\lceil\frac{t}{u}\rceil^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3})
. Each subset sum
x\in\mathcal{S}(A,t)
is contained in
S
with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
k=\lfloor\frac{t}{u}\rfloor
and randomly partition
A
into
2k^{2}
subsets
A=A_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{2k^{2}}
. We compute the set
S:=((A_{1}\cup\{0\})+\dots+(A_{2k^{2}}\cup\{0\}))\cap[0,t]
by
2k^{2}
prefix-restricted sumset computations (
Theorem

1.5
). The running time is indeed bounded by
\widetilde{O}(k^{2}\cdot|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3})
. Concerning the correctness, first note that
S\subseteq\mathcal{S}(A,t)
as claimed. Next, observe that any fixed subset sum
x\in\mathcal{S}(A,t)
can be expressed as the sum of at most
k
distinct elements from
A
, say
x=a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}
. It is easy to check that with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
no two of these
k
elements have been inserted into the same part
A_{i}
. Thus, with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, we have
x\in S
.
∎

D.5
First Level.

There is a Monte Carlo algorithm that, given a multiset
A\subseteq[u,2u]
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, computes a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{S}(A,t)
in time
\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3})
. Each subset sum
x\in\mathcal{S}(A,t)
is contained in
S
with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

First, if
u\leq\frac{t}{2n}
then we can compute
\mathcal{S}(A,t)=\mathcal{S}(A)
in near-linear time by
Theorem

D.1
. Otherwise, let
k=\lfloor\frac{t}{u}\rfloor
and randomly partition
A
into
k
subsets
A=A_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{k}
. Let
t^{\prime}=12\log k\cdot u
. For each
i\leftarrow 1,\dots,k
, we compute a set
S_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(A_{i},t^{\prime})
by
Lemma

D.4
; by repeating this algorithm for
O(\log k)
times, we achieve that each subset sum
x\in\mathcal{S}(A_{i},t^{\prime})
is contained in
S_{i}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{4k}
. We finally compute
S:=((S_{1}\cup\{0\})+\dots+(S_{k}\cup\{0\}))\cap[0,t]
by
k-1
prefix-restricted sumset computations (
Theorem

1.5
) in a binary-tree fashion. Specifically, we compute in levels
\ell\leftarrow\log k,\dots,0
the sets
S_{\ell,0},\dots,S_{\ell,2^{\ell}-1}
defined by
S_{\log k,i}:=S_{i}
at the leaf level, and otherwise
S_{\ell,i}:=(S_{\ell+1,2i}+S_{\ell+1,2i+1})\cap[0,t]
, and ultimately return
S=S_{0,0}
.

For the correctness, note that clearly
S\subseteq\mathcal{S}(A,t)
. Now fix any subset sum
x\in\mathcal{S}(A,t)
. Again,
x
can be expressed as the sum of at most
k
distinct elements from
A
, say
x=a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}
. By a standard argument the probability that any part
A_{i}
contains more than
s:=6\log k
elements from
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
is at most
k\cdot k^{-s}\cdot\binom{k}{s}\leq k\cdot(e/s)^{s}\leq k\cdot 2^{-s}\leq k^{-5}
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-k^{-5}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, the contribution of
a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}
that is mapped to the part
A_{i}
is indeed contained in
S_{i}
, and thus
x\in S
.

We finally consider the running time. In the calls to
Lemma

D.4
, note that
\frac{t^{\prime}}{u}\leq O(\log k)
and therefore the overhead of
\lceil\frac{t^{\prime}}{u}\rceil^{2}
becomes negligible in the running time. For this reason, and by
Theorem

1.5
, we can bound the total running time by
\widetilde{O}(\sum_{\ell,i}|S_{\ell,i}|^{4/3})
. To further bound this by
|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3}
, we distinguish two cases: On the one hand, note that for all levels
\ell\geq\ell^{*}:=\log(24\log k)
we have that
S_{\ell,i}\subseteq[0,t/2]
and therefore the capping with
[0,t]
does not play any role. By
Lemma

D.3
we can thus bound

\sum_{i=0}^{2^{\ell}-1}|S_{\ell,i}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2^{\ell^{*}}-1}|S_{\ell^{*},i}|+2^{\ell}\leq 2^{\ell^{*}}\cdot|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|+k\leq\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|),

using that
k\leq O(n)\leq O(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|)
. On the other hand, all levels
\ell<\ell^{*}
consist of less than
2^{\ell^{*}}\leq O(\log k)
computations, so we can similarly bound
\sum_{i}|S_{\ell,i}|\leq\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|)
. All in all, we can therefore bound the total running time by
\widetilde{O}(\sum_{\ell,i}|S_{\ell,i}|^{4/3})\leq\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3})
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

We face an additional difficulty: We do not know the size
s=|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|
in advance (which is necessary as we have to boost the calls to
Lemma

D.5
). We therefore add one more recursive layer to the algorithm to approximate
s
. Specifically, we arbitrarily partition
A=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
into two halves, and recursively compute
\mathcal{S}(A_{1},\frac{t}{2})
and
\mathcal{S}(A_{2},\frac{t}{2})
. We thereby obtain a good approximation of
s
as, writing
s_{1}=|\mathcal{S}(A_{1},\frac{t}{2})|
and
s_{2}=|\mathcal{S}(A_{2},\frac{t}{2})|
,

s_{1}+s_{2}-1\leq s\leq n^{4}\cdot s_{1}^{4}\cdot s_{2}^{4}.

The first inequality is due to
Lemma

D.2
. For the second inequality we show that any
x\in\mathcal{S}(A,t)
can be expressed as the sum of (i) at most four elements from
A\cap(\frac{t}{4},t]
, plus (ii) at most four subset sums from
\mathcal{S}(A_{1},\frac{t}{2})
, plus (iii) at most four subset sums from
\mathcal{S}(A_{2},\frac{t}{2})
. To see this, note that we can express
x=x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{2}
, where (i)
x_{0}
is the sum of at most four elements from
A\cap(\frac{t}{4},t]
, and (ii)
x_{1}\in\mathcal{S}(A_{1}\cap[0,\frac{t}{4}],t)
and (iii)
x_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(A_{2}\cap[0,\frac{t}{4}])
. Next, express
x_{1}=a_{1}+\dots+a_{\ell}
for some items
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\in A_{1}\cap[0,\frac{t}{4}]
. We peel of the largest prefix sum
x_{1,1}=a_{1}+\dots+a_{j}\leq\frac{t}{2}
, and remark that
x_{1,1}\geq\frac{t}{4}
. We similarly peel of at most three other prefix sums
x_{1,2},x_{1,3},x_{1,4}\in\mathcal{S}(A_{1},\frac{t}{2})
to express
x_{1}=x_{1,1}+x_{1,2}+x_{1,3}+x_{1,4}
. A similar argument shows that
x_{2}
can be expressed as the sum of at most four elements from
\mathcal{S}(A_{2},\frac{t}{2})
.

We proceed as follows. Let
A^{(\ell)}=A\cap(\frac{t}{2^{\ell+1}},\frac{t}{2^{\ell}}]
for
0\leq\ell\leq L:=\lceil\log n\rceil
and
A^{(>L)}=A\cap[0,\frac{t}{2^{L}}]
, and note that
A^{(0)}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A^{(L)}\sqcup A^{(>L)}
forms a partition of
A
. We compute
\mathcal{S}(A^{(>L)},t)=\mathcal{S}(A^{(>L)})
in near-linear time by
Theorem

D.1
. We compute all other sets
\mathcal{S}(A^{(\ell)},t)
by calls to
Lemma

D.5
. By repeating each call
\log(n^{102}\cdot s_{1}^{2}\cdot s_{2}^{2})
times, say, we can compute the correct set
\mathcal{S}(A^{(\ell)},t)
with high probability
1-\frac{1}{n^{101}}
. We combine all sets using
L
prefix-restricted sumset computations (
Theorem

1.5
) to
\mathcal{S}(A,t)=\mathcal{S}(A^{(0)},t)+\dots+\mathcal{S}(A^{(L)},t)+\mathcal{S}(A^{(>L)},t)
. Taking a union bound over the
n
recursive calls, the entire algorithm succeeds with high probability
1-\frac{1}{n^{100}}
. Moreover, similar to before the running time can be bounded by
\widetilde{O}(|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|^{4/3})
, using the fact that in each recursive level the total size of all sets of subset sums is upper bounded by
|\mathcal{S}(A,t)|
(by
Lemma

D.3
).
∎
