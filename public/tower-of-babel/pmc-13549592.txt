Progress in Intra-Arterial Stem Cell Therapy for Ischemic Stroke: A Review
Authors: Nadia McMillan, Roshni Thakkar, Shreyas Kuddannaya, Vishal Chavda, Shashvat Desai, Dileep R. Yavagal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke kills almost 130 000 Americans each year—that is 1 in every 18 deaths. Until 2015, intravenous thrombolysis was the only approved acute reperfusion therapy available but has several limitations in its application. The last decade has witnessed tremendous advances in acute ischemic stroke therapy, particularly with the widespread use of mechanical thrombectomy as a first-line treatment for patients presenting with acute large vessel occlusion. Mechanical thrombectomy is superior to standard medical care about recanalization and has one of the lowest number-needed-to-treat for disability in all of medicine. Yet, only about 1 in 2 patients with large vessel occlusion stroke achieve functional independence. This is due to several patient factors but ultimately results from cell death and limited ability of the brain for repair. Stem cell therapy as an adjunct to intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy in ischemic stroke is currently under investigation in both translational and clinical models to improve recovery. Intra-arterial delivery, in particular, has gained attention because clinicians can readily combine it with thrombectomy to directly target the stroke lesion in a minimally invasive manner. In this review, we summarize the current literature on stem cell therapy for stroke, highlighting translational considerations, delivery challenges, and clinical applications, with particular emphasis on the procedural, safety, and practice implications of intra-arterial administration for neurointervention.

Every year, >795 000 people in the United States have a stroke, and on average, 1 American dies from stroke every 4 minutes. About 610 000 of these are first or new strokes, whereas 1 in 4 are recurrent strokes.1 Despite advances in care including thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for those with large vessel occlusion (LVO), stroke remains a leading cause of disability with significant socioeconomic burden. The risk of stroke, including LVO, increases with age and with the ever-aging population will only continue to increase. With age comes a higher risk of mortality and poorer functional outcomes, including cognitive and motor impairment.2–4

Although the adult brain can sometimes compensate for injury by creating new connections among surviving neurons, it has a limited capacity for self-repair in part because most brain regions lack the ability for neuroplasticity and the stem cells necessary for neuronal regeneration.5 Exogenous stem cell delivery offers a promising strategy to promote neurological recovery and has demonstrated efficacy in both preclinical and early clinical studies. Intra-arterial (IA) administration has garnered attention given the feasibility of combining with thrombectomy in the acute stroke period. This review will focus on highlighting the need for stem cell therapy in stroke, provide a brief introduction to stem cells in the context of stroke treatment, including types of stem cells, dosing, and routes of administration with a focus on IA stem cell delivery, and an updated comprehensive list of ongoing clinical trials shown. Furthermore, this review will introduce ongoing efforts and next steps necessary to bring stem cell therapy to mainstream clinical practice for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke. Stem Cell Therapy for Stroke

Treatment of stroke remains a significant unmet need with a narrow therapeutic window, limited number of eligible patients, and poor access to reperfusion therapies. Intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) is the only Food and Drug Administration–approved therapy for stroke and is limited to 3 to 4.5 hours from symptom onset, whereas MT to recanalize strokes with LVO can be offered out to 24 hours. Both IVT and MT are aimed at reversing neurological injury and preventing further injury; however, <10% of patients presenting with stroke are eligible for IVT and <1% for MT.6 Even if patients meet eligibility criteria, IVT prevents disability in only 6 of 1000 patients. Despite a robust treatment effect of MT, with a number-needed-to-treat of ≈2 to 3 for functional outcomes and rapidly expanding indications, about 1 in 2 patients do not achieve functional independence.7,8 For example, >80% of patients in the MR CLEAN trial (The Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands) did not return to their previous jobs despite successful reperfusion after MT.9 Persistent functional deficits despite recanalization are related to the brain’s limited ability for repair and regeneration in addition to several processes that lead to secondary cell death and stroke expansion beyond the acute stroke period. Emerging therapies in acute stroke are aimed at providing adjunct treatment to IVT or MT, with the potential to extend the window beyond when either is available, and monotherapy for those otherwise ineligible for intervention to promote neurological recovery. Over the last few years, several therapeutic regimens focusing on neuroprotection have been explored but are yet to show substantial benefit in humans.10 Hence, new therapies are required to further improve outcomes after stroke.

Cell therapy is emerging as a promising new modality for enhancing both neuroprotection and neurorecovery in ischemic stroke.11 In the central nervous system (CNS), immune cells, signaling mediators, and counterregulatory mechanisms maintain a state of homeostasis. When this becomes disrupted after a neurological insult such as stroke, medications could theoretically be designed to target a specific signaling pathway to boost recovery. This has been tried and failed in the context of stroke.12,13 Stem cell therapy is unique in that cells can sense cues within their environment, interact, and respond only when a specific array of signals is present and are then able to affect a number of pathways to restore homeostasis. Once optimized, this means cell therapy has the potential to be particularly sensitive and specific, responding in a patient-dependent manner. Indeed, the literature predominates with basic-science studies that have demonstrated positive results in animal models of ischemic stroke after transplantation of progenitor cells derived from various sources, including CNS, adipose, human fetal/embryonic tissue, bone marrow, peripheral, and umbilical cord blood.14

Recent years have seen a push to investigate the potential of cell therapy for stroke and identify current limitations and opportunities in translational research to realize the benefit of stem cell therapy. A growing number of academic publications highlight the global interest and investment in stem cell therapy for stroke.15 The STEPS (Stem Cell Therapies as an Emerging Paradigm in Stroke) meetings have regularly brought together academic and industry leaders and experts from regulatory authorities to discuss the latest developments in cell therapies for stroke and to publish recommendations for preclinical and clinical research. The fourth STEPS meeting aimed to update previous preclinical guidelines with respect to novel stroke models, biomaterials, and advanced approaches combining cell therapies with biomaterials, drugs, or neurorehabilitation.16,17 Furthermore, given the high economic burden of stroke care and increasing costs of stroke care delivery, stem cells present a novel opportunity for a cost-effective treatment from a burden reduction and care delivery standpoint.15,18 To date, preclinical rodent and large animal models have provided the foundation for stem cell therapy in stroke. Unless otherwise specified, the data discussed in the following sections are derived from animal studies, with later discussion directed toward clinical trials (see Clinical Evidence for Stem Cell Therapy for Stroke).

Components of Stem Cell Therapy

The field of stem cell therapy is based on the idea of promoting neuroprotection or repair of injured or dysfunctional tissue with stem cells or their derivatives. The promise of stem cell therapy was first inspired by the success of bone marrow transplantation, which can now be used to treat over 75 diseases, including leukemia and other bone marrow diseases.19 To date, there has been tremendous progress and research to determine the appropriate cell type, optimal dosing, transplantation routes, and the proper time window for stem cell therapies in humans. Correspondingly, several promising clinical studies and randomized clinical trials (RCTs) of stem cell therapies have been completed or are currently ongoing to determine the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of either autologous or allogeneic stem cells in ischemic stroke.20–22 Currently, several stem cell–based therapies for stroke and many other neurological disorders are being tested at the preclinical level and in some clinical trials.

Stem Cell Types

What exactly are stem cells? Embryonic and adult stem cells are defined by their ability for self-renewal, proliferation, and differentiation into multiple cell types. The term often brings to mind human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), derived from the blastocyst stage of development. Because their use involves ethical concerns, procedures with hESCs are strictly regulated. However, other stem cell types exist without the same ethical constraints, including induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)—adult cells reprogrammed to have hESC-like properties—as well as neural stem cells (NSCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). NSCs, MSCs, and HSCs can also be derived from iPSCs or hESCs.

Adult stem cells reside in different organs including the brain, adipose tissue, muscle, liver, skin, pancreas, digestive system, and the cornea. These cells similarly have a capacity for regeneration and plasticity to replace several cell types of a specific tissue.23 MSCs are somatic cells that can be readily harvested from the mesenchymal stroma of nearly all tissues, including bone marrow MSCs, adipose tissue MSCs, umbilical cord blood MSCs, and placenta.24 MSCs express characteristic surface antigens (eg, CD [cluster of differentiation] 105 and CD90 [cluster of differentiation 90], but not CD45 [cluster of differentiation 45] or HLA-DR [human leukocyte antigen – DR isotype]), adhere to plastic, and have the ability to differentiate in vitro into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondroblasts.25 MSCs express low MHC (major histocompatibility complex)-I and lack MHC-II necessary for antigen presentation and, therefore, do not elicit a T-cell response, rendering them nonimmunogenic. Thus, the risk of rejection is low given their relatively immune-privileged status.26,27 MSCs are easily obtained, most often from the bone marrow, and can even be expanded ex vivo for autologous transplantation. A second type of stem cell, CD34+ HSCs, also resides in the bone marrow and gives rise to platelets, red blood cells, and white blood cells. While less studied in stroke, HSCs also serve as a potential therapeutic option. NSCs represent what may be the most suitable cell candidate for neurorestoration in stroke, particularly given their origin from the same tissue source and ability to differentiate into neuronal cells, glia, and oligodendrocytes. NSCs can be reprogrammed from somatic cells or harvested from their stem cell niches, the subventricular zone or the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus.28,29 Whether one cell type is better than another remains unclear and likely depends on various factors.

Selection of Optimal Cell Type as Therapy

The choice of stem cell is determined by cell origin, expression of certain markers, potential for integration, and host rejection. Size of stem cells also represents a technical challenge unique to the tissue target and delivery method. For example, risk of microvascular occlusion and secondary strokes during IA delivery of stem cells due to either stem cell size or clumping of cells is a concern. Stem cell diameters range from 4 to 6 µm for HSCs, 13 to 15 µm for NSCs, and over 25 µm for MSCs. Despite the larger size of MSCs, Yavagal et al30 confirmed that low-concentration MSCs retain their favorable features without an increase in microvascular occlusion–related complications after IA injection. MSCs are perhaps the most widely used type of stem cells in stroke and other diseases because of accessibility, lack of ethical concerns, ease of procurement, ability for production on a large scale, favorable safety profile, and multilineage potential.31 When considering the safety profile, the nonimmunogenic status is an important feature because immunosuppression is not needed and the side effects associated with immunosuppressive medications are avoided. Although MSCs are considered safe, there is a little possibility for tumor growth due to the production of cytokines, which can cause tumor cells to grow and spread.32 However, MSCs derived from adult tissue carry a lower tumorigenicity risk than pluripotent stem cells.27 Their favorable safety profile and accessibility make MSCs a practical and promising therapeutic option in preclinical models of ischemic stroke.

NSCs, however, have been used in <10% of clinical trials, with the largest number of trials registered between 2018 and 2020.33 Limited use may reflect regulatory barriers or procurement challenges, as NSCs reside in deep brain regions that complicate autologous harvesting. NSCs can be induced from hESCs, raising ethical concerns,34 or from iPSCs, discussed below. Despite slow progress, NSCs continue to hold promise for repair in stroke due to their inherent ability to differentiate into neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and to promote axonal growth and remyelination.35,36 A meta-analysis of preclinical models of neurological injury demonstrated greater behavioral improvement with NSCs compared with other cell types, indicating NSCs may be the preferred stem cell type for stroke therapy.37 The safety profile of NSCs is also favorable, with minimal risk of tumorgenicity, making them an excellent choice from a safety standpoint.38

Similar to MSCs, iPSCs can be produced from several different cell types such as fibroblasts, keratinocytes, myoblasts, and hepatocytes. iPSCs are relatively new and less studied compared with MSCs but have already been used in many preclinical studies, and several ongoing clinical trials are investigating the use of iPSCs for neurological disorders such as Alzheimer disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Parkinson disease.39,40 Major concerns include immune rejection and tumorigenicity, particularly with the use of retroviruses for reprogramming, although other methods have been introduced.41,42 Lastly, bone marrow mononuclear cells (BM-MNCS) are thought to be a rich source of HSCs and are often used in experimental treatment of ischemic injury. A small number of BM-MNCs differentiate into endothelial cells, but the major mechanism of action is proposed secretion of proangiogenic factors.43 HSCs and BM-MNCs can be readily isolated and transplanted in an autologous manner and have a good safety profile. BM-MNCs have demonstrated clinical efficacy by inducing neovascularization and angiogenesis in patients with myocardial infarction44 or limb ischemia;44 however, more complex processes, such as neuroplasticity and neurogenesis, are required for CNS repair. Although the optimal stem cell source for stroke remains undefined, both NSCs and MSCs show strong potential to protect and repair injured CNS tissue and to modulate poststroke immune responses, as our group has previously reviewed in detail.45

Stem Cell Mechanism of Action

Over the past 2 decades, preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated the safety and efficacy of stem cell therapy for functional recovery in animal models of stroke, encompassing a variety of stem cell types and modes of administration. Before going into detail regarding the role of stem cells in neuromodulation, it is important to understand the changes that occur in the brain and periphery after ischemia. After an ischemic event, inflammatory cells rapidly infiltrate the brain from the spleen, bone marrow, and gut.46 The inflammatory CNS infiltrate includes mononuclear/macrophage cells, lymphocytes, and neutrophils, in addition to local activation of microglia and astrocytes, with peak levels seen between 24 and 72 hours after stroke onset. These cells release inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and matrix metalloproteases, which drive secondary cell injury, edema, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and expansion of final infarct volume. Systemic inflammation after a stroke is known to significantly impact both the short-term and long-term outcomes and has become a crucial target for interventions to reduce injury poststroke.47,48 As such, several studies in recent years have highlighted the immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties of stem cells, which is the basis for using stem cells to limit injury and promote recovery in the acute-subacute period after stroke.

Complex processes are necessary to restore function after stroke. Several preclinical studies suggest that transplanted stem cells retain capacity to replace lost neurons and may even integrate into existing circuitry.49–52 However, a more robust effect on poststroke recovery is through the effect on multiple parallel processes that modulate the tissue microenvironment. Stem cells decrease peripheral invasion of inflammatory cells into the brain and shift the inflammatory response both in the periphery, through interactions with the spleen, and within the CNS by triggering an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as TGF (transforming growth factor)-β and IL (interleukin)-10 and a corresponding decrease in proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF (tumor necrosis factor)-α. In addition, stem cells release trophic growth factors such as IGF (insulin growth factor), BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), and VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) and decrease the release of reactive oxygen species, resulting in decreased secondary cell injury and, as a result, decreased final stroke burden.53 Additional mechanisms of action include reduced apoptosis, promotion of axonal outgrowth and synaptic remodeling, neurogenesis, angiogenesis, regulation of cerebral blood flow and blood-brain barrier permeability, and activation of endogenous restorative processes. These effects by which stem cells trigger neuroprotection, cell regeneration, and neurogenesis without direct cell replacement are termed what is known as the stem cell bystander effect.54,55 Clinical trials have confirmed a similar reduction in inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1-β in patients with stroke after stem cell delivery.56 However, comparable stem cell–mediated processes have not been demonstrated unequivocally in humans.57

Timing of Cell Delivery

No clearly defined time windows currently guide cell therapy. Trials of the 2 successful treatments in stroke, IVT and MT, were initially unsuccessful related to late reperfusion,58–61 demonstrating the importance of identifying the ideal time frame for intervention. When considering the ideal time for stem cell therapy, accounting for the targeted mechanism is important, and this varies depending on the phase of stroke. In the acute phase, attention to neuroprotection through secretion of trophic factors and modulation of inflammation is important, whereas in the chronic phase, the goal is neurorestoration of stable deficits.62 Moreover, chemokine levels—important for stem cell homing to injured areas—are increased in the acute phase, but decrease with time63 affecting stem cell transfer to the lesion site. During this early window, local repair processes are also active and may facilitate stem cell growth, survival, differentiation, or integration.64 Since the majority of brain plasticity mechanisms take place during the acute or subacute phases of a stroke, cell therapy may be less effective during the chronic stroke phase where the formation of scar tissue and the restoration of the blood-brain barrier might interfere with therapeutic effects.65

Given the pathophysiology of secondary neuronal injury and the known mechanisms of stem cell–mediated effects, the acute and subacute phases likely represent the ideal time to initiate cell therapy to attenuate the inflammatory cascade and provide trophic support to preserve vulnerable neurons. Preclinical work from our own laboratory indicates that administration of cells at 24 hours after stroke is more beneficial when compared with 1 hour.30,66 Cells transplanted within 48 hours of stroke survive at higher rates than those delivered at 6 weeks, suggesting the acute phase of stroke is the ideal time for transplantation, although benefit in terms of cell migration, neuronal differentiation, and proliferation is also seen at later time points.67 In a systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical effects of NSCs on stroke, Chen et al68 suggested NSCs should be administered within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours of stroke for best functional results. Similarly, a meta-analysis and review of BMSCs (bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells) comparing administration in the hyperacute (0–6 hours), acute (12–24 hours), subacute (2–7 days), and chronic (>7 days) time points found outcomes were consistently greatest when BM-MSCs were administered before 7 days. Administration between 0 and 6 hours showed improved sensorimotor outcomes, while 2 to 7 days resulted in better composite score outcomes. These results suggest MSC administration within the first week of stroke onset is the most beneficial, with a preference towards earlier time points.69 Mays et al70 corroborated these findings using intravenous delivery of MultiStem, a proprietary human stem cell product, in rat models of stroke and found a functional benefit when cells were administered in a window between 1 and 7 days after stroke. No improvement was seen in intravenous administration of MSCs at 30 days poststroke, whereas functional improvement was demonstrated at poststroke days 1 and 7.71 Similarly, intravenous bone marrow cells decreased stroke lesion size between 3 and 72 hours, with greater reduction seen when cells were administered earlier.72 Most preclinical studies recommend transplanting MSCs within the first 48 hours after acute stroke.73 These studies suggest greater benefit with stem cell delivery in the acute/subacute phase of stroke with a preference towards earlier time points; however, robust high-quality comparative studies evaluating optimal timing are still lacking.

Delivery Route

Multiple modes of stem cell delivery exist, including IA, intravenous, intracerebral, intracisternal/cerebroventricular, intrathecal, and intranasal. Each has its advantages and disadvantages, due to availability, technical difficulty, versatility, invasiveness, and effect on cell biodistribution within the stroke lesion. Although each delivery method has demonstrated promise in preclinical studies, no single route has emerged as clearly superior. IA delivery has gained increasing attention, particularly in the modern era of MT for acute ischemic stroke due to LVO. Because endovascular access is already established in these patients, IA administration provides a practical opportunity for targeted cell delivery to the affected vascular territory. IA approaches have been evaluated across acute, subacute, and chronic stroke phases and offer procedural flexibility, particularly in the acute setting. Promising phase 2 IA trials are expected to advance to phase 3, supporting its potential as an optimal delivery strategy.75 Intravenous delivery remains the most common and least invasive method. It is simple to perform, requires only minutes to prepare, and has been studied across all temporal stages of stroke. Intravenous administration has also been the first route to reach phase 3 clinical trials.76 Its favorable safety profile and procedural ease make it an attractive option, particularly in the acute phase when patients may be receiving thrombolysis or anticoagulation.77 Intracerebral delivery involves stereotactic injection directly into brain tissue under general anesthesia. Although this method allows precise targeting of the lesion, it is more invasive and requires prolonged operating room time. In addition, despite focal delivery, cell biodistribution within the injured tissue is limited.78 Due to its invasiveness and potential hemorrhagic risk, intracerebral administration has primarily been investigated in subacute and chronic stroke populations.79 Intrathecal delivery is performed via lumbar puncture under local anesthesia. Although less invasive than intracerebral injection, it carries procedural risks such as infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, or nerve injury, though serious complications are uncommon. Similar to intracerebral approaches, intrathecal delivery has mainly been studied in subacute and chronic settings.78,80,81 Despite extensive investigation, no consensus has been reached regarding a gold standard delivery route. Comparative studies are needed to clarify how the method of administration influences biological mechanisms and clinical outcomes across different stages of stroke.

Intravenous Versus IA Route

To directly compare the effectiveness of intravenous versus IA stem cell delivery in stroke, it is crucial to examine findings from both preclinical and clinical studies. Preclinical research consistently demonstrates that both intravenous and IA stem cell delivery reduces infarct size and improves neurological function in animal models of stroke.72 However, the route of delivery produces distinct patterns of cell distribution. After intravenous delivery, only about 4% of delivered MSCs reach the brain lesion, as most MSCs become sequestered to peripheral organs such as the liver, kidney, spleen, and the lungs.82 Intravenously delivered NSCs show a similar pattern, with most NSCs migrating to peripheral organs rather than the brain.83 On the contrary, IA delivery infuses stem cells directly into the arterial system supplying the affected brain region, typically via the ipsilateral internal carotid artery or middle cerebral artery (MCA). This approach provides a more concentrated84 and uniform distribution of stem cells directly to the site of injury, bypassing peripheral organs that sequester intravenously administered cells. Li et al85 showed that 21% of MSCs injected IA in the carotid artery distributed in the MCA territory with a survival rate of 8.6% at 14 days after MCA occlusion in adult rat models. Additional animal studies demonstrate higher rates of stem cell engraftment in ischemic brain tissue with IA compared with intravenous administration.86,87 Injection of technetium-99 m labeled BM-MNCs intravenously and IA into patients with subacute stroke demonstrated higher radioactive counts in the liver and spleen and low counts in the lungs in the IA group compared with intravenous transplantation, in which cells were largely localized to the lung. When BMSCs were delivered IA, intravenously, and intracerebrally, better and faster neurological outcomes were seen in the IA group compared with the other routes in rat MCA occlusion models.88 Similarly, BMMCs (bone marrow mononuclearcells) administered IA immediately after reperfusion in rat models of focal ischemia resulted in smaller infarct volume, whereas intravenous delivery did not produce the same effect. The authors attributed the neuroprotective effect in the IA group to the large number of transplanted cells in the brain during the early stage of reperfusion.89 Collectively, these findings demonstrate route-dependent differences in cell distribution that influence outcomes after stroke.90

Importantly, peripherally delivered cells still reduce inflammation, edema formation, and apoptosis after stroke, despite peripheral trapping of cells. In splenectomized rats, however, this protective effect disappears, suggesting that modulation of splenic inflammation contributes to recovery.91 These results indicate that components of the first-pass effect may confer benefit. Although most peripherally administered stem cells fail to cross the blood-brain barrier, secretion of paracrine factors—exosomes, bioactive substances, trophic factors, and cytokines—and interactions with the peripheral immune system work to limit injury after stroke.73

As MT plays an expanding role in the treatment of ischemic stroke, there is a strong focus on the utility of IA stem cell administration to improve outcomes while minimizing additional procedures. Future approaches may combine IA thrombectomy and stem cell delivery using the same catheter system, potentially improving patient outcomes while streamlining care.93 Taken together, these data support the authors’ view that IA delivery represents a particularly promising strategy for stem cell therapy in stroke, given its targeted distribution, dose efficiency, and compatibility with existing endovascular workflows.

Optimal Dose

Preclinical studies report wide dose ranges across delivery routes: intravenous doses range from 5.0 × 106≈ 1.2 × 109 cells, IA from 1.0 × 106≈ 9.3 × 108 cells, and intracerebral doses range from 2.0 × 106≈ 7.2 × 107 cells. Yet, the optimal dose of cell therapy depending on delivery route remains undefined. Determining the ideal dose requires balancing therapeutic efficacy with safety, particularly minimizing risks such as microvascular occlusion and other cell-related adverse effects. Although it is easy to imagine that with each increase in dose, a bigger effect is seen and the only limiting factor might be side effects, it is more likely that within a range of cell number, the effect does not change in a linear fashion.94 The optimal cell dose will maximize clinical benefit while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

Yang et al95 sought to evaluate the ideal dose of BM-MNCs delivered intravenously. Rats received an intravenous infusion of autologous BM-MNCs at varying doses (1 million, 10 million, or 30 million cells/kg) 24 hours poststroke. They found a greater reduction in neurological deficits with doses >1 million cells/kg or, rather, the groups that received either 10 or 30 million cells/kg had a maximum reduction in deficits. Notably, rats treated at 72 hours but not at 7 days showed a significant reduction in neurological deficits. Yavagal et al30 similarly sought to identify the maximum tolerated dose of IA-injected MSCs that would not compromise blood flow while maintaining efficacy. The investigators administered deescalating doses (1×106, 5×105, 2×105, 1×105, and 5×104 cells) and measured ipsilateral MCA flow with laser Doppler. They identified 1×105 cells as the maximum tolerated dose, as this dose preserved MCA flow, improved neurological performance, and reduced stroke lesion volume compared with controls. In clinical studies, doses have ranged from 2 million to 500 million cells depending on route of administration, stem cell type, and trial phase, with higher doses typically used in peripheral delivery. In the PISCES-I dose-escalation trial (Pilot Investigation of Stem Cells in Stroke I), investigators delivered intracerebral injections of an immortalized NSC line at escalating doses starting at 2 million cells up to 20 million cells (2, 5, 10, or 20 million cells). No adverse events were seen with delivery of up to 20 million cells.104 In a different phase 1/2a trial, doses of 2, 5, and 10 million BM-MSCs were implanted in patients with chronic stroke.105 Although some functional improvement was seen at 2-year follow-up, the doses used were 5×- to 10×-fold less than the equivalent dose demonstrated to be efficacious in rat models of stroke. For example, 200 000 cells administered intracerebrally improved outcomes in rats; however, Borlongan94 calculated the equivalent dose in humans as 56 million cells, which could explain limited efficacy in some clinical trials.106 Similarly, 4 million cells delivered intravenously has demonstrated benefit in rats, which would roughly translate to 840 million cells in an average patient with stroke.17 Allometric scaling to extrapolate doses between species has been proposed.107 Stricter adherence to lessons learned in preclinical studies when designing clinical trials may improve outcomes and accelerate the translational process. Future trials should conduct systematic dose-escalation designs across delivery routes, paired with predefined biomarker end points, to identify the optimal therapeutic dosing.

Clinical Evidence for Stem Cell Therapy for Stroke

Stem cell therapy for stroke has evolved from promising preclinical efficacy to early phase safety-focused clinical trials, and more recently to randomized studies testing functional outcomes. Preclinical studies consistently demonstrate benefit in animal models, although many are heterogeneous in design, use small samples, lack blinding, or rely on surrogate end points. In contrast, definitive functional benefit in patients with stroke has not yet been established.75 Meta-analyses of prior clinical trials suggest potential benefit, yet individual RCTs have not confirmed this. For example, in a meta-analysis and review of clinical studies, improvement in disability measures (Barthel Index, modified Rankin Scale [mRS]) was seen in 11 of 14 studies without a comparator arm and in 5 out of 13 cases in those with a comparator at 6 months. Importantly, safety has been consistently demonstrated. Reported adverse effects were few, self-limited, or resolved, and no increase in tumorigenicity was noted.108 Among comparator-arm studies, most used intravenous delivery of autologous stem cells, but outcomes varied: Bang et al109 reported positive results with MSCs in the chronic phase (5–9 weeks) at 1-year follow-up, Prasad et al110 found negative results in the subacute phase (7–30 days), and Bhasin et al111 showed mixed results in the chronic phase (3 months–2 years). Another meta-analysis of 9 trials from 1995 to 2016 with comparison arms similarly demonstrated a positive effect of cell therapy on outcomes.112 Clinical trials of stem cells in ischemic stroke from 2005 to 2025 are shown in Table 2.

Stem Cell Clinical Trials for Ischemic Stroke

To date, >40 RCTs have evaluated the effect of stem cells in patients with stroke. Early clinical investigations focused on the feasibility and safety of intracerebral neuronal transplantation in chronic basal ganglia stroke. Neuronal cells derived from a human teratocarcinoma were stereotactically implanted into the brains of patients whose strokes had occurred between 6 months and 6 years prior. The results showed no adverse serological or imaging effects, and the European Stroke Scale total score improved in 6 patients, with a mean improvement of 2.9 points. In addition, 6 out of 11 positron emission tomography scans at 6 months posttransplantation indicated increased glucose metabolic activity in the tissue surrounding the stroke, correlating positively with motor performance improvements. One patient died 27 months after transplantation from a myocardial infarction, and the postmortem autopsy revealed donor-derived neurons in the graft site. These studies demonstrated feasibility and safety of human neuronal cell transplantation in patients with basal ganglia infarction, survival of transplanted cells, and the potential for benefit.113–116 However, in a separate phase 1 trial, fetal porcine cells delivered intracerebrally in 5 patients with basal ganglia stroke resulted in neurological worsening after 3 weeks in one patient, and another experienced a seizure 1 week after injection, prompting the Food and Drug Administration to stop the trial early.117 Similar side effects have not been demonstrated in subsequent trials.

Subsequent trials shifted toward regulated, protocol-driven investigations. PISCES-I/II was the first fully regulated clinical trial to study the effects of NSCs in patients with stroke in which human NSCs were delivered by intracerebral implantation in the chronic phase poststroke. The study’s primary end point was a 2-point improvement in the grasping and lifting test of the Action Research Arm Test at 3 months posttreatment. Improvement in upper limb function was seen at 3, 6, and 12 months in those with baseline upper extremity deficits.78 PISCES III (URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT03629275) is an ongoing phase 2b trial designed to identify clinically relevant measures of upper extremity function and quality of life with specific measures to evaluate for sensorimotor improvement.118

In the acute setting, the MASTERS trial (Safety and Efficacy of Multipotent Adult Progenitor Cells in Acute Ischemic Stroke) evaluated intravenous multipotent adult progenitor cells delivery within 24 to 36 hours (later extended to 48 hours) from stroke but failed to show any difference in the primary outcome of global stroke recovery at 90 days. However, post hoc analysis demonstrated significant improvement in mRS scores and other clinical outcomes in the early (<36 hours) group when those who received IVT or MT were excluded, suggesting that patients receiving intravenous BMSCs early benefited from the treatment. Consistent with preclinical data, serum proinflammatory cytokines were reduced, supporting a neuroimmunomodulatory response in humans and the translational relevance of prior animal studies.56 Similarly, the TREASURE study (Treatment Evaluation of Acute Stroke Using Regenerative Cells), a phase 2/3 RCT, evaluated intravenous administration of MultiStem, a bone marrow–derived, allogeneic, multipotent adult progenitor cell therapy within 18 to 36 hours of ischemic stroke onset, and demonstrated safety but no significant improvements in the primary analysis. Better outcomes were seen in the subgroup analysis of patients with infarct volumes of ≥50 mL and those aged ≤64 years.103 This may reflect the natural recovery trajectory of small strokes, which can obscure treatment effects, as well as age-related limitations in neural repair capacity.

Interest then expanded to IA delivery to enhance cerebral targeting. The first investigation utilizing IA delivery evaluated the effect of BM-MNCs in 10 patients presenting with MCA stroke with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ≥8 and who were able to be treated within 5 to 9 days. In this phase I/II trial, there was no change in outcomes, but there was a trend towards better outcomes as measured by the Barthel Index when higher numbers of CD34+ cells were used (P=0.09). Interestingly, BM-MNC significantly increased β-nerve growth factor levels during the first week after BM-MNC treatment compared with controls, supporting a trophic support mechanism consistent with preclinical studies.97 In a separate study, Friedrich et al98 evaluated the effect of IA transplanted autologous BM-MNCs in patients between 3 and 7 days after moderate to severe stroke. At 90 days, 8 patients (40%) demonstrated a good clinical outcome defined as mRS score ≤2, and 6 (30%) patients achieved satisfactory clinical improvement defined by mRS score and preinjection National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score. Without a control arm, conclusions are limited, but, yet again, the feasibility and safety of IA stem cell injection was demonstrated and suggested a potentially positive effect.

More recently, the RECOVER-Stroke trial similarly demonstrated the safety of IA infusion of autologous bone marrow–derived aldehyde dehydrogenase-bright stem cells in patients with subacute ischemic stroke (2–3 weeks), although there was a higher incidence of asymptomatic small lesions on brain magnetic resonance imaging in the treatment group. Although there was no difference in the primary efficacy end point of 90-day mRS score between the groups, the study provided a framework for the design and conduct of future IA cell therapy trials in stroke.75 The largest IA clinical trial was the IBIS phase II clinical trial (Intra-Arterial BM-MNCs Transplantation in Acute Ischemic Stroke) conducted in Spain. Based on prior meta-analysis that demonstrated a possible dose–response with administration of a higher cell dose being more efficacious, the investigators for IBIS sought to evaluate the effect of different doses of IA-delivered bone marrow mononuclear cells. Patients presenting with MCA stroke within 1 to 7 days from onset were assigned to 1 of 2 doses (2×106 BM-MNCs/kg or 5×106 BM-MNCs/kg) versus control. The primary efficacy outcome was the proportion of patients with mRS scores of 0 to 2 at 180 days. Although safety was demonstrated, no difference in functional outcomes at 180 days was seen. Although the primary efficacy end point was not statistically significant, the authors note the proportion of patients with a good outcome was 20% in the BM-MNC group versus none in the control group, and a higher number of patients with better mRS score, Barthel Index score, and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score outcomes in both BM-MNC groups. Moreover, end points appeared more pronounced in the low-dose group compared with the high-dose group, raising the question of whether a dose-response curve exists in patients with stroke, though differences in pretreatment thrombectomy may have contributed to the effect.119 Table 3 summarizes clinical trials evaluating IA stem cell delivery in stroke. Overall, stem cell therapy in stroke has progressed from feasibility and safety demonstration to randomized trials exploring efficacy. Although safety across delivery routes is well established, consistent functional benefit remains unproven. In particular, relatively few adequately powered RCTs have evaluated IA delivery, highlighting a critical gap in high-quality evidence and the need for rigorously designed efficacy trials with standardized end points.

Despite promising preclinical results, several factors likely contribute to the translational roadblock, including timing of stem cell delivery, route of delivery, patient selection, stem cell preparation, cell type, and patient selection. When considering the latter, it is important to remember that clinical populations are inherently heterogeneous when compared with highly controlled preclinical studies in which variables can be easily controlled, and this variability strongly influences outcomes. For example, age is important when considering that younger patients tend to have better outcomes after stroke due to several factors such as coexisting medical conditions and brain plasticity.120 In addition, time to treatment and concomitant IVT or MT are important variables. If preclinical studies are telling, additional clinical trials conducted in the acute time period are likely to demonstrate a benefit. In addition, patients presenting with LVO who receive MT are expected to recover better than their counterparts who do not receive MT, making it challenging to detect the efficacy of cell therapy due to ceiling effects. Conversely, in the absence of MT, impaired or absent blood flow may limit delivery of cells to the ischemic lesion. Carefully designed, controlled clinical trials are, therefore, necessary to define which patient populations are most likely to derive meaningful benefit from cell therapy.

Endovascular Techniques for IA Stem Cell Delivery

IA injection is commonly performed under monitored anesthesia care or generalized anesthesia followed by arterial access of either the femoral or radial artery, then catheter selection of the carotid or vertebral artery ipsilateral to the stroke lesion. Existing devices used in MT can be used for cell delivery, with procedure time ranging from as little as 30 minutes. Despite multiple clinical trials evaluating IA stem cell therapy, not much is said about the optimal endovascular technique for IA cell delivery. This is an important consideration given the potential for complications such as microembolism, hemorrhage, and decreased efficacy with inappropriate cell delivery. Therefore, both safety and efficacy should be considered for the optimal techniques. The endovascular techniques performed in recent studies were not specific to stem cell delivery. At this time, selected arteries are accessed safely with existing devices for vascular access and vessel selection. As discussed, multiple factors influence safe delivery of cells such as cell type and size, dose, and speed of delivery. From these perspectives, over 10 clinical studies have evaluated IA cell delivery in ischemic stroke. Not all studies detail the technical methods, but commonly reported variables include microcatheter, selection of injecting artery, and infusion methods. Large inner-diameter microcatheters, such as SL1018 (Distal ID 0.019 in: Boston Scientific, Target Therapeutics) or Echelon10 (Distal ID 0.017in: Covidien, Irvine, CA), were used.92,121,122 The selected injecting artery was either proximal MCA (M1) or ipsilateral terminal internal carotid artery.6,92 Infusion time varies based on the artery selected, with infusion time ranging from 10 to 30 minutes for the MCA and 2 to 3 minutes for the internal carotid artery.23,123 Intraprocedure administration of heparin was not consistently reported. In our practice, we do not administer heparin during acute stroke procedures; however, this is a nuanced discussion and can be considered on a case-by-case basis. It is worth reiterating that when these measures are taken, no major complications related to IA delivery have been reported in prior clinical studies. Furthermore, a recent brain cancer study shows injecting MSCs into more distal arteries such as M2 branches or the posterior cerebral artery is feasible20 and may allow more targeted delivery while minimizing systemic exposure. These data support the feasibility and safety of IA cell delivery across centers.

Challenges to Translate IA Stem Cell Therapy

Despite the above-mentioned preclinical and clinical trials for stem cells in stroke, there remain several questions that need to be addressed. Challenges to advancing the field of stem cells for stroke recovery include the need for additional clinical studies evaluating the ideal time frame for cell delivery, cell dose, and cell type. Animal model findings often fail to translate directly to humans, as has been demonstrated across several different specialties and several different fields, necessitating the need for large, well-designed clinical trials. This may partly reflect the greater regenerative potential of healthy young animals compared with older patients with comorbidities who more commonly present with stroke.124 These challenges in translation are now being addressed with the use of large animal models, which allow for more accurate testing of therapies. Unique challenges associated with IA delivery of stem cells include optimization of the rate of delivery and size of cells injected to balance the risk of microvascular occlusion with cell transport.

Moreover, we have described a complex inflammatory response after stroke that includes a number of cell types, including microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, inflammatory cells, cytokines, and growth factors. Stem cells are poised to respond to all these factors in a patient-specific manner to reduce the inflammatory response and promote recovery. To date, studies have been largely descriptive and have not rigorously examined stem cell–immune cross-talk. To fully harness cell therapy, investigators must define the key signaling pathways between stem cells and immune cells in the poststroke environment. Future randomized, placebo-controlled trials incorporating systematic immune profiling will be essential to clarify mechanisms and refine therapeutic timing and dosing. With this mechanistic foundation, stem cell therapies could emerge as adjuncts to standard stroke care to enhance neuroprotection.

Future Directions

The translation of stem cell–based interventions from experimental promise to clinical reality in stroke medicine will require a far more structured and mechanistically informed research agenda than has been applied to date. Although early phase IA and intravenous studies have demonstrated feasibility and general safety, the field remains limited by heterogeneous trial designs, inconsistent dosing, varied cell types, and a lack of standardized biological end points. To overcome these limitations, next-generation RCTs must adopt rigorous, harmonized frameworks that integrate mechanistic understanding with pragmatic clinical feasibility. A crucial first step is designing studies that intentionally test timing of administration across discrete stroke phases: hyperacute (<6 hours), acute (6–24 hours), subacute (1–7 days), and chronic (>7 days). Preclinical work suggests greater receptivity to trophic support and inflammatory modulation during the earlier windows, with several studies demonstrating boosted cell survival and engraftment when cells are delivered within 48 hours of insult.74 For these reasons, the acute-to-subacute interval (6–24 hours) may represent an especially promising period in which to assess efficacy, allowing sufficient stabilization for safe intervention while capturing a window of heightened neuroplasticity and chemokine-mediated homing signals.

The increasing ubiquity of MT provides an opportunity to embed cell-based delivery into established workflows. IA infusion after reperfusion could enable precise delivery directly to the affected vascular territory while minimizing additional procedural burden. Such integration also avoids the limitations of intravenous administration, including first-pass pulmonary sequestration, while reducing the logistical uncertainties associated with staging a separate endovascular procedure. Moreover, IA access during thrombectomy offers the potential to coordinate dose-finding strategies in a controlled setting, where immediate hemodynamic feedback and imaging can be used to ensure vascular safety. Because both excessive dose and cell size can compromise microvascular flow, careful dose-escalation schemas are essential for defining dose ceilings that preserve perfusion while maximizing therapeutic potency, as preclinical data have suggested a nonlinear dose-response profile with potential benefits at lower doses.74 Harmonizing these dose-finding efforts across multiple RCTs will be indispensable for ensuring comparability and meta-analytic aggregation in the future.

Beyond procedural frameworks, the selection of a lead cellular product is a pivotal challenge. The current proliferation of cell types MSCs, NSCs, bone marrow mononuclear cells, iPSC-derived progenitors has fragmented the evidence base and prevented sequential RCTs from building on one another. Focusing development around 1 or 2 lead cell types (ie, NSCS and MSCS) would promote consistency in trial methodology, allow dose optimization across studies, and accelerate regulatory pathways. Regardless of the chosen cell type, future trials should shift beyond descriptive safety profiles toward mechanistically anchored end points. Functional measures, such as the mRS, will remain essential, but pairing them with quantitative imaging biomarkers including functional magnetic resonance imaging, perfusion-weighted imaging, diffusion tensor imaging, and connectome mapping will help capture subtler improvements in network reorganization and circuit-level recovery.125 Traditional imaging metrics, such as final infarct volume, may not sufficiently predict therapeutic response, as preclinical work indicates functional gains may occur even without major changes in stroke size.126 Thus, connectivity-based biomarkers may be more discriminating indicators of neurorestorative effects.

Parallel measurement of systemic and central cytokine profiles should serve as a second pillar of outcome assessment. Given the proposed immunomodulatory mechanisms of stem cell action, quantifying changes in inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and anti-inflammatory mediators (eg, IL-10, TGF-β) before and after therapy will strengthen the biological plausibility of any observed clinical benefit. Although preclinical and early human studies demonstrate reductions in proinflammatory cytokines and increases in trophic growth factors after administration, these findings remain inconsistent across trials and have not been validated at scale in humans. Embedding robust cytokine analyses into RCTs will clarify whether the anticipated immunologic signals translate reliably across patient populations.

The field must also confront substantial gaps in mechanistic understanding. Progress will depend heavily on sustained basic-science investigations that dissect the signaling networks and intercellular interactions governing stem cell behavior in the poststroke milieu. Stem cell responses are shaped by complex cross-talk with microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, and peripheral immune cells, and unraveling these interactions is critical for developing more potent, targeted therapies. This knowledge may support the development of bioengineered or genetically enhanced stem cells, optimized to secrete specific combinations of trophic factors, chemokines, or cargo-bearing vesicles that promote synaptic remodeling, angiogenesis, and circuit reintegration. In addition, combinations of stem cells with complementary biologics such as growth factors, neuroprotective small molecules, or biomaterial scaffolds may amplify the therapeutic effect beyond what either modality can achieve alone.74

Increasing attention is being directed toward cell-free therapeutic platforms, particularly extracellular vesicles and exosomes, which can recapitulate many of the paracrine and immunomodulatory effects of live-cell therapies. Extracellular vesicles carry microRNAs, proteins, and bioactive lipids capable of modifying cellular behavior and may offer superior scalability, storage stability, and safety by obviating the risks of cell embolization or malignant transformation. Early investigations of extracellular vesicle–based treatments in stroke are underway, focusing on feasibility, preliminary efficacy, and immunologic responses. Although the same translational challenges that hinder cell therapies dose, timing, delivery route, and patient selection also apply to extracellular vesicle platforms, their inherent scalability and safety profile position them as a compelling next frontier.74

Finally, patient selection must be refined. Variations in stroke subtype, lesion location, collateral status, age, and comorbidities may strongly influence treatment responsiveness. Patients with preserved structural connectivity or favorable network reserve may derive greater benefit from neurorestorative therapies, underscoring the importance of advanced magnetic resonance imaging techniques capable of mapping structural and functional pathways.127 Identifying biological or radiographic predictors of responsiveness will help personalize cell-based interventions and avoid exposing low-likelihood responders to unnecessary procedural risk. Taken together, these efforts define a clear roadmap for the next phase of translational progress: harmonized RCT designs, integrated thrombectomy-based delivery workflows, mechanistically grounded end points, advances in basic biology, and exploration of next-generation cell-free therapeutics. As these components converge, the field will be positioned to determine whether stem cell–based interventions can genuinely and reproducibly reshape the trajectory of stroke recovery.

Summary

Significant barriers remain before stem cell therapy can transition from bench to bedside or into widespread clinical practice for the treatment of stroke. As of now, at least 5 ongoing clinical trials are aiming to answer some of the questions raised in this review (ClinicalTrials.gov). The main ones are the optimum time for delivery, ideal cell type, dose, and patient population who would benefit from IA cell therapy. In addition, although we raised the option of combination thrombectomy+stem cells to minimize the number of procedures a patient would need, those without LVO are not excluded from the potential benefits. Rigorous clinical testing will be needed to answer these questions and identify patients in which the risk of a procedure outweighs the benefit. Once future trials address these questions, stem cell therapy may fundamentally reshape stroke treatment.
