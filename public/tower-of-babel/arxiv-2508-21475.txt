MMSearch-Plus: A Simple Yet Challenging Benchmark for Multimodal Browsing Agents
Xijia Tao, Yihua Teng, Xinxing Su, Xinyu Fu, Jihao Wu, Chaofan Tao, Ziru Liu, Haoli Bai, Rui Liu, Lingpeng Kong

Abstract
Large multimodal language models (MLLMs) are increasingly deployed as web agents, yet many multimodal browsing benchmarks can be solved by shallow, fixed workflows that lean on high-recall image search and nearby text—masking the genuinely multimodal challenges of fine-grained visual reasoning, provenance verification, and long-horizon tool use. We introduce MMSearch-Plus , a benchmark of 311 tasks that highly demands multimodal understanding while preserving the difficulty profile of strong text-only browsing suites. Each item is constructed to contain multiple weak, localized visual signals that must be extracted, propagated through iterative text–image search, and cross-validated under retrieval noise before answering. Our curation procedure, Spatial–Temporal Extrapolation , seeds questions whose answers require extrapolating from spatial cues (micro-text, part-level appearance, layouts, signage) and temporal traces (broadcast overlays, seasonal context) to out-of-image facts such as events, dates, and venues. We provide a model-agnostic agent framework with browsing tools, and evaluate a range of closed and open MLLMs. The strongest agent (o3) attains 15.1% without search and 36.0% accuracy with rollout under our framework, while a strong open-source model (Qwen-2.5-VL-72B-Instruct) achieves 0.0% without search and 6.9% after 20 rounds of search. Beyond answer accuracy, we assess bounding-box production and cropped-image search, and conduct an error analysis that surfaces failures in source verification, part-based reasoning, and long-horizon planning. Introduction

Figure 1:
Example MMSearch-Plus item.
Given a 2025 concert photo and the query “What was the singer’s performance time?”, the agent’s golden path extracts localized cues—micro-text/lyrics, a crop of the singer, festival/brand signage, and a distinctive stage prop—and issues targeted image/text searches that (i) identify the artist/outfit, (ii) resolve the event (e.g., a specific festival and city/date), and (iii) retrieve and
cross-validate
the official schedule to obtain the exact performance time. The answer is not explicitly written in the prompt or the image, underscoring MMSearch-Plus’s emphasis on fine-grained multimodal reasoning with provenance checks under retrieval noise.

Recent advances in large multimodal language models (MLLMs) have enabled them to act as capable agents that integrate language, vision, and tool use to accomplish complex tasks. A growing body of work studies how MLLMs leverage external search engines—both text- and image-based—to answer information-seeking questions.

Multimodal browsing benchmarks such as MMSearch
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
primarily test a model’s ability to coordinate text and images. However, many MMSearch-style questions can be solved by relatively fixed workflows that require little genuine multimodal reasoning. In particular, MMSearch heavily relies on external image search: the MLLM often plays the role of an orchestrator rather than a visual reasoner. When the search engine retrieves highly relevant images, even a unimodal LLM—without visual perception—can frequently answer by reasoning over the accompanying page text alone. This occurs because a single strong image search can surface pages whose surrounding text already contains the answer. In such cases, the image search tool and the MLLM are partially interchangeable as sources of image-related information; the only distinctly multimodal step is often
image–source cross-validation
, a narrow slice of multimodal reasoning.

Empirically, MMSearch requires short reasoning chains and achieves relatively high model scores: GPT-4o exceeds 60% on MMSearch (end-to-end VQA), and MMSearch-R1-7B reports 54.6% average accuracy
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. By contrast, recent text-only browsing benchmarks emphasize persistence and creative, multi-step search. BrowseComp
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
targets web navigation for hard-to-find, entangled information; on this task, GPT-4o and GPT-4.5 each score below 1% in direct-answer settings and under 2% even with browsing tools
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
. “Deep Research” reports 51.5% on BrowseComp, though it was trained for the benchmark and public implementation details remain limited, while open-source agents such as WebSailor reach roughly 12% after substantial engineering
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
. Taken together, these results suggest that current multimodal benchmarks may not yet match the difficulty of complex text-only browsing, motivating a BrowseComp-style multimodal benchmark with similarly demanding reasoning.

What makes a challenging benchmark for multimodal browsing agents.

Building on these insights, we argue that a strong multimodal benchmark should: (i) require
fine-grained, exhaustive visual reasoning
that compels models to mine subtle, localized cues rather than rely on a single salient (“dominant”) entity that occupies most of the image; and (ii) enforce
provenance and source verification
under retrieval noise—i.e., discriminating authentic sources when image results are noisy or conflicting and validating images embedded in webpages. Concretely, the benchmark should stress robust cross-modal evidence gathering, careful verification, and resilience to near-duplicates. For example, two football matches from adjacent seasons may share nearly identical visuals (kits, stadium angles). Correctly answering requires inspecting localized markers (e.g., patch details, sponsor variants, scoreboard typography) and cross-checking dates and venues against trustworthy sources. In real applications, difficult multimodal information-seeking tasks exhibit three recurring challenges that our benchmark targets:

1.

Noisy or conflicting retrieval.
Image search may return non-relevant or contradictory results; agents must identify authentic sources via fine-grained multimodal understanding and cross-source consistency checks.

2.

Exhaustive, part-based visual reasoning.
When holistic matching fails, agents must reason over parts and subregions—often iteratively cropping and re-searching—to piece together fragmented clues that identify the target entity or event.

3.

Professional multimodal handling and long, tool-augmented chains.
Agents must extract answers from heterogeneous media (images, documents, videos) across the web, sustain long reasoning/tool-use chains, and invoke visual tools programmatically (e.g., cropping, OCR) when helpful, akin to advanced agents such as o3/GPT-5-Thinking.

Our benchmark.

Our work,
MMSearch-Plus
, aims to bridge the gap by combining the persistence and high-reasoning demands of BrowseComp with truly multimodal workflows. Unlike
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
, where images often collapse to a single identifiable entity, images in our benchmark contain multiple
subtle
visual signals that must be connected throughout the entire reasoning process. To systematically curate hard multimodal search questions, we introduce
Spatial-Temporal Extrapolation
, a principled curation procedure that seeds questions whose answers require extrapolating from
spatial
(localized visual cues, layouts, micro-text, uniforms, signage) and
temporal
signals (broadcast overlays, seasonal context, historical rosters/schedules) to identify events, dates, or locations not explicitly present in the prompt or image. This forces models to extract fine-grained cues, propagate them through iterative (image/text) searches, and
verify
provenance amidst retrieval noise—directly addressing the challenges above.

MMSearch-Plus targets realistic scenarios where answers cannot be read off from the prompt or the image alone. For example, given a screenshot from an esports broadcast, the agent may need to infer the match date—absent from both the query and the image—by integrating subtle overlays with external evidence. Similar patterns arise in sports, geolocation, and lifestyle vlogs, domains we explicitly include to move beyond typical web imagery.

2
Method

2.1
Spatial-Temporal Extrapolation

A central challenge in BrowseComp-like benchmarks arises from the large intermediate search space induced by soft, fuzzy constraints. This requires agents to perform non-trivial cross-validation and identify the correct target. In designing our benchmark, rather than remixing existing text-only datasets, we aim to construct problems that naturally expand the search space during multimodal information seeking, thereby testing an agent’s ability for strategic planning and uncertainty-aware reasoning in dynamic environments.

Inspired by Geoguessr-style tasks, our problems are anchored on real-world events. Agents must piece together fragmented visual information to identify the underlying source event. Task difficulty is modulated by varying the richness of both visual clues and textual context. Even a single visual fragment can expand the search space dramatically, requiring careful comparison with retrieved content and cross-validation against other multimodal evidence. In more difficult cases, this mirrors human cognition: the agent must iteratively generate hypotheses, verify them against internal knowledge or retrieved content, and refine its reasoning chain across interleaved text and images. Such processes result in extended trajectories that demand robust contextual understanding.

Figure 2:
Examples of spatial and temporal relations for question curation.

Once an event is identified, we formulate questions that probe its metadata or chain together multi-hop queries. To further elevate difficulty, we introduce
Spatial-Temporal Extrapolation
. Instead of asking what is directly visible, we query what is contextually implied but physically absent, compelling reasoning beyond the pixels to reconstruct the broader event. Spatial extrapolation targets unseen entities—individuals off-screen, facing away, or partially obscured—while temporal extrapolation probes events preceding or following the depicted moment. This design forces agents to first localize the event precisely (e.g., time, match, or episode), and then retrieve and reason over wider contextual knowledge from diverse sources.

2.2
Data Collection

Building on the Spatial-Temporal Extrapolation framework, we instantiated the data collection process as follows.

We began with a set of predefined categories and generated search keywords using either LLM assistance or manual curation. Data were primarily collected from three sources: YouTube, Bilibili, and ArXiv. Keywords from categories such as
Vlog
were searched on YouTube and Bilibili, while those in the
Academic
category were searched on ArXiv, where relevant research papers (PDFs) were retrieved. We treat both videos and papers as
events
.

For videos, human annotators manually selected keyframes suitable for constructing questions. The keyframes underwent deanoymouization for sensitive content. For papers, annotators extracted figures or tables, capturing them via screenshots. A screenshot was considered suitable if it (i) contained vague or noisy information that challenged perception, or (ii) depicted less familiar entities requiring external retrieval to answer associated questions. This ensured that events were either (a) novel, i.e., published after model knowledge cutoffs, or (b) rarely represented in training corpora, consistent with the principles in
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
.

Filtering.

Although the collection focused on new or rarely seen events, we observed that certain closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) could occasionally solve questions without external search. To counteract this, we employed an adversarial filtering procedure to increase difficulty. Specifically, annotators removed or obfuscated questions answerable without retrieval through the following methods:

•

Cross-validation:
Annotators designed problems outside their own knowledge base, then verified solvability by testing on at least two closed-source MLLMs.

•

Image perturbation:
Critical visual tokens (e.g., unique logos, labels) were blurred or masked.

•

Text sanitization:
Explicit metadata (e.g., titles, author names) was redacted while maintaining semantic content.

•

Iterative refinement:
Questions that remained trivially solvable were discarded.

2.4
Benchmark Metric and Evaluation

We adopt accuracy as the primary evaluation metric. For each question, a set of acceptable answers is provided. We use LLM-as-a-judge to compare model predictions against this set, deeming an answer correct if it matches any acceptable variant. Validation through manual inspection showed complete agreement between human judgment and LLM assessment.

We initially tested both GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro as judges and found their outputs consistent. The subsequent experiments used GPT-4o. To further support reproducibility, we optionally provide lightweight rule-based verification methods.

2.5
Search Agent Framework

We leveraged
SerpApi
to support both text and image search. The framework is compatible with sub-image cropping and sub-image search for fine-grained analysis. However, due to the limited grounding ability of the tested MLLMs, we did not incorporate sub-image cropping in the main experiments.

The framework incorporated several optimizations:

•

Search result caching
: For each image in MMSearch-Plus, we cache the corresponding image-search results (i.e., the ranked URLs and thumbnails returned by the engine). This reduces latency and token cost during repeated agent steps and evaluation, and it does not deterioriate reproducibility because the top image-search results are relatively stable across time in our setting.

•

Top-
K
search results retrieval
: The most relevant search results determined by the search engine are passed to the MLLMs.

•

Content extraction
: Retrieved webpages are summarized by an MLLM into
web_info
and
related_info
, discarding boilerplate HTML to reduce token cost.
web_info
contains information relevant to the question, whereas
related_info
captures the relevance between a webpage’s thumbnail and the query image.

•

Contextualized evaluation
: The agent is always provided with the full conversation history and prior reasoning steps.

Experiment

3.1
Experiment Setup

Evaluated Models.

We evaluate both closed-source and open-source MLLMs on our benchmark.
Closed-source models include
Gemini-2.5-Pro
(hereafter
Gemini
),
o3
, and
GPT-5
, representing the current frontier of proprietary multimodal systems.
On the open-source side, we focus on
Qwen-2.5-VL-72B-Instruct
(hereafter
Qwen
), a state-of-the-art vision–language model trained at large scale.
All models are used in their publicly released inference versions without any task-specific fine-tuning.

Implementation Details.

To ensure a fair comparison, we standardize the search-and-reasoning pipeline across all models.
We consider three search modes:

(a)

Without Search.
The MLLM answers using only the question and image inputs.

(b)

Image Search.
For each benchmark image, the top-10 image-search results are summarized by Gemini and cached. The MLLM then receives the question, the images, and these summaries as input. For multi-image questions, we append each image’s summaries to the model context.

(c)

Full Rollout.
The model runs our search-agent framework and may call search tools as needed. Image-search settings match those in
Image Search
. For text search, we retrieve the top-5 websites by default. The process is capped at 20 search rounds (combining text and image search) to balance efficiency and coverage. In each text-search round, the model generates 3–5 refined queries; in each image-search round, it issues exactly one query. For Qwen, pilot studies showed that more than 10 rounds yield no further gains, so we cap its search at 10 rounds.

5.2
Observations

In addition to the result analysis from Section
4
that focuses on search modes and difficulties, we highlight three themes that relates to world knowledge, context window and visual clue grounding.

Closed-source MLLMs exhibit broad world knowledge, especially geographic.

When prompted with rare or niche events (e.g., images from small or sparsely documented locales), some closed-source models correctly recognize the event and provide relevant context, indicating substantial factual knowledge beyond mainstream datasets. By category, the
geography
tasks achieve the highest accuracies, consistent with findings from GeoBench
(
ccmdi 2023
)
.

Limitations remain in grounded reasoning.

Despite memory advantages, models often struggle with spatial grounding—e.g., producing correct bounding boxes for downstream search. Enabling agent-driven cropping frequently
deteriorates
performance on our benchmark. Consequently, we did not run a full-scale experiment with an image-cropping tool.

o3 shows strong memory retention.

Across several examples, o3 retains context over long interaction windows—successfully coordinating over
10{+}
search rounds (Figure
5
) and considering
10\times 5
(top-5)
\approx 50{+}
retrieved items. This persistence improves factual consistency and reduces redundant tool calls, leading to smoother task completion in information-seeking settings.

Qwen sometimes cannot call tools properly.

The bottom row of Figure
5
shows a long-tailed distribution of Qwen’s image-search calls—occasionally spiking to many calls within a single trajectory—while text-search calls remain low. On the right, incorrect trajectories exhibit increasing verbosity with more calls, whereas correct ones do not benefit from additional calls and sometimes even shorten, reflecting unstable tool-use dynamics. In practice, we also observe execution failures: out of
311
tasks in MMSearch-Plus, Qwen produced invalid tool calls on
45
tasks, for a total of
421
invalid calls. These failures likely trigger cascaded retries (inflating call counts) without improving evidence quality, contributing to the observed variance and degraded accuracy.

Qualitative takeaways and implications.

Two recurring patterns emerge: (i) there is no single “golden path”—agents can succeed via varied orders of search/crop/verify; and (ii) stronger multimodal internal knowledge narrows the practical search space, enabling targeted verification over broad exploration. Accordingly, evaluation and training should reward
families
of effective strategies rather than a single script. While augmenting multimodal internal knowledge can reduce exploration overhead, end-to-end reliability still depends on robust verification and tighter cross-modal grounding.

aim to connect perception with explicit reasoning. However, these datasets are primarily self-contained and do not require dynamic interaction with the open web or tool-mediated search.

Web Browsing and Search-Enhanced Agents.

A large body of work explores language models as web agents that plan, browse, and gather evidence. Earilier works like FRAMES
(
Krishna et al. 2024
)
focus on multi-hop QAs on Wikipedia-like data. More recently, benchmarks such as BrowseComp
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
and its Chinese variant BrowseComp-ZH
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
test multi-step persistence in text-heavy web navigation, where even frontier models struggle. Open-source agents including WebSailor
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
, WebShaper
(
Tao et al. 2025
)
, and WebWatcher (BrowseComp-VL)
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
emphasize robustness, data synthesis, and limited multimodal inputs.

In parallel, reinforcement-learning–based frameworks push the boundaries of search adaptivity and depth. Search-R1
(
Jin et al. 2025
)
, R1-Searcher
(
Song et al. 2025
)
, ReSearch
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, Pangu DeepDiver
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, and DeepResearcher
(
Zheng et al. 2025a
)
train persistence and decomposition skills through reward shaping. ASearcher
(
Gao et al. 2025
)
extends this direction with large-scale asynchronous reinforcement learning, decoupling exploration from evaluation to unlock long-horizon search over dozens of hops. Complementary agentic systems such as Open Deep Search
(
Alzubi et al. 2025
)
, AutoAgent
(
Tang et al. 2025
)
, and Search-o1
(
Li et al. 2025c
)
further democratize and automate search-enhanced reasoning.

Multimodal Web Search and MM-BrowseComp.

Beyond text-only browsing, MMSearch
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
and MMSearch-R1
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
introduce image-grounded queries, but many tasks still reduce multimodality to entity matching rather than genuine visual reasoning. MM-BrowseComp
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
addresses this gap with a 224-question benchmark designed for multimodal browsing agents where key evidence may be embedded in images or videos encountered during search. The benchmark provides a verified checklist for fine-grained analysis of multimodal dependencies and reasoning paths, and reveals that even top models (e.g., OpenAI o3 with tools) achieve only
\sim\!29\%
accuracy, underscoring substantial headroom for native multimodal reasoning within browsing pipelines. Our setting is closely related to, and concurrent with, MM-BrowseComp, but differs in focusing on
sparse
visual cues that must be transformed into targeted queries rather than predominantly entity-level matching.

Thinking with Images.

A growing body of work argues that multimodality should go beyond tool orchestration to
think with images
. Methods such as V*
(
Wu & Xie 2024
)
and instruction-guided masking
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
improve visual grounding inside LLMs. RL-style training further enforces image-first reasoning: GRIT
(
Fan et al. 2025
)
, DeepEyes
(
Zheng et al. 2025b
)
, Pixel Reasoner
(
Su et al. 2025a
)
, and Visionary-R1
(
Xia et al. 2025
)
encourage explicit visual deliberation before action. Visual Agentic Reinforcement Fine-Tuning
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, OpenThinkIMG
(
Su et al. 2025b
)
, and One-RL-to-See-Them-All
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
propose unified training frameworks for multimodal agents, while broader systems like PyVision
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
and Thyme
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
generalize the paradigm.

Positioning and Summary.

Taken together, web-browsing benchmarks stress persistence and decomposition in open-world settings
(
Wei et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
;
Tao et al. 2025
;
Geng et al. 2025
)
, while multimodal benchmarks emphasize perception-heavy reasoning
(
Johnson et al. 2017
;
Krishna et al. 2016
;
Zellers et al. 2019
;
Mathew et al. 2021
)
. Recent multimodal browsing datasets—including MM-BrowseComp
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
—begin to unite these threads but still leave open how
sparse
visual evidence should drive query formulation and multi-step planning. We formulate the Spatial-Temporal Extrapolation methodology to concretely design challenging VQAs for testing multimodal search abilities. We also stress the importance of grounding and cropping images to allow fine-grained multimodal reasoning.

7
Conclusion

This work introduces
MMSearch-Plus
, a benchmark that restores the genuinely multimodal pressures of web browsing by requiring agents to extract weak, localized visual cues and propagate them through noisy retrieval to out-of-image facts. Our
Spatial–Temporal Extrapolation
curation procedure preserves the persistence and difficulty profile of text-only browsing while compelling fine-grained visual reasoning and provenance verification. Evaluating a range of open and closed MLLMs within a model-agnostic search agent framework reveals a substantial headroom: the strongest agent we tested (o3) reached only 15.1% without search and 36.0% with rollout, while a strong open-source model (Qwen-2.5-VL 72B) achieved 0.0% without search and 6.9% with rollout. These results indicate that current systems struggle to (i) reliably read and localize micro-signals (badges, micro-text, uniforms, layout geometry), (ii) decide when and how to crop and reuse visual evidence, and (iii) maintain verifiable chains of evidence across long-horizon tool use. We hope MMSearch-Plus serves as a rigorous stress test and a common yardstick for progress on multimodal browsing.

Limitations.

While diverse, our data inherits biases from public web sources and the domains we sampled; some tasks still under-represent non-English or low-visual-density pages. Our evaluation focuses on general-purpose agents with standard tool access; specialized systems with richer UI control or domain knowledge may behave differently. Finally, we primarily target image–text browsing; videos and dynamic interfaces remain underexplored.
