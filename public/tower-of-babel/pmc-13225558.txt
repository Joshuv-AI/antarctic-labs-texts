Genetic associations between polyunsaturated fatty acids and alopecia areata
Authors: Qiyu Liu, Yuchen Cai, Weidong Li, Wenjun Shi, Li Yu, Xueyao Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The influence of circulating polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) on alopecia areata (AA) development remains poorly understood, with inconsistent results reported. Our study aims to evaluate their causal associations using the bidirectional Mendelian randomization method. European ancestry genome-wide association studies were used to analyze the genetic associations between PUFAs and AA (n = 211,428). Sample sizes for PUFAs were 13,527, 13,506, 114,999, 7818, and 13,549 for plasma linoleic acid (LA), omega-6, omega-3, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and PUFAs other than 18:2 (otPUFA), respectively. The causal associations were primarily determined by the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. Circulating omega-6 (IVW odds ratio [OR] = 1.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.15–2.33, P = .0059) and LA (IVW OR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.05–2.13, P = .0257) were positively associated with AA risk. Conversely, AA was negatively correlated with circulating DHA (IVW OR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.99–1.00, P = .0324). Elevated circulating omega-6 and LA are correlated with an increased AA risk. Furthermore, AA was negatively associated with circulating DHA. These findings provide insights into the potential biological mechanisms underlying the development of AA, offering potential implications for dietary recommendations and treatment options.

1. Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA), a prevalent autoimmune hair disorder, typically presents as abrupt onset and nonscarring hair loss on the scalp or other hair-bearing areas.[1] It is believed to affect approximately 2% of the general population, leading to a huge cosmetic and psychological impact.[2] Although multifactorial etiopathogenesis has been investigated, including immune dysregulation, viral infections, psychosocial stress, and lifestyle-related factors, the exact nature of AA remains unclear.[3,4] Recently, studies using genome-wide association analysis have identified an overwhelming contribution of genetic factors in AA development.[5,6] While current interventions for AA may offer limited benefits with potential adverse effects, elucidating the causal roles of genetic susceptibility loci may provide unique insights into the underlying pathophysiology of AA, facilitating the development of more personalized preventative measures.

The dietary component is a critical modifiable lifestyle factor associated with AA development.[7] Specifically, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), as a subset of essential fatty acids, are considered critical to human health. PUFAs are the main components of phospholipids in cell membranes, to which they impart desirable physical properties, including cellular signaling, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory and immune response.[8] PUFAs can be categorized based on their double bond position into omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids (FAs).[9] Epidemiological investigations in humans have shown that omega-3, particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), has anti-inflammatory properties in the therapeutic management of both autoimmune and allergic skin disorders.[10] The application of DHA shows beneficial effects on inhibiting UV-induced inflammation, attenuating dermatitis, and accelerating skin wound healing.[11] In contrast, emerging evidence has shown that the omega-6-rich diet may impair the anti-inflammatory and inflammatory resolution capacity of omega-3 FAs.[12] In particular, linoleic acid (LA), which is a type of omega-6 FA, has been found to be associated with excess proinflammatory responses that may promote the development of many chronic inflammatory and metabolic disorders.[13] Recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have indicated that a reduced omega-6/omega-3 ratio in dietary supplements can significantly reduce serum inflammation markers, indicating a possible causal link between PUFAs and inflammatory disorders.[14] Nevertheless, traditional observational studies cannot eliminate reverse causality or confounding bias. Although the potential roles of PUFAs in AA have been proposed, their causal associations remain largely uncharacterized.

As an alternative, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis has been widely applied in medical fields to establish causal relationships between complex characteristics, without potential bias introduced by confounders.[15] MR, described as naturally occurring RCTs, uses instrumental variables (IVs) to determine causal associations between exposures and outcomes.[16] The use of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs can yield results that are less susceptible to potential biases introduced by covariates.[17] Given the uncertain relationship between circulating PUFAs and AA, a bidirectional 2-sample MR method was performed to assess causal relationships between 5 different types of PUFAs and AA. This study holds the potential to yield novel insights into risk factors for AA, thus establishing a theoretical framework for future studies and optimizing clinical management strategies for AA patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. General study design

The present study used the bidirectional two-sample MR technique, working primarily with open-access data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Five types of PUFAs in peripheral blood were included, namely omega-3, DHA, omega-6, LA, and PUFAs other than 18:2 (otPUFA). The investigation initially underwent a forward MR analysis to identify causal links between PUFAs and AA, and subsequently, a reverse MR analysis was conducted to assess risk associations between AA and varying PUFA levels. The original GWAS datasets were publicly available, and ethical approval and informed consent were obtained prior to implementation. No additional informed consent or ethical approval is required.

2.2. Data source and selection of IVs

The IVs were defined as SNPs with P < 1 × 10−5. SNPs associated with 5 types of PUFAs (DHA, LA, otPUFA, omega-3, and omega-6) were retrieved from GWAS of the European population. They are available on the IEU Open GWAS website using the following IDs: met-d-Omega_3, met-c-856, met-a-409, met-c-893, and met-c-917. The sample sizes were 7818, 13,527, 13,549, 114,999, and 13,506 for DHA, LA, otPUFA, omega-3, and omega-6, respectively. Furthermore, genetic data associated with AA were collected from IEU Open GWAS with 211,428 individuals of European descent (ID: finn-b-L12_ALOPECAREATA). Linkage disequilibrium was used to evaluate the degree of genetic linkage among these IVs, based on a stringent r2 < 0.001 threshold and a window size of 10,000 kilobases.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The “TwoSampleMR” (v.0.5.6) package was used for all MR analyses. Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) serves as the primary analytical tool for causal inference, supplemented by weighted median, MR-Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode techniques. IVW, a popular method in MR studies, provides the most accurate and unbiased estimate when IVs are valid and show no pleiotropic effects.[18] The weighted mode approach was chosen as an analytical framework for generating overall causal estimates, assuming that individual estimates are primarily derived from valid IVs.[19] Weighted median, which is used to derive a causal relationship from multiple genetic variations, is particularly suitable for scenarios where up to 50% of IVs are valid.[20] The pleiotropic effect across genetic variants was calculated using the MR-Egger model, which is an established method for robust estimation when IVs are weak.[21] All estimates with a 2-tailed P value <.05 were considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Causal effects of PUFAs on AA

A total of 124, 7, 38, 32, and 38 independent SNPs were determined as IVs to assess potential causal relationships between PUFAs and AA. It is suggested that omega-6 levels were significantly and positively associated with AA risk (IVW odds ratio [OR] = 1.64, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.15–2.33, P = .0059) (Fig. 1A and Table 1). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis performed for these genetic associations revealed no statistically significant associations with any specific SNPs (Fig. 1B). The consistent direction of the scatter plot strongly suggests a positive relationship between genetically determined omega-6 and AA risk (Fig. 1C). In addition, the funnel plot generated by MR-Egger and IVW did not reveal any significant heterogeneity in the analysis of omega-6 and AA (Fig. 1D). Similarly, LA, which is one of the main omega-6 FAs, yielded consistent results (IVW OR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.05–2.13, P = .0257), suggesting a significant association with a higher risk for AA (Fig. 2A and Table 1). Further leave-one-out sensitivity analysis validated the robustness of the results after removing outliers (Fig. 2B). The scatter plot showed no horizontal pleiotropy among SNPs (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the funnel plots visually presented the distribution of the effect of a single SNP (Fig. 2D). However, no association was found between the other 3 PUFAs (omega-3, DHA, and otPUFA) and AA using any method (Table 1).

3.2. Causal effects of AA on PUFAs

The reverse MR identified a total of 19, 8, 18, 18, and 18 independent SNPs as IVs to assess the causal relationships of AA with omega-3, DHA, omega-6, LA, and otPUFA, respectively. Genetically determined AA was negatively associated with DHA (IVW OR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.99–1.00, P = .0324) (Fig. 3A and Table 2). The leave-one-out analysis provided supporting evidence that AA was not causally associated with circulating DHA levels (Fig. 3B). The MR-Egger intercepts demonstrated no directional horizontal pleiotropy (Fig. 3C). In addition, the funnel plot suggested no significant heterogeneity among all 8 SNPs (Fig. 3D). For the other 4 PUFAs (omega-3, LA, omega-6, and otPUFA), no causal links to AA were found.

4. Discussion

The present study investigated the causal link between AA and PUFAs (LA, DHA, omega-3, omega-6, and otPUFA). It is suggested that omega-6 and LA were positively associated with AA risk in individuals of European descent. The reverse MR provided suggestive evidence that AA may lead to decreased DHA levels. Overall, the current MR study demonstrated a causal and bidirectional association between PUFAs and AA.

AA has been generally considered an autoimmune disease affecting hair follicles, with an intricate interplay between genetic and environmental factors.[22] The disease is characterized by dysregulated immune and inflammatory responses in hair follicles, leading to the disruption of the hair cycle, followed by hair follicle atrophy and dystrophy.[23] Recently, there has been increasing research attention to PUFAs and their derivatives in relation to skin inflammation.[12] Omega-6 FAs are known for their pro-inflammatory properties that contribute to the progression of many allergic and inflammatory skin conditions.[4,24] In addition, mounting evidence suggests an essential role for omega-6 FAs in the regulation of oxidative stress, monocyte chemotaxis, and cytokine production.[25] Nevertheless, previous RCTs show virtually no evidence that dietary omega-6 (especially LA) increases markers of inflammation.[26] Similar results were found in an epidemiological study that found no relationship between plasma concentrations of LA and inflammatory markers.[27] To date, the role of omega-6 FAs in the inflammatory processes of AA is characterized by mixed evidence, reflecting the complexity of lipid metabolism and its interaction with autoimmune pathways. Omega-6 FAs are precursors to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids like prostaglandins and leukotrienes.[28] These mediators are implicated in the Th1/Th17-driven inflammation seen in AA, where cytotoxic T-cells attack hair follicles.[29] Paradoxically, clinical studies report hair regrowth benefits from omega-6 supplementation. In a non-placebo-controlled RCT study, researchers found significantly increased hair density in participants with androgen-sensitive alopecia after receiving an oral supplement containing omega-3, omega-6, and antioxidants.[30] Similarly, investigators found that a 6-month regimen of omega-3/omega-6 and antioxidant supplementation provided substantial benefits against female pattern hair loss.[31] However, these studies conflated omega-6 effects with co-administered omega-3 FAs and antioxidants, precluding definitive attribution of therapeutic benefits to omega-6 itself. Our MR analysis identified a causal association between genetically elevated omega-6/LA levels and increased AA risk, suggesting that dietary omega-6/LA restriction may mitigate AA development. This contrasts with earlier observational data and highlights the need for rigorous RCTs to resolve these discrepancies. Future work should prioritize placebo-controlled interventions isolating omega-6 effects and investigate tissue-specific lipidomic profiles in AA.

The reverse MR provided suggestive evidence that AA can lead to increased DHA levels. Although no previous study investigated PUFA levels in AA patients, the decrease in DHA may be explained by underlying lipid metabolism and inflammatory modulation during AA development. DHA, being one of the primary omega-3 FAs rich in fish oil, plays an integral role in modulating the complex inflammatory course of various autoimmune diseases, such as psoriasis, asthma, and arthritis.[32] Increased DHA brought about a decrease in PGE2 and leukotriene B4 production, which are major inducers of leukocyte chemotaxis and inflammatory cytokine release.[32,33] Interestingly, in non-affected and affected atopic dermatitis skin, researchers found a significantly reduced omega-3/omega-6 ratio. At the same time, decreased serum DHA levels were observed in an atopic dermatitis mouse model, along with an increase in DHA metabolites.[34] Based on our collective findings, it is suggested that decreased circulating DHA may serve as a hallmark of the pro-inflammatory environment in AA. Further observational investigations are warranted to elucidate the biological mechanisms driving PUFA metabolic alterations in AA patients.

The current study shows several significant strengths. First, it represents the first comprehensive investigation to examine causal associations between circulating PUFAs and AA, providing a vital benchmark for future research in this field. Second, due to the scarcity of RCTs, this MR investigation may be a valuable alternative method for clinical interpretation. Third, this study employed rigorous quality control measures to ensure the reliability of the findings. The absence of any evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity further supports the robustness of the research outcome.

However, some limitations should also be recognized. First, the GWAS data used did not include age and sex information. Consequently, age- and sex-specific genetic or environmental interactions may bias causal estimates, as hormonal and immunosenescence pathways are implicated in AA pathogenesis. Second, this study focused exclusively on individuals of European descent, potentially limiting the generalizability of its findings to individuals from distinct ethnic groups with different lifestyles and cultural backgrounds. To strengthen the reliability and universal application of the study, additional analyses of individuals from other ethnic groups are needed. Third, while ORs provide relative risk estimates, they obscure quantitative clinical significance. However, we are unable to provide absolute differences between groups because the GWAS summary data does not include individual-level data or predefined groupings. Absolute differences between groups or absolute risk differences are warranted in future studies. Finally, although MR supports the correlation between PUFAs and AA, residual pleiotropy and gene-environment interactions cannot be fully excluded. Further RCTs with biomarker-guided PUFA modulation remain the gold standard for causal inference. The preliminary nature of these findings justifies subsequent mechanistic studies, including single-cell transcriptomics of hair follicle immune cells and lipidomic profiling of AA scalp microenvironments.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study strongly indicates that circulating omega-6 and LA are positively associated with AA risk. In addition, genetic predisposition to AA was negatively correlated with DHA. These findings suggest that nutritional interventions, specifically targeting omega-6 and LA intakes, may potentially serve as a preventative measure for AA. This study provides a novel direction for investigators to explore the biochemical mechanisms underlying AA and may facilitate the development of earlier clinical management strategies.
