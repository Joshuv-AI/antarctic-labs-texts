Posterior bounds on divergence time of two sequences under dependent-site evolutionary models
Joseph Mathews, Scott C. Schmidler

Abstract
Let 𝐱 \mathbf{x} and 𝐲 \mathbf{y} be two length n n DNA sequences, and suppose we would like to estimate the divergence time T T . A well known simple but crude estimate of T T is p ^ := d H ​ ( 𝐱 , 𝐲 ) / n \hat{p}:=\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})/n , the fraction of mutated sites (the p p -distance). We establish a posterior concentration bound on T T , showing that the posterior distribution of T T concentrates within a logarithmic factor of p ^ \hat{p} when d H ​ ( 𝐱 , 𝐲 ) ​ log ⁡ ( n ) / n = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\log(n)/n=o(1) . Our bounds hold under a large class of evolutionary models, including many standard models that incorporate site dependence. As a special case, we show that T T exceeds p ^ \hat{p} with vanishingly small posterior probability as n n increases under models with constant mutation rates, complementing the result of Mihaescu and Steel (Appl Math Lett 23(9):975–979, 2010). Our approach is based on bounding sequence transition probabilities in various convergence regimes of the underlying evolutionary process. Our result may be useful for improving the efficiency of iterative optimization and sampling schemes for estimating divergence times in phylogenetic inference.

Introduction

Estimation of the
species divergence time
(or
branch length
)
T
between two DNA sequences
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is a fundamental problem in phylogenetic tree reconstruction
Russo et al. 1995
;
Takahata et al. 1995
;
Glazko and Nei 2003
;
Kumar and Hedges 1998
;
Hasegawa et al. 1985
. Early tree reconstruction algorithms used notions of evolutionary (genetic) distance between observed sequences such as the well-known
p
-distance
, given by the proportion of differing sites
\hat{p}:=\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})/n
, to estimate branch lengths and tree topology
Fitch and Margoliash 1967
;
Fitch 1971
;
Saitou and Nei 1987
;
Rzhetsky and Nei 1992
. However, the
p
-distance underestimates divergence times when sequences are not closely related
\textcolor
blue
Jukes and Cantor 1969
;
Hasegawa et al. 1985
;
Kimura 1980
;
Yang and Nielsen 2008
. Later model-based approaches estimate branch lengths by assuming sequences evolve according to a continuous time Markov Chain (CTMC) with rate matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}

Felsenstein 1973
. For such models, two popular approaches to estimation are maximum likelihood
Felenstein 1981
and Bayesian inference
Yang and Rannala 1997
;
Thorne et al. 1998
. For models with constant substitution rates (e.g. Jukes-Cantor model
Jukes and Cantor 1969
), the maximum likelihood estimate of the branch length can be obtained in closed form and shown to be approximately
-\log(1-\hat{p})
. However, closed form solutions of the MLE are not available for most substitution models, and either numerical optimization or posterior sampling via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are required. To ensure tractability of the likelihood, it is typically assumed that DNA sites evolve independently. Unfortunately, the independent site assumption is known to fail to capture important biological features; examples include CpG di-nucleotide mutability
Pedersen et al. 1998
, structural constraints in RNA and proteins
Robinson et al. 2003
, and enzyme-driven somatic hypermutation in B-cell receptor affinity maturation
Wiehe et al. 2018
;
Mathews et al. 2023
. For this reason, a variety of
dependent site
models have been proposed
Robinson et al. 2003
;
Siepel and Haussler 2004
;
Jensen and Pedersen 2000
;
Larson et al. 2020
;
Hwang and Green 2004
;
von Haeseler and
Schöniger 1998
;
Christensen et al. 2005
;
Arndt and Hwa 2005
;
Lunter and Hein 2004
. However, under site dependent models, even numerical evaluation of the likelihood is generally intractable, further complicating the estimation of
T
. In such cases, the likelihood must be numerically approximated
Robinson et al. 2003
;
Pederson and Jensen 2001
; doing so within iterative optimization or sampling algorithms means that estimating
T
in dependent site settings is computationally intensive.

As a result, it is desirable to evaluate the likelihood only at values of
T
where the likelihood (or posterior density) is large, since likelihood approximations are expensive. Consequently, we would like to have a concentration bound on the posterior distribution of
T
, in order to identify the region containing such values. Such a bound was obtained by
Mihaescu and Steel 2010
for the two state symmetric evolution model (CFN model), with the authors showing that
T
exceeds
\log(n)
with vanishingly small probability as
n
increases. Here, we provide a much tighter bound for the setting – common in practical applications – of low-to-moderately mutated sequences, establishing a connection between the posterior distribution of
T
and the
p
-distance. Specifically, we show that with high probability
T
is no greater than
\hat{p}\log(n)
, provided
r\log(n)/n=o(1)
. These results hold for a large class of evolutionary models, including those exhibiting site dependence. As a special case, we show that under symmetric evolution models (e.g.
the same setting as
Mihaescu and Steel 2010
), our bound can be improved to show that
T
is no greater than
\hat{p}

with high posterior probability. Our results establish a direct relationship between the familiar and easily calculated
p
-distance notion of evolutionary distance and the high posterior credible region of
T
under general evolution models including those capturing site dependence, and imply that the posterior probability (or likelihood) that
T
lies outside this interval is exponentially small, so long as the prior is not pathological (i.e. concentrates mass outside the interval in a way that increases with data size to overwhelm the signal in the data). As a result, procedures for posterior sampling or iterative optimization of the likelihood for
T
during phylogenetic inference need not
consider values of
T
outside this interval, enabling the design of more efficient proposal distributions for Bayesian MCMC or optimization initialization procedures or constraints for maximum likelihood estimation. The latter is of particular importance for models with site dependence
Mathews and Schmidler 2025
.

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\gamma}_{i}(b;\tilde{x}_{i})&\text{ for }\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=1\text{ and }x^{\prime}_{i}=b\neq x_{i}\\

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(T\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=e^{-\lambda_{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}T}\sum^{\infty}_{m=r}\frac{(\lambda_{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}T)^{m}}{m!}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}^{m}.

(2)

Let

\displaystyle\eta(t\mid\mathbf{y}):=\frac{\mathcal{L}(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\eta_{0}(t)}{\int^{\infty}_{0}\mathcal{L}(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\eta_{0}(t)dt}.

Lemma 3
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{ij}\coloneqq\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}=j\mid\bar{Y}_{m-1}=i)=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}i}{nq\lambda}&\text{ if }j=i-1\\

\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{i}{n}\right)&\text{ if }j=i+1.\end{cases}

(22)

Then
\Prob(\bar{X}_{m}\leq r\mid X_{0}=\mathbf{x})\leq\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=0)
and as a consequence

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}^{m}\leq\Prob(\bar{X}_{m}\leq r\mid X_{0}=\mathbf{x})\leq\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=0).

Proof.

Let
b^{\prime}(\bar{Y}_{m})
and

be the birth and death probabilities defined in
\textcolor

17
) and used in (

\displaystyle X^{*}_{j}\mid X^{*}_{j-1}\sim\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\ \qquad\text{ and }\qquad\bar{Y}^{*}_{j}\mid\bar{Y}^{*}_{j-1}\sim\mathbf{B}

(23)

with

.
Note that (

(a)

Draw

\bar{Y}^{*}_{j}\mid\bar{Y}^{*}_{j-1}\sim\mathbf{B}

(b)

Draw

Marginalizing out

since by definition

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{k=0}\Prob(\bar{X}_{j}^{*}=k\mid X_{j-1}^{*})\Prob(X_{j}^{*}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\bar{X}_{j}^{*},X_{j-1}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{k=0}\mathbf{A}(X^{*}_{j-1},k)\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{X^{*}_{j-1},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}_{k}(\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}))}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{X^{*}_{j-1},\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}}\mathbbm{1}_{k}(\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{x}))}

To do so, we will show stochastic dominance holds for the conditional distributions

That is, we require to show that for

where the first inequality follows from

It follows that for

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\bar{X}_{j}>t\mid X_{j-1},\bar{X}_{j-1}=x\right)-\Prob\left(\bar{Y}_{j}>t\mid\bar{Y}_{j-1}=y\right)>0.

Case

First consider

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\bar{X}_{j}>t\mid X_{j-1},\bar{X}_{j-1}=t+1\right)-\Prob\left(\bar{Y}_{j}>t\mid\bar{Y}_{j-1}=t+1\right)

\displaystyle=1-d(X_{j-1})-(1-d^{\prime}(t+1))

The only other non-trivial case to consider is

) by

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\bar{X}_{j}>t\mid X_{j-1},\bar{X}_{j-1}=t\right)-\Prob\left(\bar{Y}_{j}>t\mid\bar{Y}_{j-1}=t\right)=b(X_{j-1})-b^{\prime}(t)\geq 0.

Consequently, conditional on

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\bar{X}_{j}>t\mid X_{j-1},\bar{X}_{j-1}=x\right)-\Prob\left(\bar{Y}_{j}>t\mid\bar{Y}_{j-1}=y\right)\geq 0.

Since (

of

. Hence,
\Prob(\cap^{m}_{j=0}\bar{X}^{*}_{j}\geq\bar{Y}^{*}_{j})=1
for any

and, in particular,

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{X}_{m}\leq r\mid X_{0}=\mathbf{x})=\Prob(\bar{X}^{*}_{m}\leq r\mid X^{*}_{0}=\mathbf{x})\leq\Prob(\bar{Y}^{*}_{m}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}^{*}_{0}=0)=\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=0).

∎

Lemma 4
.

Let

\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}>m^{*})\leq\exp(-\frac{m^{*}\tilde{\gamma}^{2}_{\min}}{8})

Proof.

Consider a biased random walk
\{\bar{W}_{m}\}

\mathbb{Z}

\bar{W}_{0}=0

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{i,j}=\Prob(\bar{W}_{m}=j\mid\bar{W}_{m-1}=i)=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}\delta}{q\lambda}&\text{ if }j=i-1\\

\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}\left(1-\delta\right)&\text{ if }j=i+1.\end{cases}

(26)

given by uniform bounds on the

\displaystyle\bar{Y}_{m\wedge\tau_{r^{*}}}=\begin{cases}\bar{Y}_{m}&\text{ for }m<\tau_{r^{*}}\\

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}>m^{\star})=\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}^{st.}>m^{\star})\quad\quad\Prob_{0}(\eta_{r^{*}}>m^{\star})=\Prob_{0}(\eta_{r^{*}}^{st.}>m^{\star})

(27)

In addition, note that since

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{(i-1)}{n}\right)\geq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}(1-\delta)=\mathbf{W}_{i-1,i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}i}{nq\lambda}\leq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}\delta}{q\lambda}=\mathbf{W}_{i,i-1}\qquad\text{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,r^{*}\}

\displaystyle\Prob(\Delta_{i}=i)=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}\delta}{q\lambda}&\text{ if }i=-1\\

Lemma 5
.

The stationary distribution of
\mathbf{B}
is a binomial distribution

\displaystyle\nu(x)=\text{Binomial}\left(x;n,\frac{q}{q+\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}\right).

In addition, the following bounds hold:

1.

\min_{x}\nu(x)=\frac{\min(\tilde{\gamma}^{n}_{\star},q^{n})}{(\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}+q)^{n}}
.

2.

\sum^{r}_{k=0}\nu(k)\leq e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{q}{q+\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}})^{2}}
provided
\frac{r}{n}<\frac{q}{2(q+\tilde{\gamma}_{\star})}
.

The second condition in Lemma

requires that

be less than half the average number of mutated sites under

r
is a low probability state under
\nu

occurs only when the original process

is sufficiently far from equilibrium, in which case the upper tail of the posterior distribution of

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\mathbf{B}
(Lemma

)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}i}{nq\lambda}\quad\quad\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{i-1}{n}\right)

Recall
\nu(x)\propto\prod^{x}_{i=1}\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}/\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}
and notice

\displaystyle\prod^{x}_{i=1}\frac{\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}}=\theta^{x}\prod^{x}_{i=1}\left(\frac{n-i+1}{i}\right)=\theta^{x}\binom{n}{x}.

where
\theta:=q\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}/\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}
. Hence,
\nu(x)=\frac{\theta^{x}\binom{n}{x}}{(1+\theta)^{n}}
. The minimum probability is obtained at one of the extrema:

\displaystyle\frac{\theta^{x}\binom{n}{x}}{(1+\theta)^{n}}\geq\frac{\min(1,\theta^{n})}{(1+\theta)^{n}}=\min_{x}\nu(x).

Finally, let
X\sim\text{Binomial}(x;n,\frac{\theta}{1+\theta})
. By Hoeffding’s inequality and our assumption on

,

\displaystyle\sum^{r}_{k=0}\pi(k)=\Prob(X\leq r)=\Prob\left(X-\frac{n\theta}{1+\theta}\leq-n\left(\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}-\frac{r}{n}\right)\right)\leq e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{\theta}{1+\theta})^{2}}.

∎

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}=r,\bar{Y}_{m-1}\neq r^{\star},\ldots,\bar{Y}_{m^{\prime}+1}\neq r^{\star}\mid\bar{Y}_{m^{\prime}}=r^{*})\leq\frac{\nu(r)}{\nu(r^{*})}\text{ for }m^{\prime}<m.

(32)

Lemma
4

) to show that, conditional on the chain reaching

, the probability that

for appropriately chosen

Lemma 6
.

Let
\delta\leq\frac{q}{q+e\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}:=\delta_{2}
, where
\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}:=\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}/\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}
. Then
\frac{\nu(r)}{\nu(r^{*})}\leq e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1}
for
r\leq\lfloor n\delta\rfloor/2
.

Proof.

Recall that
\nu(x)\propto\prod^{x}_{i=1}\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}/\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}
and
r^{*}=\lfloor n\delta\rfloor
. By the definition of
\mathbf{B}
(Lemma

)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}}{\lambda}\left(1-\frac{i-1}{n}\right)\quad\quad\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}i}{nq\lambda}

Observe that for any
i\in\{r+1,\ldots,r^{\star}\}
we have since
r^{*}=\lfloor n\delta\rfloor\leq n\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}}{\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}}=\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}{q}\left(\frac{i}{n-i+1}\right)\leq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}{q}\left(\frac{r^{\star}}{n-r^{\star}}\right)\leq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}{q}\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right).

Note that
\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}{q}(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta})\leq e^{-1}
by our choice of
\delta
and
r^{\star}-r=\lfloor n\delta\rfloor-r\geq\lfloor n\delta\rfloor-\frac{\lfloor n\delta\rfloor}{2}=\frac{\lfloor n\delta\rfloor}{2}
. Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\nu(r)}{\nu(r^{*})}=\prod^{r^{*}}_{i=r+1}\frac{\mathbf{B}_{i,i-1}}{\mathbf{B}_{i-1,i}}\leq\left(\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}}{q}\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\right)^{r^{*}-r}\leq e^{-\frac{\lfloor n\delta\rfloor}{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1},

where the final inequality follows since
\lfloor x\rfloor\geq x-1
.
∎

We will now use the inequality (

) along with Lemma

to bound the term

Lemma 7
.

Let
\delta
and

satisfy the same assumptions as in Lemma

. Then the following bound holds:

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r,\tau_{r^{*}}\leq m)\leq m^{2}e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1}.

Proof.

First write by the law of total probability

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r,\tau_{r^{*}}\leq m)=\sum^{m}_{j=0}\Prob_{0}(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r,\tau_{r^{*}}=j)

\displaystyle=\sum^{m}_{j=0}\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\tau_{r^{*}}=j)\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}=j).

(33)

Note that
\{\tau_{r^{*}}=j\}=\{\bar{Y}_{0}<r^{*},\ldots,\bar{Y}_{j-1}<r^{*},\bar{Y}_{j}=r^{*}\}
and by the Markov property

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\tau_{r^{*}}=j)=\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{j}=r^{*})=\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*}).

(34)

Fix
j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
and denote the number of times that the chain
\{\bar{Y}_{m}\}
returns to
r^{*}
among the times
\{1,\ldots,m-j\}
by

\displaystyle Z_{j}:=\sum^{m-j}_{i=1}\mathbbm{1}_{r^{*}}(\bar{Y}_{i}).

Let

be the time of the
z
th return to
r^{*}
. The joint event
\{Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\}
means that there are in total
z

r^{*}
in the first

steps of the
\{\bar{Y}_{m}\}

and
this final return to
r^{*}
occurs at time

. Write by the law of total probability

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum^{m-j}_{z=1}\sum^{m-j}_{l=1}\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r,Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

(35)

\displaystyle=\sum^{m-j-1}_{z=1}\sum^{m-j-1}_{l=1}\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r,Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*}).

(36)

Next, note
\{Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\}\subset\{\cap^{m-j}_{i=l+1}\{\bar{Y}_{i}\neq r^{*}\},\bar{Y}_{l}=r^{*}\}
for

, and so

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r,Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r,\cap^{m-j}_{i=l+1}\{\bar{Y}_{i}\neq r^{*}\},\bar{Y}_{l}=r^{*}\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}=r,\cap^{m-j}_{i=l+1}\{\bar{Y}_{i}\neq r^{*}\}\mid\bar{Y}_{l}=r^{*})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1},

where the equality follows by the Markov property and the final inequality follows by (
32
). This implies by (

\displaystyle\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum^{m-j-1}_{z=1}\sum^{m-j-1}_{l=1}\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r,Z_{j}=z,\tau^{z}_{r^{*}}=l\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1}.

Finally, recalling (

) we obtain the stated bound:

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r,\tau_{r^{*}}\leq m)=\sum^{m}_{j=0}\Prob(\bar{Y}_{m-j}\leq r\mid\bar{Y}_{0}=r^{*})\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}=j)

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}}\sum^{m}_{j=0}\Prob_{0}(\tau_{r^{*}}=j)

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}+1}.

∎

We now bound

\displaystyle\Prob_{0}(\bar{Y}_{m}\leq r)=\sum^{r}_{k=0}\mathbf{B}^{m}_{0,k}\leq\sum^{r}_{k=0}\nu(k)+\frac{re^{-\text{SpecGap}(\mathbf{B})m}}{\min_{x}\nu(x)}.

Theorem 3
.

(
Diaconis and Stroock 1991
)

Let
\mathbf{B}
be a Markov chain defined on a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
with stationary distribution
\nu
. Let
\gamma_{x,y}
denote a directed path between each element
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
x\neq y
. Then

\displaystyle\text{SpecGap}(\mathbf{B})\geq\left(\max_{a\neq b:\mathbf{B}_{a,b}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\nu(a)\mathbf{B}_{a,b}}\sum_{x\neq y:(a,b)\in\gamma_{x,y}}\nu(x)\nu(y)|\gamma_{x,y}|\right)^{-1}.

(41)

We will use Theorem
3
to prove the following result.

Lemma 9
.

The following bound holds:

\displaystyle\text{SpecGap}(\mathbf{B})\geq\frac{\min(\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}q^{-1},\tilde{\gamma}_{\min})}{\lambda n^{2}(n+1)^{2}}.

Proof.

We bound each term in (
41
) in turn.

Let
x,y\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and
\gamma_{x,y}
denote a length
|\gamma_{x,y}|
directed path from
x
and
y

\gamma_{x,y}
be the path of shortest distance between
x
and
y
, in which case
|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq n+1
.

A uniform lower bound on the transition probability
\mathbf{B}_{a,b}
is obtained at the transitions to the
\{0,n\}
states (see (
31

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\geq\frac{1}{n\lambda}\min\left(\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}}{q},\tilde{\gamma}_{\min}\right)\text{ for all }i\neq j.

(42)

Let
a,b\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and consider any path
\gamma_{x,y}
such that the edge
(a,b)\in\gamma_{x,y}
. Since
\gamma_{x,y}
is the path of shortest distance from
x
and
y
, any vertex visited along
\gamma_{x,y}
must lie between
x
and
y
and so

\displaystyle x\leq a\leq y\quad\text{ if }x<y,\quad\quad y\leq a\leq x\quad\text{ if }y<x.

To bound
\nu(x)\nu(y)/\nu(a)
,
without loss of generality suppose
x<y
and so
x\leq a\leq y
. Recall that
\nu
is a binomial distribution with mode
\lfloor(n+1)\frac{q}{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}+q}\rfloor
. Hence,

\displaystyle\nu(a)\geq\nu(x)\text{ if }a\leq\lfloor(n+1)\frac{q}{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}+q}\rfloor\quad\quad\nu(a)\geq\nu(y)\text{ if }a\geq\lfloor(n+1)\frac{q}{\tilde{\gamma}_{\star}+q}\rfloor,

(43)

which implies that for any vertex
a
that lies inside
\gamma_{x,y}
we have
\nu(x)\nu(y)/\nu(a)\leq 1
.

Finally, note that there are in total
n(n+1)
paths since there are
|\{0,\ldots,n\}|=n+1
states, with a directed path to each of the other
n
states. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\neq y:(a,b)\in\gamma_{x,y}}\frac{\nu(x)\nu(y)}{\nu(a)}\leq n(n+1).

(44)

Since
a
and
b
were arbitrary, we finally have by (
42
), (
44
), and using
|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq n+1
that

\displaystyle\max_{a\neq b:\mathbf{B}_{a,b}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\nu(a)\mathbf{B}_{a,b}}\sum_{x\neq y:(a,b)\in\gamma_{x,y}}\nu(x)\nu(y)|\gamma_{x,y}|\leq\frac{\lambda n^{2}(n+1)^{2}}{\min(\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{\max}}{q},\tilde{\gamma}_{\min})}.

∎

With the lower bound on

in hand, we now use (

) to establish an upper bound on
in (
