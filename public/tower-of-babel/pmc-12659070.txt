Gut microbial tyramine facilitates intestinal damage and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease development
Authors: Jia Wei, Shuangquan Liu, Jiayou Luo, Fei Yang, Wen Dai, Zhihang Huang, Xiongfeng Pan, Miyang Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota and intestinal injury are crucial in pediatric metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), yet the role of key gut microbial metabolites such as tyramine in pediatric MASLD remains largely unknown. In this study, we aimed to explore the role of gut microbial tyramine in intestinal damage and MASLD development in children.MethodsWe investigated the functions and mechanisms of previously isolated Enterococcus faecium B6 (E. faecium B6) and its derived tyramine in a mice model of intestinal injury and MASLD development. An integrative analysis of transcriptomics and proteomics was performed on mouse liver to explore the molecular mechanisms of tyramine in MASLD progression. Targeted metabolomics was performed using fecal samples from a hospital-based population (27 MASLD cases and 27 matched controls) to measure tyramine levels. The association of serum tyramine and MASLD risk was then validated in a school-based population, using serum samples of 294 children in the MASLD group and 235 controls.ResultsE. faecium B6 and its metabolite tyramine significantly disrupted the intestinal barrier and increased intestinal permeability in mice. Tyramine supplementation promoted MASLD-related metabolic phenotype in mice. Multi-omics analysis indicated that the PPAR signaling pathway played an important role in the molecular mechanisms. Tyramine contributed to lipid accumulation mainly by increasing lipid synthesis and lipid uptake but reducing the β-oxidation processes in the mouse liver, as shown by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction and western blot. Furthermore, we demonstrated from the hospital-based cohort that tyramine concentration was significantly higher in the MASLD group than in the control group. Consistently, the school-based cohort demonstrated a higher risk of MASLD in the high-tyramine group compared to the low-tyramine group, with adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 3.65 (95% CI: 2.66–4.32).ConclusionsThese results demonstrated that gut microbial tyramine effectively induced intestinal damage and facilitated MASLD development in mice. Tyramine was positively associated with the risk of MASLD in children. This study offered mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of MASLD and opened therapeutic opportunities for such metabolic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04461-9.

Background

The rising prevalence of obesity foreshadows a significant burden of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [1]. Globally, MASLD prevalence is rapidly increasing, with more than half of children with obesity affected worldwide [2, 3]. MASLD not only damages the liver but also exacerbates the risk of extrahepatic diseases, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and kidney injury, imposing an enormous economic burden [4, 5]. However, the etiological and pathological mechanisms of MASLD development have not been fully elucidated.

Emerging evidence highlights the gut-liver axis as a pivotal factor in MASLD pathogenesis. Gut microbiota dysbiosis and intestinal barrier dysfunction in MASLD have attracted significant attention [6, 7]. Epidemiological studies have revealed that patients with intestinal inflammation possess a higher risk of MASLD development [8, 9]. Moreover, a large cohort study indicates that MASLD is more prevalent in patients with intestinal inflammation, especially at younger ages [10]. Experimental studies also demonstrate a critical link between intestinal injury and MASLD, indicating the underlying mechanisms involving gut microbiota [11]. In particular, gut microbiota and their metabolites may modulate hepatic metabolism through the gut-liver axis [12]. Anatomically, venous blood transports microbial-derived products from the gut into the portal vein, supplying 75% of the liver’s blood. Moreover, the liver may regulate gut functions by secreting bile acids and immune molecules directly into the intestine. Mouries et al. have suggested that gut dysbiosis may contribute to intestinal damage and inflammation [7]. Increased gut permeability represents an essential mechanism in MASLD pathogenesis [13].

Gut microbiota actively participates in the metabolism of aromatic amino acids linked to MASLD. Phenylalanine-derived phenylethylamine and phenylacetic acid are implicated in metabolic syndrome pathogenesis and hepatic triglyceride accumulation [14, 15]. Increased tyrosine metabolism pathway has been correlated with obesity and MASLD [16, 17]. Notably, tyramine, a vital metabolite derived from tyrosine, is also recognized as a microbiota-associated metabolite [18, 19]. Recent studies highlight the role of tyramine in gut-liver regulation [20]. It is associated with inflammatory bowel disease, which increases the risk of colon cancer and MASLD [21, 22]. Excessive tyramine disrupts barrier function and promotes hepatic steatosis via α2A-adrenergic receptor inhibition [23]. Gram-positive bacteria-derived tyramine enhances intestinal lipid clearance via adrenergic receptor activation [24]. Consistent with its pathological potential, untargeted metabolomics shows higher tyramine levels in mice with high-fat-induced MASLD [25]. Gavage with high tyramine levels significantly aggravates high-fat diet-induced liver damage in mice [26]. However, the relationship between tyramine and MASLD in children is poorly understood. Our earlier study reveals that a tyramine-producing strain, Enterococcus faecium B6 (E. faecium B6), could significantly contribute to MASLD development [27], but its direct impacts on intestinal injury and subsequent liver reprogramming have not been explored.

In this work, based on our previously isolated E. faecium B6, we explored the role of strain B6 and its derived tyramine on intestinal damage for the first time. An integrative analysis of liver transcriptomic and proteomic datasets was conducted to provide mechanistic insights into the role of tyramine in MASLD. Furthermore, the causality of tyramine and MASLD was pioneeringly validated in both the hospital-based and school-based cohorts. Our findings indicate that gut microbial tyramine is a crucial link between intestinal damage and MASLD development in children with obesity. This study reveals the critical role of the intestine in MASLD development, providing new insights for future MASLD management.

Methods

Animal experiment

All animal experiment protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee and adhered to the Animal Ethics Statement of Central South University (XMSB-2022-0027). Procedures followed the Guidelines for Central South University Animal Experimentation, with humane care provided according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6J male mice (aged 3–4 weeks) were selected for their well-characterized susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction and to minimize the sex-specific variability. The model has been widely used in gut microbiota and MASLD research [28]. Following the 3R principles to minimize animal use and based on previous similar studies [27, 29], ten mice were allocated to each group (n = 10 per group). All mice were provided by Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China) and housed in standard filter-top cages under SPF environment with controlled conditions (24 ± 2 ℃, 12/12-h light/dark cycle, and 60 ± 10% relative humidity). Throughout the experiment, mice were monitored daily with ad libitum access to filtered water and food. The ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal experiments were followed (Additional file 2).

Animal model 1

The roles of E. faecium B6 on intestinal damage were investigated in vivo. After 1 week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into four groups using a random number table, administered with either saline or E. faecium B6 (B6 group; 3 × 109 CFU/mL, 200 μL/day) under the normal-chow diet (NCD) and the high-fat diet (HFD). Detailed information, including the NCD group, the NCD+B6 group, the HFD group, and the HFD+B6 group, is described in our previous report [27]. After 12 weeks, serum and colon tissues were collected to evaluate intestinal injury.

Animal model 2

To explore whether tyramine damaged the intestine and affected MASLD development, after 1 week of adaptive feeding, we randomly divided the mice into four groups using a random number table: normal-chow diet (the NCD group), normal-chow diet with tyramine supplied (the NCD + Tyr group), high-fat diet (the HFD group), and high-fat diet with tyramine supplied (the HFD + Tyr group). Tyramine was administered by drinking water in a 0.2% dilution. Body weight and food intake were monitored weekly. After 12 weeks, all mice were fasted overnight and euthanized. Fecal, serum, colorectal, and liver samples were collected for further analysis.

In addition, a detailed description of the histopathology, biochemical assays, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and Western blotting analysis (Additional file 1: Methods Text S1–S4) [27].

Integrative analysis of the transcriptomic and proteomic data

The transcriptomics and proteomics sequence was performed at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) (Additional file 2: Text S5-S6) [30]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to investigate the repeatability of the repetitive samples in the group and the different degrees of the samples between the groups. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were clustered by hierarchical clustering analysis and visualized using heatmaps. KEGG pathway analysis based on the hypergeometric probability distribution was used to explain the functional enrichment of differential genes and proteins. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using Human Collections in MsigDB (v.6.2), and the false discovery rate (FDR) with the Benjamini–Hochberg method lower than 0.25 (Padj < 0.25) was considered statistically significant [31]. Transcriptomics and proteomics data were integrated by mapping the UniProt accessions to Ensembl gene IDs. Venn analysis was used to identify the protein and corresponding mRNA levels. The correlation of all the identified proteins and matched mRNA expression was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis.

Study population

Hospital-based population

Children with MASLD were recruited from Hunan Children’s Hospital (Hunan, China) between April 2019 and March 2022. Children with obesity who visited the Hospital for health examinations were included as a control group. Before analysis, the eligible participants were matched using the propensity score matching method according to age, sex, and BMI. Fifty-four participants (27 simple obesity and 27 MASLD) were included. Fecal samples were used for targeted metabolomic analysis (Additional file 2: Text S7) [32–34].

School-based population

Children were recruited from primary and secondary schools (Hunan, China) for the school-based cohort between April 2021 and March 2022. Five hundred twenty-nine participants were separated into the MASLD group (n = 294) and the control group (n = 235). Dietary intake patterns were assessed via validated questionnaires. Blood samples were drawn after 12 h of fasting, and serum samples were prepared for biochemical markers measurement using an automatic biochemical analyzer at the Hunan Children’s Hospital.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants have been established to minimize the risk of confounding factors. Obesity was assessed using age-specific BMI thresholds [35]. MASLD was evaluated by liver biopsy or imaging [36]. Inclusion criteria: (1) age 6–18 years; (2) obesity; (3) no severe disability, cancer, or malignant tumors; (4) no infections within the previous month; (5) no antibiotics, prebiotics, or probiotics within 3 months before sample collection; (6) child and/or guardians provided informed consent; (7) completed relevant laboratory examinations. Exclusion criteria: (1) Excessive alcohol consumption (> 140 g/week for boys, > 70 g/week for girls); (2) other diseases including autoimmune liver disease, pancreatitis, hemochromatosis, viral liver disease, Wilson’s disease, drug-induced liver injury; (3) specific conditions causing fatty liver such as drugs, total parenteral nutrition, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, hypothyroidism, Cushing syndrome, β lipoprotein deficiency, α−1 antitrypsin deficiency, and insulin resistance-related syndromes; (4) severe comorbidities (such as renal insufficiency, heart disease, lung disease, malignant tumors) affecting metabolic state; (5) mental illness rendering them unable to understand the study. The controls were defined as children with obesity who had no metabolic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and other severe conditions. The STROBE Statement for reporting observational studies was followed (Additional file 3).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), the R software package (version 4.2.3), and SPSS software (version 24.0). Power calculations were done using G*Power 3.1 software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the distribution type of the data. Data were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range). In animal experiments, we used the Student’s T test to compare the difference in numerical variables between the two groups within the same dietary context. Moreover, in the hospital-based and school-based cohorts, the Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare the differences in numerical variables between the two groups. Multivariate logistic analysis was performed to explore the risk of tyramine in children with MASLD, with covariates such as age, sex, and BMI adjusted. A correlation between tyramine and MASLD clinical indicators was performed using Spearman correlation analysis. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless stated otherwise.

Results

E. faecium B6 colonization increases tyramine levels and promotes intestinal injury in mice

Our previous study indicated that E. faecium B6 colonization facilitated MASLD development in mice. To investigate the important role of intestinal damage in E. faecium B6 aggravating MASLD, we evaluated the structure and function of the mouse colorectum in different groups (Fig. 1A). After 12 weeks of E. faecium B6 intervention, the length of the colorectum was shorter in the NCD+B6 group and the HFD+B6 group than that of the NCD group and the HFD group, respectively (Fig. 1B, C). The histological structure of the colorectum was further studied. As shown in Fig. 1D, treatment with E. faecium B6 contributed to epithelial disruption, crypt confusion, loss of goblet cells, and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 1D). Consistently, the histological score of colorectum tissues was significantly higher in the NCD + B6, HFD, and HFD + B6 groups (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, the levels of bacterial-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS), d-lactate (d-lac), and intestinal intracellular diamine oxidase (DAO) in serum were significantly higher in the E. faecium B6 treatment group than those of the control group (Fig. 1F). Moreover, we found that the concentration of tyramine, a vital metabolite produced by E. faecium B6, was significantly increased in the E. faecium B6 colonization group (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, E. faecium B6 treatment significantly decreased the expression of tight junction proteins and inflammatory cytokines, including ZO-1, Claudin-1, Claudin-4, and IL-10 in colorectal tissues compared with the control group (Fig. 1H, I). Tyramine damages intestinal barrier functions in mice

To further validate the biological significance of tyramine on the intestine, tyramine supplementation was performed in mouse models fed NCD and HFD diets (Fig. 2A). The length of the colorectum was shorter in the NCD + Tyr, HFD, and HFD + Tyr groups when compared with the NCD group (Fig. 2B, C). Moreover, we noted that the level of tyramine was significantly higher in tyramine-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2D). Consistently, the histopathological evaluation indicated that the histological scores of colorectal tissue, such as disruption of the epithelial barrier, inflammatory cell infiltration, and enhanced erosion, were higher in tyramine-treated mice than those of the vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2E, F). The levels of LPS, D-Lac, and DAO in serum were substantially increased after long-term tyramine supplementation, which indicated the severity of intestinal leakage induced by tyramine (Fig. 2G). Moreover, the serum concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and tyramine were markedly higher in tyramine-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2H, I). Meanwhile, long-term tyramine supplementation significantly decreased the mRNA expression of genes related to tight junction (Zo-1, Claudin-1, Claudin-4, and IL-10) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) in the colorectum (Fig. 2J). Therefore, tyramine impaired intestinal barrier functions, which might allow gut microbial tyramine to translocate into the circulation and further contribute to systemic inflammation in mice. Tyramine supplementation facilitates MASLD in mice

We further asked whether tyramine could contribute to MASLD development. The results showed that tyramine supplementation significantly raised body weight, body weight change percentage, liver weight, and liver index (Fig. 3A–D). Tyramine had no significant effect on daily food or water consumption in mice (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Moreover, many fat vacuoles and inflammatory cells were found in the NCD + Tyr, HFD, and HFD + Tyr groups, and the lipid droplets were stained reddish brown with oil red O (Fig. 3E). The steatosis and inflammation scores were significantly higher in tyramine-supplemented mice than in vehicle-treated mice, with the tyramine-treated group showing significantly elevated NAFLD activity scores (NAS) (Fig. 3F). Biochemical analysis demonstrated that tyramine-treated mice appeared to have higher levels of fat storage and liver injury, given that the hepatic and serum levels of total triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), LDL-c, aspartate transaminase (AST), and alanine transaminase (ALT) were elevated, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) level was decreased, compared to the vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 3G and Additional file 2: Fig. S2). Furthermore, the tyramine-treated mice had significantly higher liver concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, major pro-inflammatory cytokines of developing steatohepatitis, congruent with histological pictures (Fig. 3H). In addition, the mRNA level of genes related to fatty acid oxidation and transporters was significantly down-regulated (Fig. 3I, J). In contrast, the mRNA level of genes related to fatty acid synthesis was significantly upregulated in the liver tissues (Fig. 3K). Tyramine changes the transcriptomic profiling of the liver

To systematically elucidate the global gene expression profiles and uncover the potential pathways underlying tyramine impacts on MASLD, we performed a supervised RNA-seq in mice livers harvested from the NCD, NCD + Tyr, HFD, and HFD + Tyr groups. Principal components analysis (PCA) showed a distinct separation among the NCD, NCD + Tyr, HFD, and HFD + Tyr groups (Fig. 4A). A total of 1050 and 569 DEGs were significantly changed in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and the HFD + Tyr vs. HFD, including 543 upregulated genes and 507 downregulated genes in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD, as well as 473 upregulated genes and 96 downregulated genes in HFD + Tyr vs. HFD (Fig. 4B). Moreover, Venn analysis identified 113 (7.50%) DEGs shared in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and HFD + Tyr vs. HFD (Fig. 4C). Next, we performed an enrichment analysis using both the upregulated and downregulated DEGs to explore the potential molecular pathways involved in tyramine-related MASLD. KEGG pathway analysis of the DEGs highlighted the common enriched pathways in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and HFD + Tyr vs. HFD, including the PPAR signaling pathway, retinol metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, AMPK signaling pathway, and insulin signaling pathway (Fig. 4D). Similarly, Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs of the two comparison groups were significantly enriched in cholesterol metabolism, lipid metabolism, and triglyceride metabolism (Additional file 2: Fig. S3). In addition, the expression pattern of commonly up- or downexpressed genes in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and HFD + Tyr vs. HFD was highlighted (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) results suggested that the DEGs associated with cholesterol biosynthesis, fatty acid beta-oxidation, and PPAR signaling pathways were prominently enriched (Fig. 4F). Cholesterol and fatty acids are essential compounds for lipid metabolism. Supplementation of tyramine might significantly influence lipid metabolism in the liver. Tyramine changes the proteomic profiling of the liver

Next, we analyzed the global protein signatures of the mouse liver. Hierarchical clustering analysis suggested significantly distinguished DEP profiles in the NCD, NCD + Tyr, HFD, and HFD + Tyr groups (Fig. 5A). Moreover, a total of 292 and 546 DEPs were identified in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and HFD + Tyr vs. HFD, of which 122 proteins were upregulated, and 170 proteins were down-regulated in the NCD + Tyr group compared with the NCD group, 158 proteins were upregulated, and 388 proteins were downregulated in the HFD + Tyr group compared with the HFD group (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the DEPs were significantly enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, and Notch signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). In addition, GO annotations suggested that the DEPs were mainly distributed in the cellular anatomical entity, cellular process, biological regulation, metabolic processes, and binding (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). Furthermore, an integrative transcriptomic and proteomic data analysis was performed to systematically evaluate the correlation between proteins and their corresponding mRNA expression. We observed a moderate expression correlation between the transcriptomic and proteomic profiles (Spearman rho = 0.559, P < 0.0001, Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Moreover, Venn analysis suggested that 134 and 97 pairs of DEGs and DEPs were matched in NCD + Tyr vs. NCD and HFD + Tyr vs. HFD. Specifically, compared to the NCD group, we found 45 upregulations and 40 downregulations of DEGs and their corresponding proteins in the HFD + Tyr group. Compared to the HFD group, we found 30 upregulations and 34 downregulations of DEGs and their corresponding proteins in the HFD + Tyr group (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). In addition, the expression of some DEGs and DEPs involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation was further verified by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 5E). Tyramine might concurrently influence the expression of these crucial genes/proteins in the liver, which probably facilitated MASLD development.

Tyramine promotes lipid accumulation and inflammation in the liver

According to the transcriptomics and proteomics data, the PPAR and AMPK signaling pathways were significantly involved in tyramine-related MASLD. Changes in these signaling pathways were closely related to fatty acid synthesis and oxidation changes. Moreover, the DEGs and DEPs with consistently changing trends were identified. The qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, such as lipid synthesis genes Ppar-γ, Fasn, and Hmgcs1, significantly increased in tyramine-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (Figs. 6A, 5E). On the contrary, the genes related to lipid oxidation (Retsat, Cpt-1α) and lipid transporters (Acot1, Acot3, Plin2) were significantly decreased (Figs. 6B, 5E). Moreover, the expression of genes related to lipid uptake (Cd36) was significantly increased (Fig. 6C). In addition, genes related to inflammation, such as Nf-κb, Tnf-α, Il-6, and Il-1β, were increased dramatically by tyramine (Figs. 6D, 5E). Thus, we performed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of these co-expressed proteins, and we observed that these crucial proteins (PPAR-γ, FASN, CD36, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, RETSAT, and CPT-1α) with a high degree of connectivity (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, Western blot analysis confirmed a significant increase in the expression of lipid synthesis-related proteins (PPAR-γ, FASN), lipid uptake-related protein CD36, and inflammatory proteins (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), while a significant decrease in expression of lipid oxidation-related proteins (RETSAT and CPT-1α) (Fig. 6F). Alteration of fecal metabolites in children with MASLD

To better explore the associations of gut microbial metabolites in childhood MASLD, we performed a targeted metabolomic analysis in the fecal samples of the hospital-based cohort containing 27 children with MASLD and 27 matched controls (Fig. 7A). Children in the MASLD and control groups did not differ significantly in age, sex, BMI, blood pressure, waist circumference, hip circumference, fat percentage, and visceral fat (P > 0.05, Additional file 2: Table S1). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) showed that the fecal metabolic profiles of children with MASLD were significantly different from those of control children (P < 0.001, Fig. 7B). Moreover, thirty-three differential metabolites were identified in the comparison of the MASLD group and the control group (Fig. 7C). In particular, there was no significant difference in tyrosine levels between the MASLD and control groups. However, tyrosine-derived tyramine was significantly increased in the MASLD group. Consistently, we observed a considerable decline in p-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid, which was downstream of tyramine, between the MASLD group and the control group (Fig. 7D). Inversely, another metabolite derived from tyrosine, 4-Hydroxycinnamic acid, had a lower level in the MASLD group when compared to the control group (Fig. 7D). Enrichment analysis on the disease signatures dataset suggested that these differential metabolites were associated with intestinal diseases and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (has been renamed MASLD) (Fig. 7D). In addition, the hospital-based cohort study had 88.07% power for the primary outcomes. This suggested that increased production and impaired degradation of tyramine probably occurred in children with MASLD. Increased tyramine level is associated with a higher risk of MASLD.

A total of 529 children were recruited from primary and secondary schools to validate our findings. These children were divided into the MASLD group (n = 294) and the control group (n = 235) based on the hepatic steatosis change (Fig. 8A). There was no significant difference in dietary habits between the MASLD cases and the obesity controls (Additional file 2: Table S2, S3). In line with the hospital-based cohort and animal experiment, the MASLD group had a higher level of tyramine than the control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 8B). Multivariate logistic regression showed that tyramine level was a risk factor for MASLD among children with MASLD after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, with the highest odds ratio (OR, 3.65; 95% CI, 2.66–4.32; P < 0.001) (Fig. 8C). Importantly, serum tyramine was positively associated with the level of alanine transaminase (ALT, r = 0.588, P < 0.001), aspartate transaminase (AST, r = 0.584, P < 0.001), total triglycerides (TG, r = 0.623, P < 0.001), total cholesterol (TC, r = 0.579, P < 0.001), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c, r = 0.455, P < 0.001), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c, r = − 0.324, P < 0.001) (Fig. 8D). Discussion

Gut microbiota and intestinal damage were crucial factors involved in MASLD development. Our previous research suggested that E. faecium B6 and its metabolite tyramine might promote MASLD. In this work, we demonstrated that E. faecium B6 and tyramine could lead to intestinal damage for the first time. Moreover, supplementation with tyramine facilitated MASLD-related metabolic phenotype by reprogramming the lipid metabolic process and inflammatory pathways in the liver. Furthermore, we also validated that tyramine significantly increased the risk of MASLD in children. Therefore, we speculate that gut microbial-related tyramine might impair intestinal barrier functions and trigger MASLD progression. These findings demonstrate that tyramine increases the risk of MASLD, impairs gut barrier function, and facilitates MASLD development. Modulating tyramine levels and ameliorating intestinal damage in high-risk children might improve MASLD management.

This study demonstrated that E. faecium B6 and its metabolite tyramine increased intestinal permeability and contributed to intestinal damage. Tyramine disrupted intestinal barrier integrity by downregulating tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin-1, Claudin-4) and elevating serum LPS, D-lactate, and DAO, which facilitated microbial metabolite translocation to the liver. Growing evidence suggested that the disruption of the so-called gut-liver axis was linked to an elevated risk of MASLD development [37]. The establishment of barriers in the gut and liver, such as the intestinal epithelium, is essential to restrict gut-derived microorganisms and their metabolites from getting into circulation [38]. Abnormally increased intestinal permeability might be one of the key mechanisms involved, which probably expedited the transfer of microbial metabolites to the liver and contributed to MASLD [39]. As is well-known, the intestines can keep harmful substances like bacteria and toxins from penetrating the intestinal mucosa and reaching other tissues, organs, and the blood circulation. Healthy intestinal morphology and a complete intestinal mucosal barrier are key elements of this defense [12]. LPS, d-lactate, and DAO were reliable circulating biomarkers for intestinal barrier integrity and bacterial translocation [40]. Tight junction proteins, such as CLAUDINS and ZO-1, were the physiological regulators of intestinal permeability and were typically associated with intestinal inflammation [41, 42]. In this work, we first found that tyramine-treated mice exhibited higher levels of inflammatory cytokines in the circulation, indicating systemic inflammation. Previous in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that tyramine might damage the intestine and promote inflammation [43, 44]. Recent studies also suggested that Enterococcus-derived tyramine might disrupt the reconstitution of the epithelial barrier and exacerbate colitis [23]. Glymenaki et al. suggested that fecal tyramine promotes colon cancer risk via increased DNA damage, cell proliferation, and inflammation [44]. Few studies have explored the important role of tyramine in the liver. Yang et al. found that combined histamine (20 mg/kg) and tyramine (100 mg/kg) administration via oral gavage for 6 days to mice with a high-fat diet could increase AST/ALT and IL-1β levels in the liver, which indicated liver damage in obese mice [26]. However, the mediated roles of gut microbial tyramine in intestinal damage and MASLD remain unclear. These findings further supported that gut microbial and intestinal disturbance were essential in promoting MASLD. Future research elucidating the crosstalk between the gut and liver to restore intestinal functions compromised by tyramine exposure could represent a promising therapeutic approach for managing MASLD in pediatric populations.

Furthermore, we demonstrated that supplementation of tyramine might facilitate MASLD development in a young mouse model. The hepatic steatosis characteristics observed in this work were consistent with the crucial features of MASLD, including lipid droplets and inflammation occurring in the liver [45]. Specifically, we found that tyramine supplementation could induce MASLD-related metabolic phenotypes even in mice fed a normal-chow diet and aggravated MASLD development in mice under a high-fat diet. This indicated synergistic effects between an unhealthy diet and excessive tyramine in driving NALFD progression. Public health initiatives could focus on reducing intake of tyramine precursors (e.g., aged cheeses) and decreasing tyramine-producing bacteria (e.g., Enterococcus spp.). Moreover, tyramine accumulation resulted in the adrenal medulla releasing excessive epinephrine above the limits of sympathetic release, which might contribute to adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes [46]. Consistently, gut microbial tyramine was essential for hypertension since it could increase blood pressure by regulating catecholamines released from nerve terminals [19, 26]. The impacts of tyramine were significantly reduced through oral administration [47]. Although few studies examined the direct influence of tyramine on targets besides nerve terminals, it was suggested that high intake of dietary tyramine in mice tended to facilitate glucose handling [48]. Besides, the inverse effect of tyramine on glucose uptake has already been demonstrated in rat adipocytes, cardiomyocytes, and human adipocytes [49, 50]. In addition, tyramine is a monoamine oxidase (MAO) substrate, and hepatic MAO-A expression has been demonstrated to increase the incidence of MASLD disease [51]. Gut microbial tyramine may play an important role in glycolipid metabolic processes, but little is known about its effects on liver metabolism, let alone its underlying mechanisms in MASLD development.

Given that gut microbial metabolites potentially contributed to the development of MASLD in multiple signaling pathways, we performed a combination of transcriptomic and proteomic analysis to fully unravel the changes in the liver caused by tyramine. This approach is well-established for investigating the mechanisms of gut metabolite-mediated host disease [52]. Although the correlation between transcriptomic and proteomic profiles is moderate, the coefficient is higher than in some previous studies [53, 54]. The inconsistency in up and downregulation trends is probably due to post-transcriptional regulation, protein translation, and protein post-translational regulation [55]. Notably, the significantly higher number of up-regulated genes suggests that tyramine mainly promotes MASLD by upregulating gene expression. While inconsistencies exist, transcriptome data reveal early regulatory events. Integrating multi-omics data is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of biological processes. We focused on the consistent transcriptomic and proteomic data in this work. Critically, our integrative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses consistently highlighted the PPAR signaling pathway as central to tyramine-induced MASLD pathogenesis. KEGG enrichment revealed PPAR pathway dysregulation in both DEGs and DEPs. Experiment results further confirmed that tyramine facilitates MASLD by promoting lipid synthesis and inflammatory pathways via the PPAR signaling pathway, with concordant upregulation and suppression observed at both mRNA and protein levels. Carpéné et al. suggested that tyramine initiated several insulin-like cellular processes, including the induction of glucose transport, lipogenesis, and adipogenesis [50]. However, the key genes and proteins responsible for tyramine-associated hepatic steatosis have not been fully identified. This multi-omics analysis indicated that PPAR signaling was a key mechanistic node mediating the effects of gut-derived tyramine on hepatic lipid metabolism and inflammation.

PPAR-γ is a vital ligand-activated receptor in the PPAR signaling pathway, which is intimately linked to the development and management of MASLD [56]. PPAR-γ expression was elevated in steatotic liver models in both humans and experimental animals, primarily in the hepatocytes, which led to hepatocyte steatosis and activated hepatic stellate cells [57, 58]. Moreover, FASN, CD36, CPT-1α, and RETSAT were crucial targets of PPAR-γ that mediated fatty acid synthase, fatty acid uptake, and mitochondrial β-oxidation; thus, abnormal expression of these genes in the liver resulted in lipid accumulation and hepatic steatosis development [59, 60]. Notably, we first found that the downregulation of RETSAT participated in MASLD development. RETSAT, all-trans-retinol 13,14-reductase, was a specific enzyme responsible for metabolizing all-trans-retinol to all-trans-13,14-dihydroretinol [61]. Consistently, our previous study suggested that all-trans-13,14-dihydroretinol was associated with metabolic syndrome in children and caused lipid accumulation in HepG2 cells [32]. Recently, Ho et al. also indicated that the Retsat-Fasn pathway contributed to lipid accumulation in livers [62]. Moreover, Retsat depletion might activate the transcription of the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway, thus catalyzing the generation of monounsaturated fatty acids [63]. We first demonstrated that Retsat was essential in tyramine-induced hepatic steatosis and MASLD development. In addition, inflammation was a typical feature of MASLD development, and tyramine exacerbated liver damage and inflammation in HFD-induced early-stage MASLD [26]. Collectively, the available data connected gut microbial tyramine to the whole complicated lipid metabolic network and inflammatory response. Tyramine activated the PPAR-γ signaling pathway, upregulating FASN and CD36 to promote lipid synthesis and uptake, while suppressing CPT-1α and RETSAT to inhibit β-oxidation and redox. Moreover, tyramine also significantly increased the expression of NF-κB, IL-6, and TNF-α, which were typical pro-inflammatory indicators. This dual effect on lipid metabolism and inflammation significantly promoted MASLD development [64]. Pharmacological inhibition of bacterial tyrosine decarboxylase and improvement of the gut barrier may reduce tyramine accumulation and suppress inflammation, offering novel insights for developing drug candidates against MASLD.

In addition, we further demonstrated that increased tyramine was associated with an enhanced risk of MASLD in children. Except for host metabolic processes, several bacteria could produce tyramine using aromatic amino acids, such as Clostridium asparagiforme, Enterococcus faecalis, and Ruminococcus gnavus [65]. Recent studies suggested that MASLD patients had a higher serum level of tyrosine, a precursor of tyramine, and tyrosine was considered a biomarker of MASLD [66, 67]. We found no significant difference in fecal tyrosine levels between the MASLD group and the control group, but observed an increase in tyrosine-derived tyramine and a decrease in tyrosine-derived p-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid in MASLD, supporting the critical roles of gut microbiota. Moreover, the association between tyrosine and inflammatory responses might be hindered by using fecal samples and not differentiating between subtypes of MASLD [68, 69]. Future studies using metabolic flux analysis and blood- or tissue-specific metabolomics based on MASLD severities could clarify the vital roles of tyrosine. To reduce confounding, we compared fecal metabolites of children using propensity score matching for age, sex, and BMI. We successfully identified the association between fecal tyramine and pediatric MASLD. Importantly, our finding was validated in a larger cohort based on children from school, which probably reduced the selection bias in hospitals. Moreover, we measured serum tyramine levels in the school-based cohort. Serum samples provided stable representation of systemic tyramine exposure, minimizing confounding by dietary fluctuations and sample collection variability, thereby allowing direct correlation between tyramine levels and MASLD markers (e.g., ALT, AST). Thus, this work provided full-scale epidemiological evidence of the association of tyramine with MASLD. Therefore, we conclude that tyramine might lead to intestinal damage, increase the risk of MASLD, and promote its development. Elevated fecal/serum tyramine levels could serve as a non-invasive biomarker for the risk of MASLD, contributing to early diagnosis, warning, and therapeutic monitoring of MASLD, especially in children with obesity.

In this work, we systematically revealed the associations of gut microbial tyramine with MASLD in mouse models and children’s cohorts. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms of MASLD driven by tyramine through the PPAR signaling pathway were elucidated by integrating transcriptomics and proteomics analysis. Notably, we demonstrated that tyramine might aggravate MASLD at least in part by disrupting the intestinal barriers and providing precise action nodes and intervention targets. This cross-validation study provided a paradigm for the mechanism study of metabolic diseases and embodied the advantages of the gut-liver axis in clinical application. The current study has some limitations that may serve as future avenues of research. While our study demonstrated a strong association between gut-derived tyramine and MASLD progression, future studies using fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from tyramine-producing or tyramine-deficient donors, or supplementation in germ-free models, could further confirm the causation. Additionally, validating the role of tyramine in both male and female pre-clinical models would provide deeper insight. Molecular mechanisms underlying post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation could be explored. Unmeasured confounders such as physical activity and other comorbid conditions might influence the risk of MASLD. Future studies with detailed lifestyle information are needed. Longitudinal studies would assess the temporal relationship between tyramine levels and MASLD progression. Collectively, this study reveals a connection between gut microbial tyramine and MASLD, providing key directions for future mechanistic work. There is a critical need to develop more effective therapeutic alternatives for MASLD and its related comorbidities.

Conclusions

In summary, tyramine could induce intestinal injury and contribute to MASLD progression by modulating both liver lipid metabolism and inflammation. Moreover, the association of gut microbial tyramine with MASLD was validated in two independent pediatric populations with obesity, highlighting the relevance of gut-derived tyramine in childhood obesity and early MASLD. These findings underscore the critical roles of the gut-liver axis in MASLD pathogenesis and identify tyramine as a key mediator, offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets of such disease. Targeting tyramine or its producing microbiota represents a promising strategy for controlling MASLD in the expanding population of children with obesity.
