1.
INTRODUCTION

Emotion education plays a critical role in the developmental years of children aged 3 to 6. According to developmental psychology, during this period, children begin to understand various emotions, such as anger, fear, and sadness, and learn to express and regulate these feelings
(
Bartlett et al. 1982
;
Saarni 1999
;
Wulandari 2021
)
.

Recent studies have explored AI-based systems that help children recognize, express, and manage their emotions. However, these systems primarily target children aged 8 and above, as they rely on independent conversations with LLM-based agents — for example, expressing emotions through conversations
(
Seo et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
)
. Such designs are not well-suited for younger children aged 3 to 6, since at this stage, parents play a foundational role in shaping children’s emotional development by guiding their early emotional learning at home
(
Fadlillah and Fauziah 2022
;
Alegre 2011
)
.
Unlike older children, those aged 3 to 6 require parental support to engage with technology
(
Toran et al. 2024
;
Morawska et al. 2023
)
and rely on their parents for emotional security and co-regulation
(
Corwin 2012
;
Brock and Kochanska 2016
)
.

However, providing effective emotion education can be challenging for parents, as most parents lack expertise in child psychology and strong communication skills
(
Sanders 1999
;
Saarni 1999
;
Cole et al. 1994
)
. In practice, parents often encounter difficulties, such as misreading children’s ambiguous emotional signals
(
Castro et al. 2015
)
or failing to support emotional regulation through conversations
(
Meng et al. 2020
)
.

To gain a more systematic understanding of these difficulties, we conducted interviews with five kindergarten teachers and five parents and identified three key challenges:
(
C_{1}
) limited parental expertise
in child psychology and communication techniques;
(
C_{2}
) insufficient feedback
on the effectiveness of emotion education for children;
(
C_{3}
) a lack of comprehensive understanding of children’s emotional states
. Furthermore, both parents and teachers expressed a clear need for AI-driven tools to facilitate emotion education at home.

We developed PACEE, an LLM-based assistant for supporting parent-AI collaborative emotion education for children aged 3 to 6.
PACEE helps parents engage in emotion education with their children through interactive adventure games.
PACEE consists of three key modules, each addressing one of the challenges mentioned above:
(1)
Parent-Child Adventure Game
. In each adventure game, parents and their children engage in a five-stage conversation that operationalizes Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
)
. The AI system provides parents with real-time guidance, such as actionable suggestions and progress tracking, thus solving
C_{1}
.
(2)
Parental Feedback Report
. This module provides parents with feedback after each adventure game. The feedback includes a comprehensive review of past parent-child conversations and actionable suggestions for future interactions, effectively addressing
C_{2}
.
(3)
Child Emotional Modeling
. This module creates an emotional profile for each child from two sources: the primary source being parents’ long-term understanding, elicited through an AI interviewer, supplemented by text-based analysis of parent-child conversation data. The emotional profile is then used to provide tailored guidance to parents during the adventure game. After each adventure game, it also offers a comparison between parents’ understanding of their child’s emotions and the AI’s analysis, thereby effectively addressing
C_{3}
.

To evaluate PACEE’s effectiveness in supporting family emotion education, we conducted a user study with 16 pairs of parents and children. Compared to a baseline (LLM-assisted) method, PACEE significantly fosters meaningful parent-child interactions and in-depth emotion education. Furthermore, with PACEE, children show greater engagement and richer emotional expression during conversations. Meanwhile, parents (1) improve their expertise and confidence in communication, (2) adopt more effective approaches in emotional education, and (3) gain a better understanding of their children’s emotional states.

The contributions of our research are as follows:

•

Through a formative study, we identified three key challenges in family emotion education, the role of AI in supporting this process, and principles for AI-assisted emotion education from the experts’ perspectives.

•

We present PACEE, an LLM-based assistant for supporting parent-AI collaborative emotion education. PACEE collaborates with parents to build children’s emotional profiles and provides personalized, parent-mediated guidance.

•

Through a user study with 16 families, we validated PACEE’s effectiveness in enhancing family emotion education. Our work advanced emotion coaching theory in both family and LLM-assisted contexts, offering valuable insights for designing AI-supported, parent-centered family education systems.

2.
RELATED WORK

2.1.
Emotion Coaching Techniques and Theories

Emotion coaching is a supportive parenting technique that helps children understand and regulate their emotions, and has been widely studied in the fields of parenting and child development
(
Fu et al. 2022
;
Havighurst and Kehoe 2021
)
. A key framework widely applied in HCI
(
Havighurst and Kehoe 2021
)
is Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
;
Gottman 2005
)
, which includes: (1) Be aware of the child’s emotion, (2) Recognize the child’s expression of emotion as a perfect moment for intimacy and teaching, (3) Listen with empathy and validate the child’s feelings, (4) Help the child learn to label their emotions with words, and (5) Set limits when helping the child to solve problems.
This guideline is often complemented by principles from positive psychology
(
Baker et al. 2017
;
Fan et al. 2018
)
, such as the PERMA model
(
Seligman 2010
)
. The PERMA model outlines five elements of well-being (Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Achievement) and has been applied in HCI areas such as well-being-centered design
(
Kovich et al. 2023
)
, persuasive technologies
(
Nebrida and Dullas 2018
)
, and digital mental health
(
Chisale and Phiri 2022
)
.

Current HCI implementations of emotion coaching primarily target older children or specific clinical populations. Research has explored using AI to enhance emotional expression and feedback for school-aged children and adolescents
(
Seo et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2018
;
Ibrahim et al. 2025
)
. Meanwhile, other research focuses on providing specialized learning support for children with clinical needs, such as autism
(
Tang et al. 2024
;
Ghiotti et al. 2023
;
Sharma et al. 2016
)
or trauma
(
Graßl and Fraser 2024
)
. However, insufficient attention is given to children aged 3 to 6, highlighting a critical gap in HCI-driven emotional education.

Compared to older children, 3- to 6-year-olds experience emotions more intensely and situationally, while having limited verbal and self-regulation skills
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
. These traits make parental guidance central for fostering emotional competence
(
Gottman et al. 1996
;
Hightower et al. 2019
)
. However, many parents face barriers such as time constraints and a lack of knowledge in addressing their children’s emotional needs
(
Ahmadpour et al. 2023
;
Quan et al. 2025
;
Brown et al. 2022
)
. Existing tools frequently overlook these developmental traits, assuming children can engage with AI independently and failing to empower parents in their crucial coaching role
(
Sharma et al. 2025
)
.

To address this gap, we propose a parent-centered, theory-grounded approach designed to empower parents as the primary emotion coaches for their 3- to 6-year-old children.

2.2.
Parent-AI Collaborative Coaching System

The introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) has opened new possibilities for children’s emotional development in modern family education. Based on how AI participates in parent-child interactions, existing research can be categorized into three types: AI-centered, parent-centered, and parent-AI collaborative systems.

AI-centered systems position AI as a “teaching tool” that interacts directly with children, offering guidance to help them
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Karunaratna et al. 2024
)
. For example, systems may generate personalized learning content
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
, support children in learning math language via story-based interaction
(
Xu et al. 2023b
)
, co-create stories to boost vocabulary and feedback quality
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, or generate context-based prompts to aid language expression
(
Seo et al. 2024
)
. These systems emphasize direct AI-child interaction.

Parent-centered systems emphasize parents as primary educators, with AI as a supportive tool that does not interact directly with the child. These studies often use interviews or co-design methods to explore parents’ needs and challenges
(
Dumaru and Al-Ameen 2023
;
Cingel and Piper 2017
;
Nikkhah et al. 2021
;
Hiniker et al. 2016
;
Mazmanian and Lanette 2017
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. For example, research shows how parents co-use digital media with children
(
Yu et al. 2024
)
and welcome daily prompts for reflection
(
Ibrahim et al. 2025
)
. However, few systems support real-world implementation, particularly in terms of sustained engagement and feedback mechanisms.

Parent-AI collaborative systems integrate the strengths of both parties through a division of labor, leveraging the complementary capabilities of each. Parents can act as information providers, content configurators, facilitators, and evaluators
(
Slovák et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Yarosh et al. 2013
)
. Meanwhile, AI is used to facilitate communication and storytelling
(
Choi et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, structure collaborative tasks and routines
(
Jonas et al. 2023
;
Xiao and Martin 2012
)
, and integrate smart devices into daily life
(
Theofanopoulou and Slovak 2022
;
Chan et al. 2017
)
. These systems establish a three-way interaction model among the parent, child, and AI.

Despite these developments, most systems still focus on school-aged children and involve direct AI-child interaction, overlooking the parents’ central role in early childhood. To address this, we propose a parent-centered, AI-supported approach, which aims to enhance, rather than replace, parental engagement.

2.3.
Modeling Children’s Emotional States

Personalized instruction is crucial in modern education and children’s emotional development
(
Bernacki et al. 2021
;
Dewey 2024
)
. Effective personalization requires modeling a child’s emotional state to enable targeted interventions. We examine the challenges of such modeling through two aspects: content design and assessment methods.

Regarding content design, a classic assessment for children’s emotional development is the Test of Emotion Comprehension (TEC)
(
Pons et al. 2004
;
Albanese et al. 2006
)
. TEC identifies nine key elements for evaluating children’s emotional competence, which have also been widely acknowledged in prior research, including: emotion recognition
(
Bullock and Russell 1985
)
, emotional expression
(
Barden et al. 1980
)
, emotion external cause understanding
(
Harris et al. 1989
)
, emotion-belief link
(
Fonagy et al. 1997
)
, emotion reminder effect
(
Harris 1983
)
, emotional masking
(
Gardner et al. 1988
)
, mixed emotional understanding
(
Arsenio and Lover 1995
)
, emotional morality
(
Harter and Whitesell 1989
)
, and emotion regulation
(
Altshuler and Ruble 1989
)
.

Regarding assessment methods, traditional approaches, such as parent-reported or teacher-reported questionnaires, offer high ecological validity but suffer from subjectivity and inefficiency
(
Denham et al. 1997
;
Rothbart et al. 2004
)
. In contrast, self-report instruments like TEIQue-CF
(
Mavroveli et al. 2008
)
provide direct insight into children’s self-perceived emotional competence. However, for young children aged 3 to 6, limited cognitive and expressive abilities
(
Wellman and Liu 2004
)
can lead to unreliable results. More recently, emerging AI systems, such as those using chatbots or games, show promise for automated observation
(
Santos et al. 2020
;
Nicolaidou et al. 2022
)
. However, these systems often struggle to interpret subtle and developmentally specific cues, especially when the data types available are limited due to privacy concerns
(
Calvo and D’Mello 2010
)
.

To address the limitations of existing assessment methods, we propose a human-AI collaborative framework that integrates the real-world understanding of parents with the multimodal analysis of AI, enabling more comprehensive emotional modeling.

4.1.
Procedure and Analysis

4.1.1.
Need-finding Interview with Experienced Kindergarten Teachers

We interviewed 5 experienced kindergarten teachers (3 female, 2 male;
M=9.40
years of experience,
SD=6.34
) recruited via social media, each receiving a $10 stipend.
Each interview session lasted about one hour and was conducted through video conferencing, moderated by two researchers.

First, we interviewed the teachers on several key topics, including common negative emotions in children aged 3-6, triggers, and the strategies employed by teachers. Next, we explored the potential role of AI in supporting emotion education. Specifically, we introduced the design objectives and scenarios of parents providing regular emotional guidance to their children at home, while the AI offers additional support throughout the process. Furthermore, we co-designed an AI-assisted system based on participants’ visions and expertise, utilizing conceptual design images to illustrate our design objectives (see Figure
3(b)
).

4.1.2.
Need-finding Interview with Parents of Children Aged 3-6

We also interviewed 5 parents (4 mothers, 1 father;
M=41.0
years old,
SD=3.16
) of children aged 3-6, recruited via social media, with $10 stipends. Each hour-long video session was moderated by two researchers.

First, we interviewed the parents about their observations of children’s emotional behaviors, challenges in guiding children’s emotions, and their understanding of emotional regulation. Next, our design scenarios explored the parents’ perspectives on AI-assisted tools, preferred features, and concerns.

Together with the kindergarten teachers’ sessions, these interview sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using a coding process by three HCI experts.

4.1.3.
Observational Experiment on Children’s Emotion Education

To additionally understand the context of how parents and children communicate and interact during emotion education conversations, we observed emotion education interactions in 5 families recruited via social media, with $10 compensation. The participants included four mothers, one father (
M=38.2
years old,
SD=3.49
), and their children (4 boys, 1 girl;
M=4.8
years old,
SD=1.17
). Each family received $10 or an equivalent gift as compensation.

Conducted in a lab over 30 minutes, parents guided their children based on a predefined script operationalizing Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
)
. Parents followed a script and selected one of three negative emotion scenarios provided. Sessions were video-recorded, analyzed, and annotated by three HCI experts. Finally, we conducted a brief interview to ask parents about their experiences with coaching and any difficulties they encountered.

Additionally, parental interviews revealed current practices and challenges in emotional communication and guidance. Combined with insights from observational experiments, we categorized the key challenges in family emotion education into three dimensions:

C_{1}

. Limited parental expertise.
All five interviewed parents (5/5) acknowledged lacking professional-level skills in child emotion education, which manifests in three key aspects. First, parents demonstrated limited psychological and educational knowledge, struggling to apply general theoretical guidelines to specific situations. For example, G2 remarked: “
I feel like I don’t have access to expert advice or reliable sources, and the examples in books don’t always apply to my kid’s situation.
” We also observed similar patterns in the observational study, as parents also reported that they “
don’t often apply every step before
”(O3) and “
don’t know what to say
” about certain steps (O1).
Second, parents reported insufficient communication techniques, emphasizing the need for “
concrete, easy-to-implement language and techniques
” (G1, G2, G5) in daily practice. Third, parents experience occasional impatience during emotional communication with their children. G3 observed that “
Sometimes my husband gets impatient when he and our child disagree, and it makes our child hesitant to open up and share feelings with him
”, illustrating how parental emotional instability can negatively impact communication effectiveness.

C_{2}

. Insufficient feedback.
Parents frequently lack objective, professional feedback when guiding children’s emotional development. As G3 noted, “
When he has strong emotions, I explain and comfort him in the moment, but I can’t tell if he really understood or whether my approach was effective
”. In the observational experiment, parents reported similar uncertainties (O4: “
I just followed the guidelines step-by-step, but I’m not sure each step worked. Next time this happens, he might forget what we discussed today.
”).

C_{3}

. A lack of understanding of children’s emotional states.
Most parents reported difficulties in accurately recognizing their children’s emotional states. This stems partially from limited awareness of school experiences, as G4 noted “
not knowing what happens at kindergarten since children don’t always share things at school.
” Parents may struggle to identify the root causes of emotions (G1: “
Once he cried because he was just too tired, but I initially thought it was because of something that happened at kindergarten.
”). Additionally, parents acknowledged their subjective biases when assessing their children (G5: “
We naturally think our child is the best, so this bias colors our evaluations. We need something objective to show how our child compares with peers.
”).

4.2.2.
Roles and Design Principles of AI in Family Emotion Education

During the interview sessions, parents expressed their vision for AI’s potential roles in emotional guidance, while experts highlighted the cognitive and emotional characteristics of young children that should guide system design.

All five participating parents expressed a preference for maintaining their primary role in emotional guidance while leveraging AI to supplement their limitations. They also envisioned specific roles for AI, including serving as a conversation facilitator, an expert resource, and a generative tool. 4/5 parents anticipated AI’s involvement as
a conversation facilitator
. For instance, G3 suggested that AI could dynamically adjust its level of participation based on the parent’s engagement: “
When parents take the lead, AI should speak less; when parents struggle, AI can step in to enrich the conversation and reduce communication barriers.
” G2 further highlighted AI’s potential to assist less experienced parents: “
If parents are unsure how to respond, AI could intervene. It could also detect and correct misguided parental guidance in real time.
”

All five parents expected AI to serve as
an expert resource
. By integrating relevant knowledge into conversations, as G4 noted: “
Providing real-time pedagogical insights or case studies, such as developmental benchmarks for 5-6-year-olds.
” Additionally, it could offer post-conversation reports to improve parents’ long-term educational skills and provide objective assessments of children’s behavior to counteract parental biases, with G4 adding: “
I hope AI can help me observe my child’s development more objectively and comprehensively.
”

Parents also proposed practical applications for AI functioning as
a generative tool
. AI could reduce communication burdens by “
generating images or videos to clarify scenarios for children
” or “
suggesting real-time conversation strategies
”. It could also produce post-interaction reports with performance feedback, as G5 mentioned: “
It could analyze which guidance techniques I applied successfully and how well I executed them.
”

The interview sessions with kindergarten teachers highlighted the key cognitive characteristics of 3- to 6-year-old children, informing the design of AI-assisted emotion education systems. First,
the system design must align with children’s developmental stage
. Considering children’s tendency toward concrete and visual thinking, the system should incorporate visual aids, such as pictorial representations, to help them better grasp emotional concepts. Additionally, given their limited language skills, the interface should guide parents on how to use age-appropriate language during emotional coaching. Second,
the system should provide personalized instruction to accommodate individual differences in emotional and cognitive development
. Experts emphasized that developmental abilities vary significantly among young children. Thus, it is crucial to consider each child’s specific cognitive traits and emotional states to deliver tailored learning strategies. An AI system could effectively track behavioral patterns and provide developmental benchmarks, enabling personalized adjustments to the learning process.

It is important to note that
these design principles enable us to delve deeper into some of the main features of the core modules
, which address three primary challenges in AI-assisted family emotion education. Since these principles are crucial design details that enhance the system’s functionality and user experience, they are not evaluated separately in subsequent studies.

5.
DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION OF PACEE

5.1.
User Interface and Design of System Modules

Based on the insights from the formative studies, we designed and implemented PACEE, an LLM-based assistant for supporting parent-AI collaborative emotion education. PACEE consists of 3 modules—
Parent-Child Adventure Game
,
Parental Feedback Report
, and
Child Emotional Modeling
— each addressing a key challenge in family emotion education.

During the five-stage conversation, PACEE offers a range of support for parents. As a conversation facilitator, PACEE can join the conversation when prompted by the parent, which helps reduce the communication burden. As an expert, PACEE provides professional advice for each of the 5 steps, considering the child’s emotional profile. It also periodically updates real-time advice, offering communication strategies tailored to the cognitive characteristics of children aged 3 to 6 years old. As a tool, PACEE can generate images of the current scenario for parents to show to their children, catering to children’s preference for visual thinking. Additionally, PACEE monitors the conversation’s progress and stage, providing useful references for parents to decide when to progress to the next stage. After completing a scenario, PACEE generates rewards, such as stars and medals, for the children, supporting their preference for encouragement-based learning.

To address the challenge of
C_{2}
insufficient feedback, we designed the
Parental Feedback Report
module. PACEE evaluates parents’ performance during the
Parent-Child Adventure Game
, and offers actionable guidance for future interactions. Over extended use, parents have the opportunity to unlock achievement badges, enhancing their sense of accomplishment.

For evaluating parental performance, PACEE analyzes real-time transcripts of parent-child conversations. This analysis provides insights into how parents effectively navigate the emotional education process and establish secure attachments with their children.

PACEE also delivers future suggestions grounded in established theories (e.g., Gottman’s guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
)
, PERMA model
(
Seligman 2010
)
). By tailoring these insights to the early learning and development guidelines for children aged 3 to 6 years
(
of Education of the People’s Republic of China 2012
)
, the system ensures the guidance is both practical and developmentally appropriate.

5.1.3.
Child Emotional Modeling

To address the challenge of
C_{3}
a lack of understanding of children’s emotional states, we designed the
Child Emotional Modeling
module. Grounded in the Test of Emotion Comprehension
(
Pons et al. 2004
;
Albanese et al. 2006
)
, we model children’s emotions across the following three dimensions:

•

Emotional understanding, including emotion recognition, emotion elicitors, emotion-related beliefs, the impact of memory on emotions, and mixed emotions.

•

Emotional expression, including emotional expression and emotional masking.

•

Emotional regulation, including emotion regulation abilities and moral emotions.

Figure 5
.

Mechanism of PACEE modeling children’s emotional profiles collaboratively with parents. It functions in two scenarios: (1) individual parent interviews conducted by an AI Interviewer and (2) AI-facilitated parent-child emotion conversations monitored by a Conversation Agent. The Cognitive Analyzer processes inputs from both scenarios, analyzing and synthesizing emotional profile data. The resulting Child’s Emotional Profile includes three sub-dimensions: emotion understanding, emotion expression, and emotion regulation. Additionally, it compares the parent’s understanding of the child’s emotions with conversational analysis by the AI, offering a nuanced perspective on the child’s emotional development.

Figure 6
.
Agent workflow of PACEE.

The primary data source for modeling is the parent’s long-term understanding of the child in the physical world (Scenario 1 in Figure
5
). This information is elicited through an AI interviewer, which follows a semi-structured interview workflow. The process begins by selecting a question from a fixed list, and based on the parent’s verbal responses, the interviewer decides whether to ask follow-up questions, adjusting the phrasing as necessary. If no further probing is required, the interviewer proceeds to the next question on the list. This question list is also derived from the Test of Emotion Comprehension
(
Pons et al. 2004
;
Albanese et al. 2006
)
. Concurrently, PACEE parses the parent’s responses to construct the child’s initial emotional profile.

The complementary data source is PACEE’s text-based analysis of parent-child conversation (Scenario 2 in Figure
5
). During the
Parent-Child Adventure Game
, PACEE segments, transcribes, and analyzes conversations to identify observations that enrich the child’s emotional profile. This process helps address limitations such as human attentional constraints and subjective biases.

Both data sources are analyzed by the cognitive analyzer. First, the profile extractor identifies incremental updates across the three dimensions of emotional understanding, emotional expression, and emotional regulation. Next, the profile integrator merges these updates into the existing profile while minimizing redundancy. The profile integrator also compares and organizes entries contributed by the parent and the system within each dimension, revealing potential cognitive gaps. Finally, PACEE presents a comparative analysis of children’s emotional states on the Child Emotional Modeling interface (see Figure
4
(c)).

5.2.
LLM-based Agent Workflow Design

In this section, we introduce the agent workflow design of PACEE (see figure
6
). In the
Parent-Child Adventure Game
, PACEE applies rapid and reflective reasoning to real-time conversation information. Inspired by how human systems of “thinking fast and slow”
(
Kahneman 2011
)
can be applied to generative agents
(
Christakopoulou et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
, we implement rapid and intuitive reasoning to generate real-time coaching support for parents, while employing reflective and deliberative reasoning steps to understand the emotional characteristics of the child progressively.

PACEE transcribes the real-time parent-child conversations and monitors explicit transitions across the
Five\ Stages
by mouse clicks. The conversation analyzer uses this information to assess the completion level of the current conversation stage and update the node status in the conversation graph.

The content generator module, combining conversation information and the child’s emotional profile, applies rapid reasoning to provide timely support for parents: (1) phase advice updated at each stage, (2) real-time advice updated every half minute, (3) agent conversation converted into voice mode, and (4) scenarios converted into image mode by text-to-image models. In addition, the content generator module integrates all conversation information from the latest
Parent-Child Adventure Game
and the child’s emotional profile to generate the
Parental Feedback Report
for the parents.

PACEE also applies reflective reasoning within the parent-child conversation to gradually build the child’s emotional profile based on real-time conversation information. At certain intervals (empirically set to every five conversation turns), the cognitive analyzer is tasked with multi-step reasoning to update the child’s emotional profile (see Section
5.1.3
).

5.3.
Implementation

We implemented the core agent workflow in Python on a Flask server. The PACEE interface, written in Vue, interacts with the server by exchanging messages via WebSocket. Children’s emotional profile and conversation graph information are stored in separate JSON files.

For most text generation tasks, we leveraged the GPT-4o API to balance speed and output quality. To minimize latency in agent conversations and real-time advice delivery, the faster GPT-4o-mini model was adopted. Speech-to-text processing utilized the TINGWU API, with audio sliced and compressed on the frontend before transcription. Text-to-speech conversion relied on Alibaba Cloud’s streaming synthesis API, while image generation integrated the Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) model.

6.4.
Procedure

Each pair of parent and child underwent a 2-hour study in our lab (see Figure
8
). We provided a touch-screen computer for each family and recorded the entire evaluation process.

Introduction session.
Before each emotion education session, we introduced the parent participants to the designated system. We demonstrated how to interact with each system and provided tips to avoid misuse. We also provided parents with a practice session using several example scenarios to familiarize them with the procedure. In addition, we informed parents that the session required joint participation from both parents and children, and it was not intended for the child to engage solely with the AI.

Emotion Education Session Using PACEE or Baseline.
In each emotion education session, we asked parents to freely use the system to learn about the specified emotional scenario with their children. Considering children aged 3 to 6 may be shy around strangers, the researcher left the room to allow parents and children to interact freely. We monitored the session via a live video feed and informed parents that they could contact us at any time if they encountered any issues.

Post-Questionnaire Filling.
Parent participants completed a post-questionnaire to evaluate their educational experience after completing all education sessions. We used a questionnaire consisting of three sections. The first section consisted of NASA-TLX
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
metrics to measure the mental demand, effort, and frustration experienced by parents when using AI systems to provide emotional guidance to their children. The second section used the Parenting Sense of Competence Scale (PSOC)
(
Gibaud-Wallston and Wandersmann 1978
)
to measure parents’ confidence, satisfaction, and self-efficacy. The last part focuses on the extent to which PACEE or Baseline addresses the three key challenges (
C_{1}
-
C_{3}
) in family emotion education. All ratings were done on a 7-point Likert scale.

Parent Interview and Child Drawing.
One researcher interviewed the parents about their user experience and perceptions. At the same time, another researcher asked the child participants to use emoji stickers to represent their feelings during conversations, the scenarios discussed, and the image of PACEE. Finally, the children drew a picture to depict the scenarios discussed with PACEE.

6.5.
Data Analysis

For qualitative analysis, we transcribed all parent interviews. Five HCI researchers with qualitative coding experience conducted a thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
;
Terry et al. 2017
;
Braun and Clarke 2019
)
. The process began with a data familiarization phase, during which all researchers reviewed the transcripts to gain a comprehensive understanding of the content.
The leading author then generated initial code themes, which were reviewed and refined with feedback from another researcher. The team then collaboratively developed a shared codebook by coding data from one participant (P1), with discussions for consensus. The remaining data were then independently coded, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. Finally, the team manually and iteratively clustered the codes to identify recurring and meaningful patterns.

For statistical analysis, we transcribed the audio recordings from the teaching sessions of both the PACEE and Baseline systems into text and then conducted automated quantitative analyses. The identification of emotion-related words was implemented using the NTUSD dictionary
(
Ku and Chen 2007
)
. To better analyze the transcript data, we applied descriptive statistical methods, calculating the mean and standard deviation for each of the four dimensions among all 16 groups of participants. Additionally, we employed significance testing methods to evaluate the statistical significance of the collected data. We used the Paired Sample t-test for interaction duration data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the number of conversation turns, word counts, and emotion-related word counts, based on the distribution characteristics of the data. Furthermore, we calculated the mean and standard deviation of conversation durations across the
Five\ Stages
.

7.2.2.
PACEE Provides New Insights on Children’s Profile for Parental Reflection

During the interviews, parents acknowledged that PACEE offers a more objective assessment of children’s emotional capacities. Some parents realized they had underestimated their child’s emotional understanding after reviewing the AI’s evaluation. As P2 noted, “
Sometimes I didn’t realize how well my child could understand emotions, but the AI, speaking with the tone of an expert, showed me that he is truly growing.
” On the other hand, some parents discovered they had overestimated their child’s abilities. As P4 shared, “
Parents often view their children through a biased lens, believing they’re performing well. However, the AI objectively highlighted certain issues, and despite its evaluation diverging from my own perspective, I found the AI more rational and unbiased.
” Additionally, AI can help parents understand the underlying reasons behind their child’s emotional responses. As P8 explained, “
When my child participated in an English competition, he was very reluctant to go on stage. I assumed he was nervous and anxious because he was afraid of the stage, but the AI suggested that the anxiety might have stemmed from the absence of his mother’s support.
”

In a broader view, parents express opinions on AI’s advantages in modeling children’s emotional profiles, including more comprehensive summaries, more objective perspectives, and more educational insights. Additionally, AI has potential disadvantages, including a lack of long-term data, insufficient specificity, and incorrect inferences. These factors can lead to cognitive differences between parents and AI regarding children’s emotional states. We further inquired how parents perceive these differences. 14/16 parents considered these differences between humans and AI as valuable new insights. They acknowledged that the individual suggestions made by AI are derived from broader patterns observed in children of similar age, allowing for more long-term and holistic reflections on a child’s emotional tendencies. As P16 explained, “
I can only see the emotional issues my child is currently experiencing and how he copes with them, I have no experience dealing with newly emerging emotions.
” In contrast, AI offers a more rational point of view that can help parents break free from fixed thought patterns. P8 remarked, “
Because our communication with the child is usually smooth, we’ve fallen into a comfortable but fixed mode of interaction. While this is pleasant for daily life, it might hinder development in certain areas. The involvement of AI could reduce this limitation.
”

However, 2/16 parents expressed concerns about the accuracy of AI-generated insights, viewing discrepancies from their own understanding as potential system errors. P15 commented, “
The AI detected a sense of compromise in my child during the conversation, but I doubt he has the capacity to express such emotion yet. The term ’compromise’ seems imprecise.
” Some parents also believed that AI could not detect when a child was lying, whereas their familiarity with their child enabled them to make more accurate judgments. As P11 noted, “
AI can’t tell whether what the child says is sincere or not —it records everything regardless of accuracy. So if the child lies, it might offer the parent an incorrect solution.
”

7.2.4.
Parents Accept PACEE in Collaborative Guidance, but with Concerns

Parents shared their perspectives on integrating PACEE into the emotional guidance process during the interviews. Participants consistently viewed themselves as the primary agents in their children’s education. Nine obviously reported making the most significant contribution during the experimental sessions, whereas only P10 regarded the PACEE as having had a larger impact. Furthermore, P5, P8, and P13 explained that their primary role was to evaluate and select which of the PACEE recommendations to implement, while P6, P9, P10, and P13 credited the system with providing the specialized professional expertise necessary to complete the tasks.

In addition, parents expressed a positive attitude toward incorporating PACEE into parent–child conversations. All participants accepted PACEE involvement, though their preferred levels and modes of engagement varied. Some parents demonstrated a high degree of acceptance (P5: “
My kid really like it(to receive emotional coaching from PACEE). I think I am very receptive.
”), whereas others felt that PACEE was not strictly necessary, especially in familiar emotional scenarios (P16: “
We often discuss this topic so my child no longer need emotional guidance in this situation.
”).
Parents recognized the system’s capacity to deliver valuable, professional, and targeted recommendations. For example, P8 highlighted that such suggestions made parent–child communication more effective, “
He felt I understood him better, so he answered every question loudly and confidently.
”

When discussing future interactions with PACEE, participants articulated clear expectations about PACEE’s role in parent–child conversations, though they differed markedly on its ideal interlocutor. Some parents (P2, P16) envisioned AI forging a peer-like relationship with their child, simultaneously acting as an educator. Others (P8) preferred that PACEE interact only with the parent, acting as a co-educator, and avoid direct communication with the child. A third group of participants (P10, P13, and P15) believed that AI should adopt a neutral stance as an observer, providing limited, objective, data-driven suggestions to parents and offering age-appropriate guidance to children, rather than engaging directly with either parent or child. For instance, P10 argued that “
It can serve as a mediator (in parent–child emotional education).
” P13 and P15 prefer PACEE to offer suggestions from a third-party perspective for both parents and children.

Crucially, parents asserted firm boundaries around AI intervention. Some parents rejected features that impose targets on children (P4: “
For example, setting goals for children… I think it is up to parents to do it.
”), and they were adamant that PACEE should not encroach on sensitive family matters (P1: “
Don’t press further… for example, on topics that parents find taboo and children find sensitive.
”), or strictly require compliance with its advice (P11: “
If it(PACEE) tells me I have to do something, I definitely won’t accept it.
”). Moreover, participants cautioned that AI should not replace emotional intimacy and empathetic support intrinsic to parent-child bonding and the cultivation of self-confidence, such as “
(I hope she can) build her confidence by communicating with others.
”(P2), “
I prefer she get emotional companionship from people…
”(P5).

7.3.1.
Enhancing Parental Expertise and Communication Confidence

In terms of
C_{1}
limited parental expertise, the
Parent-Child Adventure Game
module significantly alleviated parents’ difficulties in situations where they “don’t know what to say” or “don’t know how to guide.” During the interviews, parents acknowledged that they find it difficult to perform some steps alone, including recalling an experience (
n=4
), labeling emotions (
n=8
), expressing empathy (
n=8
), reflecting on emotions (
n=5
), and finding positive solutions (
n=6
).

The system’s conversation suggestions were praised for mitigating these expertise gaps. First, it provides a structured framework for emotional communication. Two parents specifically highlighted how the AI facilitated guiding emotional conversations with children in a gradual, structured manner. As P8 noted: “
The systematic process — understand the child first, then ask about their feelings, then summarize, encourage, and finally solve the problem. This step-by-step guidance is great, creating remarkably smoother interactions.
” Second, several parents appreciated how AI helped them adopt their child’s perspective. P10 explained: “
Placing us parents in a conversational setting that feels more like talking to a friend actually encourages us to respond more actively and calmly. It’s because of this setup that I’m more willing to take the child’s perspective, understand them, and help them manage their emotions.
” Lastly, the conversation suggestions offer ideas to steer the conversation stream effectively. P16 stated, “
AI’s reminders can offer us more ideas. During the reflection stage, both the child and I are thinking, and it’s easy to get caught up in the moment. The AI’s prompts provide a clearer perspective, which helps make the conversation more in-depth.
”

Beyond providing suggestions, the module actively enhanced parent-child communication and boosted parental confidence during real interactions. Most parents reported increased initiative and comfort in emotional conversations after using the
Parent-Child Adventure Game
module. Ten participants confirmed that the system’s conversation suggestions provided concrete, child-friendly advice for expressing emotions. As P5 explained, “
It can serve as a reference tool. For example, if you have an idea but don’t know how to express it, the system helps you organize your thoughts and express them more clearly and reasonably.
” Additionally, two parents mentioned that AI-provided prompts served as valid entry points, promoting more open communication between children and parents. P1 explained: “
As a father, I don’t talk with my child that often. For example, instead of saying, ‘Do you remember your last kindergarten performance?’ it feels more natural to ask, ‘How was your last performance?’ So the detailed suggestions provided by the system are quite helpful for me as a reference.
” Some parents also noted that the module facilitated more reciprocal exchanges, helping to enhance parent-child relationships.

While the benefits of AI assistance were frequently highlighted, some parents expressed concerns. A few parents felt that frequently checking the AI’s suggestions could slightly disrupt the conversational flow. One parent worried that the AI’s scripted suggestions might make children feel nervous or overly reliant. Another parent emphasized that AI should act as an aid rather than lead in communication, suggesting it should provide direction rather than take over the conversation entirely. Furthermore, some saw the structured nature of AI prompts as potentially limiting the child’s freedom to express diverse experiences. There was also concern that excessive reliance on AI-generated expressions might hinder parents’ own communicative abilities.

7.3.2.
Providing Trackable Feedback and Actionable Future Strategies

In terms of
C_{2}
insufficient feedback, the
Parental Feedback Report
module effectively addressed pain points such as “lack of timely feedback” and “invisible outcomes”. Eight parents highlighted how AI-generated analyses shed light on previously unnoticed shortcomings in their parenting approaches, which prompted them to engage in significant self-reflection. For instance, P6 shared, “
The system reminded me to avoid interrupting. I hadn’t realized I was doing that, but it made me see that I still need to be more patient in communication.
” Furthermore, the module provided a structured review by organizing conversation highlights, which five parents found particularly beneficial in bridging feedback gaps and making abstract parenting efforts trackable. This transformation into visible progress was crucial for parents to assess their parenting strategies.

Beyond reviews on past interactions, the module also provides actionable future strategies. Eight parents appreciated the evidence-based nature of the strategies provided, while three were pleased with the introduction of new, beneficial perspectives. P12 noted, “
For example, when a child loses control, besides suggesting enjoyable activities, the system also mentions deep breathing and meditation. I used to think those were only for adults, but this made me realize they might work for kids too.
”. Also, P1 noted: “
AI can indeed offer suggestions we wouldn’t normally think of, like encouraging the child to observe how others handle situations or simulate a scenario at home — these are quite practical.
” Parents praised that these suggestions provide a clear foundation for future communication.

The module also incorporated practical features that increased its utility. The “review and scoring” design was highlighted by four parents as a useful tool for evaluating the effectiveness of their interactions, as it allowed them to pinpoint areas needing improvement. P15 noted: “
I think the scoring in the review session is useful. It helps me understand which parts of my communication need improvement. Everyone is busy, so this kind of tool is really practical.
”. Additionally, motivational elements, such as the “badge achievement system”, were noted to enhance parental engagement and motivation.

Despite these positive aspects, some concerns remained. A few parents pointed out that a single session was insufficient to assess real outcomes, and some found the suggestions too generic, which highlights the need for the AI to better adapt to specific parental contexts.

7.3.3.
Improving Parental Understanding and Grasp of Child Developmental Trajectories

In terms of
C_{3}
, a lack of understanding of children’s emotional states, the
Child Emotional Modeling
module helped parents break through cognitive blind spots by leveraging data-driven insights to reveal their child’s emotional traits and behavioral patterns. Twelve parents reported that the system’s scientifically grounded feedback provided “new knowledge and dimensions”, helping them overcome cognitive blind spots regarding their child’s emotions and behavior. As P12 explained: “
The system’s questions prompted me to think about aspects I hadn’t paid attention to before. The conclusions helped me understand my child more systematically and comprehensively. It was very helpful.
” Thirteen parents further emphasized how AI generated “new angles” or “reference dimensions”, saying that the system helped them move beyond existing subjective impressions to enable more objective and reflective observations. This objective analysis was particularly valued in understanding the causes of children’s emotions, especially in labeling children’s emotions (
S_{2}
). P6 explained: “
AI can analyze the causes of a child’s emotions more objectively, whereas we as parents often bring in our own biases or emotions, making it hard to view the situation in a scientific and objective way.
”

Parents also discussed the alignment between their views and the system’s analyses. Some parents reported that the system’s views were highly consistent with their own, which enhanced their trust. Others felt that when discrepancies arose, the system helped them see where their perceptions diverged—even serving as a valuable reference for resolving differences within the family.

Beyond individual insights, the module also contextualized children’s behavior within broader developmental frameworks. Four parents mentioned that the AI’s use of large-scale data provided them with their first systematic understanding of “common behaviors at this age,” which helped them better assess their child’s traits. Additionally, several parents appreciated the forward-looking nature of the AI’s suggestions, which helped strengthen their overall understanding of their child’s developmental trajectory.

Despite these advantages, several limitations were noted concerning the AI’s understanding and accuracy. Seven parents were concerned that the limited interaction time during the trial meant the generated profiles might not be comprehensive. Four parents noted that the system cannot verify the truthfulness of the information entered, which may lead to biased analysis. P1 said: “
AI can only analyze based on what the child says, but children don’t always express their true feelings. Sometimes they even lie—for example, they might be very nervous but say they’re fine. This affects the AI’s judgment.
” Two parents expressed discomfort with the scoring system and said they didn’t want to receive low scores from the system.

8.
DISCUSSION

In our study, we designed PACEE from a parent-centered view. We based this approach on recognizing that the role of parents is essential in early childhood education and that the ability to interact with AI varies among children aged 3 to 6 years old. PACEE leverages multiple generative AI technologies (LLMs, text-to-image, text-to-speech) to address challenges for parents (
C_{1}
-
C_{3}
). Our evaluation studies show that parents perceive PACEE to have successfully addressed these challenges, reducing their effort and boosting their satisfaction.
Here, we further discuss the impact of generative AI on early childhood family education and the impact of PACEE on Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
)
. We then propose broader design implications for parent-centered, AI-supported education systems.

8.1.
PACEE Enhanced Children’s Learning through Improving Parents’ Performance

Prior work on AI-assisted child education has explored AI systems directly coaching the child
(
Seo et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
, AI systems for intervening with parents
(
Entenberg et al. 2023
)
, and systems that facilitate parent-child interaction simultaneously
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Theofanopoulou and Slovak 2022
)
. Similarly, we identify the need for generative AI support in our work. However, our context is different in that children aged 3 to 6 often face challenges in interacting independently with AI
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Bush and Alibakhshi 2025
;
Escobar-Planas et al. 2022
;
Thill et al. 2022
)
, and early childhood emotion coaching requires more substantial support from parents
(
Theofanopoulou and Slovak 2022
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Lee and Kim 2023
)
. Also, prior work has expressed concerns that AI alone may not be able to adequately respond to young children’s emotional needs
(
Kurian 2025
;
Kurian 2024
)
. Thus, we propose a parent-centered, AI-supported system for parent-child interaction. This parent-centered interaction pattern, where AI primarily assists parents rather than directly interacting with children, was preliminarily explored in therapeutic domains, such as for children with language delay
(
Hwang et al. 2014
)
. We further extend this pattern to the domain of early-childhood education.

In our work, PACEE enhances children’s learning outcomes mainly by improving parents’ performance. By positioning itself as a strategic partner rather than merely a tool, PACEE has fostered more effective communication between parents and their children, including higher interaction engagement and more in-depth emotional conversations. Consequently, child participants exhibited higher engagement levels and used more emotion-related words during the teaching sessions.

However, the potential impact of generative AI directly interacting with children aged 3-6 in emotional education needs further exploration. In our study, we emphasized parents’ participation and informed parents that PACEE was not intended for the child to engage solely with the AI. This decision is due to findings from our formative study, where all five participating parents preferred to maintain their primary role in emotional guidance. Regarding the extent of AI directly interacting with the children, parents showed varying acceptance levels in the interviews. We also found that children’s AI literacy significantly varied based on parents’ attitudes. Therefore, the future design of emotion education systems needs to customize AI interaction levels based on parental acceptance and the child’s AI literacy. Also, longitudinal studies are required in order to assess the impact of varying degrees of AI interaction on children’s emotional development over time.

Under our parent-centered interaction pattern, one challenge is to help parents better understand their children. To address this, we propose a framework for
AI-parent collaborative modeling of children’s emotional profiles
. In our framework, parents tend to contribute experiential knowledge of their children’s habits, while AI tends to evaluate developmental capabilities by analyzing parent-child interactions. This approach can also be extended to other areas of early childhood education, building upon prior work on personalized education systems. Prior systems either allow parents to manually set basic information and preferences
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Fang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
or automatically assess children’s learning progress
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. By combining the strengths of parental expertise and AI-driven insights, our framework fosters a dynamic process of mutual learning. Parents and AI can collaboratively refine their understanding of the child’s needs, enabling more effective and personalized educational strategies.

8.2.
Adapting Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching Guideline in the Era of Generative AI for Family Education

While previous systems have implemented Gottman’s Five Steps of Emotion Coaching guideline
(
Gottman and DeClaire 1997
)
in specific contexts
(
Seo et al. 2024
;
Ibrahim et al. 2025
;
Tang et al. 2024
)
, our work demonstrates that a direct translation of this model is insufficient for the family education of children aged 3 to 6. We discuss this adaptation across three critical interaction phases, highlighting how generative AI addresses specific developmental and parental challenges to bridge the gap between theory and practical home education.

Noticing, Recognizing, and Labeling Emotions.

Gottman’s initial steps assume the child can verbally articulate the source of their emotion. However, prior literature indicates that preschoolers often lack the memory recall and verbal proficiency to reconstruct emotional events independently
(
Channell and Barth 2013
;
Liwag and Stein 1995
)
. To address this, we emphasize active parental participation. Here, generative AI acts not only as a source of communication advice but also as a narrative scaffold. By generating virtual scenarios that align with the child’s preference for visual thinking, PACEE externalizes the child’s vague memories into concrete artifacts. This situates our design within the broader framework of mediated memory
(
Han 2023
)
, transforming the interaction from “interrogating the child” to “co-exploring a scenario.” However, limitations were identified: some parents noted that AI-generated scenarios sometimes lacked real-life relevance, reducing immersion. This suggests a trade-off in implementing Gottman’s guideline: how to balance generative inspiration with biographical accuracy to maximize the emotional bonding experience.

Listening with Empathy and Validating Feelings.

Gottman emphasizes that parents should show empathy and validate children’s feelings. Yet, our interviews reveal that parents often neglect this step in home education and find it challenging to perform it themselves. Consistent with prior work
(
Yoo et al. 2019
)
, PACEE encourages parents to adopt the child’s perspective during moments of conflict. Moreover, PACEE innovatively introduces a
Child Emotional Modeling
module, utilizing text-based analysis of parent-child conversation to provide parents with an objective “second opinion” on the child’s state. Our evaluation shows that parents prefer to extract insights from specific life events, while the AI focuses on evaluating developmental capabilities. This result provides an empirical framework of parent-AI collaborative understanding: Generative AI compensates for the parent’s subjective blind spots with developmental metrics, while the parent grounds the AI’s analysis in the real-world family context.

Setting Limits and Problem Solving.
Research suggests that children aged 3 to 6 respond well to positive reinforcement mechanisms
(
Hardy and McLeod 2020
;
Sigler and Aamidor 2005
)
. So we adapted Gottman’s guideline by integrating the PERMA model
(
Seligman 2010
)
, prioritizing the discovery of positive emotions and solutions. In this way, the parent-child interaction focus shifts from “correction” to “positive resource building.” However, our study highlights a critical tension in applying generative AI to this step: the risk of dependency and rigidity. Parents expressed concerns that rigid, AI-imposed goals might undermine the collaborative nature of parenting, and that children might over-rely on AI for solutions. Therefore, we suggest future adaptations of Gottman’s guidelines with generative AI prioritize process-oriented scaffolding (e.g., stimulating child thinking) over solution-oriented outputs (e.g., resolving the immediate conflict).

In summary,
our work refines Gottman’s dyadic “parent-child” interaction framework into a triadic “AI-Parent-Child” framework
. We demonstrate that generative AI plays a unique role in bridging the “theory-practice gap”—specifically by mitigating the developmental limitations of children aged 3 to 6 and the communication barriers faced by parents. On the other hand, we maintain that parental agency remains central; the parent needs to serve as the primary emotional anchor and final content gatekeeper, while the AI empowers the parent.

8.3.
Design Considerations for Parent-centered, AI-supported Education Systems

Parent interviews also revealed several challenges that need to be addressed. Combining our findings with prior work, we propose design considerations for future
parent-centered, AI-supported education systems
.

First, customize AI’s aiding modules based on parents’ profiles, in addition to their children’s.
Some recent work has integrated children’s profiles or preferences to facilitate a more personalized coaching experience
(
Clabaugh et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. This study supplements prior research and demonstrates the necessity of customizing AI systems based on parental profiles. During our interviews, we found that parents expressed significantly varied opinions about the “ideal support” from PACEE. Concerning content difficulty, some experienced parents criticized that AI’s guidance overlapped heavily with their own approaches, while a few parents mentioned that some suggestions were too complex to understand. Concerning the frequency of suggestions, some parents felt that frequently checking the AI’s advice could slightly disrupt the conversational flow. In contrast, others believed that PACEE could offer more real-time interactions.

These findings highlight that parent-centered educational systems should accommodate such differences in parents’ needs. For example, parents’ preferences regarding the appropriateness of early-childhood digital play, which may influence the quality of AI-assisted activities at home
(
Isikoglu Erdogan et al. 2019
)
, should be collected beforehand. Furthermore, various parental inputs can be captured during parent-child interactions, besides audio data from conversations. These inputs may include whether parental feedback during interactions is positive or negative
(
Robinson and Eyberg 1981
)
, behavioral differences between fathers and mothers
(
Cutler and Palkovitz 2020
)
, and parents’ emotional regulation abilities
(
Bariola et al. 2011
)
. By incorporating diverse data sources, the system can better tailor its functions to the specific needs of each family.

Second, avoid excessive reliance on AI capabilities and promote transparent and negotiable collaboration.
In the evaluation studies, parents expressed worries that the system cannot verify the truthfulness of the information entered, which may lead to biased analysis. They also noted that AI’s interpretation of a child’s emotions may contradict the parents’ understanding. These concerns align with the communication aspect of cooperative AI
(
Dafoe et al. 2020
)
. By explicitly sharing information, AI agents and parents can gain insights into each other’s behaviors and intentions more effectively.

Here, we recommend leveraging research findings from explainable AI to enhance transparency in collaboration
(
Liao et al. 2020
)
. Parents need to understand the rationale behind AI-generated assessments or suggestions, thus enhancing trust in AI’s decisions
(
Kim 2024
)
. In designing future systems, it is crucial to consider how to balance the parents’ cognitive burden of processing information with the goal of enhancing trust by providing a comprehensive explanation, which may be related to parents’ diverse decision-making styles
(
van Arum et al. 2025
)
.

Considering the potential conflicts between parents and AI systems, we recommend the introduction of negotiation tools
(
Crandall et al. 2018
;
Jennings et al. 2001
)
, as the current context lacks a formal agreement protocol. In the context of emotion education, it is essential to develop interactive negotiation mechanisms to address varying interpretations of children’s emotional states. Furthermore, AI can serve as a mediator to reconcile differing perspectives on children’s emotions among family members, a role that some parents expressed interest in during the interviews.

Third, respect parents’ boundaries regarding AI intervention and provide them with sufficient autonomy.
Prior work has explored the area of parents’ boundaries, such as parental concerns with AI-driven technologies in their child’s healthcare
(
Sisk et al. 2020
)
, showing that parents value shared decision-making but may resist interventions that undermine their sense of control. We supplement prior research and expand this area to include both emotion-sensitive contexts and early childhood education domains.

In our context, children’s emotional education may involve sensitive family matters. In the interviews, parents expressed that they do not want AI to discuss family privacy or parents’ professional information. Future work could look into value-sensitive AI design frameworks
(
Umbrello and Van de Poel 2021
)
and provide information sensitivity detection techniques that align with diverse parental values. Moreover, emotional education may involve delicate feelings or negative topics that could affect children’s mood. To address this issue, PACEE incorporated the PERMA positive psychology model into its design to reduce the emphasis on negative content. Future work could investigate how to further mitigate emotional overwhelm that may be perceived as harmful by parents. For example, adaptive AI systems could use real-time sentiment analysis to detect emotional intensity and compare it with predefined emotional boundaries set by parents.

As for education for children aged 3 to 6, parents consistently viewed themselves as the primary decision-makers in their children’s education. In the interviews, parents expressed concerns about the possibility of AI imposing rigid goals on children and compelling parents to follow prescribed steps. Future systems should prioritize bidirectional discussions with parents, rather than simply providing prescriptions. Furthermore, they should ensure parents maintain authority over critical decision-making in parent-AI collaboration.

8.4.
Limitations and Future Work

This study has several limitations.
First, the participants’ cultural background was limited to Chinese parents and children, which may constrain the cultural generalizability of our findings.
Second, most of the parent participants were mothers. This reflects the broader caregiving patterns noted in prior research
(
McBride and Mills 1993
;
Schoppe-Sullivan et al. 2013
;
Hernawati and Herawati 2020
)
, and likely represents the primary user group for a system designed to support caregivers of 3-6-year-olds in the real world. However, this imbalance may limit our ability to capture the full range of parental perspectives. Future work should recruit across more diverse cultural and gender contexts, and systematically examine how fathers and mothers may differ in their collaboration with AI to support children.

Third, a limitation of the current study design is the inability to fully isolate the foundation model variable. While our study’s primary focus was on the interaction framework rather than raw model output, as supported by our findings and clarified in Section
6.2
, we acknowledge that this does not entirely eliminate the potential bias inherent in the two models. Therefore, future work could employ a 2x2 factorial design — (Interface: Baseline vs. PACEE) x (Model: Doubao vs. GPT-4o) — to further isolate the effect of the model.

Currently, PACEE relies on text-based conversation analysis to assist parents in understanding their child’s emotional states. Future developments could, with parental consent, incorporate additional modalities, such as collecting children’s gestures and facial expressions. However, regardless of the modality used, it is important to note that PACEE’s role is to augment parents’ understanding, not replace the parents. Ultimately, parents remain the final decision-makers in deciding whether to adopt the system’s suggestions and how to interpret their child’s emotional states.

Finally, our study was conducted in lab-based settings using a touchscreen laptop, which may not fully reflect the long-term usage experience at home.
In addition, first-time exposure to PACEE’s interactive features may have temporarily increased participants’ engagement, reflecting a “novelty effect” commonly faced by HCI research
(
Strömel et al. 2024
;
Soler-Dominguez et al. 2024
;
Davis et al. 2025
)
. To address this issue, we provided parents with a practice session to familiarize them with the procedure. While our analyses did not reveal significant differences in comparative outcomes between early and later interactions, future work should explore how such novelty influences parents and children during longer interactions. Future work should also implement longitudinal in-home deployments to better understand how parent-AI collaborative emotion education unfolds in natural family environments.

9.
CONCLUSION

We introduced PACEE, an assistant designed to support family emotion education through parent-AI collaborative emotional profiling and guidance. Drawing on interviews with kindergarten teachers and parents, PACEE leverages generative AI to address the key challenges identified in family emotion education.
Our evaluation study with 16 pairs of parents and children demonstrated that PACEE enhanced parent-child engagement, fostering deeper emotional exchanges. Furthermore, PACEE significantly alleviated parental challenges, resulting in greater satisfaction and less effort from parents.
We hope our work will advance emotion coaching theory in both family and AI-assisted contexts. Additionally, we hope to inspire the development of parent-centered, AI-supported education systems that empower families to promote children’s well-being.
