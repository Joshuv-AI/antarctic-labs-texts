Anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins: from regulatory networks to therapeutic targeting
Authors: Zhe Wang, Mutian Tang, Marina Konopleva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The BCL-2 protein family controls the intrinsic apoptotic pathway through a delicate balance of pro- and anti-apoptotic members acting at the mitochondrial outer membrane. Anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1, BCL-W, and BCL2A1 (BFL-1) function as critical survival factors whose dysregulation contributes to cancer development and therapeutic resistance. This review systematically examines the multilayered regulatory mechanisms governing these proteins, including transcriptional control by NF-κB, STAT3/5, and HIF-1α; post-transcriptional regulation through alternative splicing and microRNAs; and post-translational modifications that determine protein stability and function. The clinical success of venetoclax, a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has established BCL-2 family targeting as an effective therapeutic strategy and fundamentally changed the management of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). However, therapeutic challenges persist: resistance emerges through MCL-1 upregulation, BCL-2 mutations, and metabolic reprogramming; BCL-XL inhibition causes dose-limiting thrombocytopenia; and MCL-1 inhibitors face class-wide cardiac toxicity. Emerging strategies to overcome these limitations include tissue-selective proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) enabling tumor-targeted delivery, next-generation inhibitors that overcome resistance mutations, and biomarker-guided patient selection. This review provides an integrated overview of the regulatory mechanisms and evolving therapeutic strategies targeting anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, outlining both prominent successes and unresolved challenges.

Introduction

Apoptosis is a tightly regulated cell death program essential for tissue homeostasis, removal of damaged cells, and suppression of oncogenesis [1]. The intrinsic apoptotic pathway responds to intracellular stress signals such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, ER stress, and growth factor withdrawal [2]. These signals induce mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), an irreversible step resulting in the release of cytochrome c and other pro-apoptotic factors from mitochondria. Cytochrome c binds apoptotic protease-activating factor 1 (Apaf-1), inducing formation of the apoptosome complex that activates initiator caspase-9 [3], which in turn activates executioner caspases-3 and -7 [4]. These caspases dismantle cellular architecture by cleaving numerous substrates. The BCL-2 protein family is the master regulator of MOMP and thus the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [5]. About 20 BCL-2 family members share one to four conserved BCL-2 homology (BH1-BH4) domains (Fig. 1) [6]. They segregate into anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2, BCL-XL, BCL-W, MCL-1, BCL2A1/BFL-1), pro-apoptotic effectors (BAX, BAK), and pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins (e.g., tBID, BIM, PUMA, NOXA) [7]. BH3-only proteins can be further divided into activators and sensitizers. Activators (such as BIM, tBID, and PUMA) can directly bind and activate BAX/BAK to initiate apoptosis. In contrast, sensitizers (such as BAD and NOXA) do not directly activate BAX/BAK but promote apoptosis by binding and neutralizing anti-apoptotic proteins, thereby freeing activators to trigger BAX/BAK activation (Table 1) [8]. Anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins enforce survival by sequestering BH3-containing partners through a conserved hydrophobic BH3-binding groove in their α-helical fold; this groove is formed mainly by the BH1-BH3 regions and comprises four pockets (P1–P4), while BH4 contributes more indirectly to structural stability and regulation [7]. Overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins is common in cancers and correlates with disease progression, treatment resistance, and immune evasion. The t(14;18) translocation places BCL2 under control of the immunoglobulin heavy-chain enhancer, driving high BCL-2 expression in ~85% of follicular lymphomas and a subset of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas [9]. In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), BCL-2 protein is constitutively overexpressed in nearly all cases through transcriptional activation and extended protein stability [9, 10]. Leukemia stem cells (LSCs) in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are uniquely dependent on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for maintenance and survival. While LSCs overexpress BCL-2, research indicates that venetoclax monotherapy may not be sufficient to inhibit OXPHOS in vivo. Instead, the combination of venetoclax and azacitidine synergistically disrupts energy metabolism by depleting glutathione and reducing SDHA glutathionylation, which inhibits electron transport chain complex II activity. This targeted metabolic disruption effectively eradicates the ROS-low LSC subpopulation by suppressing mitochondrial respiration and ATP production [11]. MCL1 is frequently amplified (for example, ~64% of lung adenocarcinomas harbor MCL1 copy gains [12]) and its high levels confer therapeutic vulnerability; MCL1 copy gains in ~30% of multiple myelomas are associated with treatment resistance [13]. BCL-XL overexpression (driven by STAT3/5 or BCL2L1 gene amplification, such as in ~18.4% of gastric cancers [14]) promotes apoptotic resistance in solid tumors [15]. BCL2A1, often transcriptionally upregulated (for instance by NF-κB or the melanoma oncogene MITF) or amplified (~30% of melanomas), confers resistance to BRAF inhibitors [16]. Beyond cancer, dysregulation of these anti-apoptotic proteins contributes to autoimmune disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and viral infections. The importance of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins in disease pathogenesis has established them as attractive therapeutic targets. Venetoclax (ABT-199), a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has markedly improved outcomes in CLL and AML [9, 17], thereby validating BCL-2 targeting and motivating the development of next-generation modalities, including proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) [18], antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) [19], and novel BH3 mimetics [20].

Despite these advances, significant challenges remain, including functional redundancy among BCL-2 family members, development of resistance mechanisms [21], and on-target toxicities [22, 24]. This review summarizes the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational regulation of the major anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, as well as the current therapeutic strategies. This highlights both the achievements and ongoing challenges in targeting these critical survival factors.

The regulatory networks governing anti-apoptotic BCL-2 proteins

Anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins (BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1, BCL-W, and BCL2A1) are controlled by multiple regulatory mechanisms that collectively determine how readily a cell undergoes apoptosis [5]. Transcriptionally, diverse transcription factors, signaling pathways, and epigenetic mechanisms establish cell type- and context-specific expression patterns. Post-transcriptionally, microRNAs, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), and alternative splicing enables rapid and reversible modulation of protein abundance. At the protein level, post-translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation and ubiquitination dynamically govern protein stability, subcellular localization, and interactions with pro-apoptotic partners. Together, these regulatory mechanisms determine anti-apoptotic capacity by controlling the binding specificity and affinity of each protein for BH3-only proteins and the effectors BAX and BAK, ultimately defining cellular susceptibility to apoptosis. Below, we examine these regulatory mechanisms for each key anti-apoptotic protein.

BCL2

BCL-2 (the founding member of the family) is controlled at several levels. Transcriptionally, survival signals (e.g., NF-κB, STAT) directly upregulate BCL2 expression [5]. NF-κB was shown to bind the BCL2 promoter (P2) and induce BCL-2 in response to TNF-α, linking inflammatory signaling to increased BCL-2 expression [25]. Chromatin organizer SATB1 mediates long-range loops between the BCL2 promoter and distal 3′ regulatory elements, enhancing transcription [26], and CREB activates BCL2 via cAMP response elements [27]. Conversely, tumor-suppressive pathways dampen BCL2 transcription, such as TP53 inducible microRNAs (e.g., miR-15/16) that target BCL2 mRNA and loss of these miRNAs (as in the 13q14 deletion of CLL) causes BCL2 overexpression [28] (Fig. 2).Fig. 2BCL-2 (B-cell lymphoma 2) regulatory mechanisms.BCL-2 transcription is induced by survival signals via NF-κB and STAT family transcription factors (e.g. downstream of cytokines such as IL-6). Post-transcriptionally, the long 3′-UTR of BCL2 mRNA contains AU-rich elements that bind RBPs to modulate mRNA stability. HuR (ELAVL1) and nucleolin bind the 3′-UTR to stabilize the transcript [29, 30], whereas ZFP36L1 promotes BCL2 mRNA decay [31]. Unlike BCL2L1 or MCL1, BCL2 is not subject to alternative splicing that produces pro-apoptotic isoforms, and is mainly expressed as a single, full-length isoform. Post-translational modifications further regulate BCL-2. The phosphorylation of BCL2 at Ser70 in its flexible loop (triggered by growth factors or microtubule stress) boosts its pro-survival function [32]. During apoptosis, caspases cleave BCL-2, removing the N-terminal BH4 domain and converting BCL-2 into a pro-death fragment that amplifies apoptosis signals [33]. In terms of subcellular localization, BCL-2 localizes mainly to the mitochondrial outer membrane (and the ER and nuclear envelope) [34], positioning it to directly guard mitochondrial integrity. (Fig. 2)

Protein-protein interactions underlie BCL-2 function, which include high-affinity binding to BH3-only proteins and the effector BAX to prevent MOMP. BCL-2 neutralizes multi-domain effectors BAX and (to a lesser extent) BAK by binding and preventing their oligomerization [35, 36], and also sequesters BH3-only activators (BIM, tBID, PUMA) which otherwise trigger BAX/BAK activation [35, 36]. The sensitizer BH3 protein BAD preferentially dimerizes with BCL-2 (and BCL-XL), demonstrating how BCL-2 serves as a sink for pro-apoptotic signals [35, 36]. Collectively, these regulatory layers tightly control BCL-2 abundance and activity, establishing the cellular apoptotic threshold. (Table 1)

BCL-XL

BCL-XL (product of the BCL2L1 gene) is regulated by multiple mechanisms to meet cellular survival needs. Many cytokines and growth factors (IL-2, IL-3, IL-6, GM-CSF, EPO, etc.) induce BCL2L1 transcription via JAK/STAT, NF-κB, AP-1, and ETS signaling pathways [37]. Several transcription factors (e.g., RELA/p65 NF-κB, STAT5, ETS family, AP-1) directly bind the BCL2L1 promoter to upregulate BCL-XL [37]. NF-κB signaling through both canonical (p65/p50) [38] and non-canonical (RelB/p52) pathways provides initial and sustained BCL2L1 transcription [39]; notably, the non-canonical pathway (via NIK stabilization) [39] is critical for long-term BCL-XL expression (as demonstrated in CD40-stimulated CLL cells) [38]. STAT3 activation correlates with elevated BCL-XL in several malignancies [40]. In rhabdomyosarcoma, PAX3 and PAX3/FKHR activate BCL2L1 transcription by binding an ATTA motif in the promoter region [41]. Additionally, c-Fos and c-Jun can trans-activate BCL2L1 through an AP1 site in the promoter [42]. Post-transcriptionally BCL2L1 pre-mRNA undergoes alternative splicing to yield two major isoforms: full-length anti-apoptotic BCL-XL and a shorter pro-apoptotic isoform, BCL-XS, which lacks the BH1 and BH2 domains [43]. The balance between BCL-XL and BCL-XS is dynamically controlled by splicing factors and signaling cues. For instance, SF3B1 and other spliceosome components favor production of BCL-XL, whereas stresses or specific splicing modulators can shift splicing toward BCL-XS [44]. The RNA-binding protein PTBP1 also influences 5′ splice site selection: PTBP1 promotes pro-apoptotic BCL-XS production, while PTBP1 depletion enhances BCL-XL [45]. Furthermore, RBM25 regulates BCL-X splicing via a unique G-quadruplex mechanism, RBM25 binding to a G-quadruplex in the pre-mRNA promotes BCL-XS production, and small-molecule stabilizers of this G-quadruplex increase BCL-XS and induce apoptosis [46]. (Fig. 3)

BCL-XL mRNA stability is likewise subject to control. AU-rich elements in the BCL2L1 3′-UTR recruit stabilizing RBPs like HuR and nucleolin (extending mRNA half-life) [47], whereas microRNAs can repress BCL-XL expression through direct or indirect mechanisms [48]. Post-translational modifications can neutralize BCL-XL function in stressed cells. DNA damage triggers deamidation of BCL-XL at Asn52/66 (converting them to Asp), which activates a conditional PEST sequence and targets BCL-XL for degradation, thereby reducing its cellular levels under genotoxic stress [49]. Some cell types actively suppress BCL-XL deamidation to maintain survival [49]. During apoptosis, caspases cleave BCL-XL at its unstructured loop, producing a truncated ΔN61 fragment (lacking the BH4 domain) that paradoxically acts pro-apoptotically [50]. The subcellular localization of BCL-XL is a dynamic process characterized by continuous shuttling between the mitochondrial outer membrane and the cytosol, known as retrotranslocation [51]. This movement is not merely a change in spatial coordinates; the mitochondrial membrane environment itself serves as a biochemical stabilizer. By significantly slowing the dissociation rate of BCL-XL from pro-apoptotic partners like PUMA or BIM, the membrane creates ‘refractory’ complexes that are resistant to displacement by BH3 mimetics. In contrast, cytosolic BCL-XL forms ‘fragile’ complexes that are easily neutralized. This mechanistic distinction suggests a clear translational path: targeting the BCL-XL C-terminal anchor or its retrotranslocation machinery could ‘disarm’ the protein by forcing it into a vulnerable cytosolic state, thereby overcoming resistance to BH3 mimetics [52] (Fig. 3).

Finally, BCL-XL engages in selective protein interactions that define its anti-apoptotic role. BCL-XL inhibits apoptosis by sequestering the effector proteins BAX and BAK, especially BAK [35, 53], which is preferentially neutralized by BCL-XL and MCL-1 rather than by BCL-2. It also tightly binds BH3-only activator proteins (such as BIM, tBID, PUMA) to block BAX/BAK activation and oligomerization [35, 36]. The sensitizer BH3 protein Bad selectively binds to BCL-XL (and BCL-2) [35, 36], displacing BCL-XL from other partners, and another sensitizer, HRK, specifically targets BCL-XL [8, 35, 36]. Through these interactions, BCL-XL functions as a central node suppressing apoptosis, and its expression and activity are finely tuned by the aforementioned regulatory mechanisms (Table 1).

MCL1

MCL-1 is distinguished by its rapid turnover and dynamic regulation, which allow cells to quickly adjust apoptotic sensitivity. Transcriptionally, MCL1 is an immediate-early gene responsive to growth and survival signals. Cytokines and growth factors (e.g., IL-3, IL-6, IL-15, EGF, VEGF) activate pathways (JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, etc.) that increase MCL1 transcription [54]. Key transcription factors include STAT3/5, HIF-1α (under hypoxic conditions), and E2F1, all of which have been shown to bind the MCL1 promoter and drive its expression [55, 56]. MCL-1 can reinforce its own expression through a GLI-dependent feedforward loop. When not membrane-bound, MCL-1 binds SUFU and promotes its turnover, relieving SUFU-mediated repression of GLI. Activated GLI then increases transcription of MCL1 (and also BCL2 and BCL2L1), establishing a pro-survival axis that can be disrupted by BH3 mimetics [57]. Post-transcriptional mechanisms fine-tune MCL-1 levels. MCL1 pre-mRNA undergoes alternative splicing to produce multiple isoforms: the predominant full-length MCL-1L (anti-apoptotic) and two shorter variants, MCL-1S [58] and MCL-1ES [59], which lack certain BH domains and act pro-apoptotically. By forming inactive heterodimers with MCL-1L, these short isoforms antagonize MCL-1’s function. Shifts in splicing toward MCL-1S/ES tip the balance toward apoptosis. Various splicing factors (e.g., SRSF1, SF3B1) and chromatin modifiers (e.g., HDACs) can influence this splice choice [44]. MCL1 mRNA also has an exceptionally short half-life, controlled by elements in its 3′-UTR. AU-rich and CU-rich sequences bind RBPs that either stabilize or destabilize the transcript. HuR binding prolongs MCL1 mRNA stability [60], whereas proteins like tristetraprolin (TTP) and AUF1 promote rapid MCL1 mRNA decay, although TTP-mediated regulation shows strong cell-type specificity (e.g., prominent in activated neutrophils but less so in macrophages) [61]. A broad spectrum of microRNAs (miR-29b, miR-15/16, miR-125b, miR-101, among others) also target MCL1, collectively limiting its translation [62]. For example, miR-29b directly downregulates MCL-1, facilitating apoptosis in cholangiocarcinoma and CLL cells [62]. High MCL1 levels in cancers are often due in part to loss or suppression of such miRNAs (Fig. 4).

The post-translational regulation strongly shapes MCL-1 function. MCL-1 protein has an extremely short half-life (~30 min). This rapid turnover is driven by constant ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Multiple E3 ubiquitin ligases target MCL-1 for proteasomal destruction. Mule (HUWE1/ARF-BP1) was the first identified E3 for MCL-1, attaching K48-linked polyubiquitin chains to MCL-1 [63]. Additionally, the SCF ubiquitin ligases SCF^Fbw7 and SCF^β-TrCP recognize phosphorylated degron motifs in the PEST region of MCL-1 and catalyze its ubiquitination [64]. Moreover, the APC/C^Cdc20 complex targets MCL-1 for destruction during mitotic exit, linking MCL-1 turnover to cell cycle progression [65]. On the other hand, deubiquitinase enzymes remove ubiquitin from MCL-1, markedly extending its half-life. USP9X binds and stabilizes MCL-1, and high USP9X can contribute to chemoresistance by sustaining MCL-1 levels [66]. Extensive phosphorylation of MCL-1 fine-tunes its stability and interactions. The protein contains multiple phospho-sites (Ser/Thr in its PEST region and elsewhere) that integrate signaling inputs. Phosphorylation by GSK3 (on Ser159/Thr163) creates the phosphodegron for Fbw7, leading to rapid MCL-1 destruction when survival kinase signaling (PI3K/AKT) is low. In contrast, ERK-mediated phosphorylation on some of the same sites can stabilize MCL-1 by interfering with GSK3-dependent degradation, thereby promoting cell survival. Other kinases (CDK1/2, JNK, p38-MAPK) phosphorylate MCL-1 under specific conditions, collectively modulating its binding to pro-apoptotic partners and its turnover. MCL-1 can also be cleaved by caspases (e.g., caspase-3) during apoptosis, removing its BH4 domain and inactivating its anti-apoptotic function. With such multilayered control, a sudden drop in MCL-1 levels (via translational inhibition, ubiquitination, or degradation) is a well-known trigger of apoptosis, especially in cells “addicted” to MCL-1 [35]. Localization-wise, MCL-1 predominantly resides at the mitochondrial outer membrane, anchored by its C-terminal transmembrane domain, where it directly interacts with pro-apoptotic proteins. A fraction of MCL-1 is also found in other mitochondrial compartments, including the mitochondrial matrix, suggesting additional roles in mitochondrial physiology [67]. Due to its rapid turnover, non-mitochondrial pools of MCL-1 (cytosolic, nuclear) are minimal under steady-state conditions [68]. (Fig. 4)

Beyond apoptosis, MCL-1 also has a mitochondria-intrinsic metabolic role. Anti-apoptotic Mcl-1 binds ACSL1 via a BH3-like helix and supports long-chain fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in mouse liver and heart [69]. Given the reliance of cardiomyocytes on FAO, this provides a plausible on-target basis for cardiac liability upon MCL-1 inhibition [69]. Gene-replacement studies further suggest that BCL-XL can partially substitute for MCL-1 in electron transport chain regulation, whereas FAO control remains MCL-1–dependent [70].

MCL-1 exerts its anti-apoptotic function by binding and neutralizing specific pro-apoptotic targets. It serves as the primary mitochondrial inhibitor of the effector protein BAK to prevent MOMP [71]. MCL-1 does not stably bind BAX but can inhibit its activation indirectly [71]; BAX is primarily sequestered by BCL-XL and BCL-2 [35, 36]. Notably, the BH3-only protein NOXA is a selective antagonist of MCL-1 (and BCL2A1): The BH3 domain of NOXA binds preferentially to MCL-1, displacing MCL-1 from BAK and other partners [8]. Induction of NOXA (for instance by p53 or hypoxia) therefore selectively neutralizes MCL-1; accordingly, many MCL-1-dependent cancers must suppress NOXA to survive [35]. MCL-1 also binds the direct activator BH3 proteins BIM, tBID, and PUMA (which can engage all anti-apoptotic members) [8, 35]. Through these interactions and modifications, MCL-1 functions as one of the crucial regulators to determine the apoptotic sensitivity of cells, which is also frequently exploited by cancer cells through elevated or dynamically adjusted MCL-1 levels. (Table 1)

BCL2A1 (A1/Bfl-1)

BCL2A1 (also known as BFL-1 in humans, A1 in mice) is an NF-κB-responsive anti-apoptotic protein with specialized roles in immune cells. NF-κB is the dominant inducer of BCL2A1 transcription, binding NFκB sites in the BCL2A1 promoter and driving robust BCL2A1 expression in response to inflammatory stimuli (e.g., TNF-α, LPS, CD40L) [38]. Other pathways (e.g., c/EBPβ [72], JAK/STAT3 [73], PI3K/AKT signaling [73]) can also induce BCL2A1 in certain contexts. Post-transcriptional control of BCL2A1 is less well-characterized than for other family members, but its mRNA contains AU-rich elements that suggest RBP regulation akin to BCL2 and MCL1 [74]. BCL2A1 (BCL2-related protein A1, alias BFL-1 in humans, A1 in mice) regulatory mechanisms.

The BCL2A1 gene is a direct NF-κB target and its transcription is also promoted by PI3K and JAK/STAT signaling downstream of inflammatory cytokines or antigen-receptor signals. BCL2A1 mRNA has a relatively short 3′-UTR and few validated microRNA regulators. BCL2A1 protein is highly unstable (~15 min half-life) because it is rapidly ubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome. As an anti-apoptotic BCL-2 paralog, BCL2A1 localizes to mitochondria and binds pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins (for example, tBID), preventing them from activating BAX/BAK. An alternatively spliced BCL2A1-S isoform lacks the C-terminal tail, localizes to the nucleus, and has enhanced anti-apoptotic activity; specific regulatory phosphorylation sites are not well established.

BCL2A1 protein is highly unstable under basal conditions, with a half-life of ~15–30 min due to continuous ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [75]. The E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM28 targets BCL2A1 at mitochondria, while TRIM17 opposes this effect by stabilizing the protein [75]. BCL2A1 is transiently induced in activated immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and T cells [74], and rapidly downregulated as stimuli abate to avoid prolonged inflammatory damage. Like other anti-apoptotic family members, it can be cleaved by caspase-3 to remove its BH4 domain, thereby neutralizing its function.

Rather than a canonical transmembrane anchor, BCL2A1 uses a C-terminal amphipathic helix (α9) for mitochondrial localization [76]. Alternative splicing generates a short isoform, BCL2A1-S, which lacks the C-terminal tail but carries a nuclear localization signal, resulting in nuclear distribution and enhanced anti-apoptotic activity [77] (Fig. 5).

BCL2A1 binds BH3-only activators (BIM, tBID, PUMA) and, like MCL-1, also binds the sensitizer NOXA [8, 35, 36], a NOXA-binding feature not shared with BCL-2, BCL-XL, or BCL-W. It does not bind BAD, underscoring its distinct interaction profile [8, 35, 36]. In co-immunoprecipitation assays, it interacts with BAK but not BAX, although it can protect against BAX-induced apoptosis [78]. This resembles MCL-1 in its ability to antagonize BAK and bind NOXA, while BCL2A1 retains a distinct interaction profile. Through this unique pattern, BCL2A1 can support survival when NOXA is present, acting as a short-lived survival factor in inflammatory settings [79]. This tightly regulated expression ensures timely support for immune cell viability while preserving the ability to terminate responses via apoptosis (Table 1).

BCL-W

BCL-W (encoded by BCL2L2) has a regulatory profile distinct from the other anti-apoptotic members, with relatively stable expression and specialized roles in certain long-lived cells (neurons, germ cells, some epithelial lineages) [80]. Its transcription is dynamic in specific contexts: NF-κB (p65/p52 subunits) [81], MEF2 [82], ETS-family factors [80], and β-catenin/TCF4 [83] can induce BCL2L2 expression in response to developmental or survival cues. BCL-W (BCL2-like protein 2) regulatory mechanisms.

BCL-W transcription is controlled by multiple transcription factors including NF-κB, MEF2, ETS1/2 and C/EBP. Wnt/β-catenin signaling (via TCF4) can also enhance BCL-W promoter activity. Post-transcriptionally, microRNAs such as miR-29 and miR-122 bind the BCL2L2 3′-UTR to repress BCL-W protein translation, whereas loss of these microRNAs (e.g., by a competing endogenous lncRNA) can de-repress BCL-W. BCL-W protein localizes to the mitochondrial outer membrane and binds/sequesters pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins, thereby inhibiting BAX/BAK activation.

Post-transcriptionally, BCL-W is tightly regulated by non-coding RNAs. Its 3′-UTR is targeted by numerous microRNAs (e.g., the miR-15/16 family, miR-122, miR-203, miR-133a) that suppress BCL-W expression [80], while certain long non-coding RNAs (FTX, SNHG1, HOTAIR) act as ceRNAs to sponge these microRNAs and restore BCL-W levels [80]. Additionally, RNA-binding and splicing factors such as U2AF1/2, hnRNPI, and SRSF2 are required for proper 3′ end processing and stability of the BCL2L2 mRNA. BCL-W protein exhibits unique conformational flexibility: in resting cells, its C-terminal helix folds into its own BH3-binding groove, keeping BCL-W in a latent, loosely membrane-associated state. Upon apoptotic stress or BH3-only protein binding, this C-terminal tail is displaced, triggering a conformational change that inserts BCL-W into the mitochondrial outer membrane, a “loose-to-tight” switch regulating its anti-apoptotic availability [84] (Fig. 6).

At the protein level, BCL-W acts as a targeting subunit for protein phosphatase 1α (PP1α), which recruits it to form a trimolecular complex with the pro-apoptotic protein BAD. This interaction facilitates the dephosphorylation of BAD [85], thereby indirectly promoting cell survival. It binds BAX with high affinity (Kd = 23 nM) to prevent its oligomerization [80], but also interacts with BAK (Kd = 114 nM) [80], contradicting the notion that it lacks BAK-binding capability entirely. Its BH3-only binding profile includes BIM, PUMA, tBID, and BAD, but excludes NOXA [8, 35, 36].

The current landscape of targeted therapies

From structure-based design to FDA approval: the venetoclax development saga

BCL-2 family proteins share a conserved α-helical fold with a BH3-binding groove formed by four distinct pockets (P1-P4) [5]. However, subtle sequence variations within this groove create opportunities for achieving binding selectivity among the BCL-2 family members [35] (Fig. 7). The development of venetoclax (ABT-199), a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, exemplifies how these structural differences can be exploited therapeutically. Early structure-guided drug design produced the inhibitor ABT-737 [86], which achieved sub-nanomolar affinity for BCL-2, BCL-XL, BCL-W, and its orally bioavailable analog navitoclax (ABT-263). However, navitoclax caused dose-limiting thrombocytopenia due to unexpected on-target BCL-XL inhibition in platelets. By identifying a unique BCL-2 crystal packing feature that enables a specific hydrogen bond with Asp103, which does not exist in BCL-XL, researchers rationally engineered ABT-199 (venetoclax) to achieve sub-nanomolar BCL-2 affinity (Ki <0.01 nM) and >4800-fold selectivity over BCL-XL, thereby eliminating dose-limiting thrombocytopenia [87].Fig. 7The structures and therapeutic targeting of BCL2 family proteins.Representative structures of major anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members (BCL-2, PDB: 1G5M; BCL-XL, PDB: 1LXL; MCL-1, PDB: 2MHS [137]; BCL-W, PDB: 1MK3; and BCL2A1, PDB: 5WHI) illustrate the conserved α-helical conformation and BH3-binding groove (highlighted in dashed box). Venetoclax received FDA accelerated approval in April 2016 for CLL with 17p deletion. The MURANO trial randomized 389 relapsed/refractory CLL patients to venetoclax-rituximab versus bendamustine-rituximab. Seven-year follow-up showed median progression-free survival (PFS) of 54.7 versus 17.0 months (HR 0.25), with 7-year PFS rate of 23% versus essentially 0% in controls. Bone marrow undetectable minimal residual disease (uMRD) in 70% of complete responders predicted median PFS exceeding 52 months from end of treatment [88]. The CLL14 trial established venetoclax-obinutuzumab as frontline standard of care. This Phase III study enrolled 432 previously untreated CLL patients with comorbidities. Six-year follow-up demonstrated median PFS of 76.2 months with venetoclax-obinutuzumab versus 36.4 months with chlorambucil-obinutuzumab, supporting fixed-duration (12-month) therapy achieving durable treatment-free remissions [89].

In AML, the VIALE-A Phase III trial compared venetoclax-azacitidine to azacitidine alone in 431 newly diagnosed patients aged ≥75 years or with comorbidities precluding intensive chemotherapy. Median overall survival was 14.7 versus 9.6 months (HR 0.66), with composite complete remission of 67% versus 29%. Three-year overall survival reached 25% versus 10%. Venetoclax-azacitidine earned FDA approval in October 2020 and represents current standard of care [17]. Molecular profiling identifies responders: NPM1-mutated AML shows particularly impressive responses with CR rates of 46–69%. IDH1/2-mutated AML demonstrates 56–70% composite CR rates. Conversely, TP53 mutations confer dismal outcomes with 0–53% response rates. FLT3-ITD, KRAS/NRAS, and PTPN11 mutations activate signaling pathways that upregulate MCL-1, driving primary resistance and shorter response duration [90].

Key resistance mechanisms to venetoclax include: MCL-1 upregulation (primary resistance mechanism across hematologic malignancies) [91], BCL-2 mutations (G101V, D103, F104 variants in ~50% of resistant CLL patients) [92], acquired BAX mutations (17% of relapsed AML) [93], TP53 clonal expansion (29–32% of relapsing patients) [90], and mitochondrial rewiring (altered OXPHOS dependency [94] and MFN2-driven mitophagy via increased mitochondria–ER tethering) [95].

Next-generation BCL-2 inhibitors address some of these limitations. Sonrotoclax (BGB-11417) employs a different P2 pocket binding mode with activity against G101V mutants, currently in Phase II/III trials in CLL [20]. Lisaftoclax (APG-2575), with enhanced cellular uptake, shows activity in venetoclax-resistant AML and has entered Phase III trials combined with azacitidine in AML [96].

BCL-XL targeting: from mechanism-based toxicity to tumor-selective strategies

BCL-XL represents a compelling cancer target with overexpression driving survival in various solid tumors and refractory leukemia clones. However, clinical development has been hampered by on-target platelet toxicity [23]. Navitoclax, a dual BCL-2/BCL-XL inhibitor, has been limited by on-target thrombocytopenia and myelosuppression that constrain dosing. In relapsed small cell lung cancer (SCLC), Phase II monotherapy showed a 2.6% response rate [97], and in myelofibrosis combination studies ~60% of patients required dose reductions [98]. AbbVie subsequently discontinued the Phase 3 TRANSFORM-2 trial (NCT04468984).

DT2216 represents a distinct advance by repurposing the navitoclax scaffold into a PROTAC that links BCL-XL ligand to von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) E3 ligase [18]. Since platelets express very little VHL, DT2216 achieves BCL-XL degradation mainly in tissues with high VHL (like tumors), which yielded over a 100-fold therapeutic window in preclinical models [18]. In an early-phase trial (NCT04886622), DT2216 demonstrated effective BCL-XL degradation with manageable thrombocytopenia at doses up to 0.4 mg/kg, supporting the feasibility of this approach [99]. Alternative PROTAC approaches include XZ739 (cereblon-based, DC50 = 2.5 nM) [100] and WH244, a dual BCL-XL/BCL-2 degrader with 6–7-fold improved potency through rational linker design [101].

Beyond PROTACs, the ADC ABBV-155 links a BCL-XL inhibitor to an anti-B7-H3 antibody, directing the drug to tumors. In a Phase I non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) trial, ABBV-155 yielded a 36% partial response rate without dose-limiting thrombocytopenia [102].

Additional strategies to mitigate platelet toxicity include prodrugs such as pelcitoclax, which has shown promise in EGFR-mutant NSCLC [103]. Clinically, understanding which tumor types absolutely depend on BCL-XL versus BCL-2 may guide optimal agent selection, or consider sequential treatment strategies (Fig. 7).

MCL-1 inhibitors face cardiac toxicity hurdle

MCL-1 upregulation represents the primary cause of venetoclax resistance in AML and predicts poor outcomes in multiple myeloma [13, 104]. As the most amplified anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family gene and essential for cardiomyocyte survival, MCL-1 is both a compelling target and significant challenge [105]. Potent compounds such as AZD5991 [22], AMG-176/397 [106], and ABBV-467 [107] produced asymptomatic troponin elevations in a majority of patients and occasional serious cardiac events, even at initial clinical doses, prompting the discontinuation of most of these programs by 2020-2021. As of late 2025, only PRT1419 (Prelude Therapeutics) remains in active development with no cardiac toxicity signals reported [5]. These class-wide on-target effects suggest that systemic MCL-1 blockade inherently risks heart damage. Potential solutions include tissue-selective PROTACs exploiting differential E3 ligase expression in tumors versus the heart, although VHL expression patterns in cardiac tissue require characterization. Development of cardiac-sparing MCL-1 inhibitors through structure-based design minimizing cardiomyocyte penetration, prodrug strategies activated preferentially in tumors, or acceptance of low-grade cardiac toxicity with close monitoring and dose optimization might enable clinical utility.

Given direct inhibitor toxicity, indirect approaches offer promise with potentially improved safety: CDK9 inhibitors (dinaciclib, AZD4573) block MCL-1 transcription exploiting its short half-life [108]; deubiquitinase inhibitors (USP9X, USP24) promote MCL-1 degradation [66]; SPHK1 inhibitors induce ceramide accumulation and NOXA upregulation, showing synergy with venetoclax in AML patient-derived xenografts [109]. An additional emerging strategy is targeted delivery of an MCL-1 inhibitor payload using an anti-CD74 ADC (S227928; payload S64315/MIK665), designed to spare non-hematologic tissues (including cardiomyocytes) and now advancing to Phase 1/2 evaluation in relapsed/refractory AML/CMML, as monotherapy or with venetoclax [110]. These approaches may avoid cardiac toxicity limiting direct MCL-1 inhibitors. (Fig. 7)

BCL-W and BCL2A1: emerging therapeutic targets in the BCL-2 family

Among the BCL-2 family, BCL-W and BCL2A1 have attracted interest as potential drug targets, although they appear less critical than BCL-2, BCL-XL, or MCL-1 in most tumors. Mice lacking Bcl-w are essentially normal except for male sterility due to Sertoli cell loss [111], suggesting BCL-W inhibition may have limited on-target toxicities compared to BCL-XL (thrombocytopenia) or MCL-1 (cardiotoxicity). BCL-W is overexpressed in many B-cell lymphomas and can cooperate with oncogenes (like Myc) to accelerate leukemia in mouse models [112], but functional genomic screens indicate few cancers are truly “addicted” to BCL-W [113]. Currently, no selective BCL-W inhibitors exist. Navitoclax targets BCL-W non-selectively alongside BCL-2 and BCL-XL. The limited cancer dependency and lack of selective inhibitors have deprioritized BCL-W as a therapeutic target [5].

Knockout mice show no apparent requirement for BCL2A1 in normal tissues, which suggests a favorable therapeutic window [74]. However, BCL2A1 has proven difficult to target with classical small molecules due to its shallow, hydrophobic BH3-binding groove and structural similarity to MCL-1. Drug development has thus focused on covalent inhibitors that exploit a unique cysteine (Cys55) in the BCL2A1 binding pocket. A recent small molecule covalent inhibitor demonstrated activity against venetoclax-resistant BCL2A1-overexpressing AML cells [114]. Indirect targeting strategies offer near-term translational potential. BET inhibitors (CPI203) suppress BCL2A1 transcription, overcoming ABT-199 resistance through simultaneous MYC and BCL2A1 downregulation [115].

Rational combination strategies and emerging therapeutic horizons

Hypomethylating agents (HMAs)

Venetoclax combinations with azacitidine or decitabine represent standard of care in AML [17]. HMAs sensitize cells to venetoclax by downregulating MCL-1 [116], inducing pro-apoptotic NOXA [117], and restoring GSDME expression to promote pyroptosis [117].

BTK inhibitor combinations

BTK inhibitors mobilize CLL cells from protective microenvironments [118], reduce MCL-1 expression [119], and target proliferating subpopulations while venetoclax targets quiescent cells [120]. Ibrutinib-venetoclax achieved 88% CR/CRi rate and 61% bone marrow uMRD in high-risk frontline CLL [121].

IDH inhibitor combinations

IDH mutations produce 2-hydroxyglutarate, which inhibits cytochrome c oxidase and drives a shift toward BCL-2 dependence [122]. Ivosidenib-venetoclax-azacitidine achieved 94% overall response rate and 87% composite CR in 31 IDH1-mutated AML patients, with 60% achieving MRD-negative status and median overall survival of 42 months. Notably, 64% cleared IDH1 mutations after cycle 5 [123].

Immunotherapy synergies

The intersection of BCL-2 inhibition with immunotherapy reveals unexpected synergies. Venetoclax increases intratumoral effector T cells and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy, as BCL-2-inhibited effector T cells compensate by upregulating BCL-XL. The combination induces Treg-to-Th17 plasticity through ceramide-induced integrated stress response [124]. In CLL, venetoclax-obinutuzumab-atezolizumab achieved 97% bone marrow uMRD with 4-year PFS of 89%, though 15% experienced checkpoint inhibitor toxicities [125]. Notably, venetoclax plus anti-CD47 (magrolimab) in AML failed in the ENHANCE-3 trial, demonstrating that mechanistic rationale alone does not always translate into clinical success [126]. BCL-2 mutant CAR-T cells (D103E mutation conferring venetoclax resistance) enable simultaneous BCL-2 inhibition in tumor cells while protecting engineered T cells, showing synergistic apoptosis induction in preclinical SCLC models [127]. Antibody-drug conjugates enable tumor-selective BCL-2 family inhibitor delivery [19]. Future ADCs might conjugate venetoclax or BCL-XL inhibitors to antibodies against CD19, CD22, CD33, BCMA, or HER2. This can potentially restrict systemic exposure while achieving high intratumoral concentrations.

Solid tumors present challenges but show combination therapy promise

Solid tumors have proven more refractory to BCL-2 family inhibition than hematologic malignancies, primarily because most solid tumors depend on multiple anti-apoptotic proteins, particularly MCL-1 and BCL-XL rather than BCL-2 alone and exhibit lower baseline apoptotic priming. Additional barriers include limited drug penetration into solid tissue and intratumoral heterogeneity with varying dependencies across tumor regions.

Breast cancer represents the most mature solid tumor application. Venetoclax combined with tamoxifen in ER + /BCL-2+ metastatic breast cancer achieved 54% radiologic response rate with median PFS of 36 weeks. Notably, 40% response occurred even in ESR1-mutant patients [128]. However, the subsequent Phase II VERONICA trial testing venetoclax-fulvestrant versus fulvestrant alone in post-CDK4/6 inhibitor patients failed to meet its primary endpoint, indicating that not all endocrine partners synergize equally and patient selection requires refinement beyond simply BCL-2 expression [129]. Melanoma dependency on BCL-XL and MCL-1 rather than BCL-2 necessitates dual targeting approaches [130]. Navitoclax combined with dabrafenib-trametinib in BRAF V600-mutant melanoma achieved 84% overall response rate with 20% complete responses, with 2-year overall survival of 74% versus 57% with doublet alone [130].

Overcoming these barriers requires: (1) biomarker-driven patient selection identifying BCL-2-high subsets (SCLC with BCL2 amplification, ER-positive breast cancer) or functional dependencies via BH3 profiling; (2) rational combinations with chemotherapy or targeted therapies that prime tumors for apoptosis (BRAF/MEK inhibitors in melanoma, endocrine therapy in breast cancer, KRAS inhibitors in pancreatic cancer); (3) dual BCL-2 family member targeting to address heterogeneous dependencies; and (4) enhanced delivery strategies using nanoparticles, ADCs, or PROTACs to overcome penetration limitations and achieve tumor-selective accumulation.

Next-generation agents and technologies reshape the therapeutic landscape

Dynamic BH3 profiling emerges as a functional predictive biomarker measuring real-time changes in mitochondrial priming after drug treatment. The technology exposes permeabilized cells to synthetic BH3 peptides representing different pro-apoptotic proteins, and measures cytochrome c release within 24 h to determine functional dependencies [131]. Microfluidic BH3 profiling reduces cell requirements tenfold, which allows screening on limited patient samples particularly valuable for solid tumor biopsies [132]. BH3 profiling can predict venetoclax combination synergies.

Nanoparticle delivery systems enable tumor-targeted BCL-2 family inhibition while reducing systemic toxicity [133]. P-selectin-targeted nanoparticles encapsulating S63845 or venetoclax achieve preferential tumor accumulation (confirmed by ex vivo imaging and mass spectrometry) with reduced hematologic toxicity and maintained efficacy in DLBCL models [133].

PROTAC degraders provide a platelet-sparing approach to targeting pro-survival BCL-2 family proteins beyond venetoclax. DT2216 is a BCL-XL selective degrader developed to mitigate thrombocytopenia and has entered first-in-human testing [99]. Related BCL-XL degraders and newer dual BCL-2/BCL-XL degraders extend this concept in preclinical studies [100, 101].

Beyond novel therapeutics, resistance monitoring technologies are advancing rapidly. Sensitive molecular assays (such as digital droplet PCR and targeted NGS) enable detection of BCL-2 G101V resistance mutations at low variant allele frequencies before clinical progression [10, 92]. Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals coexisting resistance mechanisms and heterogeneous dependencies within tumors, supporting the need for multi-targeted approaches.

Biomarker panels for venetoclax-based regimens now combine BH3 profiling with standard genetics/cytogenetics (for example, favorable: NPM1, IDH1/2; unfavorable: TP53, FLT3-ITD). Recent ASH reports propose additional tools, including the PRISM clinical-genomic score [134], a 25-gene VEN-R transcriptional score associated with nonresponse/relapse [135], and SPID protein-complex readouts that track on-treatment BCL-2 occupancy and BCL-2–BAX dynamics [136].

Conclusions

The BCL-2 family proteins exemplify how multilayered regulatory control governs fundamental cellular processes. This review has systematically examined the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and post-translational mechanisms regulating five anti-apoptotic family members. Each protein exhibits distinct regulatory signatures: BCL-2 is controlled through long-range chromatin interactions [26] and microRNA-mediated mRNA stability [28], maintaining relatively stable expression; BCL-XL undergoes dynamic alternative splicing between anti-apoptotic (BCL-XL) and pro-apoptotic (BCL-XS) isoforms [43], with G-quadruplex-dependent splicing factor recruitment representing a novel regulatory layer [46]; the exceptionally short half-life of MCL-1 (approximately 30 min) is driven by rapid ubiquitin-mediated degradation coordinated by multiple E3 ligases (Mule [63], SCF^Fbw7, SCF^β-TrCP) [64], which is counteracted by deubiquitinases (USP9X [66]) using extensive phosphorylation to fine-tune its stability; BCL2A1 demonstrates NF-κB-driven inducibility and even shorter turnover (~15–30 min) via TRIM28-mediated ubiquitination antagonized by TRIM17 [75]; BCL-W shows tissue-restricted expression [80] with unique conformational regulation [84]. These regulatory mechanisms establish cell type-specific and context-dependent apoptotic thresholds that cancer cells exploit through transcriptional upregulation, splicing alterations, or post-translational stabilization.

Understanding these regulatory networks has proven essential for therapeutic development. The observation that the rapid degradation of MCL-1 renders cells acutely sensitive to transcriptional or translational inhibition explains why CDK9 inhibitors and deubiquitinase inhibitors show promise as indirect MCL-1 targeting strategies [108]. Alternative splicing of BCL-XL offers therapeutic opportunities through small-molecule splicing modulators that shift the balance toward pro-apoptotic BCL-XS. The differential expression of E3 ligases between tumor cells and normal tissues provides the mechanistic foundation for PROTAC-based tissue-selective targeting [18]. Notably, BH3 profiling, which dynamically measures anti-apoptotic protein dependencies, serves as a functional biomarker integrating these complex regulatory networks into clinically actionable predictions of drug sensitivity.

FDA approval of venetoclax in 2016 validated the feasibility of targeting the BCL-2 family and reshaped treatment approaches in CLL [89] and AML [17]. Structure-guided optimization from ABT-737 through navitoclax to venetoclax achieved >4800-fold BCL-2 selectivity, circumventing BCL-XL-mediated thrombocytopenia [87]. However, resistance emerges through MCL-1 upregulation (primary mechanism) [91], BCL-2 mutations (G101V in ~50% of resistant CLL) [92], BAX mutations (17% of relapsed AML) [93], and metabolic reprogramming [94], limiting 7-year PFS to 23% in CLL despite impressive initial responses [88].

Targeting other family members faces distinct challenges. BCL-XL inhibition causes dose-limiting thrombocytopenia [23], as demonstrated by the 2.6% response rate of navitoclax in SCLC monotherapy [97]. PROTACs offer solutions by exploiting differential E3 ligase expression: DT2216 (VHL-recruiting BCL-XL degrader) achieved >100-fold therapeutic window preclinically and demonstrated safety in Phase I trials with minimal thrombocytopenia [99]. Alternative strategies include cereblon-based degraders (XZ739) [100], antibody-drug conjugates (ABBV-155: 36% partial response without thrombocytopenia) [102], and platelet-sparing prodrugs (pelcitoclax) [103]. MCL-1 inhibitor development has stalled due to class-wide cardiac troponin elevation reflecting on-target cardiotoxicity, with most programs (S64315, AZD5991, AMG-176, ABBV-467) discontinued [22, 107]. For MCL-1, tissue-selective PROTACs or indirect targeting via CDK9 inhibitors may circumvent cardiotoxicity [108].

The path forward requires integrating mechanistic insights with technological innovation. Critical priorities include: developing second-generation BCL-2 inhibitors against resistance mutations (sonrotoclax, lisaftoclax entering Phase II/III); achieving safe MCL-1 targeting through cardiac-sparing delivery or indirect mechanisms; implementing standardized BH3 profiling for patient selection; exploring rational combinations guided by regulatory network understanding; and expanding applications to solid tumors where complex multi-protein dependencies necessitate dual targeting and enhanced delivery strategies. From the discovery of BCL-2 to the clinical impact of venetoclax, the field exemplifies successful biology-to-bedside translation, yet continued innovation in resistance mechanisms, tissue-selective targeting, and predictive biomarkers remains essential to fulfill the therapeutic potential of apoptosis-directed cancer therapy.
