Current status and future outlook of artificial intelligence and automation in intravenous infusion systems
Authors: Islam M Ibrahim, Rahma A Elkomy, Merna A Mohamed, Fatemah A Farok, Roaa E Emam, Salma A kamel, Reham M Asem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe combination of the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), and wearable biosensors is transforming healthcare by allowing continuous, real-time monitoring and more intelligent clinical decision-making. This review explores AI-driven monitoring, smart intravenous (IV) infusion automation, and wearable biosensors, and proposes Intelligent IV Automation and Smart Patient Synchronization (INFUSYNC), a conceptual framework synchronizing monitoring, automated fluid delivery, and predictive analytics. The literature shows meaningful progress: wearables increasingly support non-contact monitoring with near clinical-grade accuracy for select parameters, while infusion pumps with dose-error-reduction and AI-assisted decision support are reported to improve delivery precision and safety. Predictive models further support early detection of physiological decline. Progress remains uneven, however: most AI-enabled infusion systems are prototype-stage, closed-loop control remains rare, and interoperability, data security, and privacy remain underdeveloped. Future systems will need compatible, security-by-design, clinically validated, feedback-controlled architectures. Overall, this review outlines a clear but early-stage trajectory toward integrated smart-health environments for IV therapy.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The arrival of the IoT, AI, and wearable sensors has fundamentally changed both the provision of healthcare as well as monitoring of patients1,2. Healthcare providers around the world are rapidly transforming how they look at individual health problems, track patient conditions, and provide care right from redesigning hospitals to rapidly developing diagnostics, drugs, and vaccines. This technological revolution provides an example of one of the most significant wholesale disruptive changes to modern medicine3. The disruptive nature of these kinds of technologies allows for ongoing, continuous, and real-time monitoring of patients, affording the healthcare system a new approach to longstanding problems in healthcare practice such as better resource utilization, patient safety, and decreased burden on healthcare workers. Contemporary healthcare delivery systems are grappling with increasingly complex problems that require innovative, technology-driven solutions 4. The sharp rise in healthcare infrastructure growth is representative of an increasing demand related to aging populations, number of people with chronic diseases, and number of patients with ongoing healthcare needs5. Likewise, the increasing complexity of surgical interventions necessitates advanced monitoring systems in order to have safe and effective care6. Healthcare professionals frequently treat patients experiencing multiple chronic conditions as opposed to just one condition, which reinforces the need for comprehensive monitoring systems which can record multiple physiological parameters, and provide early warning detection of abnormal values7,8. This is a shift from care being based on a reactive approach to proactive care supported through IoT-based real-time monitoring and timely intervention9,10. This is not only a technological step forward but wider engagement by the patient for prevention, early detection and personalized management of care. Not only are home and remote monitoring solutions extending the boundaries of patient care beyond the walls of the hospital11,12, but wearable biosensors have become the most fundamental element of contemporary patient management, whereby vital parameters can be universally monitored in a non-invasive and continuous manner which could not be done using traditional methods13. Advanced innovations which utilize Photoplethysmography (PPG) and Electrocardiography (ECG) methods within removable wearables have produced models which can reliably and reproducibly record Heart Rate (HR), Blood Pressure (BP), Respiratory Rate (RR) and Peripheral Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂) differentiation in wrist worn formats with clinically relevant accuracy14,15. Integration of several sensing technologies into one format has produced sophisticated monitoring systems, which ultimately can engage patients by providing complete health metrics in real-time16. The inclusion of AI and Machine Learning (ML) has broadened the functionality of wearable devices from basic data reporting to predictive analytics and the personalization of health data17. AI is capable of recognizing subtle patterns in data, which can indicate an early stage of health decline and allow for preventive action before symptomatic presentation18. In addition to monitoring, AI/ML are changing the ways decisions are made in the clinic, the efficiency of workers, and the management of patient’s lifestyles19,20. Nonetheless, major challenges will have to be overcome. These are accuracy of measurement across populations of patients, privacy and security of data and incorporation of wearable data into existing workflows in the clinic21. Motion artifacts, variation in skin tone, and differences in sensor calibration can all negatively affect data quality22. Health data must also ensure privacy and security provisions since this data can be so sensitive23. IV therapy is an important aspect of hospital care, with millions of patients receiving IV medications, fluids, and nutrients each day in health care environments throughout the world24. While conventional IV infusion systems work well, they are also defenseless to human error and rely on manual monitors, creating a significant burden for nurses and a risk to safety25. The increased complexity of drug protocols and infusions only worsens the possibility of dose and timing errors that can result in adverse drug reactions, failure of treatment, or life-threatening events26,27. Intelligent infusion pumps, which use Drug Error Reduction Software (DERS), drug libraries, and standardized alerts have improved dosing accuracy and patient safety28,29. These pumps have extensive medication databases that define concentration ranges, dosing limits, and compatibility standards, all in place to prevent the most common infusion errors30. In addition, with decision support tools embedded in the pump, the medication order can be processed in real-time, allowing the system to automatically detect and avert unsafe dosing31. Currently, smart infusion systems include automated monitoring and alert capabilities that reduce errors associated with improper doses, variations in infusion rates, and drug incompatibilities32. These infusion systems have now evolved to incorporate IV monitoring solutions that combine computer vision (CV), IoT sensors, and AI algorithms to analyse fluid levels, flow rates, line patency, and infusion site integrity33–35. IoT integration has enabled a shift in medical technology from stand-alone medical devices to connected medical devices that facilitate the efficient, transparent transmission of health information and validation of treatment across different care settings36. These systems will enable a shift from insurance of patient safety via stand-alone biomedical technology to real time interoperable subsystems and also to centralized computer monitoring stations37. The comprehensive patient monitoring across physiological parameters not yet achieved; however, connected and networked medical devices and sensors will help facilitate monitoring of patients in its entirety8,38. IoT-enabled healthcare solutions can bring together wearable sensors, alerting mechanisms, infusion pumps, and environmental sensors into a collaborative care delivery system39. The integration of data from multiple sources will provide a more comprehensive picture of the patient’s health and facilitate the identification of trends and anomalies not possible when looking at isolated data streams40,41. Typically, these systems take advantage of cloud computing, edge computing, and related mobile apps to allow real-time data retrieval and remote monitoring12,42. Cloud-based results promote clinician access to patient data from any location with an internet connection, while edge computing reduces delay and supports a more immediate response to emergencies40,43. AI and ML tools, effortlessly integrated into healthcare monitoring, allow systems that can analyse data, determine patterns, and create predictive models20. Specifically, ML algorithms process continuous physiological data, identify early signs of deterioration, and deliver clinical support with real-time analysis44–46. Likewise, CV applications have a high level of accuracy with automating monitoring tasks like IV bag identification, liquid level estimation, patient motion tracking and fall detection, which enhances clinician task performance while assuring consistency in monitoring33–35. While the technology has matured, it has not addressed all challenges. Data privacy, data security, and interoperability are still significant barriers to ubiquity47–49. The consequences of continuous monitoring increases new privacy issues as health data are often sensitive and personal23. There are also challenges related with interoperability between different medical devices, communication protocols, and data standards. The success of wearable biosensors relies on the adoption of shared standards, adherence to regulations and policies, consistent clinical trials to evaluate accuracy, reliability and usability among diverse populations and healthcare contexts50–52.

Despite the rapid growth of literature in each of these domains, existing reviews have largely treated them in isolation. Prior surveys on smart infusion systems51 have focused narrowly on pump automation and error reduction without integrating real-time patient physiological data. Similarly, reviews of wearable biosensors52 have concentrated on monitoring accuracy and device design without linking wearable output to therapeutic decision-making such as infusion rate adjustment. Reviews covering AI and IoT in healthcare more broadly53 provide useful taxonomies but stop short of addressing the specific workflow of IV therapy management. To our knowledge, no prior review has simultaneously synthesized wearable biosensor monitoring, smart IV infusion automation, and AI/ML-driven clinical decision support within a single unified framework. This review addresses that gap through the proposed INFUSYNC framework, which provides the first integrated perspective on how these three subsystems—continuous patient monitoring, intelligent fluid delivery, and data-driven analytics—can converge to form a safer, more responsive, and clinically viable healthcare monitoring system.

The paper is organized as follows: Introduction shows a brief to the current obstacles in healthcare delivery and the reason for integrated smart solutions. Review Methodology describes the structured approach employed to identify and review the relevant literature. Discussion presents an extensive view of the results, including general tendency, the relationship to the INFUSYNC framework, challenges and deployment readiness, and future research directions. Conclusion gives a summarizing key findings and proposed areas for future research.

Review methodology

To represent the key technologies for future intelligent health systems, a three-tier methodological approach was adopted that examined the three component areas of IV infusion systems, wearable biosensors and AI/IoT delivery platforms within health care systems because of their interdependent nature and support for each other. Infusion systems provide accurate dosing of medication. Wearable devices enable continues monitoring of patients and AI/IoT technologies provide integration of patient information, automation of healthcare processes and decision-making. The combination of these technology domains was specifically targeted to determine how these will converge together to provide safe, timely, and patient-focused healthcare system.

Eligibility criteria

The methodology in the selection process ensured reliability, relevance and quality of studies. The following content included utilizing predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria: next-generation IV infusion systems, wearable biosensors/bracelets, IoT-enabled healthcare apps, and AI-enabled patient monitoring systems (PMS).

Literature search and study selection

A thorough investigation of published literature was performed utilizing journals of high prominence including npj Digital Medicine, Scientific Reports, Healthcare, JMIR, (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) IEEE Journal, Diagnostics, and Bioengineering, and databases or platforms including Springer, Elsevier, ACM Digital Library, and ResearchGate. The literature search focused primarily on recent studies and utilized the following five terms as search criteria: smart infusion pump, automated IV therapy, wearable biosensor, smart health monitoring, AI in healthcare, and IoT hospital systems. The use of these keywords was based on their intersection with each of the core areas encompassed by the INFUSYNC Framework. Data collection process and comparison

To support a clear and systematic review, the literature was collected and organized according to the four main parts of the proposed INFUSYNC framework: (1) IV infusion systems and automation, (2) wearable biosensors and smart bracelets, and (3) AI/IoT-based healthcare applications and clinical decision support. In each part, the studies were compared using clear criteria such as research aim, method used, application area, technologies involved, experimental design, results, and limitations. This approach helps to identify the strengths and gaps in each field and shows how these different tiers can work together. Overall, it supports the development of an integrated and patient-centered smart healthcare system.

The fact that none of these security approaches have been translated into wearable biosensor system design confirms that security remains a critical unaddressed gap in the wearable monitoring literature, and that INFUSYNC must incorporate privacy-preserving data transmission as a foundational design requirement for its wristband monitoring module.

As demonstrated in the representative analysis Table 4, a fundamental trade-off exists across current IV infusion systems: clinically validated systems lack AI, while AI-advanced systems lack clinical validation. Furthermore, data security and wearable patient monitoring integration are entirely absent across all reviewed studies regardless of their technical sophistication. This gap confirms that no existing IV infusion system simultaneously achieves clinical validation, AI-driven intelligence, secure data transmission, EHR interoperability, and integration with continuous patient monitoring. AI and ML are progressively used to enhance IV infusion safety by detecting medication errors earlier than traditional rule-based systems31,55. However, many of these approaches are still limited, as most AI-based systems lack strong real-world clinical validation and rely on scanty infusion datasets27. A major gap also exists in closed-loop control, where most systems can detect problems but still require manual clinician intervention instead of automatically adjusting infusion rates56–58. In addition, interoperability with hospital systems is weak, with very few solutions integrated with EHR or compliant with HL7 FHIR56,59. Overall, this appear that IV infusion automation still requires further development in AI-based detection, closed-loop control, and system integration to match the attention given to wearable devices.

Data security, an important aspect lacking adequate coverage in the reviewed IV infusion literature. Though some studies assume safety data processing is critical in the therapeutic setting, documentation of resilient and methodically implemented security protections within IV infusion systems themselves are inconsistent or entirely absent. Such problems are more significant considering the sensitivity of medication administration data relayed between infusion pumps, monitoring systems and hospital network. In contrast, the more extensive IoT healthcare domain has explained specific implementation in developing and validating robust data protection solutions. These include blockchain-enabled federated learning (FL) frameworks for secure data sharing50. Authorized blockchain architectures drafted for energy-efficient and secure healthcare IoT systems60, blockchain-secured EHR for smart healthcare environments61, and partitioned blockchain encryption approaches that enhance privacy in IoT-based medical applications62–64. Constantly recognize security and privacy as persistent and critical challenges across IoT healthcare deployments, while65,66 provide a comprehensive taxonomy of blockchain-based solutions tailored for AI-driven healthcare systems. This limited adoption of deep-rooted security approaches emphasizes a clear gap between existing technological competencies and their practical application in IV infusion systems. To bridge this gap, there is a need to move beyond treating security as a secondary consideration and instead adopt a security-by-design approach, where data protection is integrated as a core element of system architecture from the outset.

Figure 2 compares Traditional Patient Care System (TPCS) with Smart Patient Care System (SPCS) in hospital wards for handling nurse calls and alerts 2.

Comparative illustration of a traditional patient care system and a smart patient care system in a hospital ward, demonstrating enhanced alert classification and nursing response capability in the smart system2.

The two systems being referred to here are a TPCS and a SPCS. When a patient calls for assistance, they are using one of the buttons on the system to trigger an alert, including the patient’s bed number, which is displayed on the monitors in the hallway and also at the Nursing Station. One key difference between the TPCS and SPCS is that only the bed number is displayed with the TPCS and there is no other information provided about that patient or their condition. Conversely, the SPCS will show both the bed number and the type of alert associated with the request. Because the SPCS has added the alert type classification element to the system, the nursing staff can now prioritize the urgency of their response, thereby reducing the number of unnecessary emergency response calls for routine assistance requests and more quickly respond to actual emergency situations. There was a measurable improvement in both the overall efficiency (work flow) and in response accuracy as the result of utilizing the SPCS versus TPCS in a live clinical ward evaluation. The intelligent alerting system component that is found within the SPCS is part of the INFUSYNC framework and represents a key building block in the continued development of an IoT-based nursing support network.

Discussion

General Trends

The literature reviewed indicates that there is now a convergence of AI, IoT and wearable technology used in health care for continuous monitoring, infusion management, and early warning systems1,8,12. In every study reviewed, there appears to be a movement away from isolated sensing devices toward integrated digital eco-systems consisting of biosensors, ML, and wireless communications; thus, creating real-time decision support capabilities1,40,54. However, a comparative analysis reveals that most wearable systems are more mature in terms of continuous monitoring14,69–82, while IV infusion technologies are still largely focused on basic automation and alert generation rather than intelligent decision-making24,26,53. Smart infusion systems primarily focus on safety features such as flow control and alarm systems, while wearable devices dominate in terms of performance focus as long-term patient monitoring and predictive analytics22,69,70,83, whereas smart infusion systems primarily emphasize safety features such as flow control and alarm systems30,84–86. This indicates an imbalance in technological maturity, where wearable technologies are generally more advanced in AI integration15,17,22 compared to infusion systems, which remain more hardware-centric and rule-based25,87,88.

Relation to INFUSYNC

From the perspective of INFUSYNC, the literature highlights a clear gap between monitoring systems and therapeutic delivery systems. Wearable technologies such as smart wristbands explain strong capability in continuous physiological tracking 22,69,70, while smart infusion systems mainly operate in isolation with limited intelligence and weak contextual awareness89–91. Although IoT-based infusion pumps provide basic connectivity and alerting functions57,84,89, they lack integration with real-time physiological feedback loops27,55, unlike wearable systems that increasingly incorporate AI-driven analytics15,17,22. This separation indicates that current approaches are fragmented rather than unified3,9,12, and no existing system fully bridges patient monitoring and infusion control in a closed, synchronized architecture19,27,55. Therefore, INFUSYNC addresses a critical gap by attempting to merge these two domains, enabling bidirectional interaction between patient state monitoring and infusion decision-making31,56,69. This positions the framework as a more holistic alternative compared to existing single-domain solutions26,29,32.

Challenges, limitations, and deployment readiness

Regardless of the significant advances in the literature, different persistent limitations still obstruct real-world clinical implementation. First, most AI-based IV infusion systems rest at the prototype or experimental phase with limited clinical validation33–35,55, while wearable technologies are more mature due to wider real-world adoption and testing14,69,70. This clearly shows an imbalance in development maturity between monitoring devices and therapeutic infusion systems. Second, interoperability remains a major challenge. Very few systems support integration with hospital infrastructures such as EHR systems or standardized communication protocols like HL7 FHIR56,59,92, which limits their ability to function within real clinical workflows9,49,93,94. As a result, most solutions still operate as isolated systems rather than fully connected hospital platforms. Security is another critical but underdeveloped area. None of the reviewed IV infusion systems include strong, built-in data protection mechanisms28,30,84, despite handling highly sensitive patient information. Although advanced security solutions such as blockchain, FL and encryption frameworks are widely discussed in broader IoT healthcare research 48,60–62, they are rarely implemented in actual infusion or wearable systems, highlighting a clear gap between theory and practice49,65. In addition, closed-loop control is still largely missing in most IV systems84,85,89. Current solutions mainly rely on alert-based systems57,89,91, where clinicians must manually respond to events, rather than systems automatically adjusting infusion rates. Only a few studies, such as55, attempt closed-loop control using DL, but these remain in laboratory settings without real clinical deployment27,55. This creates a gap between AI potential and real hospital implementation, where human engagement is still required in most situations56,58,59.

Overall, the analysis shows that wearable systems are closer to clinical readiness due to higher maturity and adoption 14,69,70, while IV infusion systems still lag behind in intelligence, interoperability, and autonomy31,55,57. This clear inequality highlights the need for integrated frameworks such as INFUSYNC that unify sensing, decision-making, and infusion control into a single clinically achievable system19,20,45.

Future directions and system-level perspective

The future direction of smart healthcare is clearly moving toward integrated ecosystems that integrate wearables, infusion systems, and cloud-based analytics12,40,54. However, the literature suggests that most current research still enhance individual components rather than full system integration3,9,10,49. As a result, scalability and clinical approval remain limited4,8,95. For INFUSYNC, this highlights the significance of not only integrating technologies but also ensuring clinical validation14,70, security by design48,60,62, and interoperability with hospital infrastructure56,59,92. Future systems must move beyond monitoring toward predictive and autonomous healthcare models19,45,46 that reduce clinician workload while maintaining safety21,23,47. In conclusion, the reviewed studies indicate that while enabling technologies are rapidly progressing across all clusters1,12,54, their integration remains the key obstacle3,8,9. INFUSYNC is positioned to address this gap by unifying wearable monitoring and infusion automation into a single intelligent, secure, interoperable, and clinically validated framework31,48,55,56,69.

Conclusion

This paper reviewed the convergence of wearable wristband monitoring, smart infusion automation, and continuous patient monitoring in preceded safer and more intelligent healthcare systems. The findings prove that AI, IoT, and wearable technologies are not only emerging concepts but have already achieved clinically validated performance levels in several domains. For example, AI-based monitoring systems have reported high accuracy with HR error of ±1.1 bpm, SpO₂ error of ±0.72%, and RR error of ±0.84 breaths per minute (r = 0.97)69, while wearable IoT devices have shown uniformly low measurement variations within ±1.83 bpm for HR, ±1.2% for SpO₂, and ±0.15 °C for temperature70. These results confirm that the most important sensing and analytics technologies are already achieving clinical-grade consistency. Importantly, obverse to the perception that key challenges remain unresolved, the literature provides diverse validated solutions addressing main system limitations. Security and privacy concerns have been actively tackled using blockchain-enabled FL48, permissioned blockchain frameworks60, secure EHR integration models61, and advanced encryption-based architectures62, all of which demonstrate experimentally validated effectiveness rather than theoretical proposals. Similarly, problems related to sensor instability and noise have been mitigated through in-sensor processing techniques15 and robust ML ensembles22. Furthermore, interoperability with hospital systems has already been demonstrated in clinical environments through HL7-compliant smart pump integrations in ICU and pediatric settings56,59,92, confirming that integration with EHR workflows is technically achievable. Therefore, the major restriction is not the absence of solutions, but rather the fragmentation of existing validated technologies across separate domains. Current studies largely validate wearable systems, infusion systems, and security frameworks independently, but fail to evaluate their combined performance within a unified clinical environment. No study to date has simultaneously assessed wearable bio-signal monitoring, closed-loop IV infusion control, and secure interoperable communication under real hospital conditions, leaving an important gap in system-level validation rather than component-level capability. The INFUSYNC framework directly addresses this integration gap by combining these already-validated technologies into a unified architecture and evaluating them within the same clinical workflow. This shifts the focus from proposing separate solutions to integrating and clinically validating existing high-maturity technologies in an arranged system. The proposed INFUSYNC implementation further strengthens this direction by enabling real-world testing of synchronized wearable monitoring and infusion automation.

Future work should therefore move beyond repeating isolated technological improvements and instead prioritize: (1) multi-site clinical validation of integrated systems under real workflow conditions, (2) mandatory HL7 FHIR-based interoperability as a design standard, and (3) systematic deployment of already-validated blockchain and encryption frameworks within unified healthcare architectures. Addressing integration and system-level validation, rather than re-proposing already-solved component-level challenges, will be the key step toward translating these technologies into safe, scalable, and clinically deployable healthcare solutions66.
