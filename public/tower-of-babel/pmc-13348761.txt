Calpain cleaves the distal carboxyl terminus of TRPV1 and modulates its tachyphylaxis
Authors: Jin-Yan Jiang, Xiao-Chen Wang, Jing Yang, Shu-Wen Gao, Ji-Xuan Xu, Mu-Lan Chen, Zhong-Zhe Zhang, Jiao Liu, Yun Wang, Ping Liang, Ying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransient receptor potential vanilloid-1 (TRPV1) plays a critical role in noxious heat sensation in physiological conditions and pain hypersensitivity in pathological pain. Capsaicin, a classic TRPV1 agonist, is used to relieve pain partially through induction of TRPV1 desensitization. Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to contribute to TRPV1 desensitization. However, the limiting factors or antagonistic mechanisms of TRPV1 desensitization remain poorly understood.ResultsStimulation of TRPV1 by capsaicin (1 µM) activates calpain without causing obvious cellular injury. Calpain cleaves rTRPV1 at the G819/S820 site located in the distal carboxyl terminus. The truncated mutant, TRPV1 Δ820, shows reduced plasma membrane localization partially due to impaired subunit assembly. Notably, TRPV1 Δ820 shows increased resistance to tachyphylaxis as induced by repetitive capsaicin stimulation, whereas calpain-1 knockdown slightly enhances TRPV1 tachyphylaxis. In vivo delivery of TRPV1 Δ820 rescued the pain behavioral deficits in nociceptive pain models of Tprv1−/− mice, but not in inflammatory pain models.ConclusionsCalpain is activated following the activation of TRPV1 and cleaves its distal carboxyl terminus. This reduces the plasma membrane localization of TRPV1 while enhancing its resistance to receptor tachyphylaxis. This study revealed a constraining mechanism for TRPV1 desensitization, which maintains the receptor function in an appropriate range.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01585-0.

Introduction

Chronic pain refers to pain that persists for more than three months after the initial injury or disease. It is one of the leadings causes of disability worldwide [1]. The global prevalence of chronic pain is approximately 30%, representing a great demand for safer and more effective analgesic agents. Among numerous receptors and ion channels involved in pain modulation, transient receptor potential vanilloid-1 (TRPV1) has long been of interest due to its crucial role in pain transduction and transmission. It is closely associated not only with heat sensation but also with pain hypersensitivity and peripheral sensitization in chronic pain [2–4]. These lines of evidence collectively indicate that TRPV1 is a critical candidate for the development of analgesic agents.

TRPV1 is a polymodal nociceptive detector highly expressed in primary sensory neurons. It can be activated by noxious heat (> 43 °C), protons, endocannabinoids, or other lipid metabolites. The functional TRPV1 channel exists as a tetramer, with its central pore formed by S5–S6 and flanked by S1–S4 [5, 6]. Each subunit of TRPV1 consists of six transmembrane helices (S1–S6), a pore-forming domain, and intracellular amino (N) and carboxyl (C) termini (hereafter labelled Nt and Ct respectively). Activation of TRPV1 allows the influx of cations, primarily Ca2+, which activates multiple intracellular signaling pathways and enhances cellular excitability. Despite these findings, development of TRPV1 antagonists as analgesics has been hindered by their severe side effect of elevated core body temperature. Recent studies have provided new insights into the thermoregulatory effects of TRPV1 from a conformational perspective and developed novel TRPV1-targeted analgesics without disrupting body temperature regulation [7–9].

Paradoxically, capsaicin, a classic TRPV1 agonist, has long been used as an analgesic. High concentration capsaicin (8%) patches, for example, exert persistent analgesic effects on patients with postherpetic neuralgia [10]. Cytotoxic effect of TRPV1 overactivation and subsequent ablation of axonal terminals are two key contributors to its analgesic effect. Recently, positive allosteric modulators (PAMs), which do not directly activate TRPV1 but enhance the efficacy of TRPV1 agonists such as s-RhTx, have been developed to achieve long-lasting analgesia when coadministered with capsaicin [11]. On the other hand, low concentration capsaicin (0.1–0.25%), when used repeatedly, has been shown to reduce the Visual Analogue Scale score of patients with neuropathic pain [12]. This analgesic effect of capsaicin has been associated with TRPV1 desensitization and inactivation of voltage-gated sodium channels or voltage-gated calcium channels. Regardless of its concentration, due to the initial burning sensation, concurrent anesthesia is usually needed for the application of capsaicin to relieve pain.

Further elucidation of the regulatory mechanisms of TRPV1 at the molecular level would facilitate the development of novel analgesic drugs targeting TRPV1. To this end, we noticed that TRPV1 current exhibits prominent Ca2+-dependent desensitization, as characterized by acute desensitization, a rapid reduction of channel activity during a single agonist stimulation, and tachyphylaxis, a progressive reduction in responsiveness upon repeated agonist stimulation. From a mechanistic perspective, tachyphylaxis can be partially explained by a failure of recovery from acute desensitization [13, 14]. The magnitude of TRPV1 tachyphylaxis is inversely proportional to recovery of receptors on the plasma membrane following agonist-induced internalization [15]. Multiple molecular mechanisms, including calcineurin dependent dephosphorylation [16], binding of calmodulin (CaM) to the ankyrin-like repeat domain [17–20], and activation of phospholipase C followed by phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) degradation [21–23], have been proposed to contribute to TRPV1 desensitization. In addition, recruitment of the phosphodiersterase PDE4D5 to the plasma membrane by β-arrestin-2 is also shown to be required for TRPV1 desensitization [24]. In terms of the critical domains of TRPV1 contributing to channel desensitization, both intracellular N and C termini are indispensable, not only because of the presence of phosphorylational sites, CaM binding, or PIP2 interaction sites, but also because of direct Nt-Ct interaction [25, 26]. As TRPV1 transitions from the activation state to the desensitization state, an interaction between the Nt-Ct occurs, which leads to constriction of the channel’s outer pore and subsequent reduction in channel permeability.

Based on existing data, we postulated that TRPV1 desensitization may involve regulatory mechanisms at the protein level. Therefore, we investigated the potential role of calpain, a Ca2+-activated cysteine protease, in the regulation of protein levels of TRPV1 as well as its tachyphylaxis. Our results showed that calpain was activated following stimulation of TRPV1 by a non-toxic dose of capsaicin. The potential cleavage sites and their effects on receptor localization and tachyphylaxis were characterized. Furthermore, the direct evidence for the involvement of calpain-1 in this regulatory process was provided. The contribution of this regulatory mechanism to the in vivo function of TRPV1 was also investigated. Altogether, this work reveals that calpain functions as a novel regulator of TRPV1 desensitization, which diminishes channel function and concurrently enhances its resistance to tachyphylaxis.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

PCR primers for plasmid construction were designed using SnapGene and were synthesized by RuiBiotech (Beijing, China). Detailed information is shown in Table S1. The construction approach of eukaryotic expression plasmids of GST-TRPV1 and TRPV1-GFP, the prokaryotic expression plasmids of GST-TRPV1 Nt and GST-TRPV1 Ct was described in our previous studies [27, 28]. PCR products were purified using a TIANgel Purification kit (TIANGEN, DP219-02). DNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For vectors, pEGFP-N1 was used for the eukaryotic expression and PGEX-5X-1 and pET-28a (+) were used for the prokaryotic expression. Then a seamless ligation system (GenStar, T196) was prepared at a 3:1 ratio (insert fragment: vector) and incubated at 50 °C for 15 min. The reaction products were then transformed into competent cells (DH5α for eukaryotic expression, BL21 for prokaryotic expression). All plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing.

Cell culture, plasmid transfection and drug treatment

The human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cell line was purchased from the Cell Resource Center of Peking Union Medical College. Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells stably expressing TRPV1 were described in our previous studies [27, 29]. HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM (MACGENE), while CHO cells are maintained in Ham’s F-12 (MACGENE) medium. Both media were supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). Cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cells were passaged 24 h before transfection to achieve 60–80% confluency. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). 1 µg empty vector or 2 µg recombinant plasmids were transfected in a 35 mm dish unless otherwise specified. Plasmid DNA and P3000 reagent (1:2 ratio) were mixed in 100 µL of Opti-MEM and incubated for 5 min. Meanwhile, 100 µL lipofectamine 3000 was diluted with equal volume of Opti-MEM and the mixture was added to the previous DNA mixture. After 15 min, 200 µL of the transfection mix was added to cells in 800 µL of fresh medium. The culture medium was replaced with fresh antibiotic-free medium 6 h post-transfection and cells were harvested 24–48 h after transfection.

Capsaicin (Sigma, 404-86-4) was dissolved in alcohol to prepare a stock solution of 100 mM, which was diluted to the appropriate concentration just before use. For the inhibitors experiment, Calpeptin (Merck, 03-34-0051), ALLN (Merck, 208719), MDL28170 (Merck, M6690), Z-VAD-FMK (Merck, V116), or MG132 (Merck, M8699) was added 30 min in advance.

shRNA-mediated knockdown of human CAPN1

To suppress the endogenous expression of calpain-1 in HEK293 cells, three specific shRNA plasmids targeting human CAPN1 (designed and synthesized by RuiBiotech, China) were designed. The target sequences were as follows: shRNA1: 5’-AGGCCTATGCCAAGGTAAATG-3’; shRNA2: 5’-GCCTTGCCTGCAGACTATAAA-3’; shRNA3: 5’-CATTTCCAGCTGTGGCAATTT-3’. A non-targeting scramble sequence (5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTT-3’) was used as a negative control. HEK293 cells were transfected with the shRNA plasmids as described above. The knockdown efficiency was validated 48 h later by Western Blotting.

Cell staining and fluorescence imaging

ER-Tracker Red (Yeason, 40764ES20) was diluted to 0.2 µM in Hank’s balanced salt solution (Yeason, 60147ES76) and added to the culture dish. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 20 min and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and rinsed with PBS. Cellular nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,258 dye (Yeasen, 40730ES03) for 10 min at room temperature. After rinsing with PBS, cells were mounted using a mounting medium (ZSGB-BIO, Zli-9556). Images were captured using an Olympus FV 3000 confocal laser scanning microscope with a 100× HC PL APO objective (NA 1.45; oil).

ImageJ software was used for the quantitative line scan analysis of fluorescence. First, the spatial scale was calibrated and unified according to the pixel size of the confocal system (0.155 μm/pixel). Then fluorescence channels were split, and a straight line with a fixed width of 5 pixels and a defined length of 20 μm was applied to the regions of interest (ROIs). The line was positioned to traverse representative fluorescent signals, such as the plasma membrane and adjacent intracellular compartments. The “Plot Profile” function was then employed to extract the spatial distribution of fluorescence intensity along the designated path. To ensure data consistency, all images within the same experimental group were analyzed using identical line parameters (5-pixel width, 20-µm length) and scale settings. The resulting intensity profiles were exported for further normalization and statistical analysis.

Calpain activity assay

Calpain activity was measured using the calpain activity assay kit (Abcam, ab65308) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed with extraction buffer on ice for 20 min, then scraped. Cellular lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 2 min. The supernatant was collected and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Pierce). 100–200 µg protein was subsequently isolated for incubation with reaction buffer at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity was then measured at 400 nm excitation and 505 nm emission using a spectrophotometer.

Prokaryotic protein expression and purification

The fresh bacterial culture was incubated at 37 °C, 250 rpm for 3–4 h, then 0.1 mM IPTG (Genview) was added, and incubation was continued at 20 °C, 250 rpm overnight. The next day bacteria were centrifuged at 4 °C, 4000 rpm for 10–15 min with the pellet then collected and lysed with the appropriate lysis buffer. Triton X-100 was added and the lysates were sonicated on ice. After centrifugation at 4 °C, 13,000 rpm for 30 min, the supernatant was collected.

Resin beads (Yeason, 20507ES10 or Qiagen, 30210) were loaded into a chromatography column (Smart-life, SLM007). The column was washed 10 times using the column volume of water and a further 5 times using the column volume of washing buffer. The supernatant was then added at 1 ml/min and the column washed again with 10–15 times the column volume of washing buffer. After that, 5–10 times column volume of elution buffer was added at 1 ml/min and the eluate collected and concentrated using microcentrifuge tubes (Millipore, UFC505008). The protein concentration was then measured and the protein was aliquoted. The aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until use.

GST-pull down assay

The eluate of His6-tagged proteins was added to the glutathione-Sepharose resin with bound GST or GST-tag proteins. The mixture was suspended at 4 °C for 2 h. Six column volumes of 0.1% Triton X-100/TBS were added and the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C. This procedure was repeated 6 times to remove unspecific binding. Then 10–20 µL of elution buffer was added and the mixture was suspended at 4 °C for 1 h. Thereafter, supernatant was collected and 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added followed by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

Western blotting

Approximately 50 µg protein was loaded into each lane onto 10% or 12% polyacrylamide gels and electrophoresed at 80–120 V. The proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Pall) using a wet blot transfer system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) at 200 mA for 1–2 h and the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20) for 1 h. Further incubation with primary antibodies (anti-GST antibody, Applygen, C1303, 1:2000; anti-His6 antibody, Applygen, C1301, 1:1000; anti-Flag antibody, Applygen, C1305, 1:1000; anti-GFP antibody, Santa Cruz, sc-9996, 1:1000; anti-TRPV1 Ct antibody, Alomone, ACC030, 1:1000; anti-TRPV1 extracellular domain antibody, Alomone, ACC029, 1:500; anti-transferrin receptor antibody, Abcam, ab269513, 1:1000; anti-β-actin antibody, ZSGB-BIO, TA-09, 1:2000; anti-GAPDH antibody, ZSGB-BIO, TA-08, 1:3000; anti-calpain-1 antibody, Proteintech, 10538-1-AP, 1:1000) was conducted overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST for 3 times, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoReseach) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using the chemiluminescent HRP substrate kit (Millipore).

Protein enrichment by immunoprecipitation

rProtein A/G agarose resin (Yeason, 36403ES05) was pre-cleaned. Then TBS and anti-GFP antibody (1:100) were added to the resin and the mixture was suspended at 4 °C for over 3 h. Cellular lysates were then added and the mixture was suspended at 4 °C overnight. The resulting resin was washed six times with 0.1% Triton X-100/TBS. Elution buffer was added and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was transferred to a clean tube.

Protein treatment by calpain in vitro

Protein substrate was mixed with equal volume of 2× calpain digestion solution (80 mM Hepes, 10 mM DTT, pH 7.2). Appropriate volumes of calpain-1 (Merck, 208712) were added to the reaction mixture. Thereafter, CaCl2 was added to a final concentration of 1 mM. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for the required time until boiling for inactivation.

Biotinylation of the cell surface protein

Cell cultures were washed twice with ice-cold DPBS and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo Scientific, 1 mg/ml) to biotinylate the cell surface proteins. Excess biotin reagent was quenched by washing the cells with PBS containing 100 mM glycine. Thereafter, cells were lysed and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min to yield protein extracts in the supernatant. The protein extracts were then incubated with streptavidin agarose resin 6FF (Yeason, 20512ES08) for 2 h at 4 °C to capture biotinylated surface proteins. After being washed with lysis buffer, proteins were eluted by boiling for 5 min with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and the samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis.

FRET experiment

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) was performed on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with a 63× HC PL APO objective (NA 1.40, oil) using FRET AB (acceptor bleaching) Leica software modules. Cells were plated at low density on a confocal dish and transfected with the pCMV-Calpainsensor (Addgene, 36182) or pEYFP-N1 (YouBio, VT1105) and pECF-N1 (YouBio, VT1113) plasmids. For FRET AB, ROIs were drawn and excited with the 514 nm laser line to bleach the YFP channel. Both a prebleach image and a postbleach image of the CFP and YFP channels were acquired, and the intensity change of CFP was analysed using the Leica FRET AB Wizard software (LAS AF). The FRET efficiency was calculated using the following formula: FRET% = ((CFPpost-CFPpre) / CFPpost) × 100%. A decrease in FRET efficiency corresponds to an increase in calpain activity.

Mass spectrometry analysis

The bands were excised from the SDS-PAGE gel, then reduced with 10 mM DTT and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide. Then in-gel digestion was carried out with trypsin (Promega) in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate at 37 °C overnight. The digested sample was loaded onto a C18 trap and followed by nano-LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis. The peptides were sequentially eluted from the HPLC column with a gradient of 2–45% of Buffer B (acetonitrile: water: acetic acid, 80:19.9:0.1) in Buffer A (acetonitrile: water: acetic acid, 0:99.9:0.1) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The eluted peptides were sprayed directly from the tip of the capillary column to the mass spectrometer (Thermo OE480), which was operated in the data-dependent mode. The initial MS scan recorded the mass to charge (m/z) ratios of ions over the mass range from 450 to 1800 Da. The 20 most abundant ions were automatically selected for subsequent collision-activated dissociation.

All MS/MS data were searched against a rat protein database downloaded from the uniprot database using Proteome Discoverer (Thermo). All searches were performed using a precursor mass tolerance of 0.02 Da calculated using average isotopic masses. Variable modification was set for methionine with the addition of 15.999 Da to represent methionine oxidation, static modification was set for cysteine with the addition of 57.052 Da to represent cysteine carboxyamidation. A fragment ion mass tolerance of 20 ppm was used. Enzyme cleavage specificity was set to trypsin and no more than two missed cleavages were permitted. The SEQUEST outputs were then analyzed.

Patch clamp recordings

HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 units/ml penicillin, and 50 µg/ml streptomycin. When they reached 70–80% confluence the cells were transfected with the desired DNA constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 18–24 h of transfection, the cells were trypsinized and plated onto glass coverslips. Measurements were taken 6–12 h after plating.

Patch clamp recordings were conducted using voltage-clamped on a HEKA EPC10 amplifier with PatchMaster software (HEKA) in the conventional whole-cell configuration. Patch pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass to a resistance of 2–4 MΩ. The pipette solution contained 140 mM CsCl, 5 mM EGTA, and 10 mM HEPES. The pH was adjusted to 7.2 using CsOH. The bath solution for recording consisted of 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH. For desensitization testing, the bath solution containing physiological 1.8 mM Ca2+ contained: 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES. For voltage-dependence experiments, a voltage step protocol was used. This protocol consisted of 200-ms depolarizing pulses ranging from − 100 to 160 mV at 20-mV increments. The protocol was triggered from the holding potential of − 60 mV. When recording, serial resistance was compensated by 60% to reduce voltage errors. For desensitization recording, repeatedly stimulated with 1 µM capsaicin for 1 min followed by 1-min washouts at a voltage ramps (1500 ms) from − 100 to + 100 mV were applied every 5 s from a holding potential of 0 mV. The current signal was sampled at 5 kHz and filtered at 2.9 kHz. All measurements were performed at room temperature (22–24 °C). To prepare capsaicin for the experiments, it was dissolved in pure ethanol to create a stock solution. The solution was then diluted in the bath solution to the desired concentration immediately before use.

During recording, a rapid solution changer with a gravity-driven perfusion system (RSC-200, Bio-Logic) was used to apply the capsaicin solutions. Each solution was delivered through a separate tube to prevent mixing. The G-V curves were generated by converting the maximum current values to conductance using the formula G = I/(V − VR), where G represents the conductance, I represents the peak current, V represents the command pulse potential, and VR represents the reversal potential of the ionic current obtained from the I-V curves. Normalized conductance (G/Gmax) was obtained from the steady-state current at the end of each pulse and fitted to the Boltzmann equation: G/Gmax = 1/ (1 + exp [− (Vm – V1/2)/k]), where V1/2 is the voltage at which the conductance (G) is half the maximum conductance (Gmax ), and k is the slope factor, the modified Hill equation was used to analysis concentration responses: Y = A1+(A2 − A1) / [1 + 10^(logEC50−X) × nH], in which EC50 is the half maximal effective concentration, and nH is the Hill coefficient.

Animals and exogeneous gene delivery via intrathecal injection

Trpv1−/− mice were created by TALEN technology as described in our previous studies [30]. Mice were housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C temperature, 55 ± 5% humidity and a 12:12 light/dark cycle) and had free access to food and water. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (permit number: LA2015074).

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. The bilateral iliac crests and the tip of the L5 vertebral spinous process of mice were identified. Then a needle was inserted at an angle of 70–90° into the space between the L5 transverse process and the spinous process. Upon observation of a tail flick reflex, 5 µL of the mixture containing 1–2 µg of plasmid and 2.4% in vivo-jetPEI™ (Polyplus, 101000040) reagent dissolved in 3% glucose was slowly injected using a microsyringe. The needle was kept in place for 2 min before withdrawal.

Nociceptive pain model and behavioral test

Twenty microliters of 0.5% capsaicin (MCE, HY-10448) solution dissolved in 10% DMSO (Sigma, 276855) and 90% corn oil (Sigma, 63156) was subcutaneously injected into the right hindpaw of mice. Immediately after injection, mice were placed in a transparent plastic chamber where they can move freely. The total time mice spent on licking, lifting, and flinching within 10 min was recorded.

Inflammatory pain model and behavioral tests

Twenty microliters of complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA, Sigma, F5881) was subcutaneously injected into the right hindpaw of mice to establish the inflammatory pain model.

For the heat pain assay, the mice were placed on a hot plate set at 52 °C (Harvard Apparatus). The latencies to flinching or licking of hindpaws were measured. Each mouse was tested three times with an interval of no less than 5 min between trials and the average value was calculated. A cutoff time of 30 s was set to prevent tissue injury.

For the mechanical pain assay, mice were placed in plastic cages with metal mesh floors. They were allowed to acclimate to the environment for 10 min before testing. Von Frey filaments (Stoelting) ranging from 0.02 to 2.0 g were applied to the plantar surface of the hindpaw ipsilateral to the drug injection according to the Up and Down method [31]. Each stimulus interval was no less than 5 s. The 50% mechanical withdrawal threshold was calculated using the formula.

Cryosectioning of DRG tissue

Mice were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 80 mg/kg. After euthanasia, the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) tissues of bilateral L5-L6 were rapidly dissected and fixed with 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. After fixation, the DRG were sequentially immersed in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions for gradient dehydration. The tissues were embedded in OCT (SAKURA, 4583) in a cryomold and were flash frozen immediately with dry ice. Then the tissues were cryostat sectioned at 15 μm onto adhesion microscope slides (CITOTEST, 188105). The slides were washed briefly in PBS, then incubated with anti-fading mounting medium containing DAPI (Solarbio, S2110). The images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (MICA, Leica).

Statistical analysis

Data was shown as mean ± SEM and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Differences between two groups were compared using unpaired t-tests with or without Welch’s correction. Differences between multiple groups were compared using One-way ANOVA followed by the Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, or Two-way ANOVA followed by the Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Calpain is activated subsequent to the activation of TRPV1

To determine the effect of receptor activity on the protein levels of TRPV1, we examined its expression after a regular dose (1 µM) of capsaicin stimulation. N-terminal GST-tag or C-terminal GFP-tag TRPV1 was transiently transfected into HEK293 cells. A significant decrease of TRPV1 protein was observed after stimulation with capsaicin for 10 min (Fig. 1A and B), whilst no change in the protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 was observed (Fig. 1C). This indicated the reduction of TRPV1 protein not to be associated with cellular apoptosis. Meanwhile, we used CHO cells stably expressing TRPV1 (hereafter referred to as CHO-TRPV1 cells) to detect the effect of capsaicin treatment on TRPV1 protein expression. Reduction of TRPV1 protein levels was observed 30 min after capsaicin (1 µM) stimulation (Fig. 1D). Treatment for shorter time (5–10 min), failed to induce any significant change in the protein levels of TRPV1 (Fig. S1A, B). We suspected that the temporal discrepancy for the agonist-induced diminishment of TRPV1 protein might be attributed to the differential expression levels of TRPV1 in transiently or stably expressing cells. To avoid variability in protein levels in the transient expression system, CHO-TRPV1 cells were used to further investigate the underlying mechanisms. Moreover, in order to achieve a stable and pronounced decrease of TRPV1 protein, the duration for capsaicin treatment was elongated to 1 h in CHO-TRPV1 cells.

Given the Ca2+-permeability of TRPV1 channels and the involvement of calpain in protein degradation, we examined the effect of calpain inhibitors in reversing the activity-dependent reduction of TRPV1 protein. As expected, pretreatment with distinct broad-spectrum inhibitors of calpain for 30 min, including calpeptin (20 µM), ALLN (20 µM) or MDL28170 (20 µM), significantly reversed or showed a tendency to reverse the capsaicin-induced diminishment of TRPV1 protein (Fig. 2A and B). The effect of the ubiquitin pathway inhibitor MG132 (25 µM) and the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (10 µM) was also examined (Figs. S1C, 1D). Although no statistical significance was achieved, we could not completely rule out the involvement of ubiquitin or caspase pathways in the diminishment of TRPV1 protein. Notably, transfection of CHO-TRPV1 cells with the endogenous calpain inhibitory protein calpastatin (CAST) plasmid also significantly reversed the activity-dependent diminishment of TRPV1 protein (Fig. 2C and D). Taken together, these data support the involvement of calpain in activity-dependent reduction of TRPV1 protein.

Next, we directly measured the activity of calpain following capsaicin treatment. Measurement using a calpain activity assay kit showed that capsaicin (1 µM) treatment for 30 min significantly increased the activity of calpain compared with the baseline control (Fig. S1G). To further determine the activity of calpain at the subcellular level, we used FRET acceptor photobleaching to distinguish the activity of calpain near the plasma membrane or in the cytoplasm. A decrease in FRET efficiency denoted an increase of calpain activity (Fig. 2E). We observed that calpain activity near the plasma membrane showed a tendency to decline 5 min after capsaicin treatment, with statistical significance achieved at 30 min (Figs. 2F and S1E). The calpain activity in the cytoplasm was also significantly decreased 2 h post capsaicin treatment. Meanwhile, no obvious changes in cell morphology was observed 2 h post capsaicin treatment (Fig. S1F), suggesting the calpain activation did not accompany the widespread cytotoxic effects in the current study.

Calpain cleaves TRPV1 Ct at G819/S820 site

To examine the potential action of calpain on the intracellular fragments of TRPV1, we induced the expression of TRPV1 Nt and TRPV1 Ct in vitro. Calpain-1 (µ-calpain, 2.5 U/ml) was directly added to the protein solution and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. GST was used as a control. Both the GST-TRPV1 Nt and GST-TRPV1 Ct showed a reduction in protein levels, but not GST per se (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, a remarkable cleavage band was observed in the GST-TRPV1-Ct after incubation with calpain-1. Moreover, the appearance of this cleavage band could be blocked by the calpain inhibitors calpeptin (20 µM) or MDL28170 (20 µM), indicating the specificity of the action of calpain (Fig. 3B). This cleavage band of GST-TRPV1 Ct could be detected by an anti-GST antibody (Fig. 3C, left panel) but not by an antibody against the TRPV1 Ct (residues 824–838) (Fig. 3C, right panel), indicating that the carboxyl-terminal end of the TRPV1 Ct was deleted by calpain-1 (Fig. 3D). Combined with the shift in molecular weight of the cleavage fragment, we postulate that calpain might cleave the distal carboxyl end of TRPV1 Ct.

To precisely determine the cleavage sites of calpain on TRPV1 Ct, we excised the differential protein bands in the SDS-PAGE gel of TRPV1 Ct subjected to calpain treatment for mass spectrometry analysis (Fig. S2A, GST-TRPV1 Ct and Cleavage-1, -2, -3, and - 4). G819-terminated peptide was detected in both Cleavage-1 and - 2, but not in GST-TRPV1 Ct or Cleavage-3 or -4 (Fig. S2C–G). This finding indicated that the break point at G819 was caused by the action of calpain, but not by sample digestion treatment in the mass spectrometry assay. The presence of G819-terminated peptide in Cleavage-2 was considered potentially to be due to sequential cleavage of TRPV1 Ct following breaking at the S820 site. In addition, we used GPS-CCD (Calpain Cleavage Detector), a computational program, to predict the possible cleavage sites in TRPV1 Ct (Fig. S2B) [32]. The G819/S820 site was listed as the cleavage site closest to the C-terminal end. No other specific sites were detected in Cleavage-3 and - 4, implying that these bands might be attributed to the non-specific cleavage activity of calpain. Additionally, such GS sites are noted as highly conserved in TRPV1 protein sequence from the mammals listed in (Fig. S3). Combining this with the above data, we focused on the action of calpain on the G819/S820 site of the TRPV1 Ct in the following investigations.

To optimize the conditions for calpain-1-mediated cleavage of TRPV1 Ct, we investigated the influence of incubation time and enzyme concentration on the reaction. When incubated for 15 min, the optimal concentration of calpain for the production of the G819 cleavage fragment was approximately 2.5 U/ml (Fig. 3E, F). When the calpain concentration was set at 10 U/ml, the optimal incubation time was 10 min (Fig. 3G, H). When calpain concentration or reaction time continued to increase, the amount of G819/S820 cleavage fragment gradually reduced until such fragment was completely degraded, which could be attributed to the nonspecific cleavage action of calpain.

Full-length TRPV1 is cleaved at G819/S820 site by calpain

To validate whether calpain-mediated cleavage occurs in full-length TRPV1, we inserted a Flag tag (DYKDDDDK) at the N-terminus of the TRPV1-GFP plasmid and constructed a Flag-TRPV1-GFP plasmid (Fig. 4A). Transfection of the Flag-TRPV1-GFP plasmid in HEK293 cells verified the expression of Flag-TRPV1-GFP fusion protein, as detected by anti-Flag or anti-GFP antibody (Fig. 4B). After protein enrichment by immunoprecipitation (IP) with GFP antibody, the protein concentrate was treated with calpain-1 (10 U/ml). Both coomassie brilliant blue staining of SDS-PAGE gel (Fig. 4C) and Western blotting (Fig. 4D) showed the appearance of new bands after calpain treatment, including both of truncated Flag-TRPV1 and GFP-tagged C-terminal fragment. The size of the bands was conformed with the predicted molecular weight of Flag-TRPV1 Δ820 and 820-838-GFP. Regrettably, the bands of truncated TRPV1 could not be specifically recognized by anti-Flag antibody by unknown reasons. Therefore, anti-TRPV1 antibody against its extracellular domain was used as a substitute (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, we performed concentration-dependent cleavage of TRPV1-GFP by calpain-1. Different concentrations of calpain-1 were added to the lysates of HEK293 cells transfected with TRPV1-GFP plasmid. Immunoblotting with anti-TRPV1 Ct antibody showed that the potential bands of 820-838-GFP (Fig. 4E) exhibited a dose-dependent increment as the concentration of calpain-1 increased. These data support the conclusion that calpain cleaves full-length TRPV1 at G819/S820 site in the distal Ct.

To further determine the critical amino acid residues involved in the action of calpain-mediated cleavage, we conducted point mutation studies of GST-TRPV1 Ct. A series of mutants, namely double mutant, triple mutant, quadruple mutant-1, and quadruple mutant-2 were constructed (Fig. S4A). However, none of these mutants had a significant impact on the calpain-mediated cleavage (Fig. S4B–D). This lack of impact might be caused by the feature of calpain where it recognizes substrates not only by the primary sequence of their amino acids, but also by their overall three-dimensional structure (33). Further experiments are therefore required to identify the critical recognition sites of calpain for TRPV1 Ct.

The TRPV1 Δ820 mutant exhibits reduced plasma membrane localization

To investigate the potential role of distal Ct of TRPV1 (820–838 amino acid residues) in the regulation of receptor function, we constructed a GFP-tagged truncated mutant of TRPV1, TRPV1 Δ820-GFP (Fig. S5A). TRPV1-GFP was used as a control. Effective expression of TRPV1 Δ820-GFP in HEK293 cells was confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. S5B). A small shift in protein bands to a lower molecular weight was observed in TRPV1 Δ820-GFP compared with TRPV1-GFP. Furthermore, immunoblotting with anti-TRPV1 Ct antibody examined the expression of TRPV1-GFP, but not TRPV1 Δ820-GFP in which the antibody epitope was deleted. The cellular distribution of TRPV1-GFP and TRPV1 Δ820-GFP was then observed in transfected HEK293 cells. TRPV1-GFP exhibited significant membrane localization, with green fluorescence forming a circumferential distribution along the plasma membrane (Fig. 5A, left panel). By contrast, TRPV1 Δ820-GFP did not show obvious plasma membrane localization, instead being diffusely distributed throughout cytoplasm. Quantification analysis of the GFP fluorescence intensity spectrum across the cells showed prominent double peaks in the TRPV1-GFP group (Fig. 5A, right panel), unobserved in the GFP or TRPV1 Δ820-GFP groups.

In addition, a biotin labeling experiment of the cell surface protein was conducted. Results showed that the surface expression of the TRPV1 Δ820 mutant was significantly reduced compared to that of full-length TRPV1 (Fig. 5B, C), despite their expression in cellular lysates did not differ. These findings suggest that the membrane localization of TRPV1 is compromised upon deletion of the distal Ct, which may then lead to receptor retention in ER.

The TRPV1 Δ820 mutant shows impaired subunit assembly

Previous lines of evidence have shown that functional TRPV1 is localized on the plasma membrane as a tetramer and its C-terminus mediates subunit assembly, which is a prerequisite for plasma membrane trafficking. To investigate whether removal of the distal Ct of S820 to K838 would affect subunit assembly and plasma membrane localization of TRPV1, we conducted an in vitro GST-pull down assay and growth associated protein 43 (Gap43) fragment-mediated membrane localization experiment. The GST-pull down assay showed that the level of precipitated His6-TRPV1 Ct by GST-TRPV1 Ct Δ820 was significantly lower than that precipitated by GST-TRPV1 Ct (Fig. 5D, E). In addition, we constructed Gap43-TRPV1 Ct, TRPV1 Ct-GFP, and TRPV1 Ct Δ820-GFP plasmids. Gap43 has a membrane-targeting segment of 20 amino acid residues at the N-terminus. When fused with Gap43, the cytoplasmic protein could be tethered to the plasma membrane (Fig. S5C) [34]. Western blotting experiments demonstrated the expression of these plasmids in HEK293 cells (Fig. S5D). When TRPV1 Ct-GFP or TRPV1 Ct Δ820-GFP was expressed alone, the fluorescence signal remained diffusely distributed throughout the cell and with no obvious fluorescence signal on the plasma membrane (Fig. 5F, upper panel). However, when co-transfected with Gap43-TRPV1 Ct, clear fluorescence signals were observed on the plasam membrane in the TRPV1 Ct-GFP group, but not in the TRPV1 Ct Δ820-GFP group (Fig. 5F, lower panel). Quantification analysis revealed a dual peak signal in the spatial profile of fluorescence intensity in the TRPV1 Ct-GFP group, which was attributed to the Ct-Ct interaction between Gap43-TRPV1 Ct and TRPV1 Ct-GFP. However, this interaction was compromised by deletion of the distal 19 amino acid residues.

As the amount of receptors on the plasma membrane is determined by both of receptor trafficking and internalization [15, 35], the effects of calpain activity on the constitutive internalization of TRPV1 were investigated subsequently. The biotinylation labeling method conjugated with application of 2-mercaptoethanesulfonic acid (MESNA) was adopted (Fig. S5E). Constitutive internalization of TRPV1 was significantly reduced in the presence of MDL28170 (20 µM) (Fig. S5F, G), suggesting that the activity of calpain facilitates TRPV1 internalization. Intriguingly, internalization of TfR was also inhibited by MDL28170, suggesting a broad influence of calpain on cellular endocytic machinery. Consistent with this finding, previous studies have demonstrated that calpain can cleave α- and β2- adaptins of the AP-2 complex [36], thereby modulating clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Therefore, it is possible that both reduced subunit assembly and impaired membrane trafficking and enhanced receptor internalization collectively lead to the reduced plasma membrane localization of TRPV1 upon calpain activation.

The TRPV1 Δ820 mutant exhibits reduced current density and increased resistance to tachyphylaxis

To observe the impact of distal Ct deletion on the channel function of TRPV1, we recorded the voltage-induced currents of the TRPV1 channel using the voltage clamp technique. Starting from − 100 mV we conducted stepwise increases of voltage by 20 mV until 160 mV (Fig. 6A). The current of both wild-type (WT) TRPV1 and TRPV1 Δ820 mutants was converted to conductance and normalized to that obtained at 160 mV stimulation before fitting with the Boltzmann function. There were no significant differences of the conductance-voltage (G-V) relationships between the two groups (Fig. 6B), indicating that the TRPV1 Δ820 mutation does not change the voltage dependence properties of TRPV1. This finding was expected as truncation of the distal Ct leaves the transmembrane domains and pore region unaffected. Consequently, TRPV1 Δ820 displayed a similar activation pattern as WT TRPV1 when subjected to voltage stimulation.

We next recorded the currents of TRPV1 and TRPV1 Δ820 under the stimulation of different doses of capsaicin for 20 s (Fig. 6C). Since the experimental system did not contain Ca2+, TRPV1 channels did not undergo desensitization and remained highly responsive to capsaicin. In the dose-response curve, there were no differences in the slope and EC50 between the two groups, EC50 = 0.41 ± 0.06 µM and nH = 1.94 ± 0.32 for TRPV1 and EC50 = 0.43 ± 0.05 µM and nH = 2.04 ± 0.26 for TRPV1 Δ820 (Fig. 6D), indicating similarity in their capsaicin sensitivity. This finding might be explained that activation of TRPV1 by capsaicin requires two “gates”, with the upper gate located near the pore’s selectivity filter and the lower gate near the S6 crossing [37]. Deletion of the distal 19 amino acid residues in the C-terminus of TRPV1 did not affect these critical domains for channel opening. We then compared the current density of TRPV1 and TRPV1 Δ820 induced by capsaicin. Under low concentrations of capsaicin (0.01–0.3 µM), the current densities of TRPV1 and TRPV1 Δ820 were comparable (Fig. 6E). However, when the concentration of capsaicin increased to 1 µM, the responsiveness of TRPV1 Δ820 to capsaicin began to decrease significantly compared to TRPV1. With increasing concentrations of capsaicin (1 µM, 3 µM, and 5 µM), TRPV1 Δ820 exhibited correspondingly and significantly weaker responsiveness to capsaicin, leading to smaller current densities when compared to TRPV1, e.g. 278.7 ± 29.5 pA/pF (n = 29) and 180.7 ± 19.6 pA/pF (n = 38) evoked by 1µM for TRPV1 and TRPV1 Δ820, respectively. This discrepant responsiveness of TRPV1 Δ820 might be attributed to the transient expression system used in this study. Even though the expression level of TRPV1 Δ820 on the plasma membrane was relatively low, the overexpression system guaranteed its high-level expression and ensured an adequate response to low concentrations but not higher concentrations of capsaicin.

In addition, we examined the tachyphylaxis induced by repetitive application of capsaicin (1 µM) in the presence of Ca2+ in the recording solution. The TRPV1 current showed a dramatic decline in the first capsaicin application and remained at a low level during all subsequent capsaicin stimulations (Fig. 6F, H). This was considered to be tachyphylaxis of TRPV1 channels. Of note, the maximal current to the first capsaicin application was lowered in TRPV1 Δ820 accompanied with slow inactivation kinetics. Meanwhile, the magnitude of decline in the current density was greatly attenuated in TRPV1 Δ820 (Fig. 6G, H). To compare the tachyphylaxis quantitatively, normalized responses were calculated (Fig. 6I). The data revealed that the magnitude of current decline from the second to fourth application of capsaicin was significantly diminished in the TRPV1 Δ820 group, suggesting TRPV1 Δ820 to be more resistant to capsaicin-induced tachyphylaxis.

Calpain-1 was involved in the modulation of TRPV1 tachyphylaxis

To determine the calpain isoform involved in the modulation of TRPV1 tachyphylaxis, we designed CAPN1-shRNA plasmids to knockdown the expression of calpain-1. According to the results of Western Blotting (Fig. 7A), CAPN1-shRNA plasmid 2 was used in the subsequent experiment. Compared with the scramble shRNA control, calpain-1 knockdown caused a more pronounced rundown of TRPV1 currents during repeated capsaicin applications (Fig. 7B–D). Specifically, while the first response was comparable, the current densities of the second to fifth capsaicin applications were reduced in the CAPN1-shRNA group. The normalized responses were also lower than those in the scramble control group (Fig. 7E), indicating enhanced tachyphylaxis after calpain-1 knockdown. Altogether, these data provide direct evidence for the involvement of calpain-1 in the regulation of TRPV1 tachyphylaxis.

In vivo delivery of TRPV1 Δ820 rescued the pain behavioral deficits in the nociceptive pain models but not that in the inflammatory pain models.

To examine the in vivo function of TRPV1 Δ820, we performed the behavioral test using the capsaicin-induced nociceptive pain models and CFA-induced inflammatory pain models in Trpv1−/− mice (Fig. 8A, C). Plasmids of GFP, TRPV1-GFP and TRPV1 Δ820-GFP were delivered to the Trpv1−/− mice through intrathecal injection. Green fluorescence signal was clearly visible in the sections of DRG from mice 1-week post-injection (Fig. S6), suggesting the successful expression of the exogenous genes.

In the nociceptive pain model, the reduced duration of lifting or licking behaviors after capsaicin injection in the Trpv1−/− mice could be rescued by TRPV1 Δ820-GFP expression (Fig. 8B). The effect of TRPV1 Δ820-GFP is comparable to that of WT TRPV1. Therefore, we speculate that in spite of the reduced plasma membrane localization of TRPV1 Δ820-GFP, it is sufficient to exert the protective function upon exposure to an intense noxious stimuli.

As membrane trafficking of TRPV1 and upregulation of its function are critical factors for neuronal sensitization in chronic pain, we examined the effects of TRPV1 Δ820 delivery on pain hypersensitivity in inflammatory pain models. Unlike the wild type, intrathecal injection of TRPV1 Δ820 plasmid failed to rescue the impaired thermal pain behaviors of Trpv1−/− mice 2 days after CFA injection (Fig. 8D). Meanwhile, neither WT TRPV1 nor the TRPV1 Δ820 mutant rescued the deficits in mechanical pain behaviors of Trpv1−/− mice. Mechanistically, we speculated that global gene knockout of Trpv1 might induce broad changes in gene expression, particularly during developmental stages, which could not be restored by exogenous delivery of either TRPV1 or its truncated form.

In summary, the current work demonstrates that calpain is activated following TRPV1 activation by a non-toxic dose of capsaicin. Calpain cleaves the distal C-terminus of TRPV1 at G819/S820 site. The truncated TRPV1 Δ820 mutant shows reduced plasma membrane localization but enhanced resistance to channel tachyphylaxis. At the in vivo level, TRPV1 Δ820 helps the body maintain the responsiveness to intense noxious stimuli but fails to induce thermal hyperalgesia in chronic pain. In brief, calpain-1 acts as a critical Ca2+-dependent regulator of TRPV1 and mediates the fine-tuning of TRPV1 function (Fig. 9).

Discussion

TRPV1 represents a key target for analgesic drug development. The classic TRPV1 agonist, capsaicin, exerts analgesic effects partially by inducing TRPV1 desensitization, a process that includes both acute desensitization and tachyphylaxis [38]. Ca2+-dependent proteins such as calcineurin, PLC, and CaM have been shown to contribute to TRPV1 desensitization [15–17, 39–41]. Given the Ca2+-dependence of TRPV1 desensitization and the reduced protein levels of TRPV1 after capsaicin treatment, we explored the potential role of calpain, a Ca2+-dependent cysteine protease, in TRPV1 desensitization. The main findings in this work include: (1) Activation of TRPV1 by a non-toxic dose of capsaicin is accompanied by the activation of calpain. (2) Calpain-1 cleaves distal C-terminus of rTRPV1 at the G819/S820 site. (3) The truncated TRPV1 Δ820 mutant exhibits reduced plasma membrane localization partially through reduced subunit assembly. (4) TRPV1 Δ820 exhibits reduced current density, but attenuated channel tachyphylaxis. (5) TRPV1 Δ820 mediates the pain behaviors similarly to the wild type in nociceptive pain models, but fails to contribute to thermal hyperalgesia in chronic pain. Accordingly, our study reveals an elegant regulatory role of calpain in modulating TRPV1. On one hand, calpain reduces TRPV1 current density by decreasing the channel’s localization at the plasma membrane; on the other hand, it suppresses the development of channel tachyphylaxis. This combines to ensure TRPV1 activity is maintained within an appropriate range. In accordance with this feature, TRPV1 Δ820, the mutant generated by calpain cleavage, sustains the nociceptive pain behaviors but is insufficient to induce pain hypersensitivity in chronic pain.

TRPV1-Ca2+-calpain pathway

Previous studies have suggested the existence of a TRPV1-Ca2+-calpain pathway as usually associated with cell injury or cell death. For example, in a diabetic rat model, activation of TRPV1 in the large-diameter DRG neurons caused cellular stress and injury involving the activation of caspase and calpain. However, this effect was not observed in DRG neurons from healthy rats [42]. This finding is corroborated in the current work. Here, treatment with capsaicin (1 µM, 10 min) did not induce concomitant activation of caspase 3 (Fig. 1C). In intestinal cells, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling was coupled with activation of TRPV1, which was followed by calpain activation and the cleavage of protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B). Consequently, PTP1B activity was increased and tumorigenesis was suppressed through dephosphorylation of EGFR [43]. In macrophages, excessive nitric oxide activated TRPV1, which in turn triggered calpain-mediated cleavage of liver X receptor α (LXRα), ultimately impairing cholesterol efflux [44]. Additionally, activation of the TRPV1-Ca2+-calpain-2 pathway in outer hair cells was associated with cisplatin-induced oxidative stress [45]. Recent studies have indicated that axonal degeneration followed by high-dose capsaicin (1 mM) depends on the action of calpain [46, 47]. Combined with the fact that a large-dose of capsaicin has been used to ablate DRG neurons or achieve anti-tumor effects [48], widespread activation of the TRPV1-Ca2+-calpain pathway, particularly the activation of calpain-2, is noted as detrimental to cell survival.

In the current work, we demonstrated that TRPV1-Ca2+-calpain pathway activated by a regular dose (1 µM) of capsaicin is not associated with cell toxic injury. This may be due to a region-specific activation pattern of calpain, which starts from the region under the plasma membrane and then diffuses throughout the whole cell. This is distinct from the widespread activation of calpain following a large dose of capsaicin. In addition, calpain-1 and calpain-2, the two major isoforms of calpain, differ not only in Ca2+ sensitivity but also exhibit marked differences in the downstream signaling pathways and functional effects [49–51]. In the current work, the direct contribution of calpain-1 to the cleavage of TRPV1 and modulation of receptor tachyphylaxis was demonstrated. Furthermore, the electrophysiological data of TRPV1 Δ820 are consistent with those obtained upon calpain-1 knockdown. The TRPV1 Δ820 mutant, which mimics calpain-mediated distal C-terminal cleavage, exhibits reduced tachyphylaxis, whereas genetic suppression of calpain-1 enhances receptor tachyphylaxis. These data support the involvement of calpain-1 in the modulation of TRPV1 tachyphylaxis, likely through cleavage of the distal C terminus. Nevertheless, we could not completely exclude the involvement of other calpain isoforms. Further studies are needed to clarify this question.

Characteristics of calpain-mediated substrate cleavage

A large number of substrates for calpain have been identified. These include cytoskeletal proteins, receptors and ion channels, enzymes, kinases and phosphatases. Notably, substrate recognition of calpain is independent of certain amino acid residues or consensus sequences, but depends on the overall three-dimensional structure of the target protein [33]. This functional property of calpain might lead to the failure of our attempts to identify the critical amino acid residues involved in the cleavage of the TRPV1 Ct by calpain. It has been reported that protease-mediated cleavage of substrates tends to occur among three types of secondary structure, namely extended loops, α-helices and β-sheets [52, 53], all of which are detectable in the protein sequence of the TRPV1 Ct [5, 54]. However, further studies are required to precisely determine the secondary structure or amino acid residues involved in the action of calpain on TRPV1 Ct.

Another feature of calpain’s action is that it hydrolyzes substrates to a limited extent, thereby conferring upon it the role of a modulator protease. For example, calpain-mediated cleavage of Cav1.1 or Cav1.2 produces an autoinhibitory fragment in the distal Ct. This then inhibits channel function through interaction with the proximal Ct [55, 56]. Conversely, the truncated transient receptor potential canonical 5 (TRPC5), as produced by the action of calpain and which occurs in the TRPC5 Ct, exhibits a potentiated channel function [57]. Analogically, calpain-mediated cleavage of TRPV1 also occurs at the distal Ct. This truncated form of TRPV1 therefore exhibits a finely balanced change in channel function. However, the possible functions of small fragment 820–838 amino acid residues remain to be further determined.

Functional regulation of TRPV1 resulted from calpain-mediated cleavage of its C terminus

The action of calpain on TRPV1 leads to the generation of truncated TRPV1 lacking a distal Ct. The TRPV1 Ct has been shown to play complex regulatory roles in TRPV1. For example, the TRP domain located in the proximal Ct of TRPV1 plays a critical role in subunit assembly and channel activation [58, 59]. The TRP domain, particularly K710, is involved in the activation or potentiation of TRPV1 by lysophosphatidic acid and PIP2 [60, 61]. Distal to this PIP2 binding site, there is another distal PIP2 binding site (777–820 amino acid residues), which exhibits an inhibitory effect on TRPV1 [62, 63]. Overlapping with this segment lies the sequence of 767–801 amino acid residues, where particularly L796 and W787 act as the C terminal binding sites for CaM on TRPV1 [17, 19]. As both PIP2 and CaM contribute to TRPV1 desensitization, whether there is functional relationship between the distal TRPV1 Ct and the other regions of TRPV1 Ct remains to be determined. On the other hand, recent studies have indicated that TRPV1 desensitization is accompanied with Nt-Ct interactions [25, 26]. The truncation of distal TRPV1 Ct by calpain might destroy this interaction, leading to attenuated receptor desensitization.

In addition to the possible effects on protein-protein interaction, truncation at G819/S820 leads to the loss of post-translational modification sites of TRPV1. For example, K822 is a critical site for the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) modification of TRPV1, which specifically enhances the channel’s sensitivity to heat, but not capsaicin, protons or voltage [64]. TRPV1 K822 SUMOylation is essential for the development of thermal hyperalgesia during inflammatory pain and the suppression of itching sensation via interrupting the binding between TRPV1 and histamine receptor type 1 (H1R) [65]. Therefore, calpain-mediated cleavage of the TRPV1 Ct likely reduces TRPV1 sensitization to heat stimulation by abolishing its SUMOylation. Additionally, S774 and S820 are the phosphorylation sites located in the C terminal region of TRPV1 by protein kinase A (PKA), which promotes TRPV1 sensitization and reduces desensitization in a phosphorylation-dependent manner [16, 66]. Despite contribution of these sites to the regulatory action of PKA on TRPV1 has not been acknowledged, the potential impact of S820 deletion on PKA-mediated regulation of TRPV1 sensitization and desensitization deserves further investigation.

In summary, the current studies reveal the dual regulatory role of calpain on TRPV1, where both reduction of the TRPV1 channel current and concurrent attenuation of receptor desensitization is achieved. Overall, the function of TRPV1 is downregulated after calpain activation, ensuring that TRPV1 channel activity remains within an appropriate range without disrupting the fundamental protective function of the channel.

Limitations

There are some limitations for the current studies. First, whether activation of TRPV1 couples with the elevated activity of calpain and subsequent cleavage of TRPV1 Ct in vivo remains unknown. Future investigations in the primary cultured sensory neurons or DRG tissues are needed. Optimizing the protein enrichment methods or application of the targeted proteomic assay will help address this issue. In addition, construction of TRPV1 Δ820 knock-in mice is helpful to elucidate the in vivo role of calpain-mediated cleavage of TRPV1. Another question is how we can develop approaches to specifically promote the action of calpain on TRPV1 without disrupting its action on the other proteins. This will maintain TRPV1 function in an appropriate range. Addressing these issues will shed light on the development of novel TRPV1-targeted analgesic drugs to improve the clinical treatment for chronic pain.
