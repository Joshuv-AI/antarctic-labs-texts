Effects of local vibration therapy and gabapentin on restless legs syndrome symptoms: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Gholamreza Raissi, Bijan Forogh, Hamid Ansari, Seyed Pezhman Madani, Masumeh Bagherzadeh Cham, Hosna Soleymanzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of LV in combination with gabapentin, a first-line medication for RLS, on the treatment of patients with the condition.ResultsIn total, 36 participants (18 in each group) participated in this study, with 10 (55%) being male within each group. The mean age of participants was 45 years (SD = 10.6) in the LV group and 49.1 (SD = 8.3) in the gabapentin group. In both the LV and gabapentin groups, IRLSSG scores decreased significantly after the intervention compared with baseline (p < 0.001). However, scores increased at the one-month follow-up, although they remained lower than pre-intervention values (p < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for the PSQI scores (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between groups at any timepoint (p > 0.05).Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on August 30th, 2023 (code IRCT20190306042945N4).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07918-z.

Introduction

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is a chronic condition characterized by an uncontrollable urge to move the legs, particularly during periods of rest or sleep [1]. The prevalence of RLS is estimated to be between 5% and 15%, with certain groups, such as females, individuals with repeated pregnancies, older adults, and those with chronic kidney disease, iron deficiency anemia, multiple sclerosis, and diabetes mellitus, being at higher risk of the condition [2–4]. RLS negatively affects mental health and sleep, leading to psychological conditions such as depression and anxiety, as well as sleep disorders such as insomnia, which, given its high prevalence in the communities and the high incidence of these consequences, may impose a substantial burden on communities and healthcare systems [5, 6]. It has also been shown that RLS can impair all domains of health-related quality of life and impose a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems, with 3-month costs exceeding $2,500 [7–9]. Therefore, given the consequences and negative impacts of RLS on patients’ mental health, physical health, and quality of life, timely identification of affected individuals and effective management of the condition are crucial to mitigating its associated burdens [10].

A range of treatment options, including pharmacological interventions, such as levodopa, cabergoline, and gabapentin, and non-pharmacological interventions, such as exercise therapy and manipulation techniques, are available for the management of RLS [11, 12]. Among pharmacological options, gabapentin is one of the common treatments used to manage symptoms of RLS [13]. Studies have shown that it can reduce the severity and frequency of involuntary leg movements and improve sleep quality [14]. According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), there is a strong recommendation to use gabapentin as a first-line drug treatment for RLS symptoms [15]. However, certain side effects of gabapentin, including lethargy and dizziness, limit its use in practice [16, 17]. Therefore, due to the side effects associated with pharmacological treatments for RLS, several non-pharmacological interventions have been proposed, which may reduce reliance on medications and lower the risk of adverse effects [12].

Local vibration (LV) is among the safe and simple non-invasive interventions that have been proposed for the management of RLS [12, 18]. LV stimulates mechanical receptors in the muscles and skin to provide inhibitory signals to the central nervous system (CNS), reducing the activity of neuronal circuits related to pain and discomfort, thereby relieving symptoms [19, 20]. Additionally, LV can activate the sensorimotor cortex, regulate spinal cord reflexes, and increase peripheral blood flow [21, 22]. A number of studies have evaluated the effects of LV and its efficacy for the treatment of patients with RLS, and have shown that LV has beneficial effects on the severity of RLS and sleep quality [23, 24]. However, one of the limitations of these studies is that they have primarily compared the effects of LV with placebo controls, and only a few studies have directly compared LV with pharmacological treatments, particularly first-line medications for RLS, to determine whether LV can achieve effects comparable to medication and further inform clinical practice. Therefore, to address this gap, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of LV combined with gabapentin, a first-line medication for RLS, in the treatment of patients with the condition.

Methods

This study was reported according to the CONsolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 guidelines [25]. The ethics committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol (ethics code: IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1401.386). The study protocol was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on August 30th, 2023 (code: IRCT20190306042945N4).

Trial design & setting

This single-blind, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted in 2024 on patients with RLS attending the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Department of Firouzgar Hospital, a tertiary care center in Tehran, the capital of Iran.

We recruited patients with RLS who were referred to the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Department of Firouzgar Hospital. The sample size was calculated based on the study conducted by Mousa et al. [27]. Using G*Power software with a two-tailed test (power = 0.80, α = 0.05) and an effect size of 1.2 for sleep disturbance, the required sample size was calculated to be 32 participants. However, we included 36 participants in the study, with 18 patients in each group, to account for possible attrition [27–29].

Intervention

Participants received one month of either LV or gabapentin treatment. Participants in the LV group were treated using a LV device, VBA, operating at a low voltage of 3 volts and a frequency range of 80–120 Hz. The vibration was applied to the calf muscles of the lower extremities for 35 min each night prior to sleep and during episodes of restlessness or discomfort (Supplementary Fig. 1), over a one-month period [30, 31]. Participants in the comparator group received 300 mg of gabapentin daily, administered one hour before bedtime. This fixed dose, rather than a titrated regimen, was used based on expert consultation in our center prior to the study. It was determined that the initial 300 mg dose, commonly recommended in guidelines, provides sufficient symptom relief for many patients treated at our center. Furthermore, certain side effects of gabapentin are dose-dependent. Therefore, by choosing a fixed dose of 300 mg, we aimed to balance efficacy and safety by providing clinical benefit while minimizing the risk of adverse effects [32–35].

Outcomes

The participants underwent assessments before the intervention, immediately after the intervention, and one month after the intervention. The primary outcome of this study was the severity of RLS, and the secondary outcome was sleep disturbance. We also recorded the side effects during the study.

We recorded participants’ age, sex, and RLS duration at baseline, and measured their weight and height. RLS severity

Participant symptom severity was assessed using the 10-item International Restless Legs Syndrome Study Group (IRLSSG) questionnaire. This questionnaire includes 10 items, with each being scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 4. Total scores range from 0 to 40 and are classified as follows: mild [1–10], moderate [11–20], severe [21–30], and very severe [31–40]. The reliability and validity of IRLSSG have been demonstrated in previous studies [37, 38].

Sleep quality

We used the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) to assess participants’ sleep disturbance. Although the PSQI consists of 19 items, only the sleep disturbance component was used in this study. This component includes 9 items, each rated on a Likert scale from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater sleep disturbance. We used the Persian version of PSQI in our study, whose reliability and validity have been demonstrated in previous studies [39–41].

Randomization & allocation concealment

We used computer-generated random numbers with equal allocation to the LV and gabapentin groups to perform the randomization. Based on the generated sequence, equal numbers from each group (18 LV and 18 gabapentin) were written on papers of identical size and design, marked as 1 and 2 to represent the two groups. These papers were then placed in opaque and sealed envelopes to ensure the contents could not be seen. Participants were asked to shuffle the envelopes and randomly select one. The envelopes were opened after patient enrolment, and neither the patients nor the research team were aware of the intervention assigned in each envelope.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed by a statistician who was blinded to the intervention groups, which were coded as A and B to ensure that the statistician was unaware of the group assignments. We reported mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and number and percentage for categorical ones. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check the normal distribution for each variable. The data were normally distributed, so parametric tests like the independent t test, paired t-test, and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) were used for comparisons. We also calculated Cohen’s D to determine the effect size for between-group comparisons. We used SPSS version 26 for the analyses, and we considered p < 0.05 statistically significant.

Results

In total, 36 participants (18 in each group) participated in this study who all received the interventions as intended (Fig. 1), with 10 (55%) being male within each group. The mean age of participants was 45 years (SD = 10.6) in the LV group and 49.1 (SD = 8.3) in the gabapentin group. There were no significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics between participants, including age, sex, disease duration, weight, height, and BMI (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Similarly, in the gabapentin group, PSQI scores immediately after the intervention (mean = 4.9, SD = 1.4) were significantly lower than both pre-intervention scores (mean = 11.2, SD = 1.8) and scores at the one-month follow-up (mean = 7.3, SD = 1.9) (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the IRLSSG and PSQI scores between groups at any time points (p > 0.05). With respect to adverse events, no treatment-related side effects were observed in the LV group. In contrast, four participants in the gabapentin group reported mild symptoms, including drowsiness, dizziness, and nausea.

Discussion

Our preliminary findings indicated that both LV and gabapentin could lead to improvements in IRLSSG and PSQI scores in patients with RLS, with no evidence of superiority for either group. After the intervention, IRLSSG scores decreased by more than 9 points, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference for this questionnaire and indicating that the reductions in both groups were clinically meaningful and significant [42]. Although the exact mechanism of action of LV is not yet fully understood and requires further studies using advanced electrophysiological and neuroimaging approaches, several potential mechanisms might explain the beneficial effects of LV in patients with RLS. LV might increase blood flow in the lower limbs and subsequently reduce limb pain through mechanical stimulation of the tissues [30]. Another potential effect of vibration could the modulation of tissue perfusion and vascular homeostasis, which might facilitate the circulation of blood, lymph, and other interstitial fluids [43–45]. In addition, low-frequency LV has been suggested to enhance dopamine release in the central nervous system via activation of cutaneous and muscular mechanoreceptors, which could have some beneficial effects given the central role of dopamine in regulating involuntary leg movements and sleep [43, 46, 47]. Overall, LV is likely to alleviate RLS symptoms through a combination of sensory modulation, dopaminergic activation, and vascular mechanisms. However, there is a need to better understand the mechanism behind its effects on RLS symptoms.

Limitations

This study had several limitations that are worth mentioning. First, the study sample size was relatively small, and there is a need for further studies with larger sample sizes to further evaluate the effects of LV on people with RLs. This is particularly important given that the study used to determine the sample size for the current study, also had the limitation of a relatively small sample size [27]. Second, PSQI is a subjective measure of sleep quality and sleep disturbance, which leads to certain types of reporting bias, and there is a need for studies utilizing an objective measure to better characterize the effects of LV on sleep parameters in people with RLS. Additionally, we only assessed PSQI items related to sleep disturbance, which limits our ability to provide a comprehensive and broad assessment of sleep disturbance in our study. Third, we only assessed sleep quality and RLS severity in our study, and didn’t evaluate other aspects, such as quality of life, which are crucial when evaluating the effects of treatments in people with RLS. Future more comprehensive studies are needed to better understand how LV might impact different consequences of RLS in these patients. Fourth, we only followed patients for one month, which allowed us to evaluate the short-term effects of LV in people with RLS. Considering that RLS is a chronic condition, future studies are needed to understand how LV affects these patients in the long-term and determine its effectiveness in the maintenance therapy in people with RLS. Fifth, our intervention lasted one month, which may not have been sufficient to capture the full effects of the interventions. Given that most interventional studies on RLS have a duration of at least three months, there is a need for future studies with longer intervention periods to compare the effects of LV and gabapentin in these patients. Sixth, due to the nature of this study, it was conducted as a single-blind RCT, and assessor and patient blinding was not feasible, with only the statistician being blinded to the intervention groups. Therefore, there is a need for future well-designed trials to further compare the effects of LV and gabapentin in patients with RLS with a lower risk of bias.
