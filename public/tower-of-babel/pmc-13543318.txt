Against the grain: Discovery of wood‐nesting reveals hidden substrate flexibility in a major bee lineage
Authors: Zestin W. W. Soh, John S. Ascher
Published: 2026

Nest‐site selection is a fundamental component of bee natural history, influencing reproductive success, exposure to predators and environmental stress, and ultimately constraining where populations can persist (Harmon‐Threatt, 2020). Documenting nesting behavior is therefore essential not only for understanding species biology but also for elucidating how they function within ecosystems and respond to environmental change (Antoine & Forrest, 2021; Collins et al., 2025; Kueneman et al., 2024; Orr et al., 2022; Visser et al., 2025). Yet nesting ecology remains poorly documented for many taxa, particularly in tropical regions where bee faunas are increasingly threatened by rapid habitat transformation (Orr et al., 2021; Warrit et al., 2023).

Halictidae, the second‐most speciose bee family globally, is overwhelmingly dominated by ground‐nesting species (Michener, 2007). Wood‐nesting is reported only in a small subset of taxa: lineages of Augochlorini (e.g., Megalopta, some Augochlora, and Neocorynura) (Eickwort & Sakagami, 1979), restricted to the Americas, and a minority of Lasioglossum species (Gibbs, 2011; Miyanaga et al., 2017; Stockhammer, 1967; Walker, 2022), all within the subfamily Halictinae. By contrast, the approximately 620 species of Nomiinae, distributed mainly across Africa, Asia, and Australia, have long been regarded as strictly ground‐nesting (Michener, 2007; Wcislo & Engel, 1996). Nesting studies for Nomiinae are sparse, largely limited to a review of 21 species by Wcislo and Engel (1996) and a few isolated accounts (e.g., Cane, 2008; Hannan et al., 2013), and remain scarce for palaeotropical lineages despite their importance as pollinators (Corlett, 2004; Wcislo & Engel, 1996). To our knowledge, nesting in decayed wood has not been reported for any nomiine species.

Here, we report the first evidence of wood‐nesting in Nomiinae, based on observations of Nomia (Acunomia) lusoria Cockerell, 1919, and Nomia (Hoplonomia) incerta Gribodo, 1894, in two contrasting habitats in Singapore: an intertidal mangrove and an urban park. These observations extend the known behavioral repertoire of the subfamily and suggest that tropical halictids exhibit greater nesting plasticity than previously recognized. They also raise broader ecological questions about how environmental constraints and deadwood availability influence bee nesting strategies in tropical landscapes.

The first observation was made on 23 July 2021 at 1100 h in Sungei Buloh Wetland Reserve (1.449176° N, 103.726838° E), Singapore's largest remnant mangrove. Multiple pollen‐laden female N. lusoria, a mangrove‐associated species (Ascher et al., 2022), were found entering small holes in a standing dead Sonneratia alba (Lythraceae) trunk within the intertidal zone, which is partially inundated daily by tides (Figure 1). The dead trunk (4.2 m tall and 0.9 m girth) was in an advanced state of decay, with sections of the exposed wood beneath the bark rotted and friable based on qualitative tactile assessment, and appeared sufficiently soft to allow excavation. Over 2 weeks, five active nest entrances were recorded, spaced 4.3–22.1 cm apart (mean ± SE = 15.3 ± 2.1 cm). Entrances (6–8 mm diameter) showed accumulations of loosened wood fibers (Figure 1b–g), with females observed actively excavating and removing this material. This indicates modification of the substrate, although initiation in preexisting openings cannot be excluded. Nests were situated 2.1–2.3 m above the high‐water mark. No entrance was shared. Nest provisioning occurred mainly between 0900 and 1200 h across tidal states, with foraging observed on mangrove flora, including Bruguiera gymnorrhiza (Rhizophoraceae) and Acanthus ebracteatus (Acanthaceae). However, on 21 February 2023, a female bee was also recorded excavating in back‐mangrove mud at the same site (Soh, 2025), indicating that the species may also construct subterranean nests.

A second case of wood‐nesting was recorded for N. incerta on 30 April 2025 at Gardens by the Bay (1°16′58.6″ N, 103°52′01.7″ E), an intensively managed urban park. Female bees were discovered nesting in decayed sections of living Melaleuca cajuputi (Myrtaceae) trunks (Figure 2; Soh, 2025). Follow‐up observations on 22 June 2025 documented nesting aggregations in four trees (Figure 2a), with approximately 5–40 entrances per tree from the trunk base to 2 m height, with additional activity above. Nest entrances (4–7 mm diameter) occurred exclusively at sections of exposed, softened, and partially decorticated wood (Figure 2b–e). These sections showed evidence of internal decay, with softened friable wood that could be readily excavated, and female bees were observed manipulating this material. At least 10 pollen‐laden females provisioned nests concurrently at one tree. No ground nests were detected despite extensive searches of surrounding sand, soil, and mulched substrates. In light of previous records of ground‐nesting by N. incerta in Japan and Singapore (Masuda, 1943; Okumura, 1966; Soh, 2016), the use of decaying wood by this species may be facultative. Together, these observations challenge the long‐held view that Nomiinae are exclusively ground‐nesting and expand the known nesting repertoire of palaeotropical Halictidae. Constraints on the suitability of soil substrates may be a driver of shifts to alternative substrates, including decayed wood, in otherwise soil‐nesting bees. Flooding poses a well‐documented risk to brood survival (Fellendorf et al., 2004), and in Singapore's mangroves, tidal ranges of several meters (Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore, 2025) may severely limit opportunities for subterranean nesting. Under such conditions, cavities in decayed wood elevated above the high‐tide line offer more stable nesting microhabitats. Comparable constraints may operate in tropical urban landscapes, where compacted or waterlogged soils restrict access to friable substrates (Antoine & Forrest, 2021; Tsiolis et al., 2022). N. incerta has been repeatedly recorded nesting in soil‐filled flowerpots instead of surrounding park soils (Soh, 2016), supporting the notion that fine‐scale substrate suitability influences nest‐site selection (Jackson et al., 2025). More broadly, the ability to exploit alternative nesting substrates could contribute to the species' persistence across heterogeneous environments, as suggested by recent ecological modeling (Lim et al., 2024), although this hypothesis remains to be tested explicitly.

Wood‐nesting in bees is often associated with specialized morphological adaptations, particularly enlarged or modified mandibles for excavation (Flores‐Prado et al., 2014; Michener & Fraser, 1978; Walker, 2022). Within Halictinae, obligate nesting in decayed wood has evolved in multiple Neotropical augochlorine lineages (Engel, 2000), which possess expanded mandibles (Eickwort, 1979; Tierney et al., 2008). In contrast, our examination of N. lusoria and N. incerta revealed no obvious mandibular differentiation from congeners only known to nest in soil (e.g., Nomia strigata and Nomia iridescens), suggesting that their use of decayed wood is facultative rather than specialized. These findings align with a growing recognition that nesting substrates are more heterogeneous than often assumed, reflecting the variation in substrate properties across soils and decaying wood, which form a continuum (Antoine & Forrest, 2021; Ulyshen, 2016). Although bee lineages are often broadly categorized as soil‐ or wood‐nesting, soil substrates used by bees vary widely in type, texture, and cohesiveness (Antoine & Forrest, 2021), and wood similarly undergoes progressive decomposition producing more particulate and soil‐like material (Ulyshen, 2016). Under such conditions, typically soil‐nesting groups may opportunistically nest in sufficiently softened, decayed wood without morphological innovation, a process that may also be facilitated by the presence of preexisting openings (e.g., from wood‐boring beetles) or exposed dead wood following bark loss or decay. Consistent with this, flexibility in nesting substrate use has also been reported in the reverse direction, with typically wood‐nesting halictines occasionally nesting in soil (Barrows, 1973). However, whether such facultative shifts to wood‐nesting entail higher energetic costs or increased mandibular wear, or are restricted to sufficiently softened substrates, remains an open question that could be addressed through comparative studies of nest architecture, excavation behavior, and mandible condition (Orr et al., 2016).

Deadwood is widely recognized as an important structural component of tropical invertebrate habitats (Bunnell & Houde, 2010; Ulyshen et al., 2023). In Asia, documented use of deadwood by bees has been largely limited to above‐ground‐nesting taxa, particularly members of Apidae (e.g., Xylocopa, Meliponini and Apini), as well as Hylaeus (Colletidae) and Lithurgus (Megachilidae) (Ascher et al., 2022). Our findings suggest that tropical nomiine bees may also exploit this resource, extending the range of pollinators associated with decaying wood. Given that deadwood is routinely removed from urban and peri‐urban landscapes, recognizing its value as nesting habitat has important implications for pollinator‐friendly management and conservation planning (Ranalli et al., 2025; Ulyshen et al., 2023).

In conclusion, wood‐nesting in Nomiinae reveals a previously unrecognized degree of nesting flexibility in a lineage widely assumed to be strictly ground‐nesting, underscoring gaps in natural history knowledge for tropical bees. Use of decayed wood may be favored in environments with periodically unsuitable soil conditions and may be more widespread among tropical halictids than currently documented. Decayed wood, which can exhibit mechanical properties intermediate between soil and intact wood, may facilitate such flexibility by reducing the constraints on excavation. The extent to which this reflects retained ancestral capacity or context‐dependent ecological responses, and whether the exploitation of such intermediate substrates contributes to evolutionary transitions in nesting strategies, warrants further study. As tropical environments undergo rapid land‐use and hydrological change, clarifying how bees respond to variation in nesting substrates will help inform understanding of their persistence and community assembly.
