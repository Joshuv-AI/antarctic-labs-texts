Neuronal lipid droplets play a conserved and sex-biased role in maintaining whole-body energy homeostasis
Authors: Romane Manceau, Celena M Cherian, Danie Majeur, Colin J Miller, Jasper D Fisher, Frédérick Boisjoly, J Beatrice Liston, Jennifer A Bako, Lianna W Wat, Charlotte F Chao, Audrey Labarre, Serena Hollman, Sanjana Prakash, Sébastien Audet, Lewis Depaauw-Holt, Benjamin Rogers, Anthony Bosson, Puja Biswas, Joyce J Y Xi, Catrina A S Callow, Niyoosha Yoosefi, Neele Schikora, Niki Shahraki, Yi Han Xia, Alisa Hui, Jared VanderZwaag, Khalil Bouyakdan, Demetra Rodaros, Pavel Kotchetkov, Caroline Daneault, Ghazal Fallahpour, Martine Tetreault, Marie-Ève Tremblay, Matthieu Ruiz, Baptiste Lacoste, J Alex Parker, Ciaran Murphy-Royal, Tao Huan, Stephanie Fulton, Elizabeth J Rideout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipids are essential for neuron development and physiology1–3. Yet, the central hubs that coordinate lipid supply and demand in neurons remain unclear4. Here we show the presence and functional significance of neuronal lipid droplets (nLDs) in vivo using invertebrate and vertebrate models. We validate5 the presence of nLDs in vivo and demonstrate that triglyceride metabolism enzymes and LD-associated proteins control nLD formation through both canonical and recently discovered pathways6. Modulation of nLDs has conserved and male-biased effects on whole-body energy homeostasis across flies and mice, specifically in neurons that couple environmental cues with energy homeostasis. Mechanistically, nLD-derived lipids support neuron function by providing fatty acids and phospholipids to sustain mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum function and homeostasis. This identifies a conserved role for nLDs in coordinating lipid supply and demand in neurons, which has implications for maintenance of neuronal lipid homeostasis and function in health and disease.Subject terms: Homeostasis, Glycerides, Hypothalamus, Cellular neuroscience, Metabolism

Main

Lipids are essential for neuron physiology and function1–3. While non-neuronal cells manage lipid metabolism via regulation of lipid droplets (LDs)6, lipid-storing organelles with a neutral lipid core of triglycerides (TGs) and cholesterol ester6, the prevailing view is that LDs are not normally present in neurons in vivo and TGs do not fuel neuronal energetic needs4. Growing evidence, however, suggests this view must be revisited, as LDs form in cultured neurons7–10, primary neurons in culture use TGs for ATP production10–12, and LDs accumulate in neurons in pathological contexts and disease13–16. Considering fatty acid oxidation (FAOx) was recently shown to support Drosophila neuronal ATP production17, we sought to comprehensively establish the presence, regulation and function of neuronal lipid droplets (nLDs) in vivo in invertebrate (fly) and vertebrate (mouse) models.

We visualized nLDs in vivo in adult Drosophila using neuron-specific expression of GFP fused to an established LD-targeting motif (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Methods)18,19. LD targeting was confirmed by co-labelling between GFP-LD-positive (GFP-LD+) puncta and neutral lipid stain LipidSpot (Fig. 1a–a”), and lack of overlap between GFP-LD+ puncta and mitochondria, lysosomes and protein aggregates (Extended Data Fig. 1a–c” and Supplementary Fig. 1b,c”). Because we detected nLDs in contexts where GFP-LD was absent in neurons (Fig. 1b–b”) and using additional imaging modalities (Fig. 1c,d), nLDs are not simply GFP-LD-driven artefacts. Although we cannot exclude all GFP-LD effects on LD biology or that some LDs are unlabelled by GFP-LD, we found Drosophila nLDs are small (~1 μm) and mostly but not always close to the cell body (Extended Data Fig. 1d–d”). Despite apparent non-uniform brain nLD distribution (Extended Data Fig. 1e–h), which we confirmed in another strain (Extended Data Fig. 1i,j) and explained as restricted GFP-LD expression driven by elav-GAL4 (ref. 20; Extended Data Fig. 1k–m’ and Methods), nLDs form in vivo across multiple neuron types in Drosophila (Extended Data Fig. 1n–n’’’). Consistent with prior studies9, we detected nLDs in murine hypothalamic neurons in culture where complementary methods revealed exogenous fatty acids (FAs) were esterified in nLDs in both cell lines and primary neurons (Fig. 1e–g, Extended Data Fig. 2a–d and Supplementary Fig. 1d,e’). In vivo, hypothalamic neuropeptide Y (NPY)-GFP-positive neurons showed ~10 LDs per neuron in both male and female mice (Fig. 1h–k and Extended Data Fig. 2e), in line with observations of perilipin-2-positive structures in mouse neurons in vivo5.

LDs are regulated by genes encoding proteins that impact LD biology (‘LD-regulatory genes’; Supplementary Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2f). Using annotated single-cell RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data21 we confirmed expression of LD-regulatory genes in Drosophila neurons, showing similar expression across clusters and between the sexes, and neurons of the arcuate nucleus (ARC) in mouse hypothalamus (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d)22. Despite its relatively low expression, we investigated adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) because our group and others identified phenotypes associated with neuronal inhibition or loss of ATGL7,18,23. Combining different approaches in cultured hypothalamic neurons treated with the ATGL inhibitor ATGListatin (ATGLi), we provide strong evidence that ATGL regulates nLD content, basal and stimulated lipolysis (Fig. 1l–n and Extended Data Fig. 2g–k) and the type of FAs esterified into nLDs (Extended Data Fig. 2l–s), and supplies FAs for FAOx (Fig. 1o,p). Because inhibition of TG-esterifying enzyme DGAT1 significantly reduced oleate-induced LD formation in hypothalamic neurons (Fig. 1q), DGAT1 and ATGL represent key regulators of LDs in hypothalamic neurons.

In Drosophila we developed and validated an automated counting system for in vivo nLDs across one brain region and found ~400–600 nLDs per hemisphere in this region in adults (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b), a number increased by neuron-specific loss of Drosophila ATGL (dATGL; also called brummer; Fig. 1r,s) in a cell-autonomous manner as loss of both dATGL and lipophorin receptor 2 (LpR2) still increased nLD abundance (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Neuron-specific loss of Drosophila homologues of hormone-sensitive lipase (dHSL), perilipin 1 (dPLIN1; Lsd-1), perilipin-2 (dPLIN2; Lsd-2), DGAT1/DGAT2-independent enzyme synthesizing storage lipids (dDIESL)24 and sterol response element binding protein (dSREBP)25 also altered nLD abundance (Fig. 1t–x and Extended Data Fig. 3d), whereas loss of 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate-O-acyltransferase 3 (dAGPAT3), seipin (dSEIPIN), and DDHD domain containing 2 (called phosphatidic acid phospholipase A1 PAPLA1, dDDHD2; Extended Data Fig. 3e–g) had no effect on nLDs despite LD accumulation in mammalian neurons lacking DDHD2 (ref. 10). Neuronal loss of Drosophila lipin (dLIPIN) and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (dDGAT1) caused near-total lethality at the late pupal stage (Extended Data Fig. 3h,i). Other than dSREBP and dPLIN2, LD-regulatory genes affected nLD abundance similarly between the sexes. We cannot fully rule out developmental effects of these genes on nLDs due to a 50–97% reduction in nLDs caused by Drosophila inducible expression systems (Extended Data Fig. 3j–l); however, we identify several in vivo nLD-regulatory factors.

Considering FA metabolism in mammalian hypothalamic neurons affects energy homeostasis26–28, neuronal loss of dATGL impaired Drosophila fat breakdown after fasting, a phenotype associated with energy homeostasis18, and our data showing neuronal loss of ATGL alters fat content and breakdown in Caenorhabditis elegans (Fig. 2a–i), we tested the significance of nLD regulation in vivo by monitoring energy homeostasis-related phenotypes (for example, fat storage and breakdown in flies, parameters of energy balance in mammals). In flies, neuron-specific loss of dHSL, dPLIN1, dPLIN2 and dDIESL caused defects in whole-body energy homeostasis in males with no effects in females (Fig. 2j–u). The most common defect was altered fat breakdown, where key trends were reproduced with independent RNAi lines and when data were presented as absolute TG levels (Extended Data Fig. 4a–e). We cannot rule out a contribution of development to these phenotypes due to a 50–97% reduction in nLD abundance (Extended Data Fig. 3j–l) and a 25% reduction in body fat (Extended Data Fig. 4f) that accompany use of inducible expression systems and prevent their use29. However, the normal lifespan of flies with neuronal loss of dATGL suggests no major defects are present (Extended Data Fig. 4g).

In mice, we found that fasting and cold exposure increased ATGL expression in the ARC of the hypothalamus (Extended Data Fig. 4h), which contains key neurons controlling energy homeostasis30. We thus knocked out ATGL in all ARC neurons (ARCATGLKO; Extended Data Fig. 4i–n) to assess its role in energy balance and metabolic responses to a fast and cold exposure. In normal fed conditions, except for increased lean mass and energy expenditure in males (independent of lean mass), other parameters of energy balance (Extended Data Fig. 5a–p) and glucoregulatory responses (Extended Data Fig. 5q–t) were unaffected in adult ARCATGLKO males and females. Metabolic responses to fasting were unaffected in ARCATGLKO males and females (Extended Data Fig. 5g–p). Because hypophagia in ARCATGLKO males reproduced feeding phenotypes induced by silencing ARC hunger-activated Agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons during cold exposure31, we generated a mouse model with ATGL KO in AgRP neurons (co-expressing NPY; Supplementary Fig. 3a–d; see model details in Methods). Like murine AgRP neurons, the Drosophila adipokinetic hormone (Akh)-producing cells (APCs) are neuroendocrine cells that produce a peptide (called Akh) during nutrient deprivation. Akh promotes food-seeking behaviours32,33 and directly stimulates lipolysis via Akh receptor binding34,35. After confirming nLD presence in the APCs and showing dATGL loss increased nLDs (Fig. 3s), we tested if APCs require dATGL to maintain energy homeostasis in line with ATGL function in mouse AgRP neurons. While APC-specific dATGL loss caused no effect on body fat in adult flies (Fig. 3t), showing dATGL loss does not impair APC development as genetic APC ablation augments body fat36, we identified impaired fat breakdown in male but not female flies with APC-specific dATGL loss (Fig. 3u). This reproduced the impaired fat breakdown phenotype caused by APC ablation (Extended Data Fig. 7m,n), which was specific to APC-dependent production of Akh and not caused by changes to food intake (Extended Data Fig. 7o–q). APC-specific loss of dHSL had no effect on fat metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 3k–m), mirroring mouse data on HSL in AgRP neurons26, whereas APC-specific loss of dDIESL strongly increased fat breakdown after fasting at a late but not early time point with no effect on body fat (Fig. 3v and Supplementary Fig. 3n,o) and APC-specific loss of dDGAT1 reduced body fat in females with minor effects on early fat breakdown (Fig. 3w,x and Supplementary Fig. 3p). Because dDGAT1 and dDIESL loss reproduce aspects of the lean phenotypes18 and trend towards greater fat breakdown, respectively, caused by genetic activation of APC using bacterial sodium channel NaChBac (Extended Data Fig. 7r), our data suggest nLD formation restrains APC activation. In line with no effect on nLDs, fat metabolism was unaffected by APC-specific loss of dDDHD2 (Supplementary Fig. 3q,r). Appropriate nLD regulation therefore supports the function of Drosophila hunger-activated neurons to maintain energy homeostasis.

To determine how nLD regulation affects neuronal function, we isolated whole brains from adult flies with neuronal loss of dATGL and subjected them to MS-based untargeted lipidomic profiling. We noted sex differences across multiple lipid classes and higher TG abundance as expected (Extended Data Fig. 8a–e and Supplementary Table 2). Neuronal dATGL loss caused male-biased shifts in many lipid species including reduced diacylglycerophosphoethanolamines, higher diacylglycerophosphocholines and lower cardiolipins (Extended Data Figs. 8f,g and 9a–d). This indicates profound membrane lipid remodelling where changes in the ratio of diacylglycerophosphocholines to diacylglycerophosphoethanolamines are associated with organelle dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress37. In cultured mouse neurons, untargeted lipidomics following ATGLi treatment revealed greater TG abundance, higher palmitoylcarnitine levels suggesting dysfunctional FAOx, and perturbed membrane diacylglycerophosphocholines and diacylglycerophosphoethanolamines indicating endoplasmic reticulum stress37 (Extended Data Fig. 9e–h and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Despite differences in individual phospholipids dysregulated by loss of ATGL in cultured neurons and Drosophila male brains, data from both systems suggest remodelling of major phospholipids important for mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum function. Indeed, genes most correlated with dATGL expression in Drosophila neurons include genes related to processes including mitochondrial energy production and peptide translation (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

Given these lipidome shifts, which are associated with mitochondrial defects, impaired FAOx and endoplasmic reticulum stress37, validated by RNA-seq in Drosophila brains lacking neuronal dATGL showing upregulation of factors involved in cell and endoplasmic reticulum stress (mTerf3, Hsp70Bb) and reduced expression of genes that support endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi function (GM130, CG14715; Supplementary Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 3), we characterized mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum in APCs and AgRP neurons lacking ATGL. APC-specific loss of dATGL caused fewer mitochondria in male but not female APCs, higher levels of a Drosophila ER stress reporter in both sexes, and reduced levels of nascent protein synthesis in male APCs (Fig. 4a–c). These cellular defects were significant, as they impaired Akh levels and reduced APC calcium levels indicating lower activity in males but not females (Fig. 4d,e), consistent with male-biased lipid remodelling and lack of fat metabolism phenotypes in females with APC-specific dATGL loss. Considering we reproduced the fat breakdown defects of flies with APC-specific dATGL loss in flies with reduced Akh or reduced APC activity (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b), the lowered Akh production and APC activity fully explain fat breakdown defects due to APC-specific dATGL loss given Akh’s known role in stimulating lipolysis34,35. We further show Akh-driven changes in peripheral lipid mobilization did not alter nLD (Extended Data Fig. 10c) and that lipid sensing by dSREBP may contribute to APC function (Extended Data Fig. 10d). Importantly, the trend towards greater fat breakdown caused by APC activation (Extended Data Fig. 7r) was blocked with loss of dATGL (Extended Data Fig. 10e), strengthening links between nLD and Akh signalling.

In mice, LD content in AgRP/NPY neurons was greater in AgRPATGLKO males, without affecting soma size (Fig. 4f,g). Supporting mitochondrial dysfunction in AgRP/NPY neurons of AgRPATGLKO males, mitochondrial morphology, not number, was altered (Fig. 4h–l). Our metabolomics dataset shows ATGLi in cultured neurons increased glycolysis and AMP/ATP ratio with reductions in NAD+/NADH ratio and anaplerotic amino acids (Extended Data Fig. 10f–m). Together with increased palmitoylcarnitine (Extended Data Fig. 9h), this suggests decreased mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Supporting this, ATGL loss in AgRP neurons phenocopies metabolic phenotypes observed in mice with defective FAOx in AgRP neurons27,28. Beyond mitochondria, AgRP/NPY neurons showed a greater percentage of neurons with endoplasmic reticulum dilatations, and changes in endoplasmic reticulum length and thickness in AgRPATGLKO males (Fig. 4m–s), suggesting endoplasmic reticulum stress is elevated in AgRP/NPY neurons of AgRPATGLKO mice38. Thus, APC and AgRP neurons show phenotypes consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Changes to mitochondria and endomembrane including the endoplasmic reticulum are associated with altered AgRP expression and firing in mammals27,39,40. AgRP mRNA and peptide levels were both increased in the ARC of AgRPATGLKO males, but not females (Fig. 4t,u), with no changes in the PVN where AgRP neurons project (Fig. 4v and Extended Data Fig. 10n–o’). Increased AgRP levels align with increased endoplasmic reticulum dilatation in AgRPATGL KO males (Fig. 4o–q) and reports showing endoplasmic reticulum stress augments AgRP expression39,40. Because DDHD2 inhibition in cultured neurons disrupts vesicle trafficking11, increased AgRP in the ARC, not the PVN, may result from impaired trafficking of AgRP vesicles. In addition, loss of ATGL in AgRP neurons reduced action potential frequency in AgRPATGL KO males and females (Fig. 4w,x and Extended Data Fig. 10p–s). Because ATGL loss in male AgRP neurons and silencing of AgRP neurons30 lead to similar feeding and metabolic phenotypes, reduced AgRP neuron firing may explain how ATGL loss affects energy homeostasis. Why a decrease in AgRP neuron firing in AgRPATGL KO females did not disrupt energy homeostasis (Fig. 3d–f,q,r) remains unclear, although energy homeostasis in females may be less sensitive to altered AgRP tone41 induced by loss of ATGL and/or that compensatory mechanisms maintain energy balance.

Our data across invertebrate and vertebrate models suggest a model in which appropriate nLD regulation in hunger-activated neurons influences whole-body energy homeostasis by providing these neurons with FAs and phospholipids to sustain mitochondrial energy production and function, and endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 5). Given the importance of lipids in supporting the function of all neurons, and together with foundational studies on nLD accumulation and defects in neuron–glia lipid transfer in neurodegenerative disease42, this model has broad implications for our understanding of lipid regulation in neurons across physiological and pathological contexts. We also identify key points for clarification in follow-up studies. First, the mechanisms underlying the sex-dependent effects of LD-regulatory genes on lipid metabolism, neuron function and, consequently, energy homeostasis will need to be elucidated. Second, although our studies in adult mice (ARCATGL KO model) rule out a developmental contribution of nLD regulation to energy homeostasis, an inducible gene expression system in Drosophila without effects on nLD abundance and energy homeostasis is needed to address our study’s limitation that neuronal loss of LD-regulatory genes occurs throughout development. Third, more work is needed to elucidate the precise mechanism(s) by which ATGL-dependent lipid remodelling in APC/AgRP neurons influences neuron function and energy homeostasis (for example, FA signalling and metabolism, direct versus indirect effects of lipids on endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial structure, function and signalling), and to determine whether and how loss of ATGL in APC/AgRP neurons exerts selective effects on Akh/AgRP levels. Finally, it will be critical to build a comprehensive network of LD-regulatory genes within each neuron subgroup, as it is clear that genes with similar effects on nLDs do not operate in the same neurons to cause a phenotype (for example, dATGL and dHSL) or why genes with similar molecular functions show distinct nLD phenotypes (for example, dDDHD2 and dATGL).

Methods

C. elegans

Maintenance and strains

C. elegans were maintained at 20 °C on standard NGM plates streaked with OP50 E. coli43. The TU3311 strain (uIs60 [unc-119p::YFP + unc-119p::sid-1]) was obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (P40 OD010440; University of Minnesota, Minneapolis; RRID: WB-STRAIN:WBStrain00035055).

RNAi experiments

We performed RNAi treatment by feeding E. coli HT115 containing an empty vector or atgl-1 RNAi clone from the ORFeome RNAi library (Open Biosystems; clone confirmed by sequencing). Worms were transferred onto RNAi plates enriched with 1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside at day 1 of adulthood. For fed and fasted studies, 8-day-old worms were transferred to NGM agar plates either streaked with OP50 (non-fasting) or not streaked (fasting) for 24 h.

ORO staining, imaging and quantification

ORO staining was conducted as previously reported, without the freeze–thaw and MRWB–paraformaldehyde (PFA) permeabilization44. Briefly, a working ORO stock solution (6 mM) was made by filtering a 10 mM stock and incubated overnight with age-synchronized day-9 adult worms that were dehydrated in PBS/isopropanol 60%/0.01% Triton-X for 15 min at room temperature (RT). Worms were washed three times with PBS/0.01% Triton-X, mounted on slides, and ORO staining was visualized using a brightfield Zeiss Fluorescent Microscope with a ×10 objective. Images were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ; RRID: SCR_002285) software as integrated density where the relative percentage of the signal was calculated.

BODIPY staining

BODIPY 558/568 C12 (Invitrogen D3835) was fed to live 7-day-old worms by diluting to 5 µM in either empty vector or atgl-1 RNAi bacterial suspension for 24 h at 20 °C, protected from light. Worms were then transferred to an NGM agar plate streaked with OP50 for an additional 24 h, mounted on slides with 2% agarose pads, and immobilized using a 5 mM solution of levamisole diluted in M9. Fluorescence in the anterior gut, representing LDs and fat accumulation, was detected using Zeiss fluorescent microscope and quantified using Fiji (ImageJ).

Drosophila

Drosophila rearing and strains

Strains were maintained on a 12 h–12 h light–dark cycle at 22 °C on cornmeal–sugar–yeast two-acid medium18, with 50 larvae per 10 ml of food. Male pupae were separated by the presence of sex combs and pupae eclosed into single-sex vials. Adult flies were transferred into new vials every 2–3 days, and fasted on 0.8% agar (wt/vol) in 1× PBS for either 12 h or 24 h. All experiments used 5- to 6-day-old male and female flies. We used the following strains from Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center: w1118 (3605), UAS-dHSL-RNAi (65148), UAS-dPLIN2-RNAi (32846), UAS-dDIESL-RNAi (67895), UAS-dSREBP-RNAi (34073), UAS-dDGAT1-RNAi (65963), UAS-Xbp1-eGFP/CyO (39720), UAS-PAPLA1-RNAi (76068), tubGAL80ts (‘TARGET system’, 7018), y1,v1;P{y[+t7.7]=CaryP}Msp300[attP40] (36304), y1,v1;P{y[+t7.7]=CaryP}attP2 (36303), UAS-reaper (5823), UAS-NaChBac (9468), UAS-Lst-RNAi (60400), UAS-kir2.1 (6595), elav-GAL4 (458), LexAop-CD8-GFP-2A-CD8-GFP;UAS-mLexA-VP16-NFAT,lexAop-rCD2-GFP/TM6B,Tb1 (‘CaLexA’ strain, 66542) and w1118;tubp-TIR1-T2A-GAL80.AID (‘AGES system’, 92470). We used the following strains from Vienna Drosophila Resource Center: UAS-dATGL-RNAi (37880), UAS-dHSL-RNAi_2 (109336), UAS-dPLIN1-RNAi (30884), UAS-LpR2-RNAi (25684), UAS-dPLIN1-RNAi_2 (106891), UAS-dPLIN2-RNAi_2 (102269), UAS-dAGPAT-RNAi (48593) and y,w1118;P{attP,y+,w3’} (60100). We received UAS-GFP-LD (2.6) and UAS-GFP-LD (3.4) from M. Welte, which are insertions of UAS-GFP-LD on the second and third chromosomes, respectively. We received UAS-mRFP from D. Allan, nsyb-GAL4 from M. Gordon, and Akh-GAL4 from J. Park, Akhrev and AkhA from R. Kuhnlein. All GAL4 strains were backcrossed into the w1118 background for ten generations.

Drosophila lifespan

Unmated adult male and female flies were reared and separated by sex as above, transferred every second day into fresh vials where the number of fly deaths was recorded, and maintained until no living flies remained. Experiments used ~200 flies per sex and per genotype.

Feeding

One biological replicate consisted of ten flies. Using the CAFÉ assay setup45,46, flies were kept in an adapted 15-ml conical vial with access to two capillary tubes (A-M Systems, 626000) filled with fly food media (20.5 g l−1 sucrose, 70.9 g l−1 D-glucose, 45.3 g l−1 yeast extract (Sigma-Aldrich, Y1625) in water) with 0.5 μl mineral oil in the top of each tube to prevent evaporation. The meniscus of the medium was marked at the start of the experiment and 24 h later; 1 mm = 0.15 μl food consumed (normalized to fly weight).

Inducible gene expression

TARGET system

Five-day-old male and female elav-GAL4 > UAS-GFP-LD,tubGAL80ts flies were reared and maintained at 22 °C and shifted immediately after eclosion to 29 °C for 5 days before various assays.

AGES system

Five-day-old male and female elav-GAL4 > UAS-GFP-LD,tubp-TIR1-T2A-GAL80.AID flies were reared and maintained at 22 °C and transferred immediately after eclosion to auxin-supplemented food (10 mM l−1 of 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (‘auxin’); PhytoTechnology Laboratories, N610) before various assays.

Body fat measurements

Five- to six-day-old flies were anaesthetized, weighed and snap-frozen at −80 °C (storage time <4 weeks). TG concentration was measured as described47–49 with minor modifications18 in fed (0 h post-fasting) and fasted (12 h post-fasting and 24 h post-fasting) contexts; time points were chosen due to distinct mechanisms regulating fat breakdown during each interval35. Autosomal driver nsyb-GAL4 was used to drive expression of RNAi transgenes targeting LD-regulatory genes. Percentage body fat was calculated using TG concentration with body weight as a normalizing factor, normalization that is key for comparing triglyceride abundance between genetic backgrounds50 or sexes. To draw accurate conclusions about the effects of energy homeostasis, a trait influenced by many genomic loci and by genetic background51, we compared phenotypes of males and females with RNAi-mediated knockdown of LD-regulatory genes with sex-matched controls from both parental strains (GAL4 and UAS controls), which ensures that genetic background or transgene insertion effects alone cannot explain defects in energy homeostasis in flies with neuron-specific loss of LD-regulatory genes. One biological replicate consists of five flies; each experiment contained four biological replicates and was repeated twice (n = 8). For main figures and Extended Data, all values are presented as percentage body fat or normalized fat loss, although we show trends are reproduced when we present data as absolute TG values (compare Fig. 2j–l and Extended Data Fig. 4c–e).

Monitoring and quantifying neuronal LDs

GFP-LD is a transgene where the 46-amino acid LD-targeting domain from Klarsicht (Klar) was fused with GFP and placed under the UAS control. This domain was confirmed as necessary and sufficient for LD targeting19, and likely targets LDs via an amphipathic helix (predicted by AlphaFold52; Supplementary Fig. 1a) as with other cytosolic proteins localizing to LDs53. We confirmed GFP-LD targeting to LDs by incubating brains with neutral lipid dye LipidSpot 610 (1:50 dilution; Biotium, 70069) in fresh 1× PBS for 2 h at RT (Fig. 1a) and imaging within 24 h after dissection. Neuron-specific expression of UAS-GFP-LD was driven by elav-GAL4. LDs were reproducibly observed in two brain regions: a region of the protocerebrum containing the soma of Kenyon cells, neurons whose processes comprise a structure called the mushroom body, and a region called the optic lobe, which contains neurons of the visual system (Extended Data Fig. 1e–h), a non-uniform distribution we reproduced with an independent UAS-GFP-LD insertion (Extended Data Fig. 1i,j). Because elav-GAL4 drives expression only in these brain regions20, however, non-uniform elav-GAL4 expression explains why some brain regions are enriched in GFP-LD expression. Indeed, when we assessed LD distribution earlier in development, when we confirmed elav-GAL4 drives pan-neuronal expression (Extended Data Fig. 1m), nLDs were present throughout the brain and ventral nerve cord (Extended Data Fig. 1k,l), which means most neurons are capable of LD formation. To monitor neuronal LD regulation in vivo, we developed and validated an automated method to monitor changes in neuronal LD abundance in Drosophila (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Owing to the large number of LDs within a compact area, we counted nLDs in images of the Kenyon cell soma region located in the protocerebrum (‘protocerebrum LD’) in the Drosophila brain (Extended Data Fig. 1f,h). This approach cannot count all neuronal LDs; however, we provide insight into how LD-regulatory genes affect nLDs in one region. Because elav-GAL4 is located on the X chromosome, we validated effects on LD regulation for two genes (dATGL and dDIESL) using the additional GAL4 drivers Akh-GAL4 and nsyb-GAL4, respectively (Fig. 3s and Extended Data Fig. 3d).

Whole-brain and APC sample and slide preparation

Adult flies were anaesthetized briefly and individually dissected in cold PBS at the indicated ages, fixed at RT for 45 min in 4% PFA (Electron Microscopy Sciences (EMS), 15710) and washed twice in 1 ml cold PBS (except for LysoTracker and MitoView incubation). Samples were then incubated with fluorescent dyes or antibodies. LDs were visualized using LipidSpot 610 as above. Lysosomes were visualized in live brains incubated with LysoTracker Red (1:10,000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, L7528) in PBS for 10 min at RT and imaged immediately; lack of fixation in this protocol likely led to less resolved images. Mitochondria were visualized in whole brains with MitoTracker Red (1:300 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, M7512) in 1× PBS for 45 min at RT. Mitochondrial mass was monitored in APCs by incubating tissue in cold PBS and transferring to a solution with MitoView Green (1:500 dilution in PBS; Biotium, 70054) for 30 min before fixing with 4% PFA. Protein aggregates were detected with mouse anti-ubiquitin FK2 primary antibody (1:200 dilution; MilliporeSigma, 04-263, RRID AB_612093); we validated the antibody with both positive and negative controls (Supplementary Fig. 1b,c)54. FK2 antibody was detected using goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568 (1:300 dilution; Invitrogen, A11030, RRID AB_2737024). Akh protein was detected with rabbit anti-Akh primary antibody (validated in ref. 32) with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200 dilution; Thermo Fisher, A11008, RRID AB_143165). After dye/antibody treatment, samples were incubated with Hoechst 33342 (1:500 dilution in PBS (10 mg ml−1); Invitrogen, H3570) for 30 min, washed in PBS and mounted in a saturated sucrose solution (70% wt/vol). Slides were kept at 4 °C and were imaged <30 h after mounting.

Protein synthesis

The Click-iT Plus OPP Protein Synthesis Assay kit was used to measure nascent protein synthesis in APC-containing tissue marked by mRFP. APCs were dissected in Drosophila Schneider’s medium (Gibco, 21720024) and immediately transferred to a solution with OPP reagent (1:2,000 dilution in Schneider’s medium) for 30 min at RT; after OPP removal APC-containing tissue was washed three times with Schneider’s and fixed (4% PFA). OPP was detected and visualized according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Molecular Probes, C10456).

Image analysis

Images were acquired using a Leica SP5 inverted confocal microscope with Leica Application Suite software (v.2.7.3.9723). Adult whole-brain and APC samples were imaged using a ×63 oil objective, larval brains were imaged with a ×40 oil objective; images were processed using Fiji (v2.16.0)55. nLDs and mitochondrial abundance were quantified with a custom Fiji counting macro: 3D images containing GFP-LD-marked nLDs were first cleaned using the ‘Median (3D)’ function in Fiji55, and background was removed using the ‘Subtract Background’ function (size parameter of 8). Consistent intensities for the LDs were ensured through standardizing the intensity of the processed image by applying a look-up table; look-up table values applied were the display range in the ‘Enhance Contrast’ function (saturated parameter of 0.1 on a maximum projected image of the median-filtered and background-removed image). nLDs were detected in the processed image using the ‘3D Maxima Finder’ function with ‘radiusxy = 3 radiusz = 6 noise = 150’ as arguments. Fluorescence for the Xbp-1 endoplasmic reticulum stress reporter, OPP assay and Akh peptide levels in the APCs were quantified by measuring the sum of fluorescence across three optical sections of the APCs; fluorescence for the Xbp-1 reporter and OPP assay were normalized to APC size. One biological replicate represents APCs from one adult fly.

APC activity

APC-specific expression of a calcium-responsive chimeric transcription factor LexA-VP16-NFAT, which drives GFP expression to provide an indirect read-out of cellular calcium levels, with Akh-GAL4 (ref. 56) allowed us to monitor APC activity, which we previously confirmed accurately reflects neuropeptide-producing cell activity57.

Fly Cell Atlas single-cell RNA-seq analysis

The loom file containing the 10x VSN Head (stringent) dataset of the Fly Cell Atlas was accessed on 27 November 2024, imported into R using SCopeLoomR58 (version 0.13.0), and processed via Seurat59 (version 5.1.0) using the standard pipeline. Clusters representing neuronal cells but not sensory neurons were retained, and the mixed-sex cells were removed according to loom dataset annotations. Average expression of LD-regulatory genes in each cluster was obtained via Seurat’s PseudobulkExpression function. For correlating gene expression with dATGL expression, meta cells were generated using the hdWGCNA60 package (version 0.4.00), where 90 cells were aggregated for each meta cell with the maximum number of shared cells between meta cells of 18. Cells were aggregated into meta cells by sex, genes with <50 counts across meta cells were removed, and the correlation coefficient between dATGL expression and other genes was calculated with Pearson’s correlation (P values were adjusted using Holm’s method). The enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes was performed using fgsea61 (version 1.31.2) via the clusterProfiler62 package (version 4.12.6) with correlation coefficients as the ranking metric. Gene sets between the sizes of 10 and 500 were tested. The GO annotation used was provided by the org.Dm.eg.db package in R (v3.19.1; https://bioconductor.org/packages/org.Dm.eg.db/).

RNA-seq

One biological replicate consisted of 30 Drosophila brains; we collected three replicates per sex and per genotype. Brains were dissected individually and transferred to TRIzol; all brains per replicate were pooled into 1 ml TRIzol and stored at −80 °C. RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15596018). Sample quality and RNA-seq was performed at the UBC Biomedical Research Center Sequencing Core, as previously described63,64. Differences in gene expression were identified using the DESeq2 R package65.

Drosophila lipidomics

One biological replicate consisted of 50 Drosophila brains, and we collected four replicates per sex and per genotype. We used whole brains as FACS-based isolation and sorting of neurons disrupts metabolic profiles66. Brains were dissected individually and transferred to 80% liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-grade methanol; all brains per replicate were pooled into 1 ml 80% LC–MS-grade methanol and stored at −80 °C for <1 month. The brains were sonicated in an ice-water bath for 30 min and stored at −20 °C for 4 h for protein precipitation. The solvent was evaporated, and the lysate was resuspended in 375 μl of 80% ice-cold methanol in water. Around 1 ml of methyl tert-butyl ether was added, and the mixture was shaken for 5 min. Phase separation was induced by adding 275 μl water. The upper layer containing lipids was transferred to a new tube, dried and reconstituted in acetonitrile–isopropanol (1:1, vol/vol) at a volume proportional to bicinchoninic acid assay protein quantification results. Species were only considered differentially regulated if the fold change was >0.3 different from controls and unadjusted P < 0.05. N = 4 samples per sex and per genotype. Occasional non-uniformity in changes to lipid species from a subclass, or the magnitude of change in lipid abundance, are likely because whole-brain samples contain (1) many types of neurons, not all of which will have LD at any given time, and (2) glia, in which dATGL is intact. Because we had two control groups, lipid species in Drosophila were only considered differentially regulated by neuronal dATGL loss if their abundance was different from both control groups.

Mouse neurons in culture

GT1-7 and N46 hypothalamic neurons

GT1-7 (from P. Mellon; RRID: CVCL_0281) and N46 cells (Cedarlane, CLU138; RRID:CVCL_D45) were grown in DMEM (4.5 g l−1 glucose, Gibco, 11965092) containing 10% FBS (Wisent, 10437-028), 100 mM sodium pyruvate (for GT1-7 cells, Gibco, 11360-070) and 5,000 U ml−1 of penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco, 15070-063) until 80% confluence. GT1-7 and N46 neuronal cell lines (male origin) co-express NPY and AgRP67.

Primary hypothalamic cultures

Brains from NPY-GFP or POMC-GFP postnatal day 1–2 mouse pups (males and females) were collected and the mediobasal hypothalamus was dissected in 10 ml of Lebovitz-15 media (Gibco, 11415-064) and centrifuged at 2,000g for 2 min; pellet was digested at 37 °C for 15 min using 4.5 U ml−1 lyophilized papain (Worthington Biochemical, LS003120), 5 mg ml−1 D-glucose (Sigma, G7528-1KG), 0.2 mg ml−1 L-cystein (MP, 101444), 0.2 mg ml−1 BSA (Multicell, 800-095-EG) and 20 U ml−1 DNAse I (Worthington Biochemical, LS006333) in 1× PBS and centrifuged at 2,000g for 2 min. The pellet was resuspended in 1× Neurobasal A media (Gibco, 10888-022) with 2% B-27 supplement (Gibco, 17504-044), 2% FBS (Wisent, 10437-028), 1% P/S (Gibco, 15070-063) and 1% GlutaMax (Fisher, 35050061) and filtered using a 70-μm cell strainer. Neurons grew on coverslips for 7 days in Neurobasal A media with 2% B-27, 2% FBS, 1% P/S, 1% GlutaMax and 2 μM cytosine beta-D-arabinofuranoside (AraC; Sigma, C1768-100MG) to inhibit glial cell growth. Neurons were treated with 0.27% BSA (Multicell, 800-095-EG) or 0.25 mM oleate (Nu-chek Prep, S-1120) complexed to 0.27% BSA for 5 h. Neurons were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, stained using 1:1,000 dilution HCS LipidTox Neutral Red Dye (Fisher, H34476) for neutral lipids and a 1:1,500 dilution of Hoechst 33342 for nuclei (Invitrogen, H3570) and imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss).

High-throughput LD imaging

GT1-7 and N46 cells were plated on 96-well plates (PerkinElmer, 6055302), treated with DMEM (Multicell, 319-060-CL) in 1% FBS (Wisent, 10437-028), 10 mM glucose and 0.25 mM oleate (Nu-chek Prep, S-1120) complexed to 0.27% BSA (Multicell, 800-095-EG), 50 μM ATGLi (Cedarlane, HY-15859), 5 μM FSK (Sigma, 344282-5MG), 100 nM DGAT1 inhibitor A-922500 (Sigma, A1737-1MG) or 0.1% DMSO for 2.5 or 24 h. Cells were fixed using 4% PFA, and neutral lipids were detected using 0.01 mM BODIPY 493/503 (Sigma, D3922) while nuclei were stained using a 1:1,500 dilution of Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H3570). Plates were imaged using Operetta High Throughput screening system and analysed using Harmony High-Content Imaging and Analysis Software. For analysis, one biological replicate consisted of an average of six wells with nine areas per well randomly selected for quantification.

MS-based lipidomics

Lipidomic studies used cultured neurons and were analysed by the Montreal Heart Institute Metabolomic Core Facility. Mass spectrometry (MS)-based targeted and untargeted lipidomics required 10 million and 5 million cells per condition, respectively; data were normalized to protein measured with a Bradford assay.

Profile of esterified FA analysis (TG fraction) using GC–MS

GT1-7 and N46 cells were treated with DMEM containing 1% FBS, 10 mM glucose and 0.1% DMSO or 50 µM ATGLi for 24 h, counted, harvested in screw-cap tubes of 10 million cells and flash frozen. Quantitative profiling was performed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis as described68. Cells were incubated in chloroform–methanol (2:1) and supplemented 1:4 with 0.004% butylated hydroxytoluene overnight at 4 °C before being filtered, dried under nitrogen gas and resuspended in hexane–chloroform–methanol (95:3:2). The TG fraction was obtained after separation on an aminoisopropyl column, dried under nitrogen gas following the addition of specific internal standards and resuspended in hexane/methanol with 0.004% butylated hydroxytoluene. This fraction contains whole-cell TGs; thus, we cannot rule out potential recovery of TGs from other organelles (for example, lysosomes) in addition to LDs. Esterified FAs were analysed as their methyl esters (FAMES) following a direct trans-esterification with acetyl chloride and neutralized by incorporating 6% potassium carbonate. The upper hexane phase was injected onto a 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977 mass selective detector (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a capillary column (J&W Select FAME CP7420; 100 m × 250 µm inner diameter; Agilent Technologies). The analysis was operated in positive chemical ionization mode and ammonia was used as reagent gas. Chromatographic conditions were fixed as follows: injection at 270 °C in a split mode, high-purity helium used as carrier gas and a temperature gradient beginning at 190 °C for 25 min and increased by 1.5 °C per min up to 236 °C. FAs were analysed as their [M + NH3]+ and concentrations calculated using internal standards and standard curves.

Untargeted lipidomics

GT1-7 neurons were grown on glass plates and treated with 0.1% DMSO or 50 µM ATGLi for 24 h, counted, harvested in 15-ml Falcon tubes of 5 million cells and flash frozen. Lipid species were extracted and analysed as described69. Briefly, samples were injected (from 0.6 µl to 1.1 µl according to protein concentration) into a 1290 Infinity high-resolution HPLC coupled with a 6530 Accurate Mass quadrupole time-of-flight (LC-QTOF, Agilent Technologies) system equipped with a dual electrospray ionization source. Elution was assessed using a Zorbax Eclipse plus column (C18, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Agilent Technologies) maintained at 40 °C using a 83-min chromatographic gradient of solvent A (0.2% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate in water), and B (0.2% formic acid and 5 mM ammonium formate in methanol/acetonitrile/methyl tert-butyl ether, 55:35:10 (vol/vol/vol)). Samples were analysed in positive ionization mode. MS data were processed using the Mass Hunter Qualitative Analysis software package (version B.06.00, Agilent Technologies) and an in-house bioinformatics pipeline leading to a list of MS signal features characterized by a mass, a retention time and signal intensity. Lipid annotation was performed using data alignment with an in-house database previously validated using MSMS. MSMS was used to confirm the latter annotations and identify, when possible, additional unknown MS features. In positive ion mode, 1,261 lipid features were detected. Of these, 116 were significantly affected by ATGL inhibition, including 80 unknown and 36 annotated lipids (Extended Data Fig. 9f–h).

Oleate beta-oxidation

GT1-7 neurons were treated with 0.25 mM oleate and radioactive 14C-oleate at 0.4 µCi µl−1 (PerkinElmer, NEC317050UC) complexed to 0.27% BSA, in 1% FBS and 10 mM glucose for 24 h to label intracellular lipids including TGs before measures of 14C-oleate oxidation as we described67 and 14C-oleate release into the media. Briefly, cells were treated with 0.1% DMSO or 50 μM ATGLi for 3 h in DMEM containing 1% FBS, 10 mM glucose and 1 mM L-carnitine (Sigma, 8400920025). Potassium hydroxide (Sigma, 1310-58-3) was added to filters in rubber caps to absorb CO2. After 3 h the reaction was stopped and radioactivity (in filters and media) was quantified using a liquid scintillation analyser (Tri-Carb 2800TR); values were normalized to protein levels.

Metabolomics

GT1-7 cells were plated on T25 flasks and treated with DMEM containing 1% FBS, 10 mM glucose and 0.1% DMSO or 50 μM ATGLi for 24 h. Flasks were then rinsed and flash frozen. Quantification of metabolic species was performed by the CRCHUM Metabolomics core facility. Briefly, samples were quantified using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS-MS), normalized to protein levels (Bradford protein assay), and a blank condition (no cells) was subtracted.

Mitchell and maintained for at least six generations on the C57BL/6J genetic background (C57BL/6J, 000664; RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664). AgRP-IRES-Cre mice were obtained homozygous from The Jackson Laboratory (Agrptm1(cre)Lowl/J, 012899; 129S6/SvEvTac background, RRID: IMSR_JAX:012899). Male NPY-GFP (B6.FVB-Tg(Npy-hrGFP)1Lowl/J, 006417; RRID: IMSR_JAX:006417) and POMC-eGFP (C57BL/6J-Tg(Pomc-EGFP)1Low/J, 009593; RRID: IMSR_JAX:009593) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (6–10 weeks old) and bred with C57BL/6J wild-type females to produce experimental animals. Male TH-eGFP transgenic reporter mice (C57BL/6J background)70 were used for virus validation studies.

Metabolic challenges

Naive WT (C57BL/6J) male mice were purchased at 7–8 weeks old from The Jackson Laboratory and acclimatized to a reversed cycle 2–3 weeks before experiments. Fasted mice were deprived of food for 16 h starting during the second half of the dark cycle. Cohorts of animals were exposed for 24 h to 21 °C (controls), 30 °C (thermoneutrality) or 4 °C (cold) in Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS) metabolic cages (Columbus Instruments International; RRID: SCR_016718se).

ATGL KO in ARC neurons (ARCATGLCTL versus ARCATGLKO)

Eight- to nine-week-old ATGLfl/fl males and females underwent stereotaxic surgery to knock out ATGL in the ARC using Cre-expressing viruses under the control of the human synapsin promoter (Extended Data Fig. 4k). Anaesthetized ATGLfl/fl mice (isoflurane) received bilateral viral injections of AAV9-hSyn-Cre-2A-tdTomato-SV40pA (Viroveck, 2.18 × 10−13 viral genomes (vg) per ml) or AAV9-hSyn-tdTomato-SV40pA (Viroveck, 2.24 × 10−13 vg per ml). Around 200 nl per side were simultaneously injected (0.5 nl s−1) using neurosyringes (Hamilton, 65457-01) placed at a 10° angle, using the following coordinates: AP: bregma −1.4 mm; lateral: sinus +1.2 mm; depth: dura −5.9 mm. Similar injections with AAV9-hSyn-tdTomato-SV40pA were performed in POMC-eGFP, NPY-GFP and TH-eGFP transgenic male mice to assess virus tropism by quantifying the percentage of GFP and dtTomato-positive neurons in the ARC (Extended Data Fig. 4l–o). Five minutes after the end of the injection, syringes were removed, the wound was closed and mice recovered in thermoneutrality incubators for >2 h. Animals were not used in experiments until 4 weeks after surgery. Accuracy of ARC viral injections was validated using tdTomato. Only mice harbouring more than 20% ATGL reduction in the ARC were included in the study.

ATGL KO in AgRP neurons (AgRPATGLCRE versus AgRPATGLKO)

Hemizygous AgRPCre/+ mice were bred with hemizygous NPY-GFP reporter mice to visualize targeted AgRP-NPY neurons. Male AgRPCre/+:NPYGFP/+ mice were bred with female ATGLfl/fl mice to generate AgRPCre/+:NPYGFP/+:ATGLfl/+ mice, which were then crossed with ATGLfl/+ or ATGLfl/fl to generate experimental mice: AgRPCre/+:ATGL+/+ (AgRPATGLCRE) and AgRPCre/+:ATGLfl/fl (AgRPATGLKO). Mice carrying or not the NPYGFP/+ transgene were included in experiments. Mice harbouring significant ectopic ATGL recombination (detected by genomic qPCR) were not included in experiments. Validation of the mouse model was performed by RNAscope, qPCR and NPY-GFP imaging (Supplementary Fig. 3a–j).

Metabolic cages (CLAMS)

Respiratory quotient, energy expenditure and food consumption were monitored using indirect calorimetry in CLAMS metabolic cages as previously described71. Animals were single-housed in the CLAMS apparatus in a dark–light cycle matching their housing conditions during 24 h for acclimatization, followed by measurements at 21 °C or 4 °C. FAOx was calculated from respiratory quotient and energy expenditure (EE) as FAOx = (EE × (1 − respiratory exchange ratio))/0.3. Feeding behaviour including cumulative food intake (g), average meal size (g), average inter-meal interval (s) and satiety (ratio of meal size/inter-meal interval) was analysed as we described71.

Body composition

Body composition (fat and lean mass) was measured by magnetic resonance imaging (echoMRI).

Glucose tolerance test

Mice were food deprived 5 h before intraperitoneal administration of glucose (dextrose, 1.5 g per kg body weight). Glycaemia was measured using tail-vein blood with an Accu-chek Performa glucometer at 0 min, 15 min, 30 min, 60 min and 90 min. Blood samples were collected via a capillary for insulin assays, performed by the CRCHUM cell physiology platform using commercially available ELISA kits.

Tissue collection

Brain and tissue collection were performed in mice anaesthetized with isoflurane. Tissues were weighed, frozen and stored at −80 °C. For cytomorphological experiments, perfusions were performed. Anaesthetized mice (ketamine–xylazine) were perfused transcardially with 1× PBS followed by 4% PFA. Brains were extracted, post-fixed for 2 h in 4% PFA, placed in sucrose overnight and stored at −80 °C. Brain coronal sections (30 µm) made using a microtome (Leica, SM2000R) were kept in anti-freeze at −20 °C before staining.

LD quantification with LipidSpot610

Brain sections (30 µm) from mice on the NPY-GFP background were washed twice for 5 min in 1× PBS and then stained 2 h at RT with a 1:1,000 dilution of LipidSpot 610 (VWR, 70069-T) in 1× PBS, and a 1:5,000 dilution of Hoechst 33342. After staining, sections were washed twice in 1× PBS for 5 min and mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (eLife, P36930). Imaging was performed using a Leica Stellaris 8 confocal microscope on a ×63 oil-immersion lens, with 0.3-µm z-steps (with an average of 70 z-steps per neuron) to create a 3D reconstruction of neurons and LDs with Imaris software. LDs were defined as: spots with an average diameter of 0.4 µm, background subtracted, z-diameter for model PSF elongation of 1.2, quality of 260 and region threshold for local contrast of 32.4.

Gene expression

Total RNA from frozen tissue was extracted using the TRIzol method as described71. Total RNA concentration and purity were determined using the Nanodrop 2000. cDNA synthesis and qPCR were performed as described71. Primer sequences are described in Supplementary Table 4. Gene expression is represented as the fold change from the mean of control groups, after normalization.

RNAscope

Brain sections (30 µm) were washed with 1× PBS, mounted on glass slides and dried for 30 min at 60 °C. Following the company’s instructions (ACD, Multiplex Fluorescent V2 Assay, 323100), sections were dehydrated, incubated with hydrogen peroxide, steamed for 5 min with antigen retrieval and treated with Protease III for 15 min. Pnpla2 (469441-C1) and AgRP (400711-C2) probes were incubated for 2 h at 40 °C as indicated (c2 probe diluted at 1:50 in the c1-probe). A negative probe (ACD, 320871) was used as the negative control. Slides were kept overnight, in 5× saline sodium citrate protected from light at RT. On the second day, after washing, sections were amplified three times as indicated by the company. Fluorescence signal was developed using opal dye diluted at 1:1,500, sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (eLife, P36930) and stored at 4 °C. A Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG; RRID: SCR_013672) was used for imaging. Quantification was done using Fiji software.

Immunofluorescence

Brain sections (30 µm) were successively permeabilized for 5 min using Triton solution (0.01×), blocked for 2 h with 2% goat serum, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then 2 h at RT with secondary antibodies. Sections were mounted in media with DAPI (Vectashield, VECTH1200) and imaged with a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss; RRID: SCR_013672). Primary antibodies were anti-AGRP (1:500 dilution; EPR18155-110, Abcam, 254558; RRID: AB_3076273) and anti-GFP (1:500 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 33-2600; RRID: AB_2533111). Secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were Alexa Fluor 546–goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000 dilution; A-11035; RRID: AB_2534093) and Alexa Fluor 488–goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1,000 dilution; A-11001; RRID: AB_2534069).

Electrophysiology

Stereotaxic surgeries

Seven-week-old AgRPATGLCRE and AgRPATGLKO mice underwent stereotaxic surgery to selectively induce mCherry expression in AgRP-Cre neurons. Anaesthetized mice (isoflurane) received bilateral viral injections of AAV2-CAG-DIO-mCherry-WPRE-bGHpA (Viroveck, 1.31 × 10−13 vg per ml) in the ARC as described in ‘ATGL KO in ARC neurons’. Five minutes after the end of the injection, syringes were removed, the wound was closed and mice recovered in thermoneutrality incubators for at least 2 h.

Hypothalamic slices

Coronal hypothalamic slices (300-µm thick) were obtained from AgRPATGLCRE or AgRPATGLKO mice 3 to 4 weeks after surgeries. Animals were deeply anaesthetized with isoflurane and brains were rapidly removed. The brain was cut with a vibratome VT1200 (Leica, Germany) in ice-cold N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG) cutting artificial cerebrospinal fluid containing 119.9 mM NMDG, 2.5 mM KCl, 25 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 1.4 mM NaH2P04, 26 mM NaCO3 and 20 mM D-glucose saturated with 95% O2 and 5 mM CO2. Slices containing ARC were transferred to 95% O2 and 5% CO2 saturated NMDG solution at 33 °C followed by ASCF containing 130 mM NaCl, 2.8 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 26 mM NaCO3 and 2.5 mM D-glucose for 1 h at RT before recordings.

Electrophysiology

Slices were transferred to a recording chamber and perfused with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (2 ml min−1). Whole-cell recordings were achieved from the soma of mCherry-expressing AgRP neurons. Borosilicate patch pipettes (2–5 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing 105 mM K-gluconate, 30 mM KCl, 10 mM phosphocreatine, 10 mM HEPES, 4 mM ATP-Mg, 0.3 mM GTP-Tris and 0.3 mM EGTA (pH 7.2; 290–300 mOsmol). Recordings were made at RT. Data were acquired using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices) and digitized using a Digidata 1440 A digitizer and pClamp/Clampfit 10.7 (Molecular Devices). Recordings were low-pass-filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz. Access resistance was 10–25 MΩ and regularly monitored during experiments, and data were excluded if access resistance variations were above 20% throughout the experiment. Spontaneous firing frequencies were obtained from I = 0 current–clamp mode recordings for at least 2 min.

Electron microscopy

Tissue preparation

Mice were anaesthetized with excess ketamine–xylazine and transcardially perfused successively with cold solutions: 50 ml PBS, 100 ml fixative solution (2% glutaraldehyde; EMS, 16320) + 4% paraformaldehyde (EMS, 15713-S) and 100 ml 4% PFA. Brains were extracted, post-fixed 30 min in 4% PFA, washed with PBS and freshly sliced using a vibratome (Leica VT1000S, vibration 10, speed 3) at 50 μm. Sections were stored in anti-freeze solution at −20 °C.

Immunohistochemistry on brain slices

Sections (50 μm) containing the ARC of 8-week-old AgRPATGLCRE or AgRPATGLKO mice on the NPY-GFP background were processed for immunostaining against GFP before electron microscopy processing. Successively, sections were quenched for 5 min with 0.3% H2O2 (Fisher Scientific, H325500) in PBS, incubated for 30 min in 0.1% NaBH4 in PBS, washed in PBS, blocked for 1 h at RT in a solution containing 10% FBS (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, 005-000-121), 3% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906-500G) and 0.05% Triton X-100 (MilliporeSigma, X100-1L) in PBS. Next, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in blocking buffer solution with the chicken anti-GFP antibody (1:5,000 dilution; Aves Labs, GFP-1020; RRID: AB_10000240). The following day, sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS; 50 mM, pH 7.4) and incubated for 90 min at RT with biotinylated donkey anti-chicken polyclonal secondary antibody (1:300 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 703-066-155; RRID: AB_2340355) in TBS containing 0.05% Triton X-100. Sections were washed in TBS and incubated for 1 h at RT in a 1:100 avidin–biotin complex (Vector Laboratories, PK-6100) solution in TBS. Lastly, 0.05% 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (MilliporeSigma, D5905-50TAB) activated with 0.015% H2O2 diluted in Tris buffer (0.05 M, pH 8.0) was used to reveal the staining for 90 s.

TEM

The protocol for electron microscopy sample preparation was recently detailed72. Briefly, brain sections were washed in phosphate buffer (PB) and incubated for 1 h in a solution containing equal volumes of 3% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich, P9387) with 4% osmium tetroxide (EMS, 19190) in PB. After washing with 100% PB, 50% PB/50% double-distilled water (ddH2O) and 100% ddH2O for 5 min each, sections were incubated in a 1% thiocarbohydrazide solution (diluted in MilliQ water; Sigma-Aldrich, 223220) for 20 min followed by a second incubation in 2% aqueous osmium tetroxide (diluted in MilliQ water) for 30 min. Then, sections were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol and washed with propylene oxide (Sigma-Aldrich, 110205-18L-C). Sections were embedded overnight at RT in Durcupan resin (20 g component A, 20 g component B, 0.6 g component C, 0.4 g component D; Sigma, 44610). The following day, resin-infiltrated sections were flat-embedded onto fluoropolymer films (ACLAR, EMS, 50425-25) surrounded by resin and kept at 55 °C in a convection oven for 72 h to allow for polymerization. Next, tissue sections containing the region of interest were excised and glued to a resin block-face. For TEM imaging, using a Leica ARTOS 3D ultramicrotome, 2–3 74-nm-thick sections were collected (four levels, ~8–10 μm apart) on copper grids (EMS, G150-Cu) and imaged on a TEM - 120kv JEOL 1400 Flash equipped with a 4K Gatan OneView digital camera. Using the software for GMS3, images of neurons were randomly acquired in the ARC at a resolution of 5 nm per pixel and exported in .tif file format.

Electron tomography

Tomographic imaging was conducted on brain sections using a JEM-1400 Flash transmission electron microscope (JEOL) operating at 120 kV and equipped with a 16 MP Gatan OneView digital camera, as previously described73. Tilt series were collected in bidirectional mode over a ±60° range with 1.5° increments at ×25,000 magnification using SerialEM software. Image alignment was performed via the fiducial-less method, using eTomo script of the IMOD software package, producing aligned image stacks. Tomograms were reconstructed via weighted back-projection, generating 3D volumes composed of approximately 100 images. Manual segmentation was performed in 3dmod using the normal drawing mode.

Single-cell RNA-seq

The RNA Drop-seq single-cell dataset utilized for the preliminary assessment of the transcriptional landscape in mouse hypothalamic neurons was sourced from a public Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset, using published annotations22. Reanalysis began from the merged raw counts file (mm10), and filtering of low-quality cells and doublets reproduced the original standard processing (800 < Features < 6,000; mitochondrial RNA (%) < 20). Data integration was performed according to guidelines outlined in Seurat v4.1.0 vignettes74, but experimental groups were analysed separately. Presented data focused on the initial three batches comprising 21 male mice (7,350 cells), as well as the last batch, which contained two males (358 cells) and two females (676 cells). Following standard Seurat v4 processing and re-clustering, original cell-type annotations were validated using the FindMarkers function with updated markers literature, such as the Azimuth database74. These annotations were used to subset neurons (male, 4,445; female, 425), as well as specific AgRP (male, 977; female, 41) and POMC (male, 403; female, 22) populations.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis and data presentation

GraphPad Prism (10.2.2; RRID: SCR_002798) was used for most of the data analysis and graph preparation. For P values < 0.0001 or > 0.99, Prism does not return exact P values and are listed as P < 0.0001 or P > 0.99 in figures. For fly data, Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to test for normality. For mouse data, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. If data were normally distributed, we used unpaired parametric tests (two-sided t-test and ANOVA); if data were not normally distributed, we used an unpaired nonparametric test (Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests). For ANOVA, Tukey, Fisher and Sidak post hoc tests were used to perform multiple comparisons between groups. Two-way ANOVA was used to detect sex–genotype, genotype–time, genotype–temperature and sex–diet interactions. For fly data, outliers were detected using the ROUT method and removed from the analysis. For mouse data, outliers were detected using Grubb’s test and were excluded from our analysis. Mice showing health issues after stereotaxic surgeries and ARCATGL KO mice harbouring less than 20% ATGL reduction in the ARC were also excluded from the study.

Sample sizes, randomization and data collection

For fly studies, no power calculations were used to predetermine sample sizes as N > 15 in many cases. For whole-body TG measurements, no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications18. For mouse studies, no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications71. Except for the randomization of ATGL-floxed mice across groups (ARCATGL Ctrl and KO), no other randomization was used to assign animals to experimental groups in either fly or mouse studies. For all image analysis across fly, worm and mouse models, image acquisition and analysis were performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. Data collection and analysis for all other phenotypes were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments.

Statistics and reproducibility

Drosophila data were collected across at least two animal cohorts. All in vivo experiments with mice were performed at least twice or grouped from two to three independent cohorts with the same conclusion. Mouse phenotyping studies were performed on independent cohorts of animals that always included both controls (ARCATGL WT or AgRPATGL CRE) and KO (ARCATGL KO or AgRPATGLKO) mice. All studies in N46 and GT1-7 cells were reproduced at least in three independent experiments (n = 3 to 7) with technical replicates (2 to 8) per condition in each independent experiment and led to the same conclusion. Images in Fig. 1a,b reproduce observations in >5 additional dissections. Images in Fig. 1c,d were obtained from publicly available images comprising the complete electron microscopy volume of the Drosophila brain; a comprehensive scan of all sections (~7,062) was not undertaken. Images in Fig. 1f reproduces observations in 6 additional experiments; images in Fig. 1h reproduces observations in 4 additional experiments. Images in Extended Data Fig. 1a–c reproduce observations in >5 additional dissections. Images in Extended Data Fig. 1d showing non-cell body nLDs occur more infrequently (as indicated in text), but we observed these non-cell body nLDs in >5 dissections. Images in Extended Data Fig. 1e–n reproduce observations in >5 additional dissections. Images in Extended Data Fig. 2b,c reproduce observations in 4 (NPY) and 3 (POMC) additional experiments.
