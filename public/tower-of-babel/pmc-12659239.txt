Effectiveness of mechanical and manual cardiopulmonary resuscitation: evaluation with carotid doppler and metabolic parameters
Authors: İsmail Batuhan Vergi, Mustafa Burak Sayhan, Eray Çeliktürk, Satuk Buğra Han Bozatlı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim was to compare the hemodynamic and metabolic effectiveness of manual chest compressions and a mechanical chest compression device during in-hospital cardiac arrest by monitoring carotid Doppler flow and end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) and arterial lactate levels.MethodsIn this single-center, prospective, observational cohort study, 54 adult patients with nontraumatic cardiac arrest in the emergency department were enrolled between December 2023 and February 2024. Chest compressions were delivered manually for the first three 2-minute cycles and mechanically for the subsequent two cycles. The peak-systolic velocity (PSV) and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) of the common carotid artery and the ETCO₂ and lactate levels were recorded at cycles 1, 3 and 5. The primary and secondary outcomes were the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and 24-h survival, respectively. The predictive performance of physiological markers was assessed with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsROSC was achieved in 41/54 patients (75.9%), and 22/41 patients (53.7%) were alive at 24 h. The ultrasound acquisition time decreased from 39.9 ± 7.2 s in the first (manual) cycle to 25.7 ± 5.5 s in the fifth (mechanical) cycle (p < 0.01). Compared with manual cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), mechanical compression was associated with greater increases in PSV (Δ₃–₅ = 9.0 ± 3.3 cm s⁻¹ vs. Δ₁–₃ = 2.7 ± 2.9 cm s⁻¹; p < 0.01) and EDV, a greater increase in ETCO₂ (3.5 ± 1.7 mm Hg vs. 1.3 ± 1.6 mm Hg; p < 0.01) and a more pronounced decrease in lactate levels (-0.30 ± 0.34 mmol L⁻¹ vs. -0.10 ± 0.19 mmol L⁻¹; p < 0.01). An ETCO₂ value ≥ 35 mm Hg predicted ROSC with 75.6% sensitivity and 53.9% specificity (AUC = 0.70), whereas a lactate level ≤ 5.3 mmol L⁻¹ predicted ROSC with 76.9% sensitivity and 82.9% specificity (AUC = 0.81). Background

Early, high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) markedly improves survival after cardiac arrest [1]. Continuous feedback on compression depth, compression rate and perfusion is therefore essential. End-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) is the most widely used bedside marker: a sustained increase typically heralds the return of spontaneous circulation, whereas persistently low values signal inadequate cardiac output [2–4].

Carotid Doppler ultrasonography has been proposed as an additional, noninvasive tool for assessing cerebral perfusion during CPR [5]. Experimental and clinical studies have shown that higher peak-systolic velocity (PSV) and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) are associated with effective chest compressions and favorable outcomes [6–10]. This method is rapid, repeatable and free of ionizing radiation.

Manual chest compressions are labor intensive and prone to fatigue-related deterioration [11]. Mechanical compression devices provide constant depths and rates, can be used during patient transport, and can reduce hands‒off intervals [12]. However, evidence of the benefit to mortality is contradictory, and concerns persist regarding compression-related injuries [13–15].

To date, no prospective study has simultaneously evaluated Doppler-derived carotid flow, ETCO₂ and lactate trends during sequential manual and mechanical CPR cycles in adults with in-hospital cardiac arrest. We therefore investigated whether mechanical compression improves hemodynamic (PSV, EDV) and metabolic (ETCO₂, lactate) indicators of CPR quality and whether these parameters predict short-term outcomes. Our hypotheses were that (i) Doppler velocity, ETCO₂ and lactate trends would change more favorably during mechanical compression and that (ii) ETCO₂ and lactate levels, alone or combined, would outperform Doppler indices in predicting the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC).

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center, prospective, observational cohort study was conducted in the Adult Emergency Department of Trakya University Health Research and Application Center, which manages approximately 120,000 emergency patients annually. Ethical approval was obtained from the Trakya University Faculty of Medicine Scientific Research Ethics Committee (approval date: 14 November 2022; protocol TÜTF-GOBAEK 2022/392), and the study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient’s legal representative.

Study population

Consecutive adults (≥ 18 years) who experienced nontraumatic in-hospital cardiac arrest while under observation in the emergency department between 1 December 2023 and 29 February 2024 were eligible if they received at least five complete cycles of CPR. The exclusion criteria were out-of-hospital arrest, trauma, pregnancy, the inability to position the mechanical compression device, ROSC before the third cycle, cervical anatomy precluding carotid imaging, or the refusal of consent. Resuscitation protocol

All arrests were managed according to the 2020 American Heart Association (AHA) Advanced Cardiac Life Support guidelines. The first three 2-minute cycles of chest compressions were performed manually at a rate of 100–120 min⁻¹. Immediately after endotracheal intubation, a mainstream capnometer (EMMA®, Masimo, USA) was attached. At the start of the fourth cycle, a mechanical compression device (LUCAS 3, Jolife AB, Sweden) was positioned; cycles 4 and 5 were delivered mechanically, and physiological measurements were obtained without interrupting compressions (Fig. 1).

Workflow of the CPR monitoring protocol

A five-person team operated with predefined roles throughout the protocol: team leader (EM physician directing timing and drug delivery), compressor (manual compressions during cycles 1–3 and device oversight thereafter), airway manager (bag-valve ventilation and tracheal intubation per guideline), ultrasonographer (point-of-care carotid Doppler measurements) and recorder/timekeeper (cycle timing and data logging). Measurements were scheduled at the end of cycles 1, 3 and 5 without interrupting compressions (as depicted in Fig. 1). The ultrasonographer stood on the side offering the best acoustic window, kept minimal probe pressure, aligned the sample volume in long-axis with angle correction ≤ 60°, and used a 2–3 mm sample gate; gain and wall-filter settings were kept constant across cycles. If image quality was briefly lost due to chest motion, the view was reacquired within the same cycle without pausing CPR. The ultrasonographer and recorder were not involved in clinical decision-making, which reduced operator-dependent variability and preserved workflow ergonomics. Epinephrine was administered per guideline every 3–5 min; any depiction of “q2min” in schematic figures was corrected to reflect guideline-consistent dosing.

After intubation, ventilation was standardized at ~ 10 breaths/min with a tidal volume of ~ 6–7 mL·kg⁻¹ and FiO₂ 1.0; minute ventilation was adjusted to maintain ETCO₂ within recommended targets without interrupting compressions.

Data collection

A certified emergency physician who was not involved in clinical decision-making recorded the following:

Carotid Doppler: The PSV and EDV of the common carotid artery, measured with a 6–15 MHz linear probe (X-Porte, Fujifilm Sonosite, Japan) in longitudinal view at the end of each target cycle. All carotid Doppler measurements were performed by one emergency physician with 4 years of POCUS experience and approximately 75 prior carotid scans. Before study launch, the operator completed 8-hour refresher training and a 10-patient pilot to standardize probe positioning, insonation angle (≤ 60°), gain, sample volume (2–3 mm), and minimal probe pressure. Velocities were averaged over three consecutive cardiac cycles whenever feasible; when motion artifact occurred, cine loops were reviewed and beats with artifact were excluded. If insonation was not feasible at a given time point, the attempt was recorded as “not obtained” and the reason noted (e.g., inadequate window or device positioning).

This study was exploratory; a formal a priori power calculation was not feasible due to the absence of prior estimates for the combined Doppler–ETCO₂–lactate endpoints. We therefore targeted a pragmatic sample of ~ 50–60 arrests based on the center’s monthly volume. Post-hoc, the observed effect size for the phase-related change in ETCO₂ (Δ₃–₅ vs. Δ₁–₃) indicates adequate power at α = 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed by skewness–kurtosis (-1.5 to + 1.5). Continuous variables are presented as the means ± SDs or medians (IQRs) and were compared with paired or unpaired t tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Mann‒Whitney U tests where appropriate. Categorical data were analyzed with χ² tests. Pearson correlation was used to explore the relationships among Doppler, ETCO₂ and lactate deltas. Mixed-model ANOVA was used to compare physiological changes between the manual and mechanical periods. Predictive performance was evaluated with ROC curves using fifth-cycle (mechanical phase) values for each marker; cutoff values were determined by Youden’s index, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the areas under the curve (AUCs). Significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). ROC curves were constructed using fifth-cycle (mechanical phase) values for each marker. AUC 95% confidence intervals were estimated with the Hanley–McNeil method. Significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS v19.0.

Fifty-four patients who experienced in-hospital cardiac arrest were analyzed. The median age was 69 years (IQR 63–77), and 39 patients were male (72%). ROSC was achieved in 41 patients (75.9%); 22 patients (53.7%) were alive at 24 h. The patients who ultimately died had significantly lower PSV, EDV and ETCO₂ values and higher lactate levels at every measurement point (Table 3).

Correlation analysis

Across all cycles, ΔPSV, ΔEDV and ΔETCO₂ were strongly and positively correlated (r = 0.79–0.97, p < 0.01), whereas Δlactate was negatively correlated with each of these variables (r = -0.70 to -0.88, p < 0.01).

Predictive performance

ROC analysis confirmed the limited diagnostic value of carotid Doppler velocities (AUC 0.56–0.58, p > 0.30). In contrast, an end-tidal CO₂ threshold of ≥ 35 mm Hg predicted ROSC with 75.6% sensitivity and 53.9% specificity (AUC = 0.701; Table 4). A lactate cutoff of ≤ 5.3 mmol/L performed even better, with 76.9% sensitivity and 82.9% specificity (AUC = 0.811). Entering both markers into a bivariate logistic model increased the AUC only marginally (0.812) and did not improve either sensitivity or specificity in a clinically meaningful way; therefore, for the sake of clarity and brevity, the full output is not displayed, and the single-marker metrics are shown in Table 4. Receiver-operating-characteristic analyses yielded the following AUCs (95% CI): ETCO₂ 0.811 (0.69–0.93), lactate 0.701 (0.55–0.85), PSV 0.583 (0.41–0.76), and EDV 0.557 (0.38–0.73) (Table 4).

Discussion

The 2020 AHA guidelines list mechanical chest compression devices as Class IIb or Level B devices, indicating that they may be used when high-quality manual compressions cannot be guaranteed [1]. However, the literature is far from consistent: whereas some series report superior perfusion or survival with automated systems, others show no benefit or even potential harm [16–21]. The present prospective cohort study shed light on this debate by comparing sequential manual and mechanical compressions in the same patient and by measuring three complementary markers carotid Doppler flow, ETCO₂, and arterial lactate levels at precisely defined time points. The observed reduction in ultrasound acquisition time during mechanical cycles may also reflect easier image stabilization with regular device-generated chest motion. Given the fixed sequence (manual always preceding mechanical) and time-dependent co-interventions during resuscitation, our findings should be interpreted as associations between the later phase of CPR and physiological trajectories rather than device-specific causal effects.

Multiple randomized trials and recent systematic reviews have not shown a clear clinical benefit of mechanical over manual CPR for neurological recovery; therefore, our physiological improvements should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as descriptive of within-resuscitation trajectories.

Physiological improvements observed during the later, mechanical phase may reflect the more regular compression pattern provided by the device as well as time-dependent factors (cumulative epinephrine exposure, post-intubation ventilation control, and rescuer fatigue during earlier manual cycles). During the mechanical cycles, the PSV in the common carotid artery increased by 9 cm s⁻¹ three times the increase achieved during the preceding manual cycles while the concomitant increase in EDV was of similar magnitude. These velocities approached the reference values reported for healthy adults (≈ 67 cm s⁻¹ for PSV and 18 cm s⁻¹ for EDV) [6, 8], suggesting that the device restored near-physiological carotid flow despite ongoing arrest. In addition, the Doppler acquisition time decreased from forty seconds at the first measurement to twenty-six seconds at the last measurement, indicating a rapid learning effect and underscoring the feasibility of bedside flow imaging during prolonged resuscitation. Because no previous human study has compared Doppler flow during sequential manual and mechanical CPR, these findings are consistent with more stable forward flow during device-generated compressions than during earlier manual cycles. Accordingly, we interpret these differences as phase-related associations rather than device-specific causal effects.

Hemodynamic trends toward higher carotid velocities coincided with more favorable metabolic changes. ETCO₂ an established surrogate for pulmonary blood flow increased by 3.5 mm Hg during mechanical cycles but by only 1.3 mm Hg during manual cycles, echoing the findings of laboratory studies that linked constant sternal displacement to increased cardiac output [22, 23]. An ETCO₂ threshold of 35 mm Hg predicted ROSC with 75.6% sensitivity and 53.9% specificity (AUC 0.70), values that agree with pooled estimates from large meta-analyses [24, 25]. At the same time, the arterial lactate level decreased twice as fast during the mechanical phase. Because an elevated lactate level is a recognized marker of global hypoperfusion and early postresuscitation mortality [26], the more rapid clearance of lactate provides further evidence of improved tissue oxygen delivery. Strong positive correlations were observed between changes in Doppler velocities and ETCO₂, whereas the lactate level correlated negatively with both, reinforcing the physiological link between macroscopic flow and metabolic debt. Importantly, carotid Doppler velocities did not meaningfully predict ROSC in our cohort, whereas ETCO₂ and lactate demonstrated moderate predictive value.

The steeper decline in lactate observed over the 2–4-min mechanical phase should be interpreted with caution. Lactate kinetics during cardiac arrest reflect both production and washout and may lag behind instantaneous changes in perfusion. In our protocol, the mechanical phase coincided with time-dependent factors—cumulative epinephrine exposure, post-intubation ventilation control, and potential rescuer fatigue during the preceding manual cycles—that could also accelerate lactate clearance. Accordingly, we interpret the lactate trajectory as an association with the later phase of CPR rather than a device-specific causal effect, and we have tempered causal language throughout the manuscript.

Despite their sensitivity to compression quality, neither the PSV nor the EDV distinguished survivors from nonsurvivors; the AUC was approximately 0.58. Several explanations are plausible. Once carotid flow exceeds a critical threshold, further augmentation may not influence the outcome if irreversible cerebral or myocardial injury has already occurred [13]. Moreover, our Doppler measurements were brief snapshots; continuous flow monitoring might reveal prognostically relevant patterns such as the loss of pulsatility or acceleration of diastolic decay. Finally, the insonation angle, sample-volume placement, and probe pressure remain operator-dependent variables that add noise and may reduce discriminatory power.

The cohort resembled previous in-hospital arrest reports: the median age was sixty-nine years, hypertension and coronary artery disease were the leading comorbidities, and the initial rhythm distribution was consistent with those in previous Turkish series [8, 27, 28]. ROSC was achieved in 76% of the patients higher than the figures for out-of-hospital arrest yet in line with other tightly monitored emergency settings where the response time is minimal [29]. Nevertheless, nearly half of the patients who achieved ROSC died within twenty-four hours, underscoring the familiar gap between immediate resuscitation success and durable survival.

The ROSC proportion in this series (75.9%) appears high for an undifferentiated in-hospital arrest population. This likely reflects selection related to the protocol: patients who achieved ROSC before the third cycle, or who could not complete five cycles, were not included in the analytic dataset. Consequently, the sample is enriched for arrests persisting long enough to permit measurement and may differ systematically from all in-hospital arrests at our institution. We now explicitly acknowledge this potential selection bias.

Limitations

Our single-center design and modest sample limit generalizability and preclude full multivariable modeling. We did not randomize the order of manual versus mechanical compressions; a crossover or parallel-group design would better isolate the device effect. The compression depth and recoil were not directly measured. Objective CPR-quality device logs (depth/rate) were not available. We did not assess neurological outcomes, limiting inference to short-term physiology, ROSC, and 24-hour survival. Future multicenter trials should incorporate continuous Doppler, CPR quality metrics (depth, rate, and recoil) and long-term neurological outcomes.

First, the fixed-order, pre–post design (manual cycles 1–3, mechanical cycles 4–5) is inherently confounded by time; cumulative vasopressor dosing, airway control after intubation, and progressive optimization may contribute to later improvements independent of compression mode. Second, because eligibility required completion of at least five cycles, patients with early ROSC or early termination were excluded, which likely inflated the observed ROSC proportion and limits generalizability. Third, carotid Doppler velocities showed limited discrimination for ROSC in this cohort and should not be used alone for prognostication. Finally, the shorter ultrasound acquisition time during mechanical compressions may reflect the predictability of device-generated chest motion rather than a rapid learning effect alone.

We did not perform a full multivariable or mixed-effects sensitivity analysis adjusting for time and co-interventions; thus, residual confounding is likely. Inter- and intra-operator reliability for Doppler measurements was not assessed, which may have introduced measurement variability. Compression-related injuries were not systematically monitored; the study was underpowered to evaluate harms.

Conclusion

Compared with the preceding manual cycles, the later mechanical cycles coincided with higher carotid flow velocities and more favorable ETCO₂ and lactate trajectories. ETCO₂ (≥ 35 mm Hg) and lactate (≤ 5.3 mmol L⁻¹) provided useful early prognostic information for ROSC, whereas carotid Doppler velocities showed limited discrimination. Given the fixed sequence and time-dependent co-interventions, these observations should be interpreted as associations rather than device-specific causal effects. Larger multicenter randomized or crossover studies incorporating continuous Doppler and objective CPR-quality metrics are warranted.
