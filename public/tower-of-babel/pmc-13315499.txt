Principal component analysis-based three-dimensional quantitative characterization of cracks on perforated embryo eggs
Authors: Mingyan Zhao, Haoke Chen, Chuan He, Xiaojing Zhu, Yanhong Wei, Duo Fu, Dijian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A 3D global-local framework enabled quantitative characterization of perforation-induced cracks on curved eggshell surfaces.•PCA-based global features combined with Random Forest identified 4 crack shape categories with 99.19% mean accuracy.•Local 3D analysis quantified main-crack geometry, sub-crack number, and sub-crack length more precisely than 2D methods.•The proposed method provides quantitative support for crack acceptability evaluation and potential automated screening of perforated embryo eggs.

Introduction

Currently, human vaccines for such diseases as influenza and mumps require virus cultivation within living chicken embryos. First, a flat-head trocar is used to perforate the air cell region of the eggshell (as shown in Fig. 1), followed by injection of the virus into the allantoic cavity of the 10-day-old living chicken embryo via the syringe needle for further cultivation. The virus proliferates naturally alongside the development of the chicken embryo, and finally, the allantoic fluid is collected from the embryo and subjected to subsequent processing to obtain the required vaccine (Wang et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2024). However, when the flat-head trocar impacts the eggshell during perforation, cracks may occur (as shown in Fig. 2), leading to potential failure in virus cultivation with a rejected embryo rate of 4-5% (Wang et al., 2021). In this study, cultivation failure was operationally defined as an embryo exhibiting either embryonic mortality or contamination during the post-perforation incubation period. Moreover, during collection of the allantoic fluid, it could also contaminate the same batch of virus, necessitating the removal of unqualified cracked perforated embryo eggs (Peng and Dhakal, 2011; Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025).Fig. 11. Traditional crack detection, limited by precision and analytical dimensions, allows only two-dimensional (2D) analysis. It performs eligibility judgment of perforated embryo eggs based merely on crack presence while ignoring crack acceptability. However, perforative cracks on embryo eggs are essentially three-dimensional (3D) features distributed on spatial curved surfaces; the eligibility of perforated embryo eggs depends not only on the presence of cracks but also on their shape and geometric size, namely crack acceptability (Wang et al., 2023). Dimensionality reduction analysis based on 2D images makes it difficult to obtain circumferential feature information of the cracks and, due to limitations in precision and data dimensions, fails to support precise quantitative evaluation. It only enables qualitative analysis based on the presence of cracks, thereby leading to misrejection of qualified cracked eggs or missing detection of unqualified ones (Ge et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Moreover, cultivation of different viruses exhibits variations in crack acceptability. Therefore, the potential of promoting more accurate perforated embryo egg eligibility judgment lies in advancement from qualitative crack presence detection to quantitative crack acceptability evaluation. Consequently, it is urgent to establish high-precision, comprehensive 3D quantitative characterization and shape identification methods for spatial perforative cracks, providing quantitative parameters that could support crack acceptability evaluation.

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction refers to the process of capturing the shape and appearance of real objects to generate their digital 3D representations using computer vision and computer graphics (Ashworth and Brasher, 2013; Flores-Fuentes et al., 2023). In recent years, 3D reconstruction has been gradually introduced into the crack detection field. For example, Xiao et al. conducted the 3D reconstruction of rock surface damage after blasting via CT scanning, and comprehensively evaluated the damage cracks through the combination of the multi-fractal dimension and box-counting dimension (Xiao et al., 2024). In addition, some scholars have employed 3D methods such as ultrasonic imaging or multi-view stereo reconstruction to analyze cracks inside the ceramic hollow plate membrane or on the concrete surface (Sun et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2025). However, these techniques have difficulty in handling the thin-shell structure of embryo eggs, as well as the tiny scale and curved-surface characteristics of perforation-induced cracks on embryo eggs (Buck, 1989; So et al., 2022). Meanwhile, limited 3D reconstruction studies targeting eggs have focused solely on the quality evaluation of egg appearance and have not involved egg crack detection (Liu et al., 2017).

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a method for reducing the dimensionality of a data set with multiple interrelated variables and has also been applied in the field of crack detection (Jolliffe, 2002). For example, studies have achieved identification of cracks on ceramic tiles and table eggs through PCA-based dimensionality reduction analysis based on 2D images (Wang, 2014; Li et al., 2020). It’s noteworthy that Kaddah et al. performed PCA on images of road cracks and classified them into 3 categories based on their orientation using the obtained eigenvalues and eigenvectors, making PCA take its initial form in crack quantitative characterization (Kaddah et al., 2019). However, dimensionality reduction analysis of 3D cracks would lead to the loss of spatial information, which would inevitably cause misjudgments. To address this issue, a few studies combining 3D reconstruction with PCA have provided ideas for 3D quantitative characterization of cracks. For instance, Chang et al. reconstructed cracks in the bolt hole of the rail joint using ultrasonic imaging, and combined with PCA-based maximum intensity projection, quantitatively analyzed the crack length (Chang et al., 2024). However, existing studies have neither combined global feature extraction with global-local multi-scale quantitative characterization, nor considered the acceptability of cracks, let alone the differences in crack acceptability under different conditions.

Currently, research on perforation-induced cracks on embryo eggs remains rather limited and mainly focuses on 2D visual detection. For example, Lin and Zhao adopted 2D-vision methods based on fractal theory and image segmentation to detect the presence of cracks (Lin et al., 2024; Zhao and Liu, 2026). Additionally, Fang et al. detected the presence of perforation-induced cracks based on acoustic response for the eligibility judgment of perforated embryo eggs (Fang et al., 2025). Overall, existing studies still mainly rely on 2D information and lack the ability to characterize the spatial geometric features of perforative cracks, not to mention global-local integrated analytical framework, thereby considering merely crack presence and lacking crack acceptability evaluation. This binary eligibility judgment fails to support actual production scenarios.

To address these issues, this paper proposed a global-local 3D quantitative characterization method for the potential acceptability evaluation of perforation-induced cracks on embryo eggs. First, the actual spatial morphology of perforative cracks distributed on the curved surface was obtained globally and locally through 3D reconstruction. Then, a PCA-based global geometric feature characterization method was constructed to extend the traditional dimensionality reduction tool into a 3D point cloud structure characterization tool; furthermore, machine learning was combined to achieve accurate crack shape identification. Afterward, precise quantitative characterization of the main cracks and branch cracks was realized through local point cloud analysis. Finally, a crack shape identification and multi-parameter joint evaluation mechanism was established to achieve precise 3D quantitative characterization of perforative cracks.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows: (1) point clouds of perforation-induced cracks on embryo eggs were generated based on 3D reconstruction, fully retaining the spatial geometric information of the cracks both globally and locally; (2) a global-local integrated characterization framework for perforative cracks with curved-surface characteristics was proposed; (3) a quantitative evaluation methodology system for perforation-induced cracks was constructed, which has the potential to advance the eligibility judgment of perforated embryo eggs from 2D crack presence detection to 3D quantitative crack acceptability evaluation; it is also potentially applicable to multi-virus cultivation scenarios with varying crack acceptability criteria. No biological validation was performed in this study and future work will evaluate the applicability of this global-local 3D quantitative characterization framework for judging perforated embryo egg eligibility through viability, contamination, or virus cultivation outcomes in the actual production scenarios.

Experimental equipment and perforative cracks

Experimental material and equipment

All embryo eggs used in this study were Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) eggs of the White Leghorn strain and were incubated under standard conditions according to the supplier’s recommendations until perforation. Then, perforation was performed by experienced personnel at a professional facility following standard operating procedures to ensure consistency in the perforation site and depth.

This study developed a detecting device for perforative cracks, as shown in Fig. 3. The device fixed the perforated embryo egg on an egg tray within a darkroom, enhanced crack information using a bottom light source, and captured egg images with a depth camera installed at the top. The acquired images were then transmitted to a workstation for data acquisition and subsequent processing. Due to differences in light transmittance between cracked and intact regions, illumination from the bottom light source could create significant contrast in the captured images. Meanwhile, the darkroom environment effectively eliminated external light interference, thereby further enhancing crack information and facilitating crack extraction.Fig. 3Detecting device for perforative cracks on embryo eggs.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Typical perforative cracks

Typical perforative cracks can be classified into 4 categories based on their shape, as shown in Fig. 4:(1)Category Ⅰ (no crack): The global shape is a circular hole with a diameter close to that of the perforating needle, specifically 2.1 mm-3 mm. It requires further quantitative evaluation;(3)Category Ⅲ: The global appearance is a circular-like hole similar to that of Category Ⅱ with a length of more than 4 mm, but it has one or more obvious linear cracks extending outward with a length of more than 2 mm. Crack labeling was performed by 3 laboratory technicians, each with at least 2 years of experience in embryo egg operation. Each technician independently labeled the crack categories for all samples, and for samples with discrepant labeling results, the final label was determined by group discussion and majority voting. If consensus could not be reached, the sample was then excluded. Of the initial 602 independent perforative crack samples, 600 were successfully labeled and 2 were excluded due to irresolvable disagreements, comprising the final dataset. Among the 600 retained samples, 591 were initially labeled identically by all 3 technicians, while 9 required majority voting to resolve disagreements, resulting in a percent agreement of 98.17%. This labeling process ensured reliable, reproducible, and high-quality categories for subsequent quantitative characterization and model training.

3D reconstruction module selection and light source designing

Compared with costly and space-consuming CT scanning and ultrasound imaging, 3D reconstruction based on depth cameras offers advantages in costs, efficiency, and environmental compatibility. This approach could accurately obtain the spatial information of perforative cracks while fulfilling the requirements of large-scale applications (Khoshelham and Elberink, 2012; Kulczyk et al., 2019). Given the tiny scale and complex 3D structure of the target cracks, emphasis must be placed on the depth error and operating range when evaluating factors such as resolution, depth error, real-time performance, and operating range (Foix et al., 2011). The test results revealed that when the Intel RealSense D405 depth camera was installed 14 cm away from the crack at a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels, the crack region ratio in the acquired RGB and depth images was optimal. Meanwhile, both RGB and depth information were accurately retained, yielding favorable performance in subsequent crack reconstruction. The depth error was evaluated using a planar calibration target and the camera’s intrinsic calibration parameters. In the process, depth values measured from the camera were compared with the known reference depth of the calibration target, and the mean depth error across all test points was found to be less than 1%. The depth origin (optical center) for the Realsense D405 is -3.7 mm relative to the front glass (optical surface) and the known reference depth was obtained based on this value and laser ranging. Accordingly, the Intel RealSense D405 depth camera was selected with its parameters shown as follows: Ideal operating range: 7 cm-50 cm; Maximum resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels; Typical depth error: < 0.7% at 25 cm; Maximum output frame rate: 90 fps.

White LED bulbs with a color temperature of 4000K were selected as the bottom light source and equipped with an LED regulator to ensure optimal brightness at the start of detection. Test results indicated that the best performance of the acquired RGB and depth images was achieved when 3 LED bulbs were used with a bottom optical aperture of 28 mm in diameter. Under these conditions, the overall depth information of the embryo egg was well preserved in both quantity and precision, while the crack region in the RGB image was effectively enhanced.

3D reconstruction methods of perforative cracks

Image preprocessing

The experimentally acquired images consisted of an RGB image and a depth image, both saved in PNG format with a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels. To address the issue of abrupt jumps in the depth data, an additional 9 depth images were captured immediately following the initial acquisition. For each pixel, the 10 corresponding depth values were processed by removing the zero value, maximum value, and minimum value; the mean of the remaining values was then assigned as the final depth value for that pixel. After applying this procedure to all pixels, a new depth image was generated and subsequently jointly preprocessed with the original RGB image.

Experimental results showed that the red channel of the captured RGB images provided superior contrast enhancement between the crack and the background compared to the grayscale image. Therefore, the red channel was first extracted from the RGB image, followed by bilateral filtering to reduce noise while preserving the edge features of the crack, with the filtering parameters set as d = 9, sigmaColor = 30, sigmaSpace = 15. The filtered red-channel image was then binarized using Otsu’s thresholding method with an adaptive threshold. Combined with bright spot removal, this process enabled extraction of the contours of both the embryo egg and the crack. On this basis, morphological closing was applied to fill the internal black regions (mask value of 0) within the closed crack contour by converting their mask values from 0 to 1, yielding the RGB masks of the egg and the crack. Subsequently, a logical NOT operation was applied to the crack mask, followed by an AND operation with the egg mask to generate the eggshell mask representing the non-crack region. For the depth image, only pixels with depth values between 5 and 20 cm were retained to produce the depth mask. Finally, AND operations were performed between the depth mask and the crack mask, and between the depth mask and the eggshell mask, respectively, resulting in the final crack mask, eggshell mask, and depth mask, as well as the processed eggshell image, crack image, and depth image, as shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Image preprocessing.Fig 5 dummy alt text

Image preprocessing.

For cracks requiring supplementary local quantitative analysis, particularly Category Ⅲ, this study proposed a morphological-processing-based method to extract main and branch cracks. The method first applied erosion followed by dilation to the RGB crack mask to generate the RGB mask of the main crack. Subsequently, a logical NOT operation was performed on the main crack mask, followed by an AND operation with the global crack mask to obtain the RGB mask of the branch crack, as shown in Fig. 6. By applying these masks to the processed RGB image, the RGB images of the main and branch cracks were produced, respectively. These images were then used to generate the corresponding point clouds of the main and branch cracks, thereby enabling more precise local quantification in the subsequent analysis.Fig. 6Acquisition of the main and branch cracks’ masks based on morphological processing.Fig 6 dummy alt text

3D reconstruction

Camera calibration

The intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of a depth camera are essential for converting pixel coordinates to world coordinates. The intrinsic parameters describe the camera's internal optical and geometric properties, whereas the extrinsic parameters define its position and orientation (Sunvittayakul et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2024).

In this study, the Zhang Zhengyou calibration method was adopted to calibrate the camera's intrinsic parameters (Zhang, 2000). This method utilized images of the same calibration board captured from multiple positions and postures, extracted and matched feature points, and computed the transformation matrix between the image plane and the calibration board. The intrinsic parameters, extrinsic parameters, and distortion parameters were then linearly solved using this transformation matrix. For this single-frame experiment, the extrinsic parameters were set to the identity matrix, assuming minimal impact from camera position and orientation. We acknowledge that for multi-frame setups or metric-level reconstruction, proper geometric calibration of extrinsic parameters would be required to ensure full accuracy.

Fig. 7a illustrates the camera positions and postures relative to the calibration board during calibration. As shown in Fig. 7b, the maximum error was 0.33 pixels and the mean error was 0.20 pixels, satisfying the accuracy requirement of less than 0.5 pixels.Fig. 7Calibration of the depth camera.Fig 7 dummy alt text

Point cloud generation

In this experiment, the 3D information of the perforative crack obtained through 3D reconstruction was stored as point cloud data for subsequent quantitative characterization analysis. Following the acquisition of the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters, and the depth information of a single RGB image, the transformation from pixel coordinates to world coordinates was accomplished using the coordinate system conversion relationships in the camera imaging model (Wang et al., 2024):(1)PW=T−1K−1Z(uv1)where PW denotes the world coordinate of the pixel point, T denotes the camera's extrinsic parameter matrix, K denotes the camera's intrinsic parameter matrix, u and v denote the pixel coordinate, and Z denotes the depth value corresponding to the pixel coordinate.

The intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of the camera were employed to convert the depth image into a 3D point cloud. By applying different RGB masks for filtering, the point clouds of the eggshell, crack, main crack, and branch crack were obtained respectively. All resulting point clouds were non-closed 3D models, as shown in Fig. 8. (Hartley and Zisserman, 2004).Fig. 8Original point clouds.Fig 8 dummy alt text

Point cloud processing

Due to reconstruction errors and crack depressions in the reconstructed point cloud, the original point cloud that required further quantitative analysis couldn’t accurately represent the curved surface features of the eggshell, and such distortion would adversely affect subsequent quantitative characterization. To address these issues, this study employed point cloud optimization and curved surface fitting to process the original point cloud, thereby more accurately reflecting the actual geometric characteristics of the target crack.

During point cloud optimization, only points with z-coordinates, namely depth-values, between 95 and 110 mm were retained. This range was selected based on multiple visual inspections of reconstructed point clouds to ensure that point clouds of both the crack region and surrounding non-crack region were retained while excluding partial noise points and distant discrete point clusters. The rough screening based on depth filtering could preliminarily reduce noise and ensure high-quality point clouds for further optimization. Subsequently, the statistical outlier removal algorithm was applied for noise reduction. This algorithm calculated the mean Euclidean distance from each point to its 20 nearest neighbors, established a threshold based on the global distance distribution and standard deviation, and removed points exceeding this threshold as outliers. The neighborhood size was empirically determined based on repeated visual inspection to balance effective noise removal and preservation of local crack geometry: smaller neighborhoods were more sensitive to local fluctuations, whereas larger neighborhoods tended to oversmooth the point cloud structure. After processing, isolated noise points were effectively eliminated, resulting in a smoother and cleaner overall surface. Through depth filtering and noise reduction, most edge points and abnormal points were effectively removed, thereby improving point cloud quality and providing a reliable foundation for subsequent surface fitting.

Since the crack point cloud represented a depressed region and lacked complete curved-surface features, this study performed surface fitting on the non-crack eggshell point cloud to obtain the surface function of the embryo egg, which could retain its spatial curved-surface characteristics (Amiri-Simkooei et al., 2025). The quadratic polynomial surface fitting was selected because each (x, y) coordinate corresponded to a unique z-value in the single-frame reconstruction, and the eggshell formed a smooth curved surface. Subsequently, based on this fitted surface function, the z-coordinates of all points in the crack, main crack, and branch crack point clouds were adjusted to generate new point clouds that more closely matched the actual curved-surface morphology of the eggshell, as shown in Fig. 9. These fitted point clouds were then used for the subsequent quantitative characterization analysis.Fig. 9Processed point clouds.Fig 9 dummy alt text

Global quantitative characterization based on PCA

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a linear dimensionality reduction algorithm widely employed to analyze the distribution features of point cloud data. It extracts the principal features from high-dimensional data and projects them into a lower-dimensional space. The objective is to identify the optimal projection directions that maximize the variance of the projected data, thereby preserving the maximum amount of information, while ensuring that these projection directions are mutually orthogonal to eliminate redundant information. The specific procedure is as follows:

1. Calculating the centroid of the point cloud. The centroid represents the average position of all points in the point cloud and is calculated as follows:(2)μ=1n∑i=1npi=(x¯,y¯,z¯)where x¯=1n∑i=1nxi, y¯=1n∑i=1nyi, z¯=1n∑i=1nzi, μ denotes the centroid, n denotes the number of points, and pi denotes the coordinate of the point i.

2. Calculating the covariance matrix of the point cloud. The covariance matrix Cov describes the distribution features and inter-dimensional correlations of the point cloud data. The formula is as follows:(3)Cov=1n∑i=1n(pi−μ)(pi−μ)Twhere (pi−μ) represents the vector from the point to the centroid.

The covariance matrix Cov itself is a 3 × 3 symmetric matrix reflecting the variance and covariance of the point cloud in the X, Y, and Z dimensions (Duan et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2025).

3. Decomposing the eigenvalues. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors are obtained through the eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix:(4)Cov·vi=λi·viwhere vi (i = 1, 2, 3) represents the eigenvector i of the covariance matrix, namely the direction of the principal component, and λi (i = 1, 2, 3) represents the corresponding eigenvalue, namely the variance of the principal component. The eigenvectors and eigenvalues are sorted and named according to the eigenvalues in descending order, satisfying λ1≥λ2≥λ3, and the eigenvector v1, corresponding to the largest eigenvalue λ1, represents the first principal direction of the point cloud, and likewise.

Based on the eigenvalues of the point cloud, the following feature parameters can be calculated to characterize the geometric features of the global crack point cloud:

(1) Trace Tr. Trace represents the total variation of the point cloud. A high trace value indicates a widely distributed point cloud with large variation, while a low value indicates a compact structure.(5)Tr=∑i=13λi

(2) Linearity L. Linearity measures the extent of the point cloud's extension along a single direction, with a range of [0,1]. High linearity indicates that the point cloud exhibits a linear structure.(6)L=λ1−λ2λ1

(3) Planarity P. Planarity measures the extent of the point cloud's distribution on a 2D plane, with a range of [0,1]. High planarity indicates that the point cloud exhibits a planar structure.(7)P=λ2−λ3λ1

(4) Sphericity S. Sphericity measures the extent of the point cloud's uniform distribution in 3D space, with a range of [0,1]. High sphericity indicates that the point cloud exhibits a spherical or scattered structure.(8)S=λ3λ1

(5) Omnivariance O. Omnivariance represents the overall variability and geometric complexity of the point cloud. A high value indicates a complex point cloud distribution with multi-directional variation, while a low value indicates a simple structure.(9)O=(λ1·λ2·λ3)13

(6) Anisotropy A. Anisotropy measures the directional differences in the point cloud, with a range of [0,1]. High anisotropy indicates significant variation in certain directions, while low anisotropy indicates isotropy.(10)A=λ1−λ3λ1

(7) Eigenentropy E. Eigenentropy can measure the information content and complexity of the point cloud. High eigenentropy indicates strong uncertainty in the point cloud structure, while low eigenentropy indicates an ordered structure.(11)E=−∑i=13piln(pi)where λi˜=λiλ1+λ2+λ3,i=1,2,3.

The global feature parameters obtained by global crack quantitative characterization would serve as the input for the subsequent crack shape identification. To optimize the input for the subsequent machine learning multi-classification model, this study employed the Random Forest (RF) algorithm to calculate feature importance before the model training, in order to pick out the quantitative parameters that contribute the most to distinguishing crack categories. The specific process is as follows: First, 10 PCA features (λ1, λ2, λ3, L, P, S, O, A, E, Tr) were extracted from all crack samples in the training set respectively; Then, preprocessing was performed, including removing zero values and standardization; Finally, using the RF classifier, with the number of estimators set to 100, training was conducted on the training set, and the average information gain contribution of each feature was calculated. This study selected Gini importance as the evaluation criterion, with its calculation formula as follows:(12)Imp(f)=1T∑t=1T∑node∈t|Snode|NΔGini(f,node)where, ΔGini is the split gain of feature f at the node. This score reflects the contribution of the feature in reducing node impurity.

Crack shape identification based on RF

Considering the nonlinear feature interactions between the global characterization parameters, this study employed the Random Forest algorithm to construct a crack shape identification model, which achieves multi-classification by building multiple decision trees and making decisions through voting. It excels in handling noisy data, and could enhance generalization through tree diversity, which is suitable for the structural features of the sample data in this study (Breiman, 2001). The computational process is as follows:

Assuming for the classification task, the dataset is D={(xi,yi)}i=1N, where xi is a p-dimensional feature vector, yi is the label (with K classes), the number of trees in the forest is T, the size of the random feature subset at each tree node split is m (usually m=p), and the maximum depth is d.(1)Data preparation and preprocessing. Before computation, the data is standardized to unify the scales, while class imbalance is addressed through methods such as oversampling.(2)Bootstrap sampling to generate sub-datasets. Through sampling with replacement from the original dataset D, T sub-datasets Dt(t=1...T) are generated, each Dt still of size N, reducing overfitting by introducing diversity. Local quantitative characterization

After inoculation, the perforation region is typically sealed to reduce external contamination. In this study, we focus on the quantitative characterization of local cracks, which can be used to evaluate crack propagation and local geometric features. After shape identification of the perforative cracks, Category Ⅰ cracks could be directly judged as qualified, whereas Category Ⅳ cracks could be directly judged as unqualified. However, the acceptability evaluation of Category Ⅱ and Category Ⅲ cracks requires further quantitative analysis, which primarily relies on local characterization. Since Category Ⅱ cracks and the main cracks of Category Ⅲ cracks exhibit similar geometric features, they are collectively referred to as main cracks in the subsequent analysis. Accordingly, the local quantitative evaluation of these two categories is performed through comprehensive evaluation of both the main cracks and the branch cracks.

Main crack quantitative characterization

To achieve 3D quantitative characterization of main cracks’ geometric features, this study established a quantitative characterization model for main cracks based on PCA and surface integration. The specific process is as follows:

(1) After obtaining the principal component eigenvalues λ1,λ2,λ3 and corresponding eigenvectors v1,v2,v3 through the PCA method, a rotation matrix R was constructed. This matrix can transform the point cloud from the original coordinate system to a new coordinate system, whose axes align with the principal axis directions of the point cloud.(21)R=(v1,v2,v3)where v1,v2,v3 denote the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The point cloud was then rotated based on Equation (22):(22)p′i=R·(pi−μ)where pi denotes the original point cloud data, μ denotes the centroid, and pi′ denotes the point cloud data after the rotation.

(2) In the new coordinate system, the point cloud was projected onto the xoy plane. The differences between the maximum and minimum values of the projected point set along the x and y directions were extracted and defined as the longer side Ra and shorter side Rb of the bounding rectangle, respectively, and the minimum bounding rectangle of the projected point set was constructed. As shown in Fig. 14, the generated bounding rectangle provided a good fit for the projection of the PCA-rotated main crack point cloud. Tests on 100 representative main cracks, confirmed by visual inspection, demonstrated that this method could effectively fit the point cloud projections across all representative samples. Therefore, the longer side Ra and shorter side Rb of the bounding rectangle could be used as feature parameters for the quantitative characterization of main cracks to evaluate the crack propagation and local geometric features.Fig. 14Main crack bounding rectangle.Fig 14 dummy alt text

Branch crack instance segmentation and sub-crack number

Since a branch crack may consist of one or more sub-cracks, as shown in Fig. 15, this study employed Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) to perform clustering analysis on the branch crack point cloud.Fig. 15Different types of branch cracks.Fig 15 dummy alt text

Different types of branch cracks.

This approach enabled instance segmentation of the target point cloud, thereby extracting both the number and individual point clouds of the sub-cracks. DBSCAN automatically identifies clusters based on local density features of the sample points without requiring a predefined cluster number. It also exhibits strong robustness to noise and can detect non-convex structures, making it well-suited for identifying branch cracks with complex topological configurations. The specific procedure is as follows:

Firstly, DBSCAN clustering was applied to the branch crack point cloud using Euclidean distance to identify spatially continuous point sets with locally high density. The clustering parameters were set as ε = 1.0 and min_samples = 10, which were empirically determined based on the local point density of the branch cracks. These settings allowed robust identification of sub-crack instances, even in the presence of noise and complex branch structures. Each sub-crack instance was then isolated according to its clustering label (e.g., Sub-crack 1, Sub-crack 2, etc.) and saved as a separate point cloud file to facilitate subsequent geometric feature extraction and quantitative characterization. This method demonstrated strong adaptability when handling the coexistence of multiple sub-cracks and branch extensions. It not only determined the number of sub-cracks but also provided essential data support for the subsequent sub-crack quantitative analysis.

The length of sub-cracks

The branch crack represents the most critical factor in the eligibility judgment of embryo eggs with Category Ⅲ cracks. In addition to the number of sub-cracks, the length of sub-cracks serves as an even more decisive indicator for eligibility judgment. Conventional crack length calculation based on 2D images typically relies on the minimum bounding rectangle of the crack, approximating the longer side of the rectangle as the crack length using the camera scale. However, the “pixel coordinate-world coordinate” transformation based on the camera scale suffers from limited accuracy. Moreover, since perforative cracks are distributed on a curved surface, the dimensionality reduction that substitutes the curved surface with a 2D plane inevitably results in a measured length shorter than the actual value. Such underestimation may lead to misjudgment of unqualified cracked embryo eggs as qualified. To overcome the lack of local analysis and insufficient accuracy inherent in conventional methods, this study proposed a 3D calculation method for sub-crack length based on sub-crack extraction.

Firstly, PCA was carried out on the extracted sub-crack point cloud to obtain the first principal axis direction v1, the second principal axis direction v2, and the third principal axis direction v3. Then, the point cloud was projected onto the plane defined by v1 and v2, and, using the edge points as start and end points for fitting, a continuous curve segment was generated through quadratic curve fitting, whose length was calculated as the sub-crack length. Since some sub-cracks exhibited large variance in the width direction as shown in Fig. 17a, 17b, during the calculation of their spatial length, the point cloud needed to be projected onto the plane defined by v1 and v3.Fig. 17Different types of sub-cracks and 3D crack length calculation.Fig 17 dummy alt text

Different types of sub-cracks and 3D crack length calculation.

However, only when the point cloud of the sub-crack was correctly projected, namely spatial projection, could its curved-surface characteristic be fully revealed, as shown in Fig. 17c. Otherwise, it would become planar projection, suffering from information loss, as shown in Fig. 17d. To solve this issue, this paper proposed an RMSE-based projection method for sub-crack point cloud evaluation. Since the spatial projection preserved the curved-surface characteristics of the sub-crack, the set of projected points aligned more closely with the fitted curve compared to the planar projection. To quantify this alignment, the deviation between each projected point and its corresponding fitted curve value was computed, and the Root-Mean-Square error (RMSE) was calculated for both projections. The spatial and planar projections were independently annotated by 3 experienced laboratory technicians with discrepancies among annotators resolved by group discussion and majority voting. As shown in Table 5 and Fig. 17e, across 50 representative sub-crack samples, the spatial projection yielded an RMSE of 0.02487 ± 0.02075 mm, whereas the planar projection produced an RMSE of 0.12009 ± 0.03478 mm. A paired t-test confirmed that the RMSE difference (Spatial Projection minus Planar Projection) was statistically significant (t(49) = -16.173, p = 2.767 × 10-21), with a mean difference of -0.09523 ± 0.04163 mm and a 95% confidence interval of [-0.10706, -0.08339] mm. Thus far, the potential optimization for eligibility judgment of perforated embryo eggs based on this study has been formally established: First, crack shape identification is performed through the 7 global quantitative parameters obtained from PCA-based global quantitative characterization to pick out clearly qualified and absolutely unqualified eggs while screening out potentially qualified cracked eggs that require further evaluation. Then, based on local quantitative characterization, high-precision local quantitative parameters are provided to support the final eligibility judgment of these potentially qualified eggs, according to the corresponding thresholds. These thresholds are determined by technicians based on the virus’s sensitivity to the crack morphology, and samples with local parameters exceeding these thresholds would be judged as unqualified and therefore rejected. This workflow is designed to be consistent with traditional expert-based inspection, providing quantitative support while aligning with current operational practices, and could enable more objective and precise quantitative evaluation of perforated embryo eggs. Future work will evaluate the applicability of this global-local 3D quantitative characterization framework for eligibility judging of perforated embryo eggs in the real-world production scenarios.

Conclusion

This paper proposed a global-local 3D quantitative characterization method for the potential acceptability evaluation of perforation-induced cracks on embryo eggs. Through 3D reconstruction, point cloud data of perforative cracks were obtained, fully preserving the spatial curved-surface characteristics of the cracks. Combined with PCA, a global geometric feature parameter system was constructed, achieving effective global quantitative characterization of the crack morphology. On this basis, a crack shape identification model based on the random forest algorithm was built, achieving high-precision identification of 4 typical crack shape categories. The 50 repeated cross-validation experiments yielded a mean accuracy of 99.19% (standard deviation 0.87%), and a mean macro F1-Score of 98.91% (standard deviation 1.19%); meanwhile the external validation achieved an accuracy of 99.17% and a macro F1-Score of 99.06%. Furthermore, through local point cloud analysis and spatial curve fitting, precise local quantitative characterization of perforation-induced cracks was realized, providing quantitative parameter support for cracks requiring further analysis.

Compared with traditional 2D methods, the proposed method in this paper has the following advantages in the perforative crack analysis: First, through 3D reconstruction, the actual spatial morphology of perforative cracks on the curved surface was completely preserved, avoiding the geometric distortion in 2D methods. Second, the feature parameter system constructed based on PCA could characterize the spatial distribution characteristics of perforative cracks from a statistical perspective, achieving effective characterization of tiny complex crack structures. Third, the proposed global-local analysis framework took into account both accurate global shape identification and precise local crack characterization, improving the comprehensiveness and precision of perforative crack evaluation. In addition, the method possesses high computational efficiency and can meet the real-time requirements of potential actual industrial detection. Finally, this study can also provide methodological reference for crack quantitative analysis in other thin-shell curved-surface structures.

From a theoretical perspective, this paper constructs a global-local characterization model for spatial curved-surface crack analysis. It combined statistical geometric features with local morphological characterization under curved-surface constraints, thereby providing a generalized methodological framework for quantitative modeling of complex spatial curved-surface cracks.

From an application perspective, the method proposed in this paper has the potential to realize the transition of perforated egg eligibility judgment from 'whether the crack exists' to 'whether the crack is acceptable,' thereby compensating for the deficiencies of existing methods in crack acceptability evaluation. The proposed new framework of 'crack shape identification based on global crack characterization + local crack characterization + crack acceptability evaluation' provides a potential novel technical pathway for automated screening and quality control of perforated embryo eggs and could offer parameter support for diversified crack acceptability evaluation criteria under different virus cultivation conditions. In terms of potential application in industrial vaccine production systems, the proposed method, combined with multi-station parallel imaging and linear scanning configuration, could achieve real-time screening of perforated embryo eggs in high-throughput scenarios.

Future work will further expand the data scale and improve the robustness and generalization ability of the model under different eggshell varieties (of different poultry species, shell colors, or shell textures) and conduct biological validation.
