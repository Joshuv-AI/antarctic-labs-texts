Differentiating medullary thyroid carcinoma from papillary thyroid carcinoma using ultrasound viscosity imaging: a preliminary study
Authors: Qianru Zhang, Wei Zhou, Shangyan Xu, Siqi Hua, Ronghui Wang, Zhifang Yang, Jianqiao Zhou, Lili Gao, Weiwei Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the clinical feasibility of ultrasound viscosity imaging in the differential diagnosis of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), aiming to provide more precise diagnostic tools and to support optimized treatment decisions and personalized clinical management in clinical practice.MethodsThe study included patients diagnosed with MTC and PTC (papillary thyroid carcinoma) at Shanghai Ruijin Hospital between March 2024 and December 2024. Quantitative analysis was performed for ultrasound viscosity imaging of thyroid carcinoma. Statistically significant indicators were screened and their diagnostic performance was evaluated.ResultsA total of 35 MTC patients with 35 MTC nodules and 45 PTC patients with 45 PTC nodules were finally enrolled. Various ultrasound viscosity imaging features demonstrated statistically significant value for differentiating MTC. Among them, S1-Vsd (viscosity standard deviation under the Visco mode with Shell = 1.0 mm) exhibited the best diagnostic performance with an AUC (area under the curve) of 0.70, which is superior to conventional ultrasound ACR-TIRADS (AUC = 0.63).ConclusionUltrasound viscosity imaging shows promising potential in enhancing the diagnostic accuracy of thyroid carcinoma, providing valuable support in differentiating MTC from PTC.

Introduction

As the first-line imaging modality for evaluating thyroid disease, ultrasound has been widely used in clinical practice [1, 2]. With the growing use of ultrasound screening, the detection rate of thyroid carcinoma has been increasing over recent years [3, 4]. Among the various subtypes, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) are the most common. Despite notable differences in their treatment strategies and prognosis outcomes [5, 6], they exhibit certain overlapping features on conventional ultrasound, sometimes making differential diagnosis challenging and potentially leading to misdiagnosis [7, 8]. This diagnostic limitation underscores the critical need for exploring novel approaches to improve subtype differentiation.

In recent years, ultrasound elastography has developed rapidly, emerging as a valuable tool for both qualitative and quantitative assessment of the stiffness in lesions and surrounding tissues [9, 10]. Among them, shear wave elastography which is based on the assumption that the tissue is linear, elastic, and homogeneous, estimates the Young’s modulus by analyzing the propagation of shear waves within soft tissues [11, 12]. However, biological tissues exhibit not only elastic but also viscous properties, and the speed of wave propagation varies with frequency [13]. As such, shear wave elastography alone may not fully capture the complex mechanical behavior of tissues. To address this limitation, viscosity imaging techniques have been introduced, offering a more comprehensive evaluation of tissue mechanics. Previous studies have demonstrated that viscosity imaging can aid in the characterization of breast lesions and support the diagnosis of breast cancer [14]. In addition, viscosity imaging has also been shown to have significant clinical value in various studies, including the assessment of lobular inflammation, steatosis, and fibrosis in patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [15, 16] as well as the evaluation of renal transplant status [17].

To the best of our knowledge, no studies to date have investigated the application of Ultrasound Viscosity imaging in differentiating MTC from PTC. To address this knowledge gap, this study aims to explore the feasibility and clinical value of a novel ultrasound technique in thyroid carcinoma differentiation. By applying this emerging technology, we anticipate establishing a novel complementary quantitative assessment method for thyroid cancer classification, thereby optimizing therapeutic decision-making and personalized clinical management in thyroid cancer patients.

Methods and materials

Patient selection

This study enrolled thyroid carcinoma patients who underwent ultrasound examinations at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine between March 2024 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) no previous thyroid surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; (2) cytopathological and histopathological confirmation of diagnosis (MTC for the study group, PTC for controls); (3) visible thyroid nodules on ultrasound images; (4) only the largest nodule was included for patients with multiple nodules (with at least two of which confirmed as either MTC or PTC). The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with prior treatment; (2) patients without pathological confirmation (Fig. 1). The final cohort included 35 MTC patients (35 MTC nodules) and 45 PTC patients (45 PTC nodules). The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients prior to their inclusion in the study.

Ultrasound examination

All ultrasound examinations were performed by a single ultrasound radiologist with over 40 years of experience in thyroid ultrasound imaging. The operator was blinded to the pathological results. These results were known only to the investigator responsible for patient selection, without involvement in the ultrasound procedures. The ultrasound device used was the Mindray Resona A20 (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). During ultrasound viscosity imaging, patients are instructed to hold their breath while the probe is kept stable with minimal pressure. Once the viscosity imaging mode is activated, both the grayscale ultrasound and the corresponding viscosity image are simultaneously displayed on the screen, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Viscosity imaging evaluates biological tissue mechanical properties by analyzing the frequency dependence of shear wave velocity, based on the Voigt model and linear dispersion model. Ultrasound images analysis

Two ultrasound radiologists with over 10 years of experience in thyroid ultrasound imaging independently evaluated the following ultrasound features of thyroid nodules: tumor location, size, shape, margin, composition, echogenicity, microcalcification, macrocalcification, and blood flow signal. In cases where their assessments differed for any feature, the senior ultrasound radiologist reviewed the findings and provided the final decision.

All ultrasound viscosity imaging data were analyzed using built-in quantitative analysis function of the Resona A20 ultrasound device. Regions of interest (ROI) were segmented to extract viscosity feature values in both the Visco and Disper modes. For the Voigt model-based analysis, the extracted parameters included the mean value (Vmean), maximum value (Vmax) and standard deviation (Vsd). The dispersion parameters included the mean value (Dmean), maximum value (Dmax) and standard deviation (Dsd). In addition, viscosity evaluations of the perilesional region were obtained using the system’s “Shell” function, with Shell = 1.0 mm in this study. The corresponding parameters included S1-Vmean, S1-Vmax, S1-Vsd and A′-S1-Vmean, A′-S1-Vmax, A′-S1-Vsd. To be specific, S1 represents parameters measured within the shell region only, while A′-S1 meant the values calculated for the combined area of the lesion and its corresponding shell. Figure 3 illustrates an example of ROI segmentation and Shell regulation in thyroid carcinoma.

Intra-observer agreement was determined through repeated measurements by one ultrasound radiologist without prior knowledge of previous ultrasound or pathological results at two different time points (with an interval of at least 4 weeks), while inter-observer agreement was evaluated by second ultrasound radiologist. Both intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients were calculated to determine consistency, with value ≥ 0.75 indicating good agreement for reliability.

Statistical analysis

In this study, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed with independent t-tests to determine statistical significance. For continuous variables violating normality assumptions, median and interquartile range were reported, with between-group comparisons performed using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and compared using either chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and R software (Version 4.4.2; https://www.r-project.org/). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and ultrasound features of medullary and papillary thyroid carcinoma

This study enrolled 35 medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) patients with 35 MTC nodules and 45 papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) patients with 45 PTC nodules. The detailed clinical and ultrasound features are presented in Table 1.

Regarding the clinical features of thyroid carcinoma patients, a significant difference in age distribution was observed between patients with MTC and those with PTC (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was found in sex (p = 0.538). In terms of ultrasound features, significant differences were observed between MTC and PTC nodules in the following aspects: tumor location (p = 0.020), tumor shape (p = 0.016), margin (p < 0.001), composition (p = 0.047), microcalcification (p = 0.007), and blood flow signal (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found in tumor size (p = 0.330), echogenicity (p = 0.849), or macrocalcification (p = 0.884).

According to the Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System published by the American College of Radiology (ACR-TIRADS), among 35 MTC nodules, 1 was classified as TR3 (mildly suspicious), 11 as TR4 (moderately suspicious), and 23 as TR5 (highly suspicious). Among 45 PTC nodules, 4 were classified as TR4 (moderately suspicious) and 41 as TR5 (highly suspicious).

Discussion

Biological tissues cannot be considered purely linear elastic or homogeneous materials, which implies that waves of different frequencies propagate through the tissue at varying speeds [13]. Shear wave elastography is based on the assumption that the tissues are linearly elastic and homogeneous, which does not fully reflect the actual conditions and lead to inevitable estimation bias [13, 20]. Therefore, Viscosity imaging may provide a more comprehensive representation of the intrinsic features of tissues. However, clinical studies on viscosity imaging remain very limited, and to date, no studies have applied this technique for the differential diagnosis of MTC and PTC. This study aims to quantitatively evaluate the viscosity parameters to provide valuable support in distinguishing between these two types.

There are different modes for assessing viscous properties of biological tissues using ultrasound viscosity imaging, including Visco mode and Disper mode [14]. In our study, significant differences were observed in S1-Vmean, S1-Vmax, S1-Vsd, S1 Vmean/A Vmean, S1 Vmax/A Vmax, S1 Vsd/A Vsd, Dmean, Dmax, Dsd, S1-Dmean, S1-Dmax, A’-Dmean, A’-Dmax, A’-Dsd, S1 Dsd/A Dsd (p < 0.05). The AUC values of these parameters ranged from 0.63 to 0.70. These findings suggest that viscosity features derived from both Visco and Disper mode may provide valuable information, demonstrating comparable diagnostic performance in differentiating thyroid malignancies. These parameters collectively reflect the viscosity heterogeneity of thyroid tissues. The consistent performance across multiple viscosity features further supports the stability and reliability of viscosity imaging as a quantitative evaluation tool, implying that its diagnostic value is based on an overall biomechanical pattern.

Among them, S1-Vsd (viscosity standard deviation under the Visco mode with Shell = 1.0 mm) exhibits optimal predictive performance, which may be attributed to Visco mode being based on the Voigt model. This model utilizes more sophisticated equations that incorporate various parameters such as tissue density, viscosity and elasticity, thereby providing a more comprehensive representation of velocity changes across different frequencies [21]. Moreover, the AUC of S1-Vsd is 0.70, which is superior to conventional ultrasound ACR-TIRADS (AUC = 0.63), indicating its potential as a promising adjunctive diagnostic marker for distinguishing MTC from PTC.

It is worth noting that most viscosity parameters of MTC are lower than those of PTC, which may be attributed to their different cellular origins. MTC arises from parafollicular cells, whereas PTC originates from follicular epithelial cells. The relatively loose tissue structure of MTC, along with reduced intercellular stroma and potential amyloid deposition [6, 22], may contribute to the differences in viscosity properties between MTC and PTC.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As a preliminary study, the sample size is relatively small, which necessitates larger-scale validation to further establish definitive conclusions. In addition, ultrasound viscosity imaging is inherently operator-dependent, which may affect the reproducibility and generalizability of the results.

Despite these limitations, our study still presents several innovations and strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply ultrasound viscosity imaging for the differential diagnosis between MTC and PTC. Besides, viscosity imaging provides supplementary information on the mechanical properties of biological tissues and our preliminary findings indicate that it holds promising potential in distinguishing MTC from PTC, serving as an auxiliary method for clinicians to help reduce misdiagnosis of thyroid cancer.

Conclusion

In conclusion, various ultrasound viscosity imaging features demonstrated statistical significance in the differential diagnosis of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). S1-Vsd (viscosity standard deviation under the Visco mode with Shell = 1.0 mm) exhibited the optimal diagnostic performance. Ultrasound viscosity imaging holds potential value in improving the diagnostic accuracy of thyroid carcinoma and providing robust support for more personalized treatment and management plans for patients.
