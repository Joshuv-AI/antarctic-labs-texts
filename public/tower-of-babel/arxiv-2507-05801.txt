Zhe Wang, Guowei Yu

Abstract
. In the planar n n -body problem, the problem of infinite spin occurs for both parabolic and collision solutions. Recently Moeckel and Montgomery [ 14 ] showed that there is no infinite spin for total collision solutions. Following their approach, we show it can not happen for both complete and partially parabolic solutions. Our approach also allows us to generalize Moeckel and Montgomery’s result to partial collision solutions.



1.
Introduction

Consider the planar
n
-body problem with masses
m_{i}>0
, positions
q_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for each
i\in\mathbf{n}

=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. The motion is governed by Newton’s gravitational law

(1.1)

m_{i}\ddot{q_{i}}=\nabla_{i}U(q),\;\forall i\in\mathbf{n},

where
q=(q_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and

U(q)=\underset{i<j}{\sum}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}},\;r_{ij}=|q_{i}-q_{j}|.

Due to translation symmetry, we may always fix the center of mass at origin, i.e.,

q\in\mathbb{E}:=\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:\sum_{i\in\mathbf{n}}m_{i}q_{i}=0\}.

One of the most important aspects of the
n
-body problem is to understand the final motion of the masses as time goes to infinity. The fundamental work was done by Chazy
[
5
]
, where he gave a complete classification of the final motion for the three body problem. For general
n
, it was studied by Pollard
[
15
]
and Saari
[
16
]
. In particular Marchal and Saari obtained the following result in
[
11
, Corollary 1]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given a solution
q(t)
of (
1.1
) with

R(t)=\max_{i\neq j}r_{ij}(t),\;r(t)=\min_{i\neq j}r_{ij}(t)

then as
t\to\infty
, either
R(t)/t\to\infty
, or

q_{i}(t)=A_{i}t+O(t^{\frac{2}{3}}),\;i\in\mathbf{n}\text{ and }\limsup_{t\to\infty}r(t)>0

where each
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a constant vector.

The above result shows that when
R(t)=O(t)
, as
t\to\infty
, the entire system separates into subsystems, such that the distance between any two masses inside each subsystem is bounded by
O(t^{\frac{2}{3}})
, while the distance between any two masses belong to different subsystem grows linearly as
t
. This shows that a critical case is when the distance between two masses grows in the order of
t^{\frac{2}{3}}
. Such a critical case is called
parabolic
, after parabolic solutions from the Kepler problem.

Motivated by this, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

Given a
\mathbf{k}\subset\mathbf{n}
with
|\mathbf{k}|\geq 2
, we say a solution
q:(t_{0},\infty)\to\mathbb{E}
is
\mathbf{k}
-parabolic, if there exist positive constants
C_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, such that when
t\to\infty
,

\begin{cases}C_{1}t^{\frac{2}{3}}\leq r_{ij}(t)\leq C_{2}t^{\frac{2}{3}},&\;\forall i\neq j\in\mathbf{k};\\
r_{ij}(t)\geq C_{3}t,&\;\forall i\in\mathbf{k},\forall j\in\mathbf{k}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{n}\setminus\mathbf{k}.\end{cases}

Remark 1.3
.

For any subset
\mathbf{k}
of indices,
|\mathbf{k}|
denotes its cardinality. A
\mathbf{k}
-parabolic solution is partially parabolic, if
|\mathbf{k}|<n
, and complete parabolic, if
|\mathbf{k}|=n
. Notice that there may be more than one subsystem, such that inside it the motion is parabolic.

Remark 1.4
.

In the above definition, we do not assume
R(t)=O(t)
, as
t\to\infty
.

If the motion of a subsystem is parabolic, the normalized configuration of masses in the subsystem must approach the set of central configuration. To explain this in details, we introduce the following notations.

Given a subset
\mathbf{k}\subset\mathbf{n}
, we denote the center of mass of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem as

c_{\mathbf{k}}=m^{-1}_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i\in\mathbf{k}}m_{i}q_{i},\text{ where }m_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{k}}m_{i},

the relative configuration of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem with respect to
c_{\mathbf{k}}
as

q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}=(q_{i}-c_{\mathbf{k}})_{i\in\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{k}}:=\{(q_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2|\mathbf{k}|}:\sum_{i\in\mathbf{k}}m_{i}q_{i}=0\},

the moment of inertial of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem with respect to
c_{\mathbf{k}}
as

I_{\mathbf{k}}(q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{k}}m_{i}|q_{i}-c_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2},

and the normalized relative configuration with respect to
c_{\mathbf{k}}
as

\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}=q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}/\sqrt{I_{\mathbf{k}}(q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}})}\in S_{\mathbf{k}},

where

S_{\mathbf{k}}:=\{q_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{k}}:I_{\mathbf{k}}(q_{\mathbf{k}})=1\}.

The energy of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem is defined as

h_{\mathbf{k}}=K_{\mathbf{k}}(q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}})-U_{\mathbf{k}}(q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}):=\underset{i\in\mathbf{k}}{\sum}\frac{m_{i}}{2}|\dot{q_{i}}-\dot{c}_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}-\underset{i,j\in\mathbf{k},i<j}{\sum}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}}.

The potential function between the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem and the rest of the masses is denoted by

U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}=U-U_{\mathbf{k}}-U_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}=\underset{i\in\mathbf{k},j\in\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}{\sum}\frac{m_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}}.

Definition 1.5
.

q_{\mathbf{k}}=(q_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{k}}
is a central configuration or CC of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem, if there is a constant
\lambda>0
, such that

\nabla_{i}U_{\mathbf{k}}(q_{\mathbf{k}})+\lambda m_{i}(q_{i})=0,\;\forall i\in\mathbf{k}.

We denote the set of all CCs of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem by
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{k}}
and the subset of normalized CCs as

\hat{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{k}}\cap S_{\mathbf{k}}.

Remark 1.6
.

\hat{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{k}}
is precisely the set of critical points of partial potential
U_{\mathbf{k}}
restricted on
S_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Proposition 1.7
.

If
q(t)
is a
\mathbf{k}
-parabolic solution, when
t\to\infty
,

(a).

|h_{\mathbf{k}}(t)|=O(t^{-5/3})
;

(b).

the normalized relative configuration
\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
must approach
\hat{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Remark 1.8
.

Under the assumption
R(t)=O(t)
, as
t\to\infty
, this was already proven in
[
16
]
and
[
11
]
. Here as we do not make such an assumption, a proof will be given in Section
6
.

Let
\omega(\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t))
denote the
\omega
-limit set of
\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
. Proposition
1.7
implies

\omega(\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t))\subset\hat{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{k}}.

Then naturally we can ask the following question:

Does the
\omega
-limit set contain a point, or equivalent does
\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
converge to a particular CC, as
t\to\infty
?

Due to the rotational symmetry, if
q_{\mathbf{k}}\in\hat{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{k}}
, so is
R(\theta)q_{\mathbf{k}}=(R(\theta)q_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{k}}
, for any rotation
R(\theta)\in SO(2)
. Consider the
(2|\mathbf{k}|-4)
-dim quotient manifold
S_{\mathbf{k}}/SO(2)\simeq\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|-2)
. We write the quotient map

S_{\mathbf{k}}\to\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|-2);\;q_{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto[q_{\mathbf{k}}].

Let the function on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|-2)
induced by
U_{\mathbf{k}}
be
[q_{\mathbf{k}}]\mapsto U_{\mathbf{k}}([q_{\mathbf{k}}])
. We say
q_{\mathbf{k}}
is an isolated (resp. nondegenerate or degenerate) CC, if
[q_{\mathbf{k}}]
is an isolated (resp. nondegenerate or degenerate) critical point of
U([q_{\mathbf{k}}])
.

Following the above notation, if we assume
[\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)]
converges to a single isolated CC, as
t\to\infty
, the
\omega
-limit set of
q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
could be a subset of the circle generated by the corresponding CC. In particular it may contain the entire circle as
q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
could go around infinitely many times. This is known as the
infinite spin problem
(see
[
21
]
).

To justify the assumption that
[\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)]
converges to an isolated CC. We recall the following well-known conjecture for CC, which is listed by Smale
[
19
]
as problem 6 in his list of problems for the 21st century :

For the planar
n
-body with arbitrary choice of mass, there are only finitely many normalized CCs up to the rotational symmetry.

It is not hard to show if the above conjecture is true, every CC must be isolated. Although it is widely believed the conjecture is true, so far results are only available for small
n
. For
n=3
, it is a classical result due to Euler and Lagrange, for
n=4
, it was proven by Hampton and Moeckel
[
9
]
, and for
n=5
, it was proven by Albouy and Kaloshin
[
1
]
for generic choice of mass. For
n=6
, there are some partial progresses made by Chang and Chen
[
3
]
.

Here we show there is no infinite spin for a parabolic solution.

Theorem 1.9
.

If
q(t)
is a
\mathbf{k}
-parabolic solution with the reduced and normalized relative configuration
[q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)]\in S_{\mathbf{k}}/SO(2)
converging to an isolated CC, then the normalized relative configuration
q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
converges to a particular CC in
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{k}}
, and in particular there is no infinite spin.

Our proof is based on a recent result by Moeckel and Montgomery
[
14
]
, where the authors proved there is no infinite spin for a total collision solution in the planar
n
-body problem under a similar assumption. There are some well-known similarities between collision and parabolic solutions, which we shall explain below.

Definition 1.10
.

We say a solution
q:(0,T)\to\mathbb{E}
, is
\mathbf{k}
-collision, for a
\mathbf{k}\subset\mathbf{n}
with
|\mathbf{k}|\geq 2
, if
\lim_{t\to T}q(t)=q^{*}\in\mathbb{E}
with

\begin{cases}q^{*}_{i}=q^{*}_{j},&\forall i\neq j\in\mathbf{k};\\
q^{*}_{i}\neq q^{*}_{j},&\forall i\in\mathbf{k},j\in\mathbf{k}^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Remark 1.11
.

A
\mathbf{k}
-collision solution is partially collision, if
|\mathbf{k}|<n
, and total collision, if
|\mathbf{k}|=n
. Notice that there may be more than one collision occurring at the same time.

Given a
\mathbf{k}
-collision solution
q(t)
, when the masses in the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem approach the collision, it is well-known (see
[
4
]
,
[
21
]
or
[
7
]
), the normalized relative configuration
\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem must also approach the set of normalized CCs, as
t\to T
. If we assume
[\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)]
converges to a single isolated CC, the problem of whether
\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
converges to a particular CC also occurs, and so is the problem of infinite spin.

Since the work of Chazy
[
4
]
, many people had tried to solve the problem of infinite spin for collision solutions. Several published papers had claimed to solve this problem, but all turned out to be incomplete. In
[
14
]
, Moeckel and Montgomery explained in details why all these proofs are not complete.

For a total collision solution in the planar
n
-body problem, first in
[
22
]
, Yu showed there is no infinite spin, when the center manifold is one dimensional and two dimensional in some cases. Finally use Łojasiewicz inequality
[
10
]
, Moeckel and Montgomery
[
14
]
proved there is no infinite spin stated as in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.12
.

[
14
, Theorem 1.2]

Suppose
q(t)
is a total collision of the planar
n
-body problem with
q(t)\to 0
, as
t\to T
and suppose the corresponding reduced and normalized configuration
[\hat{q}(t)]\in S_{\mathbf{n}}/SO(2)
converges to an isolated CC. Then the normalized configuration
\hat{q}(t)
converges to a particular CC in
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{n}}
, and there is no infinite spin.

Remark 1.13
.

The problem of infinite spin for collision solutions is interesting purely from a mathematical point of view, as it only make sense when the bodies are point masses. Meanwhile for parabolic solutions it may have some practical meaning, as it still make sense when the bodies are not point masses.

The story for the problem of infinite spin of parabolic solutions is more or less the same, where it was claimed to be solved for complete parabolic solution in
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
. However the proofs are incomplete for the same reason explain by Moeckel and Montgomery in
[
14
]
.

Our main contributions are the following two:

First, we find a modification of the McGehee coordinate that is suitable for the study of parabolic solution, see (
2.8
). As is well-known the McGehee coordinate was first introduced in
[
12
]
to study triple collision solutions in the collinear three body problem, but also works for collision solutions in general
n
-problem, see
[
13
]
and
[
6
]
.

Second, compare to
[
14
]
, where only total collision solutions are considered, here we not only consider complete parabolic solutions, but also partially parabolic solutions. In the later case, the problem becomes time dependent, due to the effect of the masses out of the subsystem. To overcome this, we prove the following theorem, which seems to be interesting by itself.

Consider a smooth time-dependent system

(1.2)

\dot{x}=f(x)+g(x,t),\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

with
g(x,t)
bounded for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g(x,t)=O(|x|^{2})
uniformly, for
|x|
small enough. Define the spectral gap of
Df(0)
as

\displaystyle\beta:=\min\{|\text{re}(\lambda)|:\lambda\text{ is\ an eigenvalue\ of}\ Df(0)\ \text{with\ non-zero\ real\ part}\}.

Theorem 1.14
.

Assume
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an invariant submanifold of (
1.2
) with
g|_{\mathcal{N}}\equiv 0
and
0\in\mathcal{N}
being an equilibrium point. If a solution
x(t)
of (
1.2
) satisfies

(1.3)

x(t)\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }t\to\infty,\text{ and }e^{\alpha t}|g\big(x(t),t\big)|<\infty,\;\forall t\text{ large enough},

for every
\alpha\in(0,\beta)
,
then there is a solution
y(t)
in
\mathcal{N}
and constants
C>0
,
\eta>0
, such that

\displaystyle|y(t)-x(t)|\leq Ce^{-\eta t},\;\forall t>0.

A proof will be given in Section
5
following Bressan’s proof of the center manifold theorem in
[
2
]
. Theorem
1.14
also allows us to generalize Moeckel and Montgomery’s result to partial collision solutions, as in this case the corresponding problem is time dependent as well.

Theorem 1.15
.

If
q(t)
is a
\mathbf{k}
-collision solution with the reduced and normalized relative configuration
[\hat{q}^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)]
converging to an isolated CC, when
t\to T
, then the normalized relative configuration
q^{c}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
converges to a particular CC in
\mathscr{C}_{\mathbf{k}}
, and in particular there is no infinite spin.

Although a proof of the above result has been given in
[
8
]
recently, our approach seems to be a more natural extension of Moeckel and Montgomery and we feel it is worth to include it here.

2.
Coordinate Transformation And Equations of Motion

Witho out loss of generality, let’s assume
q(t)
be a
\mathbf{k}
-parabolic solution, with
\mathbf{k}=\{1,...,k\}
for some
2\leq k\leq n
. Define a linear map
T:\mathbb{R}^{2k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2k}
, as

T(q_{1},...,q_{k})=(z_{1},...,z_{k-1},c_{\mathbf{k}}):=(z,c),

where
z_{i}=q_{i}-c_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
z=(z_{1},...,z_{k-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}
. Since the mutual distances can be expressed by
z
, the potential function
U_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
is still an analytic homogeneous function on
\mathbb{R}^{2k-2}\backslash\triangle_{\mathbf{k}}
, where

\triangle_{\mathbf{k}}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}:r_{ij}=0\ \text{ for some }\{i\neq j\}\subset\mathbf{k}\}.

Then we have a time-dependent Lagrangian

L(z,\dot{z},c,\dot{c},t)=\frac{1}{2}\dot{z}^{T}M\dot{z}+\frac{1}{2}m_{0}|\dot{c}|^{2}+U_{\mathbf{k}}+U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

where
m_{0}=\sum^{k}_{i=1}m_{i}
and

\displaystyle(T^{-1})^{T}diag(m_{1},m_{1},...,m_{k},m_{k})T^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}M&0\\
0&m_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that
U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is the time-dependent term, as it depends on
q_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(t)=(q_{i}(t))_{i=k+1}^{n}
.

We introduce a Hermitian mass metric on
\mathbb{C}^{k-1}

\displaystyle\langle\langle v,w\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\bar{v}^{T}Mw,\ \ \ \ \ \ \forall v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{k-1},

where
\bar{v}
is the complex conjugate of
v
. The real part
\langle\langle v,w\rangle\rangle=\text{re}\langle\langle v,w\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
is just the mass inner product.

Set
\mathbb{R}^{2k}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{k}
. As in
[
14
]
, a local coordinate change
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{C}^{k-2}\times\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{k}
is

(z,c)=\psi(r,\theta,s,c)=(re^{i\theta}\frac{(s,1)}{||(s,1)||},c).

Introduce velocity variables
\rho=\dot{r}
,
\omega=\dot{s}
, and

\Omega(s,\omega)=\text{im}\langle\langle(s,1),(\omega,0)\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}},\ \ G(s,\omega)=\text{re}\langle\langle(s,1),(\omega,0)\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}

We get the Lagrangian in the new coordinates
(r,\rho,\theta,\dot{\theta},s,\omega,c,\dot{c})
as

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}||\omega||_{FS}^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\frac{\Omega(x,\omega)^{2}}{||(s,1)||^{4}}+\frac{r^{2}\dot{\theta}\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)+\frac{1}{2}m_{0}|\dot{c}|^{2}+U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},

where
||\omega||=||(\omega,0)||
,
V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)=||(s,1)||U_{\mathbf{k}}(s,1)
and

\displaystyle||\omega||_{FS}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{||(s,1)||^{2}||(\omega,0)||^{2}-|\langle\langle(s,1),(\omega,0)\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}|^{2}}{(||(s,1)||^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{||\omega||^{2}}{||(s,1)||^{2}}-\frac{G(s,\omega)^{2}+\Omega(s,\omega)^{2}}{(||(s,1)||^{2})^{2}}.

We will also use the notation
F(s,\omega)=||\omega||_{FS}^{2}
, which is the local representation of the square of the Fubini-Study metric on the complex projective space.

Reverting to real coordinates, we can write the Fubini-Study norm as
F(s,\omega)=\omega^{T}A(s)\omega
where
A(s)
is a positive-definite
(2k-4)\times(2k-4)
matrix. Observe that
\frac{\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}
is real linear with respect to
\omega
, thus we can also write
\frac{\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}=B(s)\omega
where
B(s)
is a
1\times(2k-4)
matrix. Then we have the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations

(2.1)

\displaystyle\dot{r}=

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=

\displaystyle rF(s,\omega)-\frac{1}{r^{2}}V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{r^{3}}+\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial r}

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}-\frac{\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle\dot{s}=

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}(s)\nabla F(s,\omega)+\frac{1}{r^{3}}A^{-1}(s)\nabla V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)-\frac{2\rho\omega}{r}-A^{-1}(s)DA(s)(\omega)\omega

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{r^{2}}A^{-1}(s)\nabla U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\frac{\dot{\mu}}{r^{2}}A^{-1}(s)B(s)

\displaystyle\ddot{c}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{0}}\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial c}

where
\nabla
denotes the Euclidean gradient or partial gradient with respect to
s
and

\mu=L_{\dot{\theta}}=r^{2}\dot{\theta}+\frac{r^{2}\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}.

Notice that
\mu
is just the angular momentum, which means

(2.2)

\mu=\dot{z}^{T}MJz=\text{re}\langle\langle\dot{z},iz\rangle\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}.

Equation (
2.1
) is obtained by the following computation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega}=r^{2}A(s)\omega+\frac{r^{2}\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{4}}B(s)+\frac{r^{2}\dot{\theta}}{||(s,1)||^{2}}B(s)=r^{2}A(s)\omega+\mu B(s),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega}=r^{2}A(s)\dot{\omega}+2r\rho A(s)\omega+r^{2}DA(s)(\omega)\omega+\mu DB(s)\omega+\dot{\mu}\omega,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{2}\nabla F(s,\omega)+\frac{r^{2}\Omega(s,\omega)}{||(s,1)||^{2}}DB(s)\omega+r^{2}\dot{\theta}DB(s)\omega+\frac{1}{r}\nabla V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)+\nabla U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{2}\nabla F(s,\omega)+\mu DB(s)\omega+\frac{1}{r}\nabla V_{\mathbf{k}}(s)+\nabla U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}.

Meanwhile in the new coordinates, the energy of the
\mathbf{k}
-subsystem is

(2.3)

h_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2r^{2}}+\frac{r^{2}}{2}F(s,\omega)-\frac{1}{r}V_{\mathbf{k}}(s).

The following asymptotic estimates will be needed in our proofs.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume
(s(t),1)
converges to an
(s_{0},1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2k-2}\setminus\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
, then we have the following asymptotic estimates

(2.4)

\left|\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial r}(t)\right|=O(t^{-2}),\ \ \left|\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial\theta}(t)\right|=O(t^{-\frac{4}{3}}),\ \ |\nabla U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(t)|=O(t^{-\frac{4}{3}}).

Proof.

View
z_{i}=q_{i}-c_{\mathbf{k}},i=1,...,k
as functions of
(r,\theta,s)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial r}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathbf{k},j\in\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\langle-\frac{m_{i}m_{j}(q_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t))}{|q_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t)|^{3}},\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial r}\rangle.

By Definition
1.2
,
|q_{i}(t)-q_{j}(t)|^{-1}=O(t^{-1})
. Meanwhile our assumption implies
|\frac{\partial z_{i}}{\partial r}|=O(1)
, thus
\frac{\partial U_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{\partial r}(t)=O(t^{-2})
. The other two estimates can be obtained similarly, after noticing that
