Relationship between eating awareness, physical fitness, and body composition in community-living older adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Özge Cemali, Özgün Elmas, Mustafa Cemali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to compare physical fitness and body composition in older adults with high and low levels of eating awareness and to examine the relationship between these parameters.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 120 older adults aged 65–70 years (60 with high eating awareness and 60 with low eating awareness). Eating awareness was assessed with the Eating Awareness Questionnaire (MEQ-30). To determine the physical fitness level, 30-second chair sit-and-stand test, Timed Up and Go test, 6-minute walking test, one-leg stand test, hand grip strength with a Jamar dynamometer (Sammons Preston, USA), and knee extension strength with a Lafayette digital dynamometer (USA). For body composition assessment, skeletal muscle mass, body fat percentage, and body mass index (BMI) were measured with the InBody 120 (InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). Physical fitness and body composition were compared between older adults with and without eating awareness. In addition, the relationship between eating awareness, physical fitness and body composition was examined in all older adults.ResultsA statistically significant difference was found in all physical fitness and body composition assessment results between older adults with high and low eating awareness (p < 0.05). In addition, a statistically significant relationship was found between eating awareness (MEQ-30), physical fitness and body composition results in older adults (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study showed that older adults with higher eating awareness tended to have better physical fitness results, greater muscle mass, and lower body fat percentage and BMI compared to those with lower eating awareness. In addition, lower eating awareness was associated with reduced physical fitness, decreased skeletal muscle mass, and increased body fat percentage and BMI. These findings indicate that eating awareness is closely related to both body composition and physical functionality in older adults, highlighting its potential role as an important behavioral factor in healthy aging.

Introduction

In chronological terms, individuals aged 65 and over are defined as “older adults”, and the proportion of this age group is increasing worldwide. In 2021, the number of people aged 65 and over worldwide was approximately 761 million, corresponding to 9.5% of the total population. By 2050, this number is projected to reach 1.6 billion, accounting for approximately 16% of the world population [1]. Structural and environmental changes that occur with the aging process lead to impairments in the functioning of many body systems such as the musculoskeletal system, sensory, cardiovascular, digestive and metabolic systems in older adults. These physiological changes can directly affect the physical fitness and nutritional status of individuals and cause problems such as malnutrition and obesity [2].

The musculoskeletal system is one of the systems most affected by the aging process. In this process, there are significant decreases in physical performance and balance problems are more common. As we age, the proportion of muscle mass and body fluid decreases, while fat storage increases. This decrease in muscle mass is medically defined as sarcopenia and becomes increasingly more pronounced with age [3]. The literature shows that there is a 3–5% decrease in muscle mass per decade starting from the age of 30, this loss accelerates after the age of 60 and can reach up to 30% per decade starting from the age of 70. All these changes have been found to negatively affect the physical activity level and mobility skills of older adults [4].

The expected increase in the proportion of individuals aged 65 years and over in the general population makes it more important than ever to monitor the nutritional status of older adults and to diagnose eating-related problems early [5]. Two major emerging problems associated with aging are the risk of malnutrition and the increase in excessive body adiposity/obesity. Malnutrition is characterized by loss of appetite, inadequate nutrient intake, and unintended body weight changes, which negatively affect both physical and functional health [6]. In contrast, excessive fat accumulation elevates the risk of chronic health conditions such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, metabolic and systemic disorders, cardiovascular and orthopedic diseases, and heart disease [7]. One of the main causes of muscle atrophy in older adults is inadequate protein intake [8]. In this context, nutrition is of great importance in terms of maintaining the body composition of older adults and supporting healthy aging. Increasing the individual’s awareness of nutrition and eating routine has been found to be an important factor in establishing correct eating habits [9, 10].

In recent years, the concepts of nutrition awareness and eating mindfulness have become increasingly important in healthy aging. Eating awareness is a concept that enables individuals to correctly interpret hunger and satiety signals, avoid emotional or unconscious consumption and make healthier food choices [11, 12]. Preference for processed and high-calorie foods, inadequate protein intake and irregular eating habits are common in individuals with low eating awareness [13–16]. It has been shown that nutrient deficiencies resulting from inadequate and unbalanced food consumption in older adults are associated with adverse results in physical function tests [17]. On the other hand, it is reported that practices aimed at increasing nutritional awareness create positive changes in the food consumption habits of older adults and provide significant improvements in muscle function and physical performance indicators [18].

In the literature, parameters such as food awareness, nutritional status, physical performance and body composition are generally considered independently of each other [4, 6, 12]. However, no study has been found comparing the physical fitness levels and body composition of older adults according to their eating awareness levels and examining the relationship between these parameters. In addition, the current scientific data evaluating the relationship between eating awareness and physical fitness in older individuals is also quite limited. In this context, the aim of the presented study is to compare the physical fitness and body composition of older adults with high and low eating awareness levels and to evaluate the relationship between these parameters.

Methods

Study design

Permission was obtained from the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (date: 30 November 2023, decision no: 2023/223) for the study and the study was conducted between April 2024 and February 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants and the study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The data for this study were collected from older adults who were members of the Turkish Pensioners’ Association, which has more than one million registered members nationwide. All assessments were conducted face-to-face at the association’s central facility in Ankara, which had been adapted to be suitable for these evaluations.

The older adults participating in the study were first evaluated with the Eating Awareness Scale (MEQ-30). According to the Eating Awareness Scale, two groups were formed consisting of older adults with high eating awareness (scores of 3 and above) and low eating awareness (scores of less than 3). Then, the physical fitness and body composition of the older adults in both groups were evaluated. According to the evaluation results, the physical fitness levels and body composition results of the older adults with high and low eating awareness were compared. The study also examined the relationship between eating awareness, physical fitness, and body composition in all older adults. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist guidelines were followed in this study [19].

Participant

The sample size of the study was calculated by using the G power 3.1.9.6 program and selecting two groups statistical test, one tail parameter and a priori type power analysis type from the T test family. In the power analysis conducted by taking the MEQ-30 as reference, the total number of older adults required for a medium effect size (effect size d = 0.5), 95% confidence interval and 80% power was determined as 102 older adults. All volunteers were reached using the snowball sampling method and written consent was obtained from the participants. Inclusion criteria were being 65 years of age or older and not having any cognitive or communication problems. Participants who scored 3 or more on the MEQ-30 were included in the “high eating awareness” group, and those who scored below 3 were included in the “low eating awareness” group. Those with an advanced chronic disease (cancer, neurological, orthopedic, rheumatological, cardiovascular and cardiopulmonary diseases), those who had surgery in the last 6 months, those who could not walk independently, those with amputations and those with endoprostheses were excluded from the study.

The study evaluated 130 older adults (66 with high eating awareness and 64 with low eating awareness). However, 10 individuals (7 with high eating awareness and 4 with low eating awareness) were excluded from the study because they were unable to complete the assessments, and the study was completed with 120 older adults (60 with high eating awareness and 60 with low eating awareness). None of the participants had previously received nutrition education or participated in a conscious eating program.

Data collection tools

Participants’ age and gender information were recorded. Eating awareness of older adults was assessed with MEQ-30. Physical fitness level was measured with 30-second sit-to-stand test, Timed Up and Go test (TUG), 6-minute walk test, knee extensor muscle strength and handgrip strength. A digital dynamometer was used to assess knee extensor strength, and a Jamar hand dynamometer was used to measure handgrip strength. Body composition was determined using InBody device with skeletal muscle mass, body fat percentage and Body mass index (BMI) data. All assessments, conducted by a physiotherapist and a nutrition and dietetics specialist, lasted approximately 35 min.

Mindful eating questionnaire (MEQ-30)

The MEQ-30 was developed by Framson et al. to assess eating awareness skills in an adult population [20]. MEQ-30 is a scale that measures the level of eating awareness. The scale has seven sub-areas: disinhibition (unconscious eating), emotional eating, eating control, awareness, eating discipline, conscious nutrition and intervention. The scale contains 30 questions to determine the level of eating awareness in daily life. The total MEQ-30 score, which varies between 1 and 5, is found by adding all item scores and dividing by 30. A total scale score of ≥ 3 points indicates high eating awareness, and a score of < 3 points indicates low eating awareness. An increase in the total score indicates increased eating awareness. The validity and reliability of this scale in Turkish was conducted by Köse et al. (2016). The total score Cronbach alpha value of MEQ-30 was found to be 0.733 in the Turkish form [21].

Assessment of physical fitness

The physical fitness tests selected in this study are widely used and validated indicators for assessing functional capacity in older adults. The TUG test is a reliable tool for assessing balance and mobility, while the six-minute walk test reflects functional exercise capacity and cardiorespiratory endurance. The single-leg stance test provides information on static balance and fall risk, while the 30-second chair rise test measures lower extremity muscle endurance. Handgrip strength is considered a biomarker of general muscle function and frailty, while knee extensor strength is considered an important determinant of mobility and independence in daily living. Using these complementary measures together allows for a multidimensional assessment of physical fitness in older adults [4, 6, 22].

30 s sit-to-stand test

It is used to evaluate lower extremity endurance. During the evaluation, the participant was asked to sit in the middle of the chair with his back straight, feet on the floor and arms crossed in front of his chest (right hand on left shoulder, left hand on right shoulder). While the participant is in this position, the test is started with the start command and he makes as many full lifts as he can for 30 s, the number of full lifts he makes for 30 s is recorded. The evaluation was repeated 3 times and the best result was recorded [23].

Timed up and go test (TUG)

TUG is an objective, reliable, simple measurement tool used to assess balance and functional mobility. It was first developed by Podsiadlo and Richardson in 1991. During the test, the participant is asked to get up from a chair, walk 3 m, turn back to the starting point and sit down again. The time to complete the test is recorded in seconds. The evaluation was repeated 3 times and the best time was taken into account [24].

One leg standing test

The One-Legged Standing Test was applied to evaluate balance. Participants were asked to stand on one leg without shoes, without support from anywhere with their hands, with one knee bent, and the time was measured. The evaluation was made in three repetitions on the dominant leg and the best time was recorded [25].

Six-minute walk test

It was implemented to evaluate the functional exercise capacity of older adults. During the test, participants were asked to walk at the fastest walking speed they determined and the distance they covered in six minutes was measured in meters. The evaluation was repeated twice and the best performance was recorded [26].

Assessment of muscle strength

Knee extensor muscle strength of older adults was measured with Lafayette digital dynamometer (Lafayette Instrument Company, USA). The evaluation was made on the dominant side while the participants were sitting in a chair without armrests, and the results were recorded in newtons (N) [24]. In the assessment of hand grip strength, the Jamar hand dynamometer (Sammons Preston, USA) recommended by the American Association of Hand Therapists (AETD) was used. The measurement was performed in accordance with the standard protocol of the AETD; with the participant in a sitting position, shoulder adduction and neutral rotation, elbow in 90° flexion, forearm in a mid-position and supported, and wrist in a neutral position. Three measurements were made with a one-minute rest period between all measurements, and the best result was recorded [27].

Assessment of body composition

A bioelectrical impedance analyzer (BIA; InBody 120, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) with tetrapolar 8-point tactile electrodes was used in the measurements. This device can perform impedance measurements at two different frequencies, 20 kHz and 100 kHz, in five body segments covering four extremities and the trunk. The following protocols were applied prior to the test: Each participant’s age, gender, and height information was recorded in the device; to ensure the reliability of the measurement, participants were instructed not to consume caffeine (tea, coffee, energy drinks) or alcohol 24 h prior to the test, to avoid intense physical activity 8 h prior to the test, and to be in a fasting state for at least 4 h. Measurements were only taken from participants who met these conditions. Participants were asked to clean their hands and feet before touching the electrodes, and they were given a short rest period before the measurement. All evaluations were performed barefoot, with dry feet, and while standing. Metal objects were removed before the measurement, and participants were asked to stand upright with their arms at their sides and not to speak during the measurement. During the measurement, the feet were placed in the center of the electrodes on the floor; the hand electrodes were positioned so that there was no contact between the arms and the body, and this position was maintained throughout the test. After the evaluation was completed, participants were asked to release the hand electrodes and step off the device. Skeletal muscle mass (kg), body fat percentage (%) and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) of older adults were measured with this device [28].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) program was used for statistical analyses. Normality distribution of the data was evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, skewness–kurtosis values ​​and histogram graphics. As a result of these examinations, it was determined that categorical data were not normally distributed and numerical data showed a normal distribution. Within the scope of descriptive statistics, mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) for numerical variables, frequency (n) and percentage (%) values ​​were calculated for categorical data. In comparisons between the two groups, independent samples t-test was used for numerical variables and chi-square (χ²) test was used for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the relationships between variables. For the correlation coefficient (r), reference values ​​were taken as 0.00–0.19 for no or negligible relationship, 0.20–0.39 for weak relationship, 0.40–0.69 for moderate relationship, 0.70–0.89 for strong relationship, and 0.90–1.00 for very strong relationship [29]. In all analyses, the level of statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

The study included 120 (62 females and 58 males) older adults aged 65–70 years with a mean age of 67.20 ± 1.73. No statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of age (p = 0.103) and gender (p = 0.855). A statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of BMI classification (p = 0.017). It was determined that in the group of older adults with high eating awareness, there were more individuals with normal weight and fewer individuals with overweight according to BMI classification, compared to the group with low eating awareness (Table 1).

Discussion

In our study, it was observed that older adults with high eating awareness had better physical fitness levels, higher muscle mass, lower body fat percentage, and BMI compared to their peers with low eating awareness. Additionally, it was determined that as eating awareness decreased in all older adults, physical fitness parameters deteriorated, muscle mass decreased, and body fat percentage and BMI increased. Furthermore, it was concluded that the decrease in muscle mass and the increase in body fat percentage and BMI in older adults lead to an additional deterioration in physical fitness levels.

Eating awareness and physical fitness

Eating awareness emerges as a holistic concept that is predicted to affect not only individuals’ nutritional preferences but also their physical fitness levels [30]. Beslenme alışkanlıkları ve beslenme davranışlarının genel sağlık ve fiziksel performans üzerinde önemli etkileri bilinmektedir [31]. Lu et al.’s study revealed the long-term negative effects of nutrient deficiencies on physical performance in older adults. Specifically, imbalances in vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids, and homocysteine ​​levels were found to cause deterioration in key physical performance indicators such as chair rise time [17]. Rajabi et al. highlighted the role of physical activity level in determining body composition, muscle strength, and lipid profile in older adults. Individuals with high physical activity levels were observed to have lower waist circumference, better muscle strength, and healthier lipid profiles. Furthermore, it was stated that the positive effects of physical activity were limited when nutritional intake was inadequate [32]. A systematic review and meta-analysis study concluded that nutritional interventions, especially protein supplements, applied to older adults have significant effects on muscle function and physical performance. The study showed that nutritional interventions resulted in significant improvements in measures such as skeletal muscle mass, lean body mass, hand grip strength and walking speed [18]. The study conducted by Putriana and Noviekayati revealed that eating awareness approaches increase healthy eating behaviors in older adults. It was also stated that awareness-based nutrition education can positively affect not only food choices but also general health and physical performance [30]. In the present study, it was found that older adults with high eating awareness performed better than older adults with low eating awareness in physical fitness measures such as sit-to-stand test, TUG, 6-minute walk test, one-leg standing time, knee extension strength and hand grip strength. It is thought that older adults with high eating awareness make more conscious food choices, maintain their meal schedules and continue their physical activities more sustainably. In contrast, it can be predicted that physical performance may be lower in individuals with low eating awareness due to irregular eating habits and excessive energy intake. In this direction, it can be said that eating awareness approaches have the potential to prevent overeating behaviors by increasing individuals’ sensitivity to hunger and satiety signals, improve nutritional habits and indirectly support physical functionality. It is thought that structured education and practices aimed at improving eating awareness should be considered as an important strategy to support both healthy eating habits and physical fitness levels in older adults. The findings also pave the way for planning intervention-based studies evaluating the long-term effects of these approaches in the future.

Eating awareness, muscle mass, fat percentage and BMI

Eating awareness is considered an important behavioral component that can affect not only the individual’s food preferences but also physical health indicators such as body composition and muscle mass. In this context, Winkens et al. reported that eating awareness practices contribute to the development of a more regular and conscious eating behavior by increasing attention and awareness in older individuals [14]. Kerin and colleagues demonstrated a positive relationship between eating awareness and the capacity to regulate overeating behavior [13]. Benzer şekilde, Similarly, in a randomized controlled study conducted by Chiba, an educational program was implemented that addressed slow eating and chewing habits within the scope of eating awareness. As a result of the program, both an increase in eating awareness and a decrease in eating speed were observed [15]. Another study found that overweight and obese women with low mass and high fat content had lower conscious eating than normal weight women [33]. Voulgaridou et al. have shown that appropriate and balanced nutritional behaviors and consumption of protein-containing foods are important for preserving muscle mass in older adults [34]. Considering that these studies were conducted in different age groups, our current study focusing on older adults is thought to make an important contribution to the literature by revealing that individuals with high levels of eating awareness have higher skeletal muscle mass, lower body fat ratio, and lower body mass index compared to their peers with low awareness. This finding shows that in addition to adequate and balanced nutrition, conscious nutrition by being aware of what you eat, especially adequate protein intake, plays an important role in preserving muscle mass and balancing body composition in older individuals. It can be said that individuals with high eating awareness can maintain a healthier body composition in the long term by being more conscious about their food choices. Therefore, the findings revealing the relationship between eating awareness and physical fitness and body composition indicate that future intervention studies aimed at increasing eating awareness in older individuals can make significant contributions in terms of both nutritional behaviors and physical health.

The relationship between eating awareness, physical fitness, and body composition

Body composition, especially muscle mass and body fat ratio, plays a decisive role in the physical fitness of older individuals [35]. In addition, it is stated in the literature that eating awareness practices reduce overeating behaviors by increasing individuals’ sensitivity to hunger and satiety signals and contribute to the acquisition of healthy living habits [36]. In this context, the study conducted by Basir and his colleagues revealed that sustainable body weight loss and healthy behavioral changes can be achieved in individuals by integrating the eating awareness approach into workplace-based weight management programs. The research shows that eating awareness practices reduce overeating tendencies by increasing individuals’ sensitivity to hunger and satiety cues and produce more successful results when evaluated together with physical activity. Furthermore, the study emphasized that a nutritional regime supported by eating awareness contributes to physical performance and helps individuals make more conscious choices during exercise [37]. In the study conducted by Karaca et al., a positive relationship was found between the level of nutritional knowledge and eating awareness in individuals aged 18–45, and it was determined that eating awareness decreased with the decrease in nutritional knowledge level, and accordingly, BMI and waist-hip ratio were negatively affected. In addition, the regression analysis results in this study revealed that eating awareness explained 7.8% of BMI and 2% of nutritional knowledge level [38]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis study, it was concluded that a diet with appropriate, balanced and protein-rich nutrients helps older adults preserve their muscle mass [39]. In the present study, it was found that muscle mass, fat ratio and BMI decreased with increased eating awareness in older adults. In addition, it was concluded that physical fitness parameters worsened with decreased muscle mass, fat ratio and BMI. These findings reveal that eating awareness is an important behavioral factor that can affect not only the nutritional choices of individuals but also body composition and physical fitness level. In this context, it is thought that strategies to increase eating awareness in older adults should be evaluated as an effective tool in maintaining healthy body composition and physical functionality. Education aimed at improving eating awareness should not only be limited to individual health outcomes but should also be considered as a basic component of healthy aging policies. In this direction, it is important for both clinicians and academicians to develop programs to improve body composition and increase physical activity levels of individuals using cognitive behavioral approaches in order to support eating control.

Limitation

This study has some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between eating awareness, physical fitness, and body composition. Second, the use of a snowball sampling approach may have introduced selection bias, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader older adult population. Third, although participants with advanced chronic diseases were excluded, other potential confounding factors such as baseline physical activity levels, overall dietary intake, and comorbidities were not controlled for and may have influenced the observed associations. In addition, randomized controlled intervention studies including eating awareness training should be conducted both in healthy older adults living in the community and in individuals with chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cancer and neurological diseases. Furthermore, future studies should investigate the long-term effects of eating awareness on physical fitness and body composition. Studies should be designed to comprehensively evaluate multidimensional factors such as psychological status, cognitive functions, social support, physical activity level, sleep quality and health literacy that may affect the relationship between eating habits and eating awareness of older adults.

Conclusion

This study showed that older adults with low levels of eating awareness have lower physical fitness levels, lower muscle mass, and higher body fat and BMI values compared to those with high awareness. It was also determined that as eating awareness decreases, physical fitness deteriorates, muscle mass decreases, and body fat and BMI increase, and these changes negatively affect physical fitness levels. These findings reveal that eating awareness is an important determinant not only in terms of nutritional behaviors but also in terms of maintaining the physical functionality and body composition of older adults. In line with the World Health Organization’s approach to healthy aging, community-based rehabilitation models should be adopted by going beyond individual health practices. In this context, trainings that increase eating awareness, healthy nutrition counseling, and physical activity programs suitable for older individuals should be offered with an integrated approach. Central and local governments, associations, foundations, the private sector, health institutions, older adults centers, and health professionals should assume joint responsibility in this process and multi-stakeholder solutions should be developed from the primary level to the policy level. This holistic approach is of vital importance in terms of protecting the quality of life of older adults and supporting healthy aging. In this context, education and awareness programs should be created to increase eating awareness in older individuals; informative publications should be prepared for the public with the support of the written, visual and social media and sustainable nutrition programs should be introduced.
