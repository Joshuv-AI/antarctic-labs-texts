INTRODUCTION

Current professional practice, characterized by increasing competitiveness and constant pressure to achieve rapid and immediate results, has considerably intensified levels of emotional exhaustion, distress, and stress across multiple sectors [1], including healthcare. This constant pressure not only affects workplace and personal relationships, but also professional performance and the quality of care within the healthcare sector [2], increasing the predisposition to mental disorders such as anxiety and depression due to the lack of primary prevention strategies for this situation [3].

The result is a syndrome that mainly occurs in professions focused on service provision and public care, such as physicians, nurses, pharmacists, teachers, psychologists, social workers, sales personnel, customer service staff, police officers, firefighters, caregivers, etc. [1].

The term burnout was first defined in 1974 by Freudenberger as “a feeling of failure and an exhausted or worn-out existence resulting from an overload of demands on the worker’s energy, personal resources, or spiritual strength” [4]. Maslach and Jackson [5], in turn, described it as a syndrome of physical, emotional, and intellectual exhaustion that produces a negative attitude toward professional life and toward other people, accompanied by loss of self-esteem, in which the individual experiences chronic fatigue and feelings of helplessness and hopelessness.

Burnout syndrome, also referred to as occupational exhaustion syndrome, has been considered since the revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) at the 72nd World Health Assembly held in May 2019 [6] as a work-related problem (occupational disease), as it affects both the mental health of professionals and the quality of care provided to patients due to exhaustion, mental distancing from work, and reduced professional efficacy as a consequence of chronic workplace stress [6]. The new ICD-11 classification includes it in Chapter 24, “Factors influencing health status or contact with health services,” within the subcategory “Problems associated with employment or unemployment,” coded as QD85 under the designation occupational burnout syndrome (OBS) [7], characterized by the following features:

Characteristics

As indicated by the World Health Organization (WHO), OBS specifically refers to problems developed in the occupational context and should not be applied to experiences in other areas of life [6]. The development of this syndrome represents a type of chronic occupational stress resulting from the gradual accumulation of negative emotions, characterized by fatigue, irritability, anger, and sometimes resentment toward superiors, as a consequence of prolonged exposure to stressors in the workplace [1].

It is characterized by three fundamental components: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. Emotional exhaustion (EE) involves extreme depletion of physical, mental, and emotional energy, whereas depersonalization (DP) refers to negative attitudes of indifference, detachment, distancing, and lack of commitment toward coworkers or the people to whom services are provided. Reduced personal accomplishment (PA), which often goes unnoticed, manifests as a perception of inefficacy and loss of motivation at work [8]. OBS has devastating effects on both the physical and emotional well-being of professionals and on their work performance. Exhaustion may result in physical problems such as muscle pain, anxiety, insomnia, and gastrointestinal disorders, among others, whereas depersonalization generates emotional distancing, irritability, and cynicism, negatively affecting relationships with patients. Reduced personal accomplishment, in turn, may lead to negative self-assessment, frustration, and loss of confidence in professional capabilities [1].

This may result in profound physical and mental exhaustion affecting motivation and enthusiasm, and in many cases this generalized dissatisfaction with work performance may lead professionals to reduce productivity, stagnate professionally, or even consider leaving their job [9,10]. Furthermore, burnout syndrome has been associated with a greater likelihood of prescription, communication, management, and care errors leading to adverse effects and suboptimal patient care, thereby compromising patient safety [2,11].

Risk factors

The physiological basis of OBS lies in the association between sustained activation of the autonomic nervous system and dysfunction of the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary axis, together with alterations in cortisol levels, as a consequence of chronic psychological stress [12]. Individuals affected by OBS have shown higher levels of cortisol, melatonin, and thyroid hormones, biomarkers associated with exhaustion that reveal its physiological implications [13]. Its manifestation through immune and endocrine dysfunctions ultimately results in structural and functional brain changes, excitotoxicity, systemic inflammation, immunosuppression, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and premature death [12].

The factors contributing to the development of OBS are diverse and numerous. They may be grouped as follows [1,14-16]:

Working conditions: excessive or inconvenient shifts and schedules, increased workload or pace of work, staff shortages, lack of autonomy in assigned tasks, increased bureaucratic and regulatory demands, and lack of participation in decision-making.Work environment conditions: uncomfortable work environment, insufficient salary, lack of recognition, high emotional demands, constant pressure to achieve results, and poor communication.Professional conditions: professional stagnation, disappointment due to unmet professional expectations, barriers to professional development, and perceived lack of social value of work or profession.Personal and family conditions: individual idiosyncrasies, social isolation, interpersonal conflicts, degree of self-demand, lifestyle, work-life balance difficulties, and family conflict/support/pressure.

OBS in other workers

The literature contains studies on the extent and impact of occupational burnout in almost all professions focused on people-oriented care: social workers [16-18], teachers [19,20], police officers [21,22], caregivers [23], etc. However, healthcare professions have most intensively analyzed the OBS experienced by their professionals. Population healthcare has undergone major challenges in recent years, with a substantial overload borne by healthcare professionals, which has been reflected in a high risk of OBS, leading to devaluation of the healthcare professional–patient relationship and reduced quality of care. Several studies and reviews [2,11,24] demonstrate its association with decreased professional performance, patient safety problems, and lower user satisfaction.

A PubMed search using the terms: (burnout) AND (“Health workers”) Filters: from 1950/1/1–2025/3/31 yielded 426 references. Among the most recent are studies analyzing OBS in physicians and nurses jointly or separately, generally reviews [13,24,25] or observational studies [26-28], proposals for preventive actions [29] or treatments [30-32], and also studies in students [33]. The recent COVID-19 pandemic and its repercussions on the mental health of physicians and nurses were also widely analyzed and continue to be so today [34-35]. Its consideration as an occupational disease has only recently appeared in some publications [36,37].

Authors of the reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses conclude that OBS exists among physicians and nurses in proportions ranging from 6% to 40%, but reaching up to 75% depending on whether the three dimensions of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), the most widely used questionnaire, or only one of them are considered [2,13,24,38,39].

OBS in community pharmacists

The professional practice of community pharmacists (CPs) is subject to strict regulation requiring a considerable management and bureaucratic workload. Furthermore, their daily work mainly consists of attending patients who come to the pharmacy seeking resolution of health problems, medication dispensing and recommendation, information, and administrative advice. This pharmacist–patient interaction is subject, as in other professions, to psychological stress resulting from physical strain and mental exhaustion experienced in the workplace.

The number of studies analyzing occupational burnout among CPs is very limited. A Medline search similar to that conducted for healthcare workers using the terms (burnout) AND (“Community pharmacists”) Filters: from 1950/1/1–2025/3/31 retrieved only 33 results. In the current century, studies conducted in Europe [40-42], the Middle East [43-46], and North America [47-49] found moderate to high levels of burnout among surveyed CPs, somewhat lower in the oldest study [43] than in the others.

At the national level, very few studies exist. In 2007, Pérez and Díaz [50] studied burnout prevalence using the MBI in primary care pharmacists working in healthcare centers of the Galician Health Service (SERGAS). Torrecillas [51,52] found in 2011 a low level of burnout (9.5%) among Spanish rural CPs, with moderate levels in each of the three MBI dimensions. The study was limited by including only pharmacy owners practicing in rural pharmacies and therefore does not represent the general profile of Spanish CPs. More recently, several studies have analyzed the employment situation of CPs in relation to professional burnout in Spain, including reasons for changes in professional practice settings [9,53].

In 2023, the International Pharmaceutical Federation published the report “Workforce Sustainability and Supporting Positive Practice in Community Pharmacy” [54], which reported that 62% of surveyed CPs had experienced burnout or other mental health problems, highlighting the importance of evaluating and addressing the well-being of professionals working in this field. The report states that, after having been a fundamental pillar of healthcare delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic, community pharmacists have experienced significant exhaustion and burnout. Contributing factors include increasing regulatory burden, increased workload, supply chain problems, evolving professional roles, staff shortages, and unmet professional expectations, among others, potentially generating the frustration and emotional stress identified [55].

Justification

Although OBS has been widely documented in various professional groups, with the healthcare sector being one of the most affected, there is a notable lack of research on the impact of burnout syndrome among community pharmacists both in Spain and internationally. It is therefore necessary to conduct studies identifying risk factors, assessing their significance, and evaluating the well-being of this professional group, in order to propose specific interventions and implement primarily preventive strategies aimed at promoting the mental health and well-being of CPs, thereby improving service quality and patient care.

Consequently, it was considered appropriate to carry out the present study on OBS among community pharmacists in Galicia, introducing occupational aspects that have so far been scarcely studied. This study is expected to help fill this knowledge gap, enabling the design of prevention and support strategies adapted to the specific characteristics of the region, but with clear national relevance given that there are few differences in the organizational model and the employment framework is common throughout Spain. This would improve both quality of working life and pharmaceutical care, benefiting professionals as well as the patients they serve.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design

Cross-sectional observational study conducted among pharmacists practicing in community pharmacies in Galicia between September and December 2025.

Participants

The study population, consisting of all Galician pharmacists practicing in community pharmacy at the time of the survey, comprised 3,984 individuals at the end of 2023, according to the latest statistics from the General Council of Official Colleges of Pharmacists [56]. Sample composition considered sex, type of locality, and professional practice modality.

Practicing community pharmacists who access and complete the online questionnaire, expressly agreeing to participate in the study by checking the corresponding box, without which completion of the questionnaire will not be possible.

Refusal to participate in the study and/or inability to complete the questionnaire using an electronic device (mobile phone, computer, tablet, or any other compatible device).

Sample size calculation

To achieve a precision of 10.0% in estimating a proportion using a two-sided 95.0% asymptotic Normal confidence interval with finite population correction, assuming an expected proportion of 50.0% and a total population size of 3,984 [56], it will be necessary to include 94 valid surveys in the study. Anticipating possible losses of 20%, at least 125 questionnaires are expected to be obtained.

Variables

Proportion (%) of community pharmacists affected by OBS and mean degree of involvement (mean [SD]) according to the total score of the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) for healthcare professionals [5,57-61].

Sociodemographic and occupational characteristics of surveyed community pharmacists (age, sex, family situation, job position, working conditions, locality, etc.).

Scores of the questionnaire subscales: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, personal accomplishment, and each MBI-HSS item, expressed as mean (SD) and frequencies (%).

Stratification of OBS level according to MBI-HSS scores: high, medium, or low, based on the criteria described below; total sample and proportion (%) of participants in each level.

Scores related to perceptions and expectations regarding professional practice: relative frequency (%) of each response.

Type of measures adopted to improve the situation caused by OBS: frequency (%) of each response.

Possible identified risk factors: relative frequency (%) of each factor.

Comparisons between groups: differences (%), statistical significance (p<0.05).

Score calculation (Likert-type scale)

The score for each of the three subscales is obtained by summing the values assigned to each item: never (0 points), a few times a year or less (1 point), once a month or less (2 points), a few times a month (3 points), once a week (4 points), a few times a week (5 points), and every day (6 points).

Depending on the score, there are three OBS levels for each of the three subscales: low, medium, and high [59-61].

High scores in the first two subscales (EE and DP) and low scores in the third (PA) define burnout syndrome. Severe involvement will be considered when all three criteria are met (EE>26, DP>9, and PA<34), and moderate-severe involvement when one or two criteria are met. Regarding completion, the full questionnaire is simple and requires no more than 10–15 minutes. None of its sections include questions allowing identification of the respondent, thereby fully preserving anonymity, which together with the recruitment procedure ensures maintenance of confidentiality.

Stratification of pharmacies and pharmacists in the sample

The number and list of pharmacies in each Galician municipality were obtained from the SERGAS website [63] and ordered by postal code for subsequent processing.

The randomization process was performed using the App-Sorteos website [64] to obtain a series of random numbers ordered by postal code of the localities where the pharmacies are established, allowing duplication of numbers. If a locality with only one pharmacy was repeated, the pharmacy from the next postal code was selected. This generated a list of 107 localities distributed according to the aforementioned Population Zones [62]. The next step will involve contacting 125 pharmacies to invite one pharmacist from each pharmacy to participate in the study. If, after two telephone calls on consecutive days, a selected pharmacy cannot be reached, the next pharmacy on the list will be contacted. If the required number of questionnaires has not been achieved by the end of December, a new round of invitations will be conducted following a new randomization among pharmacies not selected in the first round.

Summary of the study procedure

For study development, these pharmacies will be contacted by telephone, offering one pharmacist from each pharmacy the opportunity to participate. If accepted, their email address will be recorded and used to send the link to the questionnaire, the study summary, and completion instructions.

This email will include the participant information sheet (PIS), containing the contact details of the principal investigator should participants wish to request the final study report. Participants will obtain their own results by downloading the completed form to their computer, an option that will be offered on the final page. Consent will be requested/granted upon acceptance to participate on the initial page of the online form.

If none of the pharmacists from the initially selected pharmacy agrees to participate, the next pharmacy in the locality will be contacted, or, if the selected pharmacy is the only one in that locality, the next locality according to postal code order will be approached.

To preserve the anonymity of participating pharmacists while also determining whether adequate participation has been achieved in each Population Zone, the final form was cloned into 12 identical forms (3 per population zone × 4 provinces), hosted on 12 web addresses (URLs) where completed forms will be recorded. Each pharmacist agreeing to participate will receive the link corresponding to the URL containing the form assigned to their Population Zone.

The number of refusals to participate and the number of pharmacists who initially agreed but did not access the questionnaire will be recorded anonymously.

Fifteen days after acceptance to participate, a generic message thanking participants and again requesting questionnaire completion will be sent to those who have not yet completed it. After this point, the recorded email address will be deleted.

The research team will transfer the data into an MS Excel® spreadsheet for processing and statistical analysis. Once the study is completed, individual questionnaires will be retained for three months, after which they will be deleted from the website hosting them, since respondents cannot be identified.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis will be performed using SPSS® version 22.0 for Windows®. Qualitative data will be expressed as percentages and quantitative data as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, otherwise as median and interquartile range. Chi-square tests will be used for qualitative variables, Student’s t-test for quantitative variables with normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney test for quantitative variables without normal distribution. Wilcoxon’s test will be used for paired data analysis. Pearson and/or Spearman correlation analyses will be used to assess relationships between quantitative variables. Statistical significance will be established at p<0.05.

ETHICAL AND LEGAL ASPECTS

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Galicia (CEIM-Ga) under reference number 2025.202.

The study will be conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH E6) applicable to studies of this nature. Participant autonomy will be respected in all cases, following the ethical principles of the current Declaration of Helsinki and Organic Law 3/2018 of December 5 on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights.

As described in the procedure, pharmacists voluntarily willing to collaborate in the study will receive the PIS, and the first screen of the online form will contain the equivalent of the informed consent form, including an acceptance checkbox.

Only email addresses will be collected by the pharmacist investigators, but these cannot be linked to questionnaire results because the survey is anonymous. This information will be deleted from electronic records after the reminder and thank-you email sent 15 days after agreement to participate.

Confidentiality of information collected within the study context

The research team will receive anonymous form data from the MS Forms® platform, since no identifying information is included. Data will be transferred to an MS Excel® spreadsheet and analyzed anonymously and in aggregate form.

APPLICABILITY OF RESULTS

The psychological health of community pharmacists is an issue that should be addressed not only when problems arise, but also from a preventive perspective by creating working environments that promote safety and well-being. This not only improves professionals’ quality of life but also increases productivity and enhances pharmaceutical care, even in demanding working situations.

The present study aims to establish a starting point for increasing visibility, awareness, and management of OBS among community pharmacists. It seeks to provide a situational diagnosis that will support future work in developing prevention and coping programs and strategies. We hope that dissemination as a research article, intended for publication in a journal widely read by community pharmacists, will encourage nationwide reflection involving professional organizations, scientific societies, trade unions, and pharmacy associations, enabling an in-depth analysis of the problem.
