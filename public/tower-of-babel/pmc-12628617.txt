Concentration of contezolid in cerebrospinal fluid and serum in a patient with renal allograft tuberculosis and tuberculous meningoencephalitis
Authors: Weijian Liu, Tian He, Hongjuan Qin, Peize Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis is a challenging and severe infection among solid organ transplant recipients. Contezolid, a new oxazolidinone antimicrobial, has exhibited potent activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis with a safety profile superior to linezolid, which is constrained by adverse effects such as myelosuppression and peripheral neuropathy. We present here a case of a renal recipient who suffered from allograft tuberculosis and tuberculous meningitis. A contezolid-included regimen successfully treated him with no drug-related adverse effects and drug-drug interaction. Concentration of contezolid in serum and cerebrospinal fluid at various time points during treatment was monitored. Given the increased risk of TB in patients with organ transplantation and limited evidence of contezolid for tuberculous meningitis, further clinical studies are needed to investigate the appropriate dosage and application of contezolid in patients with the most devastating type of extra-pulmonary TB.Clinical trial number Not applicable.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) stands as a severe infection in patients with solid organ transplantation. Tuberculous meningitis had been believed to be the most critical extra-pulmonary TB type and was associated with high mortality [1]. The prevalence of post-transplant TB varies between 3.1 and 15% in Asia, whereas it ranges from 1.5 to 3.5% in the Middle East and 1.7 to 5% in Europe [2]. Recipients of solid organ transplants encounter a significantly elevated risk of tuberculosis infection, ranging from 20 to 74 times higher than the general population, with a mortality rate of up to 30% [3, 4]. Clinically, atypical TB symptoms often lead to difficulty and delay in diagnosis. Immunosuppressive agents complicate the treatment process due to drug-drug interactions and adverse effects, thereby adding complexity to therapeutic decision-making.

Contezolid (CZD), a recently introduced oxazolidinone, gained approval for treating complex skin and soft tissue infections from the China National Medical Products Administration in 2021 [5]. Most studies have revealed that CZD demonstrates remarkable bactericidal activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis [6–8]. Its unique molecular structure significantly reduces the incidence of myelosuppression and neuropathy, which are the most common adverse effects of linezolid, the first-generation oxazolidinone [9]. Moreover, CZD has little interaction with other drugs, making it more suitable for application in complex clinical scenarios [10]. However, only a few cases have reported its efficacy and safety in patients with tuberculous meningitis [11, 12].Here, we report a case of a male patient with renal allograft tuberculosis and tuberculous meningoencephalitis. He suffered from delayed TB diagnosis, fluctuating TB symptoms and severe adverse effects, which was finally resolved by a CZD-included enhanced anti-TB regimen. Concentration of CZD in cerebrospinal fluid and serum were monitored during treatment. We hope to provide a reference for future anti-TB treatment and research for patients with severe TBM, particularly for those with special health problems.

Case description

A 34-year-old male, height 168 cm, weight 57 kg, was transferred to our hospital on May 12, 2024. He presented with intermittent fever and headache for 3 months, followed by sudden onset of impaired consciousness 13 days ago. He underwent renal transplantation one year ago due to hypertension and end-stage chronic kidney disease and received tacrolimus 1 mg and mizoribine 50 mg twice daily for immunosuppression.

Three months before admission, he developed intermittent fever. Chest CT scan appeared normal, but an abdominal MRI revealed a mass in the transplanted kidney. Despite empirical cephalosporin antibiotics therapy, his fever persisted, and headache was also reported. Renal allograft biopsy was performed on April 25 and the biopsy of the transplanted kidney showed no signs of malignancy, granuloma or rejection. On April 29 PET-CT scan identified a mass in the transplanted kidney, multiple abdominal and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy (Fig. 1A and B), and inflammatory changes in the cerebellar tentorium and frontal lobes. Thirteen days prior to admission, he experienced acute altered consciousness. An enhanced cranial MRI demonstrated leptomeningeal enhancement in the bilateral frontal lobes (Fig. 1C). A lumbar puncture was performed. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed increased white blood cells of 1200 × 10⁶/L (reference:0 ~ 10 × 10⁶/L), total protein of 2837.6 mg/L(reference:<350 mg/L), adenosine deaminase of 6.8 U/L (reference:0 U/L), and decreased chloride of 108.6 mmol/L(reference:120 ~ 130 mg/L). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) for CSF detected the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (40 sequences) and TB culture was negative. On May 7, a repeat biopsy of the transplanted kidney showed granulomatous inflammation and multi-nucleated giant cell (Fig. 1D), and NGS of the biopsy tissue confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (2 sequences). The diagnosis of tuberculous meningitis and renal allograft tuberculosis was confirmed. Initial treatment with HRZE (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) for one week and steroid improved headache but not fever. He was then transferred to our hospital for advanced medical care.

On admission, his sputum and urine were sent for TB culture (finally turned out negative after 8 weeks). Repeated lumbar puncture revealed an intracranial pressure exceeding 330 mmHg. White blood cells in CSF were 125 × 10⁶/L (reference:0 ~ 10 × 10⁶/L), total protein was 1600 mg/L(reference:<350 mg/L), glucose was 2.02mmol/L (reference:3.5 ~ 5.5 mmol/L), chloride was106.0mmol/L (reference:120 ~ 130 mmol/L), and adenosine deaminase was 6.50U/L(reference:0U/L). CSF for TB-DNA (Mycobacterium Nucleic Acid Detection Assay, Chengdu Bioeasy Premium Biotechnology Co., Ltd. China) was positive. The Cycle Threshold value was 32.9 (Positive Reference: <40). But CSF for acid fast staining, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, and culture were all negative. Due to the established drug-drug interaction between rifampicin and tacrolimus, the anti-TB regimen was adjusted to include isoniazid, linezolid (600 mg daily), pyrazinamide, and levofloxacin. Dexamethasone 10 mg daily was prescribed for inflammation relief and 20% mannitol 125 ml three time daily was used to reduce intra-cranial pressure. The dexamethasone dosage was reduced to 7.5 mg once daily after one week and switched to oral prednisone 30 mg once daily two weeks later, and then tapered down by 5 mg each week. His body temperature returned to normal and headache abated, but hemoglobin declined to 57 g/L and creatinine rose to 115 umol/L after 2 months of treatment. Linezolid was discontinued and replaced with contezolid (400 mg twice daily), resulting in a hemoglobin rebound to 113 g/L within two weeks. One month later, headache and blurred vision occurred. A repeated cranial MRI indicated tuberculous meningoencephalitis complicated by hydrocephalus and possible brain abscesses. The regimen was intensified to include isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, moxifloxacin, and a higher dose of CZD (800 mg q12h), aligned with dexamethasone 10 mg daily and 20% mannitol 125 ml every 6 h. Dexamethasone was tapered down by 2.5 mg at two-week intervals. After four weeks of therapy, dexamethasone was switched to oral prednisone 40 mg once daily, followed by a weekly taper of 5 mg. This adjustment led to marked improvement in symptoms. No myelosuppression or renal dysfunction were recorded. A timeline of treatment and clinical events with laboratory parameters were presented in Fig. 2. The concentration of tacrolimus remained steady during the treatment. Moreover, CZD concentrations in serum and CSF were closely monitored using High-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS, AB SCIEX 4500 Series of Instruments, Canada) (Fig. 3). Follow-up CSF analysis confirmed the resolution of meningitis. He is in good physical condition with no disability. As of press time, his anti-TB treatment and adverse events monitoring are still ongoing. We recommend him a minimum total anti-TB treatment duration of 18 months, including a 6-month course of contezolid.

Discussion

Transplant recipients are at a high risk of TB reactivation due to immunosuppressive therapy [13]. The clinical symptoms of TB in these patients are frequently atypical, posing difficulties in early diagnosis. This, in turn, delays treatment and increases the risk of disseminated tuberculosis [14]. In this case, the patient initially exhibited unexplained fever and headache. Although early microbiological evidence was unavailable, a definitive diagnosis of renal allograft tuberculosis coupled with tuberculous meningitis was established through a combination of pathological kidney examination, imaging examination, and molecular tests of cerebrospinal fluid and renal allograft tissue.

Prior to the availability of drug susceptibility testing (DST) results for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, a conventional empirical anti-TB regimen consisting of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was initiated at a local hospital. However, clinical symptom improvement was not sustained. Taking into account potential drug interactions between rifampicin and immunosuppression, as well as linezolid’s proven evidence of bactericidal activity and potent blood-brain barrier penetration in tuberculous meningitis [15–17], we replaced rifampicin with linezolid. His symptoms improved, and he was discharged. Unfortunately, his haemoglobin declined progressively to 57 g/L over two months of treatment. We attributed the anemia to the common adverse effect of linezolid and discontinued linezolid [18]. We thus switched to a contezolid-containing regimen.

Although previous studies indicate that no dose modification is necessary for CZD in patients with renal impairment [19, 20], we initially administered CZD at 400 mg twice a day to enhance anti-tuberculosis therapy. However, clinical improvement was not significant, we thus increased the dosage to 800 mg twice daily. At the same time, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) was also doubted and steroid were restored. Following this adjustment, we monitored CZD concentrations in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid. We found concentrations of CZD in serum ranging from 6.21 to 28.03ug/mL, peak serum concentration occurring at 3–4 h after administration. In CSF, CZD concentrations ranged from 0.12 to 0.64 ug/mL, peaked at 6 h after administration. The brain-to-blood ratio ranged from 0.009 to 0.061. Prior studies have documented the rapid absorption and wide tissue distribution of CZD, noting a 90% plasma protein binding, and a time-to-peak serum concentration of about 2.5 h after administration [21, 22]. A case from China reported CZD’s ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier and reach therapeutical concentration. At 7 h post-administration at the 7th and 14th week, CZD concentration of CSF was 0.54 and 1.15 ug/mL and of serum was 9.36 and 9.64 ug/mL respectively [11]. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CZD against M.tuberculosis ranges of 0.125-4.0ug/mL [6]. The observed CZD concentrations in our patient’s CSF were above the minimum inhibitory concentration, suggesting high CSF permeability and bactericidal activity of CZD for the nervous system.

While receiving CZD-containing anti-TB treatment, our patient underwent close monitoring of blood cell counts, liver and kidney function, and tacrolimus concentration. No myelosuppression or renal abnormalities were observed. The concentrations of tacrolimus were stable during treatment. These observations align with other studies describing stable tacrolimus concentration in recipients with tuberculosis receiving CZD-included regimens [23, 24]. All these studies suggested minimal drug-drug interactions and safety of CZD in complex clinical situations where multiple medications are being administered.

Our study has certain limitations. Firstly, there is a lack of phenotypic drug susceptibility results because of negative TB culture in urine and CSF. Secondly, despite NGS identification of MTB sequences, resistance gene detection is absent, which restricts the ability to provide guidance for clinical drug selection. Thirdly, CZD concentrations in both serum and CSF were not monitored when CZD 400 mg twice daily was administered, whether effective bactericidal concentrations at this dose were achieved could not be determined.

Conclusion

CZD appears to be an up-and-coming anti-tuberculosis agent with satisfactory blood-brain barrier penetration and minimal drug interactions. This suggests its potential for treating disseminated tuberculosis, particularly for those with central nervous TB. Further researches are needed to fully assess the efficacy, safety, and optimal dosage of CZD in various clinical scenarios.
