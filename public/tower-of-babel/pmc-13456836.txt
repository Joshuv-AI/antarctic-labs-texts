Severe Chronic Anemia and Tumor-Mimicking Colonic Lesions as an Atypical Presentation of Crohn's Colitis
Authors: Ali Ghanem Al Masad, Mahdi Abdulhadi Al Muammar, Omar Reda Abdelmaksoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crohn's colitis may rarely present with severe bleeding and tumor-mimicking colonic lesions, creating concern for malignancy. A 20-year-old bedridden man with cerebral palsy had recurrent hematochezia and transfusion-dependent microcytic anemia, with hemoglobin 2.8 g/dL. Colonoscopy showed diffuse raised island-like plaques with vascular-pattern loss, rectal sparing, edematous ileocecal valve, and an oozing transverse-colon lesion requiring clipping. Multisite biopsies showed chronic active colitis with crypt distortion, cryptitis, and crypt abscesses, without granulomas, dysplasia, or malignancy. Tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction was negative; interferon-gamma release assay was unavailable. Crohn's colitis was favored by clinicopathologic correlation. Bleeding resolved after corticosteroids, infliximab, and azathioprine; hemoglobin improved to 9.9 g/dL.

INTRODUCTION

Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease with heterogeneous clinical, endoscopic, and histologic manifestations.1,2 Although abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and anemia are common clinical features, severe lower gastrointestinal bleeding with profound transfusion-dependent anemia is an uncommon dominant presentation and may create diagnostic uncertainty.1,3 When Crohn's colitis produces raised, mass-like, or pseudotumoral mucosal lesions, the differential diagnosis may include colorectal cancer, lymphoma, intestinal tuberculosis, the Behçet disease, ulcerative colitis, and inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified.3,4 Published tumor-mimicking Crohn's disease cases have most often involved localized abdominal, ileocecal, or cecal masses, frequently leading to surgical resection when malignancy, abscess, obstruction, or tuberculosis could not be excluded.5–8 We describe a bleeding-dominant, tumor-mimicking colitis phenotype in which Crohn's colitis was favored after repeat colonoscopy, systematic multisite biopsies, exclusion of major infectious and neoplastic mimics, and sustained clinical response to inflammatory bowel disease-directed therapy. The case emphasizes that chronic active colitis alone is not diagnostic of Crohn's disease; rather, diagnosis requires careful integration of clinical course, endoscopic distribution, histology, radiology, mimic exclusion, and treatment response.

CASE REPORT

A 20-year-old man with motor retardation cerebral palsy, bedridden status, oral feeding, and caregiver-dependent communication presented with 3 days of fresh bleeding per rectum mixed with stool, associated with abdominal distension but no reported abdominal pain. He had a 9-month history of recurrent hematochezia requiring transfusions and intermittent diarrhea, without fever or vomiting. There was no previous diagnosis of inflammatory bowel disease, and family history was negative for inflammatory bowel disease or malignancy. Limited communication related to cerebral palsy complicated precise assessment of abdominal pain, bowel frequency, bleeding pattern, and medication adherence. Consequently, diagnostic evaluation relied on caregiver history, witnessed bleeding episodes, serial hemoglobin trends, inflammatory markers, endoscopic findings, histology, and imaging. Cerebral palsy was not considered a direct cause of Crohn's colitis, but family support was central to monitoring treatment adherence, nutritional status, and adverse effects during immunosuppressive therapy.

He appeared markedly pale but hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination was soft with left lower quadrant tenderness. Digital rectal examination demonstrated fresh blood. Laboratory evaluation revealed severe hypochromic microcytic anemia, with hemoglobin 2.8 g/dL and iron studies confirming severe iron-deficiency anemia: serum ferritin 3.7 ng/mL, serum iron 2 μmol/L, and total iron-binding capacity 49 μmol/L. Vitamin B12 was normal. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with erythrocyte sedimentation rate 40 mm/hr and C-reactive protein 28 mg/L. Leukocyte count was 8.5 × 103/µL, platelet count was 277 × 103/µL, prothrombin time was 12.7 seconds, international normalized ratio was 1.1, and activated partial thromboplastin time was 30.5 seconds. Baseline liver enzymes and renal function were within normal limits, including total bilirubin 10.2 μmol/L, direct bilirubin 2.7 μmol/L, alkaline phosphatase 46 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 5 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 11 U/L, blood urea nitrogen 2.5 μmol/L, and creatinine 49 μmol/L. He received 4 units of packed red blood cells without transfusion reaction.

Evaluation for infectious and inflammatory mimics showed negative stool ova and parasite examination, negative stool culture, and negative Clostridioides difficile testing. Chest radiograph was unremarkable, tuberculin skin testing was negative, and tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction from colonic biopsies was negative; interferon-gamma release assay was not available. No empirical antituberculous therapy was administered because the available clinical, radiologic, endoscopic, and histologic data did not support active tuberculosis. The Behçet disease was also considered, but there was no history of recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, uveitis, pathergy, vascular thrombosis, or other systemic features to suggest this diagnosis.

Previous evaluations included colonoscopy in February 2025 describing an ascending-colon submucosal-appearing lesion suspicious for a tumor and small internal hemorrhoids; upper endoscopy showed mild gastritis. Computed tomography showed focal ascending-colon wall thickening with an apparent intraluminal abnormality and bilateral inguinal and pelvic lymphadenopathy, raising concern for lymphoma vs chronic inflammatory disease. Serial colonic biopsies in March and June 2025 demonstrated chronic active colitis with crypt architectural distortion, cryptitis, and crypt abscesses, without dysplasia or malignancy. Excisional biopsy of an inguinal lymph node showed reactive changes consistent with dermatopathic lymphadenopathy. Although earlier biopsies suggested chronic inflammatory bowel disease, immunosuppression was initially deferred because neoplasia, lymphoma, and tuberculosis had not been sufficiently excluded. During the index admission, repeated multisite biopsies, reactive lymph node histology, negative tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction, exclusion of malignancy, and persistent bleeding-dominant disease completed the clinicopathologic picture favoring Crohn's colitis.

During the index admission, upper endoscopy demonstrated gastric erythema with nodularity, and biopsies were obtained. Colonoscopy demonstrated scattered raised mucosal lesions in the ascending colon and an irregular transverse-colon lesion with surface oozing concerning for neoplasia; hemostatic clips were applied for hemostasis and localization. The terminal ileum appeared normal. Index imaging consisted of routine contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis, as dedicated computed tomography (CT) enterography was not available at our center. It showed no discrete colonic mass, obstruction, abscess, fistula, or small-bowel complication. The absence of CT enterography limited radiologic assessment of subtle small-bowel or transmural Crohn's disease (Table 1).

Repeat colonoscopy demonstrated diffuse involvement from the cecum to the sigmoid colon by multiple scattered lesions with raised edges described as island-like mucosal patches lacking vascular markings (Figure 1). Some lesions demonstrated mild oozing increased with irrigation, and hemostasis was achieved with hemostatic clips; a clip was also placed as a landmark (Figure 2). The ileocecal valve was enlarged and edematous, and the terminal ileum appeared normal. The rectum was endoscopically normal (Figure 1). The discontinuous colonic involvement with rectal sparing supported Crohn's colitis over classic ulcerative colitis, although rectal sparing is not entirely specific and may be seen in treated ulcerative colitis, pediatric-onset ulcerative colitis, or atypical colitis. In this case, rectal sparing was interpreted together with patchy colonic disease, ileocecal valve edema, histologic chronicity, and repeated absence of malignancy.

Histopathology from the ascending and transverse colon demonstrated chronic active colitis with crypt architectural distortion, cryptitis, and crypt abscesses, without granulomas, dysplasia, or malignancy; tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction was negative. Although granulomas may support Crohn's disease when present, their absence does not exclude the diagnosis. Therefore, Crohn's colitis was favored by clinicopathologic correlation rather than histology alone, integrating patchy colonic involvement, rectal sparing, ileocecal valve edema, repeated exclusion of malignancy, negative available infectious evaluation, and subsequent clinical response to inflammatory bowel disease-directed therapy. Gastric biopsy showed moderate focal inactive gastritis with Helicobacter pylori identified.

Before immunosuppression, prebiologic screening was completed. Tuberculosis screening included negative tuberculin skin testing, unremarkable chest radiography, and negative tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction; interferon-gamma release assay was unavailable. Viral screening showed nonreactive hepatitis B surface antigen, negative hepatitis B core antibody, reactive hepatitis B surface antibody, nonreactive hepatitis C antibody, and nonreactive HIV testing. Intravenous methylprednisolone 60 mg once daily was administered for 3 days, followed by oral prednisolone 40 mg daily for 2 weeks with taper by 5 mg weekly. Infliximab was started at 5 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, and 6, with planned maintenance every 8 weeks. The patient weighed 50 kg. Thiopurine methyltransferase and NUDT15 testing were unavailable at our center; therefore, azathioprine was maintained cautiously at 50 mg orally once daily with close complete blood count and liver enzyme monitoring. Helicobacter pylori gastritis was treated with a proton pump inhibitor, amoxicillin, and levofloxacin, reflecting local practice and regional antimicrobial-resistance considerations.

Bleeding resolved with hemoglobin stabilization. At 6-month follow-up, the patient had no recurrent hematochezia, and hemoglobin improved to 9.9 g/dL. Follow-up colonoscopy with repeat biopsies had not yet been performed because of clinical stabilization and logistical limitations; however, endoscopic reassessment is planned to document mucosal response and further exclude occult dysplasia or malignancy.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates a bleeding-dominant, tumor-mimicking colitis phenotype in which Crohn's colitis was favored after repeated clinicopathologic assessment. The most striking features were profound transfusion-dependent iron-deficiency anemia, recurrent hematochezia, diffuse raised island-like colonic mucosal lesions, rectal sparing, and repeated biopsies showing chronic active colitis without dysplasia or malignancy. Because chronic active colitis is not specific for Crohn's disease, the diagnosis in this case relied on integration of clinical course, endoscopic distribution, histologic chronicity, radiologic assessment, exclusion of major mimics, and response to inflammatory bowel disease-directed therapy.4

The main diagnostic challenge was distinguishing Crohn's colitis from inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified and atypical ulcerative colitis. Rectal sparing and patchy discontinuous colonic involvement favored Crohn's colitis over classic ulcerative colitis, whereas the normal terminal ileum and absence of granulomas limited diagnostic certainty. Granulomas may support Crohn's disease when present, but their absence does not exclude the diagnosis. Therefore, Crohn's colitis was favored by clinicopathologic correlation, while inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified remained an early diagnostic consideration.3,4

Published tumor-mimicking, tumefactive, or pseudotumoral Crohn's disease cases most often present as localized abdominal, ileocecal, or cecal masses, frequently prompting surgery because malignancy, abscess, obstruction, or tuberculosis cannot be excluded. In the comparable reports, diagnosis was commonly established on resection pathology after nondiagnostic or insufficient endoscopic sampling (Table 2).5–8 By contrast, the present case was dominated by recurrent hematochezia and profound transfusion-dependent anemia, with diffuse mucosal pseudotumoral colonic lesions rather than a single obstructing or palpable mass. Colectomy was avoided after repeated colonoscopy, systematic multisite biopsies, exclusion of major mimics, and sustained clinical response to inflammatory bowel disease-directed therapy.

This case also highlights why immunosuppression was not started after the earliest biopsies. Although previous biopsies suggested chronic inflammatory bowel disease, the endoscopic and radiologic appearance remained concerning for neoplasia or lymphoma, and tuberculosis had not yet been sufficiently excluded. During the index admission, repeated multisite biopsies, serial exclusion of dysplasia and malignancy, reactive lymph node histology, negative tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction, and persistent bleeding-dominant disease completed the clinicopathologic picture favoring Crohn's colitis and supported inflammatory bowel disease-directed therapy.

The differential diagnosis included colorectal malignancy, lymphoma, intestinal tuberculosis, the Behçet disease, chronic infectious colitis, ulcerative colitis, and inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified.3,4 Malignancy and lymphoma were considered because of the mass-like endoscopic appearance, previous radiologic concern, and lymphadenopathy; however, serial colonic biopsies showed no dysplasia or malignancy, and excisional lymph node biopsy showed reactive dermatopathic lymphadenopathy. Intestinal tuberculosis was considered because of geographic relevance and ileocecal-region involvement; however, chest radiography and tuberculin skin testing were negative, tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction was negative, and no clinicopathologic evidence supported active tuberculosis. Interferon-gamma release assay was unavailable. The Behçet disease was considered, but there was no history of recurrent oral or genital ulcers, uveitis, pathergy, vascular thrombosis, or other supportive systemic features.

This report has limitations. First, the absence of granulomas and normal terminal ileum limited diagnostic certainty, and inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified remained an early consideration. Second, dedicated CT enterography was not available at our center, limiting assessment of subtle small-bowel or transmural Crohn's disease. Third, interferon-gamma release assay was unavailable, although chest radiography, tuberculin skin testing, and tissue tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction did not support tuberculosis. Fourth, follow-up colonoscopy with repeat biopsies had not yet been performed because of clinical stabilization and logistical limitations; however, endoscopic reassessment is planned to document mucosal response and further exclude occult dysplasia or malignancy.

Despite these limitations, the case adds an extreme anemia phenotype to the spectrum of tumor-mimicking Crohn's colitis: hemoglobin 2.8 g/dL, diffuse island-like raised mucosal lesions, focal oozing requiring endoscopic hemostasis, and clinical response to corticosteroids, infliximab, and cautious azathioprine. In young patients with profound iron-deficiency anemia and mass-like colonic lesions, Crohn's colitis should remain in the differential diagnosis, but only after careful clinicopathologic correlation and exclusion of malignancy, tuberculosis, the Behçet disease, chronic infection, ulcerative colitis, and inflammatory bowel disease-unclassified.
