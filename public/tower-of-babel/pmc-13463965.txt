Cost-effectiveness of a real-time screening strategy for bacterial vaginosis using qPCR to prevent preterm birth: results from a randomized prospective multicenter study
Authors: Sandrine Loubiere, Anderson Loundou, Jean-François Cocallemen, Florence Fenollar, Florence Bretelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveConventional diagnosis tests for vaginal flora abnormalities in pregnant women are heterogeneous and not easily reproducible, and treatments have been shown insufficient if not combined with screening for recurrence. This paper investigates, alongside a randomised controlled trial, the cost-effectiveness of testing pregnant women for bacterial vaginosis with a point-of-care polymerase chain reaction screening combined with treatment and screening for recurrence (S&T) vs. standard of care.MethodsClinical and costs data of 6,671 pregnant women were collected from the AuTop trial. Administrative databases were used to fulfill resource utilization data. The perspective of the French health insurance was adopted, using national tariffs schedules when existed, such as for qPCR. The time horizon was less than one year, corresponding to the follow-up of the trial. The reduction in preterm birth rate was used as primary outcome and subgroup analyses were performed based on risk level.ResultsCompared to standard of care, S&T reduced the rate of preterm births (3.8% vs. 4.6%) while the mean costs were higher, leading to a ratio of €13,730 per preterm birth averted. For multiparous women at low-risk of preterm birth, S&T was less effective and more costly than standard care. For nulliparous women, S&T appeared to be both more effective and less costly (-€4,663 per preterm birth averted). Probabilistic sensitivity analyses confirmed this dominance.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that S&T with a point-of-care molecular test for bacterial vaginosis offers benefits in terms of preterm birth avoided among nulliparous pregnant women and is also expected to result in cost savings in this subgroup compared to standard care.Trial registryClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02288832. 7th October 2014.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00780-7.

Introduction

Preterm birth is the world’s leading cause of death in children under 5 years of age [1]. Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a well-known risk factor [2] that increases the risk of preterm birth from 2- to 7-fold according to the gestational age at diagnosis [3, 4]. A review of the literature and practices shows that conventional diagnosis tests for vaginal flora abnormalities, using clinical assessment, Amsel criteria or Nugent score, are heterogeneous and difficult to replicate, and that treatments prove ineffective unless combined with screening for recurrence [5–12]. Based on the literature, international and French recommendations advise against screening with conventional diagnostic tools in populations at low-risk of preterm birth [13, 14]. Our team and others showed that molecular quantification of Fannyhessea (named before Atopobium) vaginae and/or Gardnerella vaginae leads to a more reproducible and specific BV diagnosis compared with the standard Nugent score [15–17]. Such screening approaches based on a molecular diagnostic tool could have significant clinical and financial impacts, namely, first, a potential reduction in the rate of preterm birth and associated costs of caring for preterm newborns, and second, an increase in the cost of screening with point-of-care qPCR compared with low-cost Nugent scoring [18–20]. To date, only one cost-effectiveness analysis of a simple screen-and-treat program for common asymptomatic vaginal infections in pregnant women has been published [21]. In this study, the women were tested with a vaginal Gram stain, by the Nugent criteria, which does not identify morphotypes associated with BV, and testing was performed in the second trimester of pregnancy, which is probably too late to prevent pregnancy complications.

In this context, the literature as a whole calls for further studies of homogeneous populations and seems to indicate the value of the systematic screening and treatment of vaginal infections in pregnant women at the beginning of the second trimester [8, 22–25]. To address this issue, a multicentre randomised controlled trial (AuTop) was designed to investigate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of a screening strategy for BV based on molecular analysis using rapid qPCR testing, followed by treatment (i.e. named the screen-and-treat strategy or S&T) compared with usual care, in pregnant women before 20 weeks of gestation and at low risk of preterm birth.

In the current study, we reported the cost-effectiveness analysis nested within the AuTop trial.

Methods

Trial design

The AuTop trial was a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled study conducted in 19 French maternity hospitals (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02288832). Details of the rationale and design of the trial have been published previously [26]. In summary, from March 2015 to December 2017, 6,671 patients were enrolled in the AuTop trial; 3,333 were allocated to the S&T group, and 3,338 were allocated to the control group. The trial protocol was approved by the South Mediterranean Committee for the Protection of Research Subjects and the French National Agency of Medicine and Health Products Safety, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were enrolled and randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio using a web-based system, stratified by centre.

We performed a cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) in accordance with Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) and the French National Authority for Health (HAS) guidelines for economic evaluation [27, 28]. This study also follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Strategies compared

Patients in the S&T group underwent systematic molecular screening for BV via analysis of their self-collected vaginal samples, followed by a treatment prescription in case of BV diagnosis. Molecular biology-based screening consisted of a rapid diagnostic tool using specific quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays to quantify the DNA levels of F. vaginae and G. vaginalis (European Patent Office N°2087134), with high specificity, sensitivity, and positive and negative predictive values [6, 29]. Patients with BV returned vaginal control self-swabs after 15 days and once per month until 28 weeks of gestation. Women in the control group received usual care according to the standard practices in the maternal hospitals, with no BV screening using a quantitative real-time PCR. Health professionals were free to prescribe a standard vaginal swab if symptoms were present and to use Amsel or Nugent criteria for the diagnosis of BV.

Participants

The AuTop trial included pregnant women with a low risk of premature birth. Participants were pregnant women over 18 years of age before 20 weeks of gestation, regardless of their parity, with no history of preterm birth or late abortion and no major risk factors for prematurity (i.e., insulin-dependent diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus, hypertension, uterine malformation, conization, multiple pregnancies, or preexisting hypertension). All patients gave their informed signed consents.

The study was approved by the South Mediterranean Committee for the Protection of Research Subjects (number 14.026) and the French National Agency of Medicine and Health Products Safety (number 140500 A-41). All patients gave their informed signed consents.

Costing study

The economic analysis considered a health care payer perspective and all resource use data in relation to testing, treatment and management care of the mother and her new-borns until discharge were collected and measured. The screening costs included the initial vaginal swabs and subsequent control vaginal swabs for women who tested positive, extraction and rapid qPCR tests, and subsequent antibiotic treatments (either azithromycin 1 g, repeated after 48 h, or amoxicillin 2 g per day for 7 days, in cases of contraindication or treatment failure). Management care of the mothers included antenatal hospital admissions for complications during pregnancy, consultations and examinations with physicians, either through inpatient or outpatient care (i.e., transvaginal or obstetrical ultrasounds for monitoring), length of stay for delivery and associated diagnostic-related groups-based fee schedule (DRG) (i.e., vaginal delivery or caesarean section with or without complications, abortion with or without aspiration). All complications arising during pregnancy were recorded in the patients’ files; those associated with a hospital stay were valued according to the corresponding DRG and the length of stay. Neonatal care for full-term and preterm infants included staying in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (i.e., related to infant weight and gestational age, with or without problems, including new-born death), all transfers to another establishment and home inpatient care. Data on resource use were collected from patient questionnaires and supplemented by local administrative databases. To address the total cost for each patient, we multiplied the quantity of resource use by the unit costs and summed all categories of costs.

Unit costs are listed in Table S1. They were valued in 2022 euros (€) and estimated using data from the French National Hospital Database, the French register of pharmaceutical specialties and national tariffs. To note, the cost of PCR by point-of-care was €135 (RIHN N131 by Multiplex PCR reagents). This tariff covers the full cost of the test (supplies, equipment and professional fees). The time horizon was less than 1 year, corresponding to the follow-up period of the trial, so a discount rate was not applied.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The AuTop trial was designed and powered to evaluate differences in proportions of women with preterm births (birth at < 37 weeks of gestation) between the two groups. We considered this binary outcome measure for our base-case cost-effectiveness analysis. We calculated the additional costs per preterm birth averted due to the intervention by dividing the total costs in the S&T group by the primary outcome (i.e. average cost of BV screening and treatment minus additional costs of managing preterm birth).

Although dichotomizing continuous outcomes (i.e. using preterm delivery instead of mean gestational age) may be clinically relevant, they may reduce power or result in spurious results if many events occur around the threshold. Therefore, a secondary clinical outcome was explored in the cost-effectiveness analysis, measured by the gestational age at birth as a continuous variable (i.e. time in weeks between the start of pregnancy and delivery). To analyse this secondary clinical outcome, we used a competing risks regression analysis to ascertain whether the estimated Cox hazard ratio was sensitive to competing events, which included spontaneous abortion (at < 22 weeks), medical abortion or in utero death [30].

The cost-effectiveness analysis was performed in four steps: First, descriptive statistics were performed on effectiveness and costs using either proportions (%) or means (SDs or 95%CI). Second, differences in effectiveness were analysed and risk ratios (RRs) were estimated with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Difference in costs were taken as the difference in total mean costs between the two groups. A Poisson regression or a quasi-Poisson regression was considered when overdispersion of the count variable was observed, as appropriate for costs as the dependent variable. Covariates included the key predictor covariates for resource use (i.e., complications during pregnancy, delivery with complications, length of NICU stay) and treatment group, as well as demographic characteristics (age and educational level). Third, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICER) were estimated by computing the difference in costs divided by the difference in effectiveness. Fourth, bootstrap confidence intervals for differences in proportion of events or in means (effectiveness and costs) between the two independent groups were calculated. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) based on a Monte Carlo microsimulation with 5,000 replications was performed to account for uncertainty and individually variability. Scatter plots were used to visually represent the different ICERs generated, where the x-axis shows the difference in effectiveness and the y-axis shows the difference in costs between strategies. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEAC) were also constructed for various thresholds of willingness to pay per additional full-term birth. All descriptive analyses and regression models were produced using R software (version 4.3.1), with statistical significance set at a two-sided P value < 0.05; PSA was undertaken using TreeAgePro 2019.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on the results of the AuTop trial [26]: with a statistically significant interaction term (Pinteraction = 0.003), the effect of the S&T intervention in the subgroup of nulliparous women was a 38% reduction in risk of preterm births. In view of this finding, the experience of childbirth (i.e. nulliparous versus multiparous status) appeared to be a particularly relevant parameter to be addressed in the present CEA.

Results

From March 2015 through December 2017, a total of 6671 patients were randomly assigned to either S&T group (n = 3333) or control group (n = 3338) (Fig. 1). Three participants in each group (< 1%) withdrew consent before the end of the trial; respectively, 144 and 156 (< 4.5%) women were lost to follow up. The demographic characteristics and medical history at baseline were reported in Table 1. The mean age was 30.6 years, 19.3% had experienced previous abortion and 22.2% had experienced early miscarriage.

Results from the base-case analysis

Resource use and costs

Table 2 outlines the use of resources by each group and details of average costs are also provided. With regard to the base-case analysis, there were no differences in medical care consumed by the mothers between the two groups, in terms of radiologic examinations, episodes of complications or hospital admissions during pregnancy. There were no differences in number of transfers or length of stay for neonates between the two groups.

On average, the cost associated with the screening and treatment of positive cases was €203 per woman. Mothers in the S&T group had a slightly shorter length-of-stay for neonates at delivery and slightly lower associated care costs than mothers who received standard care (mean neonatal costs, €807 vs. €982).

The total average costs were €5,190 in the S&T group vs. €5,161 in the control group, resulting in a mean difference in costs of €29 [95%CI -€225 to +€282].

Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness

Table 3 presents the results for the effectiveness outcomes (both primary and secondary clinical outcomes). For the whole population, the rate of preterm birth was lower in the S&T group (3.8%) than in the control group (4.6%) (RR 0.83 [95%CI 0.66–1.05]). The difference was 26 preterm births averted in the study sample, which corresponds to 8 additional full-term births per 1,000 women. Considering the secondary outcome, the average gestational age was 37.58 weeks in the S&T group and 37.52 weeks in the control group, resulting in a difference of 0.06 (RR 1.00 [95%CI 0.99–1.01]) week of gestation.

The first columns of Table 4 present the results of the cost-effectiveness analysis of the base case. The S&T strategy compared with usual care provided an additional cost per preterm birth averted at €13,730. Regarding the secondary outcome, the ICER of the S&T strategy was €483 per additional week of gestation.

Subgroup analysis

Among S&T and control groups, respectively 1671 (50.1%) and 1767 (52.9%) nulliparous women were enrolled. Table S2 in the appendix reports a comparison of characteristics at baseline according to randomly assigned group in the subgroup of nulliparous and multiparous women, with no significant differences found.

Conversely to the base-case analysis, more pronounced treatment effects were found in the subgroup of nulliparous women: the total costs were €5,634 in the S&T group vs. €5,862 in the control group (mean difference €-228) (Table S3); 43 preterm births were avoided (RR 0.62 [95%CI 0.45–0.84]) as well as indications of additional weeks of gestation (RR 1.01 [95%CI 1.00–1.02]) among women receiving the intervention (Table 3), resulting in cost savings of €1,200 per additional week of gestation (Table 4). The primary cost drivers in these two subgroups were hospitalization costs related to the delivery, whether complicated or not. Within each subgroup, the difference in mean costs between the S&T and control groups was primarily due to the additional costs of vaginal swabs and PCR tests in the intervention group.

For multiparous women, the S&T strategy was more expensive (difference €371, 95%CI €151 to €415) (Table S3) and less effective (primary outcome, RR 1.30 [95% CI, 0.90–1.87]) (Table 3).

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

Probabilistic sensitivity analyses were performed on the primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes. Here, we present the results for the secondary outcome, which refers to outcomes for individuals rather than aggregated outcomes for the study group of women (see Supplementary figures for PSA for the primary endpoint).

The bootstrap distribution of the ICER for the base-case analysis showed that 43% of the ICERs were located in the higher-right quadrant of the scatterplot (Fig. 2a), indicating lower costs and higher effectiveness for the S&T group compared with the control group. This suggests that there was less than a 50% likelihood that S&T dominate the standard of care. The CEAC shows that at a threshold of €2,000 per additional week of gestation, the probability that S&T is cost-effective was > 80% (Fig. 3a). The results were subject to the same uncertainty, whatever the effectiveness criteria considered (i.e. categorical or continuous).

If we now consider the subgroup analysis, the scatterplot of the bootstrap distribution in nulliparous women (Fig. 2b) shows that 99% of the ICERs for S&T compared with the standard of care were in the lower right quadrant of the scatterplot, suggesting a dominant strategy, i.e. S&T was more effective and less costly, and the CEAC confirms this dominance (Fig. 3b).

In the multiparous subgroup, the probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed the dominance of the standard strategy over the S&T strategy (Figs. 2c and 3c).

Discussion

Bacterial vaginosis is a well-known risk factor for preterm birth. Molecular diagnosis of BV is now available. It provides an objective, reproducible, quantitative diagnosis of vaginal flora abnormalities, particularly for the emerging pathogenic species Atopobium vaginae. The main criticism that has been levelled at molecular diagnosis is its cost. However, its cost-effectiveness in the screening and treatment of BV during pregnancy on preterm births has not been evaluated to date. A recent literature review focusing on preterm deliveries between 32 and 36 weeks’ gestation has shown that for a child born preterm alive at birth, the average cost of care during the initial period of hospitalization could be between $5,041 (€4,357 in 2025) and $35,635 (€30,798) depending on the study and the term, compared to a cost that could be between $1,334 (€1,153) and $3,860 (€3,336) for a child born at full term [31]. By rationally estimating even higher inpatient costs for newborns before 32 weeks, any intervention aimed at reducing the rate of preterm birth or increasing the term at delivery would be both clinically and economically relevant for patients and society.

The only cost-effectiveness analysis of a simple screen-and-treat program for common asymptomatic vaginal infections in pregnancy has been published in 2006 [21]. This study compared the Nugent technique versus no testing among a sample of 4050 pregnant women and showed cost savings through the first 6 years of life of the preterm infant. Our cost-effectiveness analysis included 6671 pregnant women, which represents a larger population for estimating the effects of screening and treatment of BV on preterm births and the associated costs. We showed that the screening and treatment of bacterial vaginosis using qPCR, involving a maximum of 4 vaginal swabs for positive women, and the prescription of antibiotics (azithromycin or amoxicillin) within a maximum of 24 to 48 h of detection, is a safe, effective and potentially cost-effective intervention compared to usual care depending on the threshold acceptable to the stakeholders. Finally, our trial showed the greater effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of screening and treatment for BV using molecular tool compared with standard of care in nulliparous women. To date, no biological mechanism has been identified to account for the discrepancy in outcomes observed between nulliparous and multiparous women. One potential explanatory factor is the significantly lower treatment rate among multiparous women (79% compared to 85% in nulliparous women). Furthermore, existing literature has documented notable differences in the prevalence and impact of risk factors for preterm birth between these two subgroups, as highlighted by Goldenberg et al. [31] and Mercer et al. [32]. These observations lead us to hypothesize that the disparity in results may be attributable to a higher inherent risk of preterm birth among nulliparous women, compared to the low-risk multiparous women included in our study. Specifically, the reduced risk of preterm birth in nulliparous women may stem from an initially unidentified risk profile early in pregnancy. Conversely, the multiparous women in our cohort exhibited a particularly low risk, as they had no history of preterm birth or late miscarriage. These findings underscore the need for further research to assess the impact of an optimized screening and treatment strategy among pregnant women at high risk of preterm birth. We are currently conducting this research, based on the hypothesis that a molecular biology-based screening and treatment strategy for women with a history of preterm birth or late miscarriage could be effective in reducing the number of preterm births.

The subgroup analysis shows a more obvious results, with a significant difference in clinical effects among the nulliparous population. In this subpopulation, S&T was significantly associated with a decrease in the preterm birth rate, and its relative consequence was an increase in the gestational age at birth. This effect on the prematurity rate was coupled with a significant decrease in the costs of care for neonates, offsetting the full cost of screening and treating the pregnant women. The costs associated with preterm birth have been the subject of publications over the last decade, showing that the cost per newborn varied dramatically depending on gestational age at birth, as already mentioned above [33, 34]. At a more macroeconomic level, it was estimated that premature births cost the French health care system over 1.5 billion euros per year in 2014 [35]. More recently, a UK study based on data from 28,000 very preterm and term neonates between 2014 and 2018 showed that total annual neonatal costs were £262 million (€304 million), 96% of which was attributable to routine hospital care [36]. Further research evaluating the budgetary impact of the S&T strategy should be carried out to expand our results and inform policy-makers about the expected changes in health system expenditure following the adoption of a new preventive strategy.

To note, current international and French recommendations advising against BV screening in low-risk populations were based on conventional tools and called for randomized trials using new tools [11]. Similarly, differences in the choice of treatments represented a major factor in the difficulty in interpreting the results of previous studies. US and UK guidelines recommend the use of metronidazole or clindamycin for pregnant women at high risk of preterm delivery with a diagnosis of BV [11, 37]. The National Institute for Clinical Excellence (NICE) recommends that either oral or vaginal antibiotics be considered (i.e., metronidazole, clindamycin or amoxicillin) [13]. Thus, the literature does not promote the choice of one treatment over another. In the AuTop study, using azithromycin or amoxicillin according to allergy and tolerance, the initial success rate was 46.8%, the recurrence rate was 39.2%, and the success of second-line treatment was 33.3% [26].

The points discussed above led us to propose a list of prerequisites for a cost-effective screening approach in nulliparous pregnant women: (i) screening early during pregnancy to provide treatment as soon as possible; (ii) using molecular diagnostic techniques; (iii) checking treatment efficacy in women who test positive by measuring treatment failure or recurrence with monthly vaginal control samples up to 28 weeks of gestation; and (iv) offering women who test positive appropriate standard treatment and monitoring for recurrence with the same techniques.

For a long time, microbiological diagnosis by molecular analysis was reserved for specialized laboratories, which is no longer the case, with the routine performance of real-time qPCR tests. In our analyses, the cost of molecular diagnostic tests remains a key factor. In this light, it is important to emphasize that the cost of PCR testing is expected to decrease, due, in particular, to routine use, amortization of equipment and the growing number of uses for this equipment. This means that the S&T strategy has the potential to become both significantly more effective, as already demonstrated in this study, and significantly less expensive. Across Europe, the integration of molecular diagnostics is expanding as healthcare systems shift toward preventive and personalized medical approaches. Nevertheless, the lack of uniform reimbursement frameworks across countries could hinder the widespread adoption of advanced molecular tests. Furthermore, there may be disparities in access to this type of rapid diagnostic testing among European States, as healthcare systems vary in how they assess the cost-effectiveness of healthcare innovations. The growing prevalence of bacterial and viral outbreaks in Europe, combined with increasing demand for rapid diagnostic solutions and advances in pharmacogenomics, underscores the need for cost-effectiveness studies such as those we propose.

The study strengths included that the cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted with a large population from a multicenter RCT. This analysis was implemented after conducting effectiveness analysis on the global population of the AuTop trial and subgroup analyses (i.e., with the p value for the interaction term Group * Parity being statistically significant, Pinteraction=0.003) [38]. To carry out this subgroup analysis, the requirements in terms of evidence, statistical and analytical prerequisites, and feasibility in practice were met. The choice of a competing risk analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of the S&T strategy, which considers pregnancy loss as a nonnegligible event during pregnancy, makes it feasible to maximize the available information. Finally, the question of the generalizability of our results must be discussed here. Although the results of this cost-effectiveness study are generalizable to the French population, based on data national facility data and unit costs derived from French national databases, the results are intrinsically linked to the healthcare system on which they are based and, therefore, may not be readily generalizable to healthcare systems that do not have a universal healthcare system or a robust publicly-funded healthcare option.

A potential limitation of this study lies in the follow-up period of less than one year, which prevents us from measuring the additional costs associated with caring for preterm infants beyond their discharge from hospital. However, a longer follow-up period would have been difficult to achieve because of the risk of a large number of patients being lost to follow-up after discharge from the maternity hospital. In the present study, the follow-up success rate was excellent, and > 95% of the sample had data for the primary outcome and > 90% had data for the cost outcome. Another limitation of our study is the lack of data on risk factors specific to nulliparous women compared with multiparous women. Participants were selected based on classic risk factors for preterm birth. Schuster et al. suggest that factors such as low socioeconomic status, early vaginal bleeding, and urinary tract infections may be prognostic factors for spontaneous preterm birth among low-risk nulliparous women; In a recent exploratory review, numerous risk factors for preterm birth were identified, including, but not limited to, toxicological and psychological risk factors, and risk factors related to medical history, such as previous surgery (for example, bariatric operation or cervical excision) [39, 40]. We were unable to address all risks stratification in our study. Future research should aim to identify indicators of risk for preterm birth specific to nulliparity.

In light of our findings, our results support a screening strategy for bacterial vaginosis that targets nulliparous women rather than a universal screening approach. Indeed, early and appropriate management of bacterial vaginosis in nulliparous women could limit preventable complications, improve neonatal outcomes, and reduce costs associated with prolonged hospital stays or intensive care.
