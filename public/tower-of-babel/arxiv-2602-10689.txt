An Efficient Energy Stable Structure Preserving Method for The Landau-Lifshitz Equation
Changjian Xie, Yingxi Miao, Haocheng Yang

Abstract
One of the main difficulties in micromagnetics simulation is the norm preserving constraints ‖ 𝒎 ‖ = 1 \|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\|=1 at the continuous or the discrete level. Another difficulty is the stability with the time step constraint. Using standard explicit integrators leads to a physical time step of sub-pico seconds, which is often two orders of magnitude smaller than the fastest physical time scales. Direct implicit integrators require solving complicated, coupled systems. Another major difficulty with the projection method in this field is the lack of rigorous theoretical guarantees regarding its stability of the projection step. In this paper, we introduce an first order method. Such a method is structure preserving based on a combination of a Gauss–Seidel iteration, a double diffusion iteration and a Crank-Nicolson iteration to preserve the norm constraints.

Introduction

Ferromagnetic materials find widespread application in data storage technologies, largely due to the bistable nature of their intrinsic magnetic ordering, commonly referred to as magnetization. The temporal evolution of magnetization in these systems is principally dictated by the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation
9
,
5
. This equation incorporates two fundamental terms governing magnetization dynamics: a gyromagnetic term, responsible for energy conservation, and a damping term, which models energy dissipation.
The LLG equation constitutes a vector-valued, nonlinear system characterized by a point-wise constant magnitude constraint on the magnetization vector. Significant research efforts have been dedicated to devising efficient and numerically stable methods for micromagnetic simulations, as summarized in review articles such as
8
,
4
.

To handle the point-wise constant magnitude constraint on the magnetization vector, the methods are mainly divided into the following categories:

1.

Projection methods: By projecting the numerical solution onto the unit sphere, the constraint of constant modulus is maintained, with representative work such as GSPM
12
.Although this method is simple for engineering applications, spherical projection is a nonlinear operation, which brings certain challenges to numerical analysis. Review paper refer to
6
and reference therein.

2.

Lagrange multiplier methods: such a method constructs efficient, robust higher-order schemes with different Lagrange multiplier via a predictor-corrector method, and further develops length-preserving and energy-dissipative schemes at the cost of solving one nonlinear algebraic equation
3
. Such a method need to use a construction for extra Lagrange multiplier. Another method is generalized scalar auxiliary variable (GSAV) approach to preserve the constraints, see
10
.

3.

Fully implicit methods: such a method have great stability and accuracy. However, the nonlinear system is resolved by
7
,
1
.

A notable limitation persists:
most existing methods are limited to projection methods to handle the norm preserving constraints. This projection will preserve the magnetization direction, but with normalized magnitude. Such a projection step due to its nonlinearity bring a huge challenge in stability and convergence analysis.
To address this gap, this paper presents a naive first-order accurate numerical scheme for solving the LLG equation with an existing method as a stable solution and a Crank-Nicolson’s type predictor to preserve the normalizing constraint. Therefore, there’s no constraints applied, just using the model itself to get a stable results. We also conduct comprehensive computational tests to verify the numerical stability of the proposed method. These tests further reveal that the accuracy and stability.

2
The physical model and governing equation

The Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation forms the fundamental basis of micromagnetics, providing a rigorous description of the spatiotemporal evolution of magnetization in ferromagnetic materials by incorporating two key physical phenomena: gyromagnetic precession and dissipative relaxation
9
,
2
. In nondimensional form, this governing equation is expressed as

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{t}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}-\alpha{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times({\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}\times{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}})

(1)

subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\frac{\partial{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}}{\partial{\bm{\nu}}}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(2)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=1,2,3
) represents the bounded domain of the ferromagnetic material, and
\bm{\nu}
is the unit outward normal vector on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. This boundary condition ensures no magnetic surface charge, a physically appropriate assumption for isolated ferromagnetic systems.

The magnetization field
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a three-dimensional vector field satisfying the pointwise constraint
|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|=1
, stemming from the quantum mechanical alignment of electron spins in ferromagnets. The first term on the right-hand side of
eq.

1
describes gyromagnetic precession, where magnetic moments precess around the effective field
\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}
. The second term represents dissipative relaxation, with
\alpha>0
being the dimensionless Gilbert damping coefficient that governs the rate of energy dissipation into the lattice.

From the perspective of the Gibbs free energy functional, the effective field
\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}
is obtained as the functional derivative of the Gibbs free energy
F[\mbox{\boldmath$m$}]
with respect to the magnetization, i.e.,
\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}=-\delta F/\delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
. his functional incorporates all relevant energy contributions in ferromagnetic systems—exchange, anisotropy, magnetostatic (stray), and Zeeman energies—and is given by

F[\mbox{\boldmath$m$}]=\frac{\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2}}{2}\left\{\int_{\Omega}\left(\epsilon|\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|^{2}+q\left(m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2}\right)-2\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$m$}-\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\right)\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\right\}.

(3)

Here,
\mu_{0}=4\pi\times 10^{-7}\,\text{H/m}
is the vacuum permeability,
M_{s}
is the saturation magnetization, and
\epsilon
and
q
are dimensionless parameters defined subsequently. The vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}
denote the externally applied magnetic field and the stray field, respectively. For uniaxial ferromagnetic materials with a single easy axis, the effective field
\bm{h}_{\text{eff}}
decomposes into distinct physical components, yielding the explicit expression

\displaystyle{\bm{h}}_{\text{eff}}=\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}-q(m_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+m_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3})+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e},

(4)

where
\epsilon=C_{\text{ex}}/(\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2}L^{2})
and
q=K_{u}/(\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2})
. Here,
L
is the characteristic length scale,
C_{\text{ex}}
is the exchange constant (controlling short-range spin alignment), and
K_{u}
is the uniaxial anisotropy constant (representing the energy cost for deviation from the easy axis). The unit vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3}=(0,0,1)
efine the hard axes perpendicular to the uniaxial easy axis, and
\Delta
is the Laplacian operator in
d
-dimensional space.
For Permalloy (NiFe), a common soft ferromagnetic material in spintronics, standard parameter values from the literature are:
C_{\text{ex}}=1.3\times 10^{-11}\,\text{J/m}
,
K_{u}=100\,\text{J/m}^{3}
, and
M_{s}=8.0\times 10^{5}\,\text{A/m}
. The stray field
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}
originates from magnetic charge distributions at domain boundaries and material surfaces and is mathematically represented by the integral expression

\displaystyle{\mbox{\boldmath$h$}}_{\text{s}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\nabla\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\mbox{\boldmath$y$}|}\right)\cdot{\bm{m}}({\bm{y}})\,d{\bm{y}},

(5)

which is a formulation that exhibits long-range spatial correlations. A critical computational advancement for practical micromagnetic simulations is that for rectangular domains
\Omega
, the evaluation of
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}
can be efficiently computed via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
11
, which reduces the asymptotic computational complexity from
O(N^{d})
to
O(N^{d}\log N)
for
d
-dimensional grids, enabling large-scale simulations.

To facilitate numerical discretization, we introduce the composite source term

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$f$}=-Q(m_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+m_{3}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3})+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{s}+\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{e}.

(6)

which aggregates the anisotropy, stray field, and external field contributions. Substituting this source term into
eq.

1
, the LLG equation is re-expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{t}=-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times(\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$})-\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times(\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}).)

(7)

Leveraging the vector triple product identity
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\times({\bm{b}}\times{\bm{c}})=(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\cdot{\bm{c}}){\bm{b}}-(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\cdot{\bm{b}}){\bm{c}}
and the pointwise constraint
|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|=1
(which implies
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\cdot\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}=0
via time differentiation), we simplify
eq.

7
to an equivalent formulation that is more amenable to stable numerical discretization:

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{t}=\alpha(\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$})+\alpha\left(\epsilon|\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$m$}|^{2}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$f$}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$m$}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times(\epsilon\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}+\mbox{\boldmath$f$}).

(8)

3
Proposed method

For the construction of the structure preserving method, we set
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}=0
,
\epsilon=1
and
\alpha=0
for
eq.

7
, we have the LLG equation below,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{t}=-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}.

(9)

If we consider the simple linear vectorial equation

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{t}=-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}\times\mbox{\boldmath$a$},

(10)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}^{T}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
is a constant vector. We use the Crank-Nicolson method to
eq.

10
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{2}\times\mbox{\boldmath$a$},

(11)

which is norm preserving, since that if
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}
do the inner product for both sides, leading to

\displaystyle\|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\|_{2}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}.

After rearranging the compact form for
eq.

11
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}\\
\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n+1}\\
m_{2}^{n+1}\\
m_{3}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(a_{2}m_{3}^{n}-a_{3}m_{2}^{n})\\
m_{2}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(a_{3}m_{1}^{n}-a_{1}m_{3}^{n})\\
m_{3}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(a_{1}m_{2}^{n}-a_{2}m_{1}^{n})\end{pmatrix}

We then have another form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n+1}\\
m_{2}^{n+1}\\
m_{3}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}\\
\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}\\
\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{3}&1&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{2}&\frac{1}{2}\Delta ta_{1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}\\
m_{2}^{n}\\
m_{3}^{n}\end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}\\
m_{2}^{n}\\
m_{3}^{n}\end{pmatrix}

where

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{S}\begin{pmatrix}1+\beta^{2}a_{1}^{2}&-2\beta a_{3}+\beta^{2}a_{1}a_{2}&2\beta a_{2}+\beta^{2}a_{1}a_{3}\\
2\beta a_{3}+\beta^{2}a_{1}a_{2}&1+\beta^{2}a_{2}^{2}&-2\beta a_{1}+\beta^{2}a_{2}a_{3}\\
-2\beta a_{2}+\beta^{2}a_{1}a_{3}&2\beta a_{1}+\beta^{2}a_{2}a_{3}&1+\beta^{2}a_{3}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

where
S=\det(A)=1+\beta^{2}(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2})
and
\beta=\frac{\Delta t}{2}
.

From
eq.

11
, we can get

\displaystyle m_{h}^{n+1}-m_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,(m_{h}^{n+1}+m_{h}^{n})\times\mathbf{a}.

(12)

Suppose C is a skew-symmetric matrix, then we know

\displaystyle(m_{h}^{n+1}+m_{h}^{n})\times\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle=C(m_{h}^{n+1}+m_{h}^{n}).

So we can get

\displaystyle m_{h}^{n+1}-m_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle=-\beta\,C(m_{h}^{n+1}+m_{h}^{n}).

(13)

By rearranging the terms,we know

\displaystyle(I+\beta C)\,m^{n+1}

\displaystyle=(I-\beta C)\,m^{n}.

(14)

So we can get the form

\displaystyle m^{n+1}=Am^{n},

(15)

\displaystyle where\;A=(I+\beta C)^{-1}(I-\beta C).

(16)

Taking transpose of
eq.

16
and using
(M^{-1})^{\top}=(M^{\top})^{-1}
,

A^{\top}=(I-\beta C)^{\top}\bigl((I+\beta C)^{-1}\bigr)^{\top}=(I-\beta C)^{\top}\bigl((I+\beta C)^{\top}\bigr)^{-1}.

Since
C^{\top}=-C
, we have
(I-\beta C)^{\top}=I+\beta C
and
(I+\beta C)^{\top}=I-\beta C
, hence

A^{\top}=(I+\beta C)(I-\beta C)^{-1}.

Consequently,

A^{\top}A=(I+\beta C)(I-\beta C)^{-1}(I+\beta C)^{-1}(I-\beta C)=I.

Thus
A
is an orthogonal matrix.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\|Ax\|_{2}^{2}=(Ax)^{\top}(Ax)=x^{\top}(A^{\top}A)x=x^{\top}x=\|x\|_{2}^{2},

so
\|Ax\|_{2}=\|x\|_{2}
for all
x
. By definition of the induced
2
-norm,

\|A\|_{2}=\max_{x\neq 0}\frac{\|Ax\|_{2}}{\|x\|_{2}}=1.

Hence
\|A\|_{2}
is bounded by
1
and is independent of the time step
\Delta t
.

For
eq.

9
, we propose the following structure preserving schemes:

1.

Sheme I: explicit for
\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{2}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n},

(17)

which gives a CFL-type condition for the stability.

2.

Sheme II: implicit for
\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{2}\times\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1},

(18)

which poses a difficulty for the nonlinear systems with high complexity.

3.

Scheme III: Semi-implicit method,

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}}{2}\times\Delta_{h}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{h}^{n+1}.

(19)

To be specific, we propose

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{3}^{n+1}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{2}^{n+1}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{3}^{n+1}&1&\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{1}^{n+1}\\
\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{2}^{n+1}&-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{1}^{n+1}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n+1}\\
m_{2}^{n+1}\\
m_{3}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{2}^{n+1}m_{3}^{n}-\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{3}^{n+1}m_{2}^{n})\\
m_{2}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{3}^{n+1}m_{1}^{n}-\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{1}^{n+1}m_{3}^{n})\\
m_{3}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t(\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{1}^{n+1}m_{2}^{n}-\Delta_{h}\tilde{m}_{2}^{n+1}m_{1}^{n})\end{pmatrix}

The trick is to handling the
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{h}^{n+1}
. If
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{h}^{n+1}=(I-k\Delta_{h})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}^{n}_{h}
,
such a scheme is proved to be mildly better than CFL condition. We should get a much better estimation for
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}}_{h}^{n+1}
.

The Gauss-Seidel projection method by
12
is given below,

\displaystyle m_{1}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=m_{1}^{n}+\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{2}^{n}\cdot m_{3}^{n}-\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{3}^{n}\cdot m_{2}^{n},

\displaystyle m_{2}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=m_{2}^{n}+\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{3}^{n}\cdot m_{1}^{n+1}-\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{1}^{n+1}\cdot m_{3}^{n}

\displaystyle m_{3}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=m_{3}^{n}+\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{1}^{n+1}\cdot m_{2}^{n+1}-\Delta t\Delta_{h}g_{2}^{n+1}\cdot m_{1}^{n+1},

then with a projection step
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}=\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}}{|\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}|}
. Note that here we have

\displaystyle\frac{m_{i}^{n+1}-m_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\Delta_{h}m_{i}^{n+1},

which gives
m_{i}^{n+1}=(I-\Delta t\Delta_{h})^{-1}m_{i}^{n}:=g_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}-\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n})\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}=\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}=\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{n}\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}=\Delta t\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n+1}\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}=\Delta t\Delta_{h}\mbox{\boldmath$g$}_{h}^{n+1}\times\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{h}^{n}.

4.1
Accuracy and efficiency tests

To simplify the accuracy verification, we set parameter
\epsilon=1
and forcing term
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}=0
in the governing model
eq.

7
. Analytical exact solutions are derived for both one-dimensional (1D) and three-dimensional (3D) scenarios to serve as benchmarks for error quantification.

For the 1D case, the exact magnetization solution
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}
is:

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}=\left(\cos(\cos(\pi x))\sin t,\sin(\cos(\pi x))\sin t,\cos t\right)^{T},

while the corresponding 3D exact solution is:

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}=\left(\cos(XYZ)\sin t,\sin(XYZ)\sin t,\cos t\right)^{T},

where
X=x^{2}(1-x)^{2}
,
Y=y^{2}(1-y)^{2}
and
Z=z^{2}(1-z)^{2}
.

These exact solutions satisfy the governing equation
eq.

7
when the forcing term is defined as
\mbox{\boldmath$f$}_{e}=\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}+\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}\times\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}+\alpha\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}\times(\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e}\times\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{e})
. They also comply with the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, ensuring consistency with simulation constraints.

To isolate the temporal approximation error from spatial discretization effects, the spatial resolution in the 1D test is fixed at
h=5\times 10^{-4}
—a sufficiently fine grid that makes spatial error negligible compared to temporal error. The Gilbert damping parameter is set to
\alpha=0.01
, and simulations run until final time
T=0.1
. Under this configuration, the measured error primarily reflects temporal discretization inaccuracy.

The 3D temporal accuracy test faces inherent constraints from spatial resolution, as excessively fine grids incur prohibitive computational cost. To balance spatial and temporal error contributions, we adopt a coordinated refinement strategy for spatial mesh sizes (
h_{x},h_{y},h_{z}
) and temporal step-size (
\Delta t
) tailored to the proposed method’s order:
\Delta t=h_{x}^{2}=h_{y}^{2}=h_{z}^{2}=h^{2}=T/N_{0}
. Here,
N_{0}
is a refinement level parameter, with specific values given in subsequent results. Consistent with the 1D test,
\alpha=0.01
, and the final time
T
is specified later. The first order temporal accuracy is verified from
table

7
and
table

9
for 1D and 3D tests, respectively.

5
Conclusions and discussions

In this paper, we propose a structure preserving method with first order accuracy in time and second order accuracy in space. Such a method combines an implicit Gauss-Seidel iteration, a double diffusion iteration and a Crank-Nicolson’s type iteration. The frist two step with an implicit Gauss-Seidel iteration, a double diffusion iteration gives a stable configuration for the higher order term. The last step to preserve the norm constraint. Such a method is constructed only based on the equation, not using a projection step. In the future work, we will analyze the stability of such a method and apply for micromagnetics simulations.
