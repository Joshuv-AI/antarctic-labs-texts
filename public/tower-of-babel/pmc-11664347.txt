Medication use and risk of reflux oesophagitis
Authors: Ren Ueta, Shiori Komori, Kumiko Umemoto, Masahiro Hata, Erika Masuda, Kana Seto, Yuriko Nishiie, Keigo Suzuki, Yuya Hisada, Yuka Yanai, Yuki Otake, Hidetaka Okubo, Kazuhiro Watanabe, Naoki Akazawa, Chizu Yokoi, Junichi Akiyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveReflux oesophagitis (RO) is one of the most common diseases encountered by gastroenterologists and primary care physicians. However, few epidemiological studies have investigated the association of medication use and RO. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of RO and its risk factors, particularly with respect to medication use.MethodsThis retrospective, cross-sectional study included consecutive patients who underwent oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD) and were assessed using questionnaires at the National Center for Global Health and Medicine (Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan) between October 2015 and December 2021. The questionnaire collected data on patient characteristics, medical history, smoking and alcohol consumption, and medications that patients were taking at the time of OGD.ResultsAmong the 13 993 eligible patients, the prevalence of RO was 11.8%. Multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that male sex (OR=1.52 (95% CI 1.35 to 1.72), p<0.001); obesity (OR=1.57 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.77), p<0.001); smoking (OR=1.19 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.38), p=0.026); alcohol consumption (OR=1.20 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.35), p=0.002); diabetes (OR=1.19 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.39), p=0.029); hiatal hernia (OR=3.10 (95% CI 2.78 to 3.46), p<0.001); absence of severe gastric atrophy (OR=2.14 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.56), p<0.001); and the use of calcium channel blockers (CCBs) (OR=1.22 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.40), p=0.007), theophylline (OR=2.13 (95% CI 1.27 to 3.56), p=0.004), and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (OR=1.29 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.61), p=0.026) were independent predictors of RO.ConclusionRO was present in 11.8% of patients. Use of CCBs, theophylline, and NSAIDs were independent predictors of RO.

Introduction

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is one of the most common diseases encountered by gastroenterologists and primary care physicians. GORD can be characterised by symptoms sufficient to impair an individual’s quality of life or complications that result from long-term exposure of the oesophagus to gastric contents.1 Reflux oesophagitis (RO) is diagnosed when a mucosal break is found on endoscopy. Patients with more severe forms may develop serious complications, such as bleeding, stricture, and Barrett’s oesophagus. The prevalence of RO in Japan has recently been increasing due to a decline in the rate of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection and the adoption of Western eating habits.2 It is also associated with increased healthcare use and medical costs. Thus, physicians need to provide appropriate management and care based on an established knowledge of risk factors.

The risk factors for RO proposed in previous studies include advanced age, male sex, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, hiatal hernia, absence of gastric atrophy, and diabetes.3 Few studies have investigated the epidemiological impact of medications on RO. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) and theophylline are reportedly positively associated with RO.4 5 The effect of statins on RO development is controversial, since conflicting results regarding their association have been reported.6 7 Although non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) have been shown to be risk factors for GORD and GORD symptoms,8 9 few reports have addressed their association with RO. Additionally, previous studies on the risk factors for RO have included patients taking acid-suppressive agents (proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), potassium-competitive acid blockers (PCABs), and histamine 2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs)) that may affect RO development. By excluding acid secretion inhibitors, risk factors for RO can be assessed more clearly. This study aimed to investigate RO prevalence and its risk factors, particularly in relation to medication use.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective, cross-sectional study included consecutive patients who underwent oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD) and were assessed using questionnaires at the National Center for Global Health and Medicine (Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan) between October 2015 and December 2021. We used an electronic endoscopic database (SolemioEndo, Olympus, Japan). The indications for OGD were determined by each gastroenterologist. The exclusion criteria were as follows: incomplete questionnaires or text that could not be read; current use of acid-suppressive agents PPIs, PCABs, and H2RAs; history of upper gastrointestinal (GI) tract surgery, or upper GI malignancies.

Ethics

This study was conducted at the National Center for Global Health and Medicine Center Hospital in Tokyo, Japan. The study protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research involving human subjects. An opt-out informed consent protocol was used.

Questionnaires (evaluation items)

We used a detailed questionnaire to collect the following data: patients’ characteristics (age, sex, height, and weight (free text style)), medical history (history of upper GI surgery, upper GI malignancies, and diabetes (check box style)), social history (smoking and alcohol consumption (check box style)), list of all product names of medications that patients were taking at the time of OGD (free text style). We checked medications that have been identified as potential risk factors for RO, such as CCBs, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), nitrates, theophylline, alpha-blockers, beta-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis), antiplatelets, anticoagulants, bisphosphonates, acetaminophen, statins, NSAIDs, and corticosteroids. The presence of RO, hiatal hernia, and severe gastric atrophy were evaluated using endoscopic examination. RO was defined as mucosal breaks in the esophagogastric junction and was categorised into Los Angeles classification grades A, B, C, or D.10 11 Hiatal hernia was identified if there was axial prolapse of a portion of the stomach through the diaphragmatic oesophageal hiatus. Gastric atrophy was graded according to the Kimura–Takemoto classification, and severe gastric atrophy was defined as O-2 and O-3 atrophy.12 GORD symptoms (heartburn and regurgitation) were evaluated using the Gastrointestinal Symptoms Rating Scale (GSRS), and scores of ≥3 points were defined as having GORD symptoms.13

Data collection

All analyses used data from questionnaires. Clinical data, including endoscopic findings and questionnaire responses, were collected as part of routine clinical practice and retrospectively re-analysed for the purposes of this study. We adopted an opt-out consent procedure in line with institutional guidelines, as the study involved secondary use of previously collected clinical data. The opt-out procedure was designed to ensure that participants were fully informed about the study and had the opportunity to withdraw if desired. Information about the study, including its purpose, was made publicly available on our institution’s website. Participants were informed that they could opt out by contacting the principal investigator at any time. The study protocol included only data from participants who completed the questionnaire, ensuring voluntary participation. Notably, no patients opted out after this process was implemented.

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed variables are reported as means (SD), and skewed variables are reported as medians (IQRs). Categorical variables are reported as n (%). Continuous variables were tested for deviation from normality using the Kolmogorov−Smirnov test. In the univariate analysis, Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, and χ2 test were used to evaluate the differences between patients with and without RO, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis using the enter method was performed to assess the predictive factors for RO by entering significant variables in univariate analysis. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (V.26.0; IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Of 53 367 patients, 43 044 underwent OGD in an outpatient setting between October 2015 and December 2021. Of these, 16 818 patients did not complete the questionnaire, 9527 currently used acid-suppressive agents, 1770 had a history of upper GI tract surgery, and 936 had upper GI malignancies. Eventually, 13 993 patients were eligible for inclusion in this study (figure 1). The median age was 68.0 (IQR 20) years, and the median body mass index (BMI) was 22.5 (IQR 4.6) kg/m2. There were 7528 (54%) male patients, 8117 (58%) advanced-aged patients (≥65 years), 3342 (24%) patients with obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m2), 1875 (13%) smokers, 7451 (53%) alcohol drinkers, and 1779 (13%) patients with diabetes. Heartburn was present in 2855 (20%) patients, and regurgitation was present in 2660 (19%) patients. A total of 4254 (30%) patients had hiatal hernia. The medications used included CCBs (21%), ARBs (13%), nitrates (0.6%), theophylline (0.9%), alpha-blockers (1%), beta-blockers (4%), ACEis (16%), antiplatelets (9%), anticoagulants (3%), bisphosphonates (3%), acetaminophen (2%), statins (21%), NSAIDs (6%), and corticosteroids (2%). Prevalence of RO

The prevalence of RO was 11.8% (n=1649). The proportions of the RO grades were grade A in 8.4% (n=1176), grade B in 2.8% (n=390), grade C in 0.4% (n=59), and grade D in 0.2% of patients (n=24).

Risk factors for RO

The univariate analysis revealed that an advanced age; male sex; obesity; smoking; alcohol consumption; diabetes; the presence of hiatal hernia; absence of severe gastric atrophy; and use of CCBs, theophylline, ACEis, bisphosphonates, statins, and NSAIDs were significantly associated with the presence of RO (table 1). Multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that male sex (OR=1.52 (95% CI 1.35 to 1.72), p<0.001); obesity (OR=1.57 (95% CI 1.40 to 1.77), p<0.001); alcohol consumption (OR=1.20 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.35), p=0.002); smoking (OR=1.19 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.38), p=0.026); diabetes (OR=1.19 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.39), p=0.029); hiatal hernia (OR=3.10 (95% CI 2.78 to 3.46), p<0.001); the absence of severe gastric atrophy (OR=2.14 (95% CI 1.77 to 2.58), p<0.001); and the use of CCBs (OR=1.22 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.40), p=0.007), theophylline (OR=2.13 (95% CI 1.27 to 3.56), p=0.004), and NSAIDs (OR=1.29 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.61), p=0.026) were independent predictors of RO (table 2).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of RO was 11.8%. In Japan, an epidemiological report including 42 studies conducted between 1966 and 2006 revealed that the prevalence of RO ranged from 1.4% to 52.1%. The mean prevalence was 10.6% in outpatients and 7.6% in those who underwent regular health check-ups.14 Recently, the prevalence of RO among patients visiting single centres for health check-ups in Japan from 2010 to 2019 increased to approximately 15%.15 In this study, the prevalence was lower than those reported recently. Thus, the prevalence of RO significantly differs depending on the study population.

Other well-known risk factors for RO include male sex, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and diabetes,1621 which were also observed in this study. Older age is generally considered a risk factor for RO due to decreased lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure and an increased prevalence of hiatal hernia, which increases acid exposure in the oesophagus.22 However, in this study, advanced age did not emerge as a significant risk factor, likely due to the high prevalence of H. pylori infection among older adults in Japan,23 which is a protective factor that can reduce gastric acid secretion and counteract RO development.2427 Another known protective factor is gastric atrophy, classified as closed (C-type) and open (O-type), with O-type being associated with reduced acid secretion.28 O-type gastric atrophy is more common in our institution due to the high prevalence of patients undergoing screening after H. pylori eradication, which may explain the relatively low prevalence of RO in our study.

Regarding medications, CCBs, theophylline, and NSAIDs have been suggested as risk factors for RO in this study. CCBs and theophylline are well-known LOS-relaxing agents, and manometric studies have indicated that they decrease LOS pressure in humans and rabbits.29 30 CCBs also increase the oesophageal acid exposure time. In a study that used a pH monitor, oesophageal acid exposure was prolonged after nifedipine administration.31 However, few epidemiological studies have assessed the association between LOS-relaxing agents and the risk of RO.3 4 This study strengthens the credibility of previous reports suggesting that CCBs and theophylline are risk factors for RO.

In addition to these established associations, our study also suggested a possible relation between NSAID use and RO. NSAIDs are an obvious cause of gastric and duodenal ulcers, and it has been suggested that NSAIDs may cause oesophageal ulceration and that aspirin consumption is a possible risk factor for oesophageal inflammation.32 Several reports have shown that NSAID use is a risk factor for RO or GORD.33 34 Several mechanisms of action underlying NSAIDs inducing RO have been discussed. One is the alleged ‘pill-induced oesophagitis’: The oesophagus is locally injured by the oral administration of various tablets containing NSAIDs, which often results in painful and severe ulcers. Some patients with GORD have oesophageal motility abnormalities that may slow the passage of ingested NSAIDs, making the oesophageal mucosa more susceptible to tablet damage.35 36 Another mechanism is mucosal damage caused by NSAIDs. Lanas et al studied the effects of aspirin and pepsin on the oesophageal mucosa of rabbits. Exposure of the oesophageal mucosa to acidified aspirin and pepsin significantly increased mucosal damage and barrier dysfunction compared with control experiments using acid saline or acid pepsin alone. Furthermore, prostaglandin E2 administration before acidified aspirin decreased mucosal damage.37 38 Other reports have shown that 100 mg aspirin exacerbates RO in the oesophageal mucosa of rats.39 These findings suggest that the oral administration of NSAIDs may be a risk factor for RO. Our study strengthens the evidence supporting these associations by confirming them in a large clinical dataset. The identification of NSAIDs as a risk factor for RO is a novel aspect of the present findings.

This study has several strengths. First, we used a relatively large sample size, with more than 13 000 individuals. Second, we thoroughly checked current medications and evaluated the association of each medication on the risk of RO in patients not taking acid-suppressive agents.

However, this study also has some limitations. First, our findings may not be generalisable because this study was conducted at a single referral hospital; therefore, the study population may have been limited. Second, an assessment of H. pylori status was not carried out. Third, the evaluation of RO depended on the endoscopist, which might have caused interobserver variability. Fourth, only the type of medication was surveyed, and no analysis of differences by dosage was performed. Fifth, the study was retrospective and used data collected at a single time point, so could not assess the causes or progression of RO over time.

In conclusion, RO was present in 11.8% of the patients in this study. Medications such as CCBs, theophylline, and NSAIDs were independent predictors of RO. These findings emphasise the importance of considering medication use when assessing RO risk, and further research is warranted to explore the need for medication discontinuation or change in patients with RO who take these drugs.
