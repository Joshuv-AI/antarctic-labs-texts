Genome-wide identification, structural analysis, and heterologous functional inference of FvPAT10, a protein S-acyl transferase in woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca)
Authors: Maryam Shahbazi, Alireza Motallebi-Azar, Antonio Masi, Nasser Mahna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProtein S-acyl transferases (PATs) mediate S-acylation, a reversible post-translational modification that attaches long-chain lipids, typically palmitate, to cysteine residues, thereby impacting protein trafficking, membrane association, stability, signaling, and stress responses in plants.ResultsThis study characterizes 22 FvPAT genes, focusing on FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 in woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca), a diploid model species of the Rosaceae family, regarding their genomic organization, structure, and expression, with heterologous functional validation of FvPAT9 and FvPAT10. Using yeast (akr1 mutant) and Arabidopsis (atpat10 mutant) complementation, we confirmed FvPAT10 (FvH4_1g10780) restores growth in atpat10 mutants via its protein’s conserved DHHC cysteine, unlike FvPAT9 (FvH4_6g14950), which complemented yeast but not Arabidopsis, revealing functional divergence. This cross-species validation uniquely elucidates FvPAT10’s specificity, distinguishing it from FvPAT9 and confirming its homology to AtPAT10, implicated in cell expansion, division, vascular patterning, and fertility. Genome-wide in silico analysis identified 22 FvPAT genes across seven chromosomes. These genes were named after Arabidopsis homologs, and their proteins contained a conserved DHHC domain and predominantly localized to the endomembrane system. Phylogenetic and structural analyses highlighted diverse gene architectures, while qRT-PCR revealed tissue-specific expression, with FvPAT10 prominent in ripe fruit, suggesting a potential role in fruit development.ConclusionsThis work establishes a foundation for understanding the functions of FvPAT genes in F. vesca, providing insights for molecular breeding and a robust framework for future PAT gene studies in fruit crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07871-2.Keywords: Woodland strawberry, Fragaria vesca, FvPAT, Protein S-acyl transferase, S-acylation

Background

Diploid Fragaria species, such as Fragaria vesca, play a crucial role in molecular studies due to their small genome size, which makes them ideal for genetic mapping and reverse genetics strategies [1]. They serve as valuable models for investigating important genes in fruit crops within the Rosaceae family and are highlighted for functional genomics studies [2, 3]. Diploid Fragaria species also provide genetic resources for molecular breeding and have been instrumental in addressing limitations associated with cultivated polyploid plants [4]. The diploid F. vesca has been crucial in understanding gene regulation and the responses of polyploid plants to environmental stresses and climate change [5, 6].

Protein S-acyl transferases (PATs) are enzymes that catalyze S-acylation, a reversible post-translational modification attaching fatty acids to cysteine residues via a conserved Asp-His-His-Cys (DHHC) motif, regulating protein trafficking, stability, and signaling [7, 8]. In plants, protein S-acylation has been shown to have a wide range of functions, including roles in plant growth, development, stress signaling, and environmental responses [9–11]. As a result, S-acylation of plant proteins can be found at different cellular membranes, including the endoplasmic reticulum, the golgi apparatus, or the plasma membranes [12]. AtPAT10, a key protein S-acyl transferase, plays a critical role in Arabidopsis development and salt tolerance, as its mutants exhibit dwarfism and impaired stress responses [13]. Despite F. vesca’s standing as a valuable molecular model for the Rosaceae family, and the recognized importance of PAT10 in plant growth, development, and stress signaling, the functional roles of FvPAT genes, notably FvPAT10, remain unexplored, and the FvPAT family remains incompletely characterized, limiting advances in molecular breeding.

This study addresses this gap by conducting a genome-wide identification and characterization of the FvPAT gene family in Fragaria vesca. We identified 22 FvPAT genes, named based on Arabidopsis homologs, and validated the S-acylation activity of FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 using yeast and Arabidopsis complementation tests, with FvPAT10 notably restoring growth in the Arabidopsis atpat10 mutant. Through in silico analyses of gene structure, chromosomal distribution, and topology, and qRT-PCR expression profiling across tissues and developmental stages, we elucidate the roles of FvPATs in strawberry development and stress responses. This work establishes a foundation for understanding PAT functions in F. vesca and informs strategies to enhance the resilience of the Rosaceae crop.

Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Arabidopsis SALK T-DNA line, SALK_018436, was obtained from Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC) and PCR-genotyped using primers, LBb1.3, SALK018436RP, and SALK018436LP (Table S1), in two paired PCR reactions. Seeds were surface sterilized by soaking in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by bleach (1% sodium hypochlorite) with one drop of Tween 20 for 5 min. The bleach was removed by washing the seeds with sterile distilled water 5 times. The surface-sterilized seeds were placed on a solid 1/2 MS medium at pH 5.7. Seeds were cultured initially at 4 °C in the dark for two days, then transferred to the growth chamber at 21 ± 1 °C under a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod (LD).

The seeds from perpetual flowering LD accession Hawaii-4 (H4, PI551572) of the woodland strawberry, Fragaria vesca L. ssp. vesca f. alba (Ehrh.) Staudt were used. This Fragaria vesca accession is available commercially (https://strawberryseedstore.com/store/Fragaria-vesca-ssp-vesca-Hawaii-4-p140266115) as well as from germplasm repositories. Experimental research and field studies on plants, including the collection of plant material, complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. The seeds were surface sterilized as described before. The surface-sterilized seeds were placed on a solid 1/2 MS medium at pH 5.7, containing 0.8% phytoagar and 1% sucrose. Seeds were cultured initially at 4 °C in the dark for one week and then transferred to the growth chamber (LD) to germinate. High-pressure sodium (HPS) lamps at 120 µmol m− 2 s− 1 supplemented natural light in the phytotron. Seedlings with one or more true leaves were transferred to the soil, always in the same chamber. Plate-grown seedling; stolon, root, stem, leaf of 35-day-old soil-grown WT plants; flowers (2 days after flowering); and fruit tissues from different fruit developmental stages, including small green, large green, white, turning, ripe (8, 18, 26, 32 and 40 days after flowering, respectively) were collected, stored at −80 °C and used for RNA extractions and gene expression analysis.

Plasmid construction

The plasmid constructs were created using the Gateway System. Total RNA was extracted from young leaves of woodland strawberry plants with the E.Z.N.A. Plant RNA Kit (Omega). cDNA was synthesized using the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CWBIO) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Database analysis identified two candidates for FvPAT10 (FvH4_6g14950 and FvH4_1g10780). The coding regions of FvPAT10, FvPAT9 (FvH4_6g14950), and FvPAT10 (FvH4_1g10780) were PCR-amplified using PrimeSTAR Max high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Takara). The resulting fragments were purified from gel using the Gene JET gel extraction kit (Thermo Scientific), and the quality was assessed with a Nanodrop and gel electrophoresis. The confirmed fragments were then used as a template to construct the attB-flanked coding regions.

To create the FvPAT9/10-DHHS point mutant, PCR-based mutagenesis was performed using point mutant primers FvPAT9/10-DHHS-F and FvPAT9/10-DHHS-R. In the first round of PCR, two DNA fragments were generated using the primer pairs FvPAT9/10-F and FvPAT9/10-DHHS-R or FvPAT9/10-DHHS-F and FvPAT9/10-R. The second round of PCR used these two DNA fragments as templates along with the primer pair FvPAT-att-9/10-F and FvPAT-att-9/10-R to replace cysteine with serine in the DHHC motif. The PCR conditions were as follows: 30 s of template pre-denaturation at 98˚C, followed by 10 s of template denaturation at 98˚C, 5 s of primer annealing at 55˚C, and 5 s of primer extension at 72˚C for a total of 34 cycles.

Both attB-flanked products, FvPAT9/10 and FvPAT9/10-DHHS, were purified and recombined into the attP-containing donor vector pDONR™/Zeo (Invitrogen) using BP Clonase™ to generate entry clones pDONR-FvPAT9/10 and pDONR-FvPAT9/10-DHHS, following the manufacturer’s standard protocols (Gateway technology). The sequences of FvPAT9/10 and the point mutation in FvPAT9/10-DHHS were confirmed by sequencing. Using the same methods, the FvPAT-att-9/10-Ns-R primer was used to create the fragments without a stop codon to attach to the C-terminal V5 epitope tag of the pYES-DEST52 vector for protein detection by Western blot (Invitrogen).

For expression in yeast, these constructs were recombined into the Gateway™ destination vector pYES-DEST52 (Invitrogen) using LR Clonase™. Similarly, FvPAT9/10 and FvPAT9/10-DHHS were recombined into the plant binary vector pH7FWG2-294 for expression in plants. Vector maps are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S5). The sequences of all primers used are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

S-acylation activity analysis in yeast and Arabidopsis

The transformation methods for yeast cells followed the procedures outlined by Hemsley [9]. The empty pYES2 vector was transformed into wild-type yeast BY4741 and akr1 to serve as positive and negative controls, respectively. For growth tests, all four samples (BY4741, akr1, akr1-FvPAT9/10, and akr1-FvPAT9/10-DHHS) were cultured in YNB with 2% D-raffinose and diluted with sterile water to an OD600 of 1.0. A series of dilutions (1/5, 1/10, 1/20, and 1/40) were subsequently prepared. Eight microliters of each dilution were spotted onto two identical solid basic yeast media plates containing 2% D-raffinose and 2% D-galactose to induce protein expression. One plate was incubated at 28 °C, while the other was kept at 37 °C. Images of the plates were captured on the fourth day using an Epson flatbed scanner.

Total proteins from akr1 yeast cells expressing FvPAT9 and FvPAT9-DHHS were extracted and analyzed via western blotting using anti-V5 primary antibodies and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bethyl).

Agrobacterium tumefaciens harboring specific gene constructs (FvPAT9, FvPAT9-DHHS, FvPAT10, and FvPAT10-DHHS) were transformed into the Arabidopsis thaliana SALK T-DNA mutant line of atpat10 (SALK_018436) using the floral dipping method described by Clough and Bent (1998), with two rounds of dipping at five-day intervals [14]. To select transformants, approximately 10,000 seeds collected from the dipped plants were surface sterilized and spread on 1/2 MS media containing 100 µg/ml Cefotaxime (to eliminate Agrobacteria) and 30 µg/ml Hygromycin (to select for transgenic plants). The plates were placed at 4 °C for 48 h, then they were subjected to a regime of 4–6 h light, 48 h dark, and 24 h light [15]. The plates were then returned to the growth room for normal cultivation. Positive transgenic lines exhibited longer hypocotyls. Transgenic seedlings were subsequently transferred to soil and grown in the growth room under long-day conditions, as previously described. Transgenics were screened by PCR using primers based on the vector (CaMV-35 S promoter) and gene-specific reverse primers. Additionally, PCR-based genotyping was employed to identify homozygous Salk T-DNA insertion plants. The sequences of all primers used are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Microscopy

To observe cell morphology, 20 µl of each culture was transferred into 3 ml of basic yeast media (YNB + 3 amino acids) containing 2% D-raffinose and 2% galactose. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 6 days. From each sample, 1 ml of cells was stained with 1 µg/ml DAPI (final concentration) at room temperature. The cells were then observed using DIC light microscopy and UV light with a 90i Eclipse microscope, and images were captured with a Nikon C1 LSCM camera.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from various woodland strawberry tissues, including seedlings, juvenile and mature leaves, stem, stolon, flower, and fruits at five developmental stages (small green, large green, white, turning, and ripe). The RNeasy PowerPlant Kit (Qiagen) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. One µg of total RNA was used for first-strand cDNA synthesis with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific). Briefly, samples were incubated at 65 °C for 5 min to eliminate RNA secondary structures. The reaction mixture was then incubated at 42 °C for 60 min, followed by 70 °C for 5 min to inactivate the enzyme. The synthesized cDNA products were stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

The RT-PCR for atpat10-FvPAT10 Arabidopsis plants was performed using 1 µl of cDNA in a 15 µl reaction volume with 2xEs Taq Master Mix (CWBIO). The reaction conditions followed the manufacturer’s protocol and consisted of 30 cycles. The housekeeping gene GAPc was amplified as an internal control.

Real-time PCR reactions were carried out with an Applied Biosystems™ StepOne™ qPCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) using Power SYBR green master mix (Life Technologies). The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 10 min for initial denaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. All qRT-PCRs were run in three biological and three technical replicates, and the relative transcript levels were calculated by the 2ΔCt method with the FvActin gene as an internal control [16]. The sequences of all primers used are listed in Table S1.

Identification of PAT genes in woodland strawberry

The amino acid sequences of the AtPATs (AtPAT1–AtPAT24) were downloaded from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) website [12], and the sequences of the OsPATs (30 proteins) were downloaded from the Phytozome1 database [17]. The Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) PAT10 (At3g51390) protein sequence was used as a query for BLASTp searches. The central DHHC-CRD in the obtained sequences was used to identify FvPAT proteins. BLASTP searches were performed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)2 (taxid:57918), Genome Database for Rosaceae (GDR)3, and Phytozome databases, specifically targeting the woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca) genome data v4.0.a2 [18]. Homologous sequences were identified using BLASTp with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10− 5. All hits reported in this study showed E-values ≤ 7 × 10− 9, indicating high statistical significance. Detailed information regarding the identified woodland strawberry PAT genes, encompassing genomic, transcript, coding region, and peptide lengths, as well as chromosomal locations, was acquired from the Phytozome database. The physicochemical properties of the identified FvPAT proteins, such as molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric point (pI), were calculated using the ProtParam database4.

Chromosomal mapping, gene structure, and multiple sequence alignment

Gene structure was drawn using the Gene Structure Display Server5. Chromosomal positions of FvPATs were plotted with MG2C6 [19]. L-INS-I (recommended for proteins with one conserved domain and large gaps) and G-INS-i (recommended for proteins with global homology) algorithms of MAFFT [20] were used for protein alignments7. The combination of both alignments was performed using the COMBINE program provided by T-COFFEE [21]8. The protein alignments were used for further analysis using Jalview [22], and phylogenetic analysis.

Phylogenetic, motif, and protein topology analysis

A phylogenetic tree using the maximum likelihood (ML) method was performed using the PhyML 3.09, which automatically tests different settings to provide the best ML tree [23], and VT + R was chosen as the best model. Tree robustness was further tested using a bootstrap method with 100 replicates (automatically restricted by the pipeline). The best tree provided by PhyML 3.0 was used in this analysis, and the tree was visualized using the MEGA program package version 11.0.11 [24]. To construct the neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree, MEGA 11.0.11 software was applied, using the NJ method and Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) model by partial deletion with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL v6.8.1) operated for the display and management of phylogenetic trees10. TMHMM 2.011 was used for the determination of protein topology. The main motifs of FvPATs were characterized by the MEME program version 5.5.512 and visualized by TBtools-II v. 2.0 [25].

Subcellular localization and gene expression

The prediction of subcellular localization was implemented through the WoLF PSORT 13 and CELLO v.2.514. The gene expression data of FvPATs for anther, carpel, cortex, embryo, ghost (seed coat + endosperm), leaf, ovule, pith, pollen, seed, seedling, style, wall, flower, microspore, perianth, and receptacle were collected from the Bio-Analytic resource for plant biology15 [26–28]. The data was normalized, and the heatmap was drawn using GraphPad Prism v 8.4.2.

Prediction of protein structure

Protein features, GO ID, and GO term were extracted from BUSCA16 and the Gene Ontology (GO) chord plot was drawn by SRPLOT17. The models of structures were predicted by SWISS-MODEL18.

Statistical analysis

All experiments, consisting of three independent repeats, were analyzed using IBM SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2013. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s tests (P ≤ 0.05) of mean comparison were applied to show statistical differences between means.

Results

Analysis of the NCBI, phytozome, and GDR databases concluded that 22 genes encoding PAT proteins in woodland strawberry. The overall information includes gene ID, Gene symbol, NCBI Nucleotide Accession, Chromosome number, Location in the chromosome, and Genomic nucleotide length of woodland strawberry PAT genes are summarized in Table 1. The phylogenetic analysis and analysis of multiple alignments of 24 AtPATs and 22 FvPATs revealed that two proteins of the 22 proteins harboring the DHHC-CRD domain were candidates for FvPAT10 (FvH4_6g14950 and FvH4_1g10780) due to their conserved sequences and clustered with a high bootstrap value with AtPAT10 (Figure S1).

Complementation of the akr1 mutant yeast cells by woodland strawberry PATs

To investigate the functionality of FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 proteins, the enzyme activity was tested using a yeast-based complementation assay [9]. In this experiment, the akr1 strain was selected for its DHHC-dependent palmitoylation defects, with 37 °C inducing instability in non-complemented cells [29]. The growth rate decreases, and the round shape mononucleate cells turn into bar-shaped multinucleate cells at high temperature (37 °C) [29]. The temperature-sensitive mutant yeast (akr1) was transformed with the FvPATs (9 or 10) or the point mutant form of them (replaced Ser with Cys in the DHHC motif). Comparison of transgenic cells growth at 28 °C and 37 °C helped us to find out whether these FvPATs have the S-acyl transferase function and the Cys residue in the DHHC domain is necessary for it.

The akr1 harboring FvPAT9 or FvPAT9C180S showed better growth compared with FvPAT10 or FvPAT10C196S expressed in akr1 at 28 °C. At the high temperature condition (37 °C), FvPAT9 could restore the growth defect of the yeast PAT knockout mutant akr1 to some extent, while the point mutant form of it (FvPAT9C180S) couldn’t grow (Fig. 1B). For deeper study, the phenotype of cells was investigated, and the nuclei were revealed by DAPI staining. The bar-shaped multinucleate cells of akr1 cells are similar to akr1-FvPAT9C-S (arrows), whereas the shape of akr1-FvPAT9 cells is close to round-shaped mononucleate cells of WT (Fig. 1A). akr1, akr1-FvPAT10 and akr1-FvPAT10-DHHS had similar growth at 28 °C condition and less than WT. Interestingly, none of these cells (except WT) grew at 37 °C, indicating that neither FvPAT10 nor FvPAT10C196S could rescue the growth defect of akr1 (Fig. 1D). Relatively, slightly elongated cells which some of which were multinucleate (Fig. 1C, arrows), and an elongated budding site in akr1, akr1-FvPAT10, and akr1-FvPAT10C-S were obvious in microscopic analysis, while WT cells were round with an ordinary budding site. In summary, the growth assay revealed that FvPAT9 could complement the function of AKR1, while FvPAT10 couldn’t. The S-acyl transferase activity of AKR1 protein has been demonstrated in a previous study [30]. Generally, the morphology and growth of the complemented akr1 yeast cells with FvPAT9 looked more like WT than the akr1 mutant. By contrast, none of the point mutant forms of FvPATs (FvPAT9DHHS and FvPAT10DHHS) and FvPAT10 expressing in akr1 mutant yeast could rescue the defect of sensitive to high temperature akr1 cells at 37 °C, which proved FvPAT9 has S-acyl transferase activity and the Cys residue in the DHHC motif is necessary for the functionality of FvPAT9.

Complementation test in the mutant Arabidopsis atpat10 line

The phylogenetic analysis and analysis of multiple alignments revealed that two candidates for the homolog protein of the AtPAT10 protein (FvPAT9 and FvPAT10) were clustered with a high bootstrap value. Moreover, these shared the DHHC-CRD domain sequence, CS-X1-CN-X1-EQPPRSKHC-X1-DC-X1-RCVLQFDHHC-X3-G-X1-C, suggesting their evolutionary conservation (Figure S1). In our study, yeast complementation results showed that FvPAT9 could rescue the growth defect of the yeast mutant akr1 at 37 °C, whereas FvPAT10 could not do so, suggesting that FvPAT9 might exhibit PAT activity (Fig. 1B and D). Previous studies showed, SALK_018436 is a mutant line of Arabidopsis AtPAT10 loss of function [31]. For confirmation of the initial results, the SALK T-DNA mutant line of atpat10 (SALK_018436) was transformed with FvPAT9, FvPAT9-DHHS, FvPAT10, or FvPAT10-DHHS. FvPAT10 could rescue the defect of the atpat10 mutant line, and its function lies in the DHHC domain.FvPAT10 (FvH4_1g10780), which has 1047 base pairs of nucleotides and encodes a protein of 39 kDa, with 11 exons, 10 introns, is one of the PATs within the family of 22 PATs in the woodland strawberry (Table 2). Using the online software TMHMM v2.0, it is predicted that FvPAT10 has 4 transmembrane domains (TMD) that are located between the 42nd- 61 st, 76th- 98th, 211th- 230th, and 245th- 267th amino acid positions (Table 3). The DHHC-CRD, which is the core S-acyl transferase activity functional domain, is predicted to be cytosolic and located between the second and the third TMDs. Further structural analysis indicated this gene is located in chromosome Fvb1 (Table 1).

FvPAT9 (FvH4_6g14950) consists of 1002 nucleotide base pairs and encodes a 37 kDa protein, featuring a single exon and no introns. It is one of 22 PATs identified in the woodland strawberry (Table 2). Predictions from the online software TMHMM v2.0 indicate that FvPAT9 possesses four transmembrane domains (TMDs) located at amino acid positions 34–56, 71–93, 195–217, and 230–252 (Table 3). The core S-acyl transferase activity domain, known as the DHHC-CRD, is predicted to be cytosolic and situated between the second and third TMDs. Structural analysis confirms that this gene is located on chromosome Fvb6 (Table 1).

Using the same methodology, atpat10 mutant plants were transformed with a construct containing the coding region of FvPAT9 and FvPAT9-DHHS, driven by the constitutive CaMV35S promoter. However, the resulting atpat10-FvPAT9 transgenic plants were unable to rescue the atpat10 phenotype defect, and similarly, the atpat10-FvPAT9-DHHS transgenic plants displayed the same characteristics as the atpat10 mutant (Fig. 2B). The number, length, and width of leaves of transgenic Arabidopsis plants are represented in Table S6.

The results of complementation tests in akr1 mutant yeast and atpat10 Arabidopsis mutant indicate that FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 are not functionally equivalent. It demonstrates that the FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 have functionality, but the FvPAT, which is a homolog of AtPAT10, is FvPAT10, and the DHHC domain is necessary for its functionality.

Genomic organization and structure of FvPAT genes

The distribution of introns allows a gene to produce different proteins. The intron distribution of the FvPATs was identified through comparative analyses of DNA sequences of the genes and their coding sequences. The group (a) of FvPAT genes had the least number of exons. For instance, FvPAT9 had only one exon, while five members of this group (FvPAT1, FvPAT4, FvPAT5, FvPAT7, and FvPAT8) had five exons each. FvPAT6, with six exons, also belonged to the group (a). All members of the group (b) (FvPAT11-FvPAT16) exhibited seven exons and six introns, except for FvPAT11, which had four exons and three introns. The highest number of exons was found in group (c). Members of this group of FvPATs include FvPAT22 (9 exons), FvPAT19 and FvPAT21 (10 exons), FvPAT3 (11 exons), FvPAT24 (13 exons), and FvPAT23 (14 exons). However, there were exceptions within groups (a) and (c). FvPAT10 and FvPAT17 each had eleven and ten exons, respectively, in group (a), while FvPAT18 in group (c) had fewer exons compared to other members (7 exons and 6 introns). Among all FvPATs, 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) were identified, except for FvPAT9, which lacks both 5’ and 3’ UTRs, and FvPAT3, which lacks a 3’ UTR (Fig. 3A, B & C).

To investigate the evolutionary relationships between PAT proteins, the phylogenetic tree of three plant species, Arabidopsis thaliana (24 proteins), Oryza sativa (30 proteins), and Fragaria vesca (22 proteins) was constructed.

In Fig. 4C, each leaf of the tree represents a single PAT protein, and the length of the branch between two leaves represents the evolutionary distance between the two proteins. Proteins that are more closely related to each other will have a shorter branch length between them. The tree can be divided into eight clades, which are groups of proteins that are more closely related to each other than they are to any other proteins in the tree. The largest clade in the tree includes twenty-five proteins (green range). These proteins are all from different species, but they are all very similar to each other, which suggests that they have a common ancestor. The smallest clade includes only two proteins (FvPAT3 and OsPAT8), which are different from other proteins in the tree.

Another interesting feature of the tree is the presence of several pairs of closely related proteins from different species. For example, the AtPAT17 and FvPAT17 proteins are very similar to each other, as are the OsPAT27 and FvPAT23 proteins. These pairs of proteins may represent orthologs, which are genes that have evolved from a common ancestral gene through speciation events.

Overall, the phylogenetic tree provides valuable information about the evolutionary relationships between PAT proteins from different plant species. The tree can be used to identify groups of related proteins, as well as to infer the functions of unknown proteins based on the functions of their close relatives (Fig. 4C).

Identification of conserved amino acid sequences within woodland strawberry PAT proteins

The alignment analysis showed that a highly conserved domain among all 22 woodland strawberry PAT proteins was DHHC-CRD, as described before [9]. As shown in Fig. 5A, this domain includes DHHC (amino acids located in 27–30 positions) and Cys residues located in 2, 5, 16, 19, 22, and 36 positions, which the Cys amino acids of 2, 5, and 22 positions are missing in FvPAT3. Gly residues in position 38 and Asn in position 41 are conserved in all FvPAT proteins, too. Furthermore, it has been reported that the [KR]PPR motif in this domain, which is conserved in most FvPAT proteins (Fig. 5A, positions 9–12) needs the last Arg for the activity of the enzyme [32]. The group of FvPATs that mainly have missed this motif belonged to FvPATs 18–22 and also FvPAT3. FvPATs 19, 21, and 22 lack Arg at position 12, but have basic residues in the central amino acids (positions 10 and 11) that can potentially take the place of the missing Arg at position 12 [12].

Further studies of the alignment of FvPATs with less conserved motifs have shown that all FvPAT proteins are common in four hydrophobic segments, which are located on both sides of the DHHC motif. DPG and Nx[TS]Tx[DE] short motifs that have been reported as the conserved motifs in the PAT protein family [33], were recognized within most of the FvPAT proteins. DPG is located after the second hydrophobic stretch, and the Nx[TS]Tx[DE] motif is located after the fourth hydrophobic stretch. The first Asn in Nx[TS]Tx[DE] is missing in FvPATs 11 and 18–22. NPY, another conserved motif within PAT family proteins, was also recognized within FvPAT proteins, mainly in FvPATs 1–8 and FvPATs 23–24. NPY is located after the fourth hydrophobic stretch and after the Nx[TS]Tx[DE] motif. The overview of FvPATs and the position of hydrophobic stretches, as well as conserved motifs within them, is shown in Fig. 5B.

Further exploration within the groups revealed that the sequence DPG in its extended form, TSGRDPGIIPRN, is highly conserved in group 1, followed by PPEx[DE] and PRxK[DE]V for the FvPATs within this group. The motifs WKG[NS]N and GRLIFGPD were identified as highly conserved motifs on the N-terminal side of group 1 of FvPATs preceding the DPG motif. Similarly, in the N-terminal side of FvPATs 18–22 (group 4), the highly conserved motif RKHGWQLPxH[PT]FQ was recognized before the first hydrophobic region. Another group exhibiting a highly conserved domain in the N-terminal region of proteins is group 5 (FvPATs 23 and 24), which consists of Ankyrin repeats.

After the fourth hydrophobic region, groups 1 and 2 contain the conserved motif TNQ[TS]TYE, which is further extended to TNQ[TS]TYENFRYRYD for group 1 and TNQ[TS]TYEYVRRRR for group 2 (FvPATs 9–10). Further analysis revealed that this motif is represented as NTTTIE in group 3, except for FvPAT11, and as [KR]KGIxTY[DE] in group 4. Group 5 contains this motif as QA[TS]QIARNITTNE following the fourth hydrophobic stretch. Therefore, this motif is present in all FvPATs except for FvPAT11. Another motif commonly found in the C-terminal region of almost all FvPATs is the NPY short motif, which extends to NPYN[RK]G in group 1, RVYPFSKGACRNLYEFCCLRSSKYRMES in group 2, and FRNPYxHG for group 5 FvPATs. Lastly, the DIEMG motif was identified in the C-terminal region within group 1 of FvPAT proteins. A schematic diagram of common motif positions within FvPATs is represented in Fig. 5C, and the full alignment is shown in Figure S4.

To conclude the alignment analysis, it has been demonstrated that all FvPAT proteins contain the DHHC-Cysteine-rich domain (CRD) and four hydrophobic segments, which are located in both sides of this domain. FvPATs1-8, except FvPAT3, showed the most similarity in sequences, and FvPAT3 has the most different protein sequence within the FvPAT protein family.

Protein topology of FvPATs

Previous studies have shown that most yeast and mammalian PAT proteins typically feature four predicted transmembrane domains (TMDs) [34, 35]. In the current study, the alignment of FvPAT proteins revealed four hydrophobic segments, suggesting the presence of four transmembrane domains in FvPATs. Subsequently, the TMHMM 2.0 bioinformatics tool was employed to elucidate the topology of FvPAT proteins. The results demonstrated that all FvPATs possess a minimum of four TMDs, with FvPAT3 and FvPAT17 each harboring two additional potential TMDs. The probability score for all TMDs ranged from 0 to 1, with most scoring 1, except for the third TMD of FvPAT10 (0.86) and the fifth TMD of FvPAT17 (0.6). The N-terminal, C-terminal, and the DHHC-CRD regions of most FvPATs were found to be intracellular, except for FvPATs 5, 6, 7, 17, 19, 21, and 22. Specifically, FvPAT17, which contains six TMDs evenly distributed around the DHHC domain, exhibited a cytosolic N and C terminal, while the DHHC-CRD was extracellular. Conversely, despite FvPAT3 also having the same number of TMDs, the distribution differed, with four located on the N-terminal side of the DHHC domain and two on the C-terminal side, resulting in both terminals and the DHHC domain being intracellular. Further bioinformatic analyses on protein topology revealed that the smallest FvPAT protein was FvPAT15 with 276 amino acids (31.37 kD), while the largest was FvPAT19 with 746 amino acids (80.49 kD) (Table 3).

Structural modeling and topology of FvPAT proteins

To enhance the understanding of the structural characteristics of FvPAT proteins, structure modeling techniques have been employed to visualize their architecture. The GMQE (Global Model Quality Estimate), which integrates information from target-template alignments and template structures, was utilized to assess the quality of the protein structures (Table S4). FvPATs 1 and 4 exhibited high structural similarity, with the majority of their structures located intracellularly, featuring four alpha-helix transmembrane segments with only a few amino acids crossing the membrane. Lower conservation and higher disorder were observed in the C-terminus of the protein models, with GMQE scores of 0.75 and 0.71 for FvPATs 1 and 4, respectively.

FvPATs 5, 6, and 7 shared similarities in their extracellular localization and possessed four alpha-helix transmembrane segments. The GMQE scores for FvPATs 5, 6, and 7 were estimated at 0.71, 0.73, and 0.73, respectively, reflecting the lower confidence in model predictions for the C-terminal region of these proteins. FvPATs 8–16 were consistently located intracellularly, featured four alpha-helix transmembrane segments, and exhibited GMQE scores ranging from 0.75 to 0.9 for their predicted models.

FvPATs 3 and 17 were distinguished by the prediction of six alpha-helix transmembrane segments, although for FvPAT17, an alpha-helix fragment was identified outside the cell, a less common feature among PAT proteins. FvPAT18 shared similarities with most FvPATs in terms of localization and transmembrane segments (located intracellularly with four transmembrane segments), but it was notably larger than FvPATs 1–17 in size.

The homology models of FvPATs 19–22, with GMQE scores ranging from 0.52 to 0.9, exhibited lower confidence levels, primarily due to the high degree of disorder, particularly in the C-terminal region, stemming from the challenge of identifying homologous proteins to serve as modeling templates. FvPATs 23 and 24, with 546 and 634 amino acids, respectively, displayed similar structures with reasonable GMQE scores of 0.87 and 0.78. Both FvPATs 23 and 24 featured an ankyrin repeat region in the N-terminal, followed by four alpha-helix transmembrane segments (Fig. 6A).

Subcellular localization of FvPAT proteins

CELLO v.2.5 [36] and WoLF PSORT [37] were utilized to analyze the subcellular localization of FvPAT proteins. In CELLO, four types of sequence coding schemes, including amino acid composition, di-peptide composition, partitioned amino acid composition, and sequence composition based on the physico-chemical properties of amino acids, were employed. The outcome was derived from the combination of their results. WoLF PSORT makes predictions based on known sorting signal motifs and some correlative sequence features such as amino acid content.

Both tools identified the plasma membrane as the most probable localization for FvPAT proteins. While WoLF PSORT recognized the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) as a second probable localization for FvPAT proteins, CELLO assigned very low reliability to ER localization for FvPATs. The highest prediction rates within the ER were observed for FvPATs 1, 6, 8, 13, and 24, followed by FvPATs 3, 10, 11, 16, 19, and 23. FvPATs 14, 17, 21, and 22, with higher reliability among all FvPATs, were predicted to localize in the Golgi apparatus. The cytosol was indicated as the most probable location for hosting FvPATs1 and 5, while the vacuole was identified as the location for FvPATs 3, 21, and 23 (Fig. 7 A and B).

Expression profiling of PAT genes in woodland strawberry

Understanding the spatial and temporal expression patterns of a gene can provide important clues for its function. Therefore, gene expression data of FvPATs for anther, carpel, cortex, embryo, ghost, leaf, ovule, pith, pollen, seed, seedling, style, wall, flower, microspore, perianth, receptacle were collected from the Bio-Analytic resource (BAR) for plant biology (https://bar.utoronto.ca) and qRT-PCR of FvPATs for seedling, leaf (juvenile and mature), stem, stolon, flower and five developing stages of fruit (SG, LG, white, turning and ripe) carried out to monitor the transcript levels of WT strawberry plants. The analysis of microarray expression data from BAR revealed that FvPATs were expressed at varying levels in almost all tissues of woodland strawberry, except for stages 3–5 of the cortex, where there was minimal to no expression of all FvPATs. Interestingly, FvPATs 1 and 16 exhibited similar expression patterns, primarily being expressed in pollen, partially in anther at stage 12, and showing minimal expression in other tissues. On the other hand, FvPAT6 demonstrated high expression levels in both pollen and anther at stage 12, with minimal expression in other tissues. Among these, only FvPATs 1, 6, 13, and 16 showed expression in pollen, while FvPATs 1, 4, 9, and 16 had no expression in the cortex and carpel. FvPATs 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 17, 19, and 23 were expressed in all tissues except for pollen (Fig. 8H).

Expression of selected PAT genes in woodland strawberry

To investigate the expression levels of PAT genes in woodland strawberry, total RNA was isolated from various parts of the plant, including seedlings, juvenile and mature leaves, stems, stolons, flowers, and five stages of fruit development (small green, large green, white, turning, and ripe). The extracted RNAs were used to synthesize complementary DNAs, and real-time PCR was performed using the cDNAs and corresponding pairs of primers. FvPAT4 exhibited steady expression across various organs and growth stages of woodland strawberry, with a higher expression detected in seedlings and ripe fruit (4090 and 4154, respectively) (Fig. 8A). FvPAT9 exhibited higher expression levels among the assessed samples in stolons, turning, white, and ripe fruits, while seedlings and stems showed comparatively lower expression, and no distinct expression was observed in the remaining tissues (Fig. 8B). FvPAT10 displayed varied expression levels, with the highest levels observed in turning and ripe fruits (19842 and 26573, respectively) and the lowest in leaf samples (3515 and 4525 for juvenile and mature leaves, respectively) (Fig. 8C). FvPAT13 had the lowest expression in juvenile and mature leaves, with increasing expression levels observed in fruit stages, peaking in ripe fruit, while other tissues showed relatively moderate expression levels (Fig. 8D). FvPAT14 demonstrated significant expression in ripe fruit, seedlings, and roots, with lower expression in stolons, mature leaves, and turning fruits, and slight expression in other analyzed plant tissues (Fig. 8E). FvPAT15 exhibited the highest expression in ripe fruit, decreasing in turning fruit and stolons. Similar expression levels in flowers, seedlings, and roots were recognized. The lowest expression levels were found in leaves and early fruit development stages (Fig. 8F). FvPAT24 displayed varying expression levels across different organs and developmental stages, with ripe fruit showing the highest expression, followed by seedlings. Other investigated samples had lower expression levels compared to the root (Fig. 8G).

In summary, the expression levels of seven FvPAT genes were analyzed across twelve organs and developmental stages of woodland strawberry. The findings indicated diverse expression patterns of FvPATs across tissues, with most genes showing the highest expression levels in ripe fruit, while leaves, particularly juvenile leaves, exhibited the lowest expression levels within the FvPAT gene family. FvPAT10, FvPAT13, and FvPAT15 showed expression across multiple plant tissues, while the rest of the FvPATs revealed diverse expression patterns within the studied tissues.

Discussion

Protein S-acylation, mediated by DHHC-cysteine-rich domain (DHHC-CRD) proteins, regulates critical plant processes including growth, stress responses, and immunity. Our study identifies 22 FvPAT genes in the Fragaria vesca genome (v4.0.a2), aligning with 24 PATs in Arabidopsis thaliana but contrasting with 211 PATs in cotton. With 22 FvPATs, F. vesca offers a compact model compared to cotton’s 211 PATs. FvPATs’ exon numbers vary from 1 in FvPAT9 to 14 in FvPAT23, reflecting structural complexity similar to rice PATs. Phylogenetic analysis groups FvPATs into three clusters, with FvPAT3 and FvPAT17 as outliers, suggesting unique evolutionary paths. Pairs like FvPAT9-10 and FvPAT23-24 indicate recent duplications, enhancing functional diversity. Conserved DHHC-CRD and hydrophobic segments support membrane localization, though FvPATs 5, 6, 7, 19, 21, and 22 show extracellular domains, unlike most Arabidopsis PATs. This positions F. vesca as a model for Rosaceae genomics, simplifying studies in polyploid crops.

To explore FvPATs functions, we focused on FvPAT10 and FvPAT9. FvPAT10 fully rescued growth defects in Arabidopsis atpat10 mutants, confirming its S-acyl transferase activity, reliant on the DHHC domain, as DHHS mutants failed to complement. This mirrors the role of AtPAT10 in cell expansion. Conversely, FvPAT9 partially complemented yeast akr1 mutants but not Arabidopsis, indicating divergent roles. Sequence alignments show the close homology of FvPAT10 to AtPAT10, while discrepancies of FvPAT9 suggest F. vesca-specific adaptations, potentially targeting unique substrates.

The divergent complementation patterns observed between yeast and Arabidopsis (FvPAT9, which is active only in yeast, and FvPAT10, which is active only in Arabidopsis) may reflect species-specific molecular contexts. Subcellular localization prediction by WoLF PSORT revealed that both FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 are primarily targeted to the plasma membrane, but FvPAT9 additionally showed minor extracellular localization signals, whereas FvPAT10 was more strongly associated with the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 8 A and B & Table S3). These subtle differences may influence their accessibility to substrates or interacting partners in a host-dependent manner. For example, the yeast membrane system and post-translational machinery differ substantially from those in plant cells, which might permit FvPAT9 to function efficiently in yeast but not in Arabidopsis. Conversely, FvPAT10 may require plant-specific cofactors or lipid environments to be active. Further experiments, such as subcellular localization assays and protein–protein interaction analyses, would be valuable to confirm these mechanistic differences.

Our findings extend PAT research across plant species. The rescue of atpat10 phenotypes by FvPAT10 validates conserved growth functions, building on AtPAT10 studies. Yeast-specific activity diverges of FvPAT9 from AtPAT10, suggesting specialized roles in woodland strawberry. The high expression of FvPAT10 in ripe fruit, unlike Arabidopsis’s broad patterns, offers a potential involvement in fruit development [13]. These results advance Arabidopsis findings by confirming conservation while uncovering F. vesca-specific traits. Our work position F. vesca as a bridge to polyploid strawberries, though its direct translational applicability is nuanced by the genomic complexity of polyploid backgrounds, growth regulation of FvPAT10 suggests applications in enhancing strawberry yield. Its homology to AtPAT10, which supports cell expansion, implies FvPAT10 bolsters vegetative growth, critical for fruit production. Its elevated expression in ripe fruit suggests a role in development, potentially via S-acylation of ripening regulators, offering targets for improving fruit quality or shelf-life. FvPAT10’s pronounced expression in ripe fruit may align with established ripening-associated signaling cascades, including abscisic acid (ABA) pathways, echoing the regulatory role of S-acylation in Arabidopsis [7]. In stress contexts, S-acylation mediates abiotic tolerance; FvPAT10 may enhance resilience to drought, salinity, or cold stress, as seen in Arabidopsis and Medicago truncatula [13, 38]. PATs also contribute to immunity; future studies could explore FvPAT10’s potential in pathogen defenses, building on PAT roles in immunity [39]. Divergence of FvPAT9 hints at novel stress or developmental roles, warranting further study. These insights could transform breeding for resilient, high-yielding Rosaceae crops, addressing climate and food security challenges.

The three-dimensional models of FvPATs (Fig. 6A) revealed highly conserved DHHC catalytic motifs and characteristic transmembrane topologies typical of S-acyl transferases. These features suggest that both FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 retain canonical structural elements required for enzymatic activity, including the cysteine-rich catalytic domain embedded within the fourth transmembrane helix. The spatial proximity of this domain to the cytosolic surface implies potential accessibility to substrate proteins at the plasma membrane, supporting the functional predictions derived from our localization analysis based on WoLF PSORT prediction data. Nevertheless, our current study did not experimentally validate these structural predictions. Domain-specific mutagenesis or biochemical assays (e.g., acyltransferase activity tests) will be essential to confirming the contribution of individual residues or motifs to enzymatic function. Thus, while the predicted structures provide valuable mechanistic suggestions, they should be regarded as preliminary frameworks guiding future functional validation.

Our study has constraints. Yeast and Arabidopsis assays may not fully reflect F. vesca biology. While integrating bioinformatics predictions indicating endomembrane roles potentially linked to stress signaling, stress or immunity responses were not explored. Localization and topology predictions lack in planta evidence. Only FvPAT9 and FvPAT10 were functionally studied through heterologous systems. To address these gaps, we prioritize stress and defense assays to test FvPAT10 under drought, salinity, or Botrytis infection, confirming resilience roles. In planta studies using CRISPR/Cas9 and GFP-tagging in F. vesca will validate the growth and localization functions of FvPAT10; however, this cross-species approach confirms homology and activity without the time-intensive generation, allowing genome-wide insights first. Additional efforts include biochemical assays for FvPAT10 substrates, broader FvPAT analysis, and Rosaceae comparisons to inform breeding. These steps will maximize F. vesca’s translational potential.

Conclusions

This paper presents a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the proteins S-acyl transferase (PAT) family in woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca), identifying 22 FvPAT genes distributed on 7 chromosomes. Each gene encodes a protein that contains a conserved DHHC domain primarily localized to the endomembrane system. Phylogenetic, structural, and expression analyses indicate variable gene structures and tissue-specific expression patterns with FvPAT10 being highly expressed in ripe fruit, suggesting the potential involvement of this gene in fruit development; however, functional validation via knockout or overexpression in F. vesca is needed to confirm roles in processes such as ripening or cell expansion. By the way, its heterologous functional characterization through omplementation of Arabidopsis atpat10 mutants identifies the conserved DHHC cysteine residue of FvPAT10 as critical to restoring growth defects in Arabidopsis atpat10 mutants, proving its S-acyl transferase activity and homology to the essential cell expansion, division, vascular patterning, and fertility protein, AtPAT10. Conversely, FvPAT9 also complements yeast akr1 mutants but not Arabidopsis, indicating functional divergence and possible species-specific adaptations in either substrate specificity or enzyme activity. This study will be important in making F. vesca a diploid model for studying PATs in the Rosaceae family, filling in knowledge gaps regarding the roles of S-acylation in plant growth, development, and responses to stress, which remains poorly studied in fruit crops relative to the model organism Arabidopsis. It provides a background on molecular breeding to improve crop resilience, yield, and quality by clarifying conserved and novel roles of PATs, e.g., to breed strawberry fruits that ripen more completely, tolerate stress, and resist pathogens.
