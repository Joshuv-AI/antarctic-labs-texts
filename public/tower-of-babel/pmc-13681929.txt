Rare multi - regional involvement in posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome with spinal cord involvement of a young hypertensive male: a case report
Authors: Jingjing Dou, Yuxia Li, Xiangzhen Yuan, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is an acute or subacute cerebral syndrome which primarily manifests as headache, encephalopathy, seizures, and visual disturbances in various combinations. PRES with spinal cord involvement (PRES-SCI) is extremely rare and considered to be a variant of PRES.Case presentationWe present a case of PRES-SCI where the patient primarily manifested with bilateral blurred vision. The brain and spinal cord MRI showed that the lesions simultaneously affected the supratentorial region, infratentorial region, and spinal cord, and malignant hypertension was identified as the underlying etiology. After 8 days of blood pressure control, a follow-up MRI revealed obvious disappearance of the imaging lesions and full resolution of the blurred vision.ConclusionsTimely blood pressure management in PRES-SCI, even with extensive cerebral and spinal cord involvement, can lead to rapid symptom resolution and significant lesion reduction on imaging.

Introduction

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is an acute or subacute cerebral syndrome, primarily manifesting as headache, encephalopathy, seizures, and visual disturbances in diverse combinations [1, 2]. The etiologies of PRES are diverse, encompassing malignant hypertension, eclampsia, autoimmune diseases, renal failure, hematological disorders, electrolyte disturbances, as well as chemotherapeutic agents and immunosuppressants. The classic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) feature of PRES is the presence of reversible focal or confluent vasogenic edema in the posterior regions of bilateral parieto-occipital lobes [3, 4]. Involvement of frontal and temporal lobes, basal ganglia, brain stem, and cerebellum is less common. PRES with spinal cord involvement (PRES-SCI) is extremely rare and considered to be a variant of PRES [5, 6], with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature to date [5, 7–10]. Patients with PRES-SCI demonstrate characteristic lesions on MRI, which extend to the cervicomedullary junction. If timely intervention is administered, encompassing blood pressure regulation and management of the underlying disease, the majority of patients can achieve a favorable prognosis, with the imaging manifestations demonstrating a high degree of reversibility [11, 12].

We hereby present a patient with malignant hypertension who is diagnosed with PRES-SCI. The lesions of the patient rarely and concurrently affected the supratentorial area, infratentorial area, and cervical spinal cord. Following 8 days of antihypertensive therapy, an obvious reduction in the patient’s lesions and a rapid relief of symptoms were observed.

Case report

A 39-year-old male with a height of 175 cm, weight of 98 kg (BMI 31.8 kg/m²), and no previous history of cardiocerebrovascular diseases presented bilateral blurred vision without visual field defect over half month. The symptom manifested more prominently during near vision tasks and less severely during distant vision tasks, with the left eye exhibiting a more pronounced degree of blurred vision. The patient’s symptom gradually worsened. Upon admission, the patient presented with elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure was 200 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure was 132 mmHg). The neurological examination revealed bilateral decrease in visual acuity (a corrected visual acuity of 0.8 in the right eye and 0.4 in the left eye), while the examinations of the remaining cranial nerves, muscle strength, muscle tone, tendon reflexes, pathological signs or coordinated movements were all normal.

Upon admission, the patient underwent renal function test, which revealed abnormalities: urea at 8.3 mmol/L [normal range (NR): 3.1-8.0 mmol/L], urinary creatinine at 139 µmol/L (NR: 51–97 µmol/L), and an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 87.05 ml/min. Urinalysis indicated a urinary protein level of 1.0 g/L. To further assess the renal function, glomerular and tubular function tests were subsequently performed. The results showed urinary microalbumin at 321.87 mg/L (NR: 0–30 mg/L), urinary microalbumin/creatinine ratio at 496.12 (NR: 0–30), urinary transferrin at 17.57 mg/L (NR: 0–2 mg/L), urinary alpha1-microglobulin at 13.18 mg/L (NR: 0–12 mg/L), and urinary retinol-binding protein at 0.86 mg/L (NR: 0-0.7 mg/L). These findings suggested mixed glomerular and tubular renal injury. Extensive screening of infections (human immunodeficiency virus antibody, treponema pallidum antibody, hepatitis B antigen/antibody and hepatitis C virus antigen/antibody), metabolic disorders (thyroid function and homocysteine) and systemic autoimmune diseases (lupus anticoagulant, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, antinuclear antibody, rheumatoid factor and anti-streptolysin O) yielded negative results. The patient underwent a fundus examination, which revealed punctate hemorrhagic exudates and macular edema in the left retina (Fig. 1). Then, MRI and contrast-enhanced MRI scans of the brain and cervical spinal cord was conducted, which revealed abnormal signal foci in the brainstem, corona radiata, and splenium of the corpus callosum. Cervical spinal cord MRI demonstrated abnormal signal foci in the upper segment (C1 level) of the cervical spinal cord, with lesions predominantly involving the central gray matter and no spinal cord swelling observed. These lesions exhibited long T1 and long T2 signals, appeared hyperintense on T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2 FLAIR) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), while no significant diffusion restriction was observed on diffusion-weighted image (DWI) (Fig. 2A.a-A.g). No significant enhancement was observed.

Subsequently, the patient completed lumbar puncture, which revealed elevated pressure (300mmH2O), normal nucleated cells count of 1 × 106/L, and an elevated protein level of 51.36 mg/dL. Then he underwent electroencephalography, orbital MR plain scanning with contrast enhancement, and cerebral magnetic resonance venography, all of which revealed negative findings. The visual evoked potential examination revealed bilateral impairments in the visual conduction pathways. Considering the clinical manifestations and imaging features, to further determine whether the patient was suffering from a central nervous system demyelinating disease, relevant antibody tests and oligoclonal bands were performed. The results indicated negative findings.

In addition, to further determine the causes of the patient’s proteinuria and abnormal renal function, an adrenal CT plain scan and duplex ultrasonography of the renal arteries were conducted, and the results showed no abnormalities. The abdominal ultrasound revealed diffuse changes in both kidneys. The patient also underwent tests for renin, angiotensin, aldosterone, cortisol rhythm, adrenocorticotropic hormone, thyroid hormones and associated antibodies‌, all of which yielded normal results. Given that renal biopsy is an invasive procedure, the patient did not perform renal biopsy. Therefore, after excluding the aforementioned common etiologies of secondary hypertension, the patient was diagnosed with essential hypertension.

As a clinical-radiological syndrome, the diagnosis of PRES currently primarily depends on clinical symptoms and imaging findings, with no unified international diagnostic criteria in place. The current diagnostic framework includes: (1) Acute neurological symptoms (seizures, encephalopathy, headache, or visual disturbance etc.); (2) Presence of etiological factors such as hypertension or renal insufficiency; (3) Characteristic imaging findings; (5) Exclusion of other etiologies. In our case, the patient exhibited visual disturbance and showed significantly elevated blood pressure. Brain and spinal cord MRI revealed multiple abnormal signals in the intracranial area, brainstem, and upper cervical spinal cord, with hyperintensity on T2 FLAIR and ADC, indicative of vasogenic edema. After reasonably excluding other etiologies (including cerebral infarction, central nervous system infection, central nervous system demyelinating disease, and encephalomyelitis) and considering the simultaneous involvement of the cervical cord, the patient was considered to be diagnosed with PRES-SCI.

The patient was administered intravenous pumping of urapidil hydrochloride injection for antihypertensive treatment. Blood pressure decreased from 200/132 mmHg to 178/126 mmHg 1 h later, and further dropped to 158/113 mmHg 6 h later. In accordance with the consultation recommendations from the departments of nephrology and cardiology, the target blood pressure for this patient was set at 110–120/70–80 mmHg. The antihypertensive regimen was switched to oral medications, including nifedipine sustained-release tablets at a dose of 20 mg three times daily and sacubitril/valsartan sodium tablets at a dose of 100 mg twice daily. After 8 days of treatment, the patient’s blood pressure gradually declined to the target range. A follow-up cranial MRI showed a significant reduction in intracranial lesions (Fig. 2B.a-B.g) and the blurred vision was rapidly improved. The improvement in both symptoms and imaging findings corroborates our diagnosis. The patient exhibited a favorable clinical outcome and was discharged from the hospital accordingly. At discharge, the patient was instructed to monitor blood pressure daily, and attend follow-up visits at the cardiology outpatient clinic every month. During the follow-up assessment conducted 4 months post-discharge, it was found that the patient’s vision had fully returned to normal (visual acuity of 1.0 in both eyes). Urinalysis revealed a urinary protein concentration of 1.0 g/L and renal function showed a serum creatinine level of 119 µmol/L. At the 6-month follow-up, the urinary protein level remained unchanged, whereas the serum creatinine had decreased to 108 µmol/L.

Discussion

In 1996, Hinchey et al. [1] first coined the term “reversible posterior leukoencephalopathy syndrome” to describe the neurological manifestations in 15 patients, including headache, vomiting, confusion, seizures, cortical blindness and other visual abnormalities, and motor signs. In 2014, De Havenon et al. [5] reported and analyzed 2 cases and 6 from literature, proposing a syndrome named PRES-SCI.

We present a case of a young male patient with hypertension who was diagnosed with PRES-SCI. The patient primarily manifested with bilateral blurred vision. The lesions simultaneously involved the supratentorial region, infratentorial region, brainstem, and cervical spinal cord in the MRI. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid demyelinating antibodies and oligoclonal bands of the patient were all negative, excluding demyelinating diseases. Clinical and cerebrospinal fluid findings ruled out infectious/inflammatory myelitis, and the absence of DWI hyperintensity on MRI plus progressive symptom onset excluded vascular myelopathy. The patient was therefore diagnosed with PRES-SCI.

There are multiple etiological factors for PRES-SCI (Table 1). Hypertension is the most prevalent cause [4]. In our case, the patient was diagnosed with malignant hypertension. After 8 days of antihypertensive treatment, the patient exhibited a notable amelioration of clinical symptoms, and MRI revealed a marked reduction in the number and extent of the lesions. Ultimately, we supposed hypertension as the etiology of PRES-SCI in this patient. Given that the patient had no prior history of renal dysfunction, developed hypertensive retinopathy, and exhibited improved renal function after antihypertensive therapy, we hypothesize that the patient’s renal impairment was caused by hypertension. Additionally, the patient in our case was classified as Class Ⅰ obesity. Obesity significantly elevates the risk of hypertension, which is one of the critical pathogenic factors of PRES-SCI. Therefore, obesity may serve as a potential contributing factor in our patient.

Etiology of Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome with Spinal Cord Involvement

The exact underlying pathophysiology of PRES-SCI is uncertain and might not be uniform in all forms of PRES. A prevailing theory suggests that hypertension surpasses the upper threshold of cerebral blood flow autoregulation, thereby inducing cerebral hyperperfusion, which subsequently triggers endothelial cell damage and blood-brain barrier disruption, culminating in vasogenic edema [13]. The anterior and posterior spinal arteries both arise from the vertebrobasilar arterial system. Notably, the sympathetic nerve distribution in the vertebrobasilar arterial system is sparser than that in the internal carotid arterial system, and its autoregulatory capacity is relatively compromised. Consequently, vasogenic edema and hyperperfusion resulting from cerebral blood flow autoregulatory dysfunction may concurrently impact the spinal cord. Furthermore, cytotoxins, metabolic byproducts, cytokines, and other factors can induce dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells, increase vascular permeability, and compromise the integrity of the blood-brain barrier. Despite the extensive distribution of lesions in our patient, the clinical manifestations remained mild with minimal neurological functional deficits. This can be explained by the fact that the lesions of PRES-SCI are predominantly caused by vasogenic edema, which is typically reversible and exerts substantially less severe impairment on neurological function than cytotoxic injury.

The clinical manifestations of PRES-SCI are non-specific, including headache, visual disturbance, vomiting, encephalopathy, seizures, and other neurological deficits. We searched domestic and international databases, and approximately 70 cases of PRES-SCI have been reported to date, among which about 80% of patients presented with visual disturbance as the initial symptom. In our case, the patient exhibited isolated visual disturbance as the predominant initial symptom. Lumbar puncture revealed elevated cerebrospinal fluid pressure, and we consider that the patient’s visual disturbance is the combined consequence of hypertensive retinopathy and increased intracranial pressure. In addition, PRES-SCI typically presents with an acute or subacute onset, while in our case, the patient’s symptom lasted for 2 weeks. This atypical presentation may be attributed to the intracranial hypertension induced by malignant hypertension, which subsequently leads to visual impairment. The imaging features of PRES-SCI are highly specific. The spinal cord lesions in PRES-SCI mostly originate from the medulla-cervical junction, often presenting as continuous long-segment lesions involving at least four spinal cord segments, with lesions predominantly concentrated in the central gray matter on axial sections. On MRI, the lesions predominantly demonstrate hyperintensity on T2-weighted sequences, while no significant diffusion restriction is observed, which is indicative of vasogenic edema. In our case, the patient’s lesions simultaneously affected the supratentorial region, infratentorial region, and the spinal cord, a situation that is relatively rare in previously reported cases [5, 11, 14–19]. Notably, only one previously reported case had concurrent lesions involving the supratentorial, infratentorial regions and the spinal cord [20].

The key to the treatment of PRES-SCI lies in early detection, accurate identification, and appropriate symptomatic management. Generally, PRES-SCI has a favorable prognosis. Previous case reports have indicated that, following proper treatment, PRES lesions typically resolve within a few weeks to several months, with the shortest duration being 10 days [15]. However, in our case, the patient exhibited a faster and more complete recovery. After 8 days of blood pressure control, a follow-up MRI of the brain and spinal cord revealed obvious disappearance of the imaging lesions and full resolution of the blurred vision symptoms. This finding underscores the critical significance of identifying the underlying etiology in the management of PRES-SCI. When targeted therapy is instituted according to the identified cause, patients’ symptoms can exhibit rapid amelioration, thereby contributing to a favorable prognosis. The key to preventing PRES-SCI recurrence lies in eliminating and rectifying predisposing factors. For patients associated with hypertension, long-term stable blood pressure management and avoidance of severe blood pressure fluctuations are imperative, which is critical for preventing PRES-SCI recurrence.

Our case report also has certain limitations. The patient declined the brain and spinal cord MRI examinations at the 4-month follow-up visit, which resulted in the absence of follow-up imaging data at the 4-month follow-up.
