Correlation between illness perceptions and self-reported degree-of-worry in somatic ill patients in emergency care: a Danish cross-sectional study
Authors: Andrea Nedergaard Jensen, Thomas Kallemose, Lisbeth Frostholm, Hejdi Gamst-Jensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe study aimed to investigate the correlation between illness perceptions and self-reported degree-of-worry (DOW) in patients presenting to the emergency department with acute somatic illness.DesignA cross-sectional survey study collected data from a survey on DOW, the Brief-illness Perception Questionnaire (B-IPQ) and demographics.SettingAn emergency department at a university hospital in the Capital Region of Denmark.Participants944 patients (≥18 years) presenting to the emergency department with acute somatic illness. Data collection was conducted during day and evening shifts on weekdays from 13 January to 19 December 2020.MethodsCorrelations between B-IPQ scores and DOW, including (1) the total B-IPQ score, (2) emotional representations (emotion and illness concern combined) and (3) the single items, respectively. Secondary analysis: linear relationships between the single B-IPQ items and DOW. Analyses included the variables sex, age, reason for contact, self-reported comorbidity, employment, highest educational attainment, cohabitation status and region of origin.ResultsThe study found moderate correlations between the total illness perceptions score and DOW of 0.47 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.52) and emotional representations and DOW of 0.57 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.61). Linear regression showed the largest and most significant changes in DOW with an increasing score of the B-IPQ items for consequence, illness concern and emotion.ConclusionIllness perceptions overall and perceptions of consequences of symptoms, illness concern and emotional representations are correlated with patients’ DOW, with increasing DOW related to more negative illness perceptions. This finding contributes to insight into how illness perceptions are related to illness worry when presenting to the emergency department. Incorporating the novel DOW scale may aid triage and clinical encounters in the emergency department.Study registrationClinicalTrails.gov reference: NCT04226040.Keywords: Emergency Departments, Behavior, Cross-Sectional Studies, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Surveys and Questionnaires, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

In the past decade, rates of emergency department (ED) presentations have increased,1 and more than 70% of all Danish hospital contacts are acute care contacts.2 Consequently, the ED plays an increasingly important role in healthcare services.2 With the increasing burden on the ED due to the ageing population,2 lack of resources and staff shortages,3 novel insights into individuals’ rationales for acute healthcare-seeking and perceptions of acute illness are needed.

Individuals who present to the ED are a heterogeneous group concerning morbidity, health status and their healthcare-seeking behaviour.2 4 5 Many present with non-specific complaints, and 7%–30% of ED patients end up with an unspecified diagnosis.2 6 Healthcare-seeking behaviour is complex and relies not solely on the presence of physical symptoms but also on psychological factors and patients’ own perceptions and beliefs about their condition.7,9 These include worry, a sense of perceived urgency, lack of knowledge, fear of consequences and views of family and friends.10,12

The Common-Sense Model developed by Leventhal and colleagues13,15 unfolds this mechanism, suggesting that individuals confronted with illness create dynamic organised patterns of lay perceptions about their symptoms, so-called illness perceptions (IPs).7 IPs are organised around certain components, including the perceived consequences of the illness, the perceived timeline, control or cure and lay perceptions of the name of the illness, including causal beliefs.16 IPs guide the individual’s coping efforts and are key determinants of self-management behaviour, including healthcare seeking and are further associated with adherence and various health outcomes.7,917 IPs have been found to be associated with patients’ healthcare use and help-seeking behaviour across diseases, including colorectal cancer,21 irritable bowel syndrome22 and back pain23 and have been found to influence the use of primary healthcare24 and urgent healthcare-seeking behaviour.25,27 Although IPs and ED presentations have not been widely studied, emotional representations such as anxiety and worry seem to play an important role in individuals’ decisions to seek urgent healthcare.26 27

Consequently, to measure acute patients’ own perceptions of worry and urgency, the novel patient-reported degree-of-worry (DOW) scale was developed for prehospital telephone triage.28,30 The 10-point scale instrument is based on a single question: ‘How worried are you about the condition that brings you here today, on a scale from 1 to 10?’ (where 1 = minimal worry and 10=maximum worry).28 As with the numeric rating scale for conveying the subjective pain experience,31 DOW translates the subjective feeling of worry into an objective measure.28 DOW is strongly associated with face-to-face consultations at EDs and subsequently acute hospitalisation (≥24 hours) within 48 hours after calling a medical helpline, in a dose-response manner with higher ORs of hospitalisation with higher DOW (maximum DOW OR vs minimum DOW OR 6.1, 95% CI 3.9 to 9.6.).28 30 DOW has mainly been used within telephone triage; however, it may provide valuable insights if used within the ED, as the brief nature of the instrument takes rapid triage and workflow into account.32

Conceptually, IPs and DOW are clearly related as they both address lay perceptions of health threats, which is confirmed by studies from prehospital emergency care, where callers to the medical helpline draw on the components from the Common-Sense Model when rating their DOW during telephone triage.33 This suggests that complex cognitive and emotional processes are involved in patients’ sense of urgency.34 However, as DOW is a novel instrument, the interrelation between IPs and DOW in the setting of emergency care remains to be explored, including the investigation of which IPs (if any) ED patients’ draw upon when rating their DOW.35 Insights into these matters may help us to understand the rationales for presenting to the ED in relation to DOW. Therefore, the hypothesis is that IPs and DOW have interrelations in patients presenting to the ED, where an increase in IPs would correspondingly lead to an increase in DOW.

The aim of this study was to investigate the correlations between ED patients’ IPs measured using the B-IPQ and self-reported DOW. Secondarily, to describe the associations between IPs and self-reported DOW.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study in an ED at a university hospital in the Capital Region of Denmark. Denmark is divided into five regions, with each region responsible for health services. The Capital Region of Denmark serves approximately 1.8 million inhabitants36 and includes five EDs located at different hospitals. This study took place at a 29-bed ED, which serves a population of approximately 550 000 citizens, with roughly 70 000 patient contacts/year (2019 numbers). The ED coordinates the admissions of 45–60 patients per day and receives all types of acute patients with few exceptions (ie, paediatric, gastroenterological and obstetric patients who are directed to specialised departments).37

Participants

Patients aged ≥18 years presenting to the ED with acute somatic illness were approached for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they: (1) had insufficient Danish language skills to answer the survey in Danish; (2) presented with life-threatening illness (triaged to the highest urgency level in a five-level criterion-based triage tool); (3) arrived with injuries or (4) were cognitively impaired (demented, mentally or physically too weak to participate and/or under the influence of alcohol or drugs).

Data collection

Data collection was carried out between 13 January and 19 December 2020. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the data collection was paused between 19 March and 2 June 2020. Data collection was ended in late December 2020 due to COVID-19 restrictions. Using a convenience sampling strategy, data was collected during day and evening shifts on weekdays, with trained interviewers (nursing students, nurses or medical students) conducting the in-person surveys using tablets to enter the secure digital survey platform Research Electronic Data Capture). The interviewers strived to include all patients who were screened for eligibility upon arrival. If deemed eligible, the patients were invited to participate in the survey. Patients received an oral and written briefing of the study, after which they consented or declined participation. In case of waiting time to be seen by an ED physician, the survey was completed before being clinically assessed. Clinical assessment and treatment were always a priority; however, DOW was asked at the very beginning of the survey to prevent recall bias in case of interruption due to assessment. If DOW had been completed, the survey was resumed following the clinical assessment.

Survey

The survey consisted of DOW, the B-IPQ and demographic variables (eg, sex, age, highest educational attainment and household income). The survey was developed using existing validated surveys and scientific literature. A pilot test of a draft of the full survey in Danish was carried out among four researchers with survey expertise, followed by a pilot test among 10 ED patients. The pilot test led to a minor rephrasing of a few items regarding the presence of chronic illness to facilitate understanding.

Degree-of-worry

Patients’ level of worry was measured using the DOW scale, asking the single question, ‘How worried are you about the condition that brings you here today, on a scale from 1 to 10, where 1 is minimal worried and 10 is maximum worried?’.30 DOW has shown to be feasible for patients to quantify when calling the medical helpline to aid telephone triage28 30 and shows good face validity and external validity.28 Also, DOW seems to capture medical helpline callers’ own perception of urgency.30 33 35 However, as the DOW scale is novel, it remains to be fully validated.

Illness perceptions

To measure IPs in a brief quantitative manner, the short scale: Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (B-IPQ) was constructed in 2006.38 The scale assesses the cognitive and emotional components of the Common-Sense Model with good test–retest and concurrent validity.38 In this study, IPs were measured using the Danish version of the B-IPQ. B-IPQ is an eight-item questionnaire and items are scored on a numeric scale (0–10) and assess: consequences (impact of illness on one’s life, item 1), timeline (acute/chronic—perception of the length of illness and item 2), personal control (the individual’s control over the symptoms/illness, item 3), treatment control (perception of effect of treatment, item 4), identity (the label used to describe the illness or symptoms, item 5), illness concern (concern about the illness, item 6), illness coherence (the individual’s understanding of the illness, item 7) and emotions (emotional reactions to illness, item 8). Furthermore, a qualitative question asking the respondents to formulate their own perceptions about their condition can be applied. The B-IPQ is a widely used instrument and has good psychometric properties.39 Broadbent et al38 argue that the two items—emotions and illness concern—theoretically relate to emotional representations, thus emotional reactions to health threats. Therefore, these two items were combined into an emotional representation score.38 The total B-IPQ score was calculated by reversing the personal control, treatment control and coherence items, and a higher score indicates a higher perceived illness threat.40 41

Covariates

To account for potential confounders related to DOW, IPs and ED presentation, the following covariates were included: sex, age, reason for contact (acute illness, deterioration of chronic illness, other), self-reported comorbidity (no/yes), employment (employed, student, unemployed, disability pension, stay-at-home, voluntary retirement and state pension), highest educational attainment (primary, upper secondary, postsecondary non-tertiary, short-cycle tertiary, bachelors, masters and other), cohabitation status (alone, with spouse, with children and with parents) and region of origin (Western, non-Western and other).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to describe patient characteristics by frequencies with percentage, mean with SD and median with IQR where appropriate. To evaluate possible participation bias, similar estimates were made for variables available for non-responders (age and sex) as well as comparison to responders by t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test or χ2 test/fisher’s exact test.

The correlation between (1) total B-IPQ score and DOW; (2) emotional representations and DOW and (3) the individual eight items of B-IPQ and DOW were analysed by Spearman correlation and presented as a correlation coefficient with 95% bootstrap CIs. For the secondary outcome, the association between the total B-IPQ, emotional representations and DOW and between the eight B-IPQ items and DOW was analysed using linear regression models, with separate models for each independent variable. Two types of models were fitted: a model with only B-IPQ/emotional representations/single items (crude) and a model adjusted for sex, age, reason for contact, highest educational attainment and region of origin (adjusted). Model results are presented as mean DOW change with 95% CI. Multiple testing was adjusted by Bonferroni correction by upscaling p-values.

Missing data were assumed to be missing at random based on not observing any patterns explaining the missingness in the data during data collection. Missing data were imputed by multiple imputations (100 imputations) using either predictive mean matching or polytomous logistic regression as imputation methods. All demographic variables and B-IPQ items were used in the imputations.

All analyses were performed for both imputed and non-imputed data and differences between imputed and non-imputed results are described in the results. All analyses were done using R 4.1.242 with the mic packages43 being used for the imputation.

Ethics

The Danish Data Protection Agency approved the study (P-2019-762), which was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki44 and the General Data Protection Regulation. The regional ethics committee was consulted and deemed that an ethical approval was not necessary, as the study did not involve biological material (H-19070022).

Results

1945 patients were screened for inclusion; of these, 677 were excluded based on criteria, and a further 255 did not consent to participate. If patients had more than one presentation to the ED during the data collection period, only the first was included. Furthermore, we excluded entries where data on DOW was missing. Accordingly, the final study population consisted of 944 individuals. The flow chart is illustrated in figure 1.

Correlation: B-IPQ and DOW

The analysis of the relationship between the total B-IPQ and DOW showed a moderate positive correlation of 0.47 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.52) and for emotional representations and DOW a moderate correlation of 0.57 (95% CI 0.52 to 0.61). The relationship between the single items of the B-IPQ and DOW showed moderate positive correlations for perceptions of consequence, illness concern and emotional representation, with correlation coefficients between 0.42 and 0.59. Discussion

Principal findings

This study is the first to investigate the correlation between IPs and worry about a presenting health complaint in emergency care, measured by the B-IPQ and the DOW scale. We found moderate correlations between IPs overall and DOW, as well as for perceived consequences, illness concern and emotional representations. When modelling the association between IP items and DOW, the same three items showed the most pronounced positive relationship. The findings suggest that patients’ DOW at the time of presenting to the ED reflects emotional distress, concerns about the illness or symptoms and perceptions of symptoms being severe with serious future consequences. These findings contribute to valuable insights into acutely ill patients’ worries and specific aspects of their perceptions.

Findings in relation to other studies

In line with previous studies,18 45 the present study suggests that patients own beliefs about symptoms and illness, particularly emotional representations, impact healthcare-seeking behaviour, as the DOW scale is proposed to elucidate the mechanisms influencing the behaviour of acutely ill patients.35 Within the Common-Sense Model, these perceptions and others (consequence, timeline, cause, identity and emotional representation) have been characterised as ‘threat perceptions’, as they tend to signal a health threat and illness-related outcomes such as anxiety.46 Similar to this, a previous study investigating DOW in relation to illness representation in prehospital emergency care found that the IPs among callers to a medical helpline indicated high perceptions of urgency and uncertainty.33 Thus, this may indicate that DOW reflects urgency, fear of consequences, and more obviously, worry, leading to the decision to seek acute care, possibly guided by the patient’s efforts to manage their own emotional distress.46 However, this is a rather complex relationship that needs to be further investigated.

Our findings are in line with a Danish mixed-methods study among individuals calling a medical helpline. The study aimed to assess the callers’ ability to quantify their DOW while also exploring the thematic content of the callers’ worry. The study showed that DOW ratings ranged from being bothered by a condition and seeking information from the call handlers to distress and fear of the consequences of the illness or symptoms.28 Furthermore, another mixed-methods study exploring the relationship between illness representations and DOW among callers to a medical helpline found that callers referred to five components of the Common-Sense Model: identity, timeline, cause, cure/control and consequence, when describing their illness to the call handlers, regardless of their self-rated DOW.33 Interestingly, our present study identified a moderate correlation between only three out of the eight IPs and DOW (perceived consequences, illness concern and emotional representations). While we have not explored this matter, the variation in the number of IP items related to DOW may be attributed to differences in the study setting and methods used for data collection. Previous studies investigating DOW were conducted within a prehospital emergency setting (out-of-hours medical helpline) by call handlers, while the present study was conducted within the ED by using trained in-person interviewers. Thus, the variation may indicate a dynamic nature in the relationship between IPs and DOW along the patient and treatment trajectory. As such, within the framework of the Common-Sense Model lies the perception that IPs have a dynamic and iterative nature by which individuals perceive, interpret and respond to health threats and information provided.8 16 Thus, the prehospital emergency setting is the caller’s initial encounter with emergency care, by which the caller is likely to draw on IPs formed by, that is, own bodily sensations and previous experiences.8 16 When presenting to the ED, however, the patient has already been in contact with a call handler, often an expert (ie, a nurse or medical doctor), and the information provided by the expert most likely impacts the IPs that the patient draws on, as the representations may have been modified.8 16 46 These differences in findings may therefore suggest that changes in IPs, coping and illness outcomes occur over time.46

Lastly, our findings suggest that DOW upon arrival in emergency care reflects both cognitive and emotional distress and concerns. Although in a different healthcare setting, these findings align with a study on IPs and primary healthcare utilisation. Here, researchers found that individuals who experienced emotional distress regarding symptoms and perceived them to have serious, long-lasting consequences had a higher primary healthcare use.24 These same IPs have previously been linked to predicting worse prospective health outcomes in patients presenting new health complaints in general practice.18 While our study applied a cross-sectional design and therefore did not specifically explore prospective health in relation to IPs and DOW, prior research has demonstrated a dose-response association between DOW and acute hospitalisation (≥24 hours).35 This suggests that DOW may reflect the individuals’ sensations of broader biopsychosocial issues and urgency, ultimately predicting acute hospitalisation35 and potentially other health outcomes among ED patients.

Strengths and limitations of the study

A strength of this study was the timing of the data collection, conducted upon the ED presentation, prior to clinical assessment by the treating physician. This approach minimised the risk of recall bias, thus reflecting patients’ immediate DOW. However, certain limitations of the present study should be considered. First, IPs were measured using a Danish version of the B-IPQ, which has not been validated. Yet, the questionnaire has been validated in Norwegian with similar wording to the Danish version,41 and the pilot test of the survey only led to minor rephrasing. Second, there is a risk of potential selection bias due to the convenience sampling strategy as well as the exclusion of a large proportion of the screened patients due to mental impairment, inadequate Danish language skills or triage to the highest urgency level. Third, the present study lacks adjustment for potentially important clinical variables, including level of urgency, which may have introduced bias and thus confounded the results. Fourth, the timing of the research has to be considered as a potential confounding factor impacting our study results. The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. The governmental response to the pandemic, including lockdowns and stay-at-home orders, along with public fear of infection, have been shown to affect the individuals’ patterns of care seeking.47 This included decreasing ED presentations, declines in triage acuity and compositions of ED visits.47,49 Therefore, we cannot exclude that our results reflect these global patterns, especially as we are investigating IPs and worry. Fifth, the assumption of the data being missing at random was based on a lack of observed patterns explaining the missingness. However, the lack of patterns is not proof of none being present and as such, the assumption might not be correct. Additionally, some deviations were seen in the estimates for imputed compared with non-imputed data, specifically for B-IPQ-item ‘Identity’. This was likely caused by the large differences in the number of observations between the estimates but could also indicate that data might not be missing at random. Based on our empirical observations during data collection, we speculate whether the missing observations for B-IPQ-item ‘Identity’ may be caused by patients being rather challenged in scaling this item at this point in their illness trajectory. Yet, we did not collect data systematically on this matter. However, this appears to be a general issue rather than specific to a group of patients. The large number of missing values for B-IPQ-item ‘Identity’ still gives a larger uncertainty to the estimates of this item, by which we have used imputation to account for this. However, this should still be considered a limitation. Lastly, although this study was limited to a one-sided facility, the findings may contribute to understanding how IPs are related to DOW during acute somatic illness. This understanding holds the potential to guide future studies in similar settings.

Implications for research and clinical practice

The findings from this study indicate that the simple and brief DOW scale holds the potential to be a feasible addition to assessment during clinical ED encounters. Implementing DOW as a starting point in the ED may accommodate the increasing focus on the provision of person-centred care32 50 and the formation of therapeutic connections.51 The scale may facilitate bringing the patient’s perspectives and perceptions of illness and symptoms into play and act as a vantage point for approaching ED encounters in a more person-centred manner. This may improve patient satisfaction, addressing the concern that ED patients experience that their treating ED physician has inadequate insights into their perspective.52

This study implies the need for further research into the concept of DOW in the ED setting to expand our understanding of how DOW reflects the individual sense of urgency. First, a qualitative research approach may help unfold and nuance our understanding of individuals’ rationales for acute healthcare-seeking behaviour in relation to IPs and DOW. Additionally, qualitative inquiry will enable exploration of the potential of using the DOW scale to aid person-centred care approaches and systematic patient engagement in acute care settings characterised by rapid assessments and time constraints.32 Second, the findings of the correlation between DOW and especially the emotional representations of illness raise questions about the timing of the data collection upon arrival to the ED, further highlighted by the variation in the number of IP items related to DOW depending on the healthcare setting. The timing of the data collection may likely have influenced the results. Thus, we suggest that at the time of discharge, illness control may have a more profound association with DOW than found in this study. Therefore, investigation of the dynamic variations of IPs and DOW along the acute illness and treatment trajectory is warranted. This includes an investigation of the association between DOW and subclinical deterioration (eg, early warning score) to assess the possibility that DOW may have a predictive nature for prospective health outcomes among ED patients, as that of self-rated health but within a shorter timespan (acute vs chronic).35 Lastly, given the timing of this research, potentially a confounding factor, we recommend that future research replicate this study to investigate the correlations at a different point in time.

Conclusion

DOW was found to be moderately correlated with the overall IPs and for perceptions of consequence, illness concern and emotional representation among acutely ill patients presenting to the ED, with increasing illness perceptions related to increasing DOW. This finding suggests an interplay between IPs and DOW, with DOW reflecting the individual’s sense of urgency. This finding contributes to insight into acutely ill patients’ rationales for presenting to the ED. Incorporating the novel DOW scale may aid triage and encounters in the ED.
