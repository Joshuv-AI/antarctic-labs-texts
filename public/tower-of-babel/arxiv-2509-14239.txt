Accelerating Mirrors as a Physical Realization of the Kappa Plane-Wave Vacuum
Arash Azizi

Abstract
The kappa plane-wave vacuum is a family of kinematically defined quantum states whose thermal properties are well-understood, but whose physical origin has remained an open question. In this paper, we provide a concrete dynamical realization for this vacuum, demonstrating that it is physically and operationally equivalent to the quantum state produced by an accelerating Carlitz–Willey (CW) mirror on future null infinity. The equivalence is established through a three-pronged proof, showing that both systems exhibit identical Bogoliubov squeeze parameters, identical non-local thermal kernels in their Wightman functions, and identical Planckian responses in an Unruh-DeWitt detector. This result grounds an abstract kinematic construction in a well-understood dynamical model, identifying the parameter κ \kappa with the mirror’s physical acceleration. We further generalize our findings by deriving the complete class of mirror trajectories that reproduce the same asymptotic thermal state and show that only the pure exponential CW trajectory generates a constant, stationary flux.



1
Introduction

A foundational result in modern physics is that the vacuum state of a quantum field is an observer-dependent concept. Observers in non-inertial motion or in the presence of event horizons perceive the Minkowski vacuum as a thermal bath of particles, a phenomenon that underpins both the Unruh and Hawking effects
Rindler66
;
Fulling1973
;
Unruh1976
;
Hawking1975
;
Davies1975
. This thermality can be understood from two complementary perspectives: one kinematic, the other dynamic.

From the kinematic viewpoint, thermality arises from the mathematical structure of the quantum state itself. By quantizing a field in a non-inertial coordinate system, such as Rindler coordinates for a uniformly accelerated observer, one finds that the inertial vacuum state is a many-particle state with a precisely thermal distribution
Unruh1976
;
Takagi1986
. This is encoded in the Bogoliubov transformation relating the different mode bases, and the thermal nature of the state is formally guaranteed by the KMS condition, a property rooted in the analytic structure of the vacuum’s correlation functions
Martin_Schwinger1959
;
Bisognano_Wichmann1975
;
Sewell1982
. This approach has led to the exploration of generalized or "deformed" vacuum states, which are defined purely by their characteristic mode-mixing and correlation properties, often without reference to a specific physical spacetime or mechanism that might produce them.

From the dynamic viewpoint, these thermal effects find a powerful and exactly solvable model in the form of an accelerating boundary, or “moving mirror,” in
(1+1)
dimensions. Originating from the study of quantum fields in cavities with time-dependent boundaries
Moore1970
, the moving mirror model was quickly identified as a crucial theoretical laboratory for understanding particle creation in curved spacetimes, serving as a simplified yet insightful analogue for black hole evaporation
Fulling_Davies1976
;
Fulling_Davies1977
;
Birrell_Davies1982
. In this framework, the entire dynamical content of the particle creation process is encoded in the mirror’s trajectory, specified by a ray-tracing map
v=p(u)
. For a conformal field theory, the resulting asymptotic energy flux is elegantly determined by the Schwarzian derivative of this map,
\{p,u\}
. A particularly important trajectory is the exponential path investigated by Carlitz and Willey, which generates a constant, thermal energy flux at a temperature set by the mirror’s acceleration parameter,
\kappa
, making it a perfect model for the steady, continuous emission of Hawking radiation
Carlitz_Willey1987
. This conceptually simple model remains a vibrant area of research, connecting foundational questions about quantum vacuum radiation to diverse topics including the dynamical Casimir effect and analogue gravity experiments
Svidzinsky2018PRL
;
Chen_Mourou2017PRL
;
Good2020Wilczek
.

A significant body of recent work has developed families of generalized vacua, parameterized by a continuous parameter
\kappa
, that are defined by their characteristic mode mixing. This framework was first introduced in the context of Rindler spacetime, establishing a
\kappa
-Rindler vacuum
constructed from a linear combination of Rindler modes. This state uniquely interpolates between the standard Rindler vacuum (in the
\kappa\to 0
limit) and the Minkowski vacuum (at
\kappa=1
), leading to novel detector physics that modifies the standard Unruh effect
Azizi2022Kappashort
;
Azizi2023JHEP
;
Azizi2025hottercolderUnruh
.

The construction was subsequently adapted to inertial frames, defining the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum
from a combination of Minkowski plane-wave modes
Azizi2025KappaPW
. Unlike its Rindler counterpart, this state does not interpolate between two different vacua; rather, it represents a continuous family of single-mode squeezes of the Minkowski vacuum. For any non-zero
\kappa
, it is a thermal state whose temperature is set by the squeezing parameter, and it smoothly reduces to the standard Minkowski vacuum only in the
\kappa\to 0
limit. It is this
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum that is the primary focus of the present work. Further generalizations have even incorporated non-stationary, phase-dependent particle creation
Azizi2025KappaGamma
. In all these formulations, the
\kappa
-states are fundamentally kinematic constructions; they are defined by their structure without being tied
a priori
to a specific boundary condition or dynamical process that could generate them.

In this paper, we bridge this gap by providing a concrete physical realization for the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum. We demonstrate that the quantum state of the right-moving radiation field produced by a Carlitz–Willey mirror with acceleration parameter
\kappa
is physically and operationally identical to the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum on future null infinity (
\mathscr{I}^{+}
). We establish this equivalence through a three-pronged proof:

1.

Wightman functions and KMS condition:
We show that the chiral two-point functions for both systems possess the exact same non-local thermal kernel, a universal
\ln\sinh
form that satisfies the KMS condition at an inverse temperature of
2\pi/\kappa
.

2.

Bogoliubov transformations:
We demonstrate that the Bogoliubov transformation for the CW mirror, when diagonalized in the natural Mellin (log-frequency) basis, becomes a single-mode squeeze with the same Boltzmann factor
|\beta|^{2}/|\alpha|^{2}=e^{-2\pi\omega/\kappa}
that defines the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum relative to the Minkowski state.

3.

Detector response:
We prove that an inertial Unruh–DeWitt detector registers an identical, perfectly Planckian thermal response at temperature
T=\kappa/(2\pi)
when coupled to either system.

Taken together, these results confirm that the Carlitz–Willey mirror provides the precise dynamical mechanism that generates the state described by the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum. This work grounds a formal mathematical structure in a well-understood physical model, clarifying the connection between kinematic and dynamic descriptions of quantum thermal phenomena. The paper is organized as follows. Section
2

3
derives and compares the Wightman functions, while Section
4
calculates the identical detector responses. Section
5
generalizes our findings by deriving the complete class of mirror trajectories that produce the same asymptotic thermal state. We conclude in Section
6
.

The thermal phenomena at the heart of this paper originate from the mismatch between different notions of positive frequency, which is mathematically encoded by a Bogoliubov transformation. In this section, we derive the Bogoliubov transformations for both the kinematic
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum and the dynamic Carlitz–Willey mirror. We show that while they arise from different physical starting points, they are fundamentally equivalent, each describing the same single-mode thermal squeeze.

2.1
The
\bm{\kappa}
-plane-wave vacuum: a single-mode squeeze

The relationship between any two such bases, parameterized by
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
, is a Bogoliubov transformation. Expanding the quantum field in each basis, we can match the coefficients of the underlying plane waves
e^{\mp i\Lambda u}
to find that the transformation reduces to a simple,
single-mode
SU(1,1) squeeze for each frequency
\Lambda>0
:

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{\Lambda,\kappa^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Lambda}(\kappa^{\prime},\kappa)\,{\cal A}_{\Lambda,\kappa}+\beta_{\Lambda}(\kappa^{\prime},\kappa)\,{\cal A}^{\dagger}_{\Lambda,\kappa},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh(Y+X)}{\sqrt{\sinh(2X)\,\sinh(2Y)}},\quad\beta_{\Lambda}=\frac{\sinh(Y-X)}{\sqrt{\sinh(2X)\,\sinh(2Y)}},

(1)

with the shorthand
X:=\pi\Lambda/(2\kappa)
and
Y:=\pi\Lambda/(2\kappa^{\prime})
. These coefficients satisfy the canonical relation
|\alpha_{\Lambda}|^{2}-|\beta_{\Lambda}|^{2}=1
. The transformation can be parameterized by a squeeze parameter
r_{\Lambda}
, where

\displaystyle\tanh r_{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{\Lambda}}{\alpha_{\Lambda}}=\frac{\sinh(Y-X)}{\sinh(Y+X)}=\frac{\tanh Y-\tanh X}{\tanh Y+\tanh X},

(2)

The most important case is the transformation between the
\kappa
-basis and the standard Minkowski basis, which corresponds to the limit
\kappa^{\prime}\to 0
(or
Y\to\infty
). In this limit, the ratio of the Bogoliubov coefficients yields the characteristic thermal Boltzmann weight:

\displaystyle\frac{|\beta_{\Lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\Lambda}|^{2}}=\frac{\sinh^{2}(Y-X)}{\sinh^{2}(Y+X)}\xrightarrow{\,Y\to\infty\,}e^{-4X}=e^{-2\pi\Lambda/\kappa}.

(3)

This implies that the
\kappa
-vacuum state
|0_{\kappa}\rangle
, defined by
{\cal A}_{\Lambda,\kappa}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=0
, contains a thermal distribution of Minkowski particles. The mean number of Minkowski quanta in the
\kappa
-vacuum is given by

\displaystyle\langle 0_{\kappa}|a_{\Lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\Lambda}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=|\beta_{\Lambda}(\kappa,0)|^{2}=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\Lambda/\kappa}-1},

(4)

which is a perfect Planckian spectrum at the temperature
T_{\kappa}=\kappa/(2\pi)
.

2.2
The Carlitz–Willey mirror: Mellin diagonalization

For the Carlitz–Willey mirror, the Bogoliubov transformation relates the “in” modes, defined on past null infinity, to the “out” modes, defined on future null infinity. The physical worldline of the mirror corresponding to the exponential ray-tracing function
p(u)=-A\,e^{-\kappa u}
is derived in detail, along with its full kinematics, in App.
A
. The quantum field is expanded in properly normalized “in” and “out” modes; the explicit construction of these modes, their Klein-Gordon inner products, and the general integral forms for the Bogoliubov kernels are presented in App.
B
.

While the transformation relating the “in” and “out” annihilation operators,
a^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}
and
a^{\rm out}_{\omega}
, is generally a complicated integral kernel, for the specific CW trajectory it simplifies dramatically in the
\kappa
-adapted Mellin (log-frequency) basis for the “in” operators:

\displaystyle b_{\Omega}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,(\omega^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}-i\Omega/\kappa}\,a^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}\,,\qquad[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}),

(5)

where the commutation relation is proved in App.
C
. In this basis, the CW kernel becomes delta-diagonal, reducing to a simple single-mode squeeze for each Mellin-frequency
\Omega
:

\displaystyle a^{\rm out}_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Omega}\,b_{\Omega}+\beta_{\Omega}\,b_{\Omega}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Omega}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\Omega}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,e^{+\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\Gamma\!\Big(\frac{i\Omega}{\kappa}\Big)A^{-i\Omega/\kappa},\quad\beta_{\Omega}=\frac{\sqrt{\Omega}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,e^{-\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\Gamma\!\Big(\frac{i\Omega}{\kappa}\Big)A^{-i\Omega/\kappa}.

(6)

These coefficients preserve the canonical commutation relations,
|\alpha_{\Omega}|^{2}-|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}=1
. Since the “in”-vacuum is annihilated by
a^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}
and therefore also by the Mellin operators
b_{\Omega}
, the mean number of outgoing particles created by the mirror is

\displaystyle\langle 0_{\rm in}|\,a^{\rm out\,\dagger}_{\Omega}a^{\rm out}_{\Omega}\,|0_{\rm in}\rangle=|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\Omega/\kappa}-1}.

(7)

This demonstrates that the outgoing radiation field is in a perfect thermal state with temperature
T_{\rm CW}=\kappa/(2\pi)
.

2.3
Equivalence of the Bogoliubov structures

The previous subsections analyzed the Bogoliubov transformations for the kinematic
\kappa
-vacuum and the dynamic CW mirror independently. We now show they are physically equivalent. The key is to compare the relationship of each system to the standard Minkowski vacuum.

On the
\kappa
-plane-wave side, the transformation to the Minkowski basis yields a thermal ratio of Bogoliubov coefficients:

\displaystyle\frac{|\beta_{\Lambda}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\Lambda}|^{2}}=e^{-2\pi\Lambda/\kappa}.

(8)

On the mirror side, after Mellin diagonalization, the transformation from the “in” vacuum (which is the Minkowski vacuum) to the “out” basis yields an identical thermal ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}}{|\alpha_{\Omega}|^{2}}=e^{-\frac{2\pi\Omega}{\kappa}}.

(9)

Identifying the frequency labels
\Lambda\leftrightarrow\Omega
, it is clear that both systems implement the exact same single-mode squeeze relative to the Minkowski vacuum. This is confirmed by comparing the mean particle spectra, which are identical:

\displaystyle\langle 0_{\kappa}|a_{\Lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\Lambda}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\Lambda/\kappa}-1}\quad\equiv\quad\langle 0_{\rm in}|\,a^{\rm out\,\dagger}_{\Omega}a^{\rm out}_{\Omega}\,|0_{\rm in}\rangle_{\text{CW mirror}}.

(10)

Thus, at the fundamental level of the operator algebra, the kinematic structure of the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum and the dynamic scattering off the Carlitz-Willey mirror are physically indistinguishable. They represent the same thermal physics.

3
Wightman functions

Having established the equivalence at the level of the operator algebra, we now demonstrate it at the level of the two-point correlation functions. We derive the Wightman functions for both the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum and the Carlitz-Willey mirror state, showing that their physically significant nonlocal parts, which dictate the thermal properties on future null infinity, are identical.

3.1
The
\bm{\kappa}
-plane-wave vacuum

The Wightman function for the right-moving field in the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum, defined by
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=0
, is given by
W^{>}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})=\bra{0_\kappa}\Phi_{\rm RTW}(u)\Phi_{\rm RTW}(u^{\prime})\ket{0_\kappa}
. The full calculation (see App.
D.1
) shows that the two-point function decomposes into a stationary piece dependent only on the separation
\Delta u:=u-u^{\prime}
, and a non–stationary, local piece dependent on the sum
u+u^{\prime}
. The exact result is

\displaystyle W^{\rm RTW}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}\,(\Delta u-i\epsilon)\Big)\Big]\;-\;\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\cosh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}\,(u+u^{\prime})\Big),

(11)

and
W^{<}_{\kappa}
is obtained by the replacement
i\epsilon\to-i\epsilon
in the stationary term. For the purposes of analyzing thermality, one isolates the stationary part,

\displaystyle W^{>}_{\kappa,\text{stationary}}(\Delta u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}\,(\Delta u-i\epsilon)\Big)\Big],

(12)

\displaystyle W^{<}_{\kappa,\text{stationary}}(\Delta u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}\,(\Delta u+i\epsilon)\Big)\Big],

(13)

which manifestly obeys the KMS condition at temperature
T=\kappa/2\pi
:

\displaystyle W^{>}_{\kappa,\text{stationary}}(\Delta u-i\,2\pi/\kappa)=W^{<}_{\kappa,\text{stationary}}(\Delta u).

(14)

In the
\kappa\to 0
limit, the stationary part correctly reduces to the Minkowski correlator,
-(4\pi)^{-1}\ln[\mu^{2}(\Delta u\mp i\epsilon)]
, while the non-stationary part vanishes as
\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2})
.

3.2
The Carlitz–Willey mirror

T=\kappa/(2\pi)
set by the mirror’s acceleration.

For the mirror, the Wightman function
W^{>}(u,v;u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=\bra{0_{\rm in}}\Phi(u,v)\Phi(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\ket{0_{\rm in}}
is constructed by summing over the “in”-modes. This yields the standard image form for the bulk two-point function:

\displaystyle W^{>}(u,v;u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\left[\frac{(v-v^{\prime}-i\epsilon)\,\big(p(u)-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)}{\big(v-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)\,\big(p(u)-v^{\prime}-i\epsilon\big)}\right].

(15)

To compare with the chiral
\kappa
-vacuum, we must take the chiral limit on future right null infinity (
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
). By holding
u,u^{\prime}
fixed and sending
v,v^{\prime}\to+\infty
with
v-v^{\prime}
finite, the mixed terms involving both
u
and
v
cancel (up to local terms absorbed into the scale
\mu
), leaving a purely right-moving correlator whose non-local structure is determined by
p(u)-p(u^{\prime})
. For the specific Carlitz-Willey trajectory,
p(u)=-Ae^{-\kappa u}
, we have

\displaystyle p(u)-p(u^{\prime})=-2A\,e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}(u+u^{\prime})}\,\sinh\!\Big(\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(u-u^{\prime})\Big).

(16)

The resulting chiral Wightman function on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
is therefore

\displaystyle W^{(\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R})\,>}(u,u^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Bigg[\mu^{2}\,\frac{p(u)-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon}{\,u-u^{\prime}-i\epsilon\,}\Bigg].

(17)

After substituting the expression for
p(u)-p(u^{\prime})
, the
e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}(u+u^{\prime})}
factor contributes only a local term, while the
\sinh(\frac{\kappa}{2}(u-u'))
term provides the non-local thermal dependence.

3.3
Equivalence on
\bm{\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}}
and Bulk Inequivalence

Comparing the results from the two systems reveals their precise relationship. The stationary part of the
\kappa
-plane-wave Wightman function, Eq. (
12
), is

\displaystyle W^{>}_{\kappa,\text{stationary}}(\Delta u)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\Bigg[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(\Delta u-i\epsilon)\Big)\Bigg].

(18)

For the CW mirror, the chiral limit of its Wightman function, after accounting for the local
u+u^{\prime}
terms, has the exact same non-local dependence:

\displaystyle W^{(\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R})\,>}(u,u^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Bigg[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(u-u^{\prime}-i\epsilon)\Big)\Bigg]+\text{local}.

(19)

Thus, both descriptions share exactly the same KMS-thermal kernel on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
. A direct physical consequence of this equivalence is that they produce the same renormalized energy flux. Point-splitting the Wightman function, or equivalently using the Schwarzian derivative of the mirror map, gives the constant thermal plateau:

\displaystyle F(u)=\langle T_{uu}\rangle=-\frac{1}{24\pi}\{p,u\}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{48\pi}.

(20)

However, it is crucial to recognize that this equivalence is sectoral and asymptotic. For generic bulk points, the two states are different. The full CW Wightman function, Eq. (
15
), is not a sum of independent left- and right-moving functions due to the cross terms like
\ln(v-p(u'))
. Consequently, it is not stationary and does not satisfy the KMS condition with respect to inertial time in the bulk, a key distinction from the factorized
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum state.

4
Unruh–DeWitt detector responses

Scope.
The final proof of equivalence comes from a direct physical observable: the excitation rate of a particle detector. We consider an inertial Unruh–DeWitt (UDW) detector with a derivative coupling to probe the right–moving sector on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
. In the long-time (adiabatic) limit, we show that the stationary excitation rate is identically Planckian in both the
\kappa
–plane–wave vacuum and the Carlitz–Willey (CW) background. This agreement pertains to the
right–moving, asymptotic response
and does not assert equality of bulk correlators or KMS with respect to inertial time in the interior.

4.1
Response in the
\bm{\kappa}
-plane-wave vacuum

We first consider a two–level UDW detector with energy gap
\omega>0
on an inertial worldline at fixed
x=x_{0}
, coupled to the right-moving field via the interaction Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\rm int}(t)=g\,\chi(t)\,m(t)\,\partial_{t}\Phi_{\rm RTW}(u)\Big|_{u=t-x_{0}},

(21)

where
m(t)
is the detector’s monopole moment and
\chi(t)
is a long, smooth switching function. The detector’s response can be calculated in two equivalent ways.

First, via first–order perturbation theory, the transition amplitude to a final state containing one
\kappa
-quantum of frequency
\Lambda
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}=-\frac{ig}{\hbar}\int\!dt\,\chi(t)e^{+i\omega t}\,\partial_{t}\Phi^{*(\kappa)}_{\Lambda}(t-x_{0}),

(22)

which in the adiabatic limit
\widetilde{\chi}(\nu)\to 2\pi\,\delta(\nu)
becomes non-zero only for on-shell quanta with
\Lambda=\omega
. Squaring the amplitude and summing over all modes yields the Fermi–golden–rule rate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{g\to e}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{P_{g\to e}}{T}=\frac{g^{2}\omega}{2\hbar^{2}}\;\frac{1}{e^{\frac{2\pi\omega}{\kappa}}-1}\;\propto\;\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T_{\kappa}}-1},\qquad T_{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}.

(23)

Alternatively, the response rate is given by the Fourier transform of the Wightman function, a quantity known as the response function
\dot{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
. Using the stationary part of the
\kappa
-vacuum Wightman function,

\displaystyle W^{>}_{\kappa}(s)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(s-i\epsilon)\Big)\Big],\qquad s:=t-t^{\prime},

(24)

one finds the rate by integrating its second derivative:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{\kappa}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!ds\,e^{-i\omega s}\,\partial_{s}^{2}W^{>}_{\kappa}(s)=\frac{\omega}{2}\,\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T_{\kappa}}-1},\qquad T_{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}.

(25)

The Fourier transform of the thermal
\mathrm{csch}^{2}
kernel directly yields the Bose factor. Both methods give the same Planckian response, and detailed balance follows immediately from the KMS property of the Wightman function.

4.2
Response in the Carlitz–Willey background

We now place the same inertial detector in the spacetime of a Carlitz–Willey mirror. As derived in Sec.
2
, the “in”-vacuum (Minkowski) evolves to an “out”-state containing a thermal distribution of particles with mean occupation number

\displaystyle\langle 0_{\rm in}|\,a^{\rm out\,\dagger}_{\Omega}a^{\rm out}_{\Omega}\,|0_{\rm in}\rangle=N_{\Omega}=|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\Omega/\kappa}-1}.

(26)

The detector’s excitation rate can be computed by finding the probability of absorbing one of these “out”-quanta. The transition amplitude at first order is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\rm(abs)}_{\Omega}=-\frac{ig}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dt\;\chi(t)\,e^{+i\omega t}\,\partial_{t}\phi^{\rm out}_{\Omega}(t-x_{0},t+x_{0})\;\langle N_{\Omega}\!-\!1|\,a^{\rm out}_{\Omega}\,|N_{\Omega}\rangle.

(27)

In the adiabatic limit, the time integral projects onto the on-shell component, and the total transition probability for excitation is

\displaystyle P_{g\to e}=\frac{g^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\Omega\;\frac{\Omega}{4\pi}\,|\widetilde{\chi}(\omega-\Omega)|^{2}\,N_{\Omega}\;\xrightarrow{\ T\to\infty\ }\;\frac{g^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{\omega}{2}\,T\,N_{\omega},

(28)

where we have used
|\widetilde{\chi}|^{2}\to 2\pi T\,\delta(\omega-\Omega)
. The stationary rate is therefore directly proportional to the particle number
N_{\omega}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm CW}(\omega)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{P_{g\to e}}{T}=\frac{g^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{\omega}{2}\,N_{\omega}.

(29)

Inserting the CW occupation number from Eq. (
26
) yields the final Planckian response:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm CW}(\omega)=\frac{g^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\,\frac{\omega}{2}\,\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\omega/\kappa}-1}\propto\frac{\omega}{e^{\omega/T_{\rm CW}}-1},\qquad T_{\rm CW}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}.

(30)

This result, derived from the particle-counting perspective, is identical to the one that would be obtained by using the chiral limit of the CW Wightman function, whose second derivative provides the same thermal
\mathrm{csch}^{2}
kernel as in the
\kappa
-vacuum case.

4.3
Equivalence of Detector Response and the Minkowski Limit

The calculations in the preceding subsections demonstrate a perfect agreement in the physically measurable response of an inertial detector. Both the kinematic
\kappa
-vacuum and the dynamic CW mirror cause the detector to thermalize at the exact same temperature
T=\kappa/(2\pi)
.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{\kappa}(\omega)\propto\frac{\omega}{e^{\omega/(\kappa/2\pi)}-1}\quad\equiv\quad\dot{\mathcal{F}}_{\rm CW}(\omega).

(31)

This establishes the equivalence of the two systems in a third, independent channel, complementing the proofs from the Bogoliubov and Wightman perspectives.

This entire framework has a smooth and well-behaved Minkowski limit as
\kappa\to 0
. In this limit, the temperature
T\to 0
, and the detector response vanishes for any positive energy gap
\omega>0
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\dot{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=0.

(32)

The detector cools to its ground state, as expected in the empty Minkowski vacuum. This limit is consistent across all channels: the Bogoliubov coefficients
\beta\to 0
, the Wightman function becomes the standard Minkowski correlator, and the mirror’s trajectory becomes trivial (inertial) after fixing an appropriate affine gauge, leading to zero particle flux. Hence, both the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum and the CW mirror state smoothly converge to the same, unique Minkowski endpoint.

5
Generalizations and Conditions for Thermality

The equivalence established in the previous sections between the single-exponential Carlitz-Willey mirror and the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum raises a natural question: which class of mirror trajectories
v=p(u)
reproduces this specific thermal physics? In this final section, we derive the complete family of trajectories that yield the universal thermal kernel on future null infinity and clarify the stricter conditions required to obtain a constant, stationary thermal flux.

For a general mirror, the right-moving Wightman function on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
is

\displaystyle W^{(\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R})\,>}(u,u^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\Bigg[\mu^{2}\,\frac{p(u)-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon}{u-u^{\prime}-i\epsilon}\Bigg].

(33)

We demand that its nonlocal dependence on
\Delta u:=u-u^{\prime}
matches the
\kappa
-plane-wave kernel up to local,
u+u^{\prime}
-dependent terms:

\displaystyle p(u)-p(u^{\prime})\;=\;H\!\Big(\tfrac{u+u^{\prime}}{2}\Big)\;\sinh\!\Big(\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(u-u^{\prime})\Big),\qquad\forall\,u,u^{\prime},

(34)

for some arbitrary function
H
. Expanding this functional relation in powers of
d=(u-u^{\prime})/2
reveals that it is equivalent to the linear ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle p^{(3)}(u)=\kappa^{2}\,p^{\prime}(u).

(35)

The general solution is a linear combination of exponentials:

\displaystyle p(u)=\alpha e^{\kappa u}+\beta e^{-\kappa u}+\gamma,

(36)

with real constants
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Furthermore, the nonlocal structure of the Wightman function is invariant under Möbius reparametrizations of the coordinate
v
,
\tilde{p}(u)=(a\,p(u)+b)/(c\,p(u)+d)
. Thus, the complete class of trajectories that exactly reproduce the thermal kernel is given by the projective family

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(u)=\frac{a(\alpha e^{\kappa u}+\beta e^{-\kappa u}+\gamma)+b}{c(\alpha e^{\kappa u}+\beta e^{-\kappa u}+\gamma)+d}\;,\qquad ad-bc\neq 0.

(37)

This result can be further relaxed. If we only require the thermal kernel to emerge asymptotically for late times (
u,u^{\prime}\to+\infty
), it is sufficient for the trajectory to have an exponential tail:

\displaystyle p(u)=v_{H}-A\,e^{-\kappa u}+o(e^{-\kappa u})\qquad(u\to+\infty),\quad A>0.

(38)

Finally, we consider the condition for a constant energy flux,
F(u)=-\tfrac{1}{24\pi}\{p,u\}
. The Schwarzian derivative for the general solution is

\displaystyle\{p,u\}=\kappa^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\kappa^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha e^{\kappa u}+\beta e^{-\kappa u}}{\alpha e^{\kappa u}-\beta e^{-\kappa u}}\right)^{\!2},

(39)

which is constant (and equals
-\kappa^{2}/2
) only if one of the exponential terms is absent (
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
). This singles out the pure, single-exponential subclasses as the unique trajectories that produce a constant thermal plateau
F(u)=\kappa^{2}/(48\pi)
:

\displaystyle p(u)=v_{H}-A\,e^{-\kappa u}\quad\text{(Carlitz\textendash Willey type)},\qquad p(u)=\tilde{v}_{0}+\tilde{A}\,e^{+\kappa u}\quad\text{(time\textendash reversed CW)}.

(40)

In summary, while a broad class of trajectories can generate an asymptotically thermal state, the condition of a perfectly stationary, constant thermal emission is much stricter, requiring a purely exponential ray-tracing map.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have established a definitive physical realization for the kinematic framework of the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum. We have demonstrated that this mathematical construction, previously defined by its characteristic thermal mode mixing, is not merely a formal convenience but is physically and operationally equivalent to the quantum state produced by an accelerating Carlitz-Willey mirror on future null infinity. This result provides a concrete, dynamical origin for the thermal properties of the
\kappa
-vacuum.

Our proof of this equivalence is built on a three-pronged analysis, showing a precise match between the two systems in three independent and complementary channels.
First, at the fundamental level of the operator algebra, we showed that the Bogoliubov transformation for the Carlitz-Willey mirror, when diagonalized in the natural Mellin (log-frequency) basis, reduces to a single-mode squeeze with the exact same thermal ratio
|\beta|^{2}/|\alpha|^{2}=e^{-2\pi\omega/\kappa}
that defines the
\kappa
-vacuum’s relationship to the Minkowski vacuum. Second, we demonstrated that the chiral Wightman functions for both systems possess identical non-local thermal kernels on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}
, both satisfying the KMS condition at a temperature
T=\kappa/(2\pi)
. This equivalence directly implies an identical, constant renormalized energy flux for both systems. Finally, we showed that these foundational equivalences manifest in the same physically measurable observable: an inertial Unruh-DeWitt detector registers an identical, perfectly Planckian thermal response in both backgrounds.

The significance of this result is that it provides a crucial bridge between the two canonical approaches to quantum thermal phenomena. It grounds the abstract, kinematic "Route 1" approach (quantization in non-inertial frames) in the concrete, dynamic "Route 2" approach (boundary-induced radiation). The parameter
\kappa
, once a purely mathematical label for a family of squeezed states, is now identified with a physical quantity: the acceleration or effective surface gravity that governs the mirror’s exponential trajectory. Furthermore, our analysis in the final section generalized this result, deriving the complete class of mirror trajectories that generate the same asymptotic thermal kernel and showing that the additional physical requirement of a stationary, constant flux uniquely singles out the single-exponential Carlitz-Willey trajectory.

This work opens several promising avenues for future investigation. The most immediate is to seek a dynamical realization for the more general
\kappa\gamma
-vacuum, whose Wightman function contains non-stationary, phase-dependent terms. It is an open question what physical system—perhaps a mirror with time-dependent reflectivity or a different boundary condition—could generate these richer correlation structures. Additionally, exploring detector trajectories that are not inertial could provide a direct probe of the non-stationary, local components of the Wightman functions analyzed in this paper. Finally, extending these ideas beyond (1+1) dimensions remains a key challenge, where understanding the relationship between kinematic vacua and dynamical boundaries could yield new insights into the nature of horizons and quantum thermal effects in more realistic settings.

Appendix A
Mirror Worldline and Kinematics

This appendix provides the explicit relationship between the abstract ray-tracing function
v=p(u)
and the physical worldline
x=z(t)
of the mirror in Minkowski coordinates. We also derive general expressions for the mirror’s kinematics.

The inverse map between null and Minkowski coordinates is

\displaystyle t=\frac{u+v}{2},\qquad x=\frac{v-u}{2}.

(41)

A mirror’s worldline is the curve
\{(u,p(u))\}
, which in
(t,x)
coordinates is parameterized by
u
:

\displaystyle t(u)=\frac{u+p(u)}{2},\qquad x(u)=\frac{p(u)-u}{2}.

(42)

Differentiating with respect to
u
gives the mirror’s coordinate velocity and Lorentz factor directly in terms of
p(u)
:

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{p^{\prime}(u)-1}{p^{\prime}(u)+1}\;=:\;v_{\rm mir}(u),\qquad\gamma(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v_{\rm mir}^{2}(u)}}=\frac{p^{\prime}(u)+1}{2\sqrt{p^{\prime}(u)}}.

(43)

The proper acceleration is

\displaystyle\alpha(u)=\gamma(u)^{3}\,\frac{dv_{\rm mir}}{dt}=\gamma(u)^{3}\,\frac{dv_{\rm mir}/du}{dt/du}=\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(u)}{2\,[p^{\prime}(u)]^{3/2}}.

(44)

To describe the worldline as
x=z(t)
, one can eliminate
u
via the implicit equation

\displaystyle t+z(t)=p\big(t-z(t)\big).

(45)

For the Carlitz–Willey (CW) trajectory, we use the specific ray-tracing map

\displaystyle p(u)=v_{H}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa u}\qquad(\kappa>0),

(46)

where
v_{H}
is the future event horizon. Solving (
45
) gives a closed form for the worldline
z(t)
in terms of the Lambert
W
function:

\displaystyle z(t)=v_{H}-t-\frac{1}{\kappa}\,W\!\Big(e^{-\kappa(2t-v_{H})}\Big).

(47)

The kinematics for the CW mirror are particularly simple because
p^{\prime}(u)=e^{-\kappa u}
. Inserting this into the general relations above yields:

\displaystyle v_{\rm mir}(u)=\frac{e^{-\kappa u}-1}{e^{-\kappa u}+1}=-\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa u}{2}\Big),\qquad\gamma(u)=\cosh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa u}{2}\Big).

(48)

The proper acceleration is then

\displaystyle\alpha(u)=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\,e^{\kappa u/2},

(49)

whose magnitude grows exponentially as the mirror asymptotes to the null line
t+x=v_{H}
.

Appendix B
Mode Normalization and Bogoliubov Kernels

This appendix details the normalization of the mirror modes via the Klein–Gordon inner product and provides the integral expressions for the Bogoliubov kernels. Throughout we assume a monotone ray–tracing map
v=p(u)
with inverse
u=f(v)=p^{-1}(v)
and
p^{\prime}(u)>0
.

The normalized
in
and
out
modes supported on the right of the mirror are

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\omega^{\prime}}}\!\left(e^{-i\omega^{\prime}v}-e^{-i\omega^{\prime}p(u)}\right),\qquad\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\omega}}\!\left(e^{-i\omega f(v)}-e^{-i\omega u}\right).

(50)

They satisfy the Dirichlet condition
\phi|_{v=p(u)}=0=\phi|_{u=f(v)}
.

On the null Cauchy surface
\mathscr{C}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}\}\cup\{v\in\mathbb{R}\}
the Klein–Gordon product reads

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle=i\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!du\;\big(f^{*}\partial_{u}g-(\partial_{u}f^{*})g\big)+i\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dv\;\big(f^{*}\partial_{v}g-(\partial_{v}f^{*})g\big).

(51)

Normalization.
A direct computation for the
in
–modes gives that the
u
–integral produces only terms
\propto\delta(\omega)
or
\propto\delta(\omega^{\prime})
, which vanish for strictly positive frequencies. Hence the normalization reduces to the
v
–integral:

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4\pi\sqrt{\omega\omega^{\prime}}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dv\,\Big[e^{i\omega^{\prime}v}(-i\omega e^{-i\omega v})-i\omega^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}v}e^{-i\omega v}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}{4\pi\sqrt{\omega\omega^{\prime}}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dv\,e^{i(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)v}=\frac{\omega+\omega^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{\omega\omega^{\prime}}}\,\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}).

(52)

An identical calculation holds for the
out
–modes, leading to

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega}\rangle=\langle\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega}\rangle=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),\qquad\langle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm in\,*}_{\omega}\rangle=\langle\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm out\,*}_{\omega}\rangle=0.

(53)

Bogoliubov kernels.
With the anti-linearity of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in the first slot,

\alpha_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}=(\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega},\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}})
and

\beta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}=-(\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega},\phi^{\rm in\,*}_{\omega^{\prime}})
.
Equivalently,
\alpha_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{*}=\langle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm out}_{\omega}\rangle
and
\beta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}^{*}=-\langle\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},\phi^{\rm out\,*}_{\omega}\rangle
.
Evaluating the mixed products yields the standard integral representations

\displaystyle\alpha_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dv\;e^{-i\omega^{\prime}v}\,e^{-i\omega f(v)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!du\;p^{\prime}(u)\,e^{-i\omega u}\,e^{-i\omega^{\prime}p(u)},

(54)

\displaystyle\beta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!dv\;e^{-i\omega^{\prime}v}\,e^{+i\omega f(v)}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!du\;p^{\prime}(u)\,e^{-i\omega u}\,e^{+i\omega^{\prime}p(u)}.

(55)

The equality of the
v
– and
u
–forms follows from the change of variables
v=p(u)
(with
dv=p^{\prime}(u)\,du
). These general expressions reduce to a
\delta
–diagonal Mellin kernel for the Carlitz–Willey trajectory.

Appendix C
Proof of the Commutation Relation for Mellin-Transformed Operators

We wish to prove that if the standard “in”-mode operators obey the canonical commutation relation
[a^{\rm in}_{\omega},a^{\rm in\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, then the Mellin-transformed operators, defined as

\displaystyle b_{\Omega}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,(\omega^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}-i\Omega/\kappa}\,a^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}},

(56)

also obey the canonical commutation relation
[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We begin by writing out the commutator using the definition of
b_{\Omega}
and its Hermitian conjugate,
b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}
:

\displaystyle[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}-i\Omega/\kappa}\,a^{\rm in}_{\omega}\ ,\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,(\omega^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}+i\Omega^{\prime}/\kappa}\,a^{\rm in\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}\right].

(57)

Using the linearity of the commutator and substituting the known relation
[a^{\rm in}_{\omega},a^{\rm in\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\,\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}-i\Omega/\kappa}(\omega^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}+i\Omega^{\prime}/\kappa}[a^{\rm in}_{\omega},a^{\rm in\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\,\omega^{-\frac{1}{2}-i\Omega/\kappa}(\omega^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}+i\Omega^{\prime}/\kappa}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}).

(58)

We use the Dirac delta function to evaluate the integral over
\omega^{\prime}
, which yields

\displaystyle[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{-1-i(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})/\kappa}.

(59)

The remaining integral is best solved with a logarithmic change of variables. Let
\omega=e^{x}
, which implies
d\omega=e^{x}dx
. The integration limits transform as
(\omega=0,\infty)\to(x=-\infty,\infty)
. The integral becomes:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\omega^{-1-i(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})/\kappa}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(e^{x}dx)\,(e^{x})^{-1-i(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})/\kappa}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\frac{(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})}{\kappa}x}\,dx.

(60)

This is a standard Fourier representation of the Dirac delta function,
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-iky}dy=2\pi\delta(k)
. Applying this identity, we find

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\frac{(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})}{\kappa}x}\,dx=2\pi\delta\left(\frac{\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}}{\kappa}\right).

(61)

Substituting this result back into our expression for the commutator and using the scaling property of the delta function,
\delta(ax)=|a|^{-1}\delta(x)
, we arrive at the final result:

\displaystyle[b_{\Omega},b_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa}\left[2\pi\delta\left(\frac{\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}}{\kappa}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\kappa}\left[\kappa\cdot\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\right]=\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}).

(62)

This confirms that the Mellin-transformed operators form a valid bosonic basis with canonical commutation relations.

Appendix D
Wightman Function Derivations

This appendix provides the detailed derivations for the Wightman functions used in the main text.

D.1
The
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum

The Wightman function for the
\kappa
-plane-wave vacuum is

W^{\mathrm{RTW}}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})=\langle 0_{\kappa}\,|\,\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)\,\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u^{\prime})\,|\,0_{\kappa}\rangle.

Using the field expansion and
[{\cal A}_{\Lambda},{\cal A}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta(\Lambda-\Lambda^{\prime})
, this reduces to a single integral:

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{RTW}}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\Lambda\,\Phi^{(\kappa)}_{\Lambda}(u)\,\Phi^{(\kappa)}_{\Lambda}(u^{\prime})^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\Lambda}{{\cal N}_{\Lambda,\kappa}}\Big(e^{\frac{\pi\Lambda}{2\kappa}}e^{-i\Lambda u}+e^{-\frac{\pi\Lambda}{2\kappa}}e^{+i\Lambda u}\Big)\Big(e^{\frac{\pi\Lambda}{2\kappa}}e^{+i\Lambda u^{\prime}}+e^{-\frac{\pi\Lambda}{2\kappa}}e^{-i\Lambda u^{\prime}}\Big),

(63)

with
{\cal N}_{\Lambda,\kappa}=8\pi\Lambda\,\sinh(\pi\Lambda/\kappa)
. Expanding the product:

W^{\mathrm{RTW}}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\Lambda}{{\cal N}_{\Lambda,\kappa}}\Big[e^{\frac{\pi\Lambda}{\kappa}}e^{-i\Lambda(u-u^{\prime})}+e^{-\frac{\pi\Lambda}{\kappa}}e^{+i\Lambda(u-u^{\prime})}+e^{-i\Lambda(u+u^{\prime})}+e^{+i\Lambda(u+u^{\prime})}\Big].

(64)

Grouping terms and using
\cosh A\cos B-\sinh A\sin B=\real\!\{\cosh(A-iB)\}
gives

\displaystyle W^{\mathrm{RTW}}_{\kappa}(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\Lambda}{4\pi\Lambda\,\sinh\!\left(\frac{\pi\Lambda}{\kappa}\right)}\,\cosh\!\Big(\frac{\pi\Lambda}{\kappa}-i\Lambda\Delta u\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\Lambda}{4\pi\Lambda\,\sinh\!\left(\frac{\pi\Lambda}{\kappa}\right)}\,\cos\!\big(\Lambda(u+u^{\prime})\big),

(65)

where
\Delta u:=u-u^{\prime}
. Evaluating these standard integrals (see, e.g.,
Birrell_Davies1982
;
Gradshteyn_Ryzhik2014
) with the causal
i\epsilon
prescription yields a stationary thermal part and a non-stationary local part:

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{stationary}}^{\mathrm{RTW}}(\Delta u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\frac{2}{\kappa}\,\sinh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(\Delta u-i\epsilon)\Big)\Big],

(66)

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{non\mbox{-}stationary}}^{\mathrm{RTW}}(u{+}u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\cosh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(u{+}u^{\prime})\Big).

(67)

Their sum gives the full right-moving Wightman function quoted in the main text. The
\Delta u
-dependence is KMS at temperature
T=\kappa/(2\pi)
; the
u{+}u^{\prime}
term encodes the local squeezing profile of the RTW state.

D.2
The Carlitz–Willey mirror

Expand the field in the “in” basis,
a_{\omega^{\prime}}\ket{0_{\rm in}}=0
. The bulk Wightman function is the mode sum

\displaystyle W^{>}(u,v;u^{\prime},v^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\bra{0_{\rm in}}\Phi(u,v)\,\Phi(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\ket{0_{\rm in}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!d\omega^{\prime}\;\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}(u,v)\,\phi^{\rm in\,*}_{\omega^{\prime}}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}}\Big[e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(v-v^{\prime})}-e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(v-p(u^{\prime}))}-e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(p(u)-v^{\prime})}+e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(p(u)-p(u^{\prime}))}\Big],

(68)

where
\phi^{\rm in}_{\omega^{\prime}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\omega^{\prime}}}\big(e^{-i\omega^{\prime}v}-e^{-i\omega^{\prime}p(u)}\big)
on the right of the mirror. Each term has the elementary form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\,e^{-i\omega x}=-\ln\!\big[\mu\,(x-i\epsilon)\big],

(69)

with the causal
i\epsilon
understood. Evaluating (
68

\displaystyle W^{>}(u,v;u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\left[\frac{(v-v^{\prime}-i\epsilon)\,\big(p(u)-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)}{\big(v-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)\,\big(p(u)-v^{\prime}-i\epsilon\big)}\right].

(70)

To obtain the chiral correlator on
\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}

u,u^{\prime}
fixed and
v,v^{\prime}\to+\infty

(v-v^{\prime})
finite. In this limit,

\ln\!\big(v-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)=\ln v+{\cal O}(v^{-1}),\qquad\ln\!\big(p(u)-v^{\prime}-i\epsilon\big)=\ln v^{\prime}+i\pi+{\cal O}(v^{\prime-1}),

while
\ln(v-v'-i\epsilon)
remains finite. The combination
\ln(v-v')-\ln v-\ln v^{\prime}
is independent of
u,u^{\prime}
and is absorbed into
\mu

\displaystyle W^{>}_{\mathscr{I}^{+}_{R}}(u,u^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\ln\!\Big[\mu^{2}\,\big(p(u)-p(u^{\prime})-i\epsilon\big)\Big].

(71)

For a static mirror (or no mirror) where
p(u)=u
, (
71
) reduces to the Minkowski right-moving correlator
-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\!\big[\mu^{2}(u-u^{\prime}-i\epsilon)\big]
, as required.
