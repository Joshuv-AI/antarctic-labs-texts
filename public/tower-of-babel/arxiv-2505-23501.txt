Optimization-based Posture Generation for Whole-body Contact Motion by Contact Point Search on the Body Surface
Masaki Murooka, Kei Okada, Masayuki Inaba

Abstract
Whole-body contact is an effective strategy for improving the stability and efficiency of the motion of robots. For robots to automatically perform such motions, we propose a posture generation method that employs all available surfaces of the robot links. By representing the contact point on the body surface by two-dimensional configuration variables, the joint positions and contact points are simultaneously determined through a gradient-based optimization. By generating motions with the proposed method, we present experiments in which robots manipulate objects effectively utilizing whole-body contact.



I
Introduction

Robots
must perform dexterous and high-load operations autonomously
to expand the range of applications to daily life support and disaster response.
In the robot motions performed so far, the robots usually contact with the environment only through certain body points (e.g., gripper and foot),
making the motions unstable and inefficient.

In this study, to solve such a problem, we propose a method to generate motion through contact at any point on the robot’s body surface.
This is a versatile method
in which the contact points on the body surface are expressed as configuration variables
and searched together with joint positions by the conventional calculation procedure of inverse kinematics.
We show the effectiveness of the proposed method by applying to a variety of whole-body contact motions.

I-A
3
Optimization-based Motion Generation

Recently, the optimization-based motion generation has been actively studied
[
10
]
.
Inverse kinematics (IK) is the fundamental but non-trivial problem in motion generation
[
11
]
.
Our method is positioned as an extension of inverse kinematics
because the conventional point-to-point IK is generalized to a body-to-body IK.
To apply the gradient-based optimization that is widely used in motion generation,
we introduce configuration variables representing points on the body surface and derive the gradient with respect to the variables.
Another approach has been proposed in which optimization problems are formulated on non-Euclidean manifolds to handle the surface contacts
[
12
]
.

I-A
4
Geometric Model Representation

The problem with searching a point on the body surface by the gradient-based optimization is the difficulty in calculating
the gradient of the point on the edge of the robot link represented by a polyhedron.
A solution to this problem is
approximating the shape of the robot link to a smooth shape represented by a sphere-torus-patch
[
13
]

or Catmull-Clark subdivision surface
[
14
]
.
However, implementation and calculation costs are involved to approximate the robot model.
In this study, normal direction smoothing enables calculation of the gradient from the raw convex mesh of the robot model without conversion.

I-B

Contributions of this Study

The contributions of this study are as follows:
(i) an optimization problem that simultaneously searches joint positions and the contact points on the body surface is formulated,
(ii) a smoothed normal direction on the body surface is introduced for gradient-based optimization,
(iii) various whole-body contact motions are shown through simulation and real robots.

In the following,
Sec. II describes the problem and provides an outline of the solution,
and Sec. III describes contact point searching on the body surface in detail.
Numerical examples are given in Sec. IV, and Sec. V demonstrates applications of the whole-body contact motion.

II-B

Problem Representation by Optimization

The problem (
1
) is formulated as an optimization problem as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\|^{2}

(2)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\ \ \mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\geq\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}

(3)

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})
represents the conditions for joint position range and collision avoidance,
while
\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})
represents the conditions for contacts.

The configuration
\hat{q}
is a time series of the joint position
\mbox{\boldmath{$\theta$}}_{t}
and contact point
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}
and is given by:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{1}^{T}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{T}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{T}

(4)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\theta$}}_{t}^{T}&\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\ \ t=1,\cdots,T

(5)

The
\mbox{\boldmath{$\theta$}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a joint position vector where
M
is the number of joints.
The parameter
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}
is a variable representing the point on the body surface,
and is described in more detail in Sec. III.

The task function
\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})
representing the contact of bodies is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{1,1}^{T}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}^{T}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{T,N}^{T}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\end{pmatrix}^{T}

(6)

As the element task function
\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})
, various kinds of kinematics tasks can be specified and combined.
In common inverse kinematics problems,
the tasks on position only (
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) and position and rotation (
\rm{SE}(3)
) are widely used
[
15
]
.
In addition, we introduce the following position and normal task (PN-task):

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{\mathit{pos}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\\
w_{\mathit{nrm}}\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{\mathit{nrm}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\end{pmatrix}

(7)

\displaystyle{\rm where}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{\mathit{pos}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})-\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})

(9)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{\mathit{nrm}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})=\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{T}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})+1

The subscript
{t,n}
is omitted while

\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}),\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represent the positions of contact points on the surface of bodies
\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}_{2}
, respectively.

\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}),\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represent the normal directions of the body surface at contact points, whereas

w_{\mathit{nrm}}
is a constant to adjust the weight of the position and normal tasks.
The norm of this task function becomes zero if and only if the contact positions coincide, and the contact normal directions are reverse
(i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}=\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}=-\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}
),
with an example shown in Fig.
2
(B).
As another candidate for the formulation of the normal task,
the following expression based on the angle between the normal directions can be considered:

\arccos(\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{T}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}})\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}))-\pi
.
However, this expression was not used
because it has the disadvantage that

\arccos
becomes indifferentiable around the point where the task function becomes zero (i.e., at the point
\arccos(-1)
).

II-C

Overview of the Problem Solution

The optimization problem (
2
) is a non-linear optimization with constraints.
Sequential quadratic programming (SQP) is a major method available to this kind of optimization problem
[
16
]
.
In the SQP,
the configuration
\hat{q}
is updated by the following equations:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k+1]}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k]}+\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{*[k]}

(10)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{*[k]}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}}\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k]})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}

(11)

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm s.t.}\ \ \mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k]})+\nabla\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k]})^{T}\Delta\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}\geq\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}

(12)

where
k
is the iteration index.
The iteration starts with some initial configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[1]}
corresponding to the nominal posture in this study,
and terminates when the norm of the task function gets small enough or the maximum number of iterations is attained.
The minimization in eq. (
11
) is a quadratic programming (QP) that is efficiently solved by the QP solver libraries.
If the contact point is fixed to the robot or environment and
the configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{t}
is without
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}
,
the formulae (
10
)-(
12
) provide an optimization-based solution for conventional IK
[
11
]
.

\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}
is a Jacobian matrix of the task function given by:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{e}$}}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}}\right|_{\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{q}$}}^{[k]}}

(13)

and
\mbox{\boldmath{$J$}}_{k}
consists of Jacobian matrices of element tasks
\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\theta$}}_{t}},\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}}
as a block matrix.

In the case the element task is PN-task (
7
),
the Jacobian matrix with respect to the joint position
\theta
becomes as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\theta$}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{pos},1}&\cdots&\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{pos},M}\\
w_{\mathit{nrm}}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{nrm},1}&\cdots&w_{\mathit{nrm}}\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{nrm},M}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \ \

(14)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{pos},m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}}{\partial\theta_{m}}-\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{2}}{\partial\theta_{m}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ m=1,\cdots,M

(15)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$j$}}_{\mathit{nrm},m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}}{\partial\theta_{m}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}^{T}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}}{\partial\theta_{m}}

(16)

The subscript
{t,n}
is omitted.

\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{i}}{\partial\theta_{m}},\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{i}}{\partial\theta_{m}}(i\in\{1,2\})
can be calculated from the kinematics model easily.
For example, if the pose of the body
\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}_{1}
depends on the
m
th joint, whose type is revolute,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}}{\partial\theta_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{a}$}}_{m}\times(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{p}$}}_{m})

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}}{\partial\theta_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{a}$}}_{m}\times\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}

(18)

\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{a}$}}_{m}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{p}$}}_{m}
represent the axis and the position of the
m
th joint, respectively.

\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}}
is a Jacobian matrix with respect to the body surface point
that is key in this study.
This is explained in detail in the next section.

III
Optimization of Contact Point

on Body Surface

III-A

Contact Configuration

In this section,
we define configuration variables representing the contact points on the body surface.

In eq. (
5
), we introduced the configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}
, that represents contact points at time index
t
.
The parameter
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t}
consists of two-dimensional configurations
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t,n,i}
corresponding to each contact point
where
n
is the index of the contact pair and
i\in\{1,2\}
is the index of the body.

From here,
we focus on one contact point and omit the subscripts
{t,n,i}
.
Assuming that the contact point of the
k
th iteration
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is given,
we map the contact configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
to the contact point of the next iteration
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k+1]}
as follows (Fig.
3
):

1.

Move locally on the tangent plane.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{p}$}}^{[k+1]}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1}\mbox{\boldmath{$\zeta$}}^{[k]}+u_{2}\mbox{\boldmath{$\eta$}}^{[k]}+\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k]}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\zeta$}}^{[k]}&\mbox{\boldmath{$\eta$}}^{[k]}\end{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k]}

(20)

\displaystyle{\rm where}

\displaystyle-u_{\mathit{lim}}\leq u_{1},u_{2}\leq u_{\mathit{lim}}

(21)

2.

Project from the tangent plane onto the body.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k+1]}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})=\mathit{project}(\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{p}$}}^{[k+1]}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}),\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}\,)

(22)

The parameters
u_{1},u_{2}
are elements of
u
.
For each iteration, we define a coordinate system with the contact point as the origin
where
\mbox{\boldmath{$\zeta$}}^{[k]},\mbox{\boldmath{$\eta$}}^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the tangential axes
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}^{[k]}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the normal axis of the body surface.
We use a smoothed normal direction as
\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}^{[k]}
, that is described in Sec. III.C.
The upper and lower limits
u_{\mathit{lim}}
are set in the contact configuration (eq. (
21
))
to prevent the error between the tangent plane and convex body becomes too large;

u_{\mathit{lim}}
of life-sized robots is set to be in the range 10-20 mm.
The function
\mathit{project}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}})
returns the point on the body
B
that is closest to the external point
p
.
For this projection, we employed an algorithm that exhaustively compares distances between
p
and each convex face of
B
.

Fig. 3:
Mapping from the contact configuration to the contact point.

The contact point of the next iteration is calculated by first moving locally on the tangent plane and then projecting it onto the body.

III-B

Optimization of Contact Point

By solving QP (
11
), we can obtain the optimal contact configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{*[k]}
and then the optimal contact point
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{*[k]}
in each iteration.
We derive the Jacobian matrix
\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{t,n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{t,n,i}}
of PN-task (
7
), that is necessary to formulate QP, as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}\\
w_{\mathit{nrm}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{2}^{T}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}_{1}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\zeta$}}&\mbox{\boldmath{$\eta$}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{1+}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{1-}}{2\varepsilon}&\frac{\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{2+}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{2-}}{2\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}

(25)

The subscripts
{t,n,i}
and superscript
k
are omitted.
Eqs. (
23
)-(
25
) describe the case when
u
represents the contact point on body
\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}_{1}
.
The formula for a contact point on
\mbox{\boldmath{$B$}}_{2}
can be derived similarly.
The Jacobian of normal direction is derived by finite difference (eq. (
25
)).

\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{1}^{i\pm}(i\in\{1,2\})
represents the normal direction at the contact point when
u_{i}=\pm\varepsilon
.

Since the contact configuration is defined only in a locally linearized coordinate system, there is no global configuration for the contact point.
Therefore, the contact configuration is not updated in eq. (
10
) in the SQP.
Instead, the new contact point
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k+1]}
is saved and used as the origin for the linearization of the next iteration.

In summary, each iteration of contact optimization is performed as follows:

0.

The contact point of the initial iteration
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[1]}
is determined by some rule (e.g., random or closest to the target).

1.

The Jacobian matrix at the contact point
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k]}
is calculated from eq. (
23
).

2.

The optimal contact configuration
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{*[k]}
is obtained by solving QP (
11
).
The point on the tangent plane
\mbox{\boldmath{$\bar{p}$}}^{[k+1]}
is simultaneously obtained (eq. (
20
)).

3.

A new contact point
\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}}^{[k+1]}
is calculated by projection from eq. (
22
).

The joint position
\theta
is also updated in each iteration.
By this optimization-based method,
a robot posture is generated while automatically searching contact points.

III-C

Normal Smoothing

To apply a gradient-based optimization like the SQP,
a differentiable task function is required.
When the body is represented by a convex polyhedron, the normal direction of the body surface is indifferentiable on the edges (Fig.
4
(A)).
This also renders the tangent direction indifferentiable, so that all components of the PN-task (
7
) become indifferentiable.

We propose to define the smoothed normal
\hat{\xi}
on the body surface point
p
as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\hat{\xi}$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})=\mathit{normalize}\left(\frac{\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{\psi}+\sum_{\bar{\psi}\in\bar{\Psi}(\psi)}w_{\bar{\psi}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{\bar{\psi}}}{1+\sum_{\bar{\psi}\in\bar{\Psi}(\psi)}w_{\bar{\psi}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})}\right)

(26)

and use it instead of the conventional normal
\xi
.
This formula represents the normalized vector of the weighted average of normals.

\psi
represents a face to which the surface point
p
belongs.

\bar{\Psi}(\psi)
represents a set of faces adjacent to
\psi
,
with
\mbox{\boldmath{$\xi$}}_{\psi}
representing a normal of the face
\psi
.

The parameter
w_{\bar{\psi}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})
is a normal weight of adjacent faces calculated as follows:

\displaystyle w_{\bar{\psi}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}})=f(x(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}},\bar{\psi}))

(27)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\ \ f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-2\left(\frac{x}{R}\right)^{2}+1&(0\leq x\leq\frac{R}{2})\\
2\left(\frac{x}{R}-1\right)^{2}&(\frac{R}{2}\leq x\leq R)\end{array}\right.

x(\mbox{\boldmath{$p$}},\bar{\psi})
is the distance on the body surface from point
p
to the adjacent face
\bar{\psi}
;

f(x)
is a smooth function for interpolation from
1
to
0
(Fig.
5
);
and
R
is the smoothing range, within which adjacent faces are searched.
Since this function is smooth and its gradient is zero at
x=0,\,R
, the smoothed normal
\hat{\xi}
becomes differentiable (Fig.
4
(B)).
Taking into account the discrete change in a set of adjacent faces
\bar{\Psi}(\psi)
,
the finite difference was used to calculate the derivative in eq. (
25
).

This method does not require prior conversion of the body shape and can handle various 3D shapes from detailed convex meshes to primitive shapes.

IV-A

Gradient-based Contact Point Searching

The gradient-based contact point searching by SQP (
10
)-(
12
) is validated (Fig.
7
).
We deal with the problem of finding the contact point on the body surface closest to the target position and the normal direction for a single body without joints.
This corresponds to the case that
T=1
and
\theta
is empty in eqs. (
4
),(
5
).
Fig.
7
(A1)-(A3) confirmed the convergence to a contact point that matches the target.
From an initial state with an error of about
2
m, the error decreased to less than
0.1
mm and

V-A

Object Holding with Dual Arms

We generated a posture of whole-body manipulation, in which a mobile manipulator PR2 holds a box-shaped object with dual arms (Fig.
11
).
Since the weight of the object is
3

insufficient friction makes it difficult to grasp with just the tip of both grippers, and it is therefore necessary to grasp with whole-arm links.

In this problem,
the joint position
\theta
represents the 17 joint angles and the base link position (x, y, yaw) relative to the object.
The task involves satisfaction of the contacts with the PN-task (
7

Six contact links of dual arms are shown in Fig.
12

13

Since a two-dimensional configuration representing the surface point on the robot link and object for each of the six pairs exists,
the contact configuration
u
comprises 24 dimensions.
Only the lower half of the object surface is specified as the contact region, so that the object is grasped stably.
The contact point of the initial iteration is determined by a non-ad hoc rule; the surface points of the robot link and object closest to the origin of the world frame were selected (Fig.
12
(A),(C)).
As a constraint, the joint position range and collision avoidance between the robot and the object were specified.
At 200 iterations of the SQP, the computation time was 15 s.
The computational cost was high owing to the many contact points and complexity of the body shape.
As mentioned above,
the experimental program in this paper has been implemented in EusLisp, which is a scripting language;
by implementing it in a compiled language,
it is expected to be considerably faster.

V-B

Pushing Operation with Knee

The proposed method based on inverse kinematics is extended to the framework of inverse kinematics-and-statics
[
20
]

by introducing the contact wrench configuration and wrench equilibrium task to the optimization problem (
2
)
[
21
]
.
By this extended method, the pushing motion by a humanoid robot HRP-2 was generated with consideration of balance.
The target object was a
20
kg shelf, and because the friction with the floor is high, it falls when pushed by hands (Fig.
14
(D)).
Therefore, the robot takes the operation strategy of pushing with the knee, in which, the shelf does not fall because the action point of the pushing force is low and the overturning moment is small (Fig.
14
(C2)).
The contact between the knee link and the shelf is a body-to-body contact, and therefore the proposed method can be used for generating a motion.
Fig.
14
(A),(C) show the generated motion and the results of the execution.
By repeatedly pushing and walking, the robot was able to carry the shelf forward.
The stabilization control based on the online sensor feedback
[
22
]
was applied to the experiment.

We represent the pushing motion with three postures (
T=3
in eq. (
1
));
pre-pushing posture without and with an applied force to the shelf, and a pushing posture.
The dimension of
\mbox{\boldmath{$q$}}_{t}
in eq. (
5
) was 62;
of which 32 were related to the joint position,
6 were related to the root link pose,
12 were related to the contact force,
and the remaining 12 were related to the contact configuration.
Each posture was calculated by a separate optimization, taking approximately 8 s for 100 iterations.
The pose of the robot’s base link was represented by three linear and three rotational virtual joints between the world frame and the base link.
The motion trajectory was generated by interpolating a sequence of statically stable postures
slowly enough that the dynamic effects could be ignored
[
23
]
.
The pushing force for each knee is
100
N.
The knee contact is represented as the PN-task (
7

\rm{SE}(3)
) task.
Fig.

Since the robot needs to bend the knee for pushing forward, the knee contact point moves on the link surface during pushing.
With the proposed method, automatic determination of the contact point for sliding and rolling motions on the link surface is possible.

VI
Conclusions

In this study,
we proposed the posture generation method to determine the joint positions and contact points simultaneously.
Various experiments on simulation and real robots showed the effectiveness of the optimization
with the local contact configurations and normal smoothing of the body surface.
As future work,
the proposed method will be extended for the dynamic motions with the continuous contact transitions (e.g., sliding and rolling).
