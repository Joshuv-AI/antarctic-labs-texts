Differential effects of romosozumab across central and peripheral skeletal sites: a prospective DXA and HR-pQCT study in high-risk postmenopausal osteoporosis
Authors: Ko Chiba, Naoko Uchida, Kayoko Ota, Mika Kono, Kounosuke Watanabe, Ryuji Niimi, Keisuke Tokunaga, Kazuteru Shiraishi, Makoto Osaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction: Romosozumab is known to markedly increase areal BMD (aBMD) at the LS and proximal femur assessed by DXA; however, its effects on peripheral volumetric BMD (vBMD) and bone microarchitecture measured by HR-pQCT remain incompletely characterized in postmenopausal women at very high fracture risk. Materials and methods: In this single-center, prospective longitudinal observational study, postmenopausal women with primary osteoporosis and a history of fragility fractures received romosozumab for 12 mo. DXA (LS, proximal femur, and radius) and HR-pQCT (distal radius and tibia) were performed at baseline and at 6 and 12 mo. Results: Twenty-five patients were enrolled; 22 completed 12 mo and were analyzed. LS (L1-L4) aBMD increased by 18.0% at 6 mo and 24.4% at 12 mo (both p < .001), whereas TH aBMD increased by 4.9% and 7.3%, respectively (p = .001 and p < .001). In contrast, radius aBMD did not change significantly at either the one-third distal or ultradistal sites. On HR-pQCT, cortical vBMD at distal radius decreased modestly (−1.0% at 6 mo, −1.2% at 12 mo, p = .001 and p = .002), while trabecular thickness increased at 12 mo (+2.0%, p = .001); no other parameters changed significantly at the distal radius. No significant changes in vBMD or bone microarchitecture were observed at the distal tibia. Discussion: These findings suggest that 12 mo of romosozumab treatment resulted in marked increases in central aBMD at the LS and proximal femur by DXA, while effects on peripheral aBMD, vBMD, and bone microarchitecture at the distal radius and tibia assessed by DXA and HR-pQCT were limited, suggesting site-specific skeletal responses.

Introduction

Romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin, was approved in 2019 and currently plays an important role in the therapeutic strategy for severe osteoporosis. In an international, multicenter phase 3 clinical trial (the Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis: FRAME), 12 mo of romosozumab treatment resulted in marked increases in BMD, with gains of 13.3% at the LS and 6.8% at the TH, demonstrating a robust anabolic effect on bone.1 Similarly, in another international, multicenter phase 3 clinical trial (the Active-controlled Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis at High Risk of Fracture: ARCH), 12 mo of romosozumab administration led to increases of 13.7% in LS BMD and 6.2% in TH BMD, confirming its substantial efficacy in increasing BMD.2

In clinical practice, BMD is assessed using DXA; however, because DXA provides only a 2-dimensional measurement, it cannot differentiate cortical from trabecular compartments or characterize bone microarchitecture. HR-pQCT is a novel imaging modality that enables in vivo visualization of human bone microarchitecture without the invasiveness of a bone biopsy.3–5 To date, various osteoporosis medications have been evaluated using HR-pQCT,6–20 but studies investigating the effects of romosozumab on bone microarchitecture with HR-pQCT remain scarce.21,22

The purpose of this study was to comprehensively investigate the effects of romosozumab on areal BMD at the LS, proximal femur, and distal radius as well as on volumetric BMD and bone microarchitecture at the distal radius and tibia, in women with osteoporosis at a high risk of fracture, using DXA and HR-pQCT.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a single-center, prospective longitudinal observational study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagasaki University Hospital (approval No. 21111516; approval date: November 16, 2021) and was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they had received a full explanation of the study. Participant enrollment was conducted between November 2021 and December 2024.

Study participants

The subjects were postmenopausal women with primary osteoporosis and a history of fragility fractures who visited the osteoporosis outpatient clinic at Nagasaki University Hospital and received 12 mo of treatment with romosozumab (Evenity, 210 mg subcutaneous injection, once a month). For all patients, vitamin D supplementation (alfacalcidol, 1.0 μg/day) was provided, reflecting routine clinical practice in Japan.

The inclusion criteria were postmenopausal women with primary osteoporosis and a history of fragility fractures. Exclusion criteria were male patients; patients with diseases that cause secondary osteoporosis; prior use of glucocorticoids, bisphosphonates, anti-RANKL antibodies, or PTH receptor agonists; and patients with contraindications to romosozumab.

Assessments

Assessments were performed at baseline and at 6 and 12 mo after treatment initiation. Areal BMD (aBMD) of the LS, proximal femur, and radius was measured by DXA. Volumetric BMD (vBMD) and bone microarchitecture of the radius and tibia were evaluated by HR-pQCT. The speed of sound (SOS) at the calcaneus was measured by quantitative ultrasound (QUS). Bone turnover markers were assessed using blood samples.

DXA

Areal BMD of the LS, proximal femur, and radius was measured using DXA (Lunar iDXA, GE HealthCare; analysis software: enCORE version 18).

The LS was measured at L1-L4. Vertebrae with abnormally high BMD values due to vertebral fractures or degenerative changes were rigorously excluded. As additional assessments, aBMD was measured separately for each vertebra (L1, L2, L3, and L4), and trabecular bone score (TBS) for L1-L4 was also assessed.

The proximal femur was measured at TH, FN, and trochanter (defined as the TH minus the FN), and additionally subdivided and analyzed at superior and inferior regions of the FN, superior region of the trochanter (greater trochanter), and inferior region of the trochanter (lesser trochanter), using the subregional measurement function of the analysis software. Both hips were scanned, and the mean value of the bilateral measurements was used for analysis.

For the radius, the non-dominant side was scanned, and measurements were performed at the distal one-third and the ultradistal sites.

The measurement precision of DXA at our institution, expressed as root mean square coefficients of variation (RMSCV), was 1.21% for L1-L4, 0.52% for the TH, 0.93% for the FN, 0.77% for the distal one-third radius, and 2.83% for the ultradistal radius.

HR-pQCT

Volumetric BMD, bone microarchitecture, and estimated bone strength at the distal radius and tibia were assessed using HR-pQCT (XtremeCT II, SCANCO Medical AG). The non-dominant forearm and lower leg of each participant were immobilized using a dedicated fixation device. Scan regions were defined using the relative offset method: for the radius, a 10.2-mm-thick region located 4% of the forearm length proximal to the wrist joint was scanned, and for the tibia, a 10.2-mm-thick region located 7.3% of the tibial length proximal to the ankle joint was acquired.

All scans were performed according to the standard protocol with the following parameters: tube voltage 68 kVp, tube current 1470 μA, integration time 4.3 ms, projections 900, field of view 140 mm, matrix size 2304 × 2304, and isotropic voxel size 60.7 μm. Each scan consisted of 168 slices with an acquisition time of 2.0 min per site. The radiation dose per scan was 10.8 mGy for CTDIvol and 11.0 mGy·cm for DLP, corresponding to an effective dose of 5 μSv.

Immediately after acquisition, motion artifacts were evaluated, and scans were repeated up to a maximum of three times until images of acceptable quality (motion grade 1 or 2) were obtained.23,24

Analysis of vBMD, bone microstructure, and estimated bone strength

Volumetric BMD and bone microarchitectural parameters were analyzed using dedicated software (TRI/3D-BON, Ratoc System Engineering Co., Ltd.). Three-dimensional matching (3D registration) was performed across datasets acquired at baseline, and at 6 and 12 mo after treatment initiation, and all measurements were conducted within the spatially matched common volume of interest.25

The analyzed parameters included total vBMD (Tt.BMD, mg HA/ccm), cortical vBMD (Ct.BMD, mg HA/ccm), cortical thickness (Ct.Th, mm), cortical porosity (Ct.Po, %), trabecular vBMD (Tb.BMD, mg HA/ccm), bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular number (Tb.N, 1/mm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), connectivity density (Conn.D), and structure model index (SMI).26,27

Volumetric BMD values were calculated from X-ray attenuation values using a linear calibration curve generated from images of a BMD phantom composed of hydroxyapatite (HA) rods. Bone microstructure analysis was performed after image binarization using guideline-defined thresholds of 320 mg/cm3 for trabecular bone and 450 mg/cm3 for cortical bone.26 Ct.Th, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp were measured using a direct distance transformation method.28 Conn.D is an index that quantifies trabecular connectivity, and SMI is an index that quantifies whether trabecular morphology is plate-like (SMI = 0) or rod-like (SMI = 3).29

Estimated bone strength was analyzed using finite element analysis with integrated software (IPL, SCANCO Medical AG). Outcome measures included stiffness (kN/mm) and failure load (FL, kN). A homogeneous, linear-elastic material model was assumed, with a Young’s modulus of 10 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3. Axial loading was applied along the longitudinal bone axis, and the failure was defined as the condition in which more than 2% of all voxels exceeded a strain threshold of 0.7%.26

The measurement precision (RMSCV) of HR-pQCT at our institution ranged from 0.9%-1.0% for total bone parameters (Tt.BMD); 0.6%-1.4% for cortical bone parameters (Ct.BMD and Ct.Th), with 6.0% for Ct.Po; 0.8%-3.3% for trabecular parameters (Tb.BMD, BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp); and 3.3%-5.8% for ConnD and SMI.25

QUS

Speed of sound of the calcaneus was measured using QUS (CM200, Furuno Electric Co., Ltd.). The measurement precision (RMSCV) of QUS at our institution was 0.59% for SOS.

Biochemical markers

As bone-related biochemical markers, serum calcium (albumin-corrected using the Payne formula, cCa), phosphate (P), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and intact PTH were measured. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b (TRACP-5b) was assessed as a bone resorption marker, and total type I procollagen N-terminal propeptide (total P1NP) was measured as a bone formation marker. In addition, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH) vitamin D) levels were assessed at baseline only.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on previously reported 12-mo HR-pQCT-derived BMD changes following anabolic therapy (mean change, 3.0%; SD, 3.5%).19 Assuming a 2-sided significance level of 0.01% and 90% statistical power, a one-sample t-test indicated that 20 participants were required. Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, a target sample size of 25 participants was set.

The analysis population comprised participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one evaluable outcome at baseline and at least one post-treatment time point.

For outcome analyses, means and SDs of baseline values and percentage changes at 6 and 12 mo after treatment initiation were calculated. Changes from baseline to 6 and 12 mo were assessed using paired t-tests, with multiplicity controlled using the Bonferroni correction. A 2-sided alpha level of 0.01 was considered statistically significant.

No imputation was performed for missing data. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.4).

Results

Study participants

A total of 25 patients were enrolled in this study. Three patients (12%) dropped out by 6 mo after treatment initiation, and no further dropouts occurred between 6 and 12 mo. The reasons for discontinuation were difficulty in hospital visits (n = 2) and the occurrence of cerebral infarction (n = 1).

As shown in Table 1, the analyzed cohort consisted of 22 women with a mean age of 71.2 yr (range, 53-85 yr) at enrollment. A history of vertebral fractures was present in 59.1%, and 18.2% had a prior proximal femoral fracture. Most patients (77.3%) were treatment-naïve for osteoporosis. Unless otherwise specified, continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD. The FRAX major osteoporotic fracture probability was 27.8 ± 12.1%, and DXA T-scores were −3.4 ± 1.0 at the LS, −2.7 ± 0.7 at the TH, and −3.1 ± 0.7 at the FN. All participants were classified as being at very high fracture risk.

In this study, changes in aBMD were also evaluated separately for each lumbar vertebra (L1, L2, L3, and L4). At 6 mo, the percent increases were 20.4%, 19.0%, 16.7%, and 16.9% for L1-L4, respectively (all p < .001), with greater increases observed in the upper lumbar vertebrae. At 12 mo, the corresponding increases were 26.5%, 23.3%, 21.3%, and 25.4% (all p < .001), with all vertebrae showing increases exceeding 20%.

Trabecular bone score (L1-L4) showed no significant change at 6 mo (+2.3 ± 4.4%, p = .081), but a significant increase was observed at 12 mo (+4.0 ± 4.1%, p = .001).

At the proximal femur, aBMD increased significantly at the TH (+4.9 ± 5.2% at 6 mo, p = .001; +7.3 ± 6.5% at 12 mo, p < .001) and at the FN (+4.4 ± 4.4% at 6 mo, p < .001; and +7.0 ± 5.5% at 12 mo, p < .001).

In this study, the proximal femur was further subdivided into the FN and trochanteric regions, and each region was additionally divided into superior and inferior subregions. At the FN, percentage increases were greater in the superior subregion (+5.3 ± 5.8% at 6 mo, p = .001; +8.6 ± 7.0% at 12 mo, p < .001) than in the inferior subregion (+3.8 ± 4.4% at 6 mo, p = .002; +6.0 ± 5.1% at 12 mo, p < .001).

At the radius, aBMD showed no significant change at 12 mo at either the one-third distal site (−1.1 ± 2.7%, p = 1.00) or the ultradistal site (+0.6 ± 7.4%, p = 1.00).

In contrast, Tb.Th increased by 2.0 ± 2.0% at 12 mo (p = .001), whereas no other parameters showed significant changes.

QUS

Discussion

This study comprehensively evaluated the effects of 12 mo of romosozumab therapy in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis and a history of fragility fractures, covering skeletal sites from central to peripheral bones, including the spine, femur, radius, tibia, and calcaneus. As shown in Figure 5, romosozumab markedly increased BMD at central skeletal sites, whereas minimal effects were observed at peripheral skeletal sites. In addition, this study carefully assessed bone microarchitecture as well as BMD using HR-pQCT; however, the effects on peripheral bone microarchitecture remained limited.

Mean (SD) changes in aBMD at the LS, proximal femur, and one-third distal radius assessed by DXA, and in vBMD and bone microarchitecture at the distal radius and tibia assessed by HR-pQCT after 12 mo of romosozumab treatment. Statistically significant changes are indicated in bold. Treatment effects differed between central and peripheral skeletal sites.

LS and proximal femur

As shown in Table 3 and Figure 2, aBMD at the LS (L1-L4) increased to a greater extent (mean + 24.4%) in the present study than that reported in clinical trials (FRAME: mean + 13.3%; ARCH: mean + 13.7%). In contrast, aBMD at the proximal femur increased comparably (mean + 7.3%) to that observed in clinical trials (FRAME: mean + 6.8%; ARCH: mean + 6.2%).

In this study, during DXA analysis of the LS, vertebrae showing abnormally elevated values were strictly evaluated using anteroposterior and lateral images, and 17 vertebrae were excluded from the analysis out of 88 vertebrae due to vertebral fractures or degeneration changes (bone sclerosis, osteophyte, scoliosis, and so). In addition, for cases showing extremely large increases in LS aBMD in Figure 2, DXA images and lateral lumbar radiographs were rechecked, confirming that these increases were not influenced by vertebral fractures or degenerative changes and therefore represented true treatment-related gains.

The FRAME study is an international clinical trial of romosozumab that included Japanese participants, and Japanese subanalyses have been reported.30,31 Compared with Western populations, Japanese patients showed greater increases in LS aBMD (mean + 16.3%), findings that are consistent with those observed in the present study.

In contrast, changes in LS TBS were smaller than those observed for aBMD, suggesting that TBS may be less useful for treatment monitoring. Changes in TBS were also reported in the phase 3 ARCH trial. Consistent with our findings, 12 mo of romosozumab treatment resulted in a 14.2% increase in LS aBMD, whereas TBS increased by only 5.1%, indicating a relatively smaller magnitude of change.32

We additionally evaluated aBMD changes at individual lumbar vertebrae (L1, L2, L3, and L4) and found a trend toward greater percentage increases in the upper LS at 6 mo after treatment. This finding may be attributable to the lower baseline aBMD values in the upper lumbar vertebrae compared with the lower lumbar levels, resulting in larger relative gains.

In the present study, aBMD at the proximal femur was analyzed by subdividing the region into 4 subregions, among which the superior FN showed the greatest percentage increase. This may be explained by the lower baseline aBMD in the superior FN compared with other subregions, resulting in larger relative gains.

It is well recognized that Japanese individuals have a higher incidence of vertebral fractures than Western populations. The greater LS aBMD response to romosozumab observed in Japanese patients, together with the early increase in aBMD at the upper LS (the thoracolumbar junction), a common site of vertebral fractures, is therefore of substantial clinical importance.

In addition, FN fractures are thought to occur following falls, typically initiating at the superior aspect of the FN, where bone density is lowest. The pronounced therapeutic effect of romosozumab in these mechanically vulnerable regions has important clinical implications.

Radius and tibia

In DXA assessments in the present study, substantial increases in aBMD were observed at the LS and proximal femur; however, as shown in Table 3 and Figure 2, no significant increase in aBMD was detected at the radius.

Consistent with these findings, a phase 2 clinical trial of romosozumab also reported no significant increase in radial aBMD,33 suggesting that the lack of response at the radius is not specific to the present study.

In the present study, we performed a detailed assessment of vBMD and microarchitecture at the radius and tibia using HR-pQCT; however, as shown in Table 4 and Figures 3 and 4, no significant effects of romosozumab were observed at these peripheral sites.

Although the small sample size (n = 22) may have limited the ability to detect treatment effects, inspection of the scatter plots (Figures 3 and 4) suggests that even with a larger sample size, the overall findings would be unlikely to change substantially. Another possibility is that the study population happened to be less responsive to romosozumab; however, given the clear treatment effects observed at the LS and proximal femur, it is unlikely that the participants constituted a treatment-resistant population. Concerns regarding the validity of HR-pQCT measurements could also be raised; nevertheless, the absence of a treatment effect at the distal radius was consistently confirmed by both DXA and HR-pQCT.

To date, in addition to the present study (n = 22; mean age, 71.2 yr), one other HR-pQCT-based study has been reported in a similar population of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis at very high fracture risk (n = 16; mean age, 71 yr). In that study, although LS and femoral aBMD increased as assessed by DXA, 44% of patients (7/16) were classified as non-responders without improvement in bone microarchitecture, and no significant increases were observed in vBMD or bone microarchitecture at the distal radius.22 Furthermore, an HR-pQCT-based study has also been reported in postmenopausal women with multiple myeloma (n = 9; mean age, 67.4 yr). In that study, DXA demonstrated increases in LS aBMD (+5.8%) and femoral aBMD (+3.3%); however, no significant improvements were observed in vBMD or bone microarchitecture at the distal radius or tibia on HR-pQCT.21

In contrast, multiple studies have investigated the effects of teriparatide using HR-pQCT. Although the findings have been heterogeneous, in a study of postmenopausal Japanese women comparable to the present cohort, 18 mo of treatment resulted in median increases of +12.0% in LS aBMD and +3.0% in femoral aBMD. At the distal radius and tibia, although no significant increase was observed in Ct.BMD, significant improvements were detected in Ct.Th (radius, +1.3%; tibia, +3.9%) and Tb.BMD (+6.4% and +3.7%, respectively).19 These results indicate that teriparatide may have more favorable effects than romosozumab at peripheral skeletal sites. However, direct comparisons between these studies are limited by differences in study populations and treatment durations.

In the phase 3 ARCH trial, treatment with romosozumab followed by alendronate was compared with alendronate alone. At 24 mo, this regimen reduced the risk of new vertebral fractures by 48% and hip fractures by 38% compared with alendronate alone, whereas the reduction in non-vertebral fractures, including peripheral skeletal sites, such as the radius and tibia, was more modest at 19%.2 These findings indicate that, compared with bisphosphonates, romosozumab may have greater fracture risk reduction at central skeletal sites than at peripheral skeletal sites, and this may be related to the rapid and substantial increases in aBMD at the LS and proximal femur observed in the present study.

Furthermore, in the phase 3 FRAME trial, 12 mo of treatment with romosozumab reduced the risk of vertebral fractures by 73% and non-vertebral fractures by 25% compared with placebo.1 In contrast, in the Fracture Prevention Trial, a phase 3 study of teriparatide published in 2001, a median of 19 mo of treatment reduced the risk of vertebral fractures by 65% and non-vertebral fragility fractures by 53% compared with placebo.34 Although direct comparisons between these trials are limited by differences in patient populations and treatment durations, the discrepancy between vertebral and non-vertebral fracture risk reduction appears smaller with teriparatide (65% vs 53%) than with romosozumab (73% vs 25%). These findings, together with the results of the present study, suggest that romosozumab may exhibit site-specific skeletal responses, with greater effects at central skeletal sites and comparatively limited effects at peripheral sites.

Although the mechanisms underlying the limited effects of romosozumab on peripheral bone remain unclear, marked responses were observed in some individuals in the present study. Moreover, a recent conference report using HR-pQCT in premenopausal women with idiopathic osteoporosis (n = 29; mean age, 31 yr) demonstrated significant effects at both the radius and tibia, indicating that further investigation is warranted.35 Because participants in the present study are currently undergoing sequential therapy with denosumab, we also plan to examine whether treatment effects on peripheral bone emerge at later time points.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The small sample size and the absence of a control group limit the robustness of the findings and constrain their interpretation.

In addition, because different imaging modalities were used to assess central and peripheral skeletal sites, it cannot be concluded that romosozumab has no effect on bone microarchitecture. If microarchitectural assessment of the vertebrae and femur had been feasible, substantial improvements in bone microarchitecture might have been observed.

Conclusions

In postmenopausal women with osteoporosis and a history of fragility fractures, 12 mo of romosozumab treatment resulted in marked increases in aBMD at the LS and proximal femur; however, no significant changes were observed in vBMD or microarchitecture at the distal radius or tibia. The mechanisms underlying the discordant effects of romosozumab between central and peripheral skeletal sites remain unclear, and further data accumulation and investigation are required.
