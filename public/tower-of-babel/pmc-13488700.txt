Improving Critical Thinking and Nursing Outcomes Through an Evidence-Based Nutritional Protocol in Critically Ill Patients: A Quasi-Experimental Study
Authors: Peibei Zhang, Fang Yang, Hongyi Sun, Yuexiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignThis study is a single-center quasi-experimental study.PurposeThis study aimed to develop an evidence-based nutritional management protocol for critically ill patients and evaluate its impact on junior nurses’ critical thinking and nursing quality.MethodsPatients and junior nurses were assigned to the study or control group according to the study implementation schedule. The study group received the evidence-based nursing (EBN) protocol with structured training, while the control group received conventional care. Outcomes included nurses’ critical thinking skills assessed via the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory-Chinese Version (CTDI-CV), patient nutritional biomarkers (serum albumin, prealbumin), complication rates (aspiration, diarrhea, abdominal distension), and patient satisfaction.ResultsThe study included 7 junior nurses in each group and a total of 84 critically ill patients (42 per group). We developed a nutritional management protocol through evidence synthesis, expert consultation, and revisions. This protocol demonstrated preliminary clinical applicability, offering a structured framework for EBN practice in critical care. After training, the study group showed a significantly greater improvement in critical thinking scores than the control group (mean increase: 12 vs 3 points; t=2.417, P < 0.05). We observed no significant intergroup differences in aspiration, diarrhea, abdominal distension, weight, systolic blood pressure, or blood glucose (all P > 0.05). However, the study group showed superior serum albumin, prealbumin, and patient satisfaction (P < 0.01).ConclusionWithin the limitations of a small sample size and single-center design, this EBN-based protocol demonstrated potential to enhance junior nurses’ specialized critical thinking and improve patient nutritional outcomes and satisfaction. These preliminary findings suggest potential value for optimizing critical care quality. We recommend further validation through larger multicenter studies with extended follow-up before considering widespread adoption.

Introduction

In critical care, effective nutritional support is crucial for improving clinical outcomes, reducing complications, and decreasing mortality among critically ill patients.1 Critically ill patients are at high risk of malnutrition. This risk results from metabolic stress and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Therefore, standardized, evidence-based nutritional protocols are critical for recovery.2,3

Evidence-based nursing (EBN) integrates research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient preferences. It offers a valuable framework for optimizing nutritional care.4,5 Junior nurses are registered nurses with ≤ 3 years of ICU experience. They play an increasingly important role in critical care. However, they often lack systematic training in nutritional management.6,7 This experience gap hinders their ability to apply specialized critical thinking.8 In clinical practice, junior nurses frequently face challenges such as limited experience in interpreting dynamic nutritional biomarkers, difficulty in individualizing feeding strategies under rapidly changing patient conditions, and heavy workload pressures that may reduce adherence to complex nutritional guidelines. These factors collectively contribute to variability in the implementation of evidence-based nutritional recommendations.

Specialized critical thinking is the capacity to analyze, evaluate, and resolve clinical problems using professional knowledge.9 In the context of nutrition, such thinking involves interpreting biomarkers, adjusting feeding strategies, and recognizing complications.10 For junior nurses, deficiencies in this competency can lead to delayed recognition of complications or over-reliance on standardized protocols.11 Improved critical thinking may therefore facilitate more accurate interpretation of patient nutritional status, more timely adjustment of feeding regimens, and earlier identification of complications, which in turn may contribute to better patient nutritional outcomes and improved overall recovery.

Recent guidelines, such as those from the European Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN) (2021)12 and the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) (2022),13 emphasize phase-specific and individualized nutritional support. Studies confirm that nurse-provided nutritional care quality affects patient recovery.13 However, practice often fails to align with these guidelines, especially in resource-limited settings.14 Few studies have concurrently examined the combined effect of an evidence-based nutritional protocol on both nurse critical thinking competency and patient outcomes. Investigating this dual impact is essential for optimizing nursing education and clinical practice. It enables the simultaneous evaluation of nursing competency development and its translation into measurable patient-level outcomes. This approach helps bridge the gap between educational interventions and clinical outcomes.

To address this gap, we developed an evidence-based nutritional management protocol. This protocol was designed for critically ill patients. We evaluated its effects on junior nurses’ specialized critical thinking and nursing quality. We hypothesize that the implementation of this EBN-based protocol, coupled with structured training, will significantly improve junior nurses’ specialized critical thinking scores and lead to better patient nutritional biomarkers and higher satisfaction compared to conventional practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a single-center quasi-experimental, two-group comparative study. We developed the study protocol following a systematic process involving evidence synthesis, expert consultation, and multidisciplinary consensus.

Study Participants

This study involved 84 critically ill patients admitted to the ICU of our hospital from May 25, 2025, to November 30, 2025. The follow-up period ended on February 28, 2026. We identified eligible patients via electronic medical records. Trained research nurses and intensivists conducted daily screening. After obtaining consent, we allocated the patients to either the study or the control group. The 18-bed ICU admits critically ill patients with severe trauma, sepsis, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and postoperative complications. It is staffed by 35 nurses (including 14 junior nurses) and 8 intensivists, with nurse-to-patient ratios of 1:1–2 during daytime and 1:3–4 at night. We assigned 14 junior nurses to the control group and the study group (7 nurses in each group). Standard nutritional support included EN within 24–48 hours and nutritional monitoring. We also took other measures to control potential interfering factors. Our patient screening involved stringent eligibility criteria to reduce heterogeneity in baseline disease severity and physiological reserve. We collected and compared baseline data for both patients and nurses. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups for both nurses and patients. Among nurses, age, educational attainment, and ICU work experience were comparable between groups (Table 1), and no significant between-group differences were observed in patient characteristics (Table 2). We obtained approval for the study protocol from the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine and strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (No. 20240625). General Information of Patients

Eligibility criteria for patients included: 1) diagnosis of critical illness according to established standards;15 2) ICU admission with full critical care resources; and 3) partial gastrointestinal function without severe obstruction or perforation, enabling EN or combined EN-parenteral nutrition (PN). Exclusion criteria were: 1) severe cognitive or psychiatric impairment (eg, advanced dementia, schizophrenia, status epilepticus) affecting assessment or adherence; 2) terminal-stage conditions (eg, malignancies, renal/hepatic failure) with a life expectancy < 7 days or receiving palliative care; 3) congenital metabolic disorders (eg, phenylketonuria) or short bowel syndrome requiring specialized nutrition; 4) pregnancy or lactation; 5) transfer, discharge, or consent withdrawal compromising data completeness.

With a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-tailed) and a power (1−β) of 0.80, the initial calculation indicated a requirement of 12 patients per group. To enhance robustness and account for the nested design involving both nurses and patients, we enrolled all 14 available junior nurses (7 per group) and expanded the patient sample accordingly. Using the same statistical parameters, the final patient sample size was set at 84 (42 per group). This ensures adequate power to detect clinically significant differences in key patient outcomes. Because the total number of eligible junior nurses in the ICU was limited, the study was not formally powered to detect differences in nurse-level outcomes, such as CTDI-CV scores; therefore, analyses of nurse critical thinking should be interpreted as exploratory.

Group Allocation and Blinding

Patients were allocated to the control group or study group according to the order of enrollment, with the first 42 eligible patients assigned to the control group and the subsequent 42 assigned to the study group. Similarly, the 14 junior nurses were assigned to the two groups based on staffing requirements and unit schedules. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after obtaining written consent (Figure 1). To minimize assessment bias, outcome assessors responsible for collecting data on patient nutritional parameters and evaluating nurse competency were kept blinded to group assignment throughout the study. Protocol Development

We established a multidisciplinary team comprising 10 specialists to formulate the nutritional management protocol. The team consisted of two intensivists, two registered dietitians, one EBN specialist, and five senior nurses. Each of them provided the team with professional knowledge and insights from different disciplines and jointly drafted this protocol. Meanwhile, We systematically used Boolean operators (AND, OR) to searched major domestic and international databases from the past five years for evidence on nutritional management for critically ill patients. Its development incorporated tools such as the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002),16 Nutritional Risk Screening in Cancer Patients,17 Malnutrition Screening and Assessment,18 and Guidelines for the Provision of Nutrition Support Therapy in the Adult Critically Ill Patient.19 The final draft protocol has fully taken into account the specific clinical situation of our institution, as well as the clinical facilities and staffing of our hospital.

Expert Consultation

To validate and refine the draft protocol, a structured expert consultation was conducted (Supplementary Documents 1 and 2). Selected consultants possessed extensive expertise in critical care, clinical nutrition, EBN, and nursing management. A hybrid model (encrypted Email and online survey) was used to distribute a structured evaluation questionnaire (Supplementary Figure S1), which was reviewed for content validity by three independent specialists. The questionnaire covered core four domains: scientific validity, clinical utility, innovation potential, and operational feasibility. It uses a 5-point Likert scale and open-ended questions to obtain feedback.

Protocol Implementation and Training

The primary distinction between the control and study groups pertained to curriculum design and delivery. The control group received a standardized, nurse-led core curriculum,20 while the study group was assigned an enhanced, multidisciplinary curriculum that incorporated advanced modules for managing complex clinical conditions. Both groups completed a structured 7-day training program, which concluded with a competency evaluation. Full details regarding the training content, implementation methods, and assessment criteria for both programs are available in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S1).

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

We assessed specialized critical thinking competency among junior nurses using the Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV),21 a 70-item instrument (Cronbach’s α = 0.872) that evaluates seven cognitive dimensions: truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analyticity, systematicity, critical thinking self-confidence, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity. We quantified responses via a 6-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating superior critical thinking capacity. We administered baseline and post-intervention (3-month) assessments to both groups under standardized conditions.

We evaluated nursing quality over 14 days, collecting endpoint data at 8:00 AM on day 15. Measures included objective nutritional parameters (body weight, serum albumin, and prealbumin) and nutrition-related complications (aspiration, diarrhea, abdominal distension, systolic blood pressure fluctuations, and glucose dysregulation).

We assessed patient satisfaction using a verbally administered 5-point Likert scale (1 = extremely dissatisfied, 5 = extremely satisfied). For conscious patients able to communicate, trained research nurses conducted face-to-face assessments on day 14 of the intervention, evaluating comfort with nutritional support (eg, tolerance of feeding routes), responsiveness to nursing needs, and clarity of health education For patients with impaired consciousness or inability to communicate, we proxy-assessed satisfaction by their legally authorized representatives familiar with the patient’s care using the same scale and dimensions, ensuring feasible and patient-aligned perspectives.

We controlled potential confounders such as training intensity and data collection bias by: 1) standardizing training duration (7 days) and assessment criteria across both groups; 2) having independent researchers collect and verify all data; and 3) using objective biomarkers to minimize subjective bias. Through stringent enrollment screening and daily monitoring, all participants (84 patients and 14 nurses) completed follow-up (100% rate). To verify the completeness of the data, two independent research assistants cross-checked all the case report forms with the original documents (including electronic medical records and laboratory reports). Immediately after data collection was completed, during the mid-stage of the study, and before conducting the statistical analysis. Any discrepancies found during the verification process were resolved through consensus with the principal investigator. No participants dropped out. Consequently, the rate of missing data was effectively 0%, and we required no data imputation techniques.

Statistical Analysis

To ensure data integrity, independent researchers double-entered all data and cross-verified them using EpiData software (The EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark) to identify and resolve discrepancies. Additionally, we performed range and logic checks to maintain data quality. We performed dual independent verification for all data prior to entry into Microsoft Excel. We conducted subsequent analyses using SPSS version 27.0. We tested the normality of continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and evaluated variance homogeneity with Levene’s test. We expressed continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and categorical data as frequencies and percentages (%). We measured expert engagement using response and proposal rates. We quantified expert authority through the authority coefficient (Cr), and evaluated consensus consistency using the coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall’s W coefficient. Patient-related outcomes were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for clustering at the nurse level. We set statistical significance at P = 0.05.

Results

Results of Expert Consultation

We conducted a two-round Delphi process with 17 experts from eight provinces and municipalities, including Guangdong, Sichuan, Hunan, Henan, and Hebei. The panel comprised 4 senior professionals with full professor-level titles, 5 associate professionals, and 8 intermediate professionals. Among them, 5 held doctoral degrees, 10 had Master’s degrees, and 2 held Bachelor’s degrees. Professional expertise included critical care medicine (n = 1), nursing management (n = 5), clinical nursing (n = 7), and nursing education (n = 3). The mean age of participants was 40.94 ± 9.45 years, with an average professional experience of 21.23 ± 13.61 years. Both consultation rounds achieved a 100.00% response rate, with proposal rates of 68.75% and 25.00% in the first and second rounds, respectively. The expert authority coefficient (Cr), calculated as (Cs + Ca)/2 (where Cs is the familiarity coefficient and Ca denotes the judgment basis coefficient), demonstrated high reliability. In the first round, Cs and Ca values were 0.93 and 0.78, respectively, yielding a Cr of 0.86. The second round showed improved values (Cs = 0.95, Ca = 0.78, Cr = 0.89). Kendall’s coefficients of concordance were 0.451 and 0.473 for the first and second rounds, respectively.

Expert Recommendations

The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.89 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was 0.96, indicating excellent content validity. We conducted a pilot test involving 10 experts (not participating in the formal consultation) to assess clarity and feasibility. Based on feedback, we revised two ambiguously worded items. The final questionnaire had a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Refinement of nutritional management for specific diseases: A critical care physician from a third-level grade A hospital highlighted the need to refine nutritional management protocols for specific critical illnesses. They deemed existing guidelines for conditions such as severe pancreatitis, hepatic failure, and renal failure overly generalized, lacking disease-specific adaptations. The expert proposed developing tailored nutritional support strategies based on distinct metabolic profiles and nutritional requirements. For instance, patients with severe pancreatitis should receive strict fat restriction during the early phase, prioritizing low-fat, high-carbohydrate EN formulas. Those with hepatic failure require protein intake adjusted according to serum ammonia levels. Renal failure patients need electrolyte and protein supplementation protocols tailored to their dialysis modality.

Implementation of intelligent monitoring technologies: An expert specializing in clinical nutrition research emphasized limitations in conventional monitoring methods, which depend heavily on periodic biochemical tests and manual documentation, resulting in inefficiency and delayed interventions. To enhance precision and timeliness, they recommended the integration of intelligent monitoring technologies, such as wearable devices for real-time tracking of energy expenditure and vital signs, combined with health information systems for automated data analysis. Such systems could facilitate early detection of nutritional imbalances and enable dynamic adjustments to nutrition protocols.

The research team incorporated targeted recommendations from the expert consultation process. For severe pancreatitis, hepatic failure, and renal insufficiency, we added tailored modules to the Nutrition Support Selection section, specifying phase-specific nutrient restrictions and formula adjustments. We excluded recommendations regarding advanced monitoring methods due to technical constraints beyond the scope of this study.

Nutritional Management Protocol for Critically Ill Patients

The Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that all continuous variables, including CTDI-CV, serum albumin, prealbumin, followed normal distribution (all P > 0.05). We developed a comprehensive nutritional management protocol, comprising 4 first-level, 10 second-level, and 25 third-level indicators. These cover nutritional assessment, selection of support modalities, monitoring and adjustment during support, and complication prevention and management.22–25 The protocol showed favorable stability, with importance scores ranging from 4.47 to 5.00 and CV of 0–0.13 (Supplementary Table S2).

Post-Training Specialized Critical Thinking Skills in Junior Nurses

The analysis included a total of 14 junior nurses (7 per group). After training, the study group (A1-A7) demonstrated a mean improvement of 12 points in critical thinking competency, which was significantly greater than the mean increase of 3 points observed in the control group (B1-B7) (t = 2.417, P = 0.03) with effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.292, 95% CI: 1.113–21.461). This indicates that the protocol-based training yielded a substantial and meaningful improvement in critical thinking skills beyond what was achieved with conventional training. Discussion

Critically ill patients often face severe threats from metabolic disorders, immune dysfunction, and multi-organ failure due to conditions like severe trauma, MODS, and sepsis.26,27 These pathologies induce a hypercatabolic state that increases nutritional demands, while gastrointestinal dysfunction and impaired consciousness limit nutrient intake and absorption, leading to a high prevalence of malnutrition.28 Malnutrition delays wound healing, compromises immunity, and increases complication risks, thus prolonging hospital stays and endangering survival.29 Despite these risks, current nutritional management for critically ill patients remains suboptimal.30 Junior nurses, in particular, often lack the systematic evidence-based knowledge and critical thinking skills required to identify underlying nutritional issues or adjust interventions appropriately. Thus, developing an EBN-based nutritional protocol is crucial for standardizing care, enhancing junior nurses’ critical thinking, with the ultimate aim of improving patient outcomes.

The EBN-based nutritional management protocol we developed aligns with the core principles of recent clinical nutrition guidelines, such as those from ESPEN and ASPEN, which emphasize early nutritional assessment, individualized nutrition support (EN, PN, or combined), and close monitoring of nutritional status.31,32

However, while these guidelines provide comprehensive recommendations, clinicians, particularly junior nurses, often face challenges in implementing these guidelines due to limited training and contextual adaptation. Our protocol addresses this gap by integrating evidence-based principles with a structured training framework specifically designed to enhance nurses’ critical thinking and practical skills, thereby facilitating the translation of guideline recommendations into daily practice. Furthermore, We incorporated a systematic review of high-quality evidence from the past five years into its development, followed by a rigorous expert consultation process involving 17 specialists in critical care and clinical nutrition. These experts provided valuable recommendations, such as refining disease-specific nutritional strategies and incorporating intelligent monitoring, which we iteratively integrated into the protocol, strengthening its clinical applicability and theoretical foundation. The protocol facilitates accurate nutritional assessment using established screening tools and supports personalized nutrition support (EN, PN, or combined) tailored to disease type, metabolic status, and gastrointestinal function, thereby reducing arbitrary decision-making.33

However, it is critical to interpret the observed outcomes by considering the distinct yet synergistic roles of the protocol itself and the enhanced training provided. Our study was designed as an integrated intervention; thus, a definitive separation of their effects is a methodological limitation. The protocol provided the standardized framework of evidence-based algorithms and care standards. The training focused on practical implementation, translating protocol content into skills through case-based learning and likely accelerating the protocol’s adoption and execution. Thus, the protocol provided the structural foundation, and the training acted as the catalyst for its implementation. The improvements in care processes and outcomes are best understood as the product of this combined approach. This integrated approach not only enhanced knowledge but also cultivated specialized critical thinking skills, promoting the transition of junior nurses from rule-based towards more adaptive practice, consistent with Benner’s model of skill acquisition.34

Our evaluation showed that the EBN-based protocol was associated with significant improvements in junior nurses’ specialized critical thinking skills. The study group demonstrated significantly greater improvement in critical thinking scores compared to the control group, with a large effect size. Through participating in nutritional assessment, support selection, and complication management during protocol implementation, nurses in the study group appeared to continuously strengthen their critical thinking abilities in practice, whereas conventional training lacked the depth and systematic focus needed to achieve comparable improvements. The substantial individual variation in the improvement of nurses’ critical thinking may reflect differences in baseline competency and learning curves, with higher pre-training scores associated with greater gains, which may be due to differences in baseline learning curves or other individual factors.35

In terms of patient outcomes, After accounting for clustering effects at the nurse level using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), general physiological indicators remained comparable between groups, while improvements in serum albumin, prealbumin, and patient satisfaction were consistently observed in the intervention group. Serum albumin and prealbumin levels serve as key indicators of nutritional status,36,37 the notable changes in serum albumin and prealbumin suggest that the EBN-based protocol more precisely meets patients’ nutritional needs. In critically ill patients, serum albumin serves as both a nutritional marker and a negative acute-phase reactant, with higher levels associated with better outcomes.38 The observed increase in prealbumin, a sensitive short-term marker due to its brief half-life, indicates effective recent nutritional support and enhanced hepatic protein synthesis, which is particularly important in hypercatabolic states.39 These improvements may be associated with the algorithm-based structure of the protocol, which standardizes nutritional assessment and supports earlier and more consistent initiation and advancement of enteral nutrition toward individualized caloric and protein targets. Collectively, these biomarker changes point toward a tangible enhancement of nutritional care quality, which aligns with the concurrent improvement in patient satisfaction.

We posit that these observed improvements in patient outcomes are consistent with the combined implementation of the protocol and its accompanying training. As discussed, the protocol defined the evidence-based care standard, while the training ensured nurses could effectively execute it. The lack of significant differences in complication rates may be attributed to two factors: (1) the relatively short follow-up (3 months), which was insufficient to detect differences in less frequent events, and (2) the high-standard routine care that both groups received, which may have minimized intergroup variation. Furthermore, Improved nurse-patient communication, fostered by the structured assessment and monitoring components of the protocol, likely allowed nurses to explain care plans more clearly and address patient concerns more responsively. With greater decision-making autonomy supported by the evidence-based framework, nurses could make more timely and tailored adjustments to nutritional interventions, directly impacting patient comfort and perceived quality of care. Furthermore, the protocol’s emphasis on systematic interventions, such as proactive management of feeding intolerance or electrolyte imbalances, may have reduced patient discomfort and complications, thereby contributing to a more positive care experience. This suggests that improved critical thinking among nurses facilitates more professional, individualized, and responsive care, which could contribute to enhanced patient experiences.40 Improved nurse-patient communication and timely management of symptoms may serve as mediating factors. The observed improvements may involve a hypothesized pathway wherein enhanced critical thinking leads to more effective care processes, ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes. However, our study design does not permit formal causal mediation testing, and future research should employ dedicated mediation analyses. Additionally, the intensive training may have influenced satisfaction through mechanisms beyond critical thinking alone, such as increased attention (Hawthorne effect). Future studies should aim to disentangle these specific and non-specific effects of complex interventions.

The findings offer insights for nursing education, clinical practice, and health policy. In education, the protocol’s core components, structured assessment and case-based reasoning, should be integrated into simulation training for nursing students and novice nurses. In practice, the protocol provides a model for standardizing ICU nutritional care. Sustainable implementation can be supported by incorporating protocol adherence into performance evaluations, establishing a train-the-trainer model to develop unit-based champions, and integrating decision-support elements into electronic health records and embedding key assessment points into daily multidisciplinary rounds to ensure consistent attention to nutritional management. At the policy level, especially in resource-variable settings, priorities should include promoting validated screening tools, developing guidelines for task delegation to optimize human resources, and supporting phased pilot implementation to secure funding for broader rollout. Furthermore, a formal health-economic evaluation should be planned alongside clinical implementation to demonstrate its long-term value.

We must consider the protocol’s practical applicability in resource-limited settings to assess its broader relevance. Such settings are often characterized by nursing staff shortages, high patient-to-nurse ratios, and limited access to specialized monitoring equipment or nutritional products.41 To enhance feasibility, the protocol’s core principles can be adapted through pragmatic strategies: (1) Employing simplified, validated nutritional risk screening tools, such as the modified NUTRIC score or the Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST), which require minimal time and resources;16 (2) Adopting task-sharing models, where core nutritional monitoring and basic feeding care are delegated to trained nursing assistants under the supervision of a senior nurse, a strategy recommended by the World Health Organization to optimize scarce human resources;42 and (3) Prioritizing foundational, high-impact interventions like ensuring reliable enteral feeding access, preventing feeding interruptions, and monitoring for basic complications. While the initial “train-the-trainer” model requires investment, it builds sustainable local capacity. The protocol’s structured framework may ultimately improve clinical efficiency by standardizing care and preventing costly complications. A phased implementation approach, starting with pilot units and integrating training into existing in-service education platforms, is advised to manage resource constraints effectively.43

An important direction arising from this study involves the implications for future validation and sustainability. The promising findings underscore the need for confirmatory multicenter studies to establish efficacy across diverse clinical settings with varying resources, staffing models, and patient populations. Such studies should employ designs (eg, factorial or cluster-randomized trials) that can help isolate the specific effects of the protocol from the training component Furthermore, investigating the long-term sustainability of both the acquired nursing competencies and the patient outcome benefits is crucial. Future research should include longer follow-up periods to assess the durability of training effects, protocol adherence over time, and the intervention’s impact on long-term patient outcomes such as recurrence of complications or readmission rates.

Nevertheless, considering feasibility and cost-effectiveness is crucial for wider implementation. Potential barriers include the initial investment in trainer development, the time allocated for dedicated training sessions, and the potential need for monitoring equipment in resource-constrained settings. These factors represent tangible costs and logistical challenges. However, these initial investments must be weighed against the protocol’s potential to reduce practice variation, improve nutritional outcomes, and enhance nursing efficiency in the long term. The improvements in nurse competency and patient satisfaction observed in this study suggest a favorable return on investment by potentially reducing complications and length of stay, though a formal cost-effectiveness analysis is needed in future studies to quantify this. To enhance feasibility, we recommend a phased implementation approach, starting with pilot units and utilizing existing hospital education infrastructure to mitigate initial costs.

For clinical practice, the protocol provides a structured framework that could be adopted by other ICUs to improve nutritional care quality. However, the generalizability of our findings is tempered by several limitations. First, because the control and study cohorts were enrolled consecutively rather than concurrently, there is a potential for temporal bias, whereby changes in ICU practice patterns, staffing, patient case mix, or seasonal admission patterns during the study period may have influenced outcomes independently of the intervention. Second, the single-center design and the specific, relatively intensive nature of the training limit generalizability to settings with different resources, workflows, or institutional cultures, and the pre-post design cannot definitively establish causality as observed improvements may be influenced by unmeasured confounders. Variations in staffing models, patient case mix, and access to specialized nutritional support may further influence the applicability of our findings across diverse ICU settings. Third, the small sample size (7 nurses and 42 patients per group) increases the risk of type II error and restricts demographic and clinical diversity; the particularly small nurse sample (n=14 total) raises concerns about the stability and generalizability of the critical thinking findings, as individual differences in learning aptitude and engagement could disproportionately influence results, and while enrolling all eligible junior nurses in the unit reduces local selection bias, it does not mitigate the limitation of small sample size for inferential statistics. In addition, although all eligible junior nurses were enrolled, the study was not formally powered to detect differences in nurse-level outcomes. Therefore, findings related to CTDI-CV scores should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution. Fourth, the relatively short 3-month follow-up period precludes assessment of the sustainability of improved nursing competencies and long-term patient outcomes such as recurrence of complications or readmission rates. Fifth, blinding of participating nurses and outcome assessors was not feasible due to the nature of the educational intervention, introducing risks of performance bias (Hawthorne effect) and detection bias—nurses in the experimental group may have been more motivated, and researchers may have been unconsciously influenced by knowledge of group allocation—though we attempted to mitigate this by using objective biomarkers where possible and clearly defined scoring criteria for nursing assessments. These limitations necessitate caution in interpreting the magnitude of the effects and highlight the need for verification in larger studies; future research should prioritize multicenter studies with larger samples, longer follow-up, and designs that can isolate the specific effects of the protocol from the training, while implementing blinding of outcome assessors where feasible and considering cluster randomization to minimize potential biases.

In conclusion, this study developed an EBN-based nutritional management protocol. The implementation of this protocol as part of an integrated intervention involving structured training suggested potential improvements in nutritional biomarkers and satisfaction in critically ill patients, as well as gains in specialized critical thinking among junior nurses. However, the specific functions of each component still need to be further clarified in future research and the effects of the protocol and training intervention could not be fully separated in this study. To confirm these preliminary findings and to disentangle the effects of the protocol itself from those of the training module, we recommend that larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods and comparative intervention arms be conducted.
