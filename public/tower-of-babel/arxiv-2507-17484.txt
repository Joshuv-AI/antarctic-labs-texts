A standard clt for triangles in a class of ergs
Elena Magnanini, Giacomo Passuello

Abstract
We prove a standard Central Limit Theorem for the (normalized) number of triangles in a class of Exponential Random Graphs derived from a slight modification of the edge-triangle model. Our main theorem covers the whole analyticity region of the free energy, and is based on a polynomial representation of the partition function. Keywords: Central limit theorem, triangles, Exponential Random Graphs, Yang-Lee theorem. MSC 2020: 60F05, 05C80.



1
Introduction

Exponential random graphs (ERGs) are a widely studied class of models that aim to incorporate typical tendencies, such as clustering, commonly observed in real networks.
As a generalization of the Erdős-Rényi model, ERGs allow for dependencies between edges. This is done by using the statistical mechanics approach of introducing an
Hamiltonian
to bias the probability measure over the space of graphs,
enhancing or penalizing the density of specific subgraph counts.
We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for a comprehensive overview.
From a statistical mechanics perspective, ERGs can be interpreted as finite spin systems, where each potential edge corresponds to a spin variable taking values in
\{0,1\}
. The absence of symmetry, typical in classical spin models, adds an additional layer of complexity and makes these graphs particularly interesting in many respects.
A broad line of literature, spanning both classical and more recent works, has developed around limit theorems and concentration inequalities for sums of dependent variables, particularly in the contexts of spin systems and random graph models (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
7
,
5
]
).
In the context of ERGs, several such results have been established, though the majority of the literature has concentrated on edge density, with significantly less known about the behavior of higher-order subgraph counts such as triangles.
A Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for the edge density was first established in
[
2
]
for a
specific class of ERGs known as the edge-triangle model, where the Hamiltonian depends exclusively on the edge and triangle densities. This result was later generalized in
[
9
]
to
a broader class of ERGs, using Stein’s method (see
[
17
]
). However, the analysis is restricted to a specific parameter regime known as Dobrushin’s uniqueness region (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Eq. (2)]
), which the result in
[
2
]
can include and extend beyond.
Finally, we mention the work of
[
14
]
, where a CLT for the edge density is established for the two-star model, a class of ERGs in which edge dependencies arise from the presence of two-stars (i.e. subgraphs consisting of three vertices with two edges sharing a common vertex).
Beyond the edge statistics, we point out the very recent work of
[
10
]
, which studies the asymptotic distribution of the number of two-stars in a model of ERG where the number of edges is conditioned to satisfy some constraint.

As mentioned, quite less is known about the fluctuations of the triangle density, which is the focus of this paper.
We are aware of only a single work from
[
16
]
. Even there, the results are confined to the Dobrushin’s region as the aforementioned edge-based results, based on Stein’s method.

1.1
Our contribution

In this paper,
we prove a standard CLT for the normalized number of triangles in a class of ERGs obtained through a slight modification of the edge-triangle model.
Our main theorem covers the whole analyticity region of the limiting free energy, which goes beyond the aforementioned Dobrushin’s uniqueness region.
The technique of Thm.
3.1
can be easily extended to other subgraph counts but also, in principle, to more general families of ERGs, provided that the phase diagram of the free energy is known (as it happens for the 3-parameter model, see Thm.
3.2
).

2
The model

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the set of all simple graphs on
n
labeled vertices
that are identified with the elements of the set
[n]=\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}
.
In ERGs, the probability distribution on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is defined via a function, called Hamiltonian, contained in an exponential term, which collects the
homomorphism densities
of the subgraphs of a graph.
A homomorphism from a fixed simple graph
H
to a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a map from the vertex set
V(H)
to the vertex set
V(G)
that preserves adjacency, i.e. it maps edges of
H
to edges of
G
. Denoting the number of such maps by
|\mathrm{hom}(H,G)|
, we define the homomorphism density of
H
into
G
as

t(H,G):=\frac{|\mathrm{hom}(H,G)|}{|V(G)|^{|V(H)|}},

(2.1)

which represents the probability that a uniformly chosen mapping from
V(H)
to
V(G)
is edge-preserving.
Given finite simple graphs
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
(e.g., edges, stars, triangles, cycles, etc.) and a parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the exponential random graph model (ERGM) assigns to each graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
the Gibbs probability density

\mu_{n;\boldsymbol{\beta}}(G)=\frac{\exp\left(\mathcal{H}_{n,\boldsymbol{\beta}}(G)\right)}{Z_{n;\boldsymbol{\beta}}},

(2.2)

with Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{n;\boldsymbol{\beta}}(G)=n^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}t(H_{j},G),

(2.3)

where

Z_{n;\boldsymbol{{\beta}}}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\exp\left(H_{n;\boldsymbol{{\beta}}}(G)\right)

(2.4)

is the normalizing
partition function
.
We specialize to the case where
H_{1}
is an edge,
H_{2}
a triangle and
\beta_{3},\beta_{4},\ldots,\beta_{k}=0
. Thus, the homeomorphism densities turn out to be
t(H_{1},G)=\frac{2E_{n}}{n^{2}}
and
t(H_{2},G)=\frac{6T_{n}}{n^{3}}
, where
E_{n}
and
T_{n}
denote, respectively, the total number of edges and triangles in
G
. This specific setting gives rise to the so-called edge-triangle model.
It is more convenient to switch to a representation of the model in terms of adjacency matrices.
We denote by
\mathcal{E}_{n}
the edge set of the complete graph
on
n
vertices, with elements labeled from 1 to
\binom{n}{2}
and we set
\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
, which is in one-to-one correspondence with the graphs in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. As a consequence, to each graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
we can associate an element
{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
where
x_{i}=1
if the edge
i
is present in
G
, and
x_{i}=0
otherwise. Now, we see the Hamiltonian in (
2.3
) as a function on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and we obtain the equivalent formulation:

\mathcal{H}_{n;\alpha,h}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}+h\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}x_{i},

(2.5)

where
\mathcal{T}_{n}:=\{\{i,j,k\}\subset\mathcal{E}_{n}:\{i,j,k\}\text{ is a triangle}\}
, and for convenience we set
h:=2{\beta}_{1}
and
{\alpha}:=6{\beta}_{2}
.
Inside the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}:=\{(\alpha,h):\alpha>-2,h\in\mathbb{R}\}
, which is called
replica symmetric
regime, the limiting free energy
f_{\alpha,h}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln({Z}_{n;\alpha,h})
associated with the Hamiltonian (
2.5
), can be obtained as the solution of a scalar problem.

2.1
Free energy

It is well-known (see
[
6
, Thms. 4.1-6.1]
), that if
\alpha,h\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}
, then

f_{{\alpha},h}=\,\sup_{0\leq u\leq 1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{6}u^{3}+\frac{h}{2}u-\frac{1}{2}I(u)\right)\,=\,\frac{{\alpha}}{6}{u^{*}}^{3}+\frac{h}{2}u^{*}-\frac{1}{2}I(u^{*}),

(2.6)

where
I(u):=u\ln u+(1-u)\ln(1-u)
and
u^{*}
is a maximizer that solves the fixed-point equation

\frac{e^{\alpha\,u^{2}+h}}{1+e^{\alpha\,u^{2}+h}}=u\,.

(2.7)

For the sake of readability, we will sometimes omit the dependence of
u^{*}
on
\alpha
and
h
, and write simply
u^{*}
; when we wish to emphasize this dependence, we will write
u^{*}_{\alpha,h}
.

Our results focus on the following modification of (
2.5
), where we take into account only the integer part of the normalized number of triangles:

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n;\alpha,h}(x):=\alpha\left\lfloor\frac{\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}{n}\right\rfloor+h\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}x_{i}.

(2.8)

We denote by
\hat{\mu}_{n;\alpha,h}
the associated Gibbs probability density and by
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\alpha,h}
,
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}
the related measure, with normalizing partition function
\hat{Z}_{n;\alpha,h}
, and expectation. Finally, we indicate by

\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\hat{Z}_{n;\alpha,h}\quad\text{ and }\quad\hat{f}_{\alpha,h}:=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}

(2.9)

the finite-size and the limiting free energy, respectively.

Importantly,
\hat{f}_{\alpha,h}=f_{\alpha,h}
. This immediately follows from the decomposition
\frac{\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}{n}=\left\lfloor\frac{\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}{n}\right\rfloor+\left\{\frac{\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}{n}\right\}
, where
\{\cdot\}\in[0,1]
denotes the fractional part.

2.2
Phase diagram of the free energy

Inside the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}
, the limiting free energy is analytic except for a critical curve, which we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{rs}
, that starts at the critical point
(\alpha_{c},h_{c}):=\left(\frac{27}{8},\ln 2-\frac{3}{2}\right)
and can be written as
h=q(\alpha)
for a (non-explicit) continuous and strictly decreasing function
q
:
\mathcal{M}^{rs}:=\left\{(\alpha,h)\in(\alpha_{c},+\infty)\times(-\infty,h_{c}):h=q(\alpha)\right\}
(see
[
15
, Prop. 3.8]
).
It is very important for our result to know how to characterize the analyiticity region, which can be expressed, in this notation, as

\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{(\alpha_{c},h_{c})\}
, where

\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}:=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{rs}
.

below, as we are going to extend our main theorem to this setting.

for all

(again, by

(2.10)

where

(2.11)

Theorem 2.1

(

.

The free energy
f_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}
is analytic in
\mathcal{D}^{rs}_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}
except for a certain continuous surface
S
which includes three bounding curves
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
, and that can be characterized as follows:

•

the surface
S
approaches the plane
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=0
as
\beta_{1}\to-\infty
,
\beta_{2}\to\infty
, and
\beta_{3}\to\infty
;

•

the curve
C_{1}
is the intersection of

with the

-plane, i.e.,

;

•

the curve

3
Main result

With a slight abuse of notation, in the following we denote by
T_{n}
the random number of triangles of a graph sampled according to
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\alpha,h}
.

Theorem 3.1

(CLT for
T_{n}
w.r.t.
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\alpha,h}
)
.

For all
(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{(\alpha_{c},h_{c})\}

\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}})}{n}\xrightarrow{\;\;\mathrm{d}\;\;}{}\mathcal{N}(0,v(\alpha,h))\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;{\alpha},h},\text{ as }n\to+\infty,

where
v(\alpha,h):=3u^{*2}_{\alpha,h}\partial_{\alpha}u^{*}_{\alpha,h}
and
\mathcal{N}(0,v(\alpha,h))
is a centered Gaussian distribution.

The theorem immediately extends to the following setting.
Consider the 3-parameter Hamiltonian obtained from (
2.3
) by taking
H_{1}
a single edge,
H_{2}
a triangle, and
H_{3}
a simple subgraph with
q\in[3,14]
edges, and setting
\beta_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 4
. Similarly to what we did for the edge-triangle case, we denote by

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{n;\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}(x)=\beta_{3}n^{2}t(H_{3},x)+\beta_{2}\lfloor n^{2}t(H_{2},x)\rfloor+\beta_{1}n^{2}t(H_{1},x),

(3.1)

and by
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}

the associated Gibbs measure. Then, the following result also holds.

Theorem 3.2

(CLT for
T_{n}
w.r.t.
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}
)
.

For all
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})\in\mathcal{D}^{rs}_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}\setminus S

\sqrt{6}\,\frac{T_{n}/n-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}(\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}})}{n}\xrightarrow{\;\;\mathrm{d}\;\;}{}\mathcal{N}(0,v(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}))\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n;\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}},\text{ as }n\to+\infty,

where
v(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}):=18u^{*2}_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}\partial_{\beta_{2}}u^{*}_{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}}
and
u^{*}
solves (
2.11
).

Note that, unlike in the definition of
\alpha,h
(see below (
2.5
)), this generalization retains a constant factor within the homomorphism density. This explains why the two variances in Thm.
3.2
and Thm.

differ by a factor of
6
.

Remark 3.3
.

Some remarks are in order.

(a)

Based on the result proved in
[
13
, Thm. 3.6]
, which considers an approximation of triangle counts in a mean-field version of the edge-triangle model, we conjecture that

v(\alpha,h)=\frac{3{{u_{\alpha,h}^{*4}}}}{4c_{0}}
, where
c_{0}\equiv c_{0}(\alpha,h):=\frac{1-2\alpha{{u_{\alpha,h}^{*2}}}(1-u_{\alpha,h}^{*})}{4\alpha{u_{\alpha,h}^{*}}(1-{u_{\alpha,h}^{*}})}
.

(b)

The choice of including triangles in the statistics of (
2.8
) and (
3.1
) is crucial, as it allows us to connect the expectation of
\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor
and hence the scaled cumulant generating function defined in (
4.1
) below, to the derivative of the finite-size free energy. This will be a key step in the proof of Thm.
3.1
.

(c)

The integer part of the normalized triangle count plays a crucial role when we represent the partition function as a polynomial (see Subsec.
4.1
).
The other subgraph counts collected in the Hamiltonian (see e.g. (
3.1
)), can be taken without such integer value, as
they contribute only to the coefficients of the polynomial, and do not affect the validity of the representation.

4
Proofs

This section is dedicated to the proof of Thm.
3.1
; the proof of Theorem
3.2
is omitted, as it follows exactly the same argument.
To describe the fluctuations of
\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor
around its mean value, in view of the decomposition
\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}=\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor+\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}
combined with Slutsky’s theorem (see
[
11
, Thm. 13.8]
), it is enough to study the asymptotic behavior of the moment generating function of
W_{n}:=\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)}{n}
. Specifically, we are going to
relate such generating function to the second order derivative of the cumulant generating function of
\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor
, which is defined as

c_{n}(t):=6n^{-2}\ln\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;{\alpha},h}[\exp(t\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)],\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.1)

Remark 4.1
.

Note that, by a direct calculation, we get

c^{\prime}_{n}(t)=\frac{6\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;{\alpha}+t,h}\left(\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor\right)}{n^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)=\frac{6\text{Var}_{n;{\alpha}+t,h}(\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)}{n^{2}}.

(4.2)

The limit of the sequence
(c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t))_{n\geq 1}
for
t=t_{n}=o(1)
will give the variance of the limiting Gaussian. The existence of this limit follows from the Yang–Lee theorem (see Thm.
4.2
). To apply it, we first need a suitable representation of the partition function, which we provide as a first step. Following this, we establish some auxiliary results that will be used in the proof, which is deferred to the end of the section.

4.1
Representation of the partition function.

We start from the partition function obtained by plugging (
2.5
) into (
2.4
), and then we incorporate the integer part. First, we have:

\displaystyle Z_{n;{\alpha},h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}e^{\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}+h\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}x_{i}}\,.

(4.3)

Notice that there is a bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{n}

and the
power set
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
,
that maps an element
x\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
to the set

S=\{i\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\,:\,x_{i}=1\}
.
We can then decompose
\mathcal{A}_{n}
in disjoint subsets as

\mathcal{A}_{n}=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\binom{n}{3}}\>\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}\>\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{n}:|S|=\ell,\\
|\{\{i,j,k\}\subset S\,:\,\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\}|=m\end{subarray}}\{x\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\,:\,x_{i}=1\Leftrightarrow i\in S\},

and write

\displaystyle Z_{n;{\alpha},h}=\sum_{m=0}^{\binom{n}{3}}e^{{\alpha}\frac{m}{n}}\>\sum_{\ell=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}\>G^{(n)}_{m,\ell}e^{h\ell},

(4.4)

where

G^{(n)}_{m,\ell}:=\left|\{S\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{n}:|S|=\ell,\\
|\{\{i,j,k\}\subset S\,:\,\{i,j,k\}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\}|=m\}\right|.

Setting
z:=e^{{\alpha}}
and
K^{(n)}_{m,h}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}\>G^{(n)}_{m,\ell}e^{h\ell}
, we obtain

Z_{n;{\alpha},h}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\binom{n}{3}}K^{(n)}_{m,h}z^{\frac{m}{n}}\,,

(4.5)

which is not a polynomial since
\frac{m}{n}
is not necessarily an integer. For example, when
n=3
we have
G^{(3)}_{0,0}=G^{(3)}_{1,3}=1
and
G^{(3)}_{0,1}=G^{(3)}_{0,2}=3
, yielding
Z_{3;{\alpha},h}=(1+3e^{h}+3e^{2h})+z^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}e^{3h}
.

Instead, by taking the integer part, we obtain the following polynomial representation:

\hat{Z}_{n;{\alpha},h}\equiv\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{n}}\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{k,h}z^{k}\,,

(4.6)

where
\bar{n}:=\lfloor\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{6}\rfloor
, and
\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{k,h}:=\sum_{m:\lfloor\frac{m}{n}\rfloor=k}{K}^{(n)}_{m,h}
.
Note that (
4.6
) can be equivalently written as

\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z)=\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{n}}\frac{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{k,h}}{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}}z^{k}.

Let
\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\bar{n}}\frac{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{k,h}}{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}}z^{k}
.
If
z_{1},\dots,z_{\bar{n}}
are the complex roots of the polynomial
\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}}(z)
, then we can write

\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}(z-z_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}z_{j}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}\left(\frac{z}{z_{j}}-1\right)

and, since
\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}z_{j}=(-1)^{\bar{n}}\frac{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{0,h}}{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}}
, we get

\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}}(z)=(-1)^{\bar{n}}(-1)^{\bar{n}}\frac{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{0,h}}{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}}\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}\left(1-\frac{z}{z_{j}}\right)=\frac{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{0,h}}{\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{\bar{n},h}}\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}\left(1-\frac{z}{z_{j}}\right).

Therefore we obtain

\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z)=\widetilde{K}^{(n)}_{0,h}\prod_{j=1}^{\bar{n}}\left(1-\frac{z}{z_{j}}\right).

(4.7)

The following theorem can be now applied to this polynomial representation.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
18
]
, Thm. 2)
.

Let
Z_{n}(z)
be the polynomial representation of
a partition function.
If there exists a region
R\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}

containing a segment of the real positive axis

as
n\to+\infty
and for

,
all quantities

\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{n}(z),\quad\frac{d^{k}}{d(\ln z)^{k}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{n}(z),\,\mbox{ with }k\in{\mathbb{N}},

(4.8)

converge to analytical limits with respect to
z
.
In particular, the limit and derivative operations switch

.

Recall from the end of Subsec.

f_{\alpha,h}=\hat{f}_{\alpha,h}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z)

Since the limiting free energy

[
15
]
, Thms. 2.1 and 3.9), the partition function (
4.7
) verifies the hypotheses of Theorem
4.2
for all
(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{({\alpha}_{c},h_{c})\}
.
Indeed, at every point where
f_{\alpha,h}
is real analytic, it admits a holomorphic extension to a complex neighborhood of that point (see, e.g.
[
12
, Subsec. 6.4]
). Suppose, by contradiction, that the zeros of the partition function are dense in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, meaning that within every arbitrarily small interval on the positive real axis there exists at least one zero. Then, this would contradict the holomorphic extendability of
f_{\alpha,h}
.
We refer the reader to
[
1
, Sec. 3]
for a comprehensive review on the role played by the location of Yang-Lee zeros on phase transitions.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
(\alpha,h)\in\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{(\alpha_{c},h_{c})\}
. Then,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{6}{n^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}=u^{*3}_{\alpha,h}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{6}{n^{2}}\partial_{\alpha\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}=3u^{*2}_{\alpha,h}\partial_{\alpha}u^{*}_{\alpha,h}

(4.9)

Proof.

The result is an immediate application of Thm.
4.2
, which holds true since we are working in the region
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{(\alpha_{c},h_{c})\}
, where the limiting free energy exists and is analytic. We observe that, since in the polynomial representation (
4.6
) we have
z=e^{\alpha}
, then
\frac{d}{d(\ln z)}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z)=\partial_{\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;{\alpha},h}
and
\frac{d^{2}}{d(\ln z)^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\hat{Z}_{\bar{n}}(z)=\partial_{\alpha\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;{\alpha},h}
. Therefore, Thm.
4.2
allows to commute limit and derivative to get

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{6}{n^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}=6\lim_{n\to+\infty}\partial_{\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}=6\partial_{\alpha}\left[\lim_{n\to+\infty}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}\right]=6\partial_{\alpha}f_{\alpha,h}=u^{*3}_{\alpha,h}.

The second limit on the r.h.s. of (
4.9
) can be proved in the same way.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Recalling (
4.2
) and the definition (
2.9
) of
\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}
, a direct computation shows that

c^{\prime}_{n}(0)=\frac{6}{n^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}\quad\text{ and }\quad c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(0)=\frac{6}{n^{2}}\partial_{\alpha\alpha}\hat{f}_{n;\alpha,h}.

Therefore, from (
4.9
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{\prime}_{n}(0)=u^{*3}_{\alpha,h}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(0)=3u^{*2}_{\alpha,h}\partial_{\alpha}u^{*}_{\alpha,h}=v(\alpha,h).

(4.10)

The proof of our main theorem is then just one step further. We will rely on the analyticity of the free energy and on the uniform convergence of the sequence
(c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t))_{n\geq 1}
guaranteed by Thm.
4.2
and Prop.
4.5
.

Proof of Thm.
3.1
.

Recall
v(\alpha,h)=3u^{*2}_{\alpha,h}\partial_{\alpha}u^{*}_{\alpha,h}
and
W_{n}=\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)}{n}
. We want to show that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}(\exp(tW_{n}))=\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}v(\alpha,h)t^{2}\right)

(4.11)

for all
t\in[0,\eta)
and some
\eta>0
.
We aim to express
\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}\left(\exp(tW_{n})\right)
in terms of
c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)
. Consider
t>0
and set
t_{n}:=\sqrt{6}t/n
. We get

\displaystyle\ln\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}(\exp(tW_{n}))

\displaystyle=\ln\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}\left(\exp(t_{n}\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)\exp\left(-t_{n}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\lfloor\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\rfloor)\right)\right)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{cgf},\eqref{link_c_avg&var_dens}}{=}\frac{n^{2}}{6}[c_{n}(t_{n})-t_{n}c^{\prime}_{n}(0)].

Notice that, since
c_{n}(0)=0
, the term in square brackets is the difference between the function
c_{n}(t_{n})
and its first order Taylor expansion at zero. Therefore, by using Taylor’s theorem with Lagrange remainder, one gets

\ln\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}(\exp(tW_{n}))=\frac{c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t^{*}_{n})t^{2}}{2},

for some
t^{*}_{n}\in[0,\sqrt{6}t/n]
. To conclude the proof of the central limit theorem, we need to control the limiting behavior of
c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t^{*}_{n})
. To this end, we recall from (
4.10
) that
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}^{\prime\prime}(0)=v(\alpha,h)
, and that, by Prop.
4.5
, the derivatives of
c_{n}(t)
converge locally uniformly. These two properties together yield the following result, which was first proved in a slightly different setting but applies unchanged in the present context.

Lemma 4.7

(
[
2
]
)
.

For
(\alpha,h)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,h}^{rs}\setminus\{(\alpha_{c},h_{c})\}
, there exists some
\eta>0
such that we have
\lim_{n\to+\infty}c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t_{n})=v(\alpha,h)
for all
t_{n}\in[0,\eta)
with
\lim_{n\to+\infty}t_{n}=0
.

From the lemma above, we obtain the convergence of
c^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t^{*}_{n})
, and, in turn, the convergence of the moment generating function. Therefore
W_{n}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,v(\alpha,h))
(see
[
3
]
, Sect. 30).
Finally, the convergence in distribution of

\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}})}{n}=W_{n}+\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\})}{n}

(4.13)

follows from Slutsky’s theorem, as
\sqrt{6}\,\frac{\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\})}{n}\to 0
in probability, being the numerator bounded almost surely.
∎

We conclude the section by shedding a light on an open point of our analysis, i.e. the comparison between
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha,h}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,h}
, the latter being the measure of the edge-triangle model associated with the Hamiltonian (
2.5
).

Remark 4.8
.

To extend the proof of Thm.
3.1
to the measure
\mathbb{P}_{n;\alpha,h}
it remains to establish

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\hat{{\mathbb{E}}}_{n;{\alpha},h}(\exp(tW_{n}))=\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n;{\alpha},h}(\exp(tW_{n}))
, the latter expectation being associated with
\mathbb{P}_{n;\alpha,h}
. A natural approach is to compare the two expectations directly. However, this requires a delicate control over the limiting behavior of the fractional part of the normalized triangle count, which remains an open point of our analysis.
In particular, with a direct computation one can show that
\mathbb{E}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{tW_{n}})=\frac{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{tW_{n}+\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})}{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{n;\alpha,h}\left(e^{tW_{n}}\right)-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}\left(e^{tW_{n}}\right)|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}\left[e^{tW_{n}}\left(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})\right)\right]\right|}{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{2tW_{n}})}\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{2\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})-\left(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})\right)^{2}}}{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})},

where the last step follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, by noticing that

\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}\left(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}}-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n;\alpha,h}(e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}})\right)^{2}
is the variance of
e^{\alpha\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}}
.
This last expression makes it evident that a sufficient condition for the asymptotic equivalence of the two expectations is the convergence
\{\nicefrac{{T_{n}}}{{n}}\}\to 0
in probability.
In that case, the dominated convergence theorem would yield the desired conclusion.
