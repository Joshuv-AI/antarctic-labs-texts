On Spectral Properties of Restricted Fractional Laplacians with Self-adjoint Boundary Conditions on a Finite Interval
Jussi Behrndt, Markus Holzmann, Delio Mugnolo

Abstract
. We describe all self-adjoint realizations of the restricted fractional Laplacian ( − Δ ) a (-\Delta)^{a} with power a ∈ ( 1 2 , 1 ) a\in(\frac{1}{2},1) on a bounded interval by imposing boundary conditions on the functions in the domain of a maximal realization; such conditions relate suitable weighted Dirichlet and Neumann traces. This is done in a systematic way by using the abstract concept of boundary triplets and their Weyl functions from extension and spectral theory of symmetric and self-adjoint operators in Hilbert spaces. Our treatment follows closely the well-known one for classical Laplacians on intervals and it shows that all self-adjoint realizations have purely discrete spectrum and are semibounded from below. To demonstrate the method, we focus on three self-adjoint realizations of the restricted fractional Laplacian: the Friedrichs extension, corresponding to Dirichlet-type boundary conditions, the Krein–von Neumann extension, and a Neumann-type realization. Notably, the Neumann-type realization exhibits a simple negative eigenvalue, thus it is not larger than the Krein–von Neumann extension.



1.
Introduction

Fractional Laplace operators, which were introduced already in
[
12
,
55
]
, appear nowadays in several fields, including relativistic
[
51
]
and non-relativistic
[
13
]
quantum mechanics (see
[
15
,
24
]
and the references therein), anomalous diffusion
[
53
]
, stochastic analysis
[
59
]
, turbulence
[
6
]
, and optics
[
7
]
. For physical systems supported on the whole space the associated operator is extensively studied, both in modeling and analysis. Owing to the spectral theorem and the subordination theory
[
50
]
, in this case there is no ambiguity when it comes to defining fractional operators: no less than ten different
but equivalent definitions of the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are known
[
48
]
. Our aim in this paper is to study the less clear case of bounded domains from an extension theoretic point of view. More precisely, we consider a specific variant of the fractional Laplacian, which we call, with a slight abuse of terminology,
restricted
fractional Laplacian: we are going to parametrize all its self-adjoint realizations by imposing suitable boundary conditions. This natural question is unanswered even in the one-dimensional case of intervals, which is considered in the present paper.

Throughout this paper, let
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
be fixed. The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{a}
on
\mathbb{R}
is the strongly singular integral operator that acts on any
f
belonging to the Sobolev space
H^{2a}(\mathbb{R})
of fractional order
2a
as

(-\Delta)^{a}f(x)=c_{a}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2a}}dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

where
c_{a}>0
is the constant in (
4.1
).
Equivalently, this operator can be described with the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
via
\mathcal{F}((-\Delta)^{a}f)(\xi)=\xi^{2a}\mathcal{F}f(\xi)
, or as the fractional power of the free Laplacian defined on
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
via the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators. In particular, the representation via the Fourier transform shows that
(-\Delta)^{a}
can be viewed as a bounded operator from
H^{2a}(\mathbb{R})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and thus, by duality and self-adjointness, it admits a bounded extension mapping from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to
H^{-2a}(\mathbb{R})
.

The main objective of this paper is to parametrize and to study the spectral properties of the self-adjoint realizations of the
fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{a}
on a bounded interval
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}
by using boundary triplets and
Weyl functions – an efficient tool in abstract extension theory of symmetric operators and spectral analysis
of their self-adjoint extensions; cf. Section
2
. We emphasize that on a bounded interval there are different, not mutually equivalent versions of fractional Laplacians. Inspired by the work of Grubb (see, e.g.,
[
34
, Section 6.1]
),
we will work with a variant of
(-\Delta)^{a}
, which we call
restricted fractional Laplacian
, and which acts on a function
f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
by first extending it by zero to an element
\mathfrak{e}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then by applying the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{a}
on
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathfrak{e}f
, and finally by restricting
(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in H^{-2a}(\mathbb{R})
to a distribution
\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
on
(\alpha,\beta)
. The maximal realization of the restricted fractional Laplacian in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
is then defined for all those
f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
such that
\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
. Note that by construction, the action of the restricted fractional Laplacian can also be described as the strongly singular integral in (
1.1
).
Let us briefly remind the reader about other common types of fractional Laplacians on domains.
The simplest one – the
spectral fractional Laplacian
– is also the least appropriate for our purposes, as it is defined by taking fractional powers (via the spectral theorem) of a non-negative realization of the Laplacian; in this way, boundary conditions are implicitly already prescribed, see
[
34
]
and the references therein. Another way of defining fractional powers that is tailored for a Neumann-like realization with nonlocal boundary conditions and has received much attention in the last years is described in
[
22
]
; again, as it is related to fixed boundary conditions, it is not suitable for the purposes of extension theory.
Finally, the so-called
regional fractional Laplacian
arises as the
L^{2}
-gradient of the Slobodeckii-seminorm. This realization is natural from the viewpoint of stochastic analysis, as it generates censored stable processes. Possibly for this reason, its Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin-type extensions have been studied (see e.g.
[
14
,
28
,
39
,
40
,
61
]
), but to the best of our knowledge no systematic characterization of all self-adjoint realizations and their spectral properties has been obtained so far.
We emphasize that the Dirichlet realizations of the restricted and the regional fractional Laplacian only differ by a positive potential; this was observed already in
[
14
, Section 2]
, see also
[
34
, Section 6.3]
and
[
28
,
61
]
.

To make this paper easily readable we first familiarize the reader with the general abstract notions
of boundary triplets and Weyl functions in Section
2
, and
illustrate in parallel these concepts for the differential expression
-\Delta
on a finite interval
(\alpha,\beta)
, which is
a particularly simple example and well known to operator theory experts. However, we believe that this
special case already displays many advantages of boundary triplets and Weyl functions and, at the same time, provides
an easy access to the abstract theory. In particular, using the Dirichlet and Neumann trace operators we parametrize
the self-adjoint realizations of
-\Delta
in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
with
the help of self-adjoint relations
\Theta\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
or pairs of
2\times 2
-matrices
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}

satisfying a symmetry and a maximality condition. The Weyl function in this case
is the (
2\times 2
-matrix valued) Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, which contains the spectral data of the self-adjoint realizations.
Furthermore, we briefly discuss semibounded self-adjoint extensions and special attention is paid to
the Friedrichs and Krein–von Neumann extension (in the nonnegative case).
It is noteworthy that the boundary conditions of the Friedrichs extension can
be identified via the limiting behavior of the Weyl function at
-\infty
; cf.
[
8
,
20
,
21
]
and Proposition
2.8
.

After the preparations and motivations in Section
2
we turn to the main objective of this paper.
To fully describe the domains of the self-adjoint realizations of the restricted fractional Laplacian

(-\Delta)^{a}
in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
suitable function spaces are necessary. While for
a=1
the
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space
W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta)
of twice weakly differentiable functions
is the natural choice, for the restricted fractional Laplacian with
a<1
the so-called Hörmander transmission spaces turn out to be useful; cf. Section
3
. These spaces, originally introduced by Hörmander in
[
43
]
, played an important role in the recent publications

[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
by Grubb, where properties of solutions of equations involving the restricted fractional Laplacian were studied. The works by
Grubb also serve as an inspiration and source for our studies.
The definition of the Hörmander transmission spaces
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
and some of their properties that are useful
in the present context are summarized in Section
3.2
.
For
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
the minimal restricted fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
, the maximal restricted fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, and the
Dirichlet realization
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
of the restricted fractional Laplacian
are then defined in Section
4.1
. It is noteworthy that for functions in the domain of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}
weighted Dirichlet and Neumann traces that appear in the boundary conditions (
1.2
) below can be defined. Regularity of solutions of elliptic problems involving
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
has been studied in
[
32
,
33
,
56
,
57
]
. Moreover, while the spectrum of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
is not easy to describe, it has already been studied in detail, see, e.g., the survey
[
60
]
and in particular
[
60
, Theorem 1.1]
, where the ground state energy of the restricted fractional Dirichlet Laplacian on a bounded interval is compared with that of the spectral fractional Dirichlet Laplacian (see also
[
19
]
). Simplicity of all eigenvalues was shown in
[
47
]
, while spectral bounds and Weyl-type asymptotics were obtained in
[
11
,
23
]
.

Based on the previous considerations
by Grubb and a Green’s identity from
[
35
]
we then obtain a boundary triplet for
the maximal restricted fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
in Section
4.2
.
This leads directly to a description of all self-adjoint fractional Laplacians as the restriction of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}
to those
f
that satisfy the boundary conditions

\Gamma(a)\mathcal{B}\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)(\alpha)\\
(t^{1-a}f)(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a+1)\mathcal{A}\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix},

(1.2)

where
\Gamma
is Euler’s gamma function,
t(x)={\rm min\,}\{x-\alpha,\beta-x\}
, and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
are such that
{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}

and
({\mathcal{A}}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}})\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 4}
has rank 2; cf. Proposition
4.5
. This characterization is also the reason for the restriction
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, as for
a\leq\frac{1}{2}
the weighted Neumann trace of functions in the domain of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}
can not be defined, and for
a>1
it is expected that the traces appearing in (
1.2
) are not sufficient to describe
all
self-adjoint realizations, as for this purpose, e.g., also boundary conditions involving the evaluation of the weighted second derivative on the boundary is necessary. Next, using
results from
[
23
]
we compute the
corresponding Weyl function
M(\lambda)
at
\lambda=0
and relying on abstract properties of the Weyl function we obtain
the form of
M(\lambda)
for all
\lambda
, and also identify the Friedrichs extension via the
limiting behavior of
M
for
\lambda\rightarrow-\infty
; here we use typical boundary triplet techniques from
[
8
,
20
,
21
]
and also rely on a result from
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, we describe the Krein–von Neumann realization of the restricted fractional Laplacian, as well as the
nonnegative and semibounded self-adjoint extensions. An interesting feature appears for the Neumann realization: In contrast to the
classical case
a=1
, the Neumann realization of the restricted fractional Laplacian for
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)

possesses a simple negative eigenvalue. In particular, it is
not larger
than the Krein–von Neumann realization and, unlike for
a=1
, it is
not
associated with a (sub-)Markovian stochastic process.
We wish to point out that formally
the observations in Section
2
for the (ordinary) Laplacian
-\Delta
can be viewed as limiting results of the findings in Section
4
for
a\rightarrow 1
.

2.1.
Boundary triplets and self-adjoint extensions

Throughout this section
S
is assumed to be a closed symmetric operator in a separable Hilbert space
{\mathfrak{H}}
with dense domain
{\text{\rm dom\,}}S
.
It follows that the adjoint operator
S^{*}
is well defined, closed, and an extension of
S
, that is,
S\subset S^{*}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
boundary triplet

\{{\mathcal{G}},\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
for
S^{*}
consists of a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{G}}

and two linear mappings
\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
such that the following properties hold:

(i)

the abstract Green identity

(S^{*}f,g)-(f,S^{*}g)=(\Upsilon_{1}f,\Upsilon_{0}g)-(\Upsilon_{0}f,\Upsilon_{1}g)

is valid for all
f,g\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}
,

(ii)

the mapping
(\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1})^{\top}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
is onto.

A boundary triplet for
S^{*}
exists if and only if the defect numbers
n_{\pm}(S)=\dim\ker(S^{*}\mp i)
of
S
coincide, and in this case one has

n_{\pm}(S)=\dim{\mathcal{G}}\leq\infty
.
Note also that a boundary triplet is not unique (unless
n_{\pm}(S)=0
). In the following let us assume that
\{{\mathcal{G}},\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
is a boundary triplet
for
S^{*}
. Then it follows that

A_{0}:=S^{*}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{1}:=S^{*}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{1}

(2.1)

are self-adjoint extensions of
S
and one has
{\text{\rm dom\,}}S=\ker\Upsilon_{0}\cap\ker\Upsilon_{1}
. Moreover, if
{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}
is equipped with the graph norm,
then the boundary mapping in Definition
2.1
(ii) is continuous, and
\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
are surjective.
With the help of the boundary triplet
\{{\mathcal{G}},\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
one can parametrize all extensions
A_{\Theta}\subset S^{*}
of
S
via linear relations

\Theta
in
{\mathcal{G}}
(i.e. via the closed linear subspaces of
{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
). More precisely, there is a bijective correspondence
\Theta\mapsto A_{\Theta}
between the set of intermediate extensions
A_{\Theta}
of
S
and the set of linear
relations in
{\mathcal{G}}
via

A_{\Theta}f=S^{*}f,\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{\Theta}=\left\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}:\begin{pmatrix}\Upsilon_{0}f\\
\Upsilon_{1}f\end{pmatrix}\in\Theta\right\}.

(2.2)

One has
\overline{A_{\Theta}}=A_{\overline{\Theta}}
and
(A_{\Theta})^{*}=A_{\Theta^{*}}
and, in particular,
A_{\Theta}
is self-adjoint (symmetric, essentially self-adjoint) in
{\mathfrak{H}}

if and only if
\Theta
is self-adjoint (symmetric, essentially self-adjoint, respectively) in
{\mathcal{G}}
; similar statements hold for (maximal) dissipative and accretive extensions; cf.
[
8
, Chapter 2.1]
.
Observe that in the special case of a linear operator
\Theta
in (
2.2
) one has

\begin{split}A_{\Theta}f&=S^{*}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{\Theta}&=\bigl\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}:\Theta\Upsilon_{0}f=\Upsilon_{1}f\bigr\}=\ker(\Upsilon_{1}-\Theta\Upsilon_{0}),\end{split}

(2.3)

(and this characterization remains valid also in the case of a general linear relation
\Theta
if the expression
\Upsilon_{1}-\Theta\Upsilon_{0}
is understood in the sense of linear relations).
Note that
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
in (
2.1
) correspond to the linear relations

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
g^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}:g^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{\begin{pmatrix}g\\
0\end{pmatrix}:g\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\},

respectively.

In many situations one prefers abstract boundary conditions of the form
(
2.3
), where
\Theta
is an operator. However, for a complete
description of
the (self-adjoint) extensions it is necessary to allow also multivalued
parameters, or to use slightly more general forms of boundary conditions
than in (
2.3
).
Making use of a representation of a closed (self-adjoint) linear
relation
\Theta
as a kernel of a row operator one can rewrite
(
2.2
) using two bounded operators.
In the next proposition this is made explicit for relations
\Theta

with
\rho(\Theta)\neq\emptyset
; cf.
[
8
, Proposition 1.10.4 and
Corollary 1.10.5]
for the existence of such a representation and

[
18
, Proposition 1.5]
for the condition on the self-adjointness.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
\{{\mathcal{G}},\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
be a boundary
triplet for
S^{*}
. Then
\Theta
is a linear relation in
{\mathcal{G}}
with
\mu\in\rho(\Theta)
if and only if
\Theta
admits the representation

\Theta=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}k\\
k^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}:{\mathcal{B}}k={\mathcal{A}}k^{\prime}\,\,\text{for all}\,\,k,k^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}

(2.4)

with
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
and
{\mathcal{B}}-\mu{\mathcal{A}}
is bijective.
With this choice the adjoint of
\Theta
is

\Theta^{*}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}^{*}h\\
\mathcal{B}^{*}h\end{pmatrix}:h\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}

(2.5)

and
the closed intermediate extension
A_{\Theta}
in (
2.2
)
can be described as

\begin{split}A_{\Theta}f&=S^{*}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{\Theta}&=\left\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}:\mathcal{B}\Upsilon_{0}f=\mathcal{A}\Upsilon_{1}f\right\}=\ker({\mathcal{A}}\Upsilon_{1}-{\mathcal{B}}\Upsilon_{0}).\end{split}

(2.6)

Furthermore, with
\Theta
in (
2.4
) it follows that

A_{\Theta}
in (
2.6
) is self-adjoint in
{\mathfrak{H}}

if and only if

{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ker\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{B}}&-{\mathcal{A}}\\
{\mathcal{A}}&{\mathcal{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\{0\}.

(2.7)

Note that for finite dimensional spaces
\mathcal{G}\cong\mathbb{C}^{N}
the condition (
2.7
) about the self-adjointness of

\Theta
in (
2.4
) is equivalent to

{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad({\mathcal{A}}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 2N}\text{ has full rank};

cf.
[
18
, Corollary 1.6]
.

The following elementary example illustrates how boundary triplets naturally appear in the context of ordinary differential operators.
The next example can also be viewed as the limiting case
a\rightarrow 1
in our discussion of fractional operators in Section
4
.

Example 2.3
.

In
{\mathfrak{H}}=L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, where
-\infty<\alpha<\beta<\infty
, we consider the minimal operator associated to
-\Delta
given by

\begin{split}Sf&=-f^{\prime\prime},\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}S&=W^{2,2}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=\bigl\{f\in W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta):f(\alpha)=f^{\prime}(\alpha)=f(\beta)=f^{\prime}(\beta)=0\bigr\},\end{split}

which is a densely defined closed symmetric operator in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
with equal defect numbers
n_{\pm}(S)=2
; here and in the following we denote by
W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta)
the
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space
of twice weakly differentiable functions and by
W^{2,2}_{0}(\alpha,\beta)
the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)
in
W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The adjoint of
S
, the maximal operator, is given by

S^{*}f=-f^{\prime\prime},\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*}=W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta),

and it is easy to see that
\{{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}\}
, where

\Upsilon_{0}f=\begin{pmatrix}f(\alpha)\\
f(\beta)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\Upsilon_{1}f=\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-f^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix},\quad f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}S^{*},

is a boundary triplet for
S^{*}
. In fact, the abstract Green identity in Definition
2.1
(i) follows from integration by parts and
the surjectivity condition Definition
2.1
(ii) can be verified by choosing, e.g. suitable polynomials with prescribed boundary values at the finite endpoints

\alpha
and
\beta
.
In the present situation we have

\begin{split}A_{0}f=-f^{\prime\prime},&\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{0}=\bigl\{f\in W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta):f(\alpha)=f(\beta)=0\bigr\},\\
A_{1}f=-f^{\prime\prime},&\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{1}=\bigl\{f\in W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta):f^{\prime}(\alpha)=f^{\prime}(\beta)=0\bigr\},\end{split}

(2.8)

so that the self-adjoint extensions
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
correspond to Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, respectively.
For symmetric matrices
\Theta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 2}
the self-adjoint extension
A_{\Theta}
in (
2.2
)-(
2.3
) is given by

A_{\Theta}f=-f^{\prime\prime},\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{\Theta}=\left\{f\in W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta):\Theta\begin{pmatrix}f(\alpha)\\
f(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-f^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}\right\}

(here
{\mathcal{A}}=I
and
{\mathcal{B}}=\Theta
in (
2.4
)) and, more generally, for any pair of matrices
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 2}
such that

{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
and
(\mathcal{A}\,|\,\mathcal{B})
has rank two the corresponding self-adjoint extension
A_{\Theta}
with
\Theta
as in (
2.4
) is given by

A_{\Theta}f=-f^{\prime\prime},\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}A_{\Theta}=\left\{f\in W^{2,2}(\alpha,\beta):{\mathcal{B}}\begin{pmatrix}f(\alpha)\\
f(\beta)\end{pmatrix}={\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-f^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

In the end of this subsection we recall a theorem of a certain inverse nature, which can be used to prove that a given
operator
T
is the adjoint of a symmetric operator
S
. At the same time one then obtains a boundary triplet for
T=S^{*}
; cf.
[
8
, Theorem 2.1.9]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
T
be an operator in
{\mathfrak{H}}
, let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a Hilbert space, and assume that

\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}T\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}

are linear mappings such that the following conditions hold:

(i)

the abstract Green identity

(Tf,g)-(f,Tg)=(\Upsilon_{1}f,\Upsilon_{0}g)-(\Upsilon_{0}f,\Upsilon_{1}g)

is valid for all
f,g\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}T
,

(ii)

(\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{1})^{\top}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}T\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
is onto and
\ker\Upsilon_{0}\cap\ker\Upsilon_{1}
is dense in
{\mathfrak{H}}
,

(iii)

T\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}
contains a self-adjoint operator
A_{0}
.

Then

S:=T\upharpoonright\bigl(\ker\Upsilon_{0}\cap\ker\Upsilon_{1}\bigr)

is a densely defined closed
symmetric operator in
{\mathfrak{H}}
such that

Definition 2.5

3.
Some properties of Hörmander transmission spaces

In this section we first summarize some notations for distributions and Sobolev spaces, and recall the concept
Hörmander transmission spaces afterwards. We also collect some useful properties of these spaces that will be needed in Section
4
,
where boundary triplets for the fractional Laplacian are constructed.
Throughout this section it is always assumed that
-\infty<\alpha<\beta<\infty
. We also remark that the following considerations remain valid for bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

3.1.
Distributions and Sobolev spaces

Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be the spaces of compactly supported and rapidly decaying
C^{\infty}
-functions on
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.
Their duals
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
are the spaces of distributions and tempered distributions, respectively. Similarly,
for the bounded open interval
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}
the space of all
C^{\infty}
-functions with compact support in
(\alpha,\beta)
is denoted by
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\beta)
and the corresponding space of distributions is
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The restriction of distributions on
\mathbb{R}
to the interval
(\alpha,\beta)
is denoted by
\mathfrak{r}
, so

\mathfrak{r}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta),\qquad\mathfrak{r}f=f_{|(\alpha,\beta)}:=f\upharpoonright\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\beta),

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\beta)
is embedded in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})
via extension by zero.
The set of all tempered distributions supported in
[\alpha,\beta]
is
\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])
, i.e.

\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])=\bigl\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}):f=0\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\setminus[\alpha,\beta]\bigr\},

where, as usual, for a (tempered) distribution
f=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[\alpha,\beta]
means
f(\varphi)=0
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\setminus[\alpha,\beta])
.
Next, the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
of order
s\in\mathbb{R}
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}):\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+x^{2})^{s}|\mathcal{F}f(x)|^{2}dx<\infty\right\},

where
\mathcal{F}
is the Fourier transform on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.
For a bounded interval
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}
we set

\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta]):=\big\{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}):f=0\text{ on }\mathbb{R}\setminus[\alpha,\beta]\big\}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\cap\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])

and

\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta):=\{\mathfrak{r}F:F\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\}.

Likewise, we write

\overline{C}^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta]):=\big\{f_{|[\alpha,\beta]}:f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\big\}.

Note that
\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])\subset\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, while
\overline{C}^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta]),\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)
.

While in general the spaces
\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
are different, for suitable
s
they coincide:

Lemma 3.1
.

For
s\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
one has
\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
.

Proof.

To see the claim for
s\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, note that
\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
coincides with the set denoted in
[
52
]
by
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
, see also
[
52
, Theorem 3.29 (ii)]
. By
[
52
, Theorem 3.40]
one has
\mathfrak{r}\widetilde{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)=\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
, which is the claim for
s\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
.

To see the claim for
s\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
, note that by
[
52
, Theorem 3.30 (i)]
the set
\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
coincides with the dual space
(\mathfrak{r}\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\alpha,\beta))^{*}
of
\mathfrak{r}\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{-s}([\alpha,\beta])
, which is, by the first part of the proof, equal to
\overline{H}^{-s}(\alpha,\beta)
. Using again
[
52
, Theorem 3.30]
one gets that
\overline{H}^{-s}(\alpha,\beta)^{*}=\mathfrak{r}\widetilde{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
. Summing up, this means that
\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
, i.e. the claim is also true for
s\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
.
∎

Finally, we construct the extension by zero
\mathfrak{e}f
of
f\in\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
with
s>-\frac{1}{2}
.
For
s\geq 0
the situation is clear as
\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)\subset L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
and
\mathfrak{e}
acts on
f\in H^{s}(\alpha,\beta)
as

\mathfrak{e}f=\begin{cases}f&\text{ in }(\alpha,\beta),\\
0&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\setminus(\alpha,\beta).\end{cases}

In the case
s\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)

we rely on Lemma
3.1
and choose
F\in\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
such that
f=\mathfrak{r}F
. Observe that
F\in\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])

is unique. In fact, if
F_{1},F_{2}\in\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
such that
\mathfrak{r}F_{1}=\mathfrak{r}F_{2}
, then
(\mathfrak{r}F_{1}-\mathfrak{r}F_{2})(\varphi)=0
for
all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\beta)
and hence

(F_{1}-F_{2})(\mathfrak{e}\varphi)=0
for the
0
-extensions

\mathfrak{e}\varphi
.
Since
-s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
it follows from
[
52
, Theorem 3.30 and Theorem 3.40]
that
the
0
-extensions of
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\beta)
-functions are dense in the dual of

\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
, which implies
F_{1}-F_{2}=0
. Therefore, if
s\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
, then the extension operator

\mathfrak{e}:\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta]),\quad\mathfrak{e}f=F,

(3.2)

is well defined.
By construction, if
s>-\frac{1}{2}
we have

\mathfrak{r}\mathfrak{e}f=f\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{e}\mathfrak{r}F=F\quad\text{for all }f\in\overline{H}^{s}(\alpha,\beta),\,F\in\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta]).

(3.3)

3.2.
The Hörmander transmission spaces
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])

In this subsection we recall the notion of Hörmander transmission spaces and we provide some useful properties of the spaces, that will be needed to
study the restricted fractional Laplacian in an operator theoretic framework. These spaces were originally introduced in
[
43
]
and further studied and developed in
[
1
,
33
,
36
]
, see also
[
32
,
34
,
35
,
37
]
.
We follow the exposition introduced in the before mentioned papers by Grubb.

In order to define the Hörmander transmission spaces, we will make for
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
use of the order reducing operator
\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}
from
[
33
, Theorem 1.3]
, which is an elliptic pseudodifferential operator on
\mathbb{R}
and classical of order
\mu
, and thus

\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}:H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow H^{s-\mu}(\mathbb{R})

is bounded for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, this operator
\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}

preserves the support in
[\alpha,\beta]
(i.e. it maps elements of
\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])
to
\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])
) and defines a homeomorphism

\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}:\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow\dot{H}^{s-\mu}([\alpha,\beta])

(3.4)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, with inverses
(\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)})^{-1}
that also preserve support in
[\alpha,\beta]
; cf.
[
33
, Theorem 1.3]
for details.

The next definition follows
[
33
, Definition 1.8]
, see also
[
33
, Proposition 1.7 and the comments after Definition 1.8]
for the equality in (
3.5
). We remark that the Hörmander transmission spaces
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
are defined in
[
33
]
for a larger set of the parameter
\mu
, but motivated by our application we will state the definition for the case
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
.
Recall that
\mathfrak{e}
and
\mathfrak{r}
denote the extension and restriction operator, see (
3.2
) and (
3.1
), respectively.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
s-\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
. Then the
\mu
-transmission space
is defined by

\begin{split}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta]):&=\Lambda_{+}^{(-\mu)}\mathfrak{e}\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)\\
&=\Big\{f\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta]):\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}f\in\dot{H}^{-1/2+\varepsilon}([\alpha,\beta])\text{ for some }\varepsilon>0\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\,\text{ and }\mathfrak{r}(\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}f)\in\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)\Big\}.\end{split}

(3.5)

Note that
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
is a Banach space when equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{\mu(s)}:=\big\|\mathfrak{r}\Lambda_{+}^{(\mu)}f\big\|_{\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)};

(3.6)

cf.
[
33
, Definition 1.8]
. Moreover, for
s\geq 0
the inclusion
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
holds.

In the following lemma we collect some known properties of
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
that will be useful for us. For the claims in (i),(iii)&(iv), we provide the simple arguments for the proof. Here and in the following, we will make use of the distance function
t
from the boundary that is defined by

t(x):=\dist(x,\partial(\alpha,\beta))={\rm min\,}\{x-\alpha,\beta-x\},\qquad x\in(\alpha,\beta).

(3.7)

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
and
s-\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
. Then the following assertions hold for the
Hörmander transmission spaces
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
in (
3.5
):

(i)

If
s-\mu<\frac{1}{2}
, then

H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])=\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta]),

and, in particular,
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(0)}([\alpha,\beta])=L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
.

(ii)

If
s-\mu>\frac{1}{2}
and
s-\mu-\frac{1}{2}\notin\mathbb{N}
, then

\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])\subset H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])\subset\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])+\mathfrak{e}t^{\mu}\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta).

(iii)

If
s_{1}<s_{2}
, then
H^{\mu(s_{2})}([\alpha,\beta])
is continuously embedded in
H^{\mu(s_{1})}([\alpha,\beta])
.

(iv)

For all
s>0
the space
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
.

(v)

The set
t^{\mu}\overline{C}^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta])
is dense in
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
.

Proof.

(i) Recall that for
s-\mu\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
one has
\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s-\mu}([\alpha,\beta])
by Lemma
3.1
. Therefore, it follows from Definition
3.2
that

H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])=\Lambda_{+}^{(-\mu)}\mathfrak{e}\overline{H}^{s-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)=\Lambda_{+}^{(-\mu)}\mathfrak{e}\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{s-\mu}([\alpha,\beta])

and hence (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) show
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])=\dot{H}^{s}([\alpha,\beta])
. Since
\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])=L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, this implies (for
s=0
) that
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(0)}([\alpha,\beta])=L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
.

(ii) This follows from
[
33
, Theorem 5.4]
, see also
[
38
, equation (13)]
.

(iii) is an immediate consequence of (
3.6
) and the fact that
\overline{H}^{s_{2}-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)
is boundedly embedded in
\overline{H}^{s_{1}-\mu}(\alpha,\beta)
, whenever
s_{1}<s_{2}
.

(iv) Choose
\widetilde{s}\in(0,{\rm min\,}\{\frac{1}{2},s\})
. Then, by (iii) we have that
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
is continuously embedded in
\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(\widetilde{s})}([\alpha,\beta])
, which coincides, by (i), with
\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{\widetilde{s}}([\alpha,\beta])
. Since
\widetilde{s}>0
, the latter space is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, which yields the claim in (iii).

(v) This claim follows from
[
33
, Proposition 4.1]
.
∎

Item (vi) in the previous lemma suggests that for
f\in H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
the function
t^{-\mu}f
admits Dirichlet traces, if
s-\mu>\frac{1}{2}
, and that
t^{-\mu}f
admits Dirichlet and Neumann traces, whenever
s-\mu>\frac{3}{2}
. In the following lemma we see that this is indeed the case and we collect some properties of the trace maps. The claims about the existence of the trace maps can be deduced from
[
35
, equation (3.12)]
, the statements about their kernels are mentioned in
[
37
, equation (2.12)]
, see also
[
33
, Section 5]
for details.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
,
s-\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, and
H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])
be defined by (
3.5
). Then, the following is true:

(i)

If
s-\mu>\frac{1}{2}
, then the map

t^{\mu}\overline{C}^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta])\ni f\mapsto\gamma_{0}^{(\mu)}f:=\begin{pmatrix}(t^{-\mu}f)(\alpha)\\
(t^{-\mu}f)(\beta)\end{pmatrix}

has a continuous extension
\gamma_{0}^{(\mu)}:\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Moreover,

\mathfrak{r}H^{(\mu+1)(s)}([\alpha,\beta])=\big\{f\in\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta]):\gamma_{0}^{(\mu)}(f)=0\big\}.

(ii)

If
s-\mu>\frac{3}{2}
, then the map

t^{\mu}\overline{C}^{\infty}([\alpha,\beta])\ni f\mapsto\gamma_{1}^{(\mu)}f:=\begin{pmatrix}(t^{-\mu}f)^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{-\mu}f)^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}

has a continuous extension
\gamma_{1}^{(\mu)}:\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Moreover,

\mathfrak{r}H^{(\mu+2)(s)}([\alpha,\beta])=\big\{f\in\mathfrak{r}H^{\mu(s)}([\alpha,\beta]):\gamma_{0}^{(\mu)}(f)=\gamma_{1}^{(\mu)}(f)=0\big\}.

4.
Self-adjoint fractional Laplacians

Throughout this section, let
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded interval
and
a\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
be fixed. The
restricted fractional Laplacian
is the operator

\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e},

where the restriction operator
\mathfrak{r}
is given by (
3.1
),
(-\Delta)^{a}
is the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}
defined by (
1.1
), and
\mathfrak{e}
is the extension operator introduced in (
3.2
). Hence,
\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}
acts on a generic
f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
by first extending it by zero to
\mathfrak{e}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, applying
(-\Delta)^{a}
to it, and then restricting the distribution
(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in H^{-2a}(\mathbb{R})
to
(\alpha,\beta)
. By construction,

\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f(x)=c_{a}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{|x-y|>\varepsilon}\frac{(\mathfrak{e}f)(x)-(\mathfrak{e}f)(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2a}}dy,\quad x\in(\alpha,\beta),

if the integral converges, where

c_{a}:=\frac{a4^{a}}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1-a)}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+2a}{2}\right).

(4.1)

We first recall the definitions and some known results about the minimal, the maximal, and the
Dirichlet realization of the restricted fractional Laplacian using suitable Hörmander transmission spaces
as their natural domains; cf. Remark
4.3
.
Afterwards, in Section
4.2
we introduce a boundary triplet for the restricted fractional Laplacian and show in the following subsections, how it can be used to deduce several properties of self-adjoint realizations of it.

4.1.
The minimal, the maximal, and the Dirichlet realization of
(-\Delta)^{a}
on
(\alpha,\beta)

In the following the Hörmander transmission space
H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])
will
play a particularly important role. Note that here
\mu=a-1
and
s=2a
in Definition
3.2

and hence
s-\mu=a+1\in(\frac{3}{2},2)
.
We will start with the
maximal realization

(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
of the restricted fractional Laplacian

\begin{split}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f&:=\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}&:=\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]),\end{split}

(4.2)

where
(-\Delta)^{a}
is the fractional Laplacian defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and mapping to
H^{-2a}(\mathbb{R})
,
and
\mathfrak{e}
and
\mathfrak{r}
denote the extension and restriction operator defined by (
3.2
) and (

), respectively.
It follows from
[
33
, Theorem 7.1
3^{\circ}
]
that
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
is a well-defined operator in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, i.e. for all
f\in\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])\subset L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
one has
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
. The terminology
maximal
will be explained later in Remark
4.3
.

Next, we define the
minimal realization

(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
of the restricted fractional Laplacian by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}f&:=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}&:=\bigl\{{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}:\gamma_{0}^{(a-1)}(f)=\gamma_{1}^{(a-1)}(f)=0\bigr\}.\end{split}

(4.3)

Taking Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.3
(i) into account (note that
2a-(a+1)\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
), one finds that

{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}=\mathfrak{r}H^{(a+1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{2a}([\alpha,\beta]).

(4.4)

In particular,
\mathfrak{e}{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}\subset H^{2a}(\mathbb{R})
and thus, the application of
(-\Delta)^{a}
in the definition of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
results in an element in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Furthermore, as
\dot{H}^{2a}([\alpha,\beta])
is a closed subspace of
H^{2a}(\mathbb{R})
, the operator
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
is closed and symmetric.
It will follow later in Theorem
4.2
that
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}=((-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a})^{*}
.

An important role in our considerations is played by the
Dirichlet realization
of
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, which is defined by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}f&:=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}&:=\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}:\gamma_{0}^{(a-1)}(f)=0\}.\end{split}

(4.5)

Note that Lemma
3.4
(i) implies

{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}=H^{a(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]).

It will turn out later in Proposition
4.10
that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
coincides with the Friedrichs extension of the minimal realization
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
.
In the next lemma known basic properties of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
are collected:

Lemma 4.1
.

The Dirichlet realization
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
is a positive self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
with purely discrete spectrum accumulating to
+\infty
.

Proof.

The fact that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
is self-adjoint and positive is mentioned in
[
34
, Section 6.2]
; it is clear that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}

is unbounded and hence the spectrum must accumulate to
+\infty
.
Moreover, as
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}=\mathfrak{r}H^{a(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, see Lemma
3.3
(iii), it follows that the spectrum of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
consists of discrete eigenvalues with finite multiplicities.
∎

As mentioned above, we will show in Proposition
4.10
that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
coincides with the Friedrichs extension of the minimal realization
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
. This operator has been investigated in
[
47

, where, in particular, a two-term Weyl-type asymptotic law for the eigenvalues was found and according to

[

are simple.
However, for our further analysis, the results stated in Lemma

are sufficient.

4.2.
The boundary triplet and self-adjoint extensions

Let
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
be the maximal realization of
(-\Delta)^{a}
on
[\alpha,\beta]
defined in (
4.2
) and let
t
be the distance function introduced in (
3.7
). Define the boundary mappings

\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}:{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}=\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}

by

\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}f:=\Gamma(a)\gamma_{0}^{(a-1)}f=\Gamma(a)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)(\alpha)\\
(t^{1-a}f)(\beta)\end{pmatrix}

(4.6)

and

\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}f:=\Gamma(a+1)\gamma_{1}^{(a-1)}f=\Gamma(a+1)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix},

(4.7)

where
\Gamma(a)
and
\Gamma(a+1)
are evaluations of Euler’s gamma function.
Observe that
\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}
are well-defined by Lemma
3.4
.
In the next theorem, we verify that
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
is a boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
. Recall that
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
and
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
are the minimal and the Dirichlet realization of
(-\Delta)^{a}
defined in (
4.3
) and (
4.5
), respectively.

Theorem 4.2
.

The minimal realization
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
of the restricted fractional Laplacian is a densely defined closed symmetric operator in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)

and its adjoint is the maximal realization
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, that is,

\bigl((-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}\bigr)^{*}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\quad\text{and}\quad(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}=\bigl((-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\bigr)^{*}.

Furthermore,
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}

is a boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
such that

(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}=(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}.

(4.8)

Remark 4.3
.

(i) The fact that
((-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a})^{*}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
justifies the terminology
maximal realization of the fractional Laplacian in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
. To see this, we note first that
(-\Delta)^{a}
is bounded from
H^{2a}(\mathbb{R})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and hence, by self-adjointness and duality
(-\Delta)^{a}
has an extension that is bounded from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to

. Thus, for any
f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
one has
\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in\overline{H}^{-2a}(\alpha,\beta)
and for all
g
in the dual space

(\overline{H}^{-2a}(\alpha,\beta))^{*}=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{2a}([\alpha,\beta])={\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm min\,}

(see, e.g.,
[

for the first identity above) one has

\bigl(\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f,g\bigr)_{\overline{H}^{-2a}(\alpha,\beta)\times(\overline{H}^{-2a}(\alpha,\beta))^{*}}=\bigl(f,\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}g\bigr)_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}=\bigl(f,(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm min\,}g\bigr)_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)},

where the (sesquilinear) duality product in the pairing

was used.
From this it follows that

satisfies
\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
if and only if

and

\mathfrak{r}(-\Delta)^{a}\mathfrak{e}f=((-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm min\,})^{*}f=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f.

(ii) We also note that by (

)

(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\big(\ker\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}\cap\ker\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\big).

This fact will be seen independently also in the proof of Theorem

in (

(iii) For

and

.

.

We apply Theorem
2.4

\begin{split}\big((-\Delta&)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,g\big)_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}-\big(f,(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}g\big)_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}\\

(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}:=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker(\mathcal{B}\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}-\mathcal{A}\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}).

(4.10)

The operator
(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}
can be described more explicitly by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}f&=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}&=\bigl\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}:\mathcal{B}\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}f=\mathcal{A}\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}f\bigr\}.\end{split}

We will be particularly interested in self-adjoint realizations
(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}
. For this, we make the following assumption:

Assumption 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
be such that

{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad({\mathcal{A}}\,|\,{\mathcal{B}})\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 4}\text{ has rank }2.

The following statement is now an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.2
and the fact that
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}=\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
; cf. Lemma
3.3
(iv).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
). Then, an operator
A
satisfying
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}\subset A\subset(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
if and only if there exist matrices
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
that satisfy Assumption
4.4
such that
A
is of the form
A=(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}
as in (
4.10
). Moreover, the spectrum of any self-adjoint extension of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm min\,}
is purely discrete.

4.3.
The
\gamma
-field and Weyl function

In this section we compute the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function for the boundary triplet
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
, see Section
2.2
for the general definition of these objects. While the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function can only be computed explicitly for the spectral point
\lambda=0
, see Proposition
4.6
, we can express the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function for all
\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a})
in terms of the resolvent
((-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a}-\lambda)^{-1}
in Corollary
4.7
. Finally, in Proposition
4.8
we use these operators to conclude a Krein type resolvent formula for a generic self-adjoint realization
(-\Delta)_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}^{a}
as in (
4.10
).

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
) and define the functions
v_{1},v_{2}\in H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])
by

\begin{split}v_{1}(x)&=\left(1-\frac{4}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2}}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{a-1},\\
v_{2}(x)&=\frac{2}{\beta-\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)v_{1}(x),\qquad\quad x\in(\alpha,\beta).\end{split}

(4.11)

Then, for the
\gamma
-field
\gamma^{(a)}
and the Weyl function
M^{(a)}
associated with
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
one has

\gamma^{(a)}(0)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{4}\right)^{a-1}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\left(\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}}{2}v_{1}+\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{2}v_{2}\right)

and

M^{(a)}(0)=\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}.

(4.12)

Observe that formally for
a=1
in (
4.12
) one recovers exactly the formulas for
\gamma(0)
and
M(0)
for the case of the classical Laplacian,
see (
2.12
)-(
2.13
). Moreover, as we will see in the proof, the functions
v_{1},v_{2}
in (
4.11
) are a basis of
\ker(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}
. In particular, constant and linear functions do not belong to
\ker(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}
as soon as
a\neq 1
, as they can not be represented as linear combinations of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

First, it is shown in
[
23
]
that the functions

\widetilde{v}_{1}(y):=(1-y^{2})^{a-1}_{+},\quad\widetilde{v}_{2}(y):=y\widetilde{v}_{1}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

satisfy the relation

(-\Delta)^{a}\widetilde{v}_{j}(y)=0,\quad y\in(-1,1),\,j\in\{1,2\};

cf.
[
23
, Tables 1 and 2]
. In other words,
\widetilde{v}_{j}(y)

belongs to the kernel of the restricted fractional Laplacian on

(-1,1)
. Hence, we find that the functions
v_{1},v_{2}

in (
4.11
), which satisfy the relation

v_{j}(x)=\widetilde{v}_{j}\left(\frac{2}{\beta-\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)\right),\quad x\in(\alpha,\beta),\,j\in\{1,2\},

belong to
\ker(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
. Since
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
is a boundary triplet for

(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, it follows from
[
8
, Theorems 2.4.1
and 2.5.1]
that
\dim\ker(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}=\dim\mathbb{C}^{2}=2
and therefore,

\ker(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}=\textup{span}\,\{v_{1},v_{2}\}.

In particular, this implies that for all
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
there exist constants
a_{1}=a_{1}(c_{1},c_{2}),a_{2}=a_{2}(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\gamma^{(a)}(0)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=a_{1}v_{1}+a_{2}v_{2}.

(4.13)

To proceed, we note that

t(x)^{1-a}v_{1}(x)=\frac{4^{a-1}}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2a-2}}\begin{cases}(\beta-x)^{a-1},&x\in(\alpha,\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}),\\
(x-\alpha)^{a-1},&x\in(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2},\beta),\end{cases}

(4.14)

and

t(x)^{1-a}v_{2}(x)=\frac{4^{a-1}}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2a-2}}\frac{2}{\beta-\alpha}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)\begin{cases}(\beta-x)^{a-1},&x\in(\alpha,\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}),\\
(x-\alpha)^{a-1},&x\in(\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2},\beta).\end{cases}

(4.15)

Therefore, a direct calculation shows that

\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}v_{1}=\Gamma(a)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}v_{1})(\alpha)\\
(t^{1-a}v_{1})(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a)\left(\frac{4}{\beta-\alpha}\right)^{a-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}

(4.16)

and

\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}v_{2}=\Gamma(a)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}v_{2})(\alpha)\\
(t^{1-a}v_{2})(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a)\left(\frac{4}{\beta-\alpha}\right)^{a-1}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Combining this with (
4.13
) and the definition of

\gamma^{(a)}(0)
(see Definition
2.5
), we get
that

\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}\gamma^{(a)}(0)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a)\left(\frac{4}{\beta-\alpha}\right)^{a-1}\left(a_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}+a_{2}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Solving this linear system of equations yields

a_{1}=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{4}\right)^{a-1}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}}{2},\qquad a_{2}=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{4}\right)^{a-1}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{2},

which gives in (
4.13
) exactly the claimed formula for

\gamma^{(a)}(0)
.

To get the claimed formula for
M^{(a)}(0)
, we note that a direct
calculation using (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) yields

\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}v_{1}=\Gamma(a+1)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}v_{1})^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{1-a}v_{1})^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a+1)\frac{4^{a-1}(1-a)}{(\beta-\alpha)^{a}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}

and

\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}v_{2}=\Gamma(a+1)\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}v_{2})^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{1-a}v_{2})^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma(a+1)\frac{4^{a-1}(1+a)}{(\beta-\alpha)^{a}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, one gets for any
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

\begin{split}M^{(a)}(0)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}&=\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\gamma^{(a)}(0)\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{4}\right)^{a-1}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\left(\frac{c_{1}+c_{2}}{2}\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}v_{1}+\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{2}\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}v_{2}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{4}\right)^{a-1}\frac{4^{a-1}}{(\beta-\alpha)^{a}}\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(a)}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

which shows the claimed formula for
M^{(a)}(0)
.
∎

In the following corollary we express the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function for the boundary triplet
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
for general
\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}})
in terms of
\gamma^{(a)}(0)
,
M^{(a)}(0)
, and
((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-\lambda)^{-1}
. This result is an immediate consequence of Proposition
4.6
, (
2.9
), and (
2.10
).

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
). Then, for all
\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}})
one has

\gamma^{(a)}(\lambda)=\bigl(1+\lambda\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}\bigr)\gamma^{(a)}(0)

and

M^{(a)}(\lambda)=\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}+\gamma^{(a)}(0)^{*}\bigl[\lambda+\lambda^{2}\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}\bigr]\gamma^{(a)}(0).

One can apply now directly Theorem
2.7
and the considerations below Theorem
2.7
(see (
2.15
) and (
2.16
)) to obtain a Birman-Schwinger principle and a Krein type resolvent formula for all self-adjoint extensions of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
from Proposition
4.5
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
), suppose that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfy Assumption
4.4
, and let
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
be given by (
4.10
).
Then the following assertions hold for all
\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}})
:

(i)

\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm p}((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})
if and only if

0\in\sigma_{\rm p}({\mathcal{B}}-{\mathcal{A}}M^{(a)}(\lambda))
, and in this case

\ker((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}-\lambda)=\gamma^{(a)}(\lambda)\ker({\mathcal{B}}-{\mathcal{A}}M^{(a)}(\lambda)).

In particular,
0\in\sigma_{\rm p}((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})
if and only if

\det\left({\mathcal{B}}-\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}{\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}\right)=0;

(ii)

\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})
if and only if

0\notin\sigma_{\textup{p}}(\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}M^{(a)}(\lambda))
and in this case

\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}=\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}+\gamma^{(a)}(\lambda)\bigl(\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}M^{(a)}(\lambda)\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal{A}\gamma^{(a)}(\overline{\lambda})^{*}.

An immediate consequence of the previous considerations is the fact that all self-adjoint extensions of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
are semibounded from below.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
), suppose that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfy Assumption
4.4
, and let
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
be given by (
4.10
).
Then
\sigma((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})\cap(-\infty,0]
consists of at most two eigenvalues (taking multiplicities into account), and the positive spectrum (discrete eigenvalues) of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}

accumulates to
+\infty
. In particular,
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is semibounded from below.

Proof.

Recall that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
is a self-adjoint positive operator by Lemma
4.1
. Denote the lower bound of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
by
\kappa_{0}>0
. It follows from Proposition
4.8
that the resolvent difference

\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}-i\big)^{-1}-\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-i\big)^{-1}=\gamma^{(a)}(i)\bigl(\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}M^{(a)}(i)\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal{A}\gamma^{(a)}(-i)^{*}

has at most rank two. Therefore, by
[
10
, § 9.3, Theorem 3]
the set
\sigma((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})\cap(-\infty,\kappa_{0})
consists of at most two eigenvalues (taking multiplicities into account) and hence
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is semibounded from below.
As
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
is an unbounded self-adjoint operator the spectrum must accumulate to
+\infty
. According to Proposition
4.5
the spectrum is purely discrete.
∎

4.4.
Further facts about
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm D}

Using the boundary triplet
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
from (
4.6
)-(
4.7
) we can prove some further results about the restricted fractional Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Our starting point is the
observation that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
is the Friedrichs extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
; see also
[
32
,
33
]
.
The following result is a direct application of Proposition
2.8
.

Proposition 4.10
.

The Friedrichs extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
is given by
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.8
the Friedrichs extension
S_{F}
of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
is the restriction of
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
to the set

{\text{\rm dom\,}}S_{F}=\left\{f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}:\begin{pmatrix}\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}f\\
\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}f\end{pmatrix}\in M^{(a)}(-\infty)\right\},

where the limit
M^{(a)}(-\infty)
of the Weyl function
M^{(a)}
is understood in the strong resolvent sense or, equivalently, in the strong graph sense.

Since
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}
, it suffices to prove that

(M^{(a)}(-\infty))^{-1}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow-\infty}(M^{(a)}(\lambda))^{-1}=0\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}.

(4.17)

For that purpose, we consider the triplet
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{0}^{(a)},\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{1}^{(a)}\}
, where

\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{0}^{(a)}=\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{1}^{(a)}=-\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}.

As
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
is a boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, also
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{0}^{(a)},\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{1}^{(a)}\}
is a boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
, for which we have

\widetilde{A}_{0}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{0}^{(a)}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}

and the corresponding Weyl function
\widetilde{M}^{(a)}
is given by

\widetilde{M}^{(a)}(\lambda)=-(M^{(a)}(\lambda))^{-1},\quad\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a})\cap\rho(\widetilde{A}_{0}).

From Proposition
4.9
it is clear that
\widetilde{A}_{0}
is bounded from below and hence we can choose
\kappa_{1}<0
such that

\widetilde{A}_{0}\geq\kappa_{1}
. As
(-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a}
is positive by Lemma
4.1
it is clear that

(-\infty,\kappa_{1})
is contained in
\rho((-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a})\cap\rho(\widetilde{A}_{0})
.

In the following consider
\lambda\in(-\infty,\kappa_{1})
.
Since
\widetilde{A}_{0}
is a restriction of
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
it is clear that

{\text{\rm dom\,}}\widetilde{A}_{0}\subset{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}=\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])
and with the help of the closed graph theorem one obtains that

(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}:L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])

is bounded. It follows that the block diagonal operator

\begin{split}&\begin{pmatrix}(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}&0\\
0&0_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\backslash(\alpha,\beta))}\end{pmatrix}:\\
&\quad\begin{pmatrix}L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\\
\{0_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\backslash(\alpha,\beta))}\}\end{pmatrix}\cong\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])=H^{(a-1)(0)}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])\end{split}

(4.18)

is also bounded. We will show at the end of the proof that the interpolation property

\big[H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]),H^{(a-1)(0)}([\alpha,\beta])\big]_{\theta}=H^{(a-1)(2a\theta)}([\alpha,\beta]),\quad\theta\in(0,1),

(4.19)

holds.
Since the operator in (
4.18
) is a positive self-adjoint operator in

\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])
it
follows with an interpolation argument, see
[
49
, Section 2.1 in Chapter I]
, and (
4.19
) that for any
\theta\in(0,1)
also

\begin{pmatrix}(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-\theta}&0\\
0&0_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\backslash(\alpha,\beta))}\end{pmatrix}:\begin{pmatrix}L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\\
\{0_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}\backslash(\alpha,\beta))}\}\end{pmatrix}\cong\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])\rightarrow H^{(a-1)(2a\theta)}([\alpha,\beta])

is bounded. This implies that

(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-\theta}:L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow\mathfrak{r}H^{(a-1)(2a\theta)}([\alpha,\beta])

is bounded. In particular, for
\theta\in(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{2a}),\frac{1}{2})
one has
2a\theta\in(a-\frac{1}{2},a)
and thus

2a\theta-(a-1)\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, so that
Lemma
3.4
(i) can be applied. It follows that the mapping

\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{1}^{(a)}(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-\theta}=-\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}(\widetilde{A}_{0}-\lambda)^{-\theta}:L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}

is bounded. Therefore, we can apply
[
9
, Theorem 6.1]
, according to which there exists a constant
C
that is independent of
\lambda
such that

\big\|(M^{(a)}(\lambda))^{-1}\big\|=\big\|\widetilde{M}^{(a)}(\lambda)\big\|\leq\frac{C}{(\kappa_{1}-\lambda)^{1-2\theta}},\quad\lambda\in(-\infty,\kappa_{1}),

holds. From this (
4.17
) and thus also the claim of this proposition follows.

It remains to prove (
4.19
), which will be done with the help of
[
49
, Theorem 14.3 in Chapter I]
. In the following we explain how the spaces in
[
49
, Theorem 14.3 in Chapter I]
can be chosen and why the assumptions there are satisfied. First, set
X=Y=\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])=H^{(a-1)(0)}([\alpha,\beta])
,
\Phi=\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])
,
\Psi=\mathfrak{r}\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}([\alpha,\beta])
, and
\partial=\mathfrak{r}\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}
. Since
\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}
is a classical pseudodifferential operator that preserves the support in
[\alpha,\beta]
,
\partial
is well defined and continuous from
\Phi
to
\Psi
. Next, set
\mathscr{X}=\overline{H}^{a+1}(\alpha,\beta)
,
\mathscr{Y}=\overline{H}^{1-a}(\alpha,\beta)
. Then,

(X)_{\partial,\mathscr{X}}:=\bigl\{f\in\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta]):\mathfrak{r}\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}f\in\overline{H}^{a+1}(\alpha,\beta)\bigr\}=H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]);

(4.20)

the inclusion
\subset
in the latter equality is clear by the second line in (
3.5
), and the other inclusion
\supset
also follows from the second line in (
3.5
) using that
H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])\subset L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
, as
s=2a\geq 0
. Likewise, one has that

(Y)_{\partial,\mathscr{Y}}:=\bigl\{f\in\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta]):\mathfrak{r}\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}f\in\overline{H}^{a-1}(\alpha,\beta)\bigr\}=H^{(a-1)(0)}([\alpha,\beta])=L^{2}(\alpha,\beta),

where the latter equality is true by Lemma
3.3
(i). To proceed, set

\tilde{\mathscr{X}}=\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{1-a}([\alpha,\beta])
. Then, with Lemma
3.1
we see that

\mathscr{X}=\overline{H}^{1+a}(\alpha,\beta)\subset\overline{H}^{1-a}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{1-a}([\alpha,\beta])=\tilde{\mathscr{X}}

and
\mathscr{Y}=\overline{H}^{1-a}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{1-a}([\alpha,\beta])=\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}
, i.e. assumption i) in
[
49
, (14.23) in Chapter I]
is fulfilled. Moreover, due to the properties of
\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}
in (
3.4
) one has that
\partial=\mathfrak{r}\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}
is bounded from
X=Y=\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])
to
\tilde{\mathscr{X}}=\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{1-a}([\alpha,\beta])
, i.e. ii) in
[
49
, (14.23) in Chapter I]
is satisfied. Eventually, set
\mathscr{G}=(\Lambda^{(a-1)}_{+})^{-1}\mathfrak{e}
. Using again the mapping properties of
\Lambda_{+}^{(a-1)}
in (
3.4
) and (
3.3
) one has that
\mathscr{G}
is bounded from
\tilde{\mathscr{X}}=\tilde{\mathscr{Y}}=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{1-a}([\alpha,\beta])
to
X=Y=\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])
and that
\mathscr{G}
is the right inverse of
\partial
. Hence, also iii) in
[
49
, (14.23) in Chapter I]
is true (with
r\equiv 0
). Therefore, we conclude from
[
49
, Theorem 14.3 in Chapter I]
that for
\theta\in(0,1)

\begin{split}\big[H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta])&,H^{(a-1)(0)}([\alpha,\beta])\big]_{\theta}\\
&=\big([\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta]),\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])]_{\theta}\big)_{\partial,[\overline{H}^{1+a}(\alpha,\beta),\overline{H}^{1-a}(\alpha,\beta)]_{\theta}}\\
&=\big(\dot{H}^{0}([\alpha,\beta])\big)_{\partial,\overline{H}^{2a\theta+1-a}(\alpha,\beta)}\\
&=H^{(a-1)(2a\theta)}([\alpha,\beta]),\end{split}

where for the last equality again a similar argument as in (
4.20
) is used. Thus, (
4.19
) is true.
∎

Since
(-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a}
is the Friedrichs extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
, we can relate it to its associated quadratic form. Note that this quadratic form is used as starting point for the analysis of a Dirichlet realization of the fractional Laplacian in, e.g.,
[
16
,
23
,
26
,
47
,
60
]
; we refer to the review paper
[
25
]
for further references.
Recall that
\mathfrak{e}
and
\mathfrak{r}
are the extension and restriction operator defined by (
3.2
) and (
3.1
), respectively.

Corollary 4.11
.

The operator
(-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a}
is the unique self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
that is associated with the closed quadratic form

\begin{split}q[f]=\frac{c_{a}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\mathfrak{e}f(x)-\mathfrak{e}f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2a}}dxdy,\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}q=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{a}([\alpha,\beta]),\end{split}

(4.21)

where
c_{a}
is given by (
4.1
).

Proof.

Recall that the Friedrichs extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
is the unique self-adjoint operator associated to the closure of the quadratic form

q_{\rm min\,}[f,g]=((-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}f,g),\quad{\text{\rm dom\,}}q_{\rm min\,}={\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}=\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{2a}([\alpha,\beta]);

thus we have to verify that the closure of
q_{\rm min\,}
is the form
q
in (
4.21
).
In fact, note first that for

f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
a direct calculation involving (
1.1
) yields

((-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}f,f)=\frac{c_{a}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\mathfrak{e}f(x)-\mathfrak{e}f(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2a}}dxdy,

where the right hand side coincides with the Sobolev-Slobodeckii semi-norm in
H^{a}(\mathbb{R})
.
This shows

q_{\rm min\,}[f]+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}=\|f\|_{H^{a}(\alpha,\beta)}^{2},\quad f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}q_{\rm min\,}.

Since
\dot{H}^{a}([\alpha,\beta])
is a closed subspace of
H^{a}(\mathbb{R})
and
and since
\dot{H}^{2a}([\alpha,\beta])
is dense in
\dot{H}^{a}([\alpha,\beta])
(with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{a}(\mathbb{R})}
)
it follows that
{\text{\rm dom\,}}q_{\rm min\,}
is dense in
\mathfrak{r}\dot{H}^{a}([\alpha,\beta])

(with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{a}(\alpha,\beta)}
). Therefore, the closure of the form
q_{\rm min\,}
coincides with
the quadratic form
q
in (
4.21
).
∎

It is known that the operator associated with the form (
4.21
)
generates a sub-Markovian process (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Section 2]
), where the corresponding Dirichlet form is denoted by
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F}^{D})
), hence a strongly-continuous semigroup
(e^{-t(-\Delta)^{a}_{\mathrm{D}}})_{t\geq 0}
on
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
that is positivity-preserving and
L^{\infty}
-contractive. This means that

e^{-t(-\Delta)^{a}_{\mathrm{D}}}f(x)=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}p_{t}(x,y)f(y)dy,\qquad\hbox{for all }t>0,\ f\in L^{2}(\alpha,\beta),\ x\in(\alpha,\beta),

for an integral kernel
p_{t}
such that
p_{t}(x,y)\in[0,1]
for all
t>0
and a.e.
x,y\in(\alpha,\beta)
.
In particular, we can apply now a result from
[
54
]
to obtain a pointwise upper bound for all eigenfunctions of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Each eigenpair
(\lambda^{\mathrm{D}}_{n},\varphi_{n})
of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\mathrm{D}}
satisfies the pointwise estimate

\frac{|\varphi_{n}(x)|}{\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{\lambda^{\mathrm{D}}_{n}}{\Gamma(2a+1)}\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\right)^{a}\left(1-\frac{4}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2}}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{a},\qquad x\in(\alpha,\beta).

Proof.

First, by
[
23
, Table 1]
the function

\widetilde{f}(y):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2a+1)}(1-y^{2})^{a}_{+},\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}},

satisfies the relation

(-\Delta)^{a}\widetilde{f}(y)=1,\quad y\in(-1,1).

Therefore, the function

f(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2a+1)}\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\right)^{a}\left(1-\frac{4}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2}}\left(x-\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{a},\quad x\in(\alpha,\beta),

satisfies

(-\Delta)^{a}f(x)=1,\quad x\in(\alpha,\beta).

With a similar calculation as in (
4.14
) and (
4.16
) one sees that
\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}f=0
and hence
f\in{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)_{\textup{D}}^{a}
.
Using the known fact that
-(-\Delta)^{a}_{\mathrm{D}}
generates a positivity-preserving operator, we deduce the claim from
[
54
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

4.5.
Semibounded self-adjoint extensions

Since
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}
coincides with the Friedrichs extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}

by Proposition
4.10
the general principles from Section
2.3
imply
further results about the ordering of self-adjoint extensions of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
. First, we describe the Krein–von Neumann extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
, i.e. the smallest nonnegative self-adjoint extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
; cf. (
2.18
).
In the following result the boundary condition is explicit as
M^{(a)}(0)
is known from Proposition
4.6
;
again, by setting formally
a=1
, the result below reduces to the well-known formula for the Krein–von Neumann extension of the classical Laplacian in Example
2.9
.

Proposition 4.13
.

The Krein-von Neumann extension of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
is given by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{K}}f&=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{K}}&=\bigg\{f\in H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]):\\
&\quad\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)(\alpha)\\
(t^{1-a}f)(\beta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\alpha)\\
-(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\beta)\end{pmatrix}\bigg\}.\end{split}

Similarly, Proposition
2.10
together with the explicit form of

M^{(a)}(0)
lead to a characterization of all nonnegative extensions of
(-\Delta)_{\rm min\,}^{a}
.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
be the boundary triplet for
(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}
defined in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
), suppose that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
satisfy Assumption
4.4
, and let
(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}
be given by (
4.10
).
Then the following equivalence holds:

0\leq(-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{B}}^{*}={\mathcal{B}}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\geq\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}{\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\
1&-a\end{pmatrix}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}.

Finally, we turn our attention to the Neumann realization of the restricted fractional Laplacian that is defined by

\begin{split}(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}f&:=(-\Delta)_{\rm max\,}^{a}f,\\
{\text{\rm dom\,}}(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}&:=\bigl\{f\in H^{(a-1)(2a)}([\alpha,\beta]):(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\alpha)=(t^{1-a}f)^{\prime}(\beta)=0\bigr\}.\end{split}

(4.22)

The regularity of solutions of equations involving the higher dimensional counterpart of
(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}
is studied in
[
32
]
. We emphasize that
(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}
is a different fractional Neumann realization as the
spectral fractional Neumann Laplacian
investigated in
[
34
]
, the
regional fractional Neumann Laplacian
studied in
[
61
]
, and the fractional Laplacian with
nonlocal Neumann boundary conditions
introduced in
[
22
]
, and these operators have different properties as the ones found for
(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}
in Proposition
4.15
below; we refer to
[
34
, Section 6.2 and 6.3]
for an overview of different Neumann boundary conditions for the fractional Laplace operator.

Observe that with the boundary triplet in (
4.6
)-(
4.7
) the operator (
4.22
) can also be written in the form

(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}=(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)},

(4.23)

from which we conclude some interesting spectral properties of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
in the next proposition.
We point out that
0\notin\sigma_{\textup{p}}((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}})
, but
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
has one negative eigenvalue. This is a significant difference to what is known for the Neumann realization of the classical Laplacian; cf. Remark
4.16
below. Recall that
\gamma^{(a)}
and
M^{(a)}
are the
\gamma
-field and Weyl function for the boundary triplet
\{\mathbb{C}^{2},\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)},\Upsilon_{1}^{(a)}\}
, see Section
4.3
.

Proposition 4.15
.

The operator
(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)
and the following holds for all
\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}})
.

(i)

\sigma((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}})
is purely discrete and

\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm p}((-\Delta)^{a}_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}})\quad\text{if and only if}\quad 0\in\sigma_{\rm p}(M^{(a)}(\lambda)).

(4.24)

In particular,
0\notin\sigma((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}})
and
(-\Delta)_{\textup{N}}^{a}
has exactly one simple negative eigenvalue.

(ii)

\lambda\in\rho((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}})
if and only if

0\notin\sigma_{\textup{p}}(M^{(a)}(\lambda))
and in this case

\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}=\big((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}-\lambda\big)^{-1}-\gamma^{(a)}(\lambda)M^{(a)}(\lambda)^{-1}\gamma^{(a)}(\overline{\lambda})^{*}.

Proof.

From (
4.23
) it is clear that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
is of the form (
4.10
) with
{\mathcal{A}}=I
and
{\mathcal{B}}=0
. Therefore, by Proposition
4.5
the operator
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
is self-adjoint with purely discrete spectrum.
Moreover, the Birman-Schwinger principle in (
4.24
) and the Krein-type resolvent formula in (ii) follow from Proposition
4.8
.
Note that the eigenvalues of
M^{(a)}(0)
are given by

\mu_{1}(0)=\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}(-a-1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{2}(0)=\frac{a}{\beta-\alpha}(-a+1).

As
\mu_{1}(0)\not=0
and
\mu_{2}(0)\not=0
we conclude from (
4.24
) that
0\notin\sigma((-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}})
.

It remains to show that
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Let
\kappa_{0}
be the lower bound of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{D}}=(-\Delta)^{a}_{\rm max\,}\upharpoonright\ker\Upsilon_{0}^{(a)}
, recall that
\kappa_{0}>0
, and denote by
\mu_{1}(\lambda)\leq\mu_{2}(\lambda)
the eigenvalues of
M^{(a)}(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in(-\infty,\kappa_{0})
. By the analyticity and monotonicity of
(-\infty,\kappa_{0})\ni\lambda\mapsto M^{(a)}(\lambda)
also the functions

(-\infty,\kappa_{0})\ni\lambda\mapsto\mu_{j}(\lambda),\qquad j\in\{1,2\},

are continuous and non-decreasing. This and (
4.17
) imply that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow-\infty}\mu_{j}(\lambda)=-\infty,\qquad j\in\{1,2\}.

Since
\mu_{1}(0)<0
the function
\mu_{1}
has no negative zero. On the other hand, as
\mu_{2}(0)>0
, the function
\mu_{2}
has exactly one zero in
(-\infty,0)
, which by (
4.24
) is an eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
. Thus,
(-\Delta)^{a}_{\textup{N}}
has exactly one negative simple eigenvalue.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

As mentioned below Proposition
4.6
the
2\times 2
-matrix
M^{(a)}(0)
coincides with
M(0)
in (
2.13
) in the limiting case
a=1
.
The eigenvalues of
M(0)
are given by
0
and
\frac{-2}{\beta-\alpha}
and hence the proof of Proposition
4.15
would imply that
the usual Neumann Laplacian (that is,
A_{1}
in (
2.8
)) is nonnegative and has
0
as a simple eigenvalue (which is in accordance with the
well understood spectral properties of this operator).
