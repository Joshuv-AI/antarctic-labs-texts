Comment on “A High Frailty Burden Is a Strong Predictor of Adverse Postoperative Outcomes in Geriatric Lumbar Spine Surgery: A Retrospective Cohort Study”
Authors: Xiaoting Li, Yujia Cai, Zhuopeng Lin
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Cho et al, which investigated the impact of frailty on the prognosis of elderly patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery. 1 The authors are to be commended for introducing the 5-factor Modified Frailty Index (mFI-5) into risk stratification and for employing Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) to mitigate selection bias. Their finding that severe frailty (mFI-5 ≥ 3) is independently associated with hospitalization complications (OR 1.792, 95% CI 1.243–2.585, P = 0.002) adds valuable evidence to the growing body of literature concerning geriatric spine care. However, while the study successfully establishes a statistical association, we believe a deeper discussion is warranted regarding the distinction between population-level risk markers and individual-level predictive tools, the robustness of the causal inference, and the holistic interpretation of surgical benefit.

We commend Cho et al for introducing frailty assessment into the risk stratification of elderly patients undergoing lumbar spine surgery, and their IPTW-adjusted analysis demonstrated that mFI-5 ≥ 3 was independently associated with hospitalization complications (OR 1.792,95% CI 1.243–2.585, P = 0.002). However, a clear distinction must be made between statistical association and individual-level predictive power. This OR value supports mFI-5 as an important risk biomarker but does not alone establish its ability to accurately predict complication occurrence in specific patients. Furthermore, the study did not report discrimination power, calibration, sensitivity, specificity, internal validity, or the incremental predictive value of mFI-5 relative to established metrics such as ASA-PS. Thus, while the current findings strongly support an independent association between severe frailty and postoperative complications, further characterization of mFI-5 as a “robust predictive tool” may require more comprehensive evaluation of its predictive performance.

We also noted that the authors’ analytical approach to the frailty variable may have partially influenced the effect estimation. The use of IPTW to reduce confounding is commendable; however, the propensity score model only included age, gender, and baseline spinal pathology types. Despite the clear clinical significance of these factors, significant differences remained between the two groups in several preoperative characteristics associated with perioperative fragility, such as the proportions of ASA-PS III–IV levels (87.6% vs. 57.3%), anticoagulant usage rates (51.2% vs. 25.3%), and renal function. We recognize that the components of mFI-5 should not be simply omitted, and certain clinical variables may lie within causal pathways. Nevertheless, providing further clarification on the rationale for covariate selection and validating the results using a predefined causal framework along with sensitivity analyses across different propensity score models would better mitigate the impact of residual confounding on the study conclusions.

Beyond the aforementioned methodological issues regarding confounding control, we believe that incorporating patient-centered surgical benefit indicators would provide a more comprehensive interpretation of the study findings. The authors designated hospitalization complications and 1-year adverse events as primary outcome measures, which undoubtedly holds significant value for perioperative risk assessment in elderly patients; however, the clinical objectives of lumbar surgery extend beyond reducing postoperative injury to include pain relief, restoration of mobility, maintenance of functional independence, and improvement of quality of life. Thus, while this study is valuable for identifying high-risk populations requiring enhanced perioperative monitoring and management, it is insufficient to determine whether frailty indicates an unfavorable overall risk-benefit balance for surgery.

In summary, this study makes a valuable contribution by demonstrating that severe frailty, as measured by the mFI-5, is independently associated with increased hospitalization complications in elderly lumbar spine surgery. We suggest, however, that the findings may be interpreted most robustly as evidence of a population-level risk marker, rather than definitive proof of individual-level predictive accuracy or an unfavorable overall risk-benefit ratio without considering patient-centered functional outcomes. We appreciate the authors’ contribution and hope these considerations may help deepen discussion on the rigorous validation and holistic application of frailty assessment in geriatric spine care.
