Targeting Cend1-Atp5f1b interaction rescues mitochondrial dysfunction and ameliorates ischemic brain injury
Authors: Hao Li, Yi Yang, Meng Zhang, Eric Guo, Zeheng Li, Bo Wan, Zhigang Miao, Wei Ge, Gang Chen, Xingshun Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial dysfunction is a critical contributor to neuronal damage in acute ischemic stroke (AIS), and targeting mitochondrial function represents a promising therapeutic strategy. This study unveils the pivotal role of Cend1 protein in ischemic stroke and elucidates its underlying mechanisms. Using Cend1 knockout (KO) mice, Cend1 deficiency was shown to exacerbate cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, as evidenced by enlarged infarct volume, worsened neurological deficits in motor coordination and grip strength, together with alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), mPTP opening, ATP content, and the activities of respiratory Complex I and V. Mechanistically, Cend1 forms dimers via conserved GXXXA motifs in its transmembrane domain to enhance ATP synthesis. Disruption of dimerization of Cend1 (such as G130P mutation) destabilized Cend1, accelerating its degradation and abolishing ATP-enhancing effects. Atp5f1b, a mitochondrial ATP synthase subunit, was found to interact with Cend1. Furthermore, screening identified the small-molecule compound Tianeptine (TNT), which stabilizes Cend1 dimers, elevates ATP levels, and confers neuroprotection in a Cend1-dependent manner. Notably, TNT’s efficacy was abolished in Cend1 KO mice, highlighting its reliance on Cend1. The findings support the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction as a potential mitochondria-targeted mechanism, offering innovative strategies to combat ischemic stroke by enhancing bioenergetic resilience.

Introduction

Mitochondria are vital in cellular and organismal pathways that direct metabolism, stress responses, immunity, and cellular fate1, especially in terms of bioenergetics and apoptosis2,3. Due to their vital role in cellular function, and because mitochondrial dysfunction underlies many multifactorial diseases—especially neurological disorders such as acute ischemic stroke (AIS)—mitochondria have been regarded as promising therapeutic targets for these conditions4,5. AIS is one of the most common types of stroke caused by decreased blood flow and depleted adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production in the ischemic brain region6. The reduction of ATP triggers ischemic cascades such as membrane ion pump failure, plasma membrane depolarization, efflux of cellular potassium, and influx of sodium, calcium, chloride, and water7. Impaired ATP production after stroke was associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and increased mitochondrial degradation8. Functional mitochondria are essential in promoting neural survival and neurological improvement following ischemic stroke9–11, emphasizing the importance of maintaining mitochondrial functions as a therapeutic strategy for stroke treatment.

Mitochondria are enclosed by two distinct membranes: an outer membrane and an inner membrane. The inner membrane contains cristae, which greatly increase the surface area available to membrane proteins. Cell cycle exit and neuronal differentiation 1 (Cend1), also known as BM88, is a neuron-specific single-pass transmembrane protein containing a transmembrane domain of 20 hydrophobic amino acids12,13. Previous studies have shown that Cend1 is localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane, and its deletion induces mitochondrial dysfunction14. The function of Cend1 has been shown to involve coordinating cell cycle exit and differentiation of neuronal progenitors15. To explore the role of Cend1 in the central nervous system, Cend1 knockout mice were created and showed no overt abnormalities in overall appearance or brain shape compared to wild-type mice, but in behavioral tests, Cend1-deficient mice were found to exhibit decreased motor coordination, increased anxiety-related behavior, and impaired cognitive function14,16,17. In terms of histomorphology, the researchers found Cend1 knockout mice display impaired cerebellar development16, suggesting that Cend1 may play an important role in neurodevelopment.

The role of Cend1 in the neuropathophysiological context remains under investigation, although its tissue-specific expression makes it an appealing candidate for further study in ischemic stroke. This study investigated the role of Cend1 in ischemia-reperfusion injury, particularly its potential regulation of mitochondrial ATP production through interactions with other mitochondrial proteins. Furthermore, possible pharmacological interventions aimed at stabilizing the Cend1 dimer were explored, with the goal of identifying potential protective effects against ischemic brain injury.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Cend1 antibody (#8944S; 1:5000 dilution) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Tom20 antibody (A19403; 1:3000 dilution) was obtained from ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). ATP5B antibody (sc-55597; 1:1000 dilution) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). GFP antibody (ab1218; 1:1000 dilution) was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). HA-Tag (26D11) antibody (M20003; 1:5000 dilution) was purchased from Abmart (Shanghai, China). β-actin antibody (FD0060; 1:10000 dilution), β-tubulin antibody (FD0064; 1:10000 dilution), and GAPDH antibody (FD0063; 1:10000 dilution) were obtained from Fude Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Secondary antibodies for western blot analysis, including anti-rabbit lgG-HRP (111-035-003; 1:3000 dilution) and anti-mouse lgG-HRP (115-035-003; 1:3000 dilution), were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch Co., Ltd (West Grove, PA, USA). Anti-rabbit lgG-HRP (M21006S; 1:3000 dilution) was obtained from Abmart (Shanghai, China). Secondary antibodies for immunofluorescence were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and included the following: Alexa Fluor-488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG antibody (A21206; 1:200 dilution).

Cell culture

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from C57BL/6 fetal mice at embryonic day 16–18 (E16–18). Cortical tissues were carefully dissected, mechanically minced, and digested with 0.125% trypsin (Gibco, USA). The digestion was terminated by adding complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA). The resulting cell suspension was filtered and centrifuged to collect primary neurons. Cells were then seeded onto culture plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine and initially maintained in complete DMEM for 4 hours. After this incubation, the medium was aspirated, and cells were gently rinsed with pre-warmed PBS (Biosharp, China). Finally, neurons were cultured in pre-warmed Neurobasal Medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 2% B-27, 0.5 mM GlutaMAX, and 50 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA) for up to 10 days in vitro at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Primary neurons were used because they provide a physiologically relevant model to study Cend1-dependent effects on neuronal protein dimerization and mitochondrial function, which may not be fully recapitulated in immortalized cell lines.

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6J mice (20-25 g) were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the Cend1 KO mice were generated from CAM-SU Genomic Resource Center (Soochow, China). All mice were allowed free access to food and water under controlled conditions (12/12 h light/dark cycle with humidity of 50 ± 10%, 22 ± 3 °C). All the experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and its institutional animal-care committee. Every effort was made to avoid/alleviate animal pain and distress and minimize the number of animals used in the study.

Middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion surgery

Middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) was performed according to a previous description18. Mice were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane, and their anesthetized state was maintained with continuous 2% isoflurane delivery by a mask. The body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.3 °C during surgery via a temperature-regulated heating pad. A median incision was made on the neck to separate the right common carotid artery (CCA), right internal carotid artery (ICA), and right external carotid artery (ECA). After the ECA was ligated, a silicon-coated monofilament (diameter: 0.18 ± 0.01 mm, Guangzhou Jialing Co., Ltd, China) was inserted from the right ECA, through the bifurcation of CCA, and into the intracranial segment of ICA (10 ± 1 mm away from the carotid bifurcation) to block blood flow at the middle cerebral artery (MCA) for 45 min. Then, the monofilament was withdrawn to allow reperfusion. Sham-operated animals underwent the same anesthesia and surgical procedures except that the filament was not inserted into ICA. Local cerebral blood flow was monitored by a laser-Doppler blood flowmeter (Periflux 5010, Perimed, Jarfalla, Sweden) to ensure local cerebral blood flow was reduced by 80% of the baseline level.

Neurobehavioral evaluation

Neurological tests were performed 24 hours after reperfusion to assess behavioral impairments. Behavioral performance was scored according to the 6-point scoring system that monitored appetite, activity, and neurological defects19.

Stereotactic injection of adeno-associated virus

AAV2/9-Syn-ZsGreen, AAV2/9-Syn-m-Cend1-3xflag-ZsGreen, and AAV2/9-Syn-m-Cend1 mut-3xflag-ZsGreen viruses were purchased from Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Viruses were stereotactically injected into the striatum (AP + 0.6 mm; ML + 2.0 mm; DV − 3.5 mm). On the 21st day after injection, all mice injected with virus were modeled by MCAO/R.

2, 3, 5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining and infarct volume assessment

The procedures used were as described previously. After 1 to 3 days passed since undergoing MCAO/R surgery, the mice were euthanized, and their brains were cut into six 1 mm-thick coronal slices. Sections were stained with 1% 2, 3, 5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min at 37 °C, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight. The images were taken by a digital camera, and the infarct volumes were blindly analyzed with AlphaEaseFC software (Alpha Innotech, USA). Infarct sizes were expressed as percentages of the contralateral structures.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The specific protocol used has been described previously20. Total RNA was extracted from the brain tissues. The RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) by the commercially-available kits (ABScript III RT Master Mix for qPCR, RK20428, ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd.). RT-PCR was conducted with 2× Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203, ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd.) on the 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystem, USA). Primer sequences are listed as follows: Cend1-F: ACCAGCCAAGGCAGATCCT; Cend1-R: GGTCAAGTTCTCACAAGGCCA; β-actin-F: GTGACGTTGACATCCGTAAAGA; β-actin-R: GCCGGACTCATCGTACTCC. Housekeeping gene expression of β-actin was used for normalization. Relative expression was determined by the 2 −ΔΔCT method21.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously22. Brain tissues or cultured cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (FD008, Fude Biological Technology) for conventional protein detection or in Dimer-Lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 150 mM NaCl) for detection of Cend1 disulfide-linked dimers. Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 r/min, 5 min, 4 °C), and protein concentrations were measured using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (40 μg/lane) were prepared in the appropriate sample buffer. For conventional protein detection, samples were mixed with SDS buffer and denatured by heating prior to separation by SDS-PAGE. For detection of Cend1 dimers, proteins extracted with Dimer-Lysis buffer were mixed with SDS-free buffer and not heated, and separated under native/non-denaturing PAGE conditions. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% nonfat milkat room temperature (approximately 25 °C), incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (approximately 25 °C). For normalization, housekeeping proteins (GAPDH, β-tubulin, or β-actin) were used as loading controls, with the specific marker selected depending on the experimental context. This ensured comparability within each set of experiments. Although total protein staining is considered a gold standard for normalization, this approach was not applied in the current study. ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for densitometric analysis of the protein bands. Protein levels were then quantified relative to the loading control.

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation was performed as described previously23. Proteins (500 μg in 500 μl) were pre-incubated with the protein A/G plus-agarose (Santa Cruz, sc-2003; 50 μL per 500 μg protein) for 2 h. After gently centrifuging, the samples were incubated with 5 μg Cend1 antibody, or IgG antibody (as a negative control) for 12 h at 4 °C. After the addition of protein A/G plus-agarose (50 μL per 500 μg protein) for an additional 1 h incubation, the agaroses were gently centrifuged and collected. After washing three times with lysis buffer (150 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 29 mM Tris, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mL 10% Tween-20, pH 8.0, ddH2O up to 50 mL), the agaroses were eluted by a sample buffer containing Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate. Samples were collected and heated at 95 °C for 15 min for further Western blot analysis.

Tissue immunofluorescence staining

Mouse brains were perfused with 4% PFA and post-fixed for 24 hours. Tissues were dehydrated in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions sequentially, each for three days, then embedded in OCT and sectioned at 20 μm. Target regions were identified according to a mouse brain atlas. Sections were washed in PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and blocked in 5% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. After PBS washes, sections were mounted in glycerol and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. Paraffin sections were baked at 70 °C for 1 hour, deparaffinized in xylene and graded ethanol, and subjected to citrate-based antigen retrieval before antibody staining.

Cell immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on coverslips, optionally coated with poly-L-lysine. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 minutes, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and blocked in 5% BSA for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Coverslips were mounted in glycerol and images captured with a fluorescence microscope.

Cell fixation and in Cell Western assay

Cell fixation and in Cell Western (ICW) assay were performed as described previously24. Treated N2a cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized by washing them 5 times with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Non-specific sites were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. Then the sample was incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the primary antibody solution was removed, and the samples were washed five times with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS. Then the samples were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. The secondary antibody solution was washed 5 times with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS. Finally, images were acquired with an Odyssey CLx Imager Studio 3.1 (Gene Company Limited, Nanjing).

Oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion in primary neurons

Primary cortical neurons were subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) to mimic ischemia/reperfusion injury in vitro. Briefly, neurons were incubated in glucose-free medium and placed in a hypoxic chamber (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) for 2 h to induce OGD, followed by reperfusion with normal culture medium under normoxic conditions for the indicated times.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA from mouse brain tissues was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq™ platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-controlled and trimmed using FastQC and Trimmomatic. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using HISAT2. Gene expression quantification and differential expression analysis were performed with StringTie and DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an absolute log₂ fold change (|log₂FC | ) > 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05. When the number of DEGs identified under these criteria was limited, a P-value ≤ 0.05 was used instead of p-value.

Mitochondrial isolation

The Mitochondrial Isolation Kit (SM0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was utilized to isolate mitochondria according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the separation was based on differential centrifugation. Isolated mitochondria were directly used for relevant experimental analysis. The purity of the isolated mitochondria was considered high, based on the manufacturer’s instructions and previous reports, with enrichment of mitochondrial markers and minimal cytosolic contamination25. The isolated mitochondria are assumed to retain functional integrity for the intended assays.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the Enhanced Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-1) (Beyotime, #C2003). Briefly, after treatment, 293 T cells were collected and resuspended in culture medium. Cells were incubated with JC-1 staining working solution (prepared by diluting JC-1 (200X) 1:200 in JC-1 staining buffer) at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer and resuspended in the same buffer for flow cytometric analysis. The fluorescence of JC-1 monomers (green; excitation/emission: 514/529 nm) and aggregates (red; excitation/emission: 585/590 nm) was detected using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCelestar). ΔΨm was assessed by the percentage of JC-1 monomers (green fluorescence), with an increase in green monomer percentage indicating mitochondrial depolarization. Cells treated with 10 µM CCCP for 20 min served as a positive control.

Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore opening assay

Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore (mPTP) opening was evaluated using the Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore Assay Kit (Beyotime, #C2009) based on the calcein–cobalt quenching method. After treatment, 293 T cells were collected and incubated with Calcein AM staining working solution (containing 1X Calcein AM and 1X CoCl₂) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were washed twice with assay buffer and further incubated in pre-warmed culture medium (without serum) for 30 min at 37 °C to ensure complete hydrolysis of Calcein AM. The cells were then washed, resuspended in assay buffer, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 525/30 nm FITC channel, BD FACSCelestar). A decrease in calcein fluorescence indicates increased mPTP opening. Ionomycin (0.5 μM) was used as a positive control. Data were analyzed using flow cytometry software (FlowJo 10.9).

ATP content assay

ATP levels in 293T cells and mouse brain tissues were determined using the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, #S0026). Cells or homogenized tissues were lysed in ATP detection lysis buffer on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. ATP concentrations were measured by mixing 20 µl of supernatant with 100 µl of ATP detection working solution (prepared by diluting ATP detection reagent 1:9 with ATP detection reagent diluent) in a luminometer tube. Chemiluminescence was recorded using a luminometer after a 2-second delay. A standard curve was generated using known ATP concentrations for quantification. ATP levels were normalized to total protein concentration determined by BCA assay (Beyotime, P0010S).

Measurement of mitochondrial complex I and V Activities

Activities of mitochondrial complex I and V in 293 T cells were measured using the Cell Mitochondrial Complex I Activity Assay Kit (Elabscience, #E-BC-K834-M) and Complex V Activity Assay Kit (Elabscience, #E-BC-K838-M), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected and lysed in extraction buffer containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were sonicated on ice (200 W, 5 s on/10 s off, 15 cycles) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 3 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for enzymatic activity assays. Complex I and V activities were determined by monitoring NADH oxidation at 340 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax M3). Total protein concentration was measured by BCA assay for normalization.

Rotarod test

The Rotarod test was employed to measure the motor coordination of mice after cerebral ischemia treatment. The diameter of the rotarod spindle was 3 cm. Mice were placed on an accelerated rotarod. The instrument was then accelerated from 8 to 40 rpm in 60 s and 40 rpm was maintained for a maximum of 300 s. The latency to fall (in seconds) was recorded before test mice were trained to learn to run.

Grip strength test

In order to measure the grasping strength of the forepaws, the grip strength test was performed on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 after MCAO/R surgery. A digital force gauge instrument (YLS-13A Instrutherm, Shanghai, China) was used. The forepaws were put on the metal bar attached to a strain gauge. Then the animal was gently pulled by the tail until it released the bar. The data collected from the three trials were recorded in grams, and the mean value for each animal was calculated.

Foot fault(s) test

The placement dysfunction of the forelimbs was tested using the foot fault(s) test. We performed behavioral tests on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 after MCAO/R surgery. Mice were put on an elevated grid surface and placed their paws on the wire while moving along the grid. Foot fault represented paws falling between grid bars. The number of foot fault(s) on both sides was recorded for 2 min and the difference of foot faults between each side was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism version 9.0 and presented as mean ± SEM. P < 0.05 was considered as a statistical significance. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. Two-way ANOVA with a post hoc test was used for differences among groups. The experiments were performed independently on three or more samples, which refer to individual animals in in vivo experiments and independent cultures in in vitro experiments.

Results

Cend1 protein levels increased in the brain after ischemic injury

Because mitochondrial functions are a determining factor for cell fate after cerebral ischemia, we first examined Cend1 protein levels in mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions isolated from mouse striatal tissue at 24 h after MCAO/R using a mitochondrial separation kit. Western blot analysis showed that Cend1 was more abundant in the mitochondrial fraction than in the cytosolic fraction, and its expression in mitochondria was significantly increased compared with the sham group (Fig. 1A, B). Although potential contamination from other cellular compartments cannot be entirely excluded with this approach, these data suggest that Cend1 is enriched in mitochondrial preparations and upregulated after ischemia-reperfusion. Based on this observation, we next assessed the temporal profile of Cend1 expression in ischemic brain tissue. After MCAO/R, Cend1 protein levels in the ischemic hemisphere increased significantly at 24 h, peaked at 72 h, and returned to baseline by 7 d (Fig. 1C, D). In contrast, after reanalysis of the original full-size blot images with uniform background correction and housekeeping normalization, Cend1 expression remained unchanged in the contralateral non-ischemic hemisphere (Fig. 1E, F). Similarly, Cend1 protein levels were also significantly increased at 24 h and peaked at 72 h in cultured primary neurons after OGD/R (Fig. 1G–H). Double-immunofluorescent staining confirmed the ischemic insult-induced increases in Cend1 protein levels in primary neurons after 24 h of OGD/R (Fig. 1I–J). Cend1 deficiency aggravated cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice

To explore whether Cend1 plays a critical role in cerebral ischemic injury, Cend1 knockout (KO) mice were generated by deleting exon 2 of the Cend1 gene using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Fig. S1A). Exon 2 contains the translation initiation site, and its deletion introduces a frameshift and premature stop codon, thereby ensuring a null allele and complete loss of functional Cend1 protein. Cend1 KO was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, PCR, and western blotting (Fig. S1B–D). Cend1 deficiency had no effects on the body weight and shape of mice (Fig. S1E, F). Wild-type (WT) mice and Cend1 KO mice were subjected to MCAO for 45 min and were re-perfused for 24 h (Fig. 2A). Neurological function was assessed using a 6-point scoring system, where higher scores indicate more severe deficits. At 24 h after MCAO/R, Cend1 KO mice displayed markedly higher neurological deficit scores compared with WT mice (Fig. 2B). Consistently, TTC staining revealed significantly larger infarct volumes in Cend1 KO mice (Fig. 2C–D), indicating that Cend1 deficiency exacerbates both functional impairment and cerebral infarction following ischemic injury. Finally, to further determine the role of Cend1 in ischemic stroke, the sensory and motor deficits in Cend1 KO and WT mice were evaluated by the rotarod test, grip strength test, and foot-fault test. Rotarod test showed that on days 3, 5, and 7 after MCAO/R, the times on the rotarod in Cend1 KO mice were shorter than that in WT mice (Fig. 2E). In the grip strength test, Cend1 KO mice had weaker forelimb strength than WT mice at days 3, 5 and 7 after MCAO/R (Fig. 2F). The foot-fault test showed that the number of foot-fault steps in Cend1 KO mice was higher than that in WT mice at days 3 and 5 after MCAO/R (Fig. 2G). However, there was no significant change in all behavioral tests between Cend1 KO and WT mice that did not undergo the MCAO/R. Cend1 regulated cellular ATP production through Cend1 dimers

High-throughput RNA sequencing was performed on Cend1 KO and WT mouse brains to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with Cend1 deficiency. The data showed that there were 83 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), as shown in Fig. S2A. Among them, 73 genes were significantly upregulated and 10 genes were significantly downregulated, with |log₂FC | > 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05 (Fig. S2A). The enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed that they were markedly enriched in mitochondrial organization and regulation of neuron apoptotic process (Fig. S2B). Interestingly, a Protein–Protein Interaction network was constructed, and key MCODE components were extracted; the most significant component was the ATP metabolic process (Fig. S2C, D). The results indicated that Cend1 KO may worsen brain injury by impaired mitochondrial functions and cellular ATP production.

To verify whether Cend1 KO worsened brain injury by impairing mitochondrial functions and cellular ATP production, total ATP levels in brain tissues of Cend1 KO and WT mice were measured using a commercial ATP detection kit. ATP levels were normalized to protein concentration in each sample, and the results are expressed as relative ATP content compared with the mean value of WT mice. Results showed that total ATP production significantly decreased in Cend1 KO mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 3A), indicating that Cend1 may regulate mitochondrial functions and cellular ATP production. By comparing the sequence of Cend1 protein in multiple organisms, the transmembrane domain of Cend1 was found to be highly conserved in humans, mice, and rats, containing two typical GXXXA motifs. Notably, similar GXXXA motifs were also identified in avian species such as chicken and canary, although their positions within the transmembrane region were slightly shifted, suggesting that this structural feature is evolutionarily conserved (Fig. S3A). To investigate the involvement of the GXXXA motif in the dimerization of Cend1, plasmids encoding wild-type (WT) and mutant forms of Cend1 were constructed (Fig. S3A; results shown in Fig. 3E). The mutant forms included: G130P and G141P, in which Glycine 130 and Glycine 141 were substituted with a proline, respectively, and ▴TM, in which residues 125-145 were deleted. These plasmids were transfected in N2a cells, a neuronal cell line with high transfection efficiency, to analyze the expression and molecular forms (monomers and dimers) of exogenous Cend1. Our results showed that ▴TM Cend1 plasmids-transfected cells almost had no Cend1 dimer bands, and G130P/G141P Cend1-transfected cells had a slight dimer band. On the contrary, ▴TM Cend1 plasmids-transfected cells had a thick monomer band compared with WT Cend1 plasmid-transfected cells (Fig. 3F). G130P was selected as a representative mutant because its effect on dimerization and protein stability was similar to other mutants (G141, G130P/G141P, ▴TM, Fig. 3F). HEK293T cells were chosen for these assays due to their high transfection efficiency and suitability for protein expression and mutational analyses, providing a robust system to evaluate Cend1 dimerization. These findings indicate that the transmembrane domain and the GXXXA motif are crucial for the dimerization of Cend1. More importantly, ATP production significantly decreased in G130P-transfected cells compared with WT plasmid-transfected cells (Fig. 3G). Therefore, our findings indicate that Cend1 can form dimers, which may be involved in ATP production and mitochondrial-related processes.

Cend1 interacted with the mitochondrial protein Atp5f1b after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury

To explore the underlying mechanisms of Cend1 in regulating mitochondrial functions and ATP production, immunoprecipitation combined with mass spectrometry (IP-MS) was performed to identify Cend1-interacting proteins in mitochondria by using Cend1 antibody and isolated protein lysate from WT and Cend1 KO mouse brain tissues (Fig. 7A). Mass spectrometry data revealed that there were many potential Cend1-interacting proteins that were enriched in different signaling pathways, including mitochondria-related signaling pathways (Fig. 7B). Compared with Cend1 KO samples, there were 69 potential Cend1-interacting proteins in WT mouse results. After comparing the 69 potential Cend1-interacting proteins with the reported mitochondrial database (MitoCarta 3.0), only eight mitochondrial proteins were identified, including ATP synthase β subunit (Atp5f1b, Fig. 7C). Because previous studies have demonstrated that Atp5f1b encodes the catalytic β subunit of ATP synthase, which is responsible for the synthesis of the majority of cellular ATP and has also been proposed as the putative mitochondrial permeability transition pore26, further validation was conducted to assess whether Cend1 dimers form a complex with the ATP synthase β subunit to influence mitochondrial functions. The expression and cellular localization of Atp5f1b proteins were not changed in Cend1 KO mice. (Fig. S5A, B). By co-transfecting 293T cells with Flag-Cend1 and EGFP-Atp5f1b plasmids (Fig. 7D), or EGFP-Cend1 and HA-Atp5f1b plasmids (Fig. 7E), the results showed that ATP synthase β subunit (Atp5f1b) and Cend1 co-immunoprecipitated. Similarly, Atp5f1b was a co-immunoprecipitated protein of Cend1 by using Cend1 antibody to precipitate endogenous Cend1 in brain tissues (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, immunoprecipitation assays in ischemic brain tissues using Cend1 antibodies showed a significant increase in relative Atp5f1b protein compared to sham controls (Fig. 7G). Quantitative analysis confirmed the increase of relative immunoprecipitated Atp5f1b with Cend1 in ischemic conditions (Fig. 7H). Since Cend1 provides neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia injuries, it is possible to identify small molecule compounds that increase cellular Cend1 for stroke treatment. Therefore, a small molecule compound library was screened using a cell-based assay (Fig. S6A). Interestingly, the small molecule compound TNT (Fig. 8A) was found to significantly increase cellular Cend1 protein level (Fig. S6B, C). TNT modestly but significantly increased the Cend1 protein level in primary neurons and N2a cells compared with vehicle treatment at a concentration of 10 μM (Figs. 8B, C and S6D, E). TNT treatment also significantly increased Cend1 dimers and monomer levels by Western blot under nonreducing conditions (Figs. 8D and S6F). However, TNT did not have any impact on Cend1 mRNA expression in cultured primary neurons (Fig. 8E) and N2a cells (Fig. S6G), suggesting that TNT increased Cend1 expression at protein levels. Cend1 dimers in 293 T cells that were exposed to cycloheximide had improved dimer stability after being treated with TNT, as examined under nonreducing Western blot conditions (Fig. S7A, B). In Cend1-G130P-transfected cells, TNT did not improve the stability of Cend1 monomeric mutants (Fig. S7C, D). Discussion

In this study, Cend1 was first determined to be predominantly localized within the mitochondria, where it plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial function. Cend1 dimerization was confirmed as essential for regulating ATP production, with two highly conserved GXXXA motifs in its transmembrane domain, specifically G130 and G141, serving as critical dimerization sites. More importantly, Cend1 was shown to interact with the mitochondrial protein Atp5f1b, which plays a significant role in ATP synthesis, further highlighting the importance of this interaction in regulating mitochondrial function. Subsequent screening of small molecular compounds identified TNT as a potential therapeutic agent targeting the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction, suggesting its effectiveness in treating ischemic stroke. Overall, these findings emphasize that modulating the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction to enhance ATP production could provide a promising therapeutic strategy for reducing ischemic injury and supporting recovery.

Cend1 is a neuron-specific protein that was initially discovered to be expressed in the striatum neurons of pigs but not in glial cells27. It is a type-I single transmembrane protein with a short tail consisting of three positively charged residues (RKK) at the C-terminal. This unique structure allows most proteins to face the cytoplasm, which has been proven to be essential for Cend1 to correctly target the outer mitochondrial membrane28. Through purification and characterization, it has been determined that Cend1 is an integral membrane protein, lacking glycosylation, and composed of two identical polypeptide chains connected by disulfide bonds13,29. In this study, it was demonstrated that the GXXXA motif serves as a key protein dimerization motif in the formation of Cend1 dimers. Notably, G130 was identified as the critical site for Cend1 dimerization using amino acid point mutation technology. Furthermore, overexpression of the Cend1 protein leads to an increase in intracellular ATP levels, indicating its role in regulating energy metabolism. However, the G130P mutant, which disrupts the dimerization, fails to regulate ATP levels. This highlights the importance of Cend1 dimerization for its function in mitochondria.

It is well-established that the dimerization of mitochondrial proteins is essential for their proper functioning. For example, CIDE-B induces apoptosis by targeting mitochondria and forming homo-dimeric or heterodimeric complexes30. Similarly, defects in the dimerization of FoF1-ATP synthase in myocardial cells of aging mice result in abnormal mitochondrial cristae tip curvature and impaired oxidative phosphorylation efficiency31. Consistent with this, our OGD/R experiments show that the neuroprotective effects of TNT, including preservation of ΔΨm and ATP levels, depend on Cend1, as these effects are lost when Cend1 is absent. Instead of focusing on the role of dimers in the maintenance of normal mitochondrial function, it was found that the stability of Cend1 dimers is significantly greater than that of Cend1 monomers. Interestingly, the G130P mutant disrupts the dimerization of Cend1 and promotes accelerated degradation of Cend1 monomers. This parallels findings in other studies, such as the acetylation-dependent degradation of monomeric FUNDC1 through the proteasome pathway upon its transition from a dimer to a monomer32. Therefore, the dimerization of Cend1 is crucial for its proper function in mitochondria and the regulation of energy metabolism. Exploring the complex mechanisms involved in Cend1 dimerization and its impact on cellular processes may contribute to a better understanding of mitochondrial biology and potential therapeutic targets for associated disorders.

Endogenous Cend1 levels rise during cerebral ischemia/reperfusion, reflecting an intrinsic compensatory response that is insufficient to prevent injury. Experimental overexpression produces higher and more sustained Cend1 expression, which enhances neuronal survival and reduces infarct size. Both loss- and gain-of-function experiments reported here support this interpretation, demonstrating that elevated Cend1 confers protection rather than harm. Previous work also found that Cend1 overexpression in neural progenitor cells promotes neuronal differentiation and limits astrocytic scar formation in cortical injury models33. Importantly, these protective effects depend on the ability of Cend1 to form stable dimers, as disruption of dimerization (e.g., G130P mutation) abolishes its regulation of ATP levels and compromises mitochondrial function. Collectively, these data indicate that ischemia-driven Cend1 upregulation is an endogenous rescue attempt that falls short of a protective threshold, whereas therapeutic augmentation of Cend1—particularly in its dimeric form—can push the response into a neuroprotective range and facilitate brain repair.

To further investigate how Cend1 regulates energy metabolism in mitochondria, IP-MS/MS was conducted and identified Atp5f1b as an essential subunit of the mitochondrial ATP synthase complex interacting with Cend134. The mitochondrial ATP synthase complex is responsible for synthesizing the primary energy currency, ATP35. Atp5f1b is encoded by a nuclear gene and is assembled with other subunits encoded by either mitochondrial or nuclear genes to form the ATP synthase complex34. This complex functions to convert ADP and inorganic phosphate into ATP by utilizing the proton gradient across the mitochondrial inner membrane36. Under the ischemic condition, Cend1 levels increase and associate with Atp5f1b to form a protein complex, whereas Cend1 deficiency leads to reduced ATP production. These findings suggest that the interaction between Cend1 and Atp5f1b may enhance or modulate ATP synthase efficiency and influence ATP production. The Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction may play a role in ATP synthesis by influencing the assembly and activity of the ATP synthase complex. Dysfunction of this interaction could impair oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), leading to reduced mitochondrial ATP production. These results offer insight into mitochondrial energy regulation and highlight potential therapeutic targets for neurological disorders associated with mitochondrial dysfunction.

Considering that the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction influences ATP production and may contribute to cellular protection in ischemic stroke, a small molecule compound was screened to increase Cend1 expression in cells using ICW technology. Among these small compounds, TNT significantly increased Cend1 expression in cells after treatment. TNT was previously reported as a tricyclic antidepressant, which regulates the level of neurotransmitters by increasing the uptake of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) in synaptic cleft for treatment37,38. The study demonstrated that TNT alleviated the injury caused by ischemic stroke. However, our data demonstrated that the neuroprotective effect of TNT was abolished when Cend1 was knocked out in mice. This indicates that the protection of TNT on ischemic cerebral injury was dependent on cellular Cend1. Further findings showed that TNT increased the interaction between Cend1 and Atp5f1b after ischemic injury. Given that Atp5f1b is a subunit of the ATP synthase complex and plays a critical role in ATP production34, the administration of TNT is expected to enhance ATP synthesis following cerebral ischemic injury, which our results confirmed. In OGD/R models, TNT treatment increased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), enhanced ATP synthase activity, and raised ATP levels, whereas its neuroprotective effect was abolished in the absence of Cend1. Therefore, targeting the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction using TNT could be a potential strategy for treating ischemic stroke. Further research is needed to investigate the specific regulatory effect of TNT on the Cend1/Atp5f1b interaction and to evaluate its safety and efficacy in clinical applications for ischemic stroke patients.

We verified fraction purity in Fig. 1A using Tom20 and β-actin, which showed minimal mitochondrial breakage, but minor contamination from other compartments cannot be fully excluded. Different internal controls were applied across experiments, which may lead to minor variability. Future studies will consider the use of both total protein normalization and compartment-specific markers to further enhance the reliability of Western blot analyses. Although we performed additional assays (ΔΨm, mPTP opening, ATP content, Complex I and V activities) to assess mitochondrial function, the mechanistic role of Cend1 in directly regulating ATP synthase activity versus its structural role in mPTP formation under ischemic conditions remains to be clarified. Future studies will focus on these aspects to comprehensively elucidate Cend1’s role in post-injury brain repair and recovery.

In this study, it was demonstrated for the first time that dimerized Cend1 proteins regulate mitochondrial functions through interaction with Atp5f1b following cerebral ischemic injury. This interaction results in increased intracellular ATP levels and enhanced mitochondrial function. Additionally, small molecular compounds such as TNT were identified to elevate Cend1 expression in cells, indicating their potential as therapeutic agents for ischemic stroke (Fig. 10).
