Exercise intervention for children with acute leukemia: a best evidence summary
Authors: Wei Zhou, Long Li, Jingjing Ma, Junxia Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute leukemia (AL) is the most common malignancy in childhood. Despite significant improvements in survival rates, long-term high-intensity chemotherapy often leads to severe physical dysfunction and cancer-related fatigue (CRF) in pediatric patients. Although exercise intervention is a significant non-pharmacological physical therapy for improving patient prognosis, clinical practice still lacks unified, evidence-based standardized guidance.ObjectiveTo systematically retrieve, evaluate, and synthesize evidence regarding exercise interventions for children with AL, providing a decision-making reference for multidisciplinary clinical teams to develop standardized and individualized exercise rehabilitation programs.MethodsGuided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) evidence-based practice framework, a comprehensive search was conducted in domestic and international databases, including UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, JBI, PubMed, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang, to identify clinical decision supports, guidelines, expert consensuses, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses related to exercise interventions for pediatric AL. The search period spanned from database inception to January 2026. Methodological quality appraisal and data extraction were performed independently by two researchers.ResultsEighteen publications were included, consisting of 2 clinical practice guidelines and 16 systematic reviews. Seventeen items of best evidence were extracted, covering eight dimensions: intervention principles, safety, exercise modalities, intensity, frequency and timing, stage-specific strategies, intervention settings, and outcome improvement.ConclusionChildren with acute leukemia should engage in long-term, individualized, multimodal exercise interventions under professional supervision. Healthcare professionals should dynamically adjust exercise protocols based on the specific treatment phase. While ensuring safety, incorporating game-based formats can enhance patient adherence, thereby effectively improving bone mineral density, cardiorespiratory endurance, and quality of life.Systematic Review Registrationhttps://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/view/CRD420261341113/1/0, PROSPERO CRD420261341113.

Introduction

Acute leukemia (AL) is the most prevalent malignancy in childhood (1), with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) exhibiting the highest incidence, accounting for approximately 70% of all pediatric leukemia cases (2). In recent years, continuous advancements in medical technology and the implementation of multicenter clinical trials have significantly enhanced the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric AL, with the 5-year survival rate for ALL reaching 80%–90% (2). Consequently, the focus of AL research has shifted from solely improving survival rates to maintaining and restoring physical activity levels, enhancing long-term quality of life, and facilitating successful reintegration into society (3). Despite substantial improvements in survival outcomes, long-term high-intensity treatments, primarily chemotherapy, impose a heavy physiological burden on children. Prolonged pharmacological therapy and prolonged bed rest during hospitalization frequently lead to organ dysfunction, obesity, osteoporosis, and severe cancer-related fatigue (CRF), resulting in significant physical mobility impairments (4).

Studies indicate that children with leukemia undergoing induction or consolidation chemotherapy exhibit poorer gross and fine motor functions compared to age-matched, typically developing peers (5). During chemotherapy, patients experience significant declines in quadriceps strength, functional mobility, and cardiorespiratory endurance, reaching a nadir following induction therapy; this underscores the critical importance of early rehabilitation interventions (6). As a safe and effective non-pharmacological approach, exercise intervention is highly regarded in pediatric oncology rehabilitation. Supervised hospital-based exercise programs are not only safe but also provide short-term cardiopulmonary protection and help reduce the length of hospital stays, thereby alleviating the economic burden on families (7). However, in clinical practice, exercise among children with leukemia during treatment is generally characterized by low volume, low intensity, short duration, and limited variety. A lack of systematic physical exercise may lead to a continuous deterioration of motor skills during the treatment period (8).

Currently, while international systematic research on pediatric AL is increasing, a scientific and reliable standardized exercise intervention protocol has yet to be established. In clinical practice, multidisciplinary teams still lack unified evidence-based guidance regarding the timing of interventions and the formulation of stage-specific exercise plans. Therefore, this study utilizes a Best Evidence Summary (BES) design to systematically retrieve and synthesize relevant global literature, aiming to provide a scientific reference for multidisciplinary teams to implement standardized clinical rehabilitation practices.

Methods

This review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD420261341113).

Study design

This study was designed as a Best Evidence Summary based on the JBI methodology for evidence-based practice. A Best Evidence Summary aims to extract actionable clinical recommendations by integrating pre-appraised, high-level evidence. According to the “6S” evidence pyramid model, clinical guidelines and systematic reviews were combined in this study as they represent the most robust sources (summaries and syntheses) to guide clinical decision-making for frontline healthcare professionals. The reporting of this study's search and screening process was guided by the PRISMA statement.

Literature screening

The retrieved literature was imported into EndNote 21 reference management software to identify and remove duplicate records. Two researchers, both systematically trained in evidence-based medicine, independently performed the literature screening based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The initial screening involved reviewing titles and abstracts to exclude studies that were clearly irrelevant or did not align with the research design. Subsequently, a full-text review was conducted for the remaining articles during the secondary screening stage to determine the final inclusion. Upon completion of the screening process, the two researchers cross-checked their results. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third expert to reach a consensus

Quality appraisal of the literature

Two researchers, both systematically trained in evidence-based medicine, independently evaluated the quality of the included guidelines and expert consensuses using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) instrument (9). This tool comprises 23 items across six domains: scope and purpose, stakeholder involvement, rigor of development, clarity of presentation, applicability, and editorial independence. Each item is scored on a scale from 1 to 7. The standardized percentage score for each domain was calculated as follows:StandardizedDomainScore=ObtainedScore−MinimumPossibleScoreMaximumPossibleScore−MinimumPossibleScore×100%Based on these domain scores, guidelines were categorized into three grades: A (Strongly Recommended), B (Weakly Recommended), and C (Not Recommended).

For systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tool (2016 edition) was utilized (10). This checklist consists of 11 items, with response options of “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable”.

Data extraction and synthesis

Two researchers, both systematically trained in evidence-based medicine, performed data extraction from the included literature. The extracted information encompassed the year of publication, country of origin, study design, target population, specific intervention details (exercise frequency, intensity, time, and type), outcome measures, and primary conclusions.

Evidence was graded using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Levels (2014 edition) (10). The recommendation levels—Grade A (Strong Recommendation) or Grade B (Weak Recommendation)—were determined based on a comprehensive assessment of the evidence's feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness (FAME).

To ensure the precise applicability of the evidence, the specific target populations of the source literature were strictly evaluated. Evidence derived exclusively from acute leukemia (AL) cohorts was classified as direct evidence. Evidence derived from broader pediatric oncology populations or mixed cancer cohorts was classified as indirect evidence. In accordance with the JBI FAME framework, extrapolating findings from mixed populations to AL patients compromises the “Appropriateness” and “Feasibility” components due to differing treatment toxicities. Consequently, the strength of recommendations for all indirect evidence items was systematically downgraded. Every synthesized evidence item is explicitly labeled as direct or indirect to ensure complete transparency for clinical end-users.

Furthermore, a targeted assessment of the primary studies within the included systematic reviews revealed substantial principal overlap. A core group of foundational primary trials was repeatedly cited across multiple reviews, which could artificially inflate the perceived consistency of the evidence. This structural overlap was explicitly factored into the evidence grading process. The recognition that some highly consistent conclusions were driven by the repeated inclusion of the same trials, rather than genuinely independent evidence, served as an additional justification for adopting a conservative grading approach and downgrading the recommendation strengths where appropriate.

A total of 1,550 records were initially identified through systematic searches across major guideline websites and electronic databases. After removing duplicates and conducting an initial screening of titles and abstracts, 33 articles remained for full-text eligibility assessment. Following a rigorous secondary screening process, 18 moderate-to-high quality publications were ultimately included for evidence synthesis. Discussion

Based on the “6S” evidence pyramid model, this study systematically synthesized 17 best evidence items regarding exercise interventions for children with acute leukemia (AL), encompassing eight core dimensions: intervention principles, safety, exercise modalities, intensity, frequency and timing, stage-specific strategies, intervention settings, and outcome improvement. It is imperative to clarify that these 17 evidence items and their assigned recommendation grades were derived strictly and exclusively from the 18 formally appraised secondary studies (clinical guidelines and systematic reviews). To better contextualize these findings, explain potential physiological mechanisms, and offer specific examples of clinical implementation, additional primary studies (e.g., individual clinical trials or observational studies) have been cited throughout this discussion. These supplementary publications serve purely as contextual references and were not part of the predefined JBI evidence grading process. Traditional clinical nursing and home care perspectives often advocate for “absolute bed rest” to conserve energy and prevent complications such as infection or bleeding (27). However, the evidence from this study suggests a necessary paradigm shift: physical activity and exercise are generally safe and beneficial for the vast majority of clinically stable children and adolescents affected by cancer. Consequently, “staying active” should be encouraged throughout the entire treatment continuum (7, 26, 28).When implemented under rigorous assessment and professional supervision, exercise programs rarely lead to fractures or serious cardiovascular events (11, 25, 28). According to the synthesized evidence, the most frequently reported adverse events are typically mild and self-limiting, including transient fatigue, delayed-onset muscle soreness, and minor joint pain. However, it is imperative to note that the absence of reported severe adverse events in some studies must not be misinterpreted as absolute proof of safety. The overall safety of an exercise program is highly conditional; it relies on meticulous patient selection, continuous monitoring of clinical status (e.g., fluctuating platelet counts or anemia), appropriate exercise intensity, and treatment-related complications. Crucially, exercise intervention does not exert a negative suppressive effect on the fragile immune systems of these children; for instance, it does not reduce white blood cell or platelet counts. On the contrary, moderate exercise can enhance the cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells (12, 29). However, extrapolating this biological enhancement directly to hard clinical outcomes—such as reduced infection risk and shorter hospital stays—requires extreme caution, as the direct causal pathway remains to be fully validated by clinical trials. Despite identifying the general types of adverse events, a major limitation of the current evidence base is the lack of quantitative safety data. It was not possible to quantitatively report the exact number of primary studies that systematically monitored adverse events, the total pool of participants assessed for safety, the precise incidence rates of specific adverse events, or the exact frequency of exercise-related discontinuations. The included secondary source literature—comprising systematic reviews and guidelines—predominantly provided narrative summaries of safety without standardizing adverse-event definitions or conducting quantitative meta-analyses of safety outcomes. Consequently, safety must always be dynamically managed. Therefore, healthcare professionals must transform their clinical approach by introducing exercise interventions early after treatment initiation, once the patient is clinically stable. This proactive strategy is essential to seize the critical “window of opportunity” for preventing complications such as bone loss and should extend into long-term rehabilitation follow-up post-treatment (30).

The cognitive developmental stages and psychological characteristics of children often lead to notoriously poor compliance with traditional, repetitive rehabilitation exercises (31). Evidence from this study indicates that monomodal exercise interventions yield limited efficacy. Instead, a multimodal approach is recommended, encompassing aerobic exercise to enhance cardiorespiratory endurance, resistance training to counteract muscle atrophy, and flexibility training to maintain range of motion (ROM) (11, 23, 32). To improve clinical feasibility and patient adherence, the evidence strongly suggests incorporating game-based exercise formats (33). By integrating monotonous resistance and aerobic tasks into activities such as tossing games, obstacle courses, or interactive exergaming, clinicians can effectively facilitate cognitive distraction from chemotherapy-related side effects. This approach significantly boosts intrinsic motivation and engagement while mitigating negative emotional experiences (23, 34). Furthermore, for severe cancer-related fatigue (CRF), physical intervention alone often falls short of optimal outcomes (22, 35). The evidence recommends combining physical activity with psychological support (22), such as relaxation techniques and mindfulness training. Such multimodal strategies provide comprehensive rehabilitation support by addressing both physiological and psychological dimensions.

The treatment cycle for acute leukemia is prolonged, and the toxicities and adverse effects vary significantly across different stages (36); Individualized FITT parameters (e.g., 2–3 moderate sessions/week) require dynamic adjustments. Crucially, strict termination criteria—including active fever (>38 °C), severe anemia, or profound thrombocytopenia—must dictate the temporary withholding of exercise to ensure safety (25, 37). Furthermore, because the majority of current evidence is derived from acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cohorts, multidisciplinary teams must exercise additional caution and clinical judgment when applying these interventions to patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), who often undergo more intensively myelosuppressive regimens. Evidence suggests that during induction remission (primarily reflecting ALL regimens), high-dose chemotherapy causes extreme weakness and myelosuppression (19, 38). Consequently, the primary objective should focus on alleviating fatigue and maintaining basic activities of daily living (ADL) rather than high-intensity enhancement, with careful protocol-specific adaptations for AML. As patients transition into the maintenance therapy phase, their condition becomes relatively stable. During this period, intervention goals should shift toward comprehensively improving range of motion (ROM), enhancing muscle strength, increasing bone mineral density, and boosting aerobic capacity to prepare the child for reintegration into school and society (39). In terms of setting, utilizing limited hospital space for bedside strength training, indoor walking, and stretching is generally practical during inpatient stays (40). Once patients enter the maintenance phase or are discharged, home-based exercise programs—implemented through remote guidance, wearable health monitoring devices, or regular outpatient follow-ups—demonstrate acceptable feasibility. These programs not only significantly improve various health biomarkers but also alleviate the time and economic burden associated with frequent hospital visits (13, 41).

Conclusion and limitations

Conclusion

Exercise intervention for children with acute leukemia should adhere to the fundamental principles of being long-term, continuous, and individualized, and must be conducted under professional supervision. Interventions should span the entire treatment continuum, utilizing a multimodal approach that integrates aerobic, resistance, and flexibility training with psychological adjustment. Provided that safety is rigorously ensured, exercise contributes to potentially supporting immune function, alleviating cancer-related fatigue (CRF), maintaining or improving bone mineral density (BMD), and enhancing muscle strength, cardiorespiratory endurance, and overall quality of life. Implementation strategies must be dynamically adjusted according to the treatment phase, and the use of game-based formats is encouraged to improve adherence. In summary, while current evidence limitations (such as indirectness and overlapping data) warrant careful clinical application, systematic and scientific exercise intervention constitutes a highly valuable and promising non-pharmacological component of comprehensive supportive care for pediatric patients with acute leukemia, though specific adaptations for non-ALL subtypes, such as AML, require further empirical validation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the synthesized evidence predominantly focuses on children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), while exercise intervention strategies for other subtypes, such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML), remain relatively scarce. Second, significant heterogeneity exists across secondary sources regarding populations, interventions, and measurement tools. Consequently, the volume, quality, and directness of evidence vary considerably among specific outcomes (e.g., fatigue versus immune function), meaning the overall degree of clinical certainty is not uniform across all synthesized statements. Third, although we qualitatively synthesized the evidence and identified the principal overlap of foundational trials, a formal structured assessment to quantify the exact degree of overlap—such as generating a comprehensive citation matrix or calculating the Corrected Covered Area (CCA)—was not performed due to the scope of this secondary synthesis. Consequently, it remains a limitation that readers cannot fully determine whether all apparently consistent conclusions reflect genuinely independent evidence or the repeated inclusion of the same primary trials. Fourth, some extracted recommendations are derived from evidence based on mixed pediatric oncology populations rather than strictly AL cohorts. Because many of these source reviews did not report specific sub-population data, we were unable to quantify the exact proportion of AL patients within this indirect evidence. Finally, the reliance on secondary literature prevented a quantitative synthesis of safety metrics. The source systematic reviews largely lacked standardized reporting of precise adverse event frequencies and exercise-related dropout rates, leaving a critical gap in precisely quantifying intervention risks. Future research should prioritize multicenter clinical trials to further validate and refine the evidence summarized in this study.
