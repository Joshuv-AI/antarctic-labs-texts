Different plasma exosome isolation methods generated distinct microRNA and protein profiles in healthy controls and patients with advanced prostate and lung cancer
Authors: Kapil K. Avasthi, Brandon J. Manley, Bruna Pellini, Jingsong Zhang, Amanda M. Bloomer, Esther Jean-Baptiste, Douglas C. Marchion, Bin Fang, John M. Koomen, Erin M. Siegel, Liang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExosomes, extracellular vesicles pivotal in cancer intercellular communication, encapsulate biomolecules with potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Efficient isolation is essential for accurate molecular profiling. This study compares three exosome isolation methods—size exclusion chromatography (SEC), lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding—for proteomic and miRNA analysis of plasma exosomes in cancer.MethodsPlasma exosomes from patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC, N = 22), castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC, N = 7), and healthy controls (N = 15) were analyzed. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry profiled exosomal proteins, and small RNA sequencing identified miRNAs.ResultsSEC, lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods identified 122, 153, and 87 proteins, and 335, 89, and 181 miRNAs, respectively. SEC detected the most unique miRNAs (183), while lectin-binding excelled in unique protein detection (56). In CRPC, 69 proteins and 21 miRNAs differed significantly (p < 0.05) from controls, with SEC identifying 11 proteins and 6 miRNAs, lectin-binding detecting 40 proteins and 1 miRNA, and TIM4-binding revealing 18 proteins and 14 miRNAs. In NSCLC, 33 proteins and 15 miRNAs showed differential expression (p < 0.05), with SEC detecting 14 proteins and 3 miRNAs, lectin-binding identifying 2 proteins, and TIM4-binding uncovering 17 proteins and 12 miRNAs.ConclusionsThe choice of exosome isolation method profoundly influences molecular profiling, with SEC optimizing miRNA detection, lectin-binding enhancing protein capture, and TIM4-binding enriching cancer-specific miRNAs. These findings underscore the need for tailored isolation strategies to unlock exosomes’ potential as precise, multi-omic biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15492-z.

Introduction

Liquid biopsies have become an essential tool for molecular diagnosis, and more recently, for monitoring treatment response and minimal residual disease in various solid tumors [1–3]. These techniques offer a minimally invasive approach to utilizing biofluids like blood or urine for comprehensive biomarker analysis [4]. Beyond circulating tumor cells, liquid biopsies can assess a wide range of circulating molecules, including cell-free DNA, cell-free RNA, and other freely circulating molecules, as well as circulating extracellular microvesicles such as exosomes [5, 6].

Exosomes are tiny membrane-bound vesicles (30–150 nm) that have been shown to play roles in numerous biological processes and intercellular communication [7, 8]. They are a type of extracellular vesicles formed through endocytosis [9] and act as key regulators of cellular signaling. Studies have shown that exosomes can coordinate various autocrine and paracrine activities to influence the tumor microenvironment, growth, and progression [10]. The exosome cargo and lipid bilayer, including proteins, miRNA, lipids, and metabolites, reflect the characteristics of their cell of origin, making them a prime source material for liquid biopsies in oncology research. Tumor-derived exosomes safeguard RNA, DNA, proteins, lipids, and metabolites within a protective environment while reflect the tumor’s heterogeneity [6, 11, 12]. Recent studies have shown that cancer patients have higher plasma exosome counts than healthy individuals [13, 14].

Several techniques are available for isolating exosomes from plasma samples, including ultracentrifugation, size exclusion chromatography (SEC), and affinity binding [7, 15, 16]. SEC is the simplest approach, involving passing the liquid sample through a column to collect exosomes for subsequent analysis. Ultracentrifugation, which is more time-intensive, requires spinning the sample at 100,000× g for several hours [17]. Other methods target specific molecular characteristics of exosomes to isolate subpopulations, such as using lectins to bind surface carbohydrates or TIM4 to bind phosphatidylserine on cell membranes [7, 15]. Due to the heterogeneity of exosomes, the selection of isolation method can significantly bias the enrichment toward particular subsets of exosome populations.

Studies have demonstrated that different cancer subtypes generate unique exosome signatures [18, 19]. Although exosomal proteins and miRNAs have been investigated as biomarkers [20–24], the best method for exosome isolation that offers an enrichment of cancer-specific exosomal contents is still undetermined. To assess commercially available isolation methods that might enhance cancer-related exosome cargos, we conducted proteomic and miRNA transcriptomic profiling of circulating exosomes using mass spectrometry and sequencing techniques. We compared the differences of protein and miRNA profiles in exosome cargos derived from three exosome isolation methods. We also compared the differences of exosomal protein and miRNA profiles across the three methods between healthy controls and cancer patients. We showed that various exosome isolation methods produce distinct exosome populations, each with unique protein and miRNA contents. Therefore, careful consideration is required when choosing an exosome isolation method for biomarker studies.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and plasma sample preparation

Study participants were enrolled in the Total Cancer Care protocol at Moffitt Cancer Center’s Institutional biorepository (MCC#14690; Advarra IRB Pro00014441). The age of participants ranged between 60 and 85 years. Blood samples (~ 10 mL) were collected in EDTA K2 vacutainers from patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (N = 22) and castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) (N = 7). To increase the likelihood of capturing cancer-specific exosomes associated with high tumor burden, all patients were in the advanced stages. Samples from these patients were collected either before the initiation of systemic treatment or following a two-week washout period during treatment transitions. A group of age-matched controls (N = 15) was also included in the study (Supplementary Data S1). These healthy controls had no history of cancer diagnosis or other diseases. Within 45 min of collection, blood samples were centrifuged at 1300× g for 10 min at room temperature (RT), and the resulting plasma was transferred to a new 10 mL centrifuge tube. The plasma was then subjected to a second centrifugation at 5000× g for 10 min at RT to produce platelet-poor plasma. Aliquots of 500 µL plasma were immediately stored at − 80 °C until the exosome processing stage.

Plasma exosome isolation

Exosome extraction was conducted using the following kits: (1) SBI SmartSEC™ Single for EV Isolation™ (Palo Alto, CA, USA; cat# SSEC200A-1), (2) Takara Capturem™ Extracellular Vesicle Isolation Kit (Mini) (Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan; cat# 635741), and (3) Fujifilm Wako MagCapture™ Exosome Isolation Kit PS (Chuo-Ku, Osaka, Japan; cat# 295-74001). Each kit utilizes a different method for isolating exosomes from patient plasma. The SBI SmartSEC™ uses size exclusion chromatography (SEC), the Takara Capturem™ uses columns with lectin for exosome selection (lectin-binding), and the Wako MagCapture™ relies on T-cell immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain-containing protein 4 (TIM4) to selectively bind to exosomes (TIM4-binding). The preparation process for all kits involved thawing plasma aliquots (a total of 1250 µL per patient) at RT, transferring them to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes, and centrifuging at 10,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C to eliminate large vesicles and cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was then utilized for exosome extraction, following the respective manufacturer protocols for each of the three methods. For the SEC method, 250 µL of plasma was used for exosome extraction, following the manufacturer’s instructions. In the lectin-binding method, 500 µL of plasma was used, and the samples were centrifuged through a 100 kDa Amicon filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The retentate (the volume remaining above the filter) was then used for exosome isolation. In the TIM4-binding method, 500 µL of plasma was used, and two binding–elution cycles were performed. Plasma samples were randomized before processing to minimize batch effects. Subsequently, exosome elutions from each method were divided into two fractions for transcriptomics and proteomics analyses. We strictly followed manufacturers-recommended protocols for exosome isolation and did not apply any additional steps to remove lipoproteins and protein aggregates.

Our previous publication evaluated the exosome yield of the three methods. We showed that NnanoSight analysis generate significant variability in exosome concentrations across methods. SEC yielded the highest concentration (5.48 × 10¹² particles/mL), followed by lectin-binding (1.03 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL), and TIM4-binding (2.1 × 10⁹ particles/mL), confirming that isolation method influences exosome yield. Our previous study demonstrated variations in exosome yields among the three isolation methods, with SEC producing the highest yield and TIM4-binding the lowest [25]. Mass spectrometry sample preparation

Exosome samples were treated with 200 µL of urea lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 9 M urea). The samples were then sonicated using a BioRuptor for 10 cycles. After sonication, the lysate was chilled on ice for 15 min and subsequently centrifuged at 20,000 x g for 15 min at 15 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube. Reduction was performed using 4.5 mM dithiothreitol, followed by alkylation with 11 mM iodoacetamide. The samples were then diluted fourfold with 60 µL of 10x HEPES and 500 µL of water to a final volume of 800 µL. Digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C with 5 µg of trypsin. An additional 5 µg of trypsin was added the following day for a further 2-hour digestion. The digestion process was halted by acidifying the samples to 1% TFA, followed by C18 desalting using a Thermo SOLAu plate (Catalog number: 60209-001). The desalted peptides were quantified using a Pierce peptide Quantitative assay (Catalog number: 23275). Finally, 100 ng of peptides were vacuum centrifuged to dryness, resuspended in 30 µL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and loaded onto EvoSep EvoTip Pure tips in preparation for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

For LC-MS/MS, a nanoflow ultra-high performance liquid chromatograph (EvoSep) coupled with a nano electrospray orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q-Exactive plus) was employed. Samples were loaded onto an EvoTip Pure (EV2013), and trapped peptides were eluted onto the analytical column (EV1106, 15 cm length x 150 μm ID, 1.9 μm particle size). A factory default extended gradient of 88 min was used, with solvent A (water + 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid). The spray voltage was set to 1900 V, capillary temperature to 275 °C, and S lens RF level to 50. Data-dependent acquisition was performed using the Top16 precursors method. The resolution was set to 70,000 for MS and 17,500 for MS/MS. Dynamic exclusion was applied for 15 s for previously sampled peaks. Database searching was conducted using MaxQuant (version 1.6.14.0). The enzyme was set to Trypsin, allowing for up to 2 missed cleavages. The precursor mass tolerance was set at 10 ppm, and the fragment mass tolerance was set at 0.05 Da. The SwissProt Human 2023_03 protein database was used, with variable modifications, including carbamidomethylation of cysteine and oxidation of methionine.

Plasma exosomal miRNA extraction and library preparation

MiRNA extraction from exosome samples was performed using the miRNeasy Kit from Qiagen following the manufacturer’s instructions (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA; cat# 217084). The elution step involved using 15 µL of RNAse-free water. Quantification of miRNAs was accomplished with the Quantus instrument using the QuantiFluor® RNA System from Promega. miRNA libraries were generated using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA; cat#331502), with 5 µl of total RNA used for library preparation. The process began with the ligation of 3’ and 5’ adapters to the miRNAs. Complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries were then constructed through reverse transcription, followed by 22 cycles of PCR amplification. The libraries were subsequently cleaned using QMN beads. The concentration of the prepared libraries was quantified with the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer using the Qubit™ dsDNA Quantification Assay Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Middletown, VA, USA, cat#Q32851). Library quality was assessed using the Agilent High Sensitivity DS1000 method on the Agilent 2200 TapeStation (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA, cat#5067–5585). Finally, the 24 libraries were pooled in an equimolar ratio.

miRNA-seq and analysis

A pool of 24 libraries was sequenced adhering to the guidelines outlined in the NextSeq System - Denature and Dilute Libraries Guide. To increase sequence library diversity, 1% PhiX Control v3 was added into all pools. The sequencing was performed with a 75 bp single read using NextSeq 500/550 High Output v2.5 kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA, cat#20024906). Prior to alignment, fastQC (version 0.11.9) was used for quality control of the sequencing data. Adapter removal was carried out using cutadapt (version 3.3). The adapter-trimmed small RNA sequencing reads were then mapped against the miRBase database (version 21) utilizing the DNAStar tool (version 3.2).

Normalization and principal component analysis (PCA)

For proteomics, LC-MS/MS data were normalized using iterative rank-order normalization (IRON) [26]. Data analysis was performed using R V4.0.3 (CRAN), excluding proteomic data present in less than 75% of samples. miRNA-seq data were normalized to reads per 10 K and then converted to log2 scale. Outlier samples were identified and removed from miRNA-seq data through PCA. miRNAs with a median read count of ≥ 8 were used for further analysis. Differential expression analysis between cases and controls was performed using the Student’s t-test method.

Gene ontology and KEGG-pathway enrichment analysis

Target genes for the unique miRNAs were predicted using the miRSystem RNA database (http://mirsystem.cgm.ntu.edu.tw/) [27]. This database integrates seven miRNA-target prediction programs, including DIANA, miRanda, miRBridge, PicTar, PITA, rna22, and TargetScan. Only genes predicted by at least three programs were considered for further analysis to ensure high confidence in the results. To explore the functional relevance of the miRNA predicted target genes and unique proteins, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the PANTHER database (http://pantherdb.org/). Enriched GO terms were categorized into biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the KEGG and BioCarta pathways via the miRSystem database, which integrates data from multiple pathway analysis tools, including KEGG (http://www.kegg.jp/) [28], BioCarta (http://cgap.nci.nih.gov/Pathways/BioCarta_Pathways), Reactome (http://www.reactome.org/), and the Pathway Interaction Database (http://www.ndexbio.org/). The significance of enriched pathways was evaluated to identify the key biological mechanisms associated with the identified miRNAs and proteins.

Exosome enrichment index

In a previous study, we introduced the Exosome Enrichment Index (EEI) method to assess the enrichment potential of tumor-associated exosomes, as described in Soupir et al. [25]. To construct an exosome enrichment index (EEI), log2 transformed p-values from the student’s t-test were used. If a molecule was more abundant in cancer patients, it was considered positively enriched in cancer cells and the log2 p-value was converted to a positive value. Otherwise, the log2 p-value remained negative. The sum of top 50 most significant molecules was used to create an EEI that considers both statistical significance and the proportion of samples. A higher EEI indicates a more significant enrichment of cancer-related exosomes, while a negative EEI indicates a greater enrichment of exosomes specific to control samples. To ensure consistency, values were normalized to 100 molecules, accounting for the potential identification of a low number of molecules.

Exosome protein and miRNA validation

Identified miRNAs and proteins were subsequently validated against publicly available extracellular vesicle databases, including ExoCarta and Vesiclepedia, to confirm their presence in EVs and establish potential cancer-specific relevance. Differential expression analysis between patient groups and controls was conducted to highlight candidate biomarkers (Supplementary data S8-9 for detail).

Results

Exosome concentrations and size distributions across different isolation methods

As previously discussed in our earlier paper Soupir et al. 2023 [25], NanoSight analysis was performed on pooled plasma from healthy controls to characterize the concentration and size distribution of exosomes isolated using different methods. Exosome concentrations were measured across three methods (in duplicate), with values ranging from 2.1 × 10^9 particles/mL using TIM4 binding to 5.48 × 10^12 particles/mL using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). The SEC elution showed a single peak at 201 nm with a 1:75 dilution. Both lectin-binding and TIM4-binding methods also produced single peak size distributions. The particle concentration following lectin-binding exosome extraction was 1.03 × 10^10 particles/mL, approximately five times higher than that of TIM4-binding elutions.

Data quality controls and outlier removal

We performed PCA to identify potential batch effects caused during exosome isolation, protein and miRNA extraction, and library preparation. The overall PCA plots of proteomic data and miRNA profiles across SEC, Lectin-Binding and TIM-binding methods showed distinct clusters for the three isolation methods. For proteomic data (Supplementary Fig. 1A), samples were clustered into three groups, which corresponded to three exosome isolation methods. For miRNA data, however, PCA analysis demonstrated a partial overlap of SEC-based samples with those from TIM4-binding method (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To avoid confounding effects of these outliers, we subsequently removed these samples including 23 samples from SEC method, 1 sample from lectin-binding method and 5 samples from TIM4-binding method. After excluding these outliers, our PCA plot showed clean separation (Supplementary Fig. 1C), which included 23 samples from SEC, 43 samples from lectin-binding and 39 samples from TIM4-binding. More specifically, the final miRNA profiling analysis used the clean dataset, which included 10 NSCLC patients, 4 CRPC patients, and 9 healthy controls for the SEC method, 21 NSCLC patients, 7 CRPC patients, and 15 healthy controls for the Lectin-binding method, and 18 NSCLC patients, 7 CRPC patients, and 14 healthy controls for the TIM4-binding method. The heatmap and hierarchical clustering demonstrated that proteins and miRNAs profiles highlighting method-specific expression patterns (Figs. 2 and 3).

Unique proteins and miRNAs detected by different exosome isolation methods

The total number of proteins and miRNAs detected varied significantly across the three exosome isolation methods in all tested samples. For proteomic analysis, the SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods identified 122, 153, and 87 total proteins that were present in at least 75% of samples (Fig. 4A and Supplementary data S2-4 for detail), respectively. By comparing these detectable proteins among different methods, the SEC method identified 35 unique proteins, the Lectin-binding method identified 56 unique proteins, and the TIM4 method identified 25 unique proteins. Additionally, 44 proteins were commonly detected across all methods. Similarly, for miRNA analysis, the SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods identified 335, 89 and 181 miRNAs (Fig. 4B and Supplementary data S5-7 for detail), respectively, all of which showed a median read count of 8 or higher in raw reads. The SEC method detected 183 unique miRNAs, the Lectin-binding method detected 2 unique miRNAs, and the TIM4-binding method detected 24 unique miRNAs. Furthermore, 80 miRNAs were commonly detected across all methods.

GO analysis identified functional enrichment of exosomal proteins and miRNAs

To investigate the functional relevance of exosomal proteins and miRNAs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. We classified the proteins and miRNAs from different exosome isolation methods based on their involvement in cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP). For protein-based enrichment analysis, we applied 35, 56 and 25 unique proteins from SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods, respectively. For miRNA-based enrichment analysis, we first identified the miRNA targets and then used the target proteins for analysis. Specifically, we identified 4,817, 557, and 5,050 target proteins from 183, 2, and 24 unique miRNAs using the SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods, respectively.

GO analysis of exosomal proteins and miRNAs identified through SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods revealed distinct functional associations. The majority of targets across all isolation techniques were linked to cellular anatomical entities, with SEC showing the highest association (97.1%), followed by TIM4-binding (91.7%) and Lectin-binding (86.0%). In molecular function, binding activity was the most prevalent, which was observed in Sect. (66.7%), Lectin-binding (47.8%), and TIM4-binding (47.8%) exosomal cargo. Catalytic activity was also significant, particularly in Sect. (22.2%), Lectin-binding (26.1%), and TIM4-binding (21.7%), with Lectin-binding exosomes further showing a notable presence of molecular function regulators (26.1%). Biological process analysis highlighted key functional differences among the three isolation methods. SEC-derived molecules were predominantly involved in response to stimulus (28.0%), immune system processes (26.9%), cellular processes (19.4%), and metabolic processes (17.2%). Lectin-binding exosomal cargo exhibited enrichment in biological regulation (26.5%), response to stimulus (20.5%), and immune system processes (16.9%), underscoring their role in cellular signaling and regulatory pathways. TIM4-binding exosomal proteins and miRNAs were mainly associated with cellular processes (33.3%), biological regulation (11.1%), response to stimulus (11.1%), and metabolic processes (11.1%). These findings highlight the functional diversity of exosomal molecules obtained through different isolation strategies and their potential roles in various biological pathways (Fig. 5A-B).

Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of miRNAs in cancer

KEGG pathway analysis of uniquely detected miRNAs through SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding methods revealed significant enrichment in cancer-related pathways. A total of 172, 41, and 176 pathways were identified corresponding to 181, 2, and 24 unique miRNAs from SEC, Lectin-binding, and TIM4-binding exosomal cargo, respectively. The top enriched pathway was “miRNAs in cancer,” with a fold enrichment of 3.2 (FDR = 1.60E-29) in SEC-derived miRNAs (Fig. 6A), 4.4 (FDR = 2.60E-04) in Lectin-binding miRNAs (Fig. 6B), and 3.0 (FDR = 4.90E-25) in TIM4-binding miRNAs (Fig. 6C). These findings highlight the potential regulatory role of exosomal miRNAs in cancer-related pathways across different exosome isolation techniques.

Cancer-related exosome enrichment by different isolation methods

Discussion

This study examined the efficacy of three different methods for plasma exosome isolation. Our approach involved a detailed analysis of the protein and miRNA content of isolated exosomes. Functional enrichment analysis significantly enriched exosomal miRNAs and shown cancer-related pathways, emphasizing their potential regulatory role in tumor biology across different exosome isolation techniques. The distinct functional enrichments across different isolation methods underscore the heterogeneity of exosomal contents and highlight the necessity of multi-omics approaches to fully capture their biological significance. Our study showed that SEC method provided the most comprehensive exosome profile, excelling in miRNA detection with the highest number of unique miRNAs identified. Meanwhile, proteomic analysis revealed that the SEC method captured more exosomes with cancer-associated proteins in both CRPC and NSCLC. Conversely, the lectin-binding method was superior in detecting the largest number of proteins, suggesting that this method is particularly effective in capturing a broad spectrum of exosomal proteins. The TIM4-binding method, while not leading in number of detected proteins and miRNAs, outperformed other techniques in enriching cancer-related miRNAs in both patient groups. These findings emphasize the critical need to select the appropriate exosome isolation method for biomarker studies, as each method exhibits unique strengths in detecting differentially expressed proteins and miRNAs.

While our data demonstrates a significant overlap between the proteins and miRNAs identified across the three exosome isolation methods, there were numerous proteins and miRNAs uniquely detected by each method. Since SEC does not specifically isolate subpopulations of exosomes based on surface markers, many of the proteins identified by SEC were absent in the exosomes that bind to Lectin or TIM4. The size-based SEC method may isolate a broader range of exosomes and non-vesicular particles, potentially introducing non-vesicular components [29–32]. The SEC method involves a balance between exosome recovery and purity, which may impact protein and miRNA profiles. Different SEC columns vary in their efficiency at separating exosomes from lipoproteins and proteins, which also influences exosomal content. In support of these findings, a recent study of exosomal miRNAs recommends SEC columns with a 70 nm pore size to effectively remove non-EV miRNA carriers [33]. Although we selected more reproducible molecules across different samples (shared molecules in > = 75% samples), we did not evaluate variability using technical replicates. Based on our PCA and PCA and hierarchical clustering, we demonstrated consistent method-specific profiles. Additionally, differential expression analyses across patient groups showed reproducible identification of cancer-associated molecules (Tables 1, 2 and 3), and EEI scores further validated enrichment consistency (Table 4).

Alternatively, the lower recovery rate of exosomes using Lectin- or TIM4-binding methods could reduce the detection of low-abundance proteins. This highlights the possibility that Lectin and TIM4 binding enrich specific subpopulations of exosomes, particularly those associated with certain surface proteins [29, 34]. Diseases involving tissues with abundant Lectin or TIM4 markers might particularly benefit from such targeted isolation methods. Furthermore, the differences in miRNA detection across these methods point to distinct exosome subpopulations that may carry unique regulatory cargo. The SEC method detected the highest number of miRNAs, including a significant number of those not found using Lectin- or TIM4-binding, suggesting that this method captures a more diverse range of miRNA-containing vesicles. Conversely, Lectin and TIM4 binding may preferentially enrich miRNA cargo associated with specific exosomal subtypes, offering valuable insights into disease-specific biomarkers. These results strengthen the importance of method selection for exosome isolation in multi-omic analyses, where specific biomarkers may only be detectable through targeted enrichment techniques.

In our proteomic analysis using the SEC method, we have identified several proteins exhibiting differential expression when comparing cancer patients to healthy controls, reflecting their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of these malignancies. For example, we found an overexpression of polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR) protein in both CRPC and NSCLC. The PIGR protein is a transmembrane transporter of polymeric IgA through the intestinal epithelium. Its overexpression has been reported in several cancers [35–41]. The level of PIGR expression varies among cancer types and is associated with different patient outcomes and responses to chemotherapy. Several studies have shown that higher PIGR expression in hepatocellular cancer, colon cancer, pancreatic cancer, osteosarcoma and glioma are correlated with poor prognosis [35–39]. In contrast, some studies have reported favorable outcomes with PIGR expression in patients with other cancers, including upper gastrointestinal tract, lung, endometrial, ovarian and breast cancer [42–47]. Additionally, the differential expression of FIBG and FINC in NSCLC, also identified through the SEC method, supports findings from other studies that implicate these proteins in extracellular matrix remodeling and metastasis, further highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets [48, 49]. The Lectin-binding method also revealed significant changes in proteins, particularly with TETN and GELS in CRPC, that have been associated with tumor progression and metastasis in previous studies [50, 51].

Our study revealed that each exosome isolation method enriches distinct molecular cargo with functional relevance to NSCLC and CRPC biology. The SEC method enabled broad miRNA profiling and identified key regulatory molecules such as miR-4743-3p, miR-4448, and miR-542-3p. The downregulation of miR-4743-3p in both CRPC and NSCLC aligns with its reported tumor-suppressive role in other malignancies, where it inhibits cell proliferation and invasion [52, 53]. Similarly, miR-4448, also downregulated in NSCLC, has been proposed as a diagnostic marker for tumor metastasis [54]. miR-542-3p, identified through the lectin-binding method, was differentially expressed in both cancer types and is known to act as a tumor suppressor by stabilizing p53 and inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis [55–61]. Its enrichment in lectin-derived exosomes suggests that glycosylated vesicles may carry functionally relevant tumor-suppressive miRNAs. Additionally, miR-574-5p, differentially expressed in NSCLC, has been reported as a biomarker for early-stage disease [62, 63]. The TIM4-binding method enriched 24 unique miRNAs, including miR-223-3p, which was upregulated in NSCLC. This miRNA is known to regulate tumor progression by modulating inflammatory pathways such as the NLRP3 inflammasome and RHOB signaling, influencing cell migration, invasion, and immune response [64–68]. TIM4’s affinity for phosphatidylserine—a lipid exposed on tumor-derived exosomes—likely explains its selective capture of vesicles carrying oncogenic or immunomodulatory miRNAs.

On the proteomic side, lectin-binding captured the highest number of unique proteins, including PIGR (polymeric immunoglobulin receptor), TETN, and GELS. PIGR is overexpressed in NSCLC and facilitates tumor progression through immune modulation and IgA transcytosis, while in CRPC, it may contribute to immune evasion [69, 70]. TETN and GELS are involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and cytoskeletal regulation, processes central to tumor invasion and metastasis. Together, these findings highlight the biological significance of method-specific exosomal cargo and support a tailored biomarker strategy: TIM4-binding for cancer-specific miRNA detection, lectin-binding for proteomic profiling of tumor-associated proteins, and SEC for broad discovery-phase multi-omic analysis.

Our integrated miRNA and proteomic profiling of SEC-isolated EVs demonstrates that both CRPC and NSCLC patients carry unique EV molecular signatures that can discriminate disease states from healthy controls. Several miRNAs and proteins identified in our study were previously reported in ExoCarta and Vesiclepedia, supporting their bona fide EV association. Notably, molecules such as PIGR, PRDX2, and miR-4743-3p were consistently enriched in patient EVs, highlighting their potential as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers. These findings underscore the value of SEC-based EV isolation combined with multi-omic profiling for non-invasive cancer biomarker discovery (Supplementary data S8-9 for detail). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis further revealed method-specific biological enrichments, while all three isolation approaches demonstrated enrichment in cancer-related and signaling regulation pathways, SEC- and TIM4-binding–derived miRNAs showed the strongest associations with “miRNAs in cancer”. Notably, SEC-derived miRNAs were predominantly linked to immune response, cellular stress, and external stimulus–related pathways, suggesting their role in tumor–immune interactions. In contrast, TIM4-binding miRNAs were significantly enriched in cellular metabolic, transport, and vesicle-mediated processes, reflecting potential functional diversity in exosome subpopulations. Together, these results underscore the biological relevance and methodological specificity of exosomal miRNA cargo, demonstrating that different exosome isolation strategies capture distinct but functionally convergent subsets of miRNAs implicated in cancer progression and regulation.

This study has certain limitations. While we compared multiple exosome isolation methods and their molecular profiles across cancer patients and healthy controls, the sample size remains limited and may not fully represent biological variability. Although the differentially expressed miRNAs and proteins align with previous reports, further validation using orthogonal approaches such as qRT-PCR for miRNAs and ELISA or targeted MS for proteins is warranted. Future studies incorporating immunoblot confirmation of classical exosomal markers (CD9, CD63, CD81, TSG101, Alix) alongside negative markers (ApoA1, GM130) would strengthen vesicle purity assessment. Additionally, including spike-in standards for miRNA and protein quantification could enhance cross-platform normalization. Despite these constraints, our findings emphasize that isolation techniques profoundly influence exosomal molecular profiles and highlight the critical role of method selection in biomarker discovery.

Conclusions

Our study emphasizes that exosome isolation methods have a significant impact on detectable proteomic and miRNA landscapes. Selecting appropriate isolation techniques is therefore crucial for accurate biomarker identification. Future research should focus on refining and standardizing exosome isolation protocols, incorporating spike-in controls and validation of candidate biomarkers across larger and independent cohorts to enhance reproducibility and clinical translation.
