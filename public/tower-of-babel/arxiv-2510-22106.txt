Abstract
We study low-rank matrix regression in settings where matrix-valued predictors and scalar responses are observed across multiple individuals. Rather than assuming a fully homogeneous coefficient matrices across individuals, we accommodate shared low-dimensional structure alongside individual-specific deviations. To this end, we introduce a tensor-structured homogeneity pursuit framework, wherein each coefficient matrix is represented as a product of shared low-rank subspaces and individualized low-rank loadings. We propose a scalable estimation procedure based on scaled gradient descent, and establish non-asymptotic bounds demonstrating that the proposed estimator attains improved convergence rates by leveraging shared information while preserving individual-specific signals. The framework is further extended to incorporate scaled hard thresholding for recovering sparse latent structures, with theoretical guarantees in both linear and generalized linear model settings. Our approach provides a principled middle ground between fully pooled and fully separate analyses, achieving strong theoretical performance, computational tractability, and interpretability in high-dimensional multi-individual matrix regression problems.

Introduction

1.1
Heterogeneity and Homogeneity in Matrix Regression

Matrix-valued data are ubiquitous in modern scientific applications, including neuroimaging
(
Abraham and
et al. 2020
, e.g., functional connectivity matrices;)
, genomics
(
Aibar et al. 2022
, e.g., gene co-expression networks;)
, recommender systems
(
Zhang and Zhou 2021
, user-item interaction matrices;)
, and quantitative finance
(
Bollerslev, Xu and
Zhou 2021
, asset return dependency matrices;)
. In many of these domains, data are collected from multiple individuals, experimental units, or observational contexts, giving rise to multi-individual matrix regression problems with matrix-valued predictors and scalar responses. The primary goal is to model the relationship between matrix predictors and scalar outcomes while addressing high dimensionality, complex structure, and limited sample sizes per individual.

A central challenge in such settings is that regression relationships may exhibit both shared structure across individuals and individual-specific deviations. Fully pooled models, which assume a single coefficient matrix for all individuals, risk bias due to ignored heterogeneity. Conversely, fully separate models, which fit independent regressions per individual, often incur high variance due to limited data per unit. These extremes motivate the development of methods that judiciously balance shared information and individual adaptations. This problem, commonly referred to as
homogeneity pursuit
, involves identifying and leveraging latent common structures across individuals while appropriately accommodating individual variation.

In high-dimensional settings, it is typical to assume that the coefficient matrix is low rank, reflecting the influence of a small number of latent factors. Low-rank matrix regression enhances interpretability and computational efficiency, and has been extensively studied in the literature
(
Negahban and
Wainwright 2011
;
Koltchinskii, Lounici and
Tsybakov 2011
)
. However, in multi-individual settings, low-rank assumptions alone are insufficient. The same low-rank matrix may arise from many diverse factorizations, and existing methods often fail to disentangle shared structures, such as common row or column spaces, from individual heterogeneity. Moreover, conventional low-rank formulations do not explicitly model dependence across individuals, limiting their ability to pool information effectively.

To address these challenges, we propose a framework for homogeneity pursuit, in which shared low-dimensional subspaces (e.g., column and row spaces of the coefficient matrices) are explicitly modeled as common across individuals, while individual-specific deviations are captured via structured low-rank loadings. This leads to a tensor-structured representation of the coefficient matrices, where shared structure is encoded via factor matrices and individual heterogeneity is captured via a low-rank core tensor encoding individualized loadings. Our approach provides a structured and interpretable decomposition that distinguishes between common mechanisms and individual variation, offering a natural middle ground between fully pooled and fully separate analyses.

We develop a computationally scalable estimation procedure based on scaled gradient descent, tailored to the proposed tensor decomposition. The algorithm is designed to handle inherent scaling differences between shared and individual components, and we establish non-asymptotic error bounds demonstrating the estimation improvements when leveraging shared information across individuals. Specifically, convergence rates for the shared subspaces benefit from the larger number of individuals, while individual-specific components retain their distinct signals. To further enhance interpretability and reduce model complexity, we apply hard thresholding to the rows of the shared factor matrices for the recovery of sparse patterns.

The proposed methodology is applicable to both linear models, such as matrix trace regression, and generalized linear models, including matrix logistic regression, making it suitable for a broad range of response types. Overall, our approach provides a flexible, principled, and theoretically grounded framework for multi-individual matrix regression that effectively balances shared and individual information. Below, we summarize our key contributions.

1.

We introduce a tensor-structured model for multi-individual matrix regression, in which shared low-dimensional subspaces are encoded via factor matrices and individual deviations are captured by a low-rank core tensor. This decomposition enables structured homogeneity pursuit, distinguishing common mechanisms from individual variation.

2.

We formalize the notion of shared subspaces and introduce a distance metric that is invariant to orthogonal transformations. This metric supports robust estimation and provides a basis for comparing decompositions while accounting for inherent non-identifiability in tensor representations.

3.

We develop a scaled gradient descent algorithm tailored to the tensor decomposition, with adaptive scaling to balance updates across shared and individual parameters. We establish minimax optimal error bounds demonstrating improved estimation through information pooling.

4.

We extend the framework to incorporate additional sparsity in shared components to further enhance interpretability and improve efficiency. The proposed approach is applicable to both linear and generalized linear models, accommodating a wide range of scientific applications.

Related Literature

Regression with matrix-valued predictors has been extensively studied in both low- and high-dimensional settings. Classical approaches include trace regression models
(
Rodriguez and
Zhou 2011
;
Zhou, Li and Zhu 2013
)
, as well as more general linear or nonlinear models acting on the matrix structure. In high-dimensional regimes where matrix dimensions dominate the sample size, low-rank regularization has emerged as a powerful tool for achieving estimation efficiency and structural interpretability
(
Negahban and
Wainwright 2011
;
Koltchinskii, Lounici and
Tsybakov 2011
;
Yu, Gupta and Kolar 2020
)
.

Tensor decomposition methods have also been employed to model multi-way data structures, including those arising in multi-individual or multi-task regression
(
Yu and Liu 2016
;
Lock 2018
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
. While tensor-based approaches are natural for representing hierarchical or grouped structures, many existing methods lack rigorous theoretical guarantees under high-dimensional scaling. Regularized estimation methods have been developed to address identifiability issues in low-rank matrix and tensor problems
(
Tu et al. 2016
;
Wang, Zhang and Gu 2017
;
Han, Willett and Zhang 2022
)
. More recently,
Tong, Ma and Chi 2021
and
Tong, Ma and Chi 2022
proposed scaled gradient descent algorithms for low-rank matrix and tensor problems. In comparison, our work incorporates additional sparsity structures for improved interpretability and estalibshes statistical guarantees in a more complex setting involving both homogeneous and heterogeneous components.

Closer to our setting, recent advances have explored homogeneity structures in heterogeneous data analysis. Homogeneity pursuit was introduced by
Ke, Fan and Wu 2015
and was extended to panel and longitudinal data models with latent structures
(
Wang, Phillips and
Su 2018
;
Li and Sang 2019
;
Li, Yue and Zhang 2019
;
Lian, Qiao and
Zhang 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Subgroup analysis has also seen substantial development
(
Ke, Li and Zhang 2016
;
Ma and Huang 2017
;
Zhang, Wang and Zhu 2019
)
. Our work contributes to this literature by introducing the homogeneity at the level of subspaces, rather than element-wise. We explicitly model shared subspaces and individual-specific loading factors, developing an estimation procedure with provable guarantees. Our theoretical results quantify the benefits of information pooling while preserving individual heterogeneity.

Model Framework

2.1
Homogeneity Pursuit via Subspace Sharing

We consider a dataset with
n
individuals indexed by
i=1,\dots,n
. For each individual
i
, we observe
m_{i}
independent samples, each consisting of a matrix-valued covariate
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
and a scalar response
Y_{ij}
. We aim to model the relationship between the matrix predictors and scalar responses through a regression framework that accommodates high dimensionality, heterogeneity across individuals, and structured dependencies. We adopt a generalized linear model formulation, where the conditional mean of the response depends linearly on the matrix covariate via an individual-specific coefficient matrix
\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
:

f_{Y_{ij}}(y_{ij}|\mathbf{X}_{ij})\propto\exp\left\{\frac{y_{ij}\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-g(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)}{c(\sigma)}\right\},

(2.1)

where
c(\sigma)
is a scale nuisance parameter and
g(\cdot)
is a differentiable link function.

The negative log-likelihood loss is given by

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})=g(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle.

(2.2)

This framework encompasses a variety of standard regression models. For exmaple, when
g(t)=t^{2}/2
, the model reduces to matrix trace regression
(
Negahban and
Wainwright 2011
)
; when
g(t)=\log(1+\exp(t))
, it corresponds to matrix logistic regression
(
Zhou, Li and Zhu 2013
)
.

A common assumption in high-dimensional matrix regression is that
\mathbf{B}_{i}
admits a low-rank decomposition
\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times r}
are matrices of rank
r
, typically much smaller than
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. This factorization reflects the influence of latent factors and facilitates dimension reduction. The inner product can be equivalently expressed as
\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\rangle=\text{tr}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{R}_{i})
, which reparametrizes the regression in terms of transformations of the covariates induced by the low-rank factors.

However, such a decomposition is not unique: for any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, the pair
(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})
is indistinguishable from
(\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top})
. This non-identifiability does not extend to the column spaces of
\mathbf{C}_{i}
and
\mathbf{R}_{i}
, which are uniquely determined up to orthogonal transformations. These subspaces encode the dominant directions of covariate variation relevant to the response, and it is at this level that meaningful homogeneity may emerge.

Motivated by this, we focus on the subspaces spanned by
\mathbf{C}_{i}
and
\mathbf{R}_{i}
, assuming that the column spaces of
\{\mathbf{C}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
share a common
K_{1}
-dimensional subspace, and similarly for
\{\mathbf{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with dimension
K_{2}
. Formally, letting
\bar{\mathbf{C}}=[\mathbf{C}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times nr}
and
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=[\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times nr}
, we assume the existence of matrices
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
such that
\bar{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1}
and
\bar{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2}
, where
\mathbf{L}_{1}=[\mathbf{L}_{11},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{1n}]
and
\mathbf{L}_{2}=[\mathbf{L}_{21},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{2n}]
are individual-specific loading matrices. Consequently, for each individual
i
,
\mathbf{C}_{i}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{R}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2i}
. The integers
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are assumed to be small relative to
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, reflecting low-dimensional nature of the shared structure.

Under this formulation, the inner product
\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle
becomes

\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle=\text{tr}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2j}),

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{R}
is a bilinear transformation shared across individuals, modulated by individual-specific matrices
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
. When the loading matrices are identical across individuals, i.e.,
\mathbf{L}_{1i}=\mathbf{L}_{1}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}=\mathbf{L}_{2}
for all
i
, the model reduces to a fully homogeneous low-rank regression. More generally, our formulation allows for homogeneity in the underlying subspaces, while accommodating individual-level heterogeneity through the low-rank loadings.

This approach offers several advantages. First, by focusing on the shared subspaces rather than the full low-rank factors, we reduce the number of parameters required to describe the heterogeneity across individuals, leading to improved estimation efficiency, particularly in settings where the number of observations per individual is small. Second, the decomposition is interpretable, as the shared subspaces capture the common structure across individuals, while the loading matrices encode individual deviations. Third, the non-uniqueness issues associated with low-rank matrix decompositions are mitigated by working directly with the subspaces, which are uniquely defined up to orthogonal transformations.

2.2
Tensor Parameterization

To formalize the structured relationship between individuals and the shared and individualized components, we adopt a tensor-based parameterization. Specifically, we organize the individual coefficient matrices
\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
into a third-order tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times n}
, where the
i
-th frontal slice is given by
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(3)}(:,:,i)=\mathbf{B}_{i}
.

The homogeneity in the underlying subspaces is captured via a Tucker decomposition of
\mathscr{B}

(
Tucker 1966
;
De Lathauwer, De Moor and
Vandewalle 2000
)
, decomposing it into a set of shared factor matrices and an individualized core tensor. Formally, we express the decomposition as

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\times_{1}\mathbf{C}\times_{2}\mathbf{R}\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{G}_{i}\mathbf{R}^{\top},\quad\text{with }\mathbf{G}_{i}=\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top},

(2.4)

where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
are the shared factor matrices,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times K_{2}\times n}
is the core tensor, with frontal slices
\mathbf{G}_{i}=\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{2}\times r}
are individualized low-rank loading matrices, encoding individual deviations in the row and column projections. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

\mathbf{B}_{i}
\iddots

\mathscr{B}
\mathbf{C}
\iddots
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}
\mathbf{R}

Remark 2.1

(Rank Properties)
.

By the definition of Tucker ranks, we have
\textup{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)})=K_{1}
and
\textup{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)})=K_{2}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}
are the mode-1 and mode-2 matricizations of
\mathscr{B}
, respectively. Consequently, the mode-3 matricization satisfies
\text{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(3)})\leq K_{1}K_{2}
. When the number of individuals
n>K_{1}K_{2}
, an additional low-rank structure may exist along the third (individual) mode. In such cases, one may consider an extended decomposition of the form

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}=\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}}\times_{1}\mathbf{C}\times_{2}\mathbf{R}\times_{3}\mathbf{W},

(2.5)

where
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times K_{2}\times K_{1}K_{2}}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K_{1}K_{2}}
. However, such an extension does not yield additional structural or statistical benefits in our setting, and we therefore focus on the two-mode shared decomposition in (
2.4
) throughout the paper.

This tensor formulation provides a unified and interpretable framework that explicitly separates:

•

the
shared low-dimensional subspaces
encoded by
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
,

•

the
individualized loading structure
encoded by the low-rank core tensor
\mathscr{G}
, and

•

the overall
heterogeneity in individual responses
through individual-specific projections.

It balances model parsimony with flexibility, enabling joint estimation of common mechanisms and individual deviations.

2.3
Identification

A central challenge in tensor-structured or low-rank matrix decomposition is identifiability: the decomposition is inherently non-unique due to rotational and scaling ambiguities. In our setting, the factors
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
and the core tensor slices
\mathbf{G}_{i}=\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}
are not uniquely determined without further constraints. We now formalize this non-uniqueness and define an appropriate notion of equivalence and distance for estimation purposes.

The factor matrices
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
are identifiable only up to orthogonal transformations. Specifically, for any nonsingular
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\in\text{GL}(K_{1})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\text{GL}(K_{2})
, the parameter sets

(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{R},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{L}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}_{2},\{\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n})

(2.6)

yields exactly the same tensor
\mathscr{B}
and thus lead to identical predictions. Likewise, the individual loading matrices
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
are not uniquely identified, since for any invertible
\mathbf{P}_{i}\in\text{GL}(r)
, replacing
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
with
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{P}_{i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
with
\mathbf{L}_{2i}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-\top}
leaves the product
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}
unchanged.

Due to this inherent non-identifiability, we do not impose explicit constraints (e.g., orthogonality or normalization) on the factors in estimation. Instead, we work with
equivalent classes of decompositions
and define a distance metric that is invariant to these ambiguities. This allows us to focus on estimating the underlying structural components while being agnostic to the specific rotation or scaling.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
denote the true coefficient tensor, with corresponding decomposition defined by true parameters
\mathbf{C}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
,
\mathbf{R}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
,
\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{2}\times r}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. We define the true factor matrices as the leading left singular vectors of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}^{*}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}^{*}
, respectively, with
\mathbf{C}^{*\top}\mathbf{C}^{*}=\mathbf{I}_{K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}^{*\top}\mathbf{R}^{*}=\mathbf{I}_{K_{2}}
. Then, the true value of core tensor is

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\times_{1}\mathbf{C}^{*\top}\times_{2}\mathbf{R}^{*\top}.

(2.7)

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}^{*}),\dots,\sigma_{K_{1}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}^{*}))
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}^{*}),\dots,\sigma_{K_{2}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}^{*}))
be diagonal matrices containing the singular values of the tensor matricizations along modes 1 and 2, respectively. Let
\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}=\min(\sigma_{K_{1}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}^{*}),\sigma_{K_{2}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}^{*}))
denote the smallest singular value across these modes. For each fronal slice
\mathbf{G}_{i}^{*}
, we perform an SVD:
\mathbf{G}_{i}^{*}=\mathbf{U}_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\top}
, and define the true individual loading factors as

\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}=\mathbf{U}_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2},\quad\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}=\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2},

(2.8)

ensuring consistency with the structure
\mathbf{G}_{i}^{*}=\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*\top}
.

To compare decompositions up to orthogonal and scaling transformations, we define the following distance between an estimated parameter set
\mathbf{\Theta}=(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{R},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*},\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{n})
and the true parameter set
\mathbf{\Theta}^{*}
:

\begin{split}\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}=&\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{Q}_{1}\in\text{GL}(K_{1}),\\
\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\text{GL}(K_{2}),\\
\mathbf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{n}\in\text{GL}(r)\end{subarray}}\Bigg\{\|(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{1}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}_{2}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{P}_{i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\Bigg\}.\end{split}

(2.9)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{i}
’s are invertible matrices in the general linear groups that account for the inherent non-uniqueness of the tensor decomposition. This distance metric explicitly removes the effects of transformations
(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2},\mathbf{P}_{i})
and measures the discrepancy between essential structural components of the estimated and true decompositions. It is invariant to rotations and scaling, and serves as the foundation for establishing estimation guarantees.

We now establish a key property of the proposed distance metric: it tightly controls the Frobenius norm error of the underlying coefficient tensor
\mathscr{B}
, thereby linking the geometric notion of identifiability to the statistical target of estimation.

Proposition 2.2
(Distance-Error Equivalence).

For
\mathbf{\Theta}
and
\mathbf{\Theta^{*}}
, denote the corresponding parameter tensors as
\mathscr{B}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}
. If
\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})\leq\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}
,

C_{1}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\leq\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\leq C_{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},

(2.10)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are positive constants.

3
Estimation Methodology

3.1
Scaled Gradient Descent Algorithm

We propose a scaled gradient descent algorithm tailored to the tensor-structured homogeneity pursuit model introduced in Section
2
. The objective is to estimate the full parameter set
\mathbf{\Theta}=(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{R},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{L}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
, which comprises the shared low-rank factor matrices
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
, and the individualized loading matrices
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{2}\times r}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

The estimation is based on minimizing the empirical negative log-likelihood loss

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij}),\quad\text{where }\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}.

(3.1)

Here,
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is a differential loss function associated with the generalized linear model framework introduced in Section
2
.

From the Tucker decomposition in (
2.4
), we define the composite matrices

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}=[\mathbf{L}_{11}\mathbf{L}_{21}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{1n}\mathbf{L}_{2n}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}],\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}=[\mathbf{L}_{21}\mathbf{L}_{11}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{2n}\mathbf{L}_{1n}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top}],

(3.2)

so that the mode-1 and mode-2 matricizations of
\mathscr{B}
satisfy

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}=\mathbf{C}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}=\mathbf{R}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}.

(3.3)

The partial gradients of the empirical loss with respect to the parameters are given by

\begin{split}\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})\right]\widetilde{\mathbf{C}},\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left[\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})^{\top}\right]\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2i},\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i},\end{split}

(3.4)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)
is the
i
-th canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A major challenge in optimizing the above objective lies in the
inherent scaling imbalance
between the shared parameters (
\mathbf{C}
,
\mathbf{R}
), which operate in high-dimensional spaces
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
, and the individual parameters
(\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{L}_{2i})
, which lie in much lower-dimensional spaces
\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbb{R}^{K_{2}\times r}
. This is further complicated by the non-identifiability of the decomposition under orthogonal transformations, and the aggregation scope of gradients, where shared gradients pool information across all individuals, while individual gradients depend only on local data.

To address these issues, we introduce a scaled gradient descent approach that adaptively rescales the gradient for each parameter class. Specifically, for
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, we precondition the gradients by
(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}
and
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}
, respectively. For each
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
(or
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
), we scale by
(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}
and
(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2i})^{-1}
(or their counterparts for
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
), ensuring that updates are numerically stable and dimensionally balanced. This leads to the Scaled Gradient Descent Algorithm (Algorithm
1
), detailed below.

Algorithm 1

Scaled Gradient Descent for Homogeneity Pursuit

Input
: Initial values
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{R}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(0)}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, step size
\eta
, number of iterations
T

For

t=0,\dots,T-1

\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}

\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}

For

i=1,\dots,n
(in parallel)
do

\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\left(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})\left(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}

\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\left(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})\left(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}

End for

Output
:
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}=\left(\mathbf{C}^{(T)},\mathbf{R}^{(T)},\mathbf{L}_{11}^{(T)},\mathbf{L}_{21}^{(T)},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{1n}^{(T)},\mathbf{L}_{2n}^{(T)}\right)

Notably, the parallel updates for
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
enhance computational scalability, and all scaling operations involve only inverses of low-dimensional matrices, ensuring efficiency even in high-dimensional settings.

3.2
Algorithm Computational Guarantees

We now establish the general theoretical guarantees for the scaled gradient descent algorithm. Let
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}=\mathbf{C}^{*}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*\top}\mathbf{R}^{*\top}
denote the true coefficient matrices, and let
\mathbf{\Theta}^{*}=(\mathbf{C}^{*},\mathbf{R}^{*},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*},\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{n})
be the true parameter set.

Assumption 3.1
(Restricted Correlated Gradient Condition).

For each individual
i
, the loss satisfies the Restricted Correlated Gradient (RCG) condition: for
\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
,

\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})],\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\right\rangle\geq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{2}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta_{i}}\|\mathbb{E}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},

(3.5)

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}>0
are individual-specific parameters satisfy
0<\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
.

The RCG condition in Assumption
3.1
implies that, for each individual
i
, the expectation of the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})
is postively correlated with the optimal descent direction
\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}
. This condition is implied by the restricted strong convexity with parameter
\alpha_{i}
and restricted strong smoothness with parameter
\beta_{i}
of the population risk
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})]
, which are widely adopted in non-convex algorithm convergence analysis. In addition, the distributions of
(\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})
are allowed to be heterogeneous across individuals, with various
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
.

Assumption 3.2
(Gradient Stability).

For any
\mathbf{\Theta}
, the gradients are stable in the sense that there exist some constants
\phi>0
,
\xi_{C},\xi_{R},\xi_{1i},\xi_{2i}>0
such that

\begin{split}\left\|\{\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{C},\\
\left\|\{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R},\\
\left\|(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1/2}\{\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2i})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{1i},\\
\left\|(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}\{\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{2i}.\end{split}

(3.6)

The parameters
\phi
controls the performance of all partial gradients for any
\mathbf{\Theta}
, while the parameters
\xi_{C}
,
\xi_{R}
,
\xi_{1i}
’s, and
\xi_{2i}
’s represent the estimation accuracy of the partial sample gradients around their expectations. Similar definitions for the gradient stability have been considered for robust tensor estimation in
Zhang et al. 2025
. Under mild conditions, the gradient stability will be further verified for specific statistical models in Section
5
.

Theorem 3.3
(Local Linear Convergence).

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.2
, if the step size satisfies
\eta\asymp\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1}
,
\phi\lesssim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}
, and the initialization satisfies
\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})\lesssim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{1/2}\bar{\beta}^{-1/2}\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}
, then for all
t=1,2,\dots,T
,

\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\leq(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}/\bar{\beta})^{t}\cdot\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-2}m^{-2}\xi,

(3.7)

and the tensor estimation error satisfies

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\leq C(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}/\bar{\beta})^{t}\cdot\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-2}m^{-2}\xi,

(3.8)

where
m=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
,
\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}=m^{-1}\min_{1\leq i\leq n}m_{i}\alpha_{i}
,
\bar{\beta}=m^{-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}m_{i}\beta_{i}
, and
\xi=\xi_{C}+\xi_{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\xi_{1i}+\xi_{2i})
.

Theorem
3.3
states the local convergence of the proposed scaled gradient descent iterates given that the initial value
\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)}
lies in a local region around the ground truth. In both upper bounds, the first term captures the exponential decay of the optimization error within the basin of attraction around the true parameter. The second term represents the statistical error, which diminishes as the sample size
m
increases or as the gradient noise diminishes. Notably, the convergence rate is independent of the condition number of the underlying parameter matrices, i.e.,
\max(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}),\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}))/\min(\sigma_{K_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}),\sigma_{K_{2}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}))
, a favorable property in high-dimensional or ill-conditioned regimes.

Building upon Theorem
3.3
, we analyze the convergence rates of the individual parameter components after applying an alignment transformation that maps the estimated parameters to their closest transformation in the equivalence class of the true parameters. Formally, let

\begin{split}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{2},\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{1},&\dots,\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{n})=\underset{{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{Q}_{1}\in\text{GL}(K_{1}),\\
\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\text{GL}(K_{2}),\\
\mathbf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{n}\in\text{GL}(r)\end{subarray}}}{\arg\inf}\Bigg\{\|(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{Q}_{1}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{Q}_{2}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{1i}\mathbf{P}_{i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{2i}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\Bigg\}.\end{split}

(3.9)

Corollary 3.4
(Convergence Rates of Estimated Parameters).

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.3
, after a sufficient number of iterations

T\gtrsim\frac{\log(C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-2}m^{-2}\xi/\textup{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2})}{\log(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1})},

(3.10)

the following convergence rates hold

\begin{split}\textup{dist}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}},\\
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}n},\\
\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\|^{2}_{\textup{F}}&\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\sigma_{K_{1}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C})\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}},\\
\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}}\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{2}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\sigma_{K_{2}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R})\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}},\end{split}

(3.11)

and for the individual loading factors,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{1i}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{2}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{1i}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i})\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{2}m^{2}n}.

(3.12)

Corollary
3.4
presents the convergence rates of the shared and individualized parameters with the optimal alignment transformations. These bounds demonstrate that the average error
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
achieves a faster rate due to the information pooling across individuals. Meanwhile, the shared components
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
converge at rates governed by the smallest singular values of the population-level signal matrices, reflecting the distribution of heterogeneity across the shared subspaces. The individual loading matrices
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
converge at rates that depend critically on the minimal individual signal level
\min_{i}\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i})
. When this signal is bounded away from zero and the shared structure is sufficiently strong (e.g.,
\sigma_{K_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}),\sigma_{K_{2}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R})\gtrsim\sqrt{n}
), all components are estimated at statistically efficient rates.

3.3
Loss of Ignoring Homogeneity

To assess the value of the proposed homogeneity pursuit framework, we compare it with a fully heterogeneous baseline model if the latent shared structure is ignored. In this setting, each individual’s coefficient matrix is modeled as
\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}
. The corresponding loss function is

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij}),

(3.13)

and the scaled gradient descent algorithm is modified accordingly, as summarized in Algorithm
2
.

Similarly to Assumption
3.2
, we consider the analogous gradient stability conditions, which leads to the convergence rates without homogeneity pursuit.

Assumption 3.5
(Gradient Stability for Individualized Components).

For each
i
, there exist constants
\psi>0
,
\nu_{1i},\nu_{2i}>0
such that

\begin{split}&\left\|\{\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{i}}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{i}}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})]\}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\psi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\nu_{1i},\\
&\left\|\{\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{i}}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_{i}}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})]\}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\psi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\nu_{2i}.\end{split}

(3.14)

Corollary 3.6
(Convergence Rates without Homogeneity Pursuit).

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
, if
\eta_{i}\asymp\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}^{-1}
,
\psi\lesssim\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\alpha_{i}^{2}m_{i}^{2}
, and the initial estimate error satisfies

\inf_{\mathbf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{n}\in\textup{GL}(r)}\left\{\left\|(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{(0)}\mathbf{P}_{i}-\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\left\|(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{(0)}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\right\}\lesssim\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}^{-1}\sigma_{r}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}),

(3.15)

then given a sufficiently large number of iterations, for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

\begin{split}\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\nu_{1i}+\nu_{2i}}{\alpha_{i}^{2}m_{i}^{2}},\\
\|\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\widecheck{\mathbf{P}}_{i}-\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\|\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}\widecheck{\mathbf{P}}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\nu_{1i}+\nu_{2i}}{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i})\alpha_{i}^{2}m_{i}^{2}},\end{split}

(3.16)

where
\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}=\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}^{\top}
and
\widecheck{\mathbf{P}}_{i}\in\textup{GL}(r)
is the optimal alignment matrix.

As shown in Corollary
3.6
, the estimation error of each
\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}
scales as
(\nu_{1i}+\nu_{2i})/(\alpha_{i}^{2}m_{i}^{2})
, where ignoring shared structure prevents the
O(n^{-1})
error reduction achievable under homogeneity pursuit. Therefore, the homogeneity pursuit framework leads to significant statistical gains, both in terms of individual parameter estimation and recovery of shared structures, by leveraging the common low-rank subspaces across individuals. These advantages are formally reflected in the improved convergence rates and are further supported by the algorithm computational and scalability benefits.

3.4
Initialization, Rank Selection, and Implementation

The performance of the proposed Scaled Gradient Descent algorithms depends critically on the initialization quality, as well as the correct specification of the low-rank parameters
r
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
. In this subsection, we describe practical strategies for initializing the shared subspaces
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, as well as methods for selecting the ranks
r
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
.

When the ranks
r
,
K_{1}
, and
K_{2}
are known or have been pre-selected, we describe a data-driven approach to initialize the shared column and row subspaces, represented by
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
, respectively. Given preliminary estimates
\{\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i},\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(e.g., from the heterogeneous baseline Algorithm
2
), we construct the aggregate matrices

\mathbf{M}_{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}^{\top}\quad\mathbf{M}_{R}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}^{\top},

(3.17)

and take their top
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
eigenvectors (corresponding to the largest eigenvalues) to form the initial estimates
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{R}^{(0)}
. The individual loading matrices are initialized as
\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(0)}=\mathbf{C}^{(0)\top}\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(0)}=\mathbf{R}^{(0)\top}\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}
.

To select
r
, one may pre-determine a rank upper bound
\bar{r}
satisfying
r<\bar{r}\ll\bar{p}
, and obtain the initial estimators
\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})
based on
\bar{r}
. Then, we can apply the cumulative ridge-type ratio estimator to select
r

\widehat{r}={\arg\max}_{1\leq r\leq\bar{r}-1}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{r}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{p})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{r+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{p})},

(3.18)

where
\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{p})
is the ridge parameter depending on
n
,
m
and
\bar{p}
.

In real practice, given
r
, it is normal to assume both
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are small, say not greater than
Cr
for some constant
C
, for better interpretability.
To select
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, we can also apply another ridge-type ratio estimator to the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{M}_{C}
and
\mathbf{M}_{R}

\widehat{K}_{1}={\arg\max}_{1\leq k\leq Cr}\frac{\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{M}_{C})+\delta_{2}(n,m,p_{1})}{\lambda_{r+1}(\mathbf{M}_{C})+\delta_{2}(n,m,p_{1})},

(3.19)

and

\widehat{K}_{2}={\arg\max}_{1\leq k\leq Cr}\frac{\lambda_{k}(\mathbf{M}_{R})+\delta_{2}(n,m,p_{2})}{\lambda_{r+1}(\mathbf{M}_{R})+\delta_{2}(n,m,p_{2})},

(3.20)

where
\delta_{2}(n,m,p)
is another ridge parameter. The theoretical justification of the above initialization and rank determination method will be given for specific models in Section
5
.

4
Sparsity and Scaled Hard Thresholding

4.1
Sparsity in Homogeneous Subspaces

The homogeneity pursuit framework decomposes the individual regression coefficient matrices into a product of shared components (
\mathbf{C}
,
\mathbf{R}
) and individualized components (
\mathbf{L}_{1,i}
,
\mathbf{L}_{2,i}
). While the individualized components lie in low-dimensional spaces and are typically estimated with high accuracy, the shared components
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
remain high-dimensional when
p_{1}
or
p_{2}
is large.

To enhance interpretability, estimation efficiency, and computational scalability, we impose row-wise sparsity on the shared components
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
. Specifically, we assume that only a subset of rows in
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
are nonzero, thereby identifying a small number of informative covariate dimensions that contribute to the response across all individuals.

Formally, let
S_{1}\subseteq\{1,\dots,p_{1}\}
and
S_{2}\subseteq\{1,\dots,p_{2}\}
denote the index sets of nonzero rows in
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, respectively. The sparsity structure satisfies two key properties. First, the sparsity pattern is invariant under transformations of the form
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{R})\to(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}_{2})
where
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\in\text{GL}(K_{1})
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\text{GL}(K_{2})
. This ensures that the sparsity is well-defined on the underlying latent subspaces. Second, the active rows in
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
correspond to a subset of informative covariate dimensions that are predictive of the response
y_{ij}
. Specifically, the inner product

\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{C}^{\top}_{S_{1}}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})_{[S_{1},S_{2}]}\mathbf{R}_{S_{2}},\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\rangle,

(4.1)

where
(\mathbf{X}_{ij})_{[S_{1},S_{2}]}
denotes the submatrix of
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
restricted to rows in
S_{1}
and columns in
S_{2}
, and
\mathbf{C}_{S_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}_{S_{2}}
are the corresponding submatrices of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
restricted to rows in
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively. This induces a reduced effective dimensionality in the shared components, improving both statistical and computational performance.

4.2
Scaled Hard Thresholding for Structured Sparsity

To induce sparsity in
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, a natural approach is to apply hard thresholding to the rows of these matrices during the optimization process. Specifically, for a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
, we define the hard thresholding operator
\text{HT}(\mathbf{M},s):\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
as the operator that retains the
s
rows of
\mathbf{M}
with the largest Euclidean norms and sets the remaining rows to zero.

However, directly applying hard thresholding to
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
is problematic due to the non-uniqueness of the tensor decomposition. In particular, for equivalent decompositions
\mathbf{\Theta}=(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{R},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{L}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
and
\mathbf{\Theta}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}_{2},\{\mathbf{L}_{1i},\mathbf{L}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
, the set of rows with largest norms may differ, leading to inconsistent sparsity patterns across equivalent solutions.

To address this, we apply hard thresholding to scaled versions of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
that are invariant under transformations. Specifically, we consider the transformed terms
\mathbf{C}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{1/2}
, where
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
are the auxiliary variables defined in Section
3
. The row-wise Euclidean norms of these transformed matrices remain invariant under equivalent decompositions, ensuring that the sparsity structure is consistent across the equivalent class of parameters.

The scaled hard thresholding procedure is then followed by a renormalization step to restore the original scale:

\mathbf{C}\to\text{HT}(\mathbf{C}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{1/2},s_{1})(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2},~~\mathbf{R}\to\text{HT}(\mathbf{R}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{1/2},s_{2})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2}.

(4.2)

This approach is incorporated into the scaled gradient descent algorithm as shown in Algorithm
3
, where
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
denote the sparsity levels (i.e., the number of nonzero rows) for
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, respectively.

In the following analysis, denote
s_{1}^{*}
and
s_{2}^{*}
as the true row-wise sparsity levels of
\mathbf{C}^{*}
and
\mathbf{R}^{*}
, respectively. To establish theoretical guarantees, we introduce a gradient stability condition tailored to the sparsity structure included by hard thresholding.

Assumption 4.1
(Gradient Stability on Sparse Sets).

Given sparsity levels
s_{1}
and
s_{2}

\begin{split}&\left\|\{(\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}))(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2}]\}_{S_{1}}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\leq\phi\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{C,s_{1}},\\

(4.3)

uniformly over all index sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}

\textup{card}(S_{2})\leq s_{2}+s_{2}^{*}

Theorem 4.2
.

A Couple of Specific Examples

5.1
Linear Trace Regression

We first consider the linear trace regression setting, in which repeated observations are available for each of
n
individuals. For invidual
i
and observation
j
, the response is given by

Y_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle+\varepsilon_{ij},~\text{for}~j=1,\dots,m_{i},~\text{and}~i=1,\dots,n,

(5.1)

where
\varepsilon_{ij}
is a mean-zero noise term. The coefficient matrices
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}
are assumed to follow a low-rank structure with shared and individualized components, as introduced previously. Specifically, the coefficien matrix for individual
i
admits the decomposition

\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top},

(5.2)

where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times K_{1}}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times K_{2}}
are the shared components across individuals, and
\mathbf{L}_{1i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{2}\times r}
are the components specific to individual
i
.

To establish theoretical guarantees, we impose the following assumptions.

Assumption 5.1
(Sub-Gaussian Covariates and Noise).

The matrix-valued covariate
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian, i.e., for any
\lambda>0
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{v}_{2}\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{2})]\leq\lambda^{2}\kappa^{2}/2.

(5.3)

For
i=1,\dots,n
, the variance of
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
satisfies

\alpha_{x,i}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}[\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})^{\top}])\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{E}[\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})^{\top}])\leq\beta_{x,i}.

(5.4)

Moreover, the noise terms
\varepsilon_{ij}
are mean-zero and
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian,

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\varepsilon_{ij})]\leq\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2,\quad\text{for any }\lambda>0.

(5.5)

Assumption 5.2
(Balanced Sample Sizes).

For all individuals, the number of repeated samples is asymptotically identical, i.e.,
m_{1}\asymp\cdots\asymp m_{n}\asymp m
.

Assumption 5.3
(Signal Strength).

The population-level signal strength associated with the shared components satisfies

\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C})\asymp\sigma_{K_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C})\asymp\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R})\asymp\sigma_{K_{2}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R})\asymp\sqrt{n},

(5.6)

and the individual-specific signal strength associated with the individualized components satisfies that

\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})\asymp\cdots\asymp\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})\asymp C,

(5.7)

for some constant
C
.

The sub-Gaussian conditions on
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
and
\varepsilon_{ij}
in Assumption
5.1
are widely adopted in matrix regression literature. Additionally, their distributions are allowed to vary across individuals. Assumption
5.2
can simplify the theoretical results, and it can be relaxed to accommodate imbalanced sample sizes. Assumption
5.3
implies that the shared low-rank subspaces are sufficiently informative, and that each individualized component contributes to a non-negligible signal.

As Algorithm
1
can be viewed as a special case of Algorithm
2
with
s_{1}=p_{1}
and
s_{2}=p_{2}
, we only present the statistical results for the sparse setting. We apply Algorithm
2
to obtain the initial values
\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
and
\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}

We now extend the framework to the generalized linear model (GLM) with loss function

Assumption 5.7

The Lipschitz condition naturally holds for logistic and probit regression. For this class of models, we consider Assumptions
5.2
-

Assumption 5.8

7
Conclusion and Discussion

We have introduced a multi-individual matrix regression framework with homogeneity pursuit, which models matrix-valued responses across individuals by decomposing each coefficient matrix into shared low-rank components and individualized components. This structure captures common patterns across individuals while allowing for personalized deviations, improving both interpretability and estimation efficiency.

We formulated the problem under a linear trace regression model and extended it to generalized linear models (GLMs). A scaled gradient descent algorithm with scaled hard thresholding was developed to estimate the shared and individualized components, with theoretical guarantees on estimation error, rank selection consistency, and convergence rates. Our method incorporates sparsity on shared components and uses ridge-type ratio estimators for rank selection, achieving strong theoretical performance under mild conditions.

Theoretically, we demonstrated that homogeneity pursuit leads to improved convergence rates, with an additional
n^{-1}
factor reflecting the benefits of shared structure. Minimax lower bounds established the optimality of our approach in high-dimensional settings. Simulation experiments verified rank selection consistency, convergence behavior, and the advantages of low-rank and sparse modeling. In particular, the proposed estimators achieved rates aligned with our theoretical predictions.

In a real data application to the New York City Taxi Trip Dataset, the proposed method outperformed fully homogeneous, fully heterogeneous, and their low-rank or OLS counterparts, achieving the lowest prediction error and smallest variability. These results highlight the practical value of homogeneity pursuit in extracting structured information from multi-individual matrix data.

In summary, our framework provides a flexible, interpretable, and statistically efficient approach to modeling matrix-valued responses with shared and individual structures. It advances the methodology for multi-individual regression and is applicable to problems in urban analytics, personalized modeling, and beyond. Future work may explore nonlinear extensions and dynamic adaptations.

Tensor Algebra and Notations

Tensors are multi-dimensional arrays that generalize matrices to higher-order data. A
d
-th order tensor is represented as
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
, where
p_{k}
is the dimension along the
k
-th mode. In this article, we adopt the following notation:

•

Vectors
: denoted by boldface lowercase letters, e.g.,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

•

Matrices
: denoted by boldface uppercase letters, e.g.,
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
,

•

Tensors
: denoted by boldface Euler letters, e.g.,
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
.

We refer readers to
Kolda and Bader 2009
for a comprehensive review of tensor operations and decompositions.

Mode-
k
matricization

The
mode-
k
matricization
(or
unfolding
) of a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
is a matrix obtained by rearranging the fibers of
\mathscr{X}
along the
k
-th mode into columns. The result is denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{k}\times p_{-k}}
, where
p_{-k}=\prod_{\ell=1,\ell\neq k}^{d}p_{\ell}
.

Each column of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)}
corresponds to a
fiber
of
\mathscr{X}
along mode
k
, stacked in lexicographic order. The element
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{d})
of
\mathscr{X}
is mapped to the
(i_{k},j)
-th entry of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)}
, where the index
j
is given by:

j=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=1\\
s\neq k\end{subarray}}^{d}(i_{s}-1)\cdot J_{s}^{(k)},\quad\text{with}\quad J_{s}^{(k)}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell<s\\
\ell\neq k\end{subarray}}^{d}p_{\ell},\quad\text{and}\quad p_{0}=1.

(A.1)

This operation is central to defining mode-
k
products and understanding Tucker decompositions.

Mode-
k
Product

For a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
and a matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{k}\times p_{k}}
, the
mode-
k
product
, denoted
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\times_{k}\mathbf{Y}
, results in a new tensor of size
p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{k-1}\times q_{k}\times p_{k+1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}
. Its entries are given by:

\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\times_{k}\mathbf{Y}\right)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}ji_{k+1}\cdots i_{d}}=\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{p_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\cdots i_{d}}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{ji_{k}},\quad\text{for all }j=1,\ldots,q_{k}.

(A.2)

This operation recombines the tensor along mode
k
with the matrix
\mathbf{Y}
.

Inner Product

For two tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
, their
inner product
is defined as:

\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}\rangle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{p_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{p_{d}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}},

(A.3)

and it results in a
(d-d_{0})
-th order tensor with entries indexed by
(i_{d_{0}+1},\ldots,i_{d})
.
In the special case where
d=d_{0}
, the generalized inner product reduces to the standard
Frobenius inner product
, and we define the
Frobenius norm
of
\mathscr{X}
as:

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\|_{\text{F}}=\sqrt{\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\rangle}.

(A.4)

Tucker Decomposition and Tucker Ranks

The
Tucker rank
of a tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{d}}
is a vector
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
, where each
r_{k}
is the rank of the mode-
k
matricization
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)}
, i.e.,

r_{k}=\text{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)})\in\mathbb{N},\quad\text{for }k=1,\ldots,d.

(A.5)

While Tucker ranks are defined via matricization ranks, they correspond to the number of components retained in the
Tucker decomposition
of
\mathscr{X}
. If
\mathscr{X}
has Tucker ranks
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
, it can be written as:

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}\times_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{Y}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}\times_{1}\mathbf{Y}_{1}\times_{2}\mathbf{Y}_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathbf{Y}_{d},

(A.6)

where
\mathbf{Y}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}\times r_{j}}
is the factor matrix for mode
j
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
is the core tensor.

The mode-
k
matricization of
\mathscr{X}
under the Tucker decomposition can be expressed as:

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{(k)}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{Y}$}_{(k)}\left(\otimes_{j=1,j\neq k}^{d}\mathbf{Y}_{j}\right)^{\top},

(A.7)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product, and the product is taken over all modes except
k
. This structure is central to our algorithm and theoretical analysis, as it allows us to work with low-rank representations in high-dimensional spaces.

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{C}^{(t)}&=\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},~~\mathbf{G}_{R}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{1i}^{(t)}&=(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{2i}^{(t)}&=(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\end{split}

(B.2)

and their corresponding estimation errors as

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}&=\mathbf{G}_{C}^{(t)}-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}&=\mathbf{G}_{R}^{(t)}-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}&=\mathbf{G}_{1i}^{(t)}-(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}&=\mathbf{G}_{2i}^{(t)}-(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}.\end{split}

(B.3)

Then, the proposed scaled gradient descent update at step
t
can be formulated as

\begin{split}\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{G}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{1i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{G}_{2i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}_{2i}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)})](\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}.\end{split}

(B.4)

Define the optimal transformations

\begin{split}&(\mathbf{Q}_{1t},\mathbf{Q}_{2t},\mathbf{P}_{1t},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{nt})\\
=&\inf_{\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2},\mathbf{P}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{n}}\Bigg\{\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{1}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{2}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}\mathbf{P}_{i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}\mathbf{P}_{i}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\Bigg\}\end{split}

(B.5)

to align
(\mathbf{C}^{(t)},\mathbf{R}^{(t)},\mathbf{L}_{11}^{(t)},\mathbf{L}_{21}^{(t)},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{1n}^{(t)},\mathbf{L}_{2n}^{(t)})
and
(\mathbf{C}^{*},\mathbf{R}^{*},\mathbf{L}_{11}^{*},\mathbf{L}_{21}^{*},\dots,\mathbf{L}_{1n}^{*},\mathbf{L}_{2n}^{*})
.

For simplicity, as we focus on the generic step
t
, denote the decomposition components with these optimal alignments as

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-\top}
,

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-\top}
,

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{(t)}\times_{1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{\top}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{\top}
,

\mathbf{L}_{1i}=\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}\mathbf{P}_{it}
,

\mathbf{L}_{2i}=\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-\top}
,

\mathbf{R}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}_{2i}
, and

\mathbf{C}_{i}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{2i}
.
Thus, we have
(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}_{1t}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{\top}
,
(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}
,
(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}_{2t}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{\top}
,
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}
,
(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-\top}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-1}
,
(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1}=\mathbf{P}_{it}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{\top}
.

Then, the proposed scaled gradient descent can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\end{split}

(B.6)

\begin{split}\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\end{split}

(B.7)

\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{1t}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\end{split}

(B.8)

and

\begin{split}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}=&\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
=&\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{2t}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{\top}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\end{split}

(B.9)

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{e}_{n}(n)^{\top}\otimes\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{ij},y_{ij})\right],\\
\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{e}_{n}(n)^{\top}\otimes\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{ij},y_{ij})^{\top}\right],\\
\text{and }\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}&=m_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{ij},y_{ij})],\end{split}

(B.10)

such that
\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
.

Next, we present some necessary conditions. First, the de-scaled partial gradients satisfy the stability conditions

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{C},~~\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\phi\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R},\\
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{1i},~~\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\phi\|\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{2i}.\end{split}

(B.11)

Second, we assume that for all
t=0,1,2,\dots,T
,

\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})\leq\epsilon\underline{\sigma}

(B.12)

for some sufficiently small
\epsilon
. Then, by Lemma
B.2
, we have

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{(t)}\times_{1}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\times_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{2}^{-1}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\epsilon^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}

(B.13)

and

\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\epsilon^{2}\underline{\sigma}^{2}.

(B.14)

Further, by Lemma
B.1
, the following upper bounds hold for some small
C
,

\begin{split}\max(\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\|,\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\|,\|(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})_{(1)}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\|,\|(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})_{(2)}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}^{-1}\|)&\leq C\epsilon,\\
\|\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\|\leq 1+C\epsilon,~~~\|\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\|&\leq C\epsilon,\\
\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\|\leq C\epsilon,~~~\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|&\leq 1+C\epsilon,\\
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}^{-1}\|\leq C\epsilon,~~~\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|&\leq 1+C\epsilon.\end{split}

(B.15)

By symmetry, the corresponding upper bounds for
\mathbf{R}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
hold as well. Similarly, by Lemma
B.2
, we also have

\begin{split}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(\|(\mathbf{C}_{i}-\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|,\|(\mathbf{R}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|)&\leq C\epsilon,\\
\|(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*})^{-1}-\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|&\leq C\epsilon,\\
\|(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*\top}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*})^{-1}-\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|&\leq C\epsilon.\end{split}

(B.16)

Step 2.
(Upper bound for
\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})
)

By definition of
\mathbf{Q}_{1t}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{2t}
, and
\mathbf{P}_{it}
’s,

\begin{split}&\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})\\
=&\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+1}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{2,t+1}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{i,t+1}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2,t+1}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{i,t+1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{it}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(B.17)

Then, we will derive the upper bounds for the terms of
\mathbf{C}
,
\mathbf{R}
,
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
’s and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
’s, respectively.

Step 2.1
(
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
upper bounds)

We first develop an upper bound for
\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}
. By mean inequality and the upper bounds in (
B.15
), for any
\zeta>0
,

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\mathbf{C}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(B.18)

For any
\gamma>0
, the first term can be rewritten as

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\eta^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-2\eta\langle(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C},\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\rangle\\
=&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\eta^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-2\eta\langle(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*\top},\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\rangle\\
&+2\eta\langle(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C},\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*})^{-1/2}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C})\rangle\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\eta^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|^{2}-2\eta\cdot M_{C}\\
&+2\eta\cdot\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*})^{-1/2}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C\eta^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-2\eta\cdot M_{C}\\
&+C\epsilon\eta\gamma^{-1}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C\epsilon\eta\gamma\cdot\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(B.19)

where
M_{C}=\langle(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{*\top},\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\rangle
.

Similarly, for
\mathbf{R}
, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)})]_{(2)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)})]_{(2)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(B.20)

For any
\gamma>0
, the first term can be further bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C_{1}\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-2\eta\cdot M_{R}\\
&+C\epsilon\eta\gamma^{-1}\cdot\|\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C\epsilon\eta\gamma\cdot\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(B.21)

where
M_{R}=\langle(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{*\top},\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\rangle
.

Step 2.2
(Upper bounds for
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
)

Next, we focus on the terms related to
\mathbf{L}_{1i}
and
\mathbf{L}_{2i}
. For any
\zeta>0
,

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{it}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}[\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{1t}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)})]\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-1}]\mathbf{P}_{it}\\

(B.22)

For any
\gamma>0
, by (
B.15
) and (
B.16
), the first term can be bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{1i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}-\eta(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

(B.23)

where
M_{1i}=\langle(\mathbf{L}_{1i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2},\mathbf{C}^{*\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*\top}\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\rangle
.

Similarly, for any
\zeta>0
, we can have the bound for

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-\top}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

&+(1+\zeta^{-1})C\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

For any
\gamma>0
, the first term can be bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{2i}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}-\eta(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

where

.

Step 2.3
(Combined results)

Combining these results, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{L}_{1i}-\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{*}-\eta(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\mathbf{L}_{2i}-\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{*}-\eta(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)\top}[\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-\top})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

&-2\eta\left(M_{C}+M_{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{1i}+M_{2i})\right).\end{split}

(B.26)

Hence, it suffices to construct reasonable lower bounds for
M_{C}+M_{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{1i}+M_{2i})
.

Denote the tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times n}

\begin{split}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)})_{(1)}&=[m_{1}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{1}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{1j},y_{1j})],\dots,m_{n}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{n}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{nj},y_{nj})]]\\

(B.27)

such that
\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}^{2}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{ij},y_{ij})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}

\begin{split}&M_{C}+M_{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{1i}+M_{2i})\\

where

. Additionally,

\begin{split}&\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}\mathbf{R}^{*}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\times_{1}\mathbf{C}^{*}\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})+\mathbf{\Delta}_{G}\times_{1}\mathbf{C}^{*}\times_{2}\mathbf{R}^{*}\\

Therefore, by the RCG condition, we have that for all

and hence,

\begin{split}\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\rangle&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)};\mathbf{X}_{ij},y_{ij})],\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\\

&\geq\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}m}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\bar{\beta}m}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(B.31)

where
\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}(\alpha_{i}m_{i}/m)
,
\bar{\beta}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(\beta_{i}m_{i}/m)
, and

.

Then, we have

\begin{split}&M_{C}+M_{R}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{1i}+M_{2i})\\
=&\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\rangle-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n},(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}-\mathbf{\Delta}_{G})\times_{1}\mathbf{C}^{*}\times_{2}\mathbf{R}^{*}\rangle\\
&-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\rangle\\
&-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{1}\mathbf{C}^{*}\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\rangle\\
&-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}\mathbf{R}^{*}\rangle\\
&-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)},(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\rangle\\
\geq&\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}m}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\bar{\beta}m}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\bar{\beta}m}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-5\bar{\beta}m\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}-\mathbf{\Delta}_{G}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-5\bar{\beta}m\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*}\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-5\bar{\beta}m\|(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-5\bar{\beta}m\|(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-5\bar{\beta}m\|(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{G}$}^{*})\times_{1}(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\times_{2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\geq&\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}m}{2}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\bar{\beta}m}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-C\epsilon^{2}\bar{\beta}m\cdot\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}.\end{split}

(B.32)

Combining the above result, we have

\begin{split}&\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)(1+C\epsilon\eta\gamma+C\epsilon^{2}\eta\bar{\beta}m-C\eta\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}m)\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\\
&+(1+\zeta)(C\eta^{2}+C\epsilon\eta\gamma^{-1}-C\eta\bar{\beta}^{-1}m^{-1})\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{T}$}_{n}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+(1+\zeta^{-1})C\eta^{2}\left(\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})\right).\end{split}

(B.33)

Taking
\gamma=Cm\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{1/2}\bar{\beta}^{1/2}
,
\epsilon\lesssim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{1/2}\bar{\beta}^{-1/2}
,
\eta=C\bar{\beta}^{-1}m^{-1}
, and
\zeta\lesssim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1}
, we have that

\begin{split}&\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\\
\leq&(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1})\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\\
&+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-1}\bar{\beta}^{-1}m^{-2}\left(\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{2i}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})\right).\end{split}

(B.34)

Step 3.
(Induction and completion of proof)

According to the stability of

, with

\begin{split}\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}&\leq(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1})\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-1}\bar{\beta}^{-1}m^{-2}\xi.\end{split}

,

(B.36)

which guarantees that the condition (
B.12
) holds for the step
t+1
. Then, by induction,

\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\leq(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1})^{t}\cdot\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-2}m^{-2}\xi,

(B.37)

for all

.

By Lemmas
B.1
and
B.2
,

\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{(t)}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq C(1-C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}\bar{\beta}^{-1})^{t}\cdot\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-2}m^{-2}\xi,

(B.38)

and for sufficiently large

,

\max(\|\mathbf{C}^{(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{1T}-\mathbf{C}^{*}\|,\|\mathbf{R}^{(T)}\mathbf{Q}_{2T}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\|)\lesssim\underline{\sigma}^{-1}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}^{-1}m^{-1}\xi.

(B.39)

∎

B.2
Proof of Corollaries
3.4
and
3.6

Based on the results in Theorem
3.3
, when

T\gtrsim\frac{\log(C\alpha^{-2}m\xi/\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2})}{\log(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})},

(B.40)

the computational error
(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})^{T}\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}
is dominated by the statistical error

. Hence,

\text{dist}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\alpha^{2}m^{2}}

(B.41)

and

\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\alpha^{2}m^{2}}.

(B.42)

The latter further implies that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\xi}{\alpha^{2}m^{2}n}.

(B.43)

∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem
4.2

The proof of Theorem
4.2
follows a similar approach as that of Theorem
3.3
. It also consists of three major steps and is constructed in an inductive approach. In the first step, we present the notations used in the proof, and state some key conditions. In the second step, we develop the core convergence analysis. Specifically, given some conditions hold at the step
t
, we present an upper bound for the step
t+1
, i.e.,

\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t+1)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\leq(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})\cdot\text{dist}(\mathbf{\Theta}^{(t)},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}\xi.

(B.44)

In the last step, we show that the required conditions also hold for the step
t+1
, and complete the proof by induction.

The proposed scaled project gradient descent algorithm is formulated as

\begin{split}\mathbf{C}^{(t+0.5)}=&\mathbf{C}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{C}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{1t}^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
\mathbf{R}^{(t+0.5)}=&\mathbf{R}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{H}_{R}^{(t)}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{2t}^{-1}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\\
\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}=&\mathbf{L}_{1i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{1t}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{-1}\\
&-\eta\cdot(\mathbf{C}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}^{(t)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1i}^{(t)}(\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}=&\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{Q}_{2t}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}^{(t)\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{it}^{\top}\\

\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}=&\text{HT}(\mathbf{C}^{(t+0.5)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)})^{1/2},s_{1})(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)})^{-1/2}\\
\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}=&\text{HT}(\mathbf{R}^{(t+0.5)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t+0.5)})^{1/2},s_{2})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{(t+0.5)})^{-1/2}\end{split}

(B.45)

First, we analyze the scaled hard thresholding step. For any

,

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+1}-\mathbf{C}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{2,t+1}-\mathbf{R}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{R}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

(B.46)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+0.5}
,
\mathbf{Q}_{2,t+0.5}
,

,

Then, since

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+0.5}-\mathbf{C}^{*})_{\bar{S}_{1,t+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|[\text{HT}(\mathbf{C}^{(t+0.5)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)})^{1/2},s_{1})(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{(t+0.5)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{1,t+0.5}-\mathbf{C}^{*}]_{\bar{S}_{1,t+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

Similarly, we have

C
Statistical Convergence Analysis

C.1
Proof of Theorem
5.4

For simplicity, we consider the case
m=m_{1}=\cdots=m_{n}
, and the proof can be easily extended to the general case with
m\asymp m_{1}\asymp\cdots\asymp m_{n}
. For the linear trace regression

Y_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle+\varepsilon_{ij},\quad j=1,2,\dots,m\quad\text{and}\quad i=1,2,\dots,n,

(C.1)

the loss function is

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(Y_{ij}-\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{C}\mathbf{L}_{1i}\mathbf{L}_{2i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle)^{2}.

(C.2)

Then, the de-scaled partial gradients are

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})(\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{ij})\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})(\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{\top})\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1/2}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1/2}.\end{split}

(C.3)

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2}=\breve{\mathbf{C}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2}=\breve{\mathbf{R}}
,
\mathbf{R}_{i}(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{i})^{-1/2}=\breve{\mathbf{R}}_{i}
,
\mathbf{C}_{i}(\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i})^{-1/2}=\breve{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
,
\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1/2}=\ddot{\mathbf{C}}
, and
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}=\ddot{\mathbf{R}}
. They are matrices with orthonormal columns.

In the following, the proof consists of four steps. In the first two steps, we establish the stability of
\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})
,
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})
,
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
, and
\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
, respectively. In the third step, we extend the results to the initialization. In the last step, we verify that the data-driven rank selection is asymptotically consistent and conclude the proof. Therefore, in the first two steps, we assume that the ranks
(r,K_{1},K_{2})
are known and correct.

Step 1
(Stability of
\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})
and
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})
)

We first prove the stability of
\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})
with
p_{1}=s_{1}
. Note that
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{C}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
. For the given
\mathbf{\Theta}
, denote
\breve{\mathbf{C}}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{K_{1}}]
, where each
\mathbf{v}_{\ell}
is a
(p_{2}n)
-dimensional vector with
\mathbf{v}_{\ell}=(\mathbf{v}_{\ell 1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{\ell n}^{\top})^{\top}
and each
\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}
is a
p_{2}
-dimensional vector. Since
\breve{\mathbf{C}}
is an orthonormal matrix,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2}=1
.

Note that for
1\leq k\leq p_{1}
and
1\leq\ell\leq K_{1}
, the
(k,\ell)
-th entry of
\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})
is

\begin{split}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})\mathbf{e}_{\ell}(K_{1})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})(\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{ij})\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}.\end{split}

(C.4)

Therefore, the
(k,\ell)
-th entry of
\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})]
is

\begin{split}&\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-\varepsilon_{ij})\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}}_{T_{k\ell 1}}\\
&+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}-\mathbb{E}[\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}])}_{T_{k\ell 2}}.\end{split}

(C.5)

Hence, we first focus on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{ij}z_{ijk\ell}
, where
\varepsilon_{ij}
are independent
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian random variables. Given
\{z_{ijk\ell}\}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}z_{ijk\ell}
is a
(\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{ijk\ell}^{2})
-sub-Gaussian random variable. Thus, denote

S_{k\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}z_{ijk\ell},~~~R_{k\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{ijk\ell}^{2}.

(C.6)

Then, for all
\lambda>0
,

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell})|R_{k\ell}]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}R_{k\ell}}{2}.

(C.7)

Thus, by the standard Chernoff argument, for any
T_{1},T_{2}>0
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}[\{S_{k\ell}\geq T_{1}\}\cap\{R_{k\ell}\leq T_{2}\}]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\mathbb{P}[\{\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell})\geq\exp(\lambda T_{1})\}\cap\{R_{k\ell}\leq T_{2}\}]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\mathbb{P}[\{\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell})\mathbb{I}(R_{k\ell}\leq T_{2})\geq\exp(\lambda T_{1})]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1})\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell})\mathbb{I}(R_{k\ell}\leq T_{2})]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell}-\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{I}(R_{k\ell}\leq T_{2})]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{k\ell}-\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}R_{n}/2)]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)=\exp\left(-\frac{T_{1}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}T_{2}}\right).\end{split}

(C.8)

By definition,
z_{ijk\ell}
is
(\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2})
-sub-Gaussian, and
z_{ijk\ell}^{2}
is
(4\sqrt{2}\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2},4\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2})
-sub-exponential.
Hence, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[R_{k\ell}-\mathbb{E}[R_{k\ell}]\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{32m\kappa^{2}},\frac{t}{4\kappa^{2}}\right)\right)

(C.9)

and
\mathbb{E}[R_{k\ell}]\leq m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}
. Hence, we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}[S_{k\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(p_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}]\\
\leq&\mathbb{P}[\{S_{k\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(p_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}\}\cap\{R_{n}\leq 2m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}\}]+\mathbb{P}(R_{n}>2m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2})\\
\leq&\exp\left(-C\log(p_{1})\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}}{32\kappa^{2}}\right)\leq\exp(-C\log(p_{1}))+\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2}).\end{split}

(C.10)

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{k,\ell}S_{k\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(p_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}\right]\leq C\exp(-C\log(p_{1}))+\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2}),

(C.11)

and with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(p_{1}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq p_{1}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq K_{1}}T_{k\ell 1}^{2}\leq Cmp_{1}\log(p_{1})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}

(C.12)

Next, we consider the bound of
T_{k\ell 2}
. Note that

\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})^{\top}\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})^{\top}(\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}(p_{1})).

(C.13)

Since
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian,
\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(p_{1})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}
is sub-exponential with parameters
(4\sqrt{2}\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2},4\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{v}_{\ell i}\|_{2}^{2})
. Thus, we have that, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(T_{k\ell 2}\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{32m\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\kappa^{2}},\frac{nt}{4\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right)\right).

(C.14)

Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{k,\ell}T_{k\ell 2}\geq\sqrt{m\log(p_{1})/n}\kappa\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}\right]\leq\exp(-C\log(p_{1})),

(C.15)

and with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(p_{1}))
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq p_{1}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq K_{1}}T_{k\ell 2}^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}mp_{1}\log(p_{1})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

(C.16)

Combining the results, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(p_{1}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
,

\|\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}mp_{1}\log(p_{1})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mp_{1}\log(p_{1})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.17)

Similarly, for
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})
, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(p_{2}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
,

\|\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}mp_{2}\log(p_{2})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mp_{2}\log(p_{2})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.18)

More generally, for a generic pair of sparsity levels
(s_{1},s_{2})
, as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
, the sparsity levels
(s_{1}+s_{1}^{*},s_{2}+s_{2}^{*})
are directly used in the partial gradient submatrices. Therefore, we have

\|\{\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}ms_{1}\log(p_{1})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+ms_{1}\log(p_{1})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2},

(C.19)

and

\|\{\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}ms_{2}\log(p_{2})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+ms_{2}\log(p_{2})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.20)

Step 2
(Stability of
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
and
\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
)

Next, we prove the stability of
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
and
\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
. For the given
\mathbf{\Theta}
, denote
\ddot{\mathbf{C}}=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{K}]
and
\breve{\mathbf{R}}_{i}=[\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{r}]
, where each
\mathbf{u}_{k}
or
\mathbf{w}_{\ell}
is a vector with unit Euclidean norm.

Note that for
1\leq k\leq K_{1}
and
1\leq\ell\leq r
, the
(k,\ell)
-th entry of
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})
is

\begin{split}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}(K_{1})\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})\mathbf{e}_{\ell}(r)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle-Y_{ij})\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}.\end{split}

(C.21)

Therefore, the
(k,\ell)
-th entry of
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})]
is

\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-\varepsilon_{ij})\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}}_{T_{ik\ell 1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}-\mathbb{E}[\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}])}_{T_{ik\ell 2}}.

(C.22)

For
T_{ik\ell 1}
, we consider
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}s_{ijk\ell}
. Given
\{s_{ijk\ell}\}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}s_{ijk\ell}
is a
(\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{ijk\ell}^{2})
-sub-Gaussian random variable. Thus, denote

S_{ik\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{ij}s_{ijk\ell},~~~R_{ik\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{ijk\ell}^{2}.

(C.23)

Then, for all
\lambda>0
,

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell})|R_{ik\ell}]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}R_{ik\ell}}{2}.

(C.24)

Thus, by the standard Chernoff argument, for any
T_{1},T_{2}>0
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}[\{S_{ik\ell}\geq T_{1}\}\cap\{R_{ik\ell}\leq T_{2}\}]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\mathbb{P}[\{\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell})\geq\exp(\lambda T_{1})\}\cap\{R_{ik\ell}\leq T_{2}\}]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\mathbb{P}[\{\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell})\mathbb{I}(R_{ik\ell}\leq T_{2})\geq\exp(\lambda T_{1})]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1})\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell})\mathbb{I}(R_{ik\ell}\leq T_{2})]\\
=&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell}-\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{I}(R_{ik\ell}\leq T_{2})]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda S_{ik\ell}-\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}R_{ik\ell}/2)]\\
\leq&\inf_{\lambda>0}\exp(-\lambda T_{1}+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}T_{2}/2)=\exp\left(-\frac{T_{1}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}T_{2}}\right).\end{split}

(C.25)

By definition,
s_{ijk\ell}
is
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian, and
s_{ijk\ell}^{2}
is
(4\sqrt{2}\kappa^{2},4\kappa^{2})
-sub-exponential.
Hence, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[R_{ik\ell}-\mathbb{E}[R_{ik\ell}]\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{32m\kappa^{2}},\frac{t}{4\kappa^{2}}\right)\right)

(C.26)

and
\mathbb{E}[R_{ik\ell}]\leq m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}
. Hence, we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}[S_{ik\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(nK_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}]\\
\leq&\mathbb{P}[\{S_{ik\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(nK_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}\}\cap\{R_{ik\ell}\leq 2m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}\}]+\mathbb{P}(R_{ik\ell}>2m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2})\\
\leq&\exp\left(-C\log(nK_{1})\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{m\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}}{32\kappa^{2}}\right)\leq\exp(-C\log(n))+\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2}).\end{split}

(C.27)

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{i,k,\ell}S_{ik\ell}\geq C\sqrt{m\log(nK_{1})}\sigma\bar{\beta}_{x}\right]\leq C\exp(-C\log(nK_{1}))+\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2}),

(C.28)

and with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(nK_{1}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
, for all
i=1,\dots,n
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{1}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq r}T_{ik\ell 1}^{2}\leq CmK_{1}\log(nK_{1})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.29)

Next, we consider the bound of
T_{ik\ell 2}
. Note that

\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})^{\top}\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})^{\top}(\mathbf{w}_{\ell}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{k}).

(C.30)

Since
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
is
\kappa^{2}
-sub-Gaussian,
\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\rangle\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{w}_{\ell}
is sub-exponential with parameters
(4\sqrt{2}\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},4\kappa^{2}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})
. Thus, we have that, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(T_{ik\ell 2}\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{32m\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\kappa^{2}},\frac{t}{4\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right)\right).

(C.31)

Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{i,k,\ell}T_{ik\ell 2}\geq\sqrt{m\log(nK_{1})}\kappa\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}\right]\leq\exp(-C\log(nK_{1})),

(C.32)

and with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(nK_{1}))
, for any
i=1,\dots,n
,

\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{1}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq r}T_{ik\ell 2}^{2}\leq C\kappa^{2}mK_{1}\log(nK_{1})\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

(C.33)

Hence, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(nK_{1}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

\|\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\kappa^{2}mK_{1}\log(nK_{1})\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mK_{1}\log(nK_{1})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.34)

Similarly, for
\mathbf{G}_{i2}
, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(nK_{2}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
,

\|\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\kappa^{2}mK_{2}\log(nK_{2})\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mK_{2}\log(nK_{2})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.35)

Step 3
(Initial error)

For initialization, the gradient stability in Algorithm
2
can be directly extended from Step 2 by setting
K_{1}=s_{1}
and
K_{2}=s_{2}
. In other words, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log(n\bar{s}))-C\exp(-Cm\bar{\beta}_{x}^{4}\kappa^{-2})
, for any
i=1,\dots,n
and generic index sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
,

\begin{split}\|\{\mathbf{G}_{C_{i}}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C_{i}}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\kappa^{2}m\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}_{i}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+m\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2},\\
\|\{\mathbf{G}_{R_{i}}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{R_{i}}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\kappa^{2}m\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}_{i}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+m\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.\end{split}

(C.36)

Therefore, by Corollary
3.6
, when
m\gtrsim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\kappa^{2}\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})
, for any
i=1,\dots,n
,

\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}}{m}

(C.37)

and when
m\gtrsim\bar{s}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{3}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-3}
,

\text{dist}(\widecheck{\mathbf{\Theta}},\mathbf{\Theta}^{*})^{2}\lesssim nm^{-1}\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}\lesssim\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{-1}\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}^{2}.

(C.38)

Therefore, the intial error bound requirement is satisfied if
m\gtrsim\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\bar{\beta}_{x}^{3}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-3}
.

Step 4
(Rank selection consistency)

By Step 3, for a finite
\bar{r}
, we have that for any
i=1,\dots,n
,

\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}}{m}.

(C.39)

Obviously,
\text{rank}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})\leq\bar{r}+r
. By definition,

\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{r}+r}\sigma_{j}^{2}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}).

(C.40)

By Mirsky’s singular value inequality,

\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{r}+r}[\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))-\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})]^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{r}+r}\sigma_{j}^{2}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})=\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

(C.41)

It is followed by

\max_{1\leq j\leq\bar{r}+r}|\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))-\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})|\leq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{r}+r}[\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))-\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})]^{2}\right\}^{1/2}=\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r})-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}.

(C.42)

For any
1\leq j\leq\bar{r}
, as
\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})\asymp n\bar{s}^{1/4}m^{-1/4}
and
m\gtrsim\bar{s}
,

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))-\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})]+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s}).\end{split}

(C.43)

For
j>r
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})=0
and
|\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))-\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*})]|\lesssim n[m^{-1}\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}]^{1/2}=o(\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s}))
. For
j\leq r
,
\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})=o(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}))=O(n)
. Therefore, for
j>r
, as
m\to\infty
,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}\to\frac{\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}{\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}=1.

(C.44)

For
j<r
,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}\to\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}^{*})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}^{*})}.

(C.45)

For
j=r
,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}(\bar{r}))+\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}\to\frac{\delta_{1}(n,m,\bar{s})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{r}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}^{*})}=o\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq r-1}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j+1}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}^{*})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}^{*})}\right).

(C.46)

The selection consistency of
\widehat{r}
stems from these results.

Next, by the approximate equivalence of
\|\widecheck{\mathbf{B}}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
and
\|(\widecheck{\mathbf{C}}_{i}-\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\|(\widecheck{\mathbf{R}}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{i}^{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{1/2}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}
, we have that

\left\|\mathbf{M}_{C}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*\top}\right\|^{2}\leq nm^{-1}\bar{s}\log(n\bar{s})\sigma^{2}\underaccent{\bar}{\alpha}_{x}^{-2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.47)

In addition, by Assumption
5.3
,
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{*\top}\|^{2}=n
. Therefore, by the similar arguments for the selection of
r
, when
\delta_{2}\asymp nm^{-1/2}\bar{s}^{1/2}
, the selection consistency of
K_{1}
can be established. Similarly, the rank selection consistency of
K_{2}
can be derived.

C.2
Generalized Linear Model

Similar to Theorem
5.4
, we also consider the simple case with
m=m_{1}=\cdots=m_{n}
, and the proof can be easily extended to
m\asymp m_{1}\asymp\cdots m_{n}
. We consider the generalized linear model with loss function

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{i};\mathbf{X}_{ij},Y_{ij})=g(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle,\quad j=1,\dots,m\quad\text{and}\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(C.48)

Then, the de-scaled partial gradients are

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}[g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}](\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{ij})\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}[g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}](\mathbf{e}_{i}(n)^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{\top})\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}[g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}](\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{C})^{-1/2}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}[g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}](\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})^{-1/2},\end{split}

(C.49)

where
g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{B}_{i}\rangle)-Y_{ij}
is a bounded variable due to the
L
-Lipschitz condition of
g(\cdot)
and
|Y_{ij}|\leq B
.

Therefore, by the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, with a high probability,

\begin{split}\|\{\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{C}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}ms_{1}\log(p_{1})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+ms_{1}\log(p_{1})(L+B)^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2},\\
\|\{\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\}_{S_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\lesssim\frac{\kappa^{2}ms_{2}\log(p_{2})}{n}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+ms_{2}\log(p_{2})(L+B)^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2},\end{split}

(C.50)

for any index sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
\text{card}(S_{1})\leq s_{1}
and
\text{card}(S_{2})\leq s_{2}
. In addition, with a high probability,

\|\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{1i}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\kappa^{2}mK_{1}\log(nK_{1})\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mK_{1}\log(nK_{1})(L+B)^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2},

(C.51)

and

\|\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}_{2i}(\mathbf{\Theta})]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\kappa^{2}mK_{2}\log(nK_{2})\|\mathbf{B}_{i}-\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+mK_{2}\log(nK_{2})(L+B)^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}.

(C.52)

For initialization, the gradient stability in Algorithm

can be directly extended from Step 2 by setting

and
K_{2}=s_{2}
. Hence, with a high probability, for

and generic index sets

Therefore, the initial error bound requirement is satisfied if

Proof of Theorem
5.6

Let
\mathcal{T}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times n}:\text{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)})\leq r_{1},~\text{rank}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)})\leq r_{2}\}

. Consider the augmented covariate tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}\times n}
whose
i
-th slice is
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
and others are zero matrices.

Suppose that there exists a finite set
\{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{1},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{2},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{K}\}\in\mathcal{T}
of tensors such that
\log(K)\gtrsim\bar{s}^{*}\log(\bar{p})
, such that

\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{-2}\delta^{2}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{1}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq 8\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{-2}\delta^{2}.

(D.1)

Let
\widetilde{K}
be a random variable uniformly distributed over the index set
[K]
.
Then, using the standard argument in terms of multple hypothesis testing problem, it yields the lower bound

\inf_{\widetilde{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}}}\sup_{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}\bar{\beta}_{x}^{-2}\delta^{2}}{2}\right\}\geq\inf\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{K}})

(D.2)

where the infimum is taken over all estimators
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
that are measurable functions of data.

Let
X=\{\mathbf{X}_{ij},1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\}
and
Y=\{Y_{ij},1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\}
. Using Fano’s inequality, for any estimator
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{m}}|X]\geq 1-\frac{I_{X}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{K}};Y)+\log 2}{\log K}.

(D.3)

Taking expecatations over
X
on both sides, we have

\mathbb{P}[\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{m}}]\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X}[I_{X}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{K}};Y)]+\log 2}{\log K}.

(D.4)

From the convexity of mutual information, we have the upper bound

I_{X}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$};Y)\leq\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{K}D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{1}}|\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{2}}),

(D.5)

where

\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{1}}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{2}}
with

D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{1}}|\mathbb{Q}^{\ell_{2}})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{1}},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{ij}\rangle-\langle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{2}},\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{X}$}_{ij}\rangle\right)^{2}.

(D.6)

Taking the expectation over
X
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{X}[I_{X}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$};Y)]\leq\frac{\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}m}{2\sigma^{2}\binom{K}{2}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{1}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq m\delta^{2}.

(D.7)

Pluggin it into the previous bound and taking
\delta^{2}=\bar{s}^{*}\log(\bar{p})m^{-1}+\log n
, as

\mathbb{P}[\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\widetilde{K}}]\geq 1-\frac{\bar{s}\log(\bar{p})+\log n+\log 2}{\log K}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(D.8)

Hence, with probability at least
1/2
,

\inf_{\widetilde{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}}}\sup_{\scalebox{0.7}{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}\in\mathcal{T}}\|\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\gtrsim\frac{\sigma^{2}\bar{s}^{*}\log\bar{p}+\log n}{\bar{\beta}_{x}^{2}m}.

(D.9)

Finally, it suffices to construct a suitable packing for
\mathcal{T}
. As we consider row-wise sparsity in
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{R}
, as well as
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(1)}
and
s^{*}_{2}
-dimensional nonzero row index set for
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}_{(2)}
. After selecting the nonzero index sets, we consider the packing set for the low-rank tensors. By Lemmas
D.1
and
D.2

such that

and

\frac{\delta^{2}}{8}\leq\|\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{1}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathscr{B}$}^{\ell_{2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\delta^{2},

(D.10)

for any
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
and
\delta>0
. It concludes the proof
