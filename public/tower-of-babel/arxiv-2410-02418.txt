Optimal Representations of Gaussian and Eisenstein Integers using digit sets closed under multiplication
Adam Blažek, Edita Pelantová, and Milena Svobodová

Abstract
. We study two positional numeration systems which are known for allowing very efficient addition and multiplication of complex numbers. The first one uses the base β = ı − 1 \beta=\imath-1 and the digit set 𝒟 = { 0 , ± 1 , ± ı } \mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\} . In this numeration system, every non-zero Gaussian integer x x has an infinite number of representations. We focus on optimal representations of x x – i.e., representations with minimal possible number of non-zero digits. One of the optimal representations of x x has the so-called 3 3 -non-adjacent form ( 3 3 -NAF). We provide an upper bound on the number of distinct optimal representations of x x , depending on the number of non-zero digits in the 3 3 -NAF of x x . We also characterize the Gaussian integers for which the upper bound is attained. The same questions are answered also for the second numeration system with base β = ω − 1 \beta=\omega-1 and digit set 𝒟 = { 0 , ± 1 , ± ω , ± ω 2 } \mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\} , where ω = exp ⁡ ( 2 ​ π ​ ı / 3 ) \omega=\exp(2\pi\imath/3) . In this system, every Eisenstein integer has a 2 2 -NAF, which is optimal. This paper can be understood as an analogy to the result of Grabner and Heuberger obtained for the signed binary numeration system, using base β = 2 \beta=2 and digit set 𝒟 = { 0 , ± 1 } \mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1\} .

1.
Introduction

Methods for speeding up arithmetical operations have been in the center of research for mathematicians and engineers since the 1950’s. It is in cryptography, but also in other tasks requiring massive calculations, that these methods are finding their applications.

G. W. Reitwiesner
[
13
]
has shown that any integer
x
can be represented in the numeration system with base
\beta=2
and digit set
\mathcal{D}=\{-1,0,1\}
in the form
x=\sum_{k=0}^{N}d_{k}2^{k}
in such a way that at most one out of two neighbouring digits
d_{j}
and
d_{j-1}
is non-zero for each
j
. Such a representation is unique for a given integer
x
, and, moreover, it has the minimum Hamming weight amongst all representations of
x
in this numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
. Contrary to the classic binary numeration system with digit set
\{0,1\}
, where half of the digits in number representations are non-zero on average, the representations introduced by Reitwiesner have just one third of non-zero digits on average. This decreases the number of additions needed in processing of multiplication.

The idea of Reitwiesner was generalized into the notion of so-called
width-
W
non-adjacent form
– i.e., representing numbers in a basis and digit set chosen conveniently so that any
W
consecutive digits contain at most one non-zero digit. Such representations are called
W
-NAF representations. For imaginary quadratic rings, which are in the center of our interest, a detailed analysis of relations between the bases, digit sets and width
W
was performed by C. Heuberger and D. Krenn in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
.

The on-line algorithm for multiplication of real numbers proposed by K. S. Trivedi and M. D. Ercegovac
[
14
]
obtains the
n
most significant digits of the result in linear time with respect to
n
. Both input and output numbers are represented in a numeration system with base
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\beta|>1
and digit set
\mathcal{D}
allowing addition in parallel, as firstly introduced by A. Avizienis
[
2
]
. Addition is performed in a constant number of steps, irrespective of the length of the summands. That is possible, however, only when the digit set is rich enough. Suitable alphabets for parallel addition in integer bases were described by C. Y. Chow and J. E. Robertson
[
3
]
and by B. Parhami
[
11
]
, and minimal alphabets for quadratic bases can be found in
[
10
]
.

Our goal is to draw attention to two numeration systems for representing complex numbers. These systems are defined by their bases and digit sets as follows:

(1)

\displaystyle\beta=\imath-1

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\},

(2)

\displaystyle\beta=\omega-1

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\},\text{ where }\omega=\exp(2\pi\imath/3).

Both these numeration systems can express any complex number
x
in the form
x=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{N}d_{k}\beta^{k}
, where
d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}
.

When focusing on numbers with representations containing only non-negative powers of the base
\beta
, we get

\Bigl\{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}d_{k}\beta^{k}\,:\,N\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}\Bigr\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathbb{Z}[\imath]&\text{= the ring of Gaussian integers in case (1),}\\
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]&\text{= the ring of Eisenstein integers in case (2).}\end{array}\right.

These two systems are exceptional when it comes to efficiency of multiplication algorithm. The efficiency stems from the following three properties of
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
:

•

the digit set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
, in both cases (1) and (2), is closed under multiplication and thus the operation of multiplication by a single digit is carry-free;

•

the
3
-NAF representation of any element
x
of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
is available, and at the same time it is minimal with respect to the number of non-zero digits in the representation; the same is true for the
2
-NAF representation of any element of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
;

•

addition in
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
is doable in parallel, and also efficient on-line multiplication is possible.

The
W
-NAF representation of
x
has the minimal Hamming weight, but the same Hamming weight can be achieved also with other representations of
x
in the system
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
. Representations with the minimal Hamming weight shall be called
optimal
. Using a randomly chosen optimal representation of
x
, instead of its
W
-NAF representation, can improve resistance of the elliptic curve crypto-systems against differential power analysis. For the numeration system with base
\beta=2
and digit set
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1\}
, a sharp upper bound on the number of optimal representations of an integer
n
was deduced by Grabner and Heuberger in
[
6
]
. In 2010, Wu et al.
[
15
]
provided an upper bound on the number of optimal representations, their bound depends on length of the
2
-NAF representation of
x
.

In our contribution, we first compare the two systems mentioned in cases (
1
) and (
2
) from the perspective of the density of non-zero digits in representations of complex numbers. We show that the first one is slightly more advantageous in this respect.

For each of the two numeration systems
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
, we assign a matrix
A_{d}
to every digit
d\in\mathcal{D}
, and then derive a matrix formula for enumeration of
f(x)
= the number of optimal representations of
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. In fact, we show that
f(x)
is a recognisable series as introduced in
[
1
]
and the family of matrices
A_{d}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{9\times 9}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{7\times 7}
, for Gaussian and Eisenstein system respectively, together with a row vector and a column vector, form their linear representations.

We provide a sharp upper bound on
f(x)
depending on the number of non-zero digits in the
W
-NAF representation of
x
. The task of finding this bound is transformed into a related task to determine the joint spectral radius for the set of matrices
\{A_{d}:d\in\mathcal{D}\}
. We propose a graph in which a random walk assigns an optimal representation of
x
, starting from the
W
-NAF representation of
x
.

In Section
3
, we study in detail all optimal representations in the numeration system
(1)
for Gaussian integers. The digit set
\mathcal{D}
used in the numeration system
(2)
for representing Eisenstein integers has more symmetries than the digit set for system
(1)
. That greatly simplifies the process of deriving the properties of system
(2)
, therefore, in Section
4
we provide just a sketch of the proofs.

2.
Known results on two selected number systems

The two bases
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
that we study are imaginary quadratic. In particular,

•

the first base
\beta=\imath-1
is a root of the minimal polynomial
X^{2}+2X+2
and
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
is the ring of
Gaussian integers
;

•

the second base
\beta=\omega-1
is a root of
X^{2}+3X+3
and
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]=\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
is the ring of
Eisenstein integers
.

In accordance with terminology of C. Heuberger and D. Krenn, the pair consisting of the base
\beta
and of the digit set
\mathcal{D}
is called
numeration system
, when each element of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
can be expressed in the form
\sum_{k=0}^{N}d_{k}\beta_{k}
. It is clear that if
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
forms a numeration system, then the number of elements in the digit set
\mathcal{D}
must be at least equal to the norm of
\beta
– i.e.,
N(\beta)=\beta\overline{\beta}
. Moreover, the digit set must contain at least one representative from each congruence class modulo
\beta
. Still, fulfilment of both these conditions is not sufficient to form a numeration system.

W. Penney in
[
12
]
has shown that the base
\beta=\imath-1
with the binary digit set
\{0,1\}
constitute a numeration system, wherein, of course, the set
\{0,1\}
is closed under multiplication. Note that, on the contrary, a similar base
\beta=\imath+1
with the same digit set does not form a numeration system.

The base
\beta=\omega-1
has norm
N(\beta)=3
and forms a numeration system together with digit sets
\{0,1,2\}
or
\{0,1,-\omega\}
, but these digit sets are not closed under multiplication. On the other hand,
\beta=\omega-1
with the digit set
\{0,\pm 1\}
, which is closed under multiplication, do not form a numeration system; for instance,
x=\overline{\omega}
cannot be expressed as
\sum_{k=0}^{N}d_{k}\beta^{k}
.

When
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
is a numeration system and
\#\mathcal{D}
is equal to the norm
N(\beta)
, then each element of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
has a unique
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representation. This property is not necessarily advantageous. Redundant digit sets can enable representing numbers using a low number of non-zero digits, and also defining better algorithms for processing arithmetic operations. The following Theorem from
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
shows the necessary level of redundancy for parallel addition:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\beta|>1
be an algebraic integer with the minimal polynomial
f(X)
, and let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
. If the system
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
allows parallel addition, then
\#\mathcal{D}\geq|f(1)|
.

The digit set
\{0,\pm 1\}
is closed under multiplication, and it is redundant with respect to the base
\beta=\imath-1
. The minimal polynomial of
\beta
is
f(X)=X^{2}+2X+2
, and thus
f(1)=5
. According to Theorem
2.1
, the digit set
\{0,\pm 1\}
does not enable parallel addition.

Similarly, the digit set
\{0,1,\omega,\omega^{2}\}
with
\omega=\exp(2\pi\imath/3)
is closed under multiplication, and it is redundant with respect to the base
\beta=\omega-1
. But, again, this digit set does not enable parallel addition for this base
\beta
, because the minimal polynomial of
\beta
is
f(X)=X^{2}+3X+3
, and thus
f(1)=7
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer, imaginary quadratic,
W\in\mathbb{N}
,
W\geq 2
. The set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
is called a minimal norm representative digit set with respect to
W
, if it consists of
0
and exactly one representative from each residue class of
\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
modulo
\beta^{W}
that is not divisible by
\beta
, and, moreover, each representative contained in
\mathcal{D}
is of minimal norm in its residue class.

Example 2.3
.

Let us find the minimal norm representative digit sets for our two favourite bases:

\beta=\imath-1
and
W=3
:

As
\beta^{3}=2+2\imath
, there are
N(\beta^{3})=(\beta\overline{\beta})^{3}=8
residual classes modulo
\beta^{3}
with representatives of minimal norm:
0,\pm 1,\pm\imath,2,\imath\pm 1
. Three of these representatives
2=\beta\overline{\beta}
,
\imath+1=-\imath\beta
and
\imath-1=\beta
are non-zero and divisible by
\beta
. Hence

\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}\,.

\beta=\omega-1
and
W=2
:

As
\beta^{2}=-3\omega
, there are
N(\beta^{2})=(\beta\overline{\beta})^{2}=9
residual classes modulo
\beta^{2}
. Two representatives of minimal norm, namely
\pm(\omega-1)
, are non-zero and divisible by
\beta
. The remaining seven representatives form the digit set

\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\}\,.

The minimal norm representative digit sets

presented in Example

are closed under multiplication, and they also have the cardinality required by Theorem

for parallel addition. Another exceptional property of these digit sets follows from the result of C. Heuberger and D. Krenn, see Theorem 9.1 and Corollary 8.1 in

]

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
be a numeration system and

, where

(1)

,

(2)

or

-NAF representation in

.

Third exceptional property of the two numeration systems follows from the results
[
10
]
and
[
4
]
:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
be a numeration system, where

(1)

either
\beta=\imath-1
and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}

(2)

or

When

can be expressed in the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
as

with

with the string, and write simply

.

However, we are interested especially in such representations that contain the minimal number of non-zero digits. The frequency of non-zero digits appearing in

-NAF representations for our two numeration systems can be found in
[

. Let us first specify the term

Proposition 2.6
.

(1)

If
\beta=\imath-1
and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
, then the frequency

-NAF representations equals

.

(2)

If
\beta=\omega-1
and
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\}
, then the frequency

of non-zero digits of

.

It seems clear that the base

should be more advantageous for representing complex numbers. We have to consider, however, that the length of the

is approximately

. Hence, the

-NAF representation of

•

For system

, we thus have the value

•

For system

As the ratio

is bigger than

Representations of Gaussian Integers

In this section, we consider the numeration system with base
\beta=\imath-1
and digit set
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
. For easier notation, we adopt the following convention:

Convention:

•

For complex conjugation of a number
x
, we have the usual notation
\overline{x}
.

•

Besides, in digit strings representing a number
x
in the numeration system
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
, we shall denote the digits
-1
and
-\imath
by
\underline{1}
and
\underline{\imath}
, respectively.

For instance, number

is represented by strings

\underline{\imath}\underline{\imath}

\underline{\imath}010\underline{\imath}0\underline{1}0\imath 010\underline{\imath}0\underline{1}

In the sequel, we describe a transducer which converts an arbitrary

-representation of a Gaussian integer

-NAF representation in

-NAF representation of

q_{n}+x_{n}+x_{n+1}\beta+x_{n+2}\beta^{2}=q_{n+3}\beta^{3}+y_{n}
. Therefore,

x=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y_{k}\beta^{k}+\underbrace{(q_{n}+x_{n}+x_{n+1}\beta+x_{n+2}\beta^{2})}_{q_{n+3}\beta^{3}+y_{n}}\beta^{n}+\sum_{k=n+3}^{N}x_{k}\beta^{k}=

=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y_{k}\beta^{k}+y_{n}\beta^{n}+0\cdot\beta^{n+1}+0\cdot\beta^{n+2}+q_{n+3}\beta^{n+3}+\sum_{k=n+3}^{N}x_{k}\beta^{k}\,.

The new string on output
00y_{n}y_{n-1}\cdots y_{0}
obviously satisfies both (
4
) and (
5
).

It remains to show that, starting from some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, the digits
y_{n}
on output are zero for each
n>M
. It suffices to prove that there exists an index
M>N
such that the transducer gets into the state
q_{M}=0
. If that is the case, then all the next states must be equal to zero, as
x_{j}=0
, and only zeros are written on the output
y_{j}=0
, on all positions
j>M
.

Assume that
n
digits, for
n\geq N
, are already written on output and the transducer is in a state
q\neq 0
. We show that the new state
q^{\prime}\in Q
, to which we get from the initial state
q
, fulfils
|q^{\prime}|<|q|
. For
n>N
, we read
a=0
on input, and thus
w=q+a=q\in Q
. Let us discuss two cases:

•

If
q=q^{\prime}\beta
for some state
q^{\prime}\in Q
, then the output digit is
y=0
, and the new state
q^{\prime}
satisfies
|q^{\prime}|=|\beta|^{-1}|q|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|q|<|q|
.

•

If
q\notin\beta Q
, then additional two zeros are read on the input, we move to the new state
q^{\prime}\in Q
, and we write on output the triplet of digits
00d
such that
q=q^{\prime}\beta^{3}+d
, where
d\in\{\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
and
q^{\prime}\in Q
. The relation
q\notin\beta Q
implies that

–

either
q\in\{\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
, and then
d=q
and
q^{\prime}=0
, so clearly
|q^{\prime}|=0<1=|q|
;

–

otherwise
q\in\{\pm 1\pm 2\imath,\pm 2\pm\imath\}
, so
|q|=\sqrt{5}
and
|q-d|\leq\sqrt{10}
for any
d\in\mathcal{D}
, therefore
|q^{\prime}|=|\beta|^{-3}|q-d|\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{10}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}=\frac{1}{2}|q|<|q|
.

Since the number of states
q\in Q
is finite, it is clear that, after a finite number of steps, the transducer reaches the state
q_{M}=0
for some
M>N
.

In a summary, the transducer produces the output string of the form
\cdots 000y_{M}y_{M-1}\cdots y_{1}y_{0}
for some
M>N
, with
y_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
for each index
j
. Fulfilment of the properties (
4
) and (
5
) guarantees that
y_{M}y_{M-1}\cdots y_{1}y_{0}
is the
3
-NAF representation of
x
.
∎

The finite transducer introduced in Definition

Let

Transformation of a general

Note that the usual notation of a path in a graph is from left to right - i.e., starting on the left with the initial vertex and ending on the right with the final vertex of the path, which is the opposite to the direction how representations of numbers are read by the transducer - i.e., from the right to the left end of the representation.

Let us recall that the set of states

and the digit set

are invariant under multiplication by
\imath
and under complex conjugation. Moreover,
\overline{\beta}=\imath\beta
. These facts imply a number of symmetries in the graph
G
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G=(Q,E)
be the graph described in Definition
3.4
. Let
e=(q,q^{\prime})\in E
have the label
a\,|\,0
, and let
f=(p,p^{\prime})\in E
have the label
cba\,|\,00d
. Then

(1)

(\imath q,\imath q^{\prime})\in E
and its label is
(\imath a)\,|\,0
;

(2)

(\overline{q},\imath\overline{q^{\prime}})\in E
and its label is
\overline{a}\,|\,0
;

(3)

(\imath p,\imath p^{\prime})\in E
and its label is
(\imath c)(\imath b)(\imath a)\,|\,00(\imath d)
;

(4)

(\overline{p},\overline{\imath p^{\prime}})\in E
and its label is
(-\overline{c})(\imath\overline{b})\overline{a}\,|\,00\overline{d}
.

Proof.

The fact that
e=(q,q^{\prime})\in E
has the label
a\,|\,0
means that
a+q=q^{\prime}\beta\in\beta Q
. By multiplication with
\imath
and by complex conjugation, and due to
\overline{\beta}=\imath\beta
, we get

\imath a+\imath q=\imath q^{\prime}\beta
and
\overline{a}+\overline{q}=\overline{q^{\prime}}\overline{\beta}=\imath\overline{q^{\prime}}\beta
,

which proves the claims (1) and (2).

The fact that
f=(p,p^{\prime})\in E
has the label
cba\,|\,00d
means that
c\beta^{2}+b\beta+a+p=p^{\prime}\beta^{3}+d\in\beta^{3}Q+\{\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
. Hence

\imath c\beta^{2}+\imath b\beta+\imath a+\imath p=\imath p^{\prime}\beta^{3}+\imath d\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{c}\overline{\beta^{2}}+\overline{b}\overline{\beta}+\overline{a}+\overline{p}=-\overline{c}\beta^{2}+\imath\overline{b}\beta+\overline{a}+\overline{p}=\overline{p^{\prime}}\overline{\beta}^{3}+\overline{d}=\overline{\imath p^{\prime}}\beta^{3}+\overline{d},

which proves the claims (3) and (4).
∎

The symmetries of the transducer, as shown above, enable to simplify the description of its graph

3.3.
Restriction of the Transducer to Accept Only Optimal Representations

Let us recall that a
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representation of a Gaussian integer
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
is called optimal, if the number of non-zero digits in the representation equals the number of non-zero digits in the
3
-NAF representation of
x
.

Example 3.8
.

The string
100\underline{\imath}
is the
3
-NAF representation of the Gaussian integer
x=2+\imath
. It is easy to check that the strings
\imath 0\imath
and
\underline{\imath}1
also represent the same number
x
. All the three representations of
x
are optimal, as they have the minimal Hamming weight
3
.

In this section, we introduce a subgraph
\widetilde{G}
of the graph
G
presented in Definition
3.4
, which shall transform any optimal representation of an arbitrary Gaussian integer
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
into
3
-NAF representation, and, at the same time, none of the non-optimal representations is accepted by this subgraph
\widetilde{G}
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
e=(q,q^{\prime})\in E
be an edge of the graph
G
with label
In(e)\,|\,Out(e)
. The number of non-zero digits in
In(e)
minus the number of non-zero digits in
Out(e)
is called the weight of
e
, and denoted by
w(e)
. The weight
w(P)
of an oriented path
P
in
G
is defined as the sum of weights of all edges in
P
.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
In(e)\,|\,Out(e)
be the label of an edge
e=(q,q^{\prime})
in graph
G
.

(1)

If
In(e)\,|\,Out(e)=a\,|\,0
, then obviously
w(e)=|a|\in\{0,1\}
.

(2)

If
In(e)\,|\,Out(e)=cba\,|\,00d
, then
w(e)\in\{-1,0,1,2\}
, as
d\in\{\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
and
a,b,c\in\mathcal{D}
. Moreover,

•

w(e)=-1
only if
a=b=c=0
;

•

w(e)=0
if exactly two digits among
a,b,c
are zero;

•

w(e)=1
if exactly one digit among
a,b,c
is zero; and

•

w(e)=2
if
a,b,c\neq 0
.

The fact that the
3
-NAF representation of a Gaussian integer
x
contains the minimal number of non-zero digits among all representations of
x
can be reformulated as a property of the graph
G
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
P
be an oriented path in the graph
G
such that
P
starts and ends in the vertex
q_{0}=0
. Then the weight of
P
is non-negative.

Proof.

By Remark
3.5
, every path
P
in
G
which starts and ends in
q_{0}=0
corresponds to transformation of a general
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representation of a Gaussian integer to its
3
-NAF representation. By Theorem
2.4
, the weight of
P
is non-negative.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
P
be a path in the graph
G
starting in vertex
u
and ending in vertex
v
, and let
u^{\prime}\in[u]
. Then there exists a path
P^{\prime}
in
G
of the same length and the same weight as
P
, such that
P^{\prime}
starts in
u^{\prime}
and ends in some
v^{\prime}\in[v]
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
3.6
, as any transformation of an edge by means of complex conjugation or multiplication with
\imath
preserves the equivalence classes of vertices forming the edge, and also preserves the weight of the edge.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
q_{0}e_{1}q_{1}e_{2}q_{2}e_{3}q_{3}\cdots e_{\ell}q_{\ell}
be a path in the graph
G
which transforms an optimal
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representation of
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
into the
3
-NAF representation. Let
j,n\in\mathbb{N},0\leq j<n\leq\ell
,
u\in[q_{j}]
and
v\in[q_{n}]
.
Then no path (of any length) in
G
starting in
u
and ending in
v
has weight strictly smaller than the weight of the path
q_{j}e_{j+1}q_{j+1}e_{j+2}q_{j+2}\cdots e_{n}q_{n}
.

Proof.

As the path
P=q_{0}e_{1}q_{1}e_{2}q_{2}e_{3}q_{3}\cdots e_{\ell}q_{\ell}
transforms an optimal representation to the
3
-NAF representation, its weight must be
w(P)=0
, and the vertices
q_{0}=0
and
q_{\ell}=0
. Let us split the path
P
into three parts:

P_{1}=q_{0}e_{1}q_{1}e_{2}q_{2}\cdots e_{j}q_{j}\,,\quad P_{2}=q_{j}e_{j+1}q_{j+1}e_{j+2}q_{j+2}\cdots e_{n}q_{n}\,,\quad P_{3}=q_{n}e_{n+1}q_{n+1}e_{n+2}q_{n+2}\cdots e_{\ell}q_{\ell}.

Clearly,
w(P_{1})+w(P_{2})+w(P_{3})=w(P)=0
.

Assume, contrary to what we aim to prove, that there exists a path
P_{2}^{\prime}
starting in
u
and ending in
v
such that
w(P_{2}^{\prime})<w(P_{2})
.

By Lemma
3.12
, there exists a path
P^{\prime\prime}_{2}
starting in
q_{j}\in[u]
and ending in some vertex
v^{\prime}\in[v]=[q_{n}]
such that
w(P^{\prime\prime}_{2})=w(P^{\prime}_{2})
. Due to the same lemma, there exists a path
P^{\prime}_{3}
starting in

and ending in vertex
q_{l}=0
such that
w(P^{\prime}_{3})=w(P_{3})
.

Since the ending vertex of
P_{1}
coincides with the starting vertex of
P^{\prime\prime}_{2}
, and the ending vertex of

coincides with the starting vertex of

, the concatenation of the three parts produces a new path
P_{1}P^{\prime\prime}_{2}P_{3}^{\prime}
starting and ending in vertex

. The weight of this new path is

w(P_{1})+w(P^{\prime\prime}_{2})+w(P_{3}^{\prime})=w(P_{1})+w(P^{\prime}_{2})+w(P_{3})<w(P_{1})+w(P_{2})+w(P_{3})=0\,,

which is a contradiction with Lemma
3.11
.
∎

A path

We recall the notion of the recognisable series as introduced in

and

.

3.5.
Computer Experiments on the Number of Optimal Representations

Even though we mostly focus on calculating the number of optimal representations in the numeration system (1), let us now regard the number of all representations of a given number
x
in a numeration system with
\beta
being an algebraic integer, imaginary quadratic, and let
\mathcal{D}
be its minimal norm representative digit set. Of course, a string
d_{N}d_{N-1}\cdots d_{0}
represents the same number as the string
0d_{N}d_{N-1}\cdots d_{0}
. In the following lemma, we shall consider only so-called normalized representations, whose leading coefficient is non-zero. If
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
is a minimal norm representative digit set for
\beta
, then no representation of
x=0
contains any non-zero digit. Hence, by the previous convention, the representation of
x=0
is only the empty string.

Lemma 3.25
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer, imaginary quadratic, and let
\mathcal{D}
be its minimal norm representative digit set.
For a given
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
r(x,k)
the number of normalized
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representations of
x
using exactly
k
non-zero digits and having non-zero leading coefficient.

•

Then
r(0,0)=1
and
r(0,k+1)=0
.

•

If
x\neq 0
, then
r(x,0)=0
and

r\left(x,k+1\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r\left(\frac{x}{\beta},k+1\right)&\text{if }\beta\text{ divides }x\,,\\
&\\
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathcal{D}\\
\beta|(x-d)\end{subarray}}r\left(\frac{x-d}{\beta},k\right)&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By Definition
2.2
, if
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
d\neq 0
, then
\beta
does not divide
d
. Hence, if
\beta
divides
x
, then necessarily the rightmost digit of any representation of
x
must be
d_{0}=0
.

If
\beta
does not divide
x
and a digit
d\in\mathcal{D}
has the property
\beta|(x-d)
, then any string
s^{\prime}
containing
k
non-zero digits and representing the number
x^{\prime}=\frac{x-d}{\beta}
can be used to compose a string
s^{\prime}d
, which represents the number
x
in
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
.
∎

Example 3.26
.

Let us consider
\beta=\imath-1

\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}

3.6.
Gaussian Integers with Maximal Number of Optimal Representations

In this part, we derive an upper bound on the number of different optimal
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representations of a Gaussian integer
x
, depending on the number of non-zero digits in its
3
-NAF representation. We also show that this upper bound is sharp. In the case of binary signed digit representations (in base
\beta=2
) introduced by G. W. Reitwiesner, the sharp upper bound is given by the sequence of Fibonacci numbers. In the numeration system we consider here, the analogous crucial role is played by the sequence
(r_{N})_{N\geq 0}
defined recursively, as follows:

(13)

\begin{array}[]{l}r_{0}=r_{1}=1\,,\ r_{2}=3\,,\ r_{3}=8\,,\ r_{4}=17\,,\\
r_{N+3}=r_{N+2}+2r_{N+1}+2r_{N}\quad\text{ for each }N\in\mathbb{N}\,,N\geq 2\,.\end{array}

Several initial elements of the sequence
(r_{N})_{N\geq 1}
coincide with the numbers listed in Table
3
. The roots of the characteristic polynomial
X^{3}-X^{2}-2X-2
of the linear recurrent sequence
(r_{N})_{N\geq 1}
are
\lambda_{1}\sim 2.26953
and
\lambda_{2}=\overline{\lambda_{3}}
with
|\lambda_{2}|<1
. Hence
r_{N}=c_{1}\lambda_{1}^{N-1}+o(1)
, where
c_{1}\sim 1.47313
.

Theorem 3.28
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N},N\geq 1
, and let us consider
(\beta,\mathcal{D})
-representations of Gaussian integers in base
\beta=\imath-1
and alphabet
\mathcal{D}=\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
.

(1)

If the
3
-NAF representation of a Gaussian integer
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
contains exactly
N
non-zero digits, then
x
has at most
r_{N}
optimal representations.

(2)

For
N\geq 4
, there are exactly 16 different Gaussian integers
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\imath]\setminus\beta\mathbb{Z}[\imath]
whose
3
-NAF representation contains just
N
non-zero digits and which have
r_{N}
optimal representations.

The proof of Theorem
3.28
is split into several steps. In order to prove Theorem
3.28
, we have to determine, according to Proposition
3.24
, the maximum

(14)

\mathrm{Max}(N)=\max\Bigl\{\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}:d_{j}\in\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}\text{ and }\sum_{j}|d_{j}|=N\Bigr\}\,.

The number
\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}
can be expressed as a product of the row vector
\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{k}}
and the column vector
A_{d_{k+1}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}
.

We shall study which choices of the indices
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}
result in the largest components of the row vector. All matrices
A_{d_{j}}
have non-negative elements, and the upper left corner element is
1
. Therefore, also the first component of the row vector
\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{k}}
as well as of the column vector
A_{d_{k+1}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}
is
\geq 1
.

Definition 3.29
.

Let
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
be row vectors from
\mathbb{Z}^{9}
.

We denote by
\vec{u}\leq\vec{v}
when the
j^{th}
vector components satisfy
(\vec{u})_{j}\leq(\vec{v})_{j}
for each
j\in 1,\ldots,9
.

We say that
\vec{u}
is majorised by
\vec{v}
, if there exists a permutation matrix
P\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\vec{u}P\leq\vec{v}
, and we denote this fact by
\vec{u}\preceq\vec{v}
. If, moreover, the first vector components satisfy
(\vec{u}P)_{1}<(\vec{v})_{1}
, we say that
\vec{u}
is strictly majorised by
\vec{v}
, and denote that by
\vec{u}\prec\vec{v}
.

When
\vec{u}\preceq\vec{v}
and
\vec{v}\preceq\vec{u}
, we denote it by
\vec{u}\sim\vec{v}
.

It is easy to verify that the relation
\preceq
is a partial ordering on
\mathbb{Z}^{9}
, and the relation
\sim
is an equivalence on
\mathbb{Z}^{9}
.

Lemma 3.30
.

Consider
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}|d_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|f_{j}|
.

If the vector
\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{k}}
is strictly majorised by
\vec{e}A_{f_{1}}A_{f_{2}}\cdots A_{f_{m}}
, then each choice of indices
d_{k+1},\ldots,d_{n}\in\{0,\pm 1,\pm\imath\}
satisfies

\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}<\mathrm{Max}(N)\,,\text{ where $N=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|d_{j}|$}.

Proof.

We denote by
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
, respectively, the row vectors
\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{k}}
and
\vec{e}A_{f_{1}}A_{f_{2}}\cdots A_{f_{m}}
; and we denote by
\vec{w}
the column vector
A_{d_{k+1}}A_{d_{k+2}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}
. The first component of each of these three vectors is positive.

As
\vec{u}\prec\vec{v}
, there exists a permutation matrix
P\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\vec{u}P\leq\vec{v}
and
(\vec{u}P)_{1}<(\vec{v})_{1}
. Obviously,

\vec{e}A_{d_{1}}A_{d_{2}}\cdots A_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}=\vec{u}\cdot\vec{w}=(\vec{u}P)\cdot(P^{\top}\vec{w})<\vec{v}\cdot(P^{\top}\vec{w})\,.

Now, it is enough to show that
\vec{v}\cdot(P^{\top}\vec{w})\leq\mathrm{Max}(N)
.

By Remark
3.23
, we have the equality
P^{\top}\vec{w}=A_{h_{k+1}}\cdots A_{h_{n}}Q\vec{e}^{\top}
for a permutation matrix
Q\in\mathcal{P}
and some indices
h_{k+1},\ldots,h_{n}
. Moreover,
|h_{j}|=|d_{j}|
for each
j=k+1,\ldots,n
and
Q\vec{e}^{\top}=\vec{e}^{\top}
.
Hence

\vec{v}\cdot(P^{\top}\vec{w})=(\vec{e}A_{f_{1}}A_{f_{2}}\cdots A_{f_{m}})\cdot(A_{h_{k+1}}\cdots A_{h_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top})\leq\mathrm{Max}(N)\,.

In the last inequality, we used the fact that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}|d_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|f_{j}|+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}|h_{j}|=N
.
∎

Lemma 3.31
.

Let
N\in\{2,3,4\}
and

Sequence
(\vec{t}_{N})
of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{7}
:

Computer experiments suggested which Eisenstein integers with
N
non-zero digits in their
2
-NAF have the maximal number of optimal representations. To specify these
2
-NAF representations, we adopt again the convention that
\underline{d}
stands for the digit
-d
, and denote

g=010\omega 0\underline{\omega}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h=0\underline{1}0\underline{\omega}0\omega^{2}\,.

We observed that if the
2
-NAF representation of an Eisenstein integer
x
is a prefix of the infinite purely periodic word
\mathcal{S}=g\,h\,g\,h\,g\,h\,g\,h\cdots
or of the infinite periodic word
g\,\mathcal{S}=g\,g\,h\,g\,h\,g\,h\,g\,h\cdots
, then
x
has the maximal number of optimal representations.

Definition of the vectors
\vec{t}_{N}
reflects the form of the infinite word
\mathcal{S}
. Using the period
g\,h
of the word
\mathcal{S}
, and due to relation

A_{+1}A_{+\omega}A_{-\omega^{2}}A_{-1}A_{-\omega}A_{+\omega^{2}}=(A_{1}R)(A_{-1}R)(A_{-1}R)(A_{1}R)(A_{-1}R)(A_{-1}R)\,,

we define the sequence
(\vec{t}_{N})
of row vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{7}
recursively as follows:
\vec{t}_{0}=\vec{e}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
, and for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
we put

\vec{t}_{N+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vec{t}_{N}A_{1}R&\text{ if }\ N\equiv 0\mod{3}\,;\\
\vec{t}_{N}A_{-1}R&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Elements of the vector
\vec{t}_{N}
can be expressed by using the sequence
s_{N}=\lfloor\frac{1}{7}(6\cdot 2^{N}+1)\rfloor
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
s_{N}=0
for every
N<0
. By induction on
N
, one can prove for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
that

\vec{t}_{N}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(s_{N},2s_{N-3},s_{N-3},2s_{N-3},0,s_{N-1},0)&\text{ if }\ N\equiv 0\mod{3}\,;\\
(s_{N},0,s_{N-1},0,s_{N-2},s_{N-2},s_{N-2})&\text{ if }\ N\equiv 1\mod{3}\,;\\
(s_{N},s_{N-2},s_{N-2},s_{N-2},0,s_{N-1},0)&\text{ if }\ N\equiv 2\mod{3}\,.\end{array}\right.

The described explicit form of
\vec{t}_{N}
enables to show for
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
N>0
, that

(20)

\begin{array}[]{llllll}\vec{t}_{N}A_{d}&\prec&\vec{t}_{N}A_{1}&\sim\ \vec{t}_{N+1}&\text{\ \ if \ $N\equiv 0\!\!\!\mod{3}\,$}\quad\text{and}&\text{ $d\neq 1$,}\\
\vec{t}_{N}A_{d}&\prec&\vec{t}_{N}A_{-1}&\sim\ \vec{t}_{N+1}&\text{\ \ if \ $N\equiv 1\!\!\!\mod{3}\,$}\quad\text{and}&\text{ $d\neq-1$,}\\
\vec{t}_{N}A_{d}&\prec&\vec{t}_{N}A_{-1}&\sim\ \vec{t}_{N+1}&\text{\ \ if \ $N\equiv 2\!\!\!\mod{3}\,$}\quad\text{and}&\text{ $d\neq\pm 1$,}\\
\vec{t}_{N}A_{1}A_{d}&\prec&\vec{t}_{N}A_{-1}A_{1}&\sim\ \vec{t}_{N+2}&\text{\ \ if \ $N\equiv 2\!\!\!\mod{3}\,$},&\\
\end{array}

where the relation
\prec
is introduced analogously to Definition
3.29
with permutation set
\mathcal{P}=\{R^{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

The
2
-NAF representation of
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
can be viewed as a string
u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}\cdots u_{n}
over the alphabet

(21)

\mathcal{C}=\{0\}\cup\bigl\{0d:d\in\{\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\}\bigr\}\,.

If
d_{\ell}
denotes the last letters of
u_{\ell}
for every
\ell=1,2,\ldots,n
, then the number of non-zero digits in the
2
-NAF representation is
|d_{1}|+|d_{2}|+\cdots+|d_{n}|
and the number of optimal representations of
x
equals

\vec{e}{A}_{d_{1}}{A}_{d_{2}}\cdots{A}_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top},\ \text{ where }\vec{e}=(1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{7}\,.

Fix
N=|d_{1}|+|d_{2}|+\cdots+|d_{n}|
. Relation (
20
) implies
\vec{e}{A}_{d_{1}}{A}_{d_{2}}\cdots{A}_{d_{n}}\vec{e}^{\top}\leq\vec{t}_{N}\vec{e}^{\top}=s_{N}
, which proves the first part of Theorem
4.1
. The second part of the theorem is a consequence of the fact that
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and
\lambda^{k}x
have, due to symmetries of
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, the same number of optimal representations. Thus, if we multiply digits of a prefix of the infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}
or
u\mathcal{S}
by
\lambda^{k}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,5
we get
2
-NAF representation of an Eisenstein integer with maximal number of optimal representations.
