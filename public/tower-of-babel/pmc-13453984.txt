Life-threatening fetal goiter: diagnosis and outcomes in four consecutive cases with critical appraisal—why this diagnosis is not obviously trivial
Authors: Jann Lennard Scharf, Michael Gembicki, Amrei Welp, Guido Stichtenoth, Verena Sailer, Jan Weichert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe diagnosis of a fetal goiter constitutes a potentially life-threatening in utero condition for the fetus. Even with early diagnosis and close therapy with highly specialized interdisciplinary management, the prognosis remains poor. We discuss diagnostic and therapeutic challenges regarding the pre- and postnatal outcome based on four consecutive cases with a critical appraisal of latest heterogeneous recommendations in literature.MethodsA retrospective review of four cases of fetal goiter is presented that were diagnosed at a tertiary university centre during targeted ultrasound (US) examination in second and third trimester. All cases underwent close and intensive pre- and postnatal diagnostics and treatment. A detailed workup of current diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations with identifying potential pitfalls was carried out.Case seriesIn one case, the mother suffered from Graves’ disease with euthyroid blood parameters under medication. The other three gravidae largely had an uneventful medical history including euthyroid metabolism. In all cases, fetal hypothyroidism was suspected and in three of them, cordocentesis was performed and intraamniotic levothyroxine (LT4) was given. In none of the cases, sonographic signs of tracheoesophageal compression had been noticed. In all four cases, a planned cesarean section was performed between 32 and 39 weeks of gestation (weeks). Out of all four cases, only one fetus managed to survive: one fetus died antenatally at 35 weeks, one newborn died due to insufficient ventilation, and one due to fetal cardiac decompensation with bilateral hydrothorax with suspected ductal obstruction. The fetus who survived showed primary hypothyroidism. Two of the deceased fetuses underwent autopsy, one showed ectopic thyroid tissue in the trachea.ConclusionPrenatal diagnosis of congenital fetal goiter remains a serious condition in specialized centers. In practice, the following crucial considerations might impede proper application of the non-standardized recommendations suggested in available literature. In addition to the assessment of the underlying etiology, the evaluation of fetal cervical structures with determination of fetal tracheoesophageal patency and cardiac functional diagnostics requires adequate practical expertise. Interdisciplinary cooperation is mandatory when determining the time and mode of delivery and planning an ex utero intrapartum treatment (EXIT) strategy.

What does this study add to the clinical work?

This study highlights the need for interdisciplinary collaboration of fetal goiter with early referral to specialized centers, to guide prenatal treatment. Reporting unfavorable outcomes alongside successful fetal therapies may help identify critical pitfalls to improve perinatal management.

Background

Definition, incidence, and epidemiology

Fetal goiter is referred to as an abnormal enlargement of the fetal thyroid gland, which can occur independently of thyroid function but is frequently associated with functional abnormalities [1–3]. With a reported range of approximately 1 in 30,000 to 50,000 pregnancies, fetal goiter is an extremely rare pathology [1, 4–7]. The more easily determined incidence of congenital hypothyroidism is estimated at 1 in 2,000 to 5,000 live births [3, 8, 9]. Among those fetuses with congenital hypothyroidism, fetal goiter affects abou 3 to 15% [10, 11]. With a ratio of 1:2, female fetuses are affected more often than males [12]. Since its antenatal diagnosis by US first described in 1980, several case reports have been published, especially in the recent years [13].

Etiology

Fetal goiters arise primarily from disruptions in thyroid hormone production or regulation during fetal development [1]. Primary contributing factors include genetic mutations resulting in dyshormonogenesis, pregnancy-related changes like maternal autoimmune disorders or exposure to antithyroid medications during pregnancy [1–3]. As a heterogeneous disorder, thyroid dyshormonogenesis-associated congenital hypothyroidism is caused by genetic defects in any of the known thyroid-specific pathways involved in hormone biosynthesis [14–16]. Fetal goiters are typically accompanied by the clinical appearance of congenital hypothyroidism (goitrous hypothyroidism) [2, 6, 17]. The majority of cases with congenital goiters occur in mothers with known thyroid disorder, commonly Graves’ disease, whereas in cases without maternal thyroid pathology, dyshormonogenesis is the main cause [17, 18].

Clinical presentation, manifestation, and typical diagnostic findings

Fetal goiters can be associated with both, hypo- and hyperthyroidism, or even with euthyroid state [1, 2, 4–6, 9]. Although the diagnosis is initially made by prenatal US in the second or third trimester routine scan, detailed diagnosis with subtyping the underlying cause is only possible in combination with an evaluation of the mother's medical history and cordocentesis for the etiological assessment of fetal thyroid function [2, 18]. US typically reveals a symmetrical, homogeneous, echogenic, and hypervascularized mass in the anterior cervical region [2, 5, 17, 19].

Complications

The thyroid pathology of fetal goiters is a life-threatening condition with potentially fatal consequences such as fetal demise and neonatal death [2, 4, 11, 12]. Due to mass effects and its anatomical location, this disorder may cause mechanical complications including compression of cervical structures with respiratory distress by airway obstruction. Pharyngeal occlusion and hyperextension of the fetal neck with impairment of swallowing result in polyhydramnios and can considerably contribute to preterm birth. Moreover, cardinal movements of fetal descent could be impaired, leading to malpresentation, and dystocia during vaginal delivery may occur. As a result of thyroid dysfunction, fetal goiter can lead to metabolic complications like severe neurodevelopmental impairment with mental retardation. In addition, it can cause heart failure and fetal growth restriction [1–6, 9, 17, 20].

Treatment options

The management of fetal goiter is complex and depends on its etiology [3, 5]. In hypothyroid origin, if intrauterine treatment is considered that may reduce perinatal complications, cordocentesis is the most accurate method for diagnosing thyroid gland dysfunction in the fetus [4, 11]. Several reports have demonstrated the feasibility and safety of intraamniotic treatment since its first application in 1977, but no guidelines with standardized treatment for fetal goiter, especially for hypothyroidism, have been established yet [4, 5, 11, 12, 18, 20–22].

Case presentations

Four consecutive cases of fetal goiter, diagnosed and treated between 2016 and 2024 in our department, were reviewed retrospectively. This paper focuses on a holistic approach including US assessment, determination of fetal and maternal thyroid status, prenatal care, and feto-maternal outcomes against the context of missing evidence-based guidelines. US examinations were performed using high-end US systems equipped with high-resolution probes: General Electric (GE) Voluson™ E8 and E10 Expert (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), Philips iU22 (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam/Best, The Netherlands), Samsung UGEO WS80A Elite, Hera W10 and Hera Z20 (Samsung Medison, Seoul, South Korea), and Toshiba Aplio 500 and i800 (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Ōtawara, Tochigi, Japan). Case no. 1

A 38-year-old primigravida with known Graves’ disease, treated with propylthiouracil (PTU) in the first and thiamazole in the second trimester, was referred to our prenatal diagnostic center at 35 + 5 weeks due to the suspicion of a newly detected symmetrical, echogenic, and hypervascularized anterior cervical mass consistent with fetal goiter (Fig. 1). With the exception of polyhydramnios, no sonographic signs of cervical compression were detected. Fetal swallowing appeared unaffected. No further complementary imaging or invasive prenatal testing was undertaken due to advanced gestational age in accordance with the gravida. Delivery was performed by cesarean section at 38 + 0 weeks after premature rupture of membranes with prompt neonatal intensive care. The male neonate died immediately after birth due to ventilatory failure. Postmortem examination revealed ectopic hyperplastic thyroid tissue in the submucosa of the trachea resulting in a stenotic segment measuring 5 mm in length and a remaining residual diameter of 2 mm (Fig. 1d–f). Case no. 2

A 31-year-old gravida 5, para 1 was referred at 25 + 1 weeks following the sonographic detection of a fetal anterior neck mass. A routine second-trimester scan in our department was normal. Targeted US demonstrated a symmetrical, echogenic, hypervascularized bilobular thyroid enlargement (Fig. 2), consistent with fetal goiter with polyhydramnios, but no impairment of swallowing. Maternal history included spontaneous delivery and three interruptions. In the third trimester, maternal mild central hypothyroidism was suspected, and the gravida was treated with LT4. A complementary fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan performed at 26 + 5 weeks confirmed diffuse thyroid enlargement. Assessment of tracheoesophageal patency was difficult, but no compression was assumed. A cordocentesis and amniocentesis with amniotic drainage and karyotyping were performed. Fetal blood sampling indicated a thyroid dyshormonogenesis-associated congenital hypothyroidism. Due to the increase in thyroid volume, intra-amniotic LT4 therapy was initiated and repeated twice. Serial US examinations were performed to monitor effectiveness of treatment by fetal thyroid size and volume. Delivery occurred at 34 + 3 weeks by cesarean section due to preterm prelabor rupture of membranes, resulting in a liveborn male infant. Case no. 3

A 22-year-old primigravida was referred at 20 + 5 weeks for evaluation of a fetal anterior neck mass detected on routine outpatient prenatal care. US findings were consistent with a symmetric fetal goiter, showing echogenic, hypervascularized bilobular enlargement without polyhydramnios or sonographic signs of tracheal or esophageal compression (Fig. 3). Maternal history was unremarkable, and no thyroid disease was known. Laboratory results suggested latent maternal hypothyroidism. Fetal MRI was recommended but declined. Diagnostic cordocentesis and amniocentesis with karyotyping were performed. As the underlying etiological correlate, thyroid dyshormonogenesis-associated congenital hypothyroidism was suspected. Because of progressive goiter enlargement with development of mild hyperextension of the neck, intraamniotic LT4 treatment was initiated, with a total of three administrations. The effectiveness of treatment was closely monitored by serial US examinations measuring fetal thyroid size and volume. In the third trimester, prenatal US revealed signs indicative of congestive heart failure with fetal myocardial hypertrophy and a bilateral hydrothorax, consistent with constriction of the ductus arteriosus. Therefore, delivery was indicated at 31 + 1 weeks due to impaired fetal cardiac function by cesarean section with immediate neonatal intensive care. The female neonate died within an hour after birth due to insufficient ventilation with subsequent cardiovascular failure. Postmortem examination confirmed a constricted ductus arteriosus Botalli and a circumferential goiter with complete encirclement of the trachea, but without luminal stenosis. Case no. 4

A 28-year-old gravida 2, para 1 presented at 27 + 5 weeks after a suspected fetal goiter detected during routine third-trimester US. Maternal history included one Cesarean section, nicotine abuse, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety and borderline personality disorder, depression, gastric bypass, and polytoxicomania with multiple psychotropic medications. The gravida was euthyroid during pregnancy. First-trimester screening and second-trimester anomaly scan were unremarkable. At diagnosis, US demonstrated a dystrophic growth-restricted fetus with a symmetrical enlargement of the fetal thyroid gland with increased echogenicity and hypervascularization, accompanied by polyhydramnios and hyperextension of the fetal neck, but functioning deglutition (Fig. 4). The tracheal lumen appeared to be unaffected. A single umbilical artery was incidentally detected. Despite recommendations for further imaging, the gravida initially declined additional diagnostics by fetal MRI and cordocentesis due to ambivalence and limited compliance. As a result, amniocentesis was the only procedure performed. However, in further course of treatment, the gravida agreed to a cordocentesis, which was subsequently performed, revealing findings consistent with thyroid dyshormonogenesis-associated congenital hypothyroidism. Intraamniotic LT4 therapy was initiated due to progressive thyroid enlargement, with total four administrations. US was used for closely monitoring with serial assessment of fetal cardiac function showing a stable right ventricular modified myocardial performance index (RV-Mod-MPI 0.435 to 0.508) indicating no signs of cardiac failure. Despite these efforts, intrauterine fetal demise occurred at 34 + 2 weeks. Cesarean delivery was performed at maternal request. Discussion

There have been only limited reports of goitrous hypothyroidism [10] mainly focusing on successful courses of treatment with favorable neonatal outcomes. As important as collecting favorable outcomes by fetal therapy, as propagated by Tamura et al., to establish a therapeutic standard, is the registration of unfavorable outcomes focusing on crucial pitfalls [4]. The majority of reports and reviews emphasize successful fetal therapies with high survival rates and described a significant reduction in thyroid gland volume with improvement of associated obstetric morbidity [11, 12, 17, 18, 23–26], which does not correspond with our clinical experience. For example, Nemescu et al. identified preterm delivery as the most common adverse outcome [12]. Others consider exophthalmos or neonatal tachycardia as serious complications [27]. However, the fact that adverse outcomes can occur even in highly specialized perinatal centers despite the maximum possible interdisciplinary management is often not mentioned and can be mentally challenging for a well-trained medical team. The consequences of fetal goiter can be complex and at worst, culminate in fetal demise and neonatal death. As Vasudevan et al. have also noted, there is a trend toward underreporting of adverse outcomes [28]. We therefore present four consecutive cases of life-threatening fetal goiter with varying manifestations diagnosed and treated between 2016 and 2024, of which only one neonate survived. We focus on critical appraisal of current therapeutic recommendations and identifying potential pitfalls. Over the past quarter century, we identified less than ten cases with fetal goiter mentioned rather casually associated with intrauterine fetal death or neonatal death [28–31]. The case published by Vasudevan et al. stressed that the intrauterine demise was related to intraamniotic triiodothyronine (T3) administration, but in the absence of post-mortem examination, the exact cause of death can also only be conjectured [28].

Diagnosis by thorough ultrasound examination

Before initiation of fetal treatment, an appropriate diagnosis by comprehensive US examination is required and both, maternal and fetal thyroid status should be thoroughly evaluated in all cases of suspected fetal goiter [17]. Maternal thyroid function during gestation should be assessed using trimester-specific reference ranges [32]. Genetic testing is not routinely required in fetal goiter but should be considered in the absence of a maternal cause, suspected dyshormonogenesis, positive family history, or additional anomalies [20, 21, 33]. To estimate potential peri- and postnatal complications, the tracheoesophageal patency by measuring the diameter of the trachea and assessing the fetal swallowing pattern should be evaluated. In addition, the fetal cardiac function, which may be impaired by intrathyroid vascular shunting resulting in high-output cardiac failure, should be determined, and the integrity of common cervical vessels like the carotid arteries and internal jugular veins including evaluation of lateral displacement should be examined [18, 34]. It remains to be discussed whether a fetal MRI could provide any additional diagnostic value. However, its complementary use to sonography is often described [11, 12, 17, 18, 20, 28, 35]. One of the rare cases of fetal goiter with tracheal obstruction despite intrauterine treatment reported to date was published by Mastrolia et al., who emphasize the technical difficulty of performing and evaluating tracheal patency using fetal MRI [18]. For best therapeutic management, an all-embracing diagnosis as early as possible is mandatory.

Importance of three-dimensional (3D) ultrasound

Today, well-established as a standard technique in the diagnostic workflow, the acquisition of a three-dimensional (3D) volume of a fetal goiter with subsequent rendering was still featured as the Picture of the Month in medical literature in 2005 [36]. Using 3D US in diagnosing fetal goiter is crucial and its advantages are obvious: 3D US permits definitive confirmation of the diagnosis of fetal goiter. It provides precise visualization of its spatial arrangement and extent within the fetal neck—possibly intrathoracically—and vascular architecture. Reconstruction via rendering and slicing of the recorded 3D volume provides valuable insights in the assessment of tracheoesophageal patency and surrounding tissue affection. The realistic images facilitate effective communication of the findings to the patient, and make them more understandable, thereby enhancing comprehension and compliance to essential therapies (Fig. 4c). Therefore, US with multiplanar imaging is instrumental in evaluating the severity and progression of the fetal goiter as well as in monitoring the therapeutic response [36]. During fetal organogenesis, the thyroid gland is the first endocrine organ detectable on prenatal US. It shows significant growth from the 12th weeks onwards. Active secretion of thyroid hormones begins around the middle of the second trimester, between the 16th and 17th weeks [5]. In general, fetal thyroid dysfunction can be detected from this period onwards [8]. Therefore, after detailed retrospective examination of case no. 4, the fetal cervical mass could have been identified as early as 21 + 5 weeks in the mid-sagittal plane, when applying the software 5D CNS + ™ (Samsung Healthcare, Gangwon-do, South Korea) as optional part of second-trimester routine scan (Fig. 5). Similar to other rarely published cases, it could also have been one of the earliest diagnosed cases of fetal goiter [21, 24, 37–41]. Distinguishing between fetal hypo- and hyperthyroidism by analyzing the vascularization pattern and characteristic sonographic findings

Differentiating fetal goiter due to hypo- or from hyperthyroidism is a clinical challenge that requires comprehensive in utero evaluation and treatment [11]. It is known that fetal hyperthyroidism is much rarer than fetal hypothyroidism [6]. Therefore, in cases of fetal goiter in euthyroid gravidae, as predominantly in this case series, it is highly probable that dyshormonogenesis is causal [27]. While polyhydramnios is unspecifically related to the volume of a compressive fetal neck mass, several indirect findings are indicative of an altered fetal thyroid metabolism [17, 30]. Luton et al. suggested deducing the type of dysfunction of fetal goiters from the assessment of the vascularization pattern of the thyroid gland [31, 42]. Huel et al. developed a US scoring system which analyzes the following parameters in addition to vascularization: bone maturation, fetal heart rate, and fetal movements [30]. Further common US features could include fetal growth restriction (FGR), craniosynostosis, and heart failure [11, 17, 30, 43, 44]. Although, according to Huel et al., this scoring system already has correctly classified 36 cases of congenital hypo- or hyperthyroidism, we evaluate the clinical benefit of this score rather cautiously, even though its items are pathophysiologically comprehensible [30]. In clinical practice, this score would not have enabled us to determine the underlying etiology of fetal goiter. In none of the presented cases, we observed any pathognomonic signs on US indicating the type of thyroid dysfunction. We agree with other authors that the practical applicability of this US scoring system is rather limited [11, 21, 36].

Treatment of fetal goiter with focus on thyroid replacement therapy

Specific treatment of fetal goiter depends on the subtype of thyroid dysfunction [3, 32]: in gravidae with hyperthyroidism and fetal hypothyroidism, maternal antithyroid medication is usually advised to be reduced or discontinued, and in cases with fetal hyperthyroidism, PTU is recommended as first-line medication. In gravidae with primary fetal hypothyroidism, antenatal thyroid hormone treatment with intraamniotic LT4 administration is the preferred treatment of choice [2]. Currently, optimal management of fetal goiter requires further attention, despite numerous case reports of successful fetal therapy. Standardized, evidence-based guidelines are lacking [4]. Reliable measurement of fetal thyroid hormone levels with direct quantification of fetal thyroid state for precise diagnosis and guiding early treatment should be made invasively by fetal blood sampling via cordocentesis rather than the assessment of amniotic fluid levels of thyroid hormones and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), because amniotic fluid LT4 concentration shows no correlation with fetal serum LT4 [2, 4, 9, 11, 12, 17, 18, 21, 36]. Patient-oriented counseling is mandatory. Delay et al. conclude that there is no indication for cordocentesis for diagnosis if fetal dyshormonogenesis suspected in euthyroid women. However, to determine the appropriate dosage of LT4, it could be used to estimate the fetal requirement for thyroid hormones [27]. Reference ranges for fetal thyroid hormones during pregnancy in fetal blood or amniotic fluid should be used for interpretation [45–49]. Because the transfer of maternal LT4 across the placenta is limited, the only effective treatment of fetal hypothyroidism is the substitution of LT4 into the intraamniotic cavity or via cordocentesis directly into the fetal blood circulation [11, 26, 36]. Different authors stated that even today, non-standardized recommendations of initiation of treatment, wide range dosage with different routes, varying numbers and intervals of administration are still valid [9, 11, 12, 17, 18, 23, 24]. The intraamniotic doses administered ranged commonly between 200 and 500 µg (a single dose of 250 µg appears to be preferred) at varying intervals (1 to 7 repetitions, weekly), consistently initiated in the third trimester. Usually, LT4 treatment should be initiated immediately once the diagnosis is confirmed and successful treatment within short interval of administration is reported [12, 23]. Our administered dosages at the chosen intervals, initiation of treatment, and intended delivery period were consistent with the published heterogeneous protocols (tab. 2).

Close monitoring of fetal goiter and response to fetal treatment

The optimal method to monitor fetal thyroid function is still debated controversially [17]. Various approaches are often used for therapeutic monitoring [6, 12, 25, 26]: serial US for follow-up of fetal goiter volume, and amniocentesis or cordocentesis for indirect or direct fetal thyroid hormone measurement. Although less invasive, the informative value of indirect detection of fetal thyroid status by determining TSH, T3, and T4 levels in amniotic fluid is limited. Therefore, to monitor fetal response to treatment and for accurate dose adjustment accordingly, assessment of fetal serum TSH and thyroid hormones by cordocentesis is still preferable [17]. According to most patients’ decisions against the repetitive procedure of serial cordocentesis with increased risk compared to amniocentesis, close monitoring of our cases was performed non-invasively using sonographic volume assessment of fetal goiter and invasively using amniocentesis during LT4 substitution. It should be noted that fetal thyroid volume correlates with gestational age and estimated fetal weight, showing an accelerated growth with advanced pregnancy, especially after 32 weeks. Ho et al. calculated the ratio of fetal thyroid volume to estimated fetal weight (V/W ratio) and found to be constant throughout gestation [50]. Consequently, nomograms with reference ranges of fetal thyroid size and volume throughout gestation facilitate the sonographic monitoring of fetal goiter treatment [50–53]. We recommend a weekly follow-up with advancing pregnancy after initial diagnosis.

Failure of intraamniotic treatment of fetal goiter and aspects of pharmacokinetics of LT4

In our presented cases with antenatal intraamniotic LT4 administration, there was a limited effect on both the decrease of fetal goiter volume and fetal thyroid hormone levels. It remains elusive, if any stabilization in size of fetal goiter should already be recognized as a therapeutic success? In addition, as mentioned above, physiological growth of the fetal thyroid gland throughout pregnancy should be considered [26]. These observations raise questions about the underlying causes of treatment failure: the dosage of LT4 may be too low and therefore inefficient, or swallowing may be impaired by goiter formation [18, 54]. It is assumed that the fetus ingests LT4 contained in the amniotic fluid through swallowing and mainly converts to T3 [6, 11, 20, 26, 55], but exact pharmacokinetics of human fetal LT4 uptake, absorption and conversion from amniotic fluid remain unclear [6, 12, 17, 23, 26]. Intramuscular or umbilical cord injection can be considered as alternative application routes, if deglutition is impaired [6, 11, 26, 54]. Switching to intraamniotic T3 administration could also be advantageous [11, 12, 26, 56]. In fact, our findings with subsequent considerations of causing failure are consistent with other authors, but differ from most published case reports, describing a significant decrease in goiter size [11, 17, 18, 20, 24, 27]. Furthermore, it should be noted that despite an intraamniotic LT4 administration, this effective treatment of goitrous hypothyroidism does not inevitably ensure euthyroidism at birth [10, 11, 20, 24]. Discussing treatment failure and effect of intraamniotic LT4 on prognosis, it must particularly be mentioned that the period between the last administration and delivery remains controversial [18, 21, 24].

Determination of time and mode of delivery

The entire course of therapy is accompanied by considerations concerning both timing and mode of delivery. It is the key determinant in fetal goiter management [18], especially in intrauterine treatment failure [11] and should preferably be planned at specialized centers [44]. Generally, there is no indication for an elective cesarean section, although it becomes more advisable depending on the expected risk for perinatal complications by mechanical impact of goiter. An EXIT procedure should be planned [11], even if it is argued that a delivery could be done without EXIT, since fetal thyroid tissue is soft tissue, which would not compromise neonatal intubation [18]. The deleterious outcome in our case no. 1 represents the exact opposite and is intended to be thought-provoking. Across most case reports with fetal goiter, the gestational age for delivery was 37th to 38th weeks [11, 12, 18, 23–25]. Based on current knowledge, there is no evidence-based rationale for forcing an earlier delivery without any indications of peripartum complications.

A critical reflection from the perspective of neonatology

In severe cases, fetal goiter can impact neonatal resuscitation, leading to extrinsic upper airway obstruction due to a large anterior neck mass, which can result in tracheal compression, deviation, and rarely, infiltration. This clinical situation may be associated with polyhydramnios or neck hyperextension. The presence of an open upper airway, particularly with regard to the subglottic structures, is of critical interest for neonatal resuscitation. This information needs to be specified using prenatal imaging techniques aiming clear identification of the presence of polyhydramnios, cervical hyperextension, a clear supra- and subglottic airway, and any tracheal deviation, compression or infiltration, and to assess the growth dynamics of the thyroid gland. If critical and specific information is unavailable, airway intervention strategies must be accessible. Additionally, it must be evaluated whether an EXIT procedure is warranted. Thus, the upper airways could be secured during persistent uteroplacental circulation before the umbilical cord is clamped. Diawtipsukon et al. reviewed 15 EXIT procedures in context with fetal goiters and similar cases and found a successful intubation in 58% [57]. Although the EXIT procedure therefore represents a promising alternative, securing the airway is only possible to a limited extent, and thus a degree of unpredictability remains despite prenatal imaging. Specific neonatal equipment for securing a difficult airway is needed, including small airway tubes, an airway exchange catheter with a diameter of less than 2 mm, a video laryngoscope and/or fiber-optic equipment (i.e., imaging techniques for the supraglottic airways). If the upper airway is extremely narrowed, creating a ‘pinhole’ effect, the lung could become overinflated due to a valve effect. This pathophysiological condition was most likely present in our case no. 1. Thus, active exhalation may be necessary during resuscitation. To this end, we have introduced the Ventrain system, which enables active exhalation as a backup option [58]. A team of pediatric surgeons must also be on standby to perform a tracheotomy if necessary. Not all goiters require an EXIT procedure. In case of a developing fetal goiter, pregnancies should be screened as early as 22nd weeks, allowing a timely in utero treatment [59]. Maintaining a correct maternal free thyroxine (fT4) level should protect the fetus from neurological damage. In the absence of a goiter, the long-term outcome of fetal hypothyroidism is normal. This conclusion is based on follow-up studies of neonatal hypothyroidism. If a goiter is present, the aim of intraamniotic treatment with LT4 is simply to restrict its growth. Whether and how long a treatment is needed is an individual decision [60]. If treatment is unsuccessful, thyroid growth may lead to a severe goiter. The long-term risks of severe fetal goiters include neurodevelopmental impairment, which is linked to prolonged hypothyroidism and hypoxic perinatal insult. The general risk of congenital hypothyroidism was recently reviewed by Mégier et al. [59]. The risk depends on the cause of the goiter. A Japanese follow-up study of thyroid function and morphology of 103 adults with goitrous congenital hypothyroidism concludes that most patients had genetic dyshormonogenesis and were at risk of developing thyroid nodules [61]. Neonates exposed to fetal hypothyroidism need a specific pediatric endocrinologic follow-up including a genetic screening and counseling. These patients should undergo periodic thyroid ultrasonography.

Mandatory autopsy in cases with adverse outcomes

Determining the precise cause of death is essential for quality assurance. This knowledge raises awareness of future preventable pitfalls and contributes to optimized management of further cases. As early as 1999, Lima et al. emphasized the clear added value of an autopsy in cases of fetal goiter with adverse outcome. Clinicians thus obtained a detailed understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms and pathogenic alterations leading to death [62].

Limitations

Some limitations of the present case series must be mentioned. The retrospective character, the small number of patients—although substantial in the context of the total amount of published case reports—and individual treatment decisions by informed consent in accordance with different preferences of expectant parents challenge generalization.

Perspective

After thorough review of our case series, it must certainly be discussed if the standard prenatal sonographic assessment of the fetal thyroid gland—or at least an axial plane of the fetal neck—should be included in the requirements for mid-trimester fetal anatomical survey. In clinical practice, a dilemma arises: maternal medical history does not necessarily suggest a diagnosis of fetal goiter. However, in all four cases presented, detailed assessment of fetal neck mass was performed in anatomical relationship to the trachea and esophagus, which did not prevent the neonatal colleagues from fatal postnatal complications. There is an urgent need for an evidence-based guideline on the consistent perinatal management of fetal goiters. Even in this retrospective single-center study, the management of fetal goiter was heterogeneous (Tab. 2). The only recommendations published to date are from the European Society for Pediatric Endocrinology in 2013, but remain vague regarding treatment [33]. Prenatal therapy currently focuses primarily on reducing the volume of fetal goiter minimizing feto-maternal complications, but should not ignore the restoration of thyroid function [10].

Conclusion

In addition, the following practice-oriented considerations complicate the formulation of a consistent procedure. In clinical practice, beyond clarification of the underlying etiology, the complex peripartum management is complicated by the evaluation of fetal cervical vascular and nervous structures with the assessment of fetal tracheal compression and the cardiac functional diagnostics for possible fetal heart failure and requires adequate practical expertise. Interdisciplinary cooperation is mandatory, especially when determining the time and mode of delivery and planning an EXIT strategy. Each of us should be aware of common shortcomings to minimize fatal peripartal outcomes. In case of fetal/perinatal death, an autopsy is inevitable. It is the way for the final determination of the exact cause of fetal demise.
