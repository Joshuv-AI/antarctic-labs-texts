Association of dietary glycemic and insulin index/load with odds of major depressive disorder: a case-control study
Authors: Mahtab Karami Talandashti, Hossein Shahinfar, Mohammad Effatpanah, Jamileh Abolghasemi, Reyhaneh Morad Abbasi, Shima Jazayeri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDietary carbohydrate quality, reflected by glycemic index/load and insulin index/load, may influence brain function and mood through mechanisms such as glycemic fluctuations, oxidative stress, and inflammation. While several studies have assessed these indices in relation to depressive symptoms, their association with clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder (MDD) has not been investigated. We aimed to examine these associations in the present study.MethodsThis case-control study included 194 patients with MDD, diagnosed by a psychiatrist according to DSM-5 criteria, and 194 healthy individuals. Dietary information was collected using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Dietary glycemic index (DGI), glycemic load (DGL), insulin index (DII), and insulin load (DIL) were calculated based on FFQ data. The severity of MDD was assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). Multivariable odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.ResultsThe odds of MDD was significantly higher in the third (T3) versus first tertile (T1) of DGI and DII (odds ratio: 1.73 (95% CI: 1.01–3.02) and 1.80 (95% CI: 1.03–3.12), respectively). However, there was no significant relation between DGL or DIL and MDD (third versus first tertile odds ratio: 1.43 (95% CI: 0.74–2.79) and 1.59 (95% CI: 0.66–3.83), respectively).ConclusionsHigher DGI and DII may be associated with increased odds of MDD, while DGL and DIL showed no significant associations. These findings highlight the importance of carbohydrate quality and insulin response in MDD, warranting further confirmation in large-scale prospective studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Glycemic index, Glycemic load, Insulin index, Insulin load, MDD

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a common psychiatric disorder worldwide, affecting approximately 280 million individuals in 2017 and accounting for about 3.4% of the global population [1, 2]. Importantly, while the term depression is often used to describe a broad range of depressive symptoms, Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) refers to a specific psychiatric disorder diagnosed according to standardized clinical criteria, such as those outlined in the DSM-5. It is currently ranked as the third leading cause of disability globally, projections by the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate that MDD will become the leading cause of disability by 2030 [3, 4]. The etiology of depression is complex and multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, biological factors, environmental influences, and psychological determinants [5]. The main symptoms of MDD include a persistent depressed mood, markedly reduced interest or pleasure in activities, and pervasive feelings of guilt or worthlessness. Additional features often observed are disturbances in sleep and appetite, significant changes in weight, diminished energy and concentration, and, in severe cases, suicidal thoughts [6, 7].

It is crucial to explore innovative strategies to prevent MDD, with nutritional interventions emerging as a promising approach to achieving this objective [8]. Diets high in refined carbohydrates, such as sugary foods and sweetened beverages, have raised significant concerns [9]. Dietary carbohydrate quality can be assessed using indices such as the Glycemic Index (GI) and Glycemic Load (GL), which reflect the impact of individual foods on blood glucose levels. However, to evaluate the overall glycemic effect of a person’s entire diet, the Dietary Glycemic Index (DGI) is used, which calculates a weighted average of the GI values of all consumed foods based on their carbohydrate content and consumption frequency. Similarly, the Dietary Insulin Index (DII) and Dietary Insulin Load (DIL) assess the insulin response elicited by the total diet. These indices provide a more comprehensive view of how diet influences metabolic and mental health outcomes [10–12].

Previous studies on DGI, DGL, and depressive symptoms have reported inconsistent results, often due to methodological differences and reliance on self-reported questionnaires [13–16]. Among these, two studies [13, 14] found a positive association between higher dietary glycemic index and dietary glycemic load and increased depressive symptoms, whereas the other two studies reported no significant association [15, 16]. To our knowledge, no previous study has employed structured clinical interviews to diagnose MDD. Given the established role of glucose and insulin metabolism in brain function and mood regulation, investigating dietary glycemic and insulin indices may provide insights into modifiable risk factors for clinically diagnosed MDD. Accordingly, the present study aimed to examine the associations between dietary glycemic index/load and insulin index/load and the odds of MDD in adults assessed by a psychiatrist.

Methods

Study population

The required sample size was calculated to be 388 participants based on data from previous studies, with 80% power and a 95% confidence interval using the formula n=(Z1−α/2+Z1−β)2σ2(μ1−μ2)2n=(μ1−μ2)2(Z1−α/2+Z1−β)2σ2, where α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 [17]. The case group consisted of 194 patients with MDD referred from the psychiatric clinic at Imam Khomeini Hospital. MDD was diagnosed through a structured psychiatric interview based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Eligibility criteria for the case group included a Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) score above 17, a diagnosis made within the past three months, and no history of medication use during this period. The control group also included 194 participants, recruited from other hospital departments, such as orthopedics and emergency. Individuals in the control group had no history of depressive or other mental health disorders and scored below 9 on the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).

The inclusion criteria for both groups were as follows: willingness to participate in the study, no use of psychiatric medications (including antidepressants or sedatives) in the past three months, age between 18 and 65 years, abstinence from alcohol consumption, and no history of significant systemic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, liver or kidney failure, thyroid or parathyroid disorders, or infectious diseases. However, metabolic disorders, including dyslipidemia, hypertension, and pre-diabetes, were included in the study. Participants who completed less than 80% of the FFQ or reported a daily caloric intake of less than 800 kcal or more than 4200 kcal were excluded from the study [18].

Demographic data collection

Participants’ demographic and lifestyle information, including gender (male/female), age (continuous), education level (Undergraduate/Diploma/College), marital status (Married/Single), use of supplements or medications for dyslipidemia, hypertension, or pre-diabetes (Yes/No), and smoking habits (Yes/No), was collected through direct interviews. Participants with major systemic diseases, such as diagnosed diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or other chronic systemic conditions, were excluded to minimize potential confounding. The remaining participants were categorized by health status as Healthy (no systemic disease) or Non-Healthy (having dyslipidemia, hypertension, or pre-diabetes).

Physical activity over the previous seven days was assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [19], which measures vigorous, moderate, walking, and sedentary activity. Total weekly physical activity was expressed in MET-min/week, and participants were classified into three activity levels: very low ( < 600 MET-min/week), low (600–3000 MET-min/week), and moderate to high ( > 3000 MET-min/week) [20].

Anthropometric data collection

Participants’ weight was measured using a digital scale (Seca 707; Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany; accuracy ±0.1 kg) while wearing minimal clothing without shoes. Their height was also measured using a stadiometer (Seca, Germany) in a standing position without shoes, with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was also calculated by dividing weight (kilograms) by the square of height (square meters).

Dietary intake assessment

To estimate the participants’ dietary intake, we used a semi-quantitative, validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) with 168 food items, designed based on Iranian food patterns [21]. For each food item, participants answered two questions: the frequency of consumption of food groups (annually, monthly, weekly, and daily during the past year) and the approximate amount consumed per occasion. Then, using “household portion size”, the frequency and quantity of all food items were converted into grams per day [22]. The contents of macronutrients and micronutrients of the diets were also calculated using the modified Nutritionist IV software designed (version 7.0; N-Squared Computing, Salem, OR, USA).

Calculation of carbohydrate indices

GI values were obtained from both Iranian and international GI-GL tables, using glucose as the reference food (GI = 100). Carbohydrate and fiber contents were derived from the USDA Food Composition Table. Of 85 carbohydrate-containing items, GI values of six items were taken from Iranian sources [23, 24], 62 from international tables, and 17 were estimated based on comparable foods or standard recipes [24–26]. For mixed dishes, GI was calculated as a weighted average of their components. The Food Insulin Index (FII) reflects the incremental postprandial insulin response over a two-hour period following the consumption of 1,000 kJ (239 kcal) of a test food. FII values were obtained from previously published studies [27–29]. For food items lacking specific FII values, estimates were made based on their nutritional composition, including energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, and fiber content, by comparing them with similar foods for which FII data were available. Dietary carbohydrate indices were calculated from the food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) using the following formulas:

Dietary Glycemic Index (DGI) = [Σ (Carbohydrate content of each food item) × (Number of servings per day) × (Glycemic index)]/Total daily carbohydrate intake.

Dietary Glycemic Load (DGL) = [Σ (Carbohydrate content of each food item × Number of servings per day) × Glycemic index]

Dietary Insulin Index (DII) = Dietary Insulin Load/[Σ (Energy content of food (Kcal/serving) × Frequency of consumption (servings of food per day))]

Dietary Insulin Load (DIL) = [Σ (Food insulin index × Kilocalories per gram × Grams per day)]

Assessment of depressive disorder

Diagnosis was based on an interview conducted by a psychiatrist, and depression severity was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [30]. Depressive disorder was defined according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria [31]. For the control group, depressive symptoms were screened through face-to-face administration of the Persian validated version of the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), a 21-item questionnaire widely used to assess depression severity.

Statistical analysis

Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Shapiro-Wilk, and Q-Q plot tests were used to evaluate the normality of data distribution. Participants were divided into groups based on the tertiles of DGI, DGL, DII, and DIL scores. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare quantitative variables among the tertiles of DGI, DGL, DII, and DIL, while the chi-square test was used for qualitative variables. Data were reported as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Dietary intakes were adjusted for energy using the residual method [32]. Multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for covariates, including age, gender, energy intake, marital status, education level, medication use (for dyslipidemia, hypertension, or pre-diabetes), vitamin supplement use, smoking status, physical activity, health status (Healthy: no systemic disease; Non-Healthy: dyslipidemia, hypertension, or pre-diabetes), and body mass index (BMI), and was applied across different models. P-trend values were calculated by modeling the median value of each tertile of the dietary indices as a continuous variable in the regression models, to assess the linear trend across tertiles. Carbohydrate intake was adjusted for in DGI/DII models to separate quality effects from total intake, but not in DGL/DIL models since load metrics already incorporate carbohydrate amount, avoiding over adjustment [21, 22]. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 25; SPSS Inc.). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered.

Results

The current case-control study was conducted between 2023 and 2024. Initially, 400 participants were enrolled. After data cleaning and applying inclusion criteria, 12 participants were excluded, resulting in a final sample size of 388 participants. The general characteristics of study participants for both cases and controls are presented in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the case and control groups in terms of sex, smoking habits, dietary supplement use, medication use (for dyslipidemia, hypertension, or pre-diabetes), and physical activity levels. However, the case group exhibited significantly lower height and higher weight and BMI. The general characteristics of study participants across tertiles of GI, GL, II, and IL scores are provided in Table 2. The mean height significantly differed across tertiles of GI, and the distribution of education status significantly differed across tertiles. Additionally, the distribution of supplement use differed significantly among tertiles of DGL. The mean BMI showed significant differences across tertiles of DII, and the distribution of supplement intake varied significantly among tertiles of DIL.

Discussion

Based on our case-control study, we found that, after adjusting for dietary and non-dietary confounding factors, dietary GI and II were directly associated with increased odds of MDD. In contrast, the associations between dietary GL and IL with the odds of MDD were not statistically significant. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association of dietary glycemic and insulin index and load with the odds of MDD. Our findings align with some previous studies that have demonstrated a direct association between DGI, DGL, DII, and DIL with mental disorders [33, 34]. A cross-sectional study found that higher DGI was associated with an increased likelihood of depressive symptoms and, in the final model, with a higher likelihood of anxiety. Conversely, DGL was associated with a lower risk of mental disorders, including depressive symptoms and psychological distress [35]. In a systematic review, Rahim Lo et al. concluded that a diet with a lower glycemic index may reduce the risk of depression, whereas the relationship with glycemic load was inverse [36]. Similarly, a systematic review by Salari Moghaddam et al. examined the relationship between DGI, DGL, and depression. This review included various types of studies, including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and clinical trials. While cross-sectional studies did not find a significant association between DGI or DGL and depressive symptoms, cohort studies revealed a positive and statistically significant association between DGI and depressive symptoms. Additionally, clinical trials have demonstrated significant changes in depressive symptoms following the consumption of diets with varying GL. These findings highlight the need for high-quality studies to further explore the potential role of dietary GI and GL in mental health, particularly given the low GRADE rating of the studies included in the review [15]. Another prospective cohort study by James et al. found that a high glycemic index diet may serve as a potential risk factor for depressive symptoms in menopausal women [37]. A cross-sectional study by Anjom Shoa et al. found a significant positive association between the dietary insulin index and both the burden and likelihood of depressive symptoms among women; however, no such association was observed in men. Notably, these findings were not observed for anxiety and psychological distress [16]. The apparent inconsistencies between their findings and ours may be attributed to several methodological differences. In their study, depressive symptoms were self-reported using the HADS questionnaire, whereas our study included clinically diagnosed MDD cases confirmed through structured psychiatric interviews. In addition, their study was cross-sectional, while ours was a case-control design focusing on newly diagnosed MDD cases. These differences could influence the observed associations and help explain why our results differ from theirs.

In contrast to our results, a cross-sectional study by Minobe et al. showed that a higher dietary GI was associated with a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms. However, similar to our study, no association was found between dietary GL and depressive symptoms. They noted limitations, such as the cross-sectional design, which does not establish causality, and the high socioeconomic status of the participants, as all were recruited from the same location [38]. In this study, depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) questionnaire rather than structured diagnostic interviews. The absence of strict clinical diagnoses may have led to the inclusion of individuals with chronic fatigue syndrome or atypical depression, potentially contributing to the observed inconsistency. Another cross-sectional study found that diets with higher DII and DIL scores were associated with lower stress levels. However, no significant associations were observed between DII or DIL and depression or anxiety scores [34]. The use of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 items (DASS-21) for anxiety assessment and the reliance on self-reported depression might explain the inconsistent findings.

Several mechanisms may explain the positive association between DGI and odds of depression [39]. Low-GI diets, which include fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and fish, may positively influence emotion regulation and cognitive function [40]. Although direct studies on the Dietary Insulin Index (DII) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) are limited, existing evidence suggests that diets eliciting higher insulin responses may affect brain function through mechanisms such as insulin secretion, insulin resistance, inflammation, and neurotransmitter regulation, all of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD [34]. Therefore, including DII alongside DGI provides a more comprehensive perspective on the dietary impacts on MDD. The exact cause of the association between dietary insulin index and depressive symptoms is not fully understood, but several mechanisms have been proposed. Experimental and human studies suggest that dietary insulin regulation can influence mood disorders [41–43]. For instance, animal studies have demonstrated that inactivation of the insulin receptor in the hypothalamus can lead to systemic insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, and depressive-like behaviors [44]. Additionally, high-glycemic-load diets may contribute to insulin resistance, which has been linked to brain volume reductions and cognitive impairments similar to those observed in individuals with depression [45]. Another potential pathway involves chronic inflammation, which may be exacerbated by diets high in refined carbohydrates and processed foods, further increasing susceptibility to depression [46].

Our study had several strengths. First, unlike many previous studies that have assessed depression symptoms through questionnaires, MDD was diagnosed through a structured psychiatric interview based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) in the present study. As a serious psychological illness, MDD is distinct from typical depressive symptoms and warrants more in-depth investigation [31]. Second, a validated FFQ was used to assess dietary intake, providing more accurate and reliable data compared to other methods. Third, cases were recruited from a large referral hospital, which is representative of MDD patients in the community, while controls were recruited from other hospital departments, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. These factors collectively strengthen the quality, reliability, and applicability of our study findings. However, the present study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. One limitation was the case-control design, which precludes the establishment of clear temporal relationships between variables. Additionally, since the FFQ relies on participants’ memory, there is a potential for recall bias. Moreover, blood glucose and insulin resistance data were not available for all participants, so these variables could not be included as covariates. Another limitation is that we did not perform a specific clinical assessment of anxiety and insomnia, and this should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Although MDD cases were newly diagnosed, it is possible that participants had experienced early symptoms prior to diagnosis, which may have influenced their dietary behaviors. Therefore, reverse causality remains a plausible concern. Clinically, our results highlight the potential role of diet as a modifiable factor in MDD prevention, suggesting that dietary counseling could be integrated into mental health care and public health strategies. Future longitudinal and interventional studies with larger sample sizes and objective measures are recommended to confirm these associations and explore underlying mechanisms.

Clinical and public health implications

Our findings suggest that diet may be an important modifiable factor in the prevention of MDD. Clinicians could consider incorporating dietary counseling and education into mental health care plans. From a public health perspective, promoting healthy dietary patterns may help reduce the odds of MDD in the general population. These results support the integration of nutrition-focused strategies into mental health and community health programs. In particular, focusing on foods with lower glycemic and insulin indices—such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and fish—may help promote healthier dietary habits.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this case-control study suggest that a diet with a high dietary glycemic index (DGI) and dietary insulin index (DII) may be associated with increased odds of major depressive disorder (MDD), while no such association was found for dietary glycemic load (DGL) and insulin load (DIL). Overall, these findings suggest that emphasizing foods with lower glycemic and insulin indices—including fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and fish—could contribute to healthier dietary patterns. Given the potential role of diet in prevention, it is essential to conduct large-scale prospective studies to further investigate the relationship between diet and MDD.
