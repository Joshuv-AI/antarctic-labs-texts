Effects of nitrogen-assisted post-curing and layer thickness on the properties of a 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced composite resin for permanent crowns under different aging conditions: an in vitro study
Authors: Aylin ER, Ipek Caglar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground3D printing protocols play a critical role in determining the mechanical and physical properties of resin materials, with potential implications for their longevity and the clinical durability of prosthetic restorations. The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of layer thickness and nitrogen-assisted post-curing conditions on water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, and flexural strength of a 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced composite resin (CRC) for permanent crown restorations under two different aging conditions.MethodsA total of 120 disc-shaped specimens were fabricated from VarseoSmile TriniQ (BEGO GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) using two layer thicknesses (50 μm and 100 μm) and three post-curing conditions (2000, 4000, and 8000 flashes) with an Otoflash G171 unit under nitrogen atmosphere. Following post-curing, specimens were assigned to either 24 h distilled water storage (Group 1) or thermal aging for 5000 cycles at 5–55 °C (Group 2). Water sorption and solubility, surface roughness (SR), surface morphology, and flexural strength (FS) were evaluated in both groups. Data were analysed using two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsNeither layer thickness nor post-curing conditions significantly affected water sorption or solubility (P > 0.05). Surface roughness was lowest at 4000 flashes for both layer thicknesses regardless of aging, with 50 µm specimens exhibiting lower values than 100 µm specimens at higher post-curing levels. Flexural strength was significantly influenced by post-curing conditions, with specimens post-cured at 2000 flashes showing lower values than those post-cured at 4000 and 8000 flashes (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the two aging groups in water sorption or flexural strength; however, surface roughness was significantly higher in the thermally aged group for 100 µm specimens post-cured at 2000 flashes (P < 0.05).”ConclusionThe physical and mechanical performance of the tested 3D-printed CRC was primarily influenced by post-curing conditions and layer thickness, regardless of aging conditions. Insufficient post-curing (2000 flashes) resulted in suboptimal surface and mechanical properties, while 4000 flashes provided the most favorable overall performance among the tested conditions. Within the limitations of this in vitro study, these findings highlight the importance of adequate post-curing for achieving favorable physical and mechanical properties of 3D-printed CRC restorations.Keywords: 3D printing, ceramic-reinforced composite resin, layer thickness, post-curing, thermal aging

Introduction

In recent years, technological advancements in dentistry have accelerated the adoption of three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies, enabling innovative manufacturing approaches in clinical practice [1]. The digital workflow begins with converting Computer-Aided Design and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD-CAM) generated models into Standard Tessellation Language (STL) format, which allows layer-by-layer fabrication through the printing system [2]. Stereolithography (SLA) and digital light processing (DLP) constitute two of the primary methods used in dental 3D printing applications. SLA cures photosensitive resin using ultraviolet light or laser, whereas DLP achieves polymerization via a digital light projector [3]. A major advantage of DLP over conventional SLA is its ability to cure an entire layer within seconds, substantially reducing production time [4]. For both techniques, printed restorations undergo cleaning and post-curing to ensure full polymerization, enabling the precise, efficient, and low-waste fabrication of complex designs [3–5].

Indirect dental restorations are commonly fabricated from ceramics, hybrid materials (including ceramic-reinforced composite resins, resin nanoceramics, and polymer-infiltrated ceramic network materials), and composite-based systems. Recently, 3D printing technology has enabled the development of a new generation of ceramic-reinforced composites (CRCs) that combine the advantages of machinable materials with enhanced customization and more efficient material use [5]. These CRCs offer favorable optical and mechanical properties and ease of clinical handling, making them a promising alternative to conventional ceramics. Most studies on 3D-printed dental resins have focused on temporary crown restorations and denture base applications; however, scientific evidence regarding their use for permanent crown restorations remains limited [6–8]. To overcome this gap, a recently developed CRC resin (VarseoSmile Triniq; Bego, Bremen, Germany) has been introduced for permanent 3D-printing applications. This material consists of a Bis-EMA–based methacrylate matrix, silanized dental glass fillers (30–50 wt%, 0.7 μm), and a phosphine oxide photoinitiator, enabling its use in permanent dental restorations [9]. This material is indicated not only for single crowns but also for three-unit fixed partial prosthesis in both anterior and posterior regions [8].

In DLP-based additive manufacturing, the printing process initiates photopolymerization through layer-by-layer light exposure. However, many resin materials do not achieve complete monomer conversion during printing, necessitating post-curing to complete the polymer network and optimize material properties [10]. The mechanical behavior and surface characteristics of 3D-printed dental materials are influenced by several manufacturing parameters, including layer thickness, build orientation, and exposure settings, as well as post-processing protocols such as the post-curing device, curing atmosphere, and curing conditions [11]. Among these parameters, several studies have demonstrated that layer thickness directly influences the physical and mechanical properties of materials [12, 13]. Thinner layers are associated with more homogeneous interlayer bonding and reduced porosity, whereas thicker layers are linked to weaker interfacial adhesion, increased surface irregularities, and lower structural integrity [12, 13].

Post-curing plays a critical role in completing polymerization and improving the mechanical performance of additively manufactured resins [11]. Conventional post-curing units typically use ultraviolet (UV) light and/or heat to increase the degree of conversion. In contrast, the Otoflash G171 unit employs xenon flash lamps that deliver high-energy light pulses over a broad wavelength spectrum, enabling activation of various photoinitiator systems. The unit can also be operated under a nitrogen atmosphere supplied by an external nitrogen source, which reduces oxygen inhibition and may enhance polymerization efficiency and degree of conversion [14]. Previous studies have reported that nitrogen-assisted post-curing may improve the mechanical performance of 3D-printed resins by minimizing oxygen inhibition during polymerization [15, 16]. However, no previous study has simultaneously evaluated the combined effects of nitrogen-assisted post-curing and layer thickness on the physical and mechanical properties of permanent ceramic-reinforced composite crown resins under different aging conditions. Therefore, further investigation is required to clarify the effects of post-curing condition on these materials.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of layer thickness and post-curing conditions on the water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, and flexural strength of 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced composite crown resins under two different aging conditions. The two null hypotheses were defined: (1) layer thickness has no significant effect on the water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, or flexural strength of the tested material; (2) post-curing conditions have no significant effect on the water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, or flexural strength of the tested material under different aging conditions.

Materials and methods

Disc geometry specimens with a diameter of 15 mm and a final thickness of 1.5 mm were designed using computer-aided design software (DentalCAD; exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) and exported as STL files. The digital files were imported into Asiga Composer (Asiga, Sydney, Australia) for build preparation. Slicing was performed using Asiga Slicer software (Asiga, Sydney, Australia), with the specimens designed at a 90° build orientation. Printing files were generated according to the manufacturer’s recommended parameters. Specimens were fabricated using an Asiga MAX 2 UV printer (Asiga, Sydney, Australia) with VarseoSmile TriniQ resin (BEGO GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany). After printing, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in 99% isopropyl alcohol according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and dried with compressed air before post-curing.

A total of 120 specimens were fabricated and allocated to two layer thickness groups: 50 μm (n = 60) and 100 μm (n = 60). These layer thicknesses were selected based on values commonly investigated in the literature [17–20] and represent clinically applicable printing parameters for this material. These specimens were then randomly divided into three post-curing groups (n = 20 per group) using a computer-generated randomization list. The Otoflash G171 unit (NK-Optik GmbH, Baierbrunn, Germany) was used for post-curing in a nitrogen atmosphere at 1.5 bar, delivering high-energy light pulses over a wavelength range of 300–700 nm with peaks at approximately 480 and 530 nm [15]. The specimens were post-cured using 2000, 4000, and 8000 flashes, representing insufficient, optimal, and excessive post-curing conditions, respectively. The 4000-flash protocol was selected in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendation of 2 × 2000 flashes for this material, while 2000 and 8000 flashes were included to evaluate the effects of insufficient and excessive post-curing on material properties [3, 16, 21]. Specimens were post-cured in a bidirectional orientation, with each surface exposed for half of the total post-curing condition. Following post-curing, specimens were subjected to aging procedures. Water storage in distilled water was carried out at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h for half of the specimens (n = 10) (Group 1), whereas the remaining specimens (n = 10) underwent thermal aging in a thermocycling device (Gökçeler Medikal, Sivas, Türkiye) for 5000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C. Each cycle consisted of a dwell time of 20 s and a transfer time of 10 s. The thermal aging protocol was selected based on protocols commonly used in studies evaluating the aging behavior of 3D-printed resins [22, 23]. Water sorption and solubility measurement

ISO 4049:2019 was used for solubility and water sorption measurements [24]. Prior to aging, specimens were conditioned in a desiccator (Isotherm Lab., Türkiye) with freshly dried silica gel and then kept in a dry heat oven (Dry Heat Oven FN 055; Nüve, Ankara, Türkiye) at 37 °C for 22 ± 1 h. Subsequently, the desiccator was removed from the oven, the silica gel was replaced, and the specimens were further dried at 23 ± 1 °C for 2 h. Following the drying protocol, specimen weights were determined using an analytical balance accurate to 0.0001 g (KERN ABJ 220-4 M; KERN & Sohn GmbH, Balingen, Germany), and the mass was recorded as m₁. The diameter of each specimen was then measured at three mutually perpendicular points with a digital calliper providing a resolution of 0.01 mm. (Holex 150 mm; Hoffmann Group, Munich, Germany) to calculate the mean diameter. Thickness measurements were obtained from the specimen centre and four equidistant peripheral points, and the mean thickness (h) was calculated. Specimen volume (V, mm³) was determined according to the equation V = πr²h, with r indicating the radius and h referring to the mean thickness. After recording m₁, specimens were assigned to aging protocols (n = 10). Specimens in Group 1 were individually stored in glass containers containing 50 mL of distilled water and kept in an incubator (Dry Heat Oven FN 055; Nüve, Ankara, Türkiye) at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h according to their respective curing conditions. Following immersion, specimens were removed, rinsed, and air-dried for 15 s to achieve surface dryness. The same procedure was repeated after thermal aging (5–55 °C, 5000 thermocycles, 20 s exposure time, 10 s transition time). The second mass (m₂) was then recorded using the same analytical balance. For solubility determination, the drying protocol applied for m₁ was repeated stable mass was achieved, and the third mass (m₃) was recorded. Subsequent calculations of water sorption (Wsp) and water solubility (Wsl) were performed following the ISO 4049 standard [24].

m₁: Mass after initial drying (µg), m₂: Mass after water immersion (µg), m₃: Mass after re-drying (µg), V: Specimen volume (mm³), Unit: µg/mm³.

Surface roughness measurement

Surface roughness measurements were conducted with a high precision profilometer (Taylor Hobson, Leicester, United Kingdom). The device was configured using a measurement length of 0.8 mm, a traverse speed of 0.1 mm/s, and a cut-off value of 1.6 mm. The stylus–specimen contact angle was maintained at 90°, and each specimen was carefully positioned on the standard measurement stage. For each specimen, measurements were obtained from three different surface regions, average surface roughness was determined as the arithmetic mean of the measured values (Ra). To ensure measurement reliability, the instrument was recalibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions after every 10 measurements.

Scanning electron microscopy analysis

A representative specimen from each group was analysed using scanning electron microscopy to characterize surface morphology. (JSM-6610; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Dried specimens were coated with a thin gold layer to enhance electrical conductivity. Imaging was performed at a magnification of ×1000 to obtain detailed surface topography.

Flexural strength measurement

The piston-on-three-ball test was employed on a universal testing machine to determine the flexural strength of the specimens (Instron 3344, Metalinspec, Canton, USA). Specimens were placed on a three-ball support (3.2 mm diameter balls) arranged at 120° intervals. Prior to loading, a 0.5 N preload was applied. The load was then applied using a 1.6 mm diameter piston at a constant crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The maximum load (P) recorded at fracture was used to calculate the flexural strength (σ) according to the equation specified in ISO 6872 [25]. The Poisson’s ratio (ν) was assumed to be 0.30.

Statistical analysis

Specimen size calculation was performed using G*Power software (Universität Düsseldorf, GPower, 2019, Germany). Based on the effect size of 0.62, a statistical power of 0.80, and a significance level of α = 0.05, the required sample size was calculated as 60 specimens for each layer thickness (50 μm and 100 μm), for a total of 120 specimens in the present study. Data were analysed using SPSS (version 27; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with normality and variance homogeneity assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests. The independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare two groups according to data normality. For three or more groups, One-Way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. Two-way ANOVA was applied to analyse interaction effects, with Bonferroni post hoc testing used for multiple comparisons at a significance level of 0.05.

Discussion

The in-vitro study evaluated the effect of layer thickness, post curing conditions on water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, and flexural strength of 3D printed ceramic-reinforced composite resin under different aging conditions. According to the results of the present study, layer thickness and post-curing conditions significantly influenced surface roughness and flexural strength, while having no significant effect on water sorption or solubility. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two aging groups in water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, or flexural strength. Therefore, the two null hypotheses — (1) layer thickness has no significant effect on the water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, or flexural strength of the tested material; (2) post-curing conditions have no significant effect on the water sorption, solubility, surface roughness, or flexural strength of the tested material under different aging conditions — were partially rejected.

Dental materials are influenced by fluctuating moisture levels and temperature changes in the oral environment, which may increase water sorption over time [26–28]. In the present study, water sorption and solubility values remained below the ISO 10477:2020 limits for all groups, regardless of layer thickness or post-curing conditions [29]. Although Kaushik et al. [30] reported a parabolic relationship between layer thickness and water uptake, no such effect was observed in the present study, possibly because the thinnest layer tested (50 μm) already falls within the optimal range described by those authors. Differences in resin composition and post-curing conditions between studies may also contribute to this discrepancy. From a clinical perspective, neither layer thickness nor post-curing condition significantly affected water sorption or solubility, suggesting that hydrolytic stability of the material is not compromised by variations in these processing parameters.

Lee et al. [31] similarly reported that curing parameters did not significantly affect water sorption or solubility, whereas non-cured specimens showed markedly higher values, suggesting that the presence of post-curing is more critical than its condition. Although Alharbi et al. [32] reported that increasing post-curing condition significantly reduces water sorption, this discrepancy may be attributed to the use of nitrogen-assisted Otoflash curing in the present study, which may reduce the influence of curing condition on hydrolytic properties compared to conventional UV-based systems.

Surface roughness is a critical parameter for biological compatibility and long-term clinical success of intraoral restorations [33]. Consistent with the literature [34, 35], surface roughness increased with greater layer thickness in the present study, which may be attributed to the reduction of topographical irregularities at layer interfaces during fine-layer fabrication, promoting a more uniform and homogeneous surface. However, this increase was not statistically significant across all post-curing conditions [17, 18], suggesting that the effect of layer thickness on surface roughness is dependent on post-curing parameters and should not be evaluated in isolation. For chairside and laboratory workflows, layer thickness selection should therefore be considered alongside post-curing conditions to optimize surface quality.

Previous reports indicate that post-curing conditions does not significantly affect surface roughness [36, 37], consistent with the present study. However, specimens post-cured at 4000 flashes exhibited the lowest surface roughness values, suggesting that an optimal curing level exists. This finding may suggest that excessive curing could alter surface characteristics, possibly reflecting a higher degree of polymer network formation, whereas insufficient curing may indicate reduced polymerization efficiency and polymer network heterogeneity, both of which may adversely affect surface topography. These findings highlight the importance of standardizing post-curing conditions in clinical and laboratory settings to ensure optimal surface quality.

SEM analysis revealed more irregular and heterogeneous surface morphologies in specimens fabricated with 100 μm layer thickness, consistent with the profilometric surface roughness findings. This may be attributed to reduced light penetration into underlying layers at greater layer thicknesses, resulting in reduced polymerization efficiency and surface undulations within the polymer network. Previous studies have similarly reported that thinner layer thicknesses (25–50 μm) produce smoother and more homogeneous surfaces, whereas 100 μm layers show more pronounced surface irregularities including grooves and microstructural defects [38–40].

Flexural strength is affected by additively manufacturing procedures, including layer thickness, polymerization method, and post curing procedures [41]. Previous studies [19–43] have indicated that the effect of layer thickness on mechanical strength cannot be evaluated independently from printing orientation. Due to differences in resolution between the printing axes (X, Y, and Z), mechanical properties may vary depending on both parameters. It has been reported that thinner layers (25–50 μm) may result in higher strength in vertically printed specimens due to improved resolution along the Z-axis, whereas thicker layers (100 μm) may provide higher strength in horizontally printed specimens due to relatively better resolution in the X–Y plane [44]. In the present study, all specimens were printed at a 90° orientation. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups (p > 0.05), specimens printed with 100 μm layer thickness exhibited higher mean strength values compared to those printed with 50 μm. This result likely due to the to lower number of interlayer interfaces in thicker layers, leading to fewer potential weak regions. However, previous study has also reported that thicker layers may contain larger voids and increased porosity, which can negatively affect mechanical properties [20]. Therefore, the mechanical behavior of additively manufactured resins reflects a balance between these competing effects, and layer thickness alone should not be considered a dominant factor. Clinicians and dental laboratories should consider printing orientation alongside layer thickness when optimizing mechanical performance.

Otoflash systems have been shown to yield higher degree of conversion values compared to other post-curing methods [15], yet studies specifically comparing different post-curing levels using Otoflash systems remain limited [32, 43, 45]. Considering that degree of conversion is closely related to the mechanical properties of the material, this may also have a significant effect on flexural strength [10, 15, 21]. In the present study, post-curing conditions did not significantly affect flexural strength at lower layer thickness (50 μm), whereas a significant effect was observed at higher layer thickness (100 μm). Insufficient post-curing (2000 flashes) resulted in lower flexural strength, while increasing the number of flashes beyond 4000 did not lead to further significant improvement. The significant effect of post-curing conditions observed in the 100 μm group may be attributed to the greater dependence of thicker layers on post-curing for achieving adequate polymerization. Thicker layers may require greater post-curing energy to achieve comparable polymerization. According to ISO 4049:2019 [24], the minimum required flexural strength for resin materials is 100 MPa; in the present study, specimens post-cured with 2000 flashes remained below this threshold at both layer thicknesses, whereas those cured with 4000 and 8000 flashes met this requirement. Therefore, insufficient post-curing (2000 flashes) may result in lower mechanical performance, whereas higher post-curing levels (4000 and 8000 flashes) provide improved polymerization and strength. These findings indicate that 2000 flashes is insufficient for clinical use regardless of layer thickness, and that degree of conversion should be evaluated in future studies to better understand the relationship between post-curing parameters and mechanical performance [32, 46].

Thermal aging may increase water sorption in resins fabricated by three-dimensional printing and induce hydrolytic degradation within the polymer network [47]. While some studies have reported no significant change in water sorption of interim crown resins after thermal aging [48], others have observed increased water sorption without a consistent trend in solubility [26]. Although small incremental increases were detected in the aged group, no statistically significant changes in water sorption and solubility were observed in the present study. This behaviour may be attributed to the diffusion of water into the polymer matrix, which facilitates the leaching of low-molecular-weight components, suggesting that water sorption and solubility are closely associated through hydrolytic interactions. These findings may suggest that the applied post-curing conditions were sufficient to stabilize the material against hydrolytic degradation. Previous reports have indicated that surface roughness values of permanent crown resins remain within a similar range before and after aging [22, 49]. Consistent with these findings, no statistically significant change was detected in the present study except under one condition. The statistically significant reduction in mechanical performance in the aged group observed in 100 μm specimens polymerized with 2000 flashes may be due to insufficient light penetration into thicker layers, which may indicate reduced polymerization efficiency, whereas the thinner 50 μm layers achieved more uniform polymerization even at the same curing condition, preventing a similar effect.

Thermal aging has been associated with a deterioration in the mechanical properties of additively manufactured resins [47], with several studies demonstrating a diminution in flexural strength following thermal aging [7, 23]. Repeated temperature fluctuations are thought to promote microstress accumulation within the polymer matrix, leading to microcrack formation and interfacial weakening between filler and matrix. In agreement with the literature reporting that water sorption weakens the mechanical performance of resin-based materials [50], the present study revealed a decreasing trend in flexural strength in the aged group, although this change did not reach statistical significance.

Although differences between 4000 and 8000 flashes were generally not statistically significant, the findings suggest that following the manufacturer’s recommended post-curing protocol (4000 flashes) is sufficient to achieve favorable physical and mechanical performance without unnecessarily increasing laboratory processing time. Furthermore, insufficient post-curing (2000 flashes) resulted in suboptimal surface and mechanical properties, highlighting the critical importance of adhering to recommended post-curing protocols in both chairside and laboratory manufacturing workflows.

This study has certain limitations. Only one permanent crown resin and one three-dimensional printing technology were evaluated, limiting the generalizability of the results. In addition, post-curing was performed using a single device (Otoflash G171) and specific flash parameters, while other curing systems and conditions were not investigated. Although thermal aging simulated intraoral conditions, other clinical stressors were not included. Future studies should investigate the relationship between degree of conversion, fracture patterns, and the physical and mechanical properties of 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced composite resins subjected to different post-curing conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, the following conclusions were drawn:

• Insufficient post-curing (2000 flashes) resulted in inferior physical and mechanical performance, characterized by lower flexural strength and higher surface roughness values compared with the other post-curing conditions.
