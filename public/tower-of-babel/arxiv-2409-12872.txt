Abstract
. We compute the nuclear dimension of extensions of C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras involving commutative unital quotients and stable Kirchberg ideals. We identify the finite directed graphs whose C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras are covered by this theorem.



1.
Introduction

By the Gelfand–Naimark theorem, every abelian
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra arises as the algebra
C_{0}(X)
of continuous functions that vanish at infinity on some locally compact Hausdorff topological space
X
. Thus
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras provide a framework for non-commutative topology. Nuclear dimension is a non-commutative generalisation of covering dimension for topological spaces to the setting of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras due to Winter and Zacharias (
[
69
]
). By Ostrand’s theorem (
[
52

), the covering dimension of a normal topological space

is the smallest

such that every locally finite open cover of
X
has a refinement that can be
(n+1)
-coloured with sets of the same colour pairwise disjoint. The nuclear dimension of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is defined by ‘colouring’ the finite-dimensional approximations from the Choi–Effros–Kirchberg characterisation of nuclearity (
[
18
,
40
]
) with approximants of the same colour mutually orthogonal. In particular,
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras of finite nuclear dimension are nuclear; see Definition
2.1
below. For unital or separable abelian
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, nuclear dimension matches covering dimension: the classical proof that abelian
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras satisfy the completely positive approximation property shows that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(C_{0}(X))\leq\dim X
; the reverse inequality is obtained by taking a good finite nuclear dimension approximation for a partition of unity (see
[
15
]
, for example).

Since its introduction, nuclear dimension has been intimately linked with the Elliott classification programme for simple separable nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. The additional control given by an
(n+1)
-coloured completely positive approximations, as opposed to standard completely positive approximations, has structural consequences (see, for example,
[

,

[

,
36
,

[

). Conversely, classification has been essential in obtaining optimal values of the nuclear dimension both directly (by transferring explicit finite dimensional approximations from families of model
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras to the general setting in
[

,
27
,
58
,
57
]
) and more indirectly (through the use of classification theorems for maps to construct finitely colourable approximations as in
[

Theorem B
.

Let
J
be a stable Kirchberg algebra and

a compact metric space. Let

(1.7)

0\rightarrow J\rightarrow E\xrightarrow{\pi}C(X)\rightarrow 0

be an essential extension. Then
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(E)=\max(1,\dim(X))
.

When
\dim X>1
, the quotient has strictly bigger dimension than the ideal, and our result fits in to the Brake–Winter framework.
The main challenge comes when

2

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(E)\leq 3

\mathbb{M}_{2}(J)\cong J
; see
[
6

.

An extension of a separable nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
by a separable stable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

with Busby invariant

is

is strongly unitarily equivalent to
\zeta
for all trivial extensions
\sigma
.

For unital extensions, we need to adapt the definition slightly because

is never strongly unitarily equivalent to
\zeta\oplus 0
if
\zeta
is unital.
A unital extension is

if it admits a splitting that is a unital
∗
-homomorphism. A unital extension with Busby invariant
\zeta\colon B\rightarrow Q(J)
is

if
\zeta\oplus\sigma
is strongly unitarily equivalent to
\zeta
for every unitally trivial extension
\sigma
.

The following theorem is well-known to experts but we do not know an explicit reference for the form we need, and so we show how to deduce it from existing results.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
J
be a stable Kirchberg algebra and
B
a separable nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. Then every (unital) essential extension of
B
by
J
is (unitally) absorbing.

Proof.

The unital version of this result follows from the general theory of Elliott and Kucerovsky on absorbing extensions by combining
[
26
, Theorem 6]
with
[
26
, Theorem 17(ii)]
(noting that, as
B
is nuclear, an extension of
B
by
J
is ‘absorbing in the nuclear sense’ as given by
[
26
, Theorem 6]
precisely when it is absorbing). However, this special case was first proven by Kirchberg (see
[
41
, Theorem 6]
).

For the non-unital version, there are some additional subtleties first observed by Gabe in
[
31
]
.
Let
\zeta\colon B\rightarrow Q(J)
be the Busby invariant of a non-unital essential extension of

by
J
.
Then
\zeta
is injective and non-unital.
It follows that the map
\zeta^{\prime}\colon B^{\sim}\rightarrow Q(J)
given by

is also injective. Since

is a stable Kirchberg algebra,

is simple by

, so

is unitisably full in the sense of
[

∎

3.
Machinery of proof

In this section, we outline the global strategy for proving Theorem

and isolate the technical results needed.
The starting point is a decomposition that has its roots in
[
10
]
.
We formulate this as Proposition
3.1
, and will refer to this proposition throughout the paper to fix notation.

Proposition 3.1

(The Brake–Winter decomposition)
.

Let

(3.1)

0\rightarrow J\xrightarrow{\iota}E\xrightarrow{\pi}C(X)\rightarrow 0

be an extension with
\mu\colon C(X)\rightarrow E
a c.p.c. splitting. Let
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a quasicentral approximate unit for
J
relative to
E
.
Set

\displaystyle A_{n}=\overline{h_{n}Jh_{n}},\ B_{n}=\overline{(h_{n+1}-h_{n})J(h_{n+1}-h_{n})},\text{ and }

(3.2)

\displaystyle C_{n}=\overline{(1-h_{n+1})E(1-h_{n+1})}.

Define three sequences of c.p.c. maps

(3.3)

\begin{split}\alpha_{n}\colon E\rightarrow A_{n},\quad a&\mapsto h_{n}^{1/2}ah_{n}^{1/2},\\
\beta_{n}\colon C(X)\rightarrow B_{n},\quad f&\mapsto(h_{n+1}-h_{n})^{1/2}\mu(f)(h_{n+1}-h_{n})^{1/2},\\
\gamma_{n}\colon C(X)\rightarrow C_{n},\quad f&\mapsto(1-h_{n+1})^{1/2}\mu(f)(1-h_{n+1})^{1/2}.\end{split}

Let
\alpha\colon E\rightarrow\prod_{n\to\omega}A_{n}\subseteq E_{\omega}
,
\beta\colon C(X)\rightarrow\prod_{n\to\omega}B_{n}\subseteq E_{\omega}
and
\gamma\colon C(X)\rightarrow\prod_{n\to\omega}C_{n}\subseteq E_{\omega}
be the induced c.p.c. maps into the respective ultraproducts.
Then
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
are c.p.c. order zero maps, and we have

(3.4)

\alpha(a)+\beta(\pi(a))+\gamma(\pi(a))=a

for all
a\in E
.

Proof.

Write
h=[h_{n}]_{n=1}^{\infty}\in E_{\omega}
. Since
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is quasicentral,
\alpha(a)=ha=ah
for all
a\in E
. Hence, if
a,b\in E_{+}
satisfy

, then
\alpha(a)\alpha(b)=habh=0
. Therefore,
\alpha
is a c.p.c. order zero map.

Write
\hat{h}=[h_{n+1}]_{n=1}^{\infty}\in E_{\omega}
. Since
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an approximate unit for
J
, we have
ha=a=\hat{h}a
for all
a\in J\subseteq E_{\omega}
. Hence,
(\hat{h}-h)a=0=(1-\hat{h})a
for all
a\in J\subseteq E_{\omega}
. Since
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is quasicentral,
(\hat{h}-h)
and
(1-\hat{h})
commute with all elements of
E\subseteq E_{\omega}
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in C(X)_{+}
. Then
\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})-\mu(f_{1}f_{2})\in J
. Hence,
(\hat{h}-h)\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})=(\hat{h}-h)\mu(f_{1}f_{2})
and
(1-\hat{h})\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})=(1-\hat{h})\mu(f_{1}f_{2})
. Therefore, if
f_{1}f_{2}=0
, then

(3.5)

\beta(f_{1})\beta(f_{2})=(\hat{h}-h)^{2}\mu(f_{1})\mu(f_{2})=(\hat{h}-h)^{2}\mu(f_{1}f_{2})=0,

and similarly
\gamma(f_{1})\gamma(f_{2})=0
. Hence,
\beta
and
\gamma
are c.p.c. order zero maps.

Let
a\in E
. Then
\pi(\mu(\pi(a)))=\pi(a)
. Hence,
\mu(\pi(a))-a\in J
. Therefore,
(\hat{h}-h)\mu(\pi(a))=(\hat{h}-h)a
and
(1-\hat{h})\mu(\pi(a))=(1-\hat{h})a
.
Hence

\displaystyle\alpha(a)+\beta(\pi(a))+\gamma(\pi(a))

\displaystyle=ha+(\hat{h}-h)\mu(\pi(a))+(1-\hat{h})\mu(\pi(a))

(3.6)

\displaystyle=ha+(\hat{h}-h)a+(1-\hat{h})a

\displaystyle=a.\qed

The power of the Brake–Winter decomposition comes from the fact that when the quasicentral approximate unit

is almost idempotent, the hereditary subalgebras

and

are orthogonal. This is important as a sum of order zero maps with orthogonal ranges is again order zero. This means that the colours used in nuclear dimension approximations for

can be re-used in nuclear dimension approximations for

, so that

(3.7)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\alpha_{n}+\gamma_{n}\circ\pi)\leq\max(\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\alpha_{n}),\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\gamma_{n}\circ\pi)).

Since the image of
\alpha_{n}
lies in
J
, we have
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\alpha_{n})\leq\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(J)
; since
\gamma_{n}\circ\pi
factors through

, the estimate
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\gamma_{n}\circ\pi)\leq\dim(X)
is true in a limiting sense as

.
6
6

6

The careful choice of wording is because
\gamma_{n}
is only
approximately
order zero.

Therefore, Question
A
can be answered affirmatively for a given extension by finding a suitable strategy for handling the
\beta_{n}
term without increasing the estimate of
\max(\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{nuc}}(J),\dim(X))
.
7
7

7

Note that, if the extension is a direct sum or has a quasicentral approximate unit consisting of projections, then

can be taken to be zero.

The strategy for handling the
\beta_{n}
terms in the proof of Theorem

is as follows:
After approximating

using a

-coloured open cover of

to include (some) Borel functions that are not continuous.

In Section

, we use Gabe’s

-stable uniqueness theorem to prove a suitable extension theorem for the c.p.c. order zero map

in the Brake–Winter decomposition under the hypothesis that

is injective.

8

8

Recall from (

is the

-homomorphism whose domain is a cone corresponding to the c.p.c. order zero map

In Section

As discussed in the introduction, to prove Theorem

3.1.
Extending order zero maps

Starting from the Brake–Winter decomposition (Proposition
3.1
), the key idea in
[
34
]
is to choose the quasicentral approximate unit

so that the

are matrix algebras and then use Lin’s uniqueness theorem for approximately multiplicative maps into matrix algebras

, Theorem 2.10]
to extend

to a c.p.c. order zero map on the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

of all bounded Borel functions on

The appearance of matrix algebras and the applicability of Lin’s uniqueness theorem is specific to the extensions considered in
[
34
]
where the ideal is
\mathbb{K}
. To transfer this idea to purely infinite ideals, we would like to prove that a c.p.c. order zero map

into an ultraproduct of Kirchberg algebras can be extended to a c.p.c. order zero map defined on the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

of all bounded Borel functions on

abelian
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra containing

is injective. This is sufficient for the proof of Theorem

.

We begin by deriving the version of Gabe’s uniqueness theorem (
[
33
, Theorem B]
) that we need to prove the extension theorem.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
B_{n}
be a sequence of Kirchberg algebras. Write

.

9

9

Let

Let

Applying Proposition

3.2.
Ensuring injectivity

The Brake–Winter decomposition works for any quasicentral approximate unit
(h_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
. In this section, we shall show that, for any essential extension of
C(X)
by a stable Kirchberg algebra, an almost idempotent quasicentral approximate unit can be chosen such that the c.p.c. order zero map

in the Brake–Winter decomposition (using the notation of Proposition

The method of proof is to first construct a trivial extension where the result holds, and then use that essential extensions of separable nuclear
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras by Kirchberg algebras absorb trivial representations (see Theorem

). This technique has its roots in Gardner and Tikuisis’ work on the nuclear dimension of generalised Toeplitz algebras

]
.

5.
Graph algebras

In this section we relate our results to graph
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, describing exactly which finite graphs have
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras that satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
B
(or rather, Corollary
4.2
).

If
E
is a directed graph, then its vertex and edge sets are denoted
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
respectively. The range and source maps
r,s:E^{1}\to E^{0}
encode the directions of the edges. For
n>1
, we write
E^{n}=\{e_{1}\cdots e_{n}:e_{i}\in E^{1},r(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})\}
for the set of paths of length
n
, and for
\mu=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}\in E^{n}
we write
s(\mu)=s(e_{1})
and
r(\mu)=r(e_{n})
. We write
E^{*}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}E^{n}
. Note that our edge direction conventions are as per the earlier papers on graph algebras, as these form the primary sources for almost all the results we cite. Today it is more typical to use the opposite direction conventions.

A
sink
in
E
is a vertex
v\in E^{0}
such that
s^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
. So we say that
E

has no sinks
if
s:E^{1}\to E^{0}
is surjective. We say that
E
is
connected
if the smallest equivalence relation
\sim
on
E^{0}
such that
r(e)\sim s(e)
for all
e\in E^{1}
is the complete equivalence relation. We say that
E
is
strongly connected
if for all
v,w\in E^{0}
there exists
\mu\in E^{*}
such that
s(\mu)=v
and
r(\mu)=w
. (With these conventions, the graph with one vertex and no edges is strongly connected and has a sink.)

A subset
V\subseteq E^{0}
is
hereditary
if whenever
e\in E^{1}
satisfies
s(e)\in V
, we also have
r(e)\in V
. Equivalently,
V
is hereditary if whenever
\mu\in E^{*}
satisfies
s(\mu)\in V
we also have
r(\mu)\in V
.

A subset
V\subseteq E^{0}
is
saturated
if
v\in V
whenever
v\in E^{0}
has the property that
s^{-1}(v)\neq\emptyset
and
r(e)\in V
for every
e\in s^{-1}(v)
.
The
saturation

\overline{V}
of a subset
V\subseteq E^{0}
is the smallest saturated set containing
V
. If
E
is finite and has no sinks, as is the case for the graphs in this paper, then an induction on the length of paths shows that

(5.1)

\{v\in E^{0}:\exists n\geq 0\text{ s.t. }\forall\mu\in E^{n},\big(s(\mu)=v\implies r(\mu)\in V\big)\}\subseteq\overline{V}.

If, additionally,
V
is hereditary, then we have equality in (
5.1
). If
V
is hereditary, then
\overline{V}
is also hereditary.

Given any subset
V\subseteq E^{0}
, the
subgraph of
E
over
V
is the directed graph with
E^{0}_{V}=V
and
E^{1}_{V}=\{e\in E^{1}:s(e)\in V\text{ and }r(e)\in V\}
, and with source and range maps inherited from
E
. If
V
is a hereditary set, then
E^{1}_{V}
is precisely

.

A
cycle
in
E
is a path
\mu=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}\in E^{*}\setminus E^{0}
such that
s(\mu)=r(\mu)
. An
exit
from this cycle is an edge

such that

but

for some

. For

, we say that

is a
simple cycle
if there is a cycle

. This forces

, so the graph with one vertex and no edges is not a simple cycle.

The
graph
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

\mathrm{C}^{*}(E)

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra generated by mutually orthogonal projections
\{p_{v}:v\in E^{0}\}
and partial isometries

s_{e}^{*}s_{e}=p_{r(e)}

s_{e}s_{e}^{*}\leq p_{s(e)}
for all

, and
p_{v}=\sum_{s(e)=v}s_{e}s_{e}^{*}
whenever
0<|s^{-1}(v)|<\infty
. For
V\subseteq E^{0}
, we write

for the ideal of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(E)
generated by
\{p_{v}:v\in V\}
.

For
\mu=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}\in E^{*}
. we write
s_{\mu}=s_{e_{1}}\cdots s_{e_{n}}
. For any distinct

. Therefore, for any

s_{\mu}s_{\mu}^{*}

s_{\nu}s_{\nu}^{*}
are orthogonal unless either
\mu

\nu\mu^{\prime}
or
\nu
factorises as
\mu\nu^{\prime}
.

If
\mu

\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(E)
