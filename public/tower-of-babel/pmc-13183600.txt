ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONTo examine the generalizability of Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarker models in real‐world older adults, we examined AD biomarker relationships with cognition in two multicenter cohorts that differ with respect to recruitment approach and health risk factors but were matched on a variety of characteristics.METHODSWe compared harmonized health and demographic data, AD and cerebrovascular biomarkers, and cognitive performance in the community‐recruited U.S. Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (U.S. POINTER) Imaging substudy and a matched sample from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) which recruited primarily from academic specialty clinics.RESULTSElevated β‐amyloid (Aβ) and tau were associated with cognitive performance in ADNI but not U.S. POINTER. Findings were consistent across different cohort matching schemes, and were not explained by discrepancies in vascular risk.DISCUSSIONThe role of Aβ and tau in cognitive performance may be reduced in real world samples compared to academic specialty clinics.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers enable measurement of hallmark β‐amyloid (Aβ) and tau pathology in vivo, prior to the onset of significant cognitive impairment. This has led to development of biomarker‐based criteria for AD that does not necessarily include clinical AD dementia symptoms. Recently approved Aβ‐modifying treatments were developed in trials of individuals with both abnormal Aβ (biomarker‐based AD) and cognitive impairment (clinical AD symptoms), but there is an increasing emphasis on biomarker‐based AD in the absence of cognitive symptoms, with therapeutic trials underway to reduce or prevent AD pathology accumulation in asymptomatic individuals.

A major limitation of AD biomarker research across the spectrum of clinical impairment is that most study cohorts are not representative of the U.S. older adult population because of convenience sampling or recruitment based on AD‐specific criteria that excludes comorbidities. In such highly selected cohorts, the roles for AD biomarkers in disease progression have been well‐established 1 , with extensive evidence that Aβ precedes cognitive symptoms by over a decade 2 , and, together with tau burden, predicts poor cross‐sectional cognitive performance even in unimpaired older adults 3 . These findings have been corroborated in large multicohort samples and AD therapeutic trials, suggesting that they are generalizable 4 , but there is increasing recognition that AD biomarker research findings are not representative of the older adult population with respect to health and sociodemographic characteristics such as cardiovascular risk and ethnoracial group membership 5 which may in turn modify AD biomarkers 6 , 7 . The role of sample representativeness in AD biomarker research and potential impact on treatment disparities has been highlighted in recent clinical trials suggesting that the efficacy of anti‐amyloid therapy may not be consistent across sex, age, and ethnoracial subgroups 8 .

The U.S. Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (U.S. POINTER) is a clinical trial of a multidomain lifestyle intervention in a cohort without significant impairment, but with characteristics designed to increase risk for future impairment and to increase generalizability of the results to the older adult population (recruitment via targeting mailing and grassroots outreach, enrichment for cardiovascular disease and under‐represented ethnoracial group membership) 9 , 10 . To examine whether associations between AD biomarkers and cognition are consistent across older adult cohorts, we compared baseline biomarker and cognitive measurements in the U.S. POINTER imaging substudy participants to a subset of Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) participants matched on key characteristics and recruited via academic center referrals and online advertising without emphasis on health risk characteristics.

METHODS

Participants and study design

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We examined PubMed for observational studies examining relationships between Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers and cognition and found that most studies report that AD pathology (elevated β‐amyloid (Aβ) and tau) is associated with poorer cognitive performance, including in unimpaired samples. However, these findings are primarily based on participants recruited from memory clinics in academic centers who may not be representative of the older adult population. Interpretation: We found that canonical relationships between AD biomarkers and cognitive performance were observed in a memory clinic‐recruited sample but were not replicated in a community‐recruited cohort despite matching based on key characteristics. This discrepancy was not explained by differences in cardiovascular risk or other non‐AD specific biomarkers. Future directions: Longitudinal examination of individuals in real‐world clinical settings will be critical for determining whether frequently reported AD biomarker‐cognition relationships generalize to community‐recruited samples.

We identified ADNI and POINTER participants with contemporaneous Aβ and tau positron emission tomography (PET) images, structural MRI, apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotype, and clinical data; then matched the cohorts on basic demographic and clinical characteristics: age, sex, ApoE4, clinical status assessed with Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDRsb) score 11 ; see the Statistical Methods section.

Protocols for each cohort were approved by local and central Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All neuroimaging and clinical measurements were acquired and analyzed identically or harmonized as described below to enable direct cohort comparisons 12 .

U.S. POINTER imaging

U.S. POINTER Imaging is a substudy of the U.S. POINTER trial, which recruited participants using targeted mass mailings of individuals likely to meet study criteria based on electronic medical records and grassroots community outreach 13 from five U.S. locations selected for broad geographical representation (see Supplementary Data) and is testing whether random assignment to either of two multidomain lifestyle interventions affects 2‐year cognitive change 9 . U.S. POINTER trial participants were aged 60–79 years who lack significant memory impairment (global CDR ≤ 0.5), report sedentary lifestyle and suboptimal diet, and are at risk for future cognitive decline based on family history of memory impairment, under‐represented racial or ethnic group (URG) membership, and/or cardiovascular risk factors. Individuals with prevalent cerebrovascular disease were not excluded 9 , 10 and a small proportion were diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) based on central diagnostic adjudication 14 (see Supplementary Materials for detailed criteria), and willingness to undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and PET imaging was not required. The imaging substudy enrolled 1052 participants from the parent trial (about 50%) across all five U.S. sites 15 . Prior to cohort matching, there were 911 POINTER imaging participants with baseline Aβ (18F‐florbetaben; FBB), MRI, tau PET (18F‐MK6240), cardiovascular risk, and cognitive measurements.

ADNI

The ADNI is a longitudinal natural history study of AD that includes a variety of biomarker and clinical assessments and is designed to serve as a model for clinical trials 16 . Participants are recruited from 59 academic and specialty health centers (e.g. memory clinics) throughout the U.S. Participants were recruited without emphasis on health risk characteristics via ADNI sites in‐clinic referrals and outreach events, multimedia advertising, and an online registry 17 . Participants were excluded if they had elevated vascular risk (Hachinski Ischemic Scale scores > 4 18 , 19 and if they were unwilling to undergo neuroimaging, lumbar puncture (optional in some cases), and a venipuncture for blood sampling (see Supplementary Materials for more details). We identified 846 ADNI participants without a clinical dementia diagnosis and with MMSE and CDRsb measurements within the same range as POINTER participants (MMSE ≥ 23, CDRsb ≤ 2) and contemporaneous tau PET (18F‐flortaucipir; FTP), Aβ PET (18F‐FBB or 18F‐florbetapir; FBP), MRI, cardiovascular risk, and cognitive measurements, and included these as candidates for subsequent matching to the U.S. POINTER imaging cohort.

Demographic and clinical assessments

Age, sex, ethnoracial group, and education were collected via self‐report. We dichotomized ethnoracial group membership as URG and non‐Hispanic White. We assessed global cognitive measurements (global CDR and CDRsb measurements, Mini‐Mental State Exam (MMSE) 20 ), ApoE4 carriership, Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) 21 , and cardiovascular risk measurements (body mass index [BMI], systolic blood pressure, presence of diabetes, smoking status) in both cohorts. Area Deprivation Index (ADI) 22 and socioeconomic status dichotomized as > 47 on the Hollingshead Scale 23 were calculated in POINTER and a subset (58%) of ADNI participants. Framingham Risk Scores (FRS) were used as a composite estimate of cardiovascular risk based on age, sex, systolic blood pressure, use of antihypertensive medications, BMI, presence of smoking and diabetes history 24 . Because FRS is weighted by age and sex, individual components were examined separately in models comparing a variety of demographic, health, and biomarker predictors of cognition. Finally, after defining the matched cohorts (see Statistical analysis), we calculated a modified five‐component Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) 25 which included 4 tests acquired and scored identically between cohorts (Trails B (log‐transformed), MMSE, Logical Memory IIa delayed recall subtest from the Wechsler Memory Scale 26 , and verbal fluency/animals subtest) and a cohort‐specific test of word list learning (Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test, POINTER only; ADAS word recall, ADNI only). Each component was first standardized using means and standard deviations across the matched ADNI+POINTER sample (this was preceded by an initial cohort‐specific Z‐score step for the word list learning tests), then the five resulting Z‐scores were averaged to generate a PACC score for each participant.

PET and MRI methods

PET and MRI acquisition and quantification methods were harmonized to enable cohort comparisons 12 . Briefly, T1 images were processed with FreeSurfer v7.1 to define cortical regions of interest and reference regions in native space for both Aβ and tau PET quantification, and measurement of hippocampal and cortical gray matter volumes (made up of cortical, subcortical, and supratentorial gray matter structures) were defined based on a multiatlas approach 27 and were adjusted for intracranial volume as previously described 15 . White matter hyperintensities (WMH) were quantified using fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and T1 images, adjusted for intracranial volume, and log‐transformed 28 .

Global cortical SUVrs were calculated from FBB scans (202/409 matched ADNI participants; all POINTER participants) and FBP scans (207/409 matched ADNI participants) relative to whole cerebellum and transformed to Centiloids (CLs) using tracer‐ and pipeline‐specific equations and thresholds for Aβ positivity (FBB, 18 CL; FBP, 20 CL) 12 .

SUVrs within FreeSurfer‐defined entorhinal cortex (ERC) and the temporal metaROI (entorhinal, amygdala, parahippocampal, fusiform, inferior temporal, and middle temporal regions) were calculated for FTP scans (ADNI) and MK6240 (POINTER) relative to inferior cerebellar gray matter 12 . Tau positivity for each tracer was defined for descriptive purposes using a threshold of two standard deviations greater than the mean of Aβ‐ individuals. For statistical models that included tau measures from both cohorts, we standardized FTP and MK6240 ERC and temporal metaROI measurements using unimpaired Aβ‐ individuals aged 60–70 years as reference groups to define Z‐score means and standard deviations 12 .

Statistical methods

We matched ADNI participants to POINTER participants on age, sex, CDRsb scores, and ApoE4 status using propensity score matching in R (MatchIt; see Supplementary Materials ). ADNI participants were matched to POINTER participants 1:1 with possible replacement, such that any single ADNI participant could be matched at most two times to a POINTER participant in order to maximize the number of POINTER participants who could be included. We then carried out two‐sided t‐tests and chi‐squared tests to compare (a) the matched and unmatched POINTER samples and (b) the matched ADNI and matched POINTER cohorts on continuous and categorical variables. We examined FRS and Cohort (and Aβ ± status and Aβ×Cohort terms in tau models only since age and sex are key components of the FRS) as independent variables with Aβ (CL), Z‐scored entorhinal tau, Z‐scored temporal metaROI tau, and PACC scores as outcomes.

Merged‐cohort models

We then fit linear regression models with Aβ and PACC as outcomes and with both cohorts included in the same models. Predictors included cohort, age, sex, ApoE4 carriership, and cohort interactions with age, sex, and ApoE4. PACC models included Aβ and cohort×Aβ; PACC models also included Aβ×tau, and cohort×Aβ×tau, education, and number of PACC exposures, as 16% of ADNI participants had > 1 exposure to PACC tests. Models with tau outcomes did not meet linear regression assumptions of normally distributed errors, so in order to examine whether Aβ and tau relationships differed by cohort, Spearman Rank Correlations between Aβ and tau (entorhinal, temporal) were calculated for each cohort and compared the resulting ρ values using Fisher's z test.

Cohort‐specific models

We also fit separate models predicting PACC scores for each cohort with the same predictors included in the merged‐cohort models above (excluding cohort interaction terms) as well as a variety of additional demographic, health, and biomarker variables: URG status, GDS, FRS component measures (smoking and diabetes status, systolic blood pressure, BMI), WMH, hippocampal volume, and cerebral gray matter volume. Because entorhinal and temporal metaROI tau are strongly correlated, we examined separate PACC models with each tau region as a predictor. We estimated the proportion of variance (partial η2) independently associated with each statistically significant (p < 0.05) or marginally significant (0.05 < p < 0.10) independent variable for each cohort. Continuous independent variables were centered so that model results could be interpreted with respect to a 70‐year‐old non‐smoker, non‐diabetic female ApoE4 carrier of non‐URG status with 12 years of education, a BMI of 30, GDS of 0, systolic blood pressure of 120, 20 CLs, and with a tau SUVr of 1.0 (no uptake relative to the reference region; see Supplementary materials). Regression model residuals were examined for normality using graphical approaches.

We carried out several sensitivity analyses of the primary merged‐cohort PACC model in order to exclude the possibility that biases introduced by sample selection and/or matching criteria accounted for any observed cohort differences: (a) inclusion of all 911 POINTER participants (matched and unmatched), (b) replacing CDRsb with MMSE in the primary matching criteria, (c) replacing clinical diagnosis (MCI, cognitively unimpaired) with CDRsb and ApoE4 in the primary matching criteria, (d) adding AD pathology buden (Aβ and entorhinal tau) to the matching criteria in addition to the primary criteria, (e) further restricting the analysis to Aβ+ individuals only, and (f) inclusion of AD and socioeconomic status (SES) in the POINTER‐only model, as availability of this data was limited in ADNI.

RESULTS

Matched and unmatched samples

A total of 520 ADNI and 155 POINTER participants who met initial eligibility criteria (see the Methods section) were excluded during matching. Due to differences between the cohorts prior to matching, the matched (compared to unmatched) POINTER participants were older, more likely to be male, had higher ApoE4 carriership, and overall had more unfavorable cognitive and biomarker characteristics than the unmatched POINTER group, including higher amyloid and entorhinal tau, more WMH, and higher FRS (Table 1). Subsequent analyses used only the matched samples except for a sensitivity analysis with the entire (matched + unmatched) POINTER cohort.

Matched POINTER versus matched ADNI samples

Matched POINTER participants did not differ from the ADNI matched cohort on characteristics used for matching, except for age: POINTER subjects were slightly younger (68.9 ± 5.2 years) than ADNI (69.8 ± 5.4 years) despite matching (Table 1). The matched POINTER sample was less likely to have a diagnosis of MCI, and had lower education, higher GDS, BMI and FRS, a marginally higher proportion of URG individuals than ADNI, and were more likely to be treated for hypertension and to have a history of smoking, though systolic blood pressure did not differ. Finally, POINTER subjects had modestly lower cognition despite less entorhinal and temporal tau burden based in continuous measures, lower temporal but not entorhinal tau positivity and greater cortical gray volume than ADNI, but the samples did not differ on Aβ or hippocampal volumes.

Associations with cardiovascular risk composite scores

FRS was not associated with Aβ, entorhinal tau, or temporal tau (alone or separately for Aβ ± groups) in either cohort Figure (1A‐C). FRS was associated with PACC scores and there was no interaction with cohort (Figure 1D; B = ‐0.007 [confidence interval {CI} ‐0.009–0.005], p < 0.001), but this association did not survive addition of age, sex, and education to the model. In a further examination of possible interactions between FRS and AD pathology, there were no Aβ×FRS interactions on tau, or tau×FRS interactions on PACC scores in either cohort.

Associations with Aβ PET, tau PET, and cognitive performance in simple merged‐cohort models

There was no cohort difference in Aβ, but across both cohorts higher Aβ was associated with female sex (B = 4.90 [CI 1.84–8.13], p = 0.002), older age (B = 1.11 [CI 0.81–1.40], p < 0.001), and ApoE4 positivity (B = 21.09 [cccc 17.78–24.40], p < 0.001; Table S1). There was no difference between cohorts in the association between Aβ and entorhinal tau (Fisher's z = 1.64, p = 0.10, based on ρ = 0.356 in ADNI and ρ = 0.266 in POINTER; Figure 2A) and this effect was similar when examining the Aβ+ groups only (p = 0.09). On the other hand, the association between Aβ and temporal tau was higher in ADNI than POINTER (Fisher's z = 2.87, p = 0.004, based on ρ = 0.388 in ADNI and ρ = 0.230 in POINTER; Figure 2B), but the cohort difference was only marginally significant in the Aβ+ individuals (p = 0.07).

Entorhinal tau was associated with lower PACC scores among Aβ+ individuals in ADNI to a greater extent than POINTER (Figure 3A; Cohort×Aβ×tau; B = ‐0.001 [CI ‐0.002‐ ‐0.0001], p < 0.001; Table S2A), with similar results with temporal tau instead of entorhinal tau as a predictor in a separate model (Figure 3B; Table S2B). Sensitivity analyses in merged‐cohort models predicting PACC scores

We carried out several sensitivity analyses of the primary cohort×Aβ×tau findings (e.g. Figure 3) in order to exclude several potential sources of bias that may have contributed to the stronger association between AD biomarkers and cognition in ADNI than POINTER. To examine the possibility that POINTER participants selected by the matching process drove association, we examined the entire (matched+unmatched) POINTER cohort in the merged‐cohort models predicting PACC and found comparable cohort×Aβ tau effects for both models with entorhinal tau (t = ‐3.09, p = 0.002) and temporal tau (t = ‐4.11, p < 0.001) as predictors (Figure S2).

To examine the possibility that the use of CDR resulted in biased matching for clinical severity, we examined alternative matching schemes using MMSE rather than CDRsb (in addition to age, sex, and ApoE4) and diagnosis rather than CDRsb (in addition to age and sex). The MMSE‐based matching scheme resulted in 415 ADNI participants (87% overlap with the primary matched sample) matched to 790 POINTER participants (92% overlap with the primary matched sample), and found similar results, with a significant cohort×Aβ×tau effect in the entorhinal tau model (t = ‐2.40, p = 0.017). The cohort×Aβ×tau effect in the temporal tau model did not reach statistical significance (t = 1.19, p = 0.236), but the Aβ×tau effect on PACC scores was statistically significant in ADNI (t = ‐2.01, p = 0.045) but not POINTER (t = 0.74, p = 0.462; Figure S3). The clinical diagnosis‐based matching scheme resulted in 80% overlap with the primary matched sample, and there were similar cohort×Aβ×tau effects for both the entorhinal (t = ‐3.94, p < 0.001) and temporal (t = ‐3.44, p < 0.001) regions (not shown).

Similar results were also obtained when Aβ and tau were added to the matching criteria (Figure S4; cohort×Aβ×tau effect with entorhinal tau: t = ‐2.3, p = 0.019 and with temporal tau: t = ‐3.34, p < 0.001), and when Aβ‐ individuals were excluded from the model (cohort×tau effect with entorhinal tau: t = ‐3.9, p < 0.001 and with temporal tau: t = ‐3.2, p = 0.002). Finally, in order to exclude the possibility that practice effects in ADNI influenced cognitive performance, we found similar results when excluding 16% of the ADNI sample that had > 1 exposure to PACC tests prior to the session used in this study.

Associations with cognitive performance in expanded cohort‐specific models

In models predicting PACC scores, demographic variables (age, sex, education, URG status) accounted for a similar amount of PACC variance in POINTER (24%) and ADNI (23%). Also, in ADNI but not POINTER, 15% of additional variance was explained by AD biomarkers (entorhinal tau and Aβ×tau interaction) and other neuroimaging biomarkers (hippocampal volume, cerebral gray matter, and WMH), whereas in POINTER these biomarkers together only accounted for 1% of variance. In POINTER but not ADNI there was a small amount of additional variance (1%) explained by blood pressure (Figure 4; Table S3A). Models with temporal tau as a predictor instead of entorhinal tau were similar (Table S4A). Finally, GDS scores accounted for 5% variance in ADNI but 0% in POINTER.

In sensitivity analyses to examine the influence of different cohort matching approaches on our primary results, we examined the effect of adding entorhinal tau and Aβ status as additional matching variables, and the results were comparable, with similar variance accounted for by demographic variables across cohorts (POINTER: 25%, ADNI: 22%), and with 11% of variance explained by AD and other neuroimaging biomarkers in ADNI but only 2% in POINTER (Table S3B and Figure S5A). Finally, in these matched cohorts, we examined the same models in Aβ+ individuals, and found that a larger proportion of variance was explained by demographic variables in POINTER (22%) than ADNI (15%), but in ADNI, 28% of variance was explained by AD biomarkers (19% of which was entorhinal tau), whereas in POINTER only 4% of variance was explained by biomarkers (0% was entorhinal tau; Table S3C and Figure S5B). Models with temporal tau instead of entorhinal tau were similar (Tables S4B, S4C).

Finally, we added sociodemographic data (ADI, SES) to the POINTER model only, as these variables were unavailable in almost half of the ADNI sample. ADI was a predictor of PACC scores but only explained 1% of variance in PACC scores, while SES was not associated with PACC.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to determine whether the canonical relationships reported widely between AD neuroimaging biomarkers, health and demographic measures, and cognition were consistent in two multicenter neuroimaging cohorts of older adults matched on key characteristics but recruited using different approaches. Participants from the U.S. POINTER imaging study, a clinical trial cohort with community‐based recruitment enriched for health risk and other demographic characteristics that were designed to approximate the U.S. older adult population, were compared to a subsample from ADNI, an observational cohort without emphasis on health risk and recruited primarily via academic specialty clinic referrals and multimedia advertising. Notably, no clinically diagnosed AD dementia patients were included, but there was considerable variability in cognitive performance in this predominantly unimpaired sample. Although the ADNI participants were matched to U.S. POINTER participants on basic demographic and clinical status, but not matched on biomarkers, the cohorts did not differ on Aβ, WMH, or hippocampal volumes, although ADNI had higher tau and lower cerebral gray matter whereas POINTER had lower cognitive performance. However, in models examining a variety of factors predicting cognition in each cohort, a greater proportion of explained variance in cognitive performance was consistently observed in ADNI than POINTER.

This larger proportion of variance explained in cognition was driven by the greater role of biomarkers in ADNI than POINTER. In ADNI, all AD‐specific (Aβ, tau) and non‐AD specific (hippocampal volume, WMH, cerebral gray matter) neuroimaging biomarkers together accounted for a larger amount of variance in cognitive performance (15%) than the same measures did in POINTER (1%), and this was driven by the contribution of tau (10%). This discrepancy was not accounted for by the contribution of demographic variables (age, sex, education, or URG status), which together explained a similar amount of variance in cognition in both cohorts (ADNI, 23%; POINTER, 24%), or by health risk (e.g. blood pressure), which unexpectedly accounted for minimal variance in cognition in either cohort (Figure S3, Figure 4). Depression symptoms were higher in POINTER, but these symptoms accounted for 5% of variance in PACC scores in ADNI but none in POINTER.

The weak biomarker‐cognition associations we observed in POINTER conflict with recent studies demonstrating the utility of biomarkers in cognition, including a large multicohort analysis in unimpaired individuals 4 , raising questions about whether aspects of our sampling, matching, or analysis methods may have driven the findings. However, these findings were generally consistent across two key regions of tau accumulation (entorhinal tau, temporal tau) as predictors of cognition in separate models, and also survived several sensitivity analyses, including matching for clinical severity using a different cognitive assessment (MMSE or diagnosis instead of CDR), matching for Aβ and tau burden in addition to the primary matching characteristics, and examination of only individuals on the AD pathway based on Aβ positivity. The examination of factors predicting cognition when the sample was restricted to those on the AD pathway highlighted a particularly stark difference between cohorts, where in ADNI, entorhinal tau accounted for 19% of PACC variance (14% for temporal tau), whereas among POINTER Aβ+ individuals, neither entorhinal nor temporal tau was a significant predictor of cognition.

The primary cohort differences in the association between biomarkers and cognition also survived additional sensitivity analyses: inclusion of the entire POINTER imaging cohort as opposed to the matched cohort, accounting for cognitive test exposures and, separately, excluding ADNI participants with cognitive testing exposures prior to imaging, accounting for cardiovascular risk characteristics that were emphasized in recruitment of the U.S. POINTER sample but not the ADNI cohort, and examination of sociodemographic variables (ADI, SES) in the POINTER models only alongside other health and biomarker variables which accounted for 1% of additional variance in cognition.

The observation that variance explained in cognition was consistently higher in ADNI than POINTER raises the question of what factors do explain these measurements in POINTER. A key difference in these cohorts was recruitment strategy; POINTER participants were recruited from the community via targeted mass mailings emphasizing health risk characteristics and community outreach events 13 , 32 , with the goal of enrolling a sample that is more representative of U.S. older adults than most observational studies and trials. In ADNI, participants were primarily recruited from academic medical center referrals and may therefore be likely less representative of the older adult population. Another surprising finding was that although recruitment based on health risk in POINTER suggested an increased role for cardiovascular disease in accounting for lower cognitive performance, we found that cardiovascular risk was minimally associated with AD pathophysiology or cognition in either cohort. FRS (a composite measure of cardiovascular risk heavily weighted by age and sex) was not associated with AD biomarkers or cognition in either cohort after accounting for age and sex and education. Further, component measures of the FRS (systolic blood pressure, BMI, diabetes, and smoking status) explained only minimal variance in cognition in either cohort. These findings conflict with previous research indicating that cardiovascular risk increases tau accumulation and cognitive decline 7 , 33 , although such findings have been inconsistent 34 , 35 , 36 , and may depend on more on cardiovascular risk during midlife more than later life 37 . Furthermore, the causal relationship between cardiovascular disease and cognitive impairment remains unclear in clinical trials of hypertension control 38 , 39 . Our findings suggest that cardiovascular risk is not closely related to AD biomarkers or cognition in two predominantly unimpaired large multicenter cohorts, suggesting that the cognitive effects of later‐life vascular risk are minimally detectable in this predominantly unimpaired sample, but may be evident at a later stage of disease progression, or that the measures we had access to in this study were not sensitive to effects on cognition. Finally, it is critical to note that additional copathology such as alpha‐synuclein and TDP‐43, though not possible to measure in sample, may be key factors in explaining cognitive performance.

There are several limitations to note. First, these analyses were limited to the set of health and biomarker variables available for both cohorts. Sociodemographic factors such as neighborhood disadvantage and socioeconomic status likely play an important role in cognitive performance but were not possible to examine comprehensively, as they were only available in a subset of the ADNI sample. Second, longitudinal measures were not available in the matched cohorts but will be critical for understanding how these discrepancies influence longer term participant trajectories. Third, the discrepancies observed in POINTER and ADNI may reflect features specific to U.S. healthcare and population characteristics, and therefore may not extend to other older adult populations internationally. Fourth, different tau PET ligands were used in ADNI (FTP) and POINTER (MK6240) although we addressed these differences by standardizing measurements in merged‐cohort models, and the tau differences we observed (ADNI FTP > POINTER MK6240) were the opposite direction of what would be expected if tracer differences drove our findings 40 . Finally, cognitive performance is likely influenced by many factors such as assessment setting, familiarity with psychometric testing and other research experiences, and participant motivation which were not measured here, but were unlikely to differ substantially between cohorts in a way that would influence the biomarker results. POINTER participants’ willingness to undergo randomization and an experimental intervention is driven by complex motivations that may differ from those related to willingness to participate in an observational study 41 , 42 , but it is unclear how this may have contributed to the cohort differences we describe.

Together, our findings suggest that AD biomarkers that have been frequently linked to cognitive performance in numerous studies with highly selected participants play a reduced role in explaining cognition in POINTER, and further, that cardiovascular risk and non‐AD specific biomarkers (WMH and hippocampal and cerebral gray matter volume) unexpectedly account for only minimal additional variance, despite the presumed influence of these variables on cognition in heterogeneous, community‐based cohorts 43 , 44 . Although there were multiple differences in measured characteristics between the matched cohorts, the unexplained variance in cognition that remained even after accounting for these characteristics suggests either that the effects of variables in this study were not captured by statistical models used here, or that additional health characteristics or sociodemographic features not measured in these studies nonetheless play an important role in cognitive performance. Our findings suggest that frequently reported associations between AD biomarkers and cognition that have been frequently reported in participants recruited from academic specialty clinics may not generalize to samples that are more representative of older U.S. adults.
