Geometric Tear Configuration Influences Intraoperative Reparability after Arthroscopic Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: Hyun Gon Kim, Yeong Jeon, Dong Hun Suh, Jung Ho Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotator cuff (RC) tear morphology is thought to influence reparability and healing potential, yet few clinical studies have compared outcomes across distinct tear patterns. The purpose of this study was to determine whether different RC tear patterns affect reparability and retear rate following arthroscopic repair.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 134 patients who underwent arthroscopic repair for posterosuperior RC tears between 2018 and 2022. Tears were classified arthroscopically into 5 geometric patterns—crescent, anterior L-shaped, posterior L-shaped, U-shaped, and trapezoid—based on a modified Burkhart-Davidson system. Reparability was assessed by the ability to achieve ≥ 50% footprint coverage at the time of surgery. Structural integrity was evaluated by magnetic resonance imaging at 6 months postoperatively. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was performed by matching crescent and anterior L-shaped tears based on tear size using propensity score matching.ResultsCrescent-shaped tears were most common (47.8%), followed by anterior L-shaped (28.4%), trapezoid-shaped (11.2%), U-shaped (9.0%), and posterior L-shaped tears (3.7%). Interobserver agreement for tear pattern classification was moderate (κ = 0.634). Incomplete repair was associated with larger tear dimensions and tear pattern, with trapezoid-shaped tears demonstrating the highest rate of incomplete repair. Anterior L-shaped tears showed the lowest incomplete repair rate. Retear was associated with larger tear size, lower muscle occupation ratio, and greater fatty infiltration on univariate analysis; however, no variables remained independently associated with retear on multivariable analysis. In the propensity score–matched cohort (32 anterior L-shaped vs. 32 crescent-shaped tears), anterior L-shaped tears demonstrated a lower rate of incomplete repair (18.8% vs. 43.8%, p = 0.031), while retear rates were similar between groups.ConclusionsRC tear pattern was associated with intraoperative reparability after arthroscopic repair but did not independently predict postoperative structural healing. Anterior L-shaped tears demonstrated higher reparability than crescent-shaped tears, whereas retear rates were comparable. Tear pattern recognition may assist surgical planning rather than serve as a standalone prognostic factor for tendon healing.

Rotator cuff (RC) tears present with diverse 3-dimensional morphologies, which are increasingly recognized as important determinants of surgical planning and prognosis. Tear morphology classification was first described by Ellman and Gartsman,1) and Davidson and Burkhart2) subsequently introduced a geometric system incorporating tear mobility and configuration, describing crescent, U-shaped, L-shaped, and massive retracted patterns. These tear patterns guide intraoperative decisions regarding reduction techniques, anchor placement, and suture configuration to achieve low-tension anatomic repair and optimize healing. Various repair techniques have since been developed to accommodate specific tear configurations and enhance footprint coverage.345)

Biomechanical studies have suggested that the geometric configuration of an RC tear may influence stress distribution and propagation patterns within the tendon.67) However, clinical studies have reported conflicting findings regarding the influence of tear pattern on healing outcomes; some have reported no association between tear pattern and healing, while others have identified specific patterns, such as L-shaped or U-shaped tears, as being more prone to failure.89) Therefore, the clinical relevance of tear morphology as an independent predictor of structural healing remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

In RC repair, achieving tension-free footprint coverage, also known as reparability, is considered critical for promoting tendon healing and improving clinical outcomes.1011) Insufficient footprint coverage or excessive tension at the repair site has been associated with higher rates of structural failure. Although various factors such as tear size, tendon mobility, and muscle quality contribute to reparability, the role of tear morphology—specifically, whether certain tear patterns are inherently more or less reducible—has not been sufficiently elucidated. Few clinical studies have directly evaluated how different tear patterns influence the degree to which the torn tendon can be reduced to its anatomical footprint without excessive tension. As such, the relationship between tear configuration and reparability remains an underexplored but clinically relevant issue. Mechanistically, tear geometry may influence the direction of reducibility vectors, the feasibility of margin convergence, and the continuity of the infraspinatus (ISP)–supraspinatus (SSP) complex and rotator cable. Even for tears with similar overall dimensions, a longitudinal split configuration may allow strain reduction through side-to-side closure, whereas broad tears without a split may require sheet-like mobilization and may remain under higher residual tension.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine whether different RC tear patterns affect reparability and retear rate following arthroscopic repair. We hypothesized that tear pattern is an independent predictor of both reparability and successful structural tendon healing, even when controlling for tear size and other known risk factors.

METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Ansan Hospital (IRB No. 2025AS0211), with the need for informed consent waived.

Patient Selection

We identified all patients who underwent primary arthroscopic RC repair (ARCR) for RC tears at a single tertiary hospital between January 2018 and December 2022. All surgeries were performed by 1 senior orthopedic shoulder surgeon (JHP). Inclusion criteria were full-thickness tears of the SSP with or without ISP involvement, and a minimum clinical follow-up of 24 months after surgery. Patients were excluded if they had isolated subscapularis (SSC) tears, revision RC surgery, additional procedures (such as patch augmentation, superior capsule reconstruction [SCR], and tendon transfer), acute traumatic cuff tears, advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis, prior shoulder infection, or any significant systemic disease that could affect healing. Eligible patients’ medical records and imaging were reviewed to extract demographic data and tear characteristics. RC tears were diagnosed based on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and confirmed by intraoperative arthroscopic findings.

Tear Pattern Classification

Intraoperatively, the senior surgeon classified each RC tear according to its geometric pattern. The classification was based on the Burkhart-Davidson system with slight modifications to define 5 distinct tear shapes: C-shaped (crescent), U-shaped, anterior L-shaped, posterior L-shaped, and trapezoid-shaped (Fig. 1).2) A C-shaped (crescent) tear was defined by a centrally located tendon apex, with an anteroposterior (AP)-to-mediolateral (ML) ratio ≥ 1.2 (AP length measured on oblique coronal images; ML retraction on oblique sagittal images). A U-shaped tear was defined as a centrally located apex, with an AP-to-ML ratio ≤ 0.8. For borderline cases with AP/ML ratios between 0.8 and 1.2, we applied the following stepwise arthroscopic criteria. First, the presence of a centrally located medial apex was confirmed, as both crescent-shaped and U-shaped tears share this feature. Second, a grasper probe was used to assess the predominant direction of tendon mobility intraoperatively. Cases in which the tendon demonstrated predominantly medial-to-lateral reducibility were classified as crescent-shaped, whereas cases with predominant anterior-to-posterior mobility were classified as U-shaped. L-shaped tears were characterized by a longitudinal split in the tendon, creating 2 “leaflets” with a visible apex at one end of the split. If the apex of the split was located anteriorly at the leading edge of the SSP, the tear was classified as an anterior L-shape; if the apex was at the junction between the SSP and ISP (more posteriorly), it was classified as a posterior L-shape. Trapezoid-shaped tears, corresponding to the ‘massive contracted’ type described by Davidson and Burkhart,2) were defined as broad tears with a relatively flat medial edge spanning the AP width of the SSP insertion, accompanied by substantial ML retraction (ML retraction ≥ 30 mm) and absence of a discrete apex. Arthroscopic surgery videos were later reviewed by a second independent orthopedic surgeon (HGK) blinded to the original classification, and tear patterns were recorded again. In cases of discrepancy, both reviewers re-evaluated the case using predefined criteria and reached a consensus classification.

Radiological Evaluation

All patients had preoperative shoulder MRI and a routine postoperative MRI at 6 months after surgery. In addition, a second follow-up MRI was selectively performed in patients who reported recurrent pain or functional deterioration to further evaluate tendon integrity. MRI examinations were reviewed by a fellowship-trained shoulder surgeon (HGK) who was not involved in the surgeries and was blinded to intraoperative findings and outcomes. On preoperative MRI, tear size of the SSP and ISP tendons was measured in 2 orthogonal directions: the AP length (on oblique coronal T2-weighted images) and the ML retraction (on oblique sagittal images). The larger of these 2 dimensions was recorded as the tear size in centimeters, as originally defined by DeOrio and Cofield in 1984.12) For subgroup analyses, a tear size cutoff of 2.5 cm was applied to the maximum tear dimension measured on preoperative MRI. We also documented the presence of SSC tendon tears.

RC muscle quality was evaluated on preoperative MRI using 2 parameters: the occupation ratio and the Goutallier fatty infiltration grade. For the SSP muscle, the occupation ratio was calculated on the T1-weighted oblique sagittal Y-view image as the cross-sectional area of the muscle belly divided by the area of the corresponding SSP fossa.13) Fatty degeneration of each muscle was graded 0 to 4 according to the Goutallier classification on the same oblique sagittal images. Grade 0 represents entirely normal muscle, and grades 1–4 represent increasing degrees of fat infiltration.1314) These MRI evaluations were performed independently of the surgical team to minimize bias.

Postoperative tendon integrity was assessed on the 6-month follow-up MRI using Sugaya’s classification.15) Cuff repairs were categorized from type I (intact with sufficient thickness) to type V (full-thickness retear with large defect). For the purposes of this study, a retear was defined as Sugaya type IV or V.

Surgical Technique and Reparability Assessment

All patients underwent ARCR in the beach-chair position under general anesthesia. A standard posterior glenohumeral portal was used for diagnostic arthroscopy, followed by subacromial visualization. Debridement of torn tendon edges and subacromial decompression (bursectomy and acromioplasty if required) were carried out to improve visualization and healing environment. The long head of the biceps tendon was addressed according to intraoperative pathology; if a partial tear involving more than 50% of the tendon thickness or subluxation of the long head of the biceps tendon was observed, a biceps tenotomy or tenodesis was performed.

The RC was repaired according to the tear pattern.2) Crescent-shaped tears were repaired directly to the footprint with minimal tension, typically using a single-row of suture anchors or a suture-bridge (double-row) technique to maximize footprint coverage. U-shaped tears were first converted into a more linear defect by performing side-to-side margin convergence sutures to close the longitudinal medial gap. After reducing the U-shaped tear with these side-to-side stitches, the remaining tendon was secured to bone with suture anchors to complete the repair. L-shaped tears were managed by identifying the “L” apex and repairing the longitudinal split before fixing the tendon down. For anterior L-shaped tears, the anteriorly based leaflet was mobilized and stitched to the posterior leaflet (side-to-side or margin convergence) to close the split, and then the tendon was repaired to the footprint with anchors. For posterior L-shaped tears, the posterior limb (often at the ISP–SSP junction) was sutured side-to-side to the anterior tendon, followed by fixation of the anterior SSP portion to the greater tuberosity with anchors. Trapezoid-shaped tears were repaired using a combination of side-to-side sutures and multiple suture anchors in a “best fit” manner. In all cases, care was taken to avoid undue tension on the repair; if necessary, medial advancement or interval slides were performed for massively retracted tendons. The number of suture anchors and configuration (single-row vs. double-row) were decided case by case based on tear size and tissue quality after reduction, aiming to optimize the contact area for healing.

Reparability of the tear was assessed intraoperatively after completion of the repair, based on the extent of footprint coverage achieved by the repaired cuff (Fig. 2). Footprint coverage was assessed post-fixation using a calibrated 5-mm premarked arthroscopic probe. Importantly, the reparability assessment was an outcome of the repair attempt rather than a decision criterion—we attempted tension-free repair in all cases and assessed footprint coverage after fixation was complete. Reparability was defined as the ability to achieve ≥ 50% footprint coverage under acceptable tension after repair completion. Coverage was estimated intraoperatively using a calibrated 5-mm premarked arthroscopic probe and recorded immediately after fixation. If less than 50% of the footprint could be securely covered—or if the tendon could not be sufficiently mobilized despite releases—the repair was classified as incomplete. The ≥ 50% footprint coverage threshold was based on Yoo et al., who demonstrated that incomplete repair (< 50% coverage) was associated with a 45.5% retear rate in large to massive RC tears.10) The surgeon documented this assessment of repair completeness at the end of the procedure.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation was standardized for all patients. The arm was immobilized in an abduction sling for 6 weeks following surgery. Gentle passive range-of-motion exercises for the elbow, wrist, and hand were initiated on postoperative day 1. Patients were instructed to avoid any active shoulder elevation or excessive external rotation during the initial 6-week protection phase. After 6 weeks, the abduction brace was discontinued and patients began gradual active-assisted and active range of motion exercises for the shoulder. Strengthening exercises and resistance training for the RC and periscapular musculature were introduced at around 12 weeks postoperatively. Patients were typically allowed to return to full daily activities by 6 months, with heavy labor or sports by around 9–12 months, depending on their recovery.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies or percentages. For group comparisons of patient demographics and tear characteristics among the different tear pattern groups (C, U, anterior L, posterior L, trapezoid), 1-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-parametric data. When overall group differences were significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were planned with the Tukey method or Dunn’s test, respectively. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test employed when expected cell counts were < 5.

The interobserver reliability of the tear pattern classification was assessed by calculating Cohen’s kappa coefficient for agreement between the senior surgeon’s intraoperative classification and the independent video review.16) A kappa value > 0.75 was considered excellent agreement, 0.40–0.75 moderate, and < 0.40 poor agreement.

To identify independent predictors of incomplete repair and of postoperative retear, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables with p < 0.10 on univariate analysis were entered into the multivariable models using a stepwise selection approach. In addition, a propensity score–matched subgroup analysis was conducted to directly compare outcomes between anterior L-shaped tears and crescent-shaped tears, as these 2 groups were of particular clinical interest. We performed a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) between patients with anterior L-shaped tears and those with crescent-shaped tears to account for baseline differences in tear size. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression, with the primary matching covariate being the size of the SSP/ISP tear (maximum tear dimension, reflecting tear magnitude).17) Nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was used, with a caliper set to 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. After matching, we assessed the balance of baseline variables between the 2 matched groups by calculating standardized mean differences (SMDs). An absolute SMD < 0.20 was considered indicative of adequate balance between the anterior L-shaped and crescent-shaped groups for a given variable. Post-matching, outcomes were compared between the matched pairs: continuous outcomes were analyzed with Student t-tests if normally distributed (or Mann–Whitney U-tests if non-parametric), and categorical outcomes were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. The propensity score–matched comparison was prespecified as an exploratory analysis; therefore, p-values were interpreted descriptively with attention to effect sizes and confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (v1.3.959, build 554; PBC), with statistical significance defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics and Tear Pattern Distribution

A total of 229 patients who underwent ARCR for symptomatic full-thickness RC tears were initially reviewed. Of these, 9 patients were excluded due to revision surgery, 20 due to isolated SSC tendon tears, 13 due to acute trauma, 11 due to glenohumeral osteoarthritis, 7 due to prior shoulder infection, and 6 due to systemic disease. An additional 13 patients who underwent procedures such as patch augmentation, SCR, or tendon transfer, and 16 who were lost to follow-up or missed postoperative imaging were also excluded. Consequently, 134 patients were included in the final analysis. The mean patient age was 62.0 ± 6.9 years (range, 40–79 years), and the cohort consisted of 50 females (37.3%). The average follow-up period was 40.65 ± 17.03 months (Table 1).

Based on arthroscopic evaluation, 5 distinct tear pattern morphologies were identified (Table 1). Crescent-shaped tears were the most common (n = 64, 47.8%), followed by anterior L-shaped tears (n = 38, 28.4%). Less frequent patterns included posterior L-shaped tears (n = 5, 3.7%), U-shaped tears (n = 12, 9.0%), and trapezoidal tears (n = 15, 11.2%). Among the 134 patients, 24 cases (17.9%) had borderline AP/ML ratios (0.8–1.2). To ensure classification reliability, interobserver agreement for tear pattern classification was assessed, yielding a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.634, indicating moderate agreement.

Baseline demographics were largely comparable across tear pattern groups, except for age. However, tear size differed significantly according to tear pattern. ML tendon retraction showed significant between-group differences (p = 0.007), with post hoc analysis revealing that ML length was greater in trapezoid-shaped tears than crescent-shaped tears (p < 0.001), and also greater in anterior L-shaped than crescent-shaped tears (p = 0.046). Similarly, AP tear length differed significantly by tear pattern (p = 0.003). Post hoc comparisons showed that trapezoid-shaped tears had significantly greater AP length than anterior L-shaped tears and U-shaped tears (p = 0.007 and p = 0.003, respectively).

Reparability and Structural Integrity According to Tear Pattern

The frequency of incomplete repair and postoperative retear both varied significantly across tear patterns (Table 1). Anterior L-shaped tears showed the lowest incomplete repair rate, with only 21.1% (8 / 38) requiring incomplete repair, followed by crescent-shaped tears at 34.4% (22 / 64). In contrast, trapezoid-shaped (73.3%, 11 / 15), posterior L-shaped (60.0%, 3 / 5), and U-shaped tears (50.0%, 6 / 12) demonstrated substantially higher rates of incomplete repair. Crescent-shaped tears showed the lowest retear rate (17.2%, 11 / 64), followed by anterior L-shaped tears (23.7%, 9 / 38), U-shaped tears (33.3%, 4 / 12), trapezoid-shaped tears (60%, 9 / 15), and posterior L-shaped tears (60%, 3 / 5).

Factors Associated with Incomplete Repair and Postoperative Retear

Incomplete repair was significantly associated with larger tear dimensions, including ML retraction (p = 0.039) and AP tear length (p = 0.046) (Table 2). Tear pattern also influenced reparability: compared to anterior L-shaped tears, trapezoid-shaped tears had higher odds of incomplete repair (p = 0.058), while U-shaped (p = 0.071) and posterior L-shaped (p = 0.083) patterns showed borderline significance. Lower occupation ratio (p < 0.001), SSP fatty infiltration (p = 0.088), and ISP fatty infiltration (p = 0.335) were also associated in univariate analysis, though only tear dimensions remained significant in the multivariate model.

Retear was likewise associated with tear morphology and tendon quality. On univariate analysis, retear was associated with larger AP tear length (p = 0.021), ML retraction (p < 0.001), lower occupation ratio (p < 0.001), and greater fatty infiltration of the SSP (p = 0.004) and ISP (p = 0.003). Trapezoid-shaped tears had a higher retear rate than anterior L-shaped tears (p = 0.015). In the multivariate model, none of the variables showed significant association.

Matched Cohort Comparison: Crescent vs. L-Shaped Tears

To further examine the influence of tear geometry independent of tear size, we performed a matched cohort analysis comparing crescent-shaped tears and anterior L-shaped tears. A total of 32 shoulders with anterior L-shaped tears were matched to 32 crescent-shaped tears based on SSP tear size. The SMD for SSP tear size was 0.1023, indicating an acceptable balance between groups. Table 3 shows the demographics and tear characteristics of the matched cohort.

Despite having similar tear sizes, anterior L-shaped tears exhibited better reparability. Incomplete repair occurred in only 6 of 32 anterior L-shaped tears (18.8%) compared to 14 of 32 crescent-shaped tears (43.8%) (p = 0.031). This difference was even more pronounced in the subgroup with larger tears (≥ 2.5 cm), where 61.9% of crescent-shaped tears underwent incomplete repair compared to only 22.7% of anterior L-shaped tears (p = 0.009) (Table 4). However, retear rates did not differ significantly between groups. Retear occurred in 6 of 32 patients (18.8%) with crescent-shaped tears and 7 of 32 patients (21.9%) with anterior L-shaped tears (p = 0.756). Subgroup analysis by tear size also showed no significant differences between groups (p = 0.943 for tear size < 2.5 cm; p = 0.795 for tear size ≥ 2.5 cm).

DISCUSSION

Our hypothesis was partially supported. Tear pattern was independently associated with intraoperative reparability—as demonstrated through propensity score-matched analysis showing anterior L-shaped tears had superior reparability despite similar tear size—but did not independently predict retear after multivariable adjustment. Clinically, pattern recognition may assist surgeons in anticipating the likelihood of achieving footprint restoration and in selecting tension-reduction strategies, rather than serving as a standalone predictor of healing. Our tear pattern distribution differs from some previous reports, with anterior L-shaped tears representing the second most common pattern (28.4%) and a relatively low prevalence of posterior L-shaped tears (3.7%).89181920) This can be explained by several factors. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the classification of trapezoid-shaped tears in our study. This category, adapted from the modified Burkhart-Davidson system, may overlap with U-shaped tears in prior classifications, thereby reducing the apparent frequency of U-shaped patterns in our cohort.2) In addition, our cohort was limited to posterosuperior full-thickness tears involving the SSP with or without ISP involvement, which may have influenced the relative distribution of anterior versus posterior L-shaped tears. Anterior L-shaped tears with preserved ISP continuity may have been more readily classified as a distinct pattern in this setting, whereas posterior L-shaped tears at the SSP-ISP junction may have been grouped differently or underrepresented compared with broader classifications used in prior studies. Furthermore, considering the reparability and repair technique, the inclusion of large retracted tears as “U-shaped” in earlier reports may be overly broad. We propose that U-shaped tears be limited to those with a longitudinal split pattern (similar to the V-shaped tears described by Sallay et al.19)), while extensively retracted tears with substantial tendon loss should be classified separately as trapezoid or massive retracted types. Furthermore, interobserver agreement in our study, while moderate (κ = 0.634), indicates a degree of subjectivity in tear pattern classification. This variability may reflect not only differences in interpretation but also surgeon-specific tendencies in conceptualizing reduction vectors and footprint restoration strategies. Thus, while classification systems provide useful frameworks, they remain susceptible to intra- and interobserver variation, which may influence both surgical planning and comparative research outcomes. From a clinical standpoint, this level of agreement suggests that pattern-based surgical decisions may vary between surgeons, and clinicians should exercise judgment when applying classification-guided repair strategies in practice.

We observed significantly higher reparability in anterior L-shaped tears, which can be explained by their distinct anatomy and biomechanics. An anterior L-shaped tear involves a longitudinal split with its apex at the anterior SSP (toward the rotator interval), effectively dividing the tendon into 2 leaflets.2) This configuration allows the surgeon to convert a large tear into a smaller defect by first closing the longitudinal split (suturing the 2 leaflets together) before repairing the tendon to bone. Biomechanically, this follows Burkhart’s principle of margin convergence: by suturing the free margins of a U- or L-shaped tear, the gap size and strain on the remaining tendon are reduced, facilitating a tension-free footprint repair.2122) In an anterior L-shaped tear, the posterior cuff (ISP and posterior SSP) remains largely intact and mobile because the split propagates anteriorly toward the rotator interval rather than between the SSP and ISP.23) This preserved ISP continuity provides a robust, mobile tissue flap that can be advanced anteriorly once the split is closed. Surgeons often place an anchor at the corner (apex) of an L-shaped tear and repair that corner first, then suture the longitudinal tear side-to-side. This sequence allows the posterior-to-anterior mobilization of the cuff tissue for an anatomic reduction.23) By contrast, a broad crescent-shaped tear has no longitudinal rent to relieve tension; the entire tendon must be mobilized as 1 sheet, which can be more difficult when the tear is large. Our finding that anterior L-shaped tears had the lowest incomplete repair rate among patterns is consistent with this rationale. Our findings should not be interpreted as anterior L-shaped tears being universally easier to repair. Rather, within our cohort and classification framework, anterior L-shaped tears that retained a mobilizable posterior leaflet and allowed effective margin convergence demonstrated higher rates of footprint restoration. Severely retracted anterior L-shaped tears may have been underrepresented or excluded by surgical indications, which could have biased reparability estimates.

Although anterior L-shaped tears allowed for more complete footprint coverage at the time of repair, this did not result in a reduced retear rate compared to crescent-shaped tears. One hypothesis for this finding is related to the integrity of the rotator cable. The rotator cable is a thick band of fiber that arcs through the cuff (deep to the SSP and ISP) and inserts anteriorly near the biceps groove and posteriorly near the ISP insertion.2425) It functions as a suspension bridge, distributing loads and shielding the thinner crescent portion of the tendon from excessive tension. In a healthy cuff, the cable helps dissipate stress and prevents edge tears from propagating; if the cable is compromised, the remaining tendon is more prone to gapping and failure. In anterior L-shaped tears, the tear includes an anterior longitudinal split––essentially cleaving through the rotator cable’s anterior attachment. As a result, the rotator cable’s continuous ring is disrupted. Shin et al. describe that a retracted anterior L-shaped tear is challenging precisely because of “destruction of the rotator cable” and poor tendon mobility.4) Even though we can anatomically repair the tendon, the loss of a functional anterior cable may leave the repair more vulnerable to concentrated stress at the repair site. Biomechanical evidence supports the importance of the cable: conservatively managed tears with an intact rotator cable show significantly less propagation over time than those with a disrupted cable.26) Thus, it is plausible that an anterior L-shaped tear’s compromised cable negates some of the benefit of a more complete repair, leading to a retear risk similar to other patterns. Another consideration is that anterior L-shaped tears often coincide with delamination, which can impair healing even after a seemingly successful repair.27) The combination of cable disruption and intratendinous delamination in L-shaped tears could create a “weak link” in the repaired tendon.

The apparent discrepancy between the univariate analysis (where trapezoid-shaped tears showed a 60% retear rate versus 17.2% for crescent-shaped tears, p = 0.002) and the multivariable analysis (where no tear pattern remained independently associated with retear) likely reflects several factors. First, the small number of trapezoid-shaped tears (n = 15) and total retear events (n = 36) limited statistical power in multivariable modeling. Second, tear pattern likely serves as a proxy for other unfavorable characteristics—trapezoid-shaped tears had significantly greater ML retraction (36.9 ± 12.4 mm vs. 23.6 ± 10.7 mm for crescent-shaped tears, p = 0.007), greater fatty infiltration, and higher rates of incomplete repair. When these specific factors were included in the multivariable model, tear pattern per se no longer contributed additional independent predictive value, suggesting that the tear pattern effect operates through these measurable mediators rather than as an independent risk factor.

Surgical strategies for RC repair should be tailored to the geometric configuration of the tear. Crescent-shaped tears, which typically demonstrate minimal retraction and preserved tissue mobility, are generally amenable to direct footprint repair using either single-row techniques or double-row suture bridge constructs. These methods can reliably restore footprint contact and provide favorable healing conditions. However, for more complex tear morphologies such as L-shaped, U-shaped, or trapezoid-shaped tears, pattern-matched techniques may improve surgical outcomes by addressing tissue tension, retraction, and load distribution. The concept of margin convergence, introduced by Burkhart et al., involves side-to-side repair of longitudinally split tendon edges to reduce tension at the medial aspect of the footprint.22) This method is particularly useful in U- or L-shaped tears with significant longitudinal extension and retraction.2) In recent years, with increasing recognition of the biomechanical role of the rotator cable, there has been a growing interest in reconstructing the anterior cable in anterior L-shaped tears where it is frequently disrupted. In these cases, rotator cable reconstruction using the long head of the biceps tendon has been proposed as a strategy to restore the suspension bridge mechanism of the cuff.4) One such technique involves tenotomy of the proximal biceps and re-routing it across the anterior interval to recreate cable continuity. Yang et al. reported that in patients with anterior L-shaped tears and disrupted cable, biceps-based anterior cable reconstruction resulted in improved healing integrity on postoperative MRI, with 84.2% of patients demonstrating Sugaya type I or II healing.28) These findings support the rationale that re-establishing the anterior cable can enhance tendon healing by restoring the suspension bridge mechanism. For U-shaped tears, medial row fixation is considered essential to reduce strain and achieve stable repair, as emphasized by Kim et al.29) This technique addresses the central defect and retraction commonly seen in this pattern and contributes to biomechanical stability. Massive retracted tears, particularly those with delamination or trapezoid configurations, may require dermal allograft augmentation or SCR to restore superior stability and prevent proximal migration of the humeral head.30) Taken together, these evolving strategies emphasize the value of precise tear pattern recognition in guiding the selection of biomechanically appropriate repair techniques.

This study has several limitations. First, while we found moderate interobserver agreement, there is an inherent subjectivity in defining tear shapes. Misclassification or borderline cases could affect the analysis. Formal intraobserver reliability testing was not performed. Second, we did not report functional outcome scores in this study. Third, the follow-up MRI to assess healing was done at 6 months, which is relatively short-term. Some retears might occur later with longer follow-up. Fourth, the small number of posterior L-shaped tears (n = 5) severely limits the statistical power and generalizability of findings for this tear pattern. Fifth, the small number of retear events (n = 36) relative to the number of variables in the multivariable model (events-per-variable ratio of approximately 5) may have limited statistical power, resulting in wide confidence intervals that preclude detection of true independent associations. Sixth, we used PSM based on tear size alone rather than multiple variables. While this approach maximized the number of matched pairs and was justified by the strong correlation between tear size and other prognostic factors, unmeasured confounding may remain. Seventh, surgical techniques were appropriately tailored to tear pattern following established principles (margin convergence for U- and L-shaped tears, direct repair for crescent-shaped tears). While this treatment heterogeneity reflects standard clinical practice, it represents a limitation in isolating the pure effect of tear pattern morphology independent of surgical technique. Finally, the 50% footprint coverage threshold for defining reparability, while based on prior literature, has not been formally validated as the optimal cutoff value. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and functional correlations are warranted to confirm the influence of RC tear patterns on both healing and clinical outcomes.

RC tear pattern was associated with intraoperative reparability after arthroscopic repair but did not independently predict postoperative structural healing. Anterior L-shaped tears demonstrated higher reparability than crescent-shaped tears, whereas retear rates were comparable. Tear pattern recognition may assist surgical planning rather than serve as a standalone prognostic factor for tendon healing.
