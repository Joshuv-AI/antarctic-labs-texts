1.
Introduction

Fix
d\geqslant 2
and
T>0
. In this paper, we investigate the following second-order stochastic differential equation driven by standard Brownian motion
W_{t}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\ddot{X_{t}}=b(t,X_{t},\dot{X_{t}})+\sqrt{2}\dot{W_{t}},\ t\in[0,T],

(1.1)

where the drift
b
is a time-dependent distribution. A typical example is Gaussian random fields. In this case,
X_{t}
models the evolution of particles in random environment (see
[
7
,
16
]
for the first order case). By introducing the velocity variable
V_{t}=\dot{X_{t}}
, the SDE (
1.1
) can be written as the following first-order degenerate system: for
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=V_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\\
&{\mathord{{\rm d}}}V_{t}=b(t,X_{t},V_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

Let
Z_{t}:=(X_{t},V_{t})
. For
z=(x,v)
, we define

B(t,z)=B(t,x,v):=(v,b(t,x,v))^{\rm t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},\ \sigma:=(0,\sqrt{2}{\mathbb{I}})^{\rm t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
{\rm t}
denotes the transpose of a matrix. Then the SDE (
1.2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}=B(t,Z_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ Z_{0}\sim\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d}),

(1.3)

where
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
is the space of all probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
.

The main challenging part of our article is that the drift
b
is distribution-valued; hence, the drift term is meaningless in the classical sense as we cannot assign a value to a distribution at the point
(X_{t},V_{t})
. To solve this problem, as in
[
16
]
, we employ a mollifying approximation to define a weak solution. Let
\Gamma_{n}(z)=\Gamma_{n}(x,v):=n^{4d}\Gamma(nx,n^{3}v)
, where
\Gamma\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
is a smooth probability density function with compact support. We define the approximation of
b
as follows:

\displaystyle b_{n}(t,z):=(b(t,\cdot)*\Gamma_{n})(z),\ B_{n}(t,z)=B_{n}(t,x,v):=(v,b_{n}(t,x,v))^{\rm t},

(1.4)

where
*
denotes convolution in the distributional sense.

To begin, we introduce the concept of weak solutions to the above degenerate system with a distributional drift.

Definition 1.1

(Weak solutions)
.

Let
\mathfrak{F}:=(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},{\mathbf{P}})
be a stochastic basis, and
(Z,W)
be a pair of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
-valued continuous
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-adapted processes on
\mathfrak{F}
. We call
(\mathfrak{F},Z,W)
a weak solution of SDE (
1.3
) with initial distribution
\mu\in{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
if
W
is an
{\mathscr{F}}_{t}
-Brownian motion,
{\mathbf{P}}\circ Z_{0}^{-1}=\mu
, and

\displaystyle Z_{t}=Z_{0}+A_{t}^{B}+\sigma W_{t},\ t\in[0,T],\ a.s.,

(1.5)

where
A_{t}^{B}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}B_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s
exists in the
L^{2}
-sense.

It should be noted that the definition of a weak solution in Definition
1.1
relies on the choice of mollifiers
\Gamma_{n}
(see Remark
1.5
below). Our goal is to determine how weak the condition on
b
can be for SDE (
1.3
) to have a solution in the sense of Definition
1.1
.

1.1.
Main results

To state our main results, we first introduce the following notations. Define the scaling parameter
{\boldsymbol{a}}:=(3,1)
and the corresponding anisotropic distance: for
z=(x,v)
and
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,

\displaystyle|z-z^{\prime}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}:=|x-x^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{3}}+|v-v^{\prime}|.

(1.6)

We need the following weight function as well: for
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\kappa}(z):=((1+|x|^{2})^{1/3}+1+|v|^{2})^{-\kappa/2}\asymp(1+|z|_{\boldsymbol{a}})^{-\kappa},\ z=(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

(1.7)

It is easy to see that for any
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a constant
C=C(\kappa,j,d)>0
such that

\displaystyle|\nabla^{j}_{v}\rho_{\kappa}(z)|\lesssim_{C}\rho_{\kappa}(z)\rho_{j}(z),\ |\nabla^{j}_{x}\rho_{\kappa}(z)|\lesssim_{C}\rho_{\kappa}(z)\rho_{3j}(z).

(1.8)

For any
\kappa,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we define

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha}_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\rho_{\kappa})}:=\|\rho_{\kappa}f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}},

(1.9)

where
{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
denotes the anisotropic Hölder space (see Definition
2.4
below for precise definition). The following theorems are our main results:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and
\kappa\in[0,\frac{1+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{3+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}]
. Suppose that

b\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\rho_{\kappa}),\ \mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}.

Let
\alpha\in(\frac{2}{q_{b}}+\frac{3\kappa-1}{1-\kappa},\alpha_{b}]
and
q\in(\frac{2-2\kappa}{1+(1-\kappa)\alpha-3\kappa},q_{b}]
. Then for any
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})
and
\lambda\geqslant 0
, there exists a unique weak solution to PDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{v}u-v\cdot\nabla_{x}u-\lambda u+b\cdot\nabla_{v}u+f,\ u_{0}\equiv 0,

(1.10)

such that for any
\theta\in[0,2-\frac{2}{q})
,

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{2+\alpha-2/q-\theta}(\rho_{\nu})}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})},

where
\nu:=\frac{2\kappa}{1+\alpha-2/q}+\delta
and the constant
C>0
is independent of
\lambda
.

Remark 1.3
.

The detailed version of Theorem
1.2
is given in Theorem
3.6
.
We observe that when
\kappa=\delta=0
, the estimate reduces to the classical Schauder’s estimate (cf.
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
,
[
13
, Theorem 2.17]
).
This theorem demonstrates that the growth or decay behavior of
u
is jointly determined by both the growth of
b
and the growth or decay of
f
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and
\kappa\in[0,1+\alpha_{b})
. Suppose that
b\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\rho_{\kappa})
has a bounded divergence in
v
. Then for any
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
p\geqslant 2
,
there exists a unique weak solution
(\mathfrak{F},Z,W)
to SDE (
1.3
) starting from
z_{0}
such that the following Krylov’s estimate holds: for any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{1}C_{b}\cap{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(-1,\alpha_{b}]
,
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha},q_{b}]
and
0\leqslant t_{0}<t_{1}\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{C}(t_{1}-t_{0})^{\frac{2+\alpha-2/q}{2}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}},

(1.11)

where the constant
C
is independent of
t_{0}
,
t_{1}
and
f
. Additionally, we have the following moment estimate: for any
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{\delta}(Z_{t})\bigg)\lesssim_{C(\delta)}\rho_{\delta}(z_{0}).

Furthermore, if
\kappa\in[0,\frac{1+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{3+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}]
, then for any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{1}C_{b}(\rho_{\delta})\cap{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})
with
\alpha\in(\frac{2}{q_{b}}+\frac{3\kappa-1}{1-\kappa},\alpha_{b}]
,
q\in(\frac{2-2\kappa}{1+(1-\kappa)\alpha-3\kappa},q_{b}]
and
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have for
\nu:=\frac{2\kappa}{1+\alpha-2/q}+\delta
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{C}\rho_{-\nu}(z_{0})(t_{1}-t_{0})^{\frac{2+\alpha-2/q}{2}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})},

(1.12)

where the constant
C
is also independent of
t_{0}
,
t_{1}
and
f
.

Remark 1.5
.

We note that, as stated in Proposition
4.3
below, the solution mentioned above does not depend on the choice of mollifiers. Furthermore, for
A^{B}_{t}
defined in (
1.5
), the function
t\mapsto A^{B}_{t}
admits a Hölder continuous version with index
\beta<\frac{2+\alpha-2/q}{2}
.

Remark 1.6
.

The Krylov’s estimate (
1.11
) implies that for any
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and Lebesgue almost all
t>0
, the process
Z_{t}(z_{0})
admits a density
\rho(t,z_{0},z)
. Furthermore, by (

) we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}f(t,z)\rho(t,z_{0},z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\lesssim\rho_{-\nu}(z_{0})\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})}.

(1.13)

Hence, by duality,
\rho
belongs to the weighted space
{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q^{\prime}}{\mathbf{B}}_{1,{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{-\alpha,1}(\rho_{-\delta})
(see
[
1
, Proposition 2.76]
), exhibiting certain growth or decay properties.

Remark 1.7
.

In fact, by a similar argument, the well-posedness remains valid for any initial distribution
\mu
with finite moment of order

m
, provided that

m(1+\alpha_{b}-\tfrac{2}{q_{b}})>2.

•

.

1.2.
Background and motivation

The kinetic equation, originally introduced by Landau in 1936, has been extensively studied in various fields such as physics, chemistry and biology, as it describes dynamic behaviors influenced by both deterministic laws and stochastic forces. For a general background introduction, we refer to
[
VanKampen11
]
; for ergodicity of the system, see
[
17
]
and
[
27
]
; and for Euler approximations of invariant measures, see
[
20
]
.

For degenerate system with singular drifts:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=V_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\ \ {\mathord{{\rm d}}}V_{t}=b(t,X_{t},V_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}\sigma(t,X_{t},V_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

(1.14)

many research has been conducted for the case where
b
is a real function. In
[
2
]
, Chaudru de Raynel first studied equation (
1.14
) under certain Hölder condition. In a subsequent work
[
28
]
, Wang and Zhang extended this result under a similar Hölder-Dini condition. In
[
8
]
, the authors derived strong well-posedness for SDE (
1.14
) with constant diffusion. Later, in
[
31
]
, under weaker drift conditions than
[
8
]
, Zhang established the strong well-posedness for SDE (
1.14
) when
b
belongs to anisotropic Sobolev spaces. In
[
32
]
, Zhang derived the maximum principle for SDE (
1.14
) with a distribution-valued inhomogeneous term. In a recent work
[
4
]
, Chaudru de Raynel and Menozzi achieved the weak well-posedness under much weaker assumptions. In
[
10
]
, the authors established the exponential ergodicity of SDE (
1.14
). Additionally, kinetic SDEs driven by
\alpha
-stable process were studied in
[
14
]
. However, it is important to note that all the aforementioned studies exclusively focus on drifts with positive regularity. For the case of negative regularity drifts, that is, when the drift
b
is distribution-valued, defining solutions to the SDE (
1.14
) presents significant challenges. In a recent work
[
18
]
, the authors established the well-posedness of SDE (
1.14
) for the regularity
\alpha\in(-\frac{1}{2},0)
by using a classical method of the martingale problem.

Here we consider the kinetic system with degenerate diffusion (
1.3
) and categorize it into three cases through a simple scaling analysis. Let
\dot{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
denote the homogeneous anisotropic Hölder space. Suppose that for some
q\in[1,\infty]
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};\dot{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha})
, and that SDE (
1.3
) has a solution
Z
. For
\lambda>0
, we define

Z_{t}^{\lambda}=(X_{t}^{\lambda},V_{t}^{\lambda}):=(\lambda^{-3}X_{\lambda^{2}t},\lambda^{-1}V_{\lambda^{2}t}),\ W_{t}^{\lambda}:=\lambda^{-1}W_{\lambda^{2}t}.

Formally, it follows that

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}^{\lambda}=B^{\lambda}(t,Z_{t}^{\lambda}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}^{\lambda},

where

B^{\lambda}(t,z)=B^{\lambda}(t,x,v):=(v,b^{\lambda}(t,x,v))^{\rm t}:=(v,\lambda b(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda^{3}x,\lambda v))^{\rm t}.

By the change of variables, we have

\|b^{\lambda}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};\dot{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha})}=\lambda^{1+\alpha-\frac{2}{q}}\|b\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};\dot{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha})}.

Letting
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, we shall categorize the following three cases:

\textbf{Subcritical:\ }\tfrac{2}{q}<1+\alpha;\textbf{\ Critical:\ }\tfrac{2}{q}=1+\alpha;\textbf{\ Supercritical:\ }\tfrac{2}{q}>1+\alpha.

Our aim is to establish the well-posedness of SDE (
1.3
) under the subcritical condition. To solve SDE (
1.3
), we need a regularity estimate for the associated Kolmogorov equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{v}u-v\cdot\nabla_{x}u-\lambda u+b\cdot\nabla_{v}u+f.

(1.15)

The study of kinetic Kolmogorov equations has been extensively explored in the literature. For example, in
[
3
]
, Chaudru de Raynel, Honoré and Menozzi derived sharp Schauder’s estimates for equation (
1.15
) under the condition that the coefficients belong to certain anisotropic Hölder spaces and that the first order term is nonlinear and unbounded. Here we focus on the case where
b\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha<0
. According to the Schauder’s theory of kinetic equation (see
[
13
]
), the solution
u
belongs to at most
{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha}
. In the classical sense, to ensure that the product
b\cdot\nabla_{v}u
is well-defined, we need
1+2\alpha>0
, which implies that
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
. This class of problems also arises in the study of singular SPDEs, such as in the analysis of the KPZ equation. To date, two principal methodologies have emerged as powerful tools for addressing these challenges: the regularity structure theory developed by Hairer
[
12
]
, and the paracontrolled distributions approach introduced by Gubinelli, Imkeller, and Perkowski
[
11
]
. Both frameworks leverage the intrinsic structure of solutions to provide rigorous interpretations for terms that are not classically well-defined. In
[
15
]
, the authors introduced the paracontrolled distribution method and performed renormalization by probabilistic calculation to establish the global well-posedness of (
1.15
) for
\alpha\in(-\frac{2}{3},-\frac{1}{2})
. However, to the best of our knowledge, the case where
\alpha\in(-1,-\frac{2}{3}]
has not yet been explored in the existing literature. In this paper, we aim to investigate this previously unexamined case. Furthermore, we introduce weighted spaces to derive uniform bounds for the solutions of the linear equation with a polynomial growth, while also handling cases where
b
includes certain Gaussian random fields (see Example
5.3
). This example has been attracting growing research interest due to its close connection with quantum field theory, in particular the stochastic quantization raised by Parisi and Wu
[
21
]
.

On the other hand, recently, in
[
16
]
, Hao and Zhang investigated the following first-order stochastic differential equation (SDE) with distributional drifts:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(t,X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

(1.16)

and established the well-posedness of SDE (
1.16
) for both subcritical and supercritical cases. In the subcritical case, they applied Zvonkin’s transformation, whereas in the supercritical case, they analyzed the generalized martingale problem by using an energy method. Motivated by this work, we aim to extend the results to the kinetic case. Notably, we must emphasize that the equation (
1.3
) is degenerate, rendering Zvonkin’s transformation potentially inapplicable, which brings us great difficulties. Roughly speaking, when
\alpha\leqslant 0
,
u\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha}\subset{\mathbf{C}}_{x}^{\frac{2+\alpha}{3}}
with
\frac{2+\alpha}{3}<1
, which implies that
\nabla_{x}u
may not exist. To tackle this problem, we establish a generalized version of Itô’s formula (see Lemma
4.10
) and consider the associated martingale problem. Thus far, we have only been able to handle the subcritical case. The challenge in the supercritical case lies in dealing with the kinetic Kolmogorov equations with supercritical drifts, which remains a target for future research.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the basic notations and propositions of weighted anisotropic Hölder spaces (cf.
[
15
]
and
[
26
]
).
Let
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
be the Schwarz space of all rapidly decreasing functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, and let
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
denote the dual space of
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, known as Schwartz generalized function (or tempered distribution) space. For any
f\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, we define the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
and inverse Fourier transform
\check{f}
respectively by

\hat{f}(\xi):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\xi\cdot z}f(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z,\ \xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

\check{f}(z):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{i\xi\cdot z}f(\xi){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,\ z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

For
q\in[1,\infty]
and a normed space
{\mathbb{B}}
, we will use the notation

{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbb{B}}:=L^{q}([0,T];{\mathbb{B}}),\ {\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}:={\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}L^{q}.

Additionally, we denote by
C_{b}=C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
(resp.
C_{c}=C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
) the Banach space of all bounded continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
(resp. the space of all continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
with compact support), and by
C_{b}^{\infty}=C_{b}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
(resp.
C_{c}^{\infty}=C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
) the space of all smooth functions with bounded derivatives of all orders (resp. the space of all smooth functions with compact support). Furthermore, we define the weighted space
C_{b}(\rho_{\kappa}):=\{f:f\rho_{\kappa}\in C_{b}\}
.

2.1.
Anisotropic weighted Hölder spaces

In this subsection we recall the definition of weighted anisotropic Hölder space.
For
r>0
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
, the ball centered at
z
and with radius
r
in terms of the anisotropic distance

|\cdot|_{\boldsymbol{a}}
(see (
1.6
)) is defined as follows:

B_{r}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(z):=\{z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}:|z-z^{\prime}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant r\},\ B_{r}^{\boldsymbol{a}}:=B_{r}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(0).

Let
\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
be a symmetric
C^{\infty}
-function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
such that

\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi)=1\text{\ for\ }\xi\in B_{1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\text{\ and\ }\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi)=0\text{\ for\ }\xi\notin B_{4/3}^{\boldsymbol{a}}.

For
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define

\displaystyle\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(2^{-j{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi)-\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(2^{-(j-1){\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi),\ \ &j\geqslant 1,\\
&\chi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi),&j=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
,
2^{s{\boldsymbol{a}}}\xi:=(2^{3s}\xi_{1},2^{s}\xi_{2}).
This definition ensures that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi)=1,\ \forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}})\subset\{\xi:2^{j-1}\leqslant|\xi|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\leqslant 2^{j+1}\},\ j\geqslant 1,\ \mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}})\subset B_{4/3}^{\boldsymbol{a}}.

(2.2)

For given
j\geqslant 0
, we define the dyadic anisotropic block operator
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
on
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f(z):=(\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\hat{f})\check{\ }(z)=\check{\phi}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}*f(z),

(2.3)

where the convolution is taken in the distributional sense. By scaling, we have

\displaystyle\check{\phi}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(z)=2^{4d(j-1)}\check{\phi}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(2^{(j-1){\boldsymbol{a}}}z),\ j\geqslant 1.

(2.4)

For
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}={\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}},\ \text{where}\ \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}:=\sum_{|i-j|\leqslant 2}{\mathcal{R}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{a}},

(2.5)

with the convention that
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}:=0
for
j<0
.

The following definition about the admissible weights is from
[
26
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
C^{\infty}
-smooth function
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\to(0,\infty)
is called an admissible weight if for each
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a constant
C_{j}>0
such that

\displaystyle|\nabla^{j}\rho(z)|\leq C_{j}\rho(z),\ \forall z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d},

and for some
C,\kappa>0
,

\displaystyle\rho(z)\leq C\rho(z^{\prime})(1+|z-z^{\prime}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\kappa}),\ \forall z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

For any admissible weight
\rho
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we define
\|f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}:=\|\rho f\|_{L^{p}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

It can be readily verified that for any
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the weight function
\rho_{\kappa}
defined in (
1.7
) is an admissible weight.

The following Bernstein’s inequality is standard and can be found in
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\rho
be an admissible weight. For any
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
and
1\leqslant p\leqslant q\leqslant\infty
, there exists a constant
C=C(\rho,{\boldsymbol{k}},p,q,d)>0
such that for all
j\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}^{k_{1}}\nabla_{v}^{k_{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{q}(\rho)}\lesssim_{C}2^{j({\boldsymbol{a}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}+4d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}))}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}.

(2.6)

Now we define the weighted anisotropic Hölder spaces as follows (see
[
26
,
15
]
).

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\rho
be an admissible weight. For any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the anisotropic Hölder space is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho):=\Big\{f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}:\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho)}:=\sup_{j\geqslant 0}2^{js}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}<\infty\Big\}.

For
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the anisotropic Hölder space is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho):=\Big\{f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}:\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho)}:=\sup_{j\geqslant 0}2^{js}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}+\|\nabla_{v}^{s}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}<\infty\Big\}

We have the following interpolation inequality.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
s_{0},s_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s_{0}\neq s_{1}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,s_{0},s_{1},\theta)>0
such that for any admissible weight
\rho
and
f\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}\vee s_{1}}(\rho)
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{\theta}}(\rho)}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}}(\rho)}^{\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho)}^{1-\theta},

(2.7)

where
s_{\theta}:=\theta s_{0}+(1-\theta)s_{1}
.

Proof.

By
[
1
, Theorem 2.80]
, we have for
s_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{\theta}}(\rho)}\overset{\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{HZZZ24}{}{}, (2.22)]}}}{=}\|\rho f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{\theta}}}\lesssim\|\rho f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}}}^{\theta}\|\rho f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}}^{1-\theta}=\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}}(\rho)}^{\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho)}^{1-\theta}.

For
s_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, similarly

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{\theta}}(\rho)}\lesssim\sup_{j\geqslant 0}2^{js_{\theta}}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}+\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\|\nabla_{v}^{s_{\theta}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}\overset{\eqref{bernstein2}}{\lesssim}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}2^{js_{\theta}}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho)}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\overset{\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{HZZZ24}{}{}, (2.22)]}}}{=}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}2^{js_{\theta}}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\rho f)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\rho f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}}}^{\theta}\|\rho f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}}^{1-\theta}=\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{0}}(\rho)}^{\theta}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho)}^{1-\theta}.

The proof is complete.
∎

2.2.
Paraproduct calculus

To ensure that the term
b\cdot\nabla_{v}u
makes sense when
b
is distribution-valued and
u\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, we recall the well-known Bony’s decomposition, which allows us to control the product of functions in anisotropic Hölder spaces under weaker regularity assumptions. For any
k\geqslant 0
, define the cut-off low frequency operator
S_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
as

S_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\overset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}f,

where we take
S_{-1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}=0
by convention. For
f,g\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, we define the following paraproducts

f\prec g:=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}S_{k-1}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g,\ f\circ g:=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}f\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g,

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
is defined in (
2.5
). The Bony’s decomposition of
fg
is formally written as (cf.
[
1
]
)

\displaystyle fg=f\prec g+f\circ g+g\prec f=:f\preceq g+f\succ g.

(2.8)

The essential property of the Bony’s decomposition is that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(S^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j-1}f{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}g)=0\ \text{for}\ |k-j|>2.

(2.9)

We now state the key paraproduct estimates (cf.
[
15
, Lemma 2.11]
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
be admissible weights and
s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s_{1}+s_{2}\notin{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

(1)

(i)
If
s_{1}<0
, then there exists a constant
C=C(d,s_{1},s_{2})>0
such that

\displaystyle\|f\prec g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho_{1})}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{2}}(\rho_{2})}.

(2.10)

For
s_{1}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|f\prec g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{2}}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho_{1})}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{2}}(\rho_{2})}.

(2.11)

(2)

(ii)
If
s_{1}+s_{2}>0
, then there exists a constant
C=C(d,s_{1},s_{2})>0
such that

\displaystyle\|f\circ g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}+s_{2}}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho_{1})}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{2}}(\rho_{2})}.

(2.12)

Remark 2.7
.

By the above lemma, if
s_{1}+s_{2}>0
with
s_{1}+s_{2}\notin{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then there exists a constant
C=C(d,s_{1},s_{2})>0
such that

\displaystyle\|fg\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}\wedge s_{2}}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{1}}(\rho_{1})}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s_{2}}(\rho_{2})}.

(2.13)

Using Bony’s decomposition we can write

\displaystyle b\cdot\nabla_{v}u:={\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}(b\preceq u)+b\succ\nabla_{v}u}-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u=:b\odot\nabla_{v}u-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u.

(2.14)

In particular, if
\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b=0
, then

b\cdot\nabla_{v}u=b\odot\nabla_{v}u.

The following regularity estimates are crucial in establishing Theorem
3.6
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
be admissible weights. For any
s\in(-1,0)
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,s)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|b\odot\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1})}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}(\rho_{2})}

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1+s}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim_{C}\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1})}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}(\rho_{2})}.

(2.16)

Proof.

For
s\in(-1,0)
, by Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.6
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}(b\preceq u)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim\|b\preceq u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s+1}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1})}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}(\rho_{2})},

and

\displaystyle\|b\succ\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})}\lesssim\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1})}\|\nabla_{v}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\rho_{2})}\lesssim\|b\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{s}(\rho_{1})}\|u\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}(\rho_{2})}.

Estimate (
2.15
) then follows directly from the above bounds,
while (
2.16
) can be obtained analogously via a similar calculation.
∎

3.
PDEs with weighted distributional drifts

In this section we study the solvability of the following PDE with
\lambda\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{v}u-v\cdot\nabla_{x}u-\lambda u+b\cdot\nabla_{v}u+f,\ u_{0}\equiv 0.

(3.1)

Below we always assume that the following condition on
b
holds:

(H
{}_{\text{w}}^{\text{sub}}
)

Let
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and
\kappa\in[0,\frac{1+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{3+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}]
. Suppose that

\displaystyle c_{b}:=\|b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\rho_{\kappa})}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}<\infty.

(3.2)

For convenience of notations, we introduce the following parameter set:

\Xi:=(T,d,\alpha_{b},q_{b},\kappa,c_{b}).

Let
p_{t}(z)=p_{t}(x,v)
be the distributional density of
Z_{t}:=(\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}B_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\sqrt{2}B_{t})
, given by

p_{t}(x,v)=\Big(\frac{2\pi t^{4}}{3}\Big)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{3|x|^{2}+|3x-2tv|^{2}}{4t^{3}}\Big).

It is easy to see that for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle p_{\lambda t}(x,v)=\lambda^{-2d}p_{t}(\lambda^{-3/2}x,\lambda^{-1/2}v).

(3.3)

For
t>0
, let
P_{t}
be the kinetic semigroup defined by

\displaystyle P_{t}f(z):={\mathbb{E}}f(\Gamma_{t}z+Z_{t})=\Gamma_{t}(p_{t}*f)(z)=(\Gamma_{t}p_{t}*\Gamma_{t}f)(z),

(3.4)

where

\Gamma_{t}f(z):=f(\Gamma_{t}z):=f(x-tv,v).

For
\lambda\geqslant 0
and a time-dependent distribution
f_{t}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, we define

\displaystyle u_{t}(z):={\mathscr{I}}_{t}^{\lambda}(f)(z):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t-s)}P_{t-s}f_{s}(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\ t>0.

(3.5)

Then it is easy to see that in the distributional sense,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=(\Delta_{v}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}-\lambda)u+f.

(3.6)

Next we give the definition of the solution to (
3.1
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T>0
and
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We call
u\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}(\rho_{\kappa})
a mild solution of PDE (
3.1
), if
u
solves the following integral equation:

u_{t}={\mathscr{I}}_{t}^{\lambda}(b\odot\nabla_{v}u-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u+f).

Remark 3.2
.

Under
(H
{}_{\text{w}}^{\text{sub}}
)
, by Lemma
2.8
one sees that all terms on the right side of the equality are well-defined. By (
3.6
), the solution
u
constructed above satisfies that for any test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
,

\partial_{t}{\langle}u,\varphi{\rangle}={\langle}u,\Delta_{v}\varphi{\rangle}+{\langle}u,v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi{\rangle}-\lambda{\langle}u,\varphi{\rangle}+{\langle}b\odot\nabla_{v}u-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u,\varphi{\rangle}+{\langle}f,\varphi{\rangle},

where
{\langle}u,\varphi{\rangle}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}u(z)\varphi(z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z
. This formulation indicates that
u
is also a weak solution of PDE (
3.1
).

The following a priori estimate with unweighted drifts is standard.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and

\displaystyle\ell_{b}:=\|b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}<\infty.

(3.7)

Then for any
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},q_{b}]
, there is a constant
C=C(T,d,\alpha_{b},q_{b},\ell_{b})>0
such that for any solution
u
to the PDE (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

(3.8)

Proof.

Let
q_{0}:=\frac{q_{b}q}{q_{b}-q}
. By Lemma
2.8
and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|b\cdot\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\overset{\eqref{decomposition}}{\leqslant}\|b\odot\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\preceq u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{0}}\lesssim\ell_{b}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}}.

(3.9)

By
[
13
, Theorem 2.17]
, we have the following Schauder’s estimate:

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{I}}_{.}^{\lambda}(f)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}\lesssim_{C}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|b\cdot\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}+\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\lesssim\ell_{b}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}}+\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}},

(3.10)

which implies that for any
t\in[0,T]
and
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},q_{b})
,

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|^{q_{0}}_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}\lesssim\ell_{b}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{q_{0}}_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\|f\|^{q_{0}}_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

Hence, by Gronwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

Note that for
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},q_{b})
,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{0}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}}\lesssim\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

(3.11)

Substituting (
3.11
) into (
3.10
), we conclude that (
3.8
) still holds for
q=q_{b}
.
∎

To establish the main result of this section, we need the following theorem, which provides a sharper estimate than Lemma
3.3
. More concretely, the right side of (
3.12
) depends polynomially on
\ell_{b}
, rather than exponentially as in Lemma
3.3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and

\displaystyle\ell_{b}:=\|b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}<\infty.

Let
\alpha\in(\frac{2}{q_{b}}-1,\alpha_{b}]
and
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha},q_{b}]
. Then for any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
and
\lambda\geqslant 0
, there is a unique solution
u
to the PDE (
3.1
) such that for any
\theta\in[0,2-\frac{2}{q})
,

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1+\beta(\alpha,q)-\theta}}\lesssim_{C}(1+\ell_{b})^{\frac{2}{\beta(\alpha,q)}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}},

(3.12)

and

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}\lesssim_{C}(1+\ell_{b})^{\frac{1-\beta(\alpha,q)}{\beta(\alpha,q)}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}},

(3.13)

where
\beta(\alpha,q):=1+\alpha-\frac{2}{q}>0
and the constant
C:=C(\Xi,\delta,\theta,q)>0
.

To prove this theorem, we need the following maximum estimate for solutions in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
,
q_{b}\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{b}},\infty]
and

\displaystyle\ell_{b}:=\|b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha_{b}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}<\infty.

Then for any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}
, there is a unique solution
u\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{1}
to the PDE (
3.1
) such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}\leqslant T\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}.

(3.14)

Proof.

Let
b_{n}(t,z):=b(t,\cdot)*\Gamma_{n}(z)
. It is well-known that there exists a unique solution
u_{n}\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}C_{b}^{2}
to the PDE (
3.1
) with only replacing
b
by
b_{n}
(cf.
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
). By
[
14
, Theorem 6.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}\leqslant T\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}.

(3.15)

For
\alpha_{b}\in(-1,0)
and

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}f(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{C}\sigma^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})}.

(4.3)

As a direct consequence of Krylov’s estimate and Kolmogorov continuity theorem, we derive the following result (cf.

Proposition 4.3
.

For given
\theta\in(0,1)
and
p\geqslant 2
, suppose that
(Z,{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta}))
satisfies the Krylov’s estimate with parameter
(p,\theta)
. For any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})
, if there exist

such that

then the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{.}f_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s
exists in
L^{p}(\Omega,C([0,T]))
and we denote the limit by

, which is independent of the choice of

. Furthermore, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|A_{t}^{f}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\sup_{s\neq t\in[0,T]}\frac{\|A_{t}^{f}-A_{s}^{f}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}{|t-s|^{(1+\theta)/2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})}.

(4.4)

Proof.

Note that
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\delta})
. Since

\{\int_{0}^{.}f_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also a Cauchy sequence in

, which implies that the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{.}f_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s
exists. Furthermore, (
4.4
) follows from (
4.3
) directly.
∎

To prove the above Krylov’s estimate, we need the following uniform moment estimate.

Lemma 4.7
.

Under
(H
{}^{\text{sub}}
)
, for any
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists a constant
C:=C(\Xi^{\prime},\delta)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{\delta}(Z_{t}^{n})\bigg)\lesssim\rho_{\delta}(z_{0}).

(4.9)

Proof.

Let
u_{n}\in{\mathbb{L}}^{\infty}_{T}C^{2}_{b}
solve the following backward PDE (cf.
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}+\Delta_{v}u_{n}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{n}+b_{1;n}\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{n}+b_{1;n}=0,\ u_{n}(T)=0.

By (
3.22
) we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{v}u_{n}\|_{\infty}\vee\|u_{n}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|u_{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}}\lesssim\|b_{1;n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{a_{b}}}\lesssim\|b_{1}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{a_{b}}}\leqslant\ell_{b_{1}}.

(4.10)

Here and below, all the implicit constants are independent of
n
. For
Z_{t}^{n}=(X_{t}^{n},V_{t}^{n})
, define
\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}:=(X_{t}^{n},V_{t}^{n}+u_{n}(t,X_{t}^{n},V_{t}^{n}))
. By Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}=\widetilde{B}_{n}(t,Z_{t}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(t,Z_{t}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ \widetilde{Z}_{0}^{n}=\widetilde{z}_{0}=(x_{0},v_{0}+u_{n}(0,x_{0},v_{0})),

(4.11)

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,z):=\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,x,v)=(v,b_{2}\cdot({\mathbb{I}}+\nabla_{v}u_{n})(s,z))^{\rm t},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(s,z):=(0,\sqrt{2}({\mathbb{I}}+\nabla_{v}u_{n})(s,z))^{\rm t}.

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle|\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,z)|\lesssim l(s)(c_{0}+c_{1}|z|_{\boldsymbol{a}}),

(4.12)

and there exists a constant
\kappa_{0}>0
such that for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leqslant|\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(s,z)\xi|^{2}\leqslant\kappa_{0}|\xi|^{2}.

(4.13)

Note that by (
4.10
),

\displaystyle 1+|\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\lesssim 1+|Z_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}+\|u_{n}\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1+|Z_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\lesssim 1+|\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}+\|u_{n}\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1+|\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}},

which implies that for any
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n})\asymp\rho_{\delta}(Z_{t}^{n}).

Hence, it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n})\bigg)\lesssim\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{z}_{0}).

(4.14)

Applying Itô’s formula, we have for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n})=\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{z}_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}^{\rm t})(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla^{2}+\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla)\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla)\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}^{n}_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

By BDG’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n})\bigg)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}))^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{z}_{0})+{\mathbf{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|(\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}^{\rm t})(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla^{2}+\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla)\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbf{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big).

(4.15)

By (
1.8
) we have

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}^{\rm t})(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla^{2})\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|\overset{\eqref{wtsigman}}{\lesssim}|\nabla^{2}\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|\lesssim\rho_{\delta+2}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})\lesssim\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n}),

\displaystyle|(\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla)\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|\lesssim|\widetilde{B}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})||\nabla\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|\overset{\eqref{wtbn}}{\lesssim}l(s)\rho_{-1}(Z_{s}^{n})\rho_{\delta+1}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})\lesssim l(s)\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n}),

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})\cdot\nabla\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|^{2}\overset{\eqref{wtsigman}}{\lesssim}|\nabla\rho_{\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})|^{2}\lesssim\rho_{2\delta+2}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n})\lesssim\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n}).

Hence,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n})\bigg)\lesssim\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{z}_{0})+\int_{0}^{T}(1+l(s))^{2}{\mathbf{E}}\rho_{2\delta}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(4.16)

Since
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
is arbitrary, by Gronwall’s inequality, we obtain (
4.14
) and complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

(i) For

and stopping times

4.2.
Existence of the solutions to subcritical kinetic SDEs

Let
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}:=C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
be the Banach space of all continuous functions and denote a path in
{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
by
\omega
. The canonical process is denoted by
\omega_{t}
. Let
{\mathscr{B}}^{s}_{t}
denote the natural filtration generated by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{B}}_{t}:={\mathscr{B}}^{0}_{t},\ {\mathscr{B}}^{s}:={\mathscr{B}}^{s}_{T}.

In this subsection we establish the existence of weak solution in the sense of Definition
1.1
by using a standard weak convergence method. To start with, we present the following tightness result of the law

of approximation solutions

defined in (

{\mathbb{C}}_{T}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Under
(H
{}^{\text{sub}}
)
, the family of probability measures
({\mathbb{P}}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is tight.

Proof.

Let
\tau\geqslant 0
be any stopping time less than T. Note that by (

\displaystyle Z_{\tau+t}^{n}-Z_{\tau}^{n}=\int_{\tau}^{\tau+t}B_{n}(s,Z_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\sigma(W_{\tau+t}-W_{\tau}),

which implies that

\displaystyle|Z_{\tau+t}^{n}-Z_{\tau}^{n}|\leqslant\int_{\tau}^{\tau+t}|V_{s}^{n}|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+t}|b_{1;n}(s,Z_{s}^{n})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+t}|b_{2}(s,Z_{s}^{n})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+|\sigma(W_{\tau+t}-W_{\tau})|.

4.5

4.9

\delta>0

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,\delta]}|Z_{\tau+t}^{n}-Z_{\tau}^{n}|\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbf{E}}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\delta}\Big(|V_{s}^{n}|+l(s)(c_{0}+c_{1}|Z_{s}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}})\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+{\mathbf{E}}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\delta}|b_{1;n}|(s,Z_{s}^{n}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{2}{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,\delta]}|W_{\tau+t}-W_{\tau}|\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{C}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(1+{\mathbf{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Z_{t}^{n}|_{\boldsymbol{a}}\bigg)+\delta^{\frac{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{2}}\|b_{1;n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{C}\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+|z_{0}|_{\boldsymbol{a}})+\delta^{\frac{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{2}}\|b_{1;n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}},

where the constant

is independent of

. Thus, by
[
30

, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{n}{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant t<t^{\prime}<t+\delta\leqslant T\end{subarray}}|Z_{t^{\prime}}^{n}-Z_{t}^{n}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg)\lesssim\delta^{\frac{1}{4}}(1+|z_{0}|_{\boldsymbol{a}})^{\frac{1}{2}}+\delta^{\frac{2+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{4}}\|b_{1;n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Then by Chebyshev’s inequality, we have for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{n}{\mathbf{P}}\Bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant t<t^{\prime}<t+\delta\leqslant T\end{subarray}}|Z_{t^{\prime}}^{n}-Z_{t}^{n}|>\varepsilon\Bigg)=0.

Hence, by

The following lemma will play a crucial role in the proof of uniqueness.

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose
(H
{}^{\text{sub}}
)
holds with
b_{2}=0
. Let
T>0
,
\alpha\in(-1,\alpha_{b}]
,
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha},q_{b}]
and
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}
. Let
u
be the unique weak solution of the following backward PDE:

\partial_{t}u+\Delta_{v}u+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u+b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}u-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq u=f,\ u(T)=0,

and let
(\mathfrak{F},Z,W)
be a weak solution of SDE (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
. Then, for all

,

u(t,Z_{t})=u(0,Z_{0})+A_{t}^{f}+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}u(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s},

where
A_{t}^{f}
is defined in Proposition
4.3
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.6
, there is a unique weak solution
u
to the above backward PDE, with the regularity that for all
\theta\in[0,2-\frac{2}{q})
,

\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{\frac{\theta}{2}}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{2+\alpha-2/q-\theta}}\leqslant C\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha}}.

(4.24)

Letting
u_{n}(t,z):=u(t,\cdot)*\Gamma_{n}(z)
, by Remark
3.2
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}+\Delta_{v}u_{n}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{n}=f_{n}+g_{n}+h_{n},

(4.25)

where

\displaystyle f_{n}:=f*\Gamma_{n},\ g_{n}:=v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{n}-(v\cdot\nabla_{x}u)*\Gamma_{n},

and

\displaystyle h_{n}=-(b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}u-\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq u)*\Gamma_{n}.

Taking
g=\nabla_{v}u_{n}
in (
4.6
), by the generalized Itô’s formula (cf.
[
9
]
,
[
29
, Lemma 3.6]
) and (
4.25
), we obtain

\displaystyle u_{n}(t,Z_{t})=u_{n}(0,Z_{0})+A_{t}^{b_{1}\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{n}+h_{n}}+\int_{0}^{t}(f_{n}+g_{n})(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}u_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

(4.26)

Next, we estimate the difference between
u_{n}
and
u
. For
\theta^{\prime}\in(0,2+\alpha-\frac{2}{q})
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|u_{n}-u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}&\leqslant\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x,v}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|u(t,x-y,v-w)-u(t,x,v)|\Gamma_{n}(y,w){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}w\\
&\leqslant\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\theta^{\prime}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}(|y|^{\frac{1}{3}}+|w|)^{\theta^{\prime}}\Gamma_{n}(y,w){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}w\overset{\eqref{control}}{\lesssim}n^{-\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{3}}.\end{split}

Thus,

\displaystyle(u_{n}(t,Z_{t}),u_{n}(0,Z_{0}))\rightarrow(u(t,Z_{t}),u(0,Z_{0}))\ \ a.s.,\ n\rightarrow\infty.

(4.27)

Similarly, there exists
\theta^{\prime\prime}\in(0,1+\alpha-\frac{2}{q})
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{v}u_{n}-\nabla_{v}u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}}\lesssim n^{-\frac{\theta^{\prime\prime}}{3}}\|u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1+\theta^{\prime\prime}}}\overset{\eqref{control}}{\lesssim}n^{-\frac{\theta^{\prime\prime}}{3}}.

By BDG’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla_{v}u_{n}(s,Z_{s})-\nabla_{v}u(s,Z_{s})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}\Big|^{2}\lesssim\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}|(\nabla_{v}u_{n}-\nabla_{v}u)(s,Z_{s})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big)=0.

(4.28)

Similarly, we note that

g_{n}(t,z)=\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{n}-(v\cdot\nabla_{x}u)*\Gamma_{n}\right)(t,z)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}u(t,x-y,v-w)w\cdot\nabla_{y}\Gamma_{n}(y,w){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y{\mathord{{\rm d}}}w,

which implies that

\|g_{n}(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim n^{-2}\|u(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim n^{-2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}|g_{n}|(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=0.

(4.29)

In addition, by Proposition
4.3
and Theorem
4.9
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}f_{n}(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s-A_{t}^{f}\Big|^{2}=0.

(4.30)

Finally, we decompose

b_{1}\cdot\nabla_{v}u_{n}+h_{n}=h_{1}^{n}+h_{2}^{n},

where

By Krylov’s estimate (
4.17
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}|A_{t}^{h_{1}^{n}}|\lesssim\|h_{1}^{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\lesssim\|b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}(u_{n}-u)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}+\|b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}u-\Gamma_{n}*(b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}u)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

\|b_{1}\odot\nabla_{v}(u_{n}-u)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\overset{\eqref{bonycontrol1}}{\lesssim}\|b_{1}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\|u_{n}-u\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{\infty}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{1}}\lesssim n^{-\varepsilon}.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{E}}|A_{t}^{h_{1}^{n}}|=0.

Similarly, we can apply Krylov’s estimate to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}|A_{t}^{h_{2}^{n}}|\lesssim\|h_{2}^{n}\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}\lesssim\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq u-\Gamma_{n}*(\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq u)\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}+\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq(u-u_{n})\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq(u-u_{n})\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{\alpha_{b}}}&\lesssim\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}b_{1}\preceq(u-u_{n})\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{0}}\\

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}|A_{t}^{h_{2}^{n}}|=0.

(4.32)

n\to\infty

4.26
) and by (

), (
4.28
), (
4.29
), (
4.30
), (
4.31
), (
4.32
) and Proposition

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{(i)}\otimes_{\tau_{R}}{\mathbb{Q}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{z}(R),\ i=1,2.

By the uniqueness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{z}^{s}(R)
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}^{(i)}\otimes_{\tau_{R}}{\mathbb{Q}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{s,z}
for
i=1,2
. It then follows from Lemma
4.16
(i) that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{(1)}={\mathbb{P}}^{(1)}\otimes_{\tau_{R}}{\mathbb{Q}}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{s,z}={\mathbb{P}}^{(2)}\otimes_{\tau_{R}}{\mathbb{Q}}={\mathbb{P}}^{(2)}\ \text{on}\ {\mathscr{B}}_{\tau_{R}}.

Thus, letting
R\to\infty
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{(1)}={\mathbb{P}}^{(2)},

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we can show the well-posedness of SDE (
1.3
).

Theorem 4.19
.

Under
(H
{}^{\text{sub}}
)
, there exists a unique weak solution
(\mathfrak{F},Z,W)
to SDE (
1.3
) starting from
z_{0}
such that the following Krylov’s estimate holds: for any
f\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{1}C_{b}\cap{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(-1,\alpha_{b}]
,
q\in(\frac{2}{1+\alpha},q_{b}]
,
p\geqslant 2
, and stopping times
0\leqslant\tau_{0}\leqslant\tau_{1}\leqslant T
,
\tau_{1}-\tau_{0}\leqslant\sigma
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}f(s,Z_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim_{C}\sigma^{\frac{2+\alpha-2/q}{2}}\|f\|_{{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}},

where the constant
C
is independent of
t_{0}
,
t_{1}
and
f
. Additionally, for any
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\rho_{\delta}(Z_{t})\bigg)\lesssim_{C(\delta)}\rho_{\delta}(z_{0}).

Proof.

The existence of solutions follows immediately from Theorem
4.9
. Regarding uniqueness, Lemma
4.14
establishes the uniqueness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{z}^{s}(R)
for all
R\in(0,\infty)
and
(s,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. Furthermore, (
4.9
) implies that
\lim_{R\to\infty}\tau_{R}=\infty
. Applying Theorem
4.18
, we consequently obtain the uniqueness of
{\mathcal{M}}_{z}^{s}
for all
(s,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. The proof is complete by observing that
{\bf P}\circ Z^{-1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{z_{0}}^{0}
.
∎

Finally we can give

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Remark
4.1
, for any
b\in{\mathbb{L}}_{T}^{q_{b}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha_{b}}
with
\kappa\in[0,1+\alpha_{b})
, assumption
(H
{}^{\text{sub}}
)
is satisfied. In this case, the well-posedness of the SDE and estimate (
1.11
) follow directly from Theorem
4.19
. Furthermore, when
\kappa\in[0,\frac{1+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}{3+\alpha_{b}-2/q_{b}}]
, assumption
(H
{}_{\text{w}}^{\text{sub}}
)
holds, and estimate (
1.12
) follows immediately from (
4.18
).
∎

5.
Applications to Gaussian fields

In this section, we construct a concrete example of a Gaussian noise
b
that satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
. Our construction proceeds in two steps: first, we establish the regularity properties stated in Theorem
5.2
, and subsequently, we explicitly construct the desired noise.

We begin by introducing the Gaussian noise with spectral measure
\mu
. Let
\mu
be a signed Radon measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
satisfying the following conditions:

(A)

\mu
is a symmetric measure, meaning that

\displaystyle\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta)=\mu(-{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta)=\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,-{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta).

Moreover, for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}(1+|\zeta|_{\boldsymbol{a}})^{\ell}|\mu|_{\text{var}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)<\infty,

where
|\cdot|_{\text{var}}
denotes the total variation measure of a signed measure.

Let
{\mathbb{H}}
be the completion of
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d};{\mathbb{R}})
with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\hat{f}(\zeta)\overline{\hat{g}(\zeta)}\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)<\infty.

Now we introduce the Gaussian random field on
{\mathbb{H}}
(see
[
19
]
).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
U
be the real-valued Gaussian random field on
{\mathbb{H}}
; that is,
U
is a continuous linear operator from
{\mathbb{H}}
to
L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbf{P}})
, and for any
f\in{\mathbb{H}}
,
U(f)
is a real-valued Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance
\|f\|_{\mathbb{H}}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}(U(f)U(g))=\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

(5.1)

We call
U
the Gaussian noise with spectral measure
\mu
.

Below, for a Banach space
{\mathbb{B}}
, denote by
{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{B}})
the
{\mathbb{B}}
-valued Hölder space. Recall
\rho_{\kappa}(z)=((1+|x|^{2})^{1/3}+1+|v|^{2})^{-\kappa/2}
for
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\kappa};{\mathbb{B}})
be the weighted
{\mathbb{B}}
-valued Hölder space with norm (see
[
15
, Theorem 2.7]
)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}(\rho_{\kappa};{\mathbb{B}})}:=\|f\rho_{\kappa}\|_{{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{B}})}.

The following main result addresses the regularity of
U
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Under
(A)
, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\kappa>\frac{3d}{p}
and
\beta<\frac{\ell}{2}-\frac{4d}{p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle U\in L^{p}(\Omega;{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\beta}(\rho_{\kappa};{\mathbb{R}})).

(5.2)

The key point of Theorem
5.2
is to view
U
as a random distribution of the variable
z:=(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
. To formalize this, we introduce the following notation. For any given
\varphi\in{\mathbb{H}}
, we define

\displaystyle(\varphi*U)(z):=U_{\varphi}(z):=U(\tau_{z}\varphi),

where
\tau_{z}\varphi(\cdot):=\varphi(\cdot-z)\in{\mathbb{H}}
. By
[
15
, Lemma B.1]
, the mapping
z\mapsto U_{\varphi}(z)
has a smooth version.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

By (
2.2
), for all
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle|\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\zeta)|\lesssim 1\wedge(2^{\gamma j}(1+|\zeta|_{\boldsymbol{a}})^{-\gamma}),\ j\geqslant 0,\ \zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.

By the hypercontractivity of Gaussian random variables, for any fixed
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
and
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U(z)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U(z)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|U(\tau_{z}\check{\phi}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{\eqref{Gaucov}}{=}\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)\Big)^{1/2}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{\ell j}{2}}\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}(1+|\zeta|_{\boldsymbol{a}})^{\ell}\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)\Big)^{1/2}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{\ell j}{2}}.

For
\kappa>\frac{3d}{p}
,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\kappa}{\mathcal{R}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}U\|^{p}_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d};L^{p}(\Omega))}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\rho_{p\kappa}(z)\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U(z)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z\lesssim 2^{-\frac{p\ell j}{2}}.

Then for
\beta<\frac{\ell}{2}-\frac{4d}{p}
, by Bernstein’s inequality (
2.6
) and Fubini’s Theorem we have

\displaystyle\Big\|\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{j\beta}\|\rho_{\kappa}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\Big\|\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{j(\beta+\frac{4d}{p})}\|\rho_{\kappa}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{j(\beta+\frac{4d}{p})}\|\rho_{\kappa}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d};L^{p}(\Omega))}\lesssim\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{j(\beta+\frac{4d}{p}-\frac{\ell}{2})}<\infty.

The proof is complete.
∎

Now we provide the example to illustrate our results.

Example 5.3
.

For
\gamma\in[0,d)
, let

\displaystyle\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta)=|\xi|^{-\gamma}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta),

where
{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi
denotes the common Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\delta_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\eta)
is the Dirac measure concentrated at 0. For any real-valued Gaussian noise
U
with above spectral noise
\mu
, by
[
24
, p. 117, Lemma 2]
, there exists some constant
c_{d,\gamma}>0
such that

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(z)=\hat{\mu}(x,v)=c_{d,\gamma}|x|^{\gamma-d},\ z=(x,v).

In particular, for any
f,g\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})
, by (
5.1
) we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(U(f)U(g))

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\hat{f}(\zeta)\hat{g}(-\zeta)\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}f(z)g(z^{\prime})\hat{\mu}(z-z^{\prime}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}z{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=c_{d,\gamma}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x,v){\mathord{{\rm d}}}v\Big)\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}g(x^{\prime},v^{\prime}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}v^{\prime}\Big)\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{\prime}}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{d-\gamma}}.

Generally, smaller
\gamma
implies worse regularity of Gaussian random field
U
. Moreover, by the change of variables we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}U(z)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\lesssim\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}|\phi_{j}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}\zeta)\Big)^{1/2}=2^{\frac{3j(d-\gamma)}{2}}\Big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\phi_{0}^{\boldsymbol{a}}(\xi,0)|^{2}\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi}{|\xi|^{\gamma}}\Big)^{1/2}.

Thus, for
\kappa>\frac{3d}{p}
and
\beta<\frac{3(\gamma-d)}{2}-\frac{4d}{p}
we conclude that

U\in L^{p}(\Omega;{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\beta}(\rho_{\kappa};{\mathbb{R}})).

Next we construct a Gaussian field that is divergence free in
v
. Fix
\varphi\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi=1
. For any
f\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we define

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}(f):=U(\widetilde{f}),\ \widetilde{f}(x,v):=f(x)\varphi(v).

In this case, for any
f,g\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\widetilde{U}(f)\widetilde{U}(g))=c_{d,\gamma}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)g(x^{\prime})\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x^{\prime}}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{d-\gamma}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\hat{f}(\xi)\hat{g}(\xi)\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi}{|\xi|^{\gamma}}.

Let
{\bf U}=(\widetilde{U}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{U}_{d})
be d-independent Gaussian noises defined above. Then it holds that for any
\kappa>\frac{3d}{p}
and
\beta<\frac{3(\gamma-d)}{2}-\frac{4d}{p}
,

{\bf U}\in L^{p}(\Omega;{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\beta}(\rho_{\kappa};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})).

In particular, one can choose
p
big enough such that
\kappa>\frac{3d}{p}
and
\beta+\frac{4d}{p}<\frac{3(\gamma-d)}{2}
, and for
{\mathbf{P}}
-almost all
\omega
,

{\bf U}(\omega,\cdot)\in{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\beta}(\rho_{\kappa}),

and

\mathord{{\rm div}}_{v}{\bf U}(\omega,\cdot)=0.

Hence, for any
\gamma\in(d-\frac{2}{3},d)
, it follows from Theorem
1.4
that there exists a unique weak solution to the following kinetic SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=V_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\ {\mathord{{\rm d}}}V_{t}={\bf U}(\omega,\cdot)(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}.
