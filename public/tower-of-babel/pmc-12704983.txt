Hemoglobin A1C in the first trimester of pregnancy as a predictor of gestational diabetes mellitus: A retrospective study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Noura Al-Khaldi, Asirvatham Alwin Robert, Ali Ahmed Almutab, Hamssa Hawsawi, Yasser Altowyan, Abdulghani Al-Saeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study evaluates the association between first-trimester hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels and glucose tolerance test (GTT) results to assess the utility of HbA1c as a predictive biomarker for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).Methods:A retrospective, descriptive study was conducted on 634 pregnant women between January and December 2022 at Prince Sultan Military Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. After excluding cases with HbA1c ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol), 552 participants were included in the final analysis. Data on demographic characteristics, HbA1c levels, biochemical parameters, and GTT results were collected and analyzed.Results:Out of 552 participants, 183 (33%) were diagnosed with GDM. Among those with elevated HbA1c levels (≥5.6), 59 (54%) had GDM. Additionally, 99 (17.9%) women had a single positive oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), while 50 (9.1%) had two positives, and 34 (6.2%) had three positives. A significant difference in fasting glucose levels was observed between pregnant women with GDM and normal HbA1c (<5.6%) (4.78 ± 0.76) and those with elevated HbA1c (≥5.6%) (5.39 ± 1.47) (P = 0.001). Pregnant women with GDM exhibited significantly higher fasting glucose (4.98 ± 1.08 mmol/L) and HbA1c levels (5.54 ± 0.43%) compared to non-GDM counterparts (4.32 ± 0.76 mmol/L; 5.31 ± 0.33%, P = 0.001).Conclusion:Measurement of HbA1c during the first trimester may be a useful approach for identifying pregnant women at an increased risk of GDM. Incorporating HbA1c testing into standard prenatal care could enable earlier risk assessment and prompt interventions, helping to reduce unfavorable outcomes for both mother and fetus. Additional research is needed to confirm these results and determine the most appropriate HbA1c cutoff values for clinical practice.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) refers to a condition characterized by impaired glucose tolerance that is first identified during pregnancy.[12] Expectant mothers with GDM are more likely to encounter pregnancy-related complications, particularly hypertensive disorders like preeclampsia and eclampsia. After childbirth, these women also face a greater likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular conditions.[345] Furthermore, babies born to mothers with GDM are at risk of several health issues, including being larger than average at birth (macrosomia), low blood sugar (neonatal hypoglycemia), breathing difficulties, and an increased chance of developing T2D in the future.[5]

The etiology of GDM is multifactorial, with several contributing factors, although the exact pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. A comprehensive overview of systematic reviews encompassing of 30 meta-analyses identified 61 potential risk factors associated with GDM. Among the most prominent were pre-pregnancy overweight and obesity, a positive family history of diabetes, thyroid dysfunction particularly hypothyroidism sleep-disordered breathing, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).[6] On a global scale, GDM is estimated to affect around 7% of all pregnancies, highlighting its role as a common pregnancy-related complication.[7] The HAPO Study shows that GDM raises the risk of mothers developing T2D and causes newborn complications such as macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, and neonatal hypoglycemia. These risks underscore the importance of early detection and treatment of GDM. Recent research also suggests that excessive fetal growth linked to GDM can begin before standard diagnosis, emphasizing the need for earlier screening and intervention to improve outcomes for both mother and child.[8]

Currently, GDM is typically screened between the 24th and 28th weeks of pregnancy using the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Nonetheless, research indicates that metabolic changes occurring in early pregnancy can have a significant impact on fetoplacental development and overall pregnancy outcomes.[8] Recent findings from a randomized controlled trial showed that initiating treatment for GDM diagnosed before 20 weeks of gestation notably decreased the risk of neonatal complications.[910] Because OGTT has practical limits, HbA1c testing is explored as an alternative to detect early or severe gestational diabetes. However, its accuracy in early pregnancy remains inconsistent.[11]

Research indicates that HbA1c levels exceeding 5.9% in early pregnancy may be associated with increased risks of GDM, preeclampsia, fetal macrosomia, and preterm delivery. However, inconsistent findings in various studies raise doubts about its reliability as a universal predictor for GDM.[12] Moreover, underlying conditions such as beta-thalassemia, iron deficiency, and vitamin B12 deficiency can influence HbA1c values, potentially compromising its diagnostic accuracy.[13] Nonetheless, systematic reviews and recent investigations continue to highlight the potential of HbA1c as a useful biomarker for GDM risk assessment. For instance, a systematic review and research reported that first-trimester HbA1c demonstrated moderate sensitivity (72.5%) and specificity (67.5%) in predicting GDM.[14]

Early detection of GDM is crucial, so large population studies are needed to clarify how first-trimester HbA1c and OGTT results relate to GDM. Women with early or severe GDM may have metabolic disturbances causing high early HbA1c. Future research should assess if first-trimester HbA1c can predict GDM and what factors affect its accuracy.[15] This research aims to assess the relationship between first-trimester HbA1c, glucose tolerance tests, and GDM, contributing to the expanding body of evidence on how HbA1c could enhance early pregnancy risk assessment and management.

Methods

Study setting and population

A retrospective analysis was conducted at Prince Sultan Military Medical City (PSMMC), Riyadh Saudi Arabia. The study included 552 pregnant women, confirmed by a positive beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) test, who underwent first-trimester HbA1c testing, with data retrieved from electronic medical records. The research complied with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and received approval from the Research and Ethics Committee of the tertiary hospital in Riyadh. To maintain participant confidentiality, all collected data were anonymized.

Duration of the study

The study was conducted over a one-year period from January to December 2022. This timeframe enabled the recruitment of participants during their first trimester, with follow-up extending until the completion of the 24–28-week oral glucose tolerance test.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Pregnant women in their first trimester were included in the study, confirmed by beta-HCG. These participants underwent a 75-g OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, with a positive result indicating GDM. Women presenting in the second or third trimester, those with pre-existing diabetes (type 1 or type 2), individuals with incomplete medical records, or those taking anti-diabetic medications were excluded.

Data collection and outcome

Demographic and clinical information was systematically collected throughout the study period. During the first prenatal appointment, maternal age and body mass index (BMI) were recorded. Blood samples obtained prior to 14 weeks of gestation were tested for glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting blood sugar (FBS). HbA1c was assessed using the Cobas C513 analyzer through the turbidimetric inhibition immunoassay (TINIA) technique on hemolyzed whole blood, with results presented both as percentages and in SI units (mmol/mol). Plasma or serum glucose concentrations were measured using the Cobas 8000 Modular Analytics system from Roche, utilizing a hexokinase-based enzymatic assay where glucose is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate in the presence of ATP.

Between gestational weeks 24 and 28, participants underwent a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), with blood samples collected at baseline and at one and two hours after glucose ingestion. Additional blood analyses from the first trimester included a complete blood count (CBC), measuring parameters such as red blood cell count, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), and red cell distribution width (RDW), along with ferritin and vitamin B12 levels. Pregnancy outcomes, including the development of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), were obtained from patients’ medical records. The study also adjusted for variables known to influence HbA1c, such as iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies. Additionally, the investigation identified an HbA1c cut-off point at which clinical intervention, including dietary changes, is recommended to maintain optimal glucose control.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Seattle, WA, USA), SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means, while Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages to reflect the distribution of different categories within the dataset. In addition to descriptive analysis, t tests and Tukey’s post hoc tests were used to look for differences among the groups. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The characteristics and clinical variables of the patients are presented in Table 1. The study included a total of 552 participants, with the majority (374 individuals) aged between 25-35 years and 178 individuals aged between 36-45 years. The mean age of the participants was 33 ± 4.79 years. The BMI distribution showed that 20% of participants had a BMI of less than 25 kg/m², while 80% had a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher, with a mean BMI of 29.9 ± 5.8 kg/m². Biochemical analysis revealed a mean ferritin level of 41.8 ± 9.6 μg/L, while the mean fasting glucose level was 4.83 ± 0.87 mmol/L. The mean HbA1c was 5.39 ± 0.38%. Hematological parameters showed a mean MCHC of 321 ± 13 g/L, a mean RBC count of 4.31 ± 0.47 × 106/μL, and a mean HGB level of 112 ± 11.6 g/L. The mean MCV was 60.8 ± 27.6 fL, and the mean MCH was 47.6 ± 28.3 pg. The mean vitamin B12 level among participants was 256 ± 112 pg/mL.

Patients’ characteristics and clinical variables

Table 2 shows the clinical and biochemical comparison in pregnant women with GDM: patients with normal vs. elevated HbA1c Levels. Among the participants, 123 women (67%) had normal HbA1c levels (<5.6), while 60 women (32%) had high HbA1c levels (≥5.6). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of age (34.1 ± 5.1 vs. 34.8 ± 4.3 years; P = 0.35) and BMI (31.1 ± 7.0 vs. 31.0 ± 8.2 kg/m²; P = 0.74). Similarly, serum ferritin (31.9 ± 14.5 vs. 53.1 ± 19.7 ng/mL; P = 0.27) and vitamin B12 levels (238 ± 97.7 vs. 253 ± 97.8 pg/mL; P = 0.67) did not differ significantly between groups. However, significant differences were observed in several hematological and glycemic parameters. Mean MCHC was lower in the elevated HbA1c group compared to the normal HbA1c group (319 ± 13.6 vs. 324 ± 12 g/L; P = 0.019). RBC count was higher in the elevated HbA1c group (4.42 ± 0.49 vs. 4.26 ± 0.45 × 10^12/L; P = 0.032). Fasting glucose levels were significantly increased in the elevated HbA1c group (5.39 ± 1.47 vs. 4.78 ± 0.76 mmol/L; P = 0.001). As expected, HbA1c values were significantly higher in the elevated group compared to the normal group (6.1 ± 0.32% vs. 5.32 ± 0.28%; P = 0.001).

Table 3 outlines the comparison of clinical and biochemical variables between pregnant women with and without GDM. Pregnant women with GDM had a higher mean age (34.3 ± 4.8 years) compared to those without GDM (32.3 ± 4.6 years), with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.001). Although the mean BMI was higher in the GDM group (31.1 ± 6.29) compared to the non-GDM group (29.3 ± 5.5), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.067). Ferritin levels were slightly lower in the GDM group (37.5 ± 13.2) than in the non-GDM group (44.1 ± 12.5), though the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.70). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in MCHC (322 ± 13.1 vs. 321 ± 14.1, P = 0.46), RBC count (4.32 ± 0.47 vs. 4.29 ± 0.47, P = 0.58), and Vitamin B12 levels (242 ± 96.6 vs. 265 ± 120, P = 0.31) between the two groups. However, fasting glucose levels were significantly higher in women with GDM (4.98 ± 1.08) compared to those without GDM (4.32 ± 0.766), with a P value of 0.001. Additionally, HbA1c levels were significantly elevated in the GDM group (5.54 ± 0.43) compared to the non-GDM group (5.31 ± 0.33), with a P value of 0.001.

Discussion

The study provides valuable insights into the metabolic and hematological profiles of pregnant women, particularly in relation to GDM. The majority of participants were between 25 and 35 years of age, with a mean age of 33 ± 4.79 years. Additionally, 80% of the women had a BMI of 25 kg/m² or higher, reflecting a high prevalence of overweight and obesity. This observation is consistent with existing literature demonstrating that elevated BMI increases the risk of GDM and its associated complications in pregnant women.[1617] In this study, GDM was diagnosed in 33% of the women. The worldwide prevalence of GDM varies significantly, largely due to differences in diagnostic criteria used across regions. According to a recent meta-analysis, the global prevalence of GDM is estimated to be 14.7% when applying the criteria established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG), which remain the most widely adopted guidelines.[1] A 2019 meta-analysis using the IADPSG criteria found the highest pooled prevalence of GDM (11.4%) in South Asia (Bangladesh, India, and Sri Lanka) as compared to other regions (ranging from 3.6% to 6.0%).[1]

Several previous studies have investigated the feasibility of first-trimester HbA1c as an early predictor of GDM.[18192021] However, most of these studies have focused on high-risk groups, applied an HbA1c cutoff of 5.7% (the prediabetes benchmark outside pregnancy), or examined cases of GDM identified during the first trimester.[19] Some studies have also assessed HbA1c levels measured in the second trimester or at the time of GDM diagnosis. Consequently, there is a paucity of research on the predictive value of first-trimester HbA1c, especially in population-based samples such as those from Saudi Arabia. This gap is significant, as early identification of risk could enable timely intervention. In this present study we focused on tracking HbA1c levels throughout pregnancy, starting in the first trimester, among women without pre-existing medical conditions, to explore its potential association with GDM development. The results of the study revealed a significant difference in fasting glucose levels was observed between pregnant women with GDM and normal HbA1c (<5.6%) (4.78 ± 0.76) and those with elevated HbA1c (≥5.6%) (5.39 ± 1.47) (P = 0.001). Previous research indicates that higher HbA1c levels are associated with increased risk of GDM; for example, women with an HbA1c of 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) have nearly triple the risk compared to those with levels of 5.2% (33 mmol/mol).[18] Our findings also demonstrated that including first-trimester HbA1c measurements alongside standard risk factors significantly improved GDM prediction. Notably, this enhancement in predictive accuracy was observed even in a population considered low risk due to the absence of prior medical conditions. Our results align with previous studies and underscore the value of early HbA1c measurement in identifying women at higher risk of developing GDM. The significant differences in fasting glucose support the role of HbA1c as a useful screening tool in early pregnancy, suggesting that its integration into routine GDM risk assessments could facilitate earlier and more accurate detection, even among low-risk populations.[1422]

The hematological analysis highlighted some notable distinctions. Women diagnosed with GDM exhibited significantly different MCHC and RBC counts compared to those without the condition (P = 0.019 and P = 0.032, respectively). These differences suggest minor alterations in erythrocyte indices that may be associated with changes in glucose metabolism. Earlier research has indicated that elevated blood sugar levels can influence RBC characteristics and promote hemoglobin glycation, which might explain these findings. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in other parameters such as ferritin, vitamin B12, and hemoglobin levels, suggesting that iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies were not major contributors to GDM in this particular group.[23]

Limitations

This study has few limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was conducted at a single center, Prince Sultan Military Medical City, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a wider population. The results may not be applicable to pregnant women in different healthcare settings or regions with diverse demographic and clinical characteristics. Second, the retrospective design of the study relied on data from electronic medical records, rather than a prospective approach. Lastly, while the study included 552 participants, the sample size may still be relatively small to fully capture the variability in first-trimester HbA1c levels and their association with GDM. A larger, more diverse sample would strengthen the validity of the conclusions and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between early HbA1c levels and GDM risk.

Conclusion

This study highlights the association between first-trimester HbA1c levels and GDM, reinforcing its potential role in early risk prediction. Our findings indicate that women with elevated HbA1c levels (≥5.6%) had significantly higher fasting glucose levels and an increased likelihood of GDM, even in a low-risk population. While HbA1c alone may not be sufficient for GDM diagnosis, its inclusion alongside conventional risk factors could enhance early detection and intervention strategies. The prevalence of GDM in Saudi Arabia is notably high and requires urgent attention from policymakers, healthcare professionals, public health researchers, and the community. Context-specific and tailored interventions are essential for the prevention and early diagnosis of GDM. Without prompt action, the long-term health and economic burdens of GDM will likely be unavoidable.
