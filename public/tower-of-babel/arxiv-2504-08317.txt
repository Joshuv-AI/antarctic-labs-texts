Introduction

Let
d\geq 1
be any integer and
T=(T_{1},...,T_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
be fixed and consider in
D=[0,T]=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,T_{i}]=[0,T_{1}]\times...\times[0,T_{d}]
the usual partial ordering.

In, for instance,
[
15
, Corollary 1, p. 683]
or
[
1
, Theorem A.1]
, and
[
2
]
, the authors exhibited examples of sequences of processes
\zeta_{n}=\{\zeta_{n}(x)\colon x\in D\}
, converging weakly to the Brownian sheet in the space of continuous functions over
D
,
\mathcal{C}(D)
, generalizing the well-known Donsker’s invariance principle and the results proved by Stroock in
[
14
]
in the multidimensional parameter set case, respectively.

A natural question to ask ourselves then is if the integrals with respect to such processes converge, in the same sense, as stochastic processes, towards integrals with respect to the Brownian sheet. Of course, this problem has already been addressed in
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
(when a single parameter is considered) and, partially, in
[
3
]
, where two parameters are considered and the convergence of the stochastic integrals of a particular integrand (the Green function associated to the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions) is then used to show the convergence of solutions of the stochastic heat equation. Mostly motivated by the latter, in
[
1
]
, the authors consider sequences of kernels
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
so that the processes
\zeta_{n}
can be written as
\zeta_{n}(x)=\int_{[0,x]}\theta_{n}(y)dy
and provide sufficient conditions for the processes

X_{n}\coloneqq\left\{X_{n}(x)=\int_{[0,x]}f(y)\theta_{n}(y)dy\colon x\in D\right\}

to weakly converge, in
\mathcal{C}(D)
, to the process

X\coloneqq\left\{X(x)=\int_{[0,x]}f(y)W(dy)\colon x\in D\right\}.

Where
W=\{W(x)\colon x\in D\}
denotes a Brownian sheet and
f
is some function in
L^{2q}(D)
for some real
q\geq 1
.

However, in many applications (for instance, in stochastic partial differential equations) the integrands involved might depend on the time/space parameter as well, which motivates the study of the same problem when the integrand depends on it. Hence, the aim of this paper is to continue with the study of the weak approximation of stochastic integrals in
[
1
]
to extend the results to integrals of the form

\int_{[0,x]}f(x,y)W(dy)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\int_{D}f(x,y)W(dy),

where
f\colon D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
will be some function satisfying some integrability conditions. Given that

\int_{[0,x]}f(x,y)W(dy)=\int_{D}\mathbb{I}_{[0,x]}(y)f(x,y)W(dy),

where
\mathbb{I}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
\mathbb{I}_{A}(y)=1
if
y\in A
and
\mathbb{I}_{A}(y)=0
otherwise), we will focus our efforts on studying the latter. We will see, as well, how these results can be applied to prove the weak convergence of solutions to the stochastic Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The paper is organized as follows, Section
2
is devoted to briefly introduce the Brownian sheet, the stochastic integral with respect to it and to provide some results regarding the weak convergence of stochastic processes. In Section
3
we state and proof the main theorem and, in Section
4

2
Preliminaries

In this section we shall provide the main definitions and tools we will be working with.

2.1
Brownian sheet and integrals with respect to it

To define the Brownian sheet and the stochastic integral with respect to such process, we will make use of the isonormal Gaussian process over a real separable Hilbert space
H
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a stochastic process
W=\{W(h)\colon h\in H\}
defined in a complete probability space is an isonormal Gaussian process if it is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function
\text{Cov}(f,g)=\langle f,g\rangle_{H}
for all
f,g\in H
.

From now on, we shall take
H=L^{2}(D)
with the usual inner product. A Brownian sheet (or
d
-parameter Wiener process) is then defined as the process
\tilde{W}=\{\tilde{W}(x)\colon x\in D\}
with

\tilde{W}(x)=W\left(\mathbb{I}_{[0,x]}\right).

For a given function
f\in L^{2}(D)
and
x\in D
, we then define the Wiener integral of
f
with respect to the Brownian sheet over
[0,x]
as
W(f\mathbb{I}_{[0,x]})
and denote it by

W(f\mathbb{I}_{[0,x]})=\int_{[0,x]}f(y)\tilde{W}(dy).

To simplify the notation, we will write
\int_{[0,x]}f(y){W}(dy)
instead of
\int_{[0,x]}f(y)\tilde{W}(dy)
.

One can easily check (via Kolmogorov’s continuity Theorem), that the Brownian sheet and the integral of an
L^{2}(D)
function with respect to it have a continuous version, so, when talking about these objects, we will be talking about the continuous versions.

2.2
Weak convergence of stochastic processes

To stablish the weak convergence (or convergence in law or in distribution) of a sequence of stochastic processes
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{C}(D)
, one usually needs to prove the convergence of the finite dimensional distributions and that it is relatively compact or, by Prokhorov’s Theorem, that it is tight. The first result we introduce (whose proof can be found in
[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
) is a result regarding the convergence in law of random variables, which will be useful when proving the convergence of the finite dimensional distributions, while the second one is a tightness criterion which generalizes Proposition 2.3, p. 95, in
[
16
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(F,||\cdot||)
be a normed vector space and
\{J^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
J
linear maps from
F
to
L^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|J^{n}(f)\right|\right]\leq C||f||

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|J(f)\right|\right]\leq C||f||

for any
f\in F
and for some positive constant
C
and assume there is a dense subset
D
of
F
with respect to the norm
||\cdot||
such that
J^{n}(f)
converges in law to
J(f)
as
n
approaches infinity for any
f\in D
. Then, for any
f\in F
,
J^{n}(f)
converges in law to
J(f)
as
n
approaches infinity.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random fields in
D
whose sample paths vanish on
\{x\in D\colon x_{1}\cdot...\cdot x_{d}=0\}
almost surely and for which there are constants
C,p>0
and
\delta>d
such that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{n}(x)-X_{n}(z)\right|^{p}\right]\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-z_{i}|\right)^{\delta}.

(1)

Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X_{n}
possesses a continuous version and the sequence of continuous versions is tight in
\mathcal{C}(D)
.

Proof.

The existence of a continuous version follows from Kolmogorov’s continuity Theorem. Hence, from now on, we shall consider that the processes
X_{n}
have a.s. continuous sample paths.

In order to prove tightness, we will see that the hypotheses of
[
4
, Theorem 3, p. 1665]
are fulfilled (when we consider the Lebesgue measure) under condition (
1
), that is, for fixed
x,z\in D
,
x\leq z
, we aim to prove that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta_{x}X_{n}(z)|^{p}\right]\leq C^{\prime}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(z_{i}-x_{i})\right)^{\delta^{\prime}}

for some
C^{\prime}>0
and
\delta^{\prime}>1
and where
\Delta_{x}X_{n}(z)
denotes the increment of
X_{n}
over the rectangle
[x,z]
, which is given by

\Delta_{x}X_{n}(z)=\sum_{\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{d}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}}X_{n}\left(x_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}(z_{1}-x_{1}),...,x_{d}+\varepsilon_{d}(z_{d}-x_{d})\right).

Now fix any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{d}\in\{0,1\}
,
j\in\{1,...,d\}
and consider the term

(-1)^{d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}}X_{n}\left(x_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}(z_{1}-x_{1}),...,x_{d}+\varepsilon_{d}(z_{d}-x_{d})\right).

For this term, there will be one (and only one) summand

(-1)^{d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}X_{n}\left(x_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}^{\prime}(z_{1}-x_{1}),...,x_{d}+\varepsilon_{d}^{\prime}(z_{d}-x_{d})\right)

in
\Delta_{x}X_{n}(z)
such that
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i\in\{1,...,d\}\backslash\{j\}
and
\varepsilon_{j}\neq\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}
. In particular, this will mean that
(-1)^{d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}}\cdot(-1)^{d-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}=-1
. Hence, by using that for all
p\geq 0
there is a constant
C_{p}
(
C_{p}=1
if
p\leq 1
and
C_{p}=2^{p-1}
if
p\geq 1
) such that
|a+b|^{p}\leq C_{p}(|a|^{p}+|b|^{p})
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and by taking
j\in\{1,...,d\}
so that
|z_{j}-x_{j}|=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}|z_{i}-x_{i}|
, we will have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Delta_{x}X_{n}(z)\right|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}C2^{d-1}|z_{j}-x_{j}|^{\delta}\leq C^{\prime}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}|z_{i}-x_{i}|\right)^{\delta^{\prime}},

where
C^{\prime}=C_{p}C2^{d-1}>0
and
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/d>1
.
∎

The reader might ask why such result needs to be derived when, as mentioned in the proof of the proposition, we already have a tightness criterion,
[
4
, Theorem 3, p. 1665]
. This is due to the fact that, in many situations, and as we will see in Section
4
, it is easier to deal with the differences
|f(x,\cdot)-f(z,\cdot)|
rather than dealing with the increments in the first variable of
f(x,\cdot)
.

3
Statement and proof of the main result

This section is devoted to prove the convergence in distribution, in
\mathcal{C}(D)
, of the processes

X_{n}\coloneqq\left\{X_{n}(x)=\int_{D}f(x,y)\theta_{n}(y)dy\colon x\in D\right\}

(2)

towards the process

X\coloneqq\left\{X(x)=\int_{D}f(x,y)W(dy)\colon x\in D\right\}.

(3)

Of course, we will have to impose some conditions on the function
f\colon D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
to make sense of integrals like
\int_{D}f(x,y)W(dy)
and to guarantee the existence of a version of the processes
X_{n}
and
X
possessing a.s. continuous sample paths so that we can talk about convergence in
\mathcal{C}(D)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\colon D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that, for fixed
z\in\{x\in D\colon x_{1}\cdot...\cdot x_{d}=0\}
,
f(z,y)=0
for almost every
y\in D
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure) and such that for each
x,z\in D
,
f(x,\cdot)\in L^{2q}(D)
for some
q\geq 1
and

||f(x,\cdot)-f(z,\cdot)||_{2q}\leq C||x-z||^{\alpha}

(4)

for some constants
C,\alpha>0
and where
||\cdot||_{2q}
is the usual norm in
L^{2q}(D)
and
||\cdot||
is some norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that, for any
g\in L^{2q}(D)
and for some
m>d/\alpha
,

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{D}g(y)\theta_{n}(y)dy\right|^{m}\right]\leq C||g||_{2q}^{m}

(5)

holds for some constant
C>0
, that the processes
\zeta_{n}=\{\int_{[0,x]}\theta_{n}(y)dy\colon x\in D\}
converge towards the Brownian sheet (in the sense of convergence of the finite dimensional distributions) and that
\theta_{n}\in L^{2}(D)
almost surely for each
n
. Then, the processes
X_{n}
and
X
defined by (
2
) and (
3
), respectively, possess a continuous version and the former converges in law to the latter in
\mathcal{C}(D)
.

Proof.

We start by observing that, since
f(x,\cdot)\in L^{2q}(D)
for any
x\in D
, we have, in particular, that
f(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(D)
and hence, the stochastic integral
\int_{D}f(x,y)W(dy)
makes sense for each
x\in D
. Similarly, since
\theta_{n}
is square integrable, the integrals defining
X_{n}
are well defined for each
x\in D
as well.

Tightness and existence of a continuous version

By (
5
) and (
4
), we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{n}(x)-X_{n}(z)\right|^{m}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{D}(f(x,y)-f(z,y))\theta_{n}(y)dy\right|^{m}\right]\leq C||f(x,\cdot)-f(z,\cdot)||_{2q}^{m}\leq C||x-z||^{m\alpha},

for some constant
C>0
independent of
x,z\in D
(which might have changed from one inequality to another) and where we have used that
f(x,\cdot),f(z,\cdot)\in L^{2q}(D)
(so their difference is in
L^{2q}(D)
as well). Given that all norms in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are equivalent, we obtain that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{n}(x)-X_{n}(z)\right|^{m}\right]\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\right)^{m\alpha}.

Since
m\alpha>d
, Proposition
2.1
gives us the existence of a continuous version for each
n
(which we shall denote by
X_{n}
as well) and the tightness of the sequence.

To show that
X
has a version with continuous sample paths, one only needs to recall that
\int_{D}\left(f(x,y)-f(z,y)\right)W(dy)
is a normal random variable with variance

||f(x,\cdot)-f(z,\cdot)||_{2}^{2}\leq C||f(x,\cdot)-f(z,\cdot)||_{2q}^{2}\leq C||x-z||^{2\alpha}\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-z_{i}|\right)^{2\alpha}.

Where we have used Hölder’s inequality and, again, the fact that all norms in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are equivalent. Hence, for any even integer
N>d/\alpha
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X(x)-X(z)\right|^{N}\right]\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|t_{i}-s_{i}|\right)^{\alpha^{\prime}}

for some positive constant
C
(independent of
x
and
z
) and for
\alpha^{\prime}=N\alpha>d
.

Convergence of the finite dimensional distributions

By the Cramér-Wold device, it suffices to show that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
z^{1},...,z^{k}\in D
and
a_{1},...,a_{k}\in D
,
S_{n}
converges in law towards
S
, where

S_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}X_{n}(z^{j}),\hskip 9.24994ptS=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}X(z^{j}).

But observe that
S_{n}=J^{n}(K)
,
S=J(K)
, where

J^{n}(g)=\int_{D}g(y)\theta_{n}(y)du,\hskip 9.24994ptJ(g)=\int_{D}g(y)W(dy),\hskip 9.24994ptK(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}f(z^{j},y).

Given that
f(z,\cdot)\in L^{2q}(D)
for each
z\in D
, we have that
K
is in
L^{2q}(D)
as well. Moreover, by (
5
) and the isometry property of the Wiener integral (alongside with Hölder’s inequality), we have that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|J^{n}(g)\right|\right]\leq C||g||_{2q},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{E}\left[\left|J(g)\right|\right]\leq C||g||_{2q},

for some positive constant
C
and for any
g\in L^{2q}(D)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.1
applied to
F=L^{2q}(D)
with the usual norm, it suffices to show that
J^{n}(g)
converges in law towards
J(g)
for simple functions
g
of the form

g(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{l}g_{j}\mathbb{I}_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}(y),

with
l\geq 1
,
g_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<...<x_{l}=T
, which are dense in
L^{2q}(D)
. To this purpose, we simply note that, for such functions
g
,

J^{n}(g)=\int_{D}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{l}g_{j}\mathbb{I}_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}(y)\right)\theta_{n}(y)dy=\sum_{j=1}^{l}g_{j}\int_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}\theta_{n}(y)dy,

which converges in law towards

\sum_{j=1}^{l}g_{j}\int_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}W(dy)=\int_{D}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{l}g_{j}\mathbb{I}_{(x_{j-1},x_{j}]}(y)\right)W(dy)=J(g)

because the finite dimensional distributions of
\zeta_{n}
converge to those of the Brownian sheet.
∎

As in
[
1
]
, condition (
5
) implies that, for each
x\in D
, the sequence
\{(X_{n}(x))^{2}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable, so

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{D}f(x,y)\theta_{n}(y)dy\right)^{2}\right]=\int_{D}f^{2}(x,y)dy.

Of course, otherwise this result would be meaningless, there are examples of sequences of kernels
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}
verifying the above hypotheses. This is the content of
[
1
, Sections 3.1 and 3.2]
, where the Donsker kernels:

\theta_{n}(x)=n^{\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{k=(k_{1},...,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}Z_{k}\mathbb{I}_{[k-1,k)}(nx),

(6)

being
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with zero mean, unitary variance and finite moments of order
m
(in this case,
\zeta_{n}
would be the analog of the linear interpolation of a random walk in the multidimensional parameter set case), and the Kac-Stroock kernels:

\theta_{n}(x)=n^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(-1)^{N_{n}(x)},

where
N_{n}
is a multiparameter Poisson process of intensity
n
, are considered.

4
Application to SPDEs

In this section we shall see how the results derived above might be applied to approximate solutions of the stochastic Poisson equation

\displaystyle-\Delta u(x)+F(u(x))=g(x)+\dot{W}(x),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in D=(0,1)^{d},

with Dirichlet boundary condition
u(x)=0
,
x\in\partial D
, and where
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous bounded function satisfying

(x-y)(F(x)-F(y))\geq-L|x-y|^{2},\hskip 9.24994ptx,y\in\mathbb{R}

(7)

for some
small
constant
L\geq 0
,
g\in L^{2}(D)
,
\dot{W}
denotes the formal derivative of the Brownian sheet and
d\in\{2,3\}
.

A
mild
solution to this problem is given by a stochastic process
u=\{u(x)\colon x\in\bar{D}\}
verifying, for almost every
x\in D
and with probability 1, the equation

u(x)+\int_{D}K(x,y)F(u(y))dy=\int_{D}K(x,y)g(y)dy+\int_{D}K(x,y)W(dy),

(8)

where
K
is the Green function associated to the Laplace equation with the same boundary conditions, which is given by

K(x,y)=G(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[G(B_{\tau},y)\right]

(9)

with

G(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|,\hskip 9.24994pt&d=2,\\
\frac{1}{4\pi}|x-y|^{-1},\hskip 9.24994pt&d=3,\end{cases}

(10)

|\cdot|
the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
B=\{B_{t}\colon t\geq 0\}
a standard Brownian motion starting from
x
,
\tau=\inf\{t>0\colon B_{t}\notin D\}
and
\mathbb{E}^{x}
the expectation with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{x}
under which
B
is a Brownian motion with these characteristics (see, for instance,
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
). Observe that
K(x,y)=K(y,x)
and that
K(x,y)=0
for almost every
y\in D
if
x\in\partial D
. Indeed, in such situation one has that
\tau=0

\mathbb{P}^{x}
-a.s., so
B_{\tau}=x

\mathbb{P}^{x}
-a.s. and therefore,
K(x,y)=G(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[G(x,y)\right]=0
.

As a consequence of Poincaré’s inequality, one has that, for any
\varphi\in L^{2}(D)
,

\left\langle\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)\varphi(y)dy,\varphi\right\rangle\geq a\left|\left|\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)\varphi(y)dy\right|\right|_{2}^{2},

(11)

for some universal constant
a>0
(see
[
5
, Lemma 2.4, p. 225]
). It is proven in
[
5
, Theorem 2.5, p. 225]
as well that, whenever
0\leq L<a
, equation (
8
) has a unique solution with a.s. continuous sample paths.

Our goal is to show that the mild solutions of the Poisson equations

-\Delta u_{n}(x)+F(u_{n}(x))=g(x)+\theta_{n}(x),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in D

with same boundary conditions as in (
8
), which are given by the unique solutions to the integral equations

u(x)+\int_{D}K(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))dy=\int_{D}K(x,y)g(y)dy+\int_{D}K(x,y)\theta_{n}(y)dy,

(12)

converge in law, in the space of continuous functions
\mathcal{C}(D)
(we write
D
instead of
\bar{D}
, the closure of
D
, to simplify the notation, but bear in mind that we are talking about weak convergence of functions defined on
\bar{D}
) to the mild solution
u
of (
8
).

Several things have to be seen in order to prove the desired convergence, for instance, one first has to check that
K(x,\cdot)
belongs to
L^{2q}(D)
for some
q\geq 1
in order to make sense of the integral
\int_{D}K(x,y)W(dy)
.

Our strategy to prove the convergence will be the following one:

1.

We will show that
K
satisfies condition (
4
) for some
q\geq 1
,
\alpha>0
. This implies that integrals like
\int_{D}K(x,y)W(dy)
are well defined as Wiener integrals and, moreover, by Theorem
3.1
, that the integral processes
\int_{D}K(x,y)\theta_{n}(y)dy
converge in law, in the space
\mathcal{C}(D)
, towards
\int_{D}K(x,y)W(dy)
, whenever the kernels
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}
verify condition (
5
).

2.

We then consider the operator
Tu(x)\coloneqq u(x)+\int_{D}K(x,y)F(u(y))dy
and
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
(where
\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
is the space of continuous functions in
\bar{D}
vanishing on
\partial D
) and show that:

2.1

if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
,
Tu
belongs to

Lemma 4.1
.

There are positive constants
\alpha,\beta
and
C
,
\alpha>2
, such that

||K(x,\cdot)-K(z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}\leq C|x-z|^{\beta}

(14)

for any
x,z\in\bar{D}
.

Proof.

We first see that, if we manage to show that

||G(x,\cdot)-G(z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}\leq C|x-z|^{\beta},

(15)

then we will have (
14
) as well. Indeed by the strong Markov property, for any
x,y,z\in\bar{D}
,

\mathbb{E}^{z}\left[G(B_{\tau},y)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[G(B_{\tau}-x+z,y)\right],

so

\displaystyle||K(x,\cdot)-K(z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq||G(x,\cdot)-G(z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}+||\,\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[G(B_{\tau},\cdot)\right]-\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[G(B_{\tau}-x+z,\cdot)\right]||_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C|x-z|^{\beta}+\left(\int_{D}\left|\int_{\Omega}\left[G(u,y)-G(u-x+z,y)\right]d\mathbb{P}^{x}(u)\right|^{\alpha}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

By Jensen’s inequality and Tonelli’s Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|\int_{\Omega}\left[G(u,y)-G(u-x+z,y)\right]d\mathbb{P}^{x}(u)\right|^{\alpha}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|G(u,y)-G(u-x+z,y)\right|^{\alpha}d\mathbb{P}^{x}(u)\right)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}||G(u,\cdot)-G(u-x+z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}^{\alpha}d\mathbb{P}^{x}(u)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\alpha}|x-z|^{\alpha\beta}.

So, all in all,

||K(x,\cdot)-K(z,\cdot)||_{\alpha}\leq 2C|x-z|^{\beta}.

Proving that it suffices to show (
15

Case
d=2

Given

0<\varepsilon<\alpha

\displaystyle\int_{D}

\displaystyle\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{\alpha}dy=\int_{D}\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{\varepsilon}\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{D}\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{\varepsilon q}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{D}\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq|x-z|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\left(\int_{D}\left|\log|x-y|-\log|z-y|\right|^{\varepsilon q}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{D}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)p}}dy+\int_{D}\frac{1}{|z-y|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)p}}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

for some

,

C|x-z|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}\left(\int_{D}|\log|x-y||^{\varepsilon q}dy+\int_{D}|\log|z-y||^{\varepsilon q}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{D}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)p}}dy+\int_{D}\frac{1}{|z-y|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)p}}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

If we let

\displaystyle\int_{D}|\log|x-y||^{\varepsilon q}dy\leq\int_{B(x,2)}|\log|x-y||^{\varepsilon q}dy=2\pi\int_{0}^{2}|\log r|^{\varepsilon q}rdr=2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\log 2}|t|^{\varepsilon q}e^{2t}dt<\infty.

Proposition 4.1
.

The map

As previously mentioned, the existence and uniqueness of solutions is a consequence of

Proposition 4.2
.

The map
\Psi\colon\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)\to\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
,
\eta\mapsto\Psi(\eta)=u
, where
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
is the unique solution of (
13
) is continuous with respect to the uniform norm.

Proof.

For each
x\in\bar{D}
and
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
, we have

\displaystyle|\Psi(\eta)(x)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}|K(x,y)||F(\Psi(\eta(y))-F(\Psi(\eta^{\prime}(y))|dy+|\eta(x)-\eta^{\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq||K(x,\cdot)||_{2}||F(\Psi(\eta))-F(\Psi(\eta^{\prime}))||_{2}+||\eta-\eta^{\prime}||_{\infty}

where
||\cdot||_{\infty}
is the uniform norm on
D
. Hence,

||\Psi(\eta)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})||_{\infty}\leq\Lambda||F(\Psi(\eta))-F(\Psi(\eta^{\prime}))||_{2}+||\eta-\eta^{\prime}||_{\infty},

(16)

where
\Lambda\coloneqq\sup_{x\in D}||K(x,\cdot)||_{2}
(which is finite by Lemma
4.1
). Now let
v_{n},v\in L^{2}(D)
be such that
v_{n}\to v
in
L^{2}(D)
, then we have that
F(v_{n})\to F(v)
in
L^{2}(D)
as well. Indeed, given that
\{v_{n}\}_{n}
converges in
L^{2}(D)
to
v
, any subsequence
\{v_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
will converge
L^{2}(D)
towards
v
as well. This will imply the existence of a further subsequence
\{v_{n_{k_{j}}}\}_{j}
converging to
v
almost everywhere in
D
. Since
F
is continuous,
F(v_{n_{k_{j}}})
will converge to
F(v)
almost everywhere in
D
and, since it is bounded, the dominated convergence theorem yields

||F(v_{n_{k_{j}}})-F(v)||_{2}\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}0.

Proving that any subsequence of
\{F(v_{n})\}_{n}
has a further subsequence converging, in
L^{2}(D)
, towards
F(v)
, which shows that
\{F(v_{n})\}_{n}
converges towards
F(v)
in
L^{2}(D)
.

Next, given a sequence
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n}\subset\mathcal{C}(D)
uniformly convergent towards
\eta\in\mathcal{C}(D)
, then we have
||\eta-\eta_{n}||_{2}\to 0
as well, so, by what we have just seen, and by (
16
), it suffices to show that
\Psi(\eta_{n})\to\Psi(\eta)
in
L^{2}(D)
to show that
\Psi(\eta_{n})
uniformly converges towards
\Psi(\eta)
.

To show the convergence in
L^{2}(D)
, we will follow some of the computations in
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
or
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
used to prove the existence of solutions to equations like
Tu=b
.

Let
u=\Psi(\eta)
and
u_{n}=\Psi(\eta_{n})
, then, for each
x\in\bar{D}
,

\eta(x)-\eta_{n}(x)=u(x)-u_{n}(x)+\int_{D}K(x,y)\left[F(u(y))-F(u_{n}(y))\right]dy.

(17)

Taking the inner product of this last expression against
F(u)-F(u_{n})
and using (
11
) (with
\varphi=F(u)-F(u_{n})
) and (
7
) (with
x=u
and
y=u_{n}
) gives

\displaystyle\langle F(u)-F(u_{n}),\eta-\eta_{n}\rangle\geq-L||u-u_{n}||_{2}^{2}+a\left|\left|\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)\left[F(u(y))-F(u_{n}(y))\right]dy\right|\right|_{2}^{2}.

(18)

On the other hand, we also have, by (
17
) again,

\left|\left|\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)\left[F(u(y))-F(u_{n}(y))\right]dy\right|\right|_{2}^{2}=||u-u_{n}||_{2}^{2}+||\eta-\eta_{n}||_{2}^{2}-2\langle u-u_{n},\eta-\eta_{n}\rangle.

Plugging this in (
18
) yields

\displaystyle a||\eta-\eta_{n}||_{2}^{2}+(a-L)||u-u_{n}||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle F(u)-F(u_{n})+2a(u-u_{n}),\eta-\eta_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq||\eta-\eta_{n}||_{2}\left(||F(u)-F(u_{n})||_{2}+2a||u-u_{n}||_{2}\right),

(19)

where in the last step we have used Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality and the triangle inequality. Finally, using the fact that

||u-u_{n}||_{2}\leq\Lambda||F(u)-F(u_{n})||_{2}+||\eta-\eta_{n}||_{2},

we deduce, from (

Since

, we obtain that

The results seen in this section hold true if the assumption on
F
being bounded is dropped but we assume that
F
is a Lipschitz function whose Lipschitz constant,

, verifies the condition

, where
\Lambda\coloneqq\sup_{x\in D}||K(x,\cdot)||_{2}
. The existence and uniqueness of solutions to (

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
F
is
L
-Lipschitz with
L<\Lambda^{-1}
, then, for each
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
, equation (
13
) has a unique solution in
\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
and the induced map
\Psi\colon\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)\to\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
is continuous with respect to the uniform norm.

Proof.

Given
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
, consider the operator
\tilde{T}\colon\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)\to\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
defined by

\tilde{T}u(x)=-\int_{D}K(x,y)F(u(y))dy+\int_{D}K(x,y)g(y)dy+\eta(x),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\bar{D}.

The fact that
\tilde{T}u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
follows from the fact that
\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)F(u(y))dy
,
\int_{D}K(\cdot,y)g(y)dy
and
\eta
vanish on
\partial D
and, for all
x,z\in\bar{D}
,

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{T}u(x)-\tilde{T}u(z)\right|\leq||K(x,\cdot)-K(z,\cdot)||_{2}\left(|F(0)|+L||u||_{\infty}+||g||_{2}\right)+|\eta(x)-\eta(z)|,

so, by Lemma
4.1
and the fact that
\eta
is continuous, we see that
\tilde{T}u
is continuous.

Now, given
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
, it is easy to see that

||\tilde{T}u-\tilde{T}u^{\prime}||\leq\Lambda L||u-u^{\prime}||_{\infty}

which, since
\Lambda L<1
, tells us that
\tilde{T}
is a contraction for fixed
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
. Given that
\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
endowed with the uniform norm is a Banach space, we obtain the existence of a unique fixed point in
\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
for the map
\tilde{T}
, proving the existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
13
).

Lastly, consider
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(D)
, then it is not so hard to see that

||\Psi(\eta)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})||_{\infty}\leq\Lambda L||\Psi(\eta)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})||_{2}+||\eta-\eta^{\prime}||_{\infty}\leq\Lambda L||\Psi(\eta)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})||_{\infty}+||\eta-\eta^{\prime}||_{\infty}

which implies, using that

,

||\Psi(\eta)-\Psi(\eta^{\prime})||_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\Lambda L}||\eta-\eta^{\prime}||_{\infty},

and thus, that
\Psi
is a continuous map, finishing the proof.
∎
