MTR4 deficiency attenuates abdominal aortic aneurysm progression through suppressing VSMC senescence via the MTR4-RAP1a axis
Authors: Yajing Li, Keying Tian, Ying Wang, Shuokang Wu, Qi Cao, Sihan Wang, Xinhua Zhang, Xugang Wang, Hongguang Lian, Xin Liu, Yiming Hao, Wei Wang, Lingdan Zhao, Qiming Yu, Long Zhang, Yu Zhang, Jinkun Wen, Bin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening vascular disease characterized by progressive aortic dilation, with insidious onset but high mortality after rupture and limited treatment options. Vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) senescence is a key driver of AAA progression. RNA helicase MTR4 has been reported to function in cardiovascular disorders, laying a foundation for exploring its aortic pathological functions. Our research findings indicate that MTR4 was significantly upregulated in both human and murine AAA tissues. Mendelian randomization indicated a suggestive causal association between elevated MTR4 expression and increased AAA risk. In vivo, MTR4 deficiency markedly attenuated VSMC senescence and suppressed AAA formation in mice. Functional studies confirmed that MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence and AAA progression by activating the Rap1 pathway through regulation of RAP1a and its activator RASGRP2. RAP1a silencing abrogated MTR4-driven VSMC senescence. This work identifies MTR4 as a potential therapeutic target for AAA.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a tumor-like lesion formed by localized abnormal dilation of the abdominal aorta.1 Its primary hazards include fatal massive hemorrhage due to rupture, thromboembolism caused by thrombus detachment, ischemic necrosis of organs, and functional impairment resulting from compression of surrounding tissues.2 Owing to the insidious nature of its progression, AAA often remains asymptomatic until critical complications develop. Rupture represents the most devastating complication of AAA.3 As the aneurysm diameter expands—typically exceeding 5 cm—the aortic wall undergoes progressive thinning and structural degradation, rendering it vulnerable to abrupt rupture triggered by factors such as acute blood pressure fluctuations, strenuous physical exertion, or accidental trauma.4 Once rupture occurs, the associated mortality rate is exceedingly high, underscoring the imperative of early screening, timely diagnosis, and proactive intervention.

Recent studies have shown that vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) senescence may affect the formation of AAA.5 For example, the expression of sirtuin 1(SIRT1) was significantly lower in both human and murine AAA samples compared to the control group, and the reduction of sirtuin 1 exacerbates the aging of VSMCs and the formation of AAA.6,7 Another study showed that the expression of myocardin-related transcription factor A (MRTFA) was increased in both human and mouse thoracic and abdominal aneurysmal tissues and that knockout the myocardin-related transcription factor A reduced VSMC senescence and AAA formation.8 Similarly, the deletion of Kruppel-like factor 5 (KLF5) induced vascular senescence, which was associated with AAA rupture.9 Although these studies have demonstrated a relationship between VSMC senescence and AAA, the mechanism by which aging induces AAA remains unknown.

MTR4, a Ski2-like RNA helicase, is an important regulator of RNA surveillance and degradation processes as a crucial component of the RNA exosome.10 Interestingly, our preliminary study found that MTR4 can inhibit hypoxia-induced myocardial injury by regulating fructose metabolism.11 Although the biochemical functions of MTR4 have been extensively characterized, its precise pathophysiological roles in VSMC senescence and AAA remain poorly defined. In the present study, we found that MTR4 was markedly upregulated in the aorta of patients with AAA. MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence and the expression of senescence-related genes by activating the Rap1 signaling pathway. Knockout of MTR4 can reverse angiotensin II-induced AAA formation in mice.

Results

MTR4 is upregulated in human and mouse aneurysmal lesions

To determine whether MTR4 is associated with AAA, we first interrogated its expression in mouse datasets (GSE197748 dataset from the GEO database) and clinical specimens. RNA-seq analysis comparing non-aneurysmal aortas with AAA tissues demonstrated a significant increase in MTR4 transcripts in AAA (Figure 1A). We next validated these findings in human surgical samples. Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence confirmed markedly higher MTR4 abundance in the aneurysmal wall compared with non-diseased vessels (Figures 1B and 1C), with enhanced staining predominantly localized within the medial layer. To assess whether MTR4 is similarly induced during aneurysm development in vivo, we established an Ang II-induced AAA model. ApoE-/- mice were maintained on a high-cholesterol/high-fat diet for three months and subjected to continuous Ang II infusion via subcutaneously implanted osmotic minipumps. AAA formation was verified by serial ultrasound imaging (Figure 1D). Relative to controls, aneurysm mice displayed an increased aortic weight-to-body weight ratio (Figure 1E) and a significant reduction in total aortic length (Figure 1F). Moreover, progressive aneurysmal dilatation was evident, as reflected by a time-dependent increase in maximal luminal diameter (Figure 1G). Histopathological analyses further revealed aneurysmal remodeling, including severe elastin disorganization and fragmentation (EVG staining) and prominent collagen deposition (Masson’s trichrome staining) within the aneurysmal wall (Figure 1H), collectively confirming robust AAA induction. We then examined MTR4 expression in murine aortas. Both immunoblotting and qRT-PCR showed a significant upregulation of MTR4 protein and mRNA in AAA tissues compared with normal aortas (Figures 1I and 1J). These results were further corroborated by IHC and IF, which demonstrated intensified MTR4 staining in aneurysmal lesions (Figures 1K and 1L). To further delineate the cell types responsible for this upregulation, we performed co-immunofluorescence staining for MTR4 with the macrophage marker CD68 in both human and mouse AAA tissues. The results demonstrated that MTR4 predominantly co-localized with α-SMA in the medial smooth muscle layer, whereas minimal co-localization was observed with CD68 (Figures 1M and 1N). These findings indicate that the pathological upregulation of MTR4 in AAA occurs mainly in VSMCs rather than in infiltrating macrophages. Mendelian randomization analysis of genetically predicted MTR4 expression and AAA risk

Using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework, we assessed whether genetically predicted MTR4 expression is causally associated with AAA. The weighted median estimator suggested a positive causal effect, with higher MTR4 expression associated with an increased risk of AAA (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.006–1.14, p = 0.032). In contrast, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analysis-the primary MR approach-did not provide evidence for a statistically significant causal association (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.98–1.07, p = 0.277) (Figures 2A–2D). MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence during AAA development

VSMC senescence is increasingly recognized as a core pathological feature that accelerates AAA development and progression.7,12 To define the senescence landscape in AAA at the transcriptomic level, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on abdominal aortic tissues from control and AAA mice. Among 128,623 detected transcripts, 16,889 were upregulated in AAA relative to controls. Functional annotation further indicated that 5,149 of these upregulated transcripts were senescence-related, highlighting senescence as a prominent biological program activated during aneurysm formation (Figures 3A and 3B). To further substantiate the involvement of senescence in human and experimental AAA specimens, we first quantified canonical senescence markers. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated robust upregulation of p16, p21, and p53 in both human AAA tissues and murine aneurysmal aortas compared with their respective controls (Figures 3C and 3D). These observations were corroborated at the transcript and protein levels by qRT-PCR and immunoblotting, respectively (Figures 3E and 3F). Because aging is a major risk factor for AAA, we next examined whether advanced age exacerbates aneurysmal pathology in vivo. AAA was induced in young (8-week-old) and aged (24-week-old) C57BL/6 mice using CaPO4 treatment. Histological assessment (H&E, EVG, and Masson’s trichrome staining) revealed substantially more severe aneurysmal remodeling in aged mice, characterized by pronounced elastic lamellar fragmentation, greater luminal dilatation, medial disorganization, thinning of the vessel wall, and increased p16 expression (Figure 3G). Together, these data indicate that aging aggravates AAA-associated structural degeneration and is accompanied by enhanced vascular senescence. We then asked whether MTR4 is linked to the vascular senescence phenotype. In human samples, correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive linear relationships between MTR4 expression and senescence-associated genes, including p16, p21, and p53, when comparing AAA lesions with adjacent non-diseased aortic tissues (Figure 3H). This clinical association suggested that MTR4 may participate in senescence regulation within the aneurysmal wall. Given that VSMCs constitute the principal cellular component of the aortic media and their senescence is mechanistically implicated in AAA pathogenesis, we established an in vitro senescence model by stimulating VSMCs with Ang II. As expected, p53, p21, and p16 levels increased progressively with the duration of Ang II exposure, confirming successful induction of cellular senescence (Figure 3I). Notably, MTR4 expression was significantly upregulated in senescent VSMCs, peaking at day 1 and remaining elevated through day 5, whereas the proliferation marker PCNA declined in a time-dependent manner (Figure 3I). To further extend this observation to another VSMC senescence-associated vascular disease, we examined atherosclerotic lesions. MTR4 expression was markedly increased in atherosclerotic vessels compared with controls and co-localized with the VSMC marker α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), mirroring the pattern observed in AAA tissues (Figure 3J). In parallel, senescence markers (p16 and p53) were also elevated and showed expression trends concordant with MTR4 (Figures 3K and 3L). Collectively, these data support a strong association between MTR4 upregulation and VSMC senescence across vascular pathologies and suggest that MTR4 may contribute to aneurysm development and progression, at least in part, through modulation of VSMC senescence.

MTR4 aggravated AngII-induced AAA formation

To further test the hypothesis that MTR4 contributes to AAA by promoting VSMC senescence, we generated MTR4-deficient (MTR4-/-) mice and crossed them with ApoE-/- mice to obtain ApoE-/- MTR4-/- double-knockout animals. AAA was induced by high-fat diet feeding combined with continuous Ang II infusion. Genetic ablation of MTR4 markedly reduced Ang II-induced aneurysm formation relative to ApoE-/- controls (Figure 4A). Histological analyses further demonstrated that MTR4 deficiency preserved aortic wall architecture. EVG and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed substantially less elastin disorganization and fragmentation and reduced extracellular matrix remodeling in the ApoE-/- MTR4-/- aneurysm group compared with the ApoE-/- AAA group (Figure 4B). Consistent with suppression of the senescence program in vivo, deletion of MTR4 significantly blunted Ang II-induced upregulation of p16 and p21, as assessed by WB and qRT-PCR (Figures 4C and 4D) and corroborated by immunohistochemistry (Figure 4E). We next asked whether MTR4 directly modulates VSMC senescence. In cultured VSMCs, MTR4 knockdown and MTR4 overexpression were performed to evaluate functional consequences. MTR4 silencing significantly reduced basal expression of senescence markers (p16, p21, and p53) at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 4F and 4G) and largely abrogated their induction by Ang II. Concordantly, SA-β-gal staining demonstrated that MTR4 depletion attenuated Ang II-triggered VSMC senescence (Figure 4H). Conversely, ectopic MTR4 expression led to a marked accumulation of SA-β-gal-positive cells, and co-treatment with Ang II further potentiated the senescence phenotype, indicating a synergistic pro-senescent effect (Figure 4I). This was accompanied by enhanced expression of senescence-associated proteins (Figure 4J). Importantly, neither MTR4 knockdown nor overexpression significantly altered VSMC death under these conditions (Figure 4K), suggesting that MTR4 primarily regulates senescence rather than cytotoxicity. MTR4 promoted VSMC senescence through the Rap1 signaling pathway

Given the marked upregulation of MTR4 in the medial layer of human AAA specimens and its functional contribution to VSMC senescence and aneurysm formation, we next sought to define the molecular circuitry downstream of MTR4 in VSMCs. We therefore re-analyzed RNA-seq data generated from Ang II-stimulated VSMCs following MTR4 silencing. KEGG pathway enrichment identified Rap1 signaling as one of the most significantly enriched pathways upon MTR4 knockdown (Figure 5A), implicating Rap1-associated programs as potential mediators of MTR4 function. Rap1 proteins (RAP1a and RAP1b) are small GTPases that cycle between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states, thereby acting as molecular switches that transduce extracellular cues into downstream effector responses. The activation state of Ras/Rap family GTPases is controlled by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), including the RASGRP family (RASGRP1-4) and related Rap-directed GEFs. To interrogate whether MTR4 modulates key components of the Rap1 axis, we manipulated MTR4 expression in VSMCs and assessed two pathway-relevant molecules, RAP1a and RASGRP2. Immunoblotting showed that MTR4 depletion significantly reduced RAP1A protein abundance, whereas RASGRP2 levels were increased (Figure 5B). Conversely, MTR4 overexpression upregulated RAP1a and decreased RASGRP2 expression (Figure 5C). Together, these data suggest that MTR4 remodels the Rap1 signaling network, with RAP1a emerging as a prominent MTR4-responsive node. To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which MTR4 regulates Rap1a, we performed actinomycin D (ActD) and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays. The results demonstrated that MTR4 interacts with Rap1a, and this interaction is weakened upon MTR4 knockdown. Moreover, MTR4 did not affect the stability of Rap1a mRNA (Figures 5D and 5E). We next examined whether Ang II directly engages this pathway. Time course analyses by immunoblotting and immunofluorescence demonstrated that Ang II induced a progressive increase in RAP1a expression in VSMCs, whereas RASGRP2 levels were minimally affected (Figures 5F–5H). To extend these observations in vivo, we examined RAP1a and RASGRP2 expression in aortic tissues from AAA mice. RAP1a abundance was elevated in aneurysmal aortas from MTR4WT mice, whereas MTR4 deficiency markedly blunted this increase (Figure 5I). In contrast, although RASGRP2 was reduced in AAA tissues, MTR4 deletion did not restore its expression (Figure 5J), suggesting that RASGRP2 downregulation in vivo may be governed by additional MTR4-independent mechanisms. Collectively, these results nominate RAP1a as the principal Rap1-pathway effector linked to MTR4 during AAA development.

To determine whether RAP1a is functionally required for Ang II-induced VSMC senescence, we silenced RAP1a using siRNA and quantified senescence responses following Ang II stimulation. RAP1a knockdown substantially attenuated Ang II-induced upregulation of senescence-associated genes and proteins (Figure 5K) and significantly reduced SA-β-gal-positive cells (Figure 5L), indicating that RAP1a promotes the senescent phenotype in VSMCs. Conversely, to test whether RAP1a overexpression alone is sufficient to mimic the pro-aging effect of MTR4, we overexpressed RAP1a in VSMCs. Notably, RAP1a overexpression significantly increased SA-β-gal-positive cells under Ang II stimulation, recapitulating the effect of MTR4 overexpression (Figure 5M). Next, to directly test whether RAP1a mediates MTR4-driven senescence, we overexpressed MTR4 in VSMCs and simultaneously depleted RAP1a. Notably, RAP1a silencing largely reversed the pro-senescent effects of MTR4 overexpression under Ang II stimulation (Figure 5N). To further establish the epistatic relationship, we knocked down MTR4 and then overexpressed RAP1a. MTR4 knockdown suppressed Ang II-induced senescence, and this suppression was rescued by forced expression of RAP1a, as evidenced by restored SA-β-gal positivity (Figure 5O). To further assess the functional recovery of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, we performed ELISA to measure IL-6 and MMP-9 levels. Consistent with the SA-β-gal results, MTR4 knockdown decreased the expression of IL-6 and MMP-9, and this reduction was partially restored by RAP1a overexpression in the MTR4-knockdown background (Figure 5P).

Taken together, these findings support a mechanistic model in which MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence predominantly through activation of the RAP1a arm of Rap1 signaling, thereby facilitating vascular degeneration and AAA progression.

Discussion

In the present study, using both Ang II and CaPO4 animal models, we demonstrated that VSMC senescence aggravates AAA formation via the MTR4-Rap1 signaling axis. The present study contributes novel insights to the existing literature. First, MTR4 aggravates AAA formation, and knockout of MTR4 attenuates AAA formation. Second, MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence, and MTR4 deficiency reverses Ang II-induced VSMC senescence. Third, MTR4 directly targets the Rap1 signaling pathway to induce the VSMC senescence phenotype. Overexpression of MTR4 and simultaneous knockdown of RAP1a can reverse Ang II-induced VSMC senescence.

MTR4 is a Ski2-like RNA helicase that plays a central role in RNA surveillance and degradation pathways as an activator of the RNA exosome. MTR4, on its own, functions to prepare RNA substrates for exosome processing while also competing with RNA export factors to determine substrate specificity.13,14 Moreover, MTR4 interacts with distinct proteins to form different complexes that recruit the exosome to various types of RNA substrates. In its association with RBM7 and ZCCHC8, MTR4 targets promoter upstream transcripts (PROMPTs) and enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) for degradation.15 Conversely, it interacts with ZFC3H1 to facilitate exosomal degradation of processed transcripts, such as prematurely terminated RNAs (ptRNAs).16 Despite its critical molecular functions, the biological function of MTR4 remains to be investigated.

This study reveals that MTR4, a critical regulator in RNA surveillance and degradation, can modulate VSMC senescence through the Rap1 signaling pathway, thereby mediating AAA formation. These findings provide critical insights into the pathogenesis of AAA and suggest that MTR4 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for this fatal disease. Additionally, atherosclerosis is a complex age-related disorder and one of the leading causes of death worldwide. Accumulating evidence suggests that VSMC senescence also plays a key role in the process of atherosclerosis.17,18,19 Studies have found that TRAP1-mediated metabolic reprogramming increases lactate-dependent H4K12la through HDAC3, promoting the expression of senescent SASP, which accelerates vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) senescence and atherosclerosis.20 VSMC senescence leads to a reduction in VSMC content, which is detrimental to plaque repair after rupture, thereby enhancing plaque fragility. Notably, atherosclerosis and AAA are age-related vascular diseases sharing chronic inflammation, vascular remodeling, and VSMC senescence. However, their pathogeneses differ greatly. Atherosclerosis features intimal lipid accumulation, foam cells, and plaque formation, which may cause plaque rupture and arterial stenosis. By contrast, AAA arises from medial degeneration, extracellular matrix breakdown, and aortic dilatation without lipid-rich plaques.21 Therefore, elucidating novel mechanisms of VSMC senescence and identifying new therapeutic targets are crucial for advancing atherosclerosis treatment. Our study found that MTR4 is not only upregulated in AAA but also exhibits an upregulation trend in the ApoE-/-mouse atherosclerosis model. This study demonstrates that MTR4 is indeed associated with cellular senescence. The aforementioned results suggest that MTR4 may serve as a regulatory factor for vascular senescence in various age-related vascular diseases. Therefore, targeting MTR4 holds significant therapeutic value in the treatment of multiple age-related vascular disorders.

In this study, MTR4 promotes VSMC senescence and exacerbates AAA formation. In ApoE gene knockout (ApoE-/-) mice, MTR4 gene knockout significantly attenuated angiotensin II (Ang II)-induced aortic dilation, maintained the integrity of elastic fibers, and reversed the upregulation of senescence markers (p16, p21, p53) in AAA lesions. In vitro experiments have confirmed that MTR4 knockdown inhibits VSMC senescence, while the combined effect of MTR4 overexpression and Ang II synergistically enhances aging-related phenotypes. Notably, the regulatory role of MTR4 did not significantly affect VSMC death, indicating that its pro-AAA effect is primarily mediated by regulating cellular senescence rather than cell death.

This study further confirms that MTR4 exerts pro-aging effects by activating the Rap1 signaling pathway. Rap1, as a member of the Ras superfamily of small GTPases, serves as a molecular switch regulating various biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and aging.22,23,24 RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and functional analysis revealed that MTR4 positively regulates the expression of RAP1a while downregulating the expression of RASGRP2, a member of the Ras guanosine nucleotide exchange factor family.25 Importantly, RAP1a knockdown blocked MTR4-induced VSMC senescence, confirming RAP1a as a key downstream mediator of MTR4 function. These findings expand our understanding of the regulatory signaling pathways of VSMC senescence and elucidate the central role of the MTR4-Rap1 axis in driving AAA progression. Mechanistically, how MTR4 functions as an RNA helicase to modulate RAP1a expression remains poorly defined in previous studies. In the present work, we further explored the regulatory mechanism and biological implications of this axis. Notably, the direct association between Rap1a signaling and AAA pathogenesis has not been documented in previous studies, and our study first links Rap1a activation with VSMC senescence and AAA progression, which enriches the pathological significance of Rap1 signaling in vascular diseases. Mechanistic experiments excluded transcriptional and mRNA stability regulation of Rap1a by MTR4, while validating a physical protein interaction between MTR4 and Rap1a. Combined with the positive regulatory effect of MTR4 on Rap1a protein abundance, we speculate that MTR4 maintains Rap1a protein levels likely via post-translational modulation through protein-protein interaction, thereby regulating VSMC senescence and AAA progression. Future RIP assays will be performed to verify the interaction between MTR4 and Rap1a mRNA. The results will help us clarify whether MTR4 regulates Rap1a expression at the translational level and further elaborate on the complete regulatory mechanism of the MTR4-Rap1a axis.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of our study warrant discussion. First, we did not directly assess Rap1 activation status in our experimental settings. Our western blot and immunofluorescence data reflect total Rap1 protein levels, not the GTP-bound active fraction. While the inverse trend between RASGRP2 and Rap1 protein expression may appear inconsistent with RASGRP2’s role as a Rap1 activator, this observation is explainable by the fact that RASGRP2 regulates Rap1 activity rather than protein abundance. Future studies will include Rap1-GTP pull-down assays to directly measure Rap1 activation status, which will provide more definitive evidence regarding the RASGRP2-Rap1 signaling relationship in our experimental models.

Second, our in vitro findings may not fully recapitulate the in vivo environment. The AAA setting involves multiple cell types (endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, immune cells) and complex extracellular matrix interactions, which can modulate signaling pathways differently than in monoculture systems.

Third, the partial recovery of RASGRP2 in MTR4 knockout mice suggests that additional regulatory mechanisms exist beyond MTR4. Identifying these MTR4-independent pathways could provide new therapeutic targets for AAA.

Fourth, although both in vitro and in vivo models reproduced key features of AAA, the clinical translational value of the findings still needs further validation in larger clinical cohorts.

Fifth, whether the MTR4-Rap1 axis affects other known VSMC aging-related pathways, such as the oxidative stress pathway, telomerase regulation pathway, and aging-related secretory phenotype pathway, requires further investigation.

Sixth, this study has limitations regarding sex as a critical biological variable. Growing evidence has demonstrated prominent sex differences in the prevalence, progression, and rupture risk of abdominal aortic aneurysm. To improve the rigor and translational potential of preclinical research, it has been emphasized that both male and female animals should be included in experimental designs.26 In the current study, only male mice were used; therefore, our findings may not be directly extrapolated to females. It remains to be determined whether the MTR4-RAP1a axis exerts similar regulatory effects in female mice or exhibits sex-specific actions in the pathogenesis of AAA. Future studies incorporating both sexes will be necessary to validate the generalizability of our results and further clarify sex-related disparities in this disease.

Finally, limitations exist in our Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis. We observed a discrepancy between the primary Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method (which showed a non-significant positive trend) and the Weighted Median method (which indicated a significant association). The non-significance of the IVW estimate prevents us from establishing a definitive causal relationship from a purely epidemiological standpoint. This limitation might be attributed to the restricted statistical power of using a small set of eQTL instruments (n = 8), where the IVW estimate can be diluted by weak variants. However, the robust Weighted Median method, combined with the consistent directional estimates (OR>1) across all five MR algorithms, provides suggestive genetic evidence. Crucially, this human genetic trend aligns seamlessly with our experimental findings. Future large-scale, tissue-specific eQTL and GWAS datasets are warranted to further validate these suggestive causal associations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides new insights into the role of MTR4 in AAA formation and highlights the potential therapeutic value of the MTR4-Rap1 signaling axis as a target. Future research should focus on developing specific MTR4 inhibitors or Rap1 pathway modulators and evaluating their efficacy in preclinical AAA models. Additionally, exploring the clinical application of MTR4 as a biomarker for AAA diagnosis or prognosis may help identify high-risk patients and enable early intervention. In summary, this study reveals the critical role of MTR4 in regulating VSMC senescence and AAA progression, providing a theoretical foundation for developing novel treatments to improve the prognosis of patients with this life-threatening disease.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical samples

A total of six male patients who were undergoing elective open AAA surgery provided tissue specimens for the study. To serve as non-aneurysmal controls, specimens of non-aneurysmal infrarenal aortic wall tissue were obtained from organ donors(n = 3). Every tissue sample was collected following an approved protocol by the Human Tissue Research Committee at Hebei Medical University. The aortic wall sample was immersed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin overnight and then prepared for standard embedding in paraffin.

We declare autonomous consent free from coercion was obtained from the donor(s) or their next of kin. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (the ethical review approval number is 20211107).

Cell culture and treatment

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used to isolate VSMCs from their aortas. VSMCs were cultured in low glucose Dulbecco’s-modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) that contains 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The VSMCs were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Only passages 3 to 6 cells at 40–60% confluence were utilized in the experiments. Primary vascular smooth muscle cells were authenticated by immunofluorescence staining for the VSMC marker α-SMA. All cell cultures were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination by PCR-based testing.

Animals and treatment

We acquired MTR4-/-mice from Jackson Laboratory in Bar Harbor, ME, USA. Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) provided the C57BL/6J mice and ApoE-/- mice. Then, we generated MTR4 knockout mice (MTR4-/-) and crossed them with ApoE-/- mice to obtain ApoE-/- MTR4-/- double knockout mice.

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (the ethical review approval number is IACUC-Hebmu-P- 2025386). The preparation steps of the AAA model are as follows: ApoE-/- mice (6-week-old male) were maintained on a high-cholesterol/high-fat diet for three months, and an Ang II-induced abdominal aortic aneurysm model was established. As previously described,27 Alzet mini-osmotic pumps (model 2004; 0000298, Durect Corporation) were implanted subcutaneously for 28 days at a delivery rate of 1000 ng/kg/min with saline or angiotensin II (HY-13948, MCE). The ultrasound Doppler imaging was used for assessing aneurysm progression. In addition, as previously described,28 CaPO4-induced AAA models were also performed using C57BL/6J mice (8–12-week-old male). For RNA, protein, histology, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining analysis, abdominal aortas were harvested.

Method details

Histopathological analyses

In order to prepare sections of the aortas of humans and mice, 10% neutral-buffered formalin was applied to the tissues, followed by the embedding in paraffin. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson trichrome, or Elastica-van Gieson staining were performed on the deparaffinized sections for histopathological analysis. The sections were observed with a Leica light microscope (Leica Microsystems Gmbh, Wetzlar, Germany). Elastin degradation was graded as 1, <25% degradation; 2, 25%–50% degradation; 3, 50%–75% degradation; or 4, >75% degradation. The data were evaluated by two independent investigators in a blinded manner and are presented as the means of three serial sections from each mouse.29 The area of collagen deposition (blue) was quantified using ImageJ software. The collagen content was expressed as the percentage of the blue-stained area relative to the total tissue area. Values from each animal were averaged for statistical analysis.30

Small interfering RNA transfection

VSMCs were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 using small interfering RNA targeting MTR4 (si-MTR4), or their corresponding control when they reached 60–70% confluency. 48 h after transfection, the cells were collected and examined.

Adenoviral vectors

pAd-EGFP and pAd-MTR4 were constructed by Shanghai Hanheng Biotechnology company. Cells were allowed to grow in complete medium to 70% density. Then virus infection was performed.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, real-time PCR

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, TRIzol from Invitrogen was used to extract the total RNA from either VSMCs or mouse aortic tissues. By using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA), the extracted RNA’s concentration and purity was determined. Reverse transcription was performed with the MonScriptTM RTⅢ Super Mix with dsDNase (Two-step) Kit (MR05201S, Monad) according to the manufacturers’ procedure. The relative mRNA expression was normalized to 18S or GAPDH. The primers for various genes were as follows:

MTR4 (forward: CAGTTGAAGGCTGCACACAT; reverse: CACTGAATGGCTCTCTTGG); p16ink4a (forward: CCCAACGCCCCGAACT; reverse: GAGCAGAAGAGCTGCTACGTGAA); p21cip1 (forward: TCCACAGCGATATCCAGACATT; reverse: CGGACATCACCAGGATTGG); 18S (forward:CGCCGCTAGAGGTGAAATTC; reverse: CCAGTCGGCATCGTTTATGG); GAPDH (forward: TTGATGGCAACAATCTCCAC; reverse: CGTCCCGTAGACAAATGGT).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity assay

Cellular SA-β-gal activity was assayed using the Senescence β-galactosidase staining Kit (Beyotime Technology, China). Briefly, pre-cold PBS was used to wash the VSMCs three times before fixing them for 15 min at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated in SA-β-gal activity solution (pH 6.0) at 37°C for 24–48 h. The enzymatic reaction was put to an end by washing them three times with PBS. Images were taken from randomly selected fields using a light microscope. To examine cellular senescence in the aorta, 4% paraformaldehyde was used to fix the samples for 12 h and then they were stained with SA-β-gal.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Abbkine, Lot: ATWC17091) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well of 96-well plates. After incubation at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 0.5–3 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. OD values ranging from 0.8 to 1.5 were analyzed to ensure reliable linear correlation with cell viability.

Western blotting

Using a protease inhibitor cocktail and RIPA lysis buffer, the proteins were isolated from VSMCs or frozen tissue samples and subsequently separated by SDS-PAGE. Polyvinylidene fluoride membranes received the isolated proteins via electrotransfer. Membranes were incubated for 2 h in 5% nonfat milk. Primary antibodies were added to the membranes and incubated overnight at 4°C. These were the antibodies that were used: MTR4 antibody (1:1000, ab70551, Abcam), p16INK4a antibody (1:1000, ab211542, Abcam), p21 antibody (1:1000, ab109199, Abcam), p53 antibody (1:1000, 10442-1-AP, proteintech), RAP1a antibody (1:1000, A0975, ABclonal), RASGRP2 (1:1000, orb454176, biorbyt) and β-actin antibody (1:5000, Santa Cruz, USA). After washing, the membranes were left at room temperature for an hour to be incubated with the matching secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology). β-actin or GAPDH was utilized as an internal reference for protein loading, protein bands were identified with Thermo Scientific’s ECL plus software.

Immunohistochemistry staining

AAA tissues with a 4 μm slice thickness were roasted for 2 h at 65°C, then dewaxed and dehydrated, repaired for 2 min under high pressure, and finally allowed to cool to room temperature. The tissue was incubated with primary antibody (MTR4, 1:200, 12719-2-AP; p16INK4a, 1:400, ab211542; p21, 1:200, ab109199; p53, 1:500, 10442-1-AP) for an entire night at 4°C according to the immunohistochemistry (PK10006, Proteintech) kit instructions. The slides were coated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and DAB was employed for visualization.

Immunofluorescence staining

VSMCs or 4 μm paraffin cross-sections were used for immunofluorescence staining. After being deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated, the paraffin cross-sections were treated in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 to permeabilize them. 10% goat serum inhibited non-specific sites for 1 h. Following an overnight incubation period at 4°C with the primary antibodies, secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor were used to probe them. DAPI was employed to stain the nuclei in every experiment. Confocal microscope was used to take fluorescence images. Following overnight culture in 6-well plates, VSMCs were treated with Ang Ⅱ (10-7 mol/L) for 0, 1, 3, and 5 days. Following a 10-min room temperature fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the cells were incubated in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 to permeabilize. The remaining procedures were the same as before.

All unprocessed, uncropped raw microscopy images acquired for histopathological analyses, immunohistochemistry staining and immunofluorescence staining from a minimum of two independent experiments were deposited in the Zenodo public repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21858349). These imaging datasets are held under embargo until manuscript acceptance and are available to qualified researchers upon reasonable request before public release.

Transcriptome sequencing

This study encompasses transcriptome sequencing data from two distinct cohorts: the first was derived from the infrarenal aorta of ApoE-/- mice infused with angiotensin II or saline, while the second was obtained from VSMCs by MTR4 siRNA (si-MTR4) compared with scramble siRNA-treated control cells (si-NC).

The sequencing steps are as follows: Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Extractor(Trizol)kit (B511311, Sangon, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and treated with RNase-free DNase I to remove genomic DNA contamination. The high quality RNA samples were subsequently submitted to the Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. for library preparation and sequencing. The high-throughput sequencing of samples was performed using the pair-end sequencing mode of the Illumina Hiseq sequencing platform. The original sequencing data were evaluated for quality using FastQC (version 0.11.2), and then trimmed for quality using Trimmomatic (0.36), resulting in relatively accurate and valid data. Using HISAT2 (version 2.0) to align the valid data of the samples to the reference genome and count the mapping information. After the transcripts were filtered and assembled using StringTie (version1.3.3b) software, GffCompare (0.10.1) was applied to compare and annotate the assembled transcripts with the known gene reference genome position information.

The above sequencing data have been submitted to the SRA database access link: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1419531; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1419767

Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis

A Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis was performed using exposure data from eQTLs in GTEx v10 (Artery Aorta) and outcome data from a publicly available GWAS (GCST90475994). Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure at a threshold of p < 1 × 10-5 after pruning for linkage disequilibrium (LD). RsID standardization was carried out using MungeSumstats. The rsIDs of the selected SNPs were harmonized and standardized using the MungeSumstats tool. For the outcome data, missing standard errors (SEs) were imputed based on confidence intervals. Causal inference was conducted using inverse-variance weighted (IVW) and MR-Egger methods, complemented by heterogeneity tests, pleiotropy assessments, and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of the findings.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of all datasets was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. For comparisons among multiple groups, homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Data satisfying the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) post hoc test for multiple comparisons. When the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated, Welch’s ANOVA followed by the Games-Howell post hoc test was used. For datasets that did not satisfy the normality assumption, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All significance asterisk markers shown in the figures are generated from the above-described statistical analyses. Sample size n denotes the number of mice for in vivo animal experiments or independent biological replicates for cellular experiments. Exact n values for each experiment and supplementary statistical information can be found in the corresponding figure legends.
