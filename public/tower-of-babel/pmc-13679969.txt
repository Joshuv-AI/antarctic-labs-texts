Factors associated with early death from de novo metastatic breast cancer in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Priya Thomas, Brooke Carter, Craig C Earle, Salimah Z Shariff, Jacques Raphael, Alyson Mahar, Sarah Knowles, Muriel Brackstone, Michael Lock, Andrea Eisen, Areej Hezam, Phillip S Blanchette
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Early death from female breast cancer is influenced by disease biology and factors such as age, marginalization, socioeconomic status, rurality, and access to primary care. Our objective was to investigate factors associated with early death in the subgroup of patients diagnosed with advanced de novo metastatic breast cancer, defined by the presence of distant metastatic disease at initial presentation.Methods:We used linked health care administrative data from Ontario, Canada, from 2010 to 2019 to conduct a population-based retrospective cohort study. We identified adults diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer through the Ontario Cancer Registry. The primary outcome was the frequency of early death, defined as death within 6 months of cancer diagnosis. We used a multivariable logistic regression model to determine the patient, cancer, and provider characteristics associated with early death.Results:Among 4004 patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer, 22.9% (n = 918) experienced early death. A higher Ontario Marginalization Index score for the domains of households and dwellings, and material resources was significantly associated with early death (odds ratio [OR] 1.27, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.04 to 1.55, and OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.46, respectively). Registration with a primary care physician was associated with significantly decreased odds of early death (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.88). We observed variability in early death across geographic regions and an increase in early death among patients living in rural areas after the start of systemic therapy.Interpretation:Marginalization, access to a primary care physician, and geography play a role in early death in patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer in Ontario. Improving access to primary care providers may help reduce early death in these patients.

Breast cancer affects 1 in 8 females during their lifetime, and 3000 patients are diagnosed with breast cancer annually in Ontario.1 De novo metastatic breast cancer is defined by the presence of distant metastatic disease at initial presentation and represents about 3% to 6% of all breast cancer diagnoses. The presentation of these patients is heterogeneous, ranging from oligometastatic disease to late-stage disease with multipleorgan involvement. Many have an aggressive disease biology, and some patients have limited access to health care, or disease denial or neglect.2

Overall survival from all forms of metastatic breast cancer is steadily improving, with advancements in systemic therapies including targeted therapies, antibody drug conjugates, and immunotherapy. Median survival ranges from 2 to 5 years depending on disease subtypes, and patients who present with de novo metastatic disease typically have better survival than patients with recurrent disease who were previously treated.3–7

Early death from de novo metastatic breast cancer is affected by many factors, including disease biology, and patient-related and health-system factors. Despite Ontario’s publicly funded health care system, long wait times and disparities in access exist. Patient factors such as age, ethnicity, marginalization, socioeconomic status, access to primary care, and rurality have been shown to influence timely access to diagnosis, time to treatment initiation, and overall health outcomes.8–10 A study from the United States found that early death from breast cancer among patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer was 25% to 30% between 2000 and 2011.11 Factors associated with early death were older age, uninsured status, and geographic residence. Other US studies have observed disparities in time to treatment and survival according to ethnicity and socioeconomic status.12–15 Delays in diagnosis and treatment have been found to negatively affect breast cancer survival.16–18

Data are lacking on early death from breast cancer and its association with sociodemographic factors in Canada. We conducted this study because patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer are often vulnerable to disparities in health equity and access to care, and most previous studies investigating these issues have been conducted in the US.12,13,15 We sought to determine the factors associated with early death from de novo metastatic breast cancer among residents of Ontario, Canada.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective observational study using linked, population-based, health care administrative data housed at ICES, an independent, nonprofit research institute in Ontario. The study cohort included patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer diagnosed from Apr. 1, 2010, to Mar. 31, 2019. Ontario is the largest Canadian province, with approximately 14 million residents, and provides public funding for most physician services and hospital admissions.19

Eligibility

Eligible patients had stage IV breast cancer documented in the Ontario Cancer Registry at the time of diagnosis. We excluded patients if they had a previous history of breast cancer, they had evidence of previous systemic therapy before their diagnosis date, or they underwent systemic therapy that was not of palliative intent.

Data sources

We analyzed and linked (using unique, encoded identifiers) administrative health care databases at ICES that captured physician billings, hospital admissions, and medications. We used the codes from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision; Canadian Classification of Health Interventions; and Drug Identification Numbers to identify all diagnoses, procedures, and medications (Appendix 1, Tables S1 to S3, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260156/tab-related-content).

Independent and exposure variable

One of the key variables of interest was the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON-Marg), a multifaceted tool that uses Canadian census data to measure 4 key aspects of community-level marginalization: households and dwellings (residential instability), material resources (material deprivation), age and labour force (dependency), and racialized and newcomer populations (ethnic concentration) (Appendix 1, Table S4).20–22 The 4 ON-Marg domains are scored into quintiles of marginalization (highest to lowest). We further categorized the quintiles as follows: quintiles 1 to 2 (lower marginalization), quintile 3, and quintiles 4 to 5 (higher marginalization). We also calculated a composite aggregate ON-Marg summary score.

Other variables of interest included age, sex, rurality (based on the Rurality Index for Ontario score), and the modified Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score (cumulative score minus score for diagnosis of metastatic cancer).23–25 Cancer-specific variables included estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, type of cancer treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy), and year of diagnosis. Systemic therapy was classified based on first-line treatment with either anti-estrogen therapy (with or without targeted therapy), anti-HER2 directed therapy, chemotherapy alone, or no systemic therapy. Health-system variables included type of cancer centre, patient’s residence in 1 of 14 Local Health Integration Networks (LHINs), whether the patient was registered to a primary care physician and managed by a medical oncologist, and factors associated with the oncologist (oncologist age, sex, international medical graduate status, and years in practice).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was early death, defined as all-cause death within 6 months of breast cancer diagnosis. In a sensitivity analysis, we defined early death as all-cause death within 3 months of diagnosis. We also investigated the percentage of patients with early death associated with hospital admission (within 30 days), intensive care unit admission (within 30 days), or death during hospital admission.

Statistical analysis

We described baseline characteristics of the cohort using absolute counts and percentages. We observed patients until death or the end of the Mar. 31, 2021, follow-up period, with a maximum 2-year follow-up. We reported early death as absolute counts and event rates.

We defined the multivariable logistic regression model in advance and included the following: age, sex, marginalization status, rurality, CCI score, and registration to a primary care physician. We assessed all 4 ON-Marg domains in our primary model. However, for the secondary and sensitivity analysis, we used an ON-Marg summary score aggregating all 4 domains. We restricted the secondary analysis to patients who received systemic therapy and included additional diagnostic, treatment, and health-system variables. We also derived an unadjusted 2-year overall survival curve according to type of first-line systemic therapy.

We included only ER or PR and HER2 status in a multivariable sensitivity analysis because of a high rate of missing data for this variable. We also performed 2 additional adjusted sensitivity analyses using provincial geographic regions (LHINs) and restricting the cohort to patients whose primary cause of death was specified as breast cancer.26

We reported all logistic regression models as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were 2-sided, and we considered a p value of 0.05 or less to be significant. We did not conduct statistical corrections for multiple testing. We performed statistical analyses using the SAS software program version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Ethics approval

ICES is a prescribed entity under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which allows research to be conducted without review by a research ethics board.27

Results

We identified a total of 4004 patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer diagnosed and treated in Ontario (Figure 1). Based on ON-Marg, 17.3% of patients had a lower summary marginalization score, 42.6% had a marginalization score of quintile 3, and 40.1% had a higher marginalization score (Table 1). In terms of preventive care, 57.5% of patients had undergone mammography in the past 2 years, and 74.8% were registered to a primary care physician. Among the 2390 patients (59.7%) who received systemic therapy, 45.1% received anti-estrogen therapy (with or without targeted therapy), 40.8% received chemotherapy alone, and 14.1% received anti-HER2 therapy as their first-line treatment. Mean follow-up time for the cohort was 5.2 (standard deviation [SD] 1.8) months.

A total of 22.9% of patients (n = 918) died within 6 months of a diagnosis of de novo metastatic breast cancer (Table 2). A multivariate adjusted logistic regression model (Table 3) showed a significant association between death within 6 months of diagnosis and older age, higher CCI score and higher marginalization measured by the domains of households and dwellings (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.55 comparing quintiles 4–5 with quintiles 1–2) and material resources (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.46). Patients who were registered to a primary care physician had 26% lower odds of early death (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.88). Rurality and sex did not significantly influence the risk of early death in our primary analysis. We present our primary model in Appendix 1, Table S5 using an ON-Marg summary score (aggregating scores from all 4 ON-Marg domains). This model demonstrated significantly higher odds of early death associated with a higher marginalization summary score compared with a lower marginalization summary score (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.88).

Among 2390 patients who received systemic therapy, those with older age, higher marginalization, and higher CCI scores had higher odds of early death (Table 4). Rurality was also associated with early death among patients who received systemic therapy. Patients residing in rural and semi-urban areas had 1.8 (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.86) and 1.5 times (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.16 to 2.07) higher odds of early death, respectively, than patients living in urban areas. However, the protective effect of being registered to a primary care physician was not observed among patients who received systemic therapy (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.24). Among patients who were eligible for mammography screening (age ≥ 50 yr, n = 3330), the rate of mammography receipt within 2 years was 59.7% among patients registered to a primary care physician compared with 42.0% among patients without a primary care physician (p < 0.001) (Appendix 1, Table S6). Figure 2 shows the unadjusted overall survival of patients within the cohort according to type of first-line systemic therapy. Survival was greatest among patients receiving anti-HER2 therapy, followed by anti-estrogen therapy (with or without targeted therapy), chemotherapy alone, and no receipt of systemic therapy. Acuity of the cancer centre, as well as the age, sex, international medical graduate status, and years in practice of the patient’s oncologist, were not significantly associated with odds of early death.

Interpretation

In our study, the early death rate (death within 6 months) in patients who presented with de novo metastatic breast cancer was 22.9%. People who lived in more marginalized communities had higher odds of early death than those in less marginalized communities. When we analyzed the 4 domains of marginalization, the domains of households and dwellings, and material resources were associated with increased odds of early death, while the domains of age and labour force, and racialized or newcomer populations were not. Previous research has demonstrated that newcomers to Canada often have better health outcomes than people born in Canada, known as a “healthy immigrant effect.”28 We also found that access to a primary care physician reduced the odds of early death.

A previous study investigating early death among patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer using Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results databases from the US11 found an early death rate of 33.2%, which was associated with older age, triple negative receptor status, uninsured health status, and geographic variation. Our results were similar and suggest that health inequities are present in health care systems with more public funding than the US.

Access to a primary care provider is a growing challenge in Canada, with 1 in 4 Canadians lacking regular access to primary care.29 Lack of access to primary care may negatively affect the timeliness of diagnosis and also lead to delays in starting important anti-cancer therapies.30,31 In terms of diagnosis, investigations may be less coordinated and more likely to occur in an emergency department. A recent Ontario study found that 35% of patients with cancer visited the emergency department within 90 days before diagnosis, and more than 51% of patients required admission to hospital at diagnosis.32 Delay in cancer diagnosis negatively affects survival outcomes; even a 4-week delay in treatment is associated with increased death across surgical, systemic, and radiotherapy interventions.33 Delays also affect survival of patients with advanced metastatic breast cancer, increasing the risk of symptomatic presentation and wider spread of metastatic disease, and can make treatment more challenging and less effective.16–18 Patients with very late disease presentations may miss a critical window to be eligible for treatment.

Primary care is pivotal in cancer prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment support and adverse-effect management, symptom control, and psychosocial and palliative care.34 The demand for cancer care is rising exponentially, and specialist oncologists are unlikely to be able to meet these care needs alone.35 General practitioners in oncology, who are family physicians with additional specialization in oncology, often provide additional assistance to extend service to patients with cancer receiving treatment in smaller and rural communities. Primary care also plays a very important role in countering medical misinformation regarding cancer prognosis and the potential benefits of treatment.34,36

Our results suggest that Ontario patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer without access to a primary care physician may not have undergone optimal breast cancer mammography screening; rates of mammography among patients (aged ≥ 50 yr) registered to a primary care physician were significantly higher than those of patients without a primary care physician (59.7% v. 42.0%). These rates are significantly lower than the average Ontario mammography screening rate of 81% published in 2024.37 A recent study from the US found that the rate of distant-stage breast cancer at presentation is increasing, especially among women from ethnic-minority populations, who experience disparities in health care access.38 Whereas all patients in our study were diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer, it is possible that patients with regular, consistent primary care follow-up and who participated in mammography screening were diagnosed with a smaller burden of disease at presentation, which contributed to better survival. Federal and provincial governments in Canada are aware of important primary care shortages and that urgent action is needed to improve potential health inequities. The Primary Care Needs OurCare report (2024) highlighted several important standards, initiatives, and recommendations, which should be rapidly adopted to improve access to primary care across Canada.29,39,40

The rate of early death from breast cancer varied across geographical regions, according to LHINs. In our primary outcome model (before any treatment), rurality did not significantly influence early death. However, once patients had seen an oncologist and received treatment, rural residence was significantly associated with early death. This may reflect challenges for rural patients accessing cancer care, which is commonly delivered in urban specialty cancer centres. Rural patients face higher out-of-pocket costs, specifically due to travel, accommodation, and sick leave for patients and caregivers.8,41 It is estimated that the projected out-of-pocket lifetime cost for Canadian patients with cancer is $33 000.41 Although virtual care is becoming more common, delivery of specialized cancer care close to home often remains a challenge.42,43 This should be a focus of future quality-improvement research and health-system adaptation to make delivery of cancer care more equitable for all patients living in rural communities.44

Limitations

We chose all-cause death as our primary outcome because information on cause of death was not always available or recorded accurately in the databases available to us. However, our results were consistent when we analyzed patients whose primary cause of death was listed as breast cancer. The ON-Marg is based on aggregate census data (postal code) reflecting an individual’s community of residence and therefore does not accurately reflect the circumstances of each individual. We also adapted the 5 quintiles of the ON-Marg summary score to 3 reference categories because of our sample size. We assumed that if a patient was registered to a primary care physician, they received regular care, which may not be the case. We may have underestimated registration to rural primary care physicians because some physicians may also work in a hospital-based setting. We did not have information about patients registered to primary care nurse practitioners. We may have over-estimated the percentage of patients not receiving any systemic therapy because our data may not have identified all outpatient prescriptions for endocrine therapy among patients aged 65 years and younger. The data we analyzed were from the years before the COVID-19 pandemic — difficulties accessing primary care may have been worse during and after the pandemic. Our health administrative data also did not include molecular pathology information, which may be of prognostic importance in patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer. Additionally, the extent and exact sites of metastatic disease were not available at diagnosis. Our results may not be generalizable to patients with early-stage breast cancer and health systems outside of Ontario.

Conclusion

Patients diagnosed with de novo metastatic breast cancer in Ontario experience health inequity. Our study showed an association between marginalization and lack of registration to a primary care physician and higher odds of early death. Optimizing cancer prevention and mammography screening in marginalized communities may reduce the likelihood of presenting with metastatic disease. Our findings highlight the need for better primary care access. Future studies to determine effective ways of optimizing rural delivery of cancer care and integration of virtual care are needed.
