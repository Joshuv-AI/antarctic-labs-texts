Laparoscopic liver biopsy in buffaloes with tru-cut needle yields superior liver samples
Authors: Luisa Pucci Bueno Borges, Kayan da Cunha Rossy, Gabriela Melo Alves dos Santos, Carla Rozilene Guimarães Silva, Rodrigo dos Santos Albuquerque, Hanna Lyce Magno de Morais, Pedro Soares Bezerra Júnior, Michel Santos e Cunha, Renato Abrantes de Oliveira, Luiz Henrique Vilela Araújo, Heytor Jales Gurgel, Rinaldo Batista Viana, Francisco Décio de Oliveira Monteiro, Pedro Paulo Maia Teixeira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver biopsy is a valuable diagnostic tool for diagnosing hepatobiliary diseases in ruminants. Although laparoscopic biopsy is established in cattle and small animals, there is no standardised comparison of the biopsy instruments for buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis).ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare the quality of liver samples obtained by laparoscopy using three instruments: Babcock forceps (B1), laparoscopic biopsy forceps (B2) and a 14G truss cut needle (B3). We hypothesised that the tru-cut needle would provide superior samples with fewer artefacts.Study designProspective experimental study.MethodsTo understand sedation and epidural anaesthesia, laparoscopic liver biopsies were performed on five healthy female buffaloes. Using a right intercostal approach, three sequential samples were collected from each animal with B1, B2, and B3. The samples were histologically evaluated for quality (presence of portal /central veins), sufficiency, and artefacts (edge squeeze and detachment). The area was measured digitally. Statistical analysis used Cochran’s Q and post-hoc McNemar tests (α = 0.05).ResultsThe truss truss cut needle (B3) produced adequate quality samples in 100% of cases, compared to 80% for B2 and 60% for B1. Sample sufficiency was higher for B2 and B3 (80% each) than for B1 (0%; P = 0.0408). Edge detachment was significantly lower for B3 (20%) than for B2 (40%) and B1 (100%; P = 0.0388). Crush artifact was absent in B3 samples but present in 60% of B1 and B2 samples.Main limitationsThe small sample size (n = 5) of healthy animals limits statistical power and generalisability to clinical cases. The fixed order of the instrument may have introduced a sequence bias.ConclusionsLaparoscopic liver biopsy with a truss cut needle yields diagnostically superior samples with significantly fewer artefacts compared to forceps-based techniques in buffaloes, recommending it as the instrument of choice for this species.

Introduction

Liver biopsy remains a critical diagnostic tool for identifying hepatic disorders in ruminants, allowing an accurate prognosis and targeted therapy [1]. In veterinary medicine, minimally invasive techniques, such as laparoscopy, have gained prominence due to their ability to reduce tissue trauma, lower morbidity, and improve animal welfare, while allowing direct visualisation of the biopsy site and immediate intervention if needed [2, 3]. Compared to percutaneous or ultrasound-guided methods, laparoscopic biopsy offers superior sample quality and reduced risk of iatrogenic injury or haemorrhage, as demonstrated in studies in equine, bovine, and small animals [4–6]. Despite these advances, detailed protocols and instrument comparisons for laparoscopic liver biopsy in buffaloes, a species of significant economic importance in many regions, remain inadequately documented [7].

Hepatobiliary diseases, including abscesses, fibrosis, parasitic infestations, and heavy metal accumulation, are prevalent in buffalo populations and often present with nonspecific clinical signs, leading to underdiagnosis and substantial economic losses at slaughter [8, 9]. The current biopsy techniques described for cattle and small ruminants may not be directly applicable to buffaloes due to anatomical and physiological differences [10]. Furthermore, while laparoscopic liver biopsy has been validated in species such as goats, dogs, and horses, there is a notable lack of standardised, instrument-specific evaluations in buffaloes understanding sedation [11, 12]. This gap limits the ability of clinicians to obtain diagnostic-quality samples efficiently and safely in this species.

Although buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis) are often managed similarly to cattle, they possess distinct anatomical and physiological characteristics that affect surgical and diagnostic procedures. Key differences include a more oblique and narrower intercostal space configuration, which limits conventional lateral flank approaches used in cattle; a comparatively smaller and more cranially positioned liver, with a different lobar distribution, particularly the right liver lobes that extend further dorsally; a thicker and less compliant abdominal wall musculature, which increases resistance to trocar penetration; and different patterns of visceral fat distribution that can obscure endoscopic landmarks. These differences have been documented in comparative topographic studies [7, 10] and directly influence the choice of portal placement, instrument trajectory, and sample retrieval technique. Consequently, validated biopsy protocols in cattle, goats, or horses cannot be directly extrapolated to buffaloes without species-specific validation.

Given the importance of the integrity of the sample for histopathological evaluation, the choice of biopsy instrument significantly influences the precision of the diagnostic. Artefacts such as tissue crushing, edge detachment, and insufficient sample size can compromise interpretation and lead to misdiagnosis [13, 14]. Previous studies in other species have compared forceps and needle-based techniques, but there is no systematic evaluation for buffaloes [15, 16]. Therefore, there is a clear need to determine the most effective laparoscopic instrument for hepatic sampling in buffaloes, considering parameters such as sample adequacy, structural preservation, and minimal artefact induction.

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare three laparoscopic instruments, Babcock forceps, laparoscopic biopsy forceps, and a 14G tru-cut needle, for liver biopsy in healthy buffaloes in a quadrupedal position. The novelty of this research lies not in the invention of new instruments, but in being the first validation of these instruments for laparoscopic liver biopsy in buffaloes, providing quantitative histology-based evidence of artefact profiles in buffalo hepatic tissue, and establishing an evidence-based reproducible protocol that addresses the distinct thoracic and abdominal anatomy of this species. We hypothesized that the tru-cut needle would yield superior sample quality, larger tissue areas, and fewer histopathological artifacts compared to forceps-based techniques.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility and comparative efficacy of a laparoscopic approach to liver biopsy in standing buffaloes, a technique previously well-described in other ruminants but not formally evaluated in this species [2, 3, 11]. The right intercostal access provided excellent visualisation and manipulation of the liver, corroborating findings from previous anatomical work in buffaloes that established this as a viable portal [10]. All procedures were completed without major complications, aligning with the established safety profile of laparoscopy in large animals, which offers direct visualisation to minimise haemorrhage and iatrogenic injury compared to blind percutaneous techniques [4, 5].

The relevance of species-specific validation is underscored by comparative anatomical studies. Unlike cattle, buffaloes possess a shorter, more curved 13th rib and narrower intercostal spaces, which limit manipulative angles during laparoscopic procedures [10]. Additionally, the buffalo liver has a proportionally larger left lobe and a more dorsal positioning of the right lobe, requiring a more cranially placed trocar than that commonly used in cattle [7]. These differences, while subtle, become critical when the path of the biopsy instrument must avoid the diaphragm and the dorsal ruminal sac. Our study confirms that despite these anatomical challenges, the intercostal approach with a tru-cut needle is both feasible and diagnostically reliable in buffaloes, a finding that cannot be assumed from bovine data alone.

Our central finding, that a 14G automatic tru-cut needle (B3) consistently yielded histologically superior samples compared to Babcock forceps (B1) and laparoscopic biopsy forceps (B2), has significant diagnostic implications. Although sample quality scores were not statistically different, the tru-cut needle achieved 100% adequacy, with an absolute difference of 40 percentage points from the Babcock forceps. More importantly, the tru-cut needle virtually eliminated the crushing artifacts (0% squeeze) that plagued both types of forceps (60% each), and minimized edge detachment (20%) far more effectively than the Babcock (100%) or biopsy forceps (40%). These artifacts, specifically crush injury and detachment, are well-documented pitfalls that can distort architecture, obscure diagnosis, and are directly attributable to the mechanical grasping action of forceps [12, 13]. Our histopathological images (Figures 2, 3 and 4) provide clear visual evidence of this mechanistic difference: forceps cause focal trauma, while the tru-cut needle’s slicing action preserves parenchymal integrity. This finding is consistent with instrument comparisons in other species; for example, in dogs, needle biopsies provided better tissue architecture than forceps [15], and in goats, the tru-cut needle was effective for laparoscopic sampling [11].

The statistically significant results for sample sufficiency and edge detachment underscore the reliability of the tru-cut needle. The complete failure of the Babcock forceps to provide a sufficient sample in any case (0%) highlights its inadequacy for diagnostic liver biopsy, relegating its utility to gross tissue manipulation only. Although biopsy forceps (B2) performed better in sufficiency, their high artifact rate remains a critical liability. The superior architectural preservation seen with the tru-cut needle is paramount for diagnosing diffuse hepatic conditions common in buffaloes, such as fibrosis, lipidosis, or heavy metal bioaccumulation, which require evaluation of intact portal and lobular relationships [7, 9].

Several methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the fixed order of instrument application (B1→B2→B3) was chosen for procedural consistency but introduces potential sequence bias, as later samples were taken from tissue adjacent to previous biopsy sites. However, this bias likely worked against our hypothesis, as prior tissue trauma might have compromised subsequent samples, yet the tru-cut needle (B3) still produced superior results. Second, the small sample size (n=5) limits statistical power; this study should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation with larger cohorts. Third, the absence of blinding during instrument handling (surgical team) is inherent to the procedure, but histopathological evaluation was fully blinded. Fourth, the use of healthy animals is necessary for initial safety validation but does not replicate clinical hepatopathy conditions where tissue friability may affect sample quality.

Another methodological limitation of this study is the absence of formal interobserver and intraobserver agreement testing for the histopathological categorical variables (sample quality, sample sufficiency, edge squeeze, and edge detachment). All histopathological evaluations were performed by a single board-certified veterinary pathologist. While this ensures consistency in application of the scoring criteria, it does not allow quantification of observer-driven variability. Intraobserver agreement (repeat assessment by the same pathologist at two different time points) and interobserver agreement (independent assessment by multiple pathologists) should be included in future studies to confirm the reproducibility of the scoring system employed here.

Finally, while laparoscopy requires specialized equipment and training, its diagnostic advantage is clear. The tru-cut needle, through a single 5 mm port, offers a compelling combination of simplicity and sample excellence. Future studies should focus on establishing standardized protocols for laparoscopic liver biopsy in diseased buffaloes, conducting cost-benefit analyses versus ultrasound-guided techniques, and training veterinarians in this method to improve the antemortem diagnosis of hepatobiliary diseases that currently cause significant economic loss in this species [7, 8].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that laparoscopic liver biopsy is a feasible and safe procedure in standing buffaloes. Among the three instruments evaluated, the 14-gauge automatic tru-cut needle consistently provided diagnostically superior tissue samples. It yielded samples with optimal architectural preservation, minimal iatrogenic crushing or detachment artefacts, and high diagnostic adequacy. In contrast, forceps-based techniques, particularly Babcock forceps, were prone to inducing significant tissue trauma and were unreliable in obtaining sufficient material. Therefore, based on the parameters of sample quality, sufficiency, and artefact minimization, the tru-cut needle is recommended as the instrument of choice for laparoscopic liver biopsy in this species. This technique provides a reliable method for antemortem diagnosis, which is crucial for the effective management and treatment of hepatobiliary diseases in buffaloes, helping to mitigate associated economic losses. Future studies should incorporate interobserver and intraobserver agreement testing for histopathological scoring to enhance the reproducibility and generalizability of findings.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted strictly in accordance with the ethical guidelines established by the National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA, Brazil). The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (CEUA) of the Federal University of Pará (Protocol Number: 5028050717). Written informed consent was obtained from the animal owner prior to inclusion in the study. The study animals came from the animal facility of the Federal Rural University of Amazonia (buffalo breeding).

Animal selection criteria

Five healthy adult female Murrah-Mediterranean crossbred buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis) with an average age of 4.5 years were used. The animals were sourced from the vivarium Bubalinoculture Unit, Federal Rural University of the Amazonia (UFRA), Belém, Pará, Brazil.

Inclusion criteria required that the animals be clinically healthy, as determined by a complete physical examination (heart rate, respiratory rate, rumen motility, rectal temperature, and mucous membrane color), and have no history of hepatic disease or previous abdominal surgery. Animals showing signs of systemic illness, abnormal behavior, or pregnancy (determined by transrectal palpation) were excluded. All animals were seronegative for common infectious hepatopathies as per routine herd health records.

An a priori formal sample size calculation was not performed for this pilot/comparative technique study, as its primary objective was to evaluate the feasibility and qualitative histopathological outcomes of three biopsy methods within the same subjects. The sample size (n=5) was chosen based on logistical constraints typical of large animal surgical research, ethical considerations to minimize animal use while obtaining preliminary comparative data and consistency with published laparoscopic technique validation studies in large ruminants [2, 10, 11]. This sample size provides data that can inform future studies.

The order of instrument application (Babcock forceps [B1] → biopsy forceps [B2] → tru-cut needle [B3]) was sequentially fixed for all animals to maintain procedural consistency and to avoid potential cross-contamination of the biopsy site by a previous instrument. True randomization of instrument order was not applied, this limitation is addressed in the Discussion. Histopathological evaluation was performed by a single board-certified pathologist who was blinded to the instrument used for each sample. To ensure blinding, samples were labeled with anonymized codes (e.g., B1, B2, B3) known only to the surgical team and not disclosed to the pathologist until after all analyses were completed.

Anesthetic and laparoscopy

Thirty minutes before epidural anesthesia, all animals received intramuscular (IM) sedation with xylazine hydrochloride (2% solution; Xilazina®, Agener União Saúde Animal, São Paulo, SP, Brazil) at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg body weight. The injection was administered into the semitendinosus or semimembranosus muscle (gluteal region) using an 18-gauge, 40 mm needle. This dose was selected to achieve sufficient sedation (head lowering, drooping of upper eyelid, reduced response to environmental stimuli) while maintaining the quadrupedal standing position without ataxia or recumbency. The sedative effect of xylazine typically began within 5–10 minutes after IM administration, peaked at 20–30 minutes, and lasted approximately 60–90 minutes, exceeding the duration of the laparoscopic procedure (20–25 minutes).

Following sedation, the sacrococcygeal region was cleaned with soap and water, degreased with alcohol, and disinfected with 2% iodine solution (Riodeine® 2%, Rioquímica, São Paulo, Brazil). Epidural anesthesia was administered in the first intercoccygeal space (Co1-Co2) using a combination of 0.2 mg/kg of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride (Xylestesin® 2%, Cristália, São Paulo, Brazil) and 0.125 mg/kg of 0.5% levobupivacaine (Novabupi® 0.5%, Cristália, São Paulo, Brazil). A Tuohy needle (18-gauge, 8 cm) was inserted with the bevel oriented cranially. Correct epidural placement was confirmed by the hanging drop technique and absence of cerebrospinal fluid or blood reflux. The total volume administered was 5–8 mL, titrated to effect. The onset of epidural anesthesia (loss of tail tone and perineal sensation) occurred within 5–10 minutes.

Before skin incision, 10 mL of 2% lidocaine without epinephrine was infiltrated subcutaneously and intramuscularly at each planned trocar insertion site (right paralumbar fossa and right penultimate intercostal space).

The combination of xylazine sedation (0.1 mg/kg IM) and epidural lidocaine/levobupivacaine provided sufficient restraint and analgesia for the standing laparoscopic procedure. The xylazine contributed sedation, muscle relaxation, and some central analgesia, while the epidural block provided regional anesthesia at the surgical sites. No animal required additional sedation or general anesthesia during the procedure.. The quality of anesthesia was assessed by response to pinprick at the incision sites; no animal showed signs of pain during skin incision or trocar placement.

The surgical fields (right flank and paralumbar fossa, right last intercostal space and sacrococcygeal region) were shaved (trichotomy), aseptically scrubbed three times with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate soap, degreased with 70% alcohol, and disinfected with 2% iodine solution (Riodeine®, Rioquímica, São Paulo, Brazil). Sterile drapes were applied. Heart rate, respiratory rate, rumen motility, and mucous membrane color were monitored at 5-minute intervals throughout the procedure. No animal required rescue analgesia or conversion to general anesthesia.

For laparoscopy following equipment and instruments were used: electronic CO₂ insufflator (Access 40, Dyonics, Michigan, USA); LED light source (GDI, Ribeirão Preto, São Paulo, Brazil); fiber optic lighting cable (495 light cable, Karl Storz SE & Co, Tuttlingen, Germany); microcamera with processor (Combo Endosurgery system, GDI); rigid 10 mm diameter endoscope with 0° viewing angle and 33 cm working length (Karl Storz SE & Co, Tuttlingen, Germany); surgical-grade monitor (AG Neovo 22, Neovo, Taipei, Taiwan); Zscan recorder and image capture software (Zscan, Brazil); and reusable rigid trocars (Bhio Supply, Sapucaia do Sul, Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil) in sizes 5 mm and 10 mm diameter with pyramidal tips and safety shields.

All laparoscopic instruments were disinfected by immersion in 2% glutaraldehyde solution (Glutacin, Cinord, Serrana, São Paulo, Brazil) for 45 minutes, followed by three rinses with sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution (Eurofarma, São Paulo, Brazil). The microcamera and fiber optic cable were covered with sterile videoendoscopic plastic covers. The tru-cut needle (14-gauge, 10 cm, single-use) was opened immediately before the procedure.

All procedures, trocar placement (two portals), were performed with the animal standing in a quadrupedal position. Two portals were established, initially in the paralumbar fossa for pneumoperitoneum and subsequently in the penultimate intercostal space for liver biopsy (Fig. 1A), based on our previously described topographic anatomical landmarks [10].

The first port was made on the right paralumbar fossa, at the center of a triangle formed by the limits of the thigh tuberosity, the transverse apophysis of the lumbar vertebrae, and the thirteenth rib. A small incision was made in the animal’s skin, and the 10-mm permanent trocar was inserted with care to prevent iatrogenic perforations. As soon as the abdominal cavity was visible through the rigid endoscope, the region was insufflated with CO2, using a 15 mmHg intra-abdominal pressure and 5 l/min insufflation speed. However, in addition to the right paralumbar fossa, an intercostal port, of 20–30 cm in the dorsoventral direction, was added between the twelfth and thirteenth ribs (Figure 1A).

After establishing pneumoperitoneum, the instrument port was established in the penultimate intercostal space under laparoscopic guidance. Sequential liver biopsies were then performed as described above. At the conclusion of the procedure, CO₂ was passively evacuated by opening the trocar cannula safety valve while applying gentle manual pressure to the abdominal wall. The trocars were removed with rotating movements, and skin incisions were closed with two horizontal mattress sutures using nylon 0 (Shalon, Sertix, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil).

Biopsy collection technique and sample processing

Under direct laparoscopic visualization, three consecutive biopsy samples were collected from adjacent sites on the accessible ventral aspect of the right liver lobe. The biopsy sequence was applied in fixed order (Figure 1B):B1: Laparoscopic Babcock forceps (5 mm diameter, 33 cm length). The forceps were advanced through the 5 mm trocar, opened, and gently closed around a visible liver lobe protrusion. Tissue prehension was maintained for exactly 5 seconds (standardized using a digital timer) before withdrawal (Figure 1C).B2: Standard laparoscopic biopsy forceps with cup-jaw design (5 mm diameter, 33 cm length, 5 mm cup size). The same 5-second prehension time was applied, and the forceps were rotated 90 degrees during closure to maximize sample volume (Figure 1D).B3: 14-gauge, 10 cm automatic tru-cut needle (Magnum® reusable core biopsy needle, Bard Biopsy Systems). The needle was introduced through the 5 mm trocar under direct visualization. The inner stylet was advanced 19 mm into the liver parenchyma. The needle was then withdrawn (Figure 1E).

After each biopsy, the instrument was removed, and the sample was immediately transferred from the instrument jaw/needle notch to a labeled cassette using a sterile 25-gauge needle, then placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Biopsy sites were spaced approximately 1–2 cm apart to avoid sampling from areas affected by previous biopsies. The order was fixed to minimize variation in operator technique; however, potential sequence bias is addressed in the Discussion.

Following the procedure, the biopsy sites were inspected for hemorrhage. Trocars were removed and the skin incisions were closed with simple interrupted sutures using nylon 0 (Shalon®). Postoperative analgesia (flunixin meglumine, 1 mg/kg IM, every 12 h for 48 h) and antibiotic prophylaxis (oxytetracycline hydrochloride, 20 mg/kg IM, single dose) were administered.

All tissue samples were immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 hours, routinely processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 µm, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Histological sections (5 μm thickness) were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) following standard protocols. Digital images were captured using a trinocular light microscope (Model CX31, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with plan achromatic objective lenses (4×, 5×, 10×, 20×, and 40×). The microscope was coupled to a digital camera (Model SC30, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; 3.1-megapixel CCD sensor). Image acquisition was performed using cellSens Standard software (v1.18, Olympus Corporation). Brightness, contrast, and sharpness were adjusted uniformly across all images using ImageJ (v1.53k, NIH, USA) for purposes of figure preparation only; no alterations affected histological interpretation. Representative images for each biopsy group (B1, B2, B3) are presented in Figs. 2, 3, and 4 at the magnifications indicated.

Outcome variables and measurement

All histopathological evaluations were performed on 5-μm Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)-stained sections by a single board-certified veterinary pathologist blinded to instrument origin.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using BioEstat software (version 5.3, Instituto de Desenvolvimento Sustentável Mamirauá, Brazil), with a significance level set at α = 0.05. The study used a design within-subjects, in which three biopsy instruments (Babcock forceps, biopsy forceps, and a tru-cut needle) were compared in the same five animals. The primary results consisted of four dichotomous categorical variables (sample quality, sufficiency, squeeze artefact, and detachment artefact) and one continuous variable (sample area).

For the related categorical data, comparisons were performed using the Cochran Q test. Significant results were followed by post-hoc pairwise McNemar tests, with a Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons. The continuous area data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The appropriate test (repeated measures ANOVA or the non-parametric Friedman test) for this variable was contingent upon verifying assumptions of normality and sphericity, the details of which were not detailed in the original report.
