Public Health Challenges in Somalia: Strengthening Health Systems for Resilient and Equitable Healthcare Delivery
Authors: Jamal Hassan Mohamoud, Mohamed Hussein Adam, Ahmed Mahad Sheikh Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSomalia continues to face multidimensional public health challenges driven by prolonged conflict, political instability, fragmented governance, climate-related disasters, displacement, poverty, and a fragile health system. The country carries a dual burden of communicable and non-communicable diseases (NCDs), alongside persistently high maternal and child mortality and recurrent nutrition and WASH emergencies. These pressures are intensified by workforce shortages, limited laboratory and surveillance capacity, unequal access to care, and heavy dependence on externally financed programmes. In this perspective, health-system resilience refers to Somalia’s capacity to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and recover from shocks while maintaining equitable delivery of essential services. We argue that resilience cannot be achieved through disease-specific or short-term humanitarian programmes alone; it requires stronger public stewardship, coordinated federal-state governance, sustainable financing, integrated primary health care, a retained and equitably distributed workforce, interoperable surveillance, and climate-resilient WASH and referral systems. The central recommendation is therefore a transition from fragmented, crisis-driven service delivery toward an accountable, primary-health-care-centred system that links communities, public and private providers, laboratories, referral facilities, and emergency response mechanisms.

Introduction

Public health systems are essential for disease prevention, health promotion, emergency preparedness, and socioeconomic development. However, fragile and conflict-affected states often face substantial barriers to building resilient healthcare systems capable of addressing population health needs effectively. Somalia represents one of the most fragile public health contexts globally because of decades of armed conflict, political instability, weak governance, recurrent humanitarian crises, climate-related disasters, displacement, and poverty.1

The collapse of state institutions following the civil war significantly weakened Somalia’s healthcare infrastructure, disease surveillance systems, healthcare workforce development, and public health governance. Although important progress has been made in rebuilding health institutions and expanding healthcare services in recent years, the country continues to face severe health system fragility and major inequities in healthcare access.2 Somalia currently faces a complex epidemiological and humanitarian transition characterized by a dual burden of communicable diseases and non-communicable diseases. Recurrent outbreaks of cholera, tuberculosis, measles, malaria, and other infectious diseases continue to threaten vulnerable populations, while non-communicable diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and mental health disorders are increasing.3 Simultaneously, environmental and climate-related crises including droughts, floods, food insecurity, water scarcity, and displacement continue to worsen population vulnerability and strain the healthcare system.4,5 Public health challenges in Somalia are further compounded by weak infrastructure, shortages of trained health professionals, inadequate financing, poor sanitation, limited laboratory capacity, and insufficient emergency preparedness. Governance is a cross-cutting determinant: fragmented responsibilities across federal and state levels, limited regulation of a large private sector, donor-dependent parallel programmes, and weak accountability can impede coordinated planning and equitable resource allocation.6,7 In this perspective, health-system resilience means the capacity to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and recover from conflict, epidemics, climate shocks, and financing disruptions while preserving essential, equitable services. The challenges discussed below were prioritized because they combine high population-health burden, repeated evidence in Somalia-specific national or international data, strong interaction with health-system capacity, and relevance to preventable mortality and health security. Our central argument is that Somalia should move from fragmented, crisis-oriented and disease-specific responses toward an integrated primary-health-care-centred model in which governance, financing, workforce, surveillance, referral, WASH, and community systems are strengthened together.

Public Health Challenges in Somalia

Maternal and Child Health Crisis

Maternal and child health indicators remain critically poor. The WHO-led 2000–2023 maternal mortality estimates place Somalia’s maternal mortality ratio at approximately 563 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births for 2023.6 UNICEF country data report an under-five mortality rate of approximately 101 deaths per 1,000 live births, with infant mortality of 65 and neonatal mortality of 34 per 1,000 live births. These levels indicate a substantially greater risk of death than global child-survival targets and underscore the need to interpret single indicators within the wider continuum of maternal, newborn, and child care.8,9

Somalia continues to experience one of the most severe maternal and child health crises globally. In 2025, WHO, UNICEF, UNFPA, and the Somali Ministry of Health reported that only 32% of births were attended by skilled health professionals and that neonatal deaths accounted for approximately one-third of under-five mortality.10 Maternal and neonatal deaths arise from interconnected clinical and health-system causes. Major pathways include obstetric haemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, sepsis, obstructed labour, complications of unsafe or delayed care, prematurity, birth complications, neonatal infection, and delayed access to emergency obstetric and newborn services. These risks are amplified by the “three delays”: delays in deciding to seek care, reaching an appropriate facility, and receiving timely quality care after arrival. Recent Somalia-focused analysis emphasizes failures across households, referral pathways, primary care, hospitals, financing, governance, and accountability.11 Rural and internally displaced populations face additional geographic, financial, security, and transport barriers.10 Priority changes should therefore establish district-level referral networks linking community health workers and midwives to 24-hour emergency obstetric and newborn care; expand emergency transport and referral communication; strengthen maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response; and use targeted financial protection for poor, rural, and displaced households. Multisectoral implementation is feasible when health authorities coordinate district health services with transport, WASH, nutrition, education, social protection, humanitarian partners, and community leadership around shared geographic priorities and measurable referral and service-coverage indicators.11

Communicable Diseases and Persistent Epidemic Threats

Communicable diseases remain a dominant public health burden in Somalia because of weak disease surveillance systems, overcrowding, poor sanitation, low immunization coverage, displacement, and climate-related emergencies. Tuberculosis (TB), cholera, measles, malaria, acute watery diarrhea, and emerging infectious diseases continue to contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality. WHO reported that Somalia’s TB incidence declined from 286 cases per 100,000 population in 2010 to 246 cases per 100,000 population in 2023.12 Although this decline reflects some progress in TB control programmes, the burden remains critically high. Delayed diagnosis, weak laboratory systems, interrupted treatment, stigma, and shortages of healthcare workers continue to hinder effective TB control. Additionally, concerns regarding multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) remain significant because incomplete treatment and weak patient follow-up systems increase resistance risks. The discussion below focuses in greater detail on TB and cholera because the cited sources provide recent, Somalia-specific incidence or outbreak data for these conditions. Measles, malaria, acute watery diarrhea, and other epidemic-prone infections remain important public health threats; their more limited discussion here reflects the scope of this perspective and the availability of comparable recent Somalia-specific data, rather than an assumption that they carry a lower disease burden.

Cholera and acute watery diarrhea continue to cause recurrent outbreaks across Somalia, particularly during flooding and drought emergencies. UNICEF reported that Somalia recorded approximately 17,000 cholera cases across 30 districts by August 2024 following severe flooding and poor WASH conditions.13 Another UNICEF response report documented approximately 16,900 cases and 136 deaths by July 2024, with a case fatality rate of 0.8%.14

The recurrence of cholera outbreaks demonstrates persistent structural failures in water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure. Overcrowded displacement camps, contaminated water sources, open defecation, and poor waste management continue to create ideal conditions for waterborne disease transmission. Climate-related disasters such as floods further exacerbate these outbreaks by damaging sanitation infrastructure and contaminating water supplies.

Somalia also remains vulnerable to vaccine-preventable diseases because of low immunization coverage and weak healthcare access. UNICEF country data estimate Somalia’s under-five mortality rate at 101 deaths per 1,000 live births, infant mortality at 65 deaths per 1,000 live births, and neonatal mortality at 34 deaths per 1,000 live births.8 These indicators highlight persistent failures in routine immunization, neonatal care, and child survival interventions.

Emerging infectious diseases such as COVID-19, Ebola, mpox, and dengue fever have further exposed Somalia’s vulnerability to epidemics. Weak laboratory capacity, delayed reporting systems, inadequate border surveillance, and shortages of rapid response teams continue to hinder preparedness and outbreak response efforts.

For communicable diseases, resilience requires a common surveillance-to-response pathway rather than parallel disease programmes: community and facility detection, timely laboratory confirmation, electronic notification, rapid investigation, vaccination or case-management response, and feedback to reporting sites. Somalia’s IDSR experience from 2016–2023 demonstrates progress in integrated surveillance while also identifying continuing implementation needs.15 Strengthening these links is especially important for private facilities and underserved districts.

Rising Burden of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs)

Although communicable diseases and humanitarian emergencies continue to demand substantial attention, NCDs are emerging as a major parallel public health challenge. Urbanization, tobacco use, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, and weak preventive services contribute to this burden. WHO’s 2023 hypertension profile estimated that approximately 1.3 million adults aged 30–79 years in Somalia were living with hypertension. The argument is not to shift resources away from communicable-disease control, but to broaden primary health care so the same frontline platform can provide vaccination and outbreak services alongside blood-pressure and diabetes screening, continuity of medicines, risk-factor counselling, and referral for chronic disease complications.16

The increasing burden of diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, and cancers reflects an epidemiological transition that Somalia’s healthcare system is poorly prepared to manage. Preventive healthcare services, routine screening programmes, diagnostic infrastructure, and chronic disease management systems remain severely limited.

Mental health disorders represent another critical but neglected public health issue. Somalia’s prolonged exposure to armed conflict, displacement, poverty, violence, food insecurity, and repeated humanitarian crises has significantly increased population exposure to trauma and psychosocial stress. Human Rights Watch has repeatedly highlighted the effects of conflict and displacement on civilian populations, worsening psychological vulnerability and social instability.17 Despite the growing burden of depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance abuse, mental health services remain extremely underdeveloped. Somalia has limited psychiatric facilities, shortages of mental health professionals, weak mental health policies, and significant social stigma surrounding mental illness. Consequently, most mental health conditions remain untreated.17 A practical approach is to integrate basic NCD and mental-health packages into existing primary-care and community platforms, train non-specialist providers, secure essential medicines, and establish referral pathways for complicated cases. This reduces the need to build separate vertical systems and makes chronic care more accessible during humanitarian and climate-related disruptions.

Environmental Health and Climate-Related Public Health Threats

Environmental and climate-related crises have become central drivers of public health vulnerability in Somalia. Recurrent droughts, floods, desertification, food insecurity, and water scarcity continue to worsen malnutrition, displacement, communicable disease transmission, and mortality. UNICEF warned in 2025 that drought, conflict, and high food prices could push approximately 4.4 million people into hunger, while an estimated 1.7 million children under five years of age were expected to suffer acute malnutrition, including approximately 466,000 children with severe acute malnutrition.4 Historical 2025 projections illustrate the scale of climate-sensitive nutrition risk: WFP projected that approximately 1.7 million children under five could face acute malnutrition through December 2025, with a large share of the burden concentrated in southern Somalia.18 Because that projection period has passed, it should be interpreted as evidence of the severity of the 2025 crisis rather than as a current 2026 prevalence estimate. The continuing policy implication is that nutrition surveillance, food-security monitoring, WASH, and climate early-warning information should be linked to health-service preparedness so that supplies, outreach, and referral capacity can be pre-positioned before drought and flood impacts peak.

Floods further compound public health risks by damaging health infrastructure, contaminating water supplies, increasing vector breeding sites, and displacing vulnerable communities. Climate-related disasters therefore represent not only environmental crises but also major public health emergencies requiring integrated climate-resilient health systems.

Health System Fragility and Workforce Shortages

Health system fragility remains one of Somalia’s most critical public health challenges. WHO and the Somali Government reported that Somalia has only four doctors, nurses, or midwives per 10,000 population, reflecting severe shortages of healthcare professionals and limited healthcare delivery capacity.2

The workforce constraint is not only a matter of absolute numbers. Retention is affected by insecure or externally financed posts, uneven remuneration, limited career pathways and continuing professional development, insecurity, and concentration of skilled personnel in urban areas. Donor-funded and project-based staffing can also produce parallel incentives and make long-term deployment difficult. Recent analyses of Somali health governance describe persistent financing and coordination constraints that affect sustainable workforce planning.6,7 These dynamics contribute to vacancies and turnover in rural and conflict-affected areas even when new health workers are trained.

Building a resilient workforce therefore requires a costed national and state-level workforce plan, transparent deployment standards, incentives for rural and underserved service, continuous competency-based training, supportive supervision, and progressive integration of essential donor-supported posts into sustainable payroll and financing arrangements. Workforce indicators should track not only headcounts but also geographic distribution, retention, competencies, absenteeism, and vacancy duration.

Inadequate healthcare financing, weak governance systems, poor infrastructure, shortages of medicines and equipment, and dependence on donor-funded programmes continue to undermine sustainability and resilience of Somalia’s healthcare system.

Disease Surveillance and Emergency Preparedness Challenges

Disease surveillance and emergency preparedness systems remain insufficient in many parts of Somalia. Although Somalia has made progress through implementation of the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) strategy, significant challenges remain in laboratory capacity, digital reporting systems, rapid response coordination, and outbreak investigation capacity.

Somalia has made measurable progress in implementing integrated disease surveillance and response, but operational gaps remain. Evidence from IDSR implementation during 2016–2023 shows the value of integrated reporting while underscoring the need for sustained capacity and coordination.19 Underreporting can arise when private and lower-level facilities are incompletely linked to national reporting channels, when laboratory results are not rapidly connected to case notification, when frontline staff lack data-analysis and outbreak-investigation competencies, and when connectivity or feedback mechanisms are weak. Recent studies also identify uneven health-information-system use across public and private facilities and continuing surveillance workforce competency gaps.19,20

Specific priorities are to formalize reporting requirements and feedback loops for public and private providers; connect laboratories, facilities, and district surveillance teams through interoperable IDSR/DHIS2 workflows; maintain specimen referral and transport networks; provide competency-based epidemiology and data-use training; and routinely audit timeliness, completeness, laboratory linkage, and response time. These changes would make surveillance a functional learning system rather than a one-way reporting obligation.

Somalia also remains vulnerable to Ebola, mpox, dengue fever, and other epidemic-prone diseases because of porous borders, high population mobility, weak disease surveillance systems, and inadequate rapid response capacity.21 Research examining outbreak preparedness in fragile states has shown that countries with weak surveillance systems and underdeveloped laboratory networks face significantly delayed outbreak detection and response times.

Cross-Cutting Priorities and Future Research

Future implementation research should test which district-level combinations of PHC strengthening, referral support, community engagement, and financial protection most effectively improve equitable service continuity during conflict and climate shocks.

Workforce research should evaluate retention incentives, rural deployment models, competency-based training, and sustainable transition of essential externally supported positions into nationally coordinated staffing arrangements.

Surveillance research should quantify reporting completeness and timeliness across public and private facilities and evaluate whether interoperable laboratory-to-notification systems, feedback loops, and targeted workforce training shorten outbreak detection and response times.

Health-financing and governance research should examine feasible pathways for increasing predictable domestic financing, pooling fragmented external resources, strengthening federal-state accountability, and protecting essential PHC services from donor volatility.

Climate-health research should evaluate integrated early-warning triggers that connect drought, flood, nutrition, WASH, and disease surveillance data to pre-positioned supplies, outreach, and referral responses.

NCD and mental-health implementation research should assess the feasibility, quality, and cost of integrating screening, essential medicines, task-sharing, and referral into routine PHC without weakening communicable-disease and emergency functions.

Conclusion

Somalia’s public health burden reflects not only multiple diseases and recurrent emergencies but also a common set of system constraints: fragmented governance and financing, workforce maldistribution and retention problems, weak referral and laboratory linkages, incomplete surveillance, unequal access, and climate-sensitive WASH and nutrition risks. The central perspective advanced here is that resilience and equity require these constraints to be addressed together. Somalia should therefore move from fragmented, crisis-driven and disease-specific responses toward accountable, PHC-centred networks that link communities, public and private providers, laboratories, referral hospitals, and emergency systems. Progress should be judged by continuity and equity of essential services during shocks, not only by the expansion of individual programmes. Sustainable domestic financing, stronger federal-state stewardship, workforce retention, interoperable surveillance, climate preparedness, and multisectoral district implementation are mutually reinforcing foundations for national health security and long-term population health.
