Nearly tight bounds for testing tree tensor network states
Benjamin Lovitz, Angus Lowe

Abstract
Tree tensor network states (TTNS) generalize the notion of having low Schmidt-rank to multipartite quantum states, through a parameter known as the bond dimension. This leads to succinct representations of quantum many-body systems with a tree-like entanglement structure. In this work, we study the task of testing whether an unknown pure state is a TTNS on n n qudits with bond dimension at most r r , or is far in trace distance from any such state. We first establish that, independent of the dimension of the state, O ⁡ ( n ​ r 2 ) O(nr^{2}) copies suffice to accomplish this task with one-sided error. We then prove that Ω ⁡ ( n ​ r 2 / log ⁡ n ) \Omega(nr^{2}/\log n) copies are necessary for any test with one-sided error whenever r ≥ 2 + log ⁡ n r\geq 2+\log n . In particular, this closes a roughly quadratic gap in the previous bounds for testing matrix product states in this setting. On the other hand, when r = 2 r=2 we show that Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(\sqrt{n}) copies are both necessary and sufficient for the related task of testing whether a state is a product of n n bipartite states having Schmidt-rank at most r r , for some choice of the qudit dimensions. We also study the performance of tests using measurements performed on a small number of copies at a time. Here, we obtain new bounds for testing rank, Schmidt-rank, and TTNS when the tester is restricted to making measurements on r + 1 r+1 copies of the state.



1
Introduction

A common task in quantum information science is to test whether an unknown state
\psi
has a given property. In particular, the problem of testing whether
\psi
is entangled according to some measure of interest arises in many areas including non-local games
[
Cla+69
,
NV17
]
, quantum cryptography
[
VV19
,
Col+19
]
, and quantum computational complexity
[
HM13
,
JW24
]
. Moreover, a recent line of work suggests that the hardness of learning and testing states could itself provide a meaningful measure of complexity in quantum systems
[
AA23
]
. Such widespread utility motivates a systematic study of the resources required to perform these tests. In the framework of
quantum property testing

[
MW16
]
, one performs a measurement on many identical copies of
\psi
, with the goal to determine whether
\psi
has the property or is far from any state with the property, typically in trace distance. The minimum number of copies required to distinguish these two cases is called the
copy complexity
of the task.

Similar to property testing of distributions in classical computer science
[
Can22
]
, the intention in the quantum case is to design tests which use far fewer copies than would otherwise be required for a complete reconstruction. For testing properties related to the entanglement of pure states, prior work has demonstrated that the copy complexity is often independent of the underlying dimension. This is desirable since for many quantum systems of interest — e.g., interacting qubits — the dimension grows exponentially in the size of the system. For example, the product test of Harrow and Montanaro
[
HM13
]
uses just two copies of an unknown multipartite pure state to determine whether it is a product state or constantly far from the set of all product states. The existence of this test in particular has a number of important implications, including that the complexity class
\mathsf{QMA}(2)
is equal to
\mathsf{QMA}(k)
for any
k\geq 2
. Another example comes from the rank test of O’Donnell and Wright
[
OW15
]
, which determines whether a mixed state has rank at most
r
or is constantly far away from any rank-
r
state using
O(r^{2})
copies.
This test can be used to determine whether a bipartite pure state is entangled, as measured by its Schmidt-rank, and this strategy was recently shown to be optimal
[
CWZ24
]
. Already for this example, however, there is a gap in our understanding of the copy complexity. Among tests with
one-sided error
— that is, tests which accept with certainty given a state in the property, AKA perfect completeness — the copy complexity of rank testing is
\Omega(r^{2})
, matching the upper bound. However, for two-sided error the best known lower bound is
\Omega(r)

[
OW15
]
.

Venturing beyond bipartite entanglement, one may also consider testing multipartite quantum states with a limited, but non-zero amount of entanglement, as suggested in
[
MW16
]
. Such properties have been considered previously in the literature. For example, Ref.
[
HLM17
]
showed that
O(n)
copies suffice to test whether a pure
n
-partite state is
not
genuinely multipartite entangled, and recently Ref.
[
JM24
]
established that this is tight up to log factors. Of most direct relevance to the present work is
[
SW22
]
which studies the task of testing matrix product states (with open boundary conditions). In a matrix product state (MPS) representation, amplitudes are computed by multiplying matrices whose dimension is bounded by a parameter known as the
bond dimension
, denoted by
r
here and throughout. Such representations are an indispensable tool for studying entanglement in physically-motivated, 1D quantum systems
[
Cir+21
]
. Testing whether a state is an MPS of bond dimension
r
on just two sites recovers the task of Schmidt-rank testing, while an MPS on
n
sites with bond dimension equal to one is a product state.

For general
n
and
r
, the MPS test proposed in
[
SW22
]
succeeds with one-sided error using at most
O(nr^{2})
copies. However, the authors conjectured that this bound is not tight, as the lower bound shown in their work leaves a gap, stating only that
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
copies are necessary for
r\geq 2
. Despite follow-up work
[
Aar+24
,
CWZ24
]
, the best-known lower bound as a function of
n
for one- or two-sided error has remained the same prior to this work. Separately, Ref.
[
SW22
]
proposed studying the copy complexity of testing other examples of tensor network states, which generalize matrix product states to higher-order tensors.

1.1
Summary of results

In this work we study the task of testing whether an unknown state admits a description as a tree tensor network state (TTNS) with bounded bond dimension, generalizing the MPS testing task considered in Ref.
[
SW22
]
. TTNS are an important extension of MPS which have been employed as representations of ground states on 2D lattices
[
TEV09
]
, ansatzes in quantum machine learning
[
Hug+19
]
, as well as in simulations of molecules (e.g., dendrimers) in quantum chemistry
[

,
NC13
]
and of other quantum many-body systems
[
Mur+10
]
, including certain measurement-based quantum computations
[
SDV06
]
. We give new bounds in two settings. In the first, the test may perform arbitrary measurements to accomplish the task, which is the setting considered in prior work. In the second, we consider restricting the test to multiple rounds of measurements performed on only a few copies of the state.

Tests using few-copy measurements

For practical applications, a drawback of the test used to prove the upper bound in
Theorem

1.1
is that it requires highly entangled measurements (based on the Schur transform) on many copies of the input state. In
Section

6
we consider testing properties using measurements performed on only a few copies of the input state at a time, once again in the setting of one-sided error. We first show in
Section

6.1
that when performing multiple rounds of measurements, adaptivity offers no advantage in many cases.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of pure states that forms an irreducible variety, and let
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
be a PC-optimal
\ell
-copy test for
\mathcal{P}
. Then among all adaptive
k
-round,
\ell
-copy tests for
\mathcal{P}
, the test
(P_{\textnormal{accept}})^{\otimes k}
is PC-optimal.

Here,
PC-optimal
refers to a test which is optimal among all tests with one-sided error (i.e. perfect completeness). See
Section

2.2
for a formal definition of optimality, and
Section

2.3
for a description of a canonical PC-optimal test for properties of pure states. A

is the common zero locus of a set of homogeneous polynomials, and an

is a variety that is not the union of two proper subvarieties. Many properties of interest form irreducible varieties, including TTNS. Notable properties that do not form irreducible varieties include the set of pure biseparable states
[
HLM17
]
and any finite set of two or more states, such as the set of stabilizer states
[
GNW21
]
.
Determining tasks for which adaptivity may help in learning or testing quantum states has been a subject of recent activity (see, e.g.,
[

Che+23

ALL22

FO24

In

(Testing trees with few-copy measurements)
.

Let

be a tree on

be a positive integer, and

{O((n-1)^{r}(r+1)!/\varepsilon^{2r})}

1.2
Overview of techniques

Similar to other works in quantum property testing
[
CHW07
,
OW15
,
SW22
]
, we use techniques from the representation theory of the symmetric and unitary groups to argue that, due to symmetries in the property being considered, it suffices to restrict one’s attention to a single, optimal measurement. Let us first describe the basic framework in slightly more detail. Suppose we are interested in a property of bipartite pure states which depends only on the Schmidt coefficients. Then, provided as input the state
|\psi\rangle^{\otimes N}
, the test should not be affected by local unitary transformations of the form
U_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes U_{B}^{\otimes N}
, nor by permutations of the
N
identical copies of
|\psi\rangle
. Furthermore, since
|\psi\rangle^{\otimes N}
is in the symmetric subspace, we can without loss of generality focus on the action of a test within this subspace. A standard averaging argument (see, e.g.,
[
MW16
, Lemma 20]
) then leads to the conclusion that, without loss of generality, the optimal measurement for testing this property is a projective measurement of a certain form. Crucially, we may interpret this measurement as leading to perfectly correlated outcomes between measurements performed locally on the two subsystems
A
and
B
, and so the property may be optimally tested by discarding the
N
copies of, say the
B
subsystem. But then this recovers the setting of mixed state testing under unitary symmetry (spectrum testing), for which the optimal measurements — known as Weak Schur Sampling — are well-understood. This argument was employed as far back as Ref.
[
MH07
]
to study compression of quantum states, and was more recently used for property testing lower bounds in
[
CWZ24
]
. It is also similar to the reasoning behind Theorem 35 in
[
SW22
]
.

The correspondence between spectrum testing and entanglement testing provides an avenue for deriving lower bounds for the properties we consider. Optimal tests for pure state properties have an intuitive description compared to those for mixed states, taking the form of a projection onto a subspace specified by the property (see
Lemma

2.1
). Our approach for the lower bound in
Theorem

1.1
leverages this description at first, and uses the correspondence to switch to the mixed state description when it is more convenient for calculations. This allows us to proceed with a direct analysis of the acceptance probability of an optimal test for trees, rather than through an information-theoretic argument based on state discrimination, as in prior work
[
SW22
,
CWZ24
]
. The task is thus reduced to proving that the rank test of O’Donnell and Wright
[
OW15
]
rejects with too small a probability on a certain hard instance of a far-away state; small enough that amplifying
n
times does not help, at least for
r
growing logarithmically in
n
. This requires a fine-grained analysis of the acceptance probabilities of the rank test based on a combinatorial interpretation of Weak Schur Sampling, building on ideas from
[
OW15
]
.

For our proof of
Theorem

1.3
we make use of an elementary algebraic-geometric characterization of PC-optimal tests for pure states. As we observe in
Section

2.3
, the (essentially unique) PC-optimal
N
-copy test for a property of pure states
\mathcal{P}
is to project onto the space
\mathcal{P}^{N}:=\text{span}(\nu_{N}(\mathcal{P}))
, where
\nu_{N}(\mathcal{P}):=\{\psi^{\otimes N}:\psi\in\mathcal{P}\}
is the
N
-th
Veronese embedding
of
\mathcal{P}
. Any variety
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to its
N
-th Veronese embedding. This allows us to carry over qualities of
\mathcal{P}
to qualities of the PC-optimal measurement. We remark that the space
\mathcal{P}^{N}
was recently used to develop semidefinite programming hierarchies for generalized notions of separability
[
Der+24
]
.

Our other results utilize additional bounds on the the rank test acceptance probabilities under simplifying assumptions about the nature of the unknown state. For example,
Theorem

5.4
relies on a computation enabled by taking the dimension of the state to be equal to
3
, while the closed-form solution for the rank test in
Section

6
uses the fact that, when measuring
r+1
copies at a time, the “reject” measurement operator is equal to the projector onto the antisymmetric subspace.

1.3
Open questions

Our work leaves many questions open. Firstly, what is the copy complexity of testing MPS or TTNS with one-sided error and
constant
bond dimension? Could
O(\sqrt{n})
copies suffice? If instead the
O(n)
upper bound is tight,
Theorem

5.4
suggests that analyzing a different hard instance — one which is
not
a product of bipartite states — may be required to prove it. It would also be interesting to see if the lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
for MPS and TTNS testing can be improved in the setting of two-sided error. Since our analysis relies on identifying a unique optimal measurement for one-sided error tests, we believe new techniques will be required to pursue this direction.

Can our techniques be extended to tensor networks on an arbitrary graph
G
? Intriguingly, our lower bound argument in
Section

4
only makes use of the assumption that the graph
G
is a tree in one step; that is
Lemma

4.1
, which shows the best approximation to a product of bipartite states by a tree is simply to take the product of the best individual approximations. Does a version of this lemma continue to hold for graphs with cycles? If so, this could imply that testing other tensor network states, such as PEPS or MPS with closed boundary conditions, has a copy complexity that scales at least linearly in the number of edges. Since little is known about the copy complexity of testing or learning more complicated tensor network states, this would be an interesting direction to pursue.

Finally, in the few-copy setting, it may be fruitful to consider the performance of tests which measure greater than
r+1
copies at a time, interpolating between the two measurement scenarios that we have considered in this work.

\mathcal{S}
, with
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
for mixed state testing and
\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
for pure state testing, as well as a distance measure
\mathrm{d}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
. For the most part we consider pure state testing. In this paper, the distance measure
\mathrm{d}
is the standard trace distance between states,
\mathrm{d}=\mathrm{d}_{\textnormal{Tr}}:\rho\times\sigma\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert_{1}
. A
property

\mathcal{P}
is a subset of
\mathcal{S}
, and a state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}
is said to be
\varepsilon
-far
from
\mathcal{P}
if
\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{d}(\rho,\sigma)\geq\varepsilon
. An
N
-copy test
for
\mathcal{P}
is a measurement operator
P_{\textnormal{accept}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
,
0\preceq P_{\textnormal{accept}}\preceq\mathds{1}
such that, given a distance
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}}\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\rho^{\otimes N})=a\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\sup_{\rho\ \varepsilon\textnormal{-far from}\ \mathcal{P}}\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\rho^{\otimes N})=b

for some
1\geq a\geq b\geq 0
. In such a case we say the test has completeness-soundness (CS) parameters
(a,b)
. Note that only
b
depends on the distance
\varepsilon
. We say the test is
successful
for the distance
\varepsilon
if
a-b\geq 1/3
, and has
one-sided error
or
perfect completeness
if
a=1
. In the setting of perfect completeness, there is a natural notion of optimality.

•

A test is
(
N
-copy) PC-optimal
for a property
\mathcal{P}
if it has perfect completeness and for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
its soundness parameter is at most that of any other test with perfect completeness.

•

A test
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
is
(
N
-copy) strongly PC-optimal
for a property
\mathcal{P}
if it is has perfect completeness and for any other
N
-copy test
Q
with perfect completeness it holds that
\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\rho^{\otimes N})\leq\Tr(Q\rho^{\otimes N})
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{S}
which is not in
\mathcal{P}
.

Every strongly PC-optimal test is also PC-optimal. Finally, given a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\dots,M_{k}\}\subset\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
, we say that
\mathcal{M}
is an
optimal measurement
for
\mathcal{P}
if for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
the following holds: for any
N
-copy test with CS parameters
(a,b)
there is an
N
-copy test
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
of
\mathcal{P}
with CS parameters
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
such that
a^{\prime}\geq a
,
b^{\prime}\leq b
, and
P_{\textnormal{accept}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}M_{j}
for some
\alpha_{j}\in[0,1]
. An operational interpretation of this definition is that
\mathcal{M}
is optimal if the performance of any test can be replicated by performing this measurement and classically post-processing the outcomes.

2.3
Optimal measurements of pure states from symmetries

In this section we describe optimal measurements for testing properties of quantum states under various assumptions. In
Section

2.3.1
we characterize the PC-optimal tests for pure state properties. In
Section

2.3.2
we determine a restricted form for the optimal measurements when the property of interest is invariant under local unitary operations. Finally, in
Section

2.3.3
we describe how Schmidt-rank testing and rank testing are equivalent tasks, which is a key step in the proof of the lower bound for testing trees in
Section

4
.

2.3.1
Pure state tests with perfect completeness

In this section we characterize the PC-optimal tests for pure state properties. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and
N
be a positive integer. Consider an
N
-copy test
P_{\textnormal{accept}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
for some pure state property
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
. Upon receiving
N
copies of an unknown pure state
\psi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
, the test accepts with probability equal to
\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes N})=\Tr(\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}P_{\textnormal{accept}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\psi^{\otimes N})
, where
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}:\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\to\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the projector onto the symmetric subspace. This motivates defining the following equivalence relation: we say that two tests
P,Q\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
are
equivalent within the symmetric subspace
if
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}P\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}=\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}Q\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any pure state property
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
, the
N
-copy test

\displaystyle P_{\textnormal{accept}}

\displaystyle:=\Proj(\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|\in\mathcal{P}\})

is strongly PC-optimal. Moreover, up to the equivalence defined above, it is the unique such test.

Proof.

Let
0\preceq Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\preceq\mathds{1}
be another test for
\mathcal{P}
with one-sided error. This means that, for every
\phi\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
\Tr(Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\phi^{\otimes N})=1
and, since the maximum eigenvalue of
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}
is
1
, it holds that
|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}
is an eigenvector of
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}
with eigenvalue
1
. By linearity, any
|\chi\rangle\in\mathcal{V}:=\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|\in\mathcal{P}\}
is also an eigenvector of
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}
with eigenvalue
1
. Therefore, we can write
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}=P_{\textnormal{accept}}+M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}
for some
M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V}^{\perp})
with eigenvalues between
0
and
1
. This implies that
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
is strongly PC-optimal. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=P_{\textnormal{accept}}+\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}.

Suppose that
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}
is strongly PC-optimal, but
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\neq 0
. Then we can set
|\xi\rangle
to be any state such that
|\xi\rangle^{\otimes N}
is not in the kernel of
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
. That it is possible to pick such a state follows from the fact that the symmetric subspace is spanned by the set of all
N
-wise tensor product states. Then

\displaystyle\Tr(Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\xi^{\otimes N})

\displaystyle=\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\xi^{\otimes N})+\Tr(M_{\mathcal{V}^{\perp}}\xi^{\otimes N})>\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\xi^{\otimes N}).

This is a contradiction with the definition of strong PC-optimality and completes the proof.
∎

Tests of the same form as the operator
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
in the above lemma have been used in several prior works
[
Wan11
,
HM13
,
MW16
]
, and in particular were used to prove optimality of the product test in
[
HM13
]
for the case of one-sided error.

2.3.2
Schur-Weyl duality applied to bipartite systems

This section reviews some of the facts from representation theory used to derive optimal measurements in property testing. See,
e.g.,
[
GW09
,
Lan12
,
FH13
]

for introductions to these topics. Let
N
be a positive integer and
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space. Also, given a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
let
W_{\pi}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
denote the permutation operator corresponding to
\pi
with the action

\displaystyle W_{\pi}|x_{1}\rangle\otimes|x_{2}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{N}\rangle=|x_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\rangle\otimes|x_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes|x_{\pi^{-1}(N)}\rangle

for any
|x_{1}\rangle,|x_{2}\rangle,\dots,|x_{N}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
. The tensor product Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}
admits a unitary representation of the group
\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
via the action
|v\rangle\mapsto U^{\otimes N}W_{\pi}|v\rangle
for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
and
U\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
. Since the representation is unitary it has a canonical decomposition into a direct sum of orthogonal isotypic components,

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}=\bigoplus_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}
. Schur-Weyl duality states that the direct sum in this equality is over partitions
\lambda\vdash N
whose length
\ell(\lambda)
is at most
\dim(\mathcal{H}):=d
, and that the corresponding isotypic component is actually irreducible and isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
are irreducible representations (irreps) of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
and
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
, respectively. We can summarize most of this information with the statement

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash N\\
\ell(\lambda)\leq d\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}).

(2)

We often identify
V_{\lambda}
with
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H})
without comment, and we let
P_{\lambda}
denote the projection onto the
\lambda^{\text{th}}
subspace in the direct sum. Performing the projective measurement
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
on a quantum state
\rho^{\otimes N}
with
\rho\in\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H})
is called
Weak Schur Sampling
(WSS) and is known to be optimal for testing properties invariant under unitary operations, i.e., properties of the spectrum of
\rho

[
MW16
]
. Notably, this argument depends on the decomposition in
Eq.

2
being multiplicity-free. We record some essential facts about WSS in
Section

2.4
.

Now consider the case where
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
for a pair of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and we are interested in a property
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
which is invariant under local unitary operations on the
A
and
B
subsystems. In other words,
\mathcal{P}
is such that, if
\psi\in\mathcal{P}
then

\displaystyle(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})\psi(U_{A}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{B}^{\dagger})\in\mathcal{P}\quad\textnormal{for all}\ \ U_{A}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\ U_{B}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B}).

(3)

To take advantage of this symmetry, one may consider applying the decomposition arising from Schur-Weyl duality to each of the subsystems. Indeed, the space
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
admits a representation of the product group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
via the action
|v\rangle\mapsto(U_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes U_{B}^{\otimes N})|v\rangle
for each
U_{A}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
U_{B}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
. Setting
d_{A}:=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
d_{B}:=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})
and using the decomposition arising from Schur-Weyl duality twice, we see

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1}\vdash N\\
\ell(\lambda_{1})\leq d_{A}\end{subarray}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{2}\vdash N\\
\ell(\lambda_{2})\leq d_{B}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\mu\vdash N}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1}\vdash N\\
\ell(\lambda_{1})\leq d_{A}\end{subarray}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{2}\vdash N\\
\ell(\lambda_{2})\leq d_{B}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}^{k_{\mu}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\mu}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

(4)

where in the second line we decomposed the product of symmetric group irreps, which results in multiplicities
k_{\mu}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
known as the Kronecker coefficients. Evidently, this decomposition of the vector space into irreps of
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
is not multiplicity-free in general. However, a useful observation, perhaps first employed within the quantum information literature in
[
MH07
]
, simplifies this decomposition greatly. Namely, for measurements performed on identical copies of pure states, the representation of interest is just the subrepresentation within the symmetric subspace, i.e., the terms in the direct sum in
Eq.

4
where the partition
\mu=(N)
. Restricting the sum to these terms and applying Schur’s Lemma (see the proof of
Lemma

A.3
in
Section

A.1
for further details) enables one to conclude that

\displaystyle\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{B}).

(5)

where the isomorphism is as a representation of the group
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
and the direct sum ranges over
\lambda\vdash N
such that
\ell(\lambda)\leq\min\{d_{A},d_{B}\}
. Alternatively,
Eq.

5
follows immediately from the fact that, within the symmetric subspace, the representations of
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
form a dual reductive pair (see, e.g.,
[
GW09
, Prop. 9.2.1]
and
[
Har05
, Sec. 5.4]
). An averaging argument similar to that in
[
MW16
, Lemma 20]
then leads to the following theorem concerning the optimal measurements for testing properties related to bipartite entanglement of pure states; that is, having the symmetry in
Eq.

3
.

Theorem 2.2

(Implied by Theorem 3.2 in
[
CWZ24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a property of pure states which is invariant under local unitary transformations, as in
Eq.

3
. Weak Schur Sampling performed locally, either on
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
or
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
, followed by classical post-processing, is an optimal measurement for
\mathcal{P}
.

The claim is slightly weaker than Theorem 3.2 in
[
CWZ24
]
, in the sense of having a stricter requirement on the symmetry
2
2

2

[
CWZ24
]
only requires invariance under local unitary transformations applied to one of the two subsystems. However, this point is not relevant for the case of testing properties related to entanglement.
. The statement may also have been implicit in earlier works, such as the proof of Lemma 36 in
[
SW22

2.3.3
Equivalence between Schmidt-rank and rank testing

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be
finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and assume without loss of generality
that
d_{A}\leq d_{B}
. For any positive integer
r\leq d_{A}
and pure state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with a Schmidt decomposition

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{A}}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle_{A}\otimes|v_{j}\rangle_{B}

(6)

such that

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d_{A}}
, let

\Delta_{r}(|\psi\rangle):=\lambda_{r+1}^{2}+\lambda_{r+2}^{2}+\dots+\lambda_{d_{A}}^{2}

and
\mathsf{SR}(r)\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
be the
property of pure states corresponding to having Schmidt-rank at most

r
. Consider testing this property with the distance measure set to the standard trace distance. Then
\psi
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{SR}(r)
if and only if

\Delta_{r}(|\psi\rangle)\geq\varepsilon^{2}
. To see this, note that

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi\in\mathsf{SR}(r)}\mathrm{d}_{\textnormal{Tr}}(\psi,\varphi)^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\max_{\varphi\in\mathsf{SR}(r)}|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{A}}\lambda_{j}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq r+1}\lambda_{j}^{2}

where the first line makes use of the fact that
\mathrm{d}_{\textnormal{Tr}}(\phi,\phi^{\prime})=\sqrt{1-|\langle\phi|\phi^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}}
for any two pure states
\phi=|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|
and
\phi^{\prime}=|\phi^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{\prime}|
and in the second line we used the following version of the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem on low-rank approximation
[
EY36
,
Mir60
]
. This version is also stated explicitly as Lemma 18 in
[
SW22
]
, and we provide an elementary proof in
Section

A.2
for completeness.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
be as defined in
Eq.

6
. It holds that

\displaystyle\max\{|\langle\psi|\varphi\rangle|^{2}:|\varphi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\ \varphi\in\mathsf{SR}(r)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}^{2}

and the maximum is attained by
(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}^{2})^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\otimes|v_{j}\rangle
.

One may alternatively be interested in the property of mixed states
corresponding to having rank at most
r
, which we denote by
\mathsf{Rank}(r)\subseteq\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
. Let
P^{(j)}_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\otimes N})
denote the orthogonal projection onto the
\lambda^{\text{th}}
irrep in
\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\otimes N}
as in
Eq.

2
, for each
j\in\{A,B\}
. Here and throughout we set

\displaystyle\Pi^{(A)}_{\leq r}:=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N\colon\ell(\lambda)\leq r}P_{\lambda}^{(A)},\quad\Pi^{(B)}_{\leq r}:=\sum_{\lambda\vdash N\colon\ell(\lambda)\leq r}P^{(B)}_{\lambda}

(7)

which are orthogonal projection operators. When the vector space being acted on is clear from context we drop the superscripts and write
\Pi_{\leq r}
to denote the same operation. Ref.
[
OW15
]
proved that
\Pi_{\leq r}
is a strongly PC-optimal test for the property
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
. Moreover, by
[
OW15
, Prop. 2.2]
it holds that
\rho
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
with respect to trace distance if and only if
\alpha_{r+1}+\alpha_{r+2}+\dots+\alpha_{d_{A}}\geq\varepsilon
, where
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{d_{A}}
is the spectrum of
\rho
. Combining these observations with
Theorem

2.2
leads to the following two key facts, the second of which is also proven in
[
CWZ24
]
.

Theorem 2.4

(PC-optimal Schmidt-rank test)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces such that
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{j})=d_{j}
for each
j\in\{A,B\}
,
N
and
r\leq d_{A}\leq d_{B}
be positive integers, and
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})
denote the projector onto the symmetric subspace
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.

1.

It holds that

\displaystyle(\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r}^{(B)})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\leq r}^{(A)}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}=\Proj\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:\SR(|\phi\rangle)\leq r\}

is a strongly PC-optimal test for
\mathsf{SR}(r)\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.

2.

The copy complexity of testing
\mathsf{SR}(r)
with one-sided error is
\Theta(r^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
.

We defer the proof of this theorem to
Section

A.1
.

be a collection of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, and
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
be defined as in

. Suppose one wishes to determine whether an unknown state is in this property or is

Theorem 3.1
.

There is a test with one-sided error for the property
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
which is successful given
O(nr^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
copies of the unknown state.

To prove this result, we make use of the following theorem which bounds the error in approximating an arbitrary state in
\bigotimes_{v\in V}\mathcal{H}_{v}
by one contained in
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
|\psi\rangle\in\bigotimes_{v\in V}\mathcal{H}_{v}
be an arbitrary pure
state on
n=|V|
sites. For each
e\in E
, let
L^{(e)},R^{(e)}\subseteq[n]
be
the bipartition of the vertices induced by removing the edge

e
. Suppose further that
|\psi\rangle
has the Schmidt decomposition

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}{\lambda_{j}^{(e)}}\ |a^{(e)}_{j}\rangle_{L^{(e)}}\otimes|b^{(e)}_{j}\rangle_{R^{(e)}}

with Schmidt coefficients
\lambda_{1}^{(e)}\geq\lambda_{2}^{(e)}\geq\dots
for every

, and that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}\leq 1.

(10)

Then there exists a state

|\phi\rangle\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
for which

\displaystyle|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|\geq 1-\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}.

This lemma is implied by Theorem 11.58 in Ref.
[
Hac19

[
VC06

. We provide a proof in the language of this paper in

for completeness. The TTNS tester then proceeds in a similar manner to that for MPS testing
[

]
, via a reduction to Schmidt-rank testing, which is enabled by the following corollary. Here, “
\varepsilon
-far” is with respect to trace distance between pure states, and “with respect to edge
e
” is shorthand for “with respect to the bipartition determined by removing the edge
e
from

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a tree and
\psi
be a pure state on
n=|V|
sites that is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
for some

. There exists an edge

such that

is
\varepsilon/\sqrt{2(n-1)}
-far from the pure state property
\mathsf{SR}(r)
comprising all states of Schmidt-rank at most

with respect to this edge.

Proof.

For each
e\in E
let
\lambda_{1}^{(e)},\lambda_{2}^{(e)},\dots
be the Schmidt coefficients of
|\psi\rangle
, in non-increasing order, with respect to
e
and set

. We may assume without loss of generality that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}/2\leq\delta\leq(n-1)\max_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}

which proves the claim since, by

\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}\geq\varepsilon^{2}/2(n-1)
if and only if the state

-far from
\mathsf{SR}(r)
, defined with respect to

. If

is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
then the greatest possible overlap between

is at most

. By

we then have

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon^{2}/2\geq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2}}\geq|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|\geq 1-\delta

for some
\phi\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
. This also leads to
Eq.

11
.
∎

We now have the tools to prove
Theorem

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
N
be a positive integer to be specified shortly. Define the TTNS test
\Pi_{\mathsf{TTNS}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N})
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathsf{TTNS}}:=\Pi_{\leq r}^{(e_{1})}\Pi_{\leq r}^{(e_{2})}\dots\Pi^{(e_{n-1})}_{\leq r}

(12)

where
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n-1}\in E
are the edges in the tree and
\Pi^{(e)}_{\leq r}:=\Proj(\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:\SR_{e}(|\phi\rangle\leq r)\})
. We claim the projections commute and therefore
\Pi_{\mathsf{TTNS}}
is also a projection operator. To see this, note that the projections in the product each have an image which is contained in the symmetric subspace. Then consider a pair of distinct edges
e
and
e^{\prime}
such that, upon removing these edges we obtain bipartitions
L^{(e)},R^{(e)}\subseteq V
and
L^{(e^{\prime})},R^{(e^{\prime})}\subseteq V
, respectively. Since
G
is a tree, it is always possible to pick one subset from each of these bipartitions such that the resulting two subsets of vertices are disjoint
3
3

3

In more detail, for the edge
e
we can pick the subset of vertices in the subgraph which does not contain
e^{\prime}
, and vice versa for
e^{\prime}
.
. Let us therefore assume without loss of generality that
L^{(e^{\prime})}
and
R^{(e)}
are disjoint sets. By a judicious choice of the equivalent expressions for
\Pi_{\leq r}^{(e)}
and
\Pi_{\leq r}^{(e^{\prime})}
established in the first item of
Theorem

2.4
we have

\displaystyle\Pi^{(e)}_{\leq r}\Pi^{(e^{\prime})}_{\leq r}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{R^{(e)}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\Pi^{(e^{\prime})}_{\leq r}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{R^{(e)}})\Pi^{(e^{\prime})}_{\leq r}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{R^{(e)}})(\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{L^{(e^{\prime})}}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{L^{(e^{\prime})}}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r})(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{R^{(e)}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{L^{(e^{\prime})}}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N}_{R^{(e)}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\Pi^{(e^{\prime})}_{\leq r}\Pi^{(e)}_{\leq r},

as claimed. Now by

, if the state
\psi
being measured is
\varepsilon
-far from

then there exists an edge with respect to which

is
\varepsilon/\sqrt{2(n-1)}
-far from the property
\mathsf{SR}(r)
. Since the projection operators in the right-hand side of

is this edge. The test has the operational interpretation of accepting if and only if each measurement in the sequence of measurements

accepts. This occurs with probability at most

which, by the second item in

for some

. On the other hand, we clearly have that

always accepts whenever
\psi\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
for any choice of

.
∎

4
Lower bound for testing TTNS

In this section we prove that for sufficiently high bond dimension, the copy complexity of testing
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
with one-sided error is at least
\Omega(nr^{2}/\log n)
.

4.1
Hard instance for testing TTNS

We first describe a family of states that are
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
, which we will use as a hard instance for testing this property. This is the construction depicted schematically in
Figure

1(b)
.
Let
d>0
be a positive integer and
G=(V,E)
be a tree graph with
n=|V|
vertices and
(\mathcal{H}_{v})
be a list of local Hilbert spaces such that for each
v\in V
we have
\mathcal{H}_{v}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes D_{v}}
, where
D_{v}
is the degree of
v
. By fixing a choice of the maximum bond dimension
r
we get a property
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)\subset\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
, with
\mathcal{H}:=\bigotimes_{v\in V}\mathcal{H}_{v}
. One example of a state in
\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
but
not
in this property is the state which we denote
|\phi_{G}\rangle
, constructed in the following way. Let
|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
be a bipartite state with Schmidt-rank at least
r+1
, and where
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathcal{H}_{B}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We may then define
|\phi_{G}\rangle\in\cal H
as a tensor product of
n-1
copies of
|\phi\rangle
where, for each
e=(u,v)\in E
, we identify
\mathcal{H}_{A}
with one of the
d
-dimensional subsystems in
\mathcal{H}_{u}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
with one of the
d
-dimensional subsystems in
\mathcal{H}_{v}
. Then
\phi_{G}=|\phi_{G}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{G}|\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
but
\phi_{G}\notin\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
.

How far away from the property is this state? The following lemma makes precise the intuition that the optimal
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
approximation to the state
\phi_{G}
is given by simply using the optimal low Schmidt-rank approximation for each state in the product comprising
|\phi_{G}\rangle
. Hence, for a family of trees
G
with
n
vertices and a fixed choice of
|\phi\rangle
the overlap of the optimal approximation to
\phi_{G}
decays exponentially in
n
. This is similar to Prop. 3.3 in
[
SW22
]
, which applies to matrix product states.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
|\phi\rangle
and
|\phi_{G}\rangle
be as defined above. If

\displaystyle\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SR}(r)}|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}=F

(13)

then

\displaystyle\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)}|\langle\psi|\phi_{G}\rangle|^{2}=F^{n-1}.

(14)

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. The base case
n=2
is trivial. Assume the claim holds for all connected trees on
n-1
vertices and suppose
G
has
n
vertices. Consider a vertex
u
which is the parent of a leaf vertex
v
. By definition,
\mathcal{H}_{u}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes D_{u}}
where
D_{u}
is the degree of
u
. Define the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
as the tensor product of
\mathcal{H}_{v}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
and the last copy of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
in the product comprising
\mathcal{H}_{u}
, and set
\mathcal{H}_{B}
to be the tensor product of the remaining copies of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
in the product comprising
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
and any state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
has a Schmidt decomposition with respect to this tensor product structure which may be written as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|a_{i}\rangle_{A}\otimes|b_{i}\rangle_{B}.

Furthermore, for each index
i
it holds that
|a_{i}\rangle\in\mathsf{SR}(r)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
while
|b_{i}\rangle\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G^{\prime},r)\subset\mathrm{P}(\bigotimes_{v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}}\mathcal{H}_{v^{\prime}})
where
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
is the tree on
n-1
vertices formed by removing the leaf
v
from
G
and the local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{u}
has been redefined as
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes D_{u}-1}
. To see this, assume for contradiction that there is an edge
e\in E^{\prime}
for which
\SR_{e}(|b_{i}\rangle)\geq r+1
for some
i
and consider the partition of
V
into disjoint subsets
S
and
V\backslash S
induced by removing
e
from the original graph
G
. Also, without loss of generality assume
V\backslash S
is the set which contains both
u
and
v
. Then the reduced state of
\psi
on the sites corresponding to the vertices in
S
is

\displaystyle\Tr_{V\backslash S}(\psi)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\Tr_{V^{\prime}\backslash S}(|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|)

which has rank at least
r+1
, contradicting the membership of
\psi
in
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
. A similar reasoning shows
|a_{i}\rangle\in\mathsf{SR}(r)
for each
i
. Next, we compute

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{G}|\psi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\langle\phi|\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\langle\phi_{G^{\prime}}|b_{i}\rangle|a_{i}\rangle\right)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=:\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}|\langle\phi_{G^{\prime}}|b_{i}\rangle|^{2}\right)|\langle\phi|\chi\rangle|^{2}

(15)

where
|\chi\rangle
is the unit vector proportional to
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\langle\phi_{G^{\prime}}|b_{i}\rangle|a_{i}\rangle=(\langle\phi_{G^{\prime}}|\otimes\mathds{1})|\psi\rangle
. By the induction hypothesis,
|\langle\phi_{G^{\prime}}|b_{i}\rangle|^{2}
is at most
F^{n-2}
for each
i
. Furthermore,
\SR(|\chi\rangle)\leq r
, which is straightforward to see since
|\psi\rangle
has Schmidt-rank at most
r
with respect to the edge between
u
and
v
, and applying a local operator cannot increase the Schmidt-rank of a vector. Hence,
|\langle\phi|\chi\rangle|^{2}\leq F
and the right-hand side of
Eq.

15
is at most
F^{n-1}
, using the fact that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=1
.

That
F^{n-1}
is also a lower bound on the left-hand side of
Eq.

14
follows immediately by taking the tensor product of
n-1
copies of the optimal solution to the optimization in the left-hand side of
Eq.

13
.
∎

Using this result, we describe a family of states which are
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}]
, but require many copies to reject with constant probability. Let
r\geq 2
,
m=n-1
be the number of edges in the tree, and

be such that

. Also, let
|\phi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a state of Schmidt-rank

defined as

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle=\sqrt{1-\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{m}}|1\rangle\otimes|1\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{m(d-1)}}\sum_{j=2}^{d}|j\rangle\otimes|j\rangle.

(16)

Then, by
Proposition

2.3
and our assumption
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}})
,

\displaystyle\max_{|\psi\rangle\in\mathsf{SR}(r)}|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{m}+\left(\frac{r-1}{d-1}\right)\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{m}\leq 1-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{m}.

Thus, by
Lemma

4.1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\min_{\psi\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)}\td(\phi_{G},\psi)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\max_{\psi\in\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)}|\langle\psi|\phi_{G}\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{1-\left(1-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{m}\right)^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon

(17)

where in the final line we used the inequality
(1-x)^{q}\leq 1-qx/2
for all

has Lagrange remainder bounded by
q(q-1)x^{2}/2
. In other words, with the above choice of Schmidt coefficients for
|\phi\rangle
, the resulting pure state
\phi_{G}
is
\varepsilon
-far from the property
\mathsf{TTNS}(G,r)
.

Proof of the lower bound

In order to show that the state
\phi_{G}
requires many copies to reject for any test with one-sided error, we relate the performance of a specific test which is easier to analyze to that of the strongly PC-optimal test. For every Hilbert space

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

Proof.

By

\displaystyle\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(G,r)=\left\{|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|:|\psi\rangle=\bigotimes_{e\in E}|\phi_{e}\rangle,\ |\phi_{e}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{e}\ \textnormal{and}\ \SR(|\phi_{e}\rangle)\leq r\ \forall e\in E\right\}.

Evidently,

\displaystyle\im(P_{\textnormal{accept}})

\displaystyle\supseteq\Span\left\{|\psi\rangle^{\otimes N}:|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\in\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(G,r)\right\}

\displaystyle\Span\left\{\bigotimes_{e\in E}|\phi_{e}\rangle^{\otimes N}:|\phi_{e}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{e}\ \textnormal{and}\ \SR(|\phi_{e}\rangle)\leq r\ \forall e\in E\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\bigotimes_{e\in E}\Span\left\{|\phi_{e}\rangle^{\otimes N}:|\phi_{e}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{e}\ \textnormal{and}\ \SR(|\phi_{e}\rangle)\leq r\right\}

\widetilde{\bm{x}}
obtained by removing all occurrences of the letter “1” from
\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}
. Since this process results in a random string which has an LDS that is either equal to
\LDS(\bm{x})
or
\LDS(\bm{x})-1
, the right-hand side of
Eq.

21
is at least

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[\LDS(\widetilde{\bm{x}})\leq r-1\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{L=0}^{\lfloor\mu(1+t)\rfloor}\Pr_{\bm{y}\sim\textnormal{Unif}([d-1]^{L})}\left[\LDS(\bm{y})\leq r-1\right]\cdot\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|=L\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:L\leq\mu(1+t)}\Pr_{\bm{y}\sim\textnormal{Unif}([d-1]^{L})}\left[\LDS(\bm{y})\leq r-1\right]\right)\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|\leq\mu(1+t)\right]

(22)

where
\mu,t>0
are two real-valued parameters to be specified shortly and in the first line we have made use of the fact that, conditioned on the event
|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|=L
the random variable
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
is uniform on
\{2,3,\dots,d\}^{L}
. We will now specify parameters
\mu
and
t
which allow us to bound the right-hand side of
Eq.

22
from below. Set
\mu=\expct_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|=4\varepsilon^{2}N/m
where the second equality is due to the fact that the random variable
|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|
follows a Binomial distribution for
N
trials with success probability
4\varepsilon^{2}/m
. By a Chernoff bound (see
Lemma

B.1
) it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|>\mu(1+t)\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\mu t^{2}}{2+t}}

for any
t\geq 0
. With some foresight, set

t=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+8\alpha\mu}\right).

Then
\mu t^{2}=(2+t)\alpha
so that, by Bernoulli’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\mu t^{2}}{2+t}}\right)^{2m}

\displaystyle\geq 1-2m\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\mu t^{2}}{2+t}}=\frac{1}{2},

which implies that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[|\widetilde{\bm{x}}|\leq\mu(1+t)\right]\geq 2^{-1/(2m)}.

(23)

Next, we prove a lower bound of
2^{-1/(2m)}
for the term in parentheses in
Eq.

22
. Since the probability in this expression is monotonically non-increasing in
L
, it suffices to establish a lower bound on this quantity when
L:=\lfloor\mu(1+t)\rfloor
. By
Lemma

2.8
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{y}\sim\textnormal{Unif}([d-1]^{L})}\left[\LDS(\bm{y})\leq r-1\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\frac{(1+\frac{r-1}{d-1})\mathrm{e}^{2}L}{(r-1)^{2}}\right)^{r-1}\geq 1-\left(\frac{3\mathrm{e}^{2}L}{2(r-1)^{2}}\right)^{r-1}

where the second inequality follows from
d-1\geq 2(r-1)
. Assume for now that
3\mathrm{e}^{2}L/(2(r-1)^{2})\leq 1
. We will validate this helpful assumption momentarily. Then by Bernoulli’s inequality once again, we have

\displaystyle\left(1-\left(\frac{3\mathrm{e}^{2}L}{2(r-1)^{2}}\right)^{r-1}\right)^{2m}

\displaystyle\geq 1-2m\left(\frac{3\mathrm{e}^{2}L}{2(r-1)^{2}}\right)^{r-1}

(24)

so in order to establish a lower bound of
2^{-1/2m}
on the term in parentheses in
Eq.

22
it suffices to show that that the right-hand side of
Eq.

24
is at least
1/2
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2(r-1)^{2}}{3\mathrm{e}^{2}L}\right)^{r-1}\geq 4m.

(25)

The assumption that
r\geq 1+\alpha
is equivalent to
\log(4m)\leq r-1
. Hence, one sees by taking the logarithm of both sides that
Eq.

25
is implied by the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{2(r-1)^{2}}{3\mathrm{e}^{2}L}\geq\mathrm{e},

(26)

which, if true, also validates the aforementioned helpful assumption. Using the definitions of
L
,
\mu
, and
t
above we find
Eq.

26
is implied by

\displaystyle\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}N}{m}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\frac{32\alpha\varepsilon^{2}N}{m}}\leq\frac{2}{3\mathrm{e}^{3}}(r-1)^{2}.

(27)

Now since
\log(4)\leq\alpha\leq r-1
and we have assumed
N\leq mr^{2}/(400\alpha\varepsilon^{2})
, the left-hand side of
Eq.

27
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{100\log(4)}+\frac{r-1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(r-1)^{2}+\frac{2}{25}r^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2}}{100}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{10}\right)r-\frac{1}{2}

which is indeed at most
2(r-1)^{2}/(3\mathrm{e}^{3})
for every
r\geq 50
.
This proves
Eq.

27
and therefore the term in parentheses in
Eq.

22
is at least
2^{-1/(2m)}
. Combining with
Eq.

23
we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bm{x}\sim p^{\otimes N}}\left[\LDS(\widetilde{\bm{x}})\leq r-1\right]\geq 2^{-1/m}

which proves the claim.
∎

5
Testing products of bipartite states with constant Schmidt-rank

Our lower bound in
Section

4
, as well as the
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
lower bounds from Refs.
[
SW22
]
and
[
CWZ24
]
rely on analyzing states in the property
\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
, which forms a subset of states with the TTNS property. Recall that
\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
is defined to be the set of all states which are
n/2
-wise products of bipartite states on the subsystems
(1,2),(3,4),\dots,(n-1,n)
each with Schmidt-rank at most
r
. In this section, we prove
Theorem

5.4
, which shows that, unlike the case where
r\geq 2+\log n
, a number of copies on the order of
\sqrt{n}
suffices to test this class of states with one-sided error when
r=2
. As mentioned in
Section

1.1
, this improves upon the copy complexity obtained using a “test-by-learning” approach. Here, a number of copies on the order of
3^{n}
would be required to learn a full description of the state, assuming the subsystems are qutrits. Even provided the guarantee that the input state is product with respect to the
n/2
subsystems, learning a description of it to within small trace distance error
\varepsilon
would seem to require linear in
n
copies. This is because learning each bipartite state to within
\varepsilon
in trace distance uses
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
copies, but results in a worst-case error of
\sqrt{1-(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{n/2}}
in trace distance for the full state, which is close to one unless
\varepsilon=O(1/\sqrt{n})
.

5.1

(N-j,j)
and
\alpha_{1}(T)
denotes the number of occurrences of the letter “1” in
T
. Substituting into
Eq.

31
we have the bound

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\LDS(\bm{w})\leq 2\right]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N/2}\binom{N}{j}(j+1)(1-2t)^{N-j}t^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{N/2}\binom{N}{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}(k+1)^{2}t^{k}

(32)

Eq.

8

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N/2}N^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}(k+1)^{2}t^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2t\sum_{k=1}^{N}(k+1)^{2}(Nt)^{k-1}

.

\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{\{a,b,c\}}

\displaystyle\Pr[\bm{\lambda}_{\beta}=\lambda]

\displaystyle=\dim(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda})s_{\lambda}\big(\beta(a),\beta(b),\beta(c)\big)\quad\textnormal{for every}\ \lambda\vdash N.

[

there is a coupling
(\bm{\lambda}_{p},\bm{\lambda}_{q})
such that
\bm{\lambda}_{p}\succ\bm{\lambda}_{q}
with certainty.

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\vdash N

\displaystyle f(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda:\ell(\lambda)\leq 2}\dim(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda})s_{\lambda}\big(p(a),p(b),p(c)\big)

\displaystyle=\Pr[\ell(\bm{\lambda}_{p})\leq 2]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[\ell(\bm{\lambda}_{q})\leq 2]

\displaystyle=f(q).

The inequality
f(p)\geq f(q)
established above proves the claim.

6
Few-copy tests with perfect completeness

In this section, we give upper bounds and matching (or nearly so) lower bounds for testing rank, Schmidt-rank, or trees with perfect completeness when the tester is restricted to performing measurements on few copies at a time, possibly adaptively. Along the way, we address an open question raised in
[
HM13
,
MW16
,
SW22
]
by giving a closed-form solution to the error probability of the rank (or Schmidt-rank) test when performed on
r+1
copies of the unknown state, where
r
is the rank parameter.

6.1
Adaptivity offers no advantage for irreducible properties of pure states

In this section we prove that if
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
is an irreducible variety, then adaptivity offers no advantage for multi-round tests with one-sided error. We first require some background on algebraic varieties.

For our purposes, a
(projective) variety
is a subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
for which

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{\psi:f_{1}(|\psi\rangle)=\dots=f_{p}(|\psi\rangle)=0\}

for some collection of homogeneous polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{H}]
. We say that
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}

cut out
\mathcal{P}
. We say that a variety
\mathcal{P}
is
irreducible
if it cannot be written as the union of two proper subvarieties, i.e.
\mathcal{P}\neq\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}
for any varieties
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{R}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}
. It is a standard fact that if
\mathcal{P}
is an irreducible variety, and
\mathcal{Q}\subsetneq\mathcal{P}
is a proper subvariety, then
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{Q}
is Euclidean dense in
\mathcal{P}

[
Mum95
, Theorem 2.33]
. If
\mathcal{P}
is a variety, then

\displaystyle\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P}):=\{\psi^{\otimes\ell}:\psi\in\mathcal{P}\}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell})

is a variety, known as the
\ell
-th
Veronese embedding
of
\mathcal{P}
. Concretely,
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})
is cut out by the union of three sets of polynomials: the linear forms
\tilde{f}_{i,|x\rangle}:=f_{i}\circ(\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\langle x|):\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

as
|x\rangle
ranges over a basis of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell-1}
and
i
ranges over
[p]
(the evaluation of the linear form at a point
|y\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell}
is given by
\tilde{f}_{i,|x\rangle}(|y\rangle)=f_{i}((\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\langle x|)|y\rangle)
), the
2\times 2
minors cutting out the set of pure product states in
\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell})
, and the linear forms that define
\vee^{\ell}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\otimes\ell}
. If
\mathcal{P}
is an irreducible variety, then
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})
is also an irreducible variety. One way to see this is to note that if
\mathcal{R}\subsetneq\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})
is a proper subvariety, then
\mathcal{Q}:=\{\psi:\psi^{\otimes\ell}\in\mathcal{R}\}
is a proper subvariety of
\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem

1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
be an irreducible variety and
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
be a PC-optimal
\ell
-copy test for
\mathcal{P}
. Then among all adaptive
k
-round,
\ell
-copy tests for
\mathcal{P}
, the test
(P_{\textnormal{accept}})^{\otimes k}
is PC-optimal.

Remark 6.1
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is not an irreducible variety, then adaptive measurements may help. For example, consider
\mathcal{P}=\{|1\rangle\!\langle 1|,|+\rangle\!\langle+|\}\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
, where we define
|+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)
and
|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|1\rangle-|2\rangle)
. Then the PC-optimal 1-copy test is to simply output “accept.” However, consider the adaptive
3
-round,
1
-copy test which repeatedly performs
\{|1\rangle\!\langle 1|,|2\rangle\!\langle 2|\}
until outcome “2” is obtained, and then performs
\{|+\rangle\!\langle+|,|-\rangle\!\langle-|\}
in the remaining rounds, outputting “reject” if and only if outcome “
-

\psi
is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
-far from
\mathcal{P}
, then outcome “reject” will occur with probability at least
\frac{1}{4}
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Throughout, we let
P_{\textnormal{accept}}=\Proj\Span\{|\psi\rangle^{\otimes\ell}:\psi\in\mathcal{P}\}
. Let

be the

round conditioned on outcomes
x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1}
being obtained in the previous rounds. Fix a set of outcomes

. Without loss of generality, the final measurement conditioned on obtaining these outcomes is a two-outcome POVM which we denote by
\{P_{0}^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}},\mathds{1}-P_{0}^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}\}
. Define

\displaystyle\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P}):=\{\psi^{\otimes\ell}:\psi\in\mathcal{P}\}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad K:=\bigg\{\phi:|\phi\rangle\in\ker(P_{x_{1}})\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{k-2}\ker(P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{j}}_{x_{j+1}})\bigg\}.

Suppose that
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})\subseteq K
. Then clearly an optimal test outputs “reject” upon obtaining the outcomes

. If instead
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})
is not contained in

then we must have

\displaystyle\Tr(P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}_{0}\phi)=1\quad\textnormal{for all}\quad\phi\in\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})\backslash K.

(41)

Indeed, assume for contradiction this were false, i.e., there exists a state
\phi=\psi^{\otimes\ell}\in\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})\backslash K
for which
\Tr(P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}_{0}\phi)<1
. Then for the input state
\psi

in the test is non-zero, and therefore the conditional probability that the test accepts given these outcomes is well-defined. This probability is in turn equal to
\Tr(P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}_{0}\psi^{\otimes\ell})<1
. But then this contradicts the assumption of perfect completeness for the test. Now, since
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})\backslash K
is dense in
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})
(since

is a proper subvariety of
\nu_{\ell}(P)

\Tr(P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}_{0}\phi)=1
for all
\phi\in\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})

of
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})

is contained in the

. It follows that
P_{\textnormal{accept}}\preceq P^{x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1}}_{0}
. Thus, the measurement

P_{\textnormal{accept}}

performs at least as well as

in the

round.

We have shown that an optimal measurement in the

round is to simply output “reject” if
\nu_{\ell}(\mathcal{P})\subseteq K

otherwise. Since there are only two possibilities, we can coarse-grain the preceding
(k-1)
-round,
\ell

\{Q_{\textnormal{accept}},Q_{\textnormal{reject}}\}
such that if the input state has the property then the outcome “accept” always occurs (i.e. it has perfect completeness). Then on input state
\psi
, the probability to output “accept” is given by

\displaystyle\Tr(Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes\ell(k-1)})\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes\ell}).

Thus, it is optimal to choose a
(k-1)
-round,
\ell
-copy measurement
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}
which accepts every state in
\mathcal{P}
and has minimal probability of accepting a state not in
\mathcal{P}
. By induction on

, an optimal such
(k-1)
-round,
\ell
-copy measurement is given by
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}=(P_{\textnormal{accept}})^{\otimes k-1}
(the base case

is trivial). Thus, an optimal
k
-round measurement is given by
(P_{\textnormal{accept}})^{\otimes k}.
This completes the proof.
∎

6.2

-copy tests

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of dimension

Recall that the

\displaystyle\beta(\varepsilon)=1-\min\left\{g_{{\lfloor z\rfloor}}\left(\varepsilon,r,\frac{1-\varepsilon}{r}\right),g_{\lceil z\rceil}\left(\varepsilon,r,\frac{\varepsilon}{\lceil z\rceil}\right),g_{d-r}\left(\varepsilon,r,\frac{\varepsilon}{d-r}\right)\right\},

where

\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\colon\mathcal{H}^{\otimes N}\to\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H})

\Tr(Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes N})=\Tr(\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\psi^{\otimes N})
for any pure state
\psi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
. Hence, using the assumption that
r(g)\mathcal{P}r(g)^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}
for every
g\in G
we have

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq\Tr(\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}(r(g)\psi r(g)^{\dagger})^{\otimes N}).

for any
\psi\in\mathcal{P}
and
g\in G
. Let
Q_{\textnormal{accept}}^{\prime}:=\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
and
\mu(\cdot)
denote the Haar measure on the group
G
. The above implies

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}}\int_{g\in G}\Tr(r(g^{-1})^{\otimes N}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}^{\prime}r(g)^{\otimes N}\psi^{\otimes N})\mathrm{d}\mu(g)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\psi\in\mathcal{P}}\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes N})

where
P_{\textnormal{accept}}:=\int_{g\in G}(r(g^{-1}))^{\otimes N}Q_{\textnormal{accept}}^{\prime}r(g)^{\otimes N}\mathrm{d}\mu(g)\in\mathrm{L}(\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}))
. By the invariance of the Haar measure under left- or right- multiplication by a group element, it follows
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
commutes with the action
r(g)^{\otimes N}
for any
g\in G
and thus, by
Fact

A.1
, it holds that
P_{\textnormal{accept}}
is of the desired form. (In this case, the operators acting on the multiplicity spaces are just one-dimensional, i.e., scalars.) Next, note that if
\psi\in\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H})
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}
then
r(g)\psi r(g)^{\dagger}
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}
for any
g\in G
, by the unitary invariance of the Schatten 1-norm. This implies that

\displaystyle b\geq\Tr(Q_{\textnormal{accept}}^{\prime}(r(g)\psi r(g)^{\dagger})^{\otimes N})

for any
g\in G
and
\psi
which is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}
. Then a similar reasoning to the above establishes that
b\geq\sup_{\psi\ \varepsilon\textnormal{-far from}\ \mathcal{P}}\Tr(P_{\textnormal{accept}}\psi^{\otimes N})
.
∎

The final ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

2.2
is the following.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})
be the projector onto the symmetric subspace
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
. It holds that

\displaystyle(P^{(A)}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes P^{(B)}_{\lambda_{2}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\Pi_{\lambda_{1}}\quad\textnormal{for every}\ \lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\vdash N,\ell(\lambda_{1})\leq d_{A},\ell(\lambda_{2})\leq d_{B}

(55)

where, for each
j\in\{A,B\}
, we let
P^{(j)}_{\lambda}
denote the projector onto the
\lambda^{\text{th}}

(\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{j}))
-irrep in
\mathcal{H}_{j}^{\otimes N}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})
is a projector onto the
(\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B}))
-irrep isomorphic to
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
within
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})
.

Proof.

The space
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
is a representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
via the action
|v\rangle\mapsto(W_{\pi}\otimes W_{\pi})(U_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes U_{B}^{\otimes N})|v\rangle
for any
U_{A}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
,
U_{B}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, and
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
. By Schur-Weyl duality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\mu}\bigoplus_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}\mathbb{C}^{k_{\mu}(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\mu}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

(56)

as representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, where in the second line we decomposed
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}^{\prime}}
into irreps of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, and
k_{\mu}(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
are the Kronecker coefficients. Under this isomorphism, the projection onto the symmetric subspace is the projection onto terms in this direct sum where
\mu=(N)
, while the projection
P^{(A)}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes P^{(B)}_{\lambda_{2}}
is onto the terms in the direct sum with indices
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}=\lambda_{1}
, and
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=\lambda_{2}
. Since these two projections are both block diagonal with respect to the orthogonal direct sum in
Eq.

56
, their product is the projection onto the intersection of their images, given by the subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
isomorphic to

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{k_{(N)}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{(N)}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{B}).

(57)

We first note that
k_{(N)}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\delta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
. Let
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})
denote the trivial representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
within
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}}
, and let
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}},\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})^{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}
denote the set of linear maps in
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}},\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})
which commute with the action of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
. By Schur’s Lemma, we have
\dim(\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}},\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})^{\mathfrak{S}_{N}})=\delta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
. Since
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})\cong\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}},\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}})^{\mathfrak{S}_{N}}
as representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, this shows that
k_{(N)}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\delta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{(N)}
is one-dimensional, the subspace in
Eq.

57
is isomorphic to
\delta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
as a representation of
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
. This completes the proof.
∎

The above proof also shows

\displaystyle\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

(
5
)

as representations of
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\times\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, as mentioned in
Section

2.3.2
. We can now prove
Theorem

2.2
, which we restate below for convenience.

Theorem

2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a property of pure states which is invariant under local unitary transformations, as in
Eq.

3
. Weak Schur Sampling performed locally, either on
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}
or
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N}
, followed by classical post-processing, is an optimal measurement for
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

A.2
and
Eq.

5
, without loss of generality a given test is of the form
P_{\textnormal{accept}}=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}
for some
\alpha_{\lambda}\in[0,1]
, where
\Pi_{\lambda}
is the projection onto the
\lambda^{\text{th}}
term in the direct sum in
Eq.

5
. Then setting
R^{(j)}_{\textnormal{accept}}:=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}P^{(j)}_{\lambda}
for either
j=A
or
j=B
, where
P^{(j)}_{\lambda}
is as in
Lemma

A.3
, we have

\displaystyle(R^{(A)}_{\textnormal{accept}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{\lambda\vdash N}\alpha_{\lambda}P^{(A)}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\alpha_{\lambda}(P^{(A)}_{\lambda}\otimes P^{(B)}_{\lambda^{\prime}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle(\textnormal{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lem:double_sw_projectors}})

\displaystyle=P_{\textnormal{accept}}

and similarly for the measurement operator
\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes R^{(B)}_{\textnormal{accept}}
.
∎

Finally, we prove
Theorem

2.4
, restated below for convenience.

Theorem

2.4

(PC-optimal Schmidt-rank test)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces such that
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{j})=d_{j}
for each
j\in\{A,B\}
,
N
and
r\leq d_{A}\leq d_{B}
be positive integers, and
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\otimes N})
denote the projector onto the symmetric subspace
\vee^{N}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.

1.

It holds that

\displaystyle(\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r}^{(B)})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{\leq r}^{(A)}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}=\Proj\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:\SR(|\phi\rangle)\leq r\}

is a strongly PC-optimal test for
\mathsf{SR}(r)\subseteq\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.

2.

The copy complexity of testing
\mathsf{SR}(r)
with one-sided error is
\Theta(r^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

We begin with the first item. We claim either one of
\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N}
or
\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r}
is a PC-optimal test for
\mathsf{SR}(r)
, which implies

\displaystyle\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\Proj\Span\{|\phi\rangle^{\otimes N}:\SR(|\phi\rangle)\leq r\}

by
Lemma

2.1
, and similarly for
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}(\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
. To see this, suppose for contradiction that there is a test
E
which has perfect completeness and accepts with probability strictly less than the acceptance probability for the test
\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N}
on some state
\psi\notin\mathsf{SR}(r)
. By
Theorem

2.2
we can take
E=E_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N}
for some
E_{A}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\otimes N})
. Then, if
\rho_{A}[\psi]
denotes the reduced state of
\psi
on
A
we have

\displaystyle\Tr(E_{A}\rho_{A}[\psi]^{\otimes N})

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((E_{A}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\psi^{\otimes N}\right)<\Tr\left((\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\psi^{\otimes N}\right)=\Tr(\Pi_{\leq r}\rho_{A}[\psi]^{\otimes N})

But then since
E_{A}
is a test for
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
with perfect completeness, this contradicts the strong PC-optimality of
\Pi_{\leq r}
for the property
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
, which is Proposition 6.1 in
[
OW15
]
. It remains to show that
\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
commutes with both
\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N}
and
\mathds{1}_{A}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r}
. Using
Lemma

A.3
we have

\displaystyle(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{1}:\ell(\lambda_{1})\leq r}\ \sum_{\lambda_{2}:\ell(\lambda_{2})\leq d_{B}}(P_{\lambda_{1}}\otimes P_{\lambda_{2}})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda:\ell(\lambda)\leq r}\Pi_{\lambda}

where the first line uses the definition of the rank test operator in
Eq.

7
and in the second line the projections
\Pi_{\lambda}
, defined explicitly in
Lemma

A.3
as projectors onto irreps, have orthogonal images. Since a product of orthogonal projection operators commutes if and only if their product is also an orthogonal projection operator, we have shown the desired commutation relation for the operator
\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{B}}^{\otimes N}
. A similar argument applies to the operator
\mathds{1}_{d_{A}}^{\otimes N}\otimes\Pi_{\leq r}
.

For the second item note that, for testing
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
at distance
\varepsilon
, the rank test
\Pi_{\leq r}
of O’Donnell and Wright succeeds using
O(r^{2}/\varepsilon)
copies, and also requires
\Omega(r^{2}/\varepsilon)
copies to be successful
[
OW15
, Lemma 6.2]
. Then, following the discussion on trace distances in
Section

2.3.3
, if
\psi
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{SR}(r)
the reduced state
\rho_{A}[\psi]
on
A
is
\varepsilon^{2}
-far from
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
and

\displaystyle\Tr\left((\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}\psi^{\otimes N}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\Pi_{\leq r}\rho_{A}[\psi]^{\otimes N})

is at most
1/3
by taking
N=O(r^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
, by the upper bound for rank testing. On the other hand, assume for contradiction that
(\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}
succeeds in testing
\mathsf{SR}(r)
at distance
\varepsilon
using
o(r^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
copies. Then, if
\rho
is
\varepsilon^{2}
-far from
\mathsf{Rank}(r)
the canonical purification
\phi^{\rho}
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{SR}(r)
and we have

\displaystyle\Tr(\Pi_{\leq r}\rho)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((\Pi_{\leq r}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}^{\otimes N})\Pi_{\textnormal{Sym}}(\phi^{\rho})^{\otimes N}\right)

is at most
1/3
by taking
N=o(r^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
. But this contradicts the lower bound for rank testing.
∎

A.2
Low-rank approximation for bipartite pure states

In this section we provide an elementary proof of
Proposition

2.3
. We will use the following version of the Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem
[
EY36
,
Mir60
]
for low-rank approximation with respect to unitarily invariant norms.

Theorem A.4

(
[
LS20
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be finite-dimensional Euclidean vector spaces and let
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
be a unitarily invariant norm on
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Suppose
A\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
has a singular value decomposition
A=\sum_{j=1}^{\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle v_{j}|
. For any positive integer
r\leq\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
, the matrix
A_{r}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle v_{j}|
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert A-A_{r}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert

for any
B\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
with rank at most
r
.

Taking
A:=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle v_{j}|
with
\lambda_{j}
,
|u_{j}\rangle
, and
|v_{j}\rangle
as defined in
Eq.

6
, the maximization in the statement of the proposition is equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\max\{|\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)|^{2}:B\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1}),\ \rank(B)\leq r,\ \left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=1\}

(58)

with the optimal solution of the original maximization being related to the matrix which attains the maximum in
Eq.

58
through a vectorization of this matrix. It thereby remains to show that
\widetilde{B}:=(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}^{2})^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle v_{j}|
is an optimal solution. To this end, consider the optimization

\displaystyle\min\{\left\lVert A-X\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}:X\in\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1}),\ \rank(X)\leq r\}.

(59)

By
Theorem

A.4
we have that the maximum is attained at
X=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\!\langle v_{j}|
. Now, suppose by way of contradiction that there exists an operator
B^{\prime}
satisfying
\left\lVert B^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=1
,
\rank(B^{\prime})\leq r
, and
\Tr(A^{\dagger}B^{\prime})=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
for some
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
r>\gamma:=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}^{2}}
. Then we must have

\displaystyle\left\lVert A-\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}B^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\gamma^{2}-2\gamma r

\displaystyle<\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\gamma^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=r+1}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{j}^{2}.

But this contradicts
X
being an optimal solution to the optimization in
Eq.

59
. Thus, we have that
|\Tr(A^{\dagger}B^{\prime})|\leq\gamma
for any choice of
B^{\prime}
satisfying the constraints in
Eq.

58
. This concludes the proof, since
|\Tr(A^{\dagger}\widetilde{B})|^{2}=\gamma^{2}
.

A.3
Faithful TTNS approximations

In this section we include a proof of
Lemma

3.2
. The proof uses the following lemma.

Lemma A.5

(Lemma 4.123 in
[
Hac19
]
)
.

Let
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
be an arbitrary vector in the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and

be a sequence of orthogonal projection operators acting on
\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle{\left\lVert(\mathds{1}-\prod_{i=1}^{N}\nolimits P_{i})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\left\lVert(\mathds{1}-P_{i})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}.

We restate the theorem for convenience. Here,
V
and
E
refer to the vertices and edges, respectively, of a tree graph
G
.

Lemma

3.2
.

Let
|\psi\rangle\in\bigotimes_{v\in V}\mathcal{H}_{v}
be an arbitrary pure
state on
n=|V|
sites. For each
e\in E
, let
L^{(e)},R^{(e)}\subseteq[n]
be
the bipartition of the vertices induced by removing the edge

e
. Suppose further that
|\psi\rangle
has the Schmidt decomposition

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}{\lambda_{j}^{(e)}}\ |a^{(e)}_{j}\rangle_{L^{(e)}}\otimes|b^{(e)}_{j}\rangle_{R^{(e)}}

with Schmidt coefficients
\lambda_{1}^{(e)}\geq\lambda_{2}^{(e)}\geq\dots
for every

, and that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}\leq 1.

Then there exists a state

for which

\displaystyle|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|\geq 1-\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}.

Proof.

We claim there exists a (potentially unnormalized) vector
|\phi\rangle\in\bigotimes_{v\in V}\mathcal{H}_{v}
such that the following hold:

\mathrm{SR}_{e}(|\phi\rangle)\leq r\quad\forall e\in E

{\left\lVert|\phi\rangle-|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}

The theorem then follows since for any

and normalized vector

,

{\left\lVert|\phi\rangle-|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}\leq\delta

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|}{\lVert|\phi\rangle\rVert_{2}}\geq\frac{1+{\lVert|\phi\rangle\rVert}_{2}^{2}-\delta}{2\ \lVert|\phi\rangle\rVert_{2}}\geq 1-\delta.

It remains to prove the two properties above. But these are implied by
[
Hac19

. We supply that argument in the language of this paper here for the interested reader, making use of the definitions established in
Section

3
. Suppose the tree
G
has depth

and root node

be the subgraph corresponding to the vertex
v
and all descendants of
v
. Note that
G(v_{\text{root}})=G
, and for any
v\in V
we have that
G(v)
is a tree. Also, let
h(v)\in\{0,\dots,D-1\}
be the height of the vertex
v\in V
, and for each

define

\displaystyle L_{j}:=\{v\in V:h(v)=j\}

i.e., the subset of vertices at height
j

is the set of all leaves.) Consider a fixed vertex

and edge

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{0}\dots\Gamma_{j-1}P^{(v)}_{u}|\psi^{\prime}\rangle

projection operator to the subsystem
G(u)
yields a vector whose Schmidt-rank is at most
r
. Thus,

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle=\Gamma_{0}\dots\Gamma_{j-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\beta_{j}|r_{j}\rangle_{G(u)}\otimes|\ell_{j}\rangle_{G(u)\backslash V}

for some real coefficients
\beta_{j}
and collections of orthogonal vectors
\{|r_{j}\rangle\}
and
\{|\ell_{j}\rangle\}
. Now note that, by construction, for any
i<j
it holds that
\Gamma_{i}=P\otimes Q
for some orthogonal projection operators
P,Q
acting solely within the subspaces corresponding to
G(u)
,
V\backslash G(u)
, respectively. Hence,
\mathrm{SR}_{e}(|\phi\rangle)\leq r
for the edge
(u,v)
, and this argument applies to any edge in the tree.

The second desired property follows from the fact that we have

\displaystyle{\left\lVert|\phi\rangle-|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle={\left\lVert(\mathds{1}-\prod_{j=0}^{D-1}\nolimits\Gamma_{j})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(u,v)\in E}{\left\lVert(\mathds{1}-P^{(v)}_{u})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle(\textnormal{\lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lem:projector_distance}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{j\geq r+1}(\lambda_{j}^{(e)})^{2}

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
lower bound at constant bond dimension

In this section we prove a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
for testing
\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
at constant
r
and distance
\varepsilon\in(0,1/\sqrt{2}]
, even with two-sided error. The proof is essentially the same as that for the lower bound for testing MPS given in
[
SW22
]
, but we include it for completeness. Throughout we assume
r\geq 2
and
d-1\geq 2(r-1)
. Assume
\varepsilon\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
, set
\theta=8\varepsilon^{2}/n
, and for each
j\in[n/2]
let
|\phi_{j}^{(0)}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a bipartite state with Schmidt coefficients
\sqrt{1-\theta},\sqrt{\theta/(d-1)},\dots,\sqrt{\theta/(d-1)}
and
|\phi_{j}^{(1)}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a bipartite state with Schmidt coefficients
\sqrt{1-\theta},\sqrt{\theta/(r-1)},\dots,\sqrt{\theta/(r-1)},0,\dots,0
. We clearly have
\phi^{(1)}:=\bigotimes_{j\in[n/2]}\phi_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
. On the other hand,
\phi^{(0)}:=\bigotimes_{j\in[n/2]}\phi_{j}^{(0)}
is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
. This is because the overlap between
\phi^{(0)}
and the best approximation to this state in
\mathsf{Prod}_{2}(n,r)
is, by
Proposition

2.3
and the assumption that
d-1\geq 2(r-1)
, at most
(1-\theta/2)^{n/2}\leq 1-\varepsilon^{2}
where the inequality follows by bounding the remainder term in the Taylor Series of
(1-x)^{q}
to deduce that
(1-x)^{q}\leq 1-qx/2
for all
x\leq 1/q
. Hence, a successful test with two-sided error distinguishes between these two states with constant probability of success, given some number of copies
N
of the unknown state prepared in either
\phi^{(0)}
or
\phi^{(1)}
. Furthermore, this argument has no dependence on the choice of Schmidt vectors for the states
|\phi_{j}^{(0)}\rangle
and
|\phi_{j}^{(1)}\rangle
. So the test also distinguishes between

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{j\in[n/2]}(U_{j}\otimes V_{j})\phi_{1}(U_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{j}^{\dagger})\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\bigotimes_{j\in[n/2]}(U_{j}\otimes V_{j}^{\dagger})\gamma_{1}(U_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes V_{j}^{\dagger})

Lemma B.1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}
be independent Bernoulli random variables and define
\bm{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}
and
\mu=\expct\bm{X}
. It holds that

\Pr[\bm{X}>\mu(1+t)]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\mu t^{2}}{2+t}}

(64)

for any
t\geq 0
.

Fact B.2
.

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1)^{2}x^{n-1}=\frac{x^{2}-3x+4}{(1-x)^{3}}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|x|<1
.

Fact B.4
.

Let
N
be a positive integer. For any integer
k\geq 1
it holds that
\sum_{j=k}^{N}\binom{N}{j}x^{j}\leq\sqrt{e}N^{k}x^{k}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0\leq x\leq 1/(2N)
.

Proof.

Observe

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{N}\binom{N}{j}x^{j}

\displaystyle\leq N^{k}x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{N-k}\frac{N^{j}x^{j}}{(j+k)!}\leq N^{k}x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{N^{j}x^{j}}{j!}\leq N^{k}x^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)^{j}}{j!}=\sqrt{e}N^{k}x^{k}

as required.
