Application of refined ICU nursing care in the treatment of severe acute pancreatitis and its impact on clinical outcomes
Authors: Yang Zhang, Yanyan Jiang, Zeng Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSevere acute pancreatitis (SAP) is associated with a relatively high mortality rate. Conventional intensive care unit (ICU) nursing care has limitations in symptom control and complication prevention, and the effectiveness of refined ICU nursing care still requires further empirical validation. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the application of refined ICU nursing care in patients with SAP.MethodsThis was a prospective randomized controlled trial. Eighty-four patients with SAP admitted to the ICU of our hospital were randomly assigned to a control group or an observation group using the random number table method. The control group received conventional nursing care (monitoring vital signs, maintaining effective circulation, gastrointestinal decompression, nutritional support, and medication guidance). The observation group received refined ICU nursing care in addition to conventional care. Core measures included: (1) establishing a specialized nursing team with training and formulating individualized care plans based on patients’ conditions; (2) dynamically monitoring vital signs and organ functions and strengthening complication prevention (e.g., preventing pressure ulcers and monitoring excretion); (3) providing disease education via videos and manuals; (4) offering targeted psychological support (sharing successful cases and encouraging family involvement); and (5) implementing staged dietary management (fasting with nutritional support during the acute phase, followed by gradual transition to a normal diet after symptom relief). Data were collected using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), the World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment Scale (WHOQOL-BREF), as well as blood and urine amylase tests. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Independent-samples or paired-samples t-tests were applied for continuous variables, and the χ² test was used for categorical variables.ResultsThe nursing effectiveness rate in the observation group (97.62%) was higher than in the control group (80.95%) (P < 0.05). The observation group exhibited shorter times to symptom resolution (fever, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting), reduced length of hospital stay, lower blood and urinary amylase levels, lower SAS and SDS scores, and higher WHOQOL-BREF scores across all dimensions (P < 0.05). The overall incidence of complications was lower in the observation group (7.14%) than in the control group (23.81%) (P < 0.05), while patient satisfaction was higher (95.24% vs. 73.81%, P < 0.05). The readmission rate did not differ significantly between the control group (7.14%, 3/42) and the observation group (11.90%, 5/42) (P = 0.713).ConclusionRefined ICU nursing care can shorten symptom resolution time and hospital stay, reduce amylase levels, alleviate negative emotions, lower complication rates, and improve patient satisfaction in SAP patients. However, as this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, further confirmation through multi-center, large-sample trials is warranted.Keywords: Acute pancreatitis, Intensive care unit, Nursing care, Amylase, Quality of life

Introduction

Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) is a pancreatic inflammatory disease characterized by critical illness and high mortality, and it has long been a major focus and challenge in the field of critical care medicine [1, 2]. Patients with SAP often develop multiple organ dysfunction and severe metabolic disturbances, requiring immediate admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for comprehensive monitoring and supportive management [1, 2]. Despite significant advances in treatment technologies and critical care practices in recent years, the mortality rate of SAP remains high [2], underscoring the limitations of current nursing strategies in addressing this complex condition.

Traditional ICU nursing models primarily emphasize condition monitoring and routine interventions, such as nutritional support and organ function maintenance, in the management of SAP [3, 4]. However, the clinical condition of SAP patients is often highly complex and variable, frequently accompanied by multiple comorbidities, which makes it difficult for conventional nursing models to meet patients’ individualized needs [4, 5].

Refined nursing care is a model rooted in medical science and integrated with humanistic principles. Its core lies in the comprehensive assessment of patients’ physiological, psychological, and social needs, thereby enabling the implementation of personalized nursing interventions [6]. Compared with traditional nursing approaches, refined ICU nursing care places greater emphasis on detail and precision throughout the entire nursing process—from initial assessment upon admission, through treatment, to follow-up care. This model tailors interventions to the specific disease type, treatment goals, and rehabilitation potential of patients, aiming to improve treatment efficacy while enhancing patient comfort and satisfaction with care [6–9]; Evidence also supports the use of refined nursing in critical care for neonatal pneumonia complicated by respiratory failure, showing improvements in clinical status, blood gas parameters, and vital signs while reducing adverse events [10]. Furthermore, refined nursing interventions have been shown to improve postoperative outcomes in elderly patients with hip fractures, alleviating delirium and pain [11].

Taken together, although refined ICU nursing has demonstrated notable benefits in the management of other critical conditions, its specific effectiveness and value in the management of SAP remain underexplored, with a lack of direct and systematic evidence. Therefore, this study aims to compare the effects of refined ICU nursing care and conventional nursing care in patients with SAP through a prospective, randomized controlled trial. We hypothesize that refined ICU nursing care will improve multiple clinical outcome indicators in SAP patients, providing a more comprehensive and effective nursing strategy for this population.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Third Clinical Medical College of the Three Gorges University, Gezhouba Center Hospital of Sinopharm. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study subjects

This was a prospective randomized controlled trial. The required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (University of Düsseldorf, Germany) with the following parameters: power = 0.80, α = 0.05, effect size = 0.70, and number of groups = 2. The calculation indicated that a minimum of 68 subjects was required. Considering a 15% attrition rate, the final sample size was set at 84.

Eighty-four patients with SAP admitted to the ICU of the Third Clinical Medical College of the Three Gorges University, Gezhouba Center Hospital of Sinopharm between February 2023 and October 2023 were enrolled. They were randomly allocated into a control group (n = 42) and an observation group (n = 42) using the random number table method.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for SAP [12]; (2) aged 18–70 years; (3) conscious, with intact memory and normal cognitive function, able to fully express their feelings; (4) in relatively stable condition; (5) possessing a smartphone and familiar with using WeChat; (6) with good communication skills; and (7) providing informed consent and voluntarily participating. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients in critical condition or unable to care for themselves; (2) those with communication disorders, cognitive impairments, or psychiatric histories; (3) pregnancy-related pancreatitis; (4) severe respiratory failure, circulatory failure, coma, or other irreversible critical conditions with an expected survival of < 7 days; (5) severe cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, or hematological diseases, immunodeficiency, malignancies, epilepsy, etc.; (6) long-term use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressants; (7) presence of a cardiac pacemaker or defibrillator; and (8) participation in another clinical trial within the past 6 months.

In the control group, there were 24 males and 18 females, aged 30–69 years, with a mean age of 49.43 ± 10.20 years. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score was 12.15 ± 2.03; 13 patients had comorbid hypertension, and 10 had diabetes. In the observation group, there were 26 males and 16 females, aged 29–70 years, with a mean age of 49.10 ± 10.61 years. The APACHE II score was 12.46 ± 2.47; 15 patients had hypertension, and 11 had diabetes. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, age, or baseline characteristics (P > 0.05).

Randomization and blinding

The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistical analyst using SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), producing random numbers from 1 to 84. Eligible patients were numbered consecutively at enrollment and randomly assigned to the control or observation group in a 1:1 ratio. The allocation sequence was sealed by the statistician and revealed by the researchers only at the time of intervention. To minimize assessment bias, a double-blind design was applied. Patients were unaware of their group assignments, and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation. All data were anonymized using patient ID numbers. Subjective assessments were completed independently by patients, sealed in envelopes, and processed by blinded assessors.

Nursing interventions

Control group: Patients in the control group received conventional nursing care. Their conditions were assessed and routine examinations were performed. Symptomatic treatment was provided, with close monitoring of physical indicators and clinical changes after treatment. Heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and body temperature were recorded every 4 h. Effective circulation was maintained through intravenous fluid replacement, with infusion rates adjusted as necessary. Blood and urine amylase levels were measured daily. Intra-abdominal pressure was monitored via bladder pressure measurement (normal range ≤ 12 mmHg): every 12 h during the first 3 days after admission; every 6 h if intra-abdominal pressure exceeded 15 mmHg; and once daily from days 4–7 if the pressure was ≤ 12 mmHg. Continuous gastrointestinal decompression was performed, and the characteristics and volume of drainage fluid were assessed every 12 h. Nutritional support was provided as prescribed. Medication guidance was given, and adverse drug reactions were monitored. Oral care (saline rinses) was performed twice daily. Patients were repositioned every 2 h on a standard mattress. Urine output was recorded, and vomitus and stool characteristics were observed, though not at fixed intervals. Any abnormalities were promptly reported to physicians. Patient and family inquiries were passively answered, and dietary guidance (e.g., low-fat diet) was provided according to medical advice. Nursing care continued until discharge.

Observation group: Patients in the observation group received refined ICU nursing care in addition to the conventional care described above. The specific implementation steps were as follows:

An ICU refined nursing team was established, consisting of one head nurse (with > 5 years of ICU nursing experience) and four primary nurses (with > 3 years of SAP nursing experience). Prior to the intervention, a 3-day specialized training program was conducted, covering SAP condition assessment, intra-abdominal pressure monitoring, and individualized dietary planning. Within 24 h of admission, the team developed a personalized nursing plan based on each patient’s APACHE II score, pancreatic CT findings, and comorbidities. The plan was adjusted weekly according to changes in the patient’s condition. To ensure quality, nursing records for three randomly selected days each week were reviewed to verify adherence to key procedures. An “implementation compliance rate” was calculated (compliance rate = actual implementation times/required implementation times × 100%), with a target of ≥ 90%. Retraining was provided within 24 h if compliance fell below this standard. Each month, three nurses from each group were assessed on-site to ensure adherence to operational standards. Nurses were also interviewed regarding implementation difficulties, and timely adjustments were made to optimize the plan.

Health education: Health education was provided once weekly, with each session lasting 30 min. Patients’ medical histories were reviewed, and targeted education was delivered according to their conditions. Multiple formats, including videos and brochures, were used to introduce information about acute pancreatitis, covering treatment methods, nursing plans, symptoms, causes, precautions, and prognosis. Questions from patients and their families were addressed to dispel doubts and enhance disease awareness. After each session, 10 min were reserved for interactive discussion. A “question-feedback method” was used to evaluate knowledge acquisition, ensuring a mastery rate of ≥ 60% for core knowledge.

Psychological nursing: Psychological interviews were conducted twice weekly (20 min each), during which three cases of successfully treated ICU SAP patients from the hospital were shared. The importance of maintaining a positive mental state in inhibiting pancreatic enzyme secretion and alleviating pain was explained to help patients reduce psychological stress. Daily communication sessions (15 min each) were held to explore the causes of emotional fluctuations and provide timely counseling. For patients who felt nervous or fearful due to medical procedures, targeted explanations and reassurance were offered to foster a positive attitude toward temporary difficulties. Patients who expressed dissatisfaction with limited visiting times and restricted visitor numbers were given appropriate explanations, emphasizing that ICU visiting restrictions were protective measures. To strengthen family support, short videos or audio recordings prepared in advance by family members were played for patients, encouraging treatment cooperation and keeping them informed about their families’ situations. Patients were encouraged to express their feelings, concerns, and difficulties, which were analyzed promptly, followed by appropriate counseling interventions such as deep-breathing exercises, meditation, positive feedback, and emotional support. Pain intensity and duration were carefully monitored, and the causes were explained. Interventions such as relaxation training, deep breathing, and soft music were provided before analgesic administration, with medications given when necessary.

Nutritional intervention: Dietitians conducted comprehensive assessments of patients’ nutritional needs, including dietary habits, weight, symptoms, activity levels, and functional status, and developed individualized nutrition plans. Patients initially received warm water and rhubarb infusion. On the following day, enteral nutrition was initiated with a starting volume of 500 mL/day at a rate of 25 mL/h. The volume was gradually increased to 2,000 mL/day with a drip rate of 100 mL/h, depending on tolerance. Gastric residual volume was evaluated every 4 h, and gastrointestinal function recovery was assessed via auscultation. Enteral feeding was suspended if gastric residual volume reached ≥ 200 mL. Nurses documented the infusion method, rate, and volume daily, and closely observed for adverse events such as gastric retention or diarrhea. Any abnormalities were promptly reported to the dietitian, who jointly discussed whether to adjust or replace the enteral nutrition preparation. Prior to discharge, patients were provided with a printed dietary plan emphasizing fat restriction (≤ 20 g/day), intake of vitamin-rich foods (e.g., spinach, apples), and high-quality proteins (e.g., fish, tofu). Patients were also instructed to maintain daily dietary records during home care. Nutritional support was continued until discharge.Follow-up: All patients were followed up for 3 months after discharge through outpatient visits, telephone calls, and communication via an online platform. Follow-up occurred once every 2 weeks in the first month, and once monthly during the second and third months. Changes in patients’ conditions were assessed, and guidance and assistance were provided to address problems encountered, with the aim of improving patients’ self-management ability.

Observation indicators

Nursing effectiveness

The nursing effectiveness of the two groups was compared. Evaluation criteria were as follows: Significantly effective: abdominal pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score = 0, intra-abdominal pressure ≤ 12 mmHg, normalization of amylase levels, and resolution of pancreatic edema on ultrasound. Effective: VAS score reduction ≥ 50%, intra-abdominal pressure reduction ≥ 30% (or 13–15 mmHg), amylase reduction ≥ 50%, and pancreatic edema/necrotic foci reduction ≥ 30% on ultrasound. Ineffective: No improvement in clinical symptoms and no improvement in pancreatic function according to relevant test results. The overall nursing effectiveness rate = (number of significantly effective + effective cases)/total number of cases × 100%. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation.

Symptom resolution time

The time to resolution of fever, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, as well as the length of hospital stay, were compared between the two groups.

Amylase levels

Before and after nursing care, venous blood and 24-hour urine samples were collected from both groups. Serum and urinary amylase levels were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer.

Negative emotions

After the intervention, negative emotions were assessed using the Chinese versions of the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS, revised by Professor Zhang Ming, 1999) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS, revised by Wang Zhengyu). Each scale contains 20 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4 points), with total scores ranging from 20 to 80. Standardized scores were calculated by multiplying the raw score by 1.25 and rounding down to the nearest integer. Higher scores indicated greater severity of negative emotions. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.83 for SAS and for 0.82 for SDS.

Quality of life

Quality of life after nursing care were assessed using the Chinese version of the World Health Organization Quality-of-Life Brief Scale (WHOQOL-BREF) (released by the WHO Collaborating Center in China in 2000). The scale comprises four dimensions: physical, psychological, social relationships, and environment. Each item is scored on a 5-point scale, with a total score of 100. Higher scores indicatebetter quality of life. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.78.

Incidence of complications

Complications, including gastrointestinal bleeding, lower extremity venous thrombosis, infections, and pressure ulcers, was recorded during nursing care in both groups.

Satisfaction with care

Patient satisfaction was assessed using a self-designed questionnaire covering nursing skills, service quality, environment, and health guidance. Scores > 90 indicated “very satisfied,” 70–90 indicated “generally satisfied,” and < 70 indicated “unsatisfied.” The satisfaction rate was calculated as: (very satisfied cases + generally satisfied cases)/total cases × 100%. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire was 0.87, with a test-retest reliability of 0.867. All 84 questionnaires were distributed and collected on-site, yielding in a 100% response rate.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normal distribution. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation . Paired-sample t-tests were used for within-group comparisons, and independent-sample t-tests were used for between-group comparisons. Categorical data were expressed as percentages (%) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Readmission rates

During the 3-month follow-up, 3 of 42 patients in the control group were readmitted for mild SAP recurrence, yielding a readmission rate of 7.14% (3/42). The median readmission time was 42 days after discharge (range, 35–68 days). In the observation group, 5 of 42 patients were readmitted for mild SAP recurrence, with a readmission rate of 11.90% (5/42). The median readmission time was 48 days after discharge (range, 41–76 days). There was no significant difference in readmission rates between the two groups (P = 0.713). All readmitted patients recovered and were discharged after treatment, with no deaths or surgical interventions reported.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that refined ICU nursing care is associated with several positive outcomes in the treatment of patients with SAP.

In this study, nursing effectiveness in the observation group was significantly higher than that in the control group, consistent with the findings of Wu et al., who reported improved recovery rates in critically ill patients following personalized nursing interventions [13]. This suggests that targeted nursing measures can directly influence patient outcomes, which aligns with the enhanced effectiveness observed with refined ICU nursing care in our study. With respect to symptom resolution, patients in the observation group experienced shorter durations of fever, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting compared to those in the control group. Previous research has demonstrated that refined nursing in the critical care of neonatal pneumonia complicated by respiratory failure improved patient conditions, blood gas indices, and vital signs [10]. Similarly, meticulous nursing interventions have been shown to improve postoperative outcomes in elderly hip fracture patients by alleviating delirium and pain [11]. Consistent with these findings, our study highlighted the importance of close monitoring of vital signs and timely adjustment of nursing plans, both of which contributed to faster symptom relief and provided targeted guidance for clinical practice. It should be noted, however, that the duration of symptom relief and length of hospital stay are influenced by multiple factors beyond nursing interventions, including drug efficacy, timing of surgical procedures, and baseline disease severity. As these factors were not strictly controlled or analyzed in this study, future investigations are needed to further clarify the independent effects of refined ICU nursing on these outcomes.

Serum and urinary amylase levels, key biomarkers reflecting SAP severity, were significantly lower in the observation group after nursing care. This finding suggests that refined ICU nursing may facilitate improved inflammatory control, likely attributable to comprehensive strategies such as fluid resuscitation, nutritional support, and timely identification of deterioration. Lan Fang et al. reported that real-time system-based nursing reduced inflammatory markers—including serum amylase, C-reactive protein, and white blood cell count—in SAP patients undergoing continuous renal replacement therapy [14]. These results parallel our findings, underscoring the beneficial role of targeted nursing interventions in alleviating inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, changes in amylase levels are part of a complex physiological process involving metabolic and endocrine regulation [15]. As this study did not explore these mechanisms in depth, future research should integrate mechanistic analyses to further clarify the relationship between refined nursing interventions and changes in biochemical markers, thereby strengthening the evidence base for nursing science.

Psychological distress, particularly anxiety and depression, is common among SAP patients and can adversely affect health-related quality of life [16]. The significant reductions in SAS and SDS scores in the observation group support the effectiveness of refined ICU nursing in alleviating these negative emotions. Huang et al. similarly demonstrated that standardized psychological interventions could effectively mitigate adverse psychological conditions in SAP patients [1]. Moreover, our study showed that the observation group achieved higher WHOQOL-BREF scores across all domains compared with the control group. Prior studies have confirmed that personalized and comprehensive nursing interventions can reduce delirium and pain, improve sleep quality, accelerate recovery, and enhance quality of life [11]. Given the severity and prolonged disease course of SAP, the psychological challenges faced by these patients are distinct from those of general ICU patients. Our tailored psychological care plans, designed to meet the specific mental health needs of SAP patients, may explain the greater improvement in psychological outcomes observed in the observation group. In addition, quality of life is a multidimensional construct influenced by several factors. Our refined nursing approach incorporated targeted strategies, such as individualized rehabilitation training and optimized nutritional support, thereby enriching both the theoretical framework and practical application of comprehensive nursing interventions to improve the quality of life in patients with complex conditions such as SAP.

With respect to complication rates, the observation group exhibited significantly lower rates than the control group, a finding consistent with Zhang and Zhao, who reported that refined nursing interventions can prevent complications in surgical patients [17]. In our study, refined ICU nursing care emphasized comprehensive monitoring and preventive strategies, such as careful positioning to avoid pressure ulcers and close surveillance of gastrointestinal function, thereby reducing complication risks. In terms of patient satisfaction, scores in the observation group were higher than those in the control group. Previous evidence has demonstrated that personalized nursing care enhances patient satisfaction, underscoring the importance of evidence-based nursing in ICU populations [18]. Similarly, Huang et al. reported that refined ICU nursing promoted postoperative recovery and improved satisfaction in elderly patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery [9]. However, patient satisfaction in that study was primarily assessed through health education and basic nursing. By contrast, our study employed a more comprehensive evaluation system encompassing not only nursing skills and service quality but also environmental factors and rehabilitation guidance, thereby providing more targeted strategies for further improving patient satisfaction.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, the generalizability of the results is limited. Second, although no significant differences in readmission rates were observed between the two groups—and all readmitted patients recovered without mortality or surgical intervention—the short follow-up duration precluded evaluation of the long-term impact of refined ICU nursing on survival, readmission, and sustained patient outcomes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that refined ICU nursing care can shorten symptom duration and hospital stays, reduce amylase levels, alleviate negative emotions, lower complication rates, and enhance patient satisfaction in patients with SAP. These findings provide novel insights and practical implications for the nursing management of SAP. However, given the limitations of this study, future research should focus on multicenter randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and standardized assessment tools to reduce bias and improve reliability. Longer follow-up is also necessary to evaluate long-term outcomes. Furthermore, mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the biological pathways through which refined ICU nursing influences inflammatory processes in SAP patients, thereby providing a stronger scientific foundation for clinical practice.
