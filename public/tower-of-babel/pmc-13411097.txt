An integrative literature review to develop a conceptual framework for spiritual nursing care in pediatric oncology
Authors: Neda Sheikhzakaryaee, Golnaz Azami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpiritual distress represents a significant yet under-addressed dimension of suffering among children with cancer. Despite its influence on emotional resilience, treatment adherence, and psychological well-being, spiritual care in pediatric oncology remains inconsistently implemented due to developmental misalignment, cultural variability, and limited professional training.AimThis study aimed to synthesize the literature and propose a a preliminary, developmentally responsive, and culturally adaptable nursing-centered conceptual framework for spiritual care in pediatric oncology, designed to guide nursing practice while supporting interdisciplinary collaboration in pediatric oncology, termed the Developmental-Spiritual Oncology Care Model (DSOCM).MethodsAn integrative literature review was conducted across six databases, yielding 227 sources, including empirical studies, theoretical works, clinical guidelines, and foundational texts. A manual inductive thematic analysis was performed to identify recurring concepts, relational patterns, and care processes relevant to pediatric spiritual care. Selected theoretical foundations—including Fowler’s Faith Development Theory, Watson’s Caring Theory, Travelbee’s Human-to-Human Relationship Model, Benner’s Competency Framework, and Mercer’s spiritual constructs— were critically evaluated and used to inform the development of the conceptual framework.ResultsDSOCM proposes core assumptions, paradigmatic and meta-paradigmatic concepts, guiding objectives, and a holistic nursing care approach. The framework highlights inclusivity, interdisciplinary collaboration, and competency-based training.ConclusionAs a preliminary literature-derived conceptual framework, DSOCM offers a structured guide for spiritual nursing care in pediatric oncology and requires further empirical testing, cross-cultural adaptation, and contextual refinement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07031-x.

Introduction

The integration of spiritual care into pediatric oncology is not merely an ethical imperative but an increasingly recognized dimension of holistic cancer care with implications for clinical, psychological, and developmental outcomes [1].

Drawing on the interdisciplinary consensus definition proposed by Puchalski et al., spirituality is understood as “the aspect of humanity that refers to the way individuals seek and express meaning and purpose and the way they experience connectedness to the moment, self, others, nature, and the significant or sacred” [2]. Within pediatric healthcare, this broader concept is adapted through the World Health Organization’s pediatric palliative care framework, which recognizes spiritual care as a core dimension of total child care alongside physical, psychological, and social care [3]. Accordingly, pediatric spiritual care may be conceptualized as the developmentally sensitive, family-centered, and culturally responsive assessment and support of children’s and adolescents’ spiritual, existential, and religious needs—including meaning, hope, connectedness, identity, and transcendence—throughout illness and healthcare experiences [2–5]. This distinction is critical because, unlike adult models, pediatric spiritual care must account for evolving cognitive development, children’s symbolic and relational modes of spiritual expression, and the central role of family systems in shaping meaning-making processes [4, 5]. Spiritual distress—a multidimensional form of suffering—commonly manifests in children with cancer as disruptions in meaning, connectedness, faith, and inner peace [6, 7]. It often requires coping mechanisms rooted in personal spiritual beliefs [8]. Empirical research in pediatric oncology indicates that spirituality and religious coping can influence how young patients interpret illness, sustain hope, and manage existential uncertainty during treatment [9]. While some scholars define spiritual distress as a loss of meaning due to disrupted connections [10], others emphasize its subjective and context-dependent nature, varying across individuals and clinical contexts [11]. Studies involving adolescents with cancer demonstrate that spiritual beliefs frequently shape perceptions of suffering, purpose, and hope during illness trajectories [12]. Empirical research underscores the clinical consequences of unaddressed spiritual distress, linking it to a 23% reduction in treatment adherence [13], increased risk of post-traumatic stress disorder [14], and heightened psychological distress and depression [15]. In pediatric oncology, where existential concerns emerge through developmentally specific expressions such as “Why me?” or “Is this punishment?” [16, 17], failure to systematically address these concerns undermines holistic care and highlights the need for structured spiritual interventions. Children’s spirituality is not merely a derivative of parental belief systems; rather, evidence suggests that children possess distinct spiritual perceptions that evolve through developmental stages and may be expressed through questions, fears, imagination, rituals, or relationships, thereby necessitating assessment frameworks specifically tailored to pediatric populations rather than extrapolated from adult spiritual care paradigms [4, 5]. Building on this context, the present study examines how existing frameworks address—or fail to address—the developmental and cultural dimensions of spiritual care for children with cancer.

Despite increasing recognition of the role of spirituality in pediatric health, the translation of spiritual care principles into clinical practice remains inconsistent. International oncology guidelines—including those of the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), the International Society of Pediatric Oncology (SIOP), and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)—acknowledge spiritual well-being as part of holistic care but provide limited guidance on how clinicians should assess and respond to children’s spiritual needs in practice (1). Emerging evidence confirms that children possess an inherent capacity for spiritual awareness that evolves dynamically throughout illness and treatment [1, 18]. Spiritual care is increasingly recognized as a core component of holistic pediatric healthcare, particularly during hospitalization, where it fosters emotional resilience and psychological integration [19]. However, current assessment tools and theoretical models—largely adult-derived—do not adequately capture children’s developmental variability in expressing spiritual needs [20, 21]. Instruments such as the Spiritual Involvement and Beliefs Scale (SIBS) and the Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS) reflect adult-centric frameworks, often overlooking how children conceptualize and communicate spiritual needs. Fowler’s Faith Development Theory (1981, 1982) offers a structured framework for understanding how children’s spiritual cognition evolves across developmental stages [22, 23]. Yet, it has been critiqued for its cognitive emphasis, gendered assumptions, and lack of structured intervention pathways for healthcare professionals [18, 24]. Similarly, Watson’s Human Caring Theory affirms the centrality of spirituality in therapeutic relationships [25, 26], but lacks prescriptive tools tailored to pediatric contexts. Mercer’s Spiritual Archetypes provide a typology of children’s coping styles [27], yet the absence of structured nursing protocols limits its clinical utility. Travelbee’s Human-to-Human Model emphasizes relational presence but was developed for adult populations and does not fully account for children’s developmental variability [28, 29]. Benner’s Competency Framework outlines a progression from novice to expert [30], however, like other foundational theories discussed, it does not provide developmentally specific spiritual care strategies applicable to pediatric oncology settings. Collectively, these theoretical perspectives demonstrate considerable conceptual depth but reveal notable practical limitations: although they offer valuable theoretical insights, they were not originally designed for pediatric oncology and therefore provide limited guidance for translating developmental spirituality into structured nursing assessment and intervention processes. Consequently, clinicians often lack comprehensive frameworks that integrate developmental, cultural, and spiritual considerations into everyday pediatric oncology care.

Cultural unawareness further complicates spiritual assessment. Western-developed tools often overlook culturally specific manifestations of spiritual suffering, such as beliefs in ancestral disappointment or spiritual detachment [31, 32]. Clinicians trained within Western paradigms may misinterpret non-Western expressions of distress, leading to underreporting and inequities in care [33]. Additionally, reliance on parental proxies can obscure children’s authentic spiritual concerns [34], while clinician discomfort often leads to avoidance of spiritual dialogue [35]. Collectively, these conceptual, developmental, and cultural constraints demonstrate the need for a more integrated framework that is empirically grounded, developmentally responsive, and culturally adaptable to the pediatric oncology context.

Study aim and conceptual contribution

Despite growing recognition of the importance of spiritual care in pediatric oncology, current approaches remain fragmented, largely adult-derived, and insufficiently integrated with developmental theory and clinical nursing practice. In light of the identified gaps in existing models and assessment tools, there is a need for a structured and integrative conceptual framework capable of guiding developmentally sensitive and culturally responsive spiritual nursing care.

Although spiritual care in pediatric oncology is inherently interdisciplinary, involving chaplains, psychologists, physicians, and family systems, this model specifically emphasizes nursing practice because nurses maintain sustained bedside presence, continuous developmental assessment, and relational proximity to children and families across the cancer trajectory [36]. DSOCM is therefore positioned not as an exclusive model of nursing ownership over spiritual care, but as a nursing-centered operational framework intended to strengthen nurses’ unique role within broader interdisciplinary spiritual care delivery.

Accordingly, this study employs an integrative literature review to synthesize existing theoretical and empirical insights and propose the Developmentally Sensitive Oncology Care Model (DSOCM). The DSOCM is introduced as a preliminary nursing-centered, interdisciplinary conceptual framework intended to organize key constructs, clarify relationships among developmental, cultural, spiritual, and collaborative dimensions, and provide structured guidance for pediatric oncology nursing practice while explicitly supporting interdisciplinary spiritual care integration. The DSOCM aims to bridge the persistent gap between conceptual discussions of pediatric spirituality and the practical needs of nurses who must assess, respond to, and coordinate care for children’s spiritual concerns within complex oncology settings.

Methodological approach for conducting an integrative literature review and proposing the DSOCM framework

Study design

This study employed an integrative literature review combined with theory synthesis to develop a preliminary, literature-derived, developmentally responsive, and culturally adaptable conceptual framework for spiritual nursing care in pediatric oncology, termed the DSOCM. The integrative review was selected because it allows for the inclusion and critical synthesis of diverse empirical, theoretical, and guideline-based sources, making it particularly suitable for the development of complex conceptual models [37].

Although the present study is structured as an integrative review, elements of scoping methodology were incorporated to comprehensively map conceptual and clinical dimensions of pediatric spiritual care. In alignment with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews, the eligibility criteria were structured according to the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework to ensure clarity, transparency, and methodological rigor.

Population (P): Children and adolescents diagnosed with cancer (0–18 years), including references to family members or caregivers when directly related to pediatric spiritual care. Studies focusing exclusively on adult oncology populations were excluded unless they provided transferable theoretical insights relevant to pediatric adaptation.

Concept: Spirituality, spiritual distress, spiritual development, faith-based coping, spiritual assessment, spiritual nursing care, and spiritually oriented clinical interventions. Both theoretical and empirical conceptualizations were eligible.

Context: Pediatric oncology care settings, including inpatient, outpatient, palliative, and survivorship contexts, as well as interdisciplinary pediatric oncology practice environments.

Guided by Walker’s (2019) model-development framework [37] and Whittemore & Knafl’s (2005) five-stage approach to integrative reviews [38], the study followed a sequential and interconnected process: (1) problem identification (2), literature search (3), data evaluation (4), data analysis, and (5) presentation of findings. These five analytical stages were used to systematically identify and synthesize relevant evidence, while Walker’s three-phase framework guided the subsequent conceptual organization and theory synthesis. Thus, the integrative review served as the evidence-generation component, and Walker’s phases structured the translation of synthesized findings into the prescriptive DSOCM framework, ensuring both methodological rigor and theoretical coherence. Given that the study relied solely on secondary sources and did not include primary clinical data or direct perspectives from patients, families, or clinicians, the proposed framework should be viewed as a preliminary conceptual approximation, warranting future empirical validation.

Phase 1: literature review and synthesis

Literature search strategy

An integrative literature review was conducted to synthesize existing evidence and inform the development of the proposed DSOCM framework in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Six electronic databases—PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar—were systematically searched for peer-reviewed literature published between 1995 and 2025. The search period was intentionally set from 1995 to 2025 to reflect the period during which contemporary conceptualizations of spirituality, holistic nursing, and pediatric psycho-oncology meaningfully expanded within healthcare scholarship. The mid-1990s marked a turning point in the integration of spirituality into holistic and patient-centered care models, supported by the growth of palliative care frameworks, increasing publication of spiritual assessment approaches in nursing, and broader recognition of spirituality as an essential component of health. During the same period, substantial improvements in pediatric cancer survival shifted research priorities toward psychosocial, existential, and developmental aspects of the illness experience, leading to an expansion of pediatric psycho-oncology literature. Literature prior to the mid-1990s tended to address spirituality in conceptual or pastoral terms rather than within structured clinical or pediatric-oncology-specific frameworks. Setting 1995 as the starting point therefore ensured the inclusion of modern theoretical, empirical, and interdisciplinary developments relevant to pediatric spiritual care while avoiding the conceptual discontinuity associated with pre-modern definitions and models.

The search strategy targeted studies addressing pediatric spiritual distress, developmental meaning-making, faith-based coping, and the integration of spiritual care into pediatric oncology. The search strategy was explicitly structured to reflect the PCC elements defined above. Search terms and subject headings were selected to capture the defined Population (children/adolescents with cancer), Concept (spirituality and spiritual care constructs), and Context (pediatric oncology settings). This structured alignment ensured consistency between the research question, eligibility criteria, and database search strategy.

To enhance methodological rigor regarding transparency, a structured search strategy combining MeSH terms and free-text keywords was developed for each database. To reduce publication bias, manual searches of reference lists, pediatric spirituality textbooks, and clinical guidelines from international professional organizations were also performed.

Eligibility criteria were operationalized according to the PCC framework. Studies were included if they: (1) addressed children or adolescents with cancer (Population); (2) examined spirituality, spiritual distress, spiritual development, faith-based coping, or spiritual care interventions (Concept); and (3) were situated within pediatric oncology or directly applicable interdisciplinary pediatric oncology contexts (Context). Exclusion criteria included: studies focusing solely on adult oncology populations without transferable conceptual relevance; case reports, editorials, conference abstracts, and commentaries without substantive conceptual or empirical content; and studies lacking accessible full-text data.

All retrieved records were imported into a reference management software (EndNote) to facilitate systematic organization and screening. Duplicate records identified across databases were removed prior to the screening process. Study selection process

Study selection followed a structured multi-stage screening protocol aligned with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The selection process consisted of four sequential stages: identification, duplicate removal, screening, and eligibility assessment.

First, all records retrieved from the database searches were compiled and duplicates were removed using reference management software and manual verification. Second, titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers to assess preliminary relevance to the defined PCC criteria. Studies that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded at this stage. Third, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed independently by both reviewers against the predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third researcher to reach consensus.

Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage included: (1) absence of relevance to pediatric oncology populations (2), lack of focus on spirituality or spiritual care constructs (3), exclusive focus on adult oncology without conceptual applicability to pediatric contexts (4), insufficient empirical or theoretical content, and (5) inaccessible full-text publications.

The complete selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram presented in Fig. 1, which details the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and ultimately included in the final synthesis. Because the purpose of this integrative review was to synthesize conceptual and theoretical insights across diverse sources, a formal methodological quality appraisal of the included studies was not performed. The review therefore focused on identifying recurring concepts and theoretical patterns relevant to spiritual care in pediatric oncology rather than evaluating the strength of evidence of individual studies.

Data extraction and thematic analysis

To ensure consistency and transparency in data handling, data from included sources were extracted using a structured extraction template designed for this review. Extracted variables included: (1) author/year (2), study design (3), conceptualization of spirituality/spiritual distress (4), developmental considerations (5), reported interventions or clinical practices, and (6) implications for pediatric oncology.

Both reviewers independently extracted data from an initial subset to calibrate the extraction process; the remaining sources were then divided between the two, with periodic cross-checks for accuracy.

A manual thematic analysis was conducted to identify recurring patterns and conceptual domains across the selected literature. This analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase framework for thematic analysis, which includes familiarization with data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. Following full-text review, key findings were extracted and organized into thematic categories based on their relevance to pediatric spiritual care.

The research question guiding the analysis was: “What conceptual and clinical elements are necessary to construct a developmentally responsive spiritual care model for pediatric oncology?” Coding was conducted at the semantic level, focusing on explicit content and language used in the literature rather than underlying assumptions. The coding and theme development were carried out by two researchers with backgrounds in pediatric nursing and spiritual care. Both coders acknowledge their positionality as clinicians committed to holistic, culturally sensitive care, which informed their interpretive lens and thematic prioritization. Reflexive journaling and peer debriefing were used to mitigate bias and enhance analytical rigor.

This process involved iterative reading, note-taking, and clustering of similar concepts to ensure coherence between theoretical insights and clinical applications. Themes were refined through constant comparison and cross-referencing with the study’s guiding frameworks. This inductive approach yielded four core domains that directly informed the development of DSOCM: (1) developmental perspectives on spiritual meaning-making (2), holistic nursing and medical models (3), clinical outcomes of spiritual distress, and (4) competency frameworks for clinician training.

Findings analysis

These four thematic domains reflect the fragmented, developmentally misaligned, and culturally narrow nature of existing spiritual care practices in pediatric oncology. They also underscore the need for a structured, literature-informed conceptual framework that integrates developmental psychology, cultural sensitivity, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Phase 2: establishing the core concept and conceptual framework

Following thematic synthesis of the literature, the conceptual architecture of the DSOCM was constructed through theory integration guided by Walker’s model development framework [37]. The central premise of DSOCM is the provision of spiritual care that is developmentally attuned to the cognitive, emotional, and existential needs of children with cancer. Although spiritual distress is widely documented in pediatric oncology, existing models frequently lack structured approaches grounded in children’s developmental capacities and spiritual experiences.

Theoretical foundations

The DSOCM is conceptually grounded in five complementary frameworks that collectively address developmental, relational, spiritual, and competency-based dimensions of pediatric oncology nursing. These theories were selected based on their conceptual relevance to themes identified during the integrative review and were critically appraised and adapted to inform model construction rather than adopted as fixed explanatory templates. The proposed DSOCM framework is informed by five foundational theories:

Fowler’s Faith Development Theory provides a developmental scaffold for understanding how children and adolescents construct spiritual meaning, progressing from symbolic and narrative expressions in early childhood to more abstract and reflective reasoning in adolescence [23].Watson’s Human Caring Theory positions spirituality as a core dimension of holistic nursing practice, emphasizing presence, compassion, intentionality, and the moral-ethical foundation of caring relationships [39].Mercer’s Spiritual Archetypes contribute a conceptual typology of faith-based coping orientations, offering insight into diverse patterns of meaning-making and spiritual response to illness [27].Travelbee’s Human-to-Human Model foregrounds relational presence and existential engagement, advocating for direct, authentic interaction with children’s spiritual concerns rather than exclusive reliance on parental mediation [40].Benner’s Clinician Training Framework, although not specific to spiritual care, provides a developmental model of nursing expertise that supports the structured cultivation of clinical judgment and competency, principles that are adaptable to spiritual care practice in pediatric oncology [30]. Recent empirical work further highlights the need for formalized spiritual care education and competency development among oncology clinicians [41].

Results

Thematic analysis of the literature yielded four core themes that informed the structure of DSOCM: (1) developmental perspectives on spiritual meaning-making (2), holistic nursing and medical models (3), clinical outcomes of spiritual distress, and (4) competency frameworks for clinician training. These themes were derived through semantic coding and constant comparison, and they form the conceptual basis of the proposed DSOCM framework. The purpose of the thematic analysis was to identify recurring conceptual patterns in the literature that could be synthesized into a prescriptive framework for spiritual care. Each theme informed the assumptions, concepts, and proposed operational strategies of DSOCM, supporting the development of a literature-informed and clinically relevant conceptual framework. These findings represent conceptual patterns derived from the literature synthesis and should therefore be interpreted as theoretical insights informing the proposed framework rather than empirically validated outcomes.

The findings are presented according to the structural components of the proposed DSOCM framework, including its assumptions, core concepts, objectives, and holistic nursing care strategies. These components are organized as follows: (i) theoretical context and background (outlined in the Introduction), (ii) defining assumptions and core concepts, (iii) outlining the model’s goals, and (iv) describing the organization and interrelationships among theoretical elements within the conceptual framework.

Integration of Theoretical Foundations into the DSOCM

Holistic nursing care approach

The proposed DSOCM framework outlines a structured, stepwise approach for integrating spiritual care into pediatric oncology nursing, grounded in holistic nursing principles: assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation. Each phase is informed by established theoretical models and supported by practical strategies for real-world application.

DSOCM incorporates culturally adaptable elements by integrating spiritual symbols, narratives, and rituals that reflect the child’s familial and faith traditions. Literature on culturally congruent care (e.g., Alvarenga et al., 2017; Cone, 2019) emphasizes the importance of tailoring interventions to the patient’s cultural context. DSOCM responds to this by offering flexible strategies—such as storytelling, music, and prayer—that can be adapted across diverse settings. The inclusion of adult‑derived spiritual assessment frameworks within DSOCM does not imply their direct or unmodified use with pediatric patients. Developmentally appropriate spiritual care emphasizes narrative expression, metaphor, and relational engagement over formal testing. Tools such as Mercer’s Spiritual Archetypes serve purely as conceptual guides for clinicians and require adaptation through child‑centered communication and clinical judgment before any application in practice [27].

To enhance transparency in the integration of theoretical foundations within the Developmentally Sensitive Oncology Care Model (DSOCM), the relationships between the underlying theories and their practical applications are explicitly illustrated in the manuscript. Table 1 maps the key theoretical perspectives informing the model to their corresponding DSOCM intervention strategies and training components, demonstrating how abstract theoretical constructs were translated into concrete clinical actions. Figure 2 presents a visual overview of the conceptual structure of DSOCM, highlighting the interaction between developmental considerations, spiritual care processes, and oncology nursing practice. In addition, Supplementary File 1 provides a detailed implementation guide that operationalizes the model through stepwise procedures for assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation, accompanied by illustrative clinical examples adapted to different developmental stages of pediatric patients. Together, these elements clarify the theoretical synthesis underlying DSOCM and demonstrate its practical applicability in pediatric oncology care.

Potential or hypothesized outcomes suggested by the literature

Because DSOCM is derived from an integrative synthesis of the literature, the following outcomes are conceptual projections rather than empirically validated effects and therefore require future empirical testing.

Expected outcomes for pediatric patients

Based on insights synthesized from the literature, the proposed DSOCM framework may contribute to improved approaches for addressing spiritual distress, supporting existential meaning-making, and strengthening resilience among pediatric oncology patients. By integrating faith-sensitive and developmentally responsive interventions, DSOCM aims to support emotional regulation, improve treatment adherence, and foster psychosocial well-being—reinforcing the essential role of spiritual care within holistic pediatric oncology management.

Reduction in existential distress

Spiritual distress among pediatric oncology patients often manifests as fear, guilt, and uncertainty, particularly in the face of illness-related existential concerns. Through structured assessments, including FICA screening and metaphor analysis, DSOCM ensures that spiritual interventions are tailored to address these concerns effectively. Spiritual assessment frameworks, including Mercer’s Spiritual Archetypes, informed the identification of core spiritual dimensions; however, these constructs were interpreted developmentally rather than applied as standardized assessment tools. For children and adolescents, spiritual expression was understood as fluid, symbolic, and relationship-based, requiring interpretation through narrative and play rather than formalized questionnaires [27]. The framework aims to support a shift from distress-driven narratives (e.g., “Is God angry at me?”) to resilience-based meaning-making, facilitated through guided existential reflection and structured therapeutic engagement.

Strengthened coping mechanisms and meaning-making

The model encourages pediatric patients to actively engage in faith-based and developmentally appropriate coping strategies, ensuring age-sensitive alignment with existential meaning-making processes. Techniques such as storytelling, hero-based resilience therapy, and guided reflection enable children to navigate spiritual distress constructively, fostering increased expressions of hope, emotional security, and adaptive spiritual engagement throughout treatment.

Improved emotional regulation and psychosocial resilience

Spiritual care interventions provide pediatric patients with structured pathways for emotional processing, contributing to the reduction of anxiety and depressive symptoms commonly associated with chronic illness. DSOCM integrates culturally adaptive meaning-making strategies, reinforcing patients’ ability to cope with spiritual and existential concerns. Engagement in legacy-building activities (e.g., letter writing, reflective storytelling) further enhances emotional stability post-treatment, ensuring continuity in meaning-making beyond the clinical setting.

Improved treatment adherence and sense of connectedness

Empirical studies suggest that higher spiritual well-being correlates with improved treatment adherence, enhanced emotional resilience, and a stronger sense of connectedness to caregivers and care teams. By addressing spiritual needs through developmentally aligned interventions, DSOCM fosters trust, motivation, and a sense of agency in pediatric patients, contributing to more consistent engagement with treatment protocols.

Expected outcomes for nurses

The implementation of DSOCM is also expected to significantly enhance nurses’ competency, emotional resilience, and interdisciplinary collaboration in pediatric oncology settings. By providing a structured approach to faith-sensitive, developmentally attuned, and culturally responsive spiritual care, DSOCM fosters professional fulfillment and strengthens nurses’ ability to deliver holistic, patient-centered interventions.

Enhanced competency in spiritual care delivery

The framework may support the development of progressive expertise among nurses in pediatric existential distress management, advancing from novice to expert proficiency in accordance with Benner’s Competency Progression Framework. This approach may enhance their ability to identify, assess, and implement individualized spiritual care interventions that are both faith-sensitive and developmentally appropriate.

Improved spiritual awareness and professional satisfaction

Through structured education and reflective practice, nurses will cultivate a deeper understanding of patients’ existential concerns, improving their ability to integrate spiritual care within broader psychosocial interventions. This training is expected to reinforce nurses’ confidence and professional satisfaction, strengthening their commitment to compassionate, holistic care.

Refinement of faith-sensitive communication strategies

The framework suggests the inclusion of case-based training workshops focused on existential dialogue techniques tailored to pediatric patients. These sessions will enhance communication effectiveness, ensuring that nurses can facilitate meaning-making conversations without imposing specific belief systems. This competency development will foster greater trust and relational engagement between healthcare providers and pediatric patients.

Improved resilience and emotional well-being

By acquiring structured spiritual care competencies, nurses are expected to experience greater emotional stability, reduced burnout, and lower levels of compassion fatigue when managing the psychological demands of pediatric oncology care. DSOCM will also enhance nurses’ adaptive capacity, enabling them to tailor interventions to diverse cultural and religious backgrounds and deliver inclusive, personalized care.

Improved interdisciplinary collaboration and care integration

DSOCM promotes collaboration among nurses, chaplains, psychologists, and oncologists to co-develop standardized spiritual care protocols. This interdisciplinary approach ensures systemic integration of faith-sensitive interventions within pediatric oncology teams. Healthcare institutions may consider adopting structured screening models (e.g., FICA Spiritual Assessment, Mercer’s Spiritual Archetypes) to provide consistent and reliable spiritual assessments across care settings.

Discussion

DSOCM as a response to theoretical fragmentation

The proposed DSOCM framework was constructed based on the integrative review findings to address limitations in existing theories and frameworks, which, while foundational, do not individually provide a comprehensive structure for spiritual care in pediatric oncology. By synthesizing insights from Fowler, Watson, Mercer, Travelbee, and Benner, DSOCM is proposed as a unified, developmentally responsive conceptual framework that aligns spiritual care with children’s cognitive, emotional, and faith-based needs. The DSOCM should therefore be understood as a preliminary conceptual framework derived from integrative literature synthesis rather than a clinically validated model.

Unlike traditional models that treat spirituality as an abstract or adult-centered domain, DSOCM conceptually outlines ways to operationalize spiritual care, individualized interventions, and competency-based training for nurses. It also integrates culturally adaptive strategies and interdisciplinary collaboration, aiming to support the integration of spiritual care within holistic pediatric oncology protocols. However, it should be acknowledged that the theoretical foundations informing DSOCM were primarily derived from literature developed in Western academic and healthcare contexts. Consequently, while the framework is conceptually designed to be culturally adaptable, its assumptions regarding spiritual expression, communication patterns, and caregiving roles may not fully reflect spiritual traditions and practices present in non-Western cultural settings.

A distinguishing feature of the proposed DSOCM framework is its integration of developmental psychology with nursing theory to produce a prescriptive, stepwise framework. While previous models often emphasize general spiritual support or rely on adult-centric constructs, DSOCM conceptually suggests age-specific assessment considerations, archetype-based intervention planning, and a competency pathway for nurses. This layered conceptual design is intended to support more direct engagement with children’s spiritual concerns, rather than proxy-based interpretation, and may support the pursuit of measurable outcomes in future clinical research. While DSOCM engages with emotional and developmental domains, these are framed within a spiritual care context. The model’s structure—from assessment to evaluation—is explicitly designed to identify and respond to spiritual distress, faith-based coping, and existential meaning-making in pediatric oncology.

The integration of five theoretical traditions was conceptually intentional to capture the complexity of pediatric spiritual care. Each theory addresses a unique domain—developmental, relational, typological, ethical, and professional—supporting the construction of a comprehensive yet preliminary conceptual framework.

Clinical implications for nursing practice

Spiritual care constitutes a vital dimension of holistic well-being for pediatric patients, particularly during periods of illness and hospitalization [19]. Nurses play a central role in the provision of this care through the assessment of spiritual needs, the planning and implementation of developmentally appropriate interventions, and the evaluation of outcomes [19, 42]. The nursing emphasis of DSOCM should not be interpreted as disciplinary exclusivity; rather, it reflects the practical reality that nurses often occupy the most sustained clinical interface with pediatric oncology patients and are therefore strategically positioned to operationalize spiritual screening, initiate supportive dialogue, and coordinate interdisciplinary referral pathways in collaboration with chaplains, psychologists, physicians, and family systems [36]. However, despite its recognized significance, the integration of spiritual care into clinical practice remains limited by challenges such as insufficient knowledge, time constraints, and a lack of formal institutional support [19].

To address these barriers, online educational programs have shown promise in strengthening nurses’ attitudes, knowledge, and competencies [43], while frameworks such as DSOCM may offer structured guidance for future clinical application. From a clinical standpoint, DSOCM is intended to enhance nurses’ ability to assess, plan, and evaluate spiritual care with developmental and cultural sensitivity—an approach that is particularly important in pediatric oncology where treatment adherence and psychosocial resilience involve both the child and caregiver [44].

The framework’s competency-based design may support incorporation into educational curricula, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration and skill development. Furthermore, if implemented, DSOCM may highlight the need for institutional recognition of spiritual care in pediatric oncology protocols, reinforcing its relevance to national and international healthcare standards [42]. Conceptually integrating DSOCM into nursing practice may help address clinical, educational, and policy-level gaps while supporting the delivery of compassionate, holistic care to children and their families.

In terms of feasibility, the framework is conceptually designed to be adaptable to busy oncology settings. Time demands could potentially be managed by embedding spiritual screening within routine assessments, while staff development may be supported through brief workshops or online modules. Resource needs include child-friendly communication tools and access to interdisciplinary support from chaplains, psychologists, or social workers. Potential barriers—such as limited staff or lack of protocols—may be mitigated through leadership engagement and gradual integration into existing care pathways. In addition, the cultural applicability of DSOCM should be interpreted cautiously. Because much of the literature informing the framework originates from Western healthcare systems, certain conceptualizations of spirituality, family roles, and child autonomy embedded in the model may differ from those found in non-Western cultural contexts. In settings where spiritual beliefs, ritual practices, or family decision-making structures differ substantially, uncritical application of the framework could risk overlooking culturally specific expressions of spiritual distress or resilience. Adaptation of the model to local cultural norms and collaboration with culturally knowledgeable professionals or community spiritual leaders may therefore be necessary when implementing DSOCM in diverse global settings.

To strengthen DSOCM’s clinical utility, future implementation should be supported by empirical data. Each phase of the framework—from assessment to intervention and evaluation—requires validation through pilot studies, implementation trials, and outcome-based research to determine its measurable impact in real-world settings.

Advancing competency and ethical engagement

DSOCM is intended to enhance nurses’ capacity to deliver developmentally appropriate, faith-sensitive care by offering conceptual guidance for assessment, intervention, and evaluation. It supports the use of tools such as FICA and Mercer’s Archetypes, while encouraging narrative, symbolic, and culturally embedded approaches to spiritual communication. The framework’s alignment with Benner’s Competency Framework may support nurses in progressing from novice to expert through structured learning and reflective practice [30, 45].

By centering the child’s voice and spiritual agency, DSOCM conceptually addresses the ethical imperative of direct engagement, while also cautioning against the uncritical application of adult-oriented spiritual assessment frameworks. Ethical pediatric spiritual care requires that any conceptual or training model derived from adult literature be flexibly adapted to align with each child’s developmental stage, communicative style, and familial context. This approach moves beyond proxy-based approaches and fosters relational trust. At the same time, ethical engagement in spiritual care also requires cultural humility. In some cultural contexts, children’s spiritual narratives may be closely mediated through family traditions, collective beliefs, or religious authorities. Therefore, culturally sensitive implementation of DSOCM should balance direct engagement with respect for family structures, community values, and culturally specific expressions of spirituality [46, 47].

Strengths and limitations of the study

A key strength of this study is its integration of multiple theoretical traditions into a preliminary, conceptually actionable framework that bridges the gap between conceptual ideals and clinical practice. DSOCM is presented as academically rigorous and potentially adaptable across diverse sociocultural contexts, offering a flexible conceptual structure for operationalizing spirituality as a core nursing responsibility [48].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the DSOCM framework has not yet undergone empirical validation in clinical settings. Although the framework was constructed through a systematic synthesis of theoretical and empirical literature, its feasibility, usability, and effectiveness in real pediatric oncology environments remain to be examined through future empirical studies involving patients, families, and healthcare professionals.

Second, a formal methodological quality appraisal of the included studies was not conducted. Because this study employed an integrative literature review aimed at achieving conceptual breadth and theoretical integration across diverse types of publications—including empirical studies, theoretical papers, books, and clinical guidelines—the review prioritized the identification and synthesis of key concepts rather than the graded evaluation of methodological rigor. Nevertheless, the absence of a formal quality assessment may influence the overall strength of the synthesized evidence.

Third, the framework was developed solely from secondary data derived from the literature and did not incorporate primary data or direct perspectives from pediatric patients, caregivers, or clinical professionals. As a result, the framework should be interpreted as a preliminary conceptual model that requires further empirical exploration and refinement.

Fourth, the cultural scope of the evidence informing DSOCM should be acknowledged. Much of the theoretical and empirical literature synthesized in this review originates from Western academic traditions and healthcare systems. As a result, the conceptualization of spirituality, developmental autonomy, and nurse–patient interaction reflected in the model may not fully represent spiritual worldviews present in non-Western societies. This limitation may affect the transferability of the framework across different cultural contexts and highlights the need for culturally grounded validation studies to ensure that DSOCM does not inadvertently overlook or misinterpret culturally specific spiritual practices.

Fifth, several spiritual and assessment frameworks informing this review were originally designed for adult populations. Although these models were used conceptually rather than prescriptively, their developmental orientation may not capture the unique ways in which spirituality is expressed by children and adolescents. Future research should therefore focus on adapting or creating pediatric-specific spiritual assessment tools and testing their validity within oncology settings.

Future research should therefore focus on empirical testing of the DSOCM framework in clinical contexts, incorporation of stakeholder perspectives, and systematic evaluations of the quality of evidence supporting specific components of the framework.

Conclusion

The DSOCM is presented as a preliminary, literature-derived conceptual framework intended to guide the integration of spiritual care into pediatric oncology. Grounded in synthesized assumptions, literature-informed intervention considerations, and conceptually aligned competency development, DSOCM positions faith-sensitive, developmentally attuned care as a core component of holistic nursing practice.
