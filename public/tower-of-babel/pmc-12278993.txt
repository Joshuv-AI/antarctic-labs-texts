Global Burden of Disease disability weights for the US National Electronic Injury Surveillance System – All Injury Program
Authors: Madeline E Moberg, Jaimie D Steinmetz, Kanyin Liane Ong, Hailey Lenox, Ted R Miller, Cora Peterson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasuring the impact of non-fatal injuries and violence is essential for informed public health policy-making and communications. This study aimed to generate new health-related quality of life data for a prominent US non-fatal injury surveillance source using disability weights derived from general population survey estimates.MethodsDisability weights reflecting severity as measured on a 0 (no disability) to 1 (death) scale, for 47 natures of injury from the Global Burden of Diseases, Risk Factors and Injuries Study (GBD) were mapped to 492 injury types in the US National Electronic Injury Surveillance System – All Injury Program (NEISS-AIP). The matching of GBD natures of injury to NEISS-AIP injury types was based on the underlying definitions for each injury diagnosis.ResultsThe average disability value weighted by incidence for all NEISS-AIP types was 0.073 (95% uncertainty interval: 0.050–0.097), and the range by injured body part and diagnosis was 0.006–0.408. Injuries that impacted large or critical body parts (lower trunk, upper trunk, head and neck) had the highest disability weights. Internal injuries, crushing, burns, nerve damage and fractures had higher disability weight values than lacerations, avulsions and contusions. The three most common NEISS-AIP non-fatal injury types during 2015–2020 were poisoning, internal injuries of the head and face lacerations, with disability weights of 0.163 (0.109–0.227), 0.168 (0.112–0.232) and 0.018 (0.010–0.029), respectively.ConclusionsMapping of publicly available disability weights data to a prominent non-fatal injury surveillance source can improve opportunities to measure and communicate the health and economic impact of injuries and violence.Keywords: Quality Of Life, Injury Diagnosis, Disability, Functional Outcome

Introduction

Comprehensive measurement of health-related quality of life is critical for assessing and communicating the health and economic impact of non-fatal injuries and violence. However, affected individuals, medical professionals and the general public may have different perceptions of functional losses related to a health incident.1,3 A direct comparison of health state valuations for a variety of short-term conditions with an explicit timeline as elicited from medical professionals versus general population respondents concluded there was consistency between those groups, while other studies based on health state valuations from injured individuals tended to indicate more impairment compared with valuations from the general public.3,5 Affected individuals are an important source of health-related quality of life information—some researchers have cogently argued that affected individuals are the most accurate source for this information2—but data collection can be prohibitively complex, expensive and have limited generalisability when affected individuals are questioned about their health states over time.2 3 6 7 A separate challenge is that combining published estimates of health-related quality of life from different groups of affected individuals can be challenging owing to a wide variety of data collection instruments—a recent systematic review identified 14 different generic instruments used in injury health state valuation studies in the previous decade.8

Discussions about whom to elicit health state valuations are important and ongoing, but high-profile guidance in the USA on this topic is clear: In 1996, the Washington Panel on Cost-effectiveness in Health and Medicine recommended that ‘for purposes of resource allocation, the relevant preferences are those of the general public.’9 This was confirmed by the Second Panel in 2016 (‘community preferences for health states are the most appropriate source of preferences for reference case analyses’).10 The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries and Risk Factors Study (GBD) has facilitated likely the most comprehensive and accessible elicitation of health state valuations from general population respondents to date.11 12 GBD health state valuations are publicly available and based on regression modelling of pairwise ranking of multiple health state vignettes (ie, ‘which description indicates better health?’) from tens of thousands of general population respondents from multiple countries, yielding scaled disability weights (0=no disability, 1=health loss equivalent to death) for 183 health conditions.

The most detailed US non-fatal injury surveillance data source, the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System – All Injury Program (NEISS-AIP), does not include data collection on health-related quality of life. Linking NEISS-AIP to an existing vetted data source for health state valuations can improve opportunities to measure and communicate the health and economic impact of injuries and violence. This study aimed to generate new health-related quality of life data for US non-fatal injury surveillance using disability weights calculated from general population surveys.

Methods

Methods used to generate GBD disability weights are detailed elsewhere.11 12 Briefly, surveys were fielded using paired comparisons to assess which health states were viewed as more debilitating. A lay description for each health state was developed by experts such that main outcomes, symptoms and impact on daily activities were clearly explained using non-clinical terminology. Household surveys in 2010 and 2013 were conducted in nine countries (Bangladesh, Indonesia, Peru, Tanzania, USA, Hungary, Italy, Netherlands and Sweden), and a web-based survey included respondents recruited through media campaigns and scientific journal editorials, yielding 60 890 total respondents. NEISS-AIP is a nationally representative probability sample of US hospitals that can provide weighted nationwide estimates of emergency department (ED) visits for injuries.13 In NEISS-AIP annual records of ED visits from 2015 to 2020 (unweighted n=2.9 million), there were 492 unique injury types by body part category, body part and injury diagnosis (eg, Arm/hand-Wrist-Fracture) based on 26 body parts and 30 diagnoses. NEISS-AIP has a higher number of injury types than GBD due to more specificity by both body part and diagnosis (eg, thermal vs chemical burns). Some NEISS-AIP body part–diagnosis types were far more common than others—head internal injuries or concussion accounted for 11% of analysed NEISS-AIP ED visit records and poisoning for 12% of records (data not shown).

Definitions underlying each injury diagnosis, including ICD-9-CM codes where available, from both NEISS-AIP and GBD were used to align the two data sources.14 15 There were 309 NEISS-AIP injury types with a direct match to a single GBD injury type. A further 183 NEISS-AIP injury types were matched to 2+GBD injury types; a weighted average of the matching GBD disability weights was generated using each contributing injury type’s incidence from GBD 2021 US national estimates for 2015–2020 (online supplemental appendix, onlinesupplemental table S1 S2).15 When the NEISS-AIP injured body part was ‘all parts,’ the GBD disability weight for ‘multiple fractures, dislocations, crashes, wounds, pains and strains’ was used (table 1). When the NEISS-AIP injured body part was ‘unknown/not stated’ but the diagnosis was provided (eg, fracture), an average of GBD weights by injury diagnosis weighted by US incidence was applied. When the NEISS-AIP injury diagnosis was ‘other’, an average of all GBD injury disability weights weighted by incidence was applied. Where possible, the GBD disability weights corresponding to a short-term injury (duration less than 1 year, indicated in table 1) were selected, to correspond most closely with the scope of injuries reported in NEISS-AIP, though this was not an option in all cases (appendix p 3). A (online supplemental table 1) reports all assessed NEISS-AIP injury types, the mapped or calculated mean GBD disability weight and corresponding 95% uncertainty interval (UI; reflecting the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the GBD standard simulation process to produce mean estimates from underlying data), and all GBD natures of injury (‘n-codes’) used to generate the disability weight.

This study complies with the Guidelines on Accurate and Transparent Health Estimate Reporting recommendations (online supplemental table S3).16

Results

Disability weights for NEISS-AIP injury types mapped from GBD ranged from 0.006 to 0.408 for all combinations of injured body part and diagnosis (figure 1). The average disability value weighted by incidence for all NEISS-AIP types was 0.073 (95% UI: 0.050–0.097) (online supplemental S1). Only 28 NEISS-AIP injury types had a disability weight greater than 0.200. Diagnoses such as internal injury, crushing, burns, nerve damage and some fractures had higher disability weights (eg, upper trunk-internal injury, 0.347 (0.235–0.471)), whereas injuries such as lacerations, avulsions and contusions typically had lower weights (eg, finger-puncture, 0.006 (0.002–0.012)). Injuries impacting the lower trunk, upper trunk, head or neck also had higher disability weights (eg, lower trunk-haemorrhage, 0.324 (0.220–0.442)). Injuries that impacted multiple body parts had higher disability weights, such as burns covering 25%–50% of the body or the entire body (0.314 (0.211–0.441)) or multiple fractured body parts (0.258 (0.172–0.356)). The most frequent injury reported in NEISS-AIP during 2015–2020 was poisoning (disability weight: 0.163 (0.109–0.227)), followed by internal injuries to the head (0.168 (0.112–0.232)) and lacerations of the face (0.018 (0.010–0.029)) (figure 2). Of the top 20 most frequent injury types, 14 were sprains/strains, lacerations or contusions (disability weight range: 0.006–0.025), and lower trunk-fracture had the highest disability weight (0.182 (0.126–0.259)) (figure 2).

Discussion

General population valuations of injury health states for NEISS-AIP derived as described here can help to improve opportunities to measure and communicate the impact of non-fatal injuries. Disability weights can be used directly to compute years lived with disability to better understand the population-level health impact of non-fatal injuries, or standard monetary valuations of reduced health-related quality of life can be generated to accompany more common cost values such as medical care and lost employment productivity.17 18 The use of general population respondents to value health states is consistent with the premise that valuations should reflect a broad social understanding of health impairments.12

Limitations

GBD disability weights depend on the validity of the corresponding health state descriptions, which are brief by design.6 11 12 However, this means the health state descriptions may not fully reflect the potential variety of experiences among patients with the same condition.11 Explicit timelines are not attached to many GBD health state descriptions; however, respondents’ valuations appear insensitive to framing in terms of temporary or chronic outcomes.11 Disability weights presented here address injuries by body region and nature of injury, rather than mechanism (eg, gunshot wound). The NEISS-AIP is more detailed than the GBD in terms of injured body region and diagnosis—for this study, 47 natures of injury from GBD were mapped to 492 NEISS-AIP injury types. NEISS-AIP injury types with limited information—such as a diagnosis of ‘other’—were assigned average disability weight values that may not reflect the nature or severity of the injury in all cases. The NEISS-AIP is based on a sample of US hospitals, relies on resource-intensive manual data abstraction from medical records to identify causal injury mechanism and intent, excludes injuries with only a diagnosis of pain, addresses only ED-treated injuries (and not direct inpatient admissions nor injuries treated in urgent care or doctor’s offices) and does not contain sufficient sample sizes to generate estimates by US state. Given that the GBD health states are designed to be comprehensive of all injuries, not only those presenting to the ED, it is possible that some disability weights may be more or less severe than the injuries documented in NEISS-AIP.

Conclusions

This study’s mapping of publicly available disability weights to a prominent non-fatal injury surveillance source can improve opportunities to quantify, monitor and communicate the health and economic impact of injuries and violence.
