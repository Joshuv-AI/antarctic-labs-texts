The TEMPO-Mi protocol (trepanation, stimulation, matrix, pressure, optimization, micrograft) for Sequential reconstruction of complex pediatric scalp avulsion
Authors: Natasha Chávez-Cruz, Evelyn Zumba-Altamirano, Anthony Mendoza-Agusto, Sergio Guillermo Choco-Salinas, Andrés Rivadeneira-Maldonado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtensive scalp avulsions in pediatric patients are rare and represent a significant reconstructive challenge, particularly when compounded by contamination, periosteal damage, and bone exposure—factors that limit conventional surgical options. Management focuses on achieving optimal functional and aesthetic outcomes.Case presentationWe report the case of a 6-year-old girl who sustained an extensive scalp avulsion following a severe attack by three Rottweiler dogs. A staged reconstructive approach using the TEMPO-Mi protocol was implemented, enabling progressive granulation tissue formation and subsequent definitive coverage with successful graft integration within a relatively short timeframe.ConclusionThe TEMPO-Mi protocol represents an alternative reconstructive strategy for the management of complex wounds with exposed bone and extensive soft-tissue avulsion. Its primary objective is to promote the early development of a well-vascularized recipient bed, enabling definitive functional reconstruction within a shorter timeframe than that typically required with conventional reconstructive strategies.

Introduction

Scalp avulsion is one of the most severe and uncommon traumatic injuries in pediatric patients. Reported etiologies include hair entanglement in agricultural machinery, oncologic surgery, and radiation therapy; however, evidence regarding its causes in children remains limited.1 In our setting, it is most associated with dog bites and traffic accidents. At our institution, dog bite injuries have increased substantially in recent years, rising from 23 annual cases (2012–2020) to 82 cases per year (2020–2024), representing more than a threefold increase compared to previous years.2,3

Several reconstructive techniques have been described for scalp, including microvascular reimplantation, skin grafts, local flaps, tissue expansion, and calvarial trepanation. Microvascular reimplantation remains the gold standard when vascular structures remain viable.4 However, there is currently no established consensus for the management of pediatric patients, primarily due to the low incidence of this condition, limitations related to total body surface area and future craniofacial growth, as well as the limited availability of specialized reconstructive surgery centers in rural settings.

We present a pediatric case of complex scalp avulsion managed using a staged reconstructive protocol termed TEMPO-Mi. This acronym comprises the following sequential stages: (T) calvarial trepanation, (E) recipient site stimulation, (M) dermal matrix application, (P) negative pressure wound therapy, (O) biological optimization of the recipient site, and (Mi) micro-skin grafting. TEMPO-Mi organizes reconstructive principles based on wound bed biology, designed to optimize the recipient bed and potentially shorten the reconstructive timeline.

Methodology

This study was conducted in accordance with the SCARE 2023 guidelines for reporting case reports and case series.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 6-year-old girl, from a rural area, sustained an extensive scalp avulsion after an attack by three Rottweiler dogs (Fig. 1). On admission, she was conscious but hemodynamically unstable, with tachycardia acute blood loss anemia (hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL, hematocrit 28.1%). Cranial CT excluded skull fractures and intracranial injury. Hemodynamic stabilization was prioritized with fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion prior to surgical intervention.

Intraoperatively, a complex defect measuring approximately 210 cm² was identified, involving bilateral parietal, right temporal, and occipital regions with bone exposure and complete transection of the superficial temporal and occipital arteries.

Given the heavily contaminated nature of the wound, devitalized tissue was sharply debrided, followed by copious irrigation with Dakin's solution. Negative pressure wound therapy with instillation (NPWTi) was then applied using a polyvinyl alcohol foam dressing at −100 mmHg. NPWTi was maintained for five days.

Once a clean wound bed had been achieved, and considering the extensive defect, exposed calvarium, and vascular compromise, a staged reconstructive approach using the TEMPO-Mi protocol was implemented. This protocol was structured to induce the formation of stable, well-vascularized granulation tissue over an otherwise avascular wound bed, thereby enabling definitive coverage with split-thickness skin grafts.

(T) calvarial trepanation

Multiple trepanations were created over the exposed skull using a custom 3D-printed acrylic template to standardize spacing and depth. Trepanations were placed 1 cm apart to approximately 0.5 cm depth until reaching the diploic space, promoting homogeneous granulation tissue formation.

(E) recipient site stimulation

An oxygenated hemoglobin spray was subsequently applied to improve local tissue oxygenation and stimulate angiogenesis over the trephined bone surface.

(M) acellular dermal matrix

An acellular dermal matrix was positioned over the wound bed, extending to the margins of viable skin to support granulation tissue formation and the development of a structurally stable neodermis. This matrix functions as an elastin-like scaffold, enhancing tissue elasticity and reducing the risk of future complications such as graft contraction.

(P) negative pressure wound therapy

NPWTi was reapplied using a white polyvinyl alcohol foam dressing placed directly over the dermal matrix, covered by a black polyurethane foam dressing, and maintained at a continuous negative pressure of −75 mmHg. The system was secured with an adhesive drape and an extra-thin hydrocolloid dressing (DuoDERM® Extra Thin).

(O) biological optimization of the wound bed

Serial follow-ups were conducted at two-week intervals, demonstrating progressive granulation tissue formation and a marked reduction in bone exposure (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). After 6 weeks, complete coverage with viable vascularized tissue was achieved.Fig. 2Wound bed demonstrates 100% granulation tissue at 5 weeks.Fig 2 dummy alt textFig. 3Clinical outcome showing complete epithelialization 9 weeks after surgery.Fig 3 dummy alt text

(Mi) skin micrografting

Definitive reconstruction was performed using autologous skin micrografts with the Meek 1:4 expansion system harvested from the left thigh (72 cm²). The grafts demonstrated successful integration, and the postoperative course was uneventful.

The patient was discharged after a 12-week hospital stay. Monthly follow-up over seven months demonstrated 100% Meek micrograft take, with excellent graft viability, durable wound coverage, unrestricted cranial growth, and no evidence of scar contracture. Complete alopecia persisted over the reconstructed scalp. At one year after reconstruction, the patient is scheduled to undergo hair transplantation using punch grafts.

Discussion

Traumatic scalp avulsions represent a major reconstructive challenge because of limited scalp elasticity and the need to minimize alopecia and scarring.

Although several conventional reconstructive techniques have been described for these injuries, their applicability may be limited by multiple factors, such as defect size, wound contamination, and vascular injury.

In their scalp reconstruction algorithm, Mahmood and Eisen4 recommend local rotation or advancement flaps for moderate-sized defects (3–5 cm), whereas tissue expansion is proposed for larger defects (>5 cm), particularly in the parieto-occipital region, to achieve favorable aesthetic outcomes. However, the authors acknowledge that both approaches become increasingly limited when the defect exceeds the amount of available adjacent tissue.

Furthermore, tissue expansion requires a staged reconstructive approach involving multiple outpatient visits over several weeks for progressive expansion of the implants before definitive wound closure, a process that is frequently associated with patient discomfort and may delay definitive reconstruction.

Similarly, López-Alvarado et al. ,5 in a case series of 18 patients, reported that local flaps were a practical reconstructive option primarily for defects smaller than 150 cm², with the largest defect in their cohort measuring 90 cm². In contrast, our patient presented with a scalp defect measuring approximately 210 cm², representing the loss of a substantial proportion of the total pediatric scalp surface area, limiting the feasibility of reconstruction using local flaps.

Sokoya et al.6 describe free tissue transfer using flaps such as the latissimus dorsi, anterolateral thigh, radial forearm, or rectus abdominis as a reconstructive option for extensive or complex scalp defects in which local or regional reconstructive techniques are either not feasible or unlikely to provide adequate coverage. However, this approach depends on the availability of suitable recipient vessels for microvascular anastomosis, which may be compromised in patients with high-energy traumatic injuries. In the present case, free flap reconstruction was not feasible because of complete transection of the superficial temporal and occipital arteries associated with the crush mechanism of injury.

Given this scenario, we developed a staged reconstructive protocol termed TEMPO-Mi, whose main contribution lies in the sequential integration of well-established surgical and biological principles into a reproducible strategy aimed at promoting the progressive development of a well-vascularized recipient bed for definitive reconstruction.

Calvarial trephinations were performed to an approximate depth of 5 mm, considering the thinner calvarial bone in pediatric patients while minimizing the risk of dural injury, a principle recognized in the Hippocratic treatise 〈On Head Wounds〉.7 Furthermore, because the rate of granulation tissue formation has been estimated at approximately 1–2 mm per day, the trephinations were spaced 1 cm apart to promote rapid and homogeneous granulation across the entire defect.

Acellular dermal matrix (Matriderm®) is a bilayer dermal substitute composed of native bovine collagen and hydrolyzed elastin, designed to preserve the three-dimensional architecture of the extracellular matrix.8 Its use promotes neovascularization, cellular migration and integration, and neodermis regeneration while improving tissue elasticity and reducing scar contracture. These properties are particularly advantageous in scalp reconstruction, where they facilitate skin graft integration and contribute to improved functional and aesthetic outcomes.

NPWT exerts macrodeformational and microdeformational forces on the wound bed,8 promoting exudate control and reduction of bacterial burden while stimulating angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, and progressive wound contraction, thereby accelerating wound healing.

The Meek micrografting system allows expansion of small split-thickness skin grafts by ratios of up to 1:9, enabling coverage of extensive defects while minimizing donor-site requirements. Wilson et al. ,9 in a series of 64 patients with burns of varying extent treated using this technique, reported a 95% graft take rate at two months together with a substantial reduction in donor-site area. This advantage is particularly relevant in pediatric patients, in whom donor skin availability is limited and the risk of pathological scarring is greater.

Although no standardized therapeutic algorithm currently exists for pediatric scalp avulsion injuries, previous reports such as Grussu et Al.,10 Shahdadi et Al.11 and Ng et Al.12 have shown that trepanation, dermal substitutes, and negative pressure wound therapy each promote granulation tissue formation over exposed calvarium.

TEMPO-Mi integrates these established principles into a predefined sequence aimed at optimizing recipient-site biology and facilitating definitive reconstruction.

Conclusion

The TEMPO-Mi protocol represents a useful alternative for the management of complex cranio-cutaneous avulsion injuries in pediatric patients, highlighting the importance of establishing a universal protocol structured on the biological basis of the surgical bed to achieve favorable results in this type of injury.

Nevertheless, this study is subject to the inherent limitations of a single case report; therefore, the reproducibility, safety, and effectiveness of the protocol should be confirmed in larger patient series with long-term follow-up. Based on the experience gained from this case, the protocol is currently being applied prospectively in selected patients at our institution with complex injuries of similar characteristics involving different anatomical regions; however, these cases are still undergoing clinical evaluation, and their outcomes are beyond the scope of the present report.
