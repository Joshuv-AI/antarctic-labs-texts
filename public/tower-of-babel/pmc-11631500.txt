Addressing public health in times of conflict
Authors: Charlotte Marchandise, Gauden Galea, Ilmo Keskimäki, Floris Barnhoorn
Published: 2024

As the European Public Health Association, we are proud to officially welcome the Ukrainian Public Health Association (UPHA) as a new member. UPHA’s dedication to public health, especially during a time of war, underscores their resilience and commitment to advancing health for all Ukrainians. Their innovations in mental health, environmental health, and public health governance offer vital lessons for the global public health community. As they become part of the EUPHA network, we look forward to collaborating and learning together to address the significant challenges ahead.

Ukraine continues to face relentless attacks that severely impact its public health infrastructure and the endager the life, the health and the mental health of its citizens.

At the same time, we must acknowledge the grave situation unfolding in Gaza, Lebanon, and Israel. The Israeli army’s destruction of critical health infrastructures and the loss of healthcare workers, leading to civilians suffering to blockades, limited access to food and water, and widespread displacement. alongside Hamas’s ongoing torture and kidnapping of hostages, have created a profound humanitarian catastrophe. These conditions constitute violations of fundamental human rights, including the right to health.

We unequivocally condemn all acts of violence against civilians, regardless of the perpetrators. This dire situation underscores the urgent need for an immediate ceasefire and concerted efforts toward peace to ensure the safety, security, and well-being of all affected populations.

Our member, the Israeli Association of Public Health Physicians, who have been actively sharing updates on the public health crisis during the ongoing conflict, including critical concerns about polio outbreaks in Gaza. Their tireless efforts to promote peace and reconciliation, despite the immense personal and professional challenges, exemplify the values of public health.

As Dr Tedros, Director General of WHO says, there is not health without peace, and no peace without health.

Considering the urgent need to address the public health challenges posed by war, we are launching a dedicated working group on public health in times of conflict. This working group will convene experts from our membership, including UPHA, the Israeli Association of Public Health Physicians, and others, to focus on how public health systems can prepare for, respond to, and recover from the devastating effects of war.

Our goal is to provide a platform for sharing knowledge, tools, and best practices to protect the health and rights of civilians during conflicts.

We invite all EUPHA members and sections to join this initiative as we work together to support those affected by war, to learn from each other’s experiences, and to advocate for peace, reconciliation, and the protection of health rights across all regions.

“Why don’t we know about this?”: shifting the narratives in public health communication on alcohol

Gauden Galea

EJPH

The evidence linking alcohol to serious health risks, including cancer, is overwhelming. Yet, most Europeans remain unaware, largely due to the alcohol industry’s efforts to downplay the risks.

Alcohol is a cause for more than 200 diseases and injuries and a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, digestive diseases, and cancers. At least 40 of these are caused by alcohol alone. Alcohol harms are experienced by people beyond the drinker, including children, families, and communities. Public health initiatives have focused on reducing the harm caused by alcohol consumption, but recent data reveals that there is still a critical gap in public awareness about the health effects of alcohol.

Despite decades of evidence linking alcohol to serious health risks, including cancer, most Europeans remain unaware. This is not a mere oversight, but a reflection of how effectively commercial interests have muddied the waters around alcohol and alcohol-related harm. If we are to protect public health, it is imperative that we communicate the facts more clearly and forcefully—in research, in advocacy and in media.

A recent study brings to light a sobering reality: <50% of Europeans are aware of the link between alcohol and cancer. (How much do Europeans know about the link between alcohol use and cancer? Results from an online survey in 14 countries | BMC Research Notes | Full Text (biomedcentral.com).) Specifically, fewer than one in three people are aware that alcohol consumption increases cancer risk. This is particularly alarming given that alcohol is responsible for approximately 7% of all cancers in Europe. (Global burden of cancer in 2020 attributable to alcohol consumption: a population-based study—The Lancet Oncology.) The scientific community has been clear about this link for years, but the message has not reached the public in a meaningful way.

October is Breast Cancer Awareness Month and when we consider the awareness of alcohol as a significant risk factor for breast cancer, our most recent data indicate that only 2 out of 10 women know the risk and have sufficient information to make informed choices.1

The low level of awareness is not just a failure of public health communication; it is also a consequence of deliberate misinformation spread by the alcohol industry. The recently published report on the commercial determinants of noncommunicable diseases highlights how industries, including alcohol, use sophisticated strategies to protect their profits at the expense of public health. These strategies include funding misleading research, lobbying against health regulations, and marketing alcohol as a normal and harmless part of daily life (Commercial Determinants of Noncommunicable Diseases in the WHO European Region).

In Europe in particular, we often find ourselves living in “alcogenic environments”—settings that normalize alcohol consumption. These are settings, where unregulated advertising and marketing of run rampant, where there is a high density of alcohol outlets, and where products are so affordable that people—specifically young people—are pushed toward consuming more alcoholic beverages. Comprehensive alcohol control policies tackle the drivers of “alcogenic environments”—availability, acceptability, and affordability of alcohol, but these measures are heavily opposed, questioned, and lobbied against by profit-seeking industries.

Effective communication is a powerful public health intervention. We have seen its success in campaigns against smoking, which have changed attitudes of the public use and related diseases over the past few decades. Similarly, campaigns promoting seatbelt and sunscreen use and vaccination have saved countless lives. The lesson here is clear: when the public is informed about health risks, they are more likely to support preventive safety measures, as well as effective population-level policies.

However, when it comes to alcohol, public health messaging has been inconsistent and often too weak to counter the pervasive influence of marketing. To address this critical gap in public awareness, we must accelerate initiatives that clearly communicate the risks associated with alcohol, particularly its link to cancer.

Firstly, health warnings on alcohol products must be made more explicit and visible, similar to the warnings found on tobacco products. The fact that alcohol, a class 1 carcinogen (IARC Publications Website—Alcohol Drinking), is the only consumable product that does not require nutritional and health warning labeling is inexplicable. Labels should clearly state the risk of cancer and other health issues associated with alcohol consumption. This is a simple but effective way to reach consumers directly.

Secondly, public health authorities need to reclaim the narrative. This means countering the alcohol industry’s messages with clear, evidence-based information, as well as impactful storytelling. Social media, traditional and mainstream media, and community outreach should all be utilized to spread the truth about the harmful effects of alcohol.

Thirdly, we must push for stronger regulations to limit the influence of the industry on public policy and research. Governments have a responsibility to protect public health, and this includes choosing people over profits, resisting industry pressure to weaken regulations, or obscure the risks associated with alcohol.

Finally, it is crucial to engage healthcare professionals. Doctors, nurses, and other healthcare providers are trusted sources of information and on the front lines of public health. They need to be equipped with the knowledge and tools to educate their patients about the risks of alcohol consumption.

It is time for public health authorities to step up and ensure that the public is fully informed about the harms of alcohol. By communicating the facts clearly and consistently, we can empower individuals to make healthier choices, tackle the drivers of “alcogenic environments” and reduce the burden of alcohol-related diseases and deaths across Europe.

Investing for sustainable health and well-being

Ilmo Keskimäki, Floris Barnhoorn

18th European Public Health Conference

11–14 November 2025

Finlandia Hall, Helsinki, Finland

Welcome to Helsinki, capital of the happiest country in the world

After the successful conference in Lisbon, we will meet again in person for the 18th European Public Health Conference in Helsinki, Finland, from 11 to 14 November. Helsinki will host the 18th EPH Conference in 2025, inviting you to reconnect with colleagues from around the globe to discuss key public health issues, explore the city, and have fun.

Known for its efficiency and safety, Helsinki is ranked the 11th best city for congresses worldwide. Additionally, Finland has been named the world’s happiest country for the seventh consecutive year in the World Happiness Report. This ranking is attributed to strong social support networks, social trust, honest governance, safe environments, and healthy lives. These topics will also be featured in the conference program.

Main theme: Investing for sustainable health and well-being

The 18th EPH Conference highlights that improving population health and well-being is an investment, not an expense. This investment benefits society and fosters a sustainable economy. Health and well-being are intricately linked with sustainable development: investing in health supports social, economic, and environmental sustainability, while a healthy planet with inclusive growth and fair societies enhances individual and community well-being. Addressing the determinants of health and reducing inequities are crucial for achieving population health and are essential for sustainable development.

Subthemes

Economy of well-being and public health

The economy of well-being emphasizes the interconnectedness of the economy, social security, healthcare, planetary boundaries, and overall welfare, focusing on long-term sustainability. It prioritizes people’s well-being in policy-making. Current economic systems, which contribute to environmental crises, social inequalities, and rising non-communicable diseases, are failing to generate true well-being. Addressing these issues requires shifting from a growth-centric model to one that measures and promotes well-being through integrated and consistent policies.

Cultural diversity and inclusivity in sustainable health policy and practice

Cultural diversity and inclusivity are crucial for sustainable health policy and practice. Recognizing diverse cultural perspectives in healthcare improves outcomes, fosters innovation, and strengthens resilience. Inclusive practices enhance patient satisfaction, trust, and equity by respecting cultural beliefs and involving marginalized communities in decision-making.

Public health in the era of misinformation and disinformation

Misinformation and disinformation pose increasing challenges to public health, spreading rapidly and often amplified by malicious bots and social media algorithms. These falsehoods can erode trust in healthcare systems, fuel vaccine hesitancy, and promote risky behaviors. Vulnerable populations are particularly at risk, which can deepen health disparities and create barriers to effective care. For public health professionals, it is crucial to explore strategies to combat these threats, ensuring accurate information prevails.

Tackling political and commercial determinants of health through policy and governance

This theme emphasizes the importance of collaboration between governments, organizations, and communities to create a sustainable health environment. It calls for transparency, accountability, and the involvement of diverse stakeholders in policy-making to foster resilient and inclusive health systems. Addressing the impact of political decisions and commercial interests on public health is crucial. Effective policies and governance can mitigate negative health outcomes. Prioritizing public well-being over profit through regulations, and ensuring political frameworks support health equity and access to healthcare for all, are essential.

Investing in health workforce to build sustainable health systems

The health workforce plays a critical role in achieving sustainable and resilient health systems. Adequate funding and resources are essential not only for training healthcare professionals but also for retaining them, ensuring continuous professional development, and maintaining high standards of care. A well-supported workforce, with a focus on their well-being, is key to system efficiency and positive health outcomes.
