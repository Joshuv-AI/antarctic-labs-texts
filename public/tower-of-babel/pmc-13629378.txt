Dynamic Occurrence and Mobilization Mechanisms of Condensate in Porous Media Revealed by Micro-CT and Two-Dimensional NMR
Authors: Rujun Wang, Furong Wang, Gengping Yan, Shuoshi Wang, Li Dai, Xingnan Ren, Chen Zhang, Xuehao Pei, Ningtao Zhang, Zeyu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During the depletion of gas-condensate reservoirs, the occurrence and mobilization of condensate in porous media are far more complex than those indicated by bulk PVT experiments because of the complexity of micro- to submicron-scale pore environments, where interfacial tension, capillary forces, and adsorption jointly govern fluid behavior. Conventional single-scale experimental approaches are insufficient to reveal the underlying microscopic mechanisms. In this study, a multiscale integrated characterization framework based on micro-CT and two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (2D NMR) was established. Representative high-permeability and low-permeability core samples were selected for in situ three-dimensional visualization by micro-CT and for real-time monitoring of the entire depletion process by online 2D NMR. The results show that the first detectable liquid response appears in smaller pore-size intervals and subsequently redistributes as pressure declines. In the high-permeability core observed by Micro-CT, condensate saturation increased mainly in the 40–100 and 100–350 μm intervals. In the low-permeability core monitored by NMR, the 10–40 μm response increased to an intermediate-pressure maximum and then declined. These trends are consistent with the combined effects of heterogeneous nucleation, capillary retention, pressure-driven redistribution, revaporization, and possible ripening. Because the two cores were characterized by different techniques, their curves are used to compare overall evolution trends rather than as a strict quantitative measure of permeability effects. Compared with the bulk PVT reference, the apparent porous-medium dew-point pressures and liquid dropout responses differed. These differences do not demonstrate a shift of the equilibrium phase envelope. Instead, they reflect the coupled influence of nucleation kinetics, capillarity, pore connectivity, gas–liquid flow, and the detection limits of micro-CT and NMR. The results provide a controlled core-scale description of condensate occurrence and redistribution during stepwise depletion.

Introduction

Gas-condensate reservoirs, as an important class of unconventional hydrocarbon resources, commonly experience the dropout of liquid condensate within the pore space of porous media when the reservoir pressure falls below the dew-point pressure during production. This phase-transition process not only substantially alters the fluid distribution within the reservoir but may also induce liquid blockage in the near-wellbore region, severely impairing gas-phase deliverability and ultimately reducing overall recovery efficiency. − Therefore, a thorough understanding of the dynamic occurrence characteristics and mobilization mechanisms of condensates in porous media is of great significance for optimizing gas-condensate reservoir development strategies.

Although conventional phase-behavior theory adequately describes equilibrium under bulk conditions, interfacial tension, capillarity, and adsorption may alter fluid behavior under nanoscale confinement. , Young–Laplace-based studies have predicted substantial dew-point changes mainly for pores below approximately 100 nm. , Such predictions should not be extrapolated directly to micrometer-scale reservoir pores. At larger pore scales, capillary forces can still govern condensate retention and mobilization without requiring a shift of the bulk equilibrium phase envelope. − Accordingly, pore-size effects should be distinguished between true thermodynamic shifts caused by strong confinement and apparent changes in phase occurrence controlled by capillary and kinetic effects. Beyond pore-size effects, the apparent onset and subsequent redistribution of a new phase may also be affected by heterogeneous nucleation and diffusion-driven ripening. Gao et al. reported rate-dependent nucleation and diffusive transport in hydrocarbon gas–liquid systems, while studies by Xu and co-workers and Blunt’s group showed that ripening can redistribute trapped ganglia through interphase mass transfer. −

To elucidate the occurrence mechanisms of condensate in micro- and nanopores, a variety of microscale experimental approaches have been developed. Nanoconfinement can alter the critical properties of fluids, thereby changing the dew-point pressure and condensate dropout behavior. The behavior of gas-condensate systems in nanopores is particularly complex because it involves both condensate dropout and flow, with major implications for gas-well productivity. − By constructing idealized pore networks, microfluidic chip technology has directly visualized the locations of condensate dropout and the associated flow-blocking behavior under different pressure-depletion paths. These studies indicate that condensate is mainly retained in regions with relatively small pore throats, while capillary forces strongly control oil–gas migration pathways. − Micro-CT, by contrast, enables nondestructive visualization of the three-dimensional pore structure and fluid distribution inside real core samples owing to its high spatial resolution. Micro-CT studies have shown that condensate in porous media is mainly retained in large pores and pore-throat regions, whereas higher capillary pressure in very small pores leads to the retention of a larger fraction of immobile condensate. , However, for reservoir structures with pore sizes in the nanometer or submicron range, micro-CT may be unable to accurately resolve fluid distributions. − In low-permeability and tight reservoirs, where pore throats are extremely small, the occurrence state of light fluids in ultrafine pores often cannot be reliably reconstructed. ,

At the same time, macroscopic experiments provide valuable insight into fluid behavior and dynamic processes in complex pore systems. Long-core displacement experiments are suitable for simulating gas–liquid two-phase flow in reservoirs, particularly below the dew-point pressure, and can be used to identify the critical point at which condensate begins to accumulate and become trapped, thereby significantly impairing flow efficiency. , Long-core experiments can also be used to investigate the re-evaporation efficiency of liquid after gas injection and its effect on recovery enhancement by analyzing phase miscibility, displacement pathways, and capillary pressure under different injection gases. However, such methods cannot directly establish the relationship between fluid distribution and specific pore structures, and therefore cannot reveal the underlying microscopic mobilization mechanisms. −

Nuclear magnetic resonance, owing to its high sensitivity to hydrogen protons, has become a powerful tool for identifying multiphase fluid saturation and pore-size distribution. When oil, gas, and water coexist in formation pores, the T2 signals obtained by one-dimensional NMR usually overlap, and conventional methods such as spectrum-shifting and difference-spectrum analysis are often insufficient to distinguish them effectively. Romero Rojas et al. applied two-dimensional T1–T2 NMR logging to the interpretation of gas-condensate reservoirs and showed that this technique can effectively discriminate among condensate gas, oil-based mud, and bound water in formations. Sun et al. further utilized the fluid-identification capability of two-dimensional T1–T2 NMR and adopted a manual zonation approach to identify and quantify condensate saturation in porous media. These studies demonstrate that NMR is an important tool for investigating multiphase flow behavior in gas-condensate reservoirs.

Despite these advances, a systematic understanding is still lacking of the microscopic mechanisms governing when condensate drops out, where it is retained, and how it is mobilized in real porous media. In particular, existing techniques are usually applied independently when characterizing the relationship between saturation and pore-size distribution: Micro-CT is advantageous in structural characterization and spatial localization but is relatively weak in fluid discrimination and limited by resolution, whereas NMR can correlate fluid types with pore-size distribution but lacks three-dimensional spatial information. To address these limitations, this study proposes a preparation method for a synthetic analog condensate gas under constrained experimental conditions. Potassium iodide was used to label formation water, enabling accurate discrimination of oil, gas, and water phases by micro-CT, reconstruction of the three-dimensional pore network of the core, and identification of the spatial distribution of condensate. Meanwhile, online two-dimensional T1–T2 NMR was employed to quantitatively characterize the evolution of condensate saturation and fluid mobility within different pore-size intervals. By integrating the complementary strengths of these two techniques, this study aims to reveal the key controlling factors governing the dynamic occurrence and mobilization of condensate in porous media, thereby providing theoretical support and experimental evidence for the efficient development of gas-condensate reservoirs.

Formulation Design and Preliminary Calculation

Because the reservoir conditions of Well B in the T gas-condensate reservoir exceeded the operating limits of both the micro-CT and NMR systems, it was necessary in this study to prepare a representative condensate-gas fluid with a field-consistent producing gas–oil ratio. The producing gas–oil ratio in the field was 2016 m3/m3, and the maximum condensate dropout during the constant-volume depletion test was 23.8%. By controlling the temperature of the condensate-gas fluid, the condensate dropout obtained from the laboratory test was matched to that measured in the field constant-volume depletion experiment.

As shown in Figure , the CMG-WinProp module was used to perform formulation prediction at 35 MPa. A complex-component dry gas was selected and blended with mixtures of different alkanes (C4–C12). Single-stage flash calculations and constant-volume depletion simulations were then conducted for the candidate synthetic condensate-gas systems, such that the predicted stock-tank gas–oil ratio and maximum condensate dropout of the synthetic sample were as close as possible to the PVT report of the original reservoir fluid sample.

CMG-predicted composition of the synthetic condensate gas-fixed mixed-oil system.

With the gas-phase composition and the mixed-oil composition fixed, different oil contents and different mixed-oil ratios were tested. As the light-end composition was varied, the producing gas–oil ratio decreased and the maximum condensate dropout increased with increasing carbon number of the added light components. Among the tested formulations, the C5 + C10 mixture yielded the lowest gas–oil ratio of 2086 m3/m3 and a maximum condensate dropout of 21.83%, both of which were close to those of the original reservoir fluid sample. These results provide the basis for the subsequent formulation of representative synthetic reservoir fluids.

The core flooding system was designed for operation at temperatures and pressures up to 200 °C and 100 MPa, respectively. The Xradia Context Micro-CT scanner enabled in situ imaging at temperatures up to 120 °C and pressures up to 40 MPa, with a maximum spatial resolution of 10 μm. Results and Discussion

Phase-Behavior Characteristics of the Representative Condensate-Gas Fluid

To systematically characterize the phase behavior and retrograde condensation behavior of the condensate gas reservoir fluid, a representative condensate gas sample was prepared based on the actual reservoir gas–oil ratio and subsequently transferred into a Canadian DBR solid deposition analyzer. The analyzer has maximum operating conditions of 200 °C and 70 MPa. Constant-composition expansion (CCE) and constant-volume depletion (CVD) experiments were then performed using this system. The CVD cell images at 90 °C are shown in Figure .

Variation in condensate dropout during constant-volume depletion at 90 °C.

Eight PVT cell images document the 90 °C CVD sequence from the single-phase reference to maximum liquid dropout and the subsequent decline. The first visible liquid signal at 34.88 MPa is treated as the apparent condensation onset in the cell rather than as a new equilibrium dew-point measurement. Liquid volume increased from 33 to 23 MPa and then decreased by 15 MPa. Gravity segregation causes liquid to accumulate at the bottom of the PVT cell, so the images provide a bulk-cell reference but do not represent pore-scale migration.

For the CCE experiments, as shown in Figure a, the variation of fluid volume with pressure was monitored at three temperatures, 70, 80, and 90 °C, to determine the dew-point pressure and compressibility coefficient at each temperature. For the CVD experiments, as shown in Figure b, two key temperatures, 70 and 90 °C, were selected. The results indicate that temperature has a significant effect on the phase behavior of the condensate-gas fluid. A comparison of the CVD condensate dropout curves at 70 and 90 °C shows that the maximum condensate dropout decreases markedly with increasing temperature. Under the 90 °C test condition, the maximum condensate dropout reached 22.38%, and the corresponding dew-point pressure was 34.88 MPa. As shown in Figure c, the dimensionless pressure used in this study is defined as the ratio of the current pressure to the dew-point pressure of the gas-condensate reservoir. A separate binary reference experiment was not conducted because a binary system would not adequately reproduce the dew-point characteristics and retrograde condensation behavior of the multicomponent gas-condensate fluid used in this study. The representative condensate-gas fluid prepared in this study reproduces the maximum condensate dropout and gas–oil ratio of the target sample well.

Online Micro-CT Experiment

Segmentation of CT Images

In the micro-CT scanning experiments conducted during the depletion process of the gas-condensate reservoir, potassium iodide solution was used as an X-ray contrast agent for the formation water to enable accurate discrimination of the gas, oil, and water phases. This treatment significantly enhanced the grayscale contrast between the water phase and oil and gas phases. By analysis of the grayscale histograms of voxels in different fluid regions, the optimal segmentation thresholds were determined. A multithreshold segmentation method was then applied to divide the volumetric dataset into gas, oil, and water phases, followed by three-dimensional reconstruction of the segmented volume.

As shown in Figure , three-dimensional renderings of the oil, gas, and water phases in the gas-condensate reservoir were obtained. Figure a presents the three-dimensional rendering of the core pore space, in which the blue regions represent pore space and the gray regions represent the rock matrix. Figure b shows the fluid saturation distribution above the dew-point pressure, where gas and water coexist as two phases. In Figure c–f, as the reservoir pressure declines below the dew-point pressure, condensate dropout begins to occur. In these images, yellow denotes natural gas and red denotes the precipitated condensate. It can be observed that the segmented condensate phase first appears within the pore space and is mainly distributed in the central regions of the pores. As pressure continues to decline, condensate dropout becomes more pronounced, and the volume of red condensate increases significantly, occupying a progressively larger fraction of the pore space.

Dynamic Evolution of Condensate Saturation in Porous Media Observed by Micro-CT

During the depletion of the porous media, five key pressure points35.17, 33.10, 27.34, 23, and 10 MPawere selected for micro-CT scanning. Based on the scans, the pore distribution of the dry core and the condensate saturation in different pore-size ranges after four depletion stages were calculated, as shown below.

The pressure at which a liquid signal was first detected is termed the apparent porous-medium dew-point pressure, as it reflects the combined effects of heterogeneous nucleation, pressure-step interval, and detection threshold rather than a direct shift in the equilibrium dew point. The current micro-CT analysis provides phase distribution and equivalent pore size but cannot reliably resolve fluid distributions in pores smaller than approximately 10 μm or reliably determine local gas–liquid interfacial curvature. Because the high-pressure gas–condensate interfacial tension was not independently measured, capillary pressure was not quantitatively calculated.

Figure presents the micro-CT characterization results of the high-permeability core 25-1 under ambient conditions. Comparing the pore-number fraction and pore-volume fraction, it is evident that the core exhibits a multiscale heterogeneous structure dominated by small pores in number and by large pores in volume.

Micro-CT pore distribution of Core 25-1.

Specifically, micropores with diameters less than 40 μm dominate in number, accounting for 75.79%, indicating a highly dense micropore network within the rock matrix. These pores contribute most of the specific surface area and determine the initial locations of fluid wetting and phase changes. However, their volumetric contribution is minimal, indicating limited overall storage capacity. In contrast, macropores larger than 100 μm are few in number but account for 87.6% of the pore volume, indicating that the main storage space and primary flow pathways are controlled by a small number of large pores.

The contrast between pore-number and pore-volume distributions controls where condensate can be detected and retained during depletion. The 10–40 μm CT-resolved pore bodies provide a relatively large surface area and may offer heterogeneous nucleation sites, whereas capillarity can retain liquid after it forms. The 100–350 μm pore bodies account for most of the resolved pore volume and provide the principal storage and transport space. These observations describe pore occupancy and redistribution and do not establish a shift of the bulk equilibrium phase envelope.

Owing to the spatial resolution limits of the current micro-CT system, subresolution pores smaller than 10 μm could not be effectively identified or reconstructed, leading to a certain underestimation in the statistics of small pores. Nevertheless, the observed pore-size distribution trend reliably reflects the physical characteristics of the bulk pore structure of the core.

During the depletion of the gas-condensate reservoir, micro-CT data were processed to classify condensate droplets by pore size: small pores (10–40 μm), medium pores (40–100 μm), and large pores (100–350 μm). The sum of condensate saturation across each pore-size interval during each depletion stage corresponds to the values listed in Table . Here, condensate saturation in a given pore-size interval is defined as the oil volume in that pore range divided by the total pore volume.

As shown in Figure , the CT-resolved condensate saturation increased across the three pore-size intervals as pressure declined. The signal first detected in the 10–40 μm interval and the later growth in the 40–100 and 100–350 μm intervals are consistent with condensate formation followed by accumulation and pressure-driven redistribution. As shown in Figure , both the T2 and T1–T2 spectra show that, as pressure increased from 10.5 to 38 MPa, the signal amplitude in each region gradually increased and the signal distribution extended toward longer relaxation times. This trend can be mainly attributed to pressure-induced changes in dry-gas properties. At higher pressure, the dry-gas density increases, resulting in more hydrogen nuclei within a given pore volume and thus a stronger NMR signal. Meanwhile, the accompanying increase in gas viscosity suppresses molecular diffusion, prolongs the transverse relaxation time, and causes the gas signal to broaden toward longer T2 values.

In the corrected condensate-gas model, the condensate is assumed to be uniformly distributed within the gas phase. However, the hydrogen index of the actual condensate gas in the gas-phase region is lower than that of the corrected condensate gas model. Consequently, negative blue regions appear in the differential spectra within the gas-phase region. These blue regions not only reflect the gas-dominated fluid distribution but also indicate that only a limited amount of condensate has precipitated in these regions; otherwise, significant condensate precipitation would weaken or offset the negative response. Therefore, the presence of distinct blue signals in the gas-phase region further demonstrates that the differential-spectrum method can effectively distinguish gas and oil phases, with relatively small errors in condensate identification.

As the pressure declined from the initial 38 MPa, red liquid signals emerged and coexisted with the blue gas-phase signals. Their shorter T2 values reflect a stronger confinement and lower liquid mobility. The liquid response increased to the maximum condensate saturation at 30.4 MPa and then decreased during further depletion, consistent with the combined effects of condensate mobilization, gas-driven redistribution, re-evaporation, and possible ripening.

Comparative Analysis of Micro-CT and NMR Experimental Results

By integrating the results from PVT constant-volume depletion (CVD), micro-CT, and NMR experiments, significant differences between condensate behavior in porous media and bulk-phase systems were revealed, as shown in Figure .

Condensate saturation trends at 90 °C from different cores and measurement methods.

In this study, micro-CT and NMR were used complementarily to characterize condensate evolution in a high-permeability core and a low-permeability core, respectively. Because each core was characterized by a different technique, the differences shown in Figure may reflect both rock properties and the measurement principle, spatial resolution, and pressure-sampling points. The present experiments cannot fully separate these effects; therefore, the curves are used to compare overall evolution trends rather than for a strict quantitative comparison. Compared with the bulk PVT results, the apparent porous medium dew point pressures and liquid dropout responses differed. These differences do not necessarily indicate a shift in the equilibrium dew point pressure but may reflect the combined effects of heterogeneous nucleation, capillary retention, pore connectivity, and detection threshold.

The complementary observations show that condensate was first detected in the smaller pore-size intervals and subsequently increased in medium and large pores as pressure declined, indicating gradual accumulation and redistribution within the pore network. Studies by Xu and co-workers and Blunt’s group suggest that, in addition to pore-size effects and pressure-driven flow, diffusion-driven ripening may transfer mass from smaller isolated clusters to larger or better-connected clusters. − The limited micro-CT resolution for pores smaller than 10 μm may also underestimate the initial condensate response in small pores.

As the pressure continued to decline, the NMR-derived condensate saturation reached a maximum at 30.4 MPa and then decreased, whereas the micro-CT results showed continued condensate accumulation at 3964 psi (27.34 MPa) and lower pressures. These differences may be related to the pore structure, measurement scale, condensate redistribution, mobilization, and revaporization rather than a common critical flow threshold.

The apparent porous-medium dew-point pressure and saturation evolution are also affected by the pressure-decline rate and equilibration time. Under field conditions, the far-field region changes pressure slowly and remains closer to phase equilibrium, whereas the larger pressure gradient near the wellbore may enhance the nonequilibrium effects. The stepwise depletion protocol with a 4 h equilibration period at each pressure point was designed to obtain core-scale responses under consistent experimental conditions. Therefore, the results provide a qualitative comparison of condensate occurrence, redistribution, mobilization, and revaporization under different pore structures and should be regarded as controlled core-scale references rather than direct representations of a specific field-depletion process.

As shown in Figure , micro-CT could not reliably resolve fluid distributions in pores below approximately 10 μm; therefore, the NMR results for the 0–10 μm interval are discussed separately, while comparison between the two methods focuses on the shared pore-size intervals above 10 μm. In the low-permeability core, condensate saturation in the 0–10 μm interval gradually increased to approximately 5% and remained relatively stable during the later depletion stage, indicating persistent capillary retention in the smallest pores. Saturation in the 10–40 μm interval increased rapidly and reached approximately 6% at 30.4 MPa, followed by a gradual decrease as pressure declined. This trend suggests that condensate initially accumulated in small pores and may subsequently have been mobilized or redistributed from these pores as pressure declined.

Comparison of condensate saturation in different pore sizes measured by micro-CT and NMR.

In the 40–100 μm interval of the low-permeability core, condensate saturation increased only slightly, reaching approximately 1.2% at low pressure, indicating limited transfer into medium-sized pores. Saturation in the 100–350 μm interval initially increased to approximately 4% and then decreased to 2.13% at 10 MPa, reflecting the combined effects of condensate mobilization, gas displacement, and revaporization during continued depletion.

In the high-permeability core, the micro-CT-derived condensate saturation in the 10–40 μm interval remained relatively stable at approximately 1–2%, indicating limited accumulation in small pores. In contrast, saturation in the 40–100 μm interval increased from approximately 1.68 to 7.22%, while that in the 100–350 μm interval increased more rapidly below 20 MPa and ultimately reached approximately 5%. These trends are consistent with progressive condensate accumulation or redistribution toward larger and better-connected pores during depletion. Overall, condensate in the low-permeability core was mainly retained in pores smaller than 40 μm, whereas the high-permeability core exhibited more evident redistribution and accumulation in medium and large pores.

Overall, condensate evolution reflects the combined effects of heterogeneous nucleation, capillary forces, pore-throat connectivity, and pressure-driven redistribution with possible contributions from ripening. Mineral surfaces may facilitate initial nucleation and accumulation, whereas possible ripening may contribute to the subsequent growth and redistribution of condensate clusters. During further depletion, capillary resistance and connectivity control condensate retention and mobilization, while revaporization may also occur at lower pressures. Therefore, the observed pore-scale changes represent dynamic condensation and redistribution rather than a direct shift in the equilibrium dew point.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidated the microscale mechanisms of condensate occurrence, migration, and re-evaporation in porous media by integrating micro-CT and two-dimensional NMR joint measurements with high-pressure physical simulation experiments. The main conclusions are as follows:1.During stepwise depletion, the condensate was first detected in the smaller pore-size intervals and subsequently increased in medium and large pores. This evolution reflects the combined effects of heterogeneous nucleation, capillary retention, pressure-driven redistribution, and possible diffusion-driven ripening with medium and large pores serving as the main regions for condensate accumulation and transport.2.Within the common 10–350 μm comparison range, condensate in the high-permeability core characterized by micro-CT accumulated mainly in the 40–100 and 100–350 μm intervals, whereas the low-permeability core characterized by NMR showed a stronger response in the 10–40 μm interval. At low pressures, the decrease in the medium- and large-pore NMR responses may be associated with condensate mobilization and revaporization. Because different cores and measurement methods were used, these differences represent qualitative pore-structure-dependent trends rather than a quantitative permeability-only comparison.3.Compared with the bulk CVD reference, the apparent porous-medium dew point pressures and saturation evolution characteristics differed owing to the combined influence of heterogeneous nucleation, capillary retention, pore connectivity, redistribution, revaporization, and possible ripening. These differences do not demonstrate a shift in the equilibrium phase envelope but indicate that bulk PVT experiments alone cannot fully describe the pore-scale condensate behavior. The results should therefore be regarded as controlled core-scale references rather than direct predictions of the field-scale depletion and recovery.
