Assessment of entropy-derived 2D river velocity profiles from surface velocity radar measurements: case study of the Ganga and Bhagirathi rivers
Authors: Abhishek Kumar, Manoj Kumar Jain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in non-contact surface velocity radar (SVR) technology have enhanced river surface velocity measurements, enabling rapid and safe data collection for discharge estimation and hydraulic studies. However, its application in mountainous rivers for studying two-dimensional flow characteristics remains limited. This study addresses the gap by evaluating entropy-based methods for 2D velocity profiling using surface velocity data from two Himalayan River sites—Ganga and Bhagirathi at Devprayag, India. Two entropy-based methods are compared: Method 1 applies an iterative dip correction factor (δ), while Method 2 applies an iterative dip ratio (). Both methods are validated against ADCP measurements across various river stages. For the Ganga River, Method 2 outperformed Method 1 with a slight margin, showing a lower average MAPE of 31.31 as compared to 33.44 for Method 1 with smaller errors near beds and sidewalls. For the Bhagirathi River, Method 1 indicated higher errors with an average MAPE of 52.38 and an increase by over 65% above 458 m (MSL), whereas Method 2 remained consistent with an average MAPE of 23.86. The same results were validated through the comparison of the estimated discharge with the observed ADCP discharge. Also, Method 2 accurately identified the location of y-axis (location of maximum cross-sectional velocity), aligning with the previous study. In summary, Method 2 can be recommended for 2D velocity profiling in similar settings with challenging field conditions, though further validation across diverse river types is advised.Keywords: 2D velocity profile, Dip correction factor, Dip ratio, Acoustic doppler current profiler (ADCP), Surface velocity radar (SVR)Subject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Understanding the spatial distribution of flow velocity is fundamental to river hydraulics. The spatial distribution of flow velocity plays a critical role in a wide range of scientific and engineering applications. Accurate information on velocity patterns is essential for reliable hydraulic modelling, which in turn supports the estimation of river discharge1–3, analysis of bedload and sediment transport4–7, assessment of channel morphology, etc8. Detailed velocity data are crucial for interpreting complex flow behavior in natural and engineered channels, particularly in areas with irregular geometry or high energy gradients. Additionally, the 2-dimensional (2D) velocity profiles are vital for the design and evaluation of hydraulic structures such as canals, bridge piers, river training works, etc9–11.. The velocity profiles help engineers to assess flow-induced forces, potential scour zones, and turbulence patterns, which are necessary for ensuring the stability, functionality, and durability of the infrastructure.

In recent times, the Acoustic Doppler Current Profilers (ADCPs) have become an essential instrument for river velocity profiling and discharge estimation with appreciable accuracy. Their non-intrusive and precise measurement capabilities have made them a standard in modern hydraulic studies, particularly for discharge measurement and detailed analysis of two-dimensional velocity profiles across a wide range of riverine and flow conditions12–14. Its recent applications include the study of 2-dimensional (2D) velocity profiles in challenging flow conditions like river confluences15,16, bends8,17, high turbulent conditions18, etc. For example, Click or tap here to enter text19. modified 2D velocity profile derived by ADCP based on a mesh approach to study the secondary currents at river confluences. In another study, the ADCP was operated at a fixed position in a river situated in the USA to determine the local boundary shear stress20.

Before initiating data collection using ADCPs, it is crucial to have a comprehensive understanding of the site-specific conditions that will influence the deployment and operation of the equipment. The choice of ADCP deployment method depends largely on the geomorphological characteristics of the river and its surrounding terrain. In case of the rivers flowing through broad floodplains, typically found in lowland or plain regions, ADCPs are mostly deployed using boats21–23. However, the scenario is quite different for rivers located in narrow valleys, often in mountainous regions. These rivers typically have steep gradients, limited channel width, and are difficult to navigate using boats due to rapids, boulders, or unstable flow conditions. In such terrains, boats are often not a viable option. Instead, a fixed cableway or pulley system (either manually or automatically operated) is installed to move the ADCP across the section24–26. While this approach allows data collection in otherwise inaccessible river sections, it comes with significant challenges. The initial investment in constructing and installing the pulley system can be substantial, requiring both time and financial resources. Moreover, operating the system under high-flow conditions pose serious safety and technical risks, as the equipment may become unstable or the cableway may be damaged. These limitations highlight the need to explore alternative, cost-effective methods for studying river flow dynamics in mountainous and difficult terrains. Developing such alternatives could expand the scope of hydrological monitoring in regions where traditional deployment methods are not feasible.

When we talk about the alternative way to study the flow characteristics of a river, the most popular and recent advancement includes the use of non-contact techniques like Doppler radar sensors, which use entropy techniques to estimate the flow as well as other river characteristics like sediment transport, bathymetry, etc27–30.. The entropy concept generally uses maximum cross-sectional velocity (or ) to estimate the discharge. The is generally found in the upper portion of the river for shallow to medium depth rivers31–34. For deep rivers, it may be present at some depth below the surface, i.e. generally at 0.6D depth35,36. Its precise location can be found by studying the vertical velocity distribution, which is only possible through the observation of a current meter or ADCP14,37. In contrast, the surface velocity is generally measured using a fixed-type Doppler radar or portable handheld radar. Furthermore, some studies have also used these radars to study the flow characteristics and associated uncertainties in the measured river discharge30,38. However, limited studies are available where the surface velocity has been used to study the 2D velocity profile of the river. One such study, where the 2D profiles were generated using the surface velocity measured through ADCP near the confluence of two rivers, was studied and compared with the full ADCP-based profile16. In another study, the surface velocity was corrected for dip at the river confluence to determine the velocity profiles, which were compared with the ADCP measurements15.

Upon reviewing several works based on the application of surface velocity to study the velocity profiles of the river15,16,31,38–41, several insights were drawn. First of all, the entropy method has emerged as a reliable approach that enables non-contact assessment of flow characteristics. Notably, two major entropy-based methods have been developed for estimating the 2D velocity profiles using the surface velocity information across the river section, which highlights the potential applicability of SVR data. The first uses a dip correction factor40, and the other utilises several channel properties like slope, aspect ratio, etc., in the entropy velocity profile equation, or simply it can be regarded as a dip ratio method42. Interestingly, no previous studies have directly used these methods with the combination of surface velocity measurements using SVR across the river section to demarcate the 2D velocity profiles. Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop the 2D river velocity profiles using two entropy-based methods based on the surface velocity data and assess their performance for the study sites. Method 1 employs an iterative dip correction factor approach, while Method 2 uses an iterative dip ratio approach based on relative depth i.e. . These methods are applied to field data collected from two rivers, the Ganga and Bhagirathi, at Devprayag, located in the mountainous region of Uttarakhand, India. The generated 2D velocity profiles have been validated against ADCP observations across multiple river stages to determine the most effective method for SVR-based hydrodynamic assessment in complex riverine environments.

Finding the parameters for 2D velocity profiles

To estimate the 2D velocity profiles, the UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator) coordinates are initially recorded for the verticals from which the SVR measurements are taken. This is done to match the coordinates with the UTM coordinates of ADCP observations. The next step involves determining the average cross-section of the river. This is done because of various uncertainties involved during the ADCP observations, which leads to distorted profiles during the operation of the instrument when the moving boat method is used48,49. This is done by averaging the bed profile observed during various transects. Further, the parameter for and , for Profiles are determined using the methodology discussed in Sect. “Estimation of 2-Dimensional velocity profile using the surface velocity data”. The values of these parameters for both the gauge stations are shown in Tables 3 and 4. The 2D velocity profile is created by linearly interpolating the estimated 2D velocity distribution for different verticals by using MATLAB’s “scatteredInterpolant” function, which estimates velocity values over a regularly spaced grid, allowing for a smooth contour visualisation of the velocity field across the river cross-section.

Field observations suggest that the increased error may be due to shallow depths near the right bank and the presence of boulders, which likely cause flow disturbances such as eddies.

In contrast, Method 1 consistently underestimates the velocities up to 450.4 m. Above this river stage, both profiles nearly overlap with each other showing similar accuracy. Also, the overestimation can be seen near the surface depth, and the underestimation can be seen at larger depths. Similar analysis for Bhagirathi River is shown in Fig. 10., where it can be observed that Method 2 replicates the ADCP profile very closely up to the river stage of 457.6 m. However, at 458.1 m stage, a significant deviation is observed, which may be attributed to the influence of secondary currents during that particular event. This explanation is supported by the fact that no notable deviations are seen in the profile corresponding to higher river stages (i.e. for 458.4 m and 459.3 m respectively). In contrast, Method 1 consistently underestimates the profile.

Discussion

The study presents a comprehensive evaluation of entropy-based 2D velocity profiles developed using the surface velocity data. The surface velocity data obtained from handheld SVR across the section at predefined verticals across the section are used for the development of 2D velocity profiles. Since these measurements form the basis of profile generation, any uncertainties associated with the SVR can lead to deviations in the resulting profiles29,54. Apart from this, the major source of error could be associated with the parameters used in the methods to generate the 2D velocity profiles. For instance, large errors are noticed in the case of Method 1, which failed to capture the profile for all the events at the Bhagirathi River. This discrepancy can be attributed to a pronounced river bend near the station, where strong secondary currents dominate the flow. Method 1 employs a dip correction factor that depends on the distance from sidewalls at the section40. Consequently, it attempts to generate the velocity profile based on localized flow patterns induced by sidewall effects within that specific section. As a result, it may overlook the influence of sidewall-induced currents originating from nearby upstream sections. Though, an experimental study conducted to simulate channel bends has validated the applicability of the method for low aspect ratio (i.e. less than 10) under smooth boundary conditions55. But the actual field conditions at the bends in Himalayan rivers are far more complex, as these sections are characterized by coarse beds with large rocks and boulders, leading to frequent development of secondary currents along the river course17,50. In contrast, Method 2 considers the dip ratio for a single vertical at a time without depending on the distance from the sidewalls. This independent approach may have improved the accuracy of Method 2 for the present site conditions. However, these claims require detailed investigation before adaptation on similar conditions.

In contrast, the Ganga River station is situated on a fairly straight reach with nearly ideal site conditions favoring any method. This may explain why Method 1 performed similarly to Method 2 in the Ganga River, which exhibits more uniform flow as compared to the Bhagirathi River. Also, Method 2 incorporates channel characteristics, including Von-Karman coefficient and the hydraulic gradient to develop the velocity profile which enhances its accuracy17,56. This confirms that Method 1 can be used to study the flow for uniform flow situations.

Apart from this, the uncertainties of ADCP should also be considered, especially during high flow conditions, where the velocity profile generated through the moving boat method could have errors due to turbulence and sediments. This arises due to the inability of the M9 radar sensors to capture the flow field accurately, leading to erroneous cells12,48,49,57. The errors are significant at higher river stages (more than the observed river stages), which are not analysed in the study. However, these errors can be eliminated by adopting the stationary boat method, which could not be done in the present study due to several constraints related to the study area. This can be addressed in future studies, which can help us understand the characteristics at higher flows. Additionally, the SVR requires more time to measure the surface velocity data at all the verticals across the section than the ADCP transect, which can cause errors. Hence, the evaluation would be much fairer if the timestamps of ADCP and surface velocity observations were exactly the same. Hence, a faster surface velocity observation is required, which can be explored by using UAV radars like those mounted on drones58.

Conclusion

The study aims to evaluate entropy-based methods for constructing 2D river velocity profiles using surface velocity data at two Himalayan River sites. Two methods are assessed: Method 1, based on an iterative dip correction factor, and Method 2, utilizing an iterative dip ratio approach. The generated profiles are evaluated against ADCP observations across multiple river stages to identify the most effective method for the study area.

Key findings of the study are as follows:

Method 2 consistently outperforms Method 1 in terms of accuracy, with lower MAPE and RMSE values across all river stages for both rivers. It also demonstrates better reliability, especially near the bed and sidewalls.

However, further validation of Method 2 across diverse river types and flow conditions is recommended to generalize its applicability.
