Growing conditioned BGW trees with log-concave offspring distributions
William FLEURAT

Abstract
We show that given a log-concave offspring distribution, the corresponding sequence of Bienaymé–Galton–Watson trees conditioned to have n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 vertices admits a realization as a Markov process ( T n ) n ≥ 1 (\mathrm{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1} which adds a new “right-leaning” leaf at each step. This applies for instance to offspring distributions which are Poisson, binomial, geometric, or any convolution of those. By a negative result of Janson, the log-concavity condition is optimal in the restricted case of offspring distributions supported in { 0 , 1 , 2 } \{0,1,2\} . We then prove a generalization to the case of an offspring distribution supported on an arithmetic progression, if we assume log-concavity along that progression. As an application, we deduce the existence of increasing couplings in an inhomogeneous model of random subtrees of the Ulam–Harris tree. This is equivalent to the statement that, in a corresponding inhomogeneous Bernouilli percolation model on a regular tree, the root cluster is stochastically increasing in its size. These results generalize a construction of Luczak and Winkler which applies to uniformly sampled subtrees with n n vertices of the infinite complete d d -ary trees. Our proofs are elementary and we tried to make them as self-contained as possible.

Main results

Central to our results will be the notion of log-concavity, which
has a rich history in combinatorics, algebra and geometry, see the classical surveys
[
Sta89

Bre89
,
Bre94
]
, as well as the more recent ones
[

Huh18

A non-negative sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is said to be
log-concave
if the following conditions are satisfied.

1.

The inequality
x_{i}^{2}\geq x_{i-1}x_{i+1}
holds for all
i\geq 1
.

2.

It has
no internal zeros
.
That is if
x_{i}x_{j}\neq 0
,
i<j
, then
x_{i}x_{i+1}\cdots x_{j}\neq 0
.

Some authors do not enforce the second condition when defining log-concavity, but we insist that it will be needed in our case.
Convolutions of log-concave sequences are log-concave, as well as pointwise limits of log-concave sequences.
Basic examples comprise binomial coefficients
(\binom{d}{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
d\geq 2
, geometric sequences
(q^{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
q>0
, or the inverse factorials
(1/k!)_{k\geq 0}
.
As another important example, if a polynomial with non-negative coefficients has only real roots then said coefficients are log-concave.
This can be derived using the preservation under convolution, or using Newton’s inequalities, which are recalled in Lemma
5.18

2
Growing random compositions

We start by introducing a model of random compositions which is closely related to BGW trees, and we study a notion of increasing couplings for this model, which will be instrumental to our constructions of increasing couplings for BGW trees.

As is usual in probability theory, by standard extension theorems we may assume that we have at our disposal a sufficiently big probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\P)
, together with realizations on it of all the random variables we may need.
Unless we expressly mention the probability distribution we are working with, our random variables will be defined on

2.2
Key lemma

It is well-known that two
real

X
and
Y
can be coupled in such a way that

if and only if their cumulative distribution functions
{F}_{X}\colon t\mapsto\P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,X\leq t\,}}\right)
and

satisfy

on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
The latter is clearly necessary.
Conversely, denote by

the generalized inverse of a function

\displaystyle F^{-1}\colon u\in[0,1]\mapsto F^{-1}(u)=\inf\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\colon F(t)\geq u\}.

Then assuming that

on
{\mathbb{R}}
, the coupling
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=({F}_{X}^{-1}(U),{F}_{Y}^{-1}(U))
satisfies

, where

is uniformly random in
[0,1]
.
If now
{F}_{X}\geq{F}_{Y}\geq{F}_{X+1}
, then for

\displaystyle\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\colon{F}_{X+1}(t)\geq u\}\subseteq\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\colon{F}_{Y}(t)\geq u\}\subseteq\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}\colon{F}_{X}(t)\geq u\},

and therefore by taking infima and observing that

is

\displaystyle X^{\prime}\leq Y^{\prime}\leq X^{\prime}+1.

\displaystyle\P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,C^{\prime}_{n}=c\;\middle|\;X_{n}=m\,}}\right)=\frac{a_{i+1}\cdot(b_{{n}_{1}}\cdots b_{{n}_{i}})}{{Z}^{{\mathbf{a}}^{+},{\mathbf{b}}}_{n-m}}=\mathbf{P}_{n-m}^{\,{{\mathbf{a}}^{+},{\mathbf{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,c\,}}\right),

as claimed.
Observe that the above expressions still hold when

∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Let

be a non-degenerate weight pair.

1.

If

(2.6)

Proof.

In the first case, the fact that

is a composition of
n
into one part, for every

.

We will leverage the basic observation that for two compositions

\displaystyle c\preceq c^{\prime}\qquad\iff\qquad\mathrm{proj}_{1}(c)\leq\mathrm{proj}_{1}(c^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{proj}_{>1}(c)\preceq\mathrm{proj}_{>1}(c^{\prime}).

(2.7)

.

Let

3
Growing conditioned BGW trees I: the non-arithmetic case

3.1
Simply generated trees

For convenience we will use the framework of
simply generated trees
in the sense of Meir and Moon
[
MM78
]
, see also Janson’s survey
[
Jan12
]
.
For
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
a non-negative sequence, we denote by
\omega
the following function on trees, which we interpret as a
\sigma
-finite measure,

\displaystyle\forall T\in\mathbb{T},\quad\omega(T)=\prod_{\mathtt{u}\in T}w_{k_{\mathtt{u}}(T)},

(3.1)

where we recall that
k_{\mathtt{u}}(T)
denotes the number of children of a vertex
\mathtt{u}
in a plane tree
T
.
The dependence in
{\mathbf{w}}
is implicit in the notation
\omega
but will always be clear from context.
The distribution of the simply generated tree with
n
vertices associated to the weight sequence
{\mathbf{w}}
is defined for
n\geq 1
by

\displaystyle\forall T\in\mathbb{T},\quad\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}(T)=\frac{\omega(T)}{b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\quad\text{and}\quad b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}\omega(T).

(3.2)

This definition only makes sense when
b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}\neq 0
, which is the case for every
n\geq 1
under the assumption that
w_{0}w_{1}>0
, since in this case the tree
T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
consisting of a single ancestral line with
n
vertices has
\omega(T)>0
.
When
\mu
is a probability distribution, the distribution
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
corresponding to
{\mathbf{w}}=(\mu(i))_{i\geq 0}
is precisely the conditioned Bienaymé–Galton–Watson distribution
\mathbf{BGW}^{\mu}_{n}
, for all
n\geq 1
such that
b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}\neq 0
.
Conversely, given a non-negative sequence
{\mathbf{w}}
such that the power series
\sum_{i}x^{i}w_{i}
has non-zero radius of convergence, there exist
a,b>0
such that the definition
\mu(i)=ab^{i}w_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
yields a probability distribution
\mu
which satisfies
\mathbf{BGW}^{\mu}_{n}=\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
such that
b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}\neq 0
.
See
[
Jan12
]
for details.

In this context, Theorem
1
can be reformulated in the following way.

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
be a non-negative sequence with
w_{0}w_{1}>0
.
If
{\mathbf{w}}
is log-concave, then the random trees with respective distributions
(\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
can be realized as a Markov process
(\mathrm{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in which at each step a right-leaning leaf is added.

This version may look stronger than Theorem
1
since simply generated trees generalize Biemaymé–Galton–Watson trees, but both versions are actually strictly equivalent.
Indeed if
{\mathbf{w}}
is log-concave, then
w_{n+1}\leq w_{n}w_{1}/w_{0}
for all
n\geq 0
so that the power series
\sum_{i}x^{i}w_{i}
has non-zero radius of convergence.
As mentioned above, this entails that there exist
a,b>0
such that setting
\mu(i)=ab^{i}w_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
defines a probability distribution
\mu
which satisfies
\mathbf{BGW}^{\mu}_{n}=\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
.
But
\mu
thus defined is log-concave and Theorem
1
applies.
The remainder of Section
3
is devoted to proving Theorem
4
and thus equivalently Theorem
1
.

3.2
Relationship with random compositions

For
n\geq 1
and a tree
T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
whose root has
i=k_{\varnothing}(T)\geq 0
children, we write
\varphi(T)=(T^{[1]},\dots,T^{[i]})
for the possibly empty collection where, for
1\leq j\leq i
, we denote by
T^{[j]}
the subtree of descendants of the
j
-th child of the root from left to right, that is, in Neveu’s formalism:

\displaystyle\varphi(T)=(T^{[1]},\dots,T^{[i]}),\quad T^{[j]}=\Bigl\{\mathtt{u}\in\mathbb{U}\colon j\mathtt{u}\in T\Bigr\},\quad 1\leq j\leq i,\quad i=k_{\varnothing}(T).

Note that the mapping
\varphi
is a bijection whose inverse mapping associates to any finite sequence of plane trees
(T^{1},\dots,T^{i})
,
i\geq 0
, the plane tree

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}(T^{1},\dots,T^{i})=\{\varnothing\}\cup\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq i}jT^{j},

where
jV=\{j\mathtt{u}\colon\mathtt{u}\in V\}
for every
j\geq 1
and every
V\subset\mathbb{U}
.
The preceding allows to associate to any plane tree
T
a composition

\displaystyle c(T)=\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{i}\qquad\text{where}\qquad n_{j}=\#\,T^{[j]},\quad 1\leq j\leq i.

Notice that
c(T)
is a composition of
{n-1}
since the sets
jT^{[j]}
,
1\leq j\leq i
, partition the non-root vertices of
T
.
Consistently, if
T
is the tree with one vertex then
c(T)=\varnothing
since in this case the collection
(T^{[1]},\dots,T^{[i]})
is empty.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
be a non-negative sequence which has no internal zeros and such that
w_{0}w_{1}>0
.
Set
{\mathbf{b}}=(b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
Then
({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}})
is a non-degenerate weight pair.
For
n\geq 1
we have
b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}={Z}^{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}_{n-1}
, and for
T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
if we write
c(T)=\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{i}
and
\varphi(T)=(T^{[1]},\dots,T^{[i]})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}(T)=\mathbf{P}_{n-1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}\bigl(c(T)\bigr)\cdot\left(\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{1}}\bigl(T^{[1]}\bigr)\cdots\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{i}}\bigl(T^{[i]}\bigr)\right).

(3.3)

Proof.

Since
w_{0}w_{1}>0
, the sequence
{\mathbf{b}}
is positive as mentioned after (
3.2
).
Since additionally
{\mathbf{w}}
has no internal zeros, the weight pair
({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}})
is indeed non-degenerate.
Let
n\geq 1
and let
T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
and
\varphi(T)=(T^{[1]},\dots,T^{[i]})
.
The vertices of
T
may be partitioned into the root vertex (which has
i
children) on the one hand, and on the other hand the elements of the sets
jT^{[j]}
,
1\leq j\leq i
.
Hence, by the product form (
3.1
) for
\omega(T)
, and the easy verification that for
1\leq j\leq i
we have
k_{j\mathtt{u}}(T)=k_{\mathtt{u}}(T^{[j]})
, we get:

\displaystyle\omega(T)=w_{i}\cdot\omega(T^{[1]})\cdots\omega(T^{[i]}).

(3.4)

In particular if
c(T)=\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{i}
then by the definition (
3.2
), the latter rewrites as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}\cdot\omega(T^{[1]})\cdots\omega(T^{[i]})}{b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}\cdot(b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{1}}\cdots b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{i}})}{b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{1}}\bigl(T^{[1]}\bigr)\cdots\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{i}}\bigl(T^{[i]}\bigr),

and thus by using the definition of
\mathbf{P}_{n-1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}
in (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}(T)=\frac{{Z}^{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}_{n-1}}{b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{n-1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{i}\,}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{1}}\bigl(T^{[1]}\bigr)\cdots\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{i}}\bigl(T^{[i]}\bigr).

(3.5)

Since the measures
(\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are probability measures, we get for all
c\>\models\>n-1
, by summing over all
T\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
such that
c(T)=c
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}(\{T\colon c(T)=c\})=\frac{{Z}^{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}_{n-1}}{b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{n-1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,c\,}}\right),

and by summing again on all
c\>\models\>n-1
and using that
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
and
\mathbf{P}_{n-1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}
are also probability measures we find
b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}={Z}^{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}_{n-1}
, and (
3.5
) becomes (
3.3
).
∎

The following proposition reduces the proof of Theorem
4
to verifying that
({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}})
is an admissible weight pair.
This is the counterpart in our setting to
[
LW04
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
be a non-negative sequence such that
w_{0}w_{1}>0
and let
{\mathbf{b}}=(b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
If
({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}})
is an admissible weight pair then the random trees with respective distributions
(\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
can be realized as a Markov process
(\mathrm{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
\mathrm{T}_{1}\subset\mathrm{T}_{2}\subset\mathrm{T}_{3}\subset\dots
.

Proof.

Our Markov process is initialized at
\mathrm{T}_{1}
the deterministic tree with one vertex.
We may now construct recursively the transition probabilities
p_{n}(T,T^{\prime})
for
n\geq 1
and
(T,T^{\prime})\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\times\mathbb{T}_{n+1}
.
It is actually sufficient to construct
p_{n}(T,T^{\prime})
when
T
and
T^{\prime}
are respectively in the supports of
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
and
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n+1}
.

Let
n\geq 1
and assume that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n-1}
have been constructed, so that we obtain a Markov process
(\mathrm{T}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{T}_{n})
which is a coupling of
(\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{m})_{1\leq m\leq n}
such that
\mathrm{T}_{1}\subset\mathrm{T}_{2}\subset\dots\subset\mathrm{T}_{n}
.
We take independent copies
(\mathrm{T}_{m}^{(j)})_{1\leq m\leq n}
indexed by
j\geq 1
of this coupling.
By the assumption that
({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}})
is an admissible weight pair, there exists a coupling
(C_{n},C_{n+1})
of the distributions
\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{n+1}^{\,{{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{b}}}}
such that
C_{n}\prec\mathrel{\mkern-5.0mu}\mathrel{\cdot}C_{n+1}
.
We choose such a coupling, independently of previously constructed random variables.
We write
C_{n}=\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{i}
and

and we set
\mathrm{T}=\varphi^{-1}(\mathrm{T}_{n_{1}}^{(1)},\dots,\mathrm{T}_{n_{i}}^{(i)})
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}=\{\varnothing\}\cup\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq i}j\mathrm{T}_{n_{j}}^{(j)},

(3.6)

Since

using the expressions (

\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
satifying
\mathrm{T}\subset\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
, and such that
\mathrm{T}
and
\mathrm{T}^{\prime}
have respective distributions
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n}
and
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n+1}
by Proposition

.
To complete the inductive construction of the transition probabilities, it suffices to set

for all plane trees

and

in the support of

and
\mathbf{SG}^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n+1}

∎

3.3
Log-concavity and the Toeplitz TP2 property

We will make use of the notion of
total positivity of order 2
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
A=(A_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 0}
be an array of nonnegative numbers. It is said to be
totally positive of order 2
(TP2), if all its
2\times 2
minors are non-negative, that is for all
i\leq i^{\prime}
and all
j\leq j^{\prime}
, we have
A_{i,j}A_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\geq A_{i,j^{\prime}}A_{i^{\prime},j}
.

Proposition
3.4
below states the well-known fact that the Toeplitz matrix of a log-concave sequence is TP2.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a non-negative sequence.
If it is log-concave then, then its Toeplitz matrix
(x_{i-j})_{i,j\geq 0}
is TP2, that is we have:

\displaystyle\forall{i}\leq{i^{\prime}},\quad\forall{j}\leq{j^{\prime}},\qquad x_{{i}-{j}}x_{{i^{\prime}}-{j^{\prime}}}\geq x_{{i}-{j^{\prime}}}x_{{i^{\prime}}-{j}},

with the convention that
x_{-1}=x_{-2}=\dots=0
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq i<i^{\prime}
and
0\leq{j}<{j^{\prime}}
.
If
x_{{i}-{j^{\prime}}}x_{{i^{\prime}}-{j}}=0
then the inequality is trivial.
Otherwise the support of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
contains
\{i-j^{\prime},i-j^{\prime}+1,\dots,i^{\prime}-j\}
, since
{\boldsymbol{x}}
has no internal zeros, even after extending with
x_{-1}=x_{-2}=\dots=0
.
For
\ell\in\{i,i+1,\dots,i^{\prime}\}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{x_{\ell-{j}}}{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}}}=\left(\frac{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+1}}{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+0}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+2}}{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+1}}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+({j^{\prime}}-{j})}}{x_{\ell-{j^{\prime}}+({j^{\prime}}-{j}-1)}}\right).

Notice that all indices in the last display belong to
\{i-j^{\prime},i-j^{\prime}+1,\dots,i^{\prime}-j\}
, hence the denominators are non-zero.
By log-concavity of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, each factor in the right-hand side is non-increasing in
\ell\in\{i,i+1,\dots,i^{\prime}\}
.
Hence we have

\frac{x_{{i}-{j}}}{x_{{i}-{j^{\prime}}}}\geq\frac{x_{{i^{\prime}}-{j}}}{x_{{i^{\prime}}-{j^{\prime}}}}

since
{i}<{i^{\prime}}
.
∎

3.4
Checking the inequalities

Consider
{\mathbf{w}}=(w_{0},w_{1},\dots)
a non-negative sequence with
w_{0}w_{1}>0
.
For
n,k\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathbb{F}_{n,k}
the set of ordered forests
F=(T_{1},\dots,T_{k})
made of
k
plane trees
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
having
n
vertices
in total
, in the sense that
\sum_{j}\#\,T_{j}=n
.
Given a forest
F=(T_{1},\dots,T_{k})\in\mathbb{F}_{n,k}
, we write
\omega(F)=\omega(T_{1})\omega(T_{2})\cdots\omega(T_{k})
.
Then, for
n,k\geq 1
, we may set

\displaystyle f^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n,k}=\sum_{\underline{n}_{1}\cdots\underline{n}_{k}\>\models\>n}b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{1}}\cdots b^{{\mathbf{w}}}_{n_{k}}=\sum_{F\in\mathbb{F}_{n,k}}\omega(F).

(3.7)

Note in particular that

The array

Definition 5.1

For

Remark 5.2
.

Notice that

is a plane tree if and only if for every

For a finite subset

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}=\bigsqcup_{S\subset\{1,2,\dots\},\,|S|<\infty}\mathbb{G}_{S}.

.

At first sight, the coupling in Proposition

The problem is that
