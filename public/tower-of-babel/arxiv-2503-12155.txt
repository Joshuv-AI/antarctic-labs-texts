On self-training of summary data with genetic applications
Buxin Su, Jiaoyang Huang, Jin Jin, Bingxin Zhao

Abstract
Prediction model training is often hindered by limited access to individual-level data due to privacy concerns and logistical challenges, particularly in biomedical research. Resampling-based self-training presents a promising approach for building prediction models using only summary-level data. These methods leverage summary statistics to sample pseudo datasets for model training and parameter optimization, allowing for model development without individual-level data. Although increasingly used in precision medicine, the general behaviors of self-training remain unexplored. In this paper, we leverage a random matrix theory framework to establish the statistical properties of self-training algorithms for high-dimensional sparsity-free summary data. Notably, we demonstrate that, within a class of linear estimators, resampling-based self-training achieves the same asymptotic predictive accuracy as conventional training methods that require individual-level datasets. These results suggest that self-training with only summary data incurs no additional cost in prediction accuracy, while offering significant practical convenience. Our analysis provides several valuable insights and counterintuitive findings. For example, while pseudo-training and validation datasets are inherently dependent, their interdependence unexpectedly cancels out when calculating prediction accuracy measures, effectively preventing overfitting in self-training algorithms. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to show that the self-training framework maintains this no-cost advantage when combining multiple methods (e.g., in ensemble learning) or when jointly training on data from different distributions (e.g., in multi-ancestry genetic data training). We numerically validate our findings through extensive simulations and real data analyses using the UK Biobank. Our study highlights the potential of resampling-based self-training to advance genetic risk prediction and other fields that make summary data publicly available.



Keywords
: Genetic prediction; Prediction models; Precision medicine; Random matrix theory; Resampling; Summary statistics.

1
Introduction

Developing prediction models—one of the major tasks in statistical learning and various scientific fields—typically relies on access to individual-level data for model training and validation. For example, in a traditional prediction model training process, individual-level datasets are required and are often split into independent training and validation subsets (or used in a cross-validation design) for parameter optimization, to avoid overfitting and ensure generalizability.
However, in many applications, accessing individual-level data is challenging. A notable example is genetic risk prediction in precision medicine
(
Lennon et al.,, 2024
)
, which uses genetic variants from genome-wide association studies (GWAS)
(
Uffelmann et al.,, 2021
)
as predictors.
Due to privacy restrictions and logistical challenges, summary-level GWAS data (such as marginal genetic effect estimates and standard errors) rather than original individual-level genetic profiles, have become the standard for data sharing in genetic research
(
Pasaniuc and Price,, 2017
)
. Using these summary statistics, polygenic risk scores (PRS) have been widely developed to assess genetic risk for various complex traits and diseases
(
Purcell et al.,, 2009
)
. Millions of genetic variants in GWAS are used as predictors, each contributing only a small amount of information
(
Boyle et al.,, 2017
)
.
Over the last two decades, a wide variety of statistical methods have been developed to improve PRS performance by better aggregating predictive power across high-dimensional genetic predictors
(
Vilhjálmsson et al.,, 2015
;
Mak et al.,, 2017
;
Ma and Zhou,, 2021
;
Ge et al.,, 2019
;
Pattee and Pan,, 2020
;
Hu et al.,, 2017
;
Yang and Zhou,, 2020
)
.
Although most PRS prediction models only require summary-level data from the training sample as model input, they typically still need access to
individual-level validation data during the training process for model parameter tuning
(
Choi et al.,, 2020
;
Mak et al.,, 2017
;
Vilhjálmsson et al.,, 2015
;
Márquez-Luna et al.,, 2021
)
. However, this individual-level validation dataset may not always be available to researchers who need to develop the prediction model, as sharing and accessing genetic datasets—even small validation data—can pose risks and raise concerns regarding data privacy and policy. Such privacy and logistical barriers have significantly limited the accessibility and scalability of PRS applications
(
Bonomi et al.,, 2020
;
Wan et al.,, 2022
;
Jin et al.,, 2025
)
.

Recent advances in genetic fields have facilitated pseudo-training for PRS models directly using high-dimensional summary-level data
(
Zhao et al., 2024c,
;
Zhao et al.,, 2021
;
Jiang et al.,, 2024
;
Chen et al.,, 2024
;
Song et al.,, 2019
)
. The principle of this approach is that, given the summary-level data, it is possible to sample pseudo-training and validation summary statistics from their underlying probability distribution.
These sampled summary statistics closely mimic what would be obtained if there were access to two independent subsets of the individual-level data.
Therefore, using these pseudo-training and validation datasets, it is possible to tune parameters and ultimately derive a prediction model, allowing for the self-training of summary statistics.
Despite their strong preliminary numerical performance in several application examples
(
Jiang et al.,, 2024
;
Jin et al.,, 2025
)
and emerging extensions to other biomedical fields
(
Wu et al.,, 2023
;
Wang et al.,, 2024
)
, little statistical research has been conducted to understand the general properties of resampling-based self-training for summary-level data.
It remains unclear whether these methods lead to potential reductions in model performance, especially regarding the trade-offs between practical convenience and decreased prediction accuracy, as well as the key factors influencing their performance in practical applications. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a framework to quantify the performance of self-training and compare it to conventional model training with individual-level data.

In this paper, we propose a general random matrix theory framework
(
Bai and Silverstein,, 2010
;
Yao et al.,, 2015
;
Dobriban and Sheng,, 2021
)
to model and understand self-training with high-dimensional summary-level data. Our study provides several key contributions.
First, we establish the statistical properties of self-training algorithms for high-dimensional predictors with a general covariance structure, without imposing sparsity constraints on regression coefficients.
We demonstrate that, under flexible conditions, a class of linear estimators achieves the same asymptotic predictive accuracy as their counterparts trained using individual-level data.
We provide detailed analytical evaluations of ridge-type estimators and marginal thresholding estimators, both of which, along with their variants, are widely used in PRS applications
(
Ma and Zhou,, 2021
)
.
These analyses provide deep insights for practical applications and reveal notable counterintuitive findings.
For example, unlike individual-level training and validation datasets, pseudo-training and validation datasets are inherently dependent, as they are derived from the same summary-level data. However, surprisingly, we find that this interdependence cancels out during the calculation of prediction accuracy measures, such as
R
-squared (
R^{2}
) or mean squared error. This phenomenon effectively prevents overfitting in self-training algorithms.
Furthermore, we show that self-training can facilitate the combination of different estimators using summary statistics within an ensemble learning framework. Self-training also enables joint training across multiple datasets from different distributions, which is particularly relevant to the emerging field of multi-ancestry genetic risk prediction
(
Kachuri et al.,, 2024
;
Zhang et al.,, 2023
;
Jin et al.,, 2024
)
.
We numerically validate our theoretical findings with extensive simulations and real data analyses from the UK Biobank
(
Bycroft et al.,, 2018
)
.

2
Self-training of summary data

2.1
The model and data

We specify the data generation model for the dataset
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
, where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The linear model relating the observation
\mathbf{y}
and design matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\epsilon},

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{y}
represents a phenotype and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
denotes
p
genetic variants. The genetic effects
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and noise
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are random variables.
The matrix
\mathbf{X}
typically contains millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from a large number of samples, for example, half a million participants in the UK Biobank
(
Bycroft et al.,, 2018
)
.
The following conditions on
\mathbf{X}
are frequently used in the application of random matrix theory for such high-dimensional data
(
Dobriban and Wager,, 2018
;
Ledoit and Péché,, 2011
;
Bai and Silverstein,, 2010
)
.
Condition
1
indicates a high-dimensional regime where the sample size and feature dimensionality are proportional.

Condition 1
.

The sample size
n\to\infty
while the dimensionality
p\to\infty
, such that the aspect ratio
p/n\to\gamma>0
.

Condition
2
outlines the regularity conditions on
\mathbf{X}
required by technical lemmas in random matrix theory, such as bounded moments and eigenvalues.
Notably, these conditions do not assume a Gaussian distribution for the elements of
\mathbf{X}
and have been shown to be robust to minor potential violations, such as the presence of small eigenvalues, which may arise in practical PRS applications due to the existence of highly correlated genetic variants
(
Zhao et al., 2024b,
)
.

Condition 2
.

We assume
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
. Entries of
\mathbf{X}_{0}
is real-value i.i.d. random variables with mean zero, variance one, and a finite
4
-th order moment. The
\bm{\Sigma}
are
p\times p
population level deterministic positive definite matrices with uniformly bounded eigenvalues. Specifically, we have
0<c\leq\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq C
for all
p
and some constants
c,C
, where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
are the smallest and largest eigenvalues of a matrix, respectively.

To establish the linear model framework, we use random-effect conditions on
\bm{\beta}
, commonly used to model a large number of small genetic effects without imposing sparsity constraints
(
Jiang et al.,, 2016
;
Su et al.,, 2024
)
.
Let
F(0,V)
denote a generic distribution with
mean zero, variance
V
, and a finite
4
-th order moment. We introduce the following conditions on genetic effects.

Condition 3
.

There exist sparsity level
0\leq\kappa\leq 1
and signal strength
\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}>0
such that
the distribution of each coordinate of
\bm{\beta}
, denoted as
\bm{\beta}_{i}
, is an i.i.d. random variable that follows

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{i}\sim(1-\kappa)\delta(0)+\kappa F(0,\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}/p).

Furthermore, as
n,p\to\infty
with
p/n\to\gamma>0
, we assume that
\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\bm{\beta}_{j})^{2}\to\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
.

The following condition imposes similar conditions on the noise vector
\bm{\epsilon}
.

Condition 4
.

Random errors in
\bm{\epsilon}
are independent random variables and each coordinate has the following distribution

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}F(0,\sigma_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{2}),\quad\mbox{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

Two types of summary-level data are typically used for training prediction models. The first relates to the estimation of genetic effects, typically provided as marginal GWAS summary statistics and can be represented as
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}

(
Pasaniuc and Price,, 2017
)
.
In practice, additional associated summary statistics, such as the variance of genetic effect estimates,
P
-values, and minor allele frequencies, are also commonly shared along with
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
.
The second is information about the linkage disequilibrium (LD) pattern,
\bm{\Sigma}
, which can be quantified by
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}
. However, in practice,
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}
is not often shared and is thus not publicly available. To address this, researchers typically use an external reference panel
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{w}\times p}
as a substitute for
\mathbf{X}
, with
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
serving as an approximation for
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}
in LD estimation. One of the most popular reference panels is the 1000 Genomes
(
1000-Genomes-Consortium,, 2015
)
.
Therefore, the summary-level data considered in this paper are associated with
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
.
Condition
5
assumes that the reference panel
\mathbf{W}
satisfies similar conditions to those imposed on
\mathbf{X}
.

Condition 5
.

We assume
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{0}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{w}\times p}
. Each entry of
\mathbf{W}_{0}
is real-value i.i.d. as that of
\mathbf{X}_{0}
. In addition, the sample size
n_{w}\to\infty
while the dimensionality
p\to\infty
, such that the aspect ratio
p/n_{w}\to\gamma_{w}>0
.

We define the heritability in genetics as follows, representing the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic predictors.
Intuitively, a larger heritability implies a higher signal-to-noise ratio
(
Dobriban and Wager,, 2018
)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Conditional on
\bm{\beta}
, the heritability
h^{2}
of the training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
is defined as

h^{2}=\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\mathrm{var}(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta})/\mathrm{var}(\mathbf{y})=\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\bm{\beta}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}/(\bm{\beta}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\epsilon}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\bm{\epsilon})
.
Thus, we have
h^{2}\in[0,1]
.
As
n,p\to\infty
with
p/n\to\gamma>0
,
h^{2}
can be asymptotically represented as

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{2}=\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\frac{\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}{\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}+\sigma_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{2}}=\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}\cdot\textrm{trace}(\bm{\Sigma})/p}{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}\cdot\textrm{trace}(\bm{\Sigma})/p+\sigma_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{2}}.\end{split}

(2.2)

2.2
Model training and performance measures

In this section, we model the processes of individual-level and summary data-based model training and outline the objectives of this paper.

2.2.1
Individual data-based model training

We first introduce the prediction estimators and their accuracy measures.
We begin with the conventional case where individual-level data is accessible and assume that model development involves splitting the whole dataset (
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{y}
) into two independent subsets: a training dataset
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}})
and a validation dataset
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
.
We model the data-splitting procedure as follows: given the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
, we sample the training dataset by considering a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{Q}={\rm diag}\{q_{1},q_{2},\cdots q_{n}\}
, where
q_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\textnormal{Bernoulli}(n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}/n)
for some
n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
that is proportional to and smaller than
n
.
Let
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{X}
, with
\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
being defined accordingly.
By Condition
2
,
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}})
is conditionally independent from
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
.
One can train a general estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
on
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}})
and evaluate its prediction performance on
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
using the out-of-sample
R^{2}
, given by

R_{\rm ind,G}^{2}(\theta)=\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)\rangle^{2}/\{\|\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}\}
.
The out-of-sample
R^{2}
is a widely used measure of prediction accuracy in genetic data prediction
(
Ma and Zhou,, 2021
)
and is closely related to the mean squared error
(
Su et al.,, 2024
)
.

In this paper,
we consider a class of general estimators
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
that are linear with respect to the summary statistic
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and parameterized by the scale or vector
\theta
, possibly incorporating the reference panel data
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
. This is because we aim to model the real-world scenario where summary statistics from the training dataset,
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, are often publicly available, and the general estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
is trained using independent individual-level validation data
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
, possibly with external LD reference panel.
Specifically, we define
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
as

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}.

(2.3)

Here
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a matrix depending on
\theta
and potentially on
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
.
We denote the estimator as
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
for a general scale or vector
\theta
.
In subsequent sections, we may use
\Theta
to explicitly denote a vector parameter. For each specific estimator analyzed below, we will clearly specify the domain of
\theta
or
\Theta
.
The out-of-sample
R^{2}
of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{\rm ind,G}^{2}(\theta)=\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)\right\rangle^{2}}{\|\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}+o_{p}(1).\end{split}

(2.4)

In the model training process, one aims to find the best hyperparameter by choosing the

\theta
that maximizes
R_{\rm ind,G}^{2}(\theta)
in (
2.4
), as specified in Algorithm
S.1
of the
supplementary material.

Estimators in the form of Equation (
2.3
) and their variants are widely used in PRS applications
(
Power et al.,, 2015
;
Privé et al.,, 2019
;
Ge et al.,, 2019
;
Ma and Zhou,, 2021
)
.
An example is the ridge-type estimator, which can be formulated as

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)=(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}

(2.5)

for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
In the case of the ridge-type estimator, the best-performing hyperparameter in
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
is selected by optimizing the following expression

\displaystyle\theta^{*}_{\rm ind,R}=\arg\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta),\quad\text{with}\quad R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\cdot\|(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}.

It is worth noting that since
n^{\textnormal{(v)}}
and
\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}
are constant across all
\theta
, the key contribution of the validation data
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
to the training process is through the item
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
. As detailed in later sections, this key insight motivates the summary data-based model training approach.

2.2.2
Summary data-based model training

Now we consider the practical scenario where only summary statistics,
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
, are available, rather than having access to
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
,
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
, and individual-level data
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
.
It follows that we are not able to obtain the
R_{\rm ind,G}^{2}(\theta)
defined in Equation (
2.4
).

We model the resampling-based self-training procedure with
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
as follows.
Given the summary statistic
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
, we sample pseudo-training and validation summary statistics,
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
, from the approximate distributions of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}

. These sampled statistics are expected to closely mimic what would be obtained if individual-level data were available. For example, when only summary statistics are available, the ridge-type estimator is now trained on
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)^{*}=(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}

for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

in place of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
, respectively.
To compare the performance of Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
S.1
, we consider
the ratio

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta).

When
R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)
converges to
1
for any
\theta
as
n
and
p\to\infty
, we say Algorithm
1
has no additional prediction accuracy cost compared to Algorithm
S.1
and expect the two algorithms return the same best-performing hyperparameter, that is, we have
\theta^{*}_{\rm sum,G}=\theta^{*}_{\rm ind,G}
.

2.3
Fixed-dimension intuition of Algorithm
1

Before presenting the formal results in the next section, we first outline the key intuitions behind resampling-based self-training when it is applied in practical contexts in precision medicine and genetic research
(
Jin et al.,, 2025
)
.
Notably, while the PRS applications of the self-training algorithm lie in high-dimensional genetic predictors, its underlying intuition originates from classical fixed-dimension arguments. However, as with many statistical phenomena, we find that the simplicity of fixed-dimension intuition of self-training does not directly extend to high-dimensional settings, where increased complexity invalidates the original reasoning. The continued effectiveness of the self-training algorithm in high-dimensional data suggests the presence of deeper underlying factors, which require investigation using entirely different approaches and technical tools, such as the random matrix theory used in our formal analysis.

We begin by closely examining the measure
R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)
. Due to the linear structure in marginal summary statistics, we always have the data split
{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}}=\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}+o_{p}(1).

Therefore, conditional on the summary data
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}
, we only need to resample once to obtain
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
as an approximation of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, and can then set
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}=\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.
In practice, Algorithm
1
samples
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
from a
p
-dimensional Gaussian random variable with mean
(n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}/n)\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
and covariance
[n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}(n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}})]/n^{2}\cdot\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})
, where
\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})
is defined to be

\displaystyle\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})=\left(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-n\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\right)\left(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-n\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\right)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}.

This sampling distribution is chosen based on the fixed-dimension scenario where
p
is fixed, while the sample size
n\to\infty
.
In such a setting, this sampling distribution of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
approaches the limiting distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
according to the multivariate central limit theorem (CLT).
To show this, note that
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}=\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{Q}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}
.
The multivariate CLT implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}-\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(q_{j}\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{y}_{j}-\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{y}_{j}\right)\overset{d}{\to}N(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}),

where
\bm{\Sigma}
is the covariance matrix given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}\Cov\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}\mathbf{X}_{j}\mathbf{y}_{j}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}(n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}})}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\Cov\left(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}\right).

It further implies that

n^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}-n^{-1/2}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}=n^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}-n^{-1/2}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\overset{d}{\to}0.

By the continuous mapping theorem, we have

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}\Bigg/\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}\overset{d}{\to}1.

Because converging in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to the constant, we have
R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)\overset{p}{\to}1
.
In summary, when the dimensionality
p
is fixed, Algorithm
1
has no additional prediction accuracy cost compared to Algorithm
S.1
by resampling
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
directly from the asymptotic distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.

While the above fixed
p
discussions provide insight into the rationale behind the construction of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
in Algorithm
1
, it is important to note that these CLT-based derivations in low dimensions cannot be easily extended to middle or high-dimensional applications, such as our PRS genetic data prediction problem, where both
n
and
p\to\infty
, as described in Condition
1
. Briefly, this is because high-dimensional CLT is much more complex, and general results across the parameter space typically do not exist
(
Chernozhukov et al.,, 2017
;
Fang and Koike,, 2021
)
.
In other words, the
p
-dimensional Gaussian distribution, from which
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
is sampled, is no longer the asymptotic distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.
Therefore, new tools and proof framework are required to evaluate the relative performance of Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
S.1
in high dimensions.

Despite this mismatch in distributions, a closer examination of
R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)
and
R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)
suggests that we may only need the following approximations to hold

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}\left\langle\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{Q}}\left\langle\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle.

This observation suggests that achieving the same prediction accuracy may only require the first and second moments of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
to match those of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, rather than matching the entire distribution.
Rigorous analyses of these approximations are provided in the next section, with the proof relying heavily on random matrix theory
(
Bai and Silverstein,, 2010
;
Yao et al.,, 2015
)
and deterministic equivalents
(
Dobriban and Sheng,, 2021
)
. We derive the closed-form asymptotic results for
R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\theta)
and
R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\theta)
, demonstrating that the no-cost property of Algorithm
1
remains valid in high dimensions without requiring the sampling distribution of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
to be the asymptotic distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.

3
Asymptotic results of self-training

In this section, we present random matrix theory results to demonstrate that self-training with
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
can achieve the same asymptotic predictive accuracy as using
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}})
in high dimensions. In Section
3.1
, we examine the reference panel-based ridge estimator defined in Equation (
2.5
). The marginal thresholding estimator is evaluated in Section
3.2
. These two concrete examples are motivated by the widely used ridge-type estimators and thresholding procedures in genetic risk prediction
(
Ge et al.,, 2019
;
Choi et al.,, 2020
)
.
In Section
3.3
, we further provide a general theorem for the class of linear estimators defined in Equation (
2.3
).

3.1
Resampling-based ridge estimator

We quantify the out-of-sample performance of reference panel-based ridge estimators
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
in Equation (
2.5
) and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)^{*}
in Equation (
2.6
) for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, denoted as
R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
and
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
, respectively.
Our analysis mainly relies on deterministic equivalents
(
Dobriban and Sheng,, 2021
)
, which is a recent tool established based on standard random matrix theory
(
Bai and Silverstein,, 2010
)
.
For deterministic or random matrix sequences
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
n,p\to\infty
proportionally, we say
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{E}
are deterministic equivalent and denote this as
\mathbf{D}\asymp\mathbf{E}
if

\lim_{n,p\to\infty}\left|\textrm{trace}\left[\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{E})\right]\right|=0

almost surely for any sequence
\mathbf{C}
of matrices with bounded trace norm, that is

\displaystyle\lim\sup_{n,p\to\infty}\textrm{trace}\left[(\mathbf{C}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{C})^{1/2}\right]<\infty.

(3.1)

Here
\mathbf{C}
,
\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{E}
are not necessarily symmetric.
We present the detailed results on deterministic equivalence in Section
S.2
of the supplementary material.
The following lemma provides the first and second-order generalization of the classic Marchenko-Pastur Law for the sample covariance matrix.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}=\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}/n
.
Under Conditions
1
-
2
, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with probability one, we have

(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\asymp(\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}

and

\begin{split}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\left(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\asymp(\rho_{n}(\theta)+1)\cdot\left(\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\left(\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Here
\tau_{n}(\theta)
and
\rho_{n}(\theta)\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
are solutions to the fixed point equations

\tau_{n}(\theta)^{-1}=1+\frac{1}{n}\textrm{trace}\left[\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}+\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\right]

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\theta)=\left(1-\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}(\theta)}{n}\textrm{trace}\left[(\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-2}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\right)^{-1}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}(\theta)}{n}\textrm{trace}\left[(\tau_{n}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-2}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right].

(3.4)

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is based on the generalized Marchenko-Pastur Law (e.g.,
Rubio and Mestre, (2011)
) and Lemma
S.2.3
in Section
S.2
of the
supplementary material, stating that differentiation and deterministic equivalence are interchangeable.
Lemma
3.2
below is also crucial to our proof, as it establishes the asymptotic results for the trace of a matrix structure involving
\mathbf{C}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}^{2}
. The detailed proof is included in Section
S.2.2
of the
supplementary material.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any deterministic positive semi-definite symmetric matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
satisfying Equation (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{C}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}^{2}\right)-\left\{\frac{1}{n}\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Sigma}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\right\}\overset{p}{\to}0.

(3.5)

Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
are used to analyze the limits of functionals involved in ridge-type estimators.
Theorem
3.3
below provides the asymptotic prediction accuracy of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)^{*}
respectively trained by Algorithm
S.1
and Algorithm
1
, demonstrating that Algorithm
1
has no additional cost compared to Algorithm
S.1
for reference panel-based ridge regression.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider any random sequence
\{\bm{\beta},\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}\}_{(p,n,n_{w})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}}
satisfying Conditions
1
-
5
and any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)^{*}
defined in Equation (
2.6
),
the out-of-sample
R^{2}
is

R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{p}\cdot\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}\cdot\frac{\left(\textrm{trace}\left[\left(\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\right)^{2}}{\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{B}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}\right)/h^{2}+n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{B}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right)}+o_{p}(1),

where
\mathbf{B}(\theta)=(\rho_{n_{w}}(\theta)+1)\left(\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\left(\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}
is the right-hand side of Equation (
3.2
),
and
\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)
and
\rho_{n_{w}}(\theta)
are defined in Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) by replacing
n
by
n_{w}
, respectively.
Furthermore, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)\overset{p}{\to}1.

Theorem
3.3
establishes the asymptotic prediction accuracy in resampling-based self-training and demonstrates its equivalence to conventional training when individual-level data are available.
By the definitions of
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
and
R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
, we have

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)\propto\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{2}\cdot\|(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)\propto\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle^{2}}{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{2}\cdot\|(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}}.

For
R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\textnormal{R}}(\theta)
is trained on
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}})
, and the out-of-sample
R_{\rm ind,R}^{2}(\theta)
is calculated on an independent dataset
(\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}},\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
.
In contrast, when computing
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
, the resampling-based pseudo-training and validation summary statistics
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
are inherently dependent. Surprisingly, Theorem
3.3
demonstrates that
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)/R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)\overset{p}{\to}1
, suggesting that tuning parameters using dependent pseudo-training/validation datasets does not lead to overfitting or reduced out-of-sample prediction performance in high dimensions. Below, we provide a proof sketch to provide insights into this counterintuitive result.

Proof sketch of Theorem
3.3
.

By the continuous mapping theorem, it suffices to show that the numerator and denominator of
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
are asymptotically equal to that of
R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
, respectively.
We take the numerator as an example and sketch the proof of Equation (
3.6
) below.

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}=\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}+o_{p}(1)

(3.6)

Using Lemma
3.1
and Lemma B.26 from
Bai and Silverstein, (2010)
, we can conclude the right-hand side of Equation (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle{\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(v)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},\right.&\left.(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}\\
&\qquad\qquad=\left\{\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[\left(\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\right\}+o_{p}(1).\end{split}

(3.7)

To show that the left-hand side of Equation (
3.6
) converges to the same limit, we first decompose it and omit the zero-limit cross terms, leaving three non-zero terms as follows

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}\right)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\left(\frac{n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}\right)/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\bm{\epsilon}\right)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\left(\frac{n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n}\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\bm{\epsilon}\right)/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}

\displaystyle-\left(\sqrt{\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}(n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}})}{n^{2}}}\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})^{1/2}\mathbf{h}\right)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}(n-n^{\textnormal{(tr)}})}{n^{2}}}\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})^{1/2}\mathbf{h}\right)/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}

\displaystyle=\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}-\text{I}^{(2)}_{\rm sum}.

We group the first two terms as
\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}
.
Intuitively,
\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}
can be interpreted as a “dependence-induced term” since it arises from the dependence between
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
, which is absent from the right-hand side of Equation (
3.6
), where the training and validation datasets are independent.
Furthermore, we refer to
\text{I}^{(2)}_{\rm sum}
as a “compensatory term”, as we will demonstrate that its limiting behavior precisely offsets the dependence-induced term.
Lemma B.26 in
Bai and Silverstein, (2010)
allows us to reorganize
\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}
as

\displaystyle\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}=\

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{2}}{n}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}\right]+o_{p}(1)

where
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}=\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}/n_{w}
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\text{I}^{(2)}_{\rm sum}=\

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})\right]+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-n\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\right)\left(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}-n\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\right)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\right]+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{2}}{n}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}-\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{n_{w}}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]+o_{p}(1).

By Lemma
3.1
, the limit of the left-hand side of Equation (
3.6
) is

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}},(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta n_{w}\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right\rangle/n^{\textnormal{(v)}}=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[\left(\tau_{n_{w}}(\theta)\bm{\Sigma}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]+o_{p}(1),

which exactly matches with Equation (
3.7
).
∎

The proof sketch of Theorem
3.3
provides several key insights.
First, the limiting behavior of
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
depends solely on the first and second-order moments of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, rather than its entire distribution. This observation suggests that resampling-based algorithms may not be sensitive to the selected resampling distribution and
matching only the first and second moments of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
to those of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
is sufficient.
Indeed, the Gaussian distribution may not be the asymptotic distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
in high dimensions. In Algorithm
1
, if
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
is sampled by replacing Gaussian
\mathbf{h}
to another
p
-dimensional random variables whose coordinates are i.i.d. with mean zero and variance one, Algorithm
1
may still have robust output and achieve the same performance as Algorithm
S.1
.

Furthermore, our analysis explains why the interdependence between
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
does not result in overfitting.
From the moment perspective,
\text{I}^{(1)}_{\rm sum}
can be regarded as a “first-moment dependence term” since it arises solely from the first moment,
n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}/n\cdot\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
in Algorithm
1
. If we match only the first moment of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
with that of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
,
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
would have inflation compared to
R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
. However,
\text{I}^{(2)}_{\rm sum}
acts as a “second-moment correction” term, originating from the matched second moment
\Cov(\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y})
in
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
. The limiting behavior of
\text{I}^{(2)}_{\rm sum}
precisely cancels out this inflation, ensuring that the resulting
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
aligns exactly with the right-hand side of Equation (
3.6
). Thus, the dependence between
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
does not negatively impact prediction accuracy. Our proof sketch focuses on the numerator for illustration. A similar behavior is observed in the denominator, where Lemma
3.2
is additionally required to analyze the involved functionals. The detailed proof of Theorem
3.3
is provided in Section
S.3
of the supplementary material.

In summary, our analysis demonstrates that even without access to individual-level data, predictive models can be trained and tuned by splitting high-dimensional summary statistics
\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
using a resampling-based approach.
If the first and second moments of the sampling distribution are properly specified,
the resulting
R^{2}_{\rm sum,R}(\theta)
, computed from summary statistics, will be asymptotically identical to

R^{2}_{\rm ind,R}(\theta)
based on individual-level data.
Consequently, the same optimal hyperparameter can be selected, regardless of whether individual data is available. In other words, asymptotically, Algorithm
1
returns the same prediction model as Algorithm
S.1
.

3.2
Resampling-based marginal thresholding

One of the most popular approaches for genetic prediction is “clumping and thresholding”
(
Choi et al.,, 2020
)
. After removing genetic variants with high correlations with other predictors, this marginal thresholding approach ranks the genetic variants by their
P
-values (or equivalent statistics) and selects the best-performing
P
-value threshold based on prediction accuracy in the validation data.
In prediction models with non-sparse signals, there is often a trade-off in marginal screening when selecting an optimal subset of variables to maximize the prediction accuracy
(
Zhao and Zhu,, 2022
)
. Selecting too many variables can reduce the out-of-sample
R^{2}
by introducing noise and overfitting, as variants without predictive power are included. Conversely, selecting too few variables can also lower the out-of-sample
R^{2}
by excluding important predictors. In resampling-based
pseudo-training, we aim to select the
P
-value threshold without access to individual-level validation data.

When individual-level data are available, the marginal thresholding estimator can be formulated as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm M}(\Theta)=\left[\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right]_{\Theta}:=\begin{cases}\left[\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right]_{i}&\text{if}\ i\in\Theta,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where the hyperparameter
\Theta\subset[p]
denotes the indexes of predictors, a subset of
[p]
, that to be selected by the marginal screening
P
-value threshold.
When only summary statistics are available, we can similarly define
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm M}(\Theta)^{*}
by replacing
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
by
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm M}(\Theta)^{*}=\left[\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right]_{\Theta}:=\begin{cases}\left[\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\right]_{i}&\text{if}\ i\in\Theta,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3.8)

Here we use
\Theta
to emphasize that it is a vector parameter.
Considering the general estimator defined in Equation (
2.3
), we can rewrite

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\Theta)_{i,i}=1\ \text{if}\ i\in\Theta,\quad\mathbf{A}(\Theta)_{i,j}=0\ \text{otherwise}.

(3.9)

We abbreviate
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)

\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}

3.3
Asymptotic results for general linear estimators

After analyzing the two concrete examples popular in PRS applications,
we now present a general theorem for the class of linear estimators
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)
defined in Equation (
2.3
), with a parameter vector
\Theta
.
When only summery statistics are available, we define the
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)^{*}
by replacing
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
to
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)^{*}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}.

(3.10)

Our analysis of this general estimator indicates that it is not coincidental that Algorithm
1
incurs no additional prediction accuracy cost compared to Algorithm
S.1
for both
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm M}(\Theta)
.
The key lies in the component
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)
.
Briefly, as long as
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)
satisfies certain first and second-order deterministic equivalent conditions, Algorithm
1
can achieve the same asymptotic prediction accuracy and select the same hyperparameter as Algorithm
S.1
, without requiring individual-level data.
We summarize these results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

Consider any random sequence
\{\bm{\beta},\bm{\epsilon},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}\}_{(p,n,n_{w})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}}
satisfying Conditions
1
-
5
and any parameter vector
\Theta
.
Suppose the sequence
\{\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)\}
satisfies

\displaystyle n_{w}\cdot\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)\asymp\mathbf{D}(\Theta)\quad\mbox{and}\quad n_{w}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)\asymp\mathbf{E}(\Theta),

(3.11)

for any
\Theta
and some matrices
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{E}
depending on
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\Theta
only. Then for
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)^{*}
defined in Equation (
3.10
),
the out-of-sample
R^{2}
is

R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\Theta)=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{p}\cdot\kappa\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}\cdot\frac{\left(\textrm{trace}\left[\mathbf{D}(\Theta)\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\right)^{2}}{\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[\mathbf{E}(\Theta)\bm{\Sigma}\right]/h^{2}+n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left[\mathbf{E}(\Theta)\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\right]}+o_{p}(1).

Furthermore, for any
\Theta
, we have

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm sum,G}(\Theta)/R^{2}_{\rm ind,G}(\Theta)\overset{p}{\to}1.

The proof of Theorem
3.6
is presented in Section
S.5
of the
supplementary material.
The
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)^{*}
in Equation (
3.10
) replaces
(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+\theta\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}
in
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)^{*}

by a general matrix
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)
, and
Theorem
3.6
demonstrates that if the first and second-order components of
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)
have well-defined limits, then
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm G}(\Theta)^{*}
will exhibit good properties in resampling-based self-training.
Equation (
3.11
) specifies these conditions, requiring the existence of first and second-order deterministic equivalents concerning a sequence of matrices
depending on
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\Theta
.
As demonstrated in our proof, Equation (
3.11
) is a crucial condition for the concentration inequalities in Lemma B.26 of
Bai and Silverstein, (2010)
to hold, thereby making the limiting behavior of the estimators traceable.
For concrete estimators
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm M}(\Theta)
, we have shown that such condition holds for their corresponding
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)
.
For example, to prove Theorem
3.3
for
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm R}(\theta)
, we provide the deterministic equivalents of

n_{w}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+n_{w}\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad n_{w}^{2}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+n_{w}\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}+n_{w}\theta\mathbf{I}_{p}\right)^{-1},

which are given in Lemma
3.1
.

Summary data-based ensemble learning

Ensemble learning aims to combine multiple models to improve prediction accuracy and has been widely applied in genetic prediction
(
Pain et al.,, 2021
;
Yang and Zhou,, 2022
;
Jin et al.,, 2025
)
. Traditional ensemble learning requires access to individual-level data to combine models. In this section, we explore whether the summary data-based model training framework can be extended to perform ensemble learning. We consider ensemble learning as a linear combination of
k
different general linear estimators defined in Equation (
2.3
)
(
Opitz and Maclin,, 1999
;
Chen et al.,, 2024
)
.
Specifically, we can model the ensemble estimator
as

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm E}(\Theta)=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
, where

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{j}\mathbf{A}_{j}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j})

and
hyperparameter vector
\Theta=\{\omega_{j},\Theta_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
for some constant
k
.
When only summary-level data is available, we similarly define
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm E}(\Theta)^{*}
by replacing
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
with
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{\rm E}(\Theta)^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{j}\mathbf{A}_{j}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j})\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}.

(4.1)

When individual-level data are available, Algorithm
S.2

of the supplementary material
details the conventional ensemble learning steps to maximize
R^{2}_{\rm ind,E}(\Theta)
. Algorithm
2
outlines the self-training approach for ensemble learning with summary data only, where
we use the same
(\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}},\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}})
to train
k
different estimators simultaneously, determining both the weights
\omega_{j}
and hyperparameter
\Theta_{j}
that maximize
R^{2}_{\rm sum,E}(\Theta)
.

\mathbf{X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W}

\mathbf{h}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{p}),

//
Sample

\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
for training.

//
Construct
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(v)}}
for validation.

//
Obtain the estimator.

//
Compute the
R^{2}_{\rm sum,E}(\Theta)
in (

).

//

return

R_{\rm sum,E}^{2}(\Theta)
and
\Theta^{*}_{\rm sum,E}
.

4.1
shows that, asymptotically, Algorithm

selects the same hyperparameter as Algorithm

.

Theorem 4.1
.

-
5

\Theta=\{\omega_{j},\Theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
. Suppose the sequence
\{\mathbf{A}_{j}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j})\}
satisfies

\displaystyle n_{w}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{j}\left(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j}\right)\asymp\mathbf{D}_{j}(\Theta)\quad\text{and}\quad n_{w}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{j}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{j}(\mathbf{W}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{W},\Theta_{j})\asymp\mathbf{E}_{j}(\Theta_{j}),

with some matrices
\mathbf{D}_{j}(\Theta_{j})
and

\bm{\Sigma}
and

(

In this section, we present and analyze Algorithm

(5.2)

6
Numerical experiments

We numerically validate our theoretical findings through extensive synthetic data simulations and real data analyses using the UK Biobank
(
Bycroft et al.,, 2018
)
.
The primary goal of the synthetic data analysis is to demonstrate that our theoretical results hold across general settings, while the real data analysis illustrates their specific applicability in real-world genetic predictions. Overall, these complementary analyses strongly support our theoretical findings.
More importantly, we provide additional insights into genetic data prediction applications. For example, we show that nonlinear estimators may also work well with resampling-based self-training. In addition, we find that resampling-based self-training can even outperform conventional individual-level data training when the tuning dataset has a limited sample size.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we develop a statistical framework to establish the properties of self-training approaches using summary data. Notably, we demonstrate that pseudo-training and validation datasets based solely on summary statistics can achieve the same asymptotic predictive accuracy as traditional methods using individual-level training and validation data. We show that these results hold in high-dimensional settings but follow a different rationale than in low-dimensional cases.
In low dimensions, the no-cost property of summary data-based training is attributed to the multivariate CLT, which ensures that pseudo-training/validation datasets share the same limiting distribution as individual-level data.
In high dimensions, however, standard high-dimensional CLT results do not apply
(
Chernozhukov et al.,, 2017
;
Fang and Koike,, 2021
)
.
To address this, we leverage random matrix theory to quantify asymptotic prediction accuracy and show that the no-cost property still holds, even without high-dimensional CLT. Notably, the sampling distribution of
\mathbf{s}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
does not need to be the limiting distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}{}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(tr)}}
.

Our proofs reveal that the key lies in the asymptotic matching of traces between individual-level and summary-level data, requiring only matched moments of the functionals rather than matching distributions.
We also demonstrate the surprising finding that, despite the lack of independence between resampling-based training and validation datasets, this does not lead to overfitting or reduced out-of-sample performance.
Inspired by recent trends in genetic data analysis, we extend our work to include algorithms and theoretical analyses for ensemble learning
(
Pain et al.,, 2021
;
Yang and Zhou,, 2022
)
and multi-ancestry data analysis
(
Kachuri et al.,, 2024
;
Zhang et al.,, 2023
;
Zhang et al.,, 2024
;
Jin et al.,, 2024
)
. In summary, these results offer deeper insights into the theoretical underpinnings of self-training with summary data and may support the broader application of self-training algorithms in fields that utilize shared summary data.

As the first statistical framework to examine the properties of resampling-based self-training,
our study has a few limitations. First, we focus on linear estimators and provide two concrete examples commonly used in genetic and dense-signal predictions
(
Choi et al.,, 2020
;
Ge et al.,, 2019
)
.
While this covers a broad class of estimators, our analysis does not include nonlinear estimators, such as Lasso and Elastic-net, which are also frequently used in similar prediction tasks
(
Mak et al.,, 2017
;
Qian et al.,, 2020
;
Wu et al.,, 2023
;
Wang et al.,, 2024
)
.
This choice is due to our approach of using random matrix theory to derive exact analytical forms for prediction accuracy, which is challenging to apply to nonlinear estimators as they typically lack closed-form solutions
(
Su et al.,, 2024
)
.
However, our real data analyses
indicate that the self-training framework performs well with more complicated nonlinear estimators, such as Lassosum
(
Mak et al.,, 2017
;
Privé et al.,, 2022
)
and LDpred2
(
Vilhjálmsson et al.,, 2015
;
Privé et al.,, 2020
)
, which are specifically designed for genetic prediction.
Thus, our theoretical insights on linear estimators may also extend to many nonlinear and more complicated estimators, which could be better explored in future studies.
Second, although summary data-based training has the no-cost property regarding asymptotic prediction accuracy,
it may exhibit greater variance compared to individual-level data training. Uncertainty analysis within the framework of random matrix theory is still in its early stages
(
Fu et al.,, 2024
)
.
It would be interesting to quantify the uncertainty of summary data-based training and to develop improved resampling strategies to reduce this variability. Such advancements could lead to more efficient self-training of prediction models using summary data.

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}2\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\right)^{2}\frac{\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}\sigma_{i,j}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq j\leq K}\omega_{j}^{2}\cdot\left[\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{j}\sigma_{j,j}^{2}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{h_{j}^{2}}\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)+\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{j}\sigma_{j,j}^{2}}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{2}\right)\right]+o_{p}(1).

It follows that

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm ind,MA}(\theta)=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left(\Lambda_{\rm ind}^{(1)}\right)^{2}}{\Lambda_{\rm ind}^{(2)}},

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm ind}^{(1)}=\

\displaystyle\omega_{1}\cdot\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{1}\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{D}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{2}\right)+\sum_{j=2}^{K}\omega_{j}\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{j}\sigma_{1,j}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)+o_{p}(1)\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm ind}^{(2)}=\

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}2\omega_{i}\omega_{j}\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\right)^{2}\frac{\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}\sigma_{i,j}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq j\leq K}\omega_{j}^{2}\cdot\left[\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{j}\sigma_{j,j}^{2}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{h_{j}^{2}}\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\right)+\left(\frac{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}{n_{w}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{j}\sigma_{j,j}^{2}}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{j}^{2}\right)\right]+o_{p}(1).

Proof of Corollary
5.3
.

When
K=2
, we have

\displaystyle R^{2}_{\rm sum,MA}(\theta)=\frac{n^{\textnormal{(v)}}}{\|\mathbf{y}^{\textnormal{(v)}}\|_{2}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\left[\omega_{1}\cdot N_{1}(\Theta)+(1-\omega_{1})N_{2}(\Theta)\right]^{2}}{\omega_{1}^{2}\cdot D_{1}(\Theta)+(1-\omega_{1})^{2}\cdot D_{2}(\Theta)+2\omega_{1}(1-\omega_{1})\cdot D_{3}(\Theta)},

where

\displaystyle N_{1}(\Theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{1}\sigma_{\bm{\beta}}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{D}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle N_{2}(\Theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\sigma_{1,2}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\right),

\displaystyle D_{1}(\Theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{1}\sigma_{1,1}^{2}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{h_{1}^{2}}\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\right)+\frac{\kappa_{1}\sigma_{1,1}^{2}}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle D_{2}(\Theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{\textnormal{(tr)}}}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{2}\sigma_{2,2}^{2}}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{h_{2}^{2}}\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\right)\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\right)+\frac{\kappa_{2}\sigma_{2,2}^{2}}{p}\textrm{trace}\left(\mathbf{E}_{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}^{2}\right),\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle D_{3}(\Theta)=\

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\sigma_{1,2}^{2}}{p}\cdot\textrm{trace}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}\right).

We abbreviate
N_{1}(\Theta),N_{2}(\Theta),D_{1}(\Theta),D_{2}(\Theta),D_{3}(\Theta)
by
N_{1},N_{2},D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
, respectively.
Taking derivative with respect to
\omega_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R^{2}_{\rm sum,MA}(\theta)}{\partial\omega_{1}}=-\frac{2\left[N_{1}\omega_{1}+N_{2}(1-\omega_{1})\right]\cdot\left[-D_{2}N_{1}(1-\omega_{1})+D_{1}N_{2}\omega_{1}+D_{3}\left(-N_{1}\omega_{1}-N_{2}\omega_{1}+N_{2}\right)\right]}{\left[D_{1}\omega_{1}^{2}+(1-\omega_{1})\left(D_{2}(1-\omega_{1})+2D_{3}\omega_{1}\right)\right]^{2}}.

The denominator above is always positive and
N_{1}\omega_{1}+N_{2}(1-\omega_{1})>0
as
\omega_{1}\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, the only solution to
{\partial R^{2}_{\rm sum}(\theta)}/{\partial\omega_{1}}=0
is given by

\displaystyle\omega_{1}=\frac{D_{2}N_{1}-D_{3}N_{2}}{D_{2}N_{1}-D_{3}N_{1}+D_{1}N_{2}-D_{3}N_{2}}.

Consider the second derivative at
\omega_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R^{2}_{\rm sum,MA}(\theta)}{\partial\omega_{1}}

\displaystyle\Bigg|_{\omega_{1}=\frac{D_{2}N_{1}-D_{3}N_{2}}{D_{2}N_{1}-D_{3}N_{1}+D_{1}N_{2}-D_{3}N_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-\frac{2\left[D_{2}N_{1}+D_{1}N_{2}-D_{3}\left(N_{1}+N_{2}\right)\right]^{4}}{\left(D_{3}^{2}-D_{1}D_{2}\right)^{2}\left(D_{2}N_{1}^{2}+D_{1}N_{2}^{2}-2D_{3}N_{1}N_{2}\right)}<0,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
D_{2}N_{1}^{2}+D_{1}N_{2}^{2}-2D_{3}N_{1}N_{2}>0
.
Therefore, we conclude that the optimal
R^{2}_{\rm sum,MA}(\theta)
is obtained when

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega_{1}=\ &\min\left\{1,\frac{D_{2}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)}{D_{2}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)+D_{1}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)}\right\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\\
\omega_{2}=\ &\max\left\{0,1-\frac{D_{2}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)}{D_{2}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{1}(\Theta)+D_{1}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)-D_{3}(\Theta)N_{2}(\Theta)}\right\}.\end{split}
