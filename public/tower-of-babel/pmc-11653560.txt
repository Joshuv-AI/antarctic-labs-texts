Hookworm infection and its determinants among schoolchildren in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abebaw Setegn, Yenesew Mihret Wondmagegn, Wassie Alemayehu Damtie, Wagaw Abebe, Gebremariam Wulie Geremew, Tekletsadik Tekleslassie Alemayehu, Yilkal Abebaw Wassie, Getasew Kibralew, Setegn Fentahun, Berihun Agegn Mengistie, Gashaw Azanaw Amare
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHookworm infection is a common problem among schoolchildren and mothers in tropical and subtropical countries, causing cognitive impairment, growth retardation in children and anaemia, low birth weight, preterm birth, and intrauterine growth delay in susceptible mothers. Since schoolchildren generally have a higher prevalence due to behavioral and hygiene-related factors, our systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the prevalence and risk factors for Hookworm infection among schoolchildren in Ethiopia.MethodsRelevant studies were retrieved from several search databases. The data were extracted via a standard data extraction format, and STATA version 11 was utilized for the analysis. Funnel plots and Egger's regression tests were used to evaluate publication bias, whereas the I2 test was used to determine heterogeneity. A random effects model meta-analysis was conducted to determine the pooled prevalence and associated factors of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren.ResultsIn this review, 48 studies with 43,285 participants were included. The pooled prevalence of Hookworm among schoolchildren was 13.01% (95% CI: 10.52, 15.51). Hookworm infection was significantly associated with the inability to use protective footwear (OR = 5.90, 95% CI: 2.86, 12.18) and the inability to use latrines (OR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.77, 5.23). Subgroup analysis by diagnostic methods revealed that the highest prevalence of Hookworm was found to be 28.50% (95% CI: 24.09, 32.91) using combinations of Kato-Katz, McMaster, and spontaneous tube sedimentation techniques.ConclusionOur study revealed that a significant prevalence of schoolchildren were affected by Hookworms, with substantial regional variations. This suggests that there is a need for better control measures such as improving hygiene, sanitation, and protective measures such as footwear. In addition, a combination of one or more diagnostic methods should be employed for better detection of parasites. This systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in PROSPERO with reference number CRD42024558542.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10309-z.Keywords: Ethiopia, Hookworm, Meta-analysis, Risk factors, Schoolchildren, Systematic review

Introduction

Intestinal parasites known as Hookworms are a subset of soil-transmitted helminths (STHs), a class of nematode infections that primarily affect people in low- and middle-income countries and infect millions of people globally [1]. The two primary species of Hookworm are Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale. Most Hookworm infections are caused by N. americanus [2, 3].

The burden of Hookworm is still significant worldwide, affecting over 500 million individuals and resulting in yearly productivity losses estimated to be between $7.5 billion and $138.9 billion [4]. The number of Hookworm infections worldwide in 2017 was estimated to be 845,000 disabled-adjusted life years (DALYs) [5].

This parasite is mostly associated with iron deficiency anaemia, hypoalbuminemia, and malnutrition, which can result in more subtle chronic health issues, such as intellectual and physical growth retardation in children and adverse pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women [6, 7]. In sub-Saharan Africa, Hookworm infection is the primary cause of anaemia. A. duodenale consumes approximately 150 mL of blood daily, whereas N. americanus only consumes 30 mL. Hookworms can have long-term effects on children's development and cognitive function; they reduce children's academic achievement by 20% [8, 9].

Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest infection load and intensity, followed by Asia, Latin America, and the Caribbean [10]. It is estimated that 7 million pregnant women and 40–50 million school-age children in sub-Saharan Africa are infected with Hookworm parasites [11]. Ethiopia has the third-highest Hookworm infection rate in sub-Saharan Africa, with over 11 million people affected [11, 12].

In general, more than 267 million pre-schoolers and over 568 million school-age children reside in regions where the parasite is highly transmissible, necessitating medical care and preventative measures [13]. This can be due to unhygienic bathroom routines, eating with dirty hands, playing in or handling infected soils, and drinking and consuming contaminated food and water, respectively [14]. Research indicates that only 53% of individuals in lower to middle-income countries have access to better sanitation facilities, suggesting that these countries feature more likely low cleanliness levels [15].

Numerous environmental, socioeconomic, and geographical factors explain the differences in the distributions and prevalence of species of Hookworm parasites across different regions. Age, sex, access to latrine, wearing shoes, education level, mothers' education level, low household income, poor personal hygiene, hand washing habits, residence, and water supply affect the risk of Hookworm infection differently [9, 16].

Although numerous original studies have been conducted on Hookworm infection and its potential risk factors among Ethiopian schoolchildren, inconsistent results have been reported. To assess the overall prevalence of Hookworm infection and determine potential risk factors contributing to infection among schoolchildren (5–18 years old) at the national level, it is necessary to systematically summarize data from existing epidemiological studies.

Combining the results of these studies from different regions of the country will generate reliable evidence on the pooled prevalence and determinants of Hookworm infections among these high-risk groups of the population to evaluate the nation's continuing control and preventive measures. Therefore, estimating the pooled prevalence and determinants of Hookworm infection among Ethiopian schoolchildren aged 5–18 years was the primary objective of this systematic and meta-analysis. The results of this study will also provide baseline data for programmers, researchers, clinicians, health managers, and other stakeholders to develop appropriate interventions such as chemotherapy and improved sanitation practices.

Methods

Study protocol

To conduct this systematic review and meta-analysis, we used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist and guidelines [17]. Our search strategy and selection of studies for the review were conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Supplementary file: 1). The protocol of this systematic review and meta-analysis was also registered in PROSPERO (Reference number: CRD42024558542; date: 26/06/2024. However, ethical clearance was not applicable for this review. We used the condition, context, and population (CoCoPop) method to establish the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Search strategy

The search was carried out in Medline via PubMed, Google Scholar, Research4Life, Scopus, African-wider, and EMBASE via search terms such as (Prevalence OR magnitude OR incidence OR epidemiology OR distribution OR burden OR rate of infection) AND (Determinants OR factors OR risk factors OR causes OR contributing factors OR socioeconomic factors OR environmental factors) AND (Hookworm infection OR Ancylostoma duodenale infection OR Necator americanus infection OR helminth infection OR soil-transmitted helminth infection OR STH infection OR geohelminth infection OR nematodes OR parasitic infection) AND (School-going children OR primary school children OR secondary school children OR children of school age OR grade-school children OR schoolchildren OR school-attending children OR Children aged 5–18 years) AND Ethiopia. The Boolean operators "AND" and "OR" were used to combine these key terms. Medical subject headings or MeSH terms were used to search PubMed for relevant original articles. The article search was not limited by publication year but was restricted to only human studies published in the English language. Using references from retrieved articles and related systematic reviews, a manual search for other relevant studies was also carried out to find any additional articles.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

We considered observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies) and articles published in peer-reviewed journals in the English language. However, our search was not restricted by publication year. We included original articles on the prevalence and/or determinants of Hookworm infection in schoolchildren in Ethiopia, ranging from 5–18 years old, as well as studies on the prevalence of any soil-transmitted helminth infection (STH) with Hookworm prevalence within the specified age range. This review also included gray literature or unpublished studies. Parasite diagnosis might be based on McMaster procedures, Flotac procedures, wet mounts, formol-ether concentrations, and Kato-Katz thick fecal smears.

Exclusion criteria

Previous literature reviews, studies on the prevalence of Hookworm infection that lacked sufficient information or did not include outcomes of interest (prevalence of Hookworm infection or adjusted odds ratio [AOR] at 95% CI), clinical trials, case reports, case series, studies that compared the sensitivity and specificity of various diagnostic techniques for Hookworms and STHs including Hookworms, and studies that did not clearly state the age range of the study participants. Additionally, studies that reported the prevalence of Hookworm infection in individuals with human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, tuberculosis, malaria, and other chronic illnesses, as well as in pre-schoolers and students undergoing treatment, were not included in this systematic review or meta-analysis.

Study selection

All the searched studies were imported into the reference management program Endnote × 9 based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria so that duplicate studies could be removed. Following the removal of duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles were independently evaluated by two reviewers to identify any papers that may be eligible and any for which a determination could not be made based on only the title and abstract. The selected full texts of the remaining articles were subsequently examined for eligibility.

Quality appraisal

A checklist for critical assessment from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) was employed to assess the quality of the cross-sectional and case–control studies included in this review [18]. Two authors used the JBI critical evaluation checklist to evaluate the quality of this review. There are nine items on this quality assessment checklist, numbered 0 through 9 (0–4 = low, 5–7 = medium, and 8 and above = good quality). The final analysis included both medium- and high-quality articles (greater than or equal to 5) (Supplementary file: 2). Other authors were consulted when there was a disagreement between two independent authors while the caliber of each article was assessed to resolve the discrepancy.

Outcome variable measurement

The primary objective of this study was to estimate the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection in Ethiopia. The prevalence was determined by dividing the number of study participants who tested positive for Hookworms by the total number of study participants and then multiplying the result by 100. The second objective of the review was to determine the factors that contribute to Hookworm infections, which were assessed via the adjusted odds ratios from the original studies.

Data extraction

A Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was used to retrieve the data, and the extraction protocol was prepared and evaluated by all authors. Two authors (AS and YMW) independently extracted all the pertinent data from the eligible articles via a standard data extraction procedure. The following information was extracted from each published article: the name of the author and year of publication, study region, target population, age range, study design, sample size, diagnostic techniques used, prevalence of Hookworm infection, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) of factors associated with Hookworm infection. Any disagreement between the two authors through the data extraction process was solved by discussion with the third and fourth authors (WAD and WA) to reach a consensus. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess the reliability of the meta-analytic results.

Statistical analysis

The extracted data were exported to STATA 11 statistical software for analysis. Text and tables were used to present the data. Forest plots were employed to estimate the pooled effect size and each study's effect together with its confidence interval (CI) to provide a graphic representation of the data. The prevalence of heterogeneity among the original studies was examined via a Higgins I-square (I2) test. The heterogeneity of the included studies was categorized as no, low, moderate, or high based on I-square values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively [19].

If heterogeneity existed, Der Simonian and Laird's random-effects meta-analysis model was used to determine the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection in the Ethiopian context. Meta-regression analysis was done to examine the impact of variables, such as publication year and sample size, on the effect sizes of the outcome variable. This analysis provided insights into variables that might account for the heterogeneity observed among the included studies. Furthermore, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the possible reason for heterogeneity in the pooled meta-analysis. The pooled estimates for Hookworm infection were then shown with matching 95% confidence intervals via tables and forest plots.

Publication bias

Egger's test and funnel plot symmetry were used to evaluate the risk of publication bias across the included studies. A funnel plot with a symmetrical, large inverted funnel has shown that the probability of publication bias was less likely. Statistical regression methods, such as Egger's test, were employed to complement the graphical assessment, and a p-value greater than 5% was considered to indicate the absence of significant publication bias in all the included articles.

Subgroup analysis and heterogeneity

We conducted subgroup and sensitivity analyses to determine the reason or the causes of heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis was carried out across the regions of the country where the studies were conducted and parasite diagnostic methods involved in the included studies. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out approach to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection.

Results

Selection of studies

All 5870 articles were identified from various electronic databases, including manual searches. Of these, 3264 duplicate articles were removed. Moreover, 2120 articles were removed because their titles and abstracts did not meet our inclusion criteria. For example, our outcome variable was unreported, and full texts were unavailable. After we reviewed the remaining 486 articles based on our specified inclusion criteria and quality assessment, 48 articles were included in the final analysis of this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study selection for meta-analysis of Hookworm infection and its determinants among schoolchildren in Ethiopia

Characteristics of the included studies

Overall, 48 eligible articles from five different regions of the country with 43,285 study participants were retrieved and included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The sample size ranges from 303 [20] to 15,455 [21]. Concerning the diagnostic methods, 5 studies used direct wet mounts, 15 studies used the Kato–Katz technique, 8 studies employed the formol-ether concentration technique, 2 studies used Ritchie’s concentration method, and 2 studies employed the McMaster technique. The remaining studies used a combination of different diagnostic procedures, such as two studies that used both wet mounts and Kato-Katz, one study that used both wet mounts and McMaster, eight studies that used wet mounts and formol-ether concentration techniques, four studies that used Kato-Katz and formol-ether concentration techniques, and one study that used Kato-Katz McMaster and spontaneous tube sedimentation techniques.

Concerning the distributions of studies across the regions, 22 studies were conducted in the Amhara region, 16 were from the Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples’ Region (NNPR), 8 were from the Oromia region, 1 was from the Tigray region, and the remaining study was from the Harari region (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren

The pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren in Ethiopia was 13.01% (95% CI: 10.52, 15.51) (Fig. 2). As shown in the forest plot, each row represents individual studies included in the review. Individual studies included demonstrated significant variations in the prevalence of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren. The black squares on the horizontal lines indicate the prevalence of Hookworm infection, or the effect size (ES), while the horizontal lines represent the 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the effect sizes, with wider lines reflecting greater uncertainty in the point estimates. In this review, the lowest prevalence was reported in a study conducted in the Oromia region (2.10%) [52]; while three studies from different areas within the Amhara region recorded the highest prevalence of infection (46.90%) [22, 29, 63].

Forest plot of pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren in Ethiopia

Moreover, the diamond at the bottom represents the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection, with its width indicating the 95% CI of the pooled estimate. This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed statistically significant heterogeneity among the included studies via a statistical test (I2 = 98.7%, p-value = 0.000). Therefore, a random effects model was used to determine the pooled prevalence. Furthermore, to explore the potential sources of this heterogeneity, subgroup and meta-regression analyses were performed.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was performed in this systematic review and meta-analysis based on the regions where the included studies were conducted and parasite diagnostic techniques employed. The subgroup analysis results revealed that the highest pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection was found in the Amhara region at 17.51% (95% CI: 13.20, 21.83), and the lowest was found in the Harari region at 3.30% (95% CI: 1.38, 5.22). Concerning parasite diagnostic methods used, eligible articles included in our systematic review and meta-analysis employed six parasitological diagnostic methods individually or in combination. Subgroup analysis of these diagnostic methods revealed that; the highest pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection was found using combinations of three different techniques such as; the Kato-Katz, McMaster, and spontaneous tube sedimentation techniques with 28.50% (95% CI: 24.09, 32.91), which were used in only a single study. Whereas the lowest pooled prevalence was found 5.25% (95% CI: 3.38, 7.13) when combinations of wet mount and formol-ether concentration techniques were used, which were employed in eight separate studies (Table 2).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Our funnel plot appears relatively asymmetrical, with more data points representing individual studies concentrated on one side of the funnel (Fig. 3). This suggests the potential presence of publication bias among the studies included in the review. However, Egger’s statistical test indicated no significant risk of bias in the included studies (p = 0.577) (Table 4).

Associated factors of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren

Although numerous factors have been identified as determinants of Hookworm infection among schoolchildren, this systematic review and meta-analysis focused on factors reported in at least two primary studies. Accordingly, the following variables were included to assess their association with Hookworm infection: eating raw vegetables, shoe-wearing habits, latrine utilization, and handwashing practices. The combined pooled odds ratio was calculated for these factors frequently associated with Hookworm infection among schoolchildren.

Five studies were included in the meta-analysis to assess the association between Hookworm infection and the shoe-wearing habits of schoolchildren. The random effects model estimate revealed that participants who did not wear shoes had 5.90 times higher odds of being infected with Hookworm compared to those who wore shoes (AOR = 5.90, 95% CI: 2.86–12.18). In addition, a meta-analysis of two studies showed that the inability to use a latrine was significantly associated with Hookworm infection. Participants who did not use a latrine had 3.04 times higher odds of being infected with Hookworm infection compared to those who properly utilized a latrine (AOR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.77–5.23) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Hookworms are soil-transmitted nematodes that can live in the small intestine of their human hosts for many years. They feed on blood, and in people with a moderate to high number of adult worms, they can induce iron deficiency anaemia (IDA), a condition known as Hookworm disease [68]. The two Hookworm species that most frequently infect people are Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale. However, N. americanus is far more prevalent in Ethiopia [69].To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of Hookworm infection and its determinants in Ethiopian schoolchildren. This review included epidemiological studies to determine risk factors for interventions and to estimate the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection. We, therefore, anticipate that our findings will assist stakeholders and policymakers in developing suitable control measures for Hookworm infection in Ethiopia.

The overall pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection among Ethiopian schoolchildren was 13.01% (95% CI: 10.52, 15.51). The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis are similar to those of a study conducted in southern Thailand (10.7%) [70]. However, the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection in this review was lower than studies conducted in Indonesia (28%) [71], India (20%) [72], Cambodia (19%) [73], Côte d’Ivoire (43.3%) [74], Ghana (39.1%) [75], two studies in Nigeria (72.5%) [76], (40.9%) [77], southern Sierra Leone (41.7%) [78], three studies conducted in different regions of Kenya (58.7%) [79], (26.1%) [80], (24.3%) [81], and Zanzibar (94%) [82]. The observed disagreement may result from variations in environmental factors such as soil type, climate, and rainfall patterns and cultural variations such as shoe-wearing habits, variations in the study design, and sample size, as most of those studies were community-based and employed a larger number of study participants.

Conversely, the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis were larger than those of studies performed in Indonesia (6.7%) [83], northern Côte d’Ivoire (1.5%) [84], and three different regions of Nigeria, Calabar, Nigeria (9%) [85], the plateau state, Nigeria (5.14%) [86], and Samaru, Zaria, Nigeria (4.9%) [87]. This could be due to differences in socioeconomic conditions, such as poverty levels, and access to clean water and sanitation facilities. A previous study reported that Ethiopia is one of the countries in sub-Saharan Africa with the lowest rates of toilet coverage and sanitation; over 52% of Ethiopians still use outdated facilities, and 35% defecate outdoors [88]. The other possible reason might be the implementation of more effective public health initiatives, such as deworming programs, better access to healthcare services, or more widespread health education in those mentioned countries compared to Ethiopia.

Significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies included in this review. This heterogeneity may be attributed to the fact that the primary studies were conducted in different geographical regions of the country, each with varying environmental and other conditions. Additionally, the original studies employed different parasitic diagnostic methods, which likely varied in sensitivity.

Therefore, subgroup analysis by regional state revealed that the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection in the Amhara region was 17.51% (95% CI: 13.20, 21.83) higher than that in other regional states. The possible reason for the variation might be the differences in the sample sizes of the studies; environmental factors such as soil moisture, humidity, temperature, and level of participants’ hygiene and sanitation; and sociocultural differences across the regions of Ethiopia. With respect to diagnostic methods, combinations of three different techniques, such as the Kato–Katz, McMaster, and spontaneous tube sedimentation techniques, presented the highest pooled prevalence compared with any other single or combination of diagnostic methods. This is supported by the fact that the use of two or more diagnostic techniques in combination yields superior sensitivity in the detection of parasites than the use of only a single diagnostic method [89].

The second objective of our systematic review and meta-analysis was to assess factors associated with Hookworm infection in schoolchildren. Compared with their counterparts, study participants who did not wear shoes were 5.90 times more likely to have a Hookworm infection. This was supported by previous studies conducted in Indonesia [83], Nigeria [76], and southern Sierra Leone [78]. A possible explanation might be that individuals who do not wear shoes are more susceptible to skin penetration by infective larvae of Hookworm parasites. Another study from rural South India found that children who walked barefoot around their village were significantly more exposed to Hookworm infection. This increased risk was attributed to open-air defecation, which results in the presence of fecal-contaminated fields [90].

In addition, study participants who did not use a latrine had a 3.040-fold greater risk of developing Hookworm infection than those who regularly utilized a latrine. This finding was in agreement with similar studies conducted in southern Sierra Leone [78]. This is because low coverage of toilets is the principal factor for the relatively high rates of parasitic infection dissemination in lower to middle-income countries [88]. A study from Honduras similarly reported that children living in households without access to toilets or latrines had higher odds of Hookworm infection compared to those with access to and usage of toilets or latrines [91].

Although our systematic review and meta-analysis provided a combined estimate of the prevalence and associated factors of Hookworm infection via comprehensive search strategies, it did not escape from limitations. The first limitation of our review was that an insufficient number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic were available for comparison. Second, most of the primary studies included in the present review had a cross-sectional study design, which affected the causal conclusions between Hookworm infection and associated factors. There was also high heterogeneity in the final meta-analysis. This may have influenced the accuracy of the pooled prevalence estimates. Possible explanations for the observed heterogeneity include differences in the study regions, the parasitic detection techniques employed, and sample size of the included studies.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of Hookworm infection among Ethiopian schoolchildren was significantly high, with regional differences. Moreover, the review revealed that the inability to use latrines and the lack of access to footwear were the two important factors associated with the prevalence of Hookworm infection. These elements play a key role in the transmission and spread of Hookworms in young people, underscoring the need to improve sanitation and provide protective footwear as essential strategies for reducing Hookworm infections in this population group.
