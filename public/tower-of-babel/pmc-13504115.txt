Screening of key genes in infectious ARDS and development of a disease risk prediction model
Authors: Xiaoyan Li, Tingting Liu, Yidan Zhang, Lin Wang, Yuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfection-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a common and severe clinical disease in critical care medicine, with high mortality and a significant burden on intensive care resources. Current treatments mainly rely on supportive care, and there is still a lack of effective targeted drugs and diagnostic tools. Therefore, in-depth exploration of its molecular mechanisms and identification of key genes have important clinical significance.ObjectiveTo screen infectious ARDS key genes, construct a risk prediction model, and explore targeted drugs.MethodsWe extracted GSE32707 data from the GEO database, analyzed infection-related ARDS samples and control samples, screened for differentially expressed genes, and identified signaling pathways using GO and KEGG methods. Core genes were subsequently selected for molecular docking using HadDOCK. Notably, this study integrates multiple analytical approaches, including differentially expressed gene analysis, pathway enrichment, ceRNA network construction, immune infiltration characterization, and molecular docking into a systematic framework, which to our knowledge has not been previously applied to infectious ARDS in a coordinated manner. By integrating ceRNA networks to reveal immune characteristics, we validated key gene expressions and predicted targeted therapies.ResultsIn patients with infectious ARDS, 3,034 differentially expressed genes were identified, enriched in T cell receptors, autophagy, and the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Network analysis pinpointed key genes including ATG5, AKT1, ATG7, and BMP9, with ATG5 strongly associated with CD4+ T cells and BMP9 linked to NK cells. Validation revealed elevated expression of BMP9, AKT1, and ATG5 in ARDS patients, while ATG7 showed low expression, with these genes showing certain diagnostic potential that warrants further validation. Based on the modeling of key genes to predict disease risk, the calibration curve showed acceptable reliability. Molecular docking suggested that sorafenib could bind to these target genes at the molecular level, indicating its theoretical potential as a candidate worthy of further investigation, though this computational finding requires experimental validation.ConclusionBMP9, AKT1, ATG5, and ATG7 may contribute to ARDS pathogenesis through immune cell infiltration and signaling pathway regulation. The risk model and sorafenib-targeted strategy provide preliminary insights and new directions for clinical diagnosis and treatment though further validation is needed.Keywords: infectious acute respiratory distress syndrome, BMP9, autophagy, bioinformatics methods

1. Introduction

Infection-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a common and severe clinical disease in critical care medicine induced by infection or sepsis, accounts for approximately 10% of intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, 1 with a mortality rate of 30%–45% in severe cases requiring mechanical ventilation, 2 which imposes a major burden on intensive care resources and clinical management. Despite current robust supportive care, the persistently high mortality of ARDS still poses a severe challenge to clinical treatment and medical resource allocation. Current treatment strategies primarily focus on supportive care, including mechanical ventilation and fluid management, while effective pharmacological interventions remain lacking. 3

In recent years, transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis have been widely applied to explore the molecular mechanisms of ARDS. Several bioinformatics studies have investigated ARDS-related gene expression profiles using publicly available datasets. For example, Li et al. screened macrophage-related differentially expressed genes in sepsis-induced ARDS and identified SGK1, DYSF, and MSRB1 as key biomarkers through WGCNA and machine learning algorithms. 4 Liu et al. explored the role of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in sepsis-associated ARDS, identifying LTF and PRTN3 as hub genes with diagnostic potential through integrative bioinformatics and molecular docking. 5 Hu et al. constructed a ceRNA regulatory network in sepsis-induced ARDS and identified the TUG1/miR-140-5p/NFE2L2 pathway as a central regulatory axis, while also characterizing differential immune cell infiltration patterns. 6 Additionally, Li et al. identified ferroptosis-related hub genes (IL1B, TXN, MAPK3, and HSPB1) in sepsis-induced ARDS through bioinformatics approaches and further validated their expression in mouse models and LPS-challenged human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells. 7 However, these studies have several notable limitations. First, most of them regarded ARDS as a single disease entity without etiological classification, and rarely focused on sepsis-induced infectious ARDS, the most common subtype in ICU with high mortality. Second, prior bioinformatics research mostly focused on isolated aspects such as gene screening alone or pathway enrichment alone and rarely systematically explored the crosstalk among autophagy, immune cell infiltration, and core regulatory genes. Third, many relevant studies remained at the data analysis stage, lacking clinical sample validation of hub genes, construction of clinical prediction models, and exploration of targeted drugs. These unaddressed issues collectively constitute a significant knowledge gap in the current understanding of infectious ARDS molecular pathogenesis.

Among the pathways implicated in ARDS pathogenesis, the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway has been consistently identified as a central regulator involved in inflammation, pulmonary edema, and tissue repair8. Bone morphogenetic protein 9 (BMP9), a member of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily, has emerged from recent studies as an important upstream regulator of this pathway. 9 BMP9 is predominantly expressed in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells and maintains pulmonary vascular barrier integrity through interaction with its specific receptor ALK1. 10 Experimental studies have shown that BMP9 can reduce endothelial hyperpermeability and pulmonary vascular leakage induced by lipopolysaccharide, and participates in regulating pulmonary inflammatory responses. 9 Given that BMP9 sits at the intersection of vascular barrier function, inflammation, and the PI3K/Akt pathway, all of which are critically involved in infectious ARDS, it represents a gene of particular interest warranting systematic investigation. However, it should be emphasized that BMP9 was not pre-selected as a target gene; rather, it was identified alongside other candidate genes through an unbiased genome-wide screening approach, and its biological plausibility is discussed herein as part of the analytical framework.

To address the aforementioned knowledge gaps, We performed multi-dimensional bioinformatics analysis to screen key genes related to the disease, analyzed the correlation between hub genes and immune infiltration and autophagy pathways, validated gene expression via clinical samples, constructed a disease risk prediction model, and predicted potential targeted drugs. This work aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the molecular characteristics of infectious ARDS and offer new clues for its early diagnosis and targeted treatment.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Data download and Differential Gene Expression Analysis

This was a retrospective bioinformatics analysis using publicly available gene expression data from the GEO database, supplemented by a prospective clinical validation cohort. The bioinformatics analysis was conducted between January 2023 and June 2023, and the clinical validation study enrolled patients between May 2023 and December 2023. The gene expression dataset GSE32707 related to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was downloaded from the GEO database using the R language GEOquery package. This dataset is derived from Homo sapiens with the tissue source of whole blood and the microarray platform of GPL10558. A total of 31 sepsis-associated ARDS samples (experimental group) and 58 simple sepsis samples (control group) were included in the study. The downloaded data was a Series Matrix normalized expression matrix (not raw CEL files), and basic background correction had been completed by GEO. In this study, quantile normalization was performed using the normalizeBetweenArrays function of the limma package, followed by log2 transformation of the expression values (log2 (expression level + 1)). Probe ID and gene Symbol matching was completed based on the annotation information of the GPL10558 platform; when multiple probes mapped to the same gene, the average value of their expression levels was taken as the final expression level of that gene. Low-expression genes were filtered out (log2 expression value > 1 in at least 50% of samples), and no significant batch effect was verified by principal component analysis (PCA). The preprocessed data was used for subsequent differential expression analysis with the limma package. 11

For the analysis of the GSE32707 dataset, we enrolled sepsis patients as the control cohort to screen for candidate genes associated with infection-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), with the aim to control for confounding differential signals against the shared underlying sepsis disease background. During the internal validation phase, we adopted a healthy population as the control cohort to assess the differential expression profiles of these candidate genes under normal physiological conditions, which further strengthened the biological relevance and validity of our validation results.

The R package limma was used to perform differential gene analysis between the ARDS group and the Sepsis group. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was applied for multiple testing correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR). The threshold for differentially expressed genes was set as |logFC| > 0 and p-value < 0.05. Genes with logFC > 0 and p value < 0.05 were classified as up-regulated differentially expressed genes, while those with logFC < 0 and p value < 0.05 were designated as down-regulated differentially expressed genes. 12

To systematically identify key hub genes associated with infection-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), we established a multi-step hierarchical prioritization and screening workflow with clear methodological criteria. First, based on differential expression analysis, we integrated the results of Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Genes involved in immune regulation, autophagy and the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway - the core pathological pathways of infectious ARDS -were prioritized for subsequent analysis. Second, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the above candidate genes, and selected core nodes with high connectivity and functional relevance as preliminary hub candidates. Third, the candidate gene set was further narrowed down via three complementary approaches: transcription factor (TF)-miRNA co-regulatory network analysis, immune cell infiltration correlation analysis, and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression modeling. Finally, reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed to validate the expression patterns of the remaining candidates. Genes with consistent results across all above analyses were ultimately defined as key hub genes, including BMP9, AKT1, ATG5 and ATG7.

2.2. Differential gene expression enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using R software. During the analysis, we employed R packages including “org.Hs.eg.db,” “clusterProfiler,” “enrichplot,” and “ggplot2.” The analysis focused on biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Concurrently, the KEGG database provided enriched signaling pathways revealed by these differentially expressed genes, with a p-value threshold set at < 0.05. Results were visualized using circle plots.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) is a method for detecting whether specific gene sets are differentially expressed across different phenotypes. 13 In this study, GSEA analysis was performed on all genes using the R package clusterProfiler (Version 4.10.0) with the following parameters: seed number 2020, 1000 permutations, minimum size exclusion 10, and maximum size exclusion 500. The gene set was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) for analysis. 14 For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), no exclusive threshold was set for the normalized enrichment score (NES) alone. The statistical significance of enriched pathways was defined by a p-value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. The directionality of NES was interpreted as follows: a positive NES(NES>0) indicated significant upregulation of the corresponding pathway in the infection-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) group, while a negative NES (NES < 0) indicated significant downregulation of the pathway in this group.

2.3. Protein-protein interaction network (PPI network) and molecular docking

The GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/) provides a theoretical basis for gene function research by analyzing gene lists and screening candidate genes for functional validation. 15 This platform identifies functionally similar genes based on massive genomic and proteomic data. Its core mechanism assigns weights to functional genomic datasets according to the predicted values of the query gene. Additionally, GeneMANIA offers gene function prediction: when a single query gene is input, the system identifies candidate genes with potentially similar functions by analyzing gene interactions. Using the GeneMANIA online platform, we successfully identified autophagy-related genes 5 (ATG5) and 7 (ATG7), protein kinase B (AKT1), and functionally similar genes highly associated with autophagy regulation, constructing a protein interaction network.

The X-ray crystal structure of ATG5 (PDB code: 4tq1) was downloaded from the RCSB PDB database. 16 Predicted structures for AKT1 (UniProt ID: P31749) and ATG7 (UniProt ID: O95352) were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AlphafoldDB). 17 The predicted protein structure reliability score pLDDT (Predicted Local Distance Difference Test) ranges from 0 to 100, with higher pLDDT values indicating greater structural reliability. The primary domains of genes ATG7 and AKT1 exhibit pLDDT > 90. Finally, HadDOCK(https://rascar.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4/) was employed to perform de novo protein-protein molecular docking without templates for the protein pairs: ATG5 and AKT1, ATG7 and AKT1, and ATG7 and ATG5.

2.4. Screening key genes and analyzing immune infiltration

The TF-miRNA co-regulation network specifically refers to interactions among transcription factors, miRNAs, and their target genes. Interaction data for TF-miRNA co-regulation were collected from the String database. Transcription factors are proteins capable of binding to specific DNA sequences, while miRNAs are small RNA molecules that regulate gene transcription and translation by binding to target genes, thereby influencing gene expression levels. We visualized the TF-miRNA co-regulation network using Cytoscape.

Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was employed to comprehensively investigate immune cell infiltration in infectious ARDS. This method was based on a predefined immune cell signature gene set (i.e., LM22 gene set), which infers the relative abundance of different immune cell subsets in each sample through enrichment scoring of the expression matrix. The reference gene set used was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb), covering a variety of innate and adaptive immune cell types, and is suitable for the evaluation of immune composition in peripheral blood/tissue transcriptome data. The “GSVA” function in R software was utilized to calculate immune cell infiltration scores for each sample based on gene sets composed of immune cell markers. 18 Concurrently, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to identify associations between key genes and immune cell types.

2.5. Validation of key genes and diagnostic performance analysis

We validated BMP9 expression in the infectious ARDS dataset. Using the pROC analysis package in R, we analyzed the diagnostic performance of the aforementioned key genes in infectious ARDS by plotting ROC curves. 19

Twenty patients hospitalized with infectious ARDS between May and December 2023 were selected as the experimental group, while 20 healthy adults undergoing physical examinations at the health screening center during the same period served as the healthy control group. Archived samples used in this study will be made publicly available on June 15, 2024. This research received approval from the Shanxi Bethune Hospital’s Clinical Research Ethics Committee (SBQLL-2020-009). The analysis phase strictly adhered to complete anonymity, involving only biomarker testing of archived samples. The research team had no access to any information that could potentially identify participants during data collection or subsequent processing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or their legally authorized representatives) prior to enrollment. Inclusion criteria: ① Trial group: Meeting the diagnostic criteria for sepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (SARDS); ② Healthy control group: ① Recommended by hospital physical examination centers with good health status; ② Age ≥18 years; ③ Signed informed consent forms. Exclusion criteria: ① Age <18 years; ② Patients who died upon admission or had incomplete clinical data; ③ Patients with organic heart disease (ischemic cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, myocarditis, etc.); ④ Patients with end-stage chronic diseases or malignant diseases; ⑤ Patients who refused to participate in the study or signed informed consent forms; ⑥ Patients with a history of mental disorders or cognitive dysfunction. Supplemental clinical information of validation cohort: The healthy control group had a mean age of 54.7 ± 10.5 years (10 males and 10 females), with 4 cases of hypertension and 2 cases of diabetes. There were no significant differences in age, gender and common chronic diseases between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Blood samples were collected after centrifugation at 4°C and 2000 rpm for 10 minutes, with the supernatant immediately frozen at −80°C.

Total RNA was extracted from the serum of subjects. The reverse transcription reaction was performed as follows: 11 μL of RNA (1 μg) was mixed with 1 μL of random primers (0.2 μg/mL) and incubated at 65°C for 5 minutes. Subsequently, a reaction mixture containing 4 μL of 5× buffer, 3 μL dNTP (10 mmol/L), 1 μL RNase inhibitor (20 U/μL), and 1 μL reverse transcriptase (20 U/μL) was added to the system. The reaction proceeded at 25°C for 10 minutes, followed by incubation at 42°C for 1 hour and finally at 72°C for 15 minutes. The real-time PCR reaction mixture consisted of 10 μL FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX), 0.5 μL upstream primer (15 μM), 0.5 μL downstream primer (15 μM), 2 μL cDNA, and 7 μL deDNAse-and RNase-free water, with a total volume of 20 μL. The primer sequences for GAPDH were as follows: upstream primer-CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT-3′, downstream primer-GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT-3′. Additionally, primer sequences for key genes were included. PCR reaction conditions: The PCR reaction was initiated with 94°C pre-denaturation for 10 minutes to activate Tag enzyme. Subsequently, the amplification process included 94°C denaturation for 15 seconds and 60°C annealing for 60 seconds, totaling 40 cycles. Cycle threshold (CT) values were determined by fluorescence quantitative PCR. First, the CT value difference between the target gene and the internal reference gene GAPDH in each sample was calculated, i.e., ΔCT = CT (hub gene) -CT (GAPDH). Then, the comparative analysis between experimental group samples and normal control group samples was obtained by subtracting the ΔCT value. Finally, the expression fold change of hub genes relative to control group samples was calculated using the 2 (−ΔΔCT) method.

2.6. Construction of disease risk prediction models

Using the “rms” package, we established clinically applicable nomograms with the four selected key genes—BMP9, AKT1, ATG5, and ATG7—as data points. Calibration curves were constructed to evaluate the diagnostic predictive capability of these nomograms for infectious ARDS.

2.7. Further screening of hub genes via machine learning

To deeply screen hub genes for infectious ARDS, we employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm from the “glmnet” package, optimizing the λ value through cv. glmnet parameters. 20 Cross-validation function cv. glmnet was utilized to optimize the penalty parameter λ, thereby constructing an optimal sparse model. For parameter configuration, the λ value range was restricted to 0–100, with model fitting completed using both binomial and categorical methods. After determining the minimum λ value, the LASSO algorithm was executed via the glmnet function with α value and binomial method parameters to ensure effective model compression while retaining the most discriminative genetic features.

2.8. Molecular docking-based prediction of targeted therapeutics for hub genes

Molecular docking experiments were conducted to further explore potential therapeutic targets for hub genes. 21 First, the Drug Signature Database (DSigDB) on the Enrichr website (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) was accessed to identify potential drugs targeting core genes. 22 Subsequently, protein structures and small-molecule drug information corresponding to the hub genes were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and the PubChem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), respectively. Finally, AutoDock (version 4.2.6, https://ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/downloads/) and PyMOL (version 2.5.2) software were employed to simulate the binding process between target proteins and small-molecule drugs. For preprocessing, target proteins were subjected to water molecule removal, hydrogenation, and charge assignment using PyMOL; small-molecule drugs were processed by removing redundant conformations, adding hydrogen atoms, and defining rotatable bonds via AutoDock Tools. Based on the known active sites of the target proteins (obtained from UniProt annotation), grid boxes were set for semi-flexible docking; the grid size was set to 60×60×60 Å with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å, and the grid center was positioned at the geometric center of the active site. The key docking parameters were set according to the actual experimental conditions: the number of genetic algorithm runs was 100, the maximum number of evaluations was 2,500,000, and the population size was 150. The best conformation was selected based on the lowest binding energy (absolute value > 5 kcal/mol) and the rationality of the binding mode (verified by PyMOL visualization of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions).

2.9. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 statistical software. Normally distributed quantitative data were described as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (P25, P75). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Screening of differentially expressed genes in infection-induced ARDS

A total of one public dataset was included for comprehensive analysis, with a total sample size of 89 cases, including 31 cases in the disease group and 58 cases in the control group. The basic information and sample composition of the dataset are described in the Methods section. Subsequent differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis were performed based on the preprocessed expression matrix to improve the stability and reliability of the results.

Differential gene analysis was performed using the limma package in R software, with criteria set at |logFC| > 0 and P < 0.05. A total of 3,034 differentially expressed genes were identified, comprising 1,713 upregulated genes and 1,321 downregulated genes. 3.2. Gene functional enrichment analysis

We performed functional enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were enriched in multiple pathways, including T cell receptor signaling, immune response-regulation of cell surface receptor signaling, immune response-activation signaling, and immune response-regulation/activation signaling (Figure 2(A)). These pathways are closely involved in regulating the body’s immune response, which is a core pathological process of infectious ARDS, as abnormal immune activation and dysregulated signaling can exacerbate lung inflammation and tissue damage. KEGG enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the T cell receptor signaling pathway (Figure 2(B)). The T cell receptor signaling pathway plays a key role in immune cell activation and proliferation, and its dysregulation may lead to excessive inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to the development and progression of ARDS-related lung injury. GSEA results indicated that DEGs were significantly enriched in the following biologically relevant functions and signaling pathways: TP53-regulated metabolic genes, IL-2 receptor pathway, autophagy, PI3K/Akt pathway, PI3K/Akt/mTOR VD3 signaling pathway, Erk/MAPK targets, selective autophagy, and Akt pathway (Figure 3). The directionality of the normalized enrichment score (NES), reflected by its positive and negative values, is applied to define the upregulated or downregulated state of enriched pathways, and is interpreted in conjunction with the pre-specified statistical significance threshold to elucidate the biological implications of the GSEA enrichment results. Furthermore, all NES values were negative, indicating that these pathways were downregulated in the case group. These downregulated pathways are closely associated with immune regulation, cell survival, and inflammatory response; their suppression may impair the body’s ability to clear pathogens and repair lung tissue, thereby worsening the pathological state of infectious ARDS.

Through TF-miRNA co-regulation network analysis, we revealed the regulatory relationships between miRNAs and transcription factors on key genes, which may represent a crucial mechanism influencing the expression of hub genes. To thoroughly analyze the role of immune cells in infectious ARDS, we systematically evaluated immune cell infiltration using data from the GSE32707 database and the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) function within the R GSVA package. Analysis of infiltration levels across 23 immune cell types (Figure 7(A) and (B)) revealed enhanced M2 macrophage polarization (P=0.002), increased Treg infiltration (P=0.013), and reduced CD8+ T cells (P=0.021) in the infectious ARDS group compared to the sepsis control group. 3.5. Validation of key genes and analysis of diagnostic performance

RT-qPCR results revealed significantly elevated expression of BMP9, AKT1, and ATG5 in the infectious ARDS group compared to the control group, with all differences being statistically significant (P < 0.05). Conversely, ATG7 expression levels were markedly reduced in the infectious ARDS group compared to the control group, with this difference being statistically significant (P < 0.01) (Figure 8).

We validated BMP9 expression in the infectious ARDS dataset. Results demonstrated significantly elevated BMP9 expression in infectious ARDS. The diagnostic value of key genes for infectious ARDS was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Results showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.682 for BMP9, 0.604 for AKT1, 0.611 for ATG5, and 0.597 for ATG7 (Figure 9). All AUC values were relatively low, indicating that each individual gene exhibits unsatisfactory diagnostic performance for infectious ARDS and cannot be used as an independent diagnostic biomarker. Nevertheless, these genes may have certain reference value and could serve as components of a combined biomarker panel for auxiliary risk assessment rather than definitive diagnosis.

Combining PCR analysis results with LASSO regression analysis, molecular docking studies were conducted for the hub genes BMP9, AKT1, and ATG5. 4. Discussion

Infection-associated ARDS remains a major challenge in critical care medicine, with persistently high mortality despite advances in supportive care.1,2 Accumulating evidence indicates that ARDS is not a homogeneous disease entity but exhibits pronounced biological heterogeneity. Calfee et al. identified two distinct ARDS subphenotypes, hyperinflammatory and hypoinflammatory, through latent class analysis across multiple RCT cohorts, demonstrating differential responsiveness to therapeutic interventions. 23 These seminal findings underscore the necessity of etiologically stratified molecular characterization. In this study, we performed an integrative bioinformatics analysis of the GSE32707 dataset to screen key genes, characterize immune infiltration patterns, construct a risk prediction model, and identify candidate therapeutic targets.

Among the 3,034 DEGs identified (1,713 upregulated, 1,321 downregulated), BMP9 was significantly upregulated in the infectious ARDS group, validated by RT-qPCR in clinical samples. BMP9, a member of the TGF-β superfamily, signals predominantly through the ALK1/BMPR2 receptor complex on pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, activating downstream SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation.9,24 This cascade transcriptionally upregulates endothelial junction proteins including VE-cadherin and claudin-5, maintaining pulmonary vascular barrier integrity. Inhibition of endogenous BMP9 induces pulmonary vascular leakage and neutrophil extravasation, phenocopying LPS treatment effects. 24 The observed BMP9 upregulation may therefore represent a compensatory protective response rather than a primary disease driver, supported by evidence that circulating BMP9 is cleaved and inactivated by neutrophil elastase during sepsis, 24 suggesting elevated mRNA expression may coexist with reduced functional protein.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that DEGs were enriched in T cell receptor (TCR) signaling, immune response-regulating cell surface receptor signaling, and the autophagy regulatory network. GSEA further demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Erk/MAPK, and selective autophagy pathways all exhibited negative normalized enrichment scores (NES), indicating functionally suppressed activity in the infectious ARDS group. Integrating these findings, we propose the following coherent mechanistic model: Infection-driven TCR activation recruits LCK/FYN kinases, initiating the MAPK signaling cascade. This cascade, together with reduced upstream growth factor signaling, suppresses the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis. Under normal physiological conditions, mTORC1 phosphorylates ULK1 at Ser757, maintaining the ULK1-ATG13-FIP200 initiation complex in its inactive state; when mTORC1 activity is suppressed, ULK1 is dephosphorylated and released, enabling Beclin-1 phosphorylation and VPS34 lipid kinase complex activation to initiate autophagosome formation. 25 However, ATG7-the E1-like enzyme essential for both ATG12-ATG5 conjugation and LC3-I-to-LC3-II lipidation 26 - is significantly downregulated. Consequently, although mTORC1 suppression relieves autophagy initiation, ATG7 downregulation impairs autophagosome elongation, causing p62/SQSTM1 accumulation that amplifies NF-κB-driven inflammation via TBK1-dependent positive feedback, 27 a state of “frustrated autophagy.” Impaired clearance of damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates fuels oxidative stress and inflammasome activation, 28 completing a coherent TCR-MAPK-PI3K/Akt/mTOR-autophagy-inflammation cascade that unifies the seemingly disparate pathway findings.

The identification of ATG5, AKT1, ATG7, and BMP9 as the final hub genes followed a rigorous multi-step screening framework. Starting from 3,034 DEGs, we intersected them with an autophagy-related gene set to enrich for autophagy-relevant candidates, then constructed a protein-protein interaction network and applied three complementary cytoHubba topological algorithms, retaining genes ranked in the top 10 by at least two of the three. These candidates were further filtered by their involvement in KEGG pathways significantly enriched in our dataset (autophagy, PI3K/Akt, and TCR signaling). LASSO regression then selected the subset with the strongest contribution to disease classification, identifying BMP9, AKT1, and ATG5. ATG7, although not selected by LASSO, was retained through PPI network analysis and functional annotation as a core autophagy component, a decision subsequently justified by its statistically significant downregulation in RT-qPCR validation (P<0.01). Only genes with significant differential expression in both discovery and validation cohorts were designated as final hub genes. This transparent, stepwise framework-progressing from broad omics screening through topological prioritization and pathway filtering to independent experimental confirmation - ensures that the final four hub genes were identified not by arbitrary or post-hoc selection, but by their consistently superior performance across connectivity, pathway relevance, and reproducible expression.

Beyond the integrated pathway model described above, the individual roles of these hub genes merit discussion. AKT1 29 sustains endothelial survival via BAD/BCL-2 modulation while concurrently suppressing autophagy through mTORC1-mediated ULK1 phosphorylation. Elevated AKT1 expression may therefore represent a protective survival program, even as mTORC1 activity is paradoxically dampened by metabolic stress and AMPK activation - “AKT-mTOR uncoupling.” Downstream, ATG5 and ATG7 showed divergent expression despite operating within the same conjugation cascade: ATG7 was significantly downregulated (P<0.01), while ATG5 was upregulated (P<0.05). Given ATG7’s role as the sole E1-like enzyme for both conjugation arms, its deficiency creates a “conjugation bottleneck” - ATG5 induction may be compensatory, but without ATG7 the unconjugated protein cannot support LC3 lipidation or autophagosome elongation. This model aligns with the spontaneous pulmonary inflammation observed in Atg7-deficient mice. 30 The TF-miRNA co-regulatory network (144 nodes, 147 connections) further suggests that post-transcriptional regulation may contribute to the divergent expression patterns of these genes, though this hypothesis requires experimental validation.

ssGSEA-based immune infiltration analysis of 23 immune cell types revealed that the sepsis-associated ARDS group exhibited increased Treg infiltration (P=0.013), enhanced M2 macrophage polarization (P=0.002), and reduced CD8+ T cell abundance (P=0.021), alongside upregulated T follicular helper (Tfh, P=0.010) and γδ T cells (P=0.004). Correlation analysis reinforced this pattern: M2 macrophages and Tregs showed a strong positive correlation (r>0.55, P<0.01), while CD8+ T cells correlated negatively with both M2 and Tregs (r≈−0.40). This finding suggests that superimposed ARDS further deepens the immunosuppressive state already present in sepsis, which may contribute to the heightened susceptibility to secondary infections observed clinically. ATG5 expression positively correlated with CD4+ naive and memory T cells, supporting the notion that these subsets are particularly dependent on autophagic homeostasis, while BMP9 correlated with activated NK cells, consistent with TGF-β superfamily involvement in NK cell maturation. We caution, however, that these associations derive from bulk transcriptome deconvolution and do not establish causality, single-cell and flow cytometric validation are required.

Compared with prior ARDS bioinformatics studies that each targeted a single molecular dimension,4–7,31 our study differs in three respects. First, we restricted the analysis to infection-associated ARDS, addressing etiological heterogeneity that single-entity designs overlook. Second, we integrated autophagy pathway analysis, immune infiltration profiling, and drug prediction within a unified pipeline. Third, the autophagy-centered gene network (ATG5, ATG7) has not been examined in prior investigations, whose hub genes cluster within classical inflammatory cascades. By bridging the autophagy-inflammation interface, our work offers a complementary perspective on ARDS pathogenesis.

We also acknowledge several discrepancies with published findings. First, our GSEA showed negative NES for the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, suggesting transcriptional suppression, whereas numerous experimental studies have demonstrated PI3K/Akt activation in ARDS lung tissue. This apparent contradiction may reflect the difference between mRNA-level pathway enrichment in peripheral blood transcriptomes and phosphorylation-dependent protein-level activation occurring predominantly in lung tissue; furthermore, circulating immune cells in systemic infection may exhibit pathway exhaustion, consistent with sepsis-induced immunosuppression. Second, while molecular docking identified sorafenib as a candidate binder of BMP9, AKT1, and ATG5 (binding energies below −6.0 kcal/mol), multiple clinical reports have documented sorafenib-associated interstitial pneumonitis with autopsy-confirmed diffuse alveolar damage. 32 This discrepancy highlights a critical limitation of molecular docking: binding affinity alone does not capture polypharmacology, tissue-specific effects, or in vivo toxicity profiles. Third, ATG7 downregulation in our infectious ARDS cohort contrasts with reports of upregulated autophagy in viral ARDS models, underscoring the etiology-dependent nature of autophagic regulation in ARDS.

From a translational perspective, the four hub genes individually showed modest diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.597–0.682), indicating they are unsuitable as standalone biomarkers but may serve as components of a multi-marker panel. The nomogram-based risk model showed acceptable calibration, though external validation in independent cohorts with prospective designs is essential before any clinical application can be contemplated. Therapeutically, while sorafenib exhibited favorable docking energies, clinical evidence of pulmonary toxicity 32 counsels against its use in ARDS. Notably, BMP9-ALK1 signaling protects the pulmonary endothelium, 9 suggesting that pharmacological activation — rather than inhibition — of this pathway may represent a more rational strategy, consistent with the ongoing development of recombinant BMP9 (MGX292) for pulmonary vascular indications. All therapeutic considerations presented here remain exploratory.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our findings derive from a single discovery dataset (GSE32707, n=89) without independent external validation; batch effects and microarray-to-RT-qPCR platform differences may contribute to the discrepancies between discovery and validation cohorts, limiting generalizability. Second, the clinical validation cohort was small, single-center, and lacked prospective sample size calculation. Third, immune deconvolution relied on the LM22 signature derived from healthy donors, which may introduce quantification bias in septic patients, and peripheral blood transcriptomes do not fully capture the lung immune microenvironment. Fourth, the absence of functional experiments and in vitro/in vivo validation of molecular docking predictions means that all mechanistic interpretations and therapeutic implications remain hypothesis-generating.

6. Conclusion

In summary, this study identified BMP9, AKT1, ATG5, and ATG7 as hub genes in infection-associated ARDS and proposed an integrated TCR-MAPK-PI3K/Akt/mTOR-autophagy-inflammation mechanistic model that warrants future investigation. The diagnostic potential of these genes as a combined panel and the predictive performance of the nomogram-based risk model represent preliminary observations requiring validation in larger, multicenter, prospectively designed cohorts. Molecular docking identified sorafenib as a candidate compound, but clinical evidence of pulmonary toxicity precludes any therapeutic recommendation at this stage; instead, the protective role of BMP9-ALK1 signaling suggests alternative avenues for targeted intervention. All findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating until confirmed by independent replication and functional experimental studies.
