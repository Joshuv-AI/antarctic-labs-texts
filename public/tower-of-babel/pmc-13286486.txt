Digital versus speculum insertion of Foley catheter for cervical ripening: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jun Zhou, Huihao Zhou, Qian Chen, Jingui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:To compare the efficacy and safety of digital and speculum-guided transcervical Foley catheter insertion for cervical ripening.Methods:Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted from database inception through October 2025. Randomized controlled trials comparing digital with speculum-guided transcervical Foley catheter insertion in term singleton pregnancies were eligible. The primary outcomes encompassed maternal pain assessment scores and catheter insertion duration. The methodological quality of the included studies was appraised in accordance with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 framework. Statistical analyses were performed with RevMan 5.3. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. Dichotomous outcomes were reported as relative risk with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and continuous outcomes as mean difference. The certainty of evidence was graded using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology.Results:Five randomized controlled trials including 1046 women met inclusion criteria. Digital insertion was not associated with reduced procedural pain compared with speculum-guided insertion (MD 0.54; 95% CI −1.89 to 2.97; I2 = 97%). Insertion time did not differ significantly between the 2 techniques (MD −0.32; 95% CI −1.06 to 0.41; I2 = 85%). No significant differences were observed in induction-to-delivery interval, cesarean delivery rate, need for additional ripening methods, or maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes.Conclusion:Digital transcervical Foley catheter insertion demonstrates procedural efficiency and obstetric safety equivalent to speculum-guided techniques.

1. Introduction

Induction of labor is one of the most commonly performed obstetric procedures worldwide, with rates continuing to rise and exceeding 25% in many high-resource healthcare systems.[1–3] This increase is largely attributable to evolving maternal demographics, advances in fetal surveillance, and a growing emphasis on proactive management at term and postterm gestation.[4,5]

Among the available methods for cervical ripening, the Foley catheter balloon remains a widely used, cost-effective, and safe mechanical method that offers efficacy comparable to pharmacological agents.[6,7] Its use is strongly recommended in ambulatory care contexts, particularly among patients susceptible to uterine tachysystole, those with previous cesarean delivery or other uterine surgical procedures, and in clinical circumstances where prostaglandin agents are inaccessible or medically contraindicated.[6,8–10]

Two primary techniques are commonly used across institutions for the placement of single-balloon devices. Some institutions insert the Foley catheter under direct visualization using a speculum, whereas others favor digital placement, reasoning that guiding the catheter with the fingers after sterile preparation and vaginal examination may reduce discomfort and lower cost. Some randomized trials have indicated that digital insertion offers reduced discomfort, shorter procedure times, and greater patient satisfaction,[11–13] whereas other studies have reported no meaningful distinctions between the 2 approaches.[14,15]

As interest in outpatient cervical ripening expands, robust clinical evidence is essential to inform practice and broaden access to safe outpatient methods. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is performed to synthesize existing evidence and provide clearer guidance.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol registration and reporting standards

This systematic review was conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The completed PRISMA checklist is presented in Guidelines Checklist 1 to 2. The study protocol was registered in advance with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration: CRD42023452160).

2.2. Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted from database inception through October 2025. The literature retrieval strategy incorporated a systematic integration of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and relevant free-text keywords, employing advanced Boolean operators. Search terms included “balloon,” “Foley,” “Intrauterine balloons,” “Foley balloon,” “Cook,” “randomized controlled trial,” “labor induction,” and “cervical ripening.” Supplementary search strategies encompassed manual reference list screening of included studies, meta-analyses, reviews, and major obstetric society guidelines. A detailed description of the search methodology is provided in Supplemental Digital Content 1,2,3 and 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.3. Eligibility criteria and study selection

Eligible studies included RCTs only, published in English-language and enrolling nulliparous and multiparous women with an unfavorable cervical status. The intervention of interest compared digital versus speculum-guided insertion of intrauterine balloons for cervical ripening. The outcomes included maternal pain scores assessed using a visual numeric rating scale, catheter insertion duration, cesarean delivery rate, induction-to-delivery interval, neonatal intensive care unit admission, chorioamnionitis, and maternal satisfaction scores. Additionally, only studies presenting robust and analyzable data were deemed eligible for inclusion. Trials employing any type of intrauterine balloon and varying inflation volumes were deemed eligible.

Exclusion criteria comprised multiple gestations, preterm delivery, contraindications to vaginal birth, non-English publications, quasi-randomized or observational study designs, and studies lacking adequate outcome data for meta-analysis.

2.4. Literature screening and data extraction

The processes of literature identification, eligibility screening, and data extraction were performed independently by 2 investigators. Extracted information encompassed the first author, study location, baseline Bishop score, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sample size, intervention specifics, and reported clinical outcomes. All data were carefully recorded on a pretested extraction form in duplicate by both reviewers. Discrepancies were reconciled through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to maintain accuracy and consistency.

2.5. Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality evaluation performed by 2 independent reviewers utilized the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials. The assessment covered 5 principal domains: selection bias, including random sequence generation and allocation concealment; performance bias, reflecting blinding of participants and personnel; detection bias, concerning blinding of outcome assessment; attrition bias, related to incomplete outcome data; and reporting bias, addressing selective outcome reporting. Each domain received categorical assessment as “low risk,” “unclear risk,” or “high risk” with detailed rationale documentation. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was engaged to achieve an impartial resolution.

2.6. Statistical analysis and data synthesis

For dichotomous outcomes, relative risks with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, whereas continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic and Cochran Q test. A fixed-effects model was employed when P ≥.1 and I2 ≤50%, indicating low heterogeneity, while a random-effects model was applied if P ≤.1 and I2 ≥50%, reflecting substantial heterogeneity. In the presence of significant heterogeneity, sensitivity analyses were performed to explore potential sources, and subgroup analyses were conducted when sufficient studies were available to further elucidate its origins. Potential reporting bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger regression test when a minimum of ten studies were available for a given outcome. When required, reported medians and interquartile ranges were transformed into estimated mean values to enable their incorporation into the pooled quantitative synthesis.[16–19]

3. Results

3.1. Study selection and flow

The systematic search strategy identified 3865 potentially relevant records across all electronic databases. After removing duplicates (n = 30), 3835 unique records were screened by title and abstract, resulting in 7 articles selected for full-text review. Ultimately, 5 RCTs met the eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. One study was excluded due to a nonrandomized design, and another was excluded because it was a duplicate publication (see PRISMA flow diagram).

3.2. Study characteristics and population demographics

The 5 included RCTs encompassed 1046 participants (521 digital insertion, 525 speculum insertion) conducted across diverse geographical regions spanning 4 continents. The eligible trials were published over the period from 2011 to 2024, with study populations varying in size from 42 to 446 participants. Three studies originated from developed healthcare systems,[11–13] while 2 were conducted in lower-middle-income countries.[14,15] Population characteristics demonstrated heterogeneous baseline demographics across studies. Gestational ages at intervention spanned 37.0 to 41.0 weeks. Initial Bishop scores consistently reflected unfavorable cervical conditions, ranging from 2.04 to 5.0 across study populations, appropriate for mechanical cervical-ripening intervention. Foley catheter diameters varied from 16F to 22F, with balloon inflation volumes spanning 50 mL to 60 mL. Maheshwari et al[15] utilized larger 22F catheters with 50 mL inflation, while other investigations employed 16F to 18F catheters with 50 to 60 mL volumes. Digital insertion techniques were consistently described across studies, though operator experience standardization varied substantially. After the withdrawal or spontaneous expulsion of the balloon, further induction strategies – including artificial rupture of membranes, intravenous oxytocin, or vaginal prostaglandins – were selected according to cervix status. 3.3. Risk of bias assessment and certainty of evidence assessment

Risk of bias evaluation revealed predominantly moderate-quality evidence with notable methodological limitations (Figs. 1 and 2). Randomization processes were adequately reported in all studies (100% low risk), with appropriate random sequence generation and allocation concealment implementation in these studies. Performance bias constituted the principal limitation, as blinding of participants and healthcare providers was not feasible due to the nature of the interventions, leading to a high risk of bias in this domain. Most studies were at high risk of detection bias, with only 1 study implementing outcome assessor blinding for objective measurements, though subjective pain assessments remained potentially susceptible to bias influences. Attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases were all at low risk.

Risk of bias summary.

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) was applied to assess the certainty of evidence across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The evaluation focused on clinically important primary and key secondary outcomes, including maternal procedural pain, catheter insertion duration, cesarean delivery rate, induction-to-delivery interval, need for additional ripening methods, maternal satisfaction, and neonatal outcomes.

3.4. Meta-analysis results

3.4.1. Delivery outcomes:

Four studies reported maternal pain scores encompassing 674 participants. Substantial heterogeneity was observed, and a random-effects model was adopted (χ2 = 97.56, P <.001, I2 = 97%). The meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant overall difference between digital and speculum insertion groups (MD = 0.54, 95% CI: −1.89, 2.97, P = .66). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated persistently high heterogeneity, with I2 values decreasing to 86% (MD = 1.50, 95% CI: −0.02, 3.01, P = .05) after exclusion of the study by Jonsson M et al[20] and increasing to 98% (MD = 0.40, 95% CI: −2.76, 3.55, P = .80) following removal of the trial conducted by Maheshwari A et al[20]; nevertheless, the specific contributors to heterogeneity of maternal pain scores could not be clearly identified. There were no significant differences observed in the duration of catheter insertion (χ2 = 85, P <.001, I2 = 85%, MD = −0.32, 95% CI: −1.06, 0.41, P = .39). Sensitivity analyses showed persistently high heterogeneity: I2 decreased to 71% (MD = −0.07, 95% CI −0.80, 0.65; P = .84) after exclusion of the study by Abu Shqara et al,[20] and increased to 89% (MD = −0.14, 95% CI −1.02, 0.73; P = .75) after removal of the trial by Jonsson M et al.[20] The origin of the heterogeneity of catheter insertion duration could not be determined. There were no significant differences observed in induction-to-delivery interval, insertion-to-expulsion or removal duration, delta bishop score, failure of insertion, and the need for other cervical-ripening methods (Table 3).

3.5. GRADE evidence quality assessment

The GRADE assessment indicated moderate-quality evidence for maternal pain scores, maternal satisfaction, and neonatal outcomes; high-quality evidence for cesarean delivery rates, induction-to-delivery interval, and the need for additional cervical-ripening methods; and low-quality evidence for catheter insertion duration (Table 5).

4. Discussion

Our meta-analysis of 5 RCTs involving 1046 women showed that digital placement of the Foley catheter did not significantly reduce procedural pain (MD = 0.54, 95% CI: −1.89, 2.97) or shorten insertion time (MD = −0.32, 95% CI: −1.06, 0.41) compared with speculum-guided placement. Moreover, no significant differences were observed in the induction-to-delivery interval (MD = 0.20, 95% CI: −1.88, 2.27), cesarean delivery rate (RR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.65–1.07), need for additional cervical-ripening methods (RR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.57–1.54), or the incidence of maternal and neonatal outcomes between the 2 techniques.

According to current operation manuals for double-balloon devices – such as that for the COOK Cervical-Ripening Balloon – insertion is advised under direct visualization with a sterile speculum to facilitate precise placement of both the uterine and vaginal balloons, thereby ensuring effective compression at the internal and external cervical os and optimizing mechanical performance.[21]

In parallel, digital cervical examination is a routine, well-established, and safe component of obstetric practice, widely used to assess cervical favorability before labor induction. Evidence from earlier prospective studies supports both its safety and its predictive value. In a cohort of 122 postterm pregnancies, digital examination – reflected by the Bishop score and individual cervical parameters such as dilation, effacement, and position – was found to be a stronger independent predictor of successful induction and vaginal delivery than transvaginal sonographic markers, including cervical length and funneling.[20] Similarly, in a study of nulliparous women undergoing induction, both the Bishop score obtained digitally and the sonographic cervical length were significantly associated with induction success, underscoring that digital examination, despite its perceived subjectivity, continues to demonstrate robust predictive validity and remains a safe, widely accepted assessment method in contemporary obstetric care.[22]

In a randomized trial of women with unripe cervices (Bishop ≤5), digital Foley catheter insertion – irrespective of catheter size (16F, 22F, or 28F) – achieved insertion success rates, procedure duration, and pain scores comparable to those of speculum-guided placement.[23] In a prospective cohort of 534 term pregnancies, Gidaszewski et al reported that pain scores were similar for digital vaginal examination and speculum insertion, but both were more uncomfortable than the Foley catheter insertion itself.[24] These results are in alignment with those reported in a prior systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized evidence comparing digital with speculum-based balloon catheter insertion, which identified 4 RCTs and concluded that digital insertion was associated with lower procedural pain and greater patient acceptability, while overall efficacy and safety remained comparable between techniques.[25]

However, more recent evidence is inconsistent with the conclusions outlined above. A non-inferiority randomized clinical trial involving 446 primigravidae at term demonstrated that digital and speculum-guided transcervical Foley catheter insertion resulted in comparable outcomes across key parameters, including insertion-related pain, time to successful insertion, and insertion failure rates.[15] In contrast to an earlier meta-analysis[25] suggesting a potential advantage of digital insertion in reducing pain and improving acceptability, our updated meta-analysis, which incorporates this latest high-quality RCT, indicates that digital and speculum-based insertion techniques perform similarly with respect to insertion-related pain, procedural efficiency, insertion success, and maternal and neonatal outcomes.

By including the recent randomized controlled trial,[15] this meta-analysis represents the largest cohort to date assessing the efficacy and safety of digital compared with speculum-guided transcervical balloon catheter insertion. Additional strengths include rigorous adherence to PRISMA guidelines and the exclusive inclusion of RCTs. In terms of evidence certainty assessed by the GRADE framework, our findings were supported by high-quality evidence for critical clinical outcomes including cesarean delivery rate, induction-to-delivery interval, and the need for additional cervical-ripening methods, indicating consistent, precise, and low-bias results across trials. Moderate-quality evidence was assigned for maternal procedural pain and satisfaction, as these subjective outcomes were affected by substantial heterogeneity and measurement variability. Notably, low-quality evidence was identified for catheter insertion duration, mainly due to significant inconsistency across studies and indirectness related to variations in operator experience and technique. Collectively, the overall certainty of evidence supports the conclusion that digital insertion is non-inferior to speculum-guided insertion for most key obstetric outcomes, although the low certainty for insertion duration warrants cautious interpretation and further investigation. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial variability existed in clinical practice parameters across the included trials, which may have contributed to significant interstudy heterogeneity. These factors included differences in Foley catheter type, catheter size, balloon inflation volume, and application of traction during catheter placement, all of which could influence ease of insertion, procedural pain perception, and technical success.[21,26,27] Second, the study populations were heterogeneous in terms of parity, which is a well-recognized confounding factor for cervical-ripening outcomes, including pain experience, insertion difficulty, and labor progression.[28] Third, although this meta-analysis encompasses the largest pooled sample to date, the number of available trials remains limited, precluding comprehensive subgroup analyses by catheter characteristics, parity, or operator experience, as well as robust assessment of rare adverse outcomes. Finally, to facilitate the meta-analysis, certain data originally reported as medians were converted to means, which could also introduce an additional potential source of bias in our results.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that digital Foley catheter insertion provides equivalent procedural efficiency and obstetric safety when compared with speculum-guided techniques.
