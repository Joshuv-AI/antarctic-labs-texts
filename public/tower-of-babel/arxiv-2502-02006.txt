Spectrally robust covariance shrinkage for Hotelling’s T 2 T^{2} in high dimensions
Benjamin D. Robinson, Van Latimer

Abstract
We investigate covariance shrinkage for Hotelling’s T 2 T^{2} in the regime where the data dimension p p and the sample size n n grow in a fixed ratio—without assuming that the population covariance matrix is spiked or well-conditioned. When p / n → ϕ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p/n\to\phi\in(0,1) , we propose a practical finite-sample shrinker that, for a maximum-entropy signal prior and any fixed significance level, (a) asymptotically optimizes power under Gaussian data, and (b) asymptotically saturates the Hanson–Wright lower bound on power in the more general sub-Gaussian case. Our approach is to formulate and solve a variational problem characterizing the optimal limiting shrinker, and to show that our finite-sample method consistently approximates this limit via extensions of recent local random matrix laws. Empirical studies on simulated and real-world data, including the Crawdad UMich/RSS data set, demonstrate up to a 50 % 50\% gain in power over leading linear and nonlinear competitors at a significance level of 10 − 4 10^{-4} .

Introduction

Comparing the means of two samples with a shared covariance matrix is a fundamental problem in multivariate statistics, and Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test is a popular procedure when the sample size
n
dominates the dimension
p

[
2
,
43
]
.
However, in the more modern high-dimensional regime of
[
41
]
where
n
scales linearly with
p
, the test is inconsistent even when well-defined
[
4
]
.
Several authors have proposed addressing this issue by shrinking the sample covariance matrix
[
27
,
38
,
50
]
: applying a linear or even nonlinear function—called a
shrinker
—to the eigenvalues while keeping the eigenvectors fixed
[
55
,
31
,
42
,
15
,
35
]
.
While such methods are effective under strong assumptions on model order or condition number, they can fall far short of optimality when many strongly correlated nuisance signals are present
[
50
]
, a common scenario in finance, genomics, and other applications
[
8
,
25
,
33
]
.
This limitation motivates the development of covariance shrinkage techniques that remain robust across a significantly broader range of spectral distributions in high dimensions.

Covariance shrinkage under general spectral conditions is challenging for several reasons.
First, linear shrinkers
[
56
,
24
,
22
,
11
,
42
,
15
,
17
,
10
]
often fail to capture the complexity of a general population spectrum since they have at most two degrees of freedom.
Previous work has indeed shown that in many contexts optimal shrinkers belong to the much richer
nonlinear
class, which consists of functions with
k
continuous derivatives for some
k
and thus has infinitely many degrees of freedom
[
55
,
18
,
44
,
20
,
34
]
. Second, implementing optimal nonlinear shrinkers can be difficult: the first practical versions, devised by Ledoit and Wolf, are still relatively new
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
Third, deriving the asymptotic null distribution of the shrinkage-modified Hotelling
T^{2}
statistic is delicate—even in the case of linear shrinkage
[
46
,
38
]
.
Finally, identifying and optimizing an effective detection criterion for the test under fully general spectral conditions remains an open problem
[
45
]
.

In this paper, we develop a method of covariance shrinkage for Hotelling’s
T^{2}
that is robust to large condition numbers, high model orders, and deviation from Gaussianity.
Building on the anisotropic local-law framework of Knowles and Yin
[
28
]
, we adopt a random matrix model that accommodates many families of matrices with such spectral features.
This model also accommodates Johnstone’s spiked model
[
26
]
and many of the generalized spiked models in
[
6
]
under finite-rank perturbations, which recent literature suggests leave the relevant asymptotic limits unaffected in analogous settings
[
19
]
.
Moreover, our method is robust to sub-Gaussian departures from the Gaussian model, as long as the data have independent components in a fixed basis.

In parallel with various recent works on eigenvector overlaps
[
19
,
39
]
, we first establish a stochastic convergence rate associated with Ledoit and Wolf’s nonlinear shrinkage eigenvalues from
[
35
]
in Theorem
4.5
—rates that provide the foundation for our remaining results. Focusing on the regime of
p\to\infty
with
p/n\to\phi\in(0,1)
, our main contributions to statistical theory are as follows:

1.

In Theorem
5.5
, we establish asymptotic control of size for the Hotelling
T^{2}
test modified with covariance shrinkage.

2.

In Theorem
5.6
, (a) we present a variational problem characterizing the shrinker that asymptotically optimizes a sub-Gaussian lower bound on power,
under a maximum-entropy signal-prior assumption [
Test
]; and (b) we express this optimal shrinker in terms of Hilbert transforms.

3.

In Theorem
5.8
, we present a practical finite-sample approximation that achieves the same asymptotic detection performance as the optimal shrinker.

Taken together, these results give the first usable shrinker that asymptotically optimizes Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test—up to sub-Gaussian concentration bounds—across a wide range of spectral complexity.

We present these results and their preliminaries in the following sections. Section

Finally, Section

2
Problem and preview of proposed shrinker

Anomaly detection in this paper, as addressed by Hotelling’s
T^{2}
, will refer to the problem of determining whether the unknown mean of a vector observation is equivalent to the unknown mean of a reference data set having the same covariance matrix.

Formulated as a hypothesis test, the question is, given i.i.d
.
random vectors

\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\sim P
and a random vector
\mathbf{y}\sim Q
with
\operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=\operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{y})
, to decide between
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{1}]\neq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]
.

Although in the traditional setting two-sample testing requires that multiple i.i.d
.
copies of both
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\mathbf{y}
are available, we focus primarily on the challenging regime of one “test” sample
\mathbf{y}
. This setting is of fundamental importance in statistical signal processing applications—both in online and offline analysis.
Some examples include distributed motion detection in the presence of complex noise and interference
[
16
]
, data censoring in radar space-time adaptive processing
[
13
,
1
]
, and out-of-distribution detection for deep neural classifiers
[
37
]
. In these settings, detection-delay costs or nonstationarity constraints may mean that it is often not sensible to accumulate test data beyond a singleton before a decision is made.

When the common covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is known, a typical test statistic for anomaly detection is the quadratic-form detector given by

(\mathbf{y}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})\underset{\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\overset{\mathcal{H}_{1}}{\gtrless}}\tau,

(2.1)

where
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
is the sample mean of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
,
\tau
is some detection threshold, and
(\,\cdot\,)^{\prime}
denotes the transpose operation.
However, unless
n
is large—
i.e.
, much larger than
p
—it is not realistic to assume that one can approximate all the
p^{2}
entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with much accuracy. In this case,
it is standard to replace
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
by some estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.
The focus of this paper is on
how to choose
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
in the
high-dimensional regime,
where
n
and
p
go to infinity and
p/n\to\phi\in(0,1)
.

Very often
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is chosen to be the Bessel-corrected sample covariance matrix of
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
, defined to be

\mathbf{S}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\overline{\mathbf{x}})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\prime}\right)\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\prime},

(2.2)

where
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is the
p\times n
matrix

[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]
,
\mathbf{I}_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is the
n\times 1
vector
(1,1,\dots,1)^{\prime}
.
The resulting plug-in quadratic-form detector can be identified with Hotelling’s
T^{2}
statistic
[
43
]
for large
n
. However, although this choice minimizes asymptotic detection loss compared to (
2.1
) when
n\gg p
, there is no reason to believe it has the same property in the high-dimensional regime.
Indeed, Hotelling’s
T^{2}
is inconsistent in this regime
[
4
]
.

A natural alternative approach is to replace the sample precision matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}
, with eigen-decomposition
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}
, by a
shrinkage estimator

f(\mathbf{S}_{n})
with eigen-decomposition
\sum_{i=1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i})\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}
, where
f
is some non-negative deterministic function we call the
shrinker
.
More in line with the literature, we overload the term shrinker to mean not only deterministic shrinkers but also more general functions
f_{n}
whose values could depend on random
n
-dependent quantities, such as the full spectrum of
\mathbf{S}_{n}

[
55
]
.
In any case, we call the resulting plug-in test statistic a
shrinkage-regularized Hotelling
T^{2}
statistic
, or
SRHT
, defined by

\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}\equiv\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}(f_{n}):=(\mathbf{y}_{n}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n})^{\prime}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})(\mathbf{y}_{n}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}).

(2.3)

The most common type of shrinker
f_{n}
corresponds to
linear
shrinkage and takes the form
f_{n}(x)=1/(a_{n}x+b_{n})
for some scalars
a_{n},b_{n}>0
.
This choice corresponds to the ridge-regularized Hotelling
T^{2}
test statistics of
[
38
]
.
Linear shrinkage is extremely well-studied, but for all the effort devoted to it, virtually all procedures for finding
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
appear to depend on prior knowledge, such as a tight bound on the minimum eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
or on its condition number or model order, which may not be practical to obtain.
We thus assume
f_{n}
is not necessarily of the linear class, and belongs to a more general class of
nonlinear
shrinkers.

In the related contexts of financial portfolio optimization and radar space-time adaptive processing, a common optimal nonlinear shrinkage estimator has been identified in
[
35
]
and
[
49
]
, respectively, under certain signal-prior assumptions and the high-dimensional regime in question. Under certain additional conditions relating to the covariance spectrum and moments of the data, one choice for this shrinkage procedure replaces
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
with
\delta(\mathbf{S}_{n})
, where

\delta(x):=\frac{x}{[1-\phi-\pi\phi x\mathcal{H}w(x)]^{2}+\pi^{2}\phi^{2}x^{2}w(x)^{2}},\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R})

(2.4)

w(x)
is the Marčenko–Pastur density
[
41
]
, and
\mathcal{H}w(x)
is the Hilbert transform of
w(x)
, given by the principal-value integral

\mathcal{H}f(x)\equiv\mathcal{H}[f](x):=\mathrm{p.v.}\frac{1}{\pi}\int\frac{f(t)}{t-x}\,dt.\qquad(f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))

(2.5)

We note that the function
\delta(x)
is a key mapping appearing throughout this paper that can be approximated for finite sample sizes using
Theorem
4.3
.

In this paper we present an asymptotically optimal choice of the function
f_{n}
appearing in the statistic
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}
of (
2.3
) for a sub-Gaussian linear model given by assumptions [
Train
] and [
Test
], which will be presented in the forthcoming sections. To make the problem well-posed, we assume a signal prior of the form
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Omega})
and that the quantities
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{u}_{i}w(\lambda_{i})
approximate
h_{n}(\lambda_{i})
for some sufficiently regular function
h
. Letting
a(x)=xw(x)\delta(x)
, an optimal shrinker is the function

f^{*}=\frac{g^{2}h+gGH}{a}-\mathcal{H}\left[\frac{G^{2}H+Ggh}{a}\right],

where

g(x):=1-\phi-\phi\pi x\mathcal{H}w(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad G(x):=-\phi\pi xw(x)

(2.6)

and
H:=\mathcal{H}h
. The shrinker
f^{*}
can be obtained by solving a singular integral equation arising from the variational problem that appears in Theorem
5.6
. Under the model to be presented, this shrinker asymptotically optimizes power for any chosen significance level if the data happen to be Gaussian, and otherwise asymptotically saturates the Hanson–Wright lower bounds on power at any chosen significance level if the data have sub-Gaussian components.
Since
f^{*}
is generally unobservable, a suitable finite-sample approximation
f_{n}
to
f^{*}
is given in Theorem
5.8
.

In what follows, we will ground these ideas in a firm theoretical foundation. To do this, our first task will be to summarize and develop key results in random matrix and nonlinear shrinkage theory over the next two sections.

Remark 2.1
.

It is worth noting before proceeding that Hotelling’s
T^{2}

is not always used to compare a singleton sample to another sample, but is frequently used to compare two samples of cardinality greater than one.
We expect our results to hold in this case as well, but some additional technical arguments are required in the non-Gaussian case to handle dependencies between sample means and covariance estimators. For ease of exposition, we omit the details of this extension except to note that the analysis of
[
38
, Section 4]
appears to provide a blueprint.

.

•

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{W}_{n}

\tau_{n1},\tau_{n2},\dots\tau_{np}
.

•

[

.

•

[

] As

, (a) defining

to be the Dirac mass at

, the population spectral distribution
\pi_{n}=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\tau_{ni}}

differs in 1-Wasserstein distance by

and (b) all population eigenvalues eventually lie in a positive compact interval.

•

[
Train5
] The sequence

\{\pi_{n}\}
is

in the sense of

—for example, when

The conditions [
Train
] strengthen somewhat but align closely with the assumptions in the main body of
[

]
. Although many of these conditions can be relaxed, doing so would substantially complicate the proofs of our main theoretical results. Nevertheless, we briefly mention some significant potential relaxations.

•

The moment condition in [
Train1
], also known as

, can be relaxed to a sub-Weibull-type condition without much conceptual difficulty, but the proofs become messier notationally.

•

The assumption of regularity in [
Train5
] appears in a wide range of practical models, includes the identity matrix as a special case

, Example 2.8]
, and ensures the highly desirable property of square-root edge-behavior of the Marčenko–Pastur density, but regularity technically excludes spiked covariances. However, it turns out we lose no generality by relaxing [

] to allow finite-rank perturbations of

—including spiked and many generalized spiked models. See
[

, Section 2.1]
and subsequent sections for the details of this extension, which we omit here.

•

A third relaxation involves the assumption in [
Train3
] that

. We discuss the delicate “singular” case of

•

We make the convergence rate assumption [
Train3
] explicit for convenience, but from a practical point of view, it is not essential. The reason: in practice, the data matrix
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is only given for a single
n
. In other words, the limiting quantity
\phi
is not observed, and can be taken to approximate
p/n
as closely as desired, or even equal it. Similarly, the convergence rate in [
Train4
] may seem restrictive, but for the same reason, does not impose any practical difficulty.
To expand, since only one sample is given, one may simply redefine
\pi_{\infty}
to be
\pi_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
, after which the convergence rate in 1-Wasserstein distance is arbitrarily fast. The value of these formulations is that (a) having an infinite-dimensional limit enables the use of a large body of existing random matrix theory, and (b) having an order of convergence enables downstream convergence-rate guarantees to be made.

We note that the assumption [
Train3
] means that we will be able to use the condition “as
n\to\infty
” as a substitute for the equivalent but more verbose condition “
n,p\to\infty
and

p/n=\phi+O(1/p)
.”
Even more briefly, when we express a convergence relation, such as
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}-\mathbf{I}_{p}\right\|\to 0
without identifying a limiting regime, it will be understood that this convergence occurs as
n
(and thus
p
) goes to infinity.
The only exception will be inside theorem environments, so that the statements of main results remain as self-contained as possible.

Our primary notion of convergence rate in probability will be that of
stochastic domination
, defined as follows as in
[
28
]
:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X_{n}(u)
and
Y_{n}(u)
be random variables, parameterized by a family of real vectors
u\in U
. We say
X_{n}
is stochastically dominated by
Y_{n}
uniformly in
u
if for all
\epsilon,D>0
we have

\sup_{u\in U}\Pr\left[X_{n}(u)<n^{\epsilon}Y_{n}(u)\right]\leq n^{-D}

for all
n
greater than some
n_{0}(\epsilon,D)
. More briefly, we write
|X_{n}|\prec Y_{n}
or
X_{n}=O_{\prec}(Y_{n})
. More generally, if for every
D>0
there is
n
such that a property holds with probability at least
1-n^{-D}
for all larger
n
, we say the property holds
with high probability
.

Quantities converging to probability in the usual sense will often be denoted by
o_{P}(1)
.

Remark 3.2
.

The underlying set
U
parameterizing random variables of interest will often not be explicitly identified; instead the notation
O_{\prec}(\,\cdot\,)
will be implicitly taken to mean uniform stochastic domination over all parameters (
e.g.
, matrix indices or complex spectral parameters) not expressly deemed constant.

We note that the sample covariance matrix can be expressed just as before, except with terms indexed by
n
to indicate their increasing dimension and dependence on
n
:

\mathbf{S}_{n}=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{ni}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n})(\mathbf{x}_{ni}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n})^{\prime}.

Further, we will frequently make use of the sample eigen-decomposition:

\mathbf{S}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{ni}\mathbf{u}_{ni}\mathbf{u}_{ni}^{\prime},

where the unit eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{ni}
are almost surely uniquely defined up to a sign since the probability of repeated sample eigenvalues is zero.

The Marčenko–Pastur Theorem is essentially a statement about the limiting behavior of the resolvent
\mathbf{R}_{n}(z)
of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
, given by

\mathbf{R}_{n}(z)=(\mathbf{S}_{n}-z\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1},

and the spectral measure of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
: namely,

\mu_{n}=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\lambda_{ni}}.

A strong form of the Marčenko–Pastur Theorem due to Knowles and Yin is reproduced below.

Theorem 3.3
(Local Marčenko–Pastur Laws
[
28
]
).

Assume
[
Train
]
and that
\mu_{n}
is the spectral distribution of the sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. Let

m_{n}(z):=p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{R}_{n}(z).

Then for all bounded
z=x+i\eta
in the complex upper half plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
with
\eta\geq n^{-1+\epsilon}
for some positive
\epsilon
,
we have

m_{n}(z)=m_{\infty}(z)+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{n\eta}\right)

(3.1)

as
n,p\to\infty
,
where
m_{\infty}(z)
is the unique solution to

m_{\infty}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\pi_{\infty}(\tau)}{\tau(1-\phi-\phi zm_{\infty}(z))-z}.

(3.2)

Furthermore, (the “small-scale” laws) there is a nonrandom measure
\mu_{\infty}
such that

\sup_{(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu_{n}(a,b)-\mu_{\infty}(a,b)\right|\prec\frac{1}{n}

(3.3)

and, uniformly in
f\in C^{2}
,

\int f\,d\mu_{n}=\int f\,d\mu_{\infty}+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n^{2}}\right),

(3.4)

as
n,p\to\infty
.

The statement spanning (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) follows directly from the cited work.
The small-scale laws then follow from well-established arguments by Helffer and Sjöstrand,
which are reproduced
in Section S.2
of the Supplementary Material for convenience.

Remark 3.4
.

Pursuant to the comments following
[
28
, Equation 2.9]
, we note that the convergence rates in Theorem
3.3
are not affected by the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
.

The first known version of (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) in the literature is due to
[
41
]
but does not include an explicit almost-sure convergence rate or uniformity over intervals
(a,b)
and pertains to the case of diagonal
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
. For example, when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
is the

identity matrix for all

, it is well-known that the density

Definition 3.5
.

4.1
Ledoit–Péché Theory

The functions
\Theta_{n}^{g}(z)
are of particular interest when

, just discussed in Theorem

, and when

Theorem 4.1
(Theorem 4 of
[
30
]
).

Assume
[
Train
]
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
Let

\Theta_{n}(z):=p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mathbf{R}_{n}(z)).

Then
\Theta_{n}(z)=\mathcal{S}[d\nu_{n}](z)
, where

\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{u}_{ni}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{ni}\delta_{\lambda_{ni}},

and
there exists a nonrandom analytic function
\Theta_{\infty}(z)
such that

\Theta_{n}(z)=\Theta_{\infty}(z)+o_{a.s.}(1)

(4.1)

as
n,p\to\infty
.
Furthermore, if
the function
\delta:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined using (
2.4
),
then

d\nu_{n}\to\delta\,d\mu_{\infty}=:d\nu_{\infty}

(4.2)

weakly almost surely as
n,p\to\infty
.

The first statement follows from diagonalization of
\mathbf{R}_{n}(z)
and the circular-permutation property of trace. Equation (
4.1
) follows from
[
30
, Theorem 2]
. Equation (
4.2
) follows from
[
30
, Theorem 4]
.

∎

The choice
g=\mathrm{id}
studied in this section is important for several reasons. First, the coefficients of the measure
\nu_{n}
resulting from

optimize various loss functions of statistical interest, such as Frobenius loss
[
30
]
, reverse Stein loss
[
34
]
, the Sharpe ratio appearing in financial portfolio optimization
[
33
]
, and the Reed–Mallet–Brennan figure of merit in radar detection
[
49
]
, assuming an ignorant signal prior for the latter two. A potential wrinkle is that

has no known closed form expression except in a handful of special cases.
However, fortunately, Ledoit and Wolf
[
35
]
have developed observable substitutes for

,
\mathcal{H}w(x)
, and
\delta(x)
that exhibit various forms of consistency, as we will discuss in Theorem

.

For the applications just mentioned, more machinery is not needed, but for the application in this paper, an explicit rate of convergence is needed between

and its substitute. We will address this rate in the next subsection, but first we need to refine Theorem

so that it includes precise convergence rates.

Theorem 4.2
(Local Ledoit–Péché Laws).

Assume
[
Train
]
and
z
is bounded in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
with with
\eta=\mathrm{Im}z\geq n^{-1+\epsilon}
for some positive

. Then, we have the following analogue of (

) from Theorem

:

\Theta_{n}(z)=\Theta_{\infty}(z)+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{n\eta}\right)

(4.3)

as
n,p\to\infty
. Furthermore (the “small-scale laws,”) we have the following analogue of (
3.3
) from Theorem
3.3

\sup_{(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}}\left|\nu_{n}(a,b)-\nu_{\infty}(a,b)\right|\prec\frac{1}{n}

(4.4)

and, uniformly in

\int f\,d\nu_{n}=\int f\,d\nu_{\infty}+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n^{2}}\right),

Equation (

[

]
or the more recent and general work of

4.2
Ledoit–Wolf Approximation

In this subsection, we present the approximations devised by Ledoit and Wolf
[
32
]
for the Marčenko–Pastur density
w(x)
and its Hilbert transform. Upon substitution in the expression for
\delta(x)
, these approximations give rise to a shrinkage function
\tilde{d}_{n}(x)
that exhibits uniform convergence in probability to
\delta(x)
on
F
. As discussed, this mode of convergence is sufficient to establish asymptotic optimality in the applications of
[
35
,
49
]
.

Our main refinement of
[
35
]
is to develop an explicit rate of convergence for
\tilde{d}_{n}(x)
to
\delta(x)
, although we study convergence in
L^{1}(d\mu_{n})
instead of in
L^{\infty}(d\mu_{\infty})
. With this essentially more relaxed convergence mode, we are able to obtain convergence at a rate of
O_{\prec}(n^{-2/3})
, which will be fast enough
to enable standardization and optimization of the SRHTs that will be our focus starting in the next section.

We now describe the main approximation result of
[
35
]
. As mentioned before, the idea is to replace
w(x)
and
\mathcal{H}w(x)
in
\delta(x)
by suitable substitutes. First, one replaces
w(x)
everywhere by

\tilde{w}_{n}(x):=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}k_{ni}(x),

(4.6)

where

k_{ni}(x):=\dfrac{1}{n^{-1/3}\lambda_{ni}}k\left(\dfrac{x-\lambda_{ni}}{n^{-1/3}\lambda_{ni}}\right)

for a suitable bump-like kernel function
k
with known Hilbert transform
K
. Second, they replace
\mathcal{H}w(x)
in
\delta(x)
by

\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}_{n}(x):=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}K_{ni}(x),

(4.7)

where

K_{ni}(x):=\dfrac{1}{n^{-1/3}\lambda_{ni}}K\left(\dfrac{x-\lambda_{ni}}{n^{-1/3}\lambda_{ni}}\right).

using invariance properties of
\mathcal{H}
.

The main theoretical result of
[
35
]
is then the following, which states that if
k
is chosen appropriately
and the above replacements are made in
\delta(x)
,
the resulting function consistently estimates
\delta(x)
in probability.

Theorem 4.3
(Theorem 4.1 of
[
35
]
).

Assume
[
Train
]
and
k(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}{\sqrt{(4-x^{2})_{+}}}
and
K(x)=(2\pi)^{-1}(-x+\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{(x^{2}-4)_{+}})
, where
(\,\cdot\,)_{+}=\max\{\,\cdot\,,0\}
. Then, with
\tilde{w}_{n}(x)
as in (
4.6
) and
\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}_{n}(x)
as in (
4.7
), the function

\tilde{d}_{n}(x)=\frac{x}{[1-\frac{p}{n}-\frac{p}{n}\pi x\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}_{n}(x)]^{2}+\frac{p^{2}}{n^{2}}\pi^{2}x^{2}\tilde{w}_{n}(x)^{2}}

(4.8)

has the property that

\sup_{x\in F}\lvert\tilde{d}_{n}(x)-\delta(x)\rvert=o_{P}(1)

as
n,p\to\infty
.

Remark 4.4
.

The only significant departure of our statement from
[
35
, Theorem 4.1]
is that Ledoit and Wolf actually choose
k(x)
to be the so-called
Epanechnikov
kernel
3(1-x^{2}/5)_{+}/(4\sqrt{5})
in the main body of their paper, but remark in
[
35
, Supplement D.5]
that the semicircular kernel above works equally well in theory. We choose the latter since the Epanechnikov kernel appears to require much more care in its implementation to avoid numerical instabilities.
As a reminder of this and other notation introduced in this section, Table
3
summarizes key symbols related to nonlinear shrinkage that will be used throughout the paper.

As indicated above, we do not analyze rates of stochastic convergence in the rather strong
L^{\infty}(\mu_{\infty})
norm that appears in the above theorem since
L^{1}(\mu_{n})
convergence will suffice for our purposes. The following theorem provides an explicit rate of
O_{\prec}(n^{-2/3})
for this type of convergence—a dramatic improvement on the
o_{P}(1)
rate in
L^{1}(\mu_{n})
that follows immediately from Theorem
4.3
—and is the culmination of the work presented so far.

Theorem 4.5
(Error Analysis of Ledoit–Wolf Eigenvalues in
L^{1}(\mu_{n})
).

Assume
[
Train
]
. Then we have

p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left|\tilde{d}_{n}(\lambda_{ni})-\delta(\lambda_{ni})\right|\prec\frac{1}{n^{2/3}}

as
n,p\to\infty
.

See Section S.3
of the Supplemental Material.
∎

Theoretical contributions

In order to discuss the asymptotic properties of our test statistic (

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{n}

\mathbf{x}_{ni}

p\times 1
and increasing in dimension as
n
increases. We assume that
\mathbf{y}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{n}
for some unknown (possibly random) vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
, where the components of
\mathbf{z}_{n}
are i.i.d
.

\sim W
and independent of
\mathbf{x}_{ni}
. In other words, we assume
\mathbf{y}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
and
\{\mathbf{x}_{ni}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are jointly i.i.d.

In Subsection
5.1
, we control asymptotic significance levels for the SRHT (Theorem
5.5
).
Then, in Subsection
5.2
, we find a surrogate functional for detection performance (Theorem
5.6
(a)) and identify an (unobservable) shrinkage function that optimizes it in the limit (Theorem
5.6
(b)). Finally, we present a practical empirical shrinkage function that approximates the deterministic optimum (Theorem
5.8
). Throughout this section we will need the following notion of a regular sequence of functions.

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
f_{n}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a sequence for which
|f_{n}|
is uniformly bounded for large enough
n
,
|f^{\prime}_{n}|\prec n^{1/3}
,
|f^{\prime\prime}_{n}|\prec n^{2/3}
, and
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\right\|_{HS}^{2}\asymp p
in probability. Then we say
f_{n}
is
regular
.

5.1
Significance level

In this subsection, we fix a regular sequence
f_{n}
and determine a valid asymptotic significance level for the statistic
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}=\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}(f_{n})
, defined in (
2.3
). Suppose we shift and rescale by empirical quantities
\tilde{m}_{n}p
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\sqrt{p}
, where
\tilde{m}_{n},\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
are order-one in probability, obtaining

\tilde{Z}_{n}:=\frac{\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}-\tilde{m}_{n}p}{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\sqrt{p}}.

(5.1)

Expanding the quadratic form
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}
, it can be shown that the mean and variance of
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}
are both almost surely of order
p
and that the quantity
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
can be replaced by
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
without affecting the limiting distribution of
\tilde{Z}_{n}
. Thus, if we define the following unobservable quantity

\tilde{Z}^{o}_{n}:=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{n}-\tilde{m}_{n}p}{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\sqrt{p}},

then
\tilde{Z}_{n}-\tilde{Z}^{o}_{n}
converges in distribution to
\delta_{0}
,
where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{n}
.

Let
Z^{o}_{n}
be the additional unobservable quantity defined as follows

Z^{o}_{n}:=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{n}-m^{o}_{n}p}{\sigma_{n}^{o}\sqrt{p}},

(5.2)

where

m^{o}_{n}\equiv m^{o}_{n}(f_{n}):=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{n}(\lambda_{ni})\mathbf{u}_{ni}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{ni}

and

(\sigma_{n}^{o})^{2}\equiv\sigma_{n}^{o}(f_{n})^{2}:=p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}).

In the special case where the fourth moment of
W
matches a standard normal’s, this statistic is asymptotically normal by conditioning on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
, applying Berry–Esseen
[
9
,
21
]
, and taking the expectation over the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
. Comparing
Z^{o}_{n}
,
\tilde{Z}^{o}_{n}
, and
\tilde{Z}_{n}
, this special case thus motivates choosing
\tilde{m}_{n}
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
to consistently approximate
m^{o}_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}^{o}
in probability. Suitable approximations will be given in Lemma
5.3
, but first we need an intermediate approximation of
(\sigma_{n}^{o})^{2}
, in terms of a deterministic quadratic functional that we denote
\sigma_{\infty}^{2}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume
[
Train
]
and let
\mathcal{K}:=L^{2}(F)
. Let
\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f)
be a quadratic functional on

defined by

\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f)=\int_{F}[\Gamma f(x)]^{2}x\delta(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x),

(5.3)

where
\Gamma:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
is defined by

\Gamma f(x)=f-\phi\pi\left(\mathcal{H}[f\delta w]-\mathcal{H}[\delta w]f\right).\qquad(x\in F)

Then, if
f_{n}
is regular we have

\sigma_{n}^{o}(f_{n})=\sigma_{\infty}(f_{n})+o_{P}(1)

as

.

See Section S.4
of the Supplemental Material.
∎

Lemma 5.3

Assume
[

]
and that

\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}f(x):=f(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(f(\lambda_{nj})-f(x))\tilde{d}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})K_{nj}(x)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\equiv\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}(f_{n}):=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left[\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}f_{n}(\lambda_{ni})\right]^{2}\lambda_{ni}\tilde{d}_{n}(\lambda_{ni}).

Then, with

and

It follows from Theorems

\frac{|m^{o}_{n}(f_{n})-\tilde{m}_{n}|p}{\sqrt{p}}=\sqrt{p}|m^{o}_{n}(f_{n})-\tilde{m}_{n}|\prec\sqrt{p}\left(\frac{\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f_{n}^{\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n}+n^{-4/3}\right)\prec\frac{\sqrt{p}}{n^{2/3}}\prec\frac{1}{n^{1/6}}

as

Remark 5.4

We note that the asymptotic normality of

, Theorem 2]
, which establishes the asymptotic normality of the closely related one-sample statistic
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\prime}f(\mathbf{S}_{n})\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}/\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\|^{2}
in the special case that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
(and
f
is analytic).
Making this assumption on
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
, the results differ primarily in that the one-sample statistic is a quadratic form in the sample mean, normalized by a sample mean factor, whereas
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}(f)
is a quadratic form in an un-normalized singleton observation. Because the one-sample statistic evaluates a sample mean, the contribution of the kurtosis of
W
vanishes asymptotically.

Consequently, to compare the two results, we look at the kurtosis-independent terms of the asymptotic variances. The variance of the one-sample statistic is shown in
[
46
]
to approach
(2/\phi)(\int f^{2}\,d\mu_{\infty}-(\int f\,d\mu_{\infty})^{2})
, whereas the kurtosis-independent term of
\mathrm{var}(\tilde{T}_{n}^{2}(f))
in (
5.1
) is structurally similar, though scaled differently and lacking the
f
-dependent corrective term:
2\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\|_{HS}^{2}=2\mathrm{tr}(f(\mathbf{S}_{n})^{2})
, which is asymptotic to
2p\int f^{2}\,d\mu_{\infty}
.

Even without an exact estimate of size, we can obtain an asymptotic significance level that is independent of
f_{n}
using sub-Gaussian concentration bounds as follows.
The Hanson–Wright inequality
[
51
]
implies that, almost surely for
\tau>0
,

\begin{split}\Pr\left[\lvert\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\mathbf{z}_{n}-m^{o}_{n}p\rvert>\tau\mid\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{0}\right]\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{C^{4}(\sigma_{n}^{o})^{2}p},\frac{\tau}{C^{2}\left\|\tilde{\Sigma}\right\|}\right)\right]\end{split}

(5.5)

for a known absolute constant
c>0
and some constant
C
depending only on the moments of
W
. Scaling
\tau
by
\sigma_{n}^{o}\sqrt{p}
, this upper bound can naturally be used to set a nontrivial asymptotic significance level, since the second term in the minimum then goes to infinity like
\sqrt{p}
, almost surely. By the definition of
Z^{o}_{n}
, then, we have:

\Pr\left[Z^{o}_{n}>\tau\mid\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{0}\right]\leq 2\exp(-c\tau^{2}/C^{4})+o_{P}(1).

Taking the expectation with respect to the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and using Lemma
5.3
gives the following main result for this subsection.

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume
[
Train
]
, that
f_{n}
is regular, and that
c
and
C
are the constants defined above. Then
\tilde{Z}_{n}
is asymptotically normal with zero mean, and

\Pr\left[\tilde{Z}_{n}>\tau\mid\mathcal{H}_{0}\right]\leq 2\exp(-c\tau^{2}/C^{4})+o(1)

as
n,p\to\infty
.

The asymptotic significance level in Theorem
5.5
holds for any component-wise sub-Gaussian tail behavior, and can be used whenever one can find an upper bound on the sub-Gaussian constant
C^{4}
. If, on the other hand, one cannot bound
C^{4}
, a natural question is whether it is still possible to control the asymptotic size. To that end, observe that the exact variance of the unscaled quadratic form
\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{n}
is given by

\gamma_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ii}^{2}+2\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\right\|_{HS}^{2},

where
\gamma_{2}
is the excess kurtosis, and the matrix elements
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ij}
are evaluated with respect to the basis in which noise components are independent.
Because the summation multiplying
\gamma_{2}
depends on the unknown basis of independence, it is generally unobservable. However, one can construct an asymptotic upper bound for the variance in the inset
above
by bounding the summation and estimating
\gamma_{2}
.
Fortunately, a procedure for consistently estimating
\gamma_{2}
in a general high-dimensional independent-components model can be found in
[
40
]
. We provide a derivation of the resulting data-driven asymptotic significance level in Section S.6
of the Supplementary Material.

5.2
Detection power and optimal shrinkage

Assume again that
f_{n}
is a regular sequence. Further, assume now that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is in force and consider the detection rate.
By expanding quadratic forms, the asymptotic variance of
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}
conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\sum_{i}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ii}^{2}+2\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\right\|_{HS}^{2}+4\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}.

(5.6)

Since large values of
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\right\|_{2}
would trivialize the hypothesis test, we assume for now that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\right\|_{2}=o_{P}(\sqrt{p})
, which allows us to ignore the term containing
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
above. Thus,
\tilde{T}^{2}_{n}
has the same conditional asymptotic variance under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Arguing similarly, define

\mathcal{U}_{n}\equiv\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n}):=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\sqrt{p}},

(5.7)

where
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}=\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(f_{n})
, as defined in Lemma
5.3
.
The difference between
\tilde{Z}_{n}
under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is given by

\frac{2\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\sqrt{p}}+\mathcal{U}_{n},

and the linear term in
\mathbf{z}_{n}
can be neglected due to the asymptotic assumption on
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\right\|_{2}
and another application of Hanson–Wright.
Thus,
\tilde{Z}_{n}-\mathcal{U}_{n}
under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\tilde{Z}_{n}
under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
almost surely have the same asymptotic distribution, conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
.

Based on our analysis so far, if we consider only
f_{n}
for which the conditional null variance of
\tilde{Z}_{n}\mid\mathbf{X}_{n}
is asymptotically constant, the quantity
\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})
would be the detection criterion of the test—the quantity to maximize in order to maximize power. In actuality, however, this variance may depend on
f_{n}
, so identifying a tractable detection criterion is a challenge. Still,
\mathcal{U}_{n}
can be seen as a reasonable surrogate detection criterion since Theorem
5.5
implies the variance of the limiting null distribution of
\tilde{Z}_{n}
is bounded independently of
f_{n}
, in terms of
c
and
C
alone. In other words,
\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})
controls the worst-case detection power over all sub-Gaussian tail behaviors, and choosing
f_{n}
to maximize it will be the main task of the remainder of this paper.

In addition to the qualitative argument above, we may quantitatively estimate this worst-case detection power by using Hanson–Wright again:

\begin{split}&\Pr\left[\tilde{Z}_{n}>\tau\mid\mathbf{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{1}\right]\\
&\geq 1-\Pr\left[\lvert\mathcal{U}_{n}-\tilde{Z}_{n}\rvert\geq(\mathcal{U}_{n}-\tau)_{+}\mid\mathbf{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{1}\right]\\
&\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c(\mathcal{U}_{n}-\tau)_{+}^{2}/C^{4}\right)+o_{P}(1).\end{split}

(5.8)

Moreover, taking the expectation over the distributions of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
and
\mathbf{X}_{n}
gives

\Pr\left[\tilde{Z}_{n}>\tau\mid\mathcal{H}_{1}\right]\geq 1-2\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-c(\mathcal{U}_{n}-\tau)_{+}^{2}/C^{4}\right)+o(1).

(5.9)

This result shows the expected monotonicity in
\mathcal{U}_{n}
for any fixed
\tau
. We add that in the special case that
W
is Gaussian, power is directly monotonic in
\mathcal{U}_{n}
—a fact that follows, as before, from Berry–Esseen.

Since the direction in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the signal
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
is unknown, it is not possible to analyze the detection criterion

\mathcal{U}_{n}
without additional information.
To address this fact, one often assumes some sort of prior on
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
. For example, in
[
38
]
, the authors make the
maximum-entropy
assumption
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n})
for some specified dispersion matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
. In particular, for convenience they assume
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
is some polynomial in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
, scaled by
n^{1/2}
to ensure that

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\right\|^{2}\asymp n^{1/2}
with high probability, which means

\mathcal{U}_{n}\asymp 1
with high probability, which means the hypothesis test is tractable but not trivially so in the limit. Examples include
p^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{p}
(ignorant prior) or
p^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
(covariance-matched prior). In this paper, we allow for more general dispersion matrices, as follows.

Assumption [Test]
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
be a mean-zero random vector independent of
\mathbf{z}_{n}
for which
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}]=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
(a.s.), and
let
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}
have the (maximum-entropy) distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n})
for some known covariance
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
with
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\right\|\asymp n^{1/2}
. Further, assume there is a measure
d\omega_{\infty}(x)=h(x)\,dx\equiv\overline{h}(x)w(x)\,dx
such that
\overline{h}
is
C^{2}
on
F
, and assume that
for any regular shrinker
f_{n}
we have

p^{-3/2}\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\right)=\int f_{n}(x)d\omega_{\infty}(x)+o_{P}(1).

(5.10)

We note that the reasons the examples of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
equal to
p^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{p}
and
p^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
satisfy (
5.10
) are the last small-scale laws in Theorems
3.3
and
4.2
. We also note that the scaling factor of
p^{1/2}
is not necessary for our proposed shrinker to be asymptotically optimal but simply guarantees convergence of the test’s power to a number in
(0,1)
: if a divergent scale is chosen, all relevant tests’ power will converge to zero or one. Thus, our test will remain asymptotically optimal even in these trivial power regimes.

In the following theorem (Theorem
5.6
), we will show that under this new assumption [
Test
], the detection criterion
\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})
, like the variance
\sigma_{n}^{o}(f_{n})
from the previous subsection, can be approximated using a deterministic limit.
Further, this deterministic limit can be optimized by solving a variational problem explicitly in terms of Hilbert transforms. In a moment, this solution will inform the approximate solution presented in Theorem
5.8
.

Recalling the definitions of
g
and
G
from (
2.6
), our claims about the deterministic limit of
\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})
are as follows.

Theorem 5.6
.

Assume
[
Train
]
and
[
Test
]
.
Then

(a)

(Deterministic Limit.) Suppose
f_{n}
is regular. Then

\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})=\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f_{n})+o_{P}(1),

as
n,p\to\infty
, where

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f)=\frac{\int f\,d\omega_{\infty}}{\sigma_{\infty}(f)}.

(b)

(Optimal Limiting Shrinkage.)
If
H(x)
is the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}h(x)
and
a(x)=x\delta(x)w(x)
, then
f\in L^{2}(F)\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f)
is maximized
by the function

f^{*}:=\frac{g^{2}h+gGH}{a}-\mathcal{H}\left[\frac{G^{2}H+Ggh}{a}\right].

(5.11)

For part (a) we argue as follows.
Since Lemma
5.2
proves
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(f_{n})=\sigma_{\infty}(f_{n})+o_{P}(1)
, it suffices to show that

p^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}=\int f_{n}\,d\omega_{\infty}+o_{P}(1).

(5.12)

By Hanson–Wright, the left side is almost-surely equivalent to

p^{-3/2}\mathrm{tr}(f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}).

Thus, part (a) follows from Assumption [
Test
].

See Section S.7
of the Supplemental Material for part (b).
∎

Remark 5.7
.

While the above theorem, like the majority of this paper, restricts to the case where
n
eventually exceeds
p
, a regime of significant practical interest is the
singular
case where
p>n
. In this case, the spectrum of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
is degenerate, having
p-n
eigenvalues equal to zero, making finding a replacement for
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}
especially difficult. At a high level, this case requires applying a shrinkage function to the nonzero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
and assigning a common positive number to every zero eigenvalue. In practice, these two tasks turn out to be a significant challenge in the singular regime, as they are tightly coupled. Nevertheless, we provide an outline for their solutions in Section S.10
of the Supplemental Material,
along with
python
code and some supporting simulations.

The main thing that remains is constructing a suitable approximation
f_{n}
to the optimizer
f^{*}
of
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
, which is accomplished by the following theorem. We note that suitable choices for
\overline{h}_{n}
in the upcoming equation (
5.13
) are
\tilde{d}_{n}
if
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
(by Theorem
4.3
) and
\overline{h}_{n}\equiv 1
if
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
(by the Marčenko–Pastur Theorem). In addition, the work of
[
30
,
35
]
can be used to show similar formulas hold if
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
is a polynomial in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
, though we do not pursue this direction for ease of exposition.

Theorem 5.8
.

Assume
[
Train
]
and
[
Test
]
. Suppose there are real-valued regular functions
\overline{h}_{n}
such that the following holds:

p^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})-\overline{h}(\lambda_{nj})\right|=o_{P}(1)

(5.13)

as
n,p\to\infty
.
Let
\phi_{n}=p/n
, let

H_{n}(x)=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{ni})K_{ni}(x-\lambda_{ni}),

and let

g_{n}(x)=1-\phi_{n}-\phi_{n}\pi x\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}_{n}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{G}_{n}(x)=-\phi_{n}\pi x.

Further, let

\xi_{n}(x)=\frac{g_{n}(x)^{2}\overline{h}_{n}(x)+g_{n}(x)\overline{G}_{n}(x)H_{n}(x)}{\tilde{d}_{n}(x)x}

for
x>0
, and

\eta_{nj}=\frac{\overline{G}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})^{2}H_{n}(\lambda_{nj})+\overline{G}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})g_{n}(\lambda_{nj})\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})}{\tilde{d}_{n}(\lambda_{nj})\lambda_{nj}}

for
j=1,2,\dots,p
. Finally, let
f_{n}(x)
be given by

\xi_{n}(x)-p^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\eta_{nj}K_{nj}(x-\lambda_{nj}).

Then
f_{n}
is regular and satisfies

\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})=\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f^{*})+o_{P}(1).

As a result, the lower bound on detection power in (
5.9
) is, asymptotically in probability, (a) maximized by
f_{n}
and (b) equivalent to

1-2\exp\left(-c(\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(f_{n})-\tau)_{+}^{2}/C^{4}\right)+o_{P}(1),

(5.14)

where

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(f):=\frac{p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{ni})f(\lambda_{ni})}{\tilde{\sigma}_{n}(f)}.

The vectors
\xi_{ni}
and
\eta_{ni}
correspond to the terms
(g^{2}h+gGH)/a
and
(G^{2}H+Ggh)/a
of (
5.11
), evaluated at
\lambda_{ni}
. See Section S.8
of the Supplemental Material for details. The expression in (
5.14
) arises from (
5.8
) after taking expectations and in-probability limits, then using concentration of quadratic forms to substitute
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(f_{n})
for
\mathcal{U}_{n}(f_{n})
. ∎

Note that although the formula above for
f_{n}(x)
can be evaluated at any
x>0
, we need only evaluate the shrinkage eigenvalues—
i.e.
,
(f_{n}(\lambda_{ni}))_{i=1}^{p}
. Since each summation in
f_{n}(x)
naively takes
O(p)
time to evaluate, computing these
p
values takes
O(p^{2})
time, which is insignificant compared to the
O(p^{3})
time generally required to compute the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. If necessary, the desired shrinkage eigenvalues can be calculated even more quickly, albeit using
O(p^{2})
memory, by computing the matrix
K_{ni}(\lambda_{nj}-\lambda_{ni})
once and using fast matrix-vector multiplication to compute the summations appearing, for example, in

and

.

of the Supplemental Material for a

Remark 5.9

(Selection of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
and
\overline{h}_{n}
)
.

In realistic settings, exact knowledge of the signal dispersion matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
, or its finite-sample spectral parameters
\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{ni})
may be unavailable.

In such cases, it would be desirable to estimate
\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda_{ni})
directly from test data. However, a singleton observation
\mathbf{y}_{n}
is reflective only of the average eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
, and provides no further information about the eigen-decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
.
This limitation persists even if one makes strong structural assumptions, such as assuming that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
is a polynomial of fixed degree in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
as in
[
38
, Section 2.3]
. A data-driven workaround is only practical when multiple i.i.d
.
samples
\mathbf{y}_{ni}\sim\mathbf{y}_{n}
are available, as we investigate in Section S.11
of the Supplemental Material.

As a result, in the regime of this paper where the test sample is a singleton, one must impose additional constraints on the hypothesis test in order to accurately specify the matrix parameter
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
. We provide three examples of how this could be done.

1.

A natural constraint is that
\mathbf{y}_{n}
should maximize Shannon entropy subject to its squared norm, which by Hanson–Wright is almost surely asymptotic to
\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n})
. Since this trace approximates
\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n})
by the scaling regime of [
Test
], we may assume
\left\|\mathbf{y}_{n}\right\|^{2}
is asymptotic to
\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n})
. Subject to this fixed-power constraint, entropy maximization gives the
isotropic prior
matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\propto\mathbf{I}_{p}
.

2.

A more pessimistic approach is to assume the anomaly is well-masked by the background noise. One way to ensure this is to assume
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}\propto\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
—a challenging setting in which there is no “easy” direction for anomaly detection and which we refer to as the
covariance-matched prior
assumption.

3.

A further potential approach would be to assign an uninformative prior to a signal’s source location based on geometric constraints. For example, in distributed sensing one might model a source
to be uniformly distributed along a line-segment path based on prior knowledge.
In this case
the signal dispersion matrix can be estimated arbitrarily well using simulated samples generated from the positional prior.

A possible concern is how sensitive priors such as the isotropic and covariance-matched priors are to misspecification.
To address this, we evaluate
our method’s size-adjusted empirical power when our algorithm is forced to assume that the signal dispersion matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
is
\propto\mathbf{I}_{p}
even though the true data-generating matrix is
\propto\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
in Figure
5
. (Further, some simulations displaying the reverse misspecification can be found in Sections S.9
and
S.10
of the Supplemental Appendix.)
As demonstrated in those simulations, our method empirically outperforms other methods when the prior is specified correctly, and remains highly robust even under prior mismatch.

6
Empirical results

In this section, we evaluate the proposed shrinkage algorithm using both synthetic data and the real-world
Crawdad
Umich/RSS data set
[
23
]
.
In the synthetic experiments, we choose the population covariance matrix and generate simulated data satisfying the moment conditions in [
Train
] and [
Test
]. In the real-world set, a sensor network collects a time series of received signal strengths during periods of activity and inactivity in a lab setting. In this case, the population covariance matrix and data model are, of course, not known in the real-world set, but the truth information of when people entered and exited the lab is.

In the simulated environment, we find that our method outperforms or nearly matches all tested competitors, which we will describe below—sometimes by significant margins. For the
Crawdad
data, we imagine that there are significant dependencies and inhomogeneities between the samples, making the effective number of independent samples different from the number of reference samples and weakening some of the detectors. Nevertheless, our detector performs as well as the best competitor in our tests.

6.1
Competing algorithms

Before we discuss our empirical results further,
we briefly describe each competing method and highlight its limitations relative to the proposed algorithm.

6.1.1
BS96
and
CQ10

In the context of broader two-sample testing, where two samples have mean
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
,
Bai and Saranadasa
[
4
]
suggest replacing Hotelling’s
T^{2}
with
\left\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right\|^{2}
, effectively using the trivial shrinkage estimator of
f(\mathbf{S}_{n})=\mathbf{I}_{p}
, under the “condition-number-type” constraint that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\right\|^{2}\ll\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}\right)
holds.

Similar to
BS96
, but also suitable to the case of
p\gg n
, Chen and Qin
[
14
]
propose a test that is asymptotically optimal given a similar constraint on the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
. To be precise, the covariance matrix is required to satisfy the condition-number-type constraint
\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{4})\ll\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{2})
. Their test statistic for samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{1i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}
and
\{\mathbf{x}_{2i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
is

\frac{\sum_{i\neq j}^{n_{1}}\mathbf{x}_{1i}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}_{1j}}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)}+\frac{\sum_{i\neq j}^{n_{2}}\mathbf{x}_{2i}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}_{2j}}{n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}-\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbf{x}_{1i}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}_{2j}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

Although this test statistic,
BS96
, and several other algorithms we describe are applicable to values of
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
that both differ from 1, we will always assume that
n_{2}=1
for the sake of comparison with ours.
In our simulations, we do not test the ultra-high-dimensional regime, apparently making
CQ10
perform equivalently to
BS96
. As a result, in the plots that follow, we will show only
CQ10
, to represent both algorithms.

Limitation
: These algorithms are designed for covariance matrices with condition numbers satisfying growth constraints, and tend to degrade in performance for ill-conditioned ones.

Knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
used
: It is assumed, in our setting, that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
can be replaced in the limit by
cp^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{p}
without any loss of asymptotic power, for some constant
c
. (For test samples of size order-
n
, the factor of
p^{1/2}
can be dropped.)

6.1.2
LAPPW20

Li et al.
[
38
]
assume that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
is a finite-rank perturbation of the identity matrix—the well-known spiked covariance model of Johnstone
[
26
]
.
In
LAPPW20
, the “pooled” sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}
(asymptotically equivalent to our
\mathbf{S}_{n}
when
n_{2}=1
) is replaced by a linear shrinkage estimator of the form
\mathbf{S}_{n}+b\mathbf{I}_{p}
, for some data-dependent

. The scalar

is chosen to locally maximize an asymptotic detection criterion similar to ours, where the signal dispersion matrix

is
\mathbf{I}_{p},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}
, with various prior probabilities. (Exponents higher than two can be incorporated, as well, but are not addressed explicitly.) The proposed method of maximization is to use a grid search from
p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_{n})
to
20\left\|\mathbf{S}_{n}\right\|
to maximize a spiked analogue of
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(1/f)
for
f(x)=x+b
.

In our implementation, we only consider signal dispersion matrices of
\mathbf{I}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
, and we perform a log-scale grid search consisting of
100
points over the suggested interval.

Limitation
: This algorithm tends to degrade in performance when the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is highly complex, consisting of many significant eigenvalues, rather than conforming to the low-rank-plus-noise model. Further, only partial guidance is provided on how to implement the grid search for

, and overly fine grid searches appear to yield spurious optima in moderate dimensions such as 200.

Knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
used
: The matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
is assumed to be a finite-rank matrix plus the identity (spiked). The signal dispersion matrix is assumed to belong to a set of matrices of the form

for some constrained set of weights

. (Canonically, the weights belong to
\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1)\}

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
or
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}

6.1.3
LW22

Based on Ledoit and Wolf’s work in
[
36
]
, we choose to replace the sample covariance matrix with the

“quadratic inverse shrinkage” estimator using code provided by the authors in their supplemental material.

Motivated by unifying their earlier shrinkage work with

The derived estimator was later proven to order-minimize the Frobenius distance from the equivariant oracle estimator

The algebraic form of their covariance estimator differs slightly from our Theorem

\mathcal{U}_{n,MV}(f_{n}):=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n}))}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n}))}}.

This differs by a factor of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

Knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
used

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
need only have limiting spectrum supported on a finite union of intervals (in particular, true for spiked models), and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}
is not assumed to be known.

Repeating this logic when
f
is instead “just barely” dominated by the indicator function of
I
provides the reverse inequality.
∎

We proceed under the assumption that
f
is compactly supported; this only strengthens the bound since the supports of
\mu
and
\nu
are bounded by some fixed constant with high probability.

Let
\hat{\mu}=\mu-\mu_{\infty}
, and let
\hat{m}=m-m_{\infty}
be the Stieltjies transform of
\hat{\mu}
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\eta=n^{-1+\epsilon}
.

Let
\chi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1])
be a smooth cutoff function with
\chi(0)>0
. Lastly, let
h\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1])
be supported on
[a-\eta,b+\eta]
, be identically 1 on
[a,b]
, and satisfy
\lVert h^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\eta^{-1},\lVert h^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\eta^{-2}
.

By Helffer-Sjöstrand formula, we may write the left-hand side of equation (
S.8.1
) as

\int f(\lambda)\hat{\mu}(d\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int dy\left(\partial_{x}+\imath\partial_{y}\right)\left[\left(f(x)+\imath yf^{\prime}(x)\right)\chi(y)\right]\hat{m}(x+\imath y),

which upon expanding and using that the left-hand side is real, reads

\begin{split}\int f(\lambda)\hat{\mu}(d\lambda)=&-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int dyf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\\
&-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int dyf(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\\
&-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int dyf^{\prime}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)y\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\end{split}

The second and third terms are easiest to bound. Since

\left|\chi^{\prime}(y)\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\right|\leq\eta
uniformly on the purely complex set
\mathrm{supp}\,\chi^{\prime}(y)\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, we bound the second term by

\begin{split}&\lessapprox\left|\int dxf(x)\int dy\chi^{\prime}(y)\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\right|\\
&\lessapprox\eta\int dx\left|f(x)\right|.\end{split}

Similarly the third term is bounded by

\begin{split}&\lessapprox\left|\int dxf^{\prime}(x)\int dy\chi^{\prime}(y)y\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\imath y)\right|\\
&\lessapprox\eta\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|.\end{split}

Now, we must bound the first term

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int dyf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y).

(S.8.4)

First we bound

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|\leq\eta}dyf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y).

(S.8.5)

It is a general fact of Stieltjies transforms
t
of positive measures that

y\mapsto y|t(x+\mathrm{i}y)|

is nondecreasing for
y>0
and for any
x
. Since
\eta|\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}\eta)|\leq\eta
with high probability, and moreover
\eta|m_{\infty}(x+\mathrm{i}\eta)|\leq\eta
since the density of
\mu_{\infty}
is bounded, we find that
\eta|m(x+\mathrm{i}\eta)|\leq\eta
.
This general fact may then be applied to both
y|m(x+\mathrm{i}y)|
and
y|m_{\infty}(x+\mathrm{i}y)|
, together with
t(\overline{z})=\overline{t(z)}
, to establish

\max_{\left|y\right|\leq\eta}\left|y\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right|\leq\eta

(S.8.6)

with high probability. Now we may bound (
S.8.5
) by

\lessapprox\int dx\left|f^{\prime\prime}(x)\right|\int_{\left|y\right|\leq\eta}dy\cdot\eta\lessapprox\eta^{2}\int dx\left|f^{\prime\prime}(x)\right|

with high probability.

Lastly we bound

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dyf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y).

(S.8.7)

This is more difficult that the previous bound because the triangle inequality for integrals is no longer affordable. The way around this is to use that
\int dxf^{\prime\prime}(x)
has a much tighter bound than
\int dx\left|f^{\prime\prime}(x)\right|
, through the fundamental theorem of calculus. Some care is still required, however, since we must not bound
\int dxf^{\prime\prime}(x)
only but the larger expression (
S.8.7
) which has another
x
-dependent factor in the integrand.

We integrate by parts: first in
x
, noting that
f^{\prime}
vanishes at
\pm\infty
,

\begin{split}&-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dyf^{\prime\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dyf^{\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\partial_{x}\operatorname{Im}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dyf^{\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\partial_{y}\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\end{split}

where in the last line we used the holomorphy of
\hat{m}
, and then integrating by parts over
y
,

\begin{split}&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dx\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dyf^{\prime}(x)\chi(y)y\partial_{y}\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dxf^{\prime}(x)\left(\left[y\chi(y)\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right]_{\eta}^{-\eta}-\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dy(\chi(y)+y\chi^{\prime}(y))\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right)\end{split}

Continuing,

\begin{split}\left|\cdot\right|&\lessapprox\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|\left|O(\eta)-\int_{\left|y\right|>\eta}dy(\chi(y)\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)+y\chi^{\prime}(y)\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y))\right|\\
&\lessapprox\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|\left(O(\eta)+\int_{\eta<\left|y\right|<2}dy\chi(y)\left|\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right|+\int_{1<\left|y\right|<2}y\left|\operatorname{Re}\hat{m}(x+\mathrm{i}y)\right|\right)\\
&\lessapprox\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|\left(O(\eta)+\int_{\eta}^{2}dy\frac{\eta}{y}+O\left(\eta\right)\right)\\
&\lessapprox\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|\left(O(\eta)-\eta\log\eta\right)\\
&\lessapprox\eta\log\eta\int dx\left|f^{\prime}(x)\right|\end{split}

with high probability, where we have used (
S.8.6
) in the middle step. Thus, we have established equations (
S.8.7
), (
S.8.4
), and ultimately, (
S.8.1
).

The proof is exactly the same when
\mu,\mu_{\infty}
are replaced with
\nu,\nu_{\infty}
.

∎

S.9
L^{1}
Error in the Ledoit–Wolf Eigenvalues

In what follows, continuing to use our convention of suppressing subscripts of
n
,
L^{1}(\mu)
will denote the
n
-dependent norm
L^{1}(\mu_{n})
, and
\tilde{w}(x)
will denote
\tilde{w}_{n}(x)
, for example.

The goal is to approximate
\delta(x)=x/\{[1-\phi-\phi x\mathcal{H}w(x)]^{2}+\phi^{2}x^{2}\pi^{2}w(x)^{2}\}
in the
L^{1}(\mu)
-norm to order
O_{\prec}(n^{-2/3})
. Since
\delta(x)
is bounded below on a neighborhood of
F
by Section
S.7
, it will suffice to show
\left\|\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}-\mathcal{H}w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}
and
\left\|\tilde{w}-w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}
are both
O_{\prec}(n^{-2/3})
. Throughout this and later appendices,
\Delta
will stand for
n^{-1/3}
.

For the sake of brevity, we will alternatively denote
\tilde{w}(x)
to be the additive convolution
\varphi_{\Delta}*\mu
, where
\varphi_{\Delta}(x)
is the even function
\Delta^{-1}k(x/\Delta)
, and
\mu
is again the empirical spectral measure.
This marks a slight departure from the multiplicative convolution with the approximation to the identity
x\mapsto\Delta^{-1}k((x-1)/\Delta)
appearing in the theorem, but the proofs are identical to order
\Delta^{2}
after appropriate logarithmic transformations and Hilbert-transform invariances are applied. The reason the multiplicative convolution was originally proposed is mainly a practical one: the smallest estimated eigenvalue converges more quickly as
n\to\infty
in the multiplicative formulation.

Our first main result of this section is the following.

Proposition S.9.1
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem 4.5.
Then
\tilde{w}
satisfies

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})-w(\lambda_{i})|\prec n^{-2/3}.

(In other words,
\left\|\tilde{w}-w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}\prec n^{-2/3}
.)

We will use the decomposition, with
w_{\Delta}:=\varphi_{\Delta}*d\mu_{\infty}
,

\displaystyle|w-\tilde{w}|

\displaystyle\leq|w-w_{\Delta}|+|\varphi_{\Delta}*(d\mu_{\infty}-d\mu)|,

(S.9.1)

first considering the
L^{1}(\mu)
norm of second term in the decomposition.

Lemma S.9.2
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem 4.5.
Then

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\varphi_{\Delta}*(d\mu_{\infty}-d\mu)|(\lambda_{i})\prec\Delta^{-1}p^{-1}

By Lemma
S.8.2
, the second term of the right-hand side of (
S.9.1
) is

\prec n^{-1}\left\lVert\varphi_{\Delta}\right\rVert_{1}+n^{-1}\left\lVert\partial_{x}\varphi_{\Delta}\right\rVert_{1}+n^{-2}\left\lVert\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi_{\Delta}\right\rVert_{1}.

We only consider the case where
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
have no more than 3 monotonic intervals. The fundamental theorem of calculus then yields

\begin{split}\prec n^{-1}+6n^{-1}\left\lVert\varphi_{\Delta}\right\rVert_{\infty}+6n^{-2}\left\lVert\partial_{x}\varphi_{\Delta}\right\rVert_{\infty}\\
\prec n^{-1}+6n^{-1}\Delta^{-1}+6n^{-2}\Delta^{-2}\prec\Delta^{-1}n^{-1}\end{split}

so that

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|w_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})-\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{-1}n^{-1}.

(S.9.2)

∎

We will now focus on the first term in the decomposition of (
S.9.1
), along with a similar result for
\mathcal{H}w
.

Lemma S.9.3
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem 4.5.
Then

(a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|w(\lambda_{i})-w_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{2},\qquad\text{\emph{and}}

(b)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\mathcal{H}w(\lambda_{i})-\mathcal{H}w_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{2}.

By Lemma
S.7.1
, we have
w^{(k)}(x)
exists and
w^{(k)}(x)\lessapprox\kappa(x)^{1/2-k}
if
x\not\in\partial F
, and we get the same properties for
\mathcal{H}w(x)
.
Thus, letting
a(x)
be a smooth function on
\mathbb{R}\backslash(\partial F)
that satisfies
a^{(k)}(x)\lessapprox\kappa(x)^{1/2-k}
, and defining
a_{\Delta}=\varphi_{\Delta}*a
,
the following is sufficient for both (a) and (b):

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|a(\lambda_{i})-a_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{2}.

To this end, for
\kappa(x)\leq 2\Delta

we have

\begin{split}|a(x)-a_{\Delta}(x)|&\leq\int_{x-\Delta}^{x+\Delta}\varphi_{\Delta}(x-t)|a(x)-a(t)|\,dt\\
&\lessapprox\int_{x-\Delta}^{x+\Delta}\left|\varphi_{\Delta}(x-t)\right|\Delta^{-1/2}\Delta\,dt\\
&\leq\Delta^{1/2},\end{split}

where we have applied the mean value theorem to
|a(x)-a(t)|
and used
a^{\prime}(x)\lessapprox\kappa(x)^{-1/2}\lessapprox\Delta^{-1/2}
.
Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\kappa(\lambda_{i})\leq 2\Delta}|a(x)-a_{\Delta}(x)|\leq\left(C(2\Delta)^{3/2}+p^{\epsilon-1}\right)\Delta^{1/2}\lessapprox\Delta^{2}

(S.9.3)

with high probability, where we estimated the number of terms in the sum using the small-scale Marčenko–Pastur law (second clause of Theorem 3.3).

For
\kappa(x)>2\Delta
, Taylor-expanding

a(t)
about
t=x
and using evenness of
\varphi
gives that

\begin{split}a(x)-a_{\Delta}(x)&=\int_{x-\Delta}^{x+\Delta}\varphi_{\Delta}(x-t)(a(x)-a(t))\,dt\\
&=\int_{x-\Delta}^{x+\Delta}\varphi_{\Delta}(x-t)\\
&\quad\cdot\left(a^{\prime}(x)(x-t)-\frac{a^{\prime\prime}(x)}{2}(x-t)^{2}+O(\Delta^{3}\max_{[x-\Delta,x+\Delta]}a^{\prime\prime\prime})\right)\,dt\\
&\lessapprox\Delta^{2}(\kappa(x)-\Delta)^{-3/2}\\
&\lessapprox\Delta^{2}\kappa(x)^{-3/2}\end{split}

(S.9.4)

where we have used
the bound
\left|a^{(k)}\right|\lessapprox\kappa^{1/2-k}
for any fixed
k\geq 1
.

Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\kappa(\lambda_{i})>2\Delta}|a(\lambda_{i})-a_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})|\lessapprox\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\kappa(\lambda_{i})>2\Delta}\Delta^{2}\kappa(\lambda_{i})^{-3/2}

The right hand side is precisely

\int\widetilde{f}d\mu,

where
\widetilde{f}(x):=\Delta^{2}\kappa(x)^{-3/2}\boldsymbol{1}_{\kappa(x)>2\Delta}
. The only difficulty in applying Lemma
S.8.2
is that
\widetilde{f}
is discontinuous, so we must adjust it. Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be
C^{\infty}
, equal
0
for
x\geq 2
, equal
1
for
x\leq 1
and satisfy
\left\lVert g^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert g^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C
. Then define

f(x)=\widetilde{f}(x)g((2\Delta)^{-1}\kappa(x)),

which is now
C^{2}
and satisfies

\begin{split}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{1}&\leq 1.\end{split}

Therefore Lemma
S.8.2
gives

\begin{split}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\kappa(\lambda_{i})>2\Delta}|a(x)-a_{\Delta}(x)|&\lessapprox\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\kappa(\lambda_{i})>2\Delta}\Delta^{2}\kappa(\lambda_{i})^{3/2}\\
&\lessapprox\int\widetilde{f}(x)d\mu(x)\\
&=\int\widetilde{f}(x)d\mu_{\infty}(x)+O_{\prec}(n^{-1})\\
&\lessapprox\int\widetilde{f}(x)\kappa(x)^{1/2}dx+O_{\prec}(n^{-1})\\
&\lessapprox\Delta^{2}\int_{\kappa(x)>2\Delta}\kappa(x)^{-3/2}\kappa(x)^{1/2}\,dx+O_{\prec}(n^{-1})\\
&\asymp\Delta^{2}\log|\Delta|\prec\Delta^{2}\end{split}

(S.9.5)

with high probability.

∎

We may now quickly prove the Proposition above.

Equations (
S.9.3
) and (
S.9.5
) together establish that

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})-w_{\Delta}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{2},

which, with equation (
S.9.2
) gives

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|w(\lambda_{i})-\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})|\prec\Delta^{2}+\Delta^{-1}n^{-1}.

Thus the choice of
\Delta=n^{-1/3}
becomes clear..

∎

The Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}w
may be treated in much the same way as
w
.

Proposition S.9.4
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem 4.5.
Then
\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}
satisfies

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})-\mathcal{H}w(\lambda_{i})|\prec n^{-2/3}.

(In other words,
\left\|\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}-\mathcal{H}w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}\prec n^{-2/3}
.)

We decompose:

\begin{split}\lvert\mathcal{H}w-\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}\rvert&\leq\lvert\mathcal{H}w-\mathcal{H}w_{\Delta}\rvert+\lvert\mathcal{H}w_{\Delta}-\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}\rvert\end{split}

Using the relation between
\mathcal{H}
and convolution, the above may be written as

\begin{split}\lvert\mathcal{H}w-\varphi_{\Delta}*(\mathcal{H}w)\rvert+\lvert(\mathcal{H}\varphi_{\Delta})*\mu_{\infty}-(\mathcal{H}\varphi_{\Delta})*\mu\rvert\end{split}

(S.9.6)

The latter term may be written, letting
\hat{\varphi}:=\mathcal{H}\varphi
, as

\lvert(\mathcal{H}\varphi_{\Delta})*\mu_{\infty}-(\mathcal{H}\varphi_{\Delta})*\mu\rvert=\lvert\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}*\mu_{\infty}-\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}*\mu\rvert

Let
f=\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}\cdot g
, where
g
is
C^{\infty}
, equals 1 on
[-C,C]
, equals 0 on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2C,2C]
, and has bounded first and second derivatives, for some large constant
C
.
As in the proof of Lemma
S.9.2
, we use Lemma
S.8.2
: uniformly in
x\leq C/2
,

\begin{split}\left|\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}*\mu_{\infty}(x)-\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}*\mu(x)\right|&=\left|f*\mu_{\infty}(x)-f*\mu(x)\right|\\
&\prec n^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}+n^{-1}\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}+n^{-2}\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\end{split}

(S.9.7)

(the appearance of
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}
is why we needed to attenuate
\hat{\varphi}_{\Delta}
by a cutoff function:
\mathcal{H}\varphi
is not
L^{1}
; we may do this because
\mu_{\infty}
and
\mu
have uniformly bounded support with high probability). Now we may conclude similarly to in the argument leading to (
S.9.2
). We have that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1}\lessapprox|\log\Delta|
, and that
f
and
f^{\prime}
may be ensured to have a bounded number of monotonic intervals, so that
\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\lessapprox\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\lessapprox\Delta^{-1}
and
\left\lVert f^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert_{1}\lessapprox\left\lVert f^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\infty}\lessapprox\Delta^{-2}
.

As a result of the above,
\|\mathcal{H}w-\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}
can be broken into
O_{\prec}(n^{-1}\Delta^{-1})

and the following

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left|\mathcal{H}w(\lambda_{i})-\varphi_{\Delta}*(\mathcal{H}w)(\lambda_{i})\right|,

which is
O_{\prec}(\Delta^{2})
by Lemma
S.9.3
(b).
Thus, choosing
\Delta=n^{-1/3}
as before completes the proof.

∎

Combining the Propositions yields precisely the goal named at the beginning of this section, that
\left\|\tilde{w}-w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}\prec n^{-2/3}
and
\left\|\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}-\mathcal{H}w\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}\prec n^{-2/3}
.

S.10
Deterministic Limiting Variance

We choose the somewhat more general task of estimating
p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}_{I}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}g_{n}(\mathbf{S}_{n}))
for some matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}
having
’

p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{ni}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}\mathbf{u}_{ni}}{\lambda_{ni}-z}=\int\frac{\xi_{\infty}(x)}{x-z}\,dx+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{n\eta}\right)

for some bounded measurable
\xi_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. By the Ledoit–Péché theorem (Theorem 4.1)
and Section
S.8
, this is certainly true of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
—the value of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}
that is of most interest—in which case
\xi_{\infty}(x)=\delta(x)
.

For this section, we will need the notation
O_{\tilde{P}}(\,\cdot\,)
, which is the in-probability analogue of
O_{\prec}(\,\cdot\,)
. In other words, writing
a_{n}=O_{\tilde{P}}(b_{n})
means for every
\epsilon>0
,

\Pr[|a_{n}|\geq n^{\epsilon}b_{n}]\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
.

The strategy is to first estimate the alternating trace above for
f
and
g
equal to resolvent functions, then use the usual limiting argument as complex arguments go the real axis to go from resolvent functions to continuous functions. For the remainder of this section, we will resume following the convention of suppressing most subscripts of
n
.

Assume as a first case that
f(\lambda)\equiv f_{n}(\lambda)=(\lambda-x_{z}-i\eta_{z})^{-1}
and
g(\lambda)\equiv g_{n}(\lambda)=(\lambda-x_{v}-i\eta_{v})^{-1}
for positive and distinct
\eta_{x}
and
\eta_{z}
, and let

z=x_{z}+i\eta_{z}
and
v=x_{v}+i\eta_{v}
. Our first main goal will be to prove the following lemma:

Lemma S.10.1
.

Assume
[
Train1
]-[
Train4
]
. Then

\begin{split}&p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Xi}f(\mathbf{S})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}g(\mathbf{S})\right)\\
&=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{i}-v)}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-z}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-v}\right)\\
&+O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{n\eta_{z}\eta_{v}}\right).\end{split}

(S.10.1)

This lemma can be improved to high-probability convergence using the recent multi-resolvent laws for sample covariance matrices
[
39
]
, but we reproduce the proof here with the weaker
O_{\tilde{P}}
mode of convergence for completeness.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{k}=n^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}_{k}
and
\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)=(\mathbf{R}(z)^{-1}-\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime})^{-1}
.
First, we prove a lemma involving the average of the recurring estimation error
\epsilon_{k}
, where

\epsilon_{k}:=\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z))}{n}.

Lemma S.10.2
.

Assume
[
Train1
]-[
Train4
]
, and define
\eta:=\eta_{z}
and
\epsilon_{k}
as above. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\epsilon_{k}=O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{n\eta}\right).

Let
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}=\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}-n^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z))
.
Since we have from
[
52
, Lemma 2.6]
that

\left|\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z))}{n}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z))}{n}\right|\leq\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|}{n\eta},

it suffices to prove that
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{p}:=p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}=O_{\tilde{P}}(n^{-1}\eta^{-1})
.

First we prove, uniformly in
k
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}|^{2}\prec\frac{1}{n\eta}.

(S.10.2)

By Hanson–Wright
[
51
]
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}|\geq t\mid\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\right]\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left(\frac{n^{2}t^{2}}{C^{4}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}},\frac{nt}{C^{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\right\|}\right)\right],

for some absolute
c>0
and some
C>0
depending only on the moments of
W
.
Thus, we would like to estimate the Hilbert-Schmidt norm above: namely,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\|\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{k}(\overline{z}))\right)

(S.10.3)

\displaystyle\lessapprox\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{k}}{|\tilde{\lambda}_{k}-z|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{i\eta}\left(\Theta^{(k)}(z)-\Theta^{(k)}(\overline{z})\right),

where
(\,\cdot\,)^{*}
denotes the Hermitian transpose,
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
are the eigenvalues/vectors, and
\Theta^{(k)}(z)
is the Ledoit–Péché analytic function, corresponding to
\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}
. Since, as discussed in Section 4.2,

\Theta^{(k)}(z)
has a limiting value as
z\to x\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, the left-hand side of (
S.10.3
) has order at most
n/\eta
as
n\to\infty
, almost surely. It can also be seen that the spectral norm has order at most
1/\eta
. Thus, taking
t
to be
n^{\epsilon}/\sqrt{\eta n}
and smoothing gives (
S.10.4
). For the sake of the analysis that follows, we also note that by the (conditional) layercake theorem we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}|^{2}\mid\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)]\prec\frac{1}{n\eta}.

(S.10.4)

Consider terms of the form
\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}\overline{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{k}
, where
\overline{(\,\,\cdot\,\,)}
denotes the complex conjugate operation and
j\neq k
.
Using the Woodbury formula, we obtain

\mathbf{R}^{(j)}(z)=\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)-\frac{\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}},

where
\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)=(\mathbf{R}^{(j)}(z)^{-1}-\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime})^{-1}
.
Using steps similar to (
S.10.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}\overline{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{k}\mid\mathbf{R}^{(jk)}(z)]=O_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\eta^{2}}\right).

(S.10.5)

As a result, by applying Markov and smoothing (
S.10.4
) and (
S.10.5
), we get

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\lvert\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{p}\rvert\geq t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}p^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}\overline{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{k}]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{n^{\epsilon}}{t^{2}n^{2}\eta}+\frac{n^{\epsilon}}{t^{2}n^{2}\eta^{2}}\right).

for any
\epsilon>0
.
Taking
t=n^{\epsilon}/(n\eta)
gives the desired result for
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{p}
, and thus, as remarked earlier in the proof, for
\mathcal{E}_{p}
.
∎

We now turn to the multi-resolvent law stated earlier.

We first observe the following:

\displaystyle p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{R}(v)\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi})

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{u}_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{i}-v)},

(S.10.6)

Consider

(S.10.7)

Since

, we can write the above as

\displaystyle p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}((\mathbf{I}+z\mathbf{R}(z))\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v)).

Writing
\mathbf{S}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}.

(S.10.8)

Appling the Woodbury formula again, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(z)=\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)-\frac{\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}},

which gives the following expansion of (
S.10.8
):

\displaystyle p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}+

\displaystyle-p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}}\right)+

\displaystyle-p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}}\right)+

\displaystyle+p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}\frac{\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}}\frac{\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}}{1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}}

\displaystyle=p^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}}{\left(1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\mathbf{c}_{k}\right)\left(1+\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}\right)}.

(S.10.9)

By calculations similar to the ones in Lemma

,

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left[\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}^{(k)}(v)\mathbf{c}_{k}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))}{n}\right]=O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}n}\right).

This together with (

\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))}{\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z))\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(v))\right)}+O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}n}\right).

Comparing to (

) and (

) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-v}+z\left(p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{u}_{i}}}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{i}-v)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))}{\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z))\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(v))\right)}+O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}n}\right)

so that, using the asymptotic boundedness of
1+\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z))
, which follows from the boundedness clause in Section
S.7
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}(z)\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{R}(v))

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-z}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-v}\right)p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Xi}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{(\lambda_{i}-z)(\lambda_{i}-v)}

\displaystyle+O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}n}\right),

as desired.
∎

We now find a deterministic limiting expression for (
S.10.1
). Applying the argument of Section
S.8
to the real and imaginary parts of

n^{-1}\sum_{i}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-z}

and using linearity allows us to replace this summation by
z\mapsto\phi\int d\nu_{\infty}(\lambda)/(\lambda-z)
, with a negligible error of
1/(n\min\{\eta_{z},\eta_{v}\})
. Similarly, using partial fractions on the remaining sum and assuming
\liminf_{n}|\eta_{z}/\eta_{v}-1|\neq 0
allows us to replace this sum by

\int\frac{\lambda\xi(\lambda)}{(\lambda-z)(\lambda-v)}w(\lambda)\,d\lambda+O_{\prec}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}p}\right).

Expanding the resulting product of three integral expressions and using Fubini’s theorem and some algebra gives

\begin{split}&p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Xi}f(\mathbf{S})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}g(\mathbf{S})\right)\\
&=\int f(x)g(x)x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)\\
&-\phi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)\frac{g(y)-g(x)}{y-x}\,x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)d\nu_{\infty}(y)\\
&-\phi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g(x)\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}\,x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)d\nu_{\infty}(y)\\
&+\phi^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{g(u)-g(x)}{u-x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}\,x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)d\nu_{\infty}(y)d\nu_{\infty}(u)+O_{\tilde{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{z}\eta_{v}n}\right).\end{split}

(S.10.10)

Next, we claim that this identity holds for general regular functions
\tilde{f}\equiv\tilde{f}_{n}
and
\tilde{g}\equiv\tilde{g}_{n}
.
For this, we simply integrate against
\tilde{f}(\eta_{z})
and
\tilde{g}(\eta_{v})
and let
\eta_{z}
and
\eta_{v}
go to zero at a rate faster than
n^{-1/3}
but slower than
n^{-1/2}
. This results in the above equality with
f\leftarrow\tilde{f}*\varphi_{\eta_{z}}
and

, where
\varphi
is the Cauchy kernel. Further, since
\tilde{f}
is regular, we have
\tilde{f}*\varphi_{\eta}(x)=\tilde{f}(x)+O_{\prec}(n^{2/3}\eta^{2})
uniformly in
x
, and so we replace these convolutions by
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
, incurring only
o_{P}(1)
error. The claim follows since our choice of
\eta_{z},\eta_{v}
implies that the error of
O_{P}(n^{-1+\epsilon}\eta_{z}^{-1}\eta_{v}^{-1})
goes to zero in probability for sufficiently small
\epsilon
.

Thus, we may assume (
S.10.10
) holds for general regular functions
f
and
g
and the right-hand side of this identity can be factored, giving

\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f;\xi):=\int[Tf(x)]^{2}x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)

(S.10.11)

since linearity of the Hilbert transform can be used to express
T

Tf(x)=f(x)-\phi\int\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}d\nu_{\infty}(y).

In particular, (
S.10.10

\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f)=\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f;\delta)
, as desired.

S.11
Consistency of Variance Estimate

Let

\mathcal{H}_{w}f\equiv\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]:=\mathcal{H}[fw]

and

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}f(x)\equiv\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f](x):=p^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i})K_{\Delta\lambda_{i}}(x-\lambda_{i}).

Intuitively,
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}f
uses a kernel to approximate
\mathcal{H}_{w}f
.
Rewriting
Tf
as
(1+\phi\pi\mathcal{H}w)f-\phi\pi\mathcal{H}_{w}[f\delta]
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}f
as
(1+(p/n)\pi\mathcal{H}\tilde{w})f-(p/n)\pi\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f\tilde{d}_{n}]
and expanding
(Tf)^{2}
and
(\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}f)^{2}
in the expressions for
\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f)
and
(\sigma_{n}^{o})^{2}
, one of the main analytic steps is to show

\int\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f\tilde{d}_{n}](x)x\tilde{d}_{n}(x)\,d\mu_{n}(x)=\int\mathcal{H}_{w}[f\delta](x)x\delta(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)+o_{P}(1).

The remaining terms in
\sigma_{n}^{o}(f)^{2}-\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(f)
can be handled similarly.

Using uniform convergence in probability of
\tilde{d}_{n}(x)
to
\delta(x)
on
F
guaranteed by one of Ledoit and Wolf’s nonlinear shrinkage results (reproduced in Theorem 4.3),
all instances of
\tilde{d}_{n}(x)
can be replaced by
\delta(x)
without affecting the desired result. The main task is essentially the same as proving

\int\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]\,d\mu_{n}=\int\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]\,d\mu_{\infty}+o_{P}(1)

for any regular function
f
, with only slight modifications needed to account for the differently weighted measures.

We first note
\int f\,d\mu_{n}
is asymptotically equivalent to
\int f\,d\mu_{\infty}
. Indeed, by Section
S.8
, the error in this approximation is of order

\frac{\left\|f\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{1}}{n^{2}}\prec\Delta^{2}

since
|f^{(k)}|\prec\Delta^{k}
by regularity for
k\leq 3
.

We next show that
\int\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]\,d\mu_{\infty}
approximates
\int\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]\,d\mu_{\infty}
in probability.
To simplify our analysis, as before, we make the replacement
\Delta\lambda_{i}\leftarrow\Delta
in
K_{\Delta\lambda_{i}}
above. Similar to before, without this replacement
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f](x)
is a convolution with respect to multiplication rather than addition, so the analyses do not significantly differ in character.

Lemma S.11.1
.

Assume
[
Train
]
and that
f
is regular. Then we have the following estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]-\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]\right)w\right\|_{q}^{q}\prec\Delta^{2}

for any finite
q\geq 1
.

Using the notation
K_{\Delta}(x)=\Delta^{-1}K(x/\Delta)
and, we have
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]=K_{\Delta}*(f\,d\mu_{n})
.
Using the Helffer-Sjöstrand argument of Section
S.8
and the fact that

\left\|(d/d\lambda)K_{\Delta}(x-\lambda)\right\|_{1}\lessapprox 1/\Delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|(d/d\lambda)^{2}K_{\Delta}(x-\lambda)\right\|_{1}\lessapprox 1/\Delta^{2},

we get that
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}f
can be approximated by

\int f(\lambda)K_{\Delta}(x-\lambda)w(\lambda)\,d\lambda=\int f(\lambda)K_{\Delta}(x-\lambda)w(\lambda)\varphi(\lambda)\,d\lambda

where
\varphi
is a smooth cutoff function with
\varphi^{\prime}\leq\Delta^{-1}
and
\varphi^{\prime\prime}\leq\Delta^{-2}
that takes the value 1 on
F
and 0 on
\mathbb{R}\backslash(F+\Delta)
.
By Section
S.8
, then, the error in the above approximation of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}f
can be written as

O_{\prec}\left(\frac{\left\|fK\right\|_{L^{1}(F)}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime}K+fK^{\prime}/\Delta\right\|_{L^{1}(F)}}{n}+\frac{\left\|f^{\prime\prime}K+f^{\prime}K^{\prime}/\Delta+fK^{\prime\prime}/\Delta^{2}\right\|_{L^{1}(F)}}{n^{2}}\right),

which is
O_{\prec}\left(\Delta^{2}\right)
since
f^{\prime\prime}
is bounded and
K,K^{\prime},
and
K^{\prime\prime}
are integrable on
F
.

We may now use the anti-self-adjointess of the Hilbert transform to obtain from the integral above

\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]*k_{\Delta}(x)=\int\mathcal{H}[fw](\lambda)k_{\Delta}(x-\lambda)\,d\lambda.

Using continuity and/or the properties of approximate identities, this function converges in various modes to the desired function
\mathcal{H}_{w}f
, but we have yet to establish the desired mode and rate of convergence.

Up to this point, we have shown that
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]
is an
O_{\prec}(\Delta^{2})
approximation to
\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]*k_{\Delta}
with repect to the Lebesgue measure’s infinity norm. We next show that
\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]*k_{\Delta}
converges to
\mathcal{H}_{w}f
at a rate of
O_{\prec}(\Delta^{2/q})
in the
L^{q}(\mu_{\infty})
norm for finite
q\geq 1
. That is,

\int|\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]*k_{\Delta}(\lambda)-\mathcal{H}_{w}f(\lambda)|^{q}w\,dx\prec\Delta^{2}.

This estimate follows from the fact that the integrand is eventually bounded and for
q=1
the left-hand side is bounded by
O_{\prec}(\Delta^{2}|\log\Delta|)=O_{\prec}(\Delta^{2})
in exactly the same way as in Lemma
S.8.2
, since square-root behavior of
w
near the spectral edges implies square-root behavior of
\mathcal{H}[fw]
near the spectral edges.
∎

S.12
Alternate significance level calculation

In this section, we expand upon the discussion at the end of Section 5.1
of the main text to formally demonstrate how to obtain an asymptotic significance level that is independent of sub-Gaussian constants.

In order to assume as little as possible about the moment structure of
W
, we use a procedure from
[
40
, Section 3.3]
to estimate the only one which is needed: the fourth moment. For notational tidiness, we denote the excess kurtosis
\mathbb{E}[W^{4}]-3
by
\gamma_{2}
. A formula from
[
5
, Equation 9.8.6]
that holds under our independent-components and spectral assumptions, provided each component has a finite eighth moment, is, in our notation,

\gamma_{2}=\frac{a_{n}-2b_{n}}{c_{n}},

where

a_{n}=\mathrm{var}\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

and

b_{n}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}\right\|_{HS}^{2}

and

c_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\overline{\sigma}_{j}^{4},

where
(\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2},\overline{\sigma}_{2}^{2},\dots\overline{\sigma}_{p}^{2})=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n})
. As discussed in
[
40
, Section 3.3]
, rate-consistent estimates for the quantities appearing above are

\hat{a}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\right\|^{2}\right)^{2}

and

\hat{b}_{n}:=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_{n}^{2})-\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_{n})^{2}

and

\hat{c}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}^{2}\right)^{2},

where
(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{j}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
component of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
in the standard observable basis.
As a result, the authors define the following consistent estimator for
\gamma_{2}
:

\hat{\gamma}_{2}:=\max\left\{3+\frac{\hat{a}_{n}-2\hat{b}_{n}}{\hat{c}_{n}},1\right\}-3

(If desired, this estimator can be set to 0 when infinite, but this choice is of no asymptotic importance.)

With this estimator in hand, we can establish the desired asymptotic significance level. Specifically, as
n,p\to\infty
, the following significance level is asymptotically valid:

\Pr\left[\tilde{Z}_{n}>\tau\mid\mathcal{H}_{0}\right]\leq\Phi\left(-\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2+\max\{0,\hat{\gamma}_{2}\}}}\right)+o_{P}(1),

where
\Phi
is the standard normal c.d.f. and
\tau>0
. To see why this holds, let
\mathbf{z}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\mathbf{z}_{n}
. We recall that the variance of

\mathbf{z}_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\mathbf{z}_{n}

can be written as

\gamma_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ii}^{2}+2\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\right\|_{HS}^{2},

(S.12.1)

where the matrix elements
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ij}
are evaluated in the basis with respect to which the noise components are independent.

If
\gamma_{2}>0
, the summation multiplying
\gamma_{2}
in (
S.12.1
) can be bounded above using the larger sum

\ \sum_{i,j=1}^{p}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n})_{ij}^{2}=\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\|_{HS}^{2},

allowing us to use the estimate
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
in place of
\gamma_{2}
. On the other hand, if
\gamma_{2}\leq 0
, the variance in (
S.12.1
) can be bounded coarsely by
2\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{n}\|_{HS}^{2}
. Thus, in both cases as
n\to\infty
, the variance or variance-upper-bound is asymptotic to
(2+\max\{0,\hat{\gamma}_{2}\})p(\sigma_{n}^{o})^{2}
. By the asymptotic equivalence of
\sigma_{n}^{o}
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
(Lemma 5.3),
this is asymptotic to the proposed variance of
(2+\max\{0,\hat{\gamma}_{2}\})p\tilde{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
.

In particular, by observing that bounds just discussed only affect the coefficient of
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
,
we note that the distribution of
\tilde{Z}_{n}
is close in total-variation norm to the distribution of a standard normal if
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
is small. This happens, for example, if the first four moments of
W
match a standard Gaussian’s.

While the estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
provides an elegant method for data-driven size control in the proportional growth regime, we note two practical considerations relating to its implementation. First, because the variance of
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
depends fundamentally on the eighth moment of
W
,
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
may high variance and converge slowly. Second, because consistency requires this eighth moment to be finite,
\hat{\gamma}_{2}
may not be reliably computable for the heavy-tailed distributions (such as the Student-
t
models
t_{4}
and
t_{6}
) evaluated in Section
S.15
.

S.13
Proof of Optimal Limiting Shrinkage (Theorem 5.6(b))

We prove the theorem for a slightly more general objective function. Let

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f;\xi_{\infty})=\frac{\int f\,d\omega_{\infty}}{\sigma_{\infty}(f;\xi_{\infty})},

where
\sigma_{\infty}(\,\cdot\,;\xi_{\infty})
and
\xi_{\infty}
were defined in (
S.10.11
).
This objective function arises, for instance, in the work of Ledoit and Wolf on financial portfolio optimization, in which case
\xi_{\infty}=\omega_{\infty}\equiv 1
, though we will not pursue this case in more detail.
More suggestively, we may re-express
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}(f;\xi_{\infty})
as follows:

\frac{\left\langle f,h\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle M_{a}Tf,Tf\right\rangle}},

where
\left\langle\,\cdot\,\right\rangle
is the inner product on
\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}
and
a(x)
is generally
xw(x)\xi_{\infty}(x)
. Taking
A=T^{\prime}M_{a}T:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
, where
T^{\prime}
is the transpose of
T:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
, Cauchy-Schwarz implies that this ratio is maximized precisely when

f=A^{-1}h.

Inversion of the operator
A
can be performed in two main steps. First, writing
A^{-1}=T^{-1}M_{a}^{-1}(T^{\prime})^{-1}
, we would like to invert
T^{\prime}
.
A next, minor step is to multiply by the reciprocal of the function
a(x)
. The second major step is to invert
T
, which can be done by writing
T^{-1}=((T^{\prime})^{-1})^{\prime}
—just the adjoint of the previously found inverse
(T^{\prime})^{-1}
.

For the first main step, observe that

T
takes the following form:

\displaystyle Tf(x)

\displaystyle=b(x)f(x)-\phi\,\text{p.v.}\int\frac{f(y)}{y-x}\,d\nu_{\infty}(y)

\displaystyle=b(x)f(x)-\phi\pi\mathcal{H}[f\delta w](x)

where

b(x):=1+\phi\pi\mathcal{H}[\delta w](x).

Thus,

\displaystyle T^{\prime}f(x)=b(x)f(x)-B(x)\mathcal{H}f(x),

(S.13.1)

where

B(x):=-\phi\pi\delta(x)w(x).

In order to invert
T^{\prime}
,
we need a lemma.

Lemma S.13.1
.

The equation
T^{\prime}f=\varphi\in\mathcal{K}

has the unique solution

f=g\varphi+G\mathcal{H}\varphi,

where
g(x)=1-\phi-\phi\pi x\mathcal{H}w(x)
and
G(x)=-\phi\pi xw(x)
.

The main idea

As in previous appendices, we will now suppress subscripts of

S.16
Outline of shrinkage method for
p>n

When
\phi>1
, the limiting sample spectral distribution of
\mathbf{S}_{n}=p^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\prime}
becomes
singular
, having a point mass at zero of weight
(1-1/\phi)
and an absolutely continuous part supported on
E:=F\backslash\{0\}\subset(0,\infty)
with density function
w
.

A shrinker in this regime may be defined as a pair of continuous functions
f=(f_{c},f_{s})
, labeled for “continuous” and “singular,” respectively. The singular component of this pair is only defined in a compact neighborhood of 0 disjoint from
E
, and the continuous component can be applied to any
\lambda\in E
. As always, both functions are allowed to tacitly depend on global limiting spectral quantities (
F
,
w
,
\mathcal{H}w
, …) as well as their main argument.

As in the non-degenerate case of Section
S.13
, the optimal shrinker is derived by maximizing the limiting utility functional
(\int f\,d\omega_{\infty})^{2}/\int[\Gamma f(x))]^{2}x\xi(x)\,d\mu_{\infty}(x)
, which leads to another Cauchy–Schwarz argument. To accommodate the newly appearing point mass in
\nu_{\infty}
in
\Gamma f
, the forward operator
T
must be extended to an operator
T_{+}
acting on pairs
f=(f_{c},f_{s})
:

\displaystyle T_{+}f(x)=Tf_{c}(x)+c_{0}\frac{f_{s}(x)}{x},

where
c_{0}=(\phi-1)\delta(0)
, and
\delta(0)
is the Ledoit–Péché
\delta
shrinkage function at zero. Ignoring a full specification of its codomain, the adjoint operator
T_{+}^{\prime}
acting on a test function
\varphi
maps to the pair

T_{+}^{\prime}\varphi=\left(T^{\prime}\varphi,\,c_{0}\int_{E}\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}\,dx\right).

The optimal shrinker is the solution
f^{o}=(f^{c},f^{s})
to the extended system
T_{+}^{\prime}M_{\alpha}T_{+}f=(h_{c},h_{s})
, where
h_{s}\equiv\left\langle\delta_{0},d\omega\right\rangle
and
h_{c}=d\omega/dx
, and
M_{\alpha}
is again the multiplication operator by the function
\alpha(x)=x\xi(x)w(x)
. Recall that for the purpose of this paper’s hypothesis test
\xi(x)
must be instantiated as
\delta(x)
.

While a fully rigorous inversion of this operator system requires a careful treatment of the relevant function spaces, the formal algebraic inversion follows steps analogous to those in Section
S.13
. Specifically, extending the inversion from Lemma
S.13.1
, one finds that applying the generalized inverse to the
(h_{c},h_{s})
yields
(T_{+}^{\prime})^{-1}(h_{c},h_{s})=(T^{\prime})^{-1}h_{c}+\phi h_{s}w
.

Subsequent application of
M_{\alpha}^{-1}
, then
T_{+}^{-1}
yields

f^{s}(0)=\phi\int_{E}f_{*}(x)w(x)\,dx+\phi^{2}h_{s}\int_{E}\frac{w(x)}{x\xi(x)}\,dx,

(S.16.1)

where
f_{*}
was defined in Appendix
S.13
. To outline a special case, if
h_{c}\equiv 1
,
h_{s}=1-1/\phi
, and
\xi\equiv 1
, the first integral in (
S.16.1
) reduces to 0, and the second integral evaluates to
\pi\mathcal{H}w(0)
. Using the relationship between the un-normalized density
w
and the normalized one
\underline{w}=\phi w
, this simplifies exactly to
f^{s}(0)=1/\delta(0)
, successfully recovering the standard Ledoit–Péché shrinkage for degenerate eigenvalues.

With this singular component in hand, solving the system for the continuous component
f^{c}
follows much as before, and yields a correction to the continuous component
f^{*}(x)
appearing in the non-singular case:

f^{c}(x)=f^{*}(x)+\phi^{2}h_{s}\pi\left(\mathcal{H}\left[\frac{w}{\xi}\right](x)-\frac{\mathcal{H}w(x)}{\xi(x)}\right).\qquad(x>0)

(S.16.2)

Equations (
S.16.1
) and (
S.16.2
) provide the exact analytical formulas for which we now propose finite-sample analogues.

To approximate
f^{c}(\lambda_{i})
, we must make a few finite-sample replacements. For example, we replace
\mathcal{H}_{w}[f]=\mathcal{H}[fw]
for sufficiently regular
f
, including
\mathcal{H}_{w}[1]=\mathcal{H}w
, with an extension of the definition of
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]
:

\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{w}[f]:=p^{-1}\sum_{i=(p-n)^{+}+1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i})K_{ni}(x-\lambda_{i}).

Additionally, we replace
\phi
with
p/n
, and estimate
h_{s}
with the presumably given quantity
(1-1/\phi)\overline{h}_{n}(0)
, again equating
p/n
with
\phi
. This gives the approximation

f^{c}_{n}(\lambda_{i})=f_{n}(\lambda_{i})+\frac{(p-n)^{+}}{n}\overline{h}_{n}(0)\pi\left(\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{w}\left[\frac{1}{\xi}\right](\lambda_{i})-\frac{\mathcal{H}\tilde{w}(\lambda_{i})}{\xi(\lambda_{i})}\right),

(S.16.3)

where
f_{n}
is given in our main approximation theorem (Theorem 5.8).
Next, to approximate
f^{s}(0)
, we simply use the fact that for a test function
\varphi
, the integral
\int\varphi(x)w(x)\,dx
is the almost sure limit of
p^{-1}\sum_{i=(p-n+1)^{+}}^{p}\varphi(\lambda_{i})
. This yields the approximation:

f^{s}_{n}(0)=\sum_{i=(p-n+1)^{+}}^{p}\left[\frac{f_{*,n}(\lambda_{i})}{n}+\frac{(p-n)^{+}}{n^{2}}\overline{h}_{n}(0)\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\xi(\lambda_{i})}\right],

(S.16.4)

where

f_{*,n}(x):=\frac{g_{n}(x)\overline{h}_{n}(x)+\overline{G}_{n}(x)H_{n}(x)}{x\tilde{d}_{n}(x)},

(S.16.5)

as inspired by (
S.13.2
).

For finite sample sizes, the theoretically optimal shrinker
f^{o}
can potentially take on negative values at zero or on a neighborhood of the limiting spectral support
F
.
Thus, it is advantageous and makes no difference asymptotically if we threshold all eigenvalues
f^{o}(\lambda_{j})
from below by zero. However, this threshold is rather conservative, and it is possible finite-sample performance could be further optimized by a tighter one. We leave investigation of this possibility to future work.

The proposed approximations of
f^{s}
and
f^{c}
described above
are implemented in Python code in Listing
S.1
. Figures
S.10
and
S.11
give examples of empirical ROC curves for the case where
p=200
and
n=100
. A full convergence analysis would involve extensive functional-analytic complications and remains future work, but the simulations indicate that the proposed shrinkage methods achieve first-order optimality, remaining highly competitive despite possessing a potentially larger finite-sample variance than highly-tuned approaches like
LW22
.

S.17
Data-driven selection of
\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda)

In this section, we briefly provide a possible framework for estimating important properties of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
when only certain structural properties of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
are known. More specifically, we provide a framework for estimating the weights
\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda)
needed for our main result: the asymptotically optimal shrinkage estimator we construct in Theorem 5.8.
As noted in our Remark on choosing a prior for
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
(Remark 5.9),
this task requires us to go beyond the setting of our paper and assume that
n_{2}\geq 2
i.i.d. samples
\mathbf{y}_{i}\sim\mathbf{y}
are available.

The structural assumption we make is that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is a polynomial in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with unknown coefficients. This assumption is restrictive, but since we do not explicitly assume
p/n_{2}\to 0
, we have no access to consistent estimators of the eigen-basis of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, so the eigen-basis must be assumed. Furthermore, polynomials in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are norm-dense in the continuous functional calculus of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and this structural assumption is not without precedent: in
[
38
]
the authors make an assumption that, in our scaling regime, translates to
p^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=w_{0}\mathbf{I}+w_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+w_{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}
. This structure corresponds to a prior assumption that
p^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is equal to
\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
or
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}
with probabilities
w_{0}+w_{1}+w_{2}=1
, which can in principle capture fairly ill-conditioned choices of
p^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

A natural approach is a moment-matching one. Letting
\tilde{S}
be the sample covariance matrix of the
\mathbf{y}_{i}
’s, a simple observation is that

\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}})=\frac{1}{(n_{2}-1)p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\overline{\mathbf{y}}\right\|^{2},

which concentrates by Hanson–Wright to

\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})=w_{0}+w_{1}\frac{\mathrm{tr}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{p}+w_{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}}{p}

where
\overline{\mathbf{y}}
is the mean of the
\mathbf{y}_{i}
’s. Similarly,
p^{-3/2}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{S})
concentrates to

\displaystyle w_{0}\frac{\mathrm{tr}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{p}+w_{1}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})}{p}+w_{2}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{3})}{p}.

Due to our assumption that
w_{0}+w_{1}+w_{2}=1
, it becomes possible to solve a
3\times 3
system for the
w_{j}
’s, assuming the moments
p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{k})
can be stably estimated for
1\leq k\leq 3
. From this, the ideal if unobservable value for

\overline{h}(\lambda_{i})
would be

\tilde{w}_{0}+\tilde{w}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}+\tilde{w}_{2}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{i},

where
\tilde{w}_{j}
are the weights obtained by solving. To enforce non-negativity for finite sample sizes, these weights may be thresholded from below by zero.

Fortunately, it is unnecessary to consistently estimate
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}_{i}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{u}_{i}
for each individual
i
. The main condition on
\overline{h}_{n}(\lambda)

is an averaged absolute error condition in Theorem 5.8.
However, establishing this condition for the special case of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
takes up the bulk of this paper’s theoretical work, and re-establishing a similar condition for the case of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}
(to handle a quadratic in
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
) would require substantial theoretical effort to fuse the explicit spectral laws of
[
30
]
with the optimal eigenvector-overlap rates of
[
39
]
. A possible solution involves trading theoretical effort and expressivity: one could avoid these theoretical difficulties, by forcing
w_{2}
to be zero, but this severely limits the expressivity of the prior for
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

A second practical trade-off lies in the estimation of the higher-order moments
p^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{k})
. While consistent estimators for these tracial moments are available in the literature
[
30
, Theorem 2]
, their finite-sample stability can degrade as
k
increases, particularly when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is ill-conditioned. Consequently, a practical implementation must carefully balance the expressivity of the prior (
i.e.
, the degree of the polynomial) against the statistical and computational stability of the moment estimates.

Ultimately, the moment-matching framework outlined above demonstrates that a data-dependent estimation of the prior weights
h_{n}(\lambda_{i})
is entirely feasible in principle when
n_{2}\geq 2
and the polynomial structural assumption is made. However, fully operationalizing this procedure and navigating the above trade-offs
represents a substantial theoretical undertaking. We leave further development of such data-driven priors to future work.
