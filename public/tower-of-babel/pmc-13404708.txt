Lower eyelid schwannoma mimicking chalazion in an adolescent
Authors: Farhat Siddiqui, Tarique Husain Khan, Zafar Ameer Zaidi, Zaman Beg, Luxmi Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To highlight a rare case of eyelid schwannoma in a teenager, clinically mimicking a common chalazion, and confirmed by histopathology and immunohistochemistry. A 15-year-old boy presented with a slowly enlarging, painless right lower eyelid nodule. Initially diagnosed as a chalazion or sebaceous cyst, the lesion was surgically excised. Histopathological examination revealed interwoven fascicles of spindle cells with Antoni A and B patterns and Verocay bodies. Immunohistochemistry revealed strong S-100 protein positivity, confirming the diagnosis of schwannoma. Eyelid schwannomas are extremely rare and often mimic benign lesions such as chalazia. An accurate diagnosis requires histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations. This case underscores the importance of considering nerve sheath tumors in the differential diagnosis of persistent eyelid swelling.

Introduction

Schwannomas, also known as neurilemmomas, are benign tumors originating from Schwann cells of the peripheral nerve sheath. These tumors are typically seen in the head-and-neck region, but their occurrence in the eyelid is exceedingly rare, accounting for <0.1% of all eyelid tumors.[1] Due to their rarity and lack of specific clinical features, eyelid schwannomas are often misdiagnosed preoperatively as more common lesions, such as chalazia or sebaceous cysts.

Case Report

A 15-year-old boy presented with a painless swelling in the right lower eyelid, which gradually increased in size over a period of 2 years. There was no history of trauma, previous eyelid surgery, or signs suggestive of neurofibromatosis in the patient. Solitary, firm, well-circumscribed subcutaneous nodule measuring approximately 10 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm, with intact but mildly corrugated overlying skin, likely reflects the encapsulated nature and surface contour of the lesion rather than true multinodularity. The lesion appeared freely mobile; however, intraoperatively, it was found to be partially adherent to the underlying tarsus.

Based on the clinical appearance, a provisional diagnosis of a chalazion was made. The patient initially presented with a painless, slow-enlarging lower eyelid swelling, clinically presumed to be a chalazion. Incision and curettage were performed; however, the lesion persisted in size. Sebaceous cysts were considered a differential diagnosis. Complete excision of the lesion was performed via an external approach. Intraoperatively, the lesion was found to be partially adherent to the underlying tarsus, and a small segment of the tarsal plate measuring approximately 1 mm × 0.5 mm, involving the full thickness of the tarsus, was excised to ensure complete tumor clearance, while preserving the lid margin. The tarsal defect was repaired using interrupted 6-0 polyglactin (Vicryl) sutures. The postoperative course was uneventful, with satisfactory wound healing and no functional or cosmetic complications noted during follow-up and submitted for histopathological examination. The mass appeared well-encapsulated, gray–white to gray–brown in color, and measured 10 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm [Figure 1].

Microscopic analysis revealed a well-circumscribed tumor composed of alternating hypercellular (Antoni A) and hypocellular (Antoni B) regions. The spindle-shaped tumor cells exhibited elongated nuclei arranged in a palisading fashion around acellular zones, forming Verocay bodies [Figures 2-4]. No significant mitotic activity or cellular atypia was observed. The background stroma showed collagenization, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, and focal hemorrhage. Immunohistochemical staining revealed strong diffuse positivity for S-100 protein, confirming the diagnosis of schwannoma [Figure 5].

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no signs of recurrence at the 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

Schwannomas are typically slow-growing, encapsulated, and benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors. Eyelid involvement is exceptionally uncommon and presents a diagnostic challenge because of its nonspecific clinical appearance, often mimicking common benign eyelid lesions.

The definitive diagnosis depends on characteristic histological features – Antoni A and B patterns, Verocay bodies, and strong S-100 protein immunoreactivity. The main differential diagnosis includes other spindle cell tumors, such as solitary circumscribed neuroma (SCN), which can show overlapping histological features but typically contains more prominent axons, less palisading, and a thinner capsule.

A literature review revealed very few reported cases of eyelid schwannomas in children and adolescents. Most cases occur in adult patients. To date, only five pediatric cases have been documented globally, with none from Southeast Asia. Our case represents a rare pediatric case of eyelid schwannoma and highlights the importance of histopathology and immunohistochemistry in diagnosis. Eyelid schwannoma is an exceptionally rare condition. Siddiqui et al. documented five cases of eyelid schwannomas. Among these, four cases involved individuals in their late adulthood, aged between 53 and 63, whereas one case involved a young adult aged 19.[2] Furthermore, López-Tizón et al. reported two additional adult cases, noting that these were the sole occurrences of schwannoma among 2400 eyelid neoplasms they observed over an 11-year period (1995–2005), indicating a prevalence rate of 0.1% for eyelid tumors.[1]

Schwannomas of the eyelid are less common in children than in adults, with only four cases documented to date. None of these cases originated in Southeast Asia. The histological observation in our case mirrored a pattern previously identified on eyelid margins by Stagner and Jakobiec in 2016.[7] It is important to emphasize that in peripheral nerve sheath tumors of the eyelid, S100 positivity can be detected through immunostaining not only in schwannomas but also in SCN, another type of eyelid tumor found in adults. Both schwannoma and SCN are characterized by densely packed spindle cells and S-100 positivity. However, SCN is histologically characterized by a more prominent axon, reduced palisading, and less substantial capsule. In contrast, the schwannoma, as described in this report, displays a unique pattern of fascicular cells interspersed with myxoid areas, along with a complete, thick, and hyalinized capsule.[6] Immunostaining for neurofilament antibody and perineurial cells (such as epithelial membrane antigen or glucose transporter-1 staining) can be useful in distinguishing between these two types of eyelid nerve sheath tumors.[6]

Due to the rarity and lack of distinctive clinical signs of eyelid schwannomas, a conclusive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination of the tumor. Fortunately, schwannomas rarely transform into malignant forms.

A chalazion is a chronic lipogranulomatous inflammation of the meibomian glands and typically presents as a painless eyelid nodule that often responds to conservative management or incision and curettage. In contrast, schwannomas are benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors that present as firm, well-circumscribed, slowly progressive nodules and do not regress with routine treatments. The clinical resemblance between these two entities frequently leads to misdiagnosis, particularly when schwannomas occur in the eyelids. Persistence of an eyelid lesion following incision and curettage, as observed in the present case, should raise suspicion for alternative diagnoses, including nerve sheath tumors. Similar presentations of eyelid schwannoma masquerading as chalazion have been reported in the literature, emphasizing the importance of histopathological evaluation for a definitive diagnosis.[12345]

Conclusion

Although extremely rare, schwannomas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of persistent eyelid swellings, especially those that do not respond to conventional treatment. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses remain essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
