A Rare Finding of Retroperitoneal Sepsis Following Robot‐Assisted Transanal Minimally Invasive Surgery (TAMIS): A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Jonathan Tiong, Khang Duy Ricky Le, Annie Jiao Wang, Jean Flanagan, Dilshan Udayasiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRobot‐assisted transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS) is a safe and effective platform for the local excision of early rectal lesions. While postoperative complications such as bleeding or contained perforation are recognized, rapidly progressive retroperitoneal sepsis remains an exceedingly rare occurrence. A 56‐year‐old male underwent robot‐assisted TAMIS for a pT1 grade 1 rectal neuroendocrine tumor (NET). Despite successful circumferential excision and defect closure with sutures, the patient developed fever, hypotension, and rising lactaemia within 24 hours. Computed tomography demonstrated localized gas in the total mesorectal excision (TME) plane. Due to hemodynamic instability and suspected retroperitoneal sepsis, a diagnostic laparotomy was performed, revealing purulent peritonitis and pneumoretroperitoneum. Concurrent flexible sigmoidoscopy revealed a healthy‐appearing TAMIS site. Management involved an end colostomy, laparostomy, and broad‐spectrum antibiotics after intra‐operative swabs grew extended‐spectrum beta‐lactamase (ESBL) E. coli . The patient was eventually discharged 15 days post return to theater. This case highlights that retroperitoneal sepsis can develop after robotic TAMIS despite no local macroscopic evidence of perforation or necrosis. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for this condition in patients who deteriorate suddenly with septic shock, particularly when abdominal examination findings do not reflect the severity of systemic toxicity. Early surgical intervention with diversion and aggressive sepsis control is critical.Keywords: colorectal surgery, retroperitoneal sepsis, robotic surgery, TAMIS

Background

TAMIS is generally regarded as a safe and effective platform for local excision of benign and selected early malignant rectal lesions, with most reported postoperative complications limited to bleeding, pelvic sepsis, and contained perforation [1]. Pneumoretroperitoneum following transanal full‐thickness excision has been described, but it is typically self‐limiting and associated with a clear rectal wall defect or intraoperative peritoneal entry [2]. In contrast, this case highlights an exceedingly rare and unexpected finding of rapidly progressive septic shock in association with extensive pneumoretroperitoneum, without clear evidence of overt perforation or necrosis of the rectum.

Case Presentation

This case report and review of the literature was conducted according to the SCARE consensus statement and CARE guidelines [3]. The patient provided informed consent for their de‐identified medical information to be used. Herein, we present the case of a 56‐year‐old male who underwent a robot‐assisted transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS) for a rectal neuroendocrine tumor (NET).

History and Examination

A 56 year old initially presented with a 24 h history of acute right iliac fossa pain, fevers, and nausea. He denied diarrhea, loss of weight, night sweats, or changes to urinary habits. His past history is significant for ureteroscopy and laser for a 15 mm proximal left ureteric calculus. He is otherwise fit and well, denying any history of smoking, abdominal surgery, endoscopic procedures, or family history of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease. The patient underwent a routine laparoscopic appendicectomy with intra‐operative findings of a localized perforated appendix that was removed. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of acute gangrenous appendicitis. He was discharged home without any issues the following day. On routine follow‐up, the patient reported persistently altered bowel habits for 2 months following appendicectomy and was referred for colonoscopy. This revealed two lesions in the rectum—most concerning was a firm submucosal lesion, described as yellow in appearance with subtle lacy vasculature overlying at 7 cm from the anal verge, palpable on digital rectal examination (DRE) (Figure 1). A second lesion was thought to be seen more proximally in the rectum, similarly very firm and mobile. The lesions were not removed endoscopically, and the patient was referred for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the pelvis for further characterization. The MRI revealed a mid rectal mass which was 9 mm in length between 10 and 11 o'clock, 7.7 cm from the anal verge without features of extramural venous invasion (EMVI). The circumferential resection margin (CRM) was at risk (due to a right pelvic side wall node) with enhancement that overall was suggestive of a rectal NET (imaging not provided). The case was discussed at our institutions' specialist Colorectal Cancer Multidisciplinary Meeting (MDM) and the consensus was to proceed with robot‐assisted TAMIS for curative resection of the T1/2 N0 M0 rectal NET (noting that MDM review of MRI deemed the right pelvic side wall node not to be pathological in appearance). The procedure was performed as per routine for the surgeon. An anal dilator was introduced prior to application of the gelpoint. The robot was docked successfully with an airseal device. The two lesions were subsequently excised circumferentially with 5 mm margins down to the pericolic fat using a combination of sharp, blunt, and diathermy dissection, and adequate hemostasis achieved. The defects were closed with 3/0 V‐LocTM sutures. There were no issues with intraoperative air leak. Histology confirmed a pT1 well‐differentiated rectal NET, Grade 1, 7.5 mm in size, extending into the submucosa, clear of deep and radial mucosal margin.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Management

24 h post operatively, the patient developed fever (38.8°C) and persistent hypotension with blood pressure (BP) around 84/49 mmHg without tachycardia, despite 2 L of intravenous (IV) crystalloid resuscitation. Biochemical investigations revealed a hemoglobin of 165 g/L (baseline pre‐operatively 166 g/L), white cell count (WCC) of 13.5 × 109/L (baseline pre‐operatively 7.1 × 109/L), platelet count of 302 × 109/L (baseline pre‐operatively 261 × 109/L), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 80 (baseline pre‐operatively > 90), C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 17.8 mg/L (baseline pre‐operatively 1.7 mg/L) and two lactate levels of 4.0 and 3.7 mmol/L. Given concerns for intra‐abdominal sepsis, a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis with rectal and intravenous (IV) contrast was organized. This demonstrated localized gas around the TME plane which was assumed to be expected post‐operative change (Figure 2). Given no improvement in clinical status, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for vasopressor support and was commenced on IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone 2 g daily and metronidazole 500 mg twice per day). The patient continued to deteriorate haemodynamically in ICU on the second day following robot‐assisted TAMIS with new tachycardia of 120 beats per minute, rising vasopressor requirement, lactaemia and uptrending inflammatory markers (Table 1). Antimicrobial therapy was escalated to piperacillin‐tazobactam (4.5 g four times a day) and a repeat CT abdomen and pelvis with rectal contrast was performed which demonstrated significant inflammatory stranding around the rectum and sigmoid colon without extravasation of rectal contrast but ongoing gas in the TME plane (Figure 3A,B).

The decision was made to return to theater for exploratory laparotomy due to concerns of bowel ischaemia. Intraoperative findings included purulent peritonitis with fibrin centred around the rectum, and pneumoretroperitoneum without evidence of colonic or enteric defects on large or small bowel (intra‐operative photographs not taken due to patient instability). A concurrent flexible sigmoidoscopy demonstrated a healthy TAMIS site with sutures appropriately placed. In the setting of rising lactate and progressive sepsis, an end colostomy was fashioned and a laparostomy was performed. The patient was returned to ICU for stabilization, further escalation of broad‐spectrum antibiotics (meropenem 2 g daily, weight and renally adjusted vancomycin and clindamycin 600 mg three times a day) to cover for potential retroperitoneal necrotising soft tissue infection and commenced on total parenteral nutrition (TPN). The patient returned to theater 72 h later for re‐look laparotomy; at this time there was no evidence of purulent peritonitis or bowel ischaemia, and the abdomen was therefore closed. Intra‐operative swabs from the index re‐look grew extended spectrum beta‐lactamase E. coli (ESBL). Subsequently, antibiotic therapy was rationalized to monotherapy IV Meropenem, where he completed an 11‐day course in total.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient was discharged from ICU to the ward 48 h following the abdominal closure. Ileus improved, TPN was weaned, and the patient was discharged on Day 15 following his initial operation. The patient was seen in the outpatient clinics and is awaiting reversal of end colostomy.

Discussion

This is the first reported case of rapidly progressive retroperitoneal sepsis and potential necrotising soft tissue infection following robot‐assisted TAMIS in the absence of identifiable macroscopic rectal perforation or necrosis. While pneumoretroperitoneum following transanal full thickness excision has previously been reported, it is generally self‐limiting and most often associated with a visible defect in the rectal wall or known intraoperative peritoneal entry [2]. In contrast, the current case highlights a scenario in which profound sepsis associated with extensive pneumoretroperitoneum despite an apparently intact excision site on both intraoperative inspection and postoperative flexible sigmoidoscopy.

When considering determinants for this deterioration, operative technique is an important factor. Transanal local excision techniques range from mucosal resection to deliberate full thickness excision, each carrying a risk profile associated with communication between rectal lumen and perirectal or retroperitoneal compartments. The pathophysiology of retroperitoneal sepsis is thus hypothesized to relate to the introduction of colonic bacteria tracking along these planes, particularly when combined with insufflation pressures.

Though the robotic platform has enabled improved dexterity and oncological outcomes, multiple studies have not demonstrated a reduction in major complications when compared with conventional minimally invasive approaches [4]. Other minimally invasive techniques have followed a similar trend, with endoscopic full thickness resection (EFTR) deliberately creating a controlled full‐thickness defect before clip closure and therefore carrying a unique extraperitoneal risk profile. Multi‐centre registries and meta‐analyses demonstrate high technical success with this approach but also report extraperitoneal gas, bleeding, and pelvic sepsis as recognized adverse events [5]. Although the majority of these complications resolve with observation or percutaneous drainage, significant complications arising from colonic perforation requiring defunctioning colostomy have been described [5].

Transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) provides the closest technical comparison to TAMIS and similarly introduces predictable risks of peritoneal entry, particularly for lesions at or above the peritoneal reflection [6]. In large prospective series, complications including pneumoretroperitoneum, bleeding, and pelvic sepsis have been described, with most cases managed conservatively or through immediate transanal closure [6]. Closure of the rectal defect has not consistently demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in pelvic sepsis rates, suggesting that mechanisms other than overt wall defects may contribute to postoperative infectious complication [7].

There are several potential mechanisms that may explain the development of retroperitoneal sepsis. The presence of an occult microperforation that allowed bacterial contamination of the mesorectal compartment while remaining undetectable is one plausible explanation. During TAMIS, deliberate full‐thickness excision exposes the mesorectal fat and therefore creates a potential path for luminal bacteria to enter extraluminal spaces. Even if the defect is closed with sutures, microscopic disruption of the mucosa may persist and permit bacterial translocation. Such microscopic defects can seal rapidly or remain below the threshold for endoscopic or contrast imaging inspection. This is in keeping with similar mechanisms proposed in cases of pelvic sepsis following transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) and endoscopic full thickness resection (EFTR), where postoperative pneumoretroperitoneum and infection occur despite apparently intact closures [5, 6].

Insufflation pressure during TAMIS may also contribute to bacterial dissemination. Robotic TAMIS platforms rely on continuous insufflation to maintain luminal distension and visualization. Pressurized carbon dioxide can dissect along tissue planes, particularly when full thickness excision exposes the mesorectal compartment. This disruption may facilitate the tracking of bacteria‐laden gas into the mesorectal space and subsequently along retroperitoneal fascial planes. This phenomenon has been described in laparoscopic and transanal surgery where pneumoretroperitoneum develops due to gas dissection rather than frank perforation [6]. The CT findings in our patient demonstrating gas within the TME plane may therefore represent early gas tracking along these fascial planes rather than a benign post operative finding. In large prospective series, perforation, bleeding, and pneumoretroperitoneum are documented complications, most often managed conservatively or via immediate transanal closure [6]. Interestingly, defect closures did not appear to correlate statistically with a reduction in pelvic sepsis [7].

Anatomically, the rectum lies within the mesorectal envelope, which communicates directly with presacral and retroperitoneal fascial planes [8]. Gas or bacteria entering the mesorectal compartment can therefore track cranially within the retroperitoneum without necessarily breaching the peritoneal cavity. This pathway likely explains the extensive pneumoretroperitoneum observed intraoperatively and the rapid systemic deterioration despite limited peritoneal contamination.

The microbiological findings in this case also provide insight into the severity of the clinical course. Cultures grew ESBL producing E. coli. ESBL organisms have been associated with aggressive soft tissue infections including retroperitoneal fasciitis in TEMS patient cohorts [9]. Once introduced into poorly vascularised retroperitoneal planes, bacteria proliferate rapidly and can cause profound systemic toxicity. While prophylactic ceftriaxone and metronidazole are given pre‐operatively as per antibiotic stewardship guidelines, targeted prophylactic antibiotic use may be important in these cohorts to mitigate the risk of virulent pathogens [10].

Another potential contributor is the patient's prior episode of perforated appendicitis. Previous intra‐abdominal infection has been associated with alterations in gut microbiota and colonization with resistant organisms such as ESBL. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the prior intra‐abdominal sepsis could have influenced this patient's intestinal profile and contributed to the pathogen being isolated in this case [11].

The most plausible explanation for this patient's deterioration is a combination of occult microperforation at the TAMIS site, insufflation‐driven dissemination of bacteria along fascial and mesorectal planes, and rapid bacterial proliferation within the retroperitoneum due to a virulent ESBL organism. This may explain the discordance between severe systemic sepsis and the absence of macroscopically obvious rectal defects.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge associated with early retroperitoneal infection following transanal surgery. Radiologic findings may initially be subtle and easily interpreted as expected postoperative change. Similarly, patients may develop profound systemic toxicity while displaying minimal abdominal signs due to the retroperitoneal location of the infection. Clinicians should have a low index of suspicion for retroperitoneal sepsis for patients who deteriorate suddenly with features of septic shock following robotic TAMIS, particularly when abdominal examination does not mirror the significant clinical deterioration of the patient. In such circumstances, laparotomy with diversion, washout and sepsis control remains the most described management course when systemic deterioration persists beyond conservative management. Targeted prophylactic antibiotic use may be relevant in high‐risk cohorts including those with a history of rare but virulent pathogens.
