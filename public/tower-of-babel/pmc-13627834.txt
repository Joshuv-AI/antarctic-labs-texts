Serum calcium dynamics and risk of all-cause hospitalization and mortality in hemodialysis patients: a landmark analysis from the international DOPPS
Authors: Ken Iseri, Taigi Yamazaki, Iori Taki, Noriko Hida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCalcium is measured repeatedly in hemodialysis care, yet most studies characterize exposure using a single value rather than its temporal behavior. We examined whether recent serum calcium dynamics are associated with short-term hospitalization and mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients.MethodsUsing the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study phases IV–VII, we conducted a quarterly dynamic landmark analysis. At each landmark, total calcium values during the preceding 180 days were summarized as the latest value, mean, linear slope, residual standard deviation (rSD), and time-below-range (TBR, <8.6 mg/dl) and time-above-range (TAR, >10.0 mg/dl). All-cause hospitalization and death were assessed over the subsequent 90 days using discrete-time survival models.ResultsAmong 81 475 patients contributing 431 618 landmarks, 68 632 hospitalizations and 12 886 deaths occurred. Latest calcium was not associated with hospitalization [hazard ratio (HR), 1.00] but was associated with higher mortality (HR, 1.16). Lower mean calcium was associated with hospitalization (HR, 0.97), whereas higher mean calcium was associated with death (HR, 1.16). A positive slope and greater rSD were consistently associated with both hospitalization (HRs, 1.10 and 1.15) and death (HRs, 1.38 and 1.37). TBR burden showed graded associations with hospitalization, whereas TAR burden was strongly and dose-dependently associated with mortality.ConclusionsRecent calcium dynamics were associated with short-term outcomes in an outcome-specific manner: low calcium and TBR with hospitalization, high calcium and TAR with death, and rising or unstable calcium with both. These findings support longitudinal assessment of serum calcium, particularly its instability, which was not captured by a single value.Keywords: calcium, hemodialysis, hospitalization, mortality, variability

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) contributes substantially to morbidity and mortality in hemodialysis, and current guidance emphasizes therapeutic decisions based on serial assessments of calcium, phosphate, and intact parathyroid hormone (PTH) rather than a single laboratory value [1–5]. Large observational studies have consistently shown U- or J-shaped associations between CKD-MBD markers and mortality, and the management of CKD-MBD remains central to improving outcomes [6–11]. An earlier Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) analysis reported a total calcium of 8.6–10.0 mg/dl was associated with the lowest mortality, whereas values above 10.0 mg/dl showed the highest risk [8]. Comparisons of uncorrected with albumin-corrected calcium show that the apparent risk of low calcium is largely attenuated after albumin adjustment, whereas the risk associated with high calcium is strengthened [12], suggesting that low total calcium may partly reflect hypoalbuminemia or illness severity, whereas high calcium may more directly reflect mineral metabolism burden.

However, most prior studies have characterized calcium exposure using a single baseline value, the most recent value, or a broad time-averaged concentration, none of which captures the temporal behavior that clinicians actually observe and make treatment decisions. In routine hemodialysis care, serum calcium is measured repeatedly, and clinicians often respond not only to the single absolute value but also to whether calcium is persistently low or high, rising over time, or fluctuating markedly. Likewise, longitudinal data suggest that cumulative calcium exposure and changes in calcium over time provide prognostic information beyond any single measurement [13]. As for phosphate and PTH, dynamic features such as the mean, slope, and variability, together with time-in-range metrics, have already been linked to clinical outcomes [14–16]. By contrast, evidence for calcium remains sparse.

A single outcome may not fully capture how calcium relates to clinical risk because hospitalization and death may reflect different biological pathways. Low total calcium may be a marker of hypoalbuminemia, malnutrition-inflammation, frailty, or acute illness that predisposes to hospitalization. In contrast, sustained or rising high calcium may reflect calcium loading or low bone buffering capacity and may be more closely related to cardiovascular and fatal events. We therefore examined both endpoints to avoid overlooking outcome-specific calcium characteristics.

Thus, we used data from the international DOPPS to investigate whether recent 180-day calcium dynamics are associated with subsequent short-term all-cause hospitalization and all-cause death in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Using a quarterly dynamic landmark design, we characterized calcium exposure with complementary metrics: the latest total calcium value, the 180-day mean, the calcium slope, the residual standard deviation (rSD) as a measure of instability, and cumulative time-below-range (TBR) and time-above-range (TAR) burden. We further assessed whether these dynamic features carried prognostic information independent of the latest single calcium value, and whether the associations were robust to albumin correction and to adjustment for PTH.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

The DOPPS is an international prospective cohort of representative in-center hemodialysis patients [17]. Laboratory values and medication prescriptions are recorded monthly, less time-varying measures (e.g. single-pool Kt/V, body mass index) every 4 months, and comorbidities at baseline. Because the study used de-identified data, informed consent was not required. The study protocol using DOPPS data was approved by the Showa University Ethics Committee (2024-230-B).

Participant selection and study design

This study used data from 131 269 hemodialysis patients enrolled in DOPPS Phases IV–VII (2009–22). Patients were excluded if they had no medication data, no serum total calcium measurement during observation, no laboratory or medication record on or after the DOPPS index date, or no follow-up time at risk, leaving 120 900 patients eligible for a quarterly dynamic landmark analysis (Fig. 1). For each patient, the first landmark was set 180 days after baseline, and subsequent landmarks were placed every 90 days thereafter. At each landmark, a 180-day look-back window was used to summarize calcium dynamics and time-varying covariates, and a 90-day forward window was used to ascertain outcome events; the forward window was divided into three consecutive 30-day intervals (days 1–30, 31–60, and 61–90) (Fig. 2). A patient re-entered the analysis at each subsequent landmark for which he or she remained at risk. A landmark was eligible for analysis if (i) it preceded the end of follow-up, so that at least one day of subsequent observation was available; (ii) the patient was not hospitalized on the landmark date; and (iii) serum total calcium had been measured in at least 4 months within the preceding 180 days, including at least one measurement within the preceding 45 days. After applying these criteria, 81 475 patients contributed 431 618 landmarks [median 5 landmarks per patient, interquartile range (IQR) 2–8].

Definition of exposure

The primary exposure was the temporal dynamics of serum total calcium over the 180 days before each landmark, summarized as three jointly modeled features: the mean concentration (per +1 mg/dl), the slope of a least-squares regression of calcium on time (per +1 mg/dl per 30 days), and the residual SD about that regression line (rSD; per +1 mg/dl; computed when ≥3 values were available). Two prespecified alternative parameterizations were also examined: (1) time-in-range burden, classifying each monthly value as below (TBR, <8.6 mg/dl), within (TIR, 8.6–10.0 mg/dl), or above range (TAR, >10.0 mg/dl) and counting months in each zone (0, 1–2, or 3–6 months), with the 8.6–10.0 mg/dl range based on the lowest-mortality range in a prior DOPPS report [8]; and (2) Payne-corrected calcium [total Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin)], used to derive analogous mean, slope, and rSD features as a sensitivity exposure.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause hospitalization and the secondary outcome was all-cause death, each within the 90-day window after a landmark. For each landmark, follow-up was censored at 90 days, at the outcome event, or at the end of the observation period, whichever occurred first. For the hospitalization outcome, follow-up additionally ended at the close of each patient’s hospitalization-data collection, whereas deaths were ascertained through the administrative end of follow-up.

Covariates

All models adjusted for the same case-mix block: age, dialysis vintage, sex, race, country/region, DOPPS phase, and 12 baseline comorbidities. Time-updated laboratory covariates included albumin, phosphate, hemoglobin, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, single-pool Kt/V, body mass index, and blood urea nitrogen. Time-updated treatment covariates included phosphate-binder category, dialysate calcium concentration, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, vitamin D receptor activators, and calcimimetics. Prior hospitalization was captured by two prespecified terms in mutually exclusive windows: the number of admissions 31–180 days before the landmark and a binary indicator of any admission within the preceding 30 days (detailed in Item S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as n (%). The association between serum calcium dynamics and each outcome was estimated using a discrete-time survival model using a complementary log–log link applied to 30-day person-period data, including an interval-specific intercept, a facility-level random intercept, and an offset for partial follow-up; exp(β) was interpreted as the hazard ratio (HR) per 30-day interval (full specification in Item S1). Calcium metrics were entered as linear terms in the primary models and as natural cubic splines in prespecified secondary analyses (latest and mean calcium, 4 df; rSD, 3 df; reference, 9.3 mg/dl for concentrations and the median for rSD).

We used eight prespecified analytical models to assess the calcium–outcome associations through complementary exposure parameterizations. The models were constructed as follows:

All models shared the facility random intercept and the same case-mix block. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis for each model (detailed in Item S1). Incremental predictive performance was assessed by comparing latest-calcium, calcium-dynamics, and augmented models using patient-grouped five-fold cross-validation, 90-day inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) and Brier score (Item S1). Furthermore, exploratory cause-specific analyses classified first hospitalizations and deaths as cardiovascular, non-cardiovascular, or unknown and estimated cause-specific hazards in subsets with available diagnosis data (Item S1).

Statistical analyses were performed using R for Windows, version 4.4.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at the level of P < .05.

Predictive performance of calcium dynamics versus the latest value

Cross-validated 90-day discrimination was almost identical for the latest-calcium and the dynamics models (hospitalization, AUC 0.696 vs 0.696; mortality, 0.785 vs 0.785). Entering the dynamics metrics in addition to the latest value increased the AUC by 0.0003 (95% CI, 0.0001–0.0006) for hospitalization and by 0.0007 (0.0003–0.0011) for mortality, with no improvement in the Brier score (Table S5). In these augmented models the rSD coefficient was essentially unchanged relative to Model 2 (mortality, β 0.320 vs 0.317; hospitalization, β 0.142 vs 0.140), whereas the mean and slope coefficients were markedly attenuated, consistent with the correlation of the latest value with the 180-day mean (r = 0.81) but not with rSD (r = −0.02).

Cause-specific hospitalization and mortality

In the subset with linked diagnosis data (13 010 patients, 58 660 landmarks), 8854 first hospitalizations (1111 cardiovascular, 7072 non-cardiovascular, 671 unknown cause) and 1043 deaths (409 cardiovascular, 531 non-cardiovascular, 103 unknown cause) occurred within 90 days. For hospitalization, higher calcium was associated specifically with cardiovascular hospitalizations (latest calcium HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.21, P = .014; 180-day mean 1.11, 1.00–1.23, P = .049), whereas the corresponding estimates for non-cardiovascular hospitalizations were null. For mortality, in contrast, the excess risk associated with higher calcium was of similar magnitude for cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular death (latest calcium 1.25, 1.09–1.43 vs 1.19, 1.05–1.34; 180-day mean 1.28, 1.09–1.50 vs 1.22, 1.06–1.41; all P ≤ .01). Greater rSD was associated with death outcome irrespective of cause. Prolonged TAR was associated with non-cardiovascular death and showed a similar but less precise positive association with cardiovascular death (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

In this study, recent calcium dynamics were associated with subsequent short-term outcomes, but the direction and robustness differed between hospitalization and all-cause death. A higher latest total calcium was associated with all-cause death but not hospitalization. Lower mean calcium was associated with hospitalization, whereas higher mean calcium was associated with death. In contrast, a positive slope and greater rSD were consistently associated with both hospitalization and mortality risk outcomes. Time-in-range analyses supported this outcome-specific pattern. Greater TBR showed a graded increase in hospitalization risk, whereas greater TAR was strongly and progressively associated with mortality.

For hospitalization, the adjusted spline curve for latest calcium (Fig. 3) showed a shallow U-shape, suggesting modestly higher risk at both extremes. This may explain the null linear association for the latest value (model 1-full), as the contrasting trajectories at the extremes were likely neutralized when calcium was treated as a single continuous variable. This is consistent with prior studies in which mineral-metabolism risk was driven more by hyperphosphatemia and hyperparathyroidism than by calcium alone [9, 18]. However, the spline curve for 180-day mean calcium suggested that the hospitalization signal was increased mainly by lower mean calcium levels. The association between lower mean total calcium and hospitalization may reflect short-term clinical vulnerability rather than a direct causal effect of low calcium itself. Total calcium is strongly influenced by serum albumin [19, 20]. Therefore, lower total calcium may partly capture hypoalbuminemia, malnutrition-inflammation, frailty, or acute illness burden. In addition, albumin-corrected calcium is an imperfect surrogate for ionized calcium in dialysis patients, and previous studies have reported misclassification of calcium status when corrected calcium is compared with directly measured ionized calcium [21, 22]. This interpretation is supported by our corrected-calcium sensitivity analysis, in which a mean corrected calcium was not associated with hospitalization risk. The results of spline analysis for hospitalization are also consistent with this interpretation. Higher hospitalization risk was observed at the lower range of 180-day mean total calcium, whereas sustained higher mean calcium did not show an increase in hospitalization risk. Therefore, low total calcium may be better considered as a surrogate marker for underlying frailty and increased susceptibility to hospitalization, rather than a causal factor implying that therapeutic calcium loading would attenuate this risk. Exploratory cause-specific hospitalization analyses did not identify a single cause accounting for the low-calcium association with all-cause hospitalization. Higher latest and mean calcium were associated with a cardiovascular hospitalization, whereas the estimate for mean calcium and non-cardiovascular hospitalization was modestly inverse but imprecise. The opposing directions of these point estimates may partly explain the null mean-calcium association in the diagnosis-available all-cause subset, although cohort restriction may also have contributed. Greater rSD was associated with non-cardiovascular hospitalization, while the cardiovascular estimate was positive but imprecise, consistent with the possibility that calcium instability reflects broader clinical vulnerability rather than a uniquely cardiovascular pathway.

Findings regarding mortality were more consistent. Higher latest calcium, higher mean calcium, a positive slope, greater instability, and greater TAR were all associated with increased mortality, in line with previous studies linking higher calcium to death in dialysis patients [8, 23, 24]. In DOPPS, the highest risk was seen above 10.0 mg/dl [8], and in a large nationwide cohort from Japan, higher calcium, but not lower calcium, was independently associated with mortality in baseline and time-dependent models [6, 7]. In a time-averaged analysis, excess risk at the high-calcium extreme was consistent, whereas low-calcium risk depended on concurrent phosphorus and PTH [13]. Our spline analysis showed risk rising predominantly on the high-calcium side. The reversal of the latest-calcium estimate from an inverse association in the minimally adjusted model to a positive association after full adjustment suggests substantial confounding by albumin, nutritional status, and illness severity. Our low-calcium spline findings differ in direction from the earlier DOPPS report, in which broad low calcium was null after full adjustment but severe hypocalcemia (calcium or albumin-corrected calcium ≤7.5 mg/dl), particularly with preserved albumin, was associated with higher mortality. This discrepancy may reflect treatment era and estimand. Cinacalcet use was minimal in DOPPS I–III (1.6%) but calcimimetics were prescribed to 21% of our DOPPS IV–VII cohort, and our models based on time-updated albumin, calcium-lowering treatments, and prior hospitalization. Therefore, the lower spline estimates at low calcium should not be interpreted as protective or as refuting risk from severe or transient hypocalcemia. High or rising calcium was the more robust adjusted mortality signal.

Furthermore, our time-above-range analysis showed a graded association with mortality according to the cumulative number of months above 10.0 mg/dl. This dose-dependent pattern is in line with previous target-achievement studies in which a longer duration within the recommended ranges was associated with lower mortality [6, 25]. These findings suggest that sustained or higher calcium exposure may be a clinically meaningful mortality-associated CKD-MBD signal. Several mechanisms may explain why high calcium exposure was more strongly related to death than to hospitalization. Sustained or rising calcium may reflect excessive calcium loading from calcium-based phosphate binders, vitamin D receptor activators, higher dialysate calcium prescription, low bone turnover, or impaired calcium buffering capacity. These factors may contribute to vascular and valvular calcification, arterial stiffness, myocardial vulnerability, and fatal cardiovascular events, among other adverse pathways. However, exploratory cause-specific mortality analyses did not suggest that the high-calcium mortality signal was confined to cardiovascular death. Higher latest and mean calcium and greater calcium instability were positively associated with both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular death. These findings may indicate broader adverse pathways associated with sustained high or unstable calcium, although the non-cardiovascular category was heterogeneous and the restricted sample precludes attribution to a specific mechanism.

The association between greater calcium variability and outcomes is a novel finding. Information on calcium variability and hard outcomes is sparse. In a small single-center study including 218 hemodialysis patients, variability in calcium and PTH was associated with cardiac mortality [26]. We extend this to all-cause mortality and, to our knowledge for the first time, to short-term hospitalization in a large international cohort using a dynamic landmark design. The association between greater rSD and outcomes was maintained after adjusting for mean calcium, calcium slope, time-varying laboratory values, medications, PTH, and previous hospitalization, and also in the corrected-calcium analysis. Therefore, variability is unlikely to be explained by albumin-related variation alone and may reflect clinical instability, repeated treatment changes, fluctuating nutritional-inflammatory status, or unstable CKD-MBD control not captured by a single value or the 180-day mean. When the latest value and the dynamics metrics were entered together, the rSD coefficient was essentially unchanged for both outcomes, whereas the mean and slope coefficients were attenuated. This indicates that instability, unlike the calcium level or its short-term trend, is not captured by a single measurement. This supports a broader concept that instability of mineral-metabolism parameters, beyond absolute levels, carries prognostic information.

Some limitations should be considered. First, the observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding may remain. Second, monthly laboratory and medication data could not fully capture within-month fluctuations or brief treatment changes. Third, ionized calcium was unavailable. Albumin-corrected calcium, used only in sensitivity analyses, may misclassify biologically active calcium. Fourth, the 90-day horizon aligned calcium dynamics with near-term risk but cannot characterize longer-term consequences of sustained calcium exposure. Cause-specific analyses were exploratory, restricted to smaller linked subsets, and limited by unknown and heterogeneous causes. The strengths of this study include the use of a large, international and prospectively collected cohort with standardized laboratory and facility data, repeated measurements that allow longitudinal characterization of calcium exposure, and a landmark design that aligns the exposure window with subsequent short-term risk.

In conclusion, recent serum calcium dynamics were associated with short-term hospitalization and mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Lower mean calcium and greater TBR were associated mainly with hospitalization, whereas higher calcium exposure and greater TAR were associated mainly with mortality. Rising calcium and greater calcium instability were associated with both outcomes. These results support longitudinal assessment of serum calcium beyond a single latest value, particularly its instability, which was not captured by a single measurement, although the gain in population-level discrimination was small. Whether interventions that reduce avoidable calcium instability or sustained high-calcium exposure improve clinical outcomes requires further study.
