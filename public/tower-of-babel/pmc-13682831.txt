SLC38A7 promotes gastric cancer progression through activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling via interaction with MYH9
Authors: Kai Li, Shuyang Sun, Yunhe Gao, Yixun Lu, Xinxin Xu, Lin Chen, Zhi Qiao, Zhanyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Gastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Solute carrier family 38 member 7 (SLC38A7), a lysosome-localized glutamine transporter, has been implicated in poor cancer prognosis and chemoresistance, but its biological role in GC is not well understood. This study aimed to investigate the biological function of SLC38A7 in GC progression and to elucidate the underlying signaling mechanism.Methods:The expression pattern and clinical relevance of SLC38A7 were analyzed using public datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, and validated in 60 paired GC tissues collected at the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments were conducted in MKN-28 and MGC-803 to evaluate the effects of SLC38A7 on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion under glutamine-replete and glutamine-deprived conditions. Protein–protein interactions were assessed by co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed to identify key residues mediating SLC38A7 function. Downstream signaling was analyzed by western blotting and ubiquitination assays. A xenograft mouse model was established to determine the role of SLC38A7 in tumor growth in vivo.Results:SLC38A7 expression was significantly upregulated in GC tissues and cell lines and was associated with poor patient prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that SLC38A7 promoted GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, particularly under glutamine-deprived conditions. Mechanistically, SLC38A7 interacted with myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9), facilitating glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) ubiquitination and degradation, which led to activation of the wingless/int-1 (Wnt)/β-catenin signaling pathway. Mutation of key SLC38A7 residues (E12 and Q3) disrupted its interaction with MYH9 and attenuated downstream signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of GSK-3β partially rescued the effects of SLC38A7 silencing in vitro. In vivo, SLC38A7 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth.Conclusion:SLC38A7 promotes gastric cancer progression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through MYH9-dependent regulation of GSK-3β ubiquitination.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide.[1] Despite advances in early detection and therapeutic strategies, the prognosis for advanced GC remains dismal, largely due to its pronounced molecular heterogeneity and the limited availability of effective targeted interventions.[2–4] Mounting evidence underscores the pivotal role of aberrant signaling pathways in driving GC progression, among which the wingless/int-1 (Wnt)/β-catenin signaling axis is particularly prominent, owing to its involvement in tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and resistance to therapy.[5,6] Within this cascade, glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) acts as a critical negative regulator by facilitating β-catenin phosphorylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation in the absence of Wnt activation. Loss of GSK-3β activity—either through degradation or inhibitory phosphorylation at serine 9 (Ser9)—results in β-catenin stabilization, nuclear accumulation, and activation of oncogenic targets such as proto-oncogene MYC (MYC) and cyclin D1 (CCND1). Pharmacologic inhibition of GSK-3β, for instance, using lithium chloride (LiCl), can mimic Wnt-pathway activation by blocking β-catenin degradation.[7,8] However, the regulatory dynamics and tumor type-specific effects of GSK-3β modulation remain incompletely understood in GC, highlighting the need to elucidate its upstream modulators.

In parallel, reprogrammed amino acid metabolism, particularly glutamine dependency, has emerged as a hallmark of tumor progression.[9,10] GC cells exhibit heightened glutamine uptake and are highly sensitive to glutamine deprivation, indicating that glutamine availability is essential for maintaining proliferation and survival under metabolic stress.[11] Identifying molecules that interface glutamine metabolism with oncogenic signaling may provide novel insights into the metabolic adaptability of GC.[12]

Among the solute carrier (SLC) family, solute carrier family 38 member 7 (SLC38A7), also known as sodium-coupled neutral amino acid transporter 7 (SNAT7), has recently attracted increasing attention for its role in amino acid transport and tumor-associated metabolic regulation.[13,14] SLC38A7 localizes to lysosomal membranes and facilitates the efflux of glutamine and asparagine into the cytosol following degradation of internalized extracellular proteins through micropinocytosis.[15,16] This process supports activation of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) in a Rag GTPase–independent manner, thereby promoting tumor cell survival under nutrient-limited conditions.[17] Moreover, SLC38A7 has been associated with poor prognosis in lung squamous cell carcinoma,[18] and shown to be involved in chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer.[19,20] Despite these emerging links, the biological role of SLC38A7 in GC remains largely undefined.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of transcriptomic profiles revealed a strong association between SLC38A7 expression and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in GC. Further proteomic investigation identified myosin heavy chain 9 (MYH9), a cytoskeletal regulator of GSK-3β stability,[21–23] as a direct interacting partner of SLC38A7. These findings led us to hypothesize that SLC38A7 may facilitate Wnt-signaling activation through MYH9-dependent degradation of GSK-3β.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the biological role of SLC38A7 in GC and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which it may contribute to tumor progression, particularly under conditions of glutamine limitation.

Methods

Clinical samples and public dataset analysis

We collected GC tissue samples and matched adjacent nontumor tissues from 60 patients who underwent surgical resection at the Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital after obtaining written informed consent. Histopathological classification followed the criteria published by the World Health Organization and the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association.[24,25] Tumor staging was based on the recommendations of the Union for International Cancer Control.[26] The clinical and pathological features of the patient cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C867. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. S2016-057-02), which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Transcriptomic data of the SLC38 gene family were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. The public dataset included 414 GC samples from TCGA, 36 normal gastric tissues from TCGA, and 174 normal stomach tissues from GTEx. Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis, version 3 (GEPIA3; https://gepia3.bioinfoliu.com/) was used for correlation analysis between gene expression levels. Prognostic analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

GC cell lines (MKN-28, MGC-803, SGC-7901, AGS, BGC-823, HGC-27, and MKN-45) and the gastric epithelial cell line (GES-1) were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Authentication relied on short tandem repeat profiling, confirming complete concordance with reference databases. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays confirmed the absence of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2. Glutamine starvation was induced by culturing cells in glutamine-free medium for 24 h.

RNA interference, gene knockout, and transfection

To investigate the functional role of SLC38A7, we established both loss- and gain-of-function models in GC cells. In MKN-28 cells, stable knockdown was achieved using short hairpin RNA targeting SLC38A7 (sh-SLC38A7), with scrambled shRNA serving as the negative control (sh-NC). In MGC-803 cells, SLC38A7 overexpression was established by transfection with a SLC38A7 expression vector, with empty vector as the control.

For transient silencing experiments, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting SLC38A7 were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9)-mediated gene knockout was performed in MKN-28 cells using single-guide RNAs targeting SLC38A7 to generate SLC38A7 knockout (KO) cells.

For rescue experiments, SLC38A7-KO cells were reconstituted with wild-type (WT) SLC38A7 or the interaction-deficient mutant (E12A) construct. Stable transfectants were selected with puromycin (5 µg/mL; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and validated by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

We extracted the total RNA using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and synthesized complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). We performed quantitative PCR with SYBR Green Master Mix (SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, Takara, Japan) and gene-specific primers. Relative expression was calculated using the ΔΔcycle threshold (Ct) method with glyceraldhyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control. Primer sequences for each target gene are listed in Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C867.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

For IHC, we stained paraffin-embedded tissue sections with anti-SLC38A7 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to standard protocols.[16] For IF, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, and incubated with primary antibodies against SLC38A7 and MYH9 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The primary and secondary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C867.

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) and mass spectrometry

We investigated protein–protein interactions by co-IP. GC cell lysates were incubated with antibodies, and antibody–protein complexes were isolated using protein A/G beads. After washing to remove nonspecific proteins, we eluted the bound complexes and subjected them to mass spectrometry and western blotting for identification and validation.

Western blotting

Total and nuclear proteins were extracted using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer or a nuclear extraction kit. After quantification by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence. Antibody information is listed in Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C867.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

We assessed cell proliferation using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA).

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

To measure proliferation, we detected EdU using the Click-iT EdU assay (Beyotime, China), and we quantified red and blue fluorescence intensities using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Colony formation assay

We plated cells in six-well plates with DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After 10–14 days, colonies (>50 cells) were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and counted.

Cell migration and invasion

We assessed invasion using transwell assays with matrigel-coated filters in 24-well plates (Costar Transwell system, Corning, USA). Migration was evaluated using a scratch-wound healing assay. Migrated cells were counted under an optical microscope, and migration rates were expressed as relative wound density over time.

Ubiquitination assay

We transfected cells with hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged ubiquitin plasmids and treated them with MG132 (10 µmol/L, 6 h) before lysis. We performed immunoprecipitation using the anti-GSK-3β antibody and detected ubiquitinated GSK-3β by anti-HA western blotting.

Xenograft tumor model

We used 4-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (Vital River Laboratory Animal Co., Beijing, China) for xenograft experiments. Mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5/group): (1) Group A (MKN-28 cells with empty plasmid), (2) Group B (MKN-28 cells with SLC38A7 overexpression plasmid), (3) Group C (MGC-803 cells with sh-NC plasmid), and (4) Group D (MGC-803 cells with sh-SLC38A7 plasmid). Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 106 cells in the flank. Tumor volume was calculated as volume = 1/2 × length × width2. After 4 weeks, tumors were excised and weighed. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing Tonghe Litai Biotechnology Co. LTD (IACUC-D2024080).

Structural modeling and site-directed mutagenesis

We predicted the SLC38A7–MYH9 interaction interface using AlphaFold3-based complex modeling and validated predictions with molecular docking. Mutant constructs (such as E12A and Q3A) were generated with site-directed mutagenesis kits and confirmed by sequencing.

Statistical analysis

We conducted statistical analyses using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) v.26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t test (two-tailed) or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons of two or more groups. Correlations between variables were examined using Pearson’s correlation test. Data were visualized using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and the ggplot2 package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Results with P <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

SLC38A7 is upregulated in GC and associated with poor clinical outcomes

To investigate the potential involvement of the SLC38 gene family in GC, we first analyzed their expression profiles in tumor and adjacent nontumor tissues using publicly available datasets from TCGA and GTEx databases. Both paired and unpaired analyses were performed. Among the SLC38 members, SLC38A6 (both P <0.001), SLC38A7 (both P <0.001), and SLC38A9 (both P <0.001) showed statistically significant upregulation in GC [Figure 1A]. ROC curve analysis further revealed that SLC38A7 exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy among the SLC38 family members (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.910, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.888–0.931), indicating a strong ability to distinguish GC tissues from normal controls [Figure 1B]. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that elevated SLC38A7 expression was significantly associated with shorter overall survival (OS; hazard ratio [HR] = 1.79, CI: 1.50–2.15, P <0.001), first progression (FP; HR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.41–2.13, P <0.001), and post-progression survival (PPS; HR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.94–3.08, P <0.001) in patients with GC [Figure 1C].

To examine whether metabolic stress regulates SLC38A7, we exposed cells to glutamine deprivation. qRT-PCR analysis showed that SLC38A7 expression was markedly upregulated under glutamine-starved conditions (P <0.001) [Figure 1D], suggesting a possible role in nutrient-stress adaptation.

The overexpression of SLC38A7 in GC was further validated in 60 paired clinical samples. Quantitative PCR and IHC consistently demonstrated significantly elevated SLC38A7 expression in tumor tissues compared with adjacent nontumor controls (P <0.001) [Figure 1E and F]. At the cellular level, analysis of seven human GC cell lines (MKN-45, MKN-28, MGC-803, BGC-823, SGC-7901, HGC-27, and AGS) and one normal GES-1 revealed widespread overexpression of SLC38A7 in tumor-derived cells, as confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting [Figure 1G and H]. IF analysis further confirmed increased SLC38A7 protein expression in MKN-28 cells relative to GES-1, with a prominent shift toward cytoplasmic localization in cancer cells [Figure 1I]. Collectively, these findings indicate that SLC38A7 is significantly upregulated in GC and may contribute to tumor progression under metabolic stress, with potential utility as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.

SLC38A7 promotes GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To elucidate the functional role of SLC38A7 in GC, we generated stable cell lines with SLC38A7 knockdown in MKN-28 cells and overexpression in MGC-803 cells [Figure 2A]. Functional assays demonstrated that silencing of SLC38A7 significantly inhibited cell proliferation, as shown by CCK-8 and colony formation assays, whereas ectopic overexpression of SLC38A7 markedly enhanced proliferative capacity in GC cells [Figure 2B and C]. These results were further supported by EdU incorporation assays, which confirmed reduced DNA synthesis upon SLC38A7 silencing and increased proliferation in cells overexpressing SLC38A7 [Figure 2D]. To investigate the role of SLC38A7 in GC cell motility and invasiveness, we performed wound healing and transwell invasion assays. The results showed that SLC38A7 overexpression statistically significantly promoted both migration and invasion of GC cells, whereas SLC38A7 knockdown led to strong suppression of these phenotypes [Figure 2E and F].

Given the known reliance of GC cells on glutamine metabolism, we further evaluated these phenotypes under glutamine-replete (4 mmol/L Gln) and glutamine-deprived (Gln−) conditions. Glutamine deprivation alone impaired GC cell proliferation and motility. Importantly, SLC38A7 overexpression partially rescued these defects, whereas SLC38A7 knockdown in the absence of glutamine further exacerbated growth and migration suppression across all functional assays [Figure 2B–F]. Together, these findings demonstrate that SLC38A7 functions as a key oncogenic driver in GC, with its tumor-promoting effects particularly pronounced under conditions of metabolic stress.

SLC38A7 promotes GSK-3β ubiquitination and activates Wnt/β-catenin pathway

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying the oncogenic function of SLC38A7 in GC, we performed GSEA based on TCGA datasets. The results revealed a significant enrichment of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in tumors with high SLC38A7 expression (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.838, P.adj = 0.003) [Figure 3A]. Consistently, data from the GEPIA database demonstrated strong positive correlations between SLC38A7 and key Wnt-pathway effectors, including CTNNB1, MYC, and CCND1 [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C867], suggesting that SLC38A7 may functionally regulate this pathway.

Western blotting analysis confirmed that overexpression of SLC38A7 led to increased nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and upregulation of its downstream targets, including c-MYC and cyclin D1, whereas SLC38A7 knockdown suppressed Wnt-pathway activity [Figure 3B]. Importantly, analysis of β-catenin phosphorylation further demonstrated that SLC38A7 depletion enhanced β-catenin phosphorylation, consistent with increased degradation, whereas SLC38A7 overexpression reduced β-catenin phosphorylation, favoring its stabilization and nuclear accumulation. In parallel, GSK-3β—the central negative regulator of β-catenin—was elevated at the total protein level but reduced in its phosphorylated (inactive) form upon SLC38A7 knockdown, particularly under glutamine-deprived conditions [Figure 3B]. Together, these findings reinforce the role of SLC38A7 in regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling through modulation of GSK-3β activity and β-catenin stability. Ubiquitination assays revealed that SLC38A7 overexpression significantly enhanced GSK-3β ubiquitination, suggesting it may promote GSK-3β degradation through the proteasomal pathway [Figure 3C]. To functionally validate this mechanism, we treated SLC38A7-knockdown MKN-28 cells with LiCl, a known pharmacological inhibitor of GSK-3β. LiCl treatment restored p-GSK-3β and nuclear β-catenin levels [Figure 3D], and partially rescued cell proliferation and migration suppressed by SLC38A7 knockdown [Figure 3E and F].

Collectively, these results indicate that SLC38A7 activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of GSK-3β, thereby enhancing the malignant phenotype of GC cells.

SLC38A7 interacts with MYH9 and is associated with GSK-3β regulation in GC cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which SLC38A7 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling in GC, we conducted co-IP followed by mass spectrometry to screen for interacting proteins. MYH9, a nonmuscle myosin heavy chain protein, was identified as a major SLC38A7-binding partner in MKN-28 cells [Figure 4A]. Bioinformatic analysis using the GEPIA platform, which integrates TCGA tumor, TCGA normal, and GTEx datasets, revealed a strong positive correlation between SLC38A7 and MYH9 expression in GC tissues [Figure 4B, R = 0.736, P <0.001]. In addition, database analysis showed that MYH9 expression was significantly elevated in gastric tumors compared with normal gastric tissues [Figure 4C, P <0.001]. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated substantial colocalization of SLC38A7 and MYH9 in the cytoplasm of GC cells [Figure 4D]. Moreover, bidirectional co-IP assays confirmed the physical interaction between SLC38A7 and MYH9 [Figure 4E and F]. Further immunoblotting of the immunoprecipitates revealed the presence of GSK-3β in protein complexes containing either SLC38A7 or MYH9 [Figure 4G], suggesting that the three proteins may form a functional complex in GC cells. To further define the functional interplay between SLC38A7 and MYH9, we performed reciprocal perturbation experiments. Overexpression of MYH9 led to a marked reduction in GSK-3β protein levels, whereas concurrent knockdown of SLC38A7 partially restored GSK-3β expression, suggesting that SLC38A7 is required for full MYH9-mediated regulation of GSK-3β. Conversely, MYH9 depletion abolished the effects of SLC38A7 overexpression on GSK-3β degradation and β-catenin stabilization, indicating that MYH9 is indispensable for SLC38A7-driven activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [Figure 4H].

These findings suggest a cooperative role of SLC38A7 and MYH9 in modulating GSK-3β stability, which may underlie their joint regulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity in GC. A schematic model summarizing the proposed SLC38A7–MYH9–GSK-3β–Wnt/β-catenin regulatory axis in GC is presented in Figure 4I.

MYH9 is essential for SLC38A7-induced GSK-3β degradation and Wnt/β-catenin activation

To elucidate the interaction between SLC38A7 and MYH9, and to further explore the mechanism by which SLC38A7 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in GC, we aimed to map the key binding regions between these two proteins. A protein complex model of SLC38A7 and MYH9 was constructed using AlphaFold3, followed by molecular docking analysis to predict potential interaction interfaces [Figure 5A]. Seven top-ranking residues from SLC38A7 (N7, E12, E424, Q3, E423, E361, and S420) were identified as critical for the interaction.

To validate these predictions, we generated seven SLC38A7 mutants, each containing a single-point mutation at one of the predicted binding residues. co-IP analysis showed that WT SLC38A7 efficiently interacted with MYH9, whereas several mutants—particularly SLC38A7E12A, SLC38A7Q3A, and SLC38A7E361A—exhibited significantly reduced interaction with MYH9, with the E12A variant showing the most pronounced impairment [Figure 5B].

Consistent with the co-IP data, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in SLC38A7–MYH9 colocalization in both SLC38A7-KO cells reconstituted with the E12A or Q3A mutants, compared to those reconstituted with wild-type SLC38A7 (WT SLC38A7) [Figure 5C and D]. These results suggest that E12 and Q3 may be critical interaction residues between SLC38A7 and MYH9.

Next, we examined the consequences of the disrupted SLC38A7–MYH9 interaction on the subsequent downstream effects of SLC38A7-mediated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. We stably transfected the SLC38A7E12A mutant into SLC38A7-knockout (KO) cells to generate MKN-28 cells with impaired SLC38A7-dependent GSK-3β degradation and Wnt/β-catenin activation [Figure 5E]. To further validate this mechanism, we performed ubiquitination assays and confirmed that the E12A mutation markedly reduced SLC38A7-mediated ubiquitination of GSK-3β, thereby directly linking the mutation to impaired GSK-3β stability [Figure 5F].

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the interaction between SLC38A7 and MYH9—particularly via the E12 residue—is critical for SLC38A7-mediated GSK-3β ubiquitination and subsequent activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in GC cells.

Given that SLC38A7 promotes GC progression at least in part through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, we hypothesized that disruption of the SLC38A7–MYH9 interaction would impair its oncogenic function. To test this, we established SLC38A7-KO MKN-28 cells and reconstituted them with vector control, WT SLC38A7, or the interaction-deficient mutant SLC38A7E12A. In addition, unmodified WT cells and SLC38A7-KO cells were included as baseline controls. CCK-8 assays showed that SLC38A7 deletion significantly reduced cell proliferation compared with WT cells, whereas reexpression of WT SLC38A7 restored proliferative capacity. By contrast, the E12A mutant failed to fully rescue the phenotype [Figure 5G]. Similarly, wound healing assays demonstrated impaired migration in SLC38A7-KO cells compared with WT, and this defect was rescued by WT SLC38A7 but not by the E12A mutant [Figure 5H]. These results demonstrate that the tumor-promoting effects of SLC38A7 in GC cells require its interaction with MYH9, and that disruption of this interaction markedly attenuates its oncogenic activity.

SLC38A7 promotes GC tumor growth in vivo

To assess the oncogenic role of SLC38A7 in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft models by injecting 4-week-old male nude mice with either MKN-28 cells stably expressing shRNA against SLC38A7 or MGC-803 cells overexpressing SLC38A7. Tumor growth was monitored over time. Mice implanted with SLC38A7-knockdown cells developed significantly smaller tumors in terms of volume [Figure 6A and B], weight [Figure 6C], and overall growth rate [Figure 6D] compared with control mice. Conversely, SLC38A7 overexpression led to a marked increase in tumor growth, supporting its tumor-promoting effect in GC.

To further explore the molecular basis of these phenotypes, we analyzed protein expression in xenograft tumor tissues by western blotting. Tumors derived from SLC38A7-knockdown cells exhibited reduced MYH9 and nuclear β-catenin levels, accompanied by increased GSK-3β expression. In contrast, tumors from SLC38A7-overexpressing cells showed elevated MYH9 and β-catenin expression with concomitant downregulation of GSK-3β [Figure 6E]. These molecular findings are consistent with our in vitro results, further substantiating the role of SLC38A7 in GC pathogenesis.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically identified SLC38A7 as a glutamine-sensitive oncogenic driver in GC. We demonstrated that SLC38A7 not only acts as a diagnostic and prognostic marker but also plays a functional role in promoting tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, SLC38A7 mediates MYH9-dependent degradation of GSK-3β, leading to Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation. These findings provide a new molecular framework linking glutamine metabolism with canonical oncogenic pathways in GC.

Previous studies have shown that amino acid transporters, including members of the SLC38 family, regulate cancer metabolism and signaling cascades under nutrient-limiting conditions.[27,28] In line with these findings, we observed that SLC38A7 was strongly upregulated under glutamine deprivation, facilitating GC cell growth. This observation complements earlier reports where SLC38A7 was shown to function as a lysosomal glutamine exporter,[15] sustaining mTORC1 activity and survival under stress.[17] Our results expand on this metabolic role by establishing a direct connection between SLC38A7 and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis.

A particularly novel finding of our study is the discovery that SLC38A7 directly binds to MYH9, a nonmuscle myosin II isoform with emerging roles in tumor biology.[21–23,29,30] We revealed that this interaction promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of GSK-3β, a key negative regulator of β-catenin stability.[31–33] Loss-of-function studies and mutational analysis demonstrated that specific residues (e.g., E12 and Q3) on SLC38A7 are essential for its binding to MYH9 and for activating the Wnt-signaling pathway. These mechanistic insights add depth to the regulatory complexity of canonical signaling pathways in GC.

From a clinical perspective, the elevated expression of SLC38A7 in GC tissues and its correlation with poor patient outcomes suggest its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker. More importantly, the dual regulation of metabolism and signaling by SLC38A7 provides a rationale for considering it as a candidate therapeutic target, although further large-scale clinical validation is warranted.

The interaction between SLC38A7 function and glutamine availability highlights the metabolic plasticity of cancer cells. Our data showed that SLC38A7 enhances GC cell growth specifically under glutamine deprivation, underscoring its role in nutrient stress adaptation. Prior studies have shown that glutamine catabolites can inactivate GSK-3β through Ser9 phosphorylation, promoting β-catenin accumulation.[34,35] Additionally, Wnt effectors such as MYC are known to regulate glutamine uptake and utilization,[36–38] suggesting a reciprocal relationship between signaling and metabolic rewiring in tumor cells.

Although our study uncovers an important functional and mechanistic role for SLC38A7 in GC, there are limitations. The upstream regulators of SLC38A7 expression under physiological and pathological conditions remain unclear. Although the in vivo experiments support our conclusions, orthotopic or metastatic GC models may better represent clinical disease. It also remains to be determined whether similar regulatory mechanisms exist in other glutamine-addicted tumors, such as pancreatic or colorectal cancers.

From a clinical perspective, the elevated expression of SLC38A7 in GC and its association with poor prognosis highlight its potential as a therapeutic target. Although no direct inhibitors currently exist, our findings provide a rationale for developing strategies to disrupt the SLC38A7–MYH9 interaction, such as competitive peptides or monoclonal antibodies. Future studies should explore the broader role of the SLC38A7–MYH9–GSK-3β axis across cancers and use structural insights to guide rational drug design. In addition, combinatorial therapies involving metabolic inhibitors and Wnt-pathway antagonists may offer synergistic benefits in preclinical GC models.

In summary, this study establishes SLC38A7 as a distinct oncogenic regulator in GC. We demonstrated that SLC38A7 binds MYH9 to promote GSK-3β ubiquitination, activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and drive malignant progression. Disruption of this interaction substantially reduced the oncogenic activity of SLC38A7. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized regulatory axis in GC and offer a foundation for developing more effective therapeutic strategies.
