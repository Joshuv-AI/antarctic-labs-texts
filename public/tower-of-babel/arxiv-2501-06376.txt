On the Partial Identifiability in Reward Learning: Choosing the Best Reward
Filippo Lazzati, Alberto Maria Metelli

Abstract
In Reward Learning (ReL), we are given feedback on an unknown target reward , and the goal is to use this information to find it. When the feedback is not informative enough, the target reward is only partially identifiable , i.e., there exists a set of rewards (the feasible set) that are equally-compatible with the feedback. In this paper, we show that there exists a choice of reward, non-necessarily contained in the feasible set that, depending on the ReL application , improves the performance w.r.t. selecting the reward arbitrarily among the feasible ones. To this aim, we introduce a new quantitative framework to analyze ReL problems in a simple yet expressive way. We exemplify the framework in a reward transfer use case, for which we devise three provably-efficient ReL algorithms.

Introduction

Reward Learning (ReL) is the problem of learning a reward function from data

(
Jeon et al. 2020
)
. When the data are demonstrations, ReL is
commonly known as Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)

(
Russell 1998
)
, whereas when the data are (pairwise) comparisons of
trajectories, ReL is usually called Preference-based Reinforcement Learning
(PbRL)
(
Wirth et al. 2017
)
or Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(RLHF)
(
Kaufmann et al. 2024
)
.

The main point of ReL is that it allows us to learn a reward function that
corresponds to “the most succinct and transferable representation of the
preferences of an agent”
(
Russell 1998
;
Arora & Doshi 2021
)
.
As such,
ideally
, it permits to use datasets of demonstrations and
comparisons for a variety of interesting applications, like reward design

(
Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017
)
, Imitation Learning (IL)

(
Abbeel & Ng 2004
)
, risk-sensitive IL
(
Lacotte et al. 2019
)
,
transferring behavior to other environments
(
Fu et al. 2017
)
, inferring
the preferences of an agent
(
Hadfield-Menell et al. 2016
)
, improving the behavior of
an agent
(
Syed & Schapire 2007
)
, and, more generally, all the tasks that can be
carried out with a reward function.

However, in practice, ReL has been successfully applied only to IL

(
Ho & Ermon 2016
)
and reward design

(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
. The most significant issue that prevents the use
of ReL algorithms to other applications is

partial identifiability

(
Cao et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Skalse et al. 2023b
)
.
Indeed, the target reward may not be
uniquely
determined from the given
feedback, but there is a set of reward functions, named the
feasible set

(
Metelli et al. 2021
;
Metelli et al. 2023
)
, that are equally “compatible”
with the feedback. Consequently, using the wrong reward from the set does not
allow us to successfully perform at the considered
application

(
Skalse et al. 2023b
)
.
For instance, suppose that we observe an agent driving very fast. From this
information (feedback) alone, we cannot say if it drives fast because it likes
doing so (reward 1) or because it is late (reward 2). If we want to predict its
behavior (application) when it is not late, these rewards give birth to two
different predictions, one where it drives fast and the other where it does not.

The most popular ReL algorithms choose
one
reward from the feasible set
with a rather
arbitrary
criterion. For instance,

Ng & Russell 2000
;
Ratliff et al. 2006
adopt the “heuristic” of margin
maximization, while

Ziebart et al. 2008
;
Boularias et al. 2011
;
Wulfmeier et al. 2016
;
Christiano et al. 2017

let the optimization algorithm break the ties.

Of course, using an
arbitrary
reward from the feasible set makes sense
only if
all the rewards in the feasible set have the same “meaning” for
the considered ReL application

(
Skalse et al. 2023b
)
. In the example, this would be true if all
the rewards provide the same prediction, i.e., that the agent will drive
fast/slow.
In literature, this condition is guaranteed by either
(i)
considering large
amounts of feedback that make the feasible set sufficiently small

(
Amin & Singh 2016
;
Cao et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2021
)
, or

(ii)
using each ReL algorithm
only
for the specific application for
which it is designed (e.g., the rewards computed by
Ramachandran & Amir 2007

and
Christiano et al. 2017
can be used only for, respectively, IL and reward
design).

Nevertheless, these solutions are unsatisfactory.
(i)
requires additional
feedback that is often unavailable, while
(ii)
is too restrictive since it
prevents us from, e.g., transferring to a new environment the reward extracted
by a ReL algorithm for IL (like GAIL

(
Ho & Ermon 2016
)
) or for reward design (like
that of
Christiano et al. 2017
).

In this paper, we present a choice of reward function, non-necessarily contained
in the feasible set that, for the ReL application at stake,
outperforms any other reward choice. Consequently, by avoiding the requirement
that all the rewards in the feasible set have the same “meaning”, our reward
choice
extends the range of applicability of ReL
.

Contributions.
The contributions of this paper can be summarized as
follows.

•

We propose a new
framework for ReL
that permits to analyze ReL
problems in a quantitative way (Section
3
).

•

We explain how to use the framework to numerically compare different
rewards, we present our
reward choice
, and we show its advantages
(Section
4
).

•

We instantiate the framework on a use case, for which we devise three
provably-efficient
ReL algorithms
, and we conduct some illustrative
simulations (Section
5
).

•

Finally, we provide insights on
model selection
through the lens
of the proposed framework (Section
6
).

The proofs of all results are reported in the Appendix
A
-
C
.

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).

\Omega\coloneqq(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{H}\times\mathcal{S}

For any

G(\omega;r)\coloneqq\sum_{h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket}r_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})

We denote the expected utility of a policy

J^{\pi}(r;p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p,\pi}\big[\sum_{h\in\llbracket H\rrbracket}r_{h}(s_{h},a_{h})\big]

\Pi^{*}(r;p)\coloneqq\argmax_{\pi}J^{\pi}(r;p)

J^{*}(r;p)\coloneqq\max_{\pi}J^{\pi}(r;p)
.

3
A Framework for Reward Learning

In this section, we present a novel framework for studying ReL problems.
Specifically, we extend
Skalse et al. 2023b
by

(i)
modelling each
feedback
as made of two components, and by
(ii)

associating a distance to every
application
, to permit quantitative
considerations.
In addition, we introduce a new ReL feedback.

3.1
Problem Formulation

In ReL
(
Jeon et al. 2020
)
, we aim to learn a
target reward

r^{\star}
from a given set of feedback
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{i}\}_{i}
, i.e., data, that “leak
information” on
r^{\star}
. The ultimate goal is to use
r^{\star}
(or a
“similar” reward) for some
downstream application
g
.

Example 3.1

(Reward design)
.

We have a learning agent and we want it to perform a task (i.e.,
r^{\star}
)
that we have in mind. Thus, we can provide demonstrations of behavior and
trajectory preferences (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}
)
(
Ibarz et al. 2018
)
to make
it learn
r^{\star}
, and then it can perform planning (i.e.,
g
) on it.

Example 3.2

(Predicting behavior)
.

We get demonstrations from an agent (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}
), and we want to predict its
behavior in a new environment. Thus, we can learn its reward
r^{\star}
, and
then perform evaluation in the new environment (i.e.,
g
).

Example 3.3

(Inferring preferences)
.

We can use demonstrations and/or trajectory comparisons from an agent (i.e.,

\mathcal{F}
) to infer its
r^{\star}
, and, then, compare the performance of certain
policies (i.e.,
g
) to analyze its preferences.

3.2
Feedback

We define each feedback
f\in\mathcal{F}
as made of two components

,
where
\mathcal{D}
is some

, and
\mathbb{A}
is some

that
relates the data

to the target reward

.

Example 3.4

(Trajectory comparisons)
.

Given trajectories

as data, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}=\{\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\sigma\}

\mathbb{P}(\sigma=1)=e^{G(\omega^{1};r^{\star})}/\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}e^{G(\omega_{i};r^{\star})}

Without

from

information
\mathcal{D}
is providing on
r^{\star}
.
Note that different assumptions can be applied to the same data

Example 3.5

Let

3.3
Applications

By application
g
we mean
any task that can be carried out with a reward
function
. Examples from the ReL literature include planning

(
Ng & Russell 2000
)
, planning in a new environment
(
Arora & Doshi 2021
)
,
constrained planning
(
Malik et al. 2021
)
, risk-sensitive planning

(
Lacotte et al. 2019
)
, finding the greedy policy

(
Zhu et al. 2023
)
, comparing the performance of trajectories

(
Ziebart et al. 2008
)
and policies
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
.

To allow for quantitative considerations,
we associate every application

g
with a premetric
d_{g}:\mathfrak{R}\times\mathfrak{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in the space of rewards
, with the
following meaning. Let
r
be the reward that we want to use for
application
g
, and let
r^{\prime}
be any other reward. Then,
d_{g}(r^{\prime};r)

represents the error we incur in if we use reward
r^{\prime}
in place of
r
for the
considered application
g
. In other words, if the application is
g
,

r^{\star}=r
and our ReL algorithm outputs
\widehat{r}=r^{\prime}
, then
d_{g}(r^{\prime};r)=d_{g}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})
is
our error.
We provide some examples below.

Example 3.7

(Planning)
.

We want to perform planning
g
on
r^{\star}
, but our ReL algorithm has
recovered
\widehat{r}\approx r^{\star}
. If we can compute optimal policies

exactly
, then we can measure the error of using
\widehat{r}
in place
of
r^{\star}
in environment
p
as:

\displaystyle d_{\text{PL},p}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})\coloneqq J^{*}(r^{\star};p)-\min\limits_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}(\widehat{r};p)}J^{\pi}(r^{\star};p),

namely, as the (worst-case) suboptimality induced by
\widehat{r}
.

Example 3.8

(Comparing the performance of policies)
.

If we use
\widehat{r}
in place of
r^{\star}
to assess and compare the
“goodness” of various policies in environment
p
, we can use:

\displaystyle d_{\text{CO},p}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})\coloneqq\max_{\pi}\big|J^{\pi}(r^{\star};p)-J^{\pi}(\widehat{r};p)\big|,

that quantifies the largest difference in performance.

Example 3.9

(Finding the greedy policy)
.

Inspired by the training of InstructGPT
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
,

Zhu et al. 2023
consider as
g
the computation of the greedy
policy
\pi^{\text{gr}}(\cdot;r)\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r(\cdot,a)
for any
r\in\mathfrak{R}
in
a stationary environment. Thus, for some distribution
\rho\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}
,
they use:

\displaystyle d_{\text{GR},\rho}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}\big[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r^{\star}(s,a)-r^{\star}(s,\pi^{\text{gr}}(s;\widehat{r}))\big].

Note that these distances are not guaranteed to be metrics:

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
p,\rho
, then

d_{\text{PL},p},d_{\text{CO},p},d_{\text{GR},\rho}
are premetrics. Moreover,
there are some
p,\rho
such that:

•

d_{\text{PL},p},d_{\text{CO},p},d_{\text{GR},\rho}
lack the identity
of indiscernibles;

•

d_{\text{PL},p},d_{\text{GR},\rho}
are not simmetric;

•

d_{\text{PL},p},d_{\text{GR},\rho}
lack the triangle
inequality.

For ease of presentation, in the following, we assume that each feedback is made

4.1
The Error of a Reward

Consider a ReL problem where
\mathcal{F}
is the set of feedback,
g
is the
application, and
d_{g}
is the premetric.
Due to its meaning, we call
d_{g}(r;r^{\star})
the
error
of reward

r\in\mathfrak{R}
.
Because of the
partial identifiability
of
r^{\star}
from
\mathcal{F}
, we cannot
compute
d_{g}(r;r^{\star})
exactly, but we can restrict it to an interval.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
r\in\mathfrak{R}
be arbitrary. Under Assumption
3.1
, the

tightest
interval containing
d_{g}(r;r^{\star})
is:

\displaystyle d_{g}(r;r^{\star})\in\Big[\min\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime}),\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})\Big].

Since, by Assumption
3.1
,
r^{\star}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, then Proposition
4.1
simply considers the “best” and “worst” choices that

r^{\star}
can take on.
In absence of partial identifiability, i.e., when
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}=\{r^{\star}\}
, the interval converges
to a point:
\min_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})=\max_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})=d_{g}(r;r^{\star})
.
Observe that, if
r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
,
then
\min_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})=0
.
The upper bound to the error represents the
worst-case
error, and
deserves a definition.

Definition 4.1

(Compatibility)
.

The
(non)compatibility
3

Lazzati et al. 2024a
, we use term

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F},g}(r)

.

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F},g}(r)
of reward

r\in\mathfrak{R}
with
g

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F},g}(r)\coloneqq\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime}).

4.2
The Robust Reward Choice

Ideally, we want a ReL algorithm that takes in input
\mathcal{F},g
, and outputs a
reward
\widehat{r}\in\mathfrak{R}
such that
d_{g}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})=0
, i.e., it
outputs either
r^{\star}
or any equivalent reward. However, due to partial
identifiability, this is not possible. Therefore, to guarantee a small error

d_{g}(\widehat{r};r^{\star})
, we propose to learn the reward

\widehat{r}=r_{\mathcal{F},g}
that minimizes the upper bound

\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F},g}(\widehat{r})
.

Definition 4.2

(Robust reward choice)
.

The
robust reward choice

r_{\mathcal{F},g}
with feedback
\mathcal{F}
and
application
g
is:

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{F},g}\in\argmin\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F},g}(r).

In words,
r_{\mathcal{F},g}
represents the
Chebyshev center
of the feasible set

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
in premetric space
(\mathfrak{R},d_{g})
. Note that it may be
non
unique
even if
d_{g}
is a metric
(
Alimov & Tsar’kov 2019
)
.
We remark that choosing
\widehat{r}=r_{\mathcal{F},g}
allows us to be
robust

6
Discussion on Model Selection

In this section, we provide some considerations on model selection that use the
representation of feedback through two components presented in Section

3
.
For simplicity of presentation, we consider a single feedback
\mathcal{F}=\{f\}
, with

f=(\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A})
, and we assume that infinite data are available.

Modelling feedback.
In practical applications, we observe the data
\mathcal{D}
, and we have to select the
assumption
\mathbb{A}
based on our knowledge on how
\mathcal{D}
has been generated.

Example 6.1

(Feedback of driving safely)
.

Let the
unknown
target reward
r^{\star}
represent the task of driving
safely. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i}
be a dataset of demonstrations collected by
an agent
\pi^{E}
that “aims to demonstrate how to drive safely in a certain
environment”.
What assumption do we adopt?

We can decide to model the problem in different ways, i.e., there are always
multiple assumptions
\mathbb{A}
that can be associated to the given data
\mathcal{D}
to

try
to capture the “true” relationship between
\mathcal{D}
and the
underlying unknown
r^{\star}
.

Example
6.1
(continue).

We can model
\pi^{E}
as an optimal policy for the “driving safely” task by
using assumption
\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}}
(see Example
3.5
). If
we think that the demonstrated
\pi^{E}
sometimes takes suboptimal actions,
then we might prefer using
\mathbb{A}^{\text{MCE}}
. Alternatively, we can simply
assume that
\pi^{E}
is at least
\epsilon
-optimal for some
\epsilon>0
,
i.e.,
J^{\pi^{E}}(r^{\star};p)\geq J^{*}(r^{\star};p)-\epsilon
. We call this
assumption
\mathbb{A}^{\text{SUB}}
.

We incur in
misspecification

if
\mathbb{A}
does not correctly describe the relationship between
r^{\star}

and
\mathcal{D}
(see
Skalse & Abate 2024

for an analysis of misspecification in IRL).

Example
6.1
(continue).

If we model data
\mathcal{D}
using assumption
\mathbb{A}^{\text{MCE}}
, but, actually,

\pi^{E}
is optimal (i.e., it follows
\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}}
), then our feedback
is misspecified.

Choosing the correct modelling assumption.
The crucial question is:
what is the
best
modelling assumption for
the given data
\mathcal{D}
?
Intuitively,
any
assumption that permits to carry
out the downstream ReL application
g

effectively
is satisfactory. In
other words, we can tolerate some misspecification error as long as the final
outcome is acceptable.

Thanks to our framework, we can make these considerations more quantitative. As
explained in Section
4
, the (non)informativeness

\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{f\},g}
represents the
minimum
error that can be achieved in the
worst-case for doing
g
using
f=(\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A})
. Under the model of feedback
f

(i.e., the assumption
\mathbb{A}
) considered, the worst-case error cannot be smaller
than
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{f\},g}
. Therefore, this is unsatisfactory if we aim to carry out

g
with an error
\Delta<\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{f\},g}
.

To solve this issue, we have to reduce the size of the feasible set
\mathcal{R}_{f}
so
that its Chebyshev radius
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{f\},g}
reduces too. There are two ways for
doing this. The best one
(i)
consists in collecting additional feedback
f^{\prime}

to obtain
\mathcal{R}_{f}\cap\mathcal{R}_{f^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{f}
(e.g., through active learning, see
Appendix
D
). However, additional feedback might not
be available in practice. The other way
(ii)
consists in imposing “more
structure” to the problem by changing the assumption
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
to
restrict the feasible set
\mathcal{R}_{\{\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A}\}}
to
\mathcal{R}_{\{\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\}}
.

Example 6.2
.

If we model
\mathcal{D}
using
\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}}
, we obtain a feasible set that is

strictly
contained into the feasible set obtained using assumption

\mathbb{A}^{\text{SUB}}
. Thus, whatever the application
g
at stake,

\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}}\},g}\leq\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A}^{\text{SUB}}\},g}
.

By adopting a more restrictive (less realistic) model
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
, we reduce the
“measurable” error
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\{\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\},g}
at the price of a larger unknown
misspecification error. This is fine as long as
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
is perceived as
sufficiently realistic.

If there is no realistic assumption with a small enough value of
informativeness
, then we conclude that the application
g
cannot be carried
out effectively with the only data
\mathcal{D}
. In other words,
the ReL problem
cannot be solved with the desired accuracy
.

7
Related Work

The framework of
Skalse et al. 2023b
permits
to understand under which conditions the baseline error
D_{\mathcal{F},g}
is exactly
zero, but it does not reveal that some rewards are better than others.

Skalse et al. 2023a

quantify
the distance between rewards but
independently of the application
g
.

To cope with partial identifiability,

Amin & Singh 2016
;
Cao et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2021

assume the availability of a various amount of feedback to reduce the size of
the feasible set.

Metelli et al. 2021
;
Metelli et al. 2023
;
Lazzati et al. 2024b
;
Lazzati et al. 2024a

circumvent the issue by learning the entire feasible set, postponing the reward
selection.

Ziebart et al. 2008
;
Wulfmeier et al. 2016
;
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ho & Ermon 2016

choose a reward arbitrarily from the feasible set, and this is acceptable for
the applications that they consider, but potentially not for others.

Ng & Russell 2000
;
Ratliff et al. 2006
;
Ramachandran & Amir 2007
make a choice
of reward meaningful for the IL application only. In particular,
the reward choice
r^{\text{BIRL}}_{\mathcal{F},g}
of
Ramachandran & Amir 2007
can
be interpreted in our framework as the minimization of the average “error”:

\displaystyle r^{\text{BIRL}}_{\mathcal{F},g}\in\argmin\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}|}\sum\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime}),

However, this does not provide any worst-case guarantee.

We mention

Ho & Ermon 2016
;
Zhu et al. 2023

because, for specific applications, as explained in Appendix
C.3
, their algorithms bypass the computation of a reward and make a

robust
choice of policy analogous to our reward choice.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a novel ReL framework for characterizing the
partial identifiability in a quantitative way. We have shown that there exists a

robust
reward choice that, depending on the application, outperforms all
the others in the worst case. Finally, we have illustrated its power in a use
case, by providing also provably-efficient algorithms.

Future directions.
We hope that the proposed framework, by enhancing the understanding of ReL
problems, will help to improve the performance of ReL algorithms in applications
beyond IL and reward design.

\displaystyle\Big[\min\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime}),\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})\Big],

. For simplicity, we prove the result only
for the upper bound, because the proof for the lower bound is analogous.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
be the feasible set and let

be a premetric. Assume that

3.1
holds, i.e.,

, and that
no other information on

is known. By contradiction, assume that

\max_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})
, and such that

,
that can vary freely inside
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, then we can set

\partial_{\lambda^{i}_{\text{TC}}}\mathcal{L},\partial_{\lambda^{i}_{\text{C}}}\mathcal{L},\partial_{\lambda^{i}_{\text{D}}}\mathcal{L}

C.3
Bypassing the Need for a Reward Function

All ReL papers make the underlying assumption that the feedback received is
“reward-rational”
(

)
, in the sense that there
exists a reward function
r^{\star}
that drives it (see Assumption
3.1
). However, the ultimate goal of ReL is
not
to learn

r^{\star}
, but is to learn some quantity
x
(that depends on
r^{\star}
). For
instance, in IL the goal is to learn a policy (optimal under
r^{\star}
), while
in the application considered in Section
5
the final goal is
to compute a scalar (the difference in performance under
r^{\star}
between

\pi^{2}
and
\pi^{1}

whether it is possible to do ReL without explicitly computing any reward
,
i.e., by directly computing the desired quantity
x
from the feedback.

We begin by extending the framework and the robust choice presented in Section

3
and Section
4
to the “ReL
without reward” problem, and then we argue that there are applications in which
this is not possible.

C.3.1
A ReL Framework without Reward Functions

In ReL, the computation of a reward function
r\in\mathfrak{R}
from data is an
intermediate step, since the ultimate goal is to “use” such reward to compute
another quantity, like a policy
\pi\in\Pi
(e.g., if the application

Formally, let

be a set of feedback with feasible set

9

9

\displaystyle r_{\mathcal{F},g}

\displaystyle\in\argmin\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}d_{g}(r;r^{\prime})

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{\makebox[0.0pt]{(1)}}}{=}\argmin\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}\max\limits_{x\in\mathfrak{M}(r)}h_{g}(r^{\prime},x)

\displaystyle=\argmin\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}\max\limits_{x\in\mathfrak{M}(r)}\Big(\max\limits_{r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}h_{g}(r^{\prime},x)\Big),

where at (1) we use the fact that the two definitions are “coherent” with each
other. Therefore, as long as at least one
r\in\mathfrak{R}
with the aforementioned
property exists, then it will be the robust reward choice, and mapping it to

\mathcal{X}
will be the robust choice of the framework without rewards.

Related works that can be aligned with this framework.

We observe that many papers in literature can be interpreted as taking the robust
choice as described in this section.
For instance, IL algorithms based on distribution matching

(
Abbeel & Ng 2004
;
Ho & Ermon 2016
)
,
basically want to find a policy
\pi^{\text{IL}}
(i.e., our quantity
x\in\mathcal{X}
) that
minimizes (in an MDP with transition model
p
, given the expert’s policy

\pi^{E}
):

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{IL}}

\displaystyle\in\argmin\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\max\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}J^{\pi^{E}}(r;p)-J^{\pi^{\prime}}(r;p)

\displaystyle=\argmin\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\Big\|d^{p,\pi^{E}}-d^{p,\pi^{\prime}}\Big\|_{1},

if we consider
\mathfrak{R}=\{r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\llbracket H\rrbracket\to[-1,+1]\}
. If we define the feasible set

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\{r\in\mathfrak{R}\,|\,J^{*}(r;p)=J^{\pi^{E}}(r;p)\}
, and we define the
loss
h_{g}:\mathfrak{R}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
for this application as:

\displaystyle h_{g}(r,\pi)\coloneqq J^{*}(r;p)-J^{\pi}(r;p),

then, clearly,
the policy extracted by IL algorithms is our robust
choice
:

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{IL}}\in\argmin\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\max\limits_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}h_{g}(r,\pi^{\prime})=\argmin\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\max\limits_{r\in\mathfrak{R}}J^{\pi^{E}}(r;p)-J^{\pi^{\prime}}(r;p),

since they look for the policy that minimizes the suboptimality (error) in the
worst case among all the rewards that make
\pi^{E}
optimal, i.e., the rewards in
the feasible set.

Similarly, the algorithm (Algorithm 1) proposed by
Zhu et al. 2023

for RLHF (reward design) makes the same robust choice as us and calls it
“pessimistic”. If we assume infinite data for their setting, then their
confidence set
\Theta
becomes our feasible set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
under the additional
assumption that
r^{\star}
is linear. Then, by setting:

\displaystyle h_{g}(r,\pi)\coloneqq-J^{\pi}(r;p),

for some
p

\displaystyle\pi\in\argmin\limits_{\pi^{\prime}}\max\limits_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{F}}}h_{g}(r,\pi^{\prime}).

C.3.2
It’s Not Always Possible

Even though it seems promising to bypass the computation of a reward function
for directly computing the quantity of interest
x
(as shown for instance by
Proposition
5.6
for the application in
Section
5
), since it saves us the computation of the mapping

\mathfrak{M}
that might be computationally expensive, unfortunately, this solution is
not always possible.

As an example, consider all the settings in which the application
g
requires
the computation of potentially an exponential/very large amount of items

x\in\mathcal{X}
, like assessing the performance of all policies or of a still unknown
policy (see Example
3.8
). In such
case, if we want to compute the performance (i.e., a scalar
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
) for all
possible policies, we have to compute and store an exponential number of
quantities (as many as there are policies). Instead, if we compute only the
robust reward choice, even if we might lose some accuracy (see the paragraph on
“multiple applications” in Section
4.2
), then we have
a single object from which we can simply assess the performance of all policies.

Appendix D
Active Learning

In the Active Learning setting
(
Lopes et al. 2009
)
, in addition
to a given set of feedback
\mathcal{F}
, we can choose to receive a new feedback
f^{\prime}

from a set
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{f_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i}
. Crucially, for any
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
,

f^{\prime}=(\mathcal{D},\mathbb{A})
, we consider the assumption
\mathbb{A}
to be known, while the
actual data
\mathcal{D}
is revealed
after
our choice.

Example D.1
.

We might choose between feedback
f_{1}=(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}})
,
consisting of demonstrations in environment
\mathcal{M}_{1}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
) from an
optimal expert (i.e.,
\mathbb{A}^{\text{OPT}}
, see Example
3.5
), or
f_{2}=(\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathbb{A}^{\text{MCE}})
, made of
demonstrations in environment
\mathcal{M}_{2}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{2}
) from a maximal causal
entropy expert (i.e.,
\mathbb{A}^{\text{MCE}}
). After our choice, the expert
demonstrates only one policy (i.e., either
\mathcal{D}_{1}
or
\mathcal{D}_{2}
).

What is the “best” choice of feedback to receive without knowing its data

\mathcal{D}
? Thanks to our framework, and specifically to the notion of
informativeness defined in Section
4
, the immediate choice
is the feedback that, whatever its true data, is the most “informative”.

Definition D.1

(Information gain)
.

We define the
information
gain

\text{IG}_{\mathcal{F},g}(f)
of a feedback
f
w.r.t.
g
given
\mathcal{F}
as:

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{\mathcal{F},g}(f)\coloneqq\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{F},g}-\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{F}\cup\{f\},g}.

In words,
\text{IG}_{\mathcal{F},g}(f)
represents the reduction of
(non)informativeness (worst-case error) of the ReL problem. Observe that

\text{IG}_{\mathcal{F},g}(f)\geq 0
, since
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{F}\cup\{f\},g}\leq\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{F},g}
,
i.e., the more feedback the less error.

Formally, the feedback that reduces the most the (non)informativeness is the
feedback
f_{j}^{\prime}
from set
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{f_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i},f_{i}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathbb{A}_{i})
, that
maximizes the
worst-case
information gain w.r.t. the data
\mathcal{D}_{i}
:

\displaystyle j\in\argmax\limits_{i}\min\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}\text{IG}_{\mathcal{F},g}(\{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathbb{A}_{i}\}).

Simply put, we select the feedback that, whatever the true data we will
receive, we know that it will bring the larger reduction of informativeness,
i.e., of error, for the ReL problem.
