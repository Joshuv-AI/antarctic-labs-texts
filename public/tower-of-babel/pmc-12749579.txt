The toxicological impact of PET-MPs exposure on atherosclerosis: insights from network toxicology, molecular docking, and machine learning
Authors: Qiang Wang, Hu Liang, Lang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyethylene terephthalate microplastics (PET-MPs) have emerged as a significant environmental issue, primarily due to their durability and associated health hazards. Nevertheless, their contribution to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (AS) is not well elucidated, highlighting the necessity for a comprehensive assessment of their molecular toxicology. In the present investigation, we utilized network toxicology and molecular docking methodologies to explore the toxic mechanisms by which PET-MPs may induce AS. Evaluations of datasets from GEO, ChEMBL, and SwissTargetPrediction led to the identification of 28 potential targets linked to PET-MPs exposure, which were narrowed down to seven key targets through machine learning techniques. Enrichment analyses involving Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways indicated that PET-MPs affect significant pathways related to inflammation and metabolism. Further, molecular docking validated robust binding affinities between PET-MPs and the identified core targets, implying their capacity to interfere with essential cellular functions. These results suggest that PET-MPs may facilitate the progression of AS by altering inflammatory responses and metabolic processes. This research offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms driving PET-MPs-related AS. It illustrates the effectiveness of network toxicology in evaluating the toxicity of novel environmental contaminants, thereby providing a theoretical framework for understanding the health implications of PET-MPs and informing strategies to alleviate their effects on cardiovascular health.Keywords: PET-MPs, Atherosclerosis, Network toxicology, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Microplastics (MPs), characterized as plastic fragments measuring less than 5 mm in diameter, have surfaced as considerable environmental contaminants that pose global risks to human health. Within the extensive spectrum of plastics, polyethylene terephthalate microplastics (PET-MPs) have gained particular attention due to their prevalent application and durability in the environment1–3. Recent investigations reveal that MPs are commonly detected across many ecosystems, encompassing terrestrial soils, freshwater bodies, and marine settings, thereby underscoring their extensive dissemination and intricate interactions with non-living and living components4–8.

The pathways through which MPs enter the human body vary, with the primary modes being consuming contaminated food or inhaling polluted air9. Following ingestion or inhalation, these particles can accumulate in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidneys, intestines, and blood vessels, disrupting metabolic functions and potentially resulting in chronic health issues10–12. Recent research increasingly highlights that the harmful effects of MPs are associated with mechanisms such as oxidative stress, the activation of inflammatory pathways, and the disruption of normal cellular activities13,14. Furthermore, evidence suggests that more minor MPs may pose an even greater threat, eliciting significant biological responses, including oxidative damage and increased inflammatory reactions15,16. Notably, the consequences of MPs exposure are not limited to localized areas; they also carry systemic health risks, which may include metabolic disorders and possible links to cardiovascular diseases17–19.

Atherosclerosis (AS) represents a multifaceted pathophysiological disorder marked by the deposition of lipids and inflammatory cells within the arterial walls, which can precipitate cardiovascular incidents20. Numerous studies have identified a range of risk factors associated with AS, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and persistent inflammation21. Moreover, the burgeoning discipline of environmental toxicology has underscored the impact of pollutants, such as MPs, on cardiovascular health22,23. There is a plausible association between MPs and cardiovascular disorders, with findings suggesting that MPs can elicit inflammatory responses that may lead to vascular damage24,25. Research indicates that the concentration of PET-MPs within arterial structures is markedly greater than that found in arteries devoid of plaques, implying a potential correlation with the pathogenesis of AS26,27. Nonetheless, the specific mechanisms through which PET-MPs contribute to the progression of AS remain inadequately elucidated, highlighting the need for further exploration into the effects of these environmental contaminants on AS.

This investigation aims to comprehensively assess the potential toxicological effects and the molecular pathways implicated in the interaction between PET-MPs and AS, employing sophisticated techniques in network toxicology and molecular docking. Through the amalgamation of computational and biological methodologies, this study aspires to clarify the mechanisms by which PET-MPs may influence vascular dysfunction and disease advancement. Ultimately, the insights garnered from this research could enhance our understanding of the involvement of microplastics in atherosclerosis, thereby underscoring the necessity for effective public health initiatives to reduce the hazards linked to environmental plastic contamination.

Methods

Collection of PET targets

PET’s molecular structure and SMILES notation were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The identification of potential targets for PET was conducted by leveraging the chEMBL (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) and SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) databases, specifically focusing on “Homo sapiens” to ensure biological pertinence. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the results, the predictions were cross-validated and standardized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Targets retrieved from multiple databases were integrated, deduplicated, and evaluated for structural consistency, establishing a comprehensive target library for PET.

Collection of AS targets

We acquired the expression data and the platform details for the GSE100927 dataset, which encompasses tissue samples from 69 patients diagnosed with AS and 35 healthy control individuals28. Log2 transformation was performed to normalize the expression data. When multiple probes corresponded to a single gene, the average of these values was calculated to represent the gene expression levels. Missing values were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) method; genes with missing rates above 20% were excluded, thereby generating the final expression matrix for subsequent analysis. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to identify potential AS-associated targets. A co-expression network was constructed utilizing the WGCNA package in R version 4.0.1, focusing on the top 5000 genes that exhibited the highest median absolute deviation (MAD) between patients with AS and healthy control subjects29. Genes exhibiting identical absolute correlation in expression levels were determined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients using the pickSoftThreshold function. The optimal soft threshold power, denoted as β, was identified to construct a scale-free network. Gene modules were derived through the dynamic branch-cutting technique. The minimum module size was set to 30, and dynamic tree cutting was applied to identify modules. The relationship between module eigengenes and clinical characteristics was assessed by Pearson correlation and represented visually using a heatmap. Moreover, the associations between modules and traits were confirmed by evaluating the correlation between gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM). Genes within the module that exhibited the most significant association with disease status were classified as targets for AS.

Cross-analysis to identify PET-related targets

The genes most closely linked to AS underwent a comparative analysis with the PET target library using Venny 2.1.0 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) to identify genes that demonstrate overlap. Such overlapping genes could potentially represent viable targets for AS triggered by PET.

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis

The PET-related AS targets were submitted to the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/), ensuring that the species filter was set to “Homo sapiens”. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) to explore the biological functions associated with the potential targets related to PET-induced AS30–32. The GO analysis was structured around three fundamental categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Conversely, the KEGG analysis was designed to identify toxicological pathways relevant to these targets. The results were visually represented through bubble charts created using the R package “ggplot2”. Significance thresholds were set at adjusted p < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg method for multiple testing correction.

Identification of core targets through machine learning techniques

Two distinct machine learning methodologies were utilized to pinpoint essential targets: the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE). The LASSO algorithm was implemented using the R package “glmnet”, with 10-fold cross-validation to select lambda.min33. In contrast, the SVM-RFE algorithm was carried out via the R package “e1071” with a linear kernel, a step size of 1, and 10-fold cross-validation34. A random seed of 12,345 was designated for the execution of these algorithms. After applying these techniques, an analysis was conducted to evaluate the overlap of targets identified by each algorithm, and the genes found in this intersection were classified as potential core targets associated with PET-induced AS.

Assessment of immune cell infiltration

To evaluate the extent of immune cell infiltration, we leveraged gene expression matrix data, which was uploaded to CIBERSORT (https://cibersort.stanford.edu/), thereby enabling the creation of an immune cell infiltration matrix. Following this, we utilized the “ggplot2” package to produce violin plots that effectively depict the fluctuations in immune cell infiltration levels. To explore the association between each core target and the 22 distinct immune cell types, we performed Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Furthermore, correlation heatmaps were constructed to represent the interrelationships among the infiltrating immune cells and the connections between the core targets and the infiltrating immune cells, utilizing the “corrplot” package in R.

Diagnose significance of core targets

Initially, box plots were employed to analyze the expression levels of the potential core targets between AS patients and controls. Subsequently, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed utilizing the R package “pROC” to demonstrate the diagnostic capabilities of these core targets35. Area under the curve (AUC) values and 95% confidence intervals were estimated with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Molecular Docking of PET with core targets

Molecular docking is crucial for predicting the interaction sites between PET and core target proteins. Given that PET is a polymer, docking it directly with proteins is computationally challenging due to its large size and complex structure. Instead, to simplify the system and make the docking feasible, the terephthalate-based monomer from PubChem database (PubChem ID: 18721140) was chosen as the ligand for PET-related docking. This monomer is a core structural component of PET, making it a biologically relevant molecule for interaction studies with proteins involved in PET degradation or recycling. After selecting the ligand, we downloaded the SDF format file from the database and performed energy minimization to optimize its conformation. This step ensures that the ligand reaches its most stable structure before docking. The optimized structure was then saved in PDB format for further processing. The ligand underwent energy minimization to reduce its energy and achieve a stable state, ensuring a realistic docking simulation. This was done using Discovery Studio (DS) software. The optimized ligand structure was then saved in PDB format for use in the docking procedure. At the same time, the best structures of core target proteins were retrieved from UniProt database and SWISS-MODLE website (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). Using PYMOL software, unnecessary ligands and water molecules were removed from the protein structure to avoid interference during the docking process. The final protein structure was saved in PDB format for further use. The protein was then prepared by adding hydrogen atoms and assigning partial charges using the AutoDockTools plugin in PYMOL. This ensured that the protein was ready to act as the receptor in the docking process. The processed protein structure was saved in PDBQT format for compatibility with AutoDock Vina. Similarly, the ligand was prepared by adding hydrogen atoms and assigning partial charges using AutoDockTools. The torsional degrees of freedom of the ligand were evaluated and adjusted to account for flexible bond rotations. The ligand was saved in PDBQT format for docking.

Since the exact binding pocket was unknown, we performed blind docking, setting the grid to cover the entire protein structure. This approach allows the docking software to explore all potential binding sites across the protein surface. The grid was designed to encompass the whole protein to ensure no potential binding sites were overlooked. The docking procedure was carried out using AutoDock Vina 1.5.6, which is well-established for protein-ligand docking studies. We performed 50 independent docking runs for each ligand to ensure reliable results. Performing more than 50 docking runs is considered standard practice, as it increases the accuracy and robustness of the results by thoroughly exploring the potential binding conformations.

After docking, we analyzed the resulting poses based on binding affinity and conformational frequency. The most favorable conformation, which showed the highest binding affinity and the most frequent occurrence across all docking runs, was selected as the final result. This conformation was visualized using DS 2019 and PYMOL 3.1 to generate both 3D structural representations and surface models.

Statistical analysis

The two groups were compared using Student’s t-test, and p < 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were carried out in R4.1.0.

Results

Identification of targets for PET-induced AS

This study initially identified 147 potential targets associated with PET via the ChEMBL database and the SwissTargetPrediction tool. These targets signify possible molecular interactions between PET and human proteins. Transcriptomic data from human AS samples were sourced from the GEO database. This dataset encompasses gene expression profiles from both normal and AS samples, thus providing a basis for identifying AS-related targets. The WGCNA methodology was employed to construct a co-expression network and to identify the modules most pertinent to AS. Genes residing in the most relevant module were designated as AS targets. To adhere to the criteria for a scale-free network, a power value of β = 12 was selected, ensuring that the scale independence reached 0.85 along with elevated average connectivity (Fig. 1A). The dynamic branch-cutting approach facilitated the identification of nine gene modules exhibiting analogous expression characteristics. Through the analysis of module-trait relationships (Fig. 1B), it was discerned that the red module (r = 0.71, p < 0.001) exhibited the strongest association with disease status (AS). The significant correlations between the red MM and GS for AS are depicted in Fig. 1C. A cumulative 3,155 genes within the red module were recognized as AS targets. Ultimately, the 147 PET-associated targets were cross-referenced with the 3,155 AS targets utilizing the Venny tool to pinpoint the potential molecular connections between PET exposure and AS. This analytical approach uncovered 28 overlapping genes (Fig. 1D), which may serve as prospective targets for PET-MPs induced AS.

Functional enrichment analysis of potential targets

Twenty-eight potential targets associated with PET-induced AS were analyzed for GO and KEGG enrichment. The findings from the GO analysis, illustrated in Fig. 2, revealed that the potential target genes are predominantly enriched in various biological processes, including mast cell degranulation, the positive regulation of interleukin-2 production, T cell receptor signaling pathways, protein phosphorylation, neutrophil chemotaxis, B cell receptor signaling pathways, and NAD+-protein mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase activity. The KEGG enrichment analysis also demonstrated that these potential target genes were significantly associated with pathways pertinent to the CAMP signaling pathway and metabolic pathways. These enrichment findings imply a potential close relationship between immunity, inflammation, and metabolism concerning AS, thereby offering critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning PET-MPs-induced AS.

Core target selection using machine learning

Two machine learning methods were employed to identify core targets. The LASSO algorithm pinpointed 11 core targets (Fig. 3A and B). Conversely, the SVM-RFE algorithm identified 13 core targets (Fig. 3C and D). By utilizing a Venn diagram to analyze the overlap between the findings of these two machine learning approaches, a total of seven definitive core targets associated with PET-MPs-induced AS was established: Tryptophan 2,3-Dioxygenase (TDO2), Aldo-Keto Reductase Family 1 Member B (AKR1B1), Phosphatidylinositol-4,5-Bisphosphate 3-Kinase Catalytic Subunit Delta (PIK3CD), Poly(ADP-Ribose) Polymerase Family Member 12 (PARP12), RAD51 Recombinase (RAD51), Phosphodiesterase 4D (PDE4D), and MAPK Interacting Serine/Threonine Kinase 1 (MKNK1) (Fig. 3E).

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

The correlation heatmap illustrated regulating the expression profiles of various immune cell types (Fig. 4A). Our analysis indicated that naïve B cells correlated negatively with AS. In contrast, activated mast cells showed a positive association with AS. Furthermore, we assessed the relationship between the seven core targets and immune cell populations (Fig. 4B). The seven core targets demonstrated significant correlations with several immune cell types, including M0 macrophages, resting memory CD4 T cells, neutrophils, gamma delta T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). This underscores the crucial role of immune response in the pathogenesis of PET-MPs-induced AS.

Diagnostic ability of core targets

Subsequent analysis of the core targets was conducted to evaluate their diagnostic efficacy for AS. The results presented in the box plot indicated that, in comparison to the control group, there was a notable upregulation of these seven genes within the AS cohort (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the ROC curves demonstrated that these genes possess a remarkable capability for diagnosing AS (Fig. 5B). These findings imply a significant association between PET-MPs-induced AS and these core targets.

Diagnostic value of core targets for AS. (A) Expression levels of core targets in different groups. (B) ROC curve analysis of core targets. AS atherosclerosis, ROC receiver operating characteristic curve.

Molecular docking of PET and core targets for AS

Molecular docking technology was employed to explore the interactions between PET and seven core genes, namely TDO2, AKR1B1, PIK3CD, PARP12, RAD51, PDE4D, and MKNK1. The findings revealed that the docking binding energies for PET were as follows: −6.3 kcal/mol for TDO2, − 8.0 kcal/mol for AKR1B1, − 6.9 kcal/mol for both PIK3CD and PARP12, − 7.1 kcal/mol for RAD51, − 6.2 kcal/mol for PDE4D, and − 5.4 kcal/mol for MKNK1. Notably, all binding energies were below − 5 kcal/mol, which indicates the capability of PET to spontaneously associate with each core target, thereby establishing robust interactions. This observation underscores the significant role of PET in the induction of AS (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a network toxicology approach to systematically identify molecular targets that may be linked to PET-MPs exposure and AS. By integrating data from multiple databases (GEO, chEMBL, and SwissTargetPrediction) and applying WGCNA and machine learning methods, we identified seven key targets—TDO2, AKR1B1, PIK3CD, PARP12, RAD51, PDE4D, and MKNK1—that play central roles in the molecular mechanisms underlying PET-MPs-induced AS. Through molecular docking, we further validated the binding affinity of these targets, improving the predictive accuracy of the toxicological assessment.

TDO2, which plays a central role in the kynurenine metabolic pathway, has been implicated in various inflammatory diseases and immune responses. Elevated TDO2 expression is correlated with increased immune cell infiltration, particularly dendritic cells, and immune checkpoint activation36.

Previous studies have also demonstrated its association with liver cancer37 and triple-negative breast cancer38, where it promotes immune evasion. Additionally, TDO2 is pivotal in metabolic disorders, especially nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and associated conditions, potentially serving as a crucial mediator for the intercommunication between hepatocytes and macrophages in regulating liver inflammation39. Our results suggest that TDO2 may be involved in regulating immune responses in PET-MPs-induced AS, facilitating chronic inflammation, and exacerbating vascular injury.

AKR1B1 is a key enzyme in the polyol pathway, which converts glucose into sorbitol, a process linked to oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction. Given its role in promoting endothelial dysfunction, AKR1B1 dysregulation is associated with diabetic complications, a well-known risk factor for AS40,41. The findings from this study suggest that AKR1B1 might mediate the vascular damage observed in PET-MPs-induced AS by contributing to oxidative stress and inflammation.

PIK3CD, the catalytic subunit of phosphoinositide 3-kinase, is crucial for regulating immune cell function. In individuals with autoimmune disorders linked to PIK3CD, the impaired regulation of immune cells may result in vascular inflammation, AS, and other cardiovascular complications, thereby exacerbating the overall disease burden in these patients42. The involvement of PIK3CD in regulating CD8+ T-cell functionality highlights its significance in the context of cardiovascular diseases. Mutations in PIK3CD that disrupt T cell regulation can lead to inappropriate immune activation, which may foster tissue inflammation and potentially accelerate the progression of AS43,44. Our findings highlight the potential role of PIK3CD in modulating immune responses in PET-MPs-induced AS, supporting previous research that links immune activation to atherosclerotic plaque formation.

PARP12 is involved in regulating DNA repair, inflammation, and cellular stress responses. It plays a critical role in the NF-κB signaling pathway, which is central to the inflammatory processes driving AS45. In addition to its involvement in inflammation, PARP12 has been recognized as a key player in the modulation of membrane trafficking and the functioning of the Golgi apparatus under stress conditions46. Furthermore, PARP12 affects mitochondrial function, an essential aspect of cardiovascular health47. Recent discoveries of PARP12 inhibitors further highlight its potential as a promising therapeutic target, especially in diseases marked by inflammatory responses and oxidative stress pathways48. Our study suggests that PARP12 may modulate inflammatory responses triggered by PET-MPs and contribute to endothelial dysfunction in AS.

RAD51 is a key player in DNA double-strand break repair through homologous recombination. Aberrant expression of RAD51 has been associated with cancer progression and vascular diseases49. Increased RAD51 expression is frequently detected in numerous tumors and has been related to resistance to chemotherapy50. Our study suggests that RAD51 may contribute to genomic instability and endothelial injury in PET-MPs-induced AS, aligning with previous findings that point to its role in vascular inflammation and cardiovascular diseases.

PDE4D regulates the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway, affecting vascular tone and cardiac contractility. It plays a significant role in the development of hypertension through pathways induced by angiotensin II51. It modulates cardiac contractility by regulating calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, functioning independently of L-type calcium currents52. PDE4D deficiency has been correlated with the development of cardiomyopathy and heart failure53. Furthermore, specific genetic polymorphisms within the PDE4D gene have been associated with an elevated risk of coronary heart disease and ischemic strokes54. Our findings indicate that PDE4D may be involved in regulating vascular function in PET-MPs-induced AS, further supporting its potential as a therapeutic target for cardiovascular diseases.

MKNK1, a key regulator in the MAPK signaling pathway, is involved in cellular stress responses, including the regulation of lipid metabolism55. Additionally, the association between MKNK1 and the behavior of neutrophils during sepsis highlights its regulatory function in the inflammatory response56. Our results suggest that MKNK1 may contribute to metabolic dysregulation in PET-MPs-induced AS, potentially exacerbating the development of AS by influencing immune cell behavior and lipid homeostasis.

The GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that immune response, inflammation, and metabolic pathways are key contributors to the progression of AS induced by PET-MPs. Prior research has indicated that immune cells, including B lymphocytes and macrophages, are often located near atherosclerotic plaques in both human and murine models57–59. The buildup of these substances initiates an inflammatory reaction characterized by heightened levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules. These factors play a crucial role in the instability and advancement of plaque60–62.

Furthermore, dendritic cells (DCs) serve as critical antigen-presenting cells that connect innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby facilitating T-cell activation that can either exacerbate inflammation or foster tolerance63,64. From a metabolic perspective, the interaction between lipid metabolism and immune cell activity is essential in modulating inflammatory responses. Dyslipidemia, especially hyperlipidemia, is linked to alterations in immune cell metabolism, contributing to a pro-inflammatory phenotype among macrophages and T cells65. Research indicates that metabolic reprogramming within these immune cells can promote trained immunity, amplifying their reactions to subsequent inflammatory challenges66. The accumulation of lipids supports plaque development and affects the functional capabilities of various immune cells, further sustaining the chronic inflammatory condition typical of AS67. These findings imply that PET-MPs may contribute to the progression of AS via inflammatory and metabolic pathways.

Our findings align with recent investigations into the toxicological impacts of PET-MPs. Prior research has established that PET-MPs can interfere with pancreatic physiological functions, modify metabolite concentrations, and influence blood lipid and calcium levels, subsequently impacting lipid metabolism. These alterations are linked to increased insulin resistance and a heightened susceptibility to pancreatitis68. Additionally, PET-MPs may affect critical signaling pathways, including Ras signaling, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses, potentially hastening conditions like intervertebral disc degeneration69. These observations indicate that PET-MPs could disrupt both inflammatory responses and metabolic mechanisms, which are vital contributors to the progression of AS.

The mechanistic insights identified in this study are supported by emerging pre-clinical and clinical evidence linking PET-MPs to the pathogenesis of AS. Pre-clinical studies in animal models have demonstrated that chronic exposure to micro- and nanoplastics induces oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, lipid metabolism disorders, and macrophage activation—processes well recognized to promote AS progression70,71. Consistent with these mechanisms, animal experiments have also shown that microplastic exposure leads to cardiac abnormalities such as altered heart rate, pericardial edema, myocardial fibrosis, and impaired cardiac function, while its toxic effects in microvascular sites include hemolysis, thrombosis, coagulation abnormalities, and vascular endothelial injury72. Clinically, microplastics including PET have been detected in human blood and atherosclerotic plaques, and their presence has been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events27. Patients with acute coronary syndrome further exhibited higher microplastic accumulation, which correlated with greater atherosclerotic burden and vascular complexity, underscoring the potential of PET-MPs to promote vascular injury and inflammation23.

Taken together, our in silico findings complement these experimental and clinical observations by elucidating the molecular basis through which PET-MPs may accelerate AS. These multi-level consistencies across computational, experimental, and clinical studies provide a coherent framework suggesting that PET-MPs may contribute to cardiovascular risk through convergent molecular and cellular mechanisms. Nevertheless, further in vivo validation is warranted to confirm the causal links and dose-dependent effects of PET-MPs on vascular pathology.

The present investigation presents several notable advantages. Firstly, by adopting a network toxicology framework to amalgamate data from various databases, this research efficiently and comprehensively delineated the intricate molecular interactions between exposure to PET-MPs and AS. This approach significantly improves the efficiency of toxicity screening, effectively addressing the drawbacks of conventional toxicology studies, which often depend on animal models, as well as pathological and immunological experiments—methods that are typically associated with prolonged experimental durations, elevated costs, and limited capacity to evaluate the burgeoning array of environmental pollutants. Secondly, by applying WGCNA alongside machine learning methodologies, this study systematically identified seven principal targets linked to PET-MPs exposure and AS, offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms that underlie PET-MPs-induced AS. This advancement mitigates the limitations of traditional approaches, which generally assess toxicity at the organismal or cellular levels and often fail to elucidate intricate molecular disturbances and overarching regulatory networks. Additionally, by employing molecular docking, this research confirmed the binding affinity and stability of the identified targets, thereby substantially enhancing the predictive accuracy of toxicological evaluations. This work lays a theoretical groundwork for comprehending these targets’ pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of AS.

While this investigation has yielded significant results, it is not without its limitations. Initially, our analysis predominantly relies on publicly accessible databases and computational simulations. Future studies should aim to validate the functionality of these targets through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, the impacts of exposure dosage, duration, and individual variability of PET-MPs on AS require additional exploration. Moreover, it is crucial to acknowledge that microplastics in the environment typically exist as complex mixtures of various components; therefore, subsequent research should consider the synergistic effects of different microplastics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research systematically pinpointed seven core targets associated with exposure to PET-MPs and AS through the integration of network toxicology, machine learning, and molecular docking methodologies. The study illuminated the role of PET-MPs in the development of AS. These results offer a novel viewpoint for comprehending the toxicological mechanisms underlying PET-MPs exposure and highlight potential targets for the prevention and therapeutic intervention of AS.
