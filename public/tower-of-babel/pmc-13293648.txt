False positive serum vascular endothelial growth factor-D in a patient with Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome
Authors: Prachi Saluja, Sarah K. Shore, Charles M. Lombard, Stephen J. Ruoss, Nishant Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffuse cystic lung diseases (DCLDs) are a heterogeneous group of disorders requiring careful evaluation due to distinct management and prognostic implications. A 32-year-old woman presented with dyspnea and chest pressure following a coughing episode and was found to have a right-sided pneumothorax. CT chest showed bilateral, small, round-elliptiform, basal-predominant cysts. Workup, including autoimmune serologies and abdominal imaging, was unrevealing. Serum vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-D was elevated at 839pg/ml, supporting a clinical diagnosis of lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM). However, critical re-evaluation of cyst morphology was more suggestive of Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome (BHD). Genetic testing revealed a pathogenic folluculin gene mutation, thus confirming the diagnosis of BHD. Surgical lung biopsy, obtained afterwards at the time of elective pleurodesis, did not reveal features of LAM. This case represents a rare instance of falsely elevated VEGF-D in patient with non-LAM DCLD and highlights the importance of critical review of chest radiology in the diagnostic evaluation of patients with cystic lung disease.

Introduction

Diffuse cystic lung diseases (DCLDs) encompass a group of pathophysiologically distinct disorders characterized by well-defined, air-filled, hyperlucent areas within the pulmonary parenchyma that interface with normal lung tissue [1]. High-resolution chest tomography (HRCT) plays a key role in the identification of cysts and revealing cyst distribution and morphological patterns that aid in definitive diagnosis. Accurate identification is crucial, as management strategies and prognosis differ substantially among the individual DCLDs.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old non-smoking woman presented with acute onset dyspnea and chest pressure following a coughing spell. Her past medical history and physical exam were otherwise unremarkable. Her only outpatient medication was a high-dose estrogen-containing oral contraceptive, which had been discontinued two months prior to presentation. Chest radiograph revealed a right-sided spontaneous pneumothorax, which was managed with a small-bore chest tube. Due to a prolonged air leak, a CT chest was obtained, revealing multiple, bilateral, small, round-elliptiform cysts with slight basal predominance (Fig. 1). She did not have any other features suggestive of underlying autoimmune disorders or Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) and her family history was unremarkable. No renal tumors or lymphadenopathy were noted on abdominal imaging. Given the clinical presentation of a premenopausal woman with spontaneous pneumothorax, bilateral cystic lung disease on chest CT, and elevated serum VEGF-D >800pg/ml, a clinical diagnosis of lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) was established in accordance with the LAM Clinical Practice Guidelines [2]. However, a critical re-evaluation of her chest CT findings suggested that the cyst features were more consistent with Birt-Hogg-Dubé (BHD) syndrome rather than LAM. This prompted genetic testing that revealed a pathogenic folliculin (FLCN) gene mutation (pF157del), thus confirming the diagnosis of BHD. To reduce the risk of future pneumothoraces and to exclude the possibility of two simultaneous disease processes, she underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) guided pleurodesis and lung biopsy, which did not reveal features of LAM and supported the diagnosis of BHD (Fig. 1).

Discussion

LAM is a rare, female-predominant, low-grade neoplasm caused by mutations in the TSC genes that causes progressive cystic lung destruction driven by hyperactivated mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling [3]. Serum VEGF-D ≥800pg/ml has been reported to be 100% specific in being able to distinguish LAM from other DCLDs and is recommended as a non-invasive confirmatory test prior to pursuing invasive options such as lung biopsy [3,4]. In the United States (US), serum VEGF-D quantification in a CAP/CLIA-accredited manner is performed exclusively at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center. To our knowledge, this is the first reported instance of false positive serum VEGF-D >800pg/ml in a woman with non-LAM DCLD in the US. A similar case of false positive VEGF-D greater 800pg/ml (860pg/ml) in patient with BHD has been previously reported in France [5].

BHD is an autosomal dominant disorder driven by mutations in the FLCN gene, a tumor suppressor gene, that predisposes patients to the development of renal tumors, hair follicle tumors and pulmonary cysts [6]. Although the mechanism of pulmonary cyst formation in BHD is not completely understood, a potential role of mTOR signaling in BHD pathogenesis has been postulated [7]. The phenotypic similarities between BHD and TSC – skin lesions, renal tumors, pulmonary cysts, further suggest the potential for mechanistic overlap between these two disorders. VEGF-D production in LAM appears to be driven by activated mTOR signaling – serum VEGF-D levels decrease in response to treatment with mTOR inhibitors and rise again after the therapy is withheld [8]. Thus, it is possible that elevated VEGF-D in this case might be driven by activated mTOR signaling. Our case and its notable finding of elevated serum VEGF-D highlight the need for further investigation of the role of mTOR signaling in the pathogenesis of BHD.

Although LAM and BHD share the radiologic phenotype of diffuse pulmonary cysts, there are some key differences between the two diseases (Table 1). Critical review of chest CT findings focusing on cyst morphology, profusion, and distribution can provide helpful clues to reliably distinguish BHD and LAM [9]. Ascertaining the correct diagnosis between BHD and LAM has significant prognostic and management implications. LAM is a progressive disease that can benefit from treatment with mTOR inhibitors. In contrast, lung disease in BHD is typically mild, doesn't seem to progress appreciably over time, and does not have known pharmacological treatment [6]. Conclusion

In our case, establishing the correct diagnosis of BHD led to a more reassuring prognostication for the patient, enabled her to make the decision to pursue pregnancy, and obviated the need for long-term treatment with sirolimus. This case cautions clinicians on relying solely on a single diagnostic test and underscores the importance of critical review of the radiological features when evaluating patients with DCLD.
