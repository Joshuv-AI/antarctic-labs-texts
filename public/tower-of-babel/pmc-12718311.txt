Reply: Matters Arising: The importance of understanding AI’s impact on physician behavior
Authors: Katie Ryan, Hyun-Joon Yang, Bohye Kim, Jane Paik Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe respond to the Matters Arising article by Bernstein et al. 1 in response to our recent article “Assessing the impact of AI on physician decision-making for mental health treatment in primary care”. We appreciate the interest shown by Bernstein et al. 1 in our recent article “Assessing the impact of AI on physician decision-making for mental health treatment in primary care”2. Their response highlights the significance of understanding the influence of AI—both unintended and underreported, as we found—on physician decision making, and a few notes regarding aspects of our study’s methodology. We welcome the opportunity to provide additional context to address their comments.

Notably, we would like to clarify our methodology relating to the calculation of the change in decision outcome. Although the primary outcome question (“[The patient] would be a good candidate for a prescription for a [SSRI]”) was a 5-point Likert scale item, this variable was ultimately dichotomized, such that physicians who agreed with the primary outcome statement (i.e. responded with 4 or above) were assigned 1, and physicians who did not agree (i.e. responded with 3 or below) were assigned 0. Therefore, when calculating the change in decision outcome, only those who did not agree that the patient was a good candidate (1, 2, 3 on the Likert scale) and then chose to prescribe or refer, or vice versa (i.e. those who agreed that the patient was a good candidate (4 or 5 on the Likert scale) and then chose not to prescribe or refer), were counted as having changed their initial decision. In this way, the “change in decision” outcome only represents physicians who, in a real-world scenario, seemingly would have changed their behavior after the introduction of the AI result. While a dichotomous question could have been usedsince physicians ultimately make a binary treatment decision (i.e. to treat or not to treat), our selection of a Likert-scale choice came from the rationale that initial clinical impressions are rarely binary and could be measured along a continuum.

We agree with Bernstein et al.’s1 comment that the search for moderators is a crucial next step in understanding how physicians interact with AI in clinical scenarios. Our study specifically focused on the combined effect of two hypothetical vignettes2. However, as a result of Bernstein et al.’s1 comment, we ran an auxiliary analysis which accounted for vignette type and found that the change-in-decision trends of the individual vignettes were consistent with the trends reported in the combined results. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, for physicians assigned to the ADHD/referring vignette who received an AI result that did not align with their initial clinical assessment, the odds of a change-in-decision was 3 when the AI recommended a referral and 0.24 when the AI did not recommend a referral. This trend persisted in the SSRI/prescribing scenario, in which the odds of a physician changing their decision were 1.57 when the AI recommended prescribing, and 0.31 when the AI did not recommend prescribing.

These findings continue to indicate that physicians appear more likely to change their decision to align with an AI recommendation when the AI recommends a course of action (in this case, a prescription or a referral), compared to when the AI does not recommend a course of action. In scenarios where the AI does not recommend action, physicians appear to be more willing to rely on their own clinical judgment and proceed with a decision that is counter to the AI recommendation. In the above auxiliary analysis, we found that physicians assigned to the ADHD/referring vignette showed a higher tendency to take action (refer) than those assigned to the SSRI/prescribing vignette. We hypothesize that such a decision may reflect a lower level of risk when referring a patient. Bernstein et al.’s summary of the potential effects of physician fears relating to legal liability, “defensive medicine,” and medico-legal consequences in clinical decision-making with AI provides excellent context for understanding these findings.

Bernstein et al.1 also raise an important point regarding the AI output’s compatibility score. In one scenario, the patient’s placement in the 52nd percentile was described as “average,” accompanied by a recommendation against treatment. Although our analysis focused primarily on the AI’s treatment recommendation (treat vs. do not treat), Bernstein et al. rightly note that the elements of the output—the “average” descriptor and percentile score—could have been interpreted ambiguously and may have introduced response variability. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study, and recognize that using a lower percentile score might have reduced variability in decision changes. In this regard, Bernstein et al.’s call for further research exploring how AI outputs should be framed for physicians is appropriate. We also recommend incorporating structured prompts for counterfactual reasoning—such as asking physicians to consider questions such as “What would I conclude if the AI were unavailable?”—to create a feedback loop to more precisely isolate the influence of AI output, and to encourage reflective use of such tools in practice.

We appreciate Bernstein et al.’s1 constructive commentary. We would note that several of the points raised are design choices: we advocate for the use of experiments with hypothetical scenarios before implementing tools at the point-of-care, given the clinical risks and the uncertainty around decisions. Our experimental approach provides a foundation for understanding AI-assisted clinical decision-making.
