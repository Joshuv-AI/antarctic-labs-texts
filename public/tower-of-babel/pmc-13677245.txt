Authors: Zhaokang Lei, Xiajie Guo, Hanqing Chen, Huanhuan Zhang, Yaorong Wu, Zhiyuan Cui, Zhenyou Liang, Liangliang Han, Chenghui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study establishes spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradients beneath the gas–liquid interface by alternately arranging soft and hard acoustic boundaries, thereby stabilizing the aggregation of cavitation bubbles below the liquid surface. The evolution of bubble aggregation structures was recorded using a high-speed imaging system with high magnification and frame rate. The morphological characteristics of the structures and the motion properties of internal bubbles were analyzed through image processing methods, providing experimental evidence for gas-nucleus circulation. Bubbles with radii less than 10 μm originating near the liquid surface were found to be drawn into the cavitation structure by the primary Bjerknes force and structural attraction. Bubbles within the structure stabilized at a depth of λ/8 below the liquid surface under the combination of primary and secondary Bjerknes forces, arranging themselves into a catenary configuration. Large bubbles float upward, driven by the primary Bjerknes force, and then break up, completing the gas-nucleus output process. The deformation and breakup patterns of large bubbles align with turbulence-induced bubble fragmentation. Statistical results indicate that a high-turbulent-energy-dissipation region exists between the cavitation structure and the liquid surface. To explain the abnormal variation in the equivalent radius of daughter bubbles during binary breakup, the bubble splitting effect was incorporated into the bubble model, accounting for phase transition and heat transfer. Excessive heat loss during the expansion phase prevents effective accumulation of ultrasonic input energy, significantly reducing the maximum expanded radius of daughter bubbles and accelerating their disappearance. The dependence of this phenomenon on parameters such as ultrasonic pressure amplitude agrees with experimental observations. Furthermore, the positional stability of this structure demonstrates its potential for patterned ultrasonic cavitation shot peening.Keywords: Ultrasonic cavitation, Bubble breakup, Bubble dynamics, Acoustic boundary conditions

Introduction

Ultrasonic cavitation has been widely applied in fields of chemical engineering, manufacturing, and biomedicine [1], [2], [3], [4]. In practice, cavitation structures exhibit inhomogeneous spatial distribution [5], [6], limiting the advancement of related applications. For example, in additive manufacturing, post-processing using ultrasonic cavitation can effectively improve the quality of a part's external surface. Yet, cavitation clouds are unable to penetrate deeply into the part's inner structure, resulting in limited treatment of internal surfaces [7]. In transdermal drug delivery, ultrasonic cavitation bubbles can enhance skin permeability [8]; however, the inability to uniformly generate cavitation bubbles on the target surface limits the efficacy of transdermal drug delivery [9]. These limitations point to a common challenge: the necessity to stably and effectively control the spatial distribution of cavitation structures.

To achieve this regulatory objective, a thorough understanding of the mechanisms underlying the evolution of cavitation structures is required. In the experimental research, researchers employed high-speed cameras to observe cavitation phenomena visible to the naked eye, and the resulting structures were classified morphologically into distinct categories, including jellyfish, starfish, clusters, flares, smokers, and conical bubble structures [10]. Among these, the upper and lower layers of the jellyfish oscillate in antiphase and are inferred to arrange symmetrically with respect to a pressure node [10]. Acoustic measurements of jellyfish structures were further conducted using rugged miniature needle hydrophones. The results indicated that the acoustic field amplitude at the center of the structures was significantly lower than at other locations, validating the inference that the upper and lower layers of the structures are situated on opposite sides of pressure nodes [11]. These results also demonstrate that the acoustic field influences the morphology and motion of cavitation structures. High-speed X-ray phase-contrast imaging, which overcomes the effects of fluid-volume overlap, was employed to elucidate the evolutionary process by which acoustic pressure gradients shape the cavitation bubble aggregation region into a conical shape through high-frame-rate, high-resolution imaging [12]. Furthermore, the distribution of supersaturated cavitation clouds in a thin liquid layer switches according to the vibration mode of the transducer’s radiating surface, and the externally injected bubbly liquid can temporarily alter the distribution of cavitation clouds in the thin liquid layer [13], [14]. These two phenomena further demonstrate that both the acoustic field and the distribution of gas nuclei can influence the spatial distribution of cavitation structures.

In theoretical terms, various methods have been developed to analyze the mechanisms underlying the evolution of cavitation structures. Nonlinear Helmholtz equations derived from the Caflish model can be used to simulate complex acoustic fields in bubbly liquids [15]. Based on this, the prediction of conical cavitation structure morphology is achieved by calculating the streamlines of the primary Bjerknes force originating from the solid boundary, according to the crevice model of gas nuclei [16], [17]. The Euler-Lagrange method was used to track the motion of bubbles under the influence of five forces—the primary Bjerknes force, drag force, added-mass force, volume-difference force, and gravitational force—revealing the formation of a conical bubble-aggregation region consistent with experimental observations [18]. Furthermore, incorporating the Euler-Lagrange method into the nonlinear Helmholtz equation enables simulations with higher precision, successfully reproducing the phenomenon of bubbles migrating from the side walls of the ultrasonic horn to the bottom [19]. These theoretical studies provide a multifaceted understanding of how the primary acoustic field and the bubble nucleus influence the spatial distribution of cavitation structures. However, the Eckart acoustic streaming induced by acoustic attenuation and the microjets of cavitation bubbles result in the simultaneous presence of both macroscopic and microscopic turbulence in the cavitation field [20], [21], and the motion of cavitation bubbles may be affected by this turbulence. Microbubbles attached to the liquid surface may be ejected into the liquid and form small bubble clusters [22], thereby disrupting the original cavitation structure [23]. Differences in the interfacial properties between rigid walls and free liquid surfaces can significantly affect the dynamic behavior of nearby bubbles [24]. Although the aforementioned theoretical methods neglect these factors, some researchers have attempted to incorporate the effects of turbulence, magnetic fields, and heat transfer into models of cavitation bubbles in superheated liquids using rigorous mathematical derivations and have derived analytical formulas for approximate solutions to the bubble radius evolution [25], [26], [27], [28]. These efforts provide a reference for overcoming the aforementioned limitations.

Currently, research is underway to modulate the spatial distribution of cavitation structures using either acoustic fields or gas nuclei. Regarding regulation based on acoustic fields, longitudinal-flexural coupled vibration has been introduced into the acoustic black hole immersed sonoreactor, altering the acoustic field distribution, thereby generating multiple cavitation regions around the probe [29]. It has been observed that a concave-shaped acoustic resonator with a spherical cavity, driven by a piezoelectric ceramic disc actuator, can generate cavitation bubbles on the target surface [9]. However, this method relies on the theoretical design of transducer vibration modes, making it difficult to adjust the spatial distribution of the cavitation structures flexibly. Regarding regulations based on gas nuclei, syringe needles, capillary tubes, and submerged jets can serve as gas nucleus sources in the trailing region of the acoustic radiation surface, forming stable cavitation structures with complex patterns [30]. Although this method allows for rapid adjustments based on specific requirements, it relies on operational experience and is susceptible to external interference. The above studies focus on the control of a single factor, making it difficult to achieve flexible and highly reproducible spatial distribution control of cavitation structures in practical applications.

In response to the above limitations, we propose a combined method integrating the regulation of the primary acoustic field with the control of the gas nucleus source. Through the coupling of spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradients with segmented gas–liquid interfaces, this method achieves stable control over the spatial distribution of cavitation structures, resulting in a curved chain-like bubble structure with excellent reproducibility. Based on this, we employed high-speed imaging, image processing, and bubble dynamics modeling to investigate the evolution and stabilization mechanisms of the trapped bubble structures, with a particular focus on the circulation mechanisms of the internal gas nuclei and the deformation and breakup characteristics of the bubbles under the influence of localized turbulence. This work contributes to expanding the understanding of the self-organization of cavitation structures, and this regulation method serves as a foundation for achieving patterned ultrasonic cavitation shot peening.

Experimental setup

As shown in Fig. 1, the experimental setup for observing the curved cavitation structure consists of ultrasonic baths (UC-3360 L and UC-4360 L operated at 28 kHz and 40 kHz, respectively, Shenzhen Jiayuanda Technology Co., Ltd., China), transparent acrylic water tanks, a high-speed camera (i-SPEED 727, iX Cameras Ltd., UK), and a light source (DannyU U-100 s, Shanghai Zhaoji Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China). The base of the small acrylic tank features evenly spaced grooves, with recessed sections measuring 6.4 mm wide and protruding sections measuring 12.4 mm wide (covered by 1 mm thick glass plates). In the large acrylic water tank, the water depth was set to (2n + 1)λ/4, where λ is the wavelength of ultrasound in water and n is an integer; for the sake of experimental observation, these were set as 28.1 cm and 27.2 cm at 28 kHz and 40 kHz, respectively. The glass plates covering the protruding sections were fully submerged to create hard acoustic boundary conditions. In contrast, the water–air interfaces formed by the recessed sections provided soft acoustic boundary conditions. The evolution of cavitation structures was recorded using the high-speed camera. Unless otherwise specified, the results presented herein are from experiments conducted at the maximum ultrasound power of 360 W.

Results

Morphological characteristics and evolutionary behavior of the curved cavitation structures

When ultrasonic waves propagate vertically upward in liquids, a pressure antinode forms at a rigid acoustic boundary, while a pressure node occurs at a soft acoustic boundary. Therefore, this setup creates spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradients on the xy plane at z=h near the periodic boundaries, resulting in a curved water surface and cavitation bubble aggregation beneath the soft boundary, forming structures with excellent positional stability. From a frontal view, both ends of the cavitation structure connect to the bottoms of two adjacent glass plates. (Fig. 2(a)). However, a side view reveals the cavitation structure extends over a considerable distance, thereby reducing focusing challenges (Fig. 2(b)). Observations from the top view further confirm that the multilevel chain-like structure is effectively confined beneath the soft acoustic interface (Fig. 2(c)). These factors facilitate observation of the structure at high frame rates and high magnification using the 12X Zoom Core.

Overall morphological features of the curved cavitation structure. (a) Observation in the yz plane; (b) Observation in the xz plane; (c) Observation in the xy plane.

To balance clarity and brightness in the field of view, the structure in Fig. 2(a-b) and Fig. 2(c) was recorded at 10 kfps and 7.5 kfps, respectively. Then, beginning with the appearance of distinct cavitation bubbles, the experimental video was processed using the minimum projection algorithm at 10 ms intervals to analyze the evolution of the curved cavitation structure during its formation stage [31]. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the curved cavitation structure originates from a hemispherical bubble cluster adhering beneath the glass plate. The cluster subsequently extends along the liquid surface (Fig. 3(b)). Once the cluster reaches a certain length, traces of ascending bubbles are visible (Fig. 3(c)). The collapse of the attached section of the bubble chain results in gas nuclei or daughter bubbles streaming into the region below the soft boundary. Therefore, in the early stage of the extension process, the growing structure remains extremely close to the liquid surface (Fig. 3(d-f)). However, once the structure passes the lowest point, it gradually stabilizes and maintains a distance of approximately λ/8 from the water surface, and a localized gas nuclei zone can be observed (Fig. 3(g-o)). Driven by the primary Bjerknes force, these gas nuclei (daughter bubbles) move from the liquid surface toward the downward-curving structure, leaving a series of nearly parallel elongated trajectories in the images; meanwhile, some bubbles merged to exceed the resonance size break away from the bubble chain and rise toward the liquid surface; these rising bubbles eventually collapse at a distance of approximately λ/16 from the water surface, thereby generating more gas nuclei (daughter bubbles) within the narrow zone between the water surface and the curved bubble structure. Moreover, free-surface oscillations may disrupt the existing gas–liquid interface equilibrium, leading to the formation of more gas bubbles at the liquid surface [32]. Therefore, this localized narrow region, which exhibits a variety of bubble behavior patterns, may offer a new perspective for observing the evolution of cavitation bubbles.

Formation process of the curved cavitation structure.

Furthermore, before the complete formation of the curved structure, trajectories of large bubbles consistently tilted to the left (Fig. 3(c-j)) rather than ascending vertically or perpendicular to the structure. It was also noted that these gas nuclei are attracted to the structure and subsequently grow into cavitation bubbles. Consequently, a two-stage cyclic process occurs in this region: first, aggregated bubbles rise from the structure and subsequently collapse, releasing bubble nuclei; then, the bubble nuclei are drawn toward the structure, thereby replenishing the cavitation bubbles to maintain the structure’s stability.

The image processing method proposed by Kuroyama et al. [33] was adapted to extract bubble size and position information within the curved structure. Images were captured at 20 kfps, as shown in Fig. 4(a1-a5). The image background was extracted from the original image by applying a median filter (Fig. 4(a2)). The image background formed by defocused cavitation structures is mainly exhibited as blotchy shadows. The background-removed image (Fig. 4(a3)) is then obtained by normalizing Fig. 4(a1) against Fig. 4(a2). Bubble edge characteristics are further enhanced through binarization. However, the high bubble density within the curved structure causes bubble overlap in Fig. 4(a4), potentially leading to overestimation of equivalent bubble radii. The bubble edges identified by the algorithm are shown in Fig. 4(a5) and can be used to output the bubble centroid positions.

Bubbles in curved cavitation structures. (a1–a5) Image processing methods; (b1–b2) Accurate description of cavitation structure morphology.

Nonlinear fitting of the bubble center coordinates indicates that the profile of curved structures corresponds to a catenary. The detailed procedure is described in Appendix A. As shown in Fig. 4(b1-b2), experimental results (red circles) in both 28 kHz and 40 kHz acoustic fields exhibit excellent fits (red solid lines). Previous studies indicate that liquid surfaces narrower than the capillary length would curve into a catenary shape due to surface tension [34]. The ultrasonic field generates acoustic pressure gradients near the liquid surface, transmitting the influence of surface tension to the curved cavitation structure. This surface shape affects the isobaric distribution of acoustic pressure, causing the cavitation structure to extend along the curved surface. Additionally, the secondary Bjerknes forces within the cavitation structure may induce effects similar to surface tension, influencing the profile shape [35]. Therefore, the morphology of the curved structure depends both on the surface curvature and on the secondary Bjerknes forces between bubbles. The locations where large bubbles collapse are also marked with blue dots in Fig. 4(b1-b2), and the nonlinear fitting results still form a catenary curve. Although the randomness of bubble breakup leads to significant errors between the spots and the fitted curve, this implies that bubble breakup may relate to the acoustic pressure amplitude and its gradient.

The dimensionless power P* is used to represent the power of ultrasonic baths across different experimental groups (P*=P/Pmax), with corresponding experimental results shown in Fig. 5. Under the same P* conditions, a relatively larger number of big bubbles formed in the curved structure, which remained stable at a distance of λ/8 for the water surface (Fig. 5(a, d)), where the primary Bjerknes force reached its maximum value. It is important to note that as frequency increases, wavelength decreases, and the gap between the structure and the surface narrows. This shortens the time it takes for bubble nuclei to return to the curved surface and reduces the circulation cycle, resulting in the formation of more and larger bubbles. Moreover, the rising trajectories of large bubbles in Fig. 5 exhibit a tendency tilted toward the side with higher bubble number density. This phenomenon indicates that the motion of the large bubbles may be modulated by the density of the cavitation bubbles.

Influence of acoustic field parameters on cavitation structure morphology.

Bubble density within the cavitation structure decreased significantly when P*=0.8 at 28 kHz, resulting in fewer large bubbles. Although cavitation structures still formed when P* was further reduced to 0.6, the decrease in acoustic pressure gradient caused some cavitation bubbles to detach from the structure. Furthermore, no significant curved cavitation structures were observed at lower P* values. Curved cavitation structures could no longer form when P*=0.6 at 40 kHz, indicating that an increase in frequency enhances the sensitivity of the cavitation structures to decreases in acoustic pressure gradients.

The width of the water–air interface influences the morphology of the curved structure. As shown in Fig. 6(a), when the soft interface width L is set to 2.5 mm, extensive cavitation structures exist throughout the region beneath the water–air interface, with the chain-like curved structure submerged within them. In this situation, the curved structure cannot be regarded as an independent cavitation structure; rather, it serves only as a localized feature. This experimental phenomenon indicates that an extremely narrow water–air interface cannot effectively modulate the acoustic field, with the grooved tank bottom approximating a uniform hard acoustic boundary. When L increases to 5 mm (Fig. 6(b)), the localized acoustic pressure gradient generated by the water–air interface becomes sufficiently strong to clear the surrounding cavitation structures from the curved structure. Although the shape of the curved structure is not yet fully apparent at this stage, it reveals that the minimum threshold (Lmin) for the width of the water–air interface is approximately 5 mm. When L increases to 10 mm (Fig. 6(c)), the curved structure depicted in Fig. 2(a) is formed. As L grows from 20 mm to 40 mm (Fig. 6(d-f)), the cavitation structure gradually elongates, and its profile shape progressively deviates from the curved form. Notably, at L=40mm, the central region of the cavitation structure becomes notably flattened. While L continues to increase, structures similar to those shown in Fig. 6(f) can still form, but these structures cannot remain stable. Consequently, an extension of the water–air interface leads to a significant reduction in the horizontal acoustic pressure gradient at its center; furthermore, the localized isobaric pressure distribution becomes nearly horizontal, resembling the shape of the free liquid surface, thereby inhibiting effective aggregation of cavitation bubbles. As a result, we obtain a controllable local field in which the aggregation, translation, fragmentation, and collapse of cavitation bubbles can be observed.

Dynamic characteristics of large bubbles

Due to pressure gradients, large bubbles deform and gradually break up as they rise. By increasing the high-speed camera frame rate to 40 kfps, one can see that the rising large bubbles exhibit chaotic surface deformation, encompassing most of the deformation and breakup characteristics observed in previous studies [36], [37], as shown in Fig. 7. Here, there are three deformation processes: side necking (Fig. 7(a)), middle necking (Fig. 7(b)), and higher-order mode deformation (Fig. 7(c)); and three breakup modes: binary breakup (Fig. 7(d)), multiple particle breakup (Fig. 7(e)), and successive breakup (Fig. 7(f)). These characteristics are observed in both the 28 kHz and 40 kHz ultrasonic cavitation fields. More examples can be found in Figs. S1 and S2 of the Supplementary Materials. Generally, middle necking leads to binary bubble breakup [37]. However, the breakup process in ultrasonic cavitation fields occurs extremely rapidly, making it impossible to capture continuous imaging of middle necking and binary breakup, even at 40 kfps. Therefore, this process is illustrated through two image series: Fig. 7(b) and (d). The bubble depicted in Fig. 7(f) exhibited multiple particle breakup events at both time points (f4 and f6), although the largest daughter bubble retained substantial volume. It ultimately fragmented completely into smaller bubbles in Fig. 7(f7). The resulting daughter bubbles typically retained a portion of the mother bubble's momentum, creating an upward streaming of microbubbles (Fig. 7(f7) and (f8)).

During binary breakup, the larger daughter bubble exhibits two opposite trends. To quantitatively analyze this phenomenon, moments of binary breakup and the resulting larger daughter bubble are marked with red arrows in Fig. 8. As seen in Fig. 8(a5–d5), the equivalent radius of the mother bubble is marked with blue circles, while those of the larger and smaller daughter bubbles are marked with red circles and triangles, respectively. It is shown that larger daughter bubbles may either grow (Fig. 8(a)) or gradually shrink until they disappear (Fig. 8(b)). This phenomenon was recorded at both 28 kHz (Fig. 8(a,b)) and 40 kHz (Fig. 8(c,d)). However, the rising distance of the daughter bubbles during this process is limited, preventing a simple attribution to variations in acoustic pressure amplitude. More in-depth theoretical analysis is indeed needed to explain this phenomenon.

Bubble breakup or collapse is a common phenomenon in nature; it releases energy and affects the velocity field in the surrounding liquid. The bubble diameters measured under non-ultrasonic conditions ranged from 3 to 4 mm [37], with a critical breakup diameter around 2 mm [38]. According to the theory of Martinez-Bazan et al., the small-diameter, extremely rapid bubble breakup indicates that the system exhibits a high turbulent energy dissipation rate [39]. The equivalent diameters of these broken bubbles presented in Fig. 7 range between 0.2 and 0.4 mm, and the turbulence energy dissipation rates were estimated using the method of Murai et al. and verified by the theory of Martinez-Bazan et al. [39], [40].

The additional model assumptions introduced to account for bubble breakup effects, along with their justifications, are as follows. First, this model only considers binary breakup events; second, for such a thermodynamic system in which no mass transfer occurs, the time required for the neck fracture during bubble breakup into two separated daughter bubbles is far shorter than tb, thus the influence of tb is neglected; third, the intensive quantities such as temperature T and bubble pressure pin remain consistent for both daughter bubbles during breakup; fourth, gas composition remains unchanged during breakup. Fifth, the two daughter bubbles undergo coupled oscillations. Sixth, since all large bubbles in the experiments burst after rising to a certain height, it is assumed that bubble breakup occurs at least after two collapse cycles. Nasibullaeva et al. [46] demonstrated that tiny bubbles with excessively small initial radii dissolve through rectified diffusion. The instantaneous radius of individual bubbles over 100 acoustic cycles was calculated, and the time at which the calculation terminated due to depleted gas within the bubble was recorded, as shown in Fig. 10. The results indicate that smaller initial radii lead to faster bubble dissipation; lower driven acoustic pressure amplitudes require larger critical initial radii to ensure bubble survival for 100 acoustic cycles. The abnormal trend in the upper-left corner of Fig. 10(a) is caused by a computational failure during bubble collapse under extreme conditions. Furthermore, the diameters corresponding to these critical initial radii (marked by the dashed line in Fig. 10) are smaller than the critical breakup diameters at corresponding frequencies shown in Fig. 9. Here, we considered that both factors together explain the disappearance of small bubbles observed in Fig. 7.

It is observed that, for the larger daughter bubble, the ratio of maximum radius during ΔT2 to that during ΔT1 is only 0.59 times (Fig. 11(a)), whereas this value reaches 1.12 in Fig. 11(c). The behavior of the two daughter bubbles might be influenced by the energy transformations W1, W2 and W3. Specifically, after the input energy W1 is consumed by W2 and W3, the remaining energy is converted into the bubble’s mechanical energy, thereby maintaining the bubble’s maximum oscillation radius or driving the bubble to expand to a larger maximum radius. For example, in Fig. 12(d), the remainder of the received ultrasound energy of the large daughter bubble is consistently greater than zero, enabling increased maximum expansion radius during ΔT2. However, in Fig. 11(b), the residual energy oscillates around zero, making it difficult to maintain the original maximum radius due to consumption by other mechanisms, such as secondary acoustic radiation. Therefore, when the parent bubbles break, the excessive consumption of ultrasonic energy by the daughter bubbles may be one of the mechanisms leading to the disappearance of the large daughter bubbles shown in Fig. 8.

Here, for Rmax2/Rmax1>1, the larger daughter bubble gradually grows larger over these two observation intervals, while for Rmax2/Rmax1<1, the larger daughter bubble oscillates over time and gradually shrinks.

Discussion

In previous studies of bubble breakup, the turbulent energy dissipation rate was typically small, generally on the order of 101 m2/s3 and 103 m2/s3 [37], [38], [39], [43]. However, the results in Fig. 9 suggest that the turbulent energy dissipation rate associated with the curved cavitation structure may be on the order of 104 m2/s3, which is higher than typical values reported in the literature. Robert et al. reported a turbulence energy dissipation rate of up to 40,767 m2/s3 in a two-dimensional thin liquid layer generated by colliding jets. Their theoretical analysis indicated that in this system, ε=P/ρV, where P is the kinetic energy dissipated per unit time by the jet collision, and V is the volume over which energy dissipation occurs [47]. Bubbles beneath the glass plate may accumulate acoustic energy and subsequently convert it into the kinetic energy of microjets during collapse. It is plausible that collisions between these jets and the glass plate dissipate this kinetic energy within this small volume. Thus, the high turbulent energy dissipation rate might originate from cavitation jets at the contact region between the curved cavitation structure and the glass plate.

The motion trajectories of small bubbles in Fig. 3(a) originate near the liquid surface, indicating that the surface provides a part of the gas nuclei. In studies on branched cavitation structures, large bubbles are less affected by turbulence and can rise to the surface despite irregular deformation [48]. Tang et al. [49] suggest that bubbles broken at the liquid surface during ultrasonic treatment can form daughter bubbles through outward or inward folding of the liquid film, thereby supplying these nuclei. Affected by high turbulence energy dissipation rates, large bubbles within curved cavitation structures break earlier and fail to reach the liquid surface, thus unable to supply nuclei through this mechanism. Faraday waves excited by ultrasound at the liquid surface can generate microdroplets [50]. These droplets may also draw in small bubbles as they fall back into the liquid [51]. Both phenomena persist under high turbulent energy dissipation rates, suggesting they may contribute to the nucleation of bubbles below the liquid surface depicted in Fig. 3(a). At high turbulence dissipation rates, large bubbles can produce microbubbles through multiparticle breakup. However, Tse et al. [52] found that ring waves generated by bubble coalescence can excite bubble instabilities, which can also produce microbubbles. Therefore, more detailed experimental studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which large bubbles generate dense microbubbles around them (as shown in Fig. 7(a3) and (e3)).

The spatial stability of curved cavitation structures offers potential for localized patterned ultrasonic cavitation processing. As shown in Fig. 14(a), a 6061 aluminum alloy plate with a thickness of 0.5 mm and a side length of approximately 7.5 cm was laid flat inside an acrylic water tank with an inner side length of 7.6 cm. The tank bottom consisted of a 1 mm-thick glass plate, which served to support the aluminum alloy plate. The depth of deionized water within the tank was approximately 1 cm. After being treated for a period of time, the sample was taken out, laid flat on the table, and photographed from a top view. As depicted in Fig. 14(b1–b4), without applying a spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradient, 28 kHz ultrasonic cavitation treatment produced only minor pitting marks on the aluminum alloy surface caused by hemispherical bubble clusters. After applying the spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradient, the curved structure is stably confined beneath the soft acoustic boundary, with its lower-end bubbles drawn toward the aluminum alloy surface, generating stable erosion. As shown in Fig. 14(c1–c4), with increasing ultrasonic treatment time, the pitting areas on the aluminum alloy surface gradually expanded, forming two distinct parallel striped regions. The spacing between these two pitted areas matched the spacing of the soft acoustic boundary. This experimental result demonstrates the potential of the curved structure for patterned cavitation processing. However, it must be acknowledged that achieving complex patterns via ultrasonic cavitation peening requires further investigation.

Additionally, benefiting from the stability of the curved cavitation structure, the experimental observation of small bubbles coalescing into large bubbles provides experimental evidence for the origin of large bubbles. As shown in Fig. 15(a), only a few small bubbles initially exist in the field of view. Subsequently, as the acoustic pressure intensified, the bubble count increased. Under the influence of secondary Bjerknes forces, the bubbles attracted each other and coalesced into small clusters (Fig. 15(c)). The formed clusters persisted for several acoustic cycles (Fig. 15(d–f)) before finally collapsing into large bubbles (Fig. 15(h–j)). A distinct bubble collapse can be observed in Fig. 15(f), followed by the emergence of a large bubble or an intermediate morphology transitioning from a bubble cluster to a large bubble in Fig. 15(g). Computational fluid dynamics simulations of multi-bubble systems indicate the outer bubbles generate jets directed toward the central bubble during cluster collapse [53], [54]. Under sufficiently small bubble spacing, these jets may break the liquid film between bubbles, connecting their gas phases. Consequently, the system's surface free energy decreases spontaneously, causing the liquid–gas interface to evolve toward a stable morphology [55], [56], thereby inducing the transformation of small clusters into large bubbles. Furthermore, Fig. 15 provides direct experimental evidence for the growth mechanism of bubble coalescence proposed by Ashokkumar et al. [57]. However, the precise evolutionary process between Fig. 15(f) and (g) still requires experimental observation with higher frame rates and resolution.

The evolution process of small bubbles coalescing into a large bubble.

Moreover, the width of the air–liquid interface above the curved structure exceeds the capillary length, suggesting that external forces may deform the liquid surface profile away from a catenary shape, as shown in Fig. 16(a and b). To quantify this deformation process, the liquid surface profile was extracted and normalized with respect to the image dimensions (Fig. 16(c)). The surface shape vectors were stacked into a matrix in time-sequential order to further characterize the evolution trend over 55 ms (Fig. 16(d)). Fig. 16(d) shows that the surface oscillation exhibits a periodicity of approximately 10 ms. The amplitude of acoustic pressure within the ultrasonic bath fluctuates over time, causing periodic deformation of the liquid surface under the influence of varying acoustic radiation forces. Studies indicate that low-frequency vertical vibrations may excite parametric instability oscillations in bubbles, manifested as symmetry breaking in bubble shape and associated horizontal motion [58]. As shown in the two dashed boxes in Fig. 16(c), the liquid surface profile observed in our experiments also exhibits weak symmetry-breaking features. It demonstrates that a curved liquid surface, as a quasi-two-dimensional case for bubbles, can also undergo parametric instability oscillations. Furthermore, the acoustic radiation force in our experiments may have just exceeded the threshold required to induce such parametric instability oscillations.

Conclusions

This study generates spatially periodic acoustic pressure gradients in the xy plane at z=h, thereby trapping cavitation bubbles and producing curved cavitation structures with strong positional stability. This stability supports high-frame-rate and magnification-rate observations via high-speed cameras. Experiments demonstrate that the width of the water–air interface influences the profile shape in curved cavitation structures. Image processing using the minimum-intensity projection algorithm provides intuitive experimental evidence for gas circulation within the cavitation structure. Tiny bubbles originating from the upper region serve as cavitation nuclei within the structure, thereby increasing the number of cavitation bubbles within the cavitation structure. In contrast, large bubbles formed by the coalescence of cavitation bubbles within the structure rise briefly before breakup, thereby reducing the number of cavitation bubbles, though the total number remains essentially constant. The distance between the curved cavitation structure and the liquid surface is approximately λ/8, resulting from a balance between the primary and secondary Bjerknes forces.

Furthermore, the curved cavitation distribution demonstrates potential for patterned ultrasonic cavitation peening of 6061 aluminum alloy. Long-term tracking of the curved cavitation structure indicates that it originates from a hemispherical cluster of small bubbles attached to the edge of the glass plate, which grows by attracting gas nuclei near the liquid surface. Under the coupled influence of liquid surface shape, acoustic field gradient, and secondary Bjerknes force, the profile of the curved cavitation structure is a catenary. The breakup locations of large bubbles can also be fit by catenaries, indicating a dependence of large-bubble breakup on acoustic pressure gradients. Tracking the evolution of large bubbles reveals that their deformation and breakup behavior conform to the mechanisms of bubble breakup under turbulence. By statistically analyzing the rise velocity, equivalent diameter variation, and breakup time of large bubbles, it is found that the curved cavitation structure creates a region with a high turbulence energy dissipation rate below the water–air interface. To investigate the disappearance of large bubbles after breakup, bubble fracturing mechanisms were incorporated into the bubble dynamics model, accounting for phase transition and heat transfer. Results indicate that at a driving acoustic pressure of 0.5 atm, bubbles with initial radii below 30 μm tend to dissolve. This critical initial radius decreases with increasing acoustic pressure, suggesting that small bubbles generated by large-bubble breakup may dissolve. When the energy input by ultrasonic waves to the bubble is mainly dissipated through thermal conduction and phase transition, the bubble cannot accumulate sufficient energy to maintain the maximum oscillation radius. Consequently, the daughter bubbles' radii decrease after bubble breakup, accelerating their disappearance. Numerical calculations indicate that this phenomenon is related to the acoustic pressure, equilibrium radius, and breakup radius, consistent with experimental observations.
