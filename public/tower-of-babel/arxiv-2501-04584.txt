A Direct-adjoint Approach for Material Point Model Calibration with Application to Plasticity
Ryan YAN, D. Thomas SEIDL, Reese E. JONES, Panayiotis PAPADOPOULOS

Abstract
This paper proposes a new approach for the calibration of material parameters in local elastoplastic constitutive models. The calibration is posed as a constrained optimization problem, where the constitutive model evolution equations for a single material point serve as constraints. The objective function quantifies the mismatch between the stress predicted by the model and corresponding experimental measurements. To improve calibration efficiency, a novel direct-adjoint approach is presented to compute the Hessian of the objective function, which enables the use of second-order optimization algorithms. Automatic differentiation is used for gradient and Hessian computations. Two numerical examples are employed to validate the Hessian matrices and to demonstrate that the Newton-Raphson algorithm consistently outperforms gradient-based algorithms such as L-BFGS-B.



Keywords:

adjoint method; automatic differentiation; model calibration; plasticity

1
Introduction

Constitutive models, also commonly known as material models, play an essential role in computational simulations of structural or mechanical systems. Every constitutive model has a number of free parameters that must be determined using experimental data from mechanical
characterization
experiments through a process known as
calibration

[
1
]
. Improvements in calibration lead to more accurate simulations and reliable predictions. While experimental methods for mechanical characterization have become quite advanced, robust calibration techniques are desperately lacking and represent an impediment in the use of sophisticated constitutive models.

Most calibration problems are posed through an optimization-based formulation,
where the goal is to minimize an objective function that measures the
discrepancy between simulation predictions and experimental measurements
by finding an optimal set of constitutive model parameters
[
2
]
. Various techniques
for performing this optimization exist and can be grouped by
the amount of derivative information they use in the optimization step.
Some studies resort to using gradient-free methods including genetic algorithms
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, particle-swarm algorithms
[
8
]
, or Rosenbrock methods
[
9
]
. Methods that only use values of the objective function are simple to implement, but could require thousands of objective function evaluations to reach convergence. Consequently, for large-scale calibration problems involving nonlinear finite element (FE) solves, relying solely on gradient-free algorithms can result in significant computational cost. Other studies have calibrated constitutive models by employing quasi-Newton gradient-based methods such as BFGS
[
10
,
11
]
. Gradient-based methods are generally considered more effective than those relying solely on objective function values. However, the process of computing the gradient introduces its own set of challenges. One widely used approach to differentiating the objective function with respect to the model parameters is the forward finite difference method
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. A significant drawback of using finite differences is that calculating each gradient component necessitates a complete FE solution. To address this limitation, a computationally efficient method for obtaining the gradient of the calibration objective function using adjoint sensitivities is presented in
[
16
]
.

Generally, optimization methods that utilize higher-order derivatives tend to converge in fewer iterations
[
17
]
. To date, the use of
Hessian
(second-order derivatives) information for material model calibration is lacking, largely because finite-difference approximations of the Hessian
are prohibitively expensive to obtain. In an attempt to circumvent this problem, some studies make the assumption that near the optimum, the Hessian can be approximated by discarding terms involving second-order derivatives
[
12
,
18
]
. In
[
19
,
20
]
, the adjoint method is used to approximate the Hessian in the vicinity of the optimal solution. However, to achieve true quadratic convergence in an optimization algorithm such as the Newton-Raphson method, the exact Hessian is required. In view of the above, this study aims to advance calibration through efficient computation of the exact parameter Hessian, the matrix of second-order derivatives of the objective function.

Practically, the calibration problem is solved numerically through the introduction of computational model of the characterization experiment. FE models, which are derived from both conservation laws and constitutive models, are commonly utilized for this purpose, but in some scenarios a material point model is sufficient. Material point models are local statements of the constitutive model that can be also interpreted as complete descriptions of a body undergoing homogeneous deformation under suitable external loading. Uniaxial tension of a materially homogeneous bar, where all points in the body experience the same stress state, is the prototypical example of a material point model, but there exist other useful such states, such as simple shear and dilation. We recommend Reference
[
21
]
for additional examples. The local state variables present in a material point model are constitutive model-specific, although there are often multiple ways to define them for a given constitutive model
[
22
]
. For elastic-plastic models with isotropic hardening, the isotropic hardening variable or equivalent plastic strain (for small-strain models) is an example of a local state variable.

This study lays the foundation for future development of a method to compute the parameter Hessian
for the calibration problem with a FE model constraint by examining the simpler material point problem, where the constraint simplifies to the local residual at a single quadrature point.
In this context, a direct-adjoint approach to computing the Hessian is presented by following the approach taken in
[
23
]
, where they did so for a steady-state PDE. Numerous checks of accuracy for the direct-adjoint Hessian are provided. Upon verification, the Hessian is applied to solve synthetic and experimental calibration problems, which shows its usefulness even in this limited context. Of course, the transition of calibration for a local material point model to a global FE representation of a boundary value problem will require comparison to experimentally observable quantities (e.g. full-field displacements and load cell measurements), which will affect the objective function and increase the complexity of the derivation of the associated Hessian.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section
2
contains an introduction to
the exemplar constitutive model under consideration in this work, a small deformation elastic-plastic model.
In Section
3
, the direct and adjoint methods for computing the gradient are reviewed. These
are then utilized in the novel direct-adjoint formulation for the Hessian matrix. Section
4
documents results from the application of our approach to synthetic plane stress
and experimental uniaxial tension examples. Finally, Section
5
summarizes and
concludes.

2
Material Point Plasticity Forward Model

This section describes the forward model of plasticity that will be calibrated in the ensuing developments. Here, the constitutive equations are defined and then compiled into a set of residual equations that comprise the overall plasticity model. Finally,
the local state variables are computed at each discrete load step to satisfy the residual equations.

2.1
Constitutive equations

In this study, an infinitesimal-deformation plasticity model is considered.
To this end, the total strain
\epsilon
is assumed to be additively decomposed as

\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\ =\ \mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{e}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}\ ,

(1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{e}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}
denote the elastic and plastic strains, respectively. Below the yield limit, the material response is assumed to be isotropic and linearly elastic. Thus, the stress
\sigma
is written as

\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}\ =\ \lambda\,\text{tr}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{e}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$I$}+2\mu\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{e}\ .

(2)

Here,
\lambda=\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}
and
\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}

are the Lamé constants, expressed in terms of the Young’s modulus,
E
, and
the Poisson’s ratio,
\nu
, while
I
denotes the second-order identity tensor.

A yield function
f
is introduced in the form

f(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$},\alpha)\ =\ \phi(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})-\bar{\sigma}(\alpha)\ ,

(3)

where
\phi(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})
is the effective stress and
\alpha
is the equivalent plastic strain.
The current yield stress
\bar{\sigma}(\alpha)=Y+H(\alpha)
is the sum of the initial yield limit
Y
, a linear hardening term, and a Voce-type hardening term, the latter two jointly expressed as

H(\alpha)\ =\ K\alpha+S(1-\text{exp}\bigl(-D\alpha)\bigr)\ .

(4)

The material constants
K
,
S
, and
D
in Equation (
4
) represent the linear hardening modulus, the hardening saturation modulus, and the hardening saturation rate,
respectively. In this work, a von Mises-type effective stress
\phi
is adopted, such that

\phi(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$})\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$s$}\lVert\ ,

(5)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}-\frac{\text{tr}[\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}]}{3}\mbox{\boldmath$I$}
is the deviatoric stress and

\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$s$}\lVert=(\mbox{\boldmath$s$}:\mbox{\boldmath$s$})^{1/2}
. The stress and the equivalent plastic strain satisfy the condition
f(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$},\alpha)\leq 0
at all times, where
f<0
implies an elastic material response (loading or unloading), while
f=0
implies plastic or neutral loading.
A strain-space representation of the yield function will be necessary later and can be effected with the aid of Equation (
2
), by defining a new function
g
such that

f(\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$},\alpha)\ =\ g(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p},\alpha)\ .

(6)

An associative flow rule for the evolution of the plastic strain is defined as

\displaystyle\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}^{p}

\displaystyle\ =\ \gamma\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}\ =\ \gamma\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\ ,

(7)

so that
\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}^{p}
is always normal to the yield surface in deviatoric stress space
[
24
]
. The plastic multiplier
\gamma
is subject to the Kuhn-Tucker loading/unloading conditions

\displaystyle\gamma\ \geq\ 0\quad,\quad f\ \leq\ 0\quad,\quad\gamma f\ =\ 0\ .

(8)

In view of the form of the effective stress in Equation (
5
),
it follows from Equation (
7
) that
\text{tr}[\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}}^{p}]=0
,
hence also that

\text{tr}[\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}]\ =\ 0\ .

(9)

This means that the plastic deformation is volume-preserving.
Lastly, the evolution of the equivalent plastic strain is governed by the rule

\dot{\alpha}\ =\ \gamma\ .

(10)

The constitutive model comprises six independent material parameters:
E
,

\nu
,
K
,
Y
,
S
, and
D
. These constants, written as components of a vector
p
, serve as the optimization parameters in the calibration of the constitutive model.

2.2
Model structure

The material point model of elastoplasticity is cast here as a set of residual equations in terms of the local state variables and constitutive model parameters. Upon formulation of the constitutive equations, the model itself takes as
input the strain
\epsilon
at a discrete number of load steps
n=1,\ldots,N_{L}
. In this regard, the model can be thought of as being strain-driven with the stress being determined at every load step as a function of strain. At each
step
n
, a vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}
of local state variables is defined, which contains the components of the plastic strain tensor
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}
and the equivalent plastic strain
\alpha
, in the form

[\epsilon^{p}_{11},\ \epsilon^{p}_{12},\ \epsilon^{p}_{13},\ \epsilon^{p}_{22},\ \epsilon^{p}_{23},\ \epsilon^{p}_{33},\ \alpha]^{n},\quad n\ =\ 1,\dots,N_{L}\ ,

(11)

where
N_{L}
denotes the total number of load steps. Additionally, note here
that
\epsilon^{p}_{33}=-\left(\epsilon^{p}_{11}+\epsilon^{p}_{22}\right)
due to the plastic incompressibility
condition (
9
), so this variable is not included in the vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}
.

To ensure that the state variables satisfy the constitutive equations, the model requires a set
\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}
of local residual functions that provides a complete mathematical description of the constitutive model. Taking into account Equations (
7
) and (
10
), this set takes the form

\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ =\ \left\{\begin{aligned} &f\ <\ 0:\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}\right)^{n}-\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}\right)^{n-1}\ ,\\
&\alpha^{n}-\alpha^{n-1}\ .\end{aligned}\right.\\
&f\ =\ 0:\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}\right)^{n}-\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p}\right)^{n-1}-\left(\alpha^{n}-\alpha^{n-1}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$n$}^{n}\ ,\\
&g(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{n}-(\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}^{p})^{n},\alpha^{n})\ ,\end{aligned}\right.\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

where the function
g
is defined in Equation (
6
).
In (
12
), a backward Euler integration method is employed for the rate-type flow equations during plastic loading
[
25
]
.
It follows from Equation (
12
) that, for given
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1}

and
p
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}
must satisfy

\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ =\ \mathbf{0},\quad n\ =\ 1,\dots,N_{L}\ .

(13)

The Newton-Raphson method is used to solve
Equation (
13
) for
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}
at each load step
n\in[1,N_{L}]
.
Each iteration of the algorithm corresponds to the linear system

\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}}\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}\ =\ -\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ ,\quad n\ =\ 1,\dots,N_{L}\ ,

(14)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n+1}=\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}+\Delta\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}
. Equation (
14
) is subject to the initial condition
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{0}=\mathbf{0}
, reflecting the state of no plastic deformation. After determining the local state variables, the stress tensors
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{n}

needed to evaluate the objective function are computed using
Equation (
2
).

3
Inverse Problem Formulation

In this section, the objective function is introduced for the material point model calibration problem. This function is defined in terms of differences of the model stress from the experimentally (or synthetically) generated one at different loading states. The
direct
and
adjoint
methods for computing the gradient of the objective function are presented and discussed. In addition to obtaining the gradient, a primary contribution of this work is to efficiently compute the Hessian of the objective function through the so-called
direct-adjoint
approach

[
23
]
. The JAX automatic differentiation (AD) library
[
26
]
is used to obtain partial derivatives of the residual and objective functions with respect to the local state variables and material parameters.

3.1
Constrained optimization formulation

The goal of material model calibration is to minimize an objective function
J
that
quantifies the mismatch between the stress
\sigma
with corresponding
experimental measurements
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}
at each load step. This function is defined here as

J(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}J^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})-\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}}^{n}\rVert^{2}\ ,

(15)

so that the optimization problem may be expressed as

\min_{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}\quad J(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ ,

\quad\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\ =\ \mathbf{0},\quad n\ =\ 1,\dots,N_{L}\ ,

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{\min}\ \leq\ \mbox{\boldmath$p$}\ \leq\ \mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{\max}\ ,

(16)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{\min}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{\max}
denote the lower and upper optimization search bounds for the material parameters in
p
. The constraints in Equation (
16
) must be satisfied at each iteration
of the optimization algorithm, which amounts to one complete solution of the forward
problem per iteration. For minimization, the L-BFGS-B optimization algorithm, as implemented by the Scientific Python (SciPy) library
[
27
]
, is initially used. This quasi-Newton algorithm requires evaluations of the objective function and its gradient. In this work,
direct
and
adjoint
local sensitivity analyses are employed to compute the gradient of the objective function. The convergence of the L-BFGS-B method is compared in Section
4
to that of the Newton-Raphson algorithm, which in addition to the gradient, requires the Hessian of the objective function.

The procedure for solving Equation (
16
) begins with an initial guess, denoted as
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}^{(0)}
. Given this fixed value of
p
, the gradient and Hessian of the objective function are computed using one of the methods described next. A gradient or Hessian based optimization algorithm such as L-BFGS-B or Newton-Raphson is employed to update
p
. This iterative process continues until the solution converges to a minimum.

3.2
Direct method

The first approach employed in this work to obtain the objective function gradient is referred to as the
direct
method. Treating the local state variables
\xi
as
implicit functions of
p
, the objective function and residual equations are
differentiated with respect to
p
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dJ}{dp_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}\right)\ ,

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\ =\ \displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}\ =\ 0\ .

(18)

Note that in Equations (
17
,
18
) and all subsequent mathematical
expressions, the index ranges are defined as follows:
q\in[1,N]
,
i,j\in[1,P]
, and
k,m\in[1,M]
, where
N
is the number of residual equations,
P

is the number of material parameters, and
M
is the number of local state
variables. It is important to note here that
M=N
, which reflects a necessary condition for the solution of the residual equations. Moreover, load steps denoted by the superscript
n
are explicitly summed, while all other indices follow the Einstein summation convention. Finally, the matrix
\left[\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\right]
is computed at each load step
by solving the system of linear equations obtained from Equation (
18
),
leading to

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\ =\ -\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\right)^{-1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}\right),\quad n\ =\ 1,\ldots,N_{L}\ .

(19)

Execution of the direct approach for computing the gradient may be combined with that of the forward solution. Additionally, this method only requires the storage of state variables and corresponding matrices from two adjacent load steps. However, the computational cost of the direct approach increases with the dimension of the material parameter space. More specifically,
P
systems of linear algebraic equations are required to solve Equation (
19
) at each load step.
For this reason, the so-called
adjoint
approach is considered as an alternative. Although the adjoint method requires extra memory, its computational cost is independent of the dimension of the material parameter space.

3.3
Adjoint method

To derive the gradient of the objective function using the adjoint method, a new
functional
\hat{J}
is defined as

\hat{J}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$p$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(J^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})+(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n})^{T}\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n},\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n-1},\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\right)\ ,

(20)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n}
is the vector of the
adjoint local state variables
.
Differentiating
\hat{J}
with respect to
p
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{J}}{dp_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{dJ^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{dC_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\right)\ ,

(21)

where
\displaystyle\frac{dJ^{n}}{dp_{i}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{dC_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}}
are given by
Equations (
17
,
18
). Substituting these into
Equation (
21
) and rearranging terms results in

\displaystyle\frac{d\hat{J}}{dp_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left[\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}+\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}+\left(\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\right)\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}\right]\ .

(22)

Upon expanding the summation in Equation (
22
), one
notices that computing explicitly all
\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}
terms can be avoided if

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{N_{L}}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{N_{L}}}+\phi_{q}^{N_{L}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{N_{L}}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{N_{L}}}\ =\ 0

(23)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}+\phi_{q}^{n}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}+\phi_{q}^{n+1}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n+1}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\ =\ 0,\quad n\ =\ N_{L}-1,\dots,1\ .

(24)

In direct notation, Equations (
23
,
24
) take the
form

\underbrace{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{N_{L}}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{N_{L}}}\right)^{T}}_{(M\times 1)}+\underbrace{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{N_{L}}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{{N_{L}}}}\right)^{T}}_{(M\times N)}\underbrace{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{N_{L}}}_{(N\times 1)}\ =\ \mathbf{0}

(25)

and

\underbrace{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}}\right)^{T}}_{(M\times 1)}+\underbrace{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}}\right)^{T}}_{(M\times N)}\underbrace{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n}}_{(N\times 1)}+\underbrace{\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$C$}^{n+1}}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{n}}\right)^{T}}_{(M\times N)}\underbrace{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n+1}}_{(N\times 1)}\ =\ \mathbf{0},\quad n\ =\ N_{L}-1,\dots,1\ ,

(26)

respectively. The above are known as the
adjoint equations
, and are solved
backwards in time for
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n}
, beginning at load step
N_{L}
and ending at step 1.
Therefore, the forward problem must be solved first so that the entire history of the state variables be known. After solving for the adjoint variables at each load step, the gradient now takes the simplified form

\displaystyle\frac{dJ}{dp_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}+\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}}\right)\ .

(27)

The computational complexity of the adjoint method is independent of the dimension of the material parameter space because only one system solution is required to compute
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n}
at each step. However, to perform the reverse recursion, one must allocate additional memory to store the local state variables over all the load steps.

3.4
Direct-adjoint method

As argued in
[
23
]
, the most computationally efficient
sensitivity-based scheme for obtaining the objective function Hessian is
referred to as the
direct-adjoint
approach. First, the functional

\hat{J}
of Equation (
20
) is twice-differentiated with respect to
p
, leading to

\frac{d\hat{J}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}J^{n}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}+\phi^{n}_{q}\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}\right)\ .

(28)

The terms
\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}J^{n}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}
and
\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}
are obtained by differentiating
Equations (
17
,
18
) with respect to
p
, resulting in

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}J}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{n=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}J^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}J^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}\partial p_{j}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}\partial\xi_{m}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{m}^{n}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\xi_{k}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\right)

(29)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{dp_{i}dp_{j}}\ =

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}\partial\xi_{m}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{m}^{n}}{dp_{j}}

(30)

\displaystyle+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}\partial\xi_{m}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{m}^{n-1}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}\partial p_{j}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\xi_{k}^{n}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}\partial\xi_{m}^{n}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{m}^{n}}{dp_{j}}

\displaystyle+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}\partial\xi_{m}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{m}^{n-1}}{dp_{j}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}\partial p_{j}}\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{dp_{i}}+\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{q}^{n}}{\partial\xi_{k}^{n-1}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\xi_{k}^{n-1}}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}\ .

Substituting Equations (
29
,
30
) into
Equation (
28
), one can again observe that explicit
computation of all

is
P
systems of linear equations to compute
\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{k}^{n}}{dp_{i}}
,
and an additional one to solve the adjoint equations. Over all load steps,

N_{L}(P+1)
linear system solutions are required to compute the objective
function Hessian matrix.

As stated earlier, the Newton-Raphson method requires
both the objective function gradient and Hessian. It follows that one
attractive property of the direct-adjoint method is that the adjoint
equations are identical to those in the adjoint approach for the gradient.
Therefore, the adjoint variables
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{n}
obtained while solving for the
gradient can be reused in computing the Hessian.

3.5
Parameter scaling

Before running an optimization algorithm such as L-BFGS-B, it is generally recommended to scale the model’s material parameters
[
17
]
. The scaled parameter vector, denoted
\eta
, is related to the original parameter vector
p
by the natural-logarithmic relation

\eta_{i}\ =\ \ln\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i,\text{ref}}}\right)\ ,

(32)

where
p_{i,\text{ref}}
denotes the reference parameter value of the
i
-th element of
p
. In this case,
p_{i,\text{ref}}
is chosen to be the initial guess for the parameter
p_{i}
.

To effectively use the scaled parameters in the optimization algorithms, the
gradient and Hessian must also be transformed into
\eta
-space. Employing the
chain rule, the transformed components of the gradient are resolved as

\frac{dJ}{d\eta_{i}}\ =\ \frac{dJ}{dp_{r}}\frac{dp_{r}}{d\eta_{i}}\ .

(33)

Differentiating Equation (
33
) with respect to
\eta
, the transformed Hessian components are given by

\frac{d^{2}J}{d\eta_{i}d\eta_{j}}\ =\ \frac{d^{2}J}{dp_{r}dp_{s}}\frac{dp_{r}}{d\eta_{i}}\frac{dp_{s}}{d\eta_{j}}+\frac{dJ}{dp_{r}}\frac{d^{2}p_{r}}{d\eta_{i}d\eta_{j}}\ ,

(34)

where
r,s\in[1,P]
.

4
Numerical Results

In this section, the proposed sensitivity-based approaches are applied to two different
problems. The first problem utilizes synthetically generated data to provide
verification of the AD-computed gradients and Hessian matrices. The second
problem demonstrates model calibration using experimental tension
test data. Both problems highlight the effectiveness of second-order
Hessian-based optimization algorithms for inverse problems, in contrast to
methods such as L-BFGS-B that rely solely on the gradient. Comparisons between the Newton-Raphson method and L-BFGS-B are made in terms of the number of iterations required for convergence. The total computational cost is estimated based on the required number of system solutions.

5
Conclusions

This article proposes a direct-adjoint approach to compute the Hessian for the calibration of elastoplastic material parameters formulated as a constrained optimization problem. A key step in this approach is the derivation of the second-order derivatives required for the Hessian calculation. By leveraging AD, these derivatives can be computed efficiently, which eliminates the need for tedious analytical derivations.

The calibration process is first tested on a synthetic plane-stress problem to confirm the accuracy of the derivations and implementation. Indeed, the Hessian matrix obtained through the direct-adjoint approach compares as desired to the one computed using the finite difference method. A subsequent calibration is performed on experimental data from a uniaxial tension test. In both numerical examples, the Newton-Raphson algorithm consistently demonstrated a faster convergence rate compared to quasi-Newton gradient-based algorithms such as L-BFGS-B, resulting in a reduced overall computational cost. The findings of this work establish a solid foundation for the future development of sensitivity-based methods for material parameter calibration from full-scale (as opposed to simply material point) calibration problems with FE model constraints.
