Effect of nursing guidelines for mothers on selected postoperative outcomes among their children with vesicoureteral reflux
Authors: Hanaa Diab Khalfallah, Nahed Alquwez, Marwa Abd Elkreem Ibrahim, Hanan Safar, Muna Alshammari, Samah Hamdy AbdElhafeez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is a pathological condition characterized by the retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the ureters, which increases the risk of renal damage. Current research highlights the role of genetic predisposition, advanced diagnostic modalities, and molecular markers in refining therapeutic strategies. In addition, mothers’ education plays a pivotal role in postoperative recovery and overall management of children affected by VUR.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of nursing guidelines provided to mothers on selected postoperative outcomes among their children with vesicoureteral reflux.MethodsA quasi-experimental research design was conducted at a specialized pediatric hospital in Egypt. A purposive sample of 60 mothers and their children diagnosed with VUR participated. Data collection tools included a structured interview questionnaire, a maternal knowledge and practice assessment (pre/post-test), a VUR complication recording sheet, and a postoperative outcomes assessment tool.ResultsChildren had a mean age of 2.96 ± 1.55 years in the study group and 2.78 ± 1.70 years in the control group. Mothers’ mean ages were 33.78 ± 6.79 and 34.61 ± 6.88 years respectively. After nursing guidelines, mother’s knowledge of VUR increased from 3.03 ± 0.927 to 6.56 ± 1.04 in the study group, compared with 2.568 ± 0.568 to 5.43 ± 0.678 in the control group; knowledge of complications increased from 1.93 ± 0.639 to 3.66 ± 0.479 in the study group versus 1.46 ± 0.507 to 1.73 ± 0.64 in the control group. Wound care practices improved from 2.76 ± 0.776 to 6.73 ± 1.04 in the study group, while the control group showed minimal change 3.33 ± 1.02 to 2.63 ± 1.09 (p < 0.001). Satisfactory knowledge increased from 26.7% to 93.3% respectively, and satisfactory practices from 30% to 90% in the study group compared with slight change in the control group (p = 0.05). Postoperatively, children in the study group had lower complication rates than control group (p = 0.001), also the study group experienced better postoperative outcomes than controls (p = 0.001).ConclusionThe study findings revealed substantial improvements in mothers’ knowledge and practices after receiving the nursing guidelines. The corresponding enhancement in postoperative outcomes among children with VUR underscores the importance of implementing targeted nursing interventions.RecommendationLongitudinal studies are recommended to examine the long-term effectiveness of various treatment approaches, including surgical and nonsurgical management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04501-6.

Background

Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is a pediatric condition in which urine flows backward from the bladder into the ureters and kidneys, leading to recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) and renal scarring (National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases [1]). Moderate to severe cases often require surgical intervention, but recovery largely depends on effective home care, particularly the mother’s role. Surgical site infections (SSI) are among the most frequent healthcare-associated infections, with reported incidence ranging from approximately 2–5% following clean surgical procedures. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2023, through the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN), provides standardized definitions that classify SSI into superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space infections. Application of CDC/NHSN criteria is essential to ensure accurate surveillance, allow valid comparison across studies, and distinguish true infections from minor postoperative wound changes. Structured nursing guidelines provide mothers with essential knowledge of wound care, medication adherence, infection prevention, and postoperative monitoring, which helps improve recovery outcomes and reduce complications [2, 3]. Children generally recover faster than adults due to greater physiological resilience, but optimal healing requires attentive and informed caregiving. Studies indicate that when mothers follow structured guidelines, children experience fewer complications, faster wound healing, and a quicker return to normal activities [4, 5]. Mothers’ involvement in pain management and daily care also supports the child’s comfort and psychological well-being. Empowering mothers enhances physical recovery and strengthens the caregiver-child bond, fostering a holistic healing environment. Clear, culturally sensitive instructions and ongoing nursing support build mothers’ confidence, promote proactive caregiving, and contribute to better postoperative outcomes and reduced hospital readmissions [6].

VUR arises from anomalies in ureteral function, bladder dynamics, or the ureterovesical junction (UVJ) [7]. Research has focused on genetic predisposition, improved diagnostics, and molecular markers to better identify children at risk of renal damage and guide targeted interventions [8]. Postoperative recovery is influenced by mothers’ involvement; providing education on wound care, vital signs monitoring, pain management, and catheter care enhances adherence and reduces anxiety [9]. VUR is the most common congenital urinary tract anomaly in children and is often diagnosed after UTIs or during postnatal evaluation of hydronephrosis or other anomalies [10]. Approximately 10% of newborns with prenatal upper tract dilatation develop VUR. Males are more frequently affected, and older children may present atypically, complicating diagnosis [11–13]. VUR is classified using the international reflux grading system, ranging from Grade I (ureteral reflux only, with normal renal pelvis and calyces) to Grade V (severe dilation, tortuous ureter, and blunted calyces) [14]. Complications occur in 30%-40% of children with UTIs. VUR may be primary (isolated) or secondary to bladder dysfunction, including neurogenic bladder or obstructive abnormalities [10]. Surgical intervention, particularly ureteral re-implantation, has a success rate exceeding 95% and significantly reduces pyelonephritis, although cystitis and renal scarring rates remain similar to those observed with medical management. Endoscopic treatment has lower success compared to open surgery, highlighting the importance of careful evaluation of long-term renal health and UTI recurrence [15].

Conservative management of VUR includes bladder and bowel training to ensure regular voiding, prevent constipation, and reduce UTI risk. Adequate hydration, dietary regulation, and low-dose targeted antibiotics are recommended until reflux resolves naturally or is corrected surgically [16]. Surgical correction involves ureteral repositioning or reimplantation to prevent retrograde urine flow [17]. Postoperative care includes intravenous antibiotics until oral intake is tolerated, management of bladder spasms, and continued antibiotic prophylaxis for approximately three months [18]. Mothers’ education is crucial to address concerns, explain treatment, and review clinical and anatomical aspects [19]. Nursing care begins with thorough assessment and includes monitoring vital signs, addressing knowledge gaps, ensuring adequate fluid administration, preventing wound infections, and providing health education. Structured nursing guidelines equip caregivers with essential knowledge and practical skills, enhancing adherence, preventing complications, and supporting children’s physical and emotional recovery [20, 21]. Mothers’ involvement improves postoperative outcomes, including fewer complications, faster recovery, and enhanced psychological comfort. Educating mothers on pain management and daily care routines strengthens the caregiver-child bond and supports holistic healing. Despite the critical role mother’s play, studies in Egypt reveal significant gaps in caregivers’ knowledge and skills regarding effective postoperative management of VUR. Many lack understanding of wound care, hydration strategies, catheter maintenance, bladder training, and infection prevention, which increases the risk of complications, anxiety, and delayed healing. Addressing these gaps through structured nursing guidelines, targeted educational programs, and follow-up interventions can enhance caregiver competence and confidence, ultimately improving pediatric health outcomes and reducing hospital readmissions.

The incidence of VUR among children and young adults with end-stage renal failure or chronic renal insufficiency requiring dialysis or transplantation is approximately 6%. In the general pediatric population, prevalence ranges from 0.4% to 1.8%, with girls affected more frequently than boys [11]. In Egypt [22], reported that 22% of pediatric patients with primary unilateral VUR were high-grade (IV-V) and associated with hydronephrosis. VUR is recognized by the Italian Society of Pediatric Nephrology (ISPN) as a risk factor for UTIs and renal parenchymal abnormalities [23]. Despite its prevalence, research on nursing interventions and mothers’ education for children with VUR in Egypt remains limited. Structured nursing guidelines that empower mothers are critical for improving adherence, reducing complications, and promoting faster, safer recovery. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of nursing guidelines for mothers on selected postoperative outcomes among their children with vesicoureteral reflux. Published Egyptian data indicate that surgical site infection (SSI) rates vary according to patient population, surgical context, and surveillance methodology [24]. A descriptive studies from Assiut University Children’s Hospital reported a pediatric SSI incidence of 15.3% during the first postoperative month, suggesting higher risk in pediatric surgical populations [25]. Surgical site infections (SSIs) are a common postoperative complication in pediatric surgery and contribute to increased morbidity. In Egypt, studies have shown that SSIs occur frequently in hospital settings, with Staphylococcus aureus as the most common pathogen and higher infection rates in patients with chronic illnesses. However, there is limited data on superficial and deep wound infections following pediatric vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) surgery. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of nursing guidelines for mothers on selected postoperative outcomes among their children with vesicoureteral reflux.

Operational definition

Selected postoperative outcomes

For the purpose of this study, the selected postoperative outcomes for VUR are defined by the occurrence of complications, including wound infection, fever, hypertension, flank and abdominal pain, ureteral obstruction, urinary tract infections (UTIs), and bladder incontinence. These outcomes will be evaluated by assessing wound condition, measuring body temperature, monitoring blood pressure (BP), using standardized pain scales, evaluating skin turgor, recording fluid intake and output, analyzing voiding patterns, conducting urine analysis, and assessing range of motion.

Methods

Research design

A quasi-experimental, pre/posttest research design was used to achieve the aim of the current study. In this sequential design, the control group was enrolled first, followed by the study group. While quasi-experimental designs resemble true experimental designs, they lack either full randomization or complete control over variables [26]. The sequential enrollment may introduce bias, as temporal changes could affect outcomes independently of the intervention. To reduce this risk, data for both groups were collected from the same hospital unit using identical clinical protocols. The clinical trial number is not applicable.

Research hypotheses

H1:

Mothers who receive the nursing guidelines will have a higher mean score in knowledge and practices regarding postoperative care than mothers in the control group.

H2:

Children of mothers who receive the nursing guidelines will experience fewer VUR postoperative complications compared with children of mothers in the control group.

H3:

Children of mothers who receive the nursing guidelines will have better health outcomes than children of mothers in the control group.

Setting

The pediatric surgical ward is on the fourth floor of Cairo University Specialized Pediatric Hospital (CUSPH). The surgical urology unit includes two rooms, each with 16 beds, providing comprehensive inpatient care for children with various urological conditions, including congenital anomalies and acquired issues requiring surgery. The unit supports both preoperative preparation and postoperative recovery. The urology outpatient clinic, on the second floor, serves as a follow-up hub for post-discharge evaluation, wound checks, treatment assessment and ongoing monitoring to ensure continuity of care and timely interventions.

Participants

The study included 60 mothers and their children with VUR, with the sample size determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 based on an effect size of 0.80, power of 0.95, and α = 0.05. Participants were divided into two groups of 30: the control group received routine postoperative care, whereas the experimental group received structured nursing guidelines on postoperative management. Participants were not randomly assigned, and the control group was recruited prior to the intervention group; therefore, causal inferences regarding the intervention’s effect should be interpreted with caution. Inclusion criteria were children up to 5 years old, diagnosed with grade I-III VUR, and mothers committed to rooming-in during surgical correction. Exclusion criteria included children with recurrent UTIs, acute pyelonephritis associated with VUR, or chronic kidney disease. This purposive sampling ensured a representative group for evaluating the impact of nursing guidelines on mothers’ knowledge and children’s postoperative outcomes.

Data collection tools

(The English version of the questionnaire was provided as supplementary File).

1. Structured Interview Questionnaire: Developed by the researchers after reviewing the literature, this tool consists of 15 items classified into two sections.

Part I

Personal characteristic data for mothers and their children included 10 questions, covering five questions about mothers (age, level of education, occupation, consanguinity, and pregnancy complaints) and five questions about children (age, gender, residence, rank, and multiple anomalies).

Part II

Medical history included seven questions about the causes of VUR, methods of diagnosis, types of VUR, types of surgery, preoperative period, and reasons of reflux repair and symptoms (which included to twelve items).

2. Mothers’ Knowledge and Practices Assessment Questionnaire (pre/post-test): questionnaire was developed by the researcher based on an extensive review of the relevant literature and clinical guidelines related to VUR and postoperative pediatric care. The questionnaire consists of two parts including a total of 44 items. Part I includes 18 questions assessing mothers’ knowledge related to VUR, postoperative care, treatment schedules, and signs of complications, follow-up care, and daily activities. Part II includes 26 questions assessing mothers’ practices related to wound care, catheter care, skin care, nutrition, range of motion, and bladder training. The questionnaire was administered as a pre- and post-test to evaluate changes in mothers’ knowledge and practices. Internal consistency reliability of the Mothers’ Knowledge and Practices Assessment Questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The knowledge subscale (18 items) yielded α = 0.84, and the practices subscale (26 items) yielded α = 0.82, indicating good reliability for both subscales and the overall instrument.

Scoring system

For mothers’ knowledge and practices, each complete response scored 2, each incomplete response scored 1, and wrong or “don’t know” responses scored 0. The total score was 88 for 44 questions [27]. reported that mothers’ scores were converted to percentages and categorized as follows: total score < 60% (< 52.8 points) was considered unsatisfactory, while ≥ 60% (≥ 52.8 points) was considered satisfactory.

3. Recording VUR Complications Sheet:

Developed by the researchers after reviewing the literature to determine complications associated with VUR. It includes seven key elements: fever, hypertension, flank and abdominal pain, ureteral obstruction, UTIs, bladder incontinence and surgical site infections (SSI) were classified according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria into superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space infections. Only cases fulfilling CDC diagnostic criteria were classified as surgical site infections. Postoperative wound findings that did not meet CDC criteria (such as localized erythema, mild discharge, or empiric antibiotic use without culture confirmation) were recorded separately as non-SSI superficial wound complications.

Wong-baker faces pain scale

Adapted from [28] and collected by the researchers. The scale included six faces: the 1st face represents a pain score of 0 (“no hurt”), the 2nd face a score of 2 (“hurts a little”), the 3rd a score of 4 (“hurts a little more”), the 4th a score of 6 (“hurts even more”), the 5th a score of 8 (“hurts a whole lot”), and the 6th a score of 10 (“hurts worst”). It is a standardized tool used freely to measure pain in children.

Scoring system

For the Faces Pain Rating Scale, adapted from [28], the six faces were classified into four categories of pain:

1st face: none.

2nd face: mild pain.

3rd and 4th faces: moderate pain.

5th and 6th faces: severe pain.

4. Selected Postoperative Outcomes Assessment Sheet: Postoperative outcomes were assessed on the fifth postoperative day using a structured assessment tool that incorporated both objective and subjective measures. The tool evaluated eight critical elements: wound healing, body temperature, blood pressure, pain intensity, skin turgor, voiding pattern, urine analysis, and range of motion. Wound healing, body temperature, blood pressure, skin turgor, voiding pattern, urine analysis, and range of motion were assessed by trained pediatric nurses using standardized clinical criteria, while pain intensity was evaluated using the Wong-Baker Faces Pain Scale. The researcher attended all evaluations for the children and recorded the data from the medical follow-up sheets and clinical records based on examinations performed by the attending physician. All assessments were conducted according to a predefined protocol to ensure consistency across participants.

Validity and reliability

Content validity of the questionnaire was assessed by a panel of three experts in pediatric nursing and pediatric urology, who evaluated the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the items. Minor modifications were made based on their feedback to enhance content validity. A pilot study was conducted to assess the clarity and feasibility of the questionnaire, and data obtained from the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis. Internal consistency reliability of the Mothers’ Knowledge and Practices Assessment Questionnaire was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The knowledge subscale (18 items) yielded α = 0.84, and the practices subscale (26 items) yielded α = 0.82, indicating good reliability for both subscales and the overall instrument. The Wong-Baker Faces Pain Scale demonstrated internal consistency reliability exceeding 0.80.

Procedure

Data were collected by the researchers over approximately twelve months, from September 2024 to August 2025. Informed consent was obtained from the mothers of all children included in both groups. Official permissions were obtained from the directors of the Specialized Pediatric Hospital of Cairo University, as well as from the head of the surgical ward and the urology outpatient clinic. Mothers and their children who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate in the study. The purpose and objectives of the research were individually explained to each mother. An individual interview was conducted with each mother to gather personal data for her and her child (Tool 1). A pre-test (Tool 2, Part I) was also administered by the researchers in the waiting room to evaluate the mothers’ baseline knowledge; this process took approximately 30 min. Data collection began with children in the control group receiving routine care in the pediatric intensive care unit, followed by those in the study group.

The researchers then provided structured nursing guidelines to mothers in the study group during two training sessions conducted in the urology unit. Each session lasted 30–45 min and included small groups of 3 to 5 mothers. The first session covered information on VUR, including the definition, types, and symptoms, stages of surgical treatment, complications, and follow-up process. One week after the session, a post-test (Tool 2, Part I) was administered to evaluate the knowledge gained by mothers in the study group. Mothers in the control group completed the same knowledge post-test one week later, at the same time, after receiving routine care. During the second session, researchers assessed mothers’ postoperative care practices for children in both the study and control groups using Tool 2, Part II, before applying the nursing guidelines to the study group. The nursing guidelines were then delivered through demonstration and re-demonstration using a doll, enabling mothers to develop and refine essential skills. This 40–60 min session focused on key practices such as wound care, measuring temperature and blood pressure, assessing pain scales, monitoring intake and output, evaluating voiding patterns, and performing range of motion exercises. Doll-based training provided a controlled environment for mothers to master these skills before applying them in clinical practice. Mothers in the control group received routine postoperative care for their children. Two weeks after completing the nursing guidelines, mothers in the study group were evaluated using the post-test (Tool 2, Part II) to assess their practice. Control group mothers were evaluated after receiving routine postoperative care in the urology unit.

Each child in both groups was assessed on the fifth postoperative day using VUR complications recording sheet and a postoperative outcomes assessment sheet (Tools 3 and 4) to determine complications and recovery outcomes. Each assessment took 15–20 min and was conducted by trained pediatric nurses using standardized clinical criteria. The researchers attended all evaluations and recorded data from medical follow-up sheets and clinical records based on examinations performed by the attending physician. All assessments were conducted according to a predefined protocol to ensure consistency across participants. Due to the sequential nature of data collection, observer blinding was not feasible. However, to minimize potential assessment bias, standardized and validated data collection tools were used, and the same evaluation criteria were consistently applied across both the control and study groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Chi-square tests were used to analyze associations between categorical variables, and McNemar’s chi-square test was applied for paired categorical data. Paired-sample t-tests and other inferential tests were used to test the study hypotheses. Binary logistic regression analysis was also performed for selected variables to evaluate their association with outcomes. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

The findings in Table 1 of the current study revealed that the majority of children in both the study and control groups were aged 1–3 years, with mean ages of 2.96 ± 1.55 and 2.78 ± 1.70 years, respectively. This aligns with [10], who reported that VUR is commonly diagnosed in younger children, often following urinary tract infections or antenatal detection of anomalies. A male predominance was observed in both groups (53.3% in the study group and 60% in the control group), which is consistent with previous reports indicating that males are more frequently affected by VUR during early childhood [12].Regarding residence, urban living was reported for 60% of the study group and 66.7% of the control group. As noted by [16], urban residency may facilitate access to healthcare services, although it may also be associated with environmental factors that influence health outcomes. The presence of multiple anomalies, reflecting greater clinical complexity, was identified in 26.7% of the study group and 30% of the control group. This finding supports earlier evidence that VUR often coexists with congenital renal anomalies and may necessitate multidisciplinary management [7]. Furthermore, the statistically significant differences observed in place of residence and causes of VUR (p = 0.05) highlight the potential influence of demographic and clinical factors, underscoring the importance of incorporating these variables into the assessment, management, and follow-up of affected children.

Clinical manifestations of VUR, as presented in Table 3, included fever, dysuria, frequent urination, abdominal pain, vomiting, and foul-smelling urine. Fever was slightly more prevalent in the study group (50%) than in the control group (43.3%), which is consistent with [29], who reported that febrile episodes are common in children with VUR. Lower urinary tract symptoms, including burning during urination and frequent urination, were more prevalent in the control group (73.3% and 86.7%, respectively) compared with the study group (60% and 70%), aligning with findings by [10] that children not receiving structured interventions may be more likely to experience pronounced urinary symptoms. Additional manifestations, including abdominal pain, vomiting, foul-smelling urine, poor weight gain, and elevated blood pressure, were also more frequent among controls, supporting evidence from [3, 12] indicating that inadequate management of VUR is associated with increased symptom burden and urinary tract–related complications. Abdominal masses were infrequently observed in both groups (20% vs. 13.3%), consistent with [30]; the slightly higher frequency in the study group may be related to earlier detection and systematic follow-up rather than a direct pathological association.

The demographic characteristics of mothers, as presented in Table 4, indicate that the majority was aged 30–40 years, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that childbearing and caregiving responsibilities are often concentrated within this age group [31]. Primary school education was the most prevalent educational level in both groups, highlighting the potential influence of mothers’ education on health-related knowledge and caregiving practices [16]. The predominance of housewives in both groups reflects traditional caregiving roles, which may be associated with adherence to postoperative and nursing guidelines and active engagement in child care [10]. Consanguinity was observed in approximately one-quarter to one-third of participants, a finding consistent with regional evidence suggesting that familial marriages may be linked to an increased likelihood of congenital anomalies, including urinary tract abnormalities [32]. The statistically significant differences observed between groups in mothers’ age and educational level (p = 0.05) indicate potential associations with mothers’ understanding of and engagement with care instructions, which may influence child health outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating maternal demographic factors when designing educational interventions and tailoring family-centered care programs for children with VUR.

The study demonstrated statistically significant improvements in mothers’ knowledge and reported practices following the intervention (Table 5). In the study group, the proportion of mothers with satisfactory knowledge increased from 26.7% pre-intervention to 93.3% post-intervention (p = 0.040), while satisfactory practices rose from 30% to 90% (p = 0.044). These findings are consistent with [33], which reported that targeted educational programs are associated with significant gains in mothers’ knowledge and caregiving practices. In contrast, changes in the control group were minimal, with no statistically significant differences observed in knowledge (p = 0.177) or practices (p = 0.346), aligning with findings reported by [34]. Studies [35, 36] similarly demonstrated that structured training programs focusing on postoperative care, wound management, and bladder training are associated with improved adherence and caregiver competence, supporting the outcomes observed in the study group. Furthermore [37, 38], confirmed that education-based interventions are linked to statistically significant improvements in mothers’ knowledge levels and caregiving behaviors. From the researcher’s perspective, these results underscore that structured nursing guidelines are associated with enhancements in both theoretical knowledge and practical caregiving performance, which are essential for supporting child recovery and may contribute to reducing postoperative complications in children with VUR. The findings also emphasize the importance of integrating continuous, evidence-based educational interventions into routine pediatric care, particularly in settings where caregivers have limited prior exposure to specialized postoperative care protocols.

The findings in Table 6 indicate that the educational intervention was associated with statistically significant improvements in mothers’ knowledge and reported practices regarding VUR. In the study group, the proportion of mothers with satisfactory knowledge increased from 26.7% pre-intervention to 93.3% post-intervention (p = 0.05), while satisfactory practices rose from 30% to 90% (p = 0.05). By contrast, the control group showed only minimal and non-significant changes. These results are consistent with previous studies [39, 40], which suggest that routine care without structured educational support may leave caregivers inadequately prepared, contributing to suboptimal management and a higher risk of complications, such as recurrent infections. When compared with international benchmarks, the control group’s knowledge and practice levels were comparatively lower, highlighting gaps in caregiver education and standardized postoperative care within the study setting. Improvements in reported caregiving practices, including postoperative care, wound management, catheter care, and bladder training, are in agreement with findings by [41, 42] and are strongly associated with structured educational interventions. These findings reinforce the importance of implementing structured, evidence-based educational programs, as demonstrated by the substantial gains observed in the study group, which are associated with improved adherence to care protocols and potentially better health outcomes in children with VUR. From the researcher’s perspective, these results indicate that even brief, targeted educational interventions are associated with enhanced caregiver competence and confidence, emphasizing the critical role of structured training in pediatric nursing practice and supporting safer and more effective child care.

The findings in Table 7 indicate that children whose mothers received structured nursing guidelines experienced fewer postoperative complications compared with the control group. Higher rates of freedom from wound infection, lower occurrences of fever, hypertension, and flank pain, as well as reduced urinary tract infections, ureteral obstruction, and bladder incontinence, suggest that caregiver education enhances adherence to postoperative care and early recognition of complications. The statistically significant differences in key outcomes highlight the effectiveness of structured, family-centered nursing interventions in improving recovery and reducing morbidity in children with VUR. These results are consistent with findings by [43, 44], which suggest that structured caregiver education and adherence to nursing protocols are associated with a reduction in postoperative complications among pediatric patients. Similarly [45], emphasized that parental guidance in postoperative care is linked to improved adherence to recommended practices, lower infection rates, and potentially faster recovery. Overall, the current findings underscore the importance of structured nursing guidelines and family-centered educational interventions in improving postoperative outcomes and reducing morbidity among children undergoing interventions for VUR. The relatively poorer outcomes observed in the control group may be attributed to limited caregiver knowledge, the absence of structured postoperative guidance, and inconsistent adherence to standard nursing protocols. In support of these findings, an Egyptian study [25] reported a high burden of surgical site infections (SSIs) in hospital settings. Othman et al. found that infections were prevalent in tertiary hospitals, with Staphylococcus aureus identified as the predominant pathogen. The study further indicated that SSI rates reached 32% among patients with chronic conditions, compared with 14% among those without comorbidities.

As indicated in Table 8, children in the study group were associated with substantially better postoperative recovery compared with the control group. Complete wound healing was attained by 90% of the study group, compared with 70% of controls, consistent with findings by [16], which suggest that structured nursing interventions support improved tissue recovery in pediatric patients. The study group also demonstrated more stable body temperature and blood pressure and experienced lower pain levels, aligning with [44], indicating that caregiver guidance may reduce postoperative discomfort. Improvements in hydration, skin turgor, and urinalysis results (90% normal vs. 63.3% in controls) support previous evidence [38], suggesting that family-centered education enhances adherence to postoperative care protocols. Normal voiding patterns and full range of motion were more frequently observed in the study group (83.3% and 80%, respectively) than in controls (56.7% and 63.3%), further supporting the association between guideline-driven care and improved recovery outcomes. Collectively, these results highlight the importance of structured, family-centered interventions in reducing complications, enhancing recovery, and improving overall well-being in children with VUR. Despite these positive findings, the quasi-experimental pre/posttest design without randomization and sequential recruitment of the control group prior to the intervention group limit causal inference. Temporal effects, baseline differences, and unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced the outcomes; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and future randomized controlled studies are recommended to confirm the intervention’s effectiveness. The suboptimal outcomes observed in the control group may be attributed to insufficient caregiver knowledge, inconsistent adherence to postoperative protocols, and lack of structured guidance, contributing to delayed recovery, greater discomfort, and higher complication rates. From the researcher’s perspective, these findings suggest that structured, family-centered interventions enhance caregiver competence and improve postoperative recovery outcomes.

Conclusion

The current study found that structured, family-centered nursing interventions were associated with higher maternal knowledge scores and more favorable caregiving practices among mothers of children with vesicoureteric reflux. Children in the study group demonstrated trends toward fewer postoperative complications, better wound healing, improved hydration, more stable vital signs, reduced pain, and enhanced functional outcomes, including normal voiding patterns and range of motion. Mothers who received the nursing guidelines showed improvements in both knowledge and reported practices, suggesting potential benefits of targeted education in translating information into practical care. These findings underscore the potential role of early, structured interventions and caregiver education in supporting pediatric recovery and promoting child health. The study may inform the development and use of standardized nursing guidelines and continuous caregiver guidance as part of comprehensive VUR management.
