Prognostic impact of neoadjuvant therapy and nomogram development in early-stage and locally advanced Siewert type II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction based on the SEER database
Authors: Pubo Shi, Pengjie Yang, Xingfu Ban, Yong Li, Benben Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the rising global incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG), treatment for Siewert type II AEG remains controversial, particularly regarding whether neoadjuvant therapy confers differential benefit according to stage. Locally advanced patients may benefit from neoadjuvant therapy, yet the benefit of additional adjuvant therapy remains unclear. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the stage-specific efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy and to explore the value of adjuvant therapy following neoadjuvant treatment in locally advanced disease.MethodsPatients diagnosed with Siewert type II AEG were retrieved from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Survival differences among patients were assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method and log‑rank test. To minimize baseline imbalances, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed, and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted before and after PSM to adjust for potential confounders. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to identify independent prognostic factors. A nomogram predicting overall survival (OS) was constructed based on the variables selected by Cox regression. The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated by the concordance index (C‑index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with area under the curve (AUC) values, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsA total of 1,847 patients were enrolled. In the overall cohort, no significant OS difference was observed between surgery alone (SA) and neoadjuvant-based therapy combined with surgery (NS) groups after PSM (P=0.14). Stage‑stratified analysis showed that neoadjuvant therapy significantly improved OS in locally advanced patients (P<0.001), but not in early‑stage patients (P=0.06). Among locally advanced patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, adding adjuvant therapy provided no additional OS benefit (P=0.87). Prognostic nomograms were constructed for early‑stage and locally advanced patients. For the early‑stage model, the C‑index was 0.696 in the training set and 0.669 in the testing set, with AUC values for 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year OS of 0.653, 0.730, and 0.748 in the training set, and 0.698, 0.702, and 0.692 in the testing set. For the locally advanced model, the C‑index was 0.671 and 0.635 in the training and testing sets, respectively, with AUC values of 0.741, 0.723, and 0.725 in the training set, and 0.693, 0.684, and 0.690 in the testing set. Calibration and DCA confirmed satisfactory predictive performance and clinical utility for both models.ConclusionsNeoadjuvant therapy, predominantly chemoradiotherapy, significantly improved OS in locally advanced Siewert type II AEG but not in early-stage disease. In locally advanced patients, adding adjuvant therapy after neoadjuvant therapy did not provide additional OS benefit. These findings suggest that treatment strategies may differ by stage, though this observation warrants further validation through prospective studies.Keywords: Siewert type II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (Siewert type II AEG), neoadjuvant therapy, early-stage, locally advanced

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study reveals that neoadjuvant therapy, predominantly chemoradiotherapy, improves overall survival (OS) exclusively in locally advanced Siewert type II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG), with no benefit in early-stage disease. Adjuvant therapy after neoadjuvant therapy did not confer a significant OS benefit in locally advanced patients.

What is known and what is new?

• It is well established that no unified treatment consensus has been reached for Siewert type II AEG. Although neoadjuvant therapy or perioperative therapy is currently recommended for AEG based on trials involving mixed populations, stage-specific and subtype-specific evidence remains insufficient.

• This study indicates that survival benefits of neoadjuvant therapy, predominantly neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, are confined to locally advanced Siewert type II AEG. Our findings indicate that adjuvant therapy following neoadjuvant treatment is not associated with a statistically significant survival benefit, suggesting that the use of perioperative regimens for this subtype remains to be further validated. Furthermore, we developed prognostic nomograms separately for early-stage and locally advanced patients using OS as the endpoint.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that early-stage and locally advanced stage may serve as important factors influencing the therapeutic effect of neoadjuvant therapy in Siewert type II AEG. Among locally advanced patients, adjuvant therapy following neoadjuvant therapy showed no statistically significant survival benefit compared with neoadjuvant therapy alone. Further prospective studies are required to corroborate these findings.

Introduction

The global incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) has risen markedly over recent decades, contributing to an increasing global health burden (1,2). AEG is defined as adenocarcinoma with the tumor epicenter located within 5 cm proximal or distal to the esophagogastric junction (EGJ) (3). According to the Siewert classification, it is further subdivided into three types. Among these, type II, with its epicenter situated between 1 cm above and 2 cm below the EGJ, is widely recognized as the true carcinoma of the cardia (4,5).

Owing to its distinctive anatomical location, biological behavior, and lymphatic drainage pattern, which all differ markedly from those of either esophageal or gastric adenocarcinoma, and because it also shares certain complex features of both, Siewert type II AEG has long lacked a unified consensus on treatment strategies (6). Currently, surgery remains the mainstay of treatment for this subtype (7). However, the indications for and survival benefit of neoadjuvant therapy compared with upfront surgery remain a matter of debate. The CROSS trial has demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy significantly improves overall survival (OS) in patients with AEG (6). Nevertheless, evidence specific to Siewert type II AEG remains limited. To date, few studies have evaluated the benefit of neoadjuvant therapy in this subtype according to clinical stage. Given that early-stage and locally advanced patients differ considerably in clinicopathological features and prognosis, stage‑specific treatment strategies may be warranted (8). Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that perioperative chemotherapy improves survival in patients with AEG (9,10). However, these studies evaluated neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy as a combined regimen, and the independent contribution and necessity of adjuvant therapy remain unclear, with few studies having addressed this question.

Current evidence remains insufficient to guide neoadjuvant therapy decisions for Siewert type II AEG, particularly regarding the individualized benefit across different clinical stages. To address this gap, this study aims to compare the prognostic outcomes of neoadjuvant-based therapy combined with surgery (NS) versus surgery alone (SA) in Siewert type II AEG using a large-scale real-world cohort, to determine whether this treatment effect varies by clinical stage, and to evaluate the additional survival benefit of adjuvant therapy following neoadjuvant therapy. Ultimately, our study aims to provide evidence to inform individualized treatment strategies for patients with Siewert type II AEG. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-2069/rc).

Methods

Study population

The present study extracted data on Siewert type II AEG from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (https://seer.cancer.gov/) using SEER*Stat (version 8.4.3). Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (I) year of diagnosis between 2004 and 2015; (II) classification as Siewert type II AEG, ascertained by the “Primary Site” code of 16.0 (cardia) and the “Collaborative Stage (CS) site-specific factor 25” coded as 982 (esophagus, gastroesophageal junction); (III) unique primary malignancy; (IV) histologically confirmed AEG according to ICD-O-3 codes 8140–8147, 8160–8162, 8180–8221, 8245, 8250–8507, 8514–8551, 8571–8574, 8576, and 8940–8941; and (V) receipt of either radical SA or NS. Exclusion criteria comprised: (I) presence of distant metastases (M); (II) fewer than one examined lymph node (ELN); (III) missing essential clinicopathologic information; and (IV) survival of one month or less. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

For patients diagnosed with Siewert type II AEG between 2004 and 2015, staging was performed within the CS framework. Pathological stage was prioritized for patients who underwent upfront radical SA, whereas staging information was predominantly derived from clinical staging for those receiving neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgical resection. The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification for gastric adenocarcinoma was used to stratify the overall patient cohort into early stage (T1–2N0M0) and locally advanced stage (T3–4 any N M0, or any TN+M0). For treatment comparison, enrolled patients were divided into two groups: SA and NS. The NS group was further split into neoadjuvant only (NO) and perioperative therapy (PT). Specifically, neoadjuvant treatments included radiotherapy, chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.

This study included the following variables: age, sex, race, marital status, tumor (T) stage, node (N) stage, grade, tumor size, surgical type, and ELNs. The endpoint was OS, which was defined as the time from diagnosis to all-cause death or the date of last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs). Propensity score matching (PSM) with a 1:1 ratio was performed to balance covariates including age, sex, race, and tumor characteristics, using a caliper width of 0.1 on the logit scale. Survival outcomes before and after PSM were compared using Kaplan-Meier curves with the log-rank test, and corresponding survival curves were plotted. To address residual confounding indicated by standardized mean differences (SMDs) >0.1 for certain covariates after PSM, we further constructed multivariable Cox regression models before and after matching, specifically targeting the subpopulation with such imbalances. Three models were fitted: Model I was unadjusted; Model II adjusted for age, sex, race, and marital status; and Model III additionally adjusted for T stage, N stage, grade, tumor size, surgical procedure, and ELN. Patients with early-stage and locally advanced disease were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a 7:3 ratio. Univariate Cox regression was first conducted in the training set to identify variables associated with OS. Variables with P<0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate Cox regression model. Independent prognostic factors with P<0.05 in the multivariate analysis were selected to construct the final OS prediction model. The discriminative performance of the model was assessed using the concordance index (C-index) and the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to verify the clinical utility of the nomogram. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 1,847 eligible patients with Siewert type II AEG were enrolled from the SEER database, including 788 patients in the SA group and 1,059 patients in the NS group. The mean age of all participants was 63.8 years. Most patients were male (1,499/18,47, 81.2%), white (1,621/1,847, 87.8%) and married (1,333/1,847, 72.2%). T3 stage (738/1,847, 40.0%), N0 stage (796/1,847, 43.1%), grade III (956/1,847, 51.8%) and tumor size of 2–4 cm (536/1,847, 29.0%) were dominant. In the NS group, chemoradiotherapy was the predominant neoadjuvant regimen (852/1,059, 80.5%). In the PT group, chemotherapy was the predominant adjuvant regimen (115/176, 65.3%), followed by radiotherapy (32/176, 18.2%) and chemoradiotherapy (29/176, 16.5%). PSM for confounder balancing

PSM was adopted to balance baseline confounders and mitigate selection bias. Matching was performed between SA and NS groups in the overall cohort, early-stage and locally advanced patients, and between NO and PT subgroups within the NS group. Baseline information before and after matching and SMD were provided to illustrate covariate distribution status (Tables S1-S4, Figures S1-S4).

Comparison of OS between SA and NS groups in the overall cohort

Before PSM, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed on the overall population, showing no statistically significant difference in OS between the SA and NS groups (P=0.15). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates in the SA group were 73.6% [95% confidence interval (CI): 70.5–76.7%], 53.3% (95% CI: 49.9–56.9%), and 44.9% (95% CI: 41.5–48.5%), respectively. The corresponding rates in the NS group were 83.8% (95% CI: 81.6–86.1%), 49.0% (95% CI: 46.1–52.1%), and 37.0% (95% CI: 34.2–40.1%) (Figure 2A).

Comparison of OS between the SA and the NS groups in the overall population before (A) and after (B) PSM. The blue solid line represents the SA group, and the red solid line represents the NS group; the light blue and light red shaded areas correspond to the 95% CIs of the survival curves for the two groups, respectively. CI, confidence interval; OS, overall survival; NS, neoadjuvant-based therapy combined with surgery; PSM, propensity score matching; SA, surgery alone.

After PSM, the OS between the two groups remained statistically insignificant (P=0.14). In the matched SA group, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 68.9% (95% CI: 64.5–73.6%), 45.5% (95% CI: 40.8–50.7%), and 36.5% (95% CI: 32.0–41.6%), respectively. In the matched NS group, the corresponding rates were 84.8% (95% CI: 81.4–88.4%), 52.7% (95% CI: 48.0–57.8%), and 40.2% (95% CI: 35.7–45.4%) (Figure 2B).

Comparison of OS between SA and NS groups in early-stage patients

Before PSM, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that early-stage patients in the SA group had significantly better OS than those in the NS group (P=0.002). The 1-, 3- and 5-year OS rates were 87.3% (95% CI: 84.3–90.5%), 75.7% (95% CI: 71.9–79.8%) and 66.7% (95% CI: 62.5–71.3%) in the SA group, and 88.4% (95% CI: 83.4–93.8%), 66.7% (95% CI: 59.5–74.7%) and 55.1% (95% CI: 47.6–63.8%) in the NS group (Figure 3A).

Comparison of OS between the SA and the NS groups in early-stage patients before (A) and after (B) PSM. The blue solid line represents the SA group, and the red solid line represents the NS group; the light blue and light red shaded areas correspond to the 95% CIs of the survival curves for the two groups, respectively. CI, confidence interval; OS, overall survival; NS, neoadjuvant-based therapy combined with surgery; PSM, propensity score matching; SA, surgery alone.

After PSM, the difference in OS rate between the SA group and NS group was no longer statistically significant among early-stage patients (P=0.06). The 1-, 3- and 5-year OS rates were 90.2% (95% CI: 85.1–95.6%), 72.2% (95% CI: 64.7–80.6%) and 59.1% (95% CI: 51.0–68.5%) in the SA group, while those in the NS group were 88.6% (95% CI: 83.2–94.4%), 65.9% (95% CI: 58.0–74.8%) and 52.8% (95% CI: 44.7–62.4%) (Figure 3B).

Table S5 presents the results of multivariable Cox regression analyses before and after PSM in patients with early-stage Siewert type II AEG. Before matching, NS group was associated with significantly worse OS in Model III after full adjustment for confounders [hazard ratio (HR) =1.31, 95% CI: 1.01–1.70, P=0.04]. However, this association was no longer statistically significant after PSM (HR =1.31, 95% CI: 0.94–1.83, P=0.11).

Comparison of OS between SA and NS groups in locally advanced patients

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that locally advanced patients in the NS group had markedly better OS than those in the SA group before PSM (P<0.001). The 1-year OS rate was 83.0% (95% CI: 80.6–85.5%) in the NS group versus 55.1% (95% CI: 50.0–60.7%) in the SA group. The 3- and 5-year OS rates were 46.1% (95% CI: 43.0–49.5%) versus 23.2% (95% CI: 19.1–28.2%) and 34.1% (95% CI: 31.1–37.3%) versus 15.6% (95% CI: 12.2–20.1%) (Figure 4A).

The blue solid line represents the SA group, and the red solid line represents the NS group; the light blue and light red shaded areas correspond to the 95% CIs of the survival curves for the two groups, respectively. CI, confidence interval; OS, overall survival; NS, neoadjuvant-based therapy combined with surgery; PSM, propensity score matching; SA, surgery alone.

After PSM, the survival advantage of the NS group over the SA group remained statistically significant among locally advanced patients (P<0.001). The 1-year OS rate was 80.4% (95% CI: 75.4–85.6%) in the NS group and 57.7% (95% CI: 51.7–64.4%) in the SA group. The 3- and 5-year OS rates were 42.3% (95% CI: 36.4–49.1%) versus 25.7% (95% CI: 20.6–31.9%) and 31.2% (95% CI: 25.8–37.7%) versus 17.9% (95% CI: 13.6–23.6%) (Figure 4B).

Table S6 presents the results of multivariable Cox regression analyses before and after PSM in patients with locally advanced Siewert type II AEG. In Model III, NS group was consistently associated with significantly improved OS both before (HR =0.53, 95% CI: 0.45–0.62, P<0.001) and after matching (HR =0.60, 95% CI: 0.48–0.75, P<0.001), indicating a robust survival benefit of neoadjuvant therapy in these patients.

Comparison of OS between NO and PT subgroups in locally advanced patients receiving NS therapy

Kaplan-Meier analysis of OS among locally advanced patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy showed no significant difference between the NO and PT groups before PSM (P=0.46). The 1-year OS rate was 84.3% (95% CI: 81.7–87.0%) in the NO group and 86.3% (95% CI: 81.4–91.6%) in the PT group. The 3-year OS rates were 47.4% (95% CI: 43.9–51.2%) versus 40.7% (95% CI: 34.0–48.7%), and the 5-year OS rates were 35.0% (95% CI: 31.7–38.6%) versus 30.2% (95% CI: 24.0–37.9%) (Figure 5A).

After PSM, no statistically significant difference in OS was observed between the two groups (P=0.87). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates in the NO group were 82.6% (95% CI: 77.1–88.6%), 44.7% (95% CI: 37.8–53.0%), and 32.5% (95% CI: 26.1–40.5%), compared with 87.5% (95% CI: 82.6–92.6%), 41.5% (95% CI: 34.6–49.7%), and 30.4% (95% CI: 24.1–38.3%) in the PT group (Figure 5B).

Table S7 presents the results of multivariable Cox regression analyses before and after PSM in patients with locally advanced Siewert type II AEG receiving neoadjuvant therapy, comparing PT group with the NO group. In Model III, PT showed no significant OS benefit over NO either before (HR =1.00, 95% CI: 0.82–1.22, P=0.97) or after matching (HR =0.97, 95% CI: 0.75–1.26, P=0.84).

Prognostic model development and evaluation for early-stage and locally advanced patients

This study established prognostic models containing treatment modalities for early-stage and locally advanced patients separately. Patients in both subgroups were randomly divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Baseline characteristics of each group are shown in Tables S8,S9.

Based on training set data, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on all clinical variables of early-stage patients. Four variables, including age, T stage, tumor size and treatment modality, with P<0.05, were enrolled into multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2). The results revealed that all indicators except treatment modality were independent prognostic factors for OS (P<0.05). Given that treatment modality was the core research focus of this study, it was still retained. These variables were finally used to construct the prognostic prediction model for early-stage patients (Figure 6A).

DCA confirmed that the model provided stable net benefit across a wide range of thresholds, indicating favorable clinical utility (Figure 7I-7N).

Discussion

The optimal therapeutic strategy for Siewert type II AEG has long been a subject of debate. Although neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy or perioperative chemotherapy is currently recommended as a treatment option for AEG, the evidence specifically targeting this subtype remains limited (11,12). Importantly, it remains unclear whether the association between neoadjuvant therapy and patient prognosis varies across clinical stages (13). Furthermore, for patients who receive neoadjuvant therapy, whether the addition of adjuvant therapy is associated with further prognostic improvement is equally unknown (14). To address these uncertainties, the present study was designed to evaluate the stage-specific prognostic impact of neoadjuvant therapy, which predominantly consisted of chemoradiotherapy in this cohort, in patients with Siewert type II AEG. Additionally, this study assessed the prognostic value of adjuvant therapy as part of the perioperative regimen in locally advanced patients.

In our study, no significant survival difference was observed between the NS and SA groups in the overall cohort either before (P=0.15) or after (P=0.14) PSM, with 5-year OS rates of 36.5% and 40.2% in the matched NS and SA groups, respectively. This finding contrasts with the CROSS trial, which demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy significantly improved OS in a mixed population of esophageal and esophagogastric-junction cancers (15). This discrepancy may be explained by the possibility that the treatment effect of neoadjuvant therapy varies across clinical stages, and that a pooled analysis of all stages may not adequately reflect the heterogeneity of treatment effects across stage subgroups. Indeed, Zhou et al. reported that neoadjuvant radiotherapy was associated with improved survival in locally advanced Siewert II AEG patients but not in early-stage patients (16). Given this consideration, we performed stage-stratified analyses to investigate whether the prognostic association of neoadjuvant therapy differs by clinical stage in Siewert type II AEG.

When stratified by clinical stage, the direction of survival benefit was completely opposite between early-stage and locally advanced patients. In early-stage patients, the SA group was associated with a significant OS advantage over the NS group before PSM (P=0.002), but this difference lost statistical significance after PSM (P=0.06). In contrast, among locally advanced patients, the NS group consistently correlated with significantly better OS than the SA group both before (P<0.001) and after (P<0.001) PSM, with matched 5-year OS rates of 31.2% vs. 17.9%.

The apparent survival benefit of the SA group in early-stage patients before PSM likely reflects selection bias and inherent limitations of clinical staging. Clinicians may have preferentially assigned younger patients with better performance status, fewer comorbidities, and lower tumor burden to the SA group, while referring those with suspicious or high-risk features to the NS group (17,18). Additionally, some patients in the NS group might have experienced disease progression, significant treatment-related toxicity, or deterioration in performance status during preoperative therapy, ultimately precluding curative resection, a well-recognized drawback of neoadjuvant approaches that could adversely affect survival outcomes in this subgroup (19). However, the disappearance of this survival advantage after PSM suggests that the apparent benefit of the SA group in early-stage disease is largely driven by confounding factors rather than a genuine therapeutic effect. Our results are consistent with an analysis of cT2N0 esophageal and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma that found no survival benefit with neoadjuvant therapy versus SA (HR =1.132, P=0.11) (20). Another study of clinical stage IB gastric cancer reported comparable 5-year OS between the neoadjuvant therapy and SA groups after PSM (58.8% vs. 50.3%, P=0.51) (21). Collectively, these findings indicate that the added value of neoadjuvant therapy is limited in early-stage Siewert type II AEG, and that SA may be sufficient for this population.

By contrast, in locally advanced patients, the NS group demonstrated a consistent and robust survival benefit both before and after PSM, supporting its role as a cornerstone of multimodal treatment (22). This conclusion is consistent with a prospective randomized trial in locally advanced Siewert type II/III AEG, which reported that neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy significantly improved 3year OS (63.4% vs. 52.2%, P=0.02) and DFS (52.4% vs. 39.1%, P=0.049) compared with SA (23). The starkly contrasting prognostic associations between early and locally advanced stages underscore the importance of clinical stage as a critical determinant of treatment response in Siewert type II AEG. From a clinical perspective, these results suggest that SA may be a reasonable initial option for early‑stage patients, whereas neoadjuvant therapy should be favored in locally advanced disease to optimize survival outcomes. Future investigations incorporating biomarkers or molecular subtypes may further refine patient selection and identify those most likely to benefit from neoadjuvant or adjuvant strategies.

This study showed no significant difference in OS between the PT group and the NO group in patients with locally advanced Siewert type II AEG (P=0.87). Similar negative findings were reported by Yan et al. in locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, where perioperative chemotherapy did not improve survival over neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone (24). However, this negative result in the overall population does not negate the potential value of adjuvant therapy in specific subgroups. The prospective cohort study by Saunders et al. and the multicenter study by Airoldi et al. both demonstrated that the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy depends on tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy, indicating that clinical decisions should be guided by post‑neoadjuvant pathological assessment to select appropriate candidates, thereby avoiding unnecessary treatment and optimizing perioperative strategies (25,26).

This study included 1,847 patients with Siewert type II AEG from the SEER database, providing a sufficient sample size. Stratified analyses by early and locally advanced stages allowed us to evaluate the differential efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy across stage subgroups, offering stage-specific evidence. In locally advanced patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, we further compared neoadjuvant-only versus PT to assess the independent incremental value of adjuvant therapy, addressing the limitation of previous studies that evaluated neoadjuvant and adjuvant as a combined regimen. We also developed separate nomogram models for early and locally advanced patients, which demonstrated good internal performance and provided practical tools for individualized prognosis estimation.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective analysis of the SEER database, the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Second, there is inherent heterogeneity in staging criteria across groups. According to SEER coding rules, pathological information takes priority over clinical information. For patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgical resection, pretreatment clinical stage is primarily documented. Given potential discrepancies between clinical and pathological assessment of tumour burden, different criteria are adopted when categorizing patients into early-stage and locally advanced stage subgroups. Third, key variables such as tumor regression grade, pathological complete response, R0 resection rate, postoperative complications, and adjuvant therapy completion were unavailable, limiting in-depth analysis of the mechanisms underlying perioperative benefit. Fourth, the nomograms were only internally validated; external validation in independent cohorts is needed to confirm their generalizability. Prospective studies are warranted to further validate our findings. In addition, the neoadjuvant therapy cohort in the present study was dominated by chemoradiotherapy. Sample sizes for neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were limited, and further prospective studies are required to validate our findings for these two neoadjuvant therapeutic regimens.

Conclusions

In this cohort, neoadjuvant therapy, predominantly chemoradiotherapy, significantly improved OS in locally advanced Siewert type II AEG but not in early-stage disease. The role of chemotherapy or radiotherapy alone warrants further validation due to limited sample sizes. Stratification by stage provided suggestive value for treatment guidance, though these findings require cautious interpretation. Among locally advanced patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, the addition of adjuvant therapy showed no statistically significant OS difference. Whether adjuvant therapy provides additional OS benefit in this setting remains to be determined through prospective studies.
