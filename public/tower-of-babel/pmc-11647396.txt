Prevalence and characteristics of liver steatosis and fibrosis in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients: a cross-sectional study in populations of eastern China
Authors: Hekai Yu, Xianghui Su, Wenxuan Tao, Weixia Sun, Xiaoyan Zhang, Qing Han, Zhuoxiao Zhao, Yan Zhang, Xiaoqian Chen, Xinliang Liu, Dianrong Jia, Li Fang, Ling Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe the prevalence, clinical characteristics and risk factors of liver steatosis and fibrosis in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients in eastern China.DesignA cross-sectional, multicentre study based on an ongoing cohort study.Setting16 clinics in eastern China, including primary clinics to tertiary hospitals.Participants1816 patients with T2DM diagnosis who met the inclusion criteria were recruited into the study.InterventionParticipants underwent elastography examination.Main outcome measuresDescriptive analysis was performed to calculate the prevalence and characteristics of liver steatosis and fibrosis. The correlated factors were analysed using single- and multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of liver steatosis in T2DM patients is 69.7%, with 46% moderate to severe steatosis. 34.6% and 6.7% of the patients were detected with liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. Steatosis patients were younger, had higher body mass index (BMI), higher levels of insulin resistance and more severe lipid metabolism disorders. Similar trends of differences were observed in patients with fibrosis. Female gender (OR=0.574, 95% CI 0.381 to 0.865), BMI (OR=1.491, 95% CI 1.375 to 1.616), disease duration, inflammation and serum lipid profile markers were risk factors of steatosis, while BMI (OR=1.204, 95% CI 1.137 to 1.275) and female gender (OR=0.672, 95% CI 0.470 to 0.961) were still the most significant predictors of liver fibrosis.ConclusionsThe prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis were high in patients with T2DM. Liver steatosis and fibrosis in these patients appeared to be more associated with lipid metabolism disorders and insulin resistance rather than glucose levels.Trial registration numberClinical trial: NCT05597709.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disease.1 Progression of the disease could result in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and subsequent cirrhosis and is considered among the causes of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).13 It could also increase the risks of extrahepatic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality.46 Considered a systemic metabolic disease, numerous studies have shown that type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and MASLD have a complex association.

Patients of T2DM are at higher risks of liver steatosis and advanced fibrosis.711 The prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis have been studied in various settings. The results showed that the liver steatosis could be presented in more than 70% of the T2DM patients, and advanced fibrosis may occur in as high as 38% of the patients.1215 Due to the high prevalence and lack of specifically approved treatments, it was suggested that regular screening for liver steatosis and fibrosis should be considered in T2DM patients.10

It has also been noticed that due to the complex and pathological mechanism which have not been fully elucidated, there could be significant variability in the risk of liver steatosis in diabetes patients.16 It has been previously suggested that liver steatosis prevalence may vary among subgroups of T2DM patients.17 T2DM patients with different ethnics and races have long been noticed to have different characteristics, such as onset age, body mass index (BMI), insulin resistance and metabolite signatures.18 19 Therefore, risks of the disease may present different features in populations. For example, correlations between MASLD and T2DM have been suggested to be different in population of different races.20

Individual studies on prevalence and risks of MASLD, including those in T2DM, have been performed. The results also indicated high risks of liver steatosis and fibrosis in Chinese T2DM patients.2124 However, to achieve the goal of stratifying high-risk populations among T2DM patients who require more attention regarding advanced fibrosis development, more data and detailed analyses are needed.

A T2DM patient cohort aimed at assessing the prevalence, clinical characteristics, and risks of liver steatosis and fibrosis was constructed. A report of characteristics associated with liver steatosis and fibrosis from a cross-sectional analysis is presented here.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was a cross-sectional, multi-centre investigation using an ongoing cohort to assess the prevalence and clinical characteristics of liver steatosis and fibrosis in patients with type 2 diabetes. The study cohort is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05597709) and has received approval from the institutional ethics committee. Between November 2022 and November 2023, inpatients with type 2 diabetes attending the endocrinology departments of 16 centres were invited to participate in an observational study, which was designed to characterise liver steatosis and fibrosis in this patient population (figure 1). The 16 centres included primary clinics and tertiary hospitals. Participant recruitment is currently in progress, and we have used data collected up to November 2023, which corresponds to the time of writing this article.

The flow chart of the cross-sectional study. T2DM, type 2 diabetes mellitus. TE, elastography examination.

Inclusion criteria included (i) subject age over 18 years, (ii) willing and able to give written informed consent, (iii) diagnosis of T2DM according to guideline for the prevention and treatment of T2DM in China (Chinese Diabetes Society, 2020 edition) and (iv) accepting FibroTouch examination. Exclusion criteria included (i) subject diagnosed as other types of diabetes, (ii) with a history of significant alcohol consumption, (iii) any other liver disease (viral hepatitis, hemochromatosis, drug-induced hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, etc), (iv) using corticosteroids and other medications which may cause liver steatosis, (v) unsuitable to participate in the study as having other serious disease or a history of other serious illness, (vi) participating in other clinical trial at the same time and (vii)unable to receive regular follow-up. The definition of significant alcohol consumption was 30 g/d for males and 20 g/d for females. Liver steatosis and fibrosis were evaluated using ultrasound elastography (FibroTouch, Hisky, Wuxi, China). Quality control for protocol adherence was performed at monthly review meetings in order to maintain the measurement consistency across the centres.

Measurements

Laboratory tests and clinical data

Demographic information of each patient, lifestyle habits, initial date of diabetes diagnosis, history of diabetic complications and treatment, as well as personal and family disease history, were collected in a face-to-face interview and their electronic medical records. Weight and height were measured using a calibrated scale after shoes and heavy clothing were removed. The BMI was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in metres. Obesity is defined as a BMI ≥28 kg/m². Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of 12 hours for the determination of profiles of serum glucose and insulin, serum lipids, markers of liver and renal functions in 16 clinics. Homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated using fasting glucose×fasting insulin/22.5.

Elastography examination

Liver steatosis and fibrosis were evaluated using elastography examination (FibroTouch, Hisky, Wuxi, China). FibroTouch measurements were performed by experienced and certificated physicians. A two-dimensional image guidance ultrasound probe was equipped in FibroTouch to avoid cysts, nodules and large blood vessels. The median value of the 10 acceptable liver stiffness measurements (LSMs) (kPa) and controlled attenuation parameters (CAPs) (dB/m) with IQR was expressed as the representative measurement of FibroTouch. Examinations were considered reliable if ≥10 valid measurements were obtained, with the IQR/median ≤30% and a success rate of ≥60%. Existence of liver steatosis was defined as a CAP ≥244 db/m was considered as steatosis. Mild (S1), moderate (S2) and severe steatosis (S3) were defined as 244–269 dB/m, 269–296 dB/m and ≥296 dB/m, respectively. Liver fibrosis was defined as LSM ≥7.85 kPa, with 7.85–10.0 kPa, 10.0–13.5 kPa and ≥13.5 kPa, which considered significant fibrosis (F2), advanced fibrosis (F3) and cirrhosis (F4), respectively.25 26

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed to calculate the prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis, as well as the characteristics of patients in each group. Continuous data normally distributed were presented as mean±SD, while skew-distributed data were presented as median (range). Categorical data were presented as frequency (percentage). Differences in demographics, disease history, and results of physical and laboratory tests between patients with different diagnoses were compared. Differences in normally distributed data between two groups were analysed using the Student t-test. Skew-distributed data were compared using non-parameter Mann-Whitney U test. Differences in categorical data were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The correlated factors for liver steatosis and fibrosis were analysed using single- and multivariate logistic regression analysis. SPSS Statistics V.23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) were used for statistical analysis. All tests were two-sided. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis

During the study period, 1816 patients with T2DM diagnosis who met the eligible criteria were recruited into the registry. A total of 136 patients with a history of significant alcohol consumption, 114 patients with other liver diseases and 53 patients who did not undergo FibroTouch examination were excluded from the study. A total of 1513 patients with valid test results of elastography examination were included in the final analysis (figure 1).

As shown in figure 2, the prevalence of liver steatosis in T2DM patients is 69.7%, with 46% exhibiting moderate to severe steatosis. A total of 34.6% of the patients were detected with liver fibrosis, with cirrhosis diagnosed in 6.7% of the patients.

Prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients. Liver steatosis and fibrosis were diagnosed using ultrasound elastography in 1513 T2DM patients. A controlled attenuation parameter ≥244 db/m was considered steatosis. Mild (S1), moderate (S2) and severe steatosis (S3) were defined as 244–269 dB/m, 269–296 dB/m and ≥296 dB/m, respectively. Liver fibrosis were defined as liver stiffness measurement ≥7.85 kPa, with 7.85–10.0 kPa, 10.0–13.5 kPa and ≥13.5 kPa, which considered significant fibrosis (F2), advanced fibrosis (F3) and cirrhosis (F4), respectively.

Characteristics of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients with liver steatosis and fibrosis

To investigate the characteristics and potential risk factors associated with liver steatosis and fibrosis in patients with T2DM, a comparative analysis was conducted between patients diagnosed with and without steatosis or fibrosis, focusing on demographic data, disease history and results from physical examinations and laboratory tests.

As shown in table 1, patients with steatosis showed significant differences from those without steatosis. Noticeably, steatosis patients were younger (56.5±14.0 vs 60.0±12.4 years), with higher percentage of female gender (42.1% vs 35.8%), higher BMI (26.0±4.04 vs 22.9±2.88 kg/m2) and significantly higher percentage of obesity (28.40% vs 6.50%) than the non-steatosis patients. Also, significant differences in serum lipid profile (higher levels of triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein and very low-density lipoprotein, and lower levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) in steatosis patients) between the two groups were detected. As for the potential relation between T2DM and steatosis, it was noticed that steatosis patients had higher levels of fast serum insulin, more significant insulin resistances indicated by higher HOMA-IR, but not necessarily higher fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels than the non-steatosis patients. In addition, the T2DM durations of steatosis patients were significantly shorter than those without steatosis. Not surprisingly, patients with liver steatosis showed higher serum levels of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase and gamma glutamyl transferase levels. In addition, steatosis patients showed slightly higher levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, leucocytes, red blood cells and platelets. No significant differences in renal functional parameters or history of other personal family diseases were identified.

Multivariable analysis of independent correlated factors of liver steatosis and fibrosis

To further explore the characteristics of liver steatosis and fibrosis in T2DM patients, multiple logistic regression analysis was performed. As shown in table 3, age was not significantly associated with steatosis in multivariate analysis. In contrast, male gender appeared to be with lower risk of steatosis and fibrosis compared with females, even when adjusted to age, BMI, HbA1c, insulin and HOMA-IR. The other most significant correlators of liver steatosis were BMI and serum lipid profile markers. In addition, inflammation seemed to be a risk factor for steatosis, indicating by positive association of hsCRP. Surprisingly, no significant correlations between serum HbA1c and glucose levels nor insulin resistance with steatosis were detected. Even more, T2DM disease duration was negatively associated with liver steatosis. Multiple variate regression analysis of predictors of fibrosis (shown in table 4) indicated that BMI and female gender were still the most significant predictors. A negative association between T2DM duration and fibrosis was also noticed. In addition, higher serum alkaline phosphatase levels were associated with an increased risk of fibrosis.

Discussion

The cross-sectional analysis of a T2DM patient cohort containing 1513 patients assessed with elastography examination showed high prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis in Chinese patients. Higher BMI and lipid metabolites were closely associated with risk of liver steatosis and fibrosis. Although insulin resistance was correlated with higher risks, the serum levels of glucose and Hb1Ac did not appear to have significant influences. Indeed, the patients with liver steatosis and fibrosis were younger and with shorter diabetes duration and higher insulin-resistance levels than the other patients. These results indicated that liver steatosis and fibrosis were not simply accumulated lesions of T2DM. In clinical screening and care, more attention may need to be paid for patients with younger onset ages and difficulties in glucose control.

The study population was recruited from both outpatient and inpatient departments from 16 centres, including community to tertiary hospitals. All data were collected according to previously set standards. Therefore, the results should provide a representative depiction of T2DM population in the study area, the eastern region of China. In the current study, liver steatosis and fibrosis were diagnosed using ultrasound elastography. Although multiple assessment tools may be used for screening of liver fibrosis, it has been suggested that variations of diagnostic accuracy were significant and ultrasound elastography is still the most accountable non-invasive method.102729 Therefore, the prevalence identified in the current study should be close to the true disease burden.

Previous studies using ultrasound elastography as a diagnostic method in USA have found that the prevalence of steatosis and fibrosis in hospital-visiting T2DM patients was 70% and 21%, with cirrhosis in 9%.14 Studies in general sampled population have also found that liver steatosis, advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis were present in 73.8%, 15.4% and 7.7% of patients, respectively.15 A study in Saudi Arabia also indicated 80.8% patients had liver steatosis, with fibrosis in 8.8%.30 Liver biopsy study in France showed that the prevalence of NASH, advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis in T2DM patients was 58%, 38, and 10%.12 Previous studies in China using retrospective or cross-sectional data with abdominal ultrasound as major diagnostic method suggested an overall prevalence of MASLD in T2DM patients from 58.67% to 63.2%.2124 Our results showed that the prevalence of liver steatosis in the studied T2DM population was 69.7%, while 34.6% and 6.7% were detected with liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. Taken together, these results indicated that prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis in T2DM patients was high in all global populations. There did not appear to be a significant difference in the population of this study.

For Chinese population, the prevalence of fatty liver disease in the general population and T2DM population is significantly different. Previous studies using ultrasound elastography in a large-scale Chinese population showed that the prevalence of liver steatosis, fibrosis and cirrhosis in T2DM patients was much higher than that in general population (steatosis 44.39%, fibrosis 2.85% and cirrhosis 0.87%, respectively).25 There are also differences in clinical features between the general population and the T2DM population, such as the association between age, gender, hepatic enzyme and liver steatosis. The research identified age as an independent predictor for liver steatosis and liver fibrosis, and women exhibit a lower prevalence of liver steatosis and fibrosis compared with men, with male gender identified as an independent risk factor for liver fibrosis in the general population. These findings indicate that the mechanisms or risk factors contributing to liver steatosis and fibrosis in individuals with T2DM may differ from those in the general population. The results diverge from our study, suggesting that the pathophysiological processes of liver steatosis and fibrosis may vary between T2DM patients and the general population. Additionally, it is possible that certain risk factors exert distinct influences on the development of MASLD in T2DM patients. This phenomenon may be linked to shared pathophysiological mechanisms between T2DM and MASLD, such as insulin resistance.7 Additional metabolic stimuli, including chronic tissue inflammation, oxidative stress, lipotoxicity and dysbiosis may also contribute to these disease burdens.31 32

The association between age and liver steatosis was not clear. Although early studies suggested an increased risk of MASLD in elderly,33 other studies suggested that MASLD prevalence did not increase with age.13 34 Some results even indicated that the prevalence of MASLD significantly decreased with increasing age in T2DM patients.35 Our results showed that patients with liver steatosis or fibrosis were younger compared with others. However, in multivariate analysis, age did not appear to be strongly associated with risks of steatosis or fibrosis. These results indicated that age may not be among the significant risk factors of liver steatosis and fibrosis in patients with type 2 diabetes. To elucidate the complex interplay among liver steatosis, liver fibrosis, diabetes and ageing, cohort studies are necessary to further investigate the potential causal relationships among these factors.

Results from current study indicated that female gender was associated with higher risk of liver steatosis and fibrosis in T2DM patients. Gender is among the influence factors of MASLD due to interactions between sex hormone and pathological factors such as lipid metabolism and cytokines.36 37 Previous studies have suggested that men had higher risks of MASLD. For example, study in young adults showed MASLD participants had a lower percentage of women than those with no MASLD.20 Similar results were also found in the study of elders.38 Some studies found a higher percentage of men with fatty liver disease compared with those without in T2DM patients.34 Previous study has found that prediabetic and diabetic women were with higher risks for MASLD than men.39 In a prospective study, MASLD patients also contain more percentage (66.5%) of women compared with those without MASLD (56.7%).13 Interestingly, an MASLD prevalence study in Japan identified higher percentage of women in MASLD (57%) than non-MASLD population (48%).40 A cross-sectional study in China also showed that being female significantly increased the risk of MASLD.24 Another study in Chinese T2DM patients indicated that patients with Liver Fibrosis may contain higher percentage of females (not statistically significant).22 The association of sex with liver fibrosis may vary in women before and after menopause35 and patients at different fibrosis stages.15 Nevertheless, these results indicated that influence of sex on liver steatosis and fibrosis may differ in T2DM patients than that in general population.

It was not surprising to see that BMI and lipid serum markers appeared to be the most significant correlators of liver steatosis and fibrosis, Numerous studies have shown that obesity and disturbed lipid metabolism are key pathological causes of MASLD and diabetes.41 42 In contrast, it was of some surprise that serum glucose was not associated with liver steatosis risk in T2DM patients. Furthermore, serum Hb1Ac levels appeared to be lower in patients with liver steatosis and fibrosis. Previous study indicated a significant association between HbA1c and liver fibrosis in ordinary people, but when HbA1c exceeds 7%, the correlation between NASH and HbA1c becomes less apparent.43 Another study pointed out that HbA1c was an independent predictor of the progression of liver fibrosis in Japanese type 2 diabetic patients with MASLD, but not liver inflammation.44 A study in Saudi Arabia indicated insignificant association between Hb1Ac levels and MASLD when liver steatosis was specified into three levels.30 Another study in China pointed out that there was a significant positive association between HbA1c levels and MASLD for HbA1c <8.00%, but not observed for HbA1c >8.00%.24 One plausible explanation for the absence of an observed association between steatosis, fibrosis and HbA1c levels is the increased use of glucose-lowering medications as HbA1c levels rise. Our cross-sectional study did not account for changes in medication regimens over time, which may have influenced the relationship between HbA1c and liver steatosis and fibrosis. Considering several glucose-lowering agents have been linked to reductions in liver steatosis, further cohort study is greatly needed to demonstrate the complex correlation between HbA1c and MASLD.

T2DM may influence liver steatosis and fibrosis through multiple mechanisms. A previous study has suggested that receptor for advanced glycation end (RAGE) products promotes dysregulation of lipid metabolism.45 Insulin resistance, on the other hand, was suggested to play a key role in development of MASLD.46 47 Both steatosis and fibrosis patients had higher levels of insulin resistance than ordinary patients. The chronic inflammation status in T2DM may be responsible for the sustained chronic liver injury and fibrosis.48 The study indicated that insulin resistance, rather than hyperglycaemia, might be more important in development of liver steatosis and fibrosis. In combination with the negative association between disease duration and liver steatosis, and younger age of steatosis patients, it indicated that liver steatosis and fibrosis were not simply accumulated lesions of T2DM.

The study was subjected to certain limitations. First, all participants were drawn from several hospitals in eastern China, leading to limited representativeness. Second, the initial diagnosis date may not accurately reflect the onset date of T2DM, due to the lack of information about frequency of health examinations. In addition, our analyses were conducted using baseline data from an ongoing cohort study, and our study is unable to determine the causal impact of these variables on disease initiation, progression and outcomes. Furthermore, the complex interaction between MASLD and T2DM warrants even larger sample size to elucidate the risk analysis, such as the relation of sex with T2DM and liver steatosis. Despite these limitations, the initial analysis of the collected data has identified some interesting hints that liver steatosis and fibrosis development in T2DM backgrounds may have different clinical features from those in total population.

In conclusion, a cross-sectional analysis of the T2DM cohort suggested that liver steatosis and fibrosis in these patients appeared to be more associated with lipid metabolism disorders and insulin resistance rather than glucose levels. Therefore, the causing reasons of liver steatosis risks in T2DM patients need to be more carefully evaluated.
