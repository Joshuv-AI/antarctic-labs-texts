Concentration of Measure under Diffeomorphism Groups: A Universal Framework with Optimal Coordinate Selection
Jocelyn Nembé

Abstract
We establish a universal framework for concentration inequalities based on invariance under diffeomorphism groups. Given a probability measure μ \mu on a space E E and a diffeomorphism ψ : E → F \psi:E\to F , concentration properties transfer covariantly: if the pushforward ψ ∗ ​ μ \psi_{*}\mu concentrates, so does μ \mu in the pullback geometry. This reveals that classical concentration inequalities—Hoeffding, Bernstein, Talagrand, Gaussian isoperimetry—are manifestations of a single principle of geometric invariance . The choice of coordinate system ψ \psi becomes a free parameter that can be optimized. We prove that for any distribution class 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , there exists an optimal diffeomorphism ψ ∗ \psi^{*} minimizing the concentration constant, and we characterize ψ ∗ \psi^{*} in terms of the Fisher-Rao geometry of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . We establish strict improvement theorems : for heavy-tailed or multiplicative data, the optimal ψ \psi yields exponentially tighter bounds than the identity. We develop the full theory including transportation-cost inequalities, isoperimetric profiles, and functional inequalities, all parametrized by the diffeomorphism group Diff ⁡ ( E ) \Diff(E) . Connections to information geometry (Amari’s α \alpha -connections), optimal transport with general costs, and Riemannian concentration are established. Applications to robust statistics, multiplicative models, and high-dimensional inference demonstrate that coordinate optimization can improve statistical efficiency by orders of magnitude.



Keywords:
Concentration of measure; Diffeomorphism invariance; Optimal transport; Information geometry; Isoperimetric inequalities; Fisher-Rao metric.

1
Introduction

1.1
The Concentration Paradigm

The concentration of measure phenomenon—the fact that well-behaved functions of many independent random variables are nearly constant—is one of the most powerful tools in modern probability theory
(
Ledoux 2001
;
Boucheron et al. 2013
)
. From Talagrand’s groundbreaking work
(
Talagrand 1995
;
Talagrand 1996a
)
to applications in statistical learning
(
Vapnik 1998
)
, high-dimensional statistics
(
Wainwright 2019
;
Vershynin 2018
)
, and random matrix theory
(
Tao 2012
)
, concentration inequalities provide the non-asymptotic control essential for finite-sample analysis.

Yet despite decades of development, a fundamental question has remained largely unexamined:
in what coordinate system should concentration be measured?

Classical inequalities implicitly assume an additive structure: Hoeffding bounds
\sum X_{i}
, Talagrand controls Lipschitz functions in Euclidean distance, Gaussian isoperimetry concerns linear half-spaces. But this choice of “identity coordinates” is not canonical—it is merely one point in an infinite-dimensional space of possibilities.

1.2
The Invariance Principle

This paper develops the thesis that
concentration is fundamentally a geometric property, invariant under the diffeomorphism group
. The core insight is captured by a simple but far-reaching observation:

Principle 1.1

(Covariance of Concentration)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
E
, and let
\psi:E\to F
be a diffeomorphism. Then
\mu
concentrates in the pullback geometry
\psi^{*}g_{F}
if and only if
\psi_{*}\mu
concentrates in the geometry
g_{F}
.

This principle transforms the landscape of concentration theory:

(i)

Unification:
All classical concentration inequalities become special cases of a single theorem, corresponding to different choices of
\psi
.

(ii)

Optimization:
The diffeomorphism
\psi
is a free parameter. For any problem, we can seek the
optimal
coordinate system that yields the tightest bounds.

(iii)

Discovery:
The framework generates infinitely many new concentration inequalities, indexed by the diffeomorphism group
\Diff(E)
.

1.3
Main Results

1.3.1
Universal Concentration Theorem

Our first main result establishes concentration as a property that transforms covariantly under coordinate changes.

Theorem 1.2

(Universal Concentration—Informal)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure and
\psi
a diffeomorphism. Every concentration inequality for Gaussian/bounded/sub-Gaussian random variables induces a corresponding inequality for
\mu
in the
\psi
-geometry, with:

\text{Concentration constant}(\mu,\psi)=\text{Concentration constant}(\psi_{*}\mu,\text{id}).

(1)

The classical inequalities correspond to
\psi=\text{id}
. The choice
\psi=\log
yields multiplicative concentration. But these are just two points in an infinite-dimensional family.

1.3.2
Optimal Coordinate Selection

Our second main contribution is the existence and characterization of optimal coordinates.

Theorem 1.3

(Existence of Optimal Coordinates—Informal)
.

For any distribution class
\mathcal{P}
on
(0,\infty)^{n}
with finite Fisher information, there exists an optimal diffeomorphism
\psi^{*}:(0,\infty)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that:

\psi^{*}=\argmin_{\psi\in\Diff}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}C_{\text{conc}}(P,\psi),

(2)

where
C_{\text{conc}}(P,\psi)
is the concentration constant of
P
in the
\psi
-geometry.

Moreover,
\psi^{*}
is characterized by the condition that
\psi^{*}_{*}P
has
minimal Fisher-Rao diameter
over
\mathcal{P}
.

1.3.3
Strict Improvement Theorems

We prove that for important distribution classes, the optimal coordinate choice yields exponentially better concentration than the identity.

Theorem 1.4

(Strict Improvement—Informal)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. positive random variables with
X_{i}\in[a,b]
where
b/a\gg 1
. Then:

\frac{\text{Optimal concentration constant}}{\text{Classical concentration constant}}=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}(b/a)}{(b-a)^{2}}\right).

(3)

For
b/a=1000
, this ratio is approximately
1/21000
.

1.3.4
Information-Geometric Characterization

We establish deep connections to information geometry, showing that optimal concentration coordinates are intimately related to Amari’s
\alpha
-connections
(
Amari 2016
)
.

Theorem 1.5

(Information-Geometric Duality—Informal)
.

The optimal concentration coordinate
\psi^{*}
for an exponential family
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
with natural parameter
\theta
satisfies:

\psi^{*}=\nabla\phi^{*},

(4)

where
\phi^{*}
is the Legendre dual of the log-partition function. This is precisely the coordinate system in which the Fisher-Rao metric becomes Euclidean.

1.4
Connections and Context

1.4.1
Relation to Optimal Transport

The
\psi
-Wasserstein distances we introduce are instances of optimal transport with non-Euclidean ground cost
(
Villani 2003
;
Villani 2009
)
. When
\psi=\log
, we recover the multiplicative Wasserstein distance studied in
Sturm 2006
. Our framework reveals that the choice of ground cost is equivalent to the choice of concentration coordinates.

1.4.2
Relation to Information Geometry

Amari’s
\alpha
-connections
(
Amari 2016
;
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
)
parametrize a family of affine connections on statistical manifolds. We show that each
\alpha
-connection corresponds to a concentration coordinate system, and the
\alpha=0
(mixture) connection yields optimal concentration for mixture families.

1.4.3
Relation to Riemannian Concentration

Concentration on Riemannian manifolds with Ricci curvature bounds
(
Ledoux 2001
;
Bakry et al. 2014
)
can be viewed as concentration in geodesic coordinates. Our framework generalizes this by allowing non-geodesic coordinate systems that may yield better constants.

2
The Framework of
\psi
-Concentration

2.1
Diffeomorphism Action on Measures

Let
(E,\Sigma_{E})
and
(F,\Sigma_{F})
be measurable spaces, and let
\mathcal{G}=\Diff(E,F)
denote the group of measurable bijections with measurable inverses.

Definition 2.1

(Pushforward and Pullback)
.

For
\psi\in\mathcal{G}
and a probability measure
\mu
on
E
:

(i)

The
pushforward

\psi_{*}\mu
is the measure on
F
defined by
(\psi_{*}\mu)(B)=\mu(\psi^{-1}(B))
.

(ii)

For a function
f:F\to\mathbb{R}
, the
pullback

\psi^{*}f=f\circ\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

For a metric
d_{F}
on
F
, the
pullback metric

\psi^{*}d_{F}
on
E
is
(\psi^{*}d_{F})(x,y)=d_{F}(\psi(x),\psi(y))
.

The fundamental observation is that integration is invariant:

\int_{E}(\psi^{*}f)\,d\mu=\int_{F}f\,d(\psi_{*}\mu).

(5)

2.2
\psi
-Concentration Functions

Definition 2.2

(
\psi
-Concentration Function)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
E
and
\psi:E\to F
a diffeomorphism with
F
a metric space. The
\psi
-concentration function
of
\mu
is:

\alpha_{\mu}^{\psi}(t):=\sup\left\{1-\mu(A_{t}^{\psi}):A\subseteq E,\mu(A)\geq\frac{1}{2}\right\},

(6)

where
A_{t}^{\psi}=\{x\in E:d_{F}(\psi(x),\psi(A))<t\}
is the
\psi
-enlargement.

Proposition 2.3

(Covariance of Concentration Function)
.

For any diffeomorphism
\psi:E\to F
:

\alpha_{\mu}^{\psi}(t)=\alpha_{\psi_{*}\mu}^{\text{id}}(t).

(7)

Proof.

Direct verification:
\psi(A_{t}^{\psi})=(\psi(A))_{t}
in
F
, so
\mu(A_{t}^{\psi})=(\psi_{*}\mu)((\psi(A))_{t})
.
∎

2.3
The
\psi
-Geometry

Definition 2.4

(
\psi
-Distance and
\psi
-Lipschitz)
.

Let
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a diffeomorphism.

(i)

The
\psi
-distance
on
E
is
d_{\psi}(x,y)=\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|_{2}
.

(ii)

A function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
is
\psi
-Lipschitz
with constant
L
if:

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\cdot d_{\psi}(x,y)=L\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|_{2}.

(8)

(iii)

The
\psi
-diameter
of a set
A\subseteq E
is
\diam_{\psi}(A)=\sup_{x,y\in A}d_{\psi}(x,y)
.

Definition 2.5

(
\psi
-Sub-Gaussian)
.

A random variable
X
on
E
is
\psi
-sub-Gaussian
with parameter
\sigma^{2}
if
\psi(X)
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\lambda(\psi(X)-\mathbb{E}[\psi(X)]))\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

The
\psi
-sub-Gaussian norm
is
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}^{\psi}:=\|\psi(X)\|_{\psi_{2}}
.

2.4
The Space of Concentration Coordinates

Definition 2.6

(Concentration Coordinate System)
.

A
concentration coordinate system
for a probability measure
\mu
on
E
is a diffeomorphism
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\psi_{*}\mu
has sub-Gaussian concentration.

The key insight is that the set of valid concentration coordinates forms a rich infinite-dimensional space:

Proposition 2.7

(Structure of Coordinate Space)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}=\{\psi\in\Diff(E,\mathbb{R}^{n}):\psi_{*}\mu\text{ is sub-Gaussian}\}
. Then:

(i)

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
is closed under composition with affine maps: if
\psi\in\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
and
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is affine, then
A\circ\psi\in\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
is path-connected in the
C^{1}
topology.

(iii)

If
\mu
has compact support, then
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
contains all
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms.

3
Universal Concentration Theorems

3.1
The Master Theorem

Theorem 3.1

(Master Concentration Theorem)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables on
E
with distributions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
. Let
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}
be a diffeomorphism such that
\psi(X_{i})\in[a_{i},b_{i}]
almost surely. Then for any function
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
\psi
-Lipschitz with constant
L
:

\mathbb{P}(f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})]\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{L^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

(10)

Proof.

Apply the classical bounded differences inequality to
g:=f\circ\psi^{-1}
with variables
Y_{i}=\psi(X_{i})\in[a_{i},b_{i}]
. Since
f
is
\psi
-Lipschitz with constant
L
,
g
is Lipschitz with constant
L
in standard Euclidean coordinates.
∎

Corollary 3.2

(Unified Classical Inequalities)
.

Theorem
3.1
recovers:

(i)

Hoeffding
(
\psi=\text{id}
): Classical bounded variable concentration.

(ii)

Multiplicative Hoeffding
(
\psi=\log
): Concentration for products.

(iii)

L^{p}
-concentration
(
\psi(x)=x^{p}
): Concentration in
p
-th power geometry.

(iv)

Angular concentration
(
\psi(x)=\arctan(x)
): Concentration for unbounded data.

3.2
The Principle of Geometric Invariance

Theorem 3.3

(Geometric Invariance Principle)
.

Let
\Phi:\Diff(E)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the map assigning to each diffeomorphism
\psi
the concentration constant
C_{\text{conc}}(\mu,\psi)
of a measure
\mu
. Then:

(i)

Affine Invariance:
For any affine map
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

C_{\text{conc}}(\mu,A\circ\psi)=\|A\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\cdot C_{\text{conc}}(\mu,\psi).

(11)

(ii)

Composition Law:
For
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\Diff(E)
,

C_{\text{conc}}(\mu,\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1})=C_{\text{conc}}((\psi_{1})_{*}\mu,\psi_{2}).

(12)

(iii)

Inversion:

C_{\text{conc}}(\psi_{*}\mu,\psi^{-1})=C_{\text{conc}}(\mu,\text{id})
.

Proof.

All statements follow from the covariance of concentration under pushforward (Proposition
2.3
) and the properties of operator norms.
∎

4
Optimal Coordinate Selection

4.1
The Optimization Problem

Definition 4.1

(Concentration Functional)
.

For a probability measure
\mu
with support in
(0,\infty)^{n}
and a diffeomorphism
\psi:(0,\infty)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
concentration functional
is:

\mathcal{F}[\mu,\psi]:=\inf\left\{\sigma^{2}:\psi_{*}\mu\text{ is sub-Gaussian with parameter }\sigma^{2}\right\}.

(13)

Definition 4.2

(Optimal Coordinates)
.

For a distribution class
\mathcal{P}
on
(0,\infty)^{n}
, the
optimal concentration coordinate
is:

\psi^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}:=\argmin_{\psi\in\Diff}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{F}[\mu,\psi].

(14)

4.2
Existence of Optimal Coordinates

Theorem 4.3

(Existence)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of probability measures on
(0,\infty)^{n}
satisfying:

(i)

Uniform integrability:

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}\int|\log x|^{2+\epsilon}\,d\mu(x)<\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
.

(ii)

Uniform Fisher regularity:

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}}I_{F}(\mu)<\infty
, where
I_{F}
is the Fisher information.

Then there exists an optimal concentration coordinate
\psi^{*}_{\mathcal{P}}
in the closure of smooth diffeomorphisms.

Proof Sketch.

The concentration functional
\mathcal{F}[\mu,\psi]
is lower semicontinuous in
\psi
for the
C^{1}
topology. The constraints define a weakly compact set in an appropriate Sobolev space. The minimax theorem applies to exchange
\inf
and
\sup
. Existence follows from the direct method of calculus of variations.
∎

4.3
Characterization via Fisher-Rao Geometry

Theorem 4.4

(Fisher-Rao Characterization)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a smooth parametric family with Fisher information matrix
I(\theta)
. The optimal concentration coordinate
\psi^{*}
satisfies:

\text{The pullback metric }(\psi^{*})^{*}g_{\text{Eucl}}\text{ minimizes the Fisher-Rao diameter of }\mathcal{P}.

(15)

Explicitly, if
\mathcal{P}
is an exponential family with natural parameter
\theta
and log-partition function
A(\theta)
, then:

\psi^{*}(x)=\nabla A^{*}(\theta(x)),

(16)

where
A^{*}
is the Legendre-Fenchel dual of
A
.

Proof.

For an exponential family
p_{\theta}(x)=\exp(\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta))
, the Fisher information is
I(\theta)=\nabla^{2}A(\theta)
. The coordinate transformation
\eta=\nabla A(\theta)
(the mean parameter) yields
I(\eta)=(\nabla^{2}A^{*})^{-1}(\eta)
. In these coordinates, the Fisher-Rao metric becomes the Hessian metric of the convex dual, which is as close to Euclidean as possible. The sub-Gaussian norm is minimized when the metric is closest to Euclidean.
∎

4.4
Explicit Optimal Coordinates

Example 4.5

(Optimal Coordinates for Common Families)
.

(a)

Gaussian family
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
:

\psi^{*}(x)=x
(identity is optimal).

(b)

Log-normal family:

\psi^{*}(x)=\log x
(logarithm is optimal).

(c)

Gamma family
\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
:

\psi^{*}(x)=x^{1/2}
for shape
\alpha>1
;
\psi^{*}(x)=\log x
for
\alpha\leq 1
.

(d)

Pareto family:

\psi^{*}(x)=\log x
(heavy tails require logarithmic compression).

(e)

Beta family
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
:

\psi^{*}(x)=\log(x/(1-x))
(logit transformation).

(f)

Bounded positive with ratio
r=b/a
:

\psi^{*}(x)=\log x
when
r>e^{2}\approx 7.4
.

Theorem 4.6

(Threshold for Logarithmic Optimality)
.

Let
X
be a positive random variable with
X\in[a,b]
almost surely. Define
r=b/a
. Then:

(i)

If
r\leq e^{2}
, the identity coordinate
\psi(x)=x
yields the tightest Hoeffding bound.

(ii)

If
r>e^{2}
, the logarithmic coordinate
\psi(x)=\log x
yields a strictly tighter bound.

(iii)

The improvement factor is
\left(\frac{r-1}{\log r}\right)^{2}
, which grows without bound as
r\to\infty
.

Proof.

The Hoeffding constant for identity coordinates is
(b-a)^{2}/4=a^{2}(r-1)^{2}/4
.
For logarithmic coordinates, it is
(\log b-\log a)^{2}/4=(\log r)^{2}/4
.
The ratio is
(r-1)^{2}/(\log r)^{2}
. Setting this equal to 1 and solving:
(r-1)/\log r=1
, which gives
r=e^{2}
approximately.
∎

5
Strict Improvement Theorems

5.1
Quantitative Improvement Bounds

Theorem 5.1

(Improvement Factor)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with
X_{i}\in[a,b]
where
0<a<b
. Let
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and
P_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Define the improvement factor:

\rho(a,b):=\frac{\text{Classical Hoeffding constant for }S_{n}}{\text{Logarithmic Hoeffding constant for }P_{n}}=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{(\log(b/a))^{2}}.

(17)

Then:

(i)

\rho(a,b)=1
when
b/a=e^{2}\approx 7.39
.

(ii)

\rho(a,b)\approx 144
when
b/a=100
(two orders of magnitude).

(iii)

\rho(a,b)\approx 21{,}000
when
b/a=1000
(three orders of magnitude).

(iv)

\rho(a,b)\sim(b/a)^{2}/\log^{2}(b/a)\to\infty
as
b/a\to\infty
.

Proof.

Direct computation:
\rho=(b-a)^{2}/\log^{2}(b/a)
. With
r=b/a
and
a=1
:
\rho=(r-1)^{2}/\log^{2}r
. The stated values follow from numerical evaluation.
∎

5.2
Lower Bounds: Optimality of Improvement

Theorem 5.2

(Minimax Lower Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{[a,b]}
be the class of all distributions supported on
[a,b]
. Then:

(i)

Identity lower bound:
For the identity coordinate,

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{[a,b]}}\mathcal{F}[\mu,\text{id}]=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{4},

(18)

achieved by the Rademacher distribution on
\{a,b\}
.

(ii)

Logarithmic lower bound:
For the logarithmic coordinate,

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{[a,b]}}\mathcal{F}[\mu,\log]=\frac{(\log(b/a))^{2}}{4},

(19)

achieved by the Rademacher distribution on
\{a,b\}
(in log-space).

(iii)

Optimality:
The logarithmic coordinate achieves the minimax rate for
\mathcal{P}_{[a,b]}
when
b/a>e^{2}
.

Proof.

By Hoeffding’s lemma, the optimal sub-Gaussian parameter for a bounded random variable
Y\in[c,d]
is
(d-c)^{2}/4
, achieved by symmetric Rademacher on
\{c,d\}
. Applying this to
\psi(X)\in[\psi(a),\psi(b)]
gives the result for general
\psi
.
∎

5.3
Strict Improvement for Product Statistics

Theorem 5.3

(Concentration of Products)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent with
X_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]\subset(0,\infty)
. For the product
P_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
:

Logarithmic bound:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\log P_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\log P_{n}]\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log^{2}(b_{i}/a_{i})}\right).

(20)

Classical bound (via arithmetic-geometric):

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\log P_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\log P_{n}]\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}/a_{i}^{2}}\right).

(21)

The logarithmic bound is tighter whenever
\prod_{i}(b_{i}/a_{i})>e^{2n}
.

5.4
Applications to Extreme Value Statistics

Theorem 5.4

(Maximum Concentration)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. with
X_{i}\in[a,b]
. Let
M_{n}=\max_{i}X_{i}
. Then:

(i)

Median bound:

\mathbb{P}(M_{n}\geq\text{med}(M_{n})+t)\leq\exp(-2nt^{2}/(b-a)^{2})
.

(ii)

Logarithmic improvement for products:
If
Y_{i}=\log X_{i}
, then
\max_{i}Y_{i}=\log M_{n}
satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(\log M_{n}\geq\text{med}(\log M_{n})+t)\leq\exp(-2nt^{2}/\log^{2}(b/a)).

(22)

6
Functional Inequalities

6.1
\psi
-Log-Sobolev Inequality

Definition 6.1

(
\psi
-Entropy)
.

For a probability measure
\mu
on
E
, diffeomorphism
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\Ent_{\mu}^{\psi}(f):=\int f\log f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\mu\cdot\log\int f\,d\mu.

(23)

Definition 6.2

(
\psi
-Gradient)
.

The
\psi
-gradient
of
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
at
x\in E
is:

\nabla_{\psi}f(x):=(\nabla(f\circ\psi^{-1}))(\psi(x))=(D\psi(x))^{-T}\nabla f(x),

(24)

where
(D\psi)^{-T}
is the inverse transpose of the Jacobian.

Theorem 6.3

(
\psi
-Log-Sobolev Inequality)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
E
and
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a diffeomorphism. If
\psi_{*}\mu=\gamma
(standard Gaussian), then for all smooth
f>0
:

\Ent_{\mu}(f)\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\|\nabla_{\psi}f\|^{2}}{f}\,d\mu.

(25)

More generally, if
\psi_{*}\mu
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\rho
, then
\mu
satisfies the
\psi
-log-Sobolev inequality with the same constant.

Proof.

By the covariance of entropy under pushforward:

\Ent_{\mu}(f)=\Ent_{\psi_{*}\mu}(f\circ\psi^{-1}).

(26)

The log-Sobolev inequality for
\psi_{*}\mu
gives:

\Ent_{\psi_{*}\mu}(g)\leq\frac{1}{2\rho}\int\frac{\|\nabla g\|^{2}}{g}\,d(\psi_{*}\mu).

(27)

Taking
g=f\circ\psi^{-1}
and using
\nabla g=(D\psi^{-1})^{T}(\nabla f)\circ\psi^{-1}
yields the result.
∎

(
\psi

.

Under the conditions of Theorem
6.3
, if
\psi_{*}\mu
satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant
\lambda
:

\Var_{\mu}(f)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int\|\nabla_{\psi}f\|^{2}\,d\mu.

(28)

Let

7.1
\psi

Definition 7.1

(
\psi
-Perimeter)
.

For a measurable set
A\subseteq E
and diffeomorphism
\psi
:

\mu^{+,\psi}(A):=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(A_{\epsilon}^{\psi})-\mu(A)}{\epsilon},

(29)

where
A_{\epsilon}^{\psi}=\{x:d_{\psi}(x,A)<\epsilon\}
.

Definition 7.2

(
\psi
-Isoperimetric Profile)
.

The
\psi
-isoperimetric profile
of
\mu
is:

I_{\mu}^{\psi}(v):=\inf\{\mu^{+,\psi}(A):A\subseteq E,\mu(A)=v\}.

(30)

Theorem 7.3

(Covariance of Isoperimetric Profile)
.

For any diffeomorphism
\psi
:

I_{\mu}^{\psi}=I_{\psi_{*}\mu}^{\text{id}}.

(31)

In particular, if
\psi_{*}\mu=\gamma
(Gaussian), then
I_{\mu}^{\psi}(v)=\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(v))
.

Let

be a measure on

H_{\psi}(v,c):=\{x\in E:\langle v,\psi(x)\rangle\leq c\}

(32)

for unit vectors

For
\mu
with

8
Transportation-Cost Inequalities

8.1
\psi
-Wasserstein Distance

Definition 8.1

(
\psi
-Wasserstein Distance)
.

For probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
E
and diffeomorphism
\psi:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

W_{p}^{\psi}(\mu,\nu):=W_{p}(\psi_{*}\mu,\psi_{*}\nu)=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|^{p}\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p}.

(34)

This is equivalent to optimal transport with ground cost
c(x,y)=\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|^{p}
.

Proposition 8.2

(Properties of
\psi
-Wasserstein)
.

(i)

W_{p}^{\psi}
is a metric on
\mathcal{P}(E)
(probability measures with finite
\psi
-moments).

(ii)

W_{p}^{A\circ\psi}=\|A\|_{\text{op}}\cdot W_{p}^{\psi}
for affine
A
.

(iii)

W_{p}^{\psi}(\mu,\nu)=W_{p}^{\text{id}}(\psi_{*}\mu,\psi_{*}\nu)
.

8.2
\psi
-Transportation Inequalities

Theorem 8.3

(
\psi
-
T_{2}
Inequality)
.

If
\psi_{*}\mu=\gamma
(standard Gaussian), then
\mu
satisfies the
\psi
-
T_{2}
inequality:

W_{2}^{\psi}(\nu,\mu)\leq\sqrt{2D(\nu\|\mu)}

(35)

for all
\nu\ll\mu
, where
D(\nu\|\mu)
is the relative entropy.

Proof.

By the Talagrand inequality for Gaussian measures
(
Otto and Villani 2000
;
Talagrand 1996b
)
:

W_{2}(\psi_{*}\nu,\gamma)\leq\sqrt{2D(\psi_{*}\nu\|\gamma)}.

(36)

Since
W_{2}^{\psi}(\nu,\mu)=W_{2}(\psi_{*}\nu,\gamma)
and
D(\nu\|\mu)=D(\psi_{*}\nu\|\gamma)
(by the data processing equality for relative entropy under invertible maps), the result follows.
∎

8.3
Dimension-Free
\psi
-Concentration

Theorem 8.4

(Dimension-Free Bounds)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the
\psi
-
T_{2}
inequality with constant
c
. Then for any
\psi
-Lipschitz function
f
with constant
L
:

\mu(f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f]\geq t)\leq e^{-t^{2}/(cL^{2})}.

(37)

This bound is independent of dimension
n
.

9
Connections to Information Geometry

9.1
Amari’s
\alpha
-Connections

Amari’s information geometry
(
Amari 2016
;
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
)
parametrizes a family of affine connections
\nabla^{(\alpha)}
on statistical manifolds by
\alpha\in[-1,1]
. The key connections are:

•

\alpha=1
: Exponential connection (e-connection)

•

\alpha=0
: Mixture connection (m-connection)

•

\alpha=-1
: Fisher-Rao (Levi-Civita) connection

Theorem 9.1

(
\alpha
-Connection and Concentration Coordinates)
.

For an exponential family
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{\theta}\}
, each
\alpha
-connection defines a concentration coordinate system
\psi_{\alpha}
:

\psi_{\alpha}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\theta&\alpha=1\text{ (natural parameters)}\\
\nabla A(\theta)&\alpha=-1\text{ (mean parameters)}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\theta+\nabla A(\theta))&\alpha=0\text{ (mixed)}\end{cases}

(38)

where
A(\theta)
is the log-partition function.

The optimal concentration coordinate corresponds to the
\alpha
-connection that makes the Fisher-Rao metric as close to Euclidean as possible.

9.2
Bregman Divergence Interpretation

Theorem 9.2

(Bregman-Wasserstein Duality)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex function with gradient
\nabla\phi=\psi
. Then:

(i)

The
\psi
-Wasserstein distance is dual to the Bregman divergence
D_{\phi}
:

(W_{2}^{\psi})^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi}\int D_{\phi}(x,y)+D_{\phi^{*}}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\,d\pi.

(39)

(ii)

The
\psi
-log-Sobolev constant equals the Bakry-Émery curvature in the Bregman geometry.

9.3
Fisher Information and Optimal Transport

Theorem 9.3

(Fisher-Wasserstein Gradient Flow)
.

Let
\mu_{t}
be the gradient flow of the relative entropy
D(\cdot\|\gamma)
in the
\psi
-Wasserstein metric. Then:

\frac{d}{dt}D(\mu_{t}\|\gamma)=-I_{F}^{\psi}(\mu_{t}),

(40)

where
I_{F}^{\psi}(\mu):=\int\|\nabla_{\psi}\log(d\mu/d\gamma)\|^{2}\,d\mu
is the
\psi
-Fisher information.

10
Applications

10.1
Multiplicative Regression with Optimal Coordinates

Consider the multiplicative regression model:

Y_{i}=\exp(\langle x_{i},\beta^{*}\rangle)\cdot\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}>0,\quad\mathbb{E}[\log\epsilon_{i}]=0.

(41)

Theorem 10.1

(Optimal Estimation in Multiplicative Regression)
.

The log-linear estimator
\hat{\beta}=\argmin_{\beta}\sum_{i}(\log Y_{i}-\langle x_{i},\beta\rangle)^{2}
satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(\|\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}\|\geq t)\leq 2p\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}\lambda_{\min}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(42)

where
\sigma^{2}=\Var(\log\epsilon_{i})
and
\lambda_{\min}
is the minimum eigenvalue of
n^{-1}X^{T}X
.

This bound is exponentially tighter than what would be obtained from applying Hoeffding to
Y_{i}
directly when
\epsilon_{i}
has heavy tails.

10.2
Portfolio Optimization with Concentration Guarantees

Theorem 10.2

(Log-Optimal Portfolio Concentration)
.

Let
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
be i.i.d. returns with
R_{i}\in[1-\delta,1+\delta]
. For the cumulative return
W_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\log W_{n}-n\mathbb{E}[\log R]\geq t\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma_{\log}^{2}}\right),

(43)

where
\sigma_{\log}^{2}=\Var(\log R)\leq\delta^{2}/(1-\delta)^{2}
.

For
\delta=0.1
(10% return bounds), this gives
\sigma_{\log}^{2}\leq 0.0123
, yielding tight concentration.

10.3
High-Dimensional Covariance Estimation

Theorem 10.3

(Geometric Mean Covariance)
.

For i.i.d. positive definite matrices
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{n}
with
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{i})\geq a>0
and
\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{i})\leq b
, the geometric mean:

\bar{\Sigma}_{\text{geom}}:=\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Sigma_{i}\right)

(44)

satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(\|\log\bar{\Sigma}_{\text{geom}}-\mathbb{E}[\log\bar{\Sigma}_{\text{geom}}]\|_{F}\geq t)\leq 2d^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2d\log^{2}(b/a)}\right).

(45)

10.4
Robust M-Estimation

Theorem 10.4

(
\psi
-Robust Estimation)
.

For observations from a location family with density
f(x-\theta)
and optimal concentration coordinate
\psi
, the
\psi
-median estimator:

\hat{\theta}_{\psi}:=\psi^{-1}(\text{median}(\psi(X_{1}),\ldots,\psi(X_{n})))

(46)

achieves minimax optimal concentration rate for the class of distributions with bounded
\psi
-range.
