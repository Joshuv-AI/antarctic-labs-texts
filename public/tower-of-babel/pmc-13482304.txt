RETRACTED ARTICLE: Machine learning-driven evaluation of mechanical and microstructural properties of agro-waste-derived geopolymer concrete
Authors: Noureddine Elboughdiri, Karim Kriaa, George Uwadiegwu Alaneme, Mutiu Shola Bakare, Obinna Onyebuchi Barah, Chemseddine Maatki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for sustainable construction materials has accelerated research into eco-friendly alternatives to traditional Portland cement. This research explores the potential of geopolymer concrete formulated from agricultural-waste ashes as a sustainable replacement for conventional Portland cement. Banana peel ash (BPA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) were employed as aluminosilicate precursors, and their combined effects were systematically examined through controlled variations in blend proportion, alkaline activator molarity, sodium silicate–to–sodium hydroxide (SS/SH) ratio, and aggregate-to-binder ratio. The influence of these parameters on fresh and hardened properties—including workability, compressive strength, and flexural strength—was rigorously evaluated. Within the defined experimental domain, an optimal formulation comprising 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA activated with 10 M NaOH achieved compressive and flexural strengths of 33.17 MPa and 9.95 MPa, respectively, demonstrating structural-grade performance suitable for practical applications. Detailed microstructural investigations employing SEM-EDS, XRD, FTIR and TGA techniques confirmed that both ashes exhibit high silica content, significant pozzolanic behaviour, and that increased activator concentration enhanced the dissolution of aluminosilicate phases leading to a denser geopolymeric matrix with improved durability. To further strengthen the analytical framework and enable predictive mix optimization, artificial intelligence–based models—Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)—were developed. Both models achieved excellent predictive performance (R2 > 0.98) with respect to slump Flexural and compressive strength; however, the GEP model consistently exhibited superior accuracy, lower error indices and better alignment with measured results than the ANN. Performance was validated through statistical metrics including Mean Square Error (MSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and coefficient of determination (R2), confirming the robustness of the machine-learning framework in capturing the complex, non-linear interactions among mix variables. The novelty of this study lies in demonstrating that low-value agricultural waste ashes can be engineered into reliable, structural-grade geopolymer binders, producing a high-performance BPA–SCBA concrete that repurposes agricultural residues and reduces the carbon footprint of cement production. Additionally, the integration of AI-based optimization provides a robust decision-support tool for mix design, enabling data-driven, sustainable construction practices.Keywords: Geopolymer concrete, Gene expression programming, Agrowaste, Artificial neural networks, Circular economy, Sustainable construction materialsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The global construction industry faces a pressing need to reduce its environmental footprint while meeting the growing demand for durable and high-performance infrastructure. Portland cement, the primary binder in conventional concrete, accounts for nearly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions due to energy-intensive clinker production1. Geopolymer concrete (GPC) has emerged as an eco-friendly alternative that utilizes aluminosilicate precursors activated by alkaline solutions, offering significant reductions in greenhouse gas emissions. When it comes to sustainability, Geopolymer Concrete (GPC) stands out not just for its eco-friendliness but also for its impressive mechanical performance, often matching or even surpassing that of traditional Portland Cement (OPC) concrete. This makes GPC a fantastic option for pushing forward sustainable construction methods2. GPC is a greener alternative to OPC, bringing along a host of environmental, technical, and economic advantages (Fig. 1). It cuts down on carbon emissions by skipping the energy-heavy clinker production process and makes great use of industrial by-products like fly ash and slag, as well as agricultural waste such as banana peel ash (BPA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA). These materials possess silica and alumina, which have excellent pozzolanic properties that help create binding gels, boosting strength, durability, and chemical resistance3,4. With mechanical performance that rivals or even exceeds that of OPC, GPC not only supports circular economy initiatives and waste valorization but also fits perfectly with global goals for green infrastructure and low-carbon construction5.

Exploring geopolymer concrete’s sustainability.

The growing demand for sustainable construction materials has sparked a keen interest in swapping out traditional Portland cement (OPC) for alternative binders like geopolymer concrete (GPC). This includes using agro-waste ashes such as banana peel ash (BPA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA). These ashes are composed of reactive silica and alumina, which can boost mechanical performance of concrete while also promoting circular economy principles3. However, figuring out and optimizing the properties of BPA–BA blended GPC can be quite tricky due to the complex interactions between chemical composition, mix proportions, and curing conditions (Fig. 2). Traditional experimental methods and regression techniques often struggle to accurately capture the nonlinear relationships that influence strength and durability. To overcome this, advanced computational models such as Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) are needed to develop reliable predictive frameworks for maximizing the performance and practical adoption of BPA–BA blended GPC6.

Optimizing geopolymer concrete mixes.

The deployment of agro-waste as precursors for geopolymer concrete has emerged as a growing research trend driven by the dual need for sustainable construction materials and effective waste management7. Agricultural residues such as banana peel ash, sugarcane bagasse ash, rice husk ash, and palm oil fuel ash are rich in reactive silica and alumina, making them suitable substitutes or supplementary materials for conventional binders in geopolymerization. Their incorporation not only diverts large volumes of waste from landfills and reduces environmental pollution from open burning but also enhances the engineering performance of concrete by improving compressive strength, durability, resistance to chemical attacks, and long-term stability8,9.

Rehman et al.10 investigated the performance of sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA)-based geopolymer cement paste activated with combined sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and potassium hydroxide (KOH) solutions at varying molarities and mixing ratios. Comprehensive chemical, mineralogical, and microstructural analyses confirmed the pozzolanic reactivity of SCBA and its suitability for geopolymerization. Results showed that while higher molarity increased water absorption and sorptivity, mixes prepared with 8 M combined activators achieved balanced compressive strength and durability, with G8N208K80 identified as the optimal formulation. The optimized SCBA geopolymer demonstrated superior durability compared to ordinary concrete and achieved a 21% reduction in global warming potential, highlighting its environmental and performance benefits. Moreover, Clark et al.11 examined the variability of sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) quality arising from combustion conditions in real sugar mill boilers and its implications for material reuse. Analysis of ash from three Australian sugar mills showed that high-efficiency co-generation boilers operate at much higher effective burn temperatures than reported, leading to silica polymorph transformations. These elevated temperatures (~ 1550 °C) resulted in ash dominated by crystalline α-quartz with reduced silica solubility, thereby limiting its reactivity for use in geopolymers, zeolites, and pozzolanic applications. The study concluded that, for the investigated ashes, SCBA is more suitable as a filler in cement and concrete, underscoring the need for accurate combustion temperature control to ensure consistent and usable ash quality. Furthermore, Kamsuwan et al.12 investigated the incorporation of banana peel charcoal powder into fly ash–based geopolymer as a sustainable alternative to cement. The geopolymer mixes contained 0%, 5%, 10%, and 15% banana peel charcoal powder by weight, combined with a sodium silicate–sodium hydroxide solution and river sand at a 1:2 ratio. Mechanical testing showed that the appropriate addition of banana peel charcoal improved compressive and flexural strength while also optimizing water absorption. Microstructural analyses using SEM and EDS confirmed the dispersion and interaction of the charcoal powder within the geopolymer matrix, supporting its effectiveness in enhancing material performance.

Soft computing techniques have proven effective in modelling concrete behaviour where relationships are highly nonlinear and datasets are heterogeneous. GEP produces explicit empirical equations and demonstrates good extrapolative interpretability, while ANN excel at capturing complex nonlinear patterns and handling noisy inputs13,14. A growing body of literature demonstrates the value of machine learning and symbolic regression for geopolymer and sustainable concrete prediction. The research study by Ahmad et al.15 applied ANN, boosting, and AdaBoost machine learning techniques to predict the compressive strength of high-calcium fly-ash-based GPC. Results showed that ensemble approaches (boosting and AdaBoost) outperformed the individual ANN model, with boosting achieving the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.96) compared to AdaBoost (R2 = 0.93) and ANN (R2 = 0.87). Boosting also recorded the lowest error values (MAE = 1.69 MPa, MSE = 4.16 MPa, RMSE = 2.04 MPa), confirmed by statistical error checks and k-fold cross-validation.

Also, experimental study by Cao et al.16 explored different machine learning (ML) models such as support vector machine (SVM), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and XGBoost (XGB) to predict the compressive strength of geopolymer concrete (GPC). Among the models, XGB achieved the highest accuracy with an R2 value of 0.98, outperforming SVM (0.91) and MLP (0.88). The XGB model also showed the lowest error values (MAE = 1.49 MPa, MSE = 3.16 MPa, RMSE = 1.78 MPa), confirmed by statistical checks and k-fold cross-validation. More so, research by Alaneme et al.17 investigates the use of AI techniques namely; ANN, ANFIS, and GEP to predict and optimize the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete made from BPA and SCBA. Experimental results showed that increasing alkaline activator molarity from 4 to 10 M significantly improved compressive strength, with 52.5% SCBA47.5% BPA mix at 10 M achieving a maximum compressive strength of 33.17 MPa. Among the AI models, ANFIS performed best (R2 = 0.998), demonstrating that AI integration can effectively optimize geopolymer concrete formulations and promote sustainable construction.

Recent studies further reinforce the argument that sustainable geopolymer systems can achieve high structural performance when precursor chemistry, activator design, and waste-material synergy are properly optimized18,19. For instance, research on eco-friendly engineered geopolymer composites has shown that geopolymer matrices can simultaneously deliver improved mechanical strength, crack resistance, and durability through matrix densification and refined interfacial bonding, while the recent valorization of recycled concrete powder, clay brick powder, and volcanic pumice powder demonstrated that these aluminosilicate-rich waste powders can enhance compressive, flexural, durability, and thermal performance at optimum replacement levels by promoting denser N–A–S–H/C–A–S–H gel formation and reduced porosity20. In addition, the use of magnetized water-based alkaline activators has emerged as a novel performance-enhancing strategy, with reported improvements in workability, compressive strength, durability, and microstructural compactness due to enhanced dissolution and reactivity of geopolymeric species21. Collectively, these studies indicate that current geopolymer research is moving beyond simple waste substitution toward integrated optimization of precursor blends, activator chemistry, and curing regimes, thereby providing a strong scientific basis for the present study on banana peel ash and sugarcane bagasse ash as sustainable geopolymer precursors22.

This study addresses the urgent need for sustainable construction materials by focusing on the development and optimization of geopolymer concrete (GPC) incorporating banana peel ash (BPA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) as supplementary cementitious materials. Traditional Portland cement production is a significant contributor to global CO2 emissions, and the construction industry faces growing pressure to adopt eco-friendly alternatives. Despite extensive research on various agro-industrial wastes in geopolymer systems, the combined utilisation of BPA and SCBA remains underexplored, particularly regarding their synergistic effects on mechanical performance, durability, and workability. This study therefore aims to bridge this gap by comprehensively evaluating the physico-chemical and microstructural characteristics of BPA and SCBA to elucidate their reactivity, pozzolanic potential, and interaction mechanisms within geopolymer matrices.

Building on this foundation, the study systematically investigates both fresh and hardened properties of BPA- and SCBA-blended geopolymer concrete to determine optimal material proportions and curing regimes. The research addresses the existing limitations in empirical studies, which often neglect the variability introduced by agro-waste compositions or fail to integrate predictive modelling to accurately forecast performance. Advanced computational tools, specifically Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), are employed to model and predict mechanical behaviour, enabling the identification of ideal blending ratios and curing conditions that maximize compressive and tensile strength while minimizing material inconsistencies. This integrative approach not only enhances understanding of material behaviour but also provides a robust framework for optimizing geopolymer concrete formulations with reproducible outcomes23,24.

The overarching objectives of this study are to systematically characterize the physicochemical and microstructural properties of BPA and SCBA to understand their reactivity in geopolymerization; optimize the blend composition and curing conditions to maximize mechanical performance of the GPC; develop and validate GEP and ANN models for accurate prediction of fresh and hardened concrete properties; and elucidate the correlations between microstructural features and mechanical performance to inform scalable industrial formulations. Through this approach, the research provides a rigorous scientific basis for sustainable GPC development, highlighting environmental advantages such as CO2 emission reduction and effective utilization of locally sourced agro-waste, while bridging critical gaps in both the theoretical understanding and practical application of agro-waste-based geopolymer concretes.

Materials and methods

Materials

Banana peel ash (BPA)

Banana (matoke) peels collected from Bushenyi, Western Uganda, were first thoroughly washed to remove dirt and debris, then oven-dried at 105 ± 5 °C for 24 h to eliminate moisture. The dried peels were subjected to controlled incineration in a muffle furnace with a heating rate of 10 °C/min, reaching a peak temperature of 700 °C, and maintained for 3 h under an ambient air atmosphere to ensure complete carbonization and enhance pozzolanic reactivity. After incineration, the resulting banana peel ash (BPA) was allowed to cool gradually in a desiccator to prevent moisture uptake and carbonation. The cooled ash was then sieved through a 75 μm mesh to obtain a uniform particle size suitable for geopolymer concrete mixing. Finally, the processed BPA was stored in airtight, moisture-proof containers under room temperature conditions (25 ± 2 °C) until use, minimizing hydration or agglomeration prior to incorporation in the geopolymer matrix18.

Bagasse ash (SCBA)

Bagasse ash, a by-product from sugar manufacturing, was sourced from a plant where sugarcane residues served as boiler fuel. To boost its reactivity, the collected ash underwent a process of oven-drying, grinding in a ball mill, and sieving through a 75 μm mesh. This treatment not only reduced the carbon content but also improved its fineness, both of which are crucial for speeding up geopolymeric reactions. The blend of SCBA and BPA was anticipated to create a synergistic effect by harmonizing the calcium-rich and silica-rich components19.

Alkaline activator

In this study, the alkaline activator solution was prepared by blending sodium silicate (Na2SiO3, industrial grade, containing 30–32% SiO2 and 8–10% Na2O by weight) with sodium hydroxide (NaOH, analytical grade, ≥ 98% purity) at molarities of 4, 6, 8, and 10 M. The selected molarity range was intended to regulate the dissolution kinetics of aluminosilicate precursors and promote effective geopolymerization. Furthermore, the Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio was systematically varied between 1.5 and 3 to control the availability of silicate and hydroxide species, which are essential for gel formation, microstructural evolution, and the development of mechanical strength15. To ensure consistency in geopolymer formation, a well-defined thermal curing protocol was adopted. After casting, all specimens were sealed to prevent moisture loss and maintained at ambient conditions (25 ± 2 °C) for 24 h prior to demoulding12. Subsequently, the specimens were subjected to isothermal oven curing at controlled temperatures of 50–100 °C for durations of 10–20 h, as specified in the experimental design. A uniform heating rate of approximately 5 °C/min was applied to reach the target curing temperature, ensuring gradual activation and minimizing thermal stresses. At the completion of the curing cycle, the specimens were allowed to cool naturally within the oven to room temperature (ambient cooling) to avoid microcracking due to thermal shock7,25. Thereafter, all samples were stored under laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity ≈ 60%) until testing. This controlled curing regime provides a reproducible thermal history, which is critical for accurately assessing the influence of activator composition and curing conditions on the geopolymerization process and the resulting concrete performance20.

Fine and coarse aggregates

The fine aggregate consisted of well-graded river sand that complies with ASTM C33, having a maximum particle size of 4.75 mm. As for the coarse aggregate, crushed granite with a nominal size of 20 mm was selected. Both aggregates were tested for specific gravity, water absorption, and bulk density to ensure compliance with standard concrete mix design requirements. The aggregates provided the skeletal framework for the geopolymer concrete, while the BPA–SCBA binder matrix contributed to strength development26.

Research methodology

This study takes a structured and integrative approach to predict the mechanical properties of banana peel ash (BPA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) blended geopolymer concrete (GPC) using Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques. By bridging the gap between experimental and computational methods, it ensures that practical material testing and advanced predictive modelling work hand in hand. As shown in Fig. 3, the methodology starts by pinpointing the research gap: while the use of agricultural waste in geopolymer concrete has been explored extensively, there’s still a significant absence of strong AI-driven predictive models that can optimize mix designs, especially when it comes to unconventional materials like BPA and SCBA. To tackle this issue, the current methodology not only focuses on experimental validation but also on creating high-performance AI models that can adapt well to various mix design scenarios.

Program flowchart.

The methodology consists of several key stages, starting with the collection and preparation of raw materials, which include BPA, SCBA, alkaline activators, and aggregates. The standard procedures were deployed for mix design formulation, batching, and sample preparation to ensure consistent and reproducible results25,27. Laboratory experiments were carried out to assess important mechanical properties such as workability (slump), compressive strength, and flexural strength under different parameters like molarity, aggregate-to-binder ratio, sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide (SS/SH) ratio, BPA-SCBA blending ratios, and curing conditions. The results from these experiments provided a high-quality dataset that serves as the backbone for developing the AI models.

After data collection, it was subjected to cleaning and pre-processing phase which involved normalization and mapping inputs to outputs. The defined independent variables for the model development are the mix design parameters, which included molarity, the aggregate-to-binder ratio, the SS/SH ratio, SCBA content, BPA content, and curing time. On the other hand, the dependent variables were slump, compressive strength, and flexural strength. Two supervised machine learning methods ANN and GEP were adapted for this research because they excel at modelling complex nonlinear relationships. The ANN model was trained, validated, and tested using a feed-forward backpropagation algorithm, while the GEP model utilized evolutionary operators to create symbolic expressions. To evaluate the model’s performance, statistical metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R²) were utilized. This methodological approach guarantees reliability, reproducibility, and applicability by combining empirical experimentation with cutting-edge AI modelling. By harnessing AI to predict the properties of BPA–SCBA blended GPC, this study plays a role in promoting sustainable construction practices, enhancing material optimization, and offering a decision-support tool for designing eco-friendly, high-performance concrete mixes28.

Methods

In this study, the process of dissolving and mixing alkaline activators was naturally exothermic, reaching temperatures around 70 °C. This meant that the activator solutions had to be prepared at least a day in advance before they could be blended with the SCBA–BPA precursors to ensure proper activation29. The blending ratios for SCBA–BPA varied from 0% to 100%. The alkaline solutions were created by carefully measuring out the necessary amount of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), which has a molar mass of 40 g for a 1 M solution, and adjusting the quantities accordingly to reach the desired molarity levels (for instance, 160 g for 4 M, 240 g for 6 M, 320 g for 8 M, and 400 g for 10 M). The measured NaOH was then dissolved in distilled water and brought up to a final volume of 1 L (1000 cm3). Key synthesis parameters included the Alkali Activator Solution (AAS)/Precursor ratio (0.25–0.6), Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio (1.5–3), NaOH molarity (4–10 M), and curing time (10–20 h) (Tables 1 and 2). These were all selected based on existing literature to strike the right balance between workability, strength, and durability30,31. Specifically, the AAS/Precursor ratio affected the fluidity of the mix and the density of the matrix, while the Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio influenced gel formation and strength development. The NaOH molarity sped up the dissolution and polymerization of aluminosilicates, and longer curing times helped develop a stable polymeric network. By fine-tuning these parameters, the study successfully produced a sustainable, high-performance geopolymer concrete, with all experiments carried out at the University of Cross River State, Nigeria (UNICROSS) Civil Materials and Structural Laboratory.

Mixture design parameters.

Slump test

The slump test is a method used to evaluate how consistent and workable fresh concrete is after all the components of the GPC mixture have been thoroughly mixed. This test provides crucial information about the concrete’s ability to flow and settle under its own weight, which is key to ensuring that the material is placed and compacted correctly during construction. In this test, the freshly mixed GPC is poured into a cone-shaped mold and compacted using standard techniques. Once the mold is filled, it is carefully lifted to allow the concrete to expand and settle by itself. The resulting deformation, known as the slump, is then measured and recorded according to the ASTM C143 specification32. The slump value, usually given in millimeters, gives insight into the concrete’s consistency. A higher slump value indicates more fluidity and workability, while a lower slump suggests stiffness and reduced workability.

Compressive strength test

The compressive strength test for geopolymer concrete is an essential process that follows the guidelines set out in BS EN 12390-3:2019, which focuses on testing hardened concrete under compression. The test procedure starts with mixing and batching cubic specimens with varying ratios of BPA and SCBA precursors, each measuring 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm, to ensure consistency and reliability in the results. These specimens are cast and cured in controlled heat curing conditions for specific durations, as detailed in BS EN 12390-2:2019 to achieve hardened samples33. This curing phase is crucial as it allows the geopolymerization reaction to fully take place, ensuring the material reaches its intended mechanical properties. Once the curing period is over, the hardened samples undergo compression testing using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM). The UTM applies a steady load at a rate of 0.6 ± 0.2 MPa/s until the specimen fails. To find the compressive strength, the maximum load the specimen can handle before breaking is divided by its cross-sectional area, and the result is given in megapascals (MPa). This test is vital for understanding the material’s load-bearing capacity, durability, and overall performance, which helps engineers and researchers assess whether geopolymer concrete is suitable for structural uses24,30.

Flexural strength test

The flexural strength test, as described in BS EN 12390-5:2009, is a vital procedure for assessing how well concrete can withstand bending stresses when under load. The test gives us crucial insights into its structural performance. The test procedure involves preparing beam specimens, which measure 100 mm × 100 mm × 400 mm, following standard dimensions34. The geopolymer mix is created by carefully measuring the ingredients to ensure everything is well-blended, and then pouring it into molds. To avoid any voids that might weaken the structure, the mixture is compacted using tamping or vibration. After casting, the specimens are cured in a controlled setting to ensure they harden properly, with the temperature and curing time adjusted to suit the geopolymer binder system. Once they’re cured, the beams are tested using a third-point loading system, where they’re supported at a span of 300 mm, and a load is gradually applied at the center with a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) until they fail. The maximum load at which failure occurs is recorded and used to calculate the flexural strength or modulus of rupture, providing essential information about the material’s ability to resist tensile stresses from bending. This standardized test is crucial for evaluating the mechanical performance of GPC mixes and optimizing their application in structural projects35,36.

Data analysis

Data analysis techniques are key to deriving meaningful insights from experimental results, particularly in advanced material studies where various interacting factors can influence performance. To optimize the mechanical properties of BPA and SCBA blended geopolymer concrete, robust computational methods were deployed which can effectively capture the complex nonlinear relationships that traditional statistical models may overlook. Two notable AI techniques—Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)— were adopted in this research study.

Artificial neural networks (ANN)

Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) are advanced computational models inspired by the architecture and functioning of biological neural networks in the human brain, designed to process complex and nonlinear relationships in data28. Structurally, ANN consist of layers of interconnected nodes namely; input, hidden, and output layers where each node, or neuron, performs simple calculations that collectively enable the network to solve intricate problems. The network learns patterns in data through forward propagation, where inputs are transformed as they pass through the layers, and backpropagation, a feedback process that iteratively adjusts weights and biases to minimize the error between predicted and actual outputs (Fig. 4)37. Key components, including weights that define the strength of inter-neuron connections, biases that add flexibility to the model, and activation functions that introduce non-linearity, allow ANN to model highly complex phenomena. Their versatility and predictive power have been successfully applied in diverse fields such as image and speech recognition, natural language processing, autonomous systems, and material science, where they are particularly effective in predicting properties of composite materials like geopolymer concrete by capturing subtle interactions among multiple influencing factors38.

ANN model development process.

Gene expression programming (GEP)

Gene Expression Programming (GEP) is an innovative evolutionary algorithm designed by Candida Ferreira. It takes the principles of Genetic Programming (GP) a step further by encoding solutions as linear chromosomes that are then expressed as nonlinear expression trees17. GEP evolves populations of these expression trees, which consist of functions, terminals, and constants, to effectively model complex relationships in various problem domains. Its unique modular gene structure allows each gene to represent a specific functional component, while the hierarchical nature of the expression trees ensures that the solutions are both comprehensive and easy to understand39. The algorithm utilizes a variety of evolutionary operators such as mutation, crossover, and gene transposition along with selection mechanisms to sustain diversity and promote the most promising solutions, with termination criteria that signal the end of the evolutionary journey40. GEP’s flexibility is evident in its wide range of applications, from symbolic regression and time series forecasting to classification, optimization, and system modeling, consistently yielding concise, mathematically interpretable expressions that highlight underlying data patterns (Fig. 5). Its blend of structured representation, adaptive evolution, and interpretability makes GEP a formidable tool for solving complex engineering and scientific problems, including the predictive modeling of material properties like the mechanical performance of blended geopolymer concretes41.

Chemical and morphological characterization

Chemical and morphological characterization is a thorough investigation into the chemical makeup and physical structure of geopolymer concrete. This analysis is crucial for gaining insights into its properties and potential performance. Through chemical analysis, the determination of elemental composition and key compounds that influence reactivity, setting behavior, and long-term stability were achieved. On the morphological side, shape, porosity, and distribution were assessed, which are vital for workability, strength development, and durability42. By combining these evaluations, researchers can refine mix designs, predict mechanical performance, and customize geopolymer concrete for various engineering applications, ultimately ensuring it is reliable, structurally sound, and durable across different environmental conditions.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy, as outlined in BS ISO 18587:2014, is a powerful analytical tool that helps identify functional groups and chemical bonds in materials. This technique is particularly useful for characterizing GPC that uses alternative binders like BPA and SBA. During the process, a GPC sample is exposed to a wide range of infrared radiation, where certain chemical bonds absorb infrared light at specific frequencies, creating unique absorption peaks in the resulting spectrum42. By examining these peaks, crucial bonds such as Si–O, Al–O, and Si–O–Al, as well as functional groups like hydroxyl (OH), carbonate (CO3), and water (H2O) were identified. This analysis provides insights into the material’s molecular structure, composition, and bonding mechanisms. Additionally, FTIR can reveal the degree of polymerization, identify impurities or contaminants, and evaluate the curing kinetics of the geopolymer matrix. It offers a comprehensive view of both the chemical interactions and structural characteristics of GPC, which is essential for optimizing mix designs and predicting mechanical performance and durability43.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) test, which follows the guidelines of BS EN 13925-1:2003, is a well-established analytical method used to uncover the crystalline structure, phase composition, and mineral characteristics of various materials. This makes it especially useful for examining geopolymers that use alternative binders like BPA and SBA44. XRD offers essential insights into how atoms are arranged within the material’s lattice, helping to identify both crystalline phases and amorphous components. These factors play a crucial role in determining the mechanical properties, durability, and reactivity of the geopolymer matrix. The process begins with careful sample preparation, where larger GPC specimens are crushed into fine, uniform particles and then placed on a suitable sample holder. The instrument scans the sample across a specific angular range, usually from 2θ = 5° to 90°, producing diffraction patterns that are matched against reference databases to pinpoint crystalline phases. These patterns can reveal important minerals like quartz, mullite, or aluminosilicate gels, and they provide insights into the degree of crystallinity, phase changes, and possible reaction products formed during geopolymerization45.

Energy dispersive scanning electron microscopy (ED-SEM)

Energy Dispersive Scanning Electron Microscopy (ED-SEM) is a cutting-edge analytical method that follows the guidelines of BS ISO 22309:2011. This test is designed to delve into the microstructural characteristics and elemental makeup of materials, such as GPC that includes BPA and SCBA. The process kicks off with careful sample preparation, where specimens are trimmed to convenient sizes and polished to a sleek finish, ensuring top-notch imaging quality46. Once the samples are in the SEM’s vacuum chamber, a finely focused electron beam interacts with the surface, generating signals like secondary electrons (SE) that unveil intricate surface details and backscattered electrons (BSE) that offer compositional insights. The high-resolution images produced during this process capture vital microstructural elements like pores, cracks, and unreacted particles, all of which play a role in the material’s mechanical properties47. At the same time, the Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) detector picks up characteristic X-rays emitted from the sample, allowing for the identification and quantification of elements, and creating elemental maps that show their distribution. This thorough analysis of both microstructure and chemistry sheds light on the geopolymerization process, the interactions between BPA and SBA in the mix, and the overall uniformity of the concrete, helping to refine mix designs and forecast the material’s durability and long-term performance48.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF)

X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) is an incredibly dependable analytical technique that’s widely used to analyze the chemical composition of geopolymer concrete that includes supplementary materials such as BPA and SBA. By using high-energy X-rays to excite atoms in a finely powdered or fused sample, this method detects the unique fluorescent emissions from individual elements, allowing for accurate measurement of oxides like SiO2, Al2O3, CaO, and Fe2O3. This information is crucial for evaluating the pozzolanic potential of the ashes, understanding the role of reactive phases in geopolymerization, and identifying any impurities that might impact the binding process. The non-destructive nature and precision of XRF make it essential for confirming the consistency of raw materials, optimizing binder formulations, and predicting performance outcomes such as strength and durability49.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) is method for investigating the thermal stability, compositional changes, and degradation behavior of geopolymer concrete that incorporates BPA and SBA. By heating a carefully measured sample in a controlled inert or oxidative atmosphere, TGA continuously monitors weight loss as the temperature climbs. This technique reveals key transitions, such as the evaporation of free and bound water, the decomposition of organic materials, and the breakdown of both crystalline and amorphous mineral phases50. For geopolymer systems, this information is invaluable for grasping the degree of geopolymerization, assessing the stability of the aluminosilicate network, and understanding how the ash components contribute to performance at high temperatures. The thermograms produced highlight specific temperature ranges for different thermal events, which can be correlated with the material’s durability, its resistance to fire or heat, and its long-term performance in tough service conditions. Thus, TGA provides essential insights that guide mix optimization, quality control of raw materials, and the prediction of service life in construction applications51.

Slump and density results

Slump and density tests play a crucial role in assessing both the fresh and hardened characteristics of geopolymer concrete. They offer valuable insights into workability, compactness, and overall structural integrity. The experimental results shown in Fig. 7a,b indicate that the density of geopolymer concrete tends to drop with extended heat curing due to excessive moisture evaporation. However, it increases with a higher molarity of the alkaline activator, which enhances geopolymerization and densification of the matrix. The average density values recorded were 2090 kg/m3, 2129 kg/m3, 2215 kg/m3, and 2192 kg/m3 for molarities of 4, 6, 8, and 10, respectively. This suggests that moderate curing conditions lead to optimal densification. On the other hand, the slump test results demonstrated an inverse relationship with both activator molarity and aggregate-binder ratio, as higher activator concentrations and greater aggregate proportions reduced workability by making the mix more viscous and restricting binder mobility54. Mean slump values of 192 mm, 183 mm, 175 mm, and 160 mm were obtained at molarities of 4, 6, 8, and 10 respectively, confirming the stiffening effect of higher activator concentrations. These findings emphasize the careful balance between workability and density in mix design. This ensures that geopolymer concrete not only allows for easy placement but also meets the necessary mechanical strength, aligning with the research conducted by Oyebisi et al.55.

Compressive strength

Compressive strength is a fundamental indicator of the structural performance of geopolymer concrete, as it directly reflects the extent of precursor dissolution, geopolymeric gel formation, and matrix densification achieved during alkali activation. As shown in Fig. 8, compressive strength increased significantly with increasing activator molarity, reaching a peak value of 33.17 MPa at 10 M NaOH for the 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA blend, whereas the lowest strength of 21.27 MPa was recorded at 4 M for the 95% SCBA–5% BPA mix. This trend extends beyond the effect of increased alkalinity alone and indicates the establishment of an optimized geopolymerization environment. At higher molarity, the elevated OH⁻ ion concentration enhances the dissolution of amorphous aluminosilicate phases, thereby increasing the availability of reactive silicate and aluminate species necessary for gel formation56. The superior performance of the 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA blend can be mechanistically attributed to the complementary chemical composition of the precursors: SCBA provides a high content of reactive SiO₂, supplying silicate species essential for network formation, while BPA contributes significant K₂O, which increases the alkalinity of the system and facilitates the dissolution of aluminosilicate structures. This synergy promotes the formation of a dense and homogeneous mixed N–A–S–H/K–A–S–H gel network, which is critical for enhanced strength development51.

Compressive strength results.

The influence of processing parameters further corroborates this mechanistic interpretation. The optimal aggregate-to-binder ratio of 2.0 likely improved particle packing density and reduced internal porosity, thereby enhancing load transfer efficiency within the hardened matrix. Similarly, the SS/SH ratio of 2.5 appears to have ensured a balanced supply of soluble silicate and hydroxide ions, enabling simultaneous dissolution and polycondensation processes without premature gel precipitation or excessive viscosity49. At lower molarity levels or non-optimal precursor ratios, insufficient ionic concentration limits precursor dissolution and disrupts gel continuity, resulting in a more porous and mechanically inferior matrix. Moreover, the observed increase in compressive strength with curing time indicates ongoing geopolymerization reactions and progressive microstructural refinement, leading to a more compact and interconnected aluminosilicate framework54. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the enhanced compressive strength is governed by a synergistic interplay between precursor chemistry, activator concentration, mix design parameters, and curing conditions. This confirms that BPA–SCBA blended geopolymer concrete can achieve structural-grade performance when the system is optimized to maximize dissolution kinetics and gel densification, thereby reinforcing its viability as a sustainable alternative to conventional Portland cement-based materials in consonance with the findings of Khan et al.57.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength is a critical mechanical property of geopolymer concrete because it reflects the material’s ability to resist tensile cracking and sustain bending stresses, which are particularly important for structural members such as beams, slabs, and pavements. In this study, the flexural behavior determined through three-point bending tests (Fig. 9) showed a pronounced dependence on activator molarity, curing duration, and precursor composition, with the highest flexural strength of 9.95 MPa obtained at 10 M NaOH for the mix containing 60% SCBA and 40% BPA, while the lowest value of 4.12 MPa occurred at 4 M for the blend with 95% SCBA and 5% BPA. The increase in flexural strength with activator concentration can be mechanistically attributed to enhanced dissolution of reactive silica and alumina from the precursor ashes, followed by improved polycondensation and gel formation. In particular, the superior flexural response of the 60% SCBA–40% BPA blend can be linked to the complementary chemical composition of the two agro-waste ashes: SCBA provides abundant reactive SiO₂, which contributes to the formation of a continuous and rigid geopolymeric backbone, while BPA supplies K2O-rich alkali constituents, which increase the local alkalinity and facilitate the dissolution of aluminosilicate species. This synergy likely promoted the formation of a more homogeneous and well-crosslinked geopolymeric matrix with improved interfacial bonding and reduced microcrack susceptibility, thereby enhancing the material’s resistance to tensile stress under bending.

Flexural strength results.

The observed influence of curing time and mix design parameters further confirms that flexural performance was governed by both chemical reactivity and microstructural refinement. Prolonged curing likely enabled continued geopolymerization and gradual development of a more compact gel network, thereby reducing internal defects and strengthening the bond between the geopolymer paste and aggregate surfaces. Likewise, the optimum aggregate-to-binder ratio of 2.0 appears to have provided effective particle packing and load transfer efficiency, while the SS/SH ratio of 2.5 likely ensured a balanced supply of soluble silicate and hydroxide ions necessary for simultaneous precursor dissolution and gel growth. At lower molarity or less favorable blend proportions, incomplete reaction and weaker gel connectivity would have promoted microvoids and interfacial discontinuities, ultimately lowering flexural resistance. Therefore, the excellent flexural performance achieved in this study can be interpreted as the result of a chemically balanced and physically optimized geopolymer system in which precursor synergy, activator chemistry, and curing collectively enhanced matrix continuity and crack-bridging capability. These findings demonstrate that BPA–SCBA blended geopolymer concrete possesses strong potential for structural applications requiring improved tensile and bending performance, while also advancing the development of sustainable, high-performance construction materials which is consistent with the findings of Peng and Unluer60.

ANN-Model

Artificial Neural Network (ANN) modelling was employed to develop a robust predictive framework capable of capturing the nonlinear relationships between mix design parameters and the mechanical properties of SCBA–BPA blended geopolymer concrete. A total of 104 experimental datasets were pre-processed in MATLAB to ensure consistency and subsequently partitioned into training and testing subsets to evaluate the model’s generalization capability (Table 5). The network architecture was configured as a two-layer feed-forward model with a 6–18–3 structure, where the six input neurons represent molarity, aggregate-to-binder ratio, Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio, SCBA content, BPA content, and curing time, while the three output neurons correspond to slump, compressive strength, and flexural strength. The hidden layer employed 18 neurons with a tansig activation function, and the output layer utilized a purelin function to ensure continuous prediction. The selection of 18 hidden neurons was achieved through an iterative trial-and-error process supported by a constrained hyperparameter search, in which different neuron configurations were evaluated based on model performance. Mean squared error (MSE) and the coefficient of determination (R2) were adopted as the primary criteria during training and validation to assess prediction accuracy and goodness of fit. The optimal architecture (6–18–3) was identified as it consistently produced the lowest MSE and highest R2 while maintaining good generalization and avoiding overfitting. The network was trained using a backpropagation learning algorithm, enabling efficient convergence and reliable prediction of the output variables (Fig. 11c).

Gene expression programming (GEP)

Gene Expression Programming (GEP) is a fascinating evolutionary algorithm that draws inspiration from nature. The computational approach takes the foundational ideas of genetic algorithms and genetic programming a step further by evolving computer programs designed to solve intricate prediction and optimization problems68. In GEP, solutions are represented as linear chromosomes, which are then transformed into expression trees (ETs) to depict the relationships among various variables. The journey begins with the creation of random chromosomes, followed by assessing each chromosome’s performance against training data through a fitness function. To foster diversity and enhance the exploration of the solution space, genetic operators such as mutation, crossover, and transposition are employed to tweak the chromosomes. Through a series of cycles involving evaluation, variation, and selection, the algorithm gradually hones the population of solutions until it meets a termination criterion. The best-performing chromosome from the final generation is selected as the optimal solution, highlighting GEP’s strength and versatility as a modeling tool69. In this research, GEP was applied to model and optimize the mechanical properties of BPA and SCBA blended geopolymer concrete. The model parameters used for the expression trees are summarized in Table 6, while Fig. 13a,c illustrate the generated ET structures and the relative importance of variables for slump, compressive strength, and flexural strength. Using GeneXpro Tools 5.0 software, three sub-ETs were developed and decoded into mathematical equations (Eqs. 1–3) to capture the relationships among key input factors. Sensitivity analysis revealed that slump was predominantly influenced by molarity, BPA, and SCBA, whereas compressive and flexural strengths were more sensitive to molarity and curing time. These findings underscore the ability of GEP to not only predict mechanical behaviour with high accuracy but also identify critical parameters influencing performance, thereby providing valuable insights for optimizing geopolymer concrete mixes70.

GPC morphological and mineralogical characterization

Morphological and mineralogical characterization of geopolymer concrete provides critical insights into its internal structure and the crystalline or amorphous phases that define its mechanical and durability performance. Using techniques such as Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS), the physical morphology, bonding characteristics, and mineral phases can be examined to understand how the geopolymerization process progresses and how well the reaction products are distributed within the matrix75. In this study, SCBA-BPA blended geopolymer concrete were examined at specific optimal levels: 52.5% SCBA combined with 47.5% BPA, and 60% SCBA with 40% BPA, both activated using 10 M NaOH. Minimal performance levels of 95% SCBA and 5% BPA with 4 M NaOH were further examined. These particular mixtures were selected based on their strength and slump responses, allowing us to draw connections between the microstructural characteristics and overall performance. Our analysis pinpointed essential minerals like amorphous silica, aluminosilicate gels (N-A-S-H), and unreacted particles. Additionally, our morphological observations highlighted aspects such as matrix densification, pore distribution, and the quality of interfacial bonding. This thorough characterization not only supports the mechanical strength findings but also sheds light on durability factors like resistance to cracking, permeability, and environmental degradation, ultimately aiding in the optimization of SCBA-BPA geopolymer concrete for real-world applications76.

ED-SEM results

The ED-SEM and EDS results provide technically robust evidence that can be defensibly correlated with the measured mechanical and durability performance of the SCBA–BPA geopolymer concrete. In the optimal 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA mix (Fig. 14a), the SEM images reveal a visibly compact and continuous gel matrix with minimal unreacted particles and limited interconnected pores, which aligns directly with its superior compressive strength and reduced permeability reported in the macroscopic tests. The corresponding EDS spectra quantitatively show high Si (54.04%), Al (10.01%), and Ca (23.48%) contents, indicating the simultaneous formation of N-A-S-H and Ca-modified aluminosilicate (C-A-S-H type) gels. The coexistence of these gels is widely recognized to promote pore refinement and matrix densification, thereby justifying the observed enhancement in strength and impermeability even in the absence of image-based porosity measurements77.

(a) SEM–EDS for 52.5% SCBA+47.5% BPA GPC sample. (b) SEM–EDS for 60% SCBA+40% BPA GPC sample. (c) SEM–EDS for 95% SCBA+5% BPA GPC sample

For the 60% SCBA–40% BPA blend (Fig. 14b), SEM micrographs show a comparatively heterogeneous matrix with visible voids and partially reacted particles, suggesting incomplete geopolymerization. This microstructural observation is supported by EDS results showing a dominant Ca content (61.15%) but relatively low Si (25.29%) and Al (3.82%), which implies an imbalance in aluminosilicate gel formation. While calcium-rich phases can contribute to early strength, insufficient Si–Al availability limits the development of a continuous geopolymer network, resulting in moderate strength and increased permeability compared to the optimal mix. Thus, the qualitative pore visibility and elemental imbalance consistently explain the numerically lower mechanical and durability indices obtained for this mixture, reinforcing the validity of SEM–EDS as a comparative diagnostic tool.

In the 95% SCBA–5% BPA mix (Fig. 14c), the SEM images clearly show extensive porosity, microcracks, and a fragmented matrix, all of which are hallmarks of poor gel cohesion and inefficient curing. The EDS spectra further reveal uneven elemental distribution with reduced effective Si–Al interaction, confirming weak aluminosilicate gel formation. These observations establish a clear causal pathway linking precursor chemistry → gel structure → pore connectivity → strength and durability, thereby resolving the disconnect between materials characterization and engineering performance78.

TGA test results

The Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) results provide critical insights into the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of SCBA-BPA blended geopolymer concrete, reflecting the efficiency of the geopolymerization process and the degree of matrix densification. The mix containing 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA exhibited the best thermal performance, with minimal weight loss at lower temperatures due to limited physically absorbed water, as shown in Fig. 15a. A slight dehydration peak occurred between 240 and 250 °C, with only a small mass reduction above 280 °C, indicating a highly compact, low-porosity matrix with strong chemical bonds, which confirms its exceptional thermal stability79. In contrast, the 60% SCBA and 40% BPA mix showed more pronounced mass loss, starting with 2–4% water loss up to 160 °C and a gradual 6–8% weight reduction from 210 °C onward (Fig. 15b), suggesting higher porosity and incomplete geopolymerization. Although moderately stable, its earlier decomposition relative to the optimal blend indicates lower resistance to thermal stress80. The mix with 95% SCBA and 5% BPA displayed the poorest thermal behavior, with high water-related mass loss (5–7% up to 100 °C) and significant decomposition beginning at 130–150 °C, leading to total weight loss exceeding 15% above 200 °C (Fig. 15c). This behavior reflects a weakly bonded, highly porous structure with poor matrix cohesion, limiting its suitability for high-temperature applications. Overall, the TGA results align with the microstructural and mechanical findings, demonstrating that optimal BPA incorporation enhances matrix densification and thermal resistance, while excessive SCBA reduces matrix cohesion and thermal stability, resulting in a more porous and less durable geopolymer network81.

FTIR results

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) serves as a critical technique for elucidating the molecular-scale reactions governing geopolymer concrete (GPC) formation, particularly the dissolution, polycondensation, and gel evolution processes. In SCBA–BPA blended systems, FTIR not only identifies functional groups but also provides insight into the nature and maturity of geopolymeric gels formed under varying precursor ratios and alkaline molarities82. The progressive shifts, intensities, and broadening of characteristic Si–O–T (T = Si or Al) bands reflect changes in the polymerization degree and the dominance of specific binding phases, such as sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N-A-S-H) and calcium aluminosilicate hydrate (C-A-S-H), as illustrated in Fig. 16a,c.

(a) FTIR spectra results for 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA GPC sample. (b) FTIR spectra for 60% SCBA + 40% BPA GPC sample. (c) FTIR spectra for 95% SCBA + 5% BPA GPC sample.

For the geopolymer concrete containing 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA, the FTIR spectrum reveals a chemically balanced system conducive to hybrid gel formation. The broad O–H stretching band at 3735–3700 cm−1 signifies structural hydroxyl groups and weakly bound water associated with geopolymer gels, indicating ongoing condensation reactions. The dominant asymmetric Si–O–T stretching peak centered at 1081.95 cm−1 is slightly shifted relative to raw precursors, confirming the transformation from crystalline silicates to an amorphous geopolymer network. This position and intensity suggest the coexistence of N–A–S–H gel derived from SCBA and C–A–S–H gel contributed by BPA, resulting in a dense and cross-linked hybrid matrix. The Al–O vibration at 515.81 cm−1 further confirms effective aluminum incorporation into the silicate backbone, which enhances network rigidity and mechanical stability.

In the 60% SCBA + 40% BPA blend, the FTIR spectrum shows increased silica-driven geopolymerization, with the main Si–O–T band shifting to a lower wavenumber (~ 986.26 cm−1. This downward shift is indicative of higher Al substitution within the silicate framework, characteristic of a more polymerized N–A–S–H–dominated gel structure. The presence of a pronounced H–O–H bending vibration at 1639.96 cm−1 reflects physically bound water trapped within a finer gel pore structure, suggesting improved gel homogeneity. Meanwhile, the reduced intensity of low-wavenumber calcium-related bands implies a diminished role of C–A–S–H gel, highlighting that increased SCBA content favors sodium aluminosilicate gel formation over calcium-rich phases.

Conversely, the geopolymer concrete with 95% SCBA and 5% BPA exhibits FTIR features indicative of silica-rich but chemically less synergistic geopolymerization. Although the strong Si–O–T band at ~ 1082.00 cm−1 confirms the formation of an aluminosilicate network, the reduced intensity and broadening of Al–O–Si bending bands suggest limited aluminum participation in the framework. This reflects a predominantly silica-rich N-A-S-H gel with lower cross-link density, likely due to insufficient calcium and reactive alumina from BPA. The broader O–H band (3826–3649 cm−1) further indicates higher retained moisture and incomplete condensation, pointing to a less compact and mechanically weaker gel structure despite successful geopolymerization.

Carbonate-related bands observed between 1421 and 1369 cm−1 across all mixtures are attributed to atmospheric carbonation of alkali-activated binders, particularly in high-alkali environments. However, their relative intensities vary significantly with mix composition. The moderate carbonate presence in the 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA blend suggests surface carbonation without disrupting the internal gel network, whereas higher carbonate intensity in high-SCBA systems reflects increased alkali availability and pore connectivity, which may adversely affect long-term durability if uncontrolled. This observation further underscores the role of calcium-rich BPA in refining pore structure and mitigating excessive carbonation.

Overall, the FTIR results demonstrate that gel chemistry evolution in SCBA–BPA geopolymer concrete is highly composition- and molarity-dependent. The 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA mix at 10 M achieves an optimal balance between N–A–S–H and C–A–S–H gel formation, producing a highly cross-linked hybrid matrix with superior structural integrity. Increasing SCBA content enhances silica-driven polymerization but shifts the system toward N–A–S–H dominance, while excessive SCBA combined with lower molarity (4 M) leads to underdeveloped gel networks and weaker chemical bonding. These findings confirm that careful optimization of SCBA-to-BPA ratio and alkaline molarity is essential for tailoring geopolymer gel chemistry, microstructural compactness, and mechanical performance in sustainable geopolymer concrete systems83.

XRD results

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis provides critical insight into the phase assemblage and reaction efficiency of geopolymer concrete by distinguishing between crystalline remnants of raw precursors and newly formed amorphous geopolymeric gels. In alkali-activated SCBA–BPA systems, the relative intensity, peak sharpness, and Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of diffraction peaks serve as indirect quantitative indicators of dissolution extent, gel formation, and microstructural densification. In particular, a balanced coexistence of residual crystalline phases and broad amorphous humps associated with aluminosilicate gels is widely correlated with improved mechanical strength and durability performance in geopolymer matrices.

For the GPC sample with 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA (Fig. 17a), the dominant diffraction peak at 27.2633° 2θ exhibits the highest relative intensity (100%) and a narrow FWHM, confirming the presence of well-crystallized quartz (SiO2). While quartz itself is largely inert, its controlled retention contributes to dimensional stability and load transfer within the matrix. Importantly, the presence of additional peaks at 24.6406°, 28.4955°, and 29.9362° 2θ, attributed to aluminosilicate phases, indicates partial dissolution of SCBA and BPA and subsequent reprecipitation into geopolymer gels. The relatively broadened peak observed at 45.6540° 2θ, compared to the quartz peak, signifies the formation of poorly ordered or semi-amorphous aluminosilicate structures, which are strongly associated with N–A–S–H and C–A–S–H gel formation. This balanced crystalline–amorphous distribution explains the superior mechanical performance observed for this mix, as amorphous gels provide binding strength while crystalline phases enhance stiffness and long-term stability84.

(a) XRD test results for 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA GPC sample. (b) XRD test results for 60% SCBA + 40% BPA GPC sample. (c) XRD test results for 95% SCBA + 5% BPA GPC sample.

In the 60% SCBA and 40% BPA blend (Fig. 17b), the most intense peak at 27.1021° 2θ (100%) again corresponds to quartz; however, the increased prominence of aluminosilicate-related peaks at 21.5698° 2θ (30.72%) and 24.5009° 2θ (24.09%) suggests enhanced precursor dissolution relative to the 52.5% SCBA mix. The broader diffraction features at 37.2340° and 43.1006° 2θ reflect a higher proportion of amorphous or semi-crystalline geopolymer gel, indicating increased geopolymerization driven by the higher silica content of SCBA. The appearance of secondary peaks at 40.2403° and 56.0680° 2θ further implies ongoing pozzolanic reactions, contributing to microstructural densification. The broad feature around 60.5202° 2θ is particularly significant, as it reflects residual amorphous geopolymeric gel, which is directly linked to improved crack resistance, reduced permeability, and enhanced durability under aggressive environmental exposure85.

For the GPC sample containing 95% SCBA and 5% BPA (Fig. 17c), the XRD pattern is dominated by crystalline phases, with the strongest quartz peak at 27.2259° 2θ (100%) and pronounced secondary peaks at 21.4418° 2θ (41.97%) and 50.5097° 2θ (42.44%). The high relative intensities and sharpness of these peaks indicate limited dissolution of SCBA particles, resulting in reduced geopolymeric gel formation. Although aluminosilicate-related peaks at 24.4568° and 37.0653° 2θ confirm some degree of geopolymerization, the comparatively weak amorphous contribution represented by the peak at 60.3988° 2θ (31.20%) which suggests an underdeveloped binding matrix. This phase assemblage implies lower interparticle bonding efficiency, which directly correlates with reduced compressive strength and higher porosity observed in high-SCBA, low-BPA systems86.

Overall, the XRD results establish a clear microstructure–property relationship in SCBA–BPA geopolymer concrete. The 52.5% SCBA–47.5% BPA mix achieves an optimal balance between crystalline quartz and amorphous geopolymer gels, leading to superior mechanical strength and durability. Increasing SCBA content to 60% enhances geopolymer gel formation and microstructural densification, potentially improving durability while maintaining adequate strength. However, excessive SCBA substitution (95% SCBA) results in a crystalline-dominated matrix with insufficient amorphous binding phases, undermining geopolymer efficiency. These findings quantitatively demonstrate that controlled crystalline retention combined with maximized amorphous gel formation is essential for optimizing the performance and sustainability of geopolymer concrete systems87.

Cost benefit analysis

A cost–benefit evaluation was conducted to assess the economic and environmental viability of BPA–SCBA–based geopolymer concrete (GPC) in comparison with conventional OPC-based systems. The partial substitution of OPC with banana peel ash and sugarcane bagasse ash significantly reduces reliance on energy-intensive cement clinker, resulting in lower raw material costs and a measurable reduction in CO2 emissions associated with cement production, thereby aligning the material with global sustainability and decarbonization targets88. In addition to emission mitigation, the utilization of BPA and SCBA promotes the valorization of agricultural waste, converting low-value by-products into functional construction materials and supporting circular economy principles while reducing landfill pressure89.

Beyond initial cost savings, BPA–SCBA GPC demonstrates notable long-term economic advantages arising from improved durability and performance stability. The integration of AI-based optimization techniques, including artificial neural networks (ANN) and gene expression programming (GEP), enabled systematic refinement of mix proportions to achieve mechanical properties comparable to, or exceeding, those of conventional concrete. This data-driven optimization minimizes material inefficiencies and overdesign, contributing to reduced construction waste and lower lifecycle maintenance and replacement costs. Collectively, these findings indicate that BPA–SCBA GPC offers a technically robust and economically competitive solution, delivering simultaneous environmental benefits and cost efficiency for sustainable large-scale infrastructure development90.

Cost analysis and economics implications

A cost–benefit analysis was carried out to compare BPA–SCBA–based geopolymer concrete (GPC) with conventional OPC concrete using unit prices reflective of local market rates in a Sub-Saharan African context. This comparison captures not only direct material costs per cubic metre but also associated labor and transportation expenses, which are particularly influential in low- and middle-income construction markets. Tables 10 and 11 summarize the cost structures and provide the basis for assessing economic viability and broader implications. For BPA–SCBA geopolymer concrete, the total production cost is USD 77.02/m3, compared with USD 86.77/m3 for OPC concrete, representing a cost saving of approximately 11.2%. The major economic advantage of the geopolymer system lies in the very low cost of SCBA and BPA precursors (USD 7.5/m3), which substantially offsets the relatively higher cost of alkaline activators, particularly sodium silicate (USD 22/m3). Although labor and transportation costs are slightly higher for GPC due to additional handling, solution preparation, and curing supervision, these increases are modest when compared to the significant reduction in binder cost achieved by replacing OPC with agro-waste-derived materials. In contrast, OPC concrete is dominated by cement cost alone (USD 48.75/m3), which accounts for more than half of the total concrete cost and is highly sensitive to energy prices and import dependence in Sub-Saharan Africa. The findings demonstrate that BPA–SCBA geopolymer concrete is not only technically viable but also economically competitive under local market conditions. The lower overall cost, combined with reduced reliance on imported cement, has important implications for affordable housing, rural infrastructure, and sustainable construction initiatives across Sub-Saharan Africa. Beyond direct cost savings, the utilization of agricultural waste materials adds value to local by-products, reduces landfill disposal, and lowers embodied carbon associated with cement production. Collectively, these economic and environmental benefits position BPA–SCBA geopolymer concrete as a strategically significant alternative to OPC concrete, particularly in regions seeking cost-effective, low-carbon, and locally sourced construction solutions.

Conclusion

This study explored the optimization of mechanical properties of BPA and SCBA blended geopolymer concrete, integrating laboratory characterization with advanced machine learning models for predictive analysis. Comprehensive material characterization confirmed that the aggregates and admixtures complied with established standards, while the fine particle distribution and chemical compositions of BPA and SCBA demonstrated their suitability as supplementary cementitious materials. High silica content in SCBA and elevated potassium levels in BPA proved crucial in enhancing geopolymerization, strengthening the aluminosilicate network, and promoting durable binder formation.

Experimental investigations revealed that mix design parameters including activator molarity, curing time, and binder-to-aggregate ratios play a decisive role in determining workability, density, compressive, and flexural strengths. Optimal performance was achieved at a sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide ratio of 2.5, an aggregate-to-binder ratio of 2, and an alkaline molarity of 10 M, producing maximum compressive (33.17 MPa) and flexural strength (9.95 MPa). These outcomes highlight the importance of balancing reactivity and packing density in tailoring geopolymer concrete for structural applications.

SCBA and BPA were characterized as geopolymer precursors using Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA). The results confirmed that both materials possess high silica content and pozzolanic properties, making them suitable for geopolymerization. The microstructural analysis further revealed that higher alkaline activator molarity led to a denser and more compact matrix, improving the mechanical strength of the geopolymer concrete.

Furthermore, the comparative modeling results demonstrated the effectiveness of both GEP and ANN in predicting mechanical behavior, with GEP showing a slight edge in predictive accuracy and generalization, particularly for slump and compressive strength. ANN, however, performed competitively in flexural strength prediction, confirming its strength in capturing nonlinear variability.

In conclusion, BPA–SCBA blended geopolymer concrete presents a sustainable, high-performance alternative to ordinary Portland cement, offering improved strength, enhanced durability, and reduced environmental impact. The integration of GEP and ANN provides powerful tools for optimizing mix proportions and predicting performance outcomes with high accuracy, supporting data-driven decision-making in sustainable construction. These findings not only advance the scientific understanding of agro-waste-based geopolymers but also open new pathways for industrial-scale adoption of eco-friendly concrete solutions, contributing to carbon reduction and resilient infrastructure development.

Recommendations for future studies

Recommendations for future studies which would contribute to the development of more effective and sustainable geopolymer concrete formulations through AI-driven insights include:

Larger and more diverse dataset

Future research should build on the current dataset by incorporating a wider range of experimental conditions, including varied binder-to-aggregate ratios, different alkaline activator concentrations, multiple curing regimes, and environmental exposure scenarios. A more diverse dataset would increase the generalization ability of AI models, improving their accuracy and robustness across different applications.

Inclusion of advanced AI models

While Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) have proven effective, future studies should explore more advanced computational approaches such as deep learning, hybrid models (e.g., combining GEP with ANN), and ensemble learning techniques. These models have the potential to capture complex nonlinear interactions between material properties and mix proportions, thereby enhancing predictive accuracy and decision support.

Multi-objective optimization

Research should move beyond single-property optimization and focus on multi-objective frameworks that simultaneously consider compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths, along with sustainability factors like embodied energy, cost efficiency, and carbon footprint reduction. Such approaches would enable the design of geopolymer concrete mixes that balance structural performance with environmental responsibility.

Durability and long-term performance

While mechanical strengths provide an initial measure of suitability, long-term durability remains critical for real-world adoption. Future studies should evaluate resistance to sulfate attack, chloride ingress, freeze–thaw cycles, carbonation, and creep. Incorporating these factors into AI models will help predict service life and ensure the reliability of geopolymer concretes in different climatic and environmental conditions.

Integration of life cycle assessment (LCA)

Incorporating LCA into future work will allow researchers to assess the environmental benefits of using BPA and SCBA beyond mechanical performance. This holistic approach will quantify reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, energy consumption, and resource depletion, strengthening the case for agro-waste-based geopolymer concrete as a sustainable alternative to conventional cementitious materials.

Validation in real-world applications

Laboratory-scale findings need to be validated in field trials and pilot-scale construction projects. Testing AI-predicted mix designs in real construction environments—such as pavements, structural beams, and blocks—will ensure the reliability and practical applicability of the models, while also identifying potential scale-up challenges related to mixing, curing, and quality control.

Investigation of additional waste materials

Expanding the material scope to include other agro-wastes (e.g., rice husk ash, palm oil fuel ash) and industrial by-products (e.g., fly ash, slag) in synergy with BPA and SCBA could provide broader insights into optimizing geopolymer concretes. This would also diversify raw material options and enhance sustainability by valorizing multiple waste streams.
