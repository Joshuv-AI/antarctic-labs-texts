Active cytomegalovirus infection in acute respiratory distress syndrome patients: key points requiring attention
Authors: Zhihui Zhang, Xuesong Liu, Rong Zhang, Sibei Chen, Dongdong Liu, Ying Pan, Chun Yang, Ling Sang, Yonghao Xu, Yimin Li, Xiaoqing Liu
Published: 2026

We noted with interest the clinical research conducted by Ma et al. [1]. This prospective study demonstrated that cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients was associated with multiple adverse clinical outcomes. Multi-omics profiling revealed distinct biological alterations characterized by compartmentalized immune responses and metabolic dysregulation. As an extension of the work by Ong et al. [2], these findings delineated the molecular determinants of CMV reactivation in ARDS, offering both mechanistic insights and clinically actionable therapeutic targets for CMV management. However, further elaboration of specific details would have strengthened the study’s depth and provided more comprehensive insights into the dynamics of active CMV infection in patients with ARDS.

First, analyses of distinct ARDS subphenotypes may provide guidance for developing more precise therapeutic strategies for active CMV infection. In recent years, the concept of ARDS subphenotypes has received increasing attention. Distinct ARDS subphenotypes (primarily characterized as hyperinflammatory and hypoinflammatory) have been identified based on clinical features and biomarkers reflecting coagulopathy, endothelial injury, and inflammatory responses [3]. These subphenotypes exhibit differential responses to ventilatory strategies, fluid management, and pharmacological interventions. Notably, the hyperinflammatory subphenotype is characterized by elevated proinflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6), increased endothelial injury biomarkers, more severe shock, and higher rates of metabolic acidosis. Patients with this subphenotype demonstrate significantly higher mortality compared to those with the hypoinflammatory subphenotype. Importantly, animal studies have demonstrated that hyperinflammatory states are more prone to CMV reactivation [4], but this association remains unconfirmed in ARDS populations. Therefore, the incidence of CMV reactivation, associated changes in clinical outcomes, and underlying risk factors across different ARDS subphenotypes warrant further investigation. Furthermore, whether prophylactic or preemptive anti-CMV therapy confers clinical benefit in different ARDS subphenotypes remains to be determined.

Second, defining CMV positivity in lower respiratory tract specimens (endotracheal aspirate and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) as reactivation lacks rigor, but CMV detection at these sites carries distinct clinical implications. The detection of CMV in blood specimens above a predefined threshold currently represents the established criterion for defining “CMV reactivation”. In the present study, the CMV positivity rate in blood specimens was approximately 17%, consistent with our team’s previous findings [5]. Nevertheless, further stratification of immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients is warranted, as the incidence of CMV reactivation is significantly higher in the former than in the latter. Furthermore, CMV reactivation is associated with multiple factors, including geographic region, sanitary conditions, and age [6, 7]. Of particular note, CMV positivity in lower respiratory tract specimens is of distinct clinical significance, as CMV reactivation in the lower respiratory tract is an independent risk factor for mortality in critically ill patients [8]. Surveillance of CMV in these specimens may have greater clinical relevance than in blood specimens, particularly for patients with pulmonary ARDS. CMV positivity in blood specimens generally indicates progression to an advanced disease stage [9], whereas detection in lower respiratory tract specimens may precede that in blood. Given that the pulmonary microenvironment in ARDS patients is characterized by heightened inflammation, inflammatory mediators (e.g., lipopolysaccharide, and tumor necrosis factor-α) may promote CMV reactivation, thereby inducing lung injury or fibrosis and contributing to adverse clinical outcomes [4, 5]. Therefore, prophylactic or preemptive antiviral therapy based on CMV positivity in lower respiratory tract specimens may improve clinical outcomes in ARDS patients, pending confirmation through clinical and mechanistic studies. Moreover, stratified analysis of CMV viral load levels across specimen types (blood and lower respiratory tract) could further identify patients at high risk for CMV reactivation, thereby informing early intervention strategies. Furthermore, comparative evaluation of different CMV detection methods in ARDS patients, including molecular assays (CMV DNA), serological markers (CMV IgG and IgM), and antigen detection (CMV pp65 antigenemia) warrants investigation.

Third, proteomics and metabolomics-based screening for specific therapeutic targets remains inherently limited and poses significant challenges for near-term clinical translation. The transition from CMV latency to reactivation is tightly regulated by complex immune mechanisms. Therefore, single-cell multi-omics sequencing combined with spatial transcriptomics may identify molecular alterations in distinct immune cell subsets during CMV reactivation along with their spatial context and microenvironmental interactions, thus enabling the identification of more specific cellular and molecular therapeutic targets [10]. Furthermore, recent studies have identified programmed death 1 (PD-1) as a critical mediator of immune dysregulation in CMV reactivation, suggesting that immunotherapy for these patients holds considerable promise [11]. Specifically, repurposing PD-1 inhibitors (currently widely used in oncology) to suppress CMV reactivation represents a compelling therapeutic strategy that warrants further investigation.

Fourth, CMV PCR results in this study were reported in copies/mL, a unit highly dependent on the specific PCR platform and assay kit definitions. Conversion to the current international standard unit of IU/mL is required [12]. Additionally, stratification based on distinct viral load levels is necessary to evaluate clinical outcomes and molecular differences among ARDS patients across different viral load strata. Furthermore, the interaction between airway CMV and other pathogens warrants further investigation.

In summary, the study by Ma et al. established a significant association between CMV reactivation and adverse outcomes in ARDS while characterizing compartment-specific biological alterations through multi-omics analysis, thereby advancing our understanding of this complex phenomenon. Further investigation into the causal mechanisms underlying these divergent immune-metabolic signatures and their organ-specific implications is essential for translating these findings into targeted therapeutic interventions for critically ill patients.
