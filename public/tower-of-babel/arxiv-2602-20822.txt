Hölder-Logarithmic Stability and Convergence Rates for an Inverse Random Source Problem Thanks: Submitted to the editors January 30th 2026.
Philipp Ronald Mickan, Thorsten Hohage

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate an inverse random source problem concerned with recovering the strength of a random, uncorrelated acoustic source from correlation measurements of emitted time-harmonic acoustic waves. Such problems arise in applications including aeroacoustics and seismic imaging. Unlike their deterministic counterparts, inverse random source problems are known to be uniquely solvable in the absence of noise. Nevertheless, due to their inherent ill-posedness, regularization is required to stably reconstruct the source strength. We derive conditional Hölder-logarithmic stability estimates under Sobolev smoothness assumptions by employing complex geometrical optics solutions. Moreover, by establishing a variational source condition, we obtain Hölder-logarithmic convergence rates for spectral regularization methods. At fixed frequency, the exponents in the logarithmic stability and convergence estimates grow unboundedly as the Sobolev regularity of the source increases. Finally, we present numerical experiments supporting our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Inverse source problems consist in finding the right-hand side of a differential equation given (usually incomplete) measurements of the solution.
Sources produced by uncontrolled processes, such as turbulence, are naturally and advantageously modeled as random processes.
This is done in a number of applications including
passive seismic imaging
[
3
,
4
]
,
helioseismic holography
[
1
,
29
,
33
]
,
noise localization in aeroacoustics
[
19
,
22
]
,
or ocean tomography
[
5
]
.
Inverse problems for virtual random sources also appear in the context of imaging in random media
[
13
]
.
The waves excited by the unknown source and measured to extract information about it are realizations of a random solution process of a partial differential equation.
The stochastic characteristics of these solution processes constitute the idealized, noise-free data of the inverse problem.

Our work is specifically motivated by noise localization in aeroacoustics and helioseismic holography. Here idealized noise-free data are given
by covariance operators of solution processes, and available noisy input data by statistical estimators, namely sample covariances computed from the measured wave fields. The quantity to be recovered in the inverse problem is the pointwise covariance of the source, which is modelled as an uncorrelated process.

In such settings uniqueness results can be established
[
10
,
19
]
even for single-frequency boundary data, which is not possible for deterministic inverse source problems. In the same references it is also shown
that without any assumptions on the covariance of a centered Gaussian random source process, the inverse problem of recovering
its covariance structure is not unique.
The aim of the present paper is to extend the aforementioned uniqueness results to conditional stability estimates
and convergence rates for a random inverse source problem.

Let us discuss some related works in the literature: In
[
28
,
40
]
,
stability is established for the one-dimensional multi-frequency inverse random source problem governed by the Helmholtz equation
using an explicit inversion formula for the Fourier transform of the source strength.
They obtain Hölder-logarithmic stability depending on a low-frequency average of the data given by the difference of the variances and means at the boundary, exploiting improved stability of a multi-frequency approach as presented in
[
6
]
.
These works make use of real-valuedness of the random sources whereas in the applications motivating our work, sources have to be modelled by
complex-valued stochastic processes.

A different type of results has been established in
[
24
]
for waves scattered by a random potential modelled by a Gaussian random process whose covariance operator is a pseudodifferential operator: It was shown that intensities of a solution to the differential equation
for only one realization of the random process, averaged over all frequencies, uniquely determine the local strength of the potential.
Similar results for a random source instead of a random potential were established in
[
25
,
27
]
.
Furthermore, over the last years considerable progress has been achieved in establishing uniqueness and stability results for
different types of time-domain passive imaging problems, see, e.g.,
[
12
,
16
,
34
,
37
]
.

The work closest to the current paper is
[
26
]
which establishes logarithmic stability estimates in the frequency domain.
We improve these results in several important ways: Most importantly, the constants in
the logarihmic stability estimates in
[
26
]
deteriorate as the
wave number tends to infinity whereas our bounds yield a Hölder rate if the
wave number grows sufficiently rapidly as the noise level tends to
0

(see
remark

only establish a stability estimate, i.e., an estimate on differences of elements in
the range of the forward operator, but also convergence rates of spectral regularization

Problem formulation

Most of this paper deals with the random inverse source problem for the Helmholtz equation

\Delta u+\kappa^{2}u=-Q\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}

(1)

with a random source

in a bounded domain

Extensions to the convected Helmholtz equation relevant in aeroacoustics will

.
We make the following assumptions on the random source

.

Assumption 1
(source process).

Let

be an open and bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\mathbf{0}\in D\subset B(r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

, such that the covariance operator
\Cov[Q]\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(D))
is the multiplication operator

for all

In the following

In contrast to the setting in
[

]

to be the boundary of a bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

Assumption 2
(measurement surface).

Let

be the boundary of a bounded Lipschitz domain

containing

for some
\tilde{r}>r
.
Moreover, suppose that

has a finite surface area and that

The data from which we wish to recover the random source is the sample covariance

\Cov_{N}(x,y):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}u_{l}(x)\overline{u_{l}(y)}\text{ for }x,y\in\M

With the well-known fundamental solution to the Helmholtz equation (

G(x,y):=\frac{\exp(i\kappa|x-y|)}{4\pi|x-y|}

(3)

(cf.
[

),
the solution process to equation (

) is described by

u(x)=\int_{D}G(x,z)Q(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(4)

We refer to
[
18
]
for a more careful analysis in which sense (
4
) defines a solution to (
1
).
By
2
the time-harmonic pressure signal can be formulated as the image under the
volume-potential operator

\mathcal{G}\colon L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(\M)
defined as

(\mathcal{G}\psi)(x):=\int_{D}G(x,z)\psi(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{ for }x\in\M.

(5)

As there is a positive distance between the source region and the measurement region
by
2
and

is infinitely smooth
off the diagonal,

is infinitely smooth.
As a random process, it has a covariance function, which
under
1
is given by

c_{q}(x,y):=\mathtt{Cov}(u(x),u(y))=\int_{D}G(x,z)q(z)\overline{G(y,z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\quad\text{ for }x,y\in\M.

This yields the covariance operator

\displaystyle\Cov[u]\colon L^{2}(\M)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\M)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{\M}c_{q}(\cdot,s)\phi(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

which is clearly well defined as
G(x,z)\overline{G(y,z)}

is continuous in all three variables.
Note that the covariance operator only depends on the deterministic source strength
q
of the random source process
Q
. Thus, the forward operator of our inverse problem
is deterministic and given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}\colon L^{\infty}(D,[0,\infty))&\to\HS(L^{2}(\M))\\
q&\mapsto\mathcal{G}M_{q}\mathcal{G}^{\ast}.\end{aligned}

(6)

Here
\HS(L^{2}(\M))
denotes the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators in

, which is
the space of compact linear operators

with square summable
singular values equipped with either the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the sequence of singular values
or the
L^{2}(\M\times\M)
-norm of the integral kernel

:

\|C(q)\|_{\HS(L^{2}(\M))}=\|c_{q}\|_{L^{2}(\M\times\M)}

(cf.
[
36
, Theorem VI.23]
).
This implies that the forward operator
\mathcal{C}
has a unique continuous extension to a bounded linear
operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to\HS(L^{2}(\M))
with norm bounded by

\|\mathcal{C}\|_{L^{2}(D)\to\HS(L^{2}(\M))}\leq\|(x,y,z)\mapsto G(x,z)\overline{G(y,z)}\|_{L^{2}(\M\times\M\times D)},

and the right-hand side is clearly finite since the kernel is infinitely smooth, and
D
and
\M
are bounded.

Stability estimates and convergence rates

A function
\phi\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is called an index function if it is continuous and monotonically increasing and if
\phi(0)=0

Let
X
and

be Hilbert spaces and

be a set of candidate solutions satisfying some prior information. An inequality of the form

\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{X}\leq\phi\big(\|F(f_{1})-F(f_{2})\|_{Y}\big)\text{ for all }f_{1},f_{2}\in U

conditional stability estimate with index function

\phi
for the set

. Such stability estimates characterize the
modulus of continuity of
(F|_{U})^{-1}
(or generalized inverses) and thus provide insight into the degree of ill-posedness of the inverse problem under the prior information
that the true solution belongs to
U
.

Variational source conditions

Variational source conditions
for general non-smooth operators of Banach spaces
F\colon X\to Y
were introduced in
[
17
]

to deduce convergence rates for Tikhonov regularization.
These conditions on

are formulated as a variational inequality

\forall f\in\dom(F):\quad\frac{1}{2}\|f^{\dagger}-f\|^{2}_{X}\leq\|f\|_{X}^{2}-\|f^{\dagger}\|^{2}_{X}+\Psi\left(\|F(f)-F(f^{\dagger})\|_{Y}^{2}\right)

(11)

with
\Psi
some index function.
The aim of this section is to review a general method for the verification of such conditions and to show how
a variational inequality of the form
eq.

11
implies the two main
theorems

3
and
4

We first argue that if a variational source conditions with index
function
\Psi
is satisfied for all
f^{\dagger}
in some subset
U\subset\dom(F)
, then a conditional stability estimate holds true for
U
:
Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in U
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\|f_{2}\|\leq\|f_{1}\|
. Then setting
f^{\dagger}=f_{1}
and
f=f_{2}
in
eq.

11
yields a conditional stability estimate of the from of
eq.

7
with the index function
\phi=\sqrt{2\Psi(\cdot^{2})}
.
From
[
21
]
we get an equivalence of variational source
conditions and convergence rates of spectral regularization methods meeting certain assumptions.

Hence, establishing a variational source condition for the random inverse source problem in
Section

4
, yields a proof of both main theorems.
To find an explicit expression for the index function
\Psi
that determines the behavior of the conditional stability
eq.

7
and the convergence rate

10

]

Let

, let

for

\displaystyle\|f^{\dagger}-P_{r}f^{\dagger}\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(r),\hskip 10.00002pt\inf_{r\in\mathcal{I}}\kappa(r)=0

\displaystyle\langle f^{\dagger},P_{r}(f^{\dagger}-f)\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(r)\|F(f^{\dagger})-F(f)\|_{Y}+\Gamma\kappa(r)\|f^{\dagger}-f\|_{X}

Proof of

As pointed out before, the main difficulty in the verification of the variational source condition in

The proof uses geometrical optics solutions as in
[

Complex geometrical optics solutions
(CGOS), first introduced by Faddeev
[
11
]
, are spatial exponentially growing solutions to the considered homogeneous partial differential equation under consideration. Their existence is known for many different equations (cf.
[
15
,
38
]
). In case of the Helmholtz equation
eq.

1
with homogeneous background the complex geometric solutions are given for each
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\xi\cdot\xi=\kappa^{2}
by

u_{\xi}(x)=\exp(\imath\xi\cdot x)\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(14)

Note these functions look like plane waves which are exponentially growing in the direction of
-\Im(\xi)
.
The main idea of this method is to choose one direction

\imath\xi
and its conjugate direction so that their product
is a plain wave of arbitrary frequency, which we can control by the imaginary part of
\xi
. Hence, we can write the integral kernel of the Fourier transform as a product of two such solutions. In this way, CGOSs can be used to bound the Fourier coefficients of the source strength, and this will be
used to derive the ill-posedness estimate
eq.

12b
for the inverse random source problem.

In preparation of the proof of the next lemma we introduce the interior Dirichlet problem in some bounded domain
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with boundary

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta u+\kappa^{2}u&=0&&\text{ in }\mathcal{O},\\

Define the

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathtt{P}\colon H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\M)&\to H^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\\

and the

by

\displaystyle S\colon H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\M)

\displaystyle\to H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\M)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{\M}G(x,y)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad y\in\M.

Recall that if the boundary

is a solution to the interior and exterior Dirichlet problem for the Helmholtz equation. The jump relations for the single-layer operator

yield that the interior and exterior trace of

Extension to convected Helmholtz equation

In aeroacoustics, one of the main fields of appplication, the convected Helmholtz equation is used as a model for sound propagation in a homogeneous background flow within a subsonic regime.
Therefore, in this section we briefly discuss the extension of our results to this equation.
Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represent the constant flow field. The Mach vector, denoted by
\mathfrak{m}=\frac{1}{c}v

with
c

Let us introduce the Mach scaled distance as

[
19
]
. Where

and
2

(33)

Similar to the Helmholtz problem we define the volume-potential operator as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\colon L^{2}(D)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\M)

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{D}G_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\kappa}(\cdot,z)\psi(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

theorem

4
.

Numerical setup

First we present the discretization of sources in the domain. We assume that source are supported in the cube
D=\Big[-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\Big]^{3}
.
Functions
f\colon D\to[0,\infty)
are represented by their values on a uniform rectangular grid of
60\times 60\times 60
points. To study the effect of smoothness of the exact solution on convergence rates, exact source strengths
q^{\dagger}
are constructed by multivariate splines of degree
1
or
3
. More precisely, let
\{b_{1}^{\lambda},\dots,b_{n}^{\lambda}\}
be the set of B-splines of degree
\lambda\in\{1,3\}
on
[-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}]
associated with equidistant knots dividing the interval into 12 subintervals. We define a basis of functions
\{f^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}\}_{k,l,m\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
on the cube
\Big[-\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\Big]^{3}
by

f^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2}):=b^{\lambda}_{k}(x_{1})b^{\lambda}_{l}(x_{2})b^{\lambda}_{m}(x_{3})=(b^{\lambda}_{k}\otimes b^{\lambda}_{l}\otimes b^{\lambda}_{m})(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}).

Note that
b^{\lambda}_{m}\in H^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\epsilon>0
. (For
\lambda=0
this follows from explicit computations, and for
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
we use the fact that

\frac{d^{\lambda}}{dx^{\lambda}}b^{\lambda}_{m}
is piecewise constant.) It follows that
f^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}\in H^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}(D)
.
We can represent each multivariate spline in this basis by spline coefficients
(c^{\lambda}_{k,l,m})_{k,l,m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
where
n
is the dimension of the one-dimensional spline space. Moreover,
q^{\dagger}:=\sum_{k,l,m}c^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}f^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}
is nonnegative if all
c^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}
are nonnegative since B-splines are nonnegative.
In our experiments, we use two types of
q^{\dagger}
, each for
\lambda=1
and
\lambda=3
: in the first type the coefficients are generated randomly so that
c^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}\sim|X^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}|
with
X^{\lambda}_{k,l,m}\sim N(0,1)
i.i.d. The second type of functions approximate a half-sphere and a dot,
as illustrated in
figs.

1(a)
and
1(d)
.

Measurements are taken on
\M=S_{R}
as a sphere of radius
R
, where functions in
L^{2}(S_{R})
are expanded in the standard (orthonormal) basis of spherical harmonics. This is convenient since the single layer potential
\mathtt{P}
in
eq.

16
can be written explicitly in this basis by spherical Hankel and Bessel functions. The same holds true for the volume potential
\mathcal{G}
and its adjoint
\mathcal{G}^{*}
as the latter essentially coincides with
\mathtt{P}
. The codomain of the forward operator is the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
L^{2}(S_{R})
, which are represented by matrices in the spherical harmonics basis.

Synthetic data are generated through autocorrelation of solutions to the Helmholtz problem from random samples of sources with variance given by the exact solution
q^{\dagger}
. More precisely,
\underline{q}^{\dagger}
denotes the discrete representation of the exact solution
q^{\dagger}
as described above, and let
\underline{\mathcal{G}}
denote the discretization of the volume-potential operator
\mathcal{G}
mapping
\underline{q}^{\dagger}
to the spherical harmonics coefficients. Then the discrete noisy data of the inverse problem is described by

\underline{C}_{\text{obs}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G\xi_{i}(G\xi_{i})^{\ast}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with iid}\quad\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\underline{q}^{\dagger})

where
N
denotes the number of samples. The discrete exact solution is given by

\underline{C}^{\dagger}=\underline{\mathcal{G}}\diag(\underline{q}^{\dagger})\underline{\mathcal{G}}^{\ast}.

The numerical setup involves the following four parameters:

(i)

the measurement distance, i.e., the radius
R
of the measurement sphere which will be fixed with
R=4
,

(ii)

the wave number
\kappa
,

(iii)

the smoothness of the exact solution
q^{\dagger}

determined by the spline degree
\lambda
,

(iv)

and the smoothness of reconstructions determined by the index
m
of the squared Sobolev
norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{m}}^{2}
used as
penalty term in the Tikhonov functional.

For each tuple
(R,\kappa,\lambda,m)
we
generate synthetic data by sampling for 18 different values of
N
in a range of
50
to
92500
.
This leads to different noise levels proportional to
N^{-1/2}
. For reconstruction, we apply classical Tikhonov regularization

\hat{q}_{\alpha}:=\argmin_{q\in H^{m}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{C}(q)-C_{\text{obs}}\|_{\HS(L^{2}(S_{R}))}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|q\|_{H^{m}(D)}^{2}\right],

(37)

using the CG method to find the minimum.
We sequentially lower the regularization parameter until a discrepancy rule is satisfied.

We observe that the theoretical logarithmic convergence orders are upper bounds of the numerically observed
convergence orders, but they are larger by a small factor. This may indicated that the exponents in our
upper bounds can be improved by a small factor, and indeed we do not have any proof or heuristic argument
of optimality.
However, we do not consider our numerical results as a strong indication for suboptimality of our upper bounds
sincean accurate numerical estimation of the optimal exponent in logarithmic stability estimates
is challenging for several reasons: For capturing the true asymptotic behavior, the data noise level must be very small, and to ensure that other source of errors such as discretization errors are not dominant, very fine discretizations are required which are limited by computational resources.
(E.g., we do not know if a slow-down of the convergence rate observable in some of the curves in

happens for all the curves at smaller noise levels.)
Moreover, the rates are defined
by a supremum over noise realizations which is difficult to realize numerically.

Wave number dependence for flat sources

Solution of the full 3D problem for wave numbers larger than
\kappa=12
was computationally infeasible
with our computational recources in view of the required fine discretizations in both the domain and the codomain.
To investigate the dependence of the limiting behavior on the wave number, we consider a setup in which the random sources are supported only in one slice of the cube
D
. In this case, sources can be regarded as supported in a thin layer, allowing us to discretize
f\colon D\to[0,\infty)
on a uniform two dimensional square grid of size
128\times 128
. Additionally, the noise model is simplified to additive Gaussian noise, which provides small noise levels without requiring an excessively large number of samples. To avoid an inverse crime the ground truth was generated on a finer uniform grid and used to construct the exact data
\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}
. In this setting we now can consider wave numbers in
\{7,14,28,42\}
by adapting the degree of the spherical harmonics.

Conclusion

In this work, we derived conditional stability estimates and convergence rates for spectral regularization methods applied to random inverse source problems under Sobolev-type smoothness assumptions on the unknown source strength.
For fixed wave number, the resulting estimates are logarithmic in the noise level. Their explicit dependence on the wave number, however, yields Hölder-type convergence rates in suitable joint limits as the wave number tends to infinity while the noise level vanishes.

Numerical experiments corroborate the predicted asymptotically logarithmic convergence behavior with respect to the noise level. The observed exponents increase with the Sobolev smoothness index and appear to be larger by a small factor than the corresponding theoretical values.
Moreover, in agreement with the theoretical analysis, the numerical results demonstrate that increasing the wave number shifts the onset of the asymptotic convergence regime to progressively smaller noise levels, leading to substantially improved reconstruction quality for practically relevant noise levels.

The present study opens several directions for future research, including a statistical convergence analysis that accounts for the Wishart distribution of correlation data with a specific covariance structure, stability results for the identification of unknown coefficients in partial differential equations from correlation data, and stability analyses for partial measurement data (e.g., open surfaces) or for particular classes of correlated sources.
