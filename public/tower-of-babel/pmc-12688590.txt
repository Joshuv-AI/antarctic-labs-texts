A Case of Portal Vein Thrombosis Due to Cholecystitis: A 10-Year Follow-Up Case Report
Authors: Tetsushi Azami, Yuichi Takano, Kozue Ito, Naoki Tamai, Jun Noda, Fumitaka Niiya, Masatsugu Nagahama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a rare case of portal vein thrombosis (PVT) secondary to acute cholecystitis with a 10-year follow-up. A 62-year-old man presented with fever and epigastric pain and was diagnosed with acute cholecystitis. Cholecystitis is a rare local intra-abdominal cause of PVT. After antibiotic treatment, jaundice developed, and imaging revealed left PVT. Anticoagulation was initiated, but thrombosis persisted. Despite the persistent thrombus, long-term follow-up revealed no portal hypertension; however, atrophy of the left lobe of the liver was observed. This case highlights the key aspects of the long-term natural history of cholecystitis-associated PVT and underscores the importance of timely diagnosis and management.LEARNING POINTSCholecystitis can be a cause of acute portal vein thrombosis.Early anticoagulation therapy is crucial for thrombus resolution.Portal vein thrombosis may lead to localized hepatic atrophy over the long term.

INTRODUCTION

Portal vein thrombosis (PVT) is widely recognized to be caused by liver cirrhosis and systemic diseases such as hematologic disorders. However, local inflammatory factors such as cholecystitis can also cause PVT, and several case reports have been documented. Nevertheless, reports on the long-term prognosis of PVT associated with cholecystitis are extremely limited, and many aspects of its natural history remain unclear. Herein, we report a rare case of PVT due to cholecystitis in which the thrombus persisted despite anticoagulant therapy, and we were able to follow the patient for 10 years.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 62-year-old man presented at a local clinic with fever and epigastric pain. His medical history included surgery (distal gastrectomy and Billroth II reconstruction) for duodenal ulcer at age 51. Blood tests and a non-contrast computed tomography (CT) led to the diagnosis of moderate acute cholecystitis, and he was admitted for conservative management. After improvement in abdominal pain and inflammatory marker levels with fasting and antibiotic therapy (cefoperazone/sulbactam), the patient was discharged. No medications were prescribed at discharge. However, 20 days after symptom onset, he developed jaundice and elevated hepatobiliary enzyme levels, suggesting the presence of cholangitis, and was referred to our hospital, despite the absence of liver dysfunction during his prior admission.

On admission, blood tests revealed levels of total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase of 6.0 mg/dl, 4.7 mg/dl, 79 U/l, 99 U/l, and 860 U/l, respectively, indicating hepatobiliary dysfunction. Coagulation parameters, including prothrombin time; activated partial thromboplastin time; and antithrombin III, protein C, and protein S levels, were within normal limits.

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed gallbladder wall thickening with collapse, but no biliary ductal abnormalities. However, a thrombus was identified in a branch of the left portal vein (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography similarly showed no biliary obstruction (Fig. 2). Liver dysfunction was diagnosed as a consequence of PVT, and anticoagulation therapy was initiated with danaparoid on day 1, followed by continuous intravenous heparin for 11 days and warfarin on day 7. Jaundice and liver enzyme elevations gradually improved, and the patient was discharged on day 12 (32 days after the initial symptom onset).

Follow-up CT confirmed the persistence of the thrombus, although hepatic function continued to recover. Three months later, the patient underwent elective cholecystectomy, with no evidence of malignancy upon pathological examination. Postoperatively, warfarin was continued; however, the thrombus persisted, suggesting that it had become organized, and anticoagulation was discontinued after 1 year. Over the subsequent 10-year follow-up, atrophy of the left lobe of the liver was noted (Fig. 3). Still, no signs of portal hypertension, such as esophagogastric varices or ascites, or abnormal liver function were observed.

DISCUSSION

PVT was classified based on the presence or absence of underlying cirrhosis. In non-cirrhotic cases, the contributing factors are broadly categorized as systemic (e.g., myeloproliferative neoplasms, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, and prothrombin gene mutations) or local (e.g., intra-abdominal infections, trauma, malignancy, and surgery), often in combination. Non-cirrhotic PVT typically presents acutely, with anticoagulation as the mainstay of treatment, and early initiation improves recanalization rates by approximately 40–45%[1].

Cholecystitis represents a rare local intra-abdominal cause of PVT. Table 1 summarizes previous reports of cholecystitis-associated PVT[2–9], including the present case. Among the nine detailed cases, the median age was 50 years (range 31–77 years), with seven male and two female patients. The thrombus locations included the main trunk (n = 4), left branch (n = 4), and right branch (n = 2), with the thrombus extending from the main trunk to the right branch in one patient. Liver dysfunction was observed in 89% of cases (n = 8). Anticoagulation was administered in all cases; recanalization was successful in three, unsuccessful in three, and unknown in three.

In this patient, the thrombus was confined to a branch of the left portal vein. The mechanisms of venous thrombosis include hypercoagulability, endothelial injury, and venous stasis. The inflammatory cytokines released during cholecystitis can induce a hypercoagulable state, whereas perivascular oedema and compression can contribute to stasis and endothelial injury. Yoshimitsu et al. reported that the cystic vein predominantly drains into the S5 (96%) and S4 (93%) branches of the portal vein[10], suggesting that the thrombus in our case may have originated in the cystic vein and propagated via the S4 branch to the left portal vein.

In this case, liver dysfunction was observed. Alternative causes, such as common bile duct stones and drug-induced liver injury, were considered but ruled out based on imaging findings and medication history. In previously reported cases of cholecystitis-associated PVT, liver dysfunction was documented in the majority of patients, even when the thrombus was confined to a portal vein branch (Table 1). It remains unclear whether the thrombus itself directly caused liver dysfunction, or whether factors such as cholestasis and systemic inflammation associated with cholecystitis acted as predisposing factors for PVT. Nevertheless, when liver dysfunction is encountered during the course of cholecystitis, the possibility of PVT should be taken into consideration and appropriately investigated.

Despite anticoagulation therapy, recanalization was not achieved. Excluding patients with unknown outcomes, the recanalization success rate was 50% (3/6). In previous reports, unsuccessful cases were characterized by delayed treatment initiation (e.g., at 54 or 30 days). In our case, anticoagulation therapy began 20 days after symptom onset, and this delay may have contributed to the persistent thrombus. In cases of cholecystitis, in addition to assessing disease severity, evaluation of portal vein blood flow (Doppler ultrasound or contrast CT) should be considered whenever possible.

In this case, the thrombus was confined to a branch of the left portal vein; therefore, portal hypertension did not develop. However, localized atrophy of the left lobe of the liver was observed, likely due to chronic reduction of portal blood flow. Long-term follow-up of cholecystitis-associated PVT is rare. This study provided valuable clinical insights.

In conclusion, although rare, cholecystitis is an important cause of PVT. Even with anticoagulation therapy, delayed initiation of treatment may result in persistent thrombus formation and subsequent localized hepatic atrophy. This case highlights the key aspects of the long-term natural history of cholecystitis-associated PVT and underscores the importance of timely diagnosis and management.
