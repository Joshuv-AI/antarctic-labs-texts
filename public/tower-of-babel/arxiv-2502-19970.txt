Quantum generative classification with mixed states
Diego H. Useche, Sergio Quiroga-Sandoval, Sebastian L. Molina, Vladimir Vargas-Calderón, Juan E. Ardila-García, Fabio A. González

Abstract
Classification can be performed using either a discriminative or a generative learning approach. Discriminative learning consists of constructing the conditional probability of the outputs given the inputs, while generative learning consists of constructing the joint probability density of the inputs and outputs. Although most classical and quantum methods are discriminative, there are some advantages of the generative learning approach. For instance, it can be applied to unsupervised learning, statistical inference, uncertainty estimation, and synthetic data generation. In this article, we present a quantum generative multiclass classification strategy, called quantum generative classification (QGC). This model uses a variational quantum algorithm to estimate the joint probability density function of features and labels of a data set by means of a mixed quantum state. We also introduce a quantum map called quantum-enhanced Fourier features (QEFF), which leverages quantum superposition to prepare high-dimensional data samples in quantum hardware using a small number of qubits. We show that the quantum generative classification algorithm can be viewed as a Gaussian mixture that reproduces a kernel Hilbert space of the training data. In addition, we developed a hybrid quantum-classical neural network that shows that it is possible to perform generative classification on high-dimensional data sets. The method was tested on various low- and high-dimensional data sets including the 10-class MNIST and Fashion-MNIST data sets, illustrating that the generative classification strategy is competitive against other previous quantum models.



I
Introduction

Classification models can be trained using discriminative or generative learning. In the discriminative approach, we learn a function that approximates the conditional probability of the outputs given the inputs
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
, while in the generative approach we approximate the joint probability of the inputs and the outputs
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are random variables that correspond to characteristics and labels, respectively. Classification is made based on the class with the highest conditional probability for discriminative models and the highest joint probability for generative models. Most supervised learning algorithms are discriminative
Boser et al. 1992
;
Rumelhart et al. 1986
;
LeCun et al. 1998
;
Berkson 1944
;
Breiman 2001
, generally reporting better predictive performance
Bernardo et al. 2007
and easier trainability compared to generative algorithms
Harshvardhan et al. 2020
. Nonetheless, some advantages of generative models
Kingma and Welling 2022
;
Ackley et al. 1985
;
Papamakarios et al. 2021
;
Rasmussen 2004
;
Goodfellow et al. 2014
;
Baum and Petrie 1966
include the potential to generate new instances that resemble the original data
Harshvardhan et al. 2020
, the ability to estimate the uncertainty of the predictions
Jebara 2004
, and their feasibility to be trained with unlabeled data
Bernardo et al. 2007
.

Although most supervised learning algorithms have been developed for classical computers
Boser et al. 1992
;
Rumelhart et al. 1986
;
LeCun et al. 1998
;
Breiman 2001
;
Ackley et al. 1985
;
Rasmussen 2004
, there is growing interest in using quantum computers for classification. Most of these quantum models fall into the category of discriminative learning
Schuld et al. 2020
;
Farhi and Neven 2018
;
Watkins et al. 2023
;
Altares-López et al. 2021
;
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Park et al. 2023
, while there are only a few quantum algorithms for generative classification
Benedetti et al. 2017
;
Zoufal et al. 2021
;
Chaudhary et al. 2023
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
. This recent enthusiasm for quantum machine learning is driven by the fact that it is possible to exploit the probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics for machine learning, and that some works
Harrow et al. 2009
;
Shor 1994
have shown that quantum computers could potentially reduce the computational complexity of some classically hard problems.

In this article, we present a variational quantum algorithm for classification called quantum generative classification (QGC) that follows the generative learning approach. It uses a variational quantum circuit to represent the joint probability of inputs and outputs
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
through a mixed quantum state. The proposed algorithm maps the features and labels of a training data set
\mathscr{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
to quantum states

}\}

\{\bm{y}_{j}\}\mapsto\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^{j}}\}

\ket{q_{_{\mathcb{A}, \mathcb{X}, \mathcb{Y}}}({\boldsymbol\theta})}

\mathcal{Y}
), inputs (
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{A}

\bm{\theta}
correspond to the parameters of the optimization, such that when tracing out the auxiliary qubits

we obtain a mixed state of the data set

that approximates the joint probability of features and labels

.

The proposed quantum generative classification method can also be viewed as a model that represents the data in a reproducing a kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
González et al. 2024
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
Schuld and Petruccione 2021

Indeed, it prepares the data in a quantum computer using a one-hot basis encoding for the outputs and a proposed quantum embedding called quantum-enhanced Fourier features (QEFF) for the inputs. The QEFF mapping is based on random Fourier features (RFF)

Furthermore, we demonstrate the feasibility of integrating the variational quantum method with a classical deep neural network using a quantum simulator from the

•

•

We present an end-to-end quantum-classical strategy for classification that combines a quantum generative classifier with deep neural networks.

Related work

Generative machine learning has been widely studied in classical computers, of which some popular models include hidden Markov models

;
Zoufal et al. 2019
;
Romero and Aspuru-Guzik 2021
, quantum circuit Born machines

, quantum Gaussian mixture models

;
Kerenidis et al. 2020
, quantum Boltzmann machines

;
Zoufal et al. 2021
;
Benedetti et al. 2017
, quantum measurement classification (QMC)

;
González et al. 2022
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
, and kernel density matrices (KDM)
González et al. 2024
.

Out of the previously presented methods, the proposed quantum generative classification algorithm shares some key similarities and differences with the models presented in Refs.
Amin et al. 2018
;
Zoufal et al. 2021
;
González et al. 2021
;
González et al. 2022
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
González et al. 2024
;
Schuld and Petruccione 2021
. For instance, Refs.
Amin et al. 2018
;
Zoufal et al. 2021
proposed a quantum Boltzmann machine that learns the joint probability
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
based on the preparation of a mixed Gibbs state in a quantum computer. One key feature of Boltzmann machines is that they were designed to learn discrete probability distributions, unlike our proposed QGC model, which can be applied to both continuous and discrete probability distributions. In addition, the presented work is closely related to the QMC and the QMC-SGD algorithms. Indeed, the QMC method
González et al. 2021
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
combines density matrices and RFF to learn a nonparametric probability density function of inputs and outputs in both classical and quantum computers. Although the QGC algorithm also combines density matrices and random features, it uses the variational quantum approach for classification, increasing its flexibility and allowing its integration with classical neural networks. In relation to the previous work, the classical QMC-SGD model by Gonzalez
et al.

González et al. 2022
uses stochastic gradient descent to discriminately optimize the QMC algorithm. Instead of a classical discriminative model, our algorithm consists of a variational quantum circuit that can be used for both generative and discriminative learning. Additionally, the classical KDM method
González et al. 2024
also combines kernels and density matrices for generative modeling. Besides being a classical algorithm, the KDM model uses an explicit representation of the kernel instead of an induced quantum feature space, which imposes a different type of constraints on the expressiveness of the model compared to the QGC strategy. Furthermore, we also present a connection between mixed states and kernels, and the representer theorem
Schölkopf and Smola 2001
for inducing a function from the data in RKHS; this link was first made in Ref.
Schuld and Petruccione 2021
but not in the context of generative learning.

In this article, we also introduce the quantum-enhanced Fourier features strategy, which leverages quantum hardware to build a quantum embedding based on random Fourier features
Rahimi and Recht 2009
. RFF is a method that maps the original data into a feature space, such that the inner product in the feature space approximates a shift-invariant kernel in the original space; in particular, the QEFF approximates the Gaussian kernel. Random features have been applied to various areas of quantum machine learning, including quantum classification
González et al. 2021
;
González et al. 2022
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
;
Sadowski 2019
, quantum regression
Peters and Schuld 2023
;
Landman et al. 2022
;
Sweke et al. 2023
, and quantum density estimation
González et al. 2022
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
;
Ardila-García et al. 2025
. Other studies have explored an opposite approach, e.g., Ref.
Landman et al. 2022
proposed the use of RFF to efficiently translate some QML models to classical computers. However, as noted in Ref.
Sweke et al. 2023
, they can not be used to dequantize some highly expressive quantum models. In addition, Refs.
Sadowski 2019
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
Yamasaki et al. 2020
;
Gil-Fuster et al. 2024
have proposed some quantum strategies for improving the RFF’s Monte Carlo sampling, of which Ref.
Yamasaki et al. 2020
has presented a possible quantum advantage. Out of these methods, the QEFF mapping is closely related to the quantum random (QRFF) and quantum adaptive Fourier features (QAFF) introduced by Useche
et al.

Useche et al. 2024
, see Sect.
III.4
. In fact, the QRFF mapping is constructed using amplitude encoding

;
Shende et al. 2006

Useche et al. 2024

III
Quantum generative classification

The proposed variational quantum algorithm for generative classification builds an estimator
\hat{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\approx p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
of the joint probability density of inputs and outputs and makes a prediction based on the label with the highest joint probability density, that is,
\argmax_{\bm{y}^{*}}\hat{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*})
. The method consists of the following three steps; see Fig.
1
:

(i)

Quantum feature map: We map the training features and labels to quantum feature states, using the QEFF mapping for the inputs
\{\bm{x}_{j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}\mapsto\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}
and the one-hot basis encoding for the outputs
\{\bm{y}_{j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}\mapsto\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}
. Consequently, we apply the same quantum mappings to the test sample
\bm{x}^{*}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}
and a possible test label
\bm{y}^{*}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^*}=\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_\mathcb{Y}}^*}
. We denote the joint quantum states of both the training and test samples by
\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}\otimes\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}
and
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}=\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}\otimes\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^*}
.

(ii)

Training phase: We build a variational quantum algorithm that minimizes the average negative log-likelihood
{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}\sim-\sum_{j}\log{\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}\rho_{{_\mathcb{X}}, {_\mathcb{Y}}}(\boldsymbol\theta)\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}}
, with parameters
\bm{\theta}
, of the expectation value between the variational mixed state
\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})=\text{Tr}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}\big[\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta)}\bra{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta)}\big]
and the quantum features of the training data; this state results by tracing out the ancilla qubits (
\mathcal{A}
) of the pure state
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta)}
, where
(\mathcal{T})
denotes the full Hilbert space of the ancilla (
\mathcal{A}
), inputs (
\mathcal{X}
), and outputs (
\mathcal{Y}
). To perform this operation, we build a quantum circuit that compiles the pure state
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}({\boldsymbol\theta})}
over all the qubits, see Fig.
1
, followed by the preparation of the quantum features of the inputs
\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}\}
in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
. We then measure the qubits of the inputs
\mathcal{X}
and outputs
\mathcal{Y}
, using as a basis the all-zeros state
\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
and the quantum features of the training labels
\{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\}
, obtaining that the probability of
P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}})
, corresponds to the expected value
\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}
. For notational purposes, the rightmost qubit in the equations corresponds to the uppermost qubit in the circuit diagrams.

(iii)

Testing phase: Once the optimal values of the optimization are obtained,
\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})}
. We make use of the previous quantum circuit to prepare the state
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}({\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{op}}})}
, followed by the test quantum state
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}
over the Hilbert space of inputs. By performing a quantum measurement of the qubits of the inputs and outputs, we find that the probability
P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^*_{_\mathcb{Y}}})=\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}
is an estimator of the joint up to a normalization constant
\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\approx p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
. The predicted class would then be obtained from
\argmax_{\bm{y}^{*}}\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*})
.

(this state results from tracing out the ancilla qubits), in step iii), we estimate the joint probability density by projecting the optimized density matrix with the joint quantum state of the test input sample and a candidate test label
\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\sim\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}
, finally, we make a prediction based on the class with largest joint probability density
\argmax_{{\bm{y}^{*}}}\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
.

III.1
Quantum circuit details

We now describe in detail the construction of the variational quantum generative classification algorithm.

Quantum feature maps:
Given a data set of
N
training data points and their corresponding categorical labels
\mathscr{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
with
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\bm{y}_{j}\in\mathbb{Y}=\{0,\cdots,L-1\}
, and a test sample
\bm{x^{*}}\in\mathbb{X}
to be classified onto one of the
L
classes, the algorithm starts by applying the proposed quantum-enhanced Fourier features mapping
\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
to the input samples
\bm{x}_{j}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}
,
\bm{x}^{*}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}
and the one-hot-basis encoding
\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
to the labels
\bm{y}_{j}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\delta_{{\bm{y}}_{j},k}\ket{k_{_\mathcb{Y}}}
, where
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}}\in{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbb{R}^{L}
,
\delta_{{\bm{y}}_{j},k}
is a Kronecker’s delta, and
\ket{k_{_\mathcb{Y}}}
is the decimal representation of the one-hot vector. The QEFF and the one-hot-basis feature map,
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}'}=\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_\mathcb{Y}}'}
approximate and induce, respectively, the Gaussian kernel
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})=e^{-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}'-\boldsymbol{x}''}^{2}}
with bandwidth parameter
h\in\mathbb{R}
and the Kronecker’s delta kernel
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime})=\delta_{\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime}}
through an inner product in the quantum features space
\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}''}}^{2}\approx\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})

\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}'}{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}''}}^{2}=\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime})

\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
and any
\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Y}

Training quantum circuit:
The training step starts by preparing a variational pure quantum state
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=\mathcal{Q}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{0_{_\mathcb{T}}}

n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}+n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}
qubits. The probability
P(\ket{0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*})=\text{Tr}\big[\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})(\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}^*}\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}^*}\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*}\bra{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*})\big]=\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}
builds an estimator of the joint probability density of inputs and outputs up to the normalization constant
M_{h}P(\ket{0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*})=\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
. Hence, prediction is made based on the class with largest joint probability density

\displaystyle\argmax_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*}\in{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}P(\ket{0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*})

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*}\in{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\bm{y}^{*}}\in\mathbb{Y}}\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*}).

(5)

Figure 2:
Training quantum circuit to estimate the expected value of the training density matrix and the
j^{\text{th}}
training feature
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}}
by measuring the Hilbert spaces of inputs and outputs
P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}})=\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}
.

Figure 3:
Test quantum circuit to estimate the joint probability of features and labels by computing the projection of the optimized density matrix and the test quantum sample
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}=\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*}
through a reading of the first
n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}+n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}
qubits
P(\ket{0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}^*})=\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}=M_{h}\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
.

In the following sections, we further describe the theoretical details of the quantum generative classification algorithm.

III.2
Classification with reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

The presented QGC strategy belongs to the category of kernel methods
Boser et al. 1992
;
Vapnik and Mukherjee 1999
;
Rosenblatt 1956
;
Parzen 1962
, whose relation to quantum algorithms has been explored in Refs.
Schuld and Petruccione 2021
;
González et al. 2024
. In particular, our model approximates a function for classification in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Here, we describe a RKHS and some previous machine learning algorithms for classification and density estimation based on RKHS.

A RKHS is a Hilbert space consisting of functions
\textsl{g}(\bm{x}^{*})
whose domain is the data space
\textsl{g}:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that result from combining linearly the functions of a kernel
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
centered on the training data points
\{\bm{x}_{j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}

\textsl{g}(\bm{x}^{*})=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{j}\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}^{*}),

(6)

where
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
j
. An example of a function in a RKHS is the kernel density estimation (KDE) method
Rosenblatt 1956
;
Parzen 1962
.

\textsl{g}_{\text{KDE}}(\bm{x}^{*})=\frac{M_{h}}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}_j-\boldsymbol{x^*}}^{2}},

(7)

where
\alpha_{j}=M_{h}/N
for all
j
and
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})=e^{-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}'-\boldsymbol{x}''}^{2}}
is the Gaussian kernel; this algorithm corresponds to a non-parametric estimator of the probability density
p(\bm{x}^{*})
of the training data.

It is possible to extend these ideas to the problem of building a classification model from a training data set
\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
by defining a function
g:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with kernels
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}:\mathbb{Y}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of a test sample
\bm{x^{*}}
and candidate label
\bm{y}^{*}

g(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{j}\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*})\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}^{*}).

(8)

Setting the
\{\alpha_{j}\}
as before we can build a classification model based on KDE, called kernel density classification (KDC)
Hastie et al. 2009

g_{\text{KDC}}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})=\frac{M_{h}}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\delta_{\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*}}e^{-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}_j-\boldsymbol{x^*}}^{2}},

(9)

where
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime})=\delta_{\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime}}
is the Kronecker’s delta kernel.

This algorithm corresponds to a nonparametric density estimator of the joint
g_{\text{KDC}}(\bm{x^{*}},\bm{y}^{*})\approx p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
, where the predicted class corresponds to the label with the largest joint probability density. It is worth mentioning that although the KDE and the KDC methods were originally developed for classical computers, they can be extended as quantum algorithms using the formalism of density matrices and kernels as described in Refs.
González et al. 2021
;
González et al. 2022
;
González et al. 2024
;
Useche et al. 2021
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
;
Ardila-García et al. 2025
.

III.3
Generative modeling with density matrices

Learning the probability distribution of a data set
\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
can be framed on the task of minimizing the KL-divergence between the true data distribution
p(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*})
and the model distribution
f(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta})
with variational parameters
\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\log{\frac{p(\boldsymbol x^*, \boldsymbol{y}^*)}{f(\boldsymbol x^*, \boldsymbol{y}^*|\boldsymbol\theta)}}\,d\bm{x}^{*}d\bm{y}^{*}.

(10)

Since the term
\int_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\log{p(\boldsymbol x^*, \boldsymbol{y}^*)}\,d\bm{x}^{*}d\bm{y}^{*}
does not have any parameters this optimization is equivalent to minimizing

-\int_{\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y}}p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})\log{f(\boldsymbol x^*, \boldsymbol{y}^*|\boldsymbol\theta)}\,d\bm{x}^{*}d\bm{y}^{*},

(11)

which in turn can be approximated using Monte Carlo integration by means of the average negative log-likelihood

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\log{f(\boldsymbol x_j, \boldsymbol y_j| \boldsymbol\theta)},

(12)

owing to the fact that the training data points
\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
are samples drawn from
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

The model distribution
f(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta})
can then be constructed using a quantum feature map of the inputs and outputs
\bm{x}^{\prime}\mapsto\ket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}
,
\bm{y}^{\prime}\mapsto\ket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{Y}}}'}
, and a variational density matrix
\bar{\rho}_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})

f(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}\bra{\bar\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}'}\bar{\rho}_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\bar\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}'},

(13)

where
\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}
, correspond to the normalization constants of the shift-invariant kernels induced through an inner product in the quantum feature spaces
\absolutevalue{\braket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}''}}^{2}=\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})
,
\absolutevalue{\braket{\bar\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}'}{\bar\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}''}}^{2}=\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime})
, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{X}}\kappa_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{*})\,d\bm{x}^{*}},\ \mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathbb{Y}}\kappa_{\mathbb{Y}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{*})\,d\bm{y}^{*}};

(14)

the shift-invariance of the kernels guaranties that they are indeed constants. Note that we have used the bar notation
\ket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}
,
\ket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{Y}}}'}
to emphasize that these quantum maps explicitly induce the kernels
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})
and
\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime})
.

The optimal parameters of the minimization of Eq.
12
would then correspond to

\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}).

(15)

Moreover, following the representer theorem
Schölkopf and Smola 2001
;
Schuld and Petruccione 2021
we have that the solution of the previous minimization problem can be written as a linear combination of the kernel functions centered at the training data points

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})=\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}\bra{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _{\mathcb{Y}}}^*}\bar{\rho}_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\bar\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{j}\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*})\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}^{*}),

(16)

with each
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. This expression corresponds to a function in a RKHS, which can also be written using the formalism of the density matrices as follows

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*})\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{x}^{*}),

(17)

with
q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\in\mathbb{R}
and
q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\geq 0
for all
j
, and
\sum_{j}q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})=1
; we formalize and prove this result in Proposition
1
, illustrating the representer theorem in the context of density matrices, see Appendix
A
.

For the present QGC algorithm, we have that the QEFF mapping
\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}
approximates the Gaussian kernel
\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}''}}^{2}\approx e^{\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}'- \boldsymbol{x}''}^{2}}
, while the one-hot basis encoding induce explicitly the Kronecker’s delta kernel
\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}'}{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}''}}^{2}=\delta_{\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}^{\prime\prime}}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}=M_{h}=(2\pi h^{2})^{-D/2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}=1
. Hence, the estimator of the probability density of a test sample
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
, see Eq.
4
, approximates a function in a RKHS of the form

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})=\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}\mathcal{M}_{{}_{\mathbb{Y}}}\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _{\mathcb{Y}}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\delta_{\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*}}\frac{1}{(2\pi h^{2})^{D/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}_j-\boldsymbol{x^*}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}q_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\delta_{\bm{y}_{j},\bm{y}^{*}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}^{*}|\bm{x}_{j},h^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D}).

(18)

This estimator is an approximation of a Gaussian mixture classifier, where
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes the Gaussian probability density function. In particular, the mixture components are centered on the training data points and they all share the same covariance matrix
h^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D}
, where
\mathbf{I}_{D}
is the
D
-dimensional identity matrix. In summary, the function
\hat{f}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\approx f(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})
would be an approximation of the joint probability density of inputs and outputs
p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
.

III.4
Quantum-enhanced Fourier features

To build the quantum features in the input space, we propose the quantum enhanced Fourier features mapping, which can be viewed as a quantum-enhanced implementation of the quantum random Fourier features
Useche et al. 2024
and a quantum analog of random Fourier features
Rahimi and Recht 2009
. The QEFF embedding maps a sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{D}
to a quantum state
\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}’}\in{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
, such that for any
\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
, the inner product in the Hilbert space approximates a Gaussian kernel in the input space
\absolutevalue{\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}''}}^{2}\approx\kappa_{{}_{\mathbb{X}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})=e^{\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\norm{\boldsymbol{x}'-\boldsymbol x''}^{2}}
, where
h
is termed the kernel bandwidth.

The QEFF encoding leverages quantum superposition for implementing the QRFF mapping
Useche et al. 2024
in quantum hardware

\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}-1}e^{i\sqrt{\frac{1}{2h^{2}}}\bm{w}_{k}\cdot\bm{x}^{\prime}}\ket{k_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}.

(19)

In this expression the data sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}
is encoded on the phases of a quantum state, denoting
(\cdot)
the standard dot product and
n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}=\log{d}
the number of qubits. In addition, the weights
\{\bm{w}_{k}\}_{0\cdots 2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
result from sampling a
D\text{-dimensional}
standard normal distribution
\bm{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
.

Figure 4:
QAFF quantum circuit strategy
Useche et al. 2024
for preparing the three-qubit QEFF mapping. In this example, we map a
D
-dimensional input sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}
to an eight-dimensional pure quantum state
\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}
whose weights
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{\alpha_{3},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}}\}_{\alpha_{3},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}\in\{0,1\}}
, excluding the all zeros index, are sampled from the same arbitrary probability distribution
\Phi
with mean
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and covariance matrix
4/(2^{3}-1)\mathbf{I}_{D}
. See Ref.
Useche et al. 2024
for the extension of the QAFF quantum circuit to a higher number of qubits.

Ref.
Useche et al. 2024
proposed the use of amplitude encoding protocols
Shende et al. 2006
;
Möttönen et al. 2005
to prepare the QRFF mapping on a quantum computer. Specifically, the dot products
\{\bm{w}_{k}\cdot\bm{x}\}
are encoded in the phases
\{\phi_{k}\}
of the canonical basis
\{\ket{k_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}\}_{0\cdots 2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}-1}
, i.e.,
\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}=(1/\sqrt{2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}})\sum_{k}e^{i\phi_{k}}\ket{k_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}
. In contrast, in this article we present the QEFF embedding which compiles the QRFF mapping using a basis based on the Pauli-z
\sigma^{z}=\ket{0}\bra{0}-\ket{1}\bra{1}
and the identity
\mathbf{I}_{2}=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1}
matrix.

The QEFF embedding starts from the
n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
zeros state
\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}
and then applies a Hadamard gate
H
to each qubit resulting in the superposition state
H^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}
with
\ket{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
, followed by a unitary matrix
U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|{\bm{\Theta}})
on
n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
qubits given by

\displaystyle U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|{\bm{\Theta}})=

\displaystyle\exp\{-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\in\{0,1\}}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2h^{2}}}\bm{\vartheta}_{\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}},\cdots,\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}}\cdot\bm{x}^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\sigma^{\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma^{\alpha_{2}}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\bigg)\bigg\},\quad\quad

(20)

where
\sigma^{0}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
,
\sigma^{1}=\sigma^{z}
, and
\bm{\Theta}=\{\bm{\vartheta}_{\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}},\cdots,\alpha_{1}}\}_{\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}},\cdots,\alpha_{1}\in\{0,1\}}
is a set
2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, with
\bm{\vartheta}_{0,0,\cdots,0}=\bm{0}
to account for the global phase and the remaining
2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}-1
weights sampled i.i.d. from an arbitrary symmetric probability distribution
\Phi(\mathbf{0},\frac{4}{(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\mathbf{I}_{D})
with zero mean
\bm{\mu}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma=\frac{4}{(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\mathbf{I}_{D}
.

The proposed method can then be summarized by a unitary matrix
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}=U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}|{\bm{\Theta}})H^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}
, which build from the zeros state the quantum feature map

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}

\displaystyle=U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\bm{\Theta}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})H^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}.

(21)

In Appendix
B
, we use the multivariate central limit theorem to show that the QEFF sampling strategy in the Pauli basis
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{\alpha_{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}},\cdots,\alpha_{1}}\}\sim\Phi(\mathbf{0},\frac{4}{(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\mathbf{I}_{D})
, effectively prepares the QRFF mapping, whose weights are identically distributed from a normal distribution
\{\bm{w}_{i}\}_{0,\cdots,(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
.

Furthermore, to implement the QEFF mapping as a quantum circuit, we use the QAFF ansatz proposed in Ref.
Useche et al. 2024
. In that article, in addition of presenting the QRFF method, the authors propose a variational feature embedding called QAFF where the Fourier weights are optimized using a variational quantum circuit. In our work, we also utilize the QAFF quantum circuit, but the weights are fixed and are obtained from the QEFF sampling procedure. Fig
4
shows an example of the QAFF quantum circuit for three qubits that prepares the eight-dimensional QEFF feature mapping; this circuit uses Hadamard, CNOT, and
R_{z}(\beta)=e^{-i\beta/2}\outerproduct{0}{0}+e^{i\beta/2}\outerproduct{1}{1}
quantum gates. It is worth noticing that the weight vector
\bm{\vartheta}_{0,\cdots,0}=\bm{0}
associated with the global phase does not need to be compiled in the quantum circuit.

III.5
Purification ansatz

As previously discussed, the QGC algorithm builds the variational density matrix by tracing out the auxiliary qubits of a three-component pure state with joint Hilbert space of inputs, outputs, and ancilla
\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})=\text{Tr}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}\big[\ket{q_{_{\mathcb{A}, \mathcb{X}, \mathcb{Y}}}({\boldsymbol\theta})}\bra{q_{_{\mathcb{A}, \mathcb{X}, \mathcb{Y}}}({\boldsymbol\theta})}\big]
. Indeed, any mixed state can be prepared from a partial measurement over a pure quantum state, also called a purification of a mixed quantum state. Although a purification of a mixed state is not unique, it has been shown that it is optimal when
n_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}=\lceil\log{r}\rceil
, where
r
is the rank of the density matrix
Useche et al. 2024
;
Ping et al. 2013
. Henceforth, in the proposed QGC method, the size of the Hilbert space of the pure state
\ket{q_{_{\mathcb{A}, \mathcb{X}, \mathcb{Y}}}}
could be at least equal to the size of the training density matrix and at most twice its size, i.e.,
n_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}+n_{{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}\geq n_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}\geq 0
.

To construct a purification of a quantum machine learning algorithm one must choose an appropriate quantum circuit ansatz. This selection usually involves a trade-off between expressibility and trainability
Holmes et al. 2022
. Namely, the use of a more expressive ansatz that explores a larger region of the Hilbert space can lead to trainability issues due to the barren plateau problem
McClean et al. 2018
. Motivated from these challenges, we propose the use of the hardware efficient ansatz (HEA)
Kandala et al. 2017
for compiling the unitary matrix
\mathcal{Q}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}(\bm{\theta})
of the purification
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta)}=\mathcal{Q}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{0_{_\mathcb{T}}}
(recall that
\mathcal{T}
is the Hilbert space of all the qubits and
n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}={n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}+n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}+n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}}
). This ansatz has been shown to be highly expressive, to have a manageable circuit depth, and to avoid the barren plateau problem when applied to certain quantum machine learning tasks
Leone et al. 2024
.

The circuit of HEA ansatz
Kandala et al. 2017
starts from the all-zeros state
\ket{0_{_\mathcb{T}}}
and then it sequentially applies to each qubit the variational
R^{y}
and
R^{z}
quantum gates. Next,
T
quantum layers are constructed, each consisting of a cascade of
n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}-1
CNOT gates that entangle adjacent qubits, followed by the qubit-wise
R^{y}
and
R^{z}
unitaries, see Fig.
5
. The single
R^{y}
and
R^{z}
quantum rotations are parameterized by
\bm{\theta}\in[0,2\pi)^{2n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}(T+1)}
, corresponding to
2n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}(T+1)
trainable parameters. Furthermore, the depth of the ansatz compromises
T(n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}}-1)
CNOT gates.

Figure 5:
Hardware-efficient ansatz for preparing the purification
\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta)}
of the training density matrix
\rho_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}(\bm{\theta})=\text{Tr}_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}(\ket{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta})\bra{q_{_\mathcb{T}}(\boldsymbol\theta))})
. The
R^{y}
and
R^{z}
are parametrized by
\bm{\theta}
which corresponds to rotation angles in
[0,2\pi)
.

III.6
Quantum discriminative learning

The presented quantum generative algorithm can also be adapted for discriminative learning, whose goal is to construct a function that maximizes the conditional probability of the outputs given the inputs
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
.

The proposed quantum discriminative classification approach shares some similarities with the quantum generative strategy, that is, we use the training quantum features
\{\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol y_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}\}
to learn a variational density matrix
\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})
. Nevertheless, in this new approach we estimate the conditional probability by performing a normalization of the circuit outputs. To achieve this, we extract from the training quantum circuit, see Fig.
3
, the probabilities of each input state
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^j}
with all classes by measuring the probabilities
\{P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}'_{_\mathcb{Y}}})\}
for all
\ket{\boldsymbol{y}'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\in\mathcal{Y}
; notice that one of these estimations corresponds to the probability associated with the true label
P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}})
. We then use a classical computer to normalize the circuit outputs

\displaystyle\hat{h}(\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{x}_{j};\bm{\theta})=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{\theta})}{\sum_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Y}}\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}^{\prime}|\bm{\theta})}=\frac{P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^j_{_\mathcb{Y}}})}{\sum_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}\in\mathcal{Y}}P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}'_{_\mathcb{Y}}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^j}}{\sum_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}\in\mathcal{Y}}\text{Tr}\big[\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{j}|\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\bra{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\big]}.

(22)

Just as in the generative case, we estimate the optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}L(\bm{\theta})
by performing a minimization of the average negative log-likelihood

\hat{L}(\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\log{\hat h(\boldsymbol{y}_j|\boldsymbol{x}_j;\boldsymbol{\theta})};

(23)

notice that in this new scenario we are maximizing the conditional probability of the true label
\bm{y}_{j}
given the input sample
\bm{x}_{j}
.

In a similar fashion, for the prediction stage, we use the optimized density matrix
\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})
to build an estimator of the conditional probability
p(\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{x}^{*})
of the candidate test label
\bm{y}^{*}\mapsto\ket{\boldsymbol y_{_\mathcb{Y}}^*}
given the test input
\bm{x}^{*}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}^*}
by a normalization of the outputs of the test quantum circuit, see Fig
3
,

\displaystyle\hat{h}(\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{x}^{*};\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})=\frac{P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}^*_{_\mathcb{Y}}})}{\sum_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}\in\mathcal{Y}}P(\ket{0_{_\mathcb{X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol{y}'_{_\mathcb{Y}}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bra{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}, _\mathcb{Y}}^*}}{\sum_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}\in\mathcal{Y}}\text{Tr}\big[\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{*}\rangle\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{*}|\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\bra{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\big]}.

Prediction is then made from
\argmax_{{\bm{y}^{*}}\in\mathbb{Y}}\hat{h}(\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{x}^{*};\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})
.

This discriminative learning strategy was first proposed as a classical algorithm called QMC-SGD by Gonzalez
et al.

González et al. 2022
;
González et al. 2021
. In this paper, we extend this model for generative learning and present both generative and discriminative approaches as variational quantum algorithms.

III.7
Computational complexity analysis

We present the computational complexity of the proposed quantum generative classification strategy on both classical and quantum computers.

III.7.1
Complexity of the quantum-enhanced Fourier features

To review the quantum complexity of the QEFF, recall that in this method we map a data sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
to a quantum state
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}'}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, where
d=2^{n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
, and sample
{d-1}
weights
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{j}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
from
\Phi(\mathbf{0},\frac{4}{(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\mathbf{I}_{D})
. First, assuming that it takes
O(1)
to sample one component of a single Fourier weight, the complexity of sampling all QEFF weights would be
O(Dd)
. Furthermore, to prepare the quantum circuit of the QEFF, it is also required
O(Dd)
to estimate the angles
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{j}\cdot\bm{x}^{\prime}\}_{1\cdots d-1}
of the gates
R_{z}
in the Pauli basis and
O(d)
to compile the QAFF circuit; in fact, the QAFF circuit
Useche et al. 2024
is analogous to a circuit for the preparation of an arbitrary quantum state
Shende et al. 2006
;
Möttönen et al. 2005
, see Fig.
4
. In short, the total quantum complexity to prepare the Fourier state
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{X}}'}
would be
O(Dd)
.

On the contrary, to prepare the QEFF mapping on a classical computer one must use the canonical basis
\{\ket{k_{_\mathcb X}}\}
instead of the Pauli basis. However, in the classical case, we do not have access to the Fourier weights on the canonical basis since these weights depend on the weights on the basis of Pauli matrices; see Eq.
53
and Appendix
B
. Alternatively, to classically implement the QEFF, one could sample for each Fourier vector
\bm{w}_{j}
on the canonical basis,
d
i.i.d. weights
\{\bm{\upsilon}_{jk}\}
from
\Phi(\mathbf{0},\frac{4}{(2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}})-1}\mathbf{I}_{D})
and perform the summation
\bm{w}_{j}=\sum_{k}\bm{\upsilon}_{jk}
, resulting in
\bm{w}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{D})
. Henceforth, following the arguments of the quantum case, it is required
O(Dd^{2})
to sample the weights
\{\bm{w}_{j}\}_{0\dots d-1}
,
O(Dd)
to estimate the dot products with the data sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}
, and
O(d)
to construct the vector
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb X}'}
. Therefore, the total complexity for preparing the QEFF mapping in a classical computer would be
O(Dd^{2})
.

III.7.2
Complexity of the training and test quantum circuits

We now study the complexity of the training and test quantum circuits of the QGC algorithm. These circuits can be divided into three distinct parts: the preparation of the purification ansatz
\ket{q_{_{\mathcb A,\mathcb X,\mathcb Y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
, the compilation of the prediction sample
\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb X}}'}
, and a quantum measurement of the probabilities
\{P(\ket{\mathbf 0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_{\mathcb Y}}})\}
for all
\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_{\mathcb Y}}}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

To begin with, the complexity of compiling the purification using the HEA ansatz is linear in the number of qubits
O(n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}}+n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}+n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}})=O(\log{(HLd))}
, defining
H=2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}}}}
as the size of the Hilbert space of the ancilla, along with
L=2^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}}
and
d=2^{n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
. Moreover, to prepare the QEFF prediction state
\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb X}'}
takes time
O(Dd)
, see previous subsection. And the estimation the probabilities of the
L
classes
\{P(\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}}\ket{y_{_{\mathcb{Y}}}})\}
requires to perform
S
measurements, which from probability theory scales as

S\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}\wp(1-\wp)},

(25)

where
\epsilon
is the error of the approximation and
\wp=\min_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}}P(\ket{\mathbf 0_{_{\mathcb X}}}\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_{\mathcb Y}}})=\min_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}}\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _{\mathcb{Y}}} '}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}' }
is the minimum value over all classes of the unnormalized joint probability density. Therefore, the total quantum complexity of both the training and test quantum circuits is

O\bigg(\frac{\log{(HdL)}+Dd}{\epsilon^{2}\wp(1-\wp)}\bigg).

(26)

In contrast, the complexity of calculating the projection
\bra{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _{\mathcb{Y}}} '}\rho_{{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{_{\mathcb{X}}, _\mathcb{Y}}' }
in a classical computer is
O(HdL+Dd^{2})
. In particular, it takes
O(HdL)
to build the training density matrix,
O(Dd^{2})
to sample the classical QEFF weights,
O(Dd)
to prepare the quantum state of the QEFF (assuming that the weights were already sampled), and
O(HdL)
to compute the expected value between the training density matrix and the prediction state.

III.7.3
Complexity of estimating the gradients

The parameters of the loss (Eq.
3
) of the QGC algorithm are updated based on the gradient descent rule

\theta_{k}^{(t+1)}=\theta_{k}^{(t)}-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial\theta_{k}^{(t)}},

(27)

being
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
the learning rate,
t
the time of the iteration, and
\theta_{k}
the
k^{\text{th}}
component of
\bm{\theta}
. These gradients can be estimated in a quantum computer using the parameter-shift rule
Mitarai et al. 2018
;
Schuld et al. 2019

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial\theta_{k}^{(t)}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{M_{h}}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{2\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{\theta}^{(t)})}\Big\{\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{\theta}^{(t)})_{\theta_{k}\rightarrow\theta_{k}+\pi/2}

\displaystyle\times-\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{\theta}^{(t)})_{\theta_{k}\rightarrow\theta_{k}-\pi/2}\Big\},\quad

(28)

where the subscript indicates that the parameter
\theta_{k}
in
\hat{f}
is shifted to
\theta_{k}\pm\pi/2
. Therefore, for each
k
and each training sample, one must estimate the projections
\hat{f}
, whose complexity is given by Eq.
26
. Assuming also that the number of parameters of the HEA ansatz scales linearly with the number of qubits
|\bm{\theta}|\sim\log{(HdL)}
, the total complexity for estimating the gradients would then be

O\Bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{\log{(HdL)}\big(\log{(HdL)}+Dd\big)}{\epsilon^{2}\wp_{j}(1-\wp_{j})}\Bigg),

(29)

with

\wp_{j}=\min_{\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_{\mathcb Y}}'}\in\mathcal{Y}}\text{Tr}\big[\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}})|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{j}|\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\bra{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\big],

(30)

is the minimum value of the joint probability density of the training sample
j
over all possible labels.

In contrast, it has been shown that in a classical neural network, a training step and making a prediction have similar computational complexity
Livni et al. 2014
. Henceforth, the complexity of estimating the gradients of a data set of
N
samples on a classical computer would be
O\big(N(HdL+Dd)+Dd^{2}\big)
. Note that the complexity of the classical sampling of the QEFF does not depend on the number of training data, since all data samples have the same QEFF weights, and therefore it is sufficient to perform this operation only once.

III.7.5
Complexity discussion

The classical and quantum complexity of the QEFF mapping are
O(Dd^{2})
and
O(Dd)
, respectively. Thus, there is a theoretical advantage of constructing the QEFF mapping in quantum hardware. However, this possible quadratic advantage may not be an actual advantage if one considers the additional load of the error-correction code
Babbush et al. 2021
; we leave a comprehensive analysis of this complexity issue for future work.

In addition, comparing the classical and quantum complexities for the training and test phases of the QGC algorithm, see Table
1
, we observe that to achieve a quantum advantage the error of the approximation of the training
\epsilon
and test
\epsilon^{*}
steps must satisfy

\epsilon^{2}>\frac{\log{(HdL)}\big(\log{(HdL)}+Dd\big)}{\big(N(HdL+Dd)+Dd^{2}\big)\sum_{j}\wp_{j}(1-\wp_{j})}

(31)

and

(\epsilon^{*})^{2}>\frac{\log{(HdL)}+Dd}{\big(K(HdL+Dd)+Dd^{2}\big)\sum_{k}\wp^{*}_{k}(1-\wp^{*}_{k})}.

(32)

Henceforth, the advantage is highly dependent on the unnormalized joint probability densities (without the
M_{h}
scaling value) of the training and test samples
\{\wp_{j}\}_{0\cdots N-1}
and
\{\wp_{k}^{*}\}_{0\cdots K-1}
. However, the objective of the QGC algorithm is to estimate these values and therefore it is not possible to conclude a possible quantum advantage. However, the previous inequalities indicate that to attain a lower error in the approximation the data must not be very sparse (the samples should not have low unnormalized joint density values).

, lastly, prediction is made from
\argmax_{{\bm{y}^{*}}\in\mathbb{Y}}\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
.

IV
Deep quantum-classical generative classification

A challenging task when building a machine learning model based on kernel density estimation
Rosenblatt 1956
;
Parzen 1962
;
González et al. 2022
consists of its application to high-dimensional data samples. In particular, the sparsity of the data increases in proportion to its dimensionality, resulting in poor pointwise estimates of the probability density. Previous works
González et al. 2022
;
González et al. 2024
;
Gallego-Mejia et al. 2024
have proposed the use of deep neural networks to reduce the dimensionality of the samples, and then apply the corresponding kernel-based method in this lower-dimensional space, also called latent space.

To overcome the curse of dimensionality, we propose the deep quantum-classical generative classification (D-QGC) algorithm. The method performs classification of high-dimensional data samples by combining classical and quantum neural networks to learn the joint probability density of inputs and outputs
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. The D-QGC model uses a deep neural network to map the data to a latent space and the QGC algorithm to learn the joint probability density in this lower-dimensional space. This strategy is also based on the combination of discriminative and generative learning for generative classification. Briefly, the variational quantum-classical algorithm, see Fig.
6
, can be divided into two training phases and one testing phase, as follows:

(i) Quantum-classical discriminative training: Given a high-dimensional data set of features and labels
\{(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\}_{0\cdots N-1}
with
\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{C}
and
\bm{y}_{j}\in\mathbb{Y}=\{0,\cdots,L-1\}
and a test input sample
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{X}
and candidate test label
\bm{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{Y}
whose density we aim to estimate. We start by building an end-to-end quantum-classical differentiable model that estimates the conditional probability
p(\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*}|\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*})
. This model combines a deep neural network with the quantum discriminative strategy presented in Sect.
III.6
. It consists of building for the input space a two-stage variational embedding of the form
\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\bm{\mathit{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, with
\bm{\mathit{X}}=\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
, where the function
\Phi:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\bm{\mathit{X}}
corresponds to the deep neural network that maps the data to the latent space and the function
\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}:\bm{\mathit{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
to the QEFF mapping. In addition, we apply the one-hot-basis quantum encoding
\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{L}
to the label space. Specifically, an input sample is mapped to
\bm{x}_{j}\mapsto\Phi(\bm{x}_{j}|\bm{\zeta})=\Phi_{{}_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}}^{j}(\bm{\zeta})
, where
\bm{\zeta}
are the trainable parameters of the classical network, followed by the QEFF encoding
\Phi_{{}_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}}^{j}(\bm{\zeta})\mapsto\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big\rangle
, while the outputs are mapped to
\bm{y}_{j}\mapsto\ket{\psi_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}=\ket{\boldsymbol{y}_{_\mathcb{Y}}^j}
. We then use the training quantum circuit, see Fig.
3
, to project the quantum features with the trainable density matrix
\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})
with parameters
\bm{\theta}
. And finally we estimate the conditional probability by normalizing the outputs of the circuit, as in Eq.
22
,

\displaystyle\hat{h}(\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{x}_{j};\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta})=

\displaystyle\frac{\big\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big|\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big\rangle}{\sum_{\ket{\boldsymbol y'}\in\mathcal{Y}}\text{Tr}\big[\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big\rangle\big\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big|\otimes\ket{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\bra{\boldsymbol y'_{_\mathcb{Y}}}\big]}

(33)

where
\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big\rangle=\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta})\big\rangle\otimes\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{j}\big\rangle
.

As previously, we solve the minimization problem

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}},\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}}L(\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\theta},\bm{\zeta}}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\log{\hat h(\boldsymbol{y}_j|\boldsymbol{x}_j;\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\zeta})}\bigg\}.

(34)

(ii) Quantum generative training: The previous discriminative training strategy allows the extraction of the training features in the latent space
\bm{x}_{j}\mapsto\Phi_{{}_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}}^{j}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})
that maximize the classification accuracy. To build an estimator of the joint probability
\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*})
, we fix these lower-dimensional features and fine-tune the quantum model using the generative learning approach. Indeed, we now neglect the normalization of the output of the training quantum circuit, see Eq.
2
. And therefore, at this stage, we apply the proposed quantum generative algorithm to the quantum features
\{\Phi_{{}_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}}^{j}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\mapsto\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle\}_{0\cdots N-1}
, setting the parameters
\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}}
fixed, and fine-tuning the starting variational parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}
. The estimator of the joint probability density then corresponds to

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}_{j}|\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}};\bm{\theta})=

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big|\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta})\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle,

(35)

where
\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle=\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{j}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle\otimes\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{j}\big\rangle
.
Finally, the fine-tuning of
\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}\rightarrow\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}^{\text{ft}}
follows the minimization procedure

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}^{\text{ft}}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\log{\hat f(\boldsymbol{x}_j, \boldsymbol{y}_j|\boldsymbol\zeta_{\text{op}};\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigg\}.

(36)

(iii) Quantum generative testing: To estimate the joint probability of the high-dimensional test sample
\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{X}
and the class
\bm{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{Y}
, we estimate the expected value of the quantum test state
\bm{x}^{*}\mapsto\Phi_{{}_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}}^{*}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\mapsto\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{*}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle
with the optimized training density matrix
\rho_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}^{\text{ft}})
using the test quantum circuit; see Fig.
3
. The estimator of the joint probability density
p(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})
would then be given by

\displaystyle\hat{p}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})=\hat{f}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*}|\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}};\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}^{\text{ft}})=

\displaystyle M_{h}\big\langle\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{*}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big|\rho_{{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}},{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}}(\bm{\theta}_{\text{op}}^{\text{ft}})\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{*}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle,

(37)

with
\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}},_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{\Phi^{*}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle=\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\Phi^{*}}(\bm{\zeta}_{\text{op}})\big\rangle\otimes\big|\psi_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}}^{*}\big\rangle
and
M_{h}=(2\pi h^{2})^{-D/2}
. Finally, the predicted label would result from
\argmax_{{\bm{y}^{*}}\in\mathbb{Y}}\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}=\bm{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}=\bm{y}^{*})
.

V
Method evaluation

V.1
One- and two-dimensional binary classification

We demonstrate the proposed quantum generative method for the binary classification of one- and two-dimensional data sets. The algorithm obtains the joint probability density of the inputs and outputs and predicts the class with the largest joint probability density. To evaluate the performance of the proposed QGC model with the QEFF mapping, we also compare it with two other state-of-the-art quantum feature maps, the QRFF embedding
Useche et al. 2024
, and the ZZ feature map (ZZFM)
Havlíček et al. 2019
; a description of the latter is presented in the Appendix
D
.

V.1.1
Data sets

We worked with a one-dimensional data set and three two-dimensional data sets. Each consisted of a training, test, and out-of-distribution (OOD) data set; an OOD data set is a data set that does not follow the probability distribution of the training data. Indeed, it is critical for the generalizability of a machine learning model to correctly predict unseen data samples that do not necessarily follow the training distribution
Caro et al. 2023
. As usual in machine learning, we used the training data to train the model and the test data to evaluate its accuracy (ACC). In addition, the OOD data set was used to evaluate the resulting estimates of the joint probability density of inputs and outputs.

One-dimensional data set:
The one-dimensional data set consisted of a sample of points distributed from a mixture of two Gaussians for class 0 and a single Gaussian for class 1. The training and test data sets had sizes of 900 and 100, respectively. And both partitions had roughly a the relative frequency of samples of two-thirds for class 0 and one-third for class 1. In addition, the OOD data set corresponded to 500 points sampled from a uniform distribution in the range
[-7,14]
.

Two-dimensional data sets:
We evaluated three two-dimensional data sets called
Moons
,
Circles
, and
Spirals
, shown respectively from top to bottom in Fig.
8
. The
Moons
and the

data sets had 1800 samples for training and 200 samples for testing, while the
Spirals
data set was divided into 900 training and 100 test data samples; all partitions of the data sets had approximately the same number of samples for each class. Furthermore, for all configurations, the OOD data set corresponded to 400 points uniformly distributed in the two-dimensional space
[0,1]\times[0,1]
.

The model achieved good classification performance with both ZZFM and QEFF mappings, illustrating that the proposed hybrid network is suitable for multiclass classification of high-dimensional data sets. We also present in Table

The results demonstrate that the D-QGC model with both quantum mappings is a viable strategy for generative learning. However, comparing the two quantum mappings, the use of QEFF resulted in a higher mean Spearman’s correlation, showing that this embedding produces a good approximation of the Gaussian kernel. It should be noticed that we did not evaluate the QRFF mapping as a part of the baseline. Indeed, we found that in a quantum-classical neural network, it is not trivial to optimize the parameters and evaluate its derivatives when using a quantum map based on amplitude encoding. This is in contrast to the QEFF and ZZFM, whose latent features could be directly embedded into the angles of the

quantum gates. In summary, the previous results illustrate that compared to the other two quantum maps, the QEFF embedding is a more suitable strategy for variational hybrid generative classification.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we presented a variational quantum algorithm for generative classification based on density matrices and Fourier features. The model uses a variational quantum circuit to prepare a mixed state that summarizes the joint probability distribution of features and labels of a data set, and performs supervised classification of a test sample based on the label with the largest joint probability density. We also introduced the quantum-enhanced Fourier features mapping, which leverages quantum superposition for approximating a Gaussian kernel through an inner product in a low-dimensional feature space. We showed that the model can be viewed as a Gaussian mixture classifier whose Gaussian components are centered on the training samples and a method for constructing a function in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. We performed various quantum demonstrations on several low- and high-dimensional data sets, illustrating that the QGC with QEFF strategy is competitive against other quantum algorithms of the state-of-the-art
Farhi and Neven 2018
;
Dilip et al. 2022
;
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Useche et al. 2024
.

In contrast to previous generative models
Amin et al. 2018
;
Zoufal et al. 2021
, the quantum demonstrations show that the QGC algorithm can be used to prepare in quantum hardware both continuous and discrete joint probability distributions through mixed quantum states. Furthermore, we showed that we can apply the variational principle to construct a quantum circuit for generative classification; this approach improves the flexibility of previous nonparametric density estimation methods based on kernel density estimation and quantum state averaging
Useche et al. 2021
;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2022
;
Useche et al. 2024
;
González et al. 2021
. Furthermore, this optimization-based strategy can be integrated with deep neural networks, allowing its application to high-dimensional data sets. For instance, we applied the hybrid quantum-classical model for the generative classification of the 10 classes MNIST and 10 classes Fashion-MNIST data sets, exhibiting good classification and density estimation performance and illustrating that the QEFF mapping is competitive against other previously proposed quantum encodings
Useche et al. 2024
;
Havlíček et al. 2019
.

Considering the multiple applications of generative learning, the proposed quantum generative classification strategy lays the groundwork for approaching other quantum machine learning problems, including uncertainty estimation
Jebara 2004
, multiclass anomaly detection
You et al. 2022
, statistical inference
Held and Sabanés Bové 2014
, and synthetic data generation
Harshvardhan et al. 2020
. Some open problems of the QGC model to be investigated for future work include its implementation on real quantum computers, the study of the effects of the barren plateau in the variational training, and the evaluation of the effects of the exponential concentration
Thanasilp et al. 2024
and the error correction
Babbush et al. 2021
of the Gaussian kernel generated by the QEFF quantum map.

Appendix C
Baseline for quantum classification

Farhi and Neven
Farhi and Neven 2018
introduced a variational quantum algorithm tailored for binary classification on near-term quantum devices. The authors used 17 qubits for the binary classification of the downsampled 4x4 MNIST handwritten numerical images. This model utilizes parameterized unitary transformations applied to input quantum states, followed by a measurement of a single qubit to infer the predicted label. Their method focused on the classification of classical n-bit data strings mapped to the computational basis, which for the 4x4 pixel images
n=16
. The algorithm performs the optimization of the parameters via stochastic gradient descent and use the Hinge loss function.

Dilip
et al.

Dilip et al. 2022
explored data compression for quantum machine learning by leveraging matrix-product states (MPS) to encode classical data into efficient quantum representations. The method focused on the classification of the 10-classes Fashion-MNIST data set, demonstrating that a MPS can reduce the quantum circuit depth and qubit requirements while maintaining the classification performance. The quantum demonstrations used the FRQI (flexible representation of quantum images) quantum feature map and a Hardware-efficient strategy based on MPS with 11 qubits for the variational ansatz. For training, they minimized the categorical cross entropy loss function using a L2 regularization. The authors claimed that the MPS-based classifier offers a competitive accuracy compared other quantum learning methods based on tensor networks and that it can be implemented on quantum computers with limited resources. Although the algorithm was designed for the classification of the 28x28 pixel images, we adjusted the model for the down-sampled 4x4 pixel images. In our implementation, we used 5 qubits to encode the corresponding
16
-dimensional flattened image vectors using the FRQI quantum map and measured only one qubit for performing the binary classification. As in the original proposal, we used the categorical cross entropy loss.

Havlicek
et al.

Havlíček et al. 2019
introduced the ZZ feature map, which encodes a data sample
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in[0,2\pi)^{n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}
to a
n_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}
-qubit quantum state

\ket{\varphi_{_{\mathcb{X}}}'}=U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\text{Z}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})H^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\text{Z}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})H^{\otimes n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\ket{0_{_{\mathcb{X}}}},

(55)

with

U_{{}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}^{\text{Z}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}x_{i}^{\prime}Z_{i}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}(\pi-x_{i}^{\prime})(\pi-x_{j}^{\prime})Z_{i}Z_{j}\right),

(56)

where
Z_{i}=\big[\bigotimes_{k=i+1}^{n_{{}_{{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}}}}\mathbf{I}_{2}\big]\otimes\sigma^{z}\otimes\big[\bigotimes_{k=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{I}_{2}\big]
is the Pauli-z matrix applied to qubit
i
and
x_{i}^{\prime}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
component of
\bm{x}^{\prime}
. In Fig.

4
