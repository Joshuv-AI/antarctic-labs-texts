Effect of introduction of an integrated electronic medical record system with semi-automated multi-trigger MET alerts on utilisation of a paediatric RRS and patient outcome
Authors: Mili Shah, Casey Fowler, Farah Zahir, Jason Acworth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQueensland Children’s Hospital (QCH) uses the Children’s Early Warning Tool (CEWT) to identify deterioration and recommend actions such as Medical Emergency Team (MET) activation. In April 2018, QCH implemented an integrated electronic medical record (ieMR) for calculating CEWT scores and a semi-automated trigger system for rapid response system (RRS) activation. This offered a mechanism to decrease obstacles to RRS activation but raised concerns of reduced clinician decision-making and increased unnecessary MET involvement.MethodsRRS activations from May 2016 to April 2020 were included. Pre- and post-intervention measures included MET dose, hospital mortality rate, in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) rate, and unplanned paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission rates. Descriptive statistics and interrupted time series (ITS) with segmental regression assessed trends and intervention impact.ObjectivesThe primary objective was to assess changes in RRS activation frequency two years before and after implementation. Secondary objectives included assessing clinical outcomes: inpatient mortality rate and unplanned PICU admissions requiring emergency interventions within 6 h of transfer.Results1284 eligible RRS activations were recorded. Post-intervention: MET dose increased 20 % (8.33 versus 6.90, p < 0.05), mean ward mortality rate decreased 52 % (0.16 versus 0.33, p < 0.05), and unplanned PICU admission rate (non-MET) decreased 15 % (3.42 versus 4.03, p < 0.05). IHCA rate, frequency of unplanned PICU admission (post-MET), or rates of emergency intervention within 6 h of PICU admission demonstrated no significant changes.ConclusionsThe semi-automated trigger for RRS activation suggested an effect of decreasing obstacles to RRS activation without associated with extra “unnecessary work” or delays in activation.

Introduction

The Medical Emergency Team (MET) model, first developed in Sydney, Australia in the early 1990s,1 was subsequently implemented internationally. The model described a hospital-wide, patient-centred system intended to enhance early identification of clinical deterioration and initiate a rapid response by a designated team with critical care expertise. Its objective was to deliver timely interventions aimed at reducing preventable adverse outcomes, including cardiac arrest and in-hospital mortality.2

Unlike traditional “code” or “arrest” team models, which typically responded after cardiorespiratory arrest occurred, the MET model targeted earlier stages of deterioration.3 It focused on recognising impending respiratory, cardiac, or neurological failure and facilitating proactive clinical management. This approach was particularly relevant to paediatric patients, in whom cardiac arrest was more often preceded by a prolonged period of potentially reversible decline, and outcomes following arrest were generally poorer compared to adults.4

Evidence from studies evaluating MET systems − more broadly termed Rapid Response Systems (RRS) − in paediatric settings have demonstrated reductions in respiratory and cardiac arrests, unexpected ward deaths, and overall hospital mortality.5, 6, 7 The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) has recommended the use of paediatric early warning systems to monitor hospitalised children and aid in the early detection of deterioration.8

To aid early recognition of deterioration, the Queensland Children’s Hospital uses the Children’s Early Warning Tool (CEWT).9 The initial introduction of CEWT involved bedside nurses entering patient clinical observations onto paper-based observation charts to generate a Children’s Early Warning Tool (CEWT) score. Depending on the recorded CEWT score, a tiered escalation approach guided actions (which sometimes involve escalation to a MET activation) initiated to improve patient condition and prevent deterioration.

Despite these systems, it was well recognised that barriers to implementation remained, resulting in delays to RRS activation and failure to rescue. Potential obstacles for bedside clinicians activating RRS10 included but were not limited to: level of clinical experience of clinicians involved in RRS activation; alterations to RRS activation criteria; reluctance of medical reviews; distribution of resources and inadequate staffing; and poor communication and documentation.

In April 2018, the Queensland Children’s Hospital implemented an integrated electronic medical record (ieMR) including a semi-automated trigger system for RRS activation. Bedside nurses entered patient clinical observations directly into the ieMR, the ieMR calculated the CEWT score and recommended appropriate actions, including MET activation if appropriate.

The introduction of the semi-automated trigger offered a potential mechanism to decrease some of the obstacles to bedside clinicians activating the RRS. There was also a risk that the semi-automation would decrease the need for clinician decision-making and could potentially increase the frequency of unnecessary MET involvement.

Our study aimed to investigate what effect the introduction into a tertiary paediatric hospital of an integrated electronic medical record system with semi-automated multi-trigger MET alerts had on utilisation of the RRS and patient outcomes.

Material and methods

This registry-based study describes the change in frequency of RRS activations at a specialist paediatric hospital from a 2-year period prior to the implementation of the system change compared to an equivalent 2-year period after the change. It examines patient demographics, patterns of activation and clinical outcomes of a cohort of patients triggering RRS activations across those two 2-year periods, including hospital mortality rate and unplanned PICU admissions requiring emergency interventions (intubation and/or commencement of an inotrope infusion) within 6 h of transfer.

Sources of data

This study reported upon events from a single specialist paediatric hospital – the Queensland Children’s Hospital (Children’s Health Queensland) in Brisbane, Australia.

RRS events recorded in the QCH RRS database from 1 May 2016 to 30 April 2020 were eligible for inclusion in the study. Data included RRS activations involving paediatric inpatients only. The RRS database records included in analysis contain data for MET activations and CODE BLUE activations to hospital inpatients.

Data related to annual patient separations (discharges) and hospital mortality was obtained from the Queensland Hospital Admitted Patient Data Collection (QHAPDC). Data related to ICU outcomes and interventions was obtained from the pre-existing dataset collected in the Queensland Children’s Hospital Paediatric Intensive Care unit (QCH PICU). The QCH PICU uses a modified measure of Critical Deterioration Event (CDE) adapted from Bonafide et al.11 including unplanned PICU admissions requiring emergency interventions (intubation and/or commencement of an inotrope infusion) within 6 h of transfer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A MET event was defined as any incident within the facility that prompted activation of the Medical Emergency Team. Triggers for MET activation included documented physiological abnormalities, staff concerns regarding patient status, or concerns raised by family members or visitors (Ryan’s Rule), in accordance with the hospital’s MET activation policies and procedures.

All MET events from 01 May 2016 to 30 April 2020 recorded in the QCH RRS database were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Database records containing data for false activations (accidental emergency button presses that did not result in MET attendance) or duplicate records were excluded from analysis. As well, data regarding RRS activations for non-hospital inpatients (e.g. visitors, staff members, outpatients, ED, ICU, and operating suite), RRS activations triggered by family members (Ryan’s Rule), and RRS activations for other “codes” (e.g. CODE ECMO, CODE STROKE) were excluded from the study.

Data collection and analysis

The study examined the utilisation of and patient outcomes from the QCH Rapid Response System in the 24 months preceding and the 24 months following implementation of the integrated electronic medical record (ieMR) including a semi-automated trigger system for RRS activation, introduced in April 2018. This four-year window was selected to enable a direct pre–post comparison while minimising the influence of known confounders.

The primary outcome was the change in MET dose (inpatient MET activations per 1000 hospital discharges) before and after the implementation of the new system, and exploration of impacts on the inputs into the system.

Secondary outcomes included the examination of impacts on patient outcomes and changes to the sensitivity of the system as determined by trends of false positives and false negatives. Examination of impacts on patient outcomes occurred through comparison of the following rates (calculated as per 1000 discharges): IHCA rate external to critical care areas, hospital mortality rate, PICU mortality rate, and the ward mortality rate. Changes to the sensitivity of the system were examined through comparisons of intubation and inotrope infusions within 6 h of admission to PICU from a ward environment.

Seven key variables were identified a priori as research questions and analysed to determine the overall impact of the intervention. Statistical analysis employed a combined descriptive and graphical approach. Descriptive statistics utilised a paired t-test comparing the pre-intervention mean to the post-intervention mean of each key variable. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.5.

To assess underlying trends ‘interrupted’ by an intervention at a known point in time, an interrupted time series (ITS) analysis with segmental regression was conducted. ITS was chosen as it allows for the distinction between changes occurring due to the intervention and those resulting from underlying secular trends, thus enhancing causal inference in the absence of a control group. As well, this study design is increasingly being used to evaluate public health interventions, particularly those introduced at a system level over a clearly defined period and targeting system-level health outcomes, like ours. The ITS model estimated three key parameters: (i) the pre-intervention trend, (ii) the immediate change in level following the intervention (immediate effect), and (iii) the post-intervention trend to assess any sustained effects (sustained effect).

Counterfactual models were also constructed to estimate outcomes had the intervention not occurred. This approach allowed a robust evaluation of the intervention’s impact on clinical outcomes by comparing actual observed results to predicted scenarios based on pre-intervention trends.

Ethical approval

The registry data collection included only data that was routinely collected as part of a patient’s admission and/or event of a MET activation. No additional patient data was collected as part of this study. Data extracted from the databases for this study did not include any identifiable data fields. A separate unique study ID number was allocated to each record for this study. The study was approved by the Children’s Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee as a Low and Negligible Risk category project [HREC/24/QCHQ/106181].

Results

Population characteristics

Between May 2016 and April 2020, a total of 3439 MET event records were entered into the database. After applying the exclusion criteria (false alarms, duplicate records), 1949 MET events were available for analysis. After excluding MET activations to non-hospital inpatients, triggered by family members, and RRS activations for other “codes”, 1284 inpatient MET activations were available. Discussion

The research conclusions can be considered in two parts. First, the system change did not produce the anticipated risks. Following the introduction of semi-automation, MET dose initially increased but ultimately stabilised over time, which may have suggested that the system potentially reached a state of equilibrium after the transitional phase. This initial increase might have reflected heightened system sensitivity or increased clinician responsiveness to the new alerts during the transition period. The subsequent stabilisation may have indicated improved clinician-system integration and growing familiarity with alert thresholds. Simultaneously, unplanned PICU admissions dependent on MET activation remained unchanged, suggesting that the system was not associated with an increase in unnecessary transfers. This consistency may have indicated that escalation decisions remained appropriate despite changes in MET activation frequency. In contrast, rates of unplanned PICU admissions independent of MET activation showed an initial decrease before returning to their previous trends. This transient decline may have reflected increased clinician vigilance in early detection of deterioration during the implementation phase, with the reversion suggesting the event as short-term adjustment rather than a sustained system effect. These observations may indicate that the system change, despite introducing a semi-automated process, did not contribute to unnecessary or redundant work for clinicians. Concerns that semi-automation might lead to a diminished ability of clinicians to make effective decisions, resulting in an increase in inappropriate or unnecessary MET activations, were not supported by the findings. Rather, the evidence suggests that patient care quality was maintained, and the clinical decision-making processes were not adversely impacted.

Second, the system change appeared to be associated with some anticipated benefits. There was an immediate decrease in ward mortality rate shortly after implementation, which may have corresponded with improved recognition and earlier escalation of deteriorating patients, potentially due to a lowered threshold for RRS activation following semi-automation. Concurrently, IHCA rate (outside of critical care areas) trended towards a decrease, which may have aligned with a pattern of earlier intervention that reduced the likelihood of cardiac arrest. Rates of intubation and inotrope use within six hours of PICU admission remained unchanged. This stability may have suggested that although patients were escalated more promptly, the severity of illness at PICU admission did not substantially differ, supporting the appropriateness of escalation decisions. It may have also indicated that the timing of escalation changed without changing the threshold for initiating critical interventions. These findings suggest that the system change may have contributed to earlier detection for at-risk patients without delaying in RRS activation. Thus, the implementation of semi-automation may have supported timely clinical escalation by reducing barriers to RRS activation and has potential in improving overall clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design, affecting both validity and generalisability. As with any observational cohort study, internal validity is limited by the potential for confounding factors (other than the described change to the RRS) that may have influenced patient and system outcomes during the study period e.g., internationally observed trends in decreasing IHCA rates outside of ICU. The interrupted time series analysis and counterfactual modelling was used to partially account for these potential biases. The selected timeframe (May 2016 to April 2020) was specifically chosen to avoid the potential confounding effects of other quality improvement initiatives (including changes to MET member composition and change to a dual-tiered system – MET or CODE BLUE activation). However, this timeframe excludes the COVID-19 era and earlier data, limiting broader applicability and insight into long-term effects. Outcome measures were selected for data reliability, but their context-dependent interpretation complicates conclusions. For instance, an increased MET dose may indicate better deterioration recognition or unnecessary activations, making it difficult to determine the intervention’s true impact. Although MET dose increased post-intervention, the effect waned over time, suggesting limited long-term benefit. The focus on intubation and inotrope usage within six hours of PICU admission provided only a narrow view of early intervention, potentially missing important aspects of pre-transfer care. Finally, the minimal effect of the automated trigger system on mortality and IHCA rates implies that other factors, such as treatment efficacy or patient complexity, likely influenced outcomes, weakening the ability to make conclusions about the system’s overall effectiveness.

Conclusion

Data from this single specialist paediatric hospital suggested that the implementation of an ieMR with semi-automated multi-trigger was not associated with extra unnecessary work in the rapid response system and may have been associated with some improved outcomes.
