Dioscin attenuates Gd-IgA1 overproduction by inhibiting the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis in IgA nephropathy
Authors: Lan Lin, HuiMei Li, ShiQiang Zhou, LiHua Zheng, ShiPeng Sun, Shen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammation is a key driver in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) pathogenesis. Recent studies have demonstrated a significant association between pro-inflammatory cytokine–triggered inflammatory cascades, particularly those involving interleukin-6 (IL-6), and disease progression in IgAN. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Dioscin (DIO), a major active compound in Yiqi Qingjie Formula— a standardized formula for IgAN treatment at Guang’anmen Hospital—has shown well-defined anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 68 patients with IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and 16 healthy controls to assess key components of the IL-6 signaling pathway. An IgAN mouse model was established to evaluate the therapeutic effects of dioscin (DIO) and its potential anti-inflammatory mechanisms. The findings were further validated through complementary in vitro experiments.ResultsWe observed aberrant activation of the IL-6-mediated STAT3 signaling pathway in patients with IgAN. In vitro experiments further confirmed that IL-6 promoted the secretion of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) by activating the JAK2/STAT3 axis, which downregulated the expression of the key glycosyltransferases Core 1 β1,3-galactosyltransferase (C1GalT1) and Core 1 β3GalT-specific molecular chaperone (Cosmc). Importantly, DIO significantly inhibited JAK2/STAT3 phosphorylation in IgAN model mice, a finding that was validated again in vitro.ConclusionThis study shows that DIO reduces Gd-IgA1 production and alleviates renal injury by targeting and suppressing the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for IgAN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04614-1.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is one of the most common primary glomerular diseases worldwide. Its hallmark pathological feature is the deposition of immune complexes in the glomerular mesangium, primarily composed of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), often accompanied by co-deposition of C3. These deposits can further induce mesangial cell proliferation and extracellular matrix expansion, ultimately leading to renal inflammation and fibrosis [1]. From an epidemiological perspective, the annual incidence of IgAN is approximately 2.5 cases per 100,000 population [2, 3], accounting for 40–50% of all renal biopsy diagnoses [4]. Notably, IgAN is a highly progressive disease: 50%–75% of patients develop end-stage renal disease within 20 years of diagnosis [5], and up to 50% of kidney transplant recipients experience recurrence of mesangial IgA deposition within 5 years after transplantation [6]. Given its high prevalence, poor prognosis, and significant recurrence risk, IgAN has emerged as a critical global public health concern. However, current treatment strategies remain largely supportive. Although novel targeted agents such as Nefecon and sparsentan have recently been approved, their clinical use is limited by high cost, relatively slow onset of action, and ongoing debate regarding their benefit-risk profiles [5]. Therefore, exploring interventions based on Traditional Chinese Medicine may provide more effective and accessible therapeutic options, offering new avenues to reduce the global burden of IgAN.

Inflammatory responses play a central role in the pathogenesis of IgAN. Patients with IgAN frequently experience episodes of gross hematuria associated with upper respiratory and/or gastrointestinal infections, suggesting a pathological link between mucosal inflammation and renal injury [7–9]. Notably, Gd-IgA1 in circulating immune complexes is predominantly polymeric—a structural characteristic typically associated with mucosal immune responses. Peyer’s patches (PPs), the key lymphoid tissues of intestinal mucosal immunity, are closely involved in the secretion of Gd-IgA1 [10]. Genetic studies further support this mechanism. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified susceptibility loci for IgAN that are enriched in genes related to innate and mucosal immunity [11]. Recent evidence has also revealed associations between pro-inflammatory cytokine genes and IgAN [12], particularly members of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) cytokine family, such as leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) and oncostatin M (OSM). Consistent with these findings, our previous research demonstrated that serum Gd-IgA1 levels were significantly elevated in IgAN patients with mucosal inflammation and positively correlated with circulating IL-6 levels [13]. These observations suggest that mucosal infections may upregulate IL-6 and other inflammatory mediators, thereby disrupting the O-glycosylation process of IgA1 and leading to excessive production of Gd-IgA1. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this process remain to be elucidated.

IL-6 activates downstream signaling pathways primarily through engagement with the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of the transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway [14]. The IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) complex comprises membrane-bound IL-6 receptor α (IL-6Rα) and the signal-transducing subunit gp130. Importantly, in cells lacking membrane-bound IL-6Rα, soluble IL-6Rα (sIL-6Rα) can bind IL-6 to form a complex that associates with membrane-bound gp130 to initiate intracellular signaling. This process, known as IL-6 trans-signaling, is primarily responsible for mediating pro-inflammatory effects [15]. Because gp130 is a common signal-transducing component shared among all IL-6 cytokine family members and is constitutively expressed in most cell types, IL-6 has the theoretical capacity to activate a broad spectrum of cells via gp130-mediated signaling.

The JAK/STAT pathway serves as a critical intracellular signaling hub that mediates receptor activation and nuclear transcriptional regulation in response to inflammatory cytokine stimulation [16]. Aberrant activation of this pathway has been documented in a variety of kidney diseases, including diabetic nephropathy, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, HIV-associated nephropathy, acute kidney injury, and obstructive uropathy, most prominently involving the JAK2/STAT3 axis [17]. In IgAN, the pathological relevance of this pathway has been well established. Phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) levels are positively correlated with disease progression [18]. Upregulation of JAK2 and p-STAT1 has been observed in glomerular mesangial and endothelial cells, while increased staining of JAK2, p-STAT1, and p-STAT3 has been detected in the tubulointerstitial compartment, indicating widespread activation of the JAK-STAT pathway in renal tissue from patients with IgAN [19].

Yiqi Qingjie Formula (YQQJ) is a standardized prescription used in the Department of Nephrology at Guang’anmen Hospital for the treatment of IgAN. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that YQQJ significantly improves renal function, reduces proteinuria, and alleviates mucosal inflammation in patients with IgAN [20]. Among its components, Dioscoreae Nipponicae Rhizoma (Chuanshanlong), a herb known for dispelling wind, eliminating dampness, and promoting blood circulation, is the most prominently used and distinctive. Using network pharmacology analysis, we identified Dioscin (DIO) as the major active compound of DNR. Our previous studies, based on molecular docking and in vitro experiments, suggested that DIO may act on a key target in IgAN—Caspase-3—to inhibit the proliferation of LPS-stimulated DAKIKI cells, a human B lymphocyte line capable of secreting IgA [21]. Furthermore, in vivo and in vitro studies have revealed that DIO suppresses the overactivation of IgA-secreting cells and enhances the expression of core 1 β1,3-galactosyltransferase (C1GalT1) and its specific molecular chaperone (Cosmc), thereby reducing Gd-IgA1 production [22].

Given the crucial role of the inflammatory microenvironment in regulating Gd-IgA1 synthesis, the present study first compared the expression levels of key molecules in the IL-6-STAT3 signaling pathway in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from IgAN patients and healthy controls. Considering the known anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties of DIO, we further investigated its anti-inflammatory mechanisms in IgAN, with a particular focus on its regulatory effect on the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 pathway. We established an IgAN model mouse using mucosal immune stimulation, followed by histopathological assessments and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis to evaluate renal and Peyer’s patch (PPs) lesions. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to detect the expression of STAT3 signaling molecules (total STAT3, p-STAT3, and p-JAK2) in PPs. In vitro, we used IL-6-stimulated DAKIKI cells to evaluate the regulatory effects of DIO on aberrant Gd-IgA1 production and the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval of participants and animals

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No. 2023-205-KY), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Animal experiments were conducted following institutional guidelines and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Centre of Guang’anmen Hospital (Protocol No. IACUC-GAMH-2023-003). Male Balb/c mice (6–7 weeks old, 20–25 g) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Guang’anmen Hospital. Before sample collection, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.4% sodium pentobarbital.

Patients

Patients with IgAN were enrolled in the Department of Nephrology at Guang’anmen Hospital between November 2023 and March 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) biopsy-confirmed diagnosis of primary IgAN, (2) age ≥ 18 years, and (3) availability of complete clinical and pathological data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) secondary forms of IgAN, such as those associated with IgA vasculitis, lupus nephritis, or chronic liver disease; and (2) receipt of immunosuppressive therapy within the preceding six months. A total of 68 patients were selected using stratified random sampling. 16 healthy individuals, matched for age and sex, were recruited as the control group. Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants on the day of clinical evaluation, processed by the Guang’anmen Hospital Biobank, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Isolation of PBMCs

Peripheral blood samples were processed within 4 h of collection into anticoagulant tubes. PBMCs were isolated using a human peripheral blood lymphocyte separation solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an equal volume of 1× PBS was added to the anticoagulated blood and mixed gently. The diluted blood was carefully layered onto the separation solution in a sterile centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 450 × g for 30 min at room temperature. The PBMC layer was collected from the interface, washed twice with PBS, and centrifuged at 450 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet (PBMCs) was resuspended in 0.5 mL of TRIzol and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Samples from IgAN patients and healthy controls were processed under identical conditions.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs using an RNA extraction reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and reverse transcription was performed using a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). qRT-PCR was carried out in a 15 µL reaction system containing 7.5 µL of 2× SYBR Master Mix (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 1.5 µL of primers at a concentration of 2.5 µmol/L. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene to normalize mRNA expression levels. The qRT-PCR amplification program was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Establishment of the IgAN mouse model and group administration

After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to two groups. The normal control group received free access to 6 mmol/L HCl-acidified water for 9 weeks and was injected with sterile saline via the tail vein for consecutive 3 days. The IgAN group was modeled according to the protocol described by Zou et al. [23]. These mice were provided with acidified drinking water containing 0.1% bovine gamma globulin (BGG; YuanYe, Shanghai, China) for 9 weeks and were injected with an equal volume of 0.1% BGG saline solution via the tail vein.

At the end of the modeling period, two mice from each group were randomly selected and subjected to renal histopathological and immunofluorescence examination to confirm model establishment. Light microscopy revealed mild mesangial cell proliferation and matrix expansion in renal tissues, while immunofluorescence staining showed granular or clumped green IgA deposition in the glomerular mesangial area, indicating successful model induction.

The successfully modeled mice were further randomized into the Model group and the DIO treatment group (DIO group). Based on the dose conversion formula from Pharmacological Experimental Methodology (calculated for a 70 kg adult) [24], mice in the DIO group received 0.06 g/kg intragastric administration of Dioscin tablets (Chengdu No.1 Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). Mice in the Control and Model groups received equal volumes of saline.

Histological evaluation

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections of 2 μm thickness were obtained from the kidneys, and 4 μm sections were obtained from PPs. Sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and examined under a light microscope to evaluate histopathological changes.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval using either citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 9.0). For immunohistochemistry (IHC), endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Non-specific binding was blocked with 10% goat serum (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-p-JAK2 (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA), anti-p-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, Framingham, MA, USA), and anti-STAT3 (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA). The next day, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAB color development. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining, after which sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted using neutral resin. For immunofluorescence (IF), sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with Goat Anti-Mouse IgA-AF488 (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA). Nuclear counterstaining was performed with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Slides were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and imaged using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Cell culture

DAKIKI cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged every 2–3 days, and those in the logarithmic growth phase were used for subsequent experiments.

CCK-8 assay

When cell confluence reached approximately 80%, cells were harvested and centrifuged at 140 × g for 5 min. Cells were then seeded into 96-well plates and treated according to experimental groupings. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, 10% volume of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Plates were further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

ELISA

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to different treatments for 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 850 × g for 10 min. The supernatants were carefully aspirated to avoid disturbing the cell pellets and transferred to new tubes. The concentrations of total IgA and Gd-IgA1 were measured using ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates, and total protein was extracted. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto 12% SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes at a constant current of 300 mA for 2 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-C1GalT1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Cosmc (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-p-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, Framingham, MA, USA), and anti-STAT3 (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using ECL reagents (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and visualized with a gel imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.4 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (P25, P75). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between two groups for normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using unpaired t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed data, comparisons between two or more groups were carried out using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Activation of the IL-6-STAT3 signaling pathway was observed in patients with IgAN

We performed quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to compare the mRNA expression levels of IL-6R, gp130, and STAT3 in PBMCs from IgAN patients and healthy controls. The baseline clinical characteristics of the 68 patients with IgAN and 16 healthy individuals are presented in Table S1. Compared with healthy controls, mRNA levels of IL-6R, gp130, and STAT3 were significantly upregulated in the IgAN group (Fig. 1), suggesting activation of the IL-6-STAT3 signaling pathway in IgAN patients.

DIO alleviated renal injury in mucosal immunity–induced IgAN model mouse

To investigate the therapeutic potential of DIO’s anti-inflammatory effects in IgAN, an IgAN mouse model was established via mucosal immunization (Fig. 2A). Compared with the control group, the model group exhibited mesangial IgA deposition in a clumped pattern (Fig. 2B), along with mesangial cell proliferation and matrix expansion (Fig. 2C). These pathological features were notably alleviated by DIO treatment.

DIO inhibited activation of the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway

PPs, a key site of mucosal immune responses, are important inductive regions for differentiating B lymphocytes into IgA-secreting plasma cells and are closely associated with Gd-IgA1 production [25]. Consistent with renal pathology, the model group exhibited marked lymphocyte hyperplasia and disrupted architecture in PPs, whereas these pathological changes were notably suppressed in the DIO-treated group (Fig. 3A). To further elucidate the underlying mechanism of DIO in IgAN, immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to evaluate the expression of key molecules in the JAK2/STAT3 pathway within PPs. Compared with the control group, the model group exhibited increased expression of p-JAK2, p-STAT3, and total STAT3 (Fig. 3B). DIO treatment markedly suppressed the expression of these molecules, suggesting that DIO may exert its effects by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.

Mechanism of IL-6–induced Gd-IgA1 secretion

To validate the anti-inflammatory mechanism of DIO in IgAN, we treated DAKIKI cells with varying concentrations of IL-6. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that IL-6 at concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 100 ng/mL significantly promoted DAKIKI cell proliferation after 24 h of stimulation, with the most pronounced effect observed at 50 ng/mL (Fig. 4A).

Gd-IgA1 plays a key role in the pathogenesis of IgAN, and total IgA production serves as an indicator of the secretory function of DAKIKI cells. ELISA results showed that IL-6 stimulation for 24 h increased IgA secretion across all tested concentrations compared to the control group, although no clear dose-dependent pattern was observed (Fig. 4B). Notably, Gd-IgA1 levels increased under IL-6 stimulation at 12.5 ng/mL and 25 ng/mL (Fig. 4C).

To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying IL–6–induced overproduction of Gd-IgA1, we performed Western blot analysis to assess the expression of key glycosylation enzymes involved in Gd-IgA1 synthesis—C1GalT1 and Cosmc. The results showed that Compared with the control group, Cosmc protein expression was significantly downregulated after 24 h of IL-6 stimulation (Fig. 4D). C1GalT1 protein levels also decreased, but only at IL-6 concentrations of 12.5 ng/mL and 25 ng/mL (Fig. 4E).

Based on these findings, we selected IL-6 at 25 ng/mL to stimulate DAKIKI cells in subsequent experiments to establish a cell model of Gd-IgA1 hypersecretion.

DIO inhibited IL-6–induced proliferation of DAKIKI cells

Our previous study demonstrated that the safe concentration range of DIO is 0.25–1.0 µg/mL [21]. CCK-8 assay results showed that IL-6 stimulation significantly promoted the proliferation of DAKIKI cells compared with the control group. DIO treatment at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 µg/mL effectively suppressed IL–6–induced cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, with the most pronounced inhibitory effect observed at 1.0 µg/mL (Fig. 5A).

DIO suppresses Gd-IgA1 secretion via the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 pathway

ELISA results demonstrated that DAKIKI cells in the model group secreted significantly higher IgA levels than the control group. DIO treatment inhibited IL–6–induced IgA secretion dose-dependently (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, IL-6 stimulation markedly increased Gd-IgA1 secretion by DAKIKI cells, which was also dose-dependently suppressed by DIO (Fig. 5C). Among the tested concentrations, 1.0 µg/mL DIO exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, with a suppression rate of 20.8%.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying DIO-mediated inhibition of Gd-IgA1 overproduction, we examined the effect of DIO on the IL–6–induced JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. As shown by qRT-PCR analysis, IL-6 stimulation significantly upregulated JAK2 (Fig. 5D) and STAT3 (Fig. 5E) mRNA levels in DAKIKI cells compared with the control group. DIO downregulated the expression of both genes in a dose-dependent manner, with the most pronounced effects observed at 1.0 µg/mL.

Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in the p-STAT3/STAT3 ratio in the model group, indicating activation of the STAT3 pathway. DIO treatment at all tested concentrations reduced the phosphorylation of STAT3 (Fig. 5F), suggesting that DIO inhibits IL–6–induced activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, thereby attenuating Gd-IgA1 secretion.

Discussion

Aberrant activation of IgA-secreting cells within the mucosal immune system, resulting in elevated circulating levels of Gd-IgA1, is a central event in the pathogenesis of IgAN [26]. Therefore, elucidating the origin of Gd-IgA1 and identifying strategies to suppress its production are critical for the prevention and treatment of IgAN. Previous studies have implicated mucosal immune dysregulation in the pathophysiology of IgAN; accumulating evidence supports the association between intestinal mucosal immune dysfunction and the development of IgAN [10, 27]. Our previous study further demonstrated that IgAN patients with concomitant mucosal inflammation exhibit significantly elevated serum levels of Gd-IgA1.

IL-6, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, has been shown to drive the production of Gd-IgA1 [18, 28, 29]. Consistent with these findings, our earlier research revealed that serum IL-6 levels are increased in IgAN patients and positively correlate with Gd-IgA1 concentrations [13]. Notably, the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of IgAN [15]. Inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation has been shown to reduce the generation of autoantigens. Mechanistically, IL-6 binds to its receptor complex, subsequently activating JAK2 and inducing the phosphorylation of STAT3, which then forms the activated p-STAT3 [18]. Based on the abnormal activation of the IL-6-STAT3 pathway observed in PBMCs from IgAN patients in this study, we hypothesize that IL-6 may promote Gd-IgA1 secretion via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis.

In this study, we employed the surface IgA-positive human B lymphocyte line DAKIKI as an in vitro model to investigate the mechanisms underlying aberrant glycosylation of IgA1 [30]. IgA1, as a highly glycosylated molecule, requires the coordinated action of C1GalT1 and its specific molecular chaperone Cosmc for proper O-glycan synthesis. Downregulation of either protein disrupts this process, leading to the production of Gd-IgA1, which contributes to immune complex deposition and triggers downstream pathogenic cascades in IgAN [8, 31]. Our findings demonstrated that IL-6 stimulation significantly suppressed the expression of both C1GalT1 and Cosmc in DAKIKI cells, resulting in increased secretion of Gd-IgA1, in agreement with previous studies [7, 32]. Concurrently, IL-6 exposure led to marked upregulation of JAK2 and STAT3 mRNA expression and increased STAT3 phosphorylation. These results suggest that IL-6 promotes excessive Gd-IgA1 production at least in part through activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Taken together, our study indicates that the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 axis may contribute to Gd-IgA1 generation by suppressing the expression of C1GalT1 and Cosmc.

DIO, a natural steroidal saponin with well-documented anti-inflammatory properties [33], was further evaluated in this study for its renal protective effects in IgAN. In IgAN model mice, DIO treatment significantly attenuated renal injury, which was accompanied by inhibition of aberrant JAK2/STAT3 activation. To further elucidate its anti-inflammatory mechanism, we established an in vitro model of Gd-IgA1 overproduction using IL-6-stimulated DAKIKI cells. DIO treatment effectively suppressed IL-6-induced phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 and significantly reduced Gd-IgA1 secretion. These findings highlight the potential of DIO to modulate IL-6-driven inflammatory signaling.

This study has several limitations. First, although our findings suggest the potential therapeutic value of DIO in IgAN, DIO tablets are currently approved only as an adjuvant therapy for coronary heart disease and angina pectoris and have not yet been authorized for the treatment of IgAN. Therefore, we were unable to administer DIO tablets to the patients enrolled in this study, which limited our ability to establish a direct connection between the clinical observations and the subsequent experimental findings. Second, the relatively small cohort of IgAN patients limited our ability to comprehensively assess all upstream and downstream regulatory factors of the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 pathway. Third, mice are unable to produce Gd-IgA1 due to species-specific characteristics, which is one of the reasons for the lack of ideal animal models for studying IgAN pathogenesis. Therefore, we are unable to provide direct in vivo evidence of DIO inhibiting Gd-IgA1. Finally, due to experimental limitations, we did not perform JAK2/STAT3 pathway inhibition or gene knockout experiments to validate the effects of DIO on this signaling pathway and its precise molecular targets. These experiments will be addressed in future studies.

Nevertheless, our study provides a compelling chain of evidence indicating that the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 axis plays a pathogenic role in IgAN and that DIO exerts its therapeutic effects, at least in part, by targeting this signaling pathway to reduce Gd-IgA1 production. Therefore, the IL-6-JAK2/STAT3 pathway may serve as a potential therapeutic target for IgAN. DIO, as a major active component of several traditional Chinese herbs, possesses clear pathway inhibitory activity and multi-target regulatory effects, making it a promising novel candidate for this disease, with particularly greater accessibility for IgAN patients in China.
