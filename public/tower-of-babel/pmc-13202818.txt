Defect morphology dictates success: suprabony lesions outperform intrabony defects in non-surgical periodontal therapy: a 3-month prospective observational study
Authors: Peipei Zhang, Ran Huang, Xueli Zhang, Wenlu Li, Zhuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the clinical response of suprabony and intrabony defects to non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) and evaluate the influence of defect morphology on treatment outcomes.MethodsA prospective observational study with a 3-month follow-up was conducted on 413 patients who underwent NSPT at a tertiary hospital. Periapical radiographs and clinical parameters, including probing pocket depth (PPD), bleeding on probing (BoP), and clinical attachment level (CAL), were assessed at baseline and 3-month follow-up. Defects were classified as suprabony or intrabony based on radiographic morphology. Multilevel modeling was used to analyze the contributions of site-, tooth-, patient-, and treatment-related factors to clinical outcomes, with pocket closure (PPD ≤ 4 mm without BoP) as the primary endpoint.ResultsIntrabony defects exhibited greater absolute PPD reduction (0.9 ± 2.4 mm) compared to suprabony defects (0.7 ± 1.8 mm), a difference attributable to their deeper baseline PPD (6.1 ± 2.4 mm vs. 4.2 ± 2.0 mm). Multilevel modeling, after adjusting for baseline severity, demonstrated that defect morphology was the strongest predictor of therapeutic success. Suprabony defects achieved a greater adjusted PPD reduction and CAL gain (by 0.6 mm each) compared to intrabony defects. Suprabony defects demonstrated superior pocket closure rates and were 2.4 times more likely to achieve closure than intrabony defects. Site-related factors accounted for 64.9% of outcome variability, followed by patient- (26.1%), tooth- (9.0%), and treatment-related factors (1%). Multilevel analysis confirmed defect morphology as the strongest predictor of success, with suprabony defects showing 0.6 mm greater PPD reduction and CAL gain.ConclusionDefect morphology significantly influences NSPT outcomes, with suprabony defects exhibiting higher pocket closure rates and better clinical improvements than intrabony defects. These findings highlight the need for morphology-adapted treatment strategies in periodontal therapy. However, the interpretation of these results should be cautious due to the relatively short 3-month follow-up period, which may not fully reflect long-term treatment stability and periodontal remodeling.

Introduction

Periodontal bone defects result from a complex interplay between dysbiotic subgingival biofilms and a dysregulated host inflammatory response, leading to the progressive destruction of the alveolar bone in periodontitis. These defects are broadly categorized into intrabony (vertical/angular) defects and suprabony (horizontal) defects, based on their morphological relationship to the residual alveolar crest [1]. In intrabony defects, the base of the periodontal pocket extends apically beyond the alveolar crest, creating a three-walled, two-walled, or one-walled osseous architecture. In contrast, suprabony defects exhibit a pocket base coronal to the bone crest, with horizontal bone loss resulting in a more uniform reduction in alveolar height [2].

The primary goal of non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT), including scaling and root planing (SRP), is to reduce probing pocket depth (PPD) and achieve clinical attachment gain, ultimately leading to pocket closure (PPD ≤ 4 mm without bleeding on probing [BoP]) [3]. While suprabony defects typically respond well to NSPT due to their accessibility for biofilm disruption and resolution of inflammation, intrabony defects present a greater therapeutic challenge. The confined anatomy of intrabony defects impedes complete debridement, perpetuating microbial persistence and impairing soft tissue reattachment. Consequently, the European Federation of Periodontology S3 treatment guidelines [4] recommend regenerative surgery for intrabony defects ≥ 3 mm with residual PPD > 5 mm and BoP after initial therapy, given their limited response to NSPT alone.

However, emerging evidence challenges this paradigm, suggesting that NSPT can induce significant clinical and radiographic improvements in intrabony defects. A prior trial reported mean PPD reductions of 2.1–3.5 mm in intrabony defects post-NSPT, though variability exists based on defect configuration and patient-specific factors. The potential influence of defect morphology (e.g., depth, width, and number of walls) was highlighted on NSPT outcomes, though high heterogeneity among studies precludes definitive conclusions [5]. While the existing literature includes extensive research on intrabony defects, there remains a relative paucity of direct comparative studies evaluating the differential response of suprabony versus intrabony defects to NSPT and the specific influence of defect morphology on non‑surgical outcomes.

The therapeutic potential of adjunctive agents in modulating NSPT outcomes has been increasingly explored, with differential responses observed between defect morphologies. In suprabony defects, adjuncts such as cross-linked xHya have demonstrated enhanced early healing and clinical attachment gain when combined with minimally invasive non-surgical techniques, as shown in a randomized controlled trial by Iorio-Siciliano et al. [6] where xHya improved early-phase outcomes in intrabony defects treated with MINST, though long-term superiority was not sustained. Similarly, adjunctive propolis, a natural phytotherapeutic agent with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, has shown promising clinical improvements in periodontal parameters when used as an adjunct to NSPT, particularly in supragingival biofilm control and inflammation reduction, as highlighted in a recent narrative review [7]. Conversely, the adjunctive use of probiotics has yielded inconsistent clinical benefits across studies, with outcomes varying by strain, formulation, and delivery protocol, underscoring the need for standardized regimens in periodontal adjunctive therapy [8]. Furthermore, the efficacy of adjuncts like sodium hypochlorite gel in conjunction with MINST has been demonstrated in reducing periodontal inflammation and improving clinical outcomes, particularly in deep pockets, reinforcing the role of localized chemotherapeutic agents in enhancing NSPT efficacy [9]. Collectively, these studies emphasize that while adjunctive therapies can enhance NSPT outcomes, their efficacy is influenced by defect morphology, agent selection, and delivery protocols, supporting the need for morphology-adapted treatment strategies in periodontal management.

Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the differential response of intrabony versus suprabony defects to NSPT and to address the existing evidence gap regarding the influence of defect morphology on treatment outcomes. While current guidelines often recommend surgical intervention for intrabony defects based on presumed limited response to non-surgical therapy, this paradigm is supported by limited comparative studies and is often confounded by baseline severity and patient-related factors. Using a cross-sectional design with multilevel modeling, this investigation tests the null hypothesis that pocket closure rates (PPD ≤ 4 mm) at 3 months post-treatment do not differ between defect types, while controlling for site-specific, patient-related, and treatment-associated variables. By isolating the effect of defect morphology and focusing on clinically relevant pocket closure as the primary endpoint, this study aims to clarify morphological predictors of NSPT success, thereby refining clinical decision-making for non-surgical versus surgical intervention and guiding the targeted use of adjunctive therapies toward more personalized periodontal care.

Patients and methods

Study design

This prospective observational study with a longitudinal follow-up component was conducted at a tertiary care hospital using clinical samples and data obtained from patients who provided written informed consent for research participation between January 2020 and December 2024. The study protocol, including data and sample collection procedures, was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Patients were included if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had a diagnosis of periodontitis, presented with at least one site exhibiting a PPD ≥ 5 mm along with radiographic evidence of either a suprabony or intrabony defect, completed a full course of NSPT, and attended the 3‑month follow‑up reassessment. Exclusion criteria comprised systemic conditions that contraindicated periodontal treatment (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes or immunosuppression), the use of antibiotics or anti‑inflammatory drugs within the three months preceding therapy, pregnancy or lactation, a history of periodontal surgery at the examined sites within the past year, and incomplete radiographic or clinical records. The final analysis included 413 consecutive patients who completed the full course of NSPT during the study period and met all inclusion criteria.

Minimization of bias

In accordance with STROBE guidelines, several measures were implemented to mitigate potential biases. Selection bias was minimized by consecutively enrolling all eligible patients and rigorously applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. To address measurement bias, periodontal and radiographic assessments were performed by calibrated examiners using standardized protocols and validated software, with inter‑examiner reliability confirmed (ICC 0.824–0.997); examiners were blinded to defect classification during follow‑up where feasible. Key confounders including baseline probing depth, age, smoking status, diabetes, oral hygiene, and tooth type were recorded and adjusted for using multilevel modeling, which also accounted for hierarchical clustering of sites, teeth, and patients. Information bias was reduced through prospective data collection with standardized case‑report forms and regular audits. Despite these precautions, residual confounding cannot be excluded given the observational design.

Clinical examination

Demographic data, medical history, and dental records were collected, including diabetes status and self-reported smoking habits (daily frequency, years of smoking, and former smoking history). Uncontrolled diabetes was defined as HbA1c > 6.5% in a pre-treatment chairside test. During the research visit, anthropometric measurements (height, weight, waist circumference) were taken. Tooth type (molar/non-molar) was recorded, and periodontal examinations were performed at six sites per tooth using a UNC-15 probe, assessing full-mouth plaque score (FMPS, binary), PPD, gingival recession (REC), BoP, tooth mobility, and furcation involvement. Clinical attachment level (CAL) was calculated as PPD + REC, where REC was recorded as a positive value in millimeters when gingival recession was present, and as 0 mm in the absence of recession.

Radiological assessment

During the first research visit, available digital long-cone periapical radiographs of all sites with PPD > 4 mm were analyzed using dedicated measurement software (Emago®, Oral Diagnostic Systems, Amsterdam). A calibrated examiner evaluated mesial and distal sites, measuring bone loss using predefined markers [10], with ICC ranging from 0.824 to 0.997 across seven parameters. Bone defects were classified as suprabony (coronal bone height difference < 2 mm at defect base) or intrabony (vertical defect depth with ≥ 2 mm horizontal bone loss). The analysis excluded missing teeth, impacted third molars, partially erupted teeth, implants, deciduous teeth, residual roots, and unclear radiographs. Interradicular regions were also excluded due to potential misclassification of anatomical features (e.g., grooves/slopes) as bone defects.

Non-surgical periodontal therapy

The initial phase of periodontal treatment involves subgingival debridement in all sites with PPD > 3 mm and full-mouth plaque score (FMPS) < 40%. This NSPT is performed by undergraduate students, graduate students, healthcare professionals, or periodontal staff using ultrasonic instruments, hand instruments, a combination of both, all under direct specialist supervision. Recorded data include baseline/follow-up FMPS (when available), therapist credentials, adjunctive therapies, number of NSPT sessions, instrumentation method, subsequent treatments (including surgery), final NSPT/reassessment dates, clinical outcomes, and last appointment date.

Periodontal re-assessment

The patient was reevaluated approximately 3 months after completing NSPT. Periodontal measurements of all bone defects were compared between baseline and follow-up assessments. The primary outcome was pocket closure, defined as a reduction in PPD to ≤ 4 mm with BoP-negative at the 3-month follow-up.

Sample calculation

According to published literature [11], approximately 74% of bone defects (including both intrabony and suprabony defects) achieve pocket closure after NSPT. Existing reports suggest that suprabony defects occur about ten times more frequently than intrabony defects. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that 80% of defects will demonstrate successful pocket closure post-treatment. For sample size calculation, we considered these defect types as independent groups: Intrabony defects (less common): 50 defects; Suprabony defects: 600 defects (assuming 10× frequency). To detect a clinically significant difference with 90% power, we accounted for ICC between sites within the same tooth (ICC = 0.5) and between teeth within the same patient (ICC = 0.2). With each tooth having two measured sites and an average of 8 affected teeth per patient, the minimum required sample size is 200 patients.

Statistic analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for patient demographics (age, BMI, gender, race, smoking status, diabetes status) and clinical characteristics (brushing frequency, interdental cleaning habits, periodontal measurements, defect classification, defect depth, and proportions of suprabony and intrabony defects). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‑normally distributed data are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Periodontal outcomes included baseline and post‑treatment measurements of BoP, PPD, and CAL. A multilevel model (MLM) was employed to assess associations between clinical outcomes (pocket closure, PPD reduction, CAL gain) and baseline predictors (defect type: suprabony vs. intrabony), with subgroup analyses performed for non‑adjuvant therapy cases. The MLM was structured to account for the hierarchical nesting of sites within teeth, and teeth within patients, defined as Level 1: Site (measurement site, n = 18,762); Level 2: Tooth (tooth containing the site, n = 10,023); and Level 3: Patient (individual patient, n = 413). Fixed effects included defect type, baseline PPD, therapist qualification, adjunct use, number of NSPT sessions, instrument type, re‑evaluation time, tooth type, patient age, sex, and diabetes status, selected based on clinical relevance and previous evidence of their potential influence on NSPT outcomes. Random effects were specified for the intercept at the tooth and patient levels to capture clustering due to shared anatomical and biological characteristics. Model consistency was verified by examining residual plots and likelihood‑ratio tests, and multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors, all of which were < 2.0. The relative contribution of predictor groups (site‑, tooth‑, patient‑, and treatment‑related factors) was quantified using variance partitioning analysis. For continuous outcomes (PPD reduction, CAL gain), linear mixed‑effects models were fitted; for binary outcomes (pocket closure), generalized linear mixed‑effects models with a logit link were used.

Smoking status was recorded at baseline (never, former, current). Initially, smoking was included as a fixed effect in the multilevel model. However, due to the relatively low prevalence of current smokers and the uneven distribution across defect‑type subgroups, the variable did not reach statistical significance and did not alter the effect estimates of the primary predictors (defect type and baseline PPD). Therefore, to maintain model parsimony and stability, smoking status was not retained in the final reported model. We acknowledge that this may limit the ability to fully adjust for smoking‑related healing impairment, particularly in populations with higher smoking prevalence.

Patients could present with either or both defect types. The analysis was conducted at the site level, with each site classified exclusively as suprabony, intrabony, or no defect based on radiographic criteria.

For defects achieving pocket closure, additional MLM‑based subgroup analyses adjusted for baseline PPD, age, gender, and smoking status. All analyses were conducted using SPSS (v27.0, IBM Corp.) and RStudio with two‑tailed α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline data

The analysis included 413 patients with a mean age of 47 ± 10 years and mean BMI of 20 ± 4 kg/m². The cohort comprised 218 females (52.8%) and 195 males (47.2%). Smoking status distribution revealed that 310 participants (75.1%) were never smokers, 63 (15.2%) were current smokers, and 40 (9.7%) were former smokers. Type 2 diabetes was present in 8.3% of patients (n = 34), with uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) accounting for 3.7% (n = 15) of cases. Oral hygiene practices showed that 307 patients (74.9%) brushed ≥ 2/day, and 351 (85.6%) performed daily interdental cleaning. Periodontitis staging indicated advanced disease, with 86.0% (n = 355) at stage 3/4. Grading reflected rapid progression in most cases (grade C: 88.5%, n = 363), while 10.0% (n = 41) exhibited moderate progression (grade B). Regarding defect distribution, 282 patients (68.3%) presented with only suprabony defects, 38 (9.2%) with only intrabony defects, and 93 (22.5%) with both defect types. The prevalence of clinically detectable tooth mobility (Grade ≥ 2) was significantly higher at intrabony defect sites (28.4%) compared to suprabony defect sites (8.9%, p < 0.001). (Table 1)

Features of examined sites

A total of 18,762 interproximal sites across 10,023 teeth from 413 patients at baseline were included. The cohort exhibited a mean BoP percentage of 38.5 ± 15.0%, with CAL and PPD averaging 4.1 ± 1.5 mm and 3.8 ± 2.0 mm, respectively. When stratified by defect type, intrabony defect sites exhibited significantly greater disease severity at baseline compared to suprabony defect sites, with higher mean PPD (6.1 ± 2.4 mm vs. 3.5 ± 1.7 mm, p < 0.001) and CAL (6.5 ± 2.2 mm vs. 3.8 ± 1.3 mm, p < 0.001). A total of 12,223 sites had PPD ≥ 5 mm, including 7,516 sites with PPD ≥ 6 mm and 4,568 sites with PPD ≥ 7 mm, reflecting substantial baseline disease severity. Defect morphology analysis identified 8,462 suprabony defects (72.3% of all defects) and 967 intrabony defects (8.3%), while 2,054 sites (17.6%) showed no detectable defects. The mean overall defect depth was 3.4 ± 2.4 mm, with suprabony components (3.3 ± 2.0 mm) contributing more significantly to total defect depth than intrabony components (0.4 ± 0.8 mm). (Table 2)

Treatment response stratified by baseline pocket depth and defect morphology

The majority of patients (n = 223, 54.0%) returned for re-assessment between 12 and 16 weeks post-NSPT. At follow-up, 54.5% of initially deep pockets (PPD >4 mm, n = 14,904) achieved successful treatment outcomes (PPD ≤ 4 mm with BoP-). Overall, sites demonstrated a mean PPD reduction of 0.58 mm and CAL gain of 0.26 mm. BoP prevalence decreased significantly from 38.5% at baseline to 20.0% post-treatment. FMPS improved from 37.0 ± 16.5% to 20.2 ± 15.1%, while patients without baseline FMPS records showed similar post-treatment plaque levels (20.5 ± 14.8%).

Treatment outcomes varied significantly according to both baseline pocket depth and defect morphology. Sites with initial PPD of 1–3 mm showed minimal changes overall (− 0.2 ± 1.1 mm PPD reduction, − 0.3 ± 1.5 mm CAL gain), with near‑complete pocket closure (92.5%). Within this shallow category, both defect types maintained high closure rates (suprabony: 93.8%, intrabony: 88.9%). Moderate pockets (4–5 mm) exhibited greater improvements overall (1.0 ± 1.1 mm PPD reduction, 0.6 ± 1.8 mm CAL gain, 70.5% closure). Within this category, suprabony defects achieved 0.8 ± 1.0 mm PPD reduction and 0.4 ± 1.7 mm CAL gain with 72.8% closure, while intrabony defects showed 1.2 ± 1.5 mm PPD reduction and 0.5 ± 2.2 mm CAL gain with 58.0% closure. Deep pockets (≥ 6 mm) demonstrated the most substantial changes overall (2.0 ± 2.0 mm PPD reduction, 1.3 ± 2.3 mm CAL gain, 45.5% closure). Within this category, suprabony defects (n = 4,292) showed 1.8 ± 1.8 mm PPD reduction and 1.1 ± 2.0 mm CAL gain with 50.5% closure, while intrabony defects (n = 380) showed 1.5 ± 1.6 mm PPD reduction and 0.8 ± 2.1 mm CAL gain with 42.0% closure (Fig. 1; Table 3).

Multilevel analysis

Defect morphology demonstrated the strongest association with treatment success. After adjustment for baseline PPD and other confounders in the multilevel model, suprabony defects were associated with a greater reduction in PPD and gain in CAL (by 0.6 mm each) compared to intrabony defects. Suprabony defects also showed 2.4-fold greater odds of pocket closure (95% CI: 1.5–3.4) compared to intrabony defects. Initial PPD was consistently associated with all outcomes (OR = 0.5 for pocket closure, β = 0.5 mm for PPD/CAL gains). Treatment-related factors showed more modest effects: postgraduate clinicians achieved marginally better outcomes than undergraduates, while instrument type and adjunct use showed no significant differences. At the patient level, age demonstrated a small negative association with CAL gain (−0.2 mm per year, 95% CI: −0.4 to −0.1), while sex and diabetes status showed no significant effects. These findings highlight defect morphology as the predominant predictor of NSPT success, with site-level factors accounting for most outcome variability (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that defect morphology significantly influences NSPT outcomes, with suprabony defects exhibiting 2.4-fold greater odds of pocket closure and superior CAL gains compared to intrabony defects, despite the latter showing greater absolute PPD reductions attributable to their deeper baseline measurements. Notably, the 0.6 mm greater PPD reduction in suprabony defects challenges conventional assumptions that intrabony defects inherently benefit more from NSPT, while their superior CAL gains (0.4 mm vs. 0.3 mm) suggest more predictable reparative potential in horizontally oriented defects. The dominance of site-specific characteristics in our variance partitioning analysis (64.9–68.3% across outcomes) reinforces defect morphology as a critical prognostic indicator, though the residual influence of patient-level factors (24.1–28.6%) underscores the importance of host modulation in comprehensive periodontal management.

While our data initially suggest greater absolute PPD reductions in intrabony defects following NSPT, this apparent advantage reflects their deeper baseline measurements (6.1 ± 2.4 mm vs. 4.2 ± 2.0 mm in suprabony defects) rather than superior therapeutic responsiveness. This finding aligns with established literature demonstrating that baseline PPD strongly predicts the magnitude of post-treatment improvement [12]. When outcomes were stratified by initial PPD categories, suprabony defects consistently outperformed intrabony defects across all severity levels, achieving 2.4-fold higher pocket closure rates (95% CI: 1.5–3.4) independent of adjunctive therapies. The differential response persisted even after controlling for baseline depth through multilevel modeling, with suprabony defects showing 0.6 mm greater CAL gain (95% CI: 0.4–0.9) - a clinically meaningful margin that underscores their enhanced reparative potential following non-surgical therapy.

Our multilevel modeling confirms that defect morphology and initial PPD significantly influence all clinical outcomes (pocket closure, PPD reduction, and CAL gain), resolving conflicting evidence from prior systematic reviews regarding NSPT responsiveness [13]. The data demonstrate that intrabony defects exhibit inherently poorer responses to conventional NSPT - an effect mediated not only by baseline PPD but also by anatomical complexity. This impaired healing likely stems from two key factors: (1) technical challenges in subgingival biofilm disruption within confined defect architectures (e.g., furcations and deep narrow pockets), as demonstrated by D’Aiuto et al. [14]; and (2) compromised bone morphology, where 1–2 wall defects or trabecular components ≥3 mm substantially limit regenerative potential. Notably, recent proteomic analyses by Santamaria et al. [15] revealed nearly identical GCF molecular profiles between defect types, suggesting that morphological constraints - rather than differential inflammatory mediator expression (e.g., IL-1α) - primarily dictate the healing trajectory. These findings collectively underscore the need for morphology-adapted treatment strategies when managing intrabony defects.

The therapeutic challenges in intrabony defects highlighted by our study may be mitigated by emerging adjunctive approaches. While our results showed limited benefits from conventional adjuncts (OR = 1.0 for pocket closure), recent evidence suggests targeted strategies could improve outcomes. Cyris et al. [16] demonstrated that guided biofilm therapy enhanced PPD reductions by 0.8 mm compared to conventional NSPT in complex defects—a margin comparable to our observed superiority of suprabony defects (0.6 mm greater CAL gain). For intrabony defects specifically, the meta-analysis by Roccuzzo et al. [17] found enamel matrix derivatives conferred an additional 1.2 mm CAL gain over NSPT alone, potentially offsetting their inherent healing limitations. Notably, Pelekos et al. [18] reported that volumetric soft tissue changes post-NSPT correlate strongly with defect morphology, suggesting 3D healing patterns may explain our observed disparities in reparative potential between defect types. These advances underscore the need for morphology-specific treatment algorithms, where intrabony defects may benefit from protocol modifications like biofilm-disrupting technologies or biologically active adjuncts, while suprabony defects achieve optimal outcomes through conventional NSPT. Importantly, the influence of tooth-level factors reported previously complements our defect-level morphological analysis. While Werner et al. [19] demonstrated that molar teeth and multi-rooted teeth with furcation involvement exhibit poorer healing, our study further clarifies that such teeth often harbor intrabony defects, thereby linking tooth anatomy with defect morphology in determining NSPT prognosis. Bumm et al. [20] further reinforced that deep residual pockets in molars and multi-rooted teeth are less likely to achieve pocket closure after re-instrumentation, echoing our observation that intrabony defects—frequently associated with posterior teeth—are more resistant to non-surgical management. Together, these findings advocate for a integrated prognostic framework that considers both tooth-related anatomical factors and defect morphology when planning personalized periodontal therapy.

Consistent with prior study [21], our study found that deeper baseline probing depths (≥ 7 mm) showed significantly lower probabilities of pocket closure compared to moderate pockets (4–6 mm), particularly in2molar sites. While smoking status could not be adequately assessed as a potential effect modifier due to limited sample size, our findings contrast with Tomasi’s report [22] regarding defect morphology - we observed significantly poorer outcomes in intrabony defects (2.4-fold lower odds of closure, 95% CI: 1.5–3.4). Subgroup analysis revealed baseline PPD as the sole significant predictor for intrabony defect resolution, with single-rooted teeth showing 1.88-fold greater closure likelihood than multi-rooted teeth (p < 0.01), aligning with Kim et al. [23]’s demonstration of superior NSPT efficacy in anterior teeth. Our outcomes demonstrate robust concordance with established benchmarks: 54.5% of pockets achieved closure at 3 months (vs. 57% in Suvan et al. [11]’s meta-analysis), though with slightly lower mean PPD reduction (0.58 mm vs. 1.0 mm). Notably, defect prevalence patterns matched historical data [24], with intrabony defects comprising 8.3% of cases. Variance partitioning confirmed site-level factors dominated outcome variability (64.9%), paralleling D’Aiuto et al. [14]’s report of 80% site-level contribution to PPD reduction. Treatment modality (whole-mouth vs. quadrant) and instrument type showed negligible effects, consistent with Sanz et al. [25]. While MINST protocols show promise for intrabony defects [1], but we excluded patients managed with MINST. Recent evidence remains conflicting, with Kučič and Gašperšič [26, 27] reporting equivalent outcomes between MINST and conventional NSPT in furcation defects. Patient-level factors exhibited expected patterns: age negatively correlated with CAL gain (β=−0.2 mm/year, 95% CI: −0.4 to −0.1), while sex and diabetes status showed no significant associations in our fully adjusted models.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the observational design precludes causal inferences, though our multilevel modeling adjusted for key confounders. Second, the 3-month follow-up may underestimate long-term outcomes, as periodontal remodeling continues beyond this period. Third, smoking prevalence was lower than population averages, potentially limiting generalizability to heavy smokers. Finally, radiographic defect morphology was assessed using 2D periapical films rather than CBCT, which may underrepresent 3D architectural complexity. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-ups, standardized adjunct protocols, and advanced 3D imaging to validate these findings. Additionally, the involvement of providers with different training levels may have introduced variability in treatment delivery, despite direct specialist supervision.

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of its sample and setting. The cohort was drawn from a tertiary hospital in China, where patients predominantly presented with advanced periodontitis, which may enhance the applicability of our results to similarly severe patient populations in referral centers. However, the lower prevalence of smoking compared to general periodontal populations, along with the exclusion of systemically compromised individuals, may limit direct extrapolation to communities with higher smoking rates or more prevalent systemic comorbidities. Additionally, all treatments were performed under direct specialist supervision in a hospital‑based environment, which may not fully reflect outcomes achievable in general dental practice. Nevertheless, the large sample size, prospective design, and use of multilevel modeling to adjust for key confounders strengthen the internal validity and support the morphological stratification of NSPT response as a clinically relevant principle. Future studies in primary‑care settings and more diverse demographic groups are warranted to confirm the external validity of these findings.

This large-scale analysis establishes defect morphology as the dominant predictor of NSPT success, with suprabony defects demonstrating 2.4-fold greater odds of pocket closure and superior CAL gains compared to intrabony defects. The hierarchical influence of factors (site > patient > tooth > treatment) challenges conventional emphasis on operator-dependent variables, instead highlighting the primacy of baseline anatomical characteristics. While intrabony defects remain challenging, their 54.5% closure rate suggests NSPT retains value as first-line therapy, particularly when combined with biofilm-disrupting technologies or EMD adjuncts. These findings support morphology-adapted treatment algorithms: suprabony defects achieve optimal outcomes through conventional NSPT, whereas intrabony defects may benefit from augmented protocols or early transition to regenerative approaches when anatomy permits. Future research should explore whether combining advanced debridement techniques with host-modulatory therapies can bridge this morphological efficacy gap. It is important to note that the 3-month follow-up period in this study represents a key limitation, as periodontal healing and tissue remodeling continue beyond this timeframe, and longer-term assessments are needed to confirm the durability of the observed outcomes.
