Research on a fast self-healing control method for tie-line faults in regional distribution networks based on source–load–storage interaction
Authors: Chuan Zhou, Suying Gui, Guomin Gao, Chaoyi Liu, Yongtao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the challenges of islanding susceptibility, complex network topology, and insufficient coordination between traditional protection and stability control under high penetration of distributed generation in regional distribution networks, a fast fault self-healing control method based on source–load–storage coordinated interaction is proposed. The frequency and voltage dynamic characteristics of regional distribution networks after disconnection from the main grid are systematically analyzed, revealing the relationship between tie-section power exchange and island stability. A full-process fast self-healing framework is established, covering fault isolation, island detection, stable control, and grid reconnection, along with a master–slave self-healing architecture enabling multi-level information sharing and coordinated decision-making. To cope with the difficulty of accurate island identification caused by variable topology, an island detection method based on a pruning-optimized search strategy is developed, improving real-time performance by eliminating unnecessary low-voltage branches. A circuit breaker position fault-tolerant mechanism based on enumeration and current criteria enhances robustness against switch status anomalies. For island stability control, a “regulation-priority with regulation–shedding coordination” principle is adopted, prioritizing rapid regulation of distributed resources such as energy storage and photovoltaics. Simulation and field tests show that the proposed method reduces recovery time by over 50% (from 6.8 s to 3.2 s), limits frequency deviation to 0.15 Hz, and decreases load loss to 12%, compared with conventional load shedding strategies. These results verify the method’s effectiveness, robustness, and practical applicability for real-time full-process dynamic self-healing in regional distribution networks.Keywords: Regional distribution network, Fast fault self-healing, Source–load–storage coordination, Island detectionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

To make rational use of renewable resources and reduce generation costs, many small hydropower plants and photovoltaic (PV) stations are directly connected to regional distribution networks in remote areas1–7. These regional distribution networks are typically weakly interconnected with the main grid, have complex network structures, and contain diverse power sources and loads, which poses significant challenges to self-healing following tie-line faults. Such regional grids often have weak electrical connections to the main grid8–11, sometimes relying on only a single transmission line or a single main transformer. Once a line or transformer trips due to a fault, the regional distribution network becomes an unplanned island.

After islanding, internal power sources mainly consist of small hydropower plants or distributed PV generation. Owing to the lack of water storage conditions in many areas, small hydropower plants are predominantly run-of-river stations. These stations generally supply only base load, with generation power determined by inflow and essentially no regulation capability12–15. PV output is even more uncertain. Neither of these sources can independently support stable islanded operation of a regional distribution network. As a result, islanded networks often fail to meet reclosing requirements, making reconnection to the main grid difficult. Whether using voltage-check or synchronism-check reclosing, failures may occur. Under voltage-check reclosing, internal generation prolongs the decay of residual island voltage, possibly exceeding reclosing or automatic transfer settings and preventing correct operation. Under synchronism-check reclosing, large fluctuations in PV and small hydropower output, combined with random load variations, make it difficult for island frequency and voltage to satisfy synchronism conditions, leading to reclosing or automatic transfer failure. In the absence of precise island stability control strategies, even if feeders are equipped with low-frequency load shedding or over-frequency generator tripping functions16–18, the island may oscillate between over-frequency and under-frequency states, preventing proper restoration actions. Ultimately, reconnection can only be achieved manually after all generators are tripped, resulting in long outages and failure to achieve rapid power restoration, potentially causing large-area blackouts19–21.

From another perspective, however, the integration of hydropower and PV also enables the possibility of self-healing in regional distribution networks. After an unplanned island is formed, small hydropower and PV can be utilized to provide uninterrupted power supply to critical loads within the island, and reconnection to the main grid can be achieved through reclosing or automatic transfer. At present, generator tripping and load shedding are key means of maintaining island stability, but they inevitably lead to load loss22–25. With the increasing maturity of distributed generation technologies, distributed power sources now possess certain power regulation capabilities. If power regulation can be combined with generator and load shedding for island stability control, load loss can be minimized and reconnection to the main grid can be expedited, thereby improving the reliability of power supply in regional distribution networks26,27. Recent studies on system resilience under dynamic disturbances28–30 also highlight the importance of coordinated structural response, which conceptually aligns with the proposed coordinated control strategy.

Focusing on the problem of rapid self-healing control of regional distribution networks following tie-line faults based on source–load–storage interaction, this study analyzes the operating characteristics after tie-line faults and reveals the relationship between pre-islanding tie-section power and island frequency and voltage stability. A rapid self-healing architecture covering fault isolation, island detection, island stability control, and grid reconnection is established. An island detection method based on pruning-optimized search is proposed, and breaker position anomaly correction is realized by combining enumeration with a zero-flow criterion, improving the real-time performance and accuracy of island identification. For island stability control, a multi-level control strategy based on source–load–storage interaction is proposed. According to the scale of tie-section power, island stability is achieved through either “regulation instead of shedding” or a combined “regulation and shedding” approach. Furthermore, a complete rapid self-healing system for multiple distribution network fault scenarios is developed, and its effectiveness and engineering practicality are verified through closed-loop simulations and field applications.

Characteristics of regional distribution networks under tie-line faults and design of a rapid self-healing architecture

Analysis of regional distribution network characteristics after tie-line faults

Taking a regional distribution network in Area A as an example, the network is connected to the main grid through a single 220 kV transmission line and one main transformer. It comprises six 110 kV substations, with several local transfer supply lines configured as emergency backup power sources. This configuration represents a typical end-of-line regional distribution network, and its topological structure is shown in Fig. 1. Within the region, multiple small hydropower plants and a 25 MW photovoltaic power station are connected in a distributed manner, resulting in high renewable energy penetration but limited regulation capability.

Topology of the regional distribution network.

Such regional distribution networks commonly suffer from weak interconnection with the main grid and single-path power supply. Once a tie-line fault leads to disconnection, islanded operation is likely to occur, causing a significant deterioration in supply reliability and power quality. Moreover, small hydropower and photovoltaic sources are highly dependent on hydrological and solar conditions and generally lack frequency and voltage regulation capabilities. They are often connected to distribution feeders via T-connections, leading to mixed source–load connections and complex operating modes. After the regional network is separated from the main grid, the loss of grid support makes the system highly susceptible to frequency and voltage instability, thereby increasing the risk of large-scale power outages.

When a fault occurs at point K5 in the interconnection channel of a regional distribution network, if other channels remain connected to the main grid, the original power flow will be redistributed; if the channel at K5 is the only connection to the main grid, an islanded section is formed. After the regional distribution network is separated, its frequency variation is determined by the active power of the islanded section: if the section was exporting active power to the main grid before disconnection, a positive active power imbalance occurs after separation, causing the frequency to gradually rise during islanded operation; if the section was importing power from the main grid, the frequency continuously decreases after separation, and the frequency deviation is proportional to the section’s active power and accumulates over time. Reactive power exchange determines the voltage level of the islanded network. Analysis indicates that after a regional distribution network is separated from the main grid, if the balance of active and reactive power within the island cannot be maintained, the network frequency and voltage will rapidly deviate from their nominal values and settle at a new equilibrium. When the sectional power is relatively small, the source-load balance is high, and frequency and voltage change slowly, allowing for rapid reconnection through protection reclosing or automatic restoration. However, when the sectional power is large, frequency and voltage fluctuate dramatically upon islanding, making reclosing and automatic restoration difficult to execute. Existing 35 kV/10 kV feeders are often equipped with over-frequency load shedding and under-frequency load reduction protection. Due to differences in settings and sensitivity, islanded operation may trigger alternating high- and low-frequency protections, causing repeated load shedding over extended periods and severely reducing supply reliability. Traditional static control strategies, executed according to preset sequences, cannot adapt to the highly variable operating modes and rapidly changing conditions of regional distribution networks. Moreover, protection, stability, and isolation mechanisms operate independently, lacking coordination, and thus fail to meet the requirements for fast fault self-healing and reconnection in modern power systems.

Design of a fast self-healing architecture for regional distribution networks

To achieve seamless and rapid self-healing after islanding in a regional distribution network, it is necessary to accurately identify islands in non-faulted areas once the faulted section is cleared, which serves as the prerequisite for implementing stable control strategies. Based on this, reasonable island stabilization should be executed according to the real-time source-load conditions, creating favorable conditions for subsequent reconnection to the main grid.

Due to closed-loop design, open-loop operation, and large-scale integration of distributed energy resources, regional distribution networks have evolved from unidirectional radial supply to multi-source bidirectional supply. The network topology is complex, and island configurations often involve multiple substations, significantly increasing the difficulty of island identification and control. During grid-connected operation, regional networks exchange power bidirectionally with the main grid; after disconnection, differentiated island operation strategies must be adopted based on source-load configurations. If no distributed generation is present, reconnection can be directly achieved via reclosing or automatic restoration. If distributed generation exists but stabilization is lacking, repeated over-frequency load shedding and under-frequency load reduction may occur. With regional stabilization systems or adjustable resources such as energy storage, island stability can be maintained through a combination of “adjustment and shedding” or “adjustment prior to shedding,” enabling synchronization-based reconnection via reclosing or regional automatic restoration.

Since conventional reclosing and automatic restoration rely on local information and are often insufficient for complex regional network scenarios, it is necessary to integrate regional-level information to coordinate protection, stabilization, reclosing, and automatic restoration, thus achieving rapid reconnection of islanded networks. After a fault occurs on the tie line, the relay protection operates and trips the breakers on both sides to isolate the fault, followed by automatic reclosing. For a temporary fault, the regional distribution network can reconnect to the main grid directly via reclosing. For a permanent fault, reclosing fails and the breakers trip again, requiring backup automatic switching to close the transfer supply breaker for grid restoration. Fault self-healing in the regional distribution network relies on coordinated execution of fault isolation, islanded operation control, and grid restoration in a prescribed sequence; failure of any step may result in unsuccessful self-healing.

In existing substations, functions such as relay protection, reclosing, backup automatic switching, and stability control are distributed across different devices and systems, each forming separate defense layers with limited information exchange and coordination capability. To achieve seamless and fast self-healing, a unified system architecture is needed to break information silos and enable coordinated decision-making and joint execution of protection, island detection, stability control, reclosing, and backup switching functions.

To meet the demand for seamless fault self-healing in regional distribution networks, this study proposes a master–slave distributed self-healing structure based on “wide-area centralized” and “station-level distributed” control. The proposed system architecture, shown in Fig. 6, comprises the process layer, station layer, and regional layer, with information exchange and sharing realized via both process-layer and regional-layer networks.

A hierarchical control architecture centered on the regional master station is established for fault self-healing. The master station handles island detection, stable control strategy, and coordination of reclosing and backup auto-reclosing. Station-level substations collect local data and execute commands, while existing protection devices form the process layer for local protection and data acquisition. Clear layer roles and high-speed data exchange enable coordinated, seamless fault self-healing.

Fast islanding identification of regional distribution networks based on pruning-optimized search strategy

When a critical interconnection point with the main grid experiences a fault, the regional distribution network may become islanded. The essence of islanding detection is to determine the connectivity between the regional network and the main grid power sources. Due to the complex topology and variable operating modes of regional distribution networks, islanding cannot be accurately determined based solely on the status of individual circuit breakers. Instead, comprehensive information across the network must be utilized, and topology search algorithms applied under the regional grid fast self-healing framework.

Traditional tree-based search methods, modeled using graph theory, traverse the network topology via breadth-first search (BFS) or depth-first search (DFS). However, in networks with numerous branches, frequent backtracking leads to high computational load, making real-time detection challenging. To address this, a pruning-optimized search method is proposed for island detection. By eliminating search branches that cannot form an island while ensuring correctness and accuracy, the method significantly reduces the number of searches and computation time, enabling fast and precise identification of regional network islands and providing a reliable basis for the timely implementation of subsequent islanding stability control strategies.

The pruning-optimized search algorithm classifies nodes in the power system into three types: system power sources, buses, and feeders. Nodes are connected via circuit breakers, disconnectors, and other switches, which determine the connectivity between nodes. To reduce the computational load of the search algorithm, all circuit breaker and disconnector statuses along a line can be logically ANDed at the substation level, and the result uploaded to the master station. With node numbers marked by numbered circles or dots, the regional distribution network can be transformed into the tree-structured equivalent diagram shown in Fig. 7. Nodes S1 and S2 represent system power source nodes, nodes A, B, C, D, E, and F correspond to six substations, and the remaining nodes represent 35 kV or 10 kV feeders under each substation.

Because the distribution network generally adopts a top-down radial supply structure, the main network power nodes are usually at higher voltage levels. One or more power plants in the regional distribution network can form islands, increasing the difficulty of island detection. Using an adjacency matrix to store the network topology, the pruning-optimized search algorithm starts from the terminal nodes and performs a radial search along the topology, eliminating branches that cannot form islands. This approach improves search efficiency while ensuring correctness and accuracy, enabling rapid island identification and providing a basis for the timely implementation of island stability control strategies, as shown in Fig. 9.

Island sets in the regional distribution network.

For a regional distribution network with m nodes, the pruning-based search algorithm first checks the status of the breakers and uses the end nodes as the starting points for outward radiating searches to form sets. Each set is then evaluated for connectivity with the main grid. If a set is connected to the main grid, island detection for that set stops; if disconnected, the search expands to other nodes, forming new sets and checking their connectivity. After covering all nodes, any detected islands have their boundaries determined for subsequent island stability control. If no islands are found, the algorithm proceeds to the next search iteration until all nodes are examined, achieving rapid island identification. The flowchart of the island search process in the regional distribution network is shown in Fig. 10.

The island detection algorithm proposed in this paper relies on breaker and disconnector position information. However, in actual operation, breaker position signals may become invalid due to secondary circuit disconnections or communication failures, affecting the determination of node connectivity. To address this, a breaker position anomaly correction method based on enumeration and current criteria is proposed, as shown in Fig. 11. For each abnormal breaker, searches are performed assuming both closed and open positions; if the results are consistent, island detection is unaffected. If the results differ, the breaker status is determined using current criteria, ensuring that the pruning-optimized search algorithm remains robust under abnormal grid topologies and improving the accuracy of island detection.

Fast fault self-healing control method for regional distribution networks based on source–load–storage interaction

After a regional distribution network is separated from the main grid, it forms a self-sustained islanded power system. Following accurate island identification, island stability control must be implemented to enable subsequent reconnection to the main grid. Generator tripping and load shedding are important means of maintaining power system stability, and extensive studies have been reported in the literature31–33. With the rapid development of distributed generation technologies, generators have gained certain power regulation capabilities. By coordinating power regulation with measures such as generator tripping or load shedding for island stability control, losses of load and generation can be minimized.

Island stability control of regional distribution networks based on PV–storage power regulation

At the initial stage of islanding, the internal power imbalance is equal to the sectional power prior to disconnection. If power was exported to the main grid before islanding, the island frequency and voltage will rise, potentially causing photovoltaic (PV) units and small hydropower plants to trip due to overvoltage or overfrequency. Conversely, if power was imported from the main grid, underfrequency or low-voltage conditions may occur, leading to generator outages. To maintain island stability, this paper proposes a fast and seamless self-healing control method based on coordinated source–load–storage interaction. The method comprehensively evaluates the characteristics of energy storage systems, PV units, small hydropower plants, and loads within the island to determine an optimal stability control strategy. As indicated by the small-signal frequency model presented in “Analysis of regional distribution network characteristics after tie-line faults”, the system frequency is highly sensitive to active power imbalance within the islanded network. Therefore, rapid mitigation of power mismatch through coordinated regulation of distributed resources is essential to ensure stable island operation.

Energy storage systems are bidirectional and highly adjustable; PV output is influenced by environmental conditions but can be considered relatively stable over short time scales; small hydropower plants and loads generally lack regulation capability and can only be curtailed. Therefore, island stability control follows the principle of “regulation first, regulation combined with tripping,” achieving power balance and secure operation, as illustrated in Fig. 12.

Generator tripping and load shedding strategy considering load levels and source–load ratio

If the power exchanged between the regional distribution network and the main grid is large and the regulation criterion is not satisfied, a “regulation–shedding combination” strategy must be adopted, to achieve stable island operation.

Because small hydropower units and loads are widely distributed and numerous, it is difficult to collect real-time power data for individual supply nodes, and precisely switching individual loads or small hydropower units is practically impossible. Therefore, in practical applications, the real-time power of low-voltage feeders within the regional distribution network can be used to represent loads or generation. By controlling the outlet breakers of these feeders, generator tripping or load shedding can be effectively implemented. Although this approach may result in some misoperation of loads or generators, if uninterrupted supply to critical loads is ensured while accelerating island reconnection, it remains a practical and effective solution.

The “regulation–shedding combination” island stability control strategy follows these steps:

Step 1: Obtain the power flow direction of each feeder in the regional distribution network before separation from the main grid to determine the feeder’s attribute as a source or load.

Step 2: Based on sectional power and the PV capacity limit, determine the amount of generation to trip or load to shed. If, before islanding, the regional network was importing power from the main grid, feeders with load attributes need to be disconnected; if the network was exporting power, feeders with generation attributes need to be disconnected. The generation or load shedding amount, , is given in Eqs. (5) and (6).

Step 3: Among all feeders, select appropriate ones for disconnection to meet the required amount of generation tripping or load shedding. Loads are generally classified into three levels based on importance: first-, second-, and third-level loads, corresponding to first-, second-, and third-level feeders. First-level feeders supply the most critical loads, where power interruption could cause personal injury or severe political and economic losses.

When selecting feeders for disconnection, the following principles should be followed:

Principle 1: Prioritize disconnecting feeders with lower load priority levels.

Principle 2: The total disconnected power should be minimized. For feeders of the same priority level, all generators and loads within the feeder are disconnected simultaneously, so is the sum of the feeder’s load and generation .

Principle 3: Minimize the number of feeders to be disconnected. Once a feeder is disconnected, manual operation is required to restore power, so the number of disconnected lines should be kept as low as possible.

When selecting feeders for disconnection, the three principles above should be considered: Principle 1 is mandatory, while Principles 2 and 3 can be appropriately balanced according to actual conditions.

To satisfy Principle 3, feeder powers are generally sorted, and feeders with larger power are disconnected first. This ensures that the required generation or load shedding amount is met while minimizing the number of feeders disconnected. However, when two or more feeders have similar power, selection becomes difficult.

In some cases, a portion of the load may be lost, but by applying the “regulation first, regulation–shedding combination” strategy, the lost load is minimized, and uninterrupted supply to critical loads is maintained.

Experimental verification

Development of the fault self-healing system

The seamless fault self-healing system consists of one regional master station and multiple station-level substations, following a “regional centralization” and “station-level distribution” model. The master station, located at the central site, develops key fault self-healing technologies and integrates data from all substations. It analyzes system-wide information, performs real-time island detection, devises stability control strategies, and manages island reconnection, then issues control commands to the substations.

At other substations, station-level units upload measurement data to the master station and execute its control instructions. They also implement distributed autonomous control at the station level and integrate key fault self-healing technology at PV sites to handle point faults. Communication systems enable data exchange between master station and substations, as well as among substations.

Given the large scale of the system, a well-organized hierarchical architecture is necessary to interconnect the master station, substations, interval layer, and network devices.

It collects breaker status, tie-line power, feeder power, and fault information from substations, identifies islands in real time, and monitors sectional power. By leveraging energy storage, PV units, and controllable loads, the master station quickly formulates island stability control strategies and dispatches commands to substations, ensuring stable island operation. After stabilization, the master station coordinates reclosing for priority reconnection; in the case of permanent faults, it activates regional backup auto-reclosing to restore connection with the main grid, achieving fast and seamless fault self-healing.

Island stability control function

High export, low local generation: When the island exports large power to the main grid and PV plus energy storage cannot balance it alone, some feeders need to be disconnected. PV units switch to manual mode with zero output, and energy storage adjusts power to maintain balance.

Low export, high local generation: When island power is small, PV and storage can maintain stability. If power slightly increases, PV manual control and energy storage can adjust to smooth the balance.

Low export, high import: When the island imports power from the main grid, frequency and voltage drop. PV remains in MPPT mode, and part of the load feeders are disconnected to maintain stability.

Hardware and software development

Interval-layer and communication devices use existing substation equipment, requiring no redevelopment. Regional master station device model: PCS-998 M.Substation device model: PCS-998 S. Both use 4U rackmount chassis with backplane connections, strict separation of high and low voltage circuits, and strong anti-interference capability.

The master station software workflow is shown in Fig. 14. Receive information from all substations. Process data and set program flag mflag for multi-branch selection. If mflag = 0, the master station performs an island search on all uploaded network topologies. If no island is detected, the main program repeats the search. If an island is detected, mflag is set to 1, and the master station enters the island stability control program, adjusting PV and energy storage outputs or executing generation/load shedding strategies to maintain stable island operation.

Master station program flow.

At the same time, the master station uses the received protection action information to determine whether reclosing has operated. If reclosing has not operated, the mflag status remains unchanged, and the island stability control strategy continues to execute. If reclosing has operated successfully, it indicates that the islanded network has been reconnected to the main grid; in this case, mflag is set to 0, and the main program returns to the island search routine. If reclosing has operated but failed, it indicates a permanent fault; mflag is set to 2, and the master station enters the regional backup auto-reclosing procedure, sending backup auto-reclosing commands to the substations, then resets mflag to 0. Through this program execution, fault self-healing is ultimately achieved.

The substation software workflow is as follows: First, it collects all breaker positions and real-time feeder branch powers within the substation and uploads the data to the master station. It then checks whether any command has been received from the master station. If a command is received, the substation enters an interrupt routine to process the master station command with priority. If no command is received, the substation checks the sflag status flag:

If sflag = 0, execute substation line backup protection and set sflag = 1.

If sflag = 1, execute substation transformer backup protection and set sflag = 2.

If sflag = 2, execute substation bus backup protection and set sflag = 3.

If sflag = 3, execute substation failure consecutive tripping protection and reset sflag = 0, then return to the main program for the next cycle.

Conclusions

This paper presents a fast self-healing framework for regional distribution networks, featuring an islanding detection method based on a pruning-optimized search strategy and an islanding stability control strategy following a “regulation-first, regulation–shedding coordinated” approach. These methods address inefficiencies in island detection and the challenges of maintaining island stability. By analyzing the characteristics of islanded networks after tie-line faults, the relationship between section power and island frequency and voltage is revealed. A full-process fast self-healing procedure-from fault isolation to island detection, stability control, and grid reconnection-was designed, supported by a master-slave distributed logical architecture.

To improve island identification under complex and variable main-grid connections, the proposed detection method prunes low-voltage branches and applies depth-first search for efficient traversal. Enumeration combined with current-based criteria corrects breaker position anomalies, ensuring fault-tolerant detection.

For islanding stability control, a source-load-storage interaction-based regulation strategy is proposed. Regulation capabilities of distributed resources, including energy storage and photovoltaics, are prioritized to maintain stable island operation without load shedding. When regulation capacity is insufficient, the method optimally selects source or load feeders for disconnection by considering remaining capacity, load importance, and source–load ratios, minimizing load shedding while maintaining stability.

Based on the characteristics of a regional distribution network in Area A, a hierarchical control architecture with centralized main-station coordination and distributed substation autonomy was implemented. Complete main and substation systems and a digital-physical closed-loop simulation platform were developed. Rapid self-healing simulations under various tie-line fault scenarios demonstrated that the proposed method reduces recovery time by more than 50% (from 6.8 s to 3.2 s), limits frequency deviation to 0.15 Hz, and decreases load loss to 12%, compared with conventional strategies. Field engineering tests-including system commissioning, island detection, stability control, and regional automatic transfer (ATS) operations-confirmed uninterrupted supply to critical loads, validating both the robustness and practical engineering applicability of the approach.
