Translation and validation of the Korean version of the Fertility Intention Scale in breast cancer survivors
Authors: Eunbi Lee, Jeonghee Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFertility intention among breast cancer survivors of childbearing age is influenced by clinical, personal, and social considerations. Therefore, multidimensional assessment is essential across treatment and survivorship. Although the Fertility Intention Scale (FIS) assesses fertility intention in women with breast cancer, no validated Korean version is currently available. This study aimed to translate and culturally adapt the FIS into Korean and evaluate its validity and reliability.MethodsThis validation study was conducted in two phases. The first phase involved translation and cultural adaptation of the FIS, including forward translation, expert panel review, back-translation, pre-testing, cognitive interviews, and linguistic refinement. The second phase evaluated psychometric properties using secondary data from 203 women aged 18–45 years with breast cancer. Construct validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), known-group validity, and convergent validity. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's α and a 2-week test–retest analysis.ResultsThe CFA indicated an overall acceptable, though marginal, model fit. Known-group validity was confirmed, as women planning pregnancy scored significantly higher on the Korean FIS (K-FIS) than those not planning pregnancy (t = 6.09, P < 0.001). Convergent validity was partially supported by a significant correlation between the K-FIS “social support” factor and perceived social support (r = 0.31, P < 0.001). Cronbach's α for the total scale was 0.93, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.798.ConclusionsThe K-FIS showed generally acceptable preliminary evidence of reliability and validity for assessing fertility intention among Korean women with breast cancer. It may support tailored fertility interventions and counseling in oncology nursing practice.Keywords: Breast cancer, Fertility intention, Oncology nursing, Psychometrics, Reliability, Validity

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide,1 and its incidence has steadily increased in Korea in recent years.2 Notably, this increase has been more pronounced among women of childbearing age.3 With the five-year survival rate of young women with breast cancer reaching 94.5%,2 increasing attention has been directed toward quality of life after treatment. For women of reproductive age, pregnancy and childbirth are key components of survivorship, influencing psychological well-being, family planning, and social relationships.4, 5, 6

Standard breast cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy, can compromise fertility,7 and pregnancy after breast cancer is often accompanied by concerns about future childbearing.4 Fertility intention refers to a psychological state situated between the desire for pregnancy and the behavioral attempt to conceive and reflects an individual's values and personal priorities surrounding pregnancy.8 Among breast cancer survivors, fertility intention is shaped not only by general factors, such as personal values, social support, and cultural context, but also by cancer-related factors, including treatment-related fertility risks, current health status, and fear of cancer recurrence.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, previous studies have often relied on single-item or dichotomous measures,14 which are insufficient to capture the complexity of fertility intention and the multiple factors involved in reproductive decisions.15

Several instruments exist to assess pregnancy-related preferences in the general population, including the Desire to Avoid Pregnancy Scale16 and the London Measure of Unplanned Pregnancy.17 However, these instruments primarily address contraceptive use and the prevention or planning of pregnancy in healthy populations and do not adequately capture the multidimensional concerns specific to women experiencing a cancer diagnosis and its treatment.

The Fertility Intention Scale (FIS) was developed in Taiwan8 and comprises four factors (pregnancy risk, disease control, social support, and happiness) and 15 items reflecting the unique experiences of women with breast cancer. The FIS has subsequently been validated in breast cancer survivors in mainland China18 and culturally adapted into Turkish for women in Türkiye,19 with both studies supporting the original four-factor structure and demonstrating acceptable reliability and validity across populations. Nevertheless, a validated Korean version is not currently available, limiting systematic assessment among Korean women with breast cancer.

Establishing a psychometrically evaluated Korean version of the FIS is essential for comprehensively evaluating fertility intention and supporting tailored interventions in this population. Recent guidelines also emphasize that fertility-related communication should be integrated into survivorship care plans for young patients with breast cancer,20,21 highlighting the need for systematic and culturally appropriate tools to support informed reproductive decision-making. Therefore, this study aimed to translate and culturally adapt the FIS into Korean and to examine its psychometric properties among breast cancer survivors.

Methods

Study design

This was a validation study conducted in two phases: (1) translation and cultural adaptation of the FIS from Chinese into Korean, and (2) psychometric evaluation of the Korean version (Fig. 1).

Translation, cultural adaptation, and psychometric evaluation of the K-FIS. K-FIS, Korean version of the Fertility Intention Scale.

Phase I: translation and cultural adaptation

This study followed the World Health Organization guidelines for instrument translation and adaptation.22 The translation and cultural adaptation of the FIS from Chinese to Korean proceeded through the following steps.

Forward translation

The original Chinese version of the FIS8 was translated into Korean by a bilingual nurse with a master's degree in nursing, whose mother tongue is Korean and who is fluent in both Chinese and Korean. The translator prioritized conceptual equivalence over literal translation and used culturally appropriate, clear, and natural wording that would be acceptable to a broad audience.

Expert panel review

An expert panel of six professionals with expertise in women's health, research, and instrument development included one advanced practice nurse in the breast surgery department, one gynecological oncologist, one breast surgeon, and three nursing professors. The panel reviewed three language versions (Chinese, English, and Korean) because the FIS has also been published in English. Two bilingual members fluent in Chinese and Korean reviewed the Chinese-Korean version, and four members fluent in English and Korean reviewed the English-Korean version. Each panel member independently provided quantitative ratings and qualitative comments regarding semantic equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and content adequacy.

Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI) at both item and scale levels. The initial item-level CVI (I-CVI) ranged from 0.67 to 1.00, and the scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.94, indicating excellent content validity.23

The research team then consolidated all qualitative comments and prepared a revised draft for items with an I-CVI below 0.83.24 For example, item 7 (“I am positive regarding my prognosis”) was initially translated into Korean as “I am positive about my health after treatment.” Based on expert feedback that the item assessed personal perception rather than medical judgment, the term “prognosis” was deemed more appropriate, and the wording was revised accordingly. The revised draft was then re-evaluated independently by the expert panel, and this process was repeated across four rounds until consensus was reached. In the final round, all items achieved an I-CVI of 1.00.

Back-translation and verification

The Korean version was back-translated into Chinese by a bilingual doctor of nursing whose mother tongue is Chinese and who is fluent in both Korean and Chinese. The translator had no prior knowledge of the FIS and followed guidelines emphasizing conceptual and cultural equivalence rather than literal translation. The back-translated version was reviewed by two experts: a bilingual Chinese nursing professor fluent in Korean and Chinese and the original developer of the FIS. Both reviewers confirmed conceptual and cultural equivalence.

Pre-testing and cognitive interviewing

A pre-test was conducted with 15 breast cancer survivors of childbearing age in Korea, who were recruited from online patient communities. Women aged 18–45 years with a history of breast cancer were eligible to participate. Participants received the questionnaire in advance and, during telephone interviews, were asked to describe their understanding of each item and explain the rationale for their responses. The average questionnaire completion time was 2.21 minutes.

Cognitive interviews confirmed that most items were clear and feasible. However, several participants did not initially recognize that the questionnaire concerned fertility intention specifically in relation to their disease and interpreted the items as general questions about fertility. For example, item 13 (“information about pregnancy”) was interpreted as referring to general pregnancy knowledge rather than disease-related pregnancy information. Accordingly, the introductory instructions were revised to explicitly state the disease-related focus of the questionnaire, and item 13 was revised from “information about pregnancy” to “information about pregnancy related to the disease.” These refinements ensured that respondents interpreted the scale within the intended context.

Linguistic refinement

The revised Korean version was professionally edited by the Korean Language Movement Center to ensure grammatical accuracy and comprehensibility. All revisions and procedures were documented and the final K-FIS was prepared for psychometric evaluation (Supplementary File 1).

Phase II: evaluation of psychometric properties

Study participants

The participants were women of childbearing age diagnosed with breast cancer. Fertility intention may vary across the cancer trajectory, and personal health often takes priority during active treatment.25,26 Therefore, this study focused on individuals who had completed active treatment, defined in this study as surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy, to assess fertility intention under more stable conditions, in accordance with previous studies.8,11,13

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) being aged between 18 and 45 years; (b) having a diagnosis of breast cancer; and (c) having completed active treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy), regardless of ongoing maintenance therapy (endocrine or targeted therapy). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) having a diagnosis of gynecologic cancer; (b) having permanently lost reproductive capacity (bilateral oophorectomy or hysterectomy); (c) having unstable health due to severe comorbidities; and (d) being currently pregnant.

Sample size was determined based on methodological guidelines. For confirmatory factor analysis, a minimum sample size of 200 participants is recommended.27 Allowing for a potential 20% dropout rate, the target sample size was set at 250. For the test–retest reliability assessment, at least 30 participants were required.28 Accounting for an anticipated attrition rate of 20%, a minimum of 38 participants was targeted.

Instruments and questionnaire

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics included age, education, religion, employment status, marital status, parenthood status, and pregnancy planning status. The clinical characteristics included age at cancer diagnosis, cancer stage, and past and current treatments.

Fertility Intention Scale (FIS)

Fertility intention was measured using the Korean version of the FIS,8 which was translated and culturally adapted for this study. The scale comprises 15 items across four factors of pregnancy risk, disease control, social support, and happiness. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a stronger fertility intention. Cronbach's α was 0.88 in the original study,8 and 0.93 in this study.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)

Social support was measured using the Korean version of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) developed by Zimet et al.29 and validated in Korean by Park et al.30 The MSPSS includes 12 items across three factors of family support, friend support, and significant other support. Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = very strongly disagree to 7 = very strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. Cronbach's α was 0.85 in the original version,29 0.95 in the Korean version,30 and 0.95 in this study. The Korean version of the MSPSS has also been used in adult Korean women with breast cancer, demonstrating high internal consistency in this population (α = 0.97).31

Data collection

This study was a secondary analysis of a previous cross-sectional study. The original data were collected from March 19 to April 11, 2025, via an online survey of young women with breast cancer. Participants provided electronic informed consent for the secondary use of their de-identified data for psychometric validation.

Of the 250 participants who completed the questionnaire, 35 were excluded based on self-reported ineligibility, resulting in 215 eligible respondents. The remaining responses were screened by the research team for implausible or contradictory clinical information. Twelve responses were excluded: five contained implausible values (e.g., a calculated age at diagnosis that fell in childhood), and seven showed clinically contradictory information (e.g., reported endocrine therapy despite hormone-receptor-negative status). This resulted in a final sample of 203 cases for analysis.

Two weeks after the initial data collection, the K-FIS was readministered to those who agreed to participate in the retest. Of the 38 participants who completed the retest, four were excluded: three had already been removed from the baseline dataset, and one showed careless responding at the retest, having selected the same response across all 15 items, with a difference of more than 20 points between the two total scores. Therefore, 34 participants were included in the test–retest analysis.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 23.0 and IBM SPSS AMOS version 26, with a significance level of P < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical characteristics as well as the main study variables.

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to examine construct validity. As the four-factor structure of the FIS had been established in the original development study and subsequent validation studies,8,18,19 CFA was used to test whether this hypothesized structure could be replicated among Korean breast cancer survivors. Multivariate normality was assessed using Mardia's coefficient before conducting CFA, as maximum likelihood estimation assumes multivariate normality. The assumption was violated (multivariate kurtosis = 26.878; critical ratio = 8.479); therefore, CFA was performed using maximum likelihood estimation with Bollen-Stine bootstrapping (2000 resamples), which corrects model fit statistics under non-normality.

Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic (χ2), relative chi-square (CMIN/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was defined as CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, SRMR ≤ 0.08,32 and χ2/df < 3.33

In conjunction with CFA, standardized factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (CR) were examined. Convergent validity was supported by AVE ≥ 0.50 and CR values ≥ 0.70.34 Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing AVE values with squared inter-construct correlations (r2), with discriminant validity supported when the AVE exceeded r2.34

Known-groups validity was assessed using a Welch's t test comparing K-FIS scores between women with and without pregnancy planning, which was used as an external grouping variable distinct from fertility intention. Convergent validity was evaluated by assessing correlations between the K-FIS “social support” factor and the MSPSS. Empirical evidence on the FIS in breast cancer populations was still limited, and reported associations with potential external measures (e.g., the Reproductive Concerns After Cancer Scale)18 had been inconsistent across previous validation studies.35,36 Therefore, no external measures that conceptually corresponded to all four factors could be identified a priori. Criterion validity was not assessed because no established gold standard instrument was available for measuring fertility intention among breast cancer survivors.

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach's α to evaluate internal consistency. Test–retest reliability was examined using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for total scores based on data collected at a two-week interval.

Participants’ characteristics

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The mean age of the participants was 36.34 ± 4.10 years, and age at cancer diagnosis was concentrated in the 30s (67.0%), followed by the 20s (23.6%). Most participants had graduated from university (87.2%), were married (87.2%), had no children (57.1%), and were diagnosed with stage II disease (50.2%) or stage I disease (30.5%). Radiotherapy (82.3%), chemotherapy (69.5%), and endocrine therapy (65.5%) were frequently reported in the treatment history. Regarding pregnancy planning status, 47.3% reported no plans to attempt pregnancy, while 52.7% reported planning to become pregnant.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Factor loadings ranged from 0.488 to 0.955 across items, with values ≥ 0.50 considered acceptable.34 Most items demonstrated acceptable to strong loadings, except for Item 1.

Although item 1 showed a factor loading of 0.488, slightly below the conventional threshold, it was retained primarily on conceptual and cross-cultural grounds, with total-scale reliability not reduced by its retention. At the total-scale level, item 1 showed an acceptable corrected item–total correlation (0.44), and its deletion did not change Cronbach's α (0.93), indicating that retaining item 1 did not reduce the scale's internal consistency (Supplementary File 2). In addition, item 1 was considered conceptually important for the pregnancy risk factor by the expert panel, and it was retained to preserve consistency with the original FIS structure for cross-cultural comparison.

Convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). The AVE values ranged from 0.591 to 0.896, and the CR values ranged from 0.860 to 0.945, demonstrating that each factor adequately captured the variance of its items.

Discriminant validity was examined by comparing the squared inter-factor correlation (r2) with the AVE for each construct. The inter-factor correlations ranged from 0.540 to 0.827. Discriminant validity was supported for most factor pairs; however, the r2 between Factor 1 and Factor 2 exceeded the AVE values of both factors, indicating that discriminant validity between the pregnancy risk and disease control factors was not supported.

Known-group validity

Known-group validity was assessed by comparing K-FIS scores between women with and without pregnancy planning. Women planning pregnancy had a significantly higher K-FIS score (52.29 ± 13.36) than those not planning pregnancy (42.38 ± 9.69) (t = 6.09, P < 0.001), demonstrating the scale's ability to distinguish groups defined by pregnancy planning status.

Convergent validity with external measures

Convergent validity was examined by assessing the correlation between the K-FIS social support factor and the MSPSS. The K-FIS social support factor was positively correlated with the MSPSS scores (r = 0.31, P < 0.001), supporting convergent validity at the subscale level.

Reliability

Internal consistency

The K-FIS demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.93 for the total scale. Each factor also showed high internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.89 for pregnancy risk, 0.86 for disease control, 0.84 for social support, and 0.94 for happiness (Table 3). Corrected item–total correlations ranged from 0.44 to 0.81, and deleting any single item did not meaningfully change the total-scale Cronbach's α. Test–retest reliability

Among the 34 participants who completed the retest after two weeks, the ICC for the total K-FIS score was 0.798 (95% CI 0.632, 0.894), indicating good test-retest reliability.28

Discussion

Main findings

The purpose of this study was to translate the FIS into Korean and evaluate the psychometric properties of the K-FIS. This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the reliability and some aspects of validity of the K-FIS as an instrument for assessing fertility intention among Korean breast cancer survivors of reproductive age.

The four-factor structure of the K-FIS was supported in this study, consistent with the previous FIS studies conducted in Taiwan,8 mainland China,18 and Türkiye.19 The cross-cultural stability of this four-factor structure suggests that the underlying construct of fertility intention captured by the FIS is broadly applicable across diverse cultural contexts. At the same time, the FIS has been adapted in different ways across cultures. The mainland Chinese validation study applied the original Chinese FIS developed in Taiwan without translation or item modification, given the shared language.18 A later mainland Chinese revision of the FIS added six items and an additional factor, fertility values, to reflect culturally embedded values such as son preference and expectations of filial support in old age.37 The Turkish adaptation involved standard linguistic translation with only minor wording changes.19 These differences highlight that, although the underlying construct is stable, item-level expression may require adaptation to local cultural contexts.

In the Korean adaptation, distinctive psychometric patterns emerged that may reflect cultural influences on the formation of fertility intention. In particular, item 1 in the pregnancy risk factor showed a slightly lower standardized factor loading. Item 1 assesses pregnancy as imposing additional physical burden in general, whereas the other items in this factor primarily concern the interaction between pregnancy and the disease, including its impact on recurrence, maternal health, and the child's health. In the Korean context, pregnancy itself is often perceived as a physically demanding period requiring careful care.38 This cultural framing may lead Korean women to respond to item 1 differently from items focused on disease-related pregnancy risks. Given that pregnancy risk perception among breast cancer survivors is itself a multidimensional construct,35,39 and that criteria for acceptable factor loadings are not absolute when constructs are broad or multifaceted,40 item 1 was retained as an essential component of the pregnancy risk factor.

A related pattern was also observed at the factor level. The strong correlation between the pregnancy risk and disease control factors indicated that discriminant validity between these two factors was not supported. Both factors reflect subjective appraisals of one's disease status: disease control captures general perceptions of prognosis, symptom management, and recurrence, whereas pregnancy risk captures how these disease-related concerns extend into pregnancy-specific outcomes. Consistent with previous qualitative research among Korean breast cancer survivors, perceptions of physical burden and potential pregnancy-related harm are often evaluated alongside broader disease-related concerns when considering future pregnancy.4 This conceptual and cultural overlap may explain the high inter-factor correlation observed in this study. Although conceptually distinct, the two factors appear to be practically interconnected within the reproductive decision-making of Korean breast cancer survivors.

In the known-group comparison, women planning pregnancy demonstrated significantly higher fertility intention than those without pregnancy plans. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that women who desire pregnancy report significantly higher fertility intention scores.19 This result supports the ability of the K-FIS to differentiate individuals based on their pregnancy plans.

The statistically significant positive correlation between the K-FIS “social support” factor and the MSPSS provides evidence of convergent validity at the subscale level. Previous studies have shown that social support is positively associated with pregnancy and childbirth intention,12 and this association has also been observed among women of reproductive age in the general population.41,42 Women who receive adequate support from family members or significant others often experience reduced psychological burden and fewer concerns related to pregnancy and childbearing,35,43 which may, in turn, enhance fertility intention. These findings underscore the importance of social support in reproductive decision-making.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Beyond its psychometric properties, the K-FIS may have clinical utility in oncology nursing practice. Assessing fertility intention using this instrument may help women reflect on their emotions, clarify reproductive goals, and communicate fertility-related concerns more effectively with health care providers. Evaluating fertility intention during survivorship may also support patient-centered reproductive counseling, particularly for women considering pregnancy after breast cancer treatment.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the K-FIS appears to be a potentially useful instrument that may be used in both clinical and research settings to support Korean women with breast cancer in navigating complex reproductive decisions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consisted exclusively of women who had completed active breast cancer treatment, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals undergoing active treatment or those recently diagnosed. Future studies should examine the scale's applicability across the breast cancer trajectory. Second, the use of a self-administered online survey and convenience sampling through online communities may have introduced self-reporting and selection biases, thereby limiting the representativeness of the sample. Third, some aspects of the internal structure of the K-FIS, including convergent and discriminant validity, as well as model fit, require further validation. Although these patterns may reflect culturally specific features of fertility intention among Korean breast cancer survivors, further studies are needed to confirm the structure of the K-FIS. Fourth, convergent validity was examined to a limited extent, and criterion validity could not be assessed due to the absence of suitable external and gold-standard measures. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the K-FIS against a broader range of external measures. Finally, the influence of parenthood status and other factors on fertility intention was not examined in this study. Future studies should investigate how these factors influence fertility intention among breast cancer survivors.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that the Korean version of the Fertility Intention Scale has generally acceptable validity and reliability for systematically assessing fertility intention among women with breast cancer in Korea, although some psychometric properties require further validation. In clinical settings, the K-FIS may help health care professionals to identify patients’ fertility-related intentions in a structured and comprehensive manner. The K-FIS may facilitate timely communication, individualized counseling, and shared decision-making regarding reproductive health in clinical practice.
