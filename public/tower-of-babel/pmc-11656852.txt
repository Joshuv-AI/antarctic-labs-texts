A comparison between lipid-based vs. glycemic-based insulin sensitivity indices for the association with abnormal ECG findings and 20-year mortality among older adults
Authors: Yonatan Moshkovits, Adam Goldman, Angela Chetrit, Hadar Moshkovitz Shrem, Rachel Dankner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA direct comparison between glycemic-based and lipid-based insulin sensitivity indices (ISIs) for ECG findings and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality is lacking.Methods963 community-dwelling older adults, examined as part of the third phase of the Glucose intolerance, Obesity, and Hypertension study between 1999 and 2008, were followed until December 2016 and December 2019 for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, respectively. Eleven different ISIs were calculated and evaluated against ECG findings, all-cause, and cardiovascular mortality with multivariable regression models. The area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) and net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis were implemented to compare ISIs performance.ResultsMean age was 72.3 ± 7 years and 471 (49%) were females. Ischemic ECG changes were observed in 107 (11.2%) individuals. Upper quartile (Q4) of triglyceride-glucose waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WTHR) was associated with 220% greater odds for ischemic changes on ECG compared with lower quartiles (Q1-3) (95%CI:1.3–3.7, p = 0.004), an association that was not observed with other ISIs. During a median follow-up of 13 [IQR-8] and 11 [IQR-6] years for all-cause and CV mortality, respectively, 466 (48.4%) participants died, of them, 179 (38.4%) were attributed to cardiovascular causes. TyG-WTHR was the only ISI that was associated with both all-cause (HR = 1.3, 95%CI:1.0–1.6, p = 0.04) and cardiovascular (HR = 1.7, 95%CI:1.2–2.4, p = 0.004) mortality. Lipid based and glycemic ISIs showed similar predicative ability with slightly better predictive performance for TyG-WTHR for all-cause mortality (AUC = 0.46, 95%CI:0.4–0.5, p = 0.02). The NRI analysis revealed better reclassification ability for triglyceride-high-density-lipoprotein ratio (95%CI: 0.02–0.27, p = 0.03) and TyG-WTHR (95%CI: 0.0004–0.01, p = 0.03) for all-cause mortality while TyG-WTHR-based model correctly reclassified 19% of participants (95%CI: 0.02–0.36, p = 0.03) for cardiovascular mortality compared with model unadjusted for any ISIs and correctly reclassified 3% (95%CI:0.003–0.05, p = 0.02) compared with QUICKI based-model for all-cause mortality.ConclusionsTyG-WTHR was the only ISI associated with ischemic changes on ECG and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality and significantly improved the predictive performance for all-cause cardiovascular mortality. While most glycemic-based and lipid-based ISIs showed similar predictive ability, TyG-WTHR stands as the preferred ISI and should be considered for screening at-risk individuals for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02533-3.Keywords: Insulin sensitivity indices, All-cause mortality, Cardiovascular mortality, ECG abnormalities, Ischemic changes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), emerges as one of the most common chronic diseases globally, with significant implications on morbidity and mortality, particularly from cardiovascular (CV) causes [1, 2]. Worldwide, it is estimated that 462 million individuals are living with the disease (about 6.3% of the world’s population) and more than a million deaths were attributed to DM in 2017, placing it as the ninth cause of death [1]. Diabetes prevalence is expected to increase from 6,059 individuals per 100,000 to 7,079 individuals per 100,000 by the year 2030, calling for urgent preventative interventions, as type 2 DM is considered reversible at early stages [1]. Several biomarkers were previously proposed by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [3], due to their correlation with insulin resistance, for the diagnosis of DM as well as a measure of glycemic control, such as plasma fasting glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin (A1C) and plasma glucose after oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Although commonly used in clinical practice, these biomarkers do not reflect the complex pathophysiology of DM, as manifested by overweight and dyslipidemia, thus identifying at-risk patients late in the course of the disease.

To address these issues while improving the diagnosis of insulin resistance at early stages, several indices were developed and validated compared with the gold standard yet intrusive euglycemic insulin clamp [4]. These indices include glycemic-based insulin sensitivity indices (ISIs) (consisting of fasting glucose, glucose or insulin after OGTT, or fasting insulin levels such as Homeostatic model assessment (HOMA) [5], the Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index (MISI) [6], the Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI) [7], and lipid-based ISIs (primarily triglyceride to high density lipoprotein ratio (TG-HDL) [8, 9], Mcauley index (MCAi) [10] triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) [11, 12] and its derivative that combines anthropometric measurements (such as TyG-waist circumference (WC) [13] and TyG-waist to height ratio (TyG-WTHR)). Previous studies have shown that lipid-based and glycemic-based ISIs were associated with ECG findings [14–16] and mortality [17–20], as well as that lipid-based ISIs were superior for predicting insulin resistance [11, 21–23]. However, a direct comparison between both types of surrogates for the association with all-cause and CV mortality, especially among the elderly, is lacking. This comparison is essential when considering which ISI should be implemented for screening at-risk populations.

We, therefore, aimed to evaluate the association between lipid-based ISIs and glycemic-based ISIs with ECG findings and all-cause and CV mortality and compare their performances among community-dwelling older adults, and further characterize the preferred ISIs in different sub-groups.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The study was conducted as part of the third phase of the Israel study on Glucose intolerance, Obesity, and Hypertension (GOH) and is further discussed elsewhere [16]. The GOH study is a longitudinal prospective cohort study initiated in 1967 when 5,710 adults were randomly sampled from the National Population Registry according to sex, origin, and birth decade. In the third phase of the study (1999–2008), which is included in the current study, 1,189 survivors were evaluated using a structured-questionnaire for their medical interview, which included information regarding any dietary constraints they were recommended for (classified into: low fat, low sugar, low calories, low salt, low protein, vegan/vegetarian, and other), co-morbidities, medications, and health habits, while 963 of them further underwent extensive blood tests, anthropometric measurements (i.e. weight, height, hip and waist circumferences) and resting electrocardiogram (ECG) recording at regional medical centers.

Blood tests were analyzed in a central laboratory while meeting the stringent standards of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Participants signed a written informed consent and the protocol was approved by the Sheba Medical Center's institutional review board (IRB request number-1180).

Insulin sensitivity indices

Lipid-based ISIs examined in the current study included: Mcauley index (MCAi), Triglyceride-Glucose index (TyG), Triglyceride-High-Density Lipoprotein ratio (TG–HDL), Triglyceride-Glucose–Waist Circumference (TyG–WC), Triglyceride-Glucose–Body Mass Index (TyG–BMI), Triglyceride-Glucose–Waist To Hip ratio (TyG–WTHpR) and Triglyceride-Glucose–Waist To Height Ratio (TyG–WTHR). Glycemic-based ISIs included: Homeostatic model assessment-Insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), Homeostatic model assessment–percent beta cell function (HOMA-%B), Matsuda Insulin Sensitivity Index (MISI), and Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI).

Indices were evaluated as a continuous variable while those who showed statistical significance were further categorized into sex-specific quartiles (upper quartile [Q4] compared to lower quartiles [Q1-3]), which corresponds to a cutoff of the 75th percentile, and examined for their association with ECG findings and mortality. Glycemic and lipid-based insulin sensitivity indices formula, description, and categorization in the current study are described in Supplementary Table 1.

ECG findings

Resting twelve-lead ECG recordings interpretation was conducted by a single cardiologist according to the Minnesota code classification system [24]. Findings were classified into 5 groups according to related abnormalities (Supplementary Table 2): nonspecific ST-T changes, ventricular/atrial disease, conduction disorder, ischemic changes, and other.

Study endpoints and covariates

As an exploratory analysis, the first endpoint was any ECG abnormality recorded at baseline while the second and third endpoints were all-cause and CV mortality, respectively, obtained from the National Population Registry (information on mortality was available until the end of follow-up, i.e., December 2019 for all-cause mortality and December 2016 for specific cause of death). Follow-up began at screening and ended at the time of death or end of follow-up, whichever came first. Cause of death was reported using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 or ICD 10.

Additional analysis

To account for non-linear relationships between the indices and both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, we conducted a restricted cubic spline analysis with 4 knots, using the mean values of the ISIs as the reference. This analysis was adjusted for the same covariates as the models for Hazard Ratios. Non-linearity was assessed using an ANOVA test.

The correlations between glycemic-ISIs and lipid-ISIs were evaluated using Pearson correlation in order to identify indices with similar properties. We compared the model's discriminative performances by the area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) and assessed the contribution of the ISIs in predicting mortality compared to a model unadjusted to ISIs and the addition of TyG-WTHR to the risk models compared to glycemic-based ISI models using the continuous and categorical net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis [25] at the median follow-up time.

A clinically relevant exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate TyG-WTHR as the preferred ISI in relation to all-cause mortality, aiming to identify any interactions or effect modifications in specific populations that might benefit most from screening with this ISI. Additionally, recognizing the impact of sex on various ISI levels, particularly those incorporating anthropometric measurements, we performed a sex-based stratification for all indices related to mortality and examined their interaction with baseline abnormal ECG findings.

To assess effect size estimates, we further explored the associations between ISIs and mortality using an expanded multivariable model. This model included low-density lipoprotein (LDL), fasting triglyceride levels, and BMI, in addition to the other variables previously mentioned.

Statistical analysis

Differences between groups were examined using the Student T test for continuous variables or the Mann–Whitney test for nonparametric variables whereas categorical variables were compared by the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for small cells. The association between ISIs and incidental ECG findings was evaluated using a multivariable logistic regression model and presented by odd ratios (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (95%CI) while the association between ISI and all-cause and CV mortality was examined using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, presented by hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% CI.

The multivariable models included known risk factors for CV disease and all-cause mortality or medications that could modify glucose and lipid levels (i.e., statins) and were adjusted for age, sex, origin (Yemen, Asia, North Africa, and Europe/North America), smoking (current, past, never), systolic blood pressure, current physical activity, statins use, low GFR (defined as GFR < 60 (mL/min) according to the Cockcroft-Gault formula), total cholesterol, glycemic state (according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [3]: normoglycemic—fasting glucose < 100 mg/dl or 2-h post-oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) plasma glucose < 140 mg/dl and no diabetes at baseline or of using anti-diabetes medications; Pre-diabetes—fasting plasma glucose 100–125 mg/dl or 2-h post-OGTT plasma glucose 140–199 mg/dl and no diabetes at baseline or using anti-diabetes medications; Diabetes—fasting plasma glucose > 125 mg/dl, or 2-h post-OGTT plasma glucose > 199 mg/dl, or the previous diagnosis of diabetes or of using anti-diabetes medications), history of myocardial infarction (MI) and selected ISI. Multi-collinearity was tested using the Spearman's rank correlation coefficient or the Pearson correlation between the study variables. Variables that showed a high correlation between them (≥ 60%) were not introduced into the same multivariable model (i.e. BMI with TyG-WTHR, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC). The proportional hazards assumption for the multivariable Cox regression model was tested using the log minus log plot.

We calculated that a minimal sample size of 840 participants was required for 80% power and two-sided 5% significance level using WINPEPI® software, assuming an average survival probability of 45% at the end of follow-up and a hazard ratio of 1.3 for overall mortality. Statistical analysis was performed with Python software (version 3.10), R software version 3.4.4 (R foundation for Statistical Computing) and SPSS software (version 25). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of ≤ 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The final cohort included 963 individuals with a mean baseline age of 72.3 ± 7 years, of them 471 (49%) were females (Table 1). 392 (41%) reported consuming a specific diet, mostly low sugar (n = 142 [15%]) and low fat (n = 138 [14%]) diets. Baseline co-morbidities included ischemic heart disease (IHD, n = 109 [11%]), malignancy (n = 152 [16%]), previous cerebrovascular accident (CVA, n = 79 [8%]), and hypertension (n = 410 [43%]).

The mean TyG-WTHR was 5.31 (± 0.8) for men and 5.58 (± 0.8) for women, with men having TyG-WTHR values ≥ 5.76 and women ≥ 6.05 classified as high TyG-WTHR (Q4) (Supplementary Table 1). Individuals in the higher quartile of TyG-WTHR (n = 231 [24%]) were significantly older (73 ± 8 vs. 72 ± 7), less active (91 [39%] vs. 385 [55%]), had higher systolic (142.9 ± 19 vs 135.9 ± 20 mmHg) and diastolic (79.3 ± 12 vs. 77.2 ± 10 mmHg) blood pressure, and had more comorbidities such as obesity (160 [70%] vs. 122 [18%]), diabetes (131 [58%] vs. 167 [25%]), and hypertension (127 [55%] vs. 264 [38%]).

ISI and ECG abnormalities

ECG abnormalities were detected in 604 (63%) individuals (Supplementary Table 2), with conduction disorder being the most frequently observed (351 [36.4%]). Ischemic ECG changes were found in 107 (11.2%) individuals.

The multivariable logistic regression model (Fig. 1, Table 2), showed greater odds for ischemic changes on ECG for every 1-unit increment in TyG–WTHR (OR = 1.5, 95%CI: 1.1–2.0, p = 0.01). Individuals in the upper quartile (Q4) of the TyG–WTHR had 220% higher odds for ischemic changes on ECG, compared to the lower quartiles (Q1-3) (OR = 2.2, 95%CI: 1.3–3.7, p = 0.004) (Table 2). Other variables that were significantly associated with increased odds for ischemic changes were renal impairment (OR = 2.1, 95%CI: 1.2–3.7, p = 0.01), diabetes (OR = 2.6, 95%CI: 1.4–4.6, p = 0.001), Europe-America origin (OR = 2.7, 95%CI: 1.4–5.6, p = 0.01), and IHD (OR = 7.3, 95%CI: 4.3–12.6, p < 0.001). TyG–WTHR was not significantly associated with other ECG findings. Other ISIs were not found to be associated with any of the recorded ECG findings (Table 2, Supplementary Table 3).

ISI and all-cause and CV mortality

During a median follow-up of 13 (IQR-8) years, 466 (48.4%) participants died, while 381(39.5%) specific causes of death were documented until December 2016. The most common cause of death was CV disorders (179 [38%]) with a median follow-up of 11 [IQR-6] years, followed by cancer-associated mortality (65 [7%]), infectious diseases (38 [4%]) and other causes of death (99 [10.3%]). Individuals with ECG classified as abnormal had a150% greater risk for CV mortality (95%CI: 1.1–2.3, p = 0.03) in the multivariable Cox analysis with borderline significance for all-cause mortality (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 0.99–1.5, p = 0.053). These associations were not observed for ischemic changes (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 0.9–1.7, p = 0.09 and HR = 0.9, 95%CI: 0.5–1.4, p = 0.6 for all-cause and CV mortality respectively).

Kaplan–Meier survival curves and the Log Rank test (Fig. 2A + B) showed a significant increase in all-cause and CV mortality for participants in the high TyG–WTHR group (p < 0.001 for both). Multivariable Cox regression model (Table 3) showed 20% and 30% greater risk for all-cause (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.0–1.4, p = 0.01) and CV (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.1–1.7, p = 0.01) mortality respectively for every 1-unit increase in TyG–WTHR.

Additional analysis

Restricted cubic spline analysis

Restricted cubic spline analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed a nonlinear relationship between TyG-WTHR (p-overall = 0.001 and p-nonlinear = 0.03) and TyG-BMI (p-overall = 0.002 and p-nonlinear = 0.01) with all-cause mortality, while other indices exhibited a linear relationship (p-nonlinear > 0.05 for all). Regarding cardiovascular mortality (Supplementary Fig. 2), all indices showed a linear association with CV mortality (p-nonlinear > 0.05 for all).

Exploratory subgroups analysis (Fig. 3) revealed a significant effect modification for the association between TyG–WTHR and all-cause mortality when the cohort was stratified by age cutoff of 70 years (HR = 1.6, 95%CI: 1.3–2.1, p = 0.004), anti-diabetic medications treatment (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.0–1.4, p = 0.04), baseline ischemic heart disease (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.1–1.4, p = 0.01), physical activity (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.0–1.7, p = 0.03), diabetes (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.0–1.6, p = 0.02), female sex (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.1–1.6, p = 0.01), and statins treatment (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.1–1.5, p = 0.01). Sensitivity analysis

Among individuals with diabetes (n = 316) (Supplementary Table 5), increasing values of TyG-WTHR were associated with a 30% greater risk for all-cause mortality (95%CI: 1.0–1.6, p = 0.02) but not for other ISIs. Higher Ln-MISI values were associated with a significant risk for CV mortality (HR = 1.6, 95%CI: 1.2–2.2, p = 0.002) while high Ln HOMA-B was associated with lower CV mortality (HR = 0.7, 95%CI: 0.5–0.9, p = 0.01).

In individuals free from diabetes, (n = 626) (Supplementary Table 5), TyG-HDL (HR = 1.1, 95%CI: 1.0–1.1, p = 0.03) and TyG-WTHpR (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.0–1.3, p = 0.01) were the only ISIs that were associated with significant risk for all-cause mortality, while TG-WC (HR = 1.003, 95%CI: 1.001–1.005, p = 0.01), TyG-WTHpR (HR = 1.3, 95%CI: 1.1–1.6, p = 0.01), and TyG-WTHR (HR = 1.4, 95%CI: 1.0–1.9, p = 0.03) were significantly associated with CV mortality.

Extended regression models (Supplementary Table 6) that included an addition of LDL, triglycerides and BMI yielded similar results, showing a 30% (95% CI: 1.1–1.6, p = 0.01) and 40% (95% CI: 1.1–1.9, p = 0.01) increased risk for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively, with higher TyG-WTHR values. Interestingly, LDL levels were found to be associated with a decreased risk of cardiovascular mortality in several of the models.

Comparing glycemic-based and lipid-based ISI

Correlation analysis (Supplementary Table 7) found that MCAi had the highest correlations to glycemic-based ISIs; Ln HOMA-IR (r = -0.8, p < 0.001), Ln HOMA-B (r = -0.588, p < 0.001), QUICKI (r = 0.819, p < 0.001) and Ln MISI (r = 0.713, p < 0.001) whereas TyG-WTHR demonstrated poorer correlation with glycemic-based ISIs.

Models’ discriminative performance (Supplementary Table 8, Fig. 4) was evaluated using the AUC and found a slightly better performance for TyG-WTHR (AUC = 0.46, 95%CI: 0.4–0.5, p = 0.02) and TyG-WTHpR (AUC = 0.46, 95%CI: 0.4–0.5, p = 0.02) for all-cause mortality without any significant difference for other lipid-based and glycemic-based ISIs.

When evaluating the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) for all-cause mortality (Supplementary Table 9) at the median follow-up time of 13 years, most of the lipid-based and glycemic-based ISIs models did not showed reclassification improvement compared with a model unadjusted to any ISI for all-cause mortality with the exception of TG-HDL based model that demonstrated 14% reclassification improvement (95%CI: 0.02–0.27, p = 0.03) and TyG-WTHR based model with 1% of the patients correctly reclassified (95%CI: 0.004–0.01, p = 0.03). Continuous net reclassification improvement analysis for CV mortality at the median follow-up time of 11 years (Supplementary Table 10) found a 19% improvement (95%CI: 0.02–0.36, p = 0.03) for the TyG-WTHR-based model over a model unadjusted to any ISI but not for other lipid-based or glycemic-based ISIs.

We did not detect any significant improvement with TyG-WTHR-based model compared with glycemic-based models for CV mortality (Supplementary Table 12).

Discussion

In this study of 963 community-dwelling older adults, evaluating 11 different ISIs, sex-specific TyG-WTHR was the only index associated with increased odds of ischemic changes on ECG and a higher risk of 13-year all-cause and 11-year cardiovascular mortality. These associations persisted after further adjusted to LDL, BMI and triglycerides. Most lipid—and glycemic-based ISIs demonstrated similar predictive abilities and did not enhance mortality risk models, except for TyG-WTHR, which correctly reclassified 19% of individuals for cardiovascular mortality compared to a model without any ISI, and 3% compared to a model based on QUICKI. Sex-stratified analysis showed that lipid-based ISIs had greater predictive power for cardiovascular mortality among women. As the preferred ISI, TyG-WTHR was further explored to assess its modification effect on all-cause mortality, considering factors like age, sex, anti-diabetic medications, statins, baseline ischemic heart disease, physical activity, and diabetes morbidity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that TyG-WTHR was the only ISI associated with all-cause mortality among individuals with diabetes, while TyG-WTHR outperformed other markers as a prognostic tool for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in non-diabetic participants. Lastly, restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non-linear association between TyG-WTHR and all-cause mortality and a linear association with CV mortality.

The first ISI, the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA)-insulin resistance (IR) and beta cell function (B) [5] were proposed almost 40 years ago to provide an indirect and non-invasive evaluation of insulin resistance. This ISI represents the balance between hepatic glucose output and insulin secretion and was validated against the gold standard, the euglycemic insulin clamp [26]. Subsequent studies supported the correlation of HOMA models with metabolic abnormalities [27, 28] as well as with all-cause and CV mortality [16, 29]. Since then, dozens of ISIs have been developed and explored for an association with insulin resistance and mortality. These markers could be classified as glycemic ISIs, which evaluates insulin resistance based on glucose/insulin levels during fasting or after OGTT (i.e. HOMA, QUICKI, MISI etc.) and lipid-based ISI, which further implement triglyceride/HDL levels (as TyG or TG-HDL), and their derivatives, which combine anthropometric measurements (i.e. TyG-BMI/ WC/ WTHpR, /WTHR, etc.). Concerning glycemic ISIs, lipid-based and anthropometric-based ISIs express early and more inclusive mechanisms in the pathophysiology of diabetes. These advanced ISIs, particularly the TyG and TyG-WTHR, were found to be superior to glycemic ISIs for diagnosing diabetes, metabolic syndrome, arterial stiffness, and CV mortality [21, 30–33].

The TyG was previously associated with several ECG abnormalities such as atrial fibrillation [14] and prolonged QT interval [15] whereas data on other TyG derivatives is scarce.

As for the association between TyG derivatives and CV disease occurrence data is still conflicting [34–37]. Ren et al. [35] followed 3,312 participants aged 58.3 years as part of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study and showed a 27% (OR = 1.27, 95%CI: 1.12–1.43) greater odds for CV disease incidence for every standard deviation elevation in TyG-WTHR. Cui and colleagues [36], analyzed a cohort with a 7-year follow-up and found that the TyG and the TyG-WTHR were significantly associated with new-onset CV disease (HR = 1.2, 95%CI: 1.088–1.356 and HR = 1.1, 95%CI: 1.002–1.235, respectively) but not for TyG-BMI and TyG-WC. This study also demonstrated better predictive performance for the TyG over TyG-WTHR and other modified TyG indices. Conversely, Xia et al. [37] found a significant association between TyG and its related parameters (TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WTHR) with CV disease incidence. Additionally, they reported a better capability of TyG-WC and TyG-WTHR in predicting CV disease events compared to TyG (IDI = 0.004, 95% CI: 0.001–0.004 and IDI = 0.004, 95% CI: 0.001, 0.004 for TyG-WC and TyG-WTHR, respectively).

In our cohort, the TyG-WTHR was the only ISI significantly associated with ECG findings, showing 220% greater odds for ischemic changes in the multivariable model. This finding reinforced the association of TyG-WTHR and the risk for CV disease events compared to other ISIs, as documented by an objective measurement of IHD-ECG recording, in concordance with previous studies [34–37].

TyG association with all-cause and CV mortality has been well established [38–40]. In a general population, Liu et al. [31] evaluated 19,420 participants as part of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), for a mean follow-up of 98.2 months, and demonstrated that high TyG index was associated with an elevated risk of all-cause (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.00–1.20) and CV mortality (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.05–1.57) among relatively young participants (mean age 47.1 ± 19.3 years). Li et al. [40] further explored this association in 27,642 individuals from the NHANES cohort and found an increased all-cause, CV, and non-CV mortality for the highest quartiles of TyG, TyG-WC, and TyG-WTHR. The study also observed significant effect modifications for all indices with a higher risk of mortality among participants younger than 65 years, those with high LDL-C levels, and those who were not treated with statins.

In patients with metabolic syndrome, Wei and colleagues [39] examined the association between TyG, TyG-WC, and TyG-WTHR and all-cause and CV mortality among 10,734 individuals with metabolic syndrome. While TyG (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.18–1.56, p < 0.001) and TyG-WTHR (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.13–1.47, p < 0.001) were associated with all-cause mortality, the TyG-WC (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.13–1.85, p = 0.004) as well as the TyG-WTHR (HR = 1.5, 95% CI, 1.17–1.92, p < 0.002) were associated with CV mortality. Min et al. [41] showed similar results among younger patients (median age of 49 years) with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver, while demonstrating only modest improvement in the performance of TyG-WTHR (C index all-cause = 0.585, C index CV = 0.615) over TyG-WC (C index all-cause = 0.579, C index CV = 0.579) and TyG index (C index all-cause = 0.563, C index CV = 0.563) for all-cause and CV mortality.

In correspondence with current evidence, our findings support the use of TyG-WTHR as a predictor of mortality among older participants as demonstrated by a 30% and 70% elevated risk for all-cause and CV mortality respectively, for TyG-WTHR of ≥ 5.76 and ≥ 6.05 for men and women respectively. Furthermore, similar to other studies, the TyG-WTHR had better predictive ability among participants younger than 70 years, physically active, free from IHD, not treated with statins or anti-diabetic medication, females, and individuals with diabetes. Among individuals free of diabetes, TyG-WTHpR was the only ISI associated with all-cause and CV mortality while TyG-WTHR was associated with CV mortality. In contrast, in diabetes patients, TyG-WTHR was associated with all-cause mortality whereas Ln HOMA-B and LN-MISI were associated with CV mortality. Finally, our findings showed that TyG-WTHR significantly improved the risk model by 19% compared to a model unadjusted for ISIs and a 3% improvement compared with a QUICKI base model. These findings further highlight the added value of implementing TyG-WTHR not only in the general population but also in specific subgroups, such as individuals with or without diabetes, and those at relatively lower cardiovascular risk, including younger individuals, women, those without ischemic heart disease, and those who are physically active. Additionally, because TyG-WTHR incorporates factors like height, waist circumference, triglyceride, and fasting glucose levels, it is anticipated that its values may be influenced by variables such as age, sex, activity level, and treatments that affect triglyceride and glucose levels, such as antidiabetic medications and statins. Our results emphasize the need to take these factors into account when establishing cutoffs for abnormal TyG-WTHR values.

In contrast with previous studies that reported significant associations between other ISIs and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, such as the NHANES [40], with mean age of the participants of 47 years, the current analysis identified only TyG-WTHR as being associated with both the primary and secondary outcomes. In a previous analysis of an earlier phase of the GOH cohort [16], when participants were 52.0 ± 8.1 years old, we found an association between ischemic changes on ECG and the MCAi, which conferred higher odds for 40-year mortality. This discrepancy could be attributed to the older age of participants in the current study, along with a higher prevalence of comorbidities like obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. Additionally, residual confounders, such as medications and comorbid conditions, may not have been fully addressed. Moreover, while MCAi was initially classified as a lipid-based ISI, correlation analysis revealed a stronger correlation with glycemic-based indices. This suggests that MCAi behaves similarly to glycemic-based indices, reflecting similar metabolic abnormalities compared to lipid-based indices and offering similar predictive capability. Conversely, TyG-WTHR and other lipid-based indices displayed weaker correlations with glycemic-based indices, indicating that they may function through distinct mechanisms in the development of insulin resistance.

The superiority of the TyG and its derivatives over glycemic—ISIs for CV morbidity and mortality can be explained by several mechanisms. High TyG was previously associated with micro—and macro angiopathies such as albuminuria and cerebrovascular accident (CVA) [42], with subclinical atherosclerosis and arterial stiffness [43], and even with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [44]. The complex pathophysiology of diabetes is initiated with the accumulation of body fat (specifically visceral obesity), insulin resistance, and increased insulin levels [45]. At early stages, this phenomenon is accompanied by endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, oxidative stress, and increased lipolysis [45–47].

Importantly, the European Association for the Study of Obesity recently issued new guidelines for the diagnosis, staging, and management of obesity in adults [48], recognizing that BMI is insufficient as a sole diagnostic criterion for obesity and that fat accumulation is a stronger predictor of cardiometabolic complications than BMI. Moreover, the guidelines introduce the WTHR as an alternative diagnostic criterion for obesity in individuals with BMI ≥ 25 (kg/m2) while acknowledging the superiority of WTHR over WC as a better cardiometabolic disease risk marker and therefore the preferred diagnostic tool. This was demonstrated in recent studies showing that the WTHR and the WTHpR had superior discriminatory power to predict cardiometabolic risk factors than BMI and WC [49–51].

Aging is characterized by an increase in body fat, sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass) [52], hyperinsulinemia [53], hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia [54], and eventually insulin resistance. Given these metabolic changes in the elderly, it is plausible that the superior performance of TyG-WTHR in predicting CV morbidity (ischemic changes in ECG) and mortality, over other glycemic-based and even lipid-based ISIs is due to the combination of TyG and WTHR, which better reflects visceral fat accumulation compared to BMI or body weight. Additionally, the interaction between lipid metabolism and hyperglycemia may make TyG-WTHR a more effective predictive marker in this population.

Identifying these high-risk individuals should prompt early interventions throughout the disease progression, such as promoting healthy lifestyle changes like improved diet and increased physical activity, implementing more aggressive treatment for other cardiovascular risk factors like dyslipidemia and obesity, and considering the initiation of anti-diabetic therapy. It could also assist in decision-making when determining the need for screening for ischemic heart disease. These interventions have the potential to contribute to both primary and secondary prevention, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality.

In this comprehensive study, we compared eleven different ISIs, while focusing on a community-dwelling elderly population and evaluating associations between ECG abnormalities and mortality. These associations should be further assessed in future studies to support and validate the implementation of TyG-WTHR in the general population.

This study has several limitations. First, it included individuals who survived until the 3rd phase of the GOH study, thus representing the younger and more fit participants, subjecting it to survival bias. Nonetheless, this cohort depicts the average elderly living in the community. Second, due to the limited sample size, we were underpowered to examine the associations of the TyG-WTHR with mortality in the sub-group analysis and sensitivity analyses, albeit our findings underscore important trends to be further evaluated. Third, MISI mean glucose and insulin plasma levels were calculated only using the available 0, 60, and 120 min during the OGTT. However, this calculation is acceptable in the literature [55]. Lastly, due to a lack of standardizations in the laboratory measurements of insulin and glucose or clear reference intervals and cut-off values in the general population and among important subgroups such as sex, age, ethnicity, and glycemic state, the implementation of ISI in clinical practice is challenging [56].

Despite these limitations, the current analysis has a number of advantages such as the representation of older adults in an ethnically diverse sample of the Israeli–Jewish population, evaluation of 10 different ISIs, a comprehensive adjustment in the multivariable model including medical treatment and co-morbidities, exploratory analysis highlighting important trends and relatively long follow up period.

Conclusion

The TyG-WTHR was associated with ischemic changes on an incidental ECG recording, all-cause and CV mortality, among older adults. This biomarker significantly improved the risk model for CV mortality while demonstrating better predictive performance compared with the QUICKI. TyG-WTHR should be considered as the preferred ISI for screening high-risk older adults for CV morbidity and mortality.
