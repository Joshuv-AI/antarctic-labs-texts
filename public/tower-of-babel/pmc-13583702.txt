PLP1-triggered NOT gate to address neurotoxicity risk of PSMA-targeted prostate cancer therapy
Authors: Sanam Shafaattalab, Alexander Partin, Aaron Winters, Richele Bruno, Vasantika Suryawanshi, Jushen Liang, Ryan Elshimali, Nicolas G Pedroncelli, Chawita Netirojjanakul, Timothy P Riley, Jingli A Zhang, Alexander Kamb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOn-target, off-tumor toxicity is a recurring challenge for solid tumor therapeutics targeted to antigens preferentially expressed on malignant cells. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is a tumor-associated antigen that is expressed on the healthy prostate and is generally upregulated in prostate cancer. It is also detected via RNA-Seq in the central and peripheral nervous system (CNS and PNS) at levels that can exceed those in the prostate itself. The CNS/PNS expression of PSMA has been largely ignored in the oncology field, and it is possible that therapeutics targeting PSMA have limited access to antigens in the nervous system. However, many PSMA-targeted therapeutics that are active in the clinic induce substantial neurotoxicity, in some cases, even treatment-associated fatalities.MethodsTo protect PSMA-expressing neural tissues from on-target, off-tumor toxicity, we applied a synthetic cellular NOT gate that uses a LIR-1-based inhibitory receptor. We selected the NOT gate target antigen by searching genomic databases for a surface protein co-expressed with PSMA in the brain.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1), a protein expressed on the surface of oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells, is co-expressed with PSMA in the CNS and PNS. A NOT gate construct (PSMA | PLP1 Tmod) protects human oligodendrocytes in vitro from the cytotoxicity of a PSMA CAR. Though PSMA is not expressed in mouse brains at high levels, we show that PLP1 CARs induce toxicity in mice, consistent with the possibility that CNS/PNS expression of the target of an immunotherapeutic poses a serious risk. This toxicity is mitigated by a Tmod construct (PLP1 | PLP1), in which a PLP1 CAR substitutes for a PSMA CAR, a proof of principle that circumvents the problematic expression pattern of PSMA in the mouse.ConclusionsTogether, these findings not only highlight some of the potential risks and benefits of immunotherapy for solid tumors regarding antigens expressed in the brain, but also suggest a specific solution for PSMA CAR-Ts.

Introduction

Engineered immune cells have emerged as a powerful mechanism to reprogram immune function for cancer therapy. Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are especially effective at redirecting T cells to eradicate B cell malignancies, and efforts to expand this approach into solid tumor indications are ongoing.1 However, all cancer treatments, including cell therapies, must face the key obstacle of selectivity; namely, a lack of cell-surface molecules that truly differentiate tumors from normal tissues. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is a prime example of this challenge. Although PSMA is predominantly found on the surface of prostate cancer cells and normal prostate, which is a dispensable organ, it is also expressed in both the central and peripheral nervous system (CNS/PNS).2 PSMA catalyzes the hydrolysis of N-acetylaspartylglutamate, a prevalent neurotransmitter in the CNS, into glutamate and N-acetylaspartate.3–5 Thus, it is possible that PSMA expression in the CNS/PNS serves a role related to neurotransmitter modulation.

Developers of CAR-Ts that target PSMA have reasoned that limited expression in healthy tissues and upregulation of PSMA in prostate tumors should create a therapeutic window.6 7 Furthermore, because the brain is considered an immunologically privileged organ protected by the blood–brain barrier (BBB), it is not clear a priori that expression of antigens in the CNS poses a safety risk for CAR-Ts. Nevertheless, CAR-Ts directed at PSMA(+) prostate tumors have encountered dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), including cytokine-release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity.7 Though unproven, it is possible that cases of severe toxicity are caused or exacerbated by on-target activation of the CAR in the brain, as proposed for a MAGE-A3 TCR.8 In addition, severe inflammatory events such as CRS can compromise the integrity of the BBB, increasing the likelihood that lymphocytes may penetrate the brain from the bloodstream.9 Thus, the occurrence of neurotoxicity in patients treated with PSMA-specific CARs suggests that additional safety measures may be necessary.

In this paper, we identify proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1), an integral membrane protein that is part of the myelin sheath surrounding neurons, as an antigen co-expressed with PSMA in human oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells. We demonstrate that a NOT gate based on the LIR-1 inhibitory receptor (referred to here as a Tmod blocker, online supplemental figure S1A) is similar in function to other blockers that have been developed for HLA antigens currently being tested in the clinic.10–12 Importantly, PLP1 CAR-Ts that express an onboard cytokine construct (ie, an “armored CAR”) are toxic to mice, suggesting that the BBB cannot reliably exclude engineered T cells. However, the PLP1 blocker protects cells in vitro and in vivo. These data suggest that: (1) PSMA expression in the brain is a safety risk for PSMA CAR-Ts, especially armored versions and (2) a PLP1 NOT gate should enhance the risk–benefit relation of PSMA CAR-Ts for prostate cancer patients by protecting PSMA(+) CNS and PNS tissue without compromising efficacy.

Results

PLP1 is co-expressed with PSMA in oligodendrocytes

To identify specific cell types at risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity for PSMA-directed CAR-T therapies, we first analyzed bulk RNA sequencing data from healthy tissues (GTEx and TCGA datasets; see the “Methods” section). As expected, PSMA (FOLH1) transcripts were highly expressed in prostate tissue and were overexpressed in the large majority of prostate tumors, supporting its suitability as a target for PSMA-directed CAR-T therapies (figure 1A, online supplemental figure S1B). Alarmingly, tissues with the highest levels of PSMA expression were detected within the CNS, comparable to median levels in prostate and above the average level of expression for genes expressed in typical mammalian cells (~20 TPM).13 Although no direct on-target, off-tumor toxicities have been attributed to PSMA-targeting therapeutics, PSMA CAR-Ts have been associated with a high incidence of CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS).7 Motivated by this potential safety concern, we searched for an antigen that might be co-expressed in PSMA-positive cells in the brain, which might serve as the gating antigen for a PSMA Tmod design and protect CNS cells without compromising efficacy of a PSMA CAR.

Given the cellular heterogeneity of the brain, we first sought to better characterize the expression of PSMA in the CNS. scRNA-Seq data suggested that PSMA expression in the CNS is primarily restricted to oligodendrocytes, a specialized type of neuroglia responsible for providing support and insulation to axons via myelin sheaths (figure 1B). Expression of PSMA protein was confirmed using immunohistochemistry of oligodendrocytes differentiated in culture from inducible pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; figure 1D). Because oligodendrocytes play a key role in the CNS, the unintended targeting of these cells by PSMA CAR-Ts could have severe neurotoxic consequences. Given the functional similarity of oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells, producing myelin in the CNS and PNS, respectively, we explored RNA expression of PSMA in Schwann cells (eg, in tibial nerve), and detected it at levels similar to oligodendrocytes, potentially implicating risk of PSMA-targeted immuno-therapeutics to tissues outside the brain (figure 1E).

To mitigate this risk, we sought to identify oligodendrocyte-cell-associated and Schwann-cell-associated proteins as potential blocker antigens for a PSMA Tmod construct. Our search was limited to transmembrane proteins with negligible expression in prostate tumors to avoid interference with PSMA CAR efficacy. Among the candidates, PLP1 emerged as a promising blocker antigen due to its highly specific expression in oligodendrocytes and minimal expression in other tissues, including the prostate (figure 1A, B and C). PLP1 is a major structural component of myelin with a small extracellular domain, a property ideally suited for blocker activity according to previously established geometric requirements.14 Based on the expression patterns of PSMA and PLP1 in the brain, we hypothesized that a PSMA | PLP1 Tmod therapy might spare the oligodendrocytes (and Schwann cells) from cytotoxic PSMA CAR-T cells, reducing the likelihood of neurotoxicity. The considerably higher levels of PLP1 relative to PSMA were considered an advantage for the Tmod mechanism, increasing the likelihood that a blocker gated by PLP1 could control the activator effectively.15

Validation of PLP1 as a Tmod blocker candidate

To test if a PSMA | PLP1 Tmod construct might function as an effective NOT gate, a PLP1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) is required. No antibodies that bind the native extracellular region of PLP1 were available as a starting point for the design of a PLP1 binder, presumably because the PLP1 extracellular region is small (<30 residues) and identically conserved in mouse, rat, rabbit, and human (online supplemental figure S2A). Thus, we took advantage of an approach developed earlier that uses FLAG-tag insertions to direct FLAG-specific blockers, thus circumventing the need for an antigen-specific binder.14 The FLAG-tag was inserted within one of the four extracellular loops of PLP1, and the hybrid protein was expressed in HeLa cells alongside PSMA (figure 2A). The FLAG blocker, consisting of the LIR-1 intracellular and transmembrane domains fused to a FLAG scFv, was co-transfected with a PSMA activator derived from the J591 antibody (figure 2B). This Tmod construct (PSMA | FLAG) expressed well and was not only activated by PSMA but also inhibited by PLP1-FLAG in a dose-dependent manner in Jurkat cell assays (figure 2C and D).

Encouraged by these results, we generated novel antibodies specific to PLP1 by immunizing PLP1-knockout (PLP1 KO) mice with neural tissue harvested from wild-type BALB/c mice. Hybridoma supernatants from the immunized PLP1 KO mice were screened against both homogenized neural tissue and HeLa cells engineered to express human PLP1 (online supplemental figure S2 C-F). This screening approach identified multiple antibodies specific for PLP1, with cross-reactivity for both human and murine proteins. 16 antibodies were converted into scFvs to generate PLP1 CARs. Functional testing of these CARs in Jurkat cells demonstrated sensitive, specific activation in response to PLP1-expressing cells. Four of the 16 PLP1 scFvs were selected for further study based on their strong activation profiles. These 4 PLP1 scFvs were used to create blockers that effectively suppressed activation of PSMA CARs in a dose-dependent manner in Jurkat cells, with increasing amounts of transfected PLP1 mRNA leading to greater inhibition (figure 2E).

To confirm activity in human T cells, we transduced primary T cells with both the J591 PSMA activator and the lead blocker candidate. These PSMA | PLP1 Tmod cells demonstrated selective cytotoxicity. They killed target cells expressing PSMA but spared cells co-expressing both PSMA and PLP1 across multiple effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (figure 2F, online supplemental figure S2 B). Together, the data in Jurkat and primary T cells suggested that a PSMA | PLP1 Tmod construct functions as a NOT gate to confer potent and selective cytotoxicity per its design.

To further test the selectivity of the Tmod construct, iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes were tested in vitro in cytotoxicity assays. These cultured cells co-expressed both PSMA and PLP1, judged by IHC, though it appeared that dissociation eliminated many of the PLP1 molecules, presumably due to axon loss during collection from the culture dishes (figure 2G). Quantification of molecule number suggested 91000 PSMA molecules/cell and 3000 PLP1 molecules/cell (online supplemental figure S3 B). When the plated oligodendrocytes were exposed to PSMA CAR-Ts, they were killed (figure 2H; online supplemental figure S3 A,C). However, PSMA | PLP1 Tmod cells spared the oligodendrocytes. A blocker construct with a deleted LIR-1 intracellular domain (ICD) was ineffective, demonstrating that the blocker ICD is responsible for inhibitory activity. Thus, PSMA CAR-Ts kill PSMA(+) oligodendrocytes in culture, but the oligodendrocytes are spared by the PLP1 blocker module. In addition, proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ measured at the end of the assay were higher in PSMA CAR co-cultures compared to PSMA | PLP1 Tmod co-cultures. As expected, secretion of IFN-γ was elevated in PSMA | ΔPLP1 Tmod constructs with the deleted LIR-1 ICD (figure 2I).

Armoring PSMA | PLP1 Tmod cells with an onboard cytokine

In the effort to improve efficacy of CAR- and TCR-Ts in solid tumor settings, drug developers have incorporated booster modules into their design, sometimes called “armored CARs” in the case of CAR-Ts. For example, in one study, a TGFβR1 dominant-negative expression module was engineered in T cells alongside a PSMA CAR.16 Though active in the clinic, this armored CAR-T was toxic, highlighting the risk of potent PSMA cell therapies.7 Tmod cells contain a mechanism that creates a therapeutic window for tumor-associated antigens like PSMA. In principle, this window confers the opportunity to push potency without sacrificing selectivity, provided the booster does not override the blocker mechanism. In the case of Tmod constructs that use a LIR-1 blocker, we and others have shown that addition of onboard cytokines does not interfere with selectivity.17 18 To confirm this behavior with the PSMA | PLP1 Tmod construct, we introduced expression modules with a constitutively activated IL-7 receptor where the alpha chain is covalently linked via a cysteine substitution in the transmembrane region to form a homodimer (di-IL7Rα; figure 3A). This booster was chosen from a set of possible cytokine constructs based on its small size and technical ease of manipulation.

Addition of the booster module had only modest effects on acute potency in primary T cell assays (figure 3B). However, in longer-term assays, it boosted expansion in the presence of exogenous IL-2, and survival in its absence (figure 3C). This booster did not affect the selectivity of the PSMA | PLP1 constructs (figure 3B). We suspect that di-IL7RA constitutive signaling substitutes for, and may add to, the effects of other major cytokines such as IL-2. Thus, the di-IL7Rα booster not only boosted long-term proliferation/survival of the PSMA | PLP1 construct but also retained the key property of selectivity against PLP1(+) cells.

PSMA CAR-Ts boosted by IL7R signaling access the brain and are toxic to mice

Although PSMA is expressed in oligodendrocytes, it is possible that the BBB protects them from engineered immune cells. Because most cancer models involve rapidly growing grafts that create a pro-inflammatory milieu with leaky blood vessels, we sought to test the possibility that CAR-T cells gain access to the CNS in a more realistic model. The expression profile of PSMA is substantially different in mice (figure 4A). For example, mouse kidney expresses especially high levels of PSMA. Thus, it is problematic to model the effect of PSMA CARs in the context of brain permeation in mice. However, PLP1 expression in mice is very similar to humans, providing an opportunity to test the possibility that a CNS-directed CAR-T may access the CNS in a setting of normal, healthy organ and tissue architecture (online supplemental figure S4 A). To do this, we took advantage of the PLP1 CARs used to test the PLP1 scFvs described above, which are also activated by mouse PLP1 in vitro (online supplemental figure S2; figure 4C).

PLP1 CARs were infused into healthy, ungrafted NGS mice via the tail vein, and the mice were monitored over time (figure 4B). PLP1 CAR-Ts did not produce observable adverse symptoms, including weight loss, suggesting that under normal conditions, CAR-Ts are unable to cross the BBB efficiently (figure 4D). However, armored PLP1 CAR-Ts that incorporated di-IL7Rα were toxic, producing weight loss and death in a dose-dependent fashion (figure 4D). They also produced high levels of IFN-γ in mouse blood (figure 4E). Immunohistochemistry confirmed infiltration of T cells into the brains, meninges, and spinal cords of the treated mice (figure 4F; online supplemental figure S4 B-E). These results suggest that CAR-Ts, if sufficiently potent, can access the CNS and cause toxicity.

Previously, we have noted that a blocker directed against the same antigen as the activator, even the same epitope, can in some cases override activation by the antigen. We took advantage of this behavior of the Tmod system to test if a PLP1 blocker could partially overcome a PLP1 activator in a PLP1 | PLP1 Tmod construct. In vitro PLP1 | PLP1 constructs were significantly less toxic to oligodendrocytes compared with PLP1 | ΔPLP1 constructs with a blocker lacking the ITIM signaling domain (online supplemental figure S5 A). PLP1 CAR-Ts were more potent than both Tmod constructs, suggesting that the ΔPLP1 blocker interferes partially with PLP1 CAR activation. This partial interference of PLP1 function was even more pronounced in assays of IFN-γ production (online supplemental figure S5 B).

Having confirmed that the PLP1 | PLP1 Tmod protects PLP1(+) cells in vitro from a PLP1 CAR, we tested these constructs in vivo. As in previous experiments, only the boosted PLP1 CAR induced body-weight loss in mice. Neither the boosted PLP1 | PLP1 nor PLP1 | ΔPLP1 constructs were toxic. However, the boosted PLP1 | PLP1 construct produced significantly lower IFN-γ levels in mouse serum compared with both the PLP1 | ΔPLP1 control and PLP1 CAR-Ts. This result is consistent with partial protection of the signaling-compromised blocker construct. More importantly, the data suggest strong protection by the signaling-proficient PLP1 blocker. Together, the in vitro and in vivo findings support the premise that the Tmod system can protect antigen-positive cells in the brain from inflammatory processes caused by CAR constructs.

Discussion

To deal with target expression in normal, healthy cell types, including neural tissues, some immunotherapy drug developers have opted to either tune down the potency of therapeutics and rely on elevated expression of antigens on tumor cells (eg, MSLN CAR-Ts)19 20 or infuse medicines locally (eg, GD2 CAR-Ts).21 The brain is considered immune privileged, protected not only from infectious agents but also from routine immune surveillance in part by the specialized tight junctions that characterize brain endothelium.22 T cells, for example, are found in healthy human brain tissue at ~100-fold lower frequencies compared with the large majority of other non-immune-related organs and tissues.23 However, there is clear evidence that disruption to BBB function can occur in circumstances of brain metastasis, stroke, and inflammation.24 25 CRS in the absence of brain antigen expression can also lead to CAR-T infiltration in macaque monkeys.26 In humans, CD19 immunotherapy can cause compromise of the BBB and infiltration by T cells.27 28

Brain expression of antigens thus may constitute a significant liability for some targeted therapeutics. The PSMA-targeted radionuclide Pluvicto is likely large enough to avoid significant biodistribution to brain tissue.29 PSMA mRNA is detected at low levels in salivary glands, and dry mouth side effects of Pluvicto have been observed.30 But with more potent agents such as immunotherapies, access to the brain may be sufficient to trigger toxicity, depending on the degree of infiltration. The T-cell engager (TCE) ImDelltra targets DLL-3, a surface protein expressed in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) cells and in healthy cerebellum. A serious form of inflammatory neurotoxicity (ICANS) is a common occurrence, though its relation to brain expression of DLL-3 is not clear.31 Perhaps the most familiar tumor-associated antigen expressed in neural tissues is GD2, a ganglioside that is present in healthy peripheral tissues but highly abundant in certain neurological cancer subtypes. GD2 antibodies are standard treatment for neuroblastoma, and GD2 CAR-Ts have been tested in the clinic for neuroblastoma and glioblastoma.32 In mouse orthotopic models of glioblastoma, infusion of GD2 CAR-Ts can be lethal on their own.33 In human trials, IV-administered GD2 CAR-Ts caused dose-dependent CRS, including ICANS at high rates, leading to study cessation.34 Interestingly, when given intraventricularly, CRS was absent. Only tumor-induced neuroinflammation was induced, suggesting that local administration of CAR-Ts can circumvent otherwise hazardous expression sites of tumor-associated antigens.

Numerous investigational PSMA-targeted immunotherapies have been advanced.35 Prostate cancer may be unusually prone to induce toxicity by immunotherapy; CRS and ICANS are common. A number of clinical trials have ended with DLTs as high as Grade V, including PSMA TCEs,36 a PSCA CAR-T,37 and multiple PSMA CAR-Ts.7 The relation between some of the serious adverse events (SAEs) observed in these trials and off-tumor expression of antigen is not clear. No unequivocal SAE linked to brain PSMA expression has been reported. Perhaps the closest is P-PSMA-101, a PSMA CAR-T that was active in a phase 1 trial but displayed serious toxicity, including 4/14 cases of blurred vision along with the typical ICANS (2/14) and CRS (8/14) (Slovin et al, ASCO 2023). The blurred vision might originate from optic nerve inflammation.

Though no clinical data prove CAR-T cells are activated by antigen in the healthy human brain—no gross infiltration or damage in the brain attributed to drug—the risk may be substantial. It seems likely that, as increasingly potent CAR-T and TCR-T therapies are designed, serious on-target toxicity caused by antigen expression in the brain is inevitable.

In principle, logic-gated engineered cells offer a solution, provided a suitable companion antigen can be found to safeguard the healthy cells. Such secondary antigens, for example, an antigen expressed on normal tissues that triggers an inhibitory receptor to override the CAR activation signal, theoretically provide such a safeguard.38 Several instances of Tmod constructs that selectively target tumor cells while sparing normal tissues have been published, and clinical studies are currently underway in solid tumor indications (eg, study NCT06051695).

We set out to test if CAR-Ts could access an antigen expressed only in the mouse CNS, and to identify an antigen—co-expressed in the human brain with PSMA—that could be targeted by the Tmod NOT gate to protect vital brain constituents if necessary. The PLP1 antigen not only possessed the proper expression profile but also served as an excellent blocker in PSMA | PLP1 constructs in vitro. Notably, the mouse is not a good surrogate model for PSMA-targeted immunotherapies, particularly for the PSMA | PLP1 Tmod construct. Unlike humans, mice express very high PSMA levels in the kidney; additionally, because of gene duplication and divergence that occurred in the higher ape lineage, humans express PSMA in astrocytes, not oligodendrocytes where PLP1 is expressed.39 However, we used the properties of the PLP1 | PLP1 construct, in which the PLP1-directed blocker can override the PLP1 activator, to demonstrate that the Tmod system can protect mice from CAR-Ts that target antigens expressed in brain tissue.

Though the divergent expression patterns of PSMA in mouse and human prevented the use of a mouse model to test PSMA | PLP1 Tmod constructs in vivo, results from tests of a PLP1 CAR suggest that brain expression is indeed a risk associated with CAR-Ts and should be considered when selecting and/or dosing CAR-Ts directed at antigens expressed in healthy brain tissues. The in vivo model used in this study mimics the potential effects of PSMA-directed T cell therapies in prostate cancer, where PSMA is also expressed in oligodendrocytes. While the BBB typically offers protection, activation-induced infiltration of T cells could lead to neurotoxicity in clinical settings. The observed toxicity in our model underscores the importance of incorporating blocker modules, such as the PLP1-specific blockers in the Tmod platform, to prevent unintended damage to healthy brain tissues that express antigens like PSMA.

Methods

Molecular cloning

All CAR and Tmod constructs were cloned using Golden Gate assembly. All gene segments were combined and assembled downstream of a human EF1α promoter contained in a lentiviral expression plasmid. Activators were generated by fusing scFv LBDs to the CD8α hinge, CD8 or CD28 transmembrane domains, and the ICDs of CD28, 4–1BB, and CD3ζ. Blockers were constructed by fusing scFv LBDs to the hinge, transmembrane, and ICDs of LIR-1.

Jurkat (activation and blocking) coculture assays

Jurkat-NFAT luciferase (JNL) cells were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.4% geneticin to maintain reporter expression, splitting by dilution every 2–3 days. They were harvested and transfected with DNA plasmids using the Neon transfection system (Invitrogen) for 100 µL reactions (# MPK10096). The JNL cells were counted, washed with 1× Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), and 2e6 viable cells were resuspended in 120 µL of R buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 2–8 µg of DNA encoding appropriate activator and blocker receptor constructs. Electroporation conditions were set to 3 pulses at 1500V for 10 ms. Immediately after transfection, cells were cultured overnight at 37°C, 5% CO2 in RPMI-containing 20% FBS.

For mRNA expression/titration experiments, HeLa target cells (maintained in EMEM with 10% FBS) were also transfected 1 day before using the 4D nucleofector (Lonza). Cells were counted, washed with 1xPBS, and resuspended to 1.1e7 cells/mL in SE transfection buffer (Lonza). Target antigen mRNA (PSMA, PLP1-Flag, or PLP1) was serially diluted twofold across 15 points in SE transfection buffer in 96-well v-bottom plates. For mRNA blocking experiments, a constant 1000 ng of PSMA mRNA was co-transfected with serially diluted PLP1 mRNA. Target cells were added to each well containing the mRNA, transferred to a 16-well Lonza 4D cuvette at 2 00 000 cells/well, and electroporated according to the manufacturer’s protocol established for HeLa cells. Post-transfection, the cells were immediately placed into MEM growth media containing serum and seeded into rows of 384-well culture plates at a density of 10 000 cells/well. Remaining transfected cells were seeded into separate 96-well plates specifically for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) expression testing. Plates were cultured for approximately 16 hours at 37°C and 5% CO2. To verify expression, 1e5-3e5 washed cells were stained with 10 µg/mL of PSMA-PE (BioLegend #342504), an internally generated anti-PLP1-APC antibody and/or 20 µg/mL monoclonal mouse anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma-Aldrich #3165) for >30 min at 4°C. After incubation, cells stained with anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich #3165) were washed 2x in FACS buffer (1×PBS+1% BSA) and stained with F(ab’)2-goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen #A21237) at 1 µg/mL in FACS buffer for >45 min at 4°C. Finally, cells were washed 2× in FACS buffer, resuspended in ~100 µL FACS buffer, transferred to a round bottom plate, and run on a BD FACS Canto II Flow Cytometer.

For co-culture assays, 10 000 activating and/or blocker receptor-expressing Jurkat-NFAT luciferase cells were combined with the transfected target cells expressing activator and/or blocker antigen as described above in duplicate wells of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 6 hours. Twenty microliters per well of luciferase substrate (BPS Biosciences ONE Step Luciferase Firefly Assay System #60690-3) was added to each well, incubated at room temp for 10 min, and read on a Tecan Spark with 100 ms integration time/well.

Anti-PLP1 binder discovery

Whole brain tissue harvested from 8-week-old WT C57BL/6 mice (Charles River) was emulsified in Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (Thermo #77140) and/or Sigma Adjuvant System (Sigma #S6322) and injected into B6;129-PLP1 tm1Kan/J mice (PLP1 KO mice, Jackson Laboratory) according to a modified Repetitive Immunization at Multiple Sites (RIMMS) schedule that targets the inguinal, axial, brachial, and popliteal lymph nodes for antibody generation. Lymph nodes were harvested and electrofused to P3X.653 myeloma cells to generate hybridomas. 384-well based ELISA screens were completed 12 days after hybridoma fusion to identify mouse IgG expressing hybridomas. Mouse IgG+ secreting hybridomas were then screened using a flow cytometry binding assay with Expi Cho cells (Thermo Fisher) transfected with huPLP1 DNA. In brief, hybridoma supernatants were added into 384 well plates containing 20 000 PLP1 expressing CHO cells, incubated, washed, and anti-PLP1 binders detected with a polyclonal Goat anti-mouse IgG Fc-specific AF 647 conjugate on a LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD). Results were analyzed using FlowJo software to generate MFI values. mRNA was isolated and cDNA generated by barcoded PCR from select mouse anti-PLP1 specific hybridomas. VH and VL sequences were recovered by Nanopore sequencing (Primordium). Selected Fv regions were cloned into CAR and blocker constructs using Golden Gate assembly

Primary T cell generation and characterization

X-VIVO 15 media (Lonza) was supplemented with 1% human AB Serum (GeminiBio). Human PBMCs or CD4/CD8-enriched and CD56-depleted T cells were grown in X-VIVO 15 media supplemented with 300 IU/mL IL-2 (STEMCELL technologies) and stimulated with 1:100 TransAct (Miltenyi) for 24 hours before transduction with virus encoding CAR or Tmod lentivirus. Booster-encoding virus was introduced at the same time into the cell mixture. Transduced cells were cultured in 24-well G-Rex plates (Wilson Wolf) with X-VIVO 15 media supplemented with 1% human serum. Fresh IL-2 (300 IU/mL) was added with media change every 2–3 days during T cell expansion. All T cell cultures were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2.

Expression of CAR or Tmod components was confirmed by flow cytometry 7~8 days post-transduction. CAR was stained with anti-G4S linker antibody. The blocker was stained with anti-Whitlow linker antibody.

T cell acute cytotoxicity assay

Target cells expressing activator antigens, blocker antigens, and RFP by native expression or engineering were used in primary T cell assays. Percentages of CAR-positive or blocker-positive T cells were determined by flow cytometry before coculture.

Adherent target cells were seeded in 384-well flat-bottom culture plates (Greiner Bio-One) at a density of 3000 (HeLa) per well and cultured in 30 µL of X-VIVO 15 media. Approximately 16–18 hours after target cell plating, T cells were serially diluted threefold and suspended in 70 µL of X-VIVO 15 media, then cocultured with target cells at desired E:T ratios for up to ~120 hours. UTD T cells were sometimes added into receptor-positive pools to ensure the receptor-positive percentage and total T cell numbers were similar for all T cell groups in the assay. Whole-well total fluorescence intensity or brightfield total cell area of the coculture were recorded every 4 hours on ImageXpress Micro Confocal imager (Molecule Device Corporation) with a 4×objective. Images were analyzed to quantify live target cells by total pixel area of residual RFP or brightfield signal above background threshold either via MetaXpress Custom Module algorithm or AI-enabled segmentation in In Carta. Plating variability was accounted for by normalizing to area at time=0 per well. Specific killing was quantified as the difference in total fluorescence or brightfield area (Ar) between CAR-T or Tmod wells vs corresponding UTD wells, then normalized to the corresponding UTD wells (% Killing=(Ar[UTD]–Ar[CAR] of Ar[Tmod])/Ar[UTD]).

Oligodendrocytes culturing

Human iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes were purchased from bit.bio (io10285) and cultured following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cryopreserved ioOligodendrocyte-like cells were thawed and plated onto poly-D-lysine and laminin-coated plates, then cultured in complete OM1 medium for 1 day. At 24 hours post-thaw, the media was completely replaced with a complete maturation media (OM2). From day 1 to day 8, half of the media was refreshed every 48 hours with fresh complete OM2 media.

On day 7 post revival, oligodendrocytes were seeded in 384-well flat-bottom culture plates (Greiner Bio-One) at a density of 2000 per well and cultured in 30 µL of complete OM2 media. Approximately 16–18 hours after oligodendrocytes plating, OM2 media was replaced by X-VIVO 15 media. Transduced T cells were serially diluted threefold and suspended in 70 µL of X-VIVO 15 media, then cocultured with oligodendrocytes at desired E:T ratios for up to 48 hours. Whole-well brightfield total cell area of the coculture was recorded every 4 hours on ImageXpress Micro Confocal imager (Molecule Device Corporation) with a 4×objective. Images were analyzed to quantify live target cells by total pixel area of brightfield signal above background threshold via MetaXpress Custom Module algorithm.

Immunofluorescence staining of human iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocyte-like cells were seeded in 96-well plates several days before staining. Cells were fixed in fixation buffer (BioLegend #420801) and permeabilized in BD Phosflow Perm/Wash Buffer I (BD#557885). They were then blocked in 5% BSA (Bioworld# 40220060-2) in MACS buffer with PBS+EDTA (miltenyi #130-091-222). Cells were incubated with purified anti-human PSMA (FOLH1) antibody (BioLegend #342502) at 10 µg/mL in 1% BSA/MACS overnight at 4°C. The following day, the oligodendrocytes were washed 3x with 1x PBS, followed by F(ab’)2 goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen #A21237) staining at 1 µg/mL in 1% BSA/MACS at room temperature for 1 hour. Cells were then washed 3x with 1x PBS and stained with an internally generated anti-PLP1-AF488 antibody at 2 µg/mL in 1% BSA/MACS at room temperature for 1 hour. Cells were finally washed 3x with 1x PBS, stained with DAPI (BD Biosciences# 564907) at 1/2500, and visualized using the ImageXpress Micro4 (Molecular Devices).

In vivo mouse study

For all in vivo studies, female NSG B2m mice (010636, Jackson Labs) 5–6 weeks old were acclimated to the housing environment prior to the initiation of the studies. Details related to the group allocation were not disclosed to Charles Rivers Laboratories. Animals were randomized into groups based on body weight prior to T cell injection. T cells were administered via tail vein (intravenous). Body weights and clinical parameters were monitored twice a week, and daily respectively. Serum was collected every 7 days starting 2–3 days post T cell infusion. Human cytokines in serum samples were measured using CBA human IFN-γ (BD Biosciences). Two independent animal studies were performed, each consisting of n=3 animals per group. IHC analysis of brain and spinal cord tissues was conducted across all animals from both studies (n=6). CD3 staining was performed on one animal per group (n=2). Given the identical dosing regimens and study designs across both studies, the data were pooled for subsequent analysis. No animals were excluded from the analysis. PLP1 | PLP1 Tmod and PLP1 | ΔPLP1 Tmod animal studies were performed with n=3 animals per unboosted group and n=4 for boosted groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. All in vitro data are representative of a minimum of n=2 experimental repeats with 2–4 technical replicates per sample, unless otherwise noted. Where applicable, technical replicates are shown as individual data points, with bars denoting means. Two-way analysis of variance was used to analyze time-dependent differences, while t-tests were used to analyze differences at individual time points.
