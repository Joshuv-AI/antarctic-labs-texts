UDP-glucose and MRS2905 agonist-bound states of the purinergic P2Y14 receptor
Authors: Jonathan F Fay, Joseph Kousouros, Zhan-Guo Gao, Matteo Pavan, Paola Oliva, Asmita Pramanik, Clayton Meyer, Siva Hariprasad Kurma, Wen Xu, Brian Krumm, Kenneth A Jacobson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractP2Y14 receptors respond to uracil-diphosphate-hexose conjugates, yet how this receptor selectively recognizes both uracil-nucleotides and hexose moieties of diverse agonists remains unclear. Here we report the active agonist-bound G protein-bound states of the P2Y14 G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) bound to endogenous agonist uridine diphosphate glucose (UDP-glucose) and 2-thiouridine-5′-O-(α,β-methylene)diphosphate (MRS2905). The cryo-EM structures of the heterotrimeric complexes with 3.19 Å and 3.05 Å global resolution, respectively, with local refinements reaching 2.87 Å and 3.22 Å for the masked receptor region. Our structures reveal a pronounced extracellular facing electronegative vestibule connecting to a smaller nucleotide binding subpocket (~300Å3 volume) that is shielded by extracellular loop 2 (ECL2). A glucose-binding subpocket is spatially delimited by residue V93; mutation to Trp selectively blocks UDP-glucose while permitting MRS2905 and antagonist binding. These findings provide atomic insights into uracil recognition, reveal how the receptor accommodates diverse flexible UDP-sugars, and promise to enable rational drug discovery of therapeutic P2Y14R modulators.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Receptor pharmacology

Introduction

Extracellular nucleotides exert important signaling roles through cell surface purinergic receptors (P2YRs), part of the δ branch of Class A G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)1. There are two P2YR subfamilies, five P2Y1-like (Gq-coupled) and three P2Y12-like (Gi-coupled). These receptors are widespread in the immune, nervous, metabolic, digestive and cardiovascular systems, and their small molecular modulators are compelling targets for the treatment of diverse chronic conditions. Despite the intense medicinal chemical efforts focused on developing therapeutic P2YR ligands, among all the P2YRs, only ligands targeting P2Y12R (antagonists as antithrombotic agents) have yet found broad clinical use.

Nevertheless, ligands targeting P2Y14 have recently been shown to reduce chronic pain in a mouse model of mechanical allodynia, indicating this receptor is a promising target for treating chronic pain2. P2Y14 also shows promise as a therapeutic target for acute kidney injury and allergic asthma3,4, and could be an important target to combat obesity and diabetes5.

Interestingly, P2Y14R, a Gi-coupled receptor, can be activated by endogenous uridine diphosphate (UDP)-sugars, principally UDP-glucose (UDPG)6. Agonists that activate P2Y14R act as danger signals (DAMPs) in the immune system, and potent agonists may ultimately have application in cancer7 and hematopoietic stem cell regeneration8. UDPG is more stable biologically than other P2YR agonists, as it is not a substrate of ecto-nucleotide triphosphate diphosphohydrolases (NTPDases), and is likely released from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell surface9. For these reasons, there have been extensive structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies of P2Y14R agonists and antagonists. These studies have identified one particularly potent and selective agonist, MRS2905, an analogue of UDP10.

Unfortunately, until recently, only two representative agonist and antagonist P2YRs structures have previously been determined: P2Y1 and P2Y1211–13. Clearly, more structures are needed to enable rational drug discovery of more P2YR modulators.

Therefore, to gain new insights into how agonists bind P2Y14R, we set out to determine cryo-EM structures of P2Y14 receptor G protein complexes using two different agonists—the endogenous UDP-glucose (UDPG) and a selective agonist, MRS2905. Our results show how these agonists interact with P2Y14R, and we were able to determine the relative importance of residues identified in the agonist binding pocket through subsequent mutational analysis and competition binding studies. Thus, our results provide a new atomic-level understanding for how UDP-sugar and UDP-like agonists bind to P2Y14R, information that will help facilitate the creation of new more selective therapeutics targeting this important receptor.

Results

Structures of agonist-bound P2Y14R G-protein complexes

We determined active state cryo-EM structures of P2Y14R-bound Gi heterotrimeric signaling complexes (Figs. 1 and S1) bound to a UDP analogue (MRS2905) and its endogenous ligand, UDPG. Our approach used a well-established strategy (co-expression of the P2Y14R with Gβ1 and γ2-Gαi1 fusion containing stabilizing mutations)14–16.

Our cryo-EM P2Y14R-complex structures with MRS2905 and UDPG were obtained at global nominal resolutions of 3.05 Å and 3.19 Å (Fig. 1 and Table 1). We were able to further refine and improve the quality of the ligand binding density and surrounding residues to 3.22 Å and 2.87 Å, respectively, by performing local refinements on the masked region of receptor and G-protein C-terminus (Figs. S1 and S2). Overall, the structures display canonical features of active-state GPCRs, for example outward movement of transmembrane helix (TM) 6 and the α5 C-terminus bound in the cytoplasmic pocket. The conserved extracellular disulfide between extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) and TM3 (between C17245.50 and C943.25) is distinctly present in both maps, and it is within 4 Å from the connecting atom between the phosphate groups in both MRS2905 and UDPG agonist-bound poses. Interestingly, the C94 backbone carbonyl also appears to participate in moderate to weak electrostatic interactions with the 3′ hydroxyl of the ribose moiety present in both agonists.

Agonist recognition by P2Y14R

The clearly resolved ligand binding densities allowed for placement of MRS2905 and UDPG into their corresponding densities (Fig. 1c, f). Both empirically docked ligand poses were further assessed using the GlideEM protocol, a modified version of the Glide docking program that incorporates EM map restraints in its scoring function17. The GlideEM docking results for MRS2905 and UDPG found tight overlapping poses with respect to the pyrimidine ring or the diphosphate group (RMDSMRS2905 1.5 ± 0.4 Å and RMSDUDP 1.4 ± 0.2 Å). In contrast, the terminal glucose moiety of UDPG showed greater variation in docking poses (Fig. S3 and Tables S1 and S2), consistent with somewhat lower local resolution surrounding the glucose moiety (~3.6 Å) compared to the pyrimidine and diphosphate region (~3.1 Å) (Figs. S1 and S2). This variation presumably reflects the greater ensemble variability for the glucose moiety and/or surrounding interactions. Of note, receptor residue D812.64 retained electrostatic interaction with the glucose for all but one of the top-scoring docking poses (Table S3).

Our structures uncovered distinct drug-receptor interactions. The main interactions occur underneath the ECL2 “shield”, in a nucleotide binding subpocket formed by TMs 3, 4, and 5 (comprising residues V993.30, Y1023.33, N1564.60, H1845.36, N1885.40, and F1915.43). The nucleotide pocket connects to a second extracellularly exposed binding pocket (~1390Å3) formed by the inward-facing residues of TMs 1, 2, 3 and 7. Of note, both cryo-EM maps showed slightly less well-resolved local resolution values (3.3–3.5 Å) around this glucose binding pocket compared to the more stable and well-resolved nucleotide binding pocket (3.0–3.3 Å), suggesting somewhat higher local variability around the sugar binding pocket. Of note, similar variability of the glucose subpocket was observed either in the presence or absence of bound glucose.

Our subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations carried out on both ligand-bound structures showed the receptor backbone RMSDs remained stable and in an active state conformation during the 500 ns simulations (See Figs. S4 and S5). Moreover, both the Gαi C-terminus and bound ligands were retained for the simulation duration (G protein backbone RMSD 1.7 ± 0.1 Å for UDPG and 2.6 ± 0.5 Å for MRS2905). The stability of the MRS2905- and UDPG-bound P2Y14 receptors during these simulations is illustrated in Videos V1 and V2. In agreement with the GlideEM docking analysis, the glucose moiety of UDPG displayed greater conformational freedoms as assessed by per-atom RMSF relative to the pyrimidine or diphosphate (see Fig. S3). An anti-conformation of the more stable uracil base was predominantly persistent while the ribose pucker was observed to confer greater flexibility, possibly due to TM4 and ECL2 dynamics observed in the simulations (see Fig. S6 for more details). Upon further investigation of the ribose pose, we found that rigid-body fitting of MD ligand ensemble cluster average pose of both UDPG and MRS2905 with the ribose pucker in either the North or South conformation (see Fig. S6) showed higher cross-correlation with the cryo-EM map ligand density when in the South conformation. This conformation was in agreement with the ligand poses in respective models of agonist-bound P2Y14R presented here.

Nucleotide and diphosphate binding pocket

Analysis of the well-ordered nucleotide binding pocket shows the pyrimidine base docks with a glycosidic bond anti-conformation. Aromatic-aromatic interactions are observed between Y1023.33 and the pyrimidine for both MRS2905 and UDPG (centroid to centroid distance 4.2 and 3.9 Å, respectively). Moreover, Y102 may also participate in hydrogen bonding with the α-phosphate. Additionally, F1915.43 lining the base of this pocket in an edge-edge stack with the uracil ring.

To assess the importance of residues involved with ligand binding we utilized competition binding assays using a fluorescently labeled antagonist, MRS4174 in conjunction with the antagonist PPTN and agonists UDPG and MRS2905 (Fig. 2a). The results show alanine mutation of Y102 (Fig. 2b) impaired agonist competition for both MRS2905 and UDPG, yet antagonist PPTN binding was insensitive, consistent with its dual role in pyrimidine and α-phosphate coordination.

Interestingly, the pyrimidine of MRS2905 sits slightly tilted by about 15o relative to UDPG in the nucleotide binding pocket likely due to hydrophobic interactions with V993.30 (3.6 Å distance) at the 2-thio modification of the uracil ring and rotation of the ring towards this hydrophobic contact. This pose contrasts with the UDP-glucose pose where the uracil 2-carbonyl is rotated closer to N1564.60 (2.4 Å distance) and N3 to S1875.39 (3.9 Å distance).

Ribose puckering was refined to a South ribose conformation on the pseudorotational cycle18 for both agonist-bound poses (see Fig. S6 for more details). Residues H1845.36 and R2536.55 appear to make critical contact with the ribose and diphosphate moieties. H184A retained antagonist binding, but both agonists MRS2905 and UDPG were unable to compete, suggesting the importance of this residue in agonist binding (Fig. 2b).

We also tested the possible role of phosphate-coordinating residue R253, as it is within hydrogen-bonding distance from Y102 and likely makes critical contact with the diphosphate group of both agonists. Our results show that an R253A mutant was unable to bind antagonist, although it was expressed on the cell surface (Fig. 2b, c). These results indicate R253 is critical for antagonist PPTN binding. Additionally, fluorescent agonist MRS418319 was also unable to bind to R253A, further directly confirming the critical role of this residue for agonist binding (Fig. S7). Of note, R2536.55 is highly conserved across the P2Y family as either an arginine or lysine.

N188A5.40 interacting with the uracil 4-carbonyl appeared to have sensitivity towards UDPG and not towards MRS2905, possibly in part due to the sulfur stabilization of the latter (Table 2). However, MRS2690, a synthetic UDPG analogue containing the 2-thio substitution, also displayed a threefold higher Ki value with N188A relative to Wt (Table S4). This suggests the interaction with the uracil 4-carbonyl may be unique to glucose-containing compounds, as N188 only makes strong electrostatic interactions with uracil in the UDPG-bound complex structure, in contrast to long-range weak electrostatic interactions when MRS2905 is bound.

Nucleobase specificity determinants

As mentioned above, F1915.43 positions itself at the base of the nucleotide sub-pocket. Of interest, P2Y12, which binds the larger adenine nucleobase, contains the corresponding residue 5.43 as a cysteine (Fig. 3). Thus, the 5.43 sidechain appears to be likely critical in nucleobase selectivity between these two subtypes. To test this, we mutated 5.43 in P2Y14 to the corresponding residue in P2Y12 (i.e., F191C) and employed a calcium transient mobilization assays in a Gαq5 activity assay system (an engineered Gq–Giα chimera that permits calcium transient readouts for Gi coupled receptors20) using ADP-glucose (ADPG) and UDPG. Our results demonstrate the 191C mutant had a significant gain in potency for ADPG compared to Wt P2Y14 (UDPG:Wt,93W: t(5) = 7.87, p = 0.0005), thus providing compelling evidence that residue 5.43 plays a critical role in nucleobase sensitivity between these two close-related receptors. Specifically, the larger 5.43F side chain appears to help coordinate smaller uracil nucleotides in contrast to the smaller 5.43C residue conferring a permissive environment for adenine binding.

Hexose (glucose) binding subpocket

The final charged amino acid (within 3 Å of the glucose moiety) in the series consisting of H1845.36, Q260 6.62, and Y2566.59, is R2747.32 which when mutated to alanine reduced affinity by 11 to 14-fold for both agonist MRS2905 and UDPG—suggesting its likely involvement in β-phosphate coordination for MRS2905 or weak electrostatic interactions with the glucose for UDPG (Table 2). Mutation of K772.60, lining the bottom of the glucose binding pocket appeared to reduce UDPG ability to compete with antagonist more than MRS2905 (14-fold increased Ki) (Table 2).

Intrigued by this complementarity, we characterized the electrostatic potential of the binding pocket (Fig. 4a, b). Our analysis shows the nucleotide and di-phosphate binding pocket are electropositive, while the upper outer edge of the glucose subpocket appeared to be more electronegative. To further probe the role of this region in ligand recognition, we introduced steric bulk into the sugar-binding cavity to assess its capacity for accommodating structural variation. Specifically, we introduced a Trp residue at V933.30. Competitive antagonist binding studies show this V93W mutant can bind both antagonist PPTN and agonist MRS2905 (devoid of glucose) but the full agonist, UDPG cannot bind (Fig. 4c). Moreover, fluorescent agonist MRS418319, a UDP-glucuronic acid derivative, was also unable to directly bind V93W further confirming the agonist sensitivity of this mutant (Fig. S7). Additionally, we further evaluated the effect of a more conservative substitution (V93A) alongside V93W in our Gαq5 activity assay system. Consistent with our binding data (Fig. 4c), the 93W reduced UDPG potency, whereas the potency of the smaller MRS2905 remained unaffected (Fig.4d). These results together suggest that steric bulk tolerance in the glucose binding region is critical for endogenous agonist UDPG binding.

Discussion

Our investigations (cryo-EM structures with subsequent ligand binding and mutational studies) provide validated ligand binding poses for endogenous agonist UDPG and synthetic agonist MRS2905. Importantly, our results are also consistent with previous SAR studies on P2Y14. The synthetic agonist MRS2905 is noteworthy for both its nanomolar binding affinity and functional potency as well as its prolonged duration of action to raise glucose levels in vivo21. The long in vivo stability is likely a function of the methylene bridge of phosphate moiety in MRS2905, leading to greater resistance to nucleotidase action relative to a diphosphate. Here we will highlight some of the SAR observations with insights gleaned from our structural results.

Our MD analysis suggests mixed populations of ribose puckering are possible (Fig. S6), while the cryo-EM atomic Coulombic potential map favors the South ribose conformation as assessed by ligand-map cross-correlation. The use of methanocarba (bicyclo[3.1.0]hexane) bicyclics to “lock-in” either a North or South pseudoribose conformation was found to be useful for the determination of a North ribose conformational preference resulting in enhanced A3 adenosine receptor potency22. The same approach applied to agonist analogues at P2Y14R, however, was unsuccessful as these ligands possessed no detectable activity10. Revealed from our P2Y14 models the extra bulk of the bicyclic methanocarba scaffold would be incompatible with the proximity to the ribose of TM3 residues A98 and V99 (3.8 Å to the furanose oxygen, Fig. S11).

P2Y14 is unique - it binds to four naturally occurring UDP sugars (UDP-glucose, UDP-galactose, UDP-glucuronic acid and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine) with UDP-glucose being the most potent. Thus, it suggests that the hexose binding pocket can accommodate some degree of structural diversity. Indeed, SAR work suggests replacement of glucose hydroxyls identified only 2-hydroxyl substitutions lead to the most severe reduction in potencies in functional assays6. This position is close to V93, which we show when mutated to a bulky residue is detrimental to UDPG binding (Fig. 4). Intriguingly, large chemical substitutions at the position 5 glucose hydroxyl can retain (or improve) functional ligand potency—consistent with the accommodatingly large extracellularly exposed sugar binding pocket observed in our cryo-EM maps reported here. Examples of extended groups tethered from the glucose moiety include: (1) MRS4183, fluorescent agonist used here, a conjugate of uridine-6’-diphosphoglucuronic acid (UDPGA) and BODIPY19, and (2) various large polyamidoamine dendrimer conjugates (ranging in molecular weight of 9.5 to 147 kDa) coupled to UDPGA23. Our findings are consistent with the receptor binding of very large dendrimer conjugates that can likely extend beyond the hexose binding pocket and into the extracellular milieu.

Previous studies of the P2Y12R found that positively charged residues on TM6, TM7, and EL2 play a role in coordinating the negatively charged phosphate moiety of agonists24.

Likewise, studies on P2Y14R mutagenesis combined with functional assays have also identified charged residues that are likely involved in mediating glucose binding25. We also examined how mutating some of these residues in P2Y14R affects ligand binding. One example is K772.60, which is located in the glucose-binding subpocket. A K77A mutant showed almost no UDPG affinity, and a lessened affinity for MRS2905 (14-fold increased Ki), consistent with previously reported fold changes in potency for UDP vs UDPG. We also find the functional role of K77 in ligand binding appears to extend beyond coordination of the hexose, as reduction in affinity was also observed for the higher affinity (glucose-free) MRS2905 synthetic ligand. Interestingly, K772.60 forms a salt bridge with E2787.36 in our model and also in the recently published P2Y14 structure26, likely indicating these residues are involved in maintaining orthosteric pocket stability (Fig. S12). Indeed, as seen in Fig. 3, the hexose subpocket is largely electronegative above K77, and D81 drives this electrostatic potential in this region. Zhao and colleagues also found replacing D812.64 or E2787.36 with alanine lowered the potency and efficacy of UDPG, while UDP was unaffected. Their findings are consistent with our cryo-EM derived models of UDPG-bound P2Y14R signaling complexes, which show clear density for D81 that exhibits electrostatic interaction with the bound glucose moiety (Fig. S12). Moreover, we also confirm that the D81A mutation similarly reduces UDPG but not MRS2905 potency (Table S5). Interestingly, terminal phosphate coordinating mutant K277A found to reduce MRS2905 binding and not UDPG in the competitive binding assays (Fig. 2) likely exhibits this difference through compensatory stabilization of the glucose with acidic residue D81. Notably, no significant rigid-body movements were observed in the extracellular regions of TM1 and TM7 (key regions of the hexose binding pocket) between our P2Y14R models bound to either MRS2905 (glucose-free) or UDP-glucose, suggesting that glucose binding does not appear to substantially alter the binding pocket.

How does P2Y14R recognize different UDP-sugars? To explore this, we docked several UDP-sugars (UDP-glucuronic acid [UDPGA], UDP-galactose [UDP-Gal], and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine [UDP-GlcNAc]) into our P2Y14R model. Among the tested ligands, UDPG showed the highest potency, followed by UDPGA, UDP-Gal, and with UDP-GlcNAc being the least potent6,25. Docking scores aligned well with published ligand potencies (see Fig. S13), reinforcing confidence in the model.

Both UDPGA and UDP-Gal exhibited strong electrostatic interactions with residue D81, consistent with their higher potency. In contrast, UDP-GlcNAc showed poor shape complementarity, leading to increased strain within the binding pocket and worse docking scores, mirroring its lower biological activity.

Enthalpically, hydrogen bonding with D81, for example, likely plays a key role in anchoring UDP-sugars into the pocket. However, sugars in bulk solvent are entropically favored due to their high conformational flexibility. Our MD simulations suggest that even when sugars do enter the pocket, they may retain some mobility. This residual freedom could offset the entropy loss during binding, but at the cost of affinity.

This concept may help explain why MRS2905, which lacks a glucose moiety, binds more tightly than UDPG (Fig. S13); the glucose unit in UDPG appears more mobile, reducing overall binding affinity. Similarly, UDPG analogue, MRS2690, despite its 2-thio-uridine substitution (similar to the uridine in MRS2905), also shows reduced potency compared to MRS2905 (Table S4), suggesting again that even enhanced binding in the nucleobase subpocket is still insufficient to overcome the energetic penalty of desolvating and confining a flexible sugar.

Taken together, these results suggest that while enthalpic interactions (for example, hydrogen bond(s) of the glucose hydroxyls with D81) help guide UDP-sugars into the binding site, the entropic cost of immobilizing the sugar especially in the hexose sub-pocket likely limits their overall binding affinity compared to more rigid, optimized ligands.

While this manuscript was in review, several new active state P2YR structures have been published26,27. Comparing structures of P2Y1, P2Y2, and P2Y4 receptors, we observe that these nucleotide ligands primarily bind with phosphates bound more deeply into the orthosteric pocket than the nucleobase which is positioned above in the binding pose and thus can participate in aromatic and hydrophobic interactions with residues in the N-terminus binding closer to TM6 and TM7 (Table S6, Figs. S14 and S15)11,12,24. Interestingly, R6.62 makes critical electrostatic contact with the sugar or alpha phosphate. In contrast for P2Y12 and P2Y14 receptors, Q6.62 makes contacts with the phosphate region, and the nucleobase pose is below the phosphates bound under the loop E2 shield and nestled between TM5, TM4, and TM324. Additional comparisons of Gq-coupled P2Y1, P2Y2, and P2Y4 receptors show these receptors bind the α5 C-terminal helix of the G-protein, but fan out in an anticlockwise rotation relative to P2Y1R (when aligned to the G protein heterotrimer). Similar alignments show the opposite for the Gi-coupled P2Y12 and P2Y14 receptors. That is, relative to P2Y1R, they exhibit a clockwise rotation when observed from the extracellular face of the receptor. This can be attributed in part to the tilt of the α5 C-terminal helix of the two different G protein isoforms and how receptor TM5 and TM6 accommodate this G protein engagement (Fig. S16).

Although P2Y14R antagonists are usually described as potential therapeutic agents, there is also a basis for the use of P2Y14R agonists for cancer treatment and immune modulation due to the receptor’s roles in tumor growth, metastasis, and inflammation. Low P2Y14R transcripts correlate with poorer survival in lung cancer7 and ovarian cancer28, highlighting its importance in tumor progression. Its endogenous agonist, UDPG, is relatively stable, allowing it to accumulate extracellularly at levels that can influence disease29. Acting as a DAMP, UDPG can modulate pro-inflammatory pathways that may either promote or suppress tumors depending on the context. For example, UDPG-treated microglia were found to suppress glioma cancer cell proliferation30.

Developing new agonists for many purinergic receptors has faced a simple, but critical problem - they all utilize a nucleotide di/triphosphate backbone. This can be problematic for traditional oral bioavailability as these compounds can be unstable due to ectonucleotidase activity. There is a clear need to identify new unique scaffolds for P2YR ligands to help further understand their biology, mechanisms in disease, and to generate improved compounds for pharmacological intervention. Overall, our results shed light on agonist binding to P2Y14R heterotrimeric signaling complexes providing atomic details of both nucleobase selectivity and diphosphate coordination which can help in the identification of non-nucleotide agonist scaffolds. Moreover, we anticipate the atomic starting template models will enable further exploration and exploitation of the more promiscuous hexose binding region to further improve the selectivity and potency of this important receptor target. One approach that could be employed would be to rigidify the flexible sugar moiety with a more conformationally restrained group which could help reduce any entropic binding penalties. This strategy may likely yield ligands with improved selectivity and potency for P2Y14R, allowing for more effective and direct targeting of this therapeutically relevant receptor.

Methods

Cloning

To generate P2Y14 constructs into pFast plasmid (MBP-TEV-receptor)31 P214-tango was used as the template with forward primer (5′-ctgtattttcagggcggtaccaccATGATTAA CAGCACTAGCACCCAGCCACCAGATG) and P2Y14 reverse primer (5′- aagcttggtaccagg ccggccttattaCAATGTATCGGTACTTTCGAGAGTAGTATTC) were used to generate insert user PCR. Vector was amplified using two primers (ctgtattttcagggcggtaccaccATGCAGGCG TTGAACAT TACCCCAGAACAGTTC and (aagcttggtaccaggccggccttattaATGTCCGCT GTCCAGTGG AGAGTTCTCAGC). Combined purified products were treated with Dpn1 and combined using HiFi assembly (NEB) before transformation into Stbl3 Escherichia coli and sequencing. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on the P2Y14-rluc construct using two step PCR32; using an outside forward primer and reverse mutant primer and its complement to generate 2 PCR products, followed by a subsequent PCR step using the two outside primers and purified PCR products to generate the desired mutant insert which was then subcloned into the Wt vector (see supplementary material for list of primers).

Fluorescent ligand binding assay

The binding of AlexaFluor-488 labeled fluorescent ligand MRS4174 to HEK293 cells stably expressing the wild-type and mutant receptors was performed essentially as previously described19,33. In brief, Lipofectamine 2000 was used for transfection of wild-type and mutant P2Y14 receptors. After 24 h post transfection, cells were then split to 96-well plates and then selected with G418 (1 mg/ml) for 3–4 weeks. Single cell colonies were picked to continue to culture until ready for experiments. Cells were split to 96-well plates and grow for 16 h before incubation for 60 min with fluorescent P2Y14R antagonist MRS4174 at increasing concentrations (0–0.4 μM) for saturation binding experiments. Nonspecific binding was measured in the presence of 10 μM PPTN. For inhibition studies, a fixed concentration of MRS4174 (50 nM) was incubated with HEK293 cells stably expressing wild-type or mutant P2Y14 receptors for 60 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator in the presence of indicated non-fluorescent inhibitor. Cells were washed twice in DPBS to remove unbound ligand and make single cell suspension to run flow cytometry. Fluorescent intensity was measured by CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA). Inhibitory constant values were calculated using the Kd determined from saturation binding and applying the Cheng-Prusoff equation Ki = IC50/(1+ [L]/Kd)34. All experiments were performed at least two times. Differences between Wt and mutant were assessed using a two-tailed Student t-test. See supplementary Table S4 for more details.

Calcium mobilization assay

Human P2Y14 receptors mutants (pcDNA3) and G protein α-subunit (Gαqi5-pcDNA3) were co-transfected transiently into cells using Lipofectamine 2000 as above at a 1:1 ratio. Post transfection (24 h) cells were transferred to clear bottom 96-well black plates. Agonist induced intracellular Ca2+ transients were measured 48 h post transfection using the FLIPR-Tetra system, with a fluorescence excitation set to 485 nm and detected emission at 525 nm in the presence of calcium sensitive fluorophore (Calcium 6 dye). All experiments were performed at least three times. Conformation of active receptors on the cell surface was determined by MRS4174 saturation binding assays, as described above. Reported EC50 values were determined using a 3-parameter logistic function on baseline subtracted normalized data with the hill slope fixed at 1. Differences between Wt and mutant were assessed using a two-tailed Student t-test.

Cell-surface expression studies

The cell-surface expression levels of wild-type P2YR14 and mutants were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Briefly, HEK293T (ATCC CRL-11268) cells were transiently transfected with wild-type P2YR14 and P2Y14 mutant DNA in suspension using TransIT-LT1 (Mirus Bio) and directly plated in poly-D-lysine (Gibco) pre-treated 96-well plates (Corning 3904) at a density of 40–50,000 cells/well. The following day, plates were decanted and fixed with 4% (w/v) formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, HT501128) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed monolayers were then washed twice with PBS and blocked for 60 min in PBS containing 5% (w/v) BSA followed by incubation with anti-Flag (M2) antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804) diluted 1:1000 in the same buffer for >60 min at room temperature. Following two PBS washes a second incubation as above was performed using anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Invitrogen, 31430). After washing five times with PBS, 1-Step Ultra-TMB ELISA substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34028) was added to the wells and plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min prior to reaction termination by the addition of 1 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4). Plates were then read at an absorbance wavelength of 450 nm using BMG Labtech Pherastar FS. Data were analyzed within Jupyter notebook.

Construct, expression, and purification of P2Y14-Gi complex

The P2Y14 pFastBac construct for protein expression was generated from essentially as described above. The baculovirus construct consisted of the hemagglutinin signal peptide and the flag tag; a deca-histidine tag was placed at the N-terminus followed by the maltose binding protein followed by a TEV protease recognition site upstream of the P2Y14 receptor (modified from ref. 31). We also expressed the full length dominant negative Gi (dnGi; containing S47N, G203A, A326S and E245A) heterotrimer fused to Gγ2 (under the P10 promoter) and Gβ1 (using the polyheydrin promoter) on a single pFastBac plasmid in addition to scFv16 plasmid reported previously publications14,35,36. The viruses were generated using Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System (Invitrogen). All three viruses were expressed by infecting sf9 cells at a density of 2–2.5 million cells/mL with virus at a multiplicity of infection ratio of 2.5:1.5:1 for receptor, G-protein heterotrimer, and scFv16. Cells were harvested around 48–96 h post infection once the cell viability was between 60 and 70% and stored at –80 °C until further use. On the day of purification, the cells expressing P2Y14-dnGi complex were thawed on ice in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) at 20 °C for 1 h, prior to homogenization using a Kontes 30 mL Dounce tissue grinder. The cell membranes were collected by centrifugation at 50,000 × g for 30 min using a Type 50.2 Ti rotor (Beckman) and solubilized in pure buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCL, 5% (w/v) glycerol] with 0.6% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG), and 0.06% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) for 4 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 50,000 × g for 1 h using a Type 50.2 Ti rotor (Beckman), the supernatant was applied to a 1 mL Talon IMAC slurry (Clonetech) with 20 mM Imidazole. The resin was collected the next day and washed with 50 column volumes of pure buffer with 0.03% LMNG, 0.003% CHS and 30 mM imidazole. Then eluted with pure buffer containing 250 mM imidazole and 0.01% LMNG, 0.001% CHS. The eluted protein was then concentrated using a Cytiva 100KDa MWCO concentrator, incubated with 5% TEV (w/v) for >24 h, prior to size exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) that was preequilibrated with SEC buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.00075% LMNG, 0.00025 glyco-diosgenin (GDN), 0.00075% CHS, and 50 μM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)]. The peak fraction was concentrated to ~1.5–3 mg/mL prior to cryo-EM grid making.

Cryo-EM data collection and 3D reconstruction

UDP-glucose (UDPG) or MRS2905 bound P2Y14-Gi sample (3.5 µL) was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 Au300 holey carbon grids and flash-frozen in liquid ethane/propane (40.1%/59.9%) mixture using a Vitrobot mark IV (ThemoScientific) set at 4 °C with 100% humidity at a blot time range from 3 to 4.5 s. Images were collected using a 200 keV Talos Artica with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector at a physical pixel size of 0.88 A (for UDP-glucose) or Glacios with a Falcon4i and Selectris Imaging Filter (set to 10 eV) at a physical pixel size of 0.92 (for MRS2905). Movies were semi-automatically recording using SerialEM as previously described37,38. Initial particles from a manually curated data set were selected using cryoSPARC39,40 blob particle picker, and initial two-dimensional (2D) classification yielded templates for subsequent template picking. Template picking was then implemented on the curated set using class-averages and then combined with surviving particles from blob picking and 2D classification. A subset of these particles was used to train Topaz, and the particles were repicked41. The combined curated unique particles from the three different picking strategies were subjected to additional final curation using 2D and/or 3D classification to resolve a final stack of particles that produced a map with resolutions reported in Table 1 (by Fourier shell correlation (FSC) using 0.143A cut off criterion)42. Local refinements were generated within CryoSparc using the global map as input at setting the rotation/shift to 5°/5 Å. Additional sharpening was carried out using deepEMhancer43.

Model building and refinement

P2Y14 models generated from AlphaFold were placed into the EM map density using COOT and Chimera44,45. The G-protein hetero trimer and scFv16 were grafted into this model using 8DZP as the primary template for input. The complex model was then truncated to include the receptor and the bound G subunit C-terminus before refinement using the more well-resolved local resolution cryo-EM map consisting of this masked region of the receptor and G-protein C-terminus. The resultant model then subjected to several iterative rounds of ROSETTA46 and phenix real space refine47. The ligands UDPG and MRS2905 were parametrized using AceDRG restraints preparations using the CCP4 package48,49 and manually placed in the cryo-EM density using COOT before iterative refinements using phenix refine and COOT to generate the final models. Ligand bind poses were further supported by use of GlideEM17 showing similar top-scoring ligand placements in addition to ligand retention within the orthosteric cavity for the duration of the MD simulations (see SI for more details).

Statistics and reproducibility

All competitive whole-cell sorting ligand binding assays were performed in singlicate and repeated in at least three independent experiments (biological replicates, n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Competitive binding assays were first performed on transiently transfected cells to screen mutant phenotypes, then confirmed again using independently generated stable cell lines expressing each mutant. This two-stage validation approach ensured that observed effects were reproducible and not artifacts of transient expression conditions. Only the stable cell line data is shown here as it had higher fidelity. Cell surface expression was performed twice each with two independent transient transfections in duplicate. Calcium transient assays were performed at least 3 independent times in singlicate.

Mutational analysis, differences between wild-type (WT) and mutant receptors were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc pairwise comparisons on pKi values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Significant values identified were then individually reported using results generated from an unpaired two-tailed t-test. For individual mutant comparisons in Figs. 2 and 3, unpaired two-tailed t-tests were performed from the calculated EC50 values.

Molecular dynamics

Each receptor-ligand complex was prepared for MD simulations using the CHARMM-GUI web server50.

At first, the receptor was embedded into a 90 Å × 90 Å 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) lipid bilayer according to the orientation predicted by the Positioning of Proteins in Membrane PPM web server51. Then, the system was solvated in a TIP3P52 box ensuring a minimum distance of 22.5 Å on the Z axis between each protein, or membrane atom and the box border. Finally, the proper number of sodium and chlorine ions was added to the box to neutralize its net charge and reach a physiological salt concentration of 0.154 M. The simulated systems contained ~90,000 atoms (~400 ns/day on the NVIDIA A100-SXM4 devices implemented in the NIH Biowulf cluster [https://hpc.nih.gov] used for this study).

All simulations were carried out through the ACEMD 3.7.3 molecular dynamics engine53. Parameters for protein, lipids, water, and ions were assigned according to the CHARMM36 force field54. Ligand parameters were attributed in agreement with the CGenFF 2.5 force field55.

Initially, 500 steps of energy minimization through the conjugate gradient algorithm were performed to remove clashes and bad contacts within the system. An equilibration simulation was conducted in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) for 40 ns. The pressure was kept at a constant value of 1 atm using a Monte Carlo barostat56. Throughout the equilibration stage, harmonic positional restraints were applied to the protein backbone (k = 1 kcal mol–1 Å–2, t = 20 ns), the sidechains (k = 0.5 kcal∙mol–1 ∙ Å–2, t = 10 ns), and the ligand atoms during the equilibration stage (k = 1 kcal∙mol–1∙Å–2, t = 20 ns) and linearly reduced after a defined amount of time.

The final state of the equilibration simulation was used as the starting point for multiple replicates (n = 3) of productive simulation carried out in the canonical (NVT) ensemble for 500 ns each. As for the equilibration stage, the temperature was maintained around 310 K through a Langevin thermostat57 (damping constant of 0.1 ps–1). The simulations were conducted using an integration timestep of 4 fs, constraining bonds involving hydrogen atoms through the M-SHAKE58 algorithm, and repartitioning59 hydrogen mass to 4.0 au. A 9 Å cutoff and a 7.5 Å switching distance were used for the computation of non-bonded interaction, while the particle mesh Ewald60 method with 1 Å grid spacing was utilized to calculate long-range electrostatic interactions.

Molecular docking

Representative UDP-sugars (UDP-galactose (UDP-Gal), UDP-glucuronic acid (UDPGA), and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), respectively) were docked to the UDPG-bound hP2Y14 structure. Initially, the structure was prepared using the Protein Preparation Workflow tool implemented in the Schrödinger 2023-02 molecular modeling suite. Then, the Receptor Grid Generation tool was used to obtain a suitable grid for molecular docking centered around the UDPG position using default parameters. Ligand structures were retrieved from PubChem and prepared for docking calculations using LigPrep with default parameters. Docking was executed with Glide-SP61 using UDPG as a template and asking for optimal superposition of the maximum common substructure (RMSD tolerance: 1.00 Å). After visual inspection, the top-scoring pose was stored. The bound ligand position was subjected to energy minimization according to the MMFF9462 force field, and the RMSD compared to the initial docking pose was calculated through an in-house RDKit Python script.
