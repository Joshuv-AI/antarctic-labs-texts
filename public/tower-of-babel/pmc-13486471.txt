Microbial desalination using Advenella faeciporci ZF1 as a novel iron-reducing bacterium isolated from oil refinery sludge
Authors: Zarrindokht Emami-Karvani, Giti Emtiazi, Iraj Nahvi, Alimohammad Ahadi, Kianoush Khosravi-Darani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvenella faeciporci strain ZF1, a newly isolated iron-reducing bacterium obtained from Esfahan Oil Refining sludge, exhibited remarkable tolerance to FeCl₃ (up to 20 g/L) and pronounced electrochemical activity under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Phylogenetic identification and physiological characterization were performed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) staining, and magnetotaxis assays. When integrated into a three-chamber microbial desalination cell (MDC), strain ZF1 achieved removal efficiencies of 70.6% for Na+, 78.6% for Ca2+, and 27.5% for Cl− from real refinery brine over 15-days operation period. Under moderate salinity condition (9 g/L NaCl), the MDC generated a maximum voltage of 410 ± 15 mV within 48 h. The corresponding peak power density reached 92 ± 5 mW m− 2 at an external resistance of 100 Ω with a current density of 1.80 ± 0.10 mA m− 2. These results identify A. faeciporci ZF1 as a robust and extremotolerant exoelectrogen with significant potential for integrated desalination and bioremediation of metal-rich industrial wastewater.Keywords: Bioelectricity generation, Electroactive Bacteria in Desalination, Iron-Reducing Bacteria (IRB), Microbial Desalination Cells (MDCs), Microbial fuel cells (MFCs)Subject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

Freshwater scarcity, driven by global population growth, industrial expansion, and climate variability, has intensified the demand for sustainable and energy-efficient desalination technologies. Conventional systems such as reverse osmosis and electrodialysis are widely implemented; however, they energy-intensive and often generate concentrated brine wastes that pose environmental disposal challenges1. In contrast, microbial desalination cells (MDCs) have emerged as an alternative bioelectrochemical technology capable of simultaneously achieving desalination, wastewater treatment, and bioelectricity generation within a single integrated system2,3. MDCs operate by exploiting the metabolic activity of electroactive bacteria, commonly referred exoelectrogens, which oxidize organic substrates at the anode and transfer electrons to an external circuit. The resulting electrochemical potential drives ion migration across selective membranes: cations migrate through a cation exchange membrane (CEM) toward the cathode, while anions pass through an anion exchange membrane (AEM) toward the anode4. The efficiency of ion transport and power generation is therefore governed by both system configuration and the metabolic and electron-transfer capabilities of the anodic microbial community5.

To date, most MDCs studies have relied on a limited group of well-characterized model exoelectrogens, particularly Geobacter sulfurreducens and Shewanella oneidensis, which possess extensively studied extracellular electron transfer (EET) mechanisms6. Despite their effectiveness under controlled conditions, these model strains often exhibit sensitivity to environmental stressors such as high salinity, heavy metals, or complex organics—conditions commonly encountered in industrial wastewaters. Therefore, the identification and application of novel, extremotolerant exoelectrogens remains a critical requirement for improving the robustness and scalability MDC systems7–9.

Recent advances in bioelectrochemical systems have demonstrated the feasibility of integrating heavy metal removal with desalination9, while optimization of membrane configurations continues to improve treatment efficiency10. The challenge of emerging contaminants in industrial wastewaters further underscores the need for robust biocatalysts11.

Industrial anaerobic sludges, particularly those originating from petrochemical and oil refining wastewater treatment facilities, represent highly selective environments enriched in metals, hydrocarbons, and redox-active compounds. Such niches are likely reservoirs of previously underexplored microbial populations with enhanced iron-reducing capacity and physiological adaptability12. However, systematic isolation of iron-reducing bacteria (IRB) from these environments for bioelectrochemical applications remain limited.

Members of the genus Advenella (family Alcaligenaceae, class β-Proteobacteria) have been reported to exhibit resistance to heavy metals and the ability for aromatic compound degradation, suggesting considerable metabolic versatility under harsh environmental conditions13,14. Nevertheless, electrochemical activity and application of Advenella species in MDC have not previously been reported. This knowledge gap limits understanding of the ecological diversity and functional potencial of non-model IRB in bioelectrochemical platforms.

Moreover, while many MDC studies focus on synthetic NaCl solutions, few have applied real industrial brine to assess system performance under complex salinity and ion competition15. Likewise, differential transport efficiencies of key ions (e.g., Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) across membranes and their impact on conductivity, power output, and desalination rate remain insufficiently characterized. Structural and biochemical changes in bacterial cells—such as those induced by iron stress—are also rarely analyzed in connection with MDC function, despite their potential relevance to electron transfer and membrane interaction16. While model exoelectrogens like Geobacter and Shewanella are well studied, industrial extremophiles remain underexplored. We hypothesized that iron-rich refinery sludge harbors novel IRB with potential for MDC application.

In this study, a novel iron-reducing bacterium, Advenella faeciporci strain ZF1, was isolated from activated sludge collected at an oil refining wastewater treatment facility in Esfahan, Iran. This strain exhibited high tolerance to Fe(III), magnetotactic behavior, and intracellular polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) granule accumulation, indicating strong stress adaptive capacity. Its phylogenetic identity, biochemical responses to iron stress, and electrochemical performance were evaluated in a laboratory-scale three-chamber MDC, using both synthetic NaCl solution and real refinery brine as feedwater. Ion removal efficiency, power density, and conductivity reduction were monitored to determine the potential of strain ZF1 as an extremotolerant exoelectrogen for integrated desalination and wastewater treatment.

Materials and methods

Sampling and isolation of iron-reducing bacteria

Anaerobic sludge samples were collected from the primary anaerobic digestion unit of the Esfahan Oil Refining Company wastewater treatment facility (Esfahan, Iran), an environment characterized by elevated concentration of hydrocarbons, sulfides, iron-containing compounds and heavy metals. Samples were collected in sterile 500 mL glass bottles, purged with nitrogen gas to maintain anoxic conditions, sealed with butyl rubber stoppers, and stored at 4 °C prior to processing.

All enrichment and isolation procedures were conducted inside an anaerobic glove box (Coy Laboratory Products, USA) under a N₂/CO₂ (80:20, v/v) atmosphere. For selective enrichment of iron-reducing bacteria (IRB), 5 mL of sludge was inoculated into 100 mL of anaerobically prepared modified DSMZ 579 medium in 120 mL serum bottles. The bottles were sealed with butyl stoppers and incubated statically at 28 °C for 7–10 days. The ATCC 1957 Geobacter enrichment medium (per liter) consisted of 1.5 g NH₄Cl, 0.6 g NaH₂PO₄, 0.1 g KCl, 2.5 g sodium acetate, 2.5 g NaHCO₃, 13.7 g Fe(III) citrate for initial enrichment. The medium was supplemented with 10 mL Wolfe’s mineral solution, 10 mL V-7 vitamin solution, and 0.25 mL Na₂WO₄·2 H₂O (0.1% w/v)17. The pH was adjusted to 6.8–7.0 using sterile 1 M NaOH. To evaluate iron tolerance, Fe(III) citrate was replaced with FeCl₃ at concentrations ranging from 5 to 20 g L⁻¹ in subsequent experiments.

Iron reduction was monitored by quantifying Fe(II) using the o-phenanthroline colorimetric assay. Briefly, 1 mL of culture sample was centrifuged (10,000 x g, 5 min), and 100 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 400 µL of o-phenanthroline solution (1 g L− 1 ) prepared in 10 mM acetate buffer (pH 4.5)18. Absorbance was measured at 512 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan)19. Colonies exhibiting the highest Fe(III)-reducing activity were isolated on Fe(III) citrate agar plates under anaerobic conditions and purified by repeated sub-culturing.

Identification of isolated strain

The isolate exhibiting the highest iron-reducing capacity, designated ZF1, was subjected to morphological, biochemical, and molecular identification. Cell morphology and Gram reaction were determined using standard Gram-staining procedures and observed under a light microscope (Zeiss Axioscope, 1000× magnification, ). Intracellular polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) granule accumulation was assessed in cultures grown under iron stress (20 g L− 1 FeCl₃) using Sudan Black B staining, followed by counterstaining with safranin20,21. Genomic DNA was extracted from overnight-grown cultures at 28 °C in nutrient broth using the boiling lysis method. The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using universal primers 27 F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-TACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’). Each 50 µL PCR reaction contained 1× Taq buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 10 pmol of each primer, and 1.25 U Taq DNA polymerase (Sinaclon, Iran) and approximately 50 ng of template DNA. PCR amplification was performed with initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 55 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products (~ 1.4 kb) were purified and sequenced (Macrogen, South Korea)18. Sequence similarity analysis were performed using the EzBioCloud database and NCBI BLAST databases. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using MEGA version 6.0 software with neighbor-joining algorithm and 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The 16 S rRNA gene sequence of ZF1 strain was submitted in GenBank under accession number KX639817. Similar isolation approaches have successfully identified heavy metal-resistant bacteria from polluted sources22.

FTIR spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to assess biochemical changes in strain ZF1 under iron stress. The bacterium was cultivated on nutrient agar (NA) and modified Geobacter agar containing 20 g/L FeCl₃ and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Biomass was harvested from the agar surface, suspended in deionized water, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The resulting pellet were washed twice with deionized water to remove residual media components and subsequently lyophilized23.

Approximately 2 mg of lyophilized biomass was homogenized with 200 mg of spectroscopic-grade KBr and pressed into transparent pellets. FTIR spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FTIR spectrometer over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, with 32 scans averaged per spectrum. Spectra were baseline-corrected and normalized prior to analysis. Peak assignments were made based on established literature for microbial FTIR spectral interpretation.

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Microbial desalination cell (MDC) setup and operation

MDC construction

A laboratory-scale three-chamber microbial desalination cell (MDC) was constructed using acrylic and glass components. The main glass tank was a 12.8 cm × 6.5 cm × 4 cm cubic chamber. The anode and cathode chambers were separated from the central desalination chamber by a Nafion 117 cation exchange membrane (CEM; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and an A201 anion exchange membrane (AEM; Tokuyama Corporation, Japan) (Fig. 1a). Each membrane sandwiched between two plexi-glass plates and rubber gasketes (Fig. 1b) by 8 stainless steel screws (Fig. 1c). The resulting internal chamber volumes were 160 mL for the anode, 80 mL for the desalination chamber, and 80 mL for the cathode.

Schematic of the three-chamber MDC. The catholite and anolite chambers are separated by cathion & anion exchange membrane. (a) Overall assembly showing chamber separation by CEM and AEM (plexi-glass plates). (b) Detail of membrane assembly with acrylic plates and rubber gaskets (c) Securing mechanism using 8 stainless steel screws.

Carbon cloth electrodes (CC-25, geometric surface area 17.5 cm²) were used as both anode and cathode materials. Electrodes were connected via copper wires through an 5Ω external resistor to complete the circuit. During operation, sodium ion as cation migrated from the desalination chamber to the cathode chamber through the CEM, while chloride ion as anion migrated toward the anode chamber through the AEM.

MDC Media and inoculation

The anode and cathode chambers were supplied with media as described by Cao et al.17. The anolyte solution contained (per liter in deionized water): 1.6 g sodium acetate, 3.4 g K2HPO4. 3H2O, 4.4 g KH2PO4, 1.5 g NH4Cl, 0.1 g CaCl2. 2H2O, 0.1 g MgCl2.6H2O, 0.1 g KCl and 10 ml of trace mineral solution. The catholyte solution contained 16.5 g K3Fe(CN)6, 8.0 g K2HPO4. 3H2O and 9 g KH2PO4 per liter deionized water. The desalination chamber was filled with either real refinery brine collected from Pond 5 of Esfahan Oil Refining company, or synthetic NaCl solutions with concentrations of 9, 15, 20–35 g L− 1. The anode chamber was inoculated with 10 ml of an actively growing culture of Advenella faeciporci strain ZF1.

Electrochemical characterization

Cell voltage (V) was continuously recorded at 10-min interval using a digital multimeter (DEC330FC, China) across a fixed external resistance (R) of 5Ω. Current (I) was then calculated according to the Ohm’s law (I = V/R ), and the power density was calculated as P=I2R, normalized to the projected surface area of the anode electrode.

Polarization and power density curves were generated by varying the external resistance and recording the corresponding steady-state voltage values. Coulombic efficiency (CE) was calculated as the ratio of total coulombs recovered as electrical current to the theoretical maximum coulombs based on complete oxidation of the supplied acetate substrate24,25.

Electrical conductivity and desalination analysis

Electrical conductivity (EC), cell voltage, and current were monitored throughout MDC operation at 10-min intervals. EC measurements were used to assess desalination efficiency in the central chamber. All MDC experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Isolation and iron-reducing capability of strain ZF1

Following anaerobic enrichment using refinery sludge supplemented with FeCl₃ (5–20 g L-1), several bacterial isolates were obtained. Among them, strain ZF1 exhibited the highest Fe(III)-reducing activity and was selected for further investigation. The organism tolerated FeCl₃ concentrations of up to 20 g L⁻¹ without significant inhibition of growth, indicating a high level of resistance to iron stress.

Morphological examination under light microscopy revealed ZF1 to be a Gram-negative, non-sporulating coccus occurring in pairs and short chains (Fig. 2a).

Morphology and Staining of Strain ZF1. 2a: Light microscopy (1000x), Gram stain → Gram-negative cocci. 2b: Sudan Black B stain → PHB granules (black dots), 2c: Magnetotaxis assay → Cell migration in magnetic field.

Such tolerance exceeds that reported for many previously described iron-reducing bacteria and suggests physiological adaptation to metal-rich environments26,27. The maximum absorbance at 512 nm correlated with Fe(II) accumulation, confirming active dissimilatory iron reduction. Notably, ZF1 was capable of growth under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions; however, quantitative Fe(III) reduction was significantly higher under anaerobic conditions (p < 0.01), consistent with its metabolic role as a facultative iron-reducing bacterium.

PHB accumulation and magnetotactic behavior

Intracellular accumulation of polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) was detected in strain ZF1 when cultivated under high iron concentrations, as confirmed by Sudan Black B staining (Fig. 2b). PHB granules were absent or sparsely distributed in cells grown under iron-limited conditions, indicating that PHB synthesis was induced by iron stress.

PHB accumulation is widely recognized as a microbial survival strategy, providing an intracellular carbon and energy reserve under unfavorable conditions28. In the context of bioelectrochemical systems, PHB may also function as a redox buffer, facilitating electron storage and transfer during fluctuating electrochemical conditions.

Strain ZF1 additionally exhibited magnetotactic behavior, forming visible aggregates in response to external magnetic fields (Fig. 2c). This behavior suggests the presence of intracellular iron-containing structures and may contribute to enhanced electron transfer efficiency by promoting spatial organization of redox-active components. Together, PHB accumulation and magnetotaxis reflect a high degree of physiological adaptation to iron-rich environments.

Molecular identification of strain ZF1

Molecular identification of strain ZF1 was conducted via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The 1395 bp sequence shared 97.25% identity with Advenella faeciporci strain M-07 (GenBank accession AB567741), placing it within the Alcaligenaceae family. Phylogenetic reconstruction using MEGA 6.0 with the neighbor-joining method (1,000 bootstrap replicates) confirmed its taxonomic position within the Advenella genus (Fig. 3). The partial 16 S sequence was submitted to GenBank under accession number KX639817, supporting the designation of this isolate as Advenella faeciporci strain ZF1.

The broad absorption band observed at 3,200–3,400 cm⁻¹, corresponding to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, exhibited increased intensity under iron stress, suggesting enhanced hydrogen bonding or changes in protein conformation. Pronounced peaks in the region of 1,650–1,540 cm⁻¹ (amide I and amide II bands) indicated modifications in cellular protein composition, potentially associated with stress-responsive enzymes or extracellular electron transfer components.

Prominent shifts were observed in peaks associated with protein and lipid content. Additionally, changes in the lipid-associated region (2,850–2,950 cm⁻¹) and carbohydrate-associated bands (1,050–1,150 cm⁻¹) were observed, implying membrane restructuring and altered polysaccharide content. These biochemical adjustments are consistent with previously reported microbial strategies for maintaining membrane integrity and redox balance under metal stress conditions.

Fe-Stressed cells displayed intensified amide I (1651 cm⁻¹) and amide II (1543 cm⁻¹) bands, representing protein C = O and N-H vibrations, respectively, suggesting increased protein synthesis or structural remodeling of cell envelope components. Stronger ester C = O stretches (1740 cm⁻¹) and aliphatic C–H bending (1385 cm⁻¹) signals were observed, consistent with elevated PHB content and membrane lipid adaptation. A broad OH stretch band at 3405 cm⁻¹ in iron-exposed cells, absent in controls, is attributed to carboxylic acid–iron coordination, implying enhanced metal binding at the cell surface. Additionally, spectral features at 600 cm⁻¹ (≡ C–H bending) and 490 cm⁻¹ (C–Br stretching) suggest stress-related biosynthesis of unusual secondary metabolites or metal-chelating exopolymers (Table 2).

Shifts in FTIR peaks (e.g., carboxylic acid dimer to monomer OH at 3405 cm⁻¹) indicate Fe-carboxyl bonding in FeCl₃-exposed cells. Collectively, these FTIR data support the hypothesis that iron exposure induces both physiological stress responses (e.g., PHB accumulation) and adaptive biochemical modifications that may enhance redox flexibility and cell integrity under MDC-relevant conditions.

Voltage generation and power output in the MDC

When inoculated into the anode chamber of the three-chamber MDC, strain ZF1 rapidly established electrochemical activity. Under moderate salinity conditions (9 g L⁻¹ NaCl), the cell voltage increased steadily and reached a maximum value of 410 ± 15 mV within 48 h (Fig. 5a). Polarization analysis revealed a maximum power density of 92 ± 5 mW/m² with an external resistance of 100 Ω at a current density of 1.80 ± 0.10 A/m² (Fig. 5b). Coulombic efficiency (CE) for this condition was calculated at 61.2% (Fig. 5c)., indicating efficient electron recovery from acetate oxidation.

The observed polarization behavior reflects typical activation, ohmic, and mass transport losses associated with bioelectrochemical systems. Although internal resistance was not explicitly calculated, the shape of the polarization curve suggests efficient electron transfer and favorable electrode–biofilm interactions. Increasing salinity up to 35 g L− 1 resulted in a gradual decline in voltage output, which can be attributed to osmotic stress on the anodic biofilm and increased ionic resistance (Fig. 5c). Nevertheless, strain ZF1 maintained measurable electrochemical activity even at elevated NaCl concentrations, underscoring its halotolerant characteristics.

Desalination performance and electrical conductivity reduction

Desalination performance was evaluated by monitoring changes in electrical conductivity (EC) and ion concentrations in the central chamber over time. During operation with real refinery brine, the MDC achieved removal efficiencies of 70.6% for Na⁺, 78.6% for Ca²⁺, and 27.5% for Cl⁻ after 15 days (Fig. 6b). These values demonstrate effective ion migration driven by the electrochemical gradient generated by strain ZF1.

As the figure shows, in the desalination of Pond 5, the concentrations of sodium, chloride, and calcium ions decreased by70.63%, 27.47%, and 78.61%, respectively, over 15 days. A similar trend, albeit with a slightly different rate, is observed in the desalination of the 9 g/L brine sample, where sodium, chloride, and calcium levels decreased by 73.95%, 57.74%, and 94.13%, respectively.

The greater removal of sodium and calcium cations could be attributed to the higher efficiency of the Nafion cation exchange membrane (CEM) compared to the Tokuyama anion exchange membrane (AEM).

The superior removal of Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ is attributed to the high cation selectivity of the Nafion® CEM, while the lower Cl⁻ removal reflects the comparatively reduced transport efficiency of the AEM and the presence of competing anions in refinery brine. Statistical analysis (p < 0.01) confirmed significant differences in cation versus anion removal, reinforcing the need to optimize membrane selection and system configuration for balanced ion transport.

Higher removal efficiencies for cations relative to anions are consistent with differences in membrane selectivity, ion mobility, and hydration rate. The comparatively lower Cl⁻ removal may also reflect competitive transport limitations and membrane fouling effects during prolonged operation with real wastewater.

Importantly, all experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the observed trends were highly reproducible, as indicated by the relatively small standard deviations. The desalination performance achieved in this study compares favorably with previously reported MDC systems employing model exoelectrogens, particularly under high-salinity conditions (Table 3).

Implications for bioelectrochemical applications

The combination of physiological flexibility (facultative Fe(III) reduction), biochemical stress responses (PHB synthesis, iron coordination), and adaptive behaviors (magnetotaxis) suggest that Advenella faeciporci ZF1 is well-equipped to function under the complex conditions typical of MDC systems. FTIR data provide compelling evidence for iron-induced remodeling of cell wall proteins and lipid architecture, which may facilitate enhanced electron transfer and resilience to high salinity or oxidative stress.

The observed biochemical adaptations, including protein remodeling, membrane restructuring, PHB accumulation, and magnetotactic behavior, likely contribute to enhanced extracellular electron transfer and biofilm stability. These traits are particularly advantageous for industrial applications, where operational conditions are often variable and harsh. Overall, the MDC inoculated with ZF1 demonstrated robust desalination performance and energy generation, particularly under moderate salinity. While high NaCl concentrations impaired performance metrics, ZF1 remained viable and functional, underscoring its potential for treating diverse saline and metal-rich wastewaters. These findings position A. faeciporci ZF1 as a promising candidate for next-generation bioelectrochemical systems. Table 4 shows a comparison of MDS. Comparative analysis with single-organism MDC studies reveals that A. faeciporci ZF1 exhibits competitive or superior performance under challenging conditions. For instance, Geobacter sulfurreducens-based MDCs typically achieve power densities of 50–80 mW/m² and Na⁺ removal of 55–65% under synthetic brine conditions17,24; 14). Shewanella oneidensis MR-1 systems report maximum voltages of 300–350 mV with Cl⁻ removal below 40% under comparable salinity6. The ZF1 strain achieved 410 mV and 70.6% Na⁺ removal at 9 g/L NaCl, with sustained activity up to 35 g/L NaCl, a salinity level that inhibits most model exoelectrogens. Notably, few studies have demonstrated desalination of real refinery brine; ZF1’s 78.6% Ca²⁺ removal from Pond 5 wastewater significantly exceeds the 45–60% typically reported for synthetic brines15. While G. sulfurreducens remains superior in coulombic efficiency (70–80% vs. ZF1’s 61.2% at moderate salinity), ZF1’s extremotolerance and facultative metabolism offer distinct advantages for industrial applications where sterile conditions cannot be maintained. The results expand the known diversity of exoelectrogenic microorganisms and underscore the potential of non-model iron-reducing bacteria as functional biocatalysts in integrated desalination and wastewater treatment technologies. Future work should explore gene expression patterns during electrochemical operation, long-term biofilm dynamics, and the scalability of ZF1-based systems under continuous-flow conditions. Integration with advanced membrane materials and co-culture strategies could further enhance system efficiency and ion removal selectivity.

Conclusion

This study reports the isolation and characterization of Advenella faeciporci strain ZF1, a previously unexplored iron-reducing bacterium exhibiting high tolerance to iron and salinity and sustained electrochemical activity. The strain demonstrated robust Fe(III) reduction, intracellular PHB accumulation, and magnetotactic behavior, reflecting pronounced physiological adaptation to metal-rich environments. FTIR spectroscopy revealed profound structural and biochemical responses to iron stress, including enhanced protein and lipid content and metal-binding functionalities, which likely contribute to electrochemical resilience.

The demonstration of A. faeciporci ZF1-mediated desalination of real refinery brine carries several environmental implications. First, the ability to treat high-salinity industrial wastewater without external energy input addresses two challenges simultaneously: brine disposal and freshwater recovery. Second, the strain’s tolerance to metal-rich conditions suggests applicability for treatment of other industrial effluents, including tannery wastewater, mine drainage, and produced water from oil and gas operations. Third, the observed PHB accumulation under iron stress indicates potential for bioplastic coproduction during wastewater treatment, aligning with circular economy principles. However, the lower Cl⁻ removal efficiency (27.5%) and sustained columbic efficiency (43.8%) under high-salinity conditions highlight current limitations that must be addressed before field deployment. Future life cycle assessments should quantify the net environmental benefit relative to conventional desalination technologies, accounting for reduced energy consumption but also considering membrane replacement and end-of-life management of the biological catalyst.”

Operation of the ZF1-based MDC generates three primary byproduct streams: (1) spent anolyte containing residual acetate and bacterial biomass, (2) concentrated brine from the desalination chamber requiring further treatment or disposal, and (3) spent catholyte containing ferricyanide or alternative electron acceptors. The spent anolyte, rich in PHB-accumulating ZF1 biomass, could be directed to anaerobic digestion for methane production, with the digestate potentially serving as agricultural fertilizer after pathogen testing. The partially desalinated brine (conductivity reduced by 49.6–58.4%) may require further treatment by reverse osmosis or evaporation ponds depending on local regulations. The catholyte, currently containing K₃Fe(CN)₆, represents a toxicity concern; future iterations should replace ferricyanide with more environmentally benign acceptors such as oxygen-reducing cathodes or biocathodes. Importantly, ZF1 biomass itself is non-pathogenic (Alcaligenaceae family) and does not produce endotoxins, simplifying disposal compared to Gram-negative pathogens. Pilot-scale studies should include mass balance analyses to quantify byproduct volumes and inform management strategies.

When integrated into a three-chamber microbial desalination cell, ZF1 facilitated effective voltage generation, with a peak power density of 92 mW/m² and a Coulombic efficiency exceeding 60% under moderate salinity. The strain enabled substantial removal of Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and Cl⁻ ions from both synthetic and real refinery brines, reducing overall conductivity by up to 58%. The observed desalination efficiencies and power output were comparable to, and in some cases exceeded, those reported for MDCs employing conventional model exoelectrogens, particularly under elevated salinity conditions. Magnetotactic behavior of ZF1 is particularly noteworthy in this study. Magnetotaxis in the genus Advenella has not been previously reported. This finding suggests potential mechanisms for enhanced electrode colonization and electron transfer that warrant further investigation. Future research should explore system optimization via membrane innovation, co-culture strategies, and continuous-flow operation to enhance long-term stability and performance in practical environmental settings.
