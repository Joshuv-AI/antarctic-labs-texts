Algorithm selection for lithium-ion battery ECM parameterization: a comparative study of trust-region, Levenberg-Marquardt, Gauss-Newton, and BFGS for accuracy and physical consistency
Authors: Arvind Yadav, Basem Abu Zneid, Muktha Eti, Byomakesh Dash, A Rameshbabu, Gaganjot Kaur, Jatin Sharma, Gurumurthy Ramaiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a comparative study of four optimization algorithms, namely Gauss-Newton (GN), Levenberg-Marquardt (LM), Trust-Region (TR) and (Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS), for parameter identification of a second-order (2RC) equivalent circuit model of lithium-ion batteries using INR21700-45E cell data over a 10%–100% state-of-charge range. The algorithms are evaluated in terms of fitting accuracy, computational efficiency, and parameter consistency. TR and BFGS achieved lower mean rmse values of 0.922 mV and 0.971 mV, respectively, with values above 0.988 across most SOC levels, while LM produced a mean rmse of 2.441 mV. The GN method showed poor convergence and unreliable parameter estimation under the tested conditions. Model validation under Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization and C/2 discharge tests showed that TR and BFGS maintained voltage prediction errors below 0.022 V and 0.065 V, respectively, whereas LM exhibited larger deviations in dynamic conditions. Computationally, LM was the fastest method (0.244 s), followed by BFGS (0.383 s) and TR (0.655 s). The results indicate that TR and BFGS provide improved estimation accuracy and robustness, while LM offers lower computational cost, providing useful guidance for optimization algorithm selection in battery modeling and battery management system applications.Keywords: Battery modeling, Parameter identification, Trust-region algorithm, Levenberg-Marquardt, Equivalent circuit model, Lithium-ion battery, INR21700-45ESubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

From grid-scale renewable integration to electric vehicles, lithium-ion batteries have emerged as the key technology for contemporary energy storage applications. These systems’ efficacy is largely dependent on accurate battery management, which necessitates precise mathematical depictions of cell behavior. Equivalent Circuit Models (ECMs), one of the modeling techniques, provide a useful trade-off between processing requirements and predicted accuracy, which makes them ideal for real-time battery management applications.

ECM architectures have progressed from simple settings to increasingly complex topologies. In order to depict dynamic voltage behavior, early models used single RC branches (1RC); however, studies have shown that dual-RC structures offer noticeably better performance1,2. Faster electrochemical reactions and slower diffusion phenomena are distinguished by the second-order RC (2RC) model, which successfully captures the multi-timescale character of battery dynamics. Applications needing high-fidelity voltage prediction under changeable operating circumstances particularly benefit from this improved representation.

Accurately determining model parameters, which show intricate dependence on operating conditions and cell state, is a recurring difficulty in ECM implementation. The estimation of parameters for ECM’s, such as the 2RC model, is a fundamental inverse problem in system identification, necessitating robust and efficient numerical strategies. These methodologies can be broadly categorized based on their underlying principles and application contexts. Analytical methods often provide a direct, computational inexpensive solution by deriving parameters from specific operational points or electrochemical impedance spectroscopy data, serving as a critical first estimate or application in linearized subsystems3,4. While useful for initial approximations, these methods exhibit significant parameter errors in 1RC/2RC battery models, particularly in capturing the coupled time constants and state-of-charge dependencies5,6. For high-precision offline estimation, gradient-based algorithms, including the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) are widely employed7; they iteratively minimize the sum of squared errors by leveraging derivative information to achieve rapid local convergence, though their efficacy is often contingent upon a judicious initial guess8.

To circumvent the challenge of local minima and enhance global search capability, evolutionary algorithms, such as Genetic Algorithms and Particle Swarm Optimization, stochastically explore the parameter space, making them particularly valuable for complex, non-convex optimization landscapes despite their inherent computational cost9–12. Recursive online methods, such as the Recursive Least Squares (RLS) and the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), provide a framework for sequential Bayesian inference. As applied in13–15, RLS focuses on tracking time-varying parameters by minimizing a weighted least-squares cost function, while the EKF addresses state estimation in nonlinear systems through local linearization, with both methods recursively updating their solutions as observations stream in.

Many existing studies focus primarily on parameter fitting accuracy without comprehensive analysis of computational efficiency and real-world applicability. The work16 effectively demonstrates the application of the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm for parameter identification in a 2RC battery model, establishing a robust framework validated under specific pulse discharge conditions. However, it is primarily focused on a single optimization algorithm (LM) and does not explore its performance relative to other advanced, second-order optimization techniques. Furthermore, the validation in16 is conducted under a single type of operational profile. The comprehensive study in17provides valuable insights into equivalent circuit model selection and compares the Levenberg-Marquardt and Trust-Region (TR) algorithms for parameter estimation from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data. However, their analysis is confined to the frequency domain and focuses primarily on impedance spectrum fitting accuracy. Recent work18 proposed an enhanced framework for LM-based parameter identification incorporating logarithmic transformation and Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) to address initial guess sensitivity and local minima issues.

In this work, we present a systematic comparative analysis of four iterative numerical algorithms for parameter identification of a 2RC equivalent circuit model. The study follows a structured approach with three main contributions.

First, a statistical initialization strategy is employed, where the initial guess for each model parameter is derived directly from the measured relaxation voltage data. This approach ensures consistent starting points for all algorithms without relying on prior assumptions about cell dynamics, enhancing the reproducibility and fairness of the comparison.

Second, beyond curve fitting accuracy, the extracted optimization coefficients are mapped to physically meaningful circuit parameters (, , , , , ). This allows assessment of not only how well each algorithm fits the data but also whether the identified parameters are physically plausible and interpretable from an electrochemical perspective.

Third, the robustness of each algorithm is validated under multiple operating scenarios, including Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization (HPPC) and constant current (C/2) discharge tests. This dual-profile validation ensures that the identified parameters generalize beyond the fitting dataset and perform reliably under both dynamic pulsed loads and sustained discharge conditions.

The performance of TR, LM, GN, and BFGS algorithms is evaluated across the full SOC range (10%−100%) in terms of fitting accuracy, computational efficiency, parameter plausibility, and validation error. The results provide practical insights for selecting appropriate optimization techniques for both offline calibration and real-time Battery Management System (BMS) applications.

Section "Results and discussion" provides a comprehensive analysis of results, featuring algorithm performance comparisons and model validation under both aggressive pulsed loading and sustained discharge conditions followed by recommendations. The paper concludes with key findings in Section “Conclusion”.

Li-ion cell equivalent circuit model & optimization

Accurate mathematical modeling of Li-ion battery cells is a cornerstone of advanced BMS, enabling precise state estimation, lifespan prediction, and optimal power control. Among the various modeling approaches, ECMs have gained widespread adoption for their favorable balance between computational efficiency and physical representativeness19. The Rint model is the simplest, using a voltage source (OCV) and a single resistor to represent internal resistance; the 1RC model adds a single RC parallel network to capture short-term diffusion and polarization dynamics20. The 2RC model extends this with two RC pairs to better separate fast and slow transient responses, where as the Partnership for a New Generation of Vehicles (PNGV) model includes an extra capacitor to account for the voltage change due to cumulative current, modeling long-term capacitance effects21,22. ECMs represent the cell’s internal electrochemical processes using an electrical circuit composed of voltage sources, resistors, and capacitors, the 2RC ECM for Li-ion battery is represented in Fig. 1.

The model complexity can be tailored to the application requirements. While a simple Rint model (a voltage source in series with a resistor) offers minimal computational load, it fails to capture the dynamic voltage behavior caused by transient diffusion and charge transfer phenomena. The Thevenin or first-order RC model introduces a single parallel RC branch to represent the dominant polarization dynamics, providing a significant improvement. However, for high-fidelity applications, a second-order RC (2RC) model is often employed. This structure effectively decouples the battery’s dynamic response into two distinct time constants: a faster one, typically associated with charge transfer dynamics at the electrode-electrolyte interface, and a slower one, representing solid-state or concentration diffusion processes within the electrodes.

However, the model’s utility depends upon the accurate identification of its internal parameters, the ohmic resistance and the values of the two RC pairs. These parameters are not constants; they vary significantly with the cell’s SOC, temperature, age, and C-rate. This dependency necessitates a robust parameter identification strategy. The process is fundamentally an optimization problem, where the goal is to find the set of model parameters that minimizes the error between the model’s simulated voltage output and the experimentally measured voltage data.

This work investigates and compares the performance of four prominent nonlinear optimization algorithms for this task, the Gauss-Newton method, Levenberg-Marquardt method, Trust-Region method and the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno method. The Trust-Region method operates by defining a region around the current iterate where a model function is trusted to be an adequate representation of the objective function. In contrast, the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm adaptively blends the gradient descent and Gauss-Newton methods to achieve stable convergence. The comparative analysis aims to determine which algorithm provides a more accurate, reliable, and computationally efficient parameter set for the 2RC model, thereby establishing a foundation for a high-performance battery simulation and management framework.

The identification of optimal parameters for ECMs, such as the second-order RC network, constitutes a constrained non-linear optimization problem. The objective is to determine the parameter vector that minimizes the discrepancy between the model’s terminal voltage and experimentally measured voltage data over a sequence of N time steps. The cost function to be minimized is the sum of squared errors (SSE), as formulated in Eq. 8. Crucially, the solution must adhere to physical realizability constraints, which mandate that all resistances and capacitances be positive.

Gauss-Newton algorithm for parameter estimation

The Gauss-Newton algorithm is an iterative method specifically designed for non-linear least squares problems, serving as the foundation for more advanced algorithms like Levenberg-Marquardt. Unlike general optimization methods, GN exploits the special structure of least squares problems by approximating the Hessian matrix using only first-order derivatives, making it computationally efficient for parameter estimation.

BFGS algorithm for parameter estimation

The Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm is a quasi-Newton method that builds an approximate Hessian matrix using gradient information from successive iterations. Unlike Gauss-Newton, BFGS does not assume a least-squares structure, making it applicable to general optimization problems. For circuit parameter estimation, it offers robustness without requiring explicit second derivative calculations.

Trust-region algorithm for parameter estimation

The trust-region method is a powerful iterative optimization algorithm that provides robust convergence properties for non-linear problems. In contrast to line search methods that first choose a direction and then a step length, the trust-region approach selects a step by defining a region around the current iterate where a model function is trusted to accurately approximate the objective function.

Results and discussion

The efficacy of an equivalent circuit model is contingent upon the accurate identification of its parameters and its ability to generalize to unseen operational data. This section is structured into two primary parts to address these critical aspects. At first stage, a comparative analysis of the parameter estimation techniques using GN, LM, TR, and BFGS algorithms is performed. The GN method was initially evaluated; however, it produced unphysical results characterized by negative R2 values and fixed time constants across all SOC levels, indicating poor convergence behavior and an inability to properly identify the 2RC model parameters. Consequently, GN was excluded from the validation analysis. The fitting performance for each algorithm is systematically examined at every 10% SOC level, discussing the convergence behavior, residual errors, and the physical plausibility of the extracted parameters.

At second stage, the model, parametrized with LM, TR and BFGS algorithm is subjected to a multi-stage validation process to assess its predictive fidelity beyond the calibration data. The model’s voltage response is validated against a dynamic HPPC test, which evaluates its performance under high-rate pulsed loads, and a C/2 constant discharge test, which challenges its accuracy over a prolonged, lower-rate discharge profile. Accounting for BMS restrictions on usable capacity, the validation focuses on the practical SOC operating range of 10% to 100% rather than the full theoretical range. This comprehensive validation confirms the model’s utility for simulating real-world battery behaviour.

Comparative analysis of parameter estimation algorithms

The comparative analysis among TR, LM, and BFGS optimization algorithms revealed significant differences in both accuracy and computational efficiency for parameter identification of the 2RC equivalent circuit model.

In Goodness of Fit Analysis, the TR and BFGS algorithms demonstrated superior accuracy compared to LM. TR achieved a mean RMSE of 0.922 mV and BFGS achieved 0.971 mV, while LM yielded 2.441 mV, representing a 62.22% and 60.23% improvement in voltage prediction accuracy for TR and BFGS, respectively. This substantial enhancement is further corroborated by the R2 values, where TR achieved 0.98933 and BFGS achieved 0.98849, compared to 0.92325 for LM, indicating significantly better explanatory power of the battery dynamics. The TR method consistently produced physically plausible parameters across all SOC levels with good accuracy (mean RMSE of 0.922 mV). It may be a suitable choice for offline model development where robustness and interpretability are important. The BFGS method achieved comparable accuracy to TR (mean RMSE of 0.971 mV) with moderate computation time (0.383 s). It offers a possible alternative when a balance between accuracy and computational cost is desired. The LM method demonstrated computational efficiency (0.244 s) in this study. However, under the given initialization and bounds, it exhibited parameter degeneracy at most SOC levels, producing capacitance values that are not physically typical for this cell type. Therefore, caution may be advised when using LM for this application.

For real-time BMS implementation, BFGS may be considered as it maintains parameter plausibility while offering acceptable computational speed. TR, while accurate, may be less suitable for resource-constrained embedded systems due to higher computation time. Conclusion

This study presented a comparative analysis of parameter identification algorithms for a 2RC equivalent circuit model of a lithium-ion INR21700-45E cell. The TR, LM, and BFGS algorithms were evaluated under HPPC and C/2 discharge conditions across the investigated SOC range. The GN method was also evaluated but produced unphysical results (negative R2 values and fixed time constants across all SOC levels); therefore, it was excluded from the final 2RC model validation.

Under the tested conditions, the TR and BFGS methods generally exhibited improved fitting performance, reflected in lower RMSE values and consistently higher R2 metrics, including improved stability in lower SOC regions where estimation challenges were more pronounced. The voltage reconstruction results indicated closer agreement with experimental data for the TR and BFGS-based parameter sets. In addition, the parameter magnitudes obtained using TR and BFGS remained within physically reasonable ranges, whereas the LM approach occasionally produced large capacitance values and degeneracy, suggesting possible numerical conditioning or identifiability limitations within the optimization process.

While the TR algorithm required relatively higher computational effort in the present offline implementation, it demonstrated stable estimation behavior under the considered test profiles. The BFGS method offered a balanced alternative with moderate computational demand and good accuracy. The LM method, although computationally efficient, may require additional constraints or regularization to ensure physically consistent parameter estimates. Overall, the results highlight the importance of algorithm selection in equivalent circuit parameter identification and provide insight into the trade-offs between computational efficiency, fitting accuracy, and parameter interpretability within the 2RC modeling framework.
