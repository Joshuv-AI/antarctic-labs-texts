Introduction

The Ramsey number of a graph
H

R(H)
, is the minimum number
N

K_{N}
, the complete graph on
N

H

Ramsey 1929
showed that

is finite for every graph
H
, and
Erdős and Szekeres 1935
obtained the first explicit upper bound in
Erdős and Szekeres 1935
.
In an influential paper,
Erdős 1947
in
Erdős 1947
proved an exponential lower bound for the case

, which established the long-standing inequalities

(1)

2^{k/2}\leq R(K_{k})\leq 4^{k}.

Since then, the lower bound of
Erdős 1947
has only been improved by a constant factor
[
31
]
.
On the other hand, the upper bound in (

[

A second (much shorter) proof of this result was given in

r

and sufficiently large

Another major open problem in the area is the natural extension of

to the setting of induced subgraphs.
We will write

to denote the following property: for any red/blue colouring of the edges of

(that is, a copy of

We then define

In particular, observe that we have
R_{\mathrm{ind}}(K_{k})=R(K_{k})
for every

, since every copy of
K_{k}
in a graph

is also an induced subgraph of
G
.
For general graphs

Deuber 1975

While none of these works provide an explicit dependency on

Nevertheless,

Note that, if true, this would be best possible, since we have

Using the techniques of

The problem is much harder for non-bipartite

By taking

Fox and Sudakov 2008

To achieve this, they pioneered a versatile approach to Ramsey-type theorems in the setting where one fixed

This approach also gave improved bounds, or alternative proofs for the best-known ones, for other notorious conjectures, like the Erdős–Hajnal problem.

A few years later,

In order to prove (

1.1.
An overview of our approach

We will prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
with the host graph
G
chosen to be the Erdős–Rényi random graph
\mathbb{G}(N,1/2)
, in which each edge of
K_{N}
is included independently at random with probability
1/2
.
Note that this is the same as choosing a (labelled) graph on
N
vertices uniformly at random.
Unlike the earlier approaches in
[
22
,
10
,
19
]
, where the authors developed ingenious deterministic algorithms to embed
H
in a pseudorandom graph
G
, we will instead use a relatively simple vertex-by-vertex embedding strategy, but heavily exploit the fact that the edges of
G
are chosen randomly.
In other words, we will show that, with (extremely) high probability, every
r
-colouring of the edges of
G\sim\mathbb{G}(N,1/2)
contains an induced monochromatic copy of an arbitrary
k
-vertex graph
H
.

One way to approach the problem is via an Erdős–Szekeres-type induction.
In other words, we might generalise to the setting in which we want to find an induced copy of
H_{i}
in colour
i
, apply the induction hypothesis inside a subset
U\subset V(G)
of size
\delta N
to find an induced copy of
H_{i}^{-}
(that is,
H_{i}
minus a vertex) in colour
i
for some
i\in[r]
, and then attempt to use the randomness between
U
and the rest of the vertices to extend this copy of
H_{i}^{-}
to a copy of
H_{i}
.

There are several major obstacles to utilizing such a strategy.
First, and most obviously, there may be no edges of colour
i
between
U
and
V(G)\setminus U
, in which case we have no chance of extending
H_{i}^{-}
to a copy of
H_{i}
in colour
i
.
We can easily deal with this issue, however, by instead using the induction hypothesis to find an induced copy of
H_{i}^{-}\subset G[U]
in colour
i
for
every

i\in[r]
, and then considering the colour that is used most often between
U
and
V(G)\setminus U
.

A second (and more challenging) issue is that we do not get to fix the colouring of the edges inside the set
U
before seeing the (random) edges between
U
and
V(G)\setminus U
.
We will deal with this problem by taking a union bound over all possible colourings of the edges of
G[U]
.
However, this does not come for free: it requires us to prove an extremely strong bound on the probability of failure in each step of the induction.

In order to prove such a strong bound, we must strengthen our induction hypothesis.
Indeed, if we only have one copy of
H_{i}^{-}
, the probability that no vertex of
V(G)\setminus U
extends this fixed copy of
H_{i}^{-}
to an induced copy of
H_{i}
in
G
is roughly
(1-2^{-k})^{N}
, which is much too large to beat the
r^{\delta^{2}N^{2}}
choices in our union bound.
In order to reduce this failure probability, we will need to instead find many copies of
H_{i}^{-}
in
G[U]
that are moreover “well-distributed” in a certain precise sense, which we define in
Section

2
(see
2.2
).
We say that a hypergraph is
(p,R)
-Janson
if its edges are well-distributed in this sense, since this definition is reminiscent of the condition in Janson’s inequality.

There is still, however, one further (and even more critical) obstruction to this approach: we must also handle all colourings of the edges between
U
and
V(G)\setminus U
, and in this case we cannot do so simply by taking a union bound, since there are too many possible colourings.
In order to deal with this more serious obstacle, we will use the method of hypergraph containers.

1.2.
Hypergraph containers

The method of hypergraph containers, which was introduced in 2015 by
Balogh, Morris, and Samotij 2015
and
Saxton and Thomason 2015
, is a flexible technique for controlling the probability that a random set avoids some forbidden substructure (see the surveys
[
5
,
28
]
).
Roughly speaking, the basic container lemma implies that the sets that avoid these substructures are “clustered”, in the sense that they can be covered by a relatively small number of sets that contain only few copies of the forbidden substructures.

The container method has been used by several different sets of authors to prove Ramsey theoretic properties of random graphs.
For example, it was used by
Nenadov and Steger 2016
and
Mousset, Nenadov, and Samotij 2020
to find monochromatic copies of fixed subgraphs in
r
-colourings of sparse random graphs, and by
Conlon, Dellamonica, La Fleur, Rödl, and Schacht 2017
and
Balogh and Samotij 2020
to bound the induced Ramsey numbers of graphs and hypergraphs.

In particular, the authors of
[
12
]
significantly improved the best-known upper bound for the induced Ramsey number of an arbitrary
s
-uniform hypergraph with
k
vertices when
s\geq 3
.
Their result was then improved when
s=2
(that is, for graphs) in
[
3
]
, where the authors introduce a new container lemma with a dramatically improved dependency on the size of the forbidden structure, and apply it to give a bound of the form

(6)

R_{\mathrm{ind}}(H;r)\leq r^{O(rk^{2})}.

However, further improvements of the dependency of the container lemma would not advance the bound beyond (
6
), and we must therefore take a different approach.

Our first significant departure from these earlier applications of the container method is the setting in which we apply the container method.
In
[
3
,
12
,
23
,
24
]
, the authors embed the whole of
H
in a single step, encoding the edge sets of induced copies of
H
using a hypergraph whose vertex set consists of
r
copies of
E(K_{n})
.
In contrast, we will have not just one but
r^{|U|^{2}}
different hypergraphs, one for each of the possible colourings of
G[U]
.
Our hypergraphs will encode the vertex sets (not the edge sets) of
monochromatic
induced copies of
H_{i}^{-}
in
G[U]
and our aim will be to find a
(p,R)
-Janson collection of (also monochromatic and induced) copies of
H_{i}
in an arbitrary
r
-colouring of the edges between
U
and
V(G)\setminus U
.

Although our final goal is to find a
(p,R)
-Janson collection of copies of
H_{i}
, applying the method of containers to find a single copy is already instructive.
This will require introducing a correspondence between independent sets in our hypergraph and pairs of neighbourhoods
\mathrm{N}_{G_{i}}(v)
(where
G_{i}
is the subgraph of
i
-coloured edges) and
\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)
that do not extend any copy of
H_{i}^{-}
.
In this simplified setting, applying a standard hypergraph container lemma is sufficient to conclude that the probability that a vertex does not extend any copies of
H_{i}^{-}
is exponentially small.

However, as our goal is to find a
(p,R)
-Janson collection of copies of
H_{i}
, rather than a single copy, another important distinction between our argument and those in
[
3
,
12
,
23
,
24
]
emerges.
Instead of using the classical container lemma from
[
4
,
30
]
, or the “efficient” container lemma proved in
[
3
]
, we require a strengthening of the container method, proved recently by
Campos and Samotij 2024
.
We will combine this result with an “efficient” container lemma in a novel and surprising way, in order to prove a new container theorem,
4.1
, for sets that are not
(p,R)
-Janson.

1.3.
Containers for sets that are not well-distributed

One of the main challenges of carrying out the outline above is that our induction hypothesis is a
global property
(being
(p,R)
-Janson) of the hypergraph that encodes monochromatic induced copies of
H_{i}
in colour
i
, whereas previous container lemmas are only able to handle
local properties
of the forbidden substructures.

Our most important technical contribution is a novel method that allows one to prove container
sections
that can handle such global properties that depend on local behaviour.
More specifically, we represent a global property as edges (of potentially linear size) in a hypergraph, and decompose the independent sets of this hypergraph into the independent sets of a large collection of
container hypergraphs
that encode all the local obstructions to this global property.
With this, we can separate the local and global obstructions to our property.
There is a vast array of tools at our disposal to deal with the local obstructions, including traditional container theorems.

We illustrate this method by proving a general container
section
for sets that are not
(p,R)
-Janson,
4.1
, which we moreover expect to have further applications.
The proof of this result starts with an application of the aforementioned result of
Campos and Samotij 2024
(see
4.3
), yielding a decomposition of the sets that are not
(p,R)
-Janson into a collection of independent sets in container hypergraphs.

4.3
roughly states that each container hypergraph that does not have a “large local part” has the following property: a random independent set
I
of size
qn
looks very similar to a binomial random set
V_{q}
.
More precisely, all sets
L
that are not edges of the container hypergraph satisfy

(7)

\mathbb{P}\big(L\subset I\big)\approx\mathbb{P}\big(L\subset V_{q}\big).

From (
7
), we will be able to deduce that the random independent set
I
is
(p,R)
-Janson with positive probability, contradicting the fact that independent sets are not
(p,R)
-Janson.
It follows from this that all container hypergraphs have a large local part, and we can then apply another container theorem (
3.4
, also proved in
[
6
]
) to the local part of each container hypergraph to finish the proof.

The next
section
provides key definitions for the rest of the paper and an important reduction.
In particular, we define two events, one encoding the induction hypothesis and the other encoding the failure to contain a
(p,R)
-Janson collection of copies of
H_{i}
.
We then state our key probabilistic
section
, which says that the probability of these two events happening simultaneously is very small, and prove that

follows from it by induction.
In the end of
Section

2
, we discuss the structure of the remainder of the paper.

2.1.
Preliminaries

The first new definition that we need is that of the hypergraph which encodes copies of some graph
F\subset G^{\prime}
that is also induced in

We will use

being the subgraph defined by the edges in some colour

In what follows and in the rest of the paper, we will identify a hypergraph with its edge set.

Definition 2.1
.

For a pair of graphs
F,G
and a subgraph
G^{\prime}\subset G
, define
\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}
to be the hypergraph with
V(\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G})=V(G)
and

\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}=\big\{L\subset V(G):\,F\cong G^{\prime}[L]=G[L]\big\},

that is, each hyperedge of
\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}
corresponds to a copy of
F\subset G^{\prime}
that is induced in
G
.

We will apply the container method to a hypergraph that is closely related to
\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}
, so we emphasise that it has

for its vertex set.
This is in contrast with using
E(G)
for the vertices of the hypergraph, the more common definition in applications of the container method.

Our second definition formalises what it means for a set of edges in a hypergraph to be “well-distributed.”
To talk about subset of edges, we will use measures, assigning zero mass to edges that are not in the subset of interest.
This allows us to refer to the whole hypergraph as having a well-distributed subset of edges without specifying what is that subset, and we say that such hypergraphs are
(p,R)
-Janson.

We call this property
(p,R)
-Janson because, under some extra assumptions, one can apply Janson’s inequality and conclude that a
p
-random subset of the hypergraph’s vertices is an independent set with probability at most
\exp(-R)
.
Even though this is the original motivation for the
section
, we will not require these extra assumptions or use Janson’s inequality.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\cal{G}
be a hypergraph and
p>0
.
For measures
\nu:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we define

(8)

e(\nu)=\sum_{E\in\cal{G}}\nu(E)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{p}(\nu)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V(\cal{G})\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}d_{\nu}(L)^{2}\,p^{-|L|},

where

d_{\nu}(L)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in\cal{G}\\
L\subset E\end{subarray}}\nu(E)

is the degree of the set
L
in the measure
\nu
.
For every
R>0
, we say that
\cal{G}
is
(p,R)
-Janson if there exists a measure
\nu:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

(9)

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)<\frac{e(\nu)^{2}}{R}.

If
R=0
, then every hypergraph is
(p,R)
-Janson.

We mention three very simple, but useful
sections
about this property, for future reference.
The first is so simple that we omit its proof.

Observation 2.3
.

If
\cal{G}
is a
(p,R)
-Janson hypergraph for
p>0
and
R\geq 0
, then for all
p^{\prime}\geq p
and
R^{\prime}\leq R
, it is also
(p^{\prime},R^{\prime})
-Janson.

The other
section
is that we can normalise any measure
\nu
satisfying (
9
) to choose the value it takes in

.

Observation 2.4
.

Let
p,R>0
, and let
\cal{G}
be a hypergraph that is
(p,R)
-Janson.
For every
y>0
, there exists
\nu:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

e(\nu)=y\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{p}(\nu)<\frac{y^{2}}{R}.

Proof.

As
\cal{G}
is
(p,R)
-Janson, there exists
\tilde{\nu}:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\Lambda_{p}(\tilde{\nu})<e(\tilde{\nu})^{2}/R
, which implies that
e(\tilde{\nu})>0
by
\Lambda_{p}(\cdot)\geq 0
.
One can now verify that taking
\nu=y\tilde{\nu}/e(\tilde{\nu})
completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we observe being
(p,R)
-Janson is monotone with respect to taking subhypergraphs.

Observation 2.5
.

Let

and
p>0
, and let
\cal{G}^{\prime},\cal{G}
be hypergraphs satisfying
\cal{G}^{\prime}\subset\cal{G}

If
\cal{G}^{\prime}
is
(p,R)
-Janson, then

-Janson.

The observation immediately follows from the fact that any

, by assigning measure zero to every

∎

For all

We now define the event that corresponds to the failure of the induction step,

Definition 2.7

Now, we can state the result from which we will deduce

Lemma 2.8

2.3.
Structure of the paper

The bulk of the paper is concerned with proving
2.8
.
However, before we proceed to the proof of
2.8
, we will need some tools.

First, we will have two expository
sections
that contain many of the ideas that we use later, but in simpler settings.
In
Section

3
, this simpler setting corresponds to showing that the probability that a vertex extends no copy of
H_{i}^{-}
in a fixed colour
i\in[r]
is exponentially small.
This section shows how to map neighbourhoods that fail to extend a copy of
H_{i}^{-}
to independent sets in a certain hypergraph
\cal{H}
, which will illustrate how the method of hypergraph containers can be applied to this problem.
It is also where we explain how our induction hypothesis, the event
\cal{E}(\mathbf{s})
, yields a supersaturation result for our application(s) of containers.

In
Section

4
, we present the proof of a container theorem for “sets that are not
(p,R)
-Janson,”
4.6
.
While this result is not sufficiently strong to prove
2.8
, its proof already presents the general argument that we will later adapt to prove the
section
that we do use in the proof of
2.8
.
We see that argument as a general method to reduce a problem about sets avoiding a global property to another problem, this one where the sets only need to avoid a local property.
In this particular instance, we deal with this local property by using a standard hypergraph container lemma.

In
Section

5
, we prove that adding a vertex to
U
turns
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[U]
, previously a
(p,R)
-Janson hypergraph, into a

-Janson hypergraph with probability

for some constant

that only depends on

.
The proof of this statement mirrors the proof in
Section

3
, but replacing the hypergraph container lemma there by the (yet unproven)

, the container theorem tailored to our application.
We postpone the proof of
5.4
to the last section since it is the most technical part of the paper.

In
Section

6
, we prove
2.8
in two stages.
The first and easier stage uses a double counting argument to show that if
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[S]
is
(p,\delta pv(G))
-Janson for all subsets

, with
|S|\geq\delta^{2/3}N
, then
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}
is
(p,pv(G))
-Janson.
The second stage proves

by induction on the Janson parameter of the family
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[U]
.
To prove the inductive step, we use

to show that the probability one cannot increase this Janson parameter by adding a vertex to

is very small.
This is also the part where we take a union bound over colourings of

, which requires care and ends up being somewhat technical.

The last section,
Section

7
, contains the proof of
5.4
.
As we mentioned before, this proof follows the blueprint in
Section

4
, but there are extra technicalities, coming from different sources.
One is that we want to combine the family of copies of
H_{i}
containing
v
with the copies that are already in

, which does not contain
v
.
The second is that the hypergraph
\cal{H}
that we use to deal with the extensions, defined in
Section

3
, is not the same as
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}
, the hypergraph appearing in the event whose probability we must bound.
Related to this is the fact that the hypergraph defined by

corresponds to copies of
H_{i}^{-}
, and we are interested in copies of
H_{i}
.
These complications make the proof more technical, but it turns out the approach we developed in
Section

4
is sufficiently flexible to deal with them.

3.
Warm-up to the extension lemma

This
section
gives an essentially complete proof of a weak “extension

”,

Very roughly, this result bounds the probability that a fixed vertex of

fails to complete any copy of a graph

We will describe the setting for this result in detail in

Afterwards, we state the main result of this

and provide a (still informal) overview of the argument to prove it.
Of central importance in the proof is a hypergraph container

The most significant of these is that of the auxiliary hypergraph

, defined in

As we will later see, each edge of this auxiliary hypergraph represents one way to extend, using a fixed vertex

, an (induced) copy of

to a copy of

.

then fulfils a crucial requirement to apply the container method, establishing that independent sets in

correspond to pairs of graphs

in which the neighbourhood of

in a fixed set

3.1.
Extension lemmas

The setting of

is that
\tilde{G}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{G}
are

-vertex graphs such that

and
\mathfrak{I}_{F^{-},\tilde{G}^{\prime},\tilde{G}}[W]

Let

and let

be the random graph

, conditioned on

.
The

bounds the probability that

contains a subgraph

with the following properties:

(a)

,

(b)

the vertex

does not have small degree in

, and

(c)

fails to extend every copy of

to an induced

in

.

In our application,

will be a set of vertices for which the colouring

is fixed,

will be a vertex not in

such that

and

In this setting, we could use this

to conclude that it is extremely likely that

extends an

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

with

,

Further let

and

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,G^{\prime}\subset G\\
:\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}G^{\prime}[U]=\tilde{G}^{\prime},\penalty\ d_{G^{\prime}}(v)\geq m/8\\
\text{ and }\penalty\ \mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}=\emptyset\end{array}\ \middle|\ G[U]=\tilde{G}\right)\leq 2\exp\big(-2^{-7}m\big),

3.2.
The auxiliary hypergraph

We want to bound the probability in (
21
) using the method of hypergraph containers.
To accomplish that, the first step is to represent the graphs
G^{\prime}\subset G
such that
G^{\prime}[U]=\tilde{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}=\emptyset
as independent sets in an auxiliary hypergraph
\cal{H}
.
This auxiliary hypergraph will be a function of
\tilde{G}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{G}
only, which crucially means that
\cal{H}
does not depend on the edges of
G
(or
G^{\prime}\subset G
) between
v
and
U
.

Define
w\in V(F)\setminus V(F^{-})
and note that the only randomness in the event inside the probability in (
21
) comes from the edges of
G
between
U
and
v
, since we condition on the event
\big\{G[U]=\tilde{G}\big\}
.
Our goal is to have each edge of
\cal{H}
correspond to one way of adding
v
to

and extend a copy of
F^{-}
in
\tilde{G}^{\prime}
, which is also induced in
\tilde{G}
, to a (still induced) copy of

.
To be precise, for each edge
L\in\mathfrak{I}_{F^{-},\tilde{G}^{\prime},\tilde{G}}
, fix a bijection
\phi_{L}:L\to V(F^{-})
such that
F^{-}\cong_{\phi_{L}}\tilde{G}^{\prime}[L]=\tilde{G}[L]
.
Now define
\cal{H}
to be the hypergraph with vertex set
U\times\{0,1\}
and edge set

(22)

\cal{H}=\Big\{E_{L}:L\in\mathfrak{I}_{F^{-},\tilde{G}^{\prime},\tilde{G}}\Big\},

where

E=E_{L}=\bigg\{\Big(u,\mathds{1}\big[\phi_{L}(u)\in\mathrm{N}_{F}(w)\big]\Big):u\in L\bigg\},

for every
L\in\mathfrak{I}_{F^{-},\tilde{G}^{\prime},\tilde{G}}
.
In words, the vertex set of
\cal{H}
is two copies of

, corresponding to the neighbours and non-neighbours of

in
F
, and its edge set has the following property: if

for every

and

for every

, then

induces a copy of
F
in the pair
(G^{\prime},G)
.

To formalise that independent sets in
\cal{H}
correspond to pairs of graphs
(G^{\prime},G)
of our interest, denote the non-neighbours of

by

(23)

\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)^{\mathsf{c}}=U\setminus\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)

and, for each

and

, let

A^{(i)}=\big\{u\in U:(u,i)\in A\big\}.

We repeat the following property, previously registered without these definitions, for future reference.

Observation 3.2
.

Let
G^{\prime}
and
G
be graphs satisfying

,
G^{\prime}[U]=\tilde{G}^{\prime}
and

If

and

, then we can extend

to a copy of

induced in

, i.e.

E^{(0)}\subset\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)^{\mathsf{c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E^{(1)}\subset\mathrm{N}_{G^{\prime}}(v).

The most useful consequence of this fact, which allows applying the method of hypergraph containers, is more conveniently stated with some notation.
Let

(24)

\Gamma_{G}=\left\{G^{\prime}\subset G:\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}G^{\prime}[U]=\tilde{G}^{\prime},\penalty\ d_{G^{\prime}}(v)\geq m/8\\
\text{and }\mathfrak{I}_{F,G^{\prime},G}=\emptyset\end{array}\right\}

be the collection of
G^{\prime}
in the probability of (

).
To index vertex subsets of
\cal{H}
by graphs
G^{\prime}
and

, further let

\iota(G^{\prime},G)=\big\{(u,0):u\in\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)^{\mathsf{c}}\big\}\cup\big\{(u,1):u\in\mathrm{N}_{G^{\prime}}(v)\big\},

and note that if

, then

(26)

I^{(0)}=\mathrm{N}_{G}(v)^{\mathsf{c}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I^{(1)}=\mathrm{N}_{G^{\prime}}(v).

Before stating
3.3
, recall that, in the probability of (
21
), we have conditioned the distribution of
G\sim\mathbb{G}(m+1,1/2)
on
\big\{G[U]=\tilde{G}\big\}
.

Observation 3.3
.

If
G[U]=\tilde{G}
and
G^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{G}
, then
\iota(G^{\prime},G)
is an independent set in

Proof.

Let

, and suppose by contradiction that

By (

and

(p,2^{-9}r^{-1}\delta p\,v(G))
-Janson for all

It is crucial that the original bad event

is contained in the variant

, which we now prove with a simple double counting argument.
Informally,

Lemma 6.2
.

For every

and graphs

,

\cal{B}(\mathbf{H})\subset\cal{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{H}),

where
\mathbf{H}=(H_{i})_{i\in[r]}

As we need to work with measures in the Janson property, the following

, albeit a trivial consequence of the definitions, will be useful when proving

Observation 6.3
.

For all

and hypergraphs
\cal{G}
, if
\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{s}:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfy

\vartheta=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\vartheta_{i},

for all

.

For completeness, we provide the proof of

in Appendix

.
We can now prove

.

Proof of
6.2
.

Assume that
G\not\in\cal{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{H})
and let
V=V(G)
and
N=|V|
.
Let

be an arbitrary
r
-colouring of the edges of
G
, and observe that, by
6.1
, for every
S\subset V
with
|S|\geq\delta^{2/3}N
there exists
j\in[r]
such that
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{j},G_{j},G}[S]
is
(p,2^{-9}r^{-1}\delta pN)
-Janson.
Thus, if for each
j\in[r]

\mathbf{V}_{j}=\Big\{S\subset V:|S|=\delta^{2/3}N\text{ and }\mathfrak{I}_{H_{j},G_{j},G}[S]\text{ is }(p,2^{-9}r^{-1}\delta pN)\text{-Janson}\Big\}

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{V}_{i}=\binom{V}{\delta^{2/3}N}

and hence there exists
i\in[r]
such that

(80)

|\mathbf{V}_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{r}\binom{N}{\delta^{2/3}N},

so fix such an

For each

, as
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[S]
is
(p,2^{-9}r^{-1}\delta pN)
-Janson by assumption, there exists
\nu_{S}:\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[S]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

(81)

by

.
Now, define
\nu:\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\nu=\sum_{S\in\mathbf{V}_{i}}\nu_{S}

and note that if

(82)

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)<\frac{e(\nu)^{2}}{pN},

then
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}
is
(p,pN)
-Janson and therefore

by definition.

Recall that
V=V(\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G})
and also that, by definition,

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}d_{\nu}(L)^{2}p^{-|L|}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\Big(\sum_{S\in\mathbf{V}_{i}}d_{\nu_{S}}(L)\Big)^{2}p^{-|L|}

Further observe that since

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\Big(\sum_{S\in\mathbf{T}_{L}}d_{\nu_{S}}(L)\Big)^{2}p^{-|L|}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}|\mathbf{T}_{L}|\sum_{S\in\mathbf{T}_{L}}d_{\nu_{S}}(L)^{2}p^{-|L|},

where the last step holds by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Now, note that, as we are only concerned with

|\mathbf{T}_{L}|\leq\binom{N-2}{\delta^{2/3}N-2}\leq\delta^{4/3}\binom{N}{\delta^{2/3}N}\leq r\delta^{4/3}|\mathbf{V}_{i}|,

where we used (
80

Substituting this into (

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)\leq r\delta^{4/3}|\mathbf{V}_{i}|\sum_{S\in\mathbf{V}_{i}}\Lambda_{p}(\nu_{S})<r\delta^{4/3}|\mathbf{V}_{i}|^{2}\frac{2^{9}r}{\delta pN}<2^{9}r^{2}\delta^{1/3}\frac{|\mathbf{V}_{i}|^{2}}{pN}.

We complete the proof by noting that, since

for all

by (

that

which, replaced in (

),

\Lambda_{p}(\nu)<2^{9}r^{2}\delta^{1/3}\frac{e(\nu)^{2}}{pN}<\frac{e(\nu)^{2}}{pN}

where the last inequality is due to our choice of

.

Therefore, the hypergraph
\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}
is
(p,pN)
-Janson, which implies that
G\not\in\cal{B}(\mathbf{H})
since the colouring

was arbitrary.
∎

Fix

Next fix graphs

Further fix

Definition 6.4

The collection

, and how we will show that it exists, fix

To find this

-Janson.
We then add to this set, one by one, vertices that increase

As we add

Lemma 6.5

For all

Fix an arbitrary

, take

and, for the remaining

, let

.
This choice satisfies

∎

Lemma 6.6
.

For all
G\in\cal{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{H})\cap\cal{E}(\mathbf{s})
and all

\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[U]

(p,R_{i})
-Janson, and

\mathfrak{I}_{H_{i},G_{i},G}[U\cup\{v\}]

We first claim that there exists a choice of

.
Indeed, let

be an arbitrary set of size

, and note that any hypergraph, and in particular

∎

6.4.
The proof

Having established that there is a

satisfying the requirements of

and the Chernoff bound with a pigeonhole argument to find many vertices

Observation 6.7
.

Let
G\sim\mathbb{G}(N,1/2)
, let

\mathbb{P}(|S^{\prime}|\leq|S|/4)\leq\exp(-2^{-6}|U|\/|S|).

For a vertex

\mathbb{P}(|S^{\prime}|\leq|S|/4)\leq 2^{|S|}\exp\bigg(-\frac{|S|}{2}\,\frac{|U|}{16}\bigg)\leq\exp(-2^{-6}|U||S|),

∎

With all of the above, the argument will then proceed by fixing sets

\Lambda_{p}(\vartheta)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L^{\prime}\subset\pi(V)\\
|L^{\prime}|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\,\sum_{L\in\overset{\leftarrow}{\pi}(L^{\prime})}d_{\lambda}(L)^{2}p^{-|L|}\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}d_{\lambda}(L)^{2}p^{-|L|}=\Lambda_{p}(\lambda)

where the last step is the definition, and the proof is complete.
∎

C.2.
General properties

Now, we prove
7.5
, which, despite its length, is as simple as restricting the original measure to another that zeroes the mass of edges containing high degree vertices.

See
7.5

Proof.

Observe that taking
W=V(\cal{G})
, we conclude that
\cal{G}=\cal{G}[W]
is
(p,R)
-Janson by assumption.
Therefore, we can apply
2.4
to obtain
\mu:\cal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

(152)

e(\mu)=\sqrt{R}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{p}(\mu)<\frac{e(\mu)^{2}}{R}.

Take such a
\mu
minimizing
\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}
, and assume by contradiction that

(153)

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}>\frac{2s^{2}e(\mu)^{2}}{\beta v(\cal{G})}.

Now, take

(154)

W=\{v\in V(\cal{G}):d_{\mu}(v)\leq s\,e(\mu)/(\beta v(\cal{G}))\}

observe that it satisfies
|W|\geq(1-\beta)v(\cal{G})
since
\cal{G}
is
s
-uniform, and therefore
G[W]
is
(p,R)
-Janson by assumption.
We conclude that there is a measure
\mu^{\prime}:\cal{G}[W]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
which satisfies

(155)

\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime})<\frac{e(\mu^{\prime})^{2}}{R}\qquad\text{and}\qquad e(\mu^{\prime})=\sqrt{R},

again by
2.4
applied with
y=\sqrt{R}>0
.
Moreover, we claim that setting

\mu^{\prime\prime}=(1-\tau)\mu+\tau\mu^{\prime},

for a suitable
0<\tau\leq 1
, also yields a measure satisfying

(156)

\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime\prime})<\frac{e(\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{R},\qquad e(\mu^{\prime\prime})=\sqrt{R}

and

(157)

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}(v)^{2}<\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}.

If
\mu^{\prime\prime}
satisfies both (
156
) and (
157
), it contradicts the minimality of our original choice of
\mu
.

We start the proof of our claims by showing that (
156
) holds.
The equality
e(\mu^{\prime\prime})=\sqrt{R}
follows by linearity of
e(\cdot)
and the fact that both
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
have edge measure equal to
\sqrt{R}
.
Expand
\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime\prime})
as

(158)

\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime\prime})=(1-\tau)^{2}\Lambda_{p}(\mu)+2\tau(1-\tau)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V(\cal{G})\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}d_{\mu}(L)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(L)p^{-|L|}+\tau^{2}\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime}).

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the second term in (
158
), we obtain

(159)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V(\cal{G})\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{\mu}(L)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(L)}{p^{|L|}}\leq\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V(\cal{G})\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{\mu}(L)^{2}}{p^{|L|}}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}L\subset V(\cal{G})\\
|L|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{\mu^{\prime}}(L)^{2}}{p^{|L|}}\bigg)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\Lambda_{p}(\mu)\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime})}.

Replacing (
159
) back in (
158
) and simplifying yields

\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime\prime})\leq(1-\tau)^{2}\Lambda_{p}(\mu)+2\tau(1-\tau)\sqrt{\Lambda_{p}(\mu)\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime})}+\tau^{2}\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime})=\big((1-\tau)\Lambda_{p}(\mu)^{1/2}+\tau\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime})^{1/2}\big)^{2},

which, by (
152
) and (
155
), establishes (
156
):

\Lambda_{p}(\mu^{\prime\prime})<\frac{1}{R}\big((1-\tau)e(\mu)+\tau e(\mu^{\prime})\big)^{2}=\frac{e(\mu^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{R}.

Towards establishing (
157
), we expand

(160)

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}(v)^{2}=(1-\tau)^{2}\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}+2\tau(1-\tau)\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)+\tau^{2}\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)^{2},

an expression whose terms we will bound separately.
Recall that
d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)=0
for
v\not\in W
, so

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)=\sum_{v\in W}d_{\mu}(v)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)

but now, (
154
) implies that

(161)

\sum_{v\in W}d_{\mu}(v)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)\leq\frac{s\,e(\mu)}{\beta v(\cal{G})}\sum_{v\in W}d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)=\frac{s^{2}e(\mu)^{2}}{\beta v(\cal{G})}

since
\cal{G}
is
s
-uniform and
e(\mu^{\prime})=e(\mu)
.
Using (
153
) in (
161
), we obtain, for the second term,

(162)

\sum_{v\in W}d_{\mu}(v)d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)<\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}.

The
s
-uniformity of
\cal{G}
and
e(\mu)=e(\mu^{\prime})
also imply an easy bound on the third term in (
160
):

(163)

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)^{2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime}}(v)\bigg)^{2}=\big(e(\mu)s\big)^{2}\leq\dfrac{\beta v(\cal{G})}{2}\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}\leq\beta v(\cal{G})\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2},

where the first inequality holds because
d_{\mu}^{\prime}
is always non-negative, and the second is (
153
).

We can now replace (
162
) and (
163
) in (
160
) and use that
\tau
is positive to obtain, after simplification, that

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}(v)^{2}<(1-\tau+\beta v(\cal{G})\tau^{2})\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}.

Choosing
\tau
to satisfy

0<\tau<\min\Big\{1,\frac{1}{\beta v(\cal{G})}\Big\}

results in

\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu^{\prime\prime}}(v)^{2}<\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}

which contradicts the fact that
\mu
minimises
\sum_{v\in V(\cal{G})}d_{\mu}(v)^{2}
and completes the proof.
∎
