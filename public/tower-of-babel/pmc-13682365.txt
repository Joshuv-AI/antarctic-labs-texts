Real-world thrombotic and survival outcomes with ESAs in cancer-related anemia
Authors: Rafi Aibani, Jennifer E. Collins, Daniyal Khan, Salah Safi, Amir Kamran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Cancer-related anemia is a common and clinically significant complication affecting a substantial proportion of patients with both solid and hematologic malignancies. It has been reported in ∼40% of patients with nonmyeloid cancers and in 30% to 80% of those with myeloid malignancies, with etiologies including treatment-related myelosuppression, bone marrow infiltration, renal dysfunction with impaired erythropoietin production, and ongoing blood loss or hemolysis.1, 2, 3 Anemia in patients with cancer is associated with fatigue, impaired functional status, and reduced quality of life, and may negatively affect the ability to deliver optimal anticancer therapy.3

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) are synthetic analogs of endogenous erythropoietin that promote red blood cell production and have widely been used in the management of cancer-related anemia. Initially approved for anemia associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD), their indication was later expanded to include chemotherapy-induced anemia (CIA) after evidence demonstrating improvements in hemoglobin levels and reductions in transfusion requirements.2,4 These benefits established ESAs as an important supportive care option for selected patients undergoing myelosuppressive cancer therapy.1,2

Despite their hematologic efficacy, the safety of ESAs in oncology populations has been the subject of ongoing concern. Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and other cardiovascular adverse events associated with ESA use in patients with cancer.5, 6, 7 In addition, some studies have reported increased mortality among patients treated with ESAs, particularly when higher hemoglobin targets were pursued or when ESAs were used outside guideline-recommended indications.5,6 Venous thromboembolism has emerged as the most consistently observed adverse outcome, which is of particular concern given the intrinsically elevated thrombotic risk associated with malignancy itself.6,7

Although ESAs are effective in raising hemoglobin levels and reducing the need for transfusions, concerns persist regarding their association with increased risks of thromboembolic and cardiovascular events. Although these risks have been reported in multicenter trials, there are limited real-world data examining outcomes across different cancer types and ESA formulations. To address these gaps, we conducted a real-world, propensity score–matched cohort study evaluating thrombotic and survival outcomes associated with ESA use in patients with cancer-related anemia.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study identified adult patients (aged ≥18 years) on the TriNetX Research Network from 1 January 2015 to 31 July 2024. This time period was chosen to allow for at least 1 year of follow-up for statistical analyses based on the initial timeline the data were run. Patients were then grouped based on cancer type for which ESA treatment is common using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 10th revision or ICD-Oncology diagnosis codes, including lung, breast, prostate, kidney, stomach, bladder, or head and neck cancers. We then selected for patients who had their first occurrence of an anemia diagnosis code on or after the occurrence of the cancer diagnosis code. Patients with a previous history of pulmonary embolisms (PEs), deep vein thromboses (DVTs), or 1 of the following medications up to 1 day before anemia occurrence were excluded: enoxaparin, dalteparin, tinzaparin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, betrixaban, dabigatran, warfarin, fondaparinux, or heparin. Finally, patients were divided based on those with no history of ESA use (darbepoetin alfa, methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta, or epoetin alfa) and those with ESA use on or after the occurrence of anemia. Once subdivided, patients receiving ESAs and those not receiving ESAs were compared based on their baseline characteristics occurring any time before the index event (anemia after cancer diagnosis). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were compared between cohorts to assess balance, with an SMD of >0.1 used as a caliper for ideal balance and an SMD of >0.2 indicating significant imbalance. Based on initial imbalances and clinical relevance, patients were then matched on age at index, race, obesity, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, CKD, heart failure, past chemotherapy encounters, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, erythrocyte count, C-reactive protein, prothrombin time, creatinine, diagnosis of kidney cancer, metastatic site (C77-C79), chemotherapy lines 1 and 2, radiation history, transfusion history, postprocedural states, orthopedic aftercare, presence of other functional implants, or abnormalities of gait and mobility. After 1:1 propensity score matching within the TriNetX platform, the measures-of-association risk assessment feature was used to assess the occurrence of PEs, DVTs, and anticoagulant use up to 12 weeks after the anemia event by calculating the risk difference (RD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a t test, and the risk ratio with 95% CIs. Survival at 1 year was then compared between cohorts using Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test and hazard ratios (HR), with patients censored after the last fact in their record. For all outcomes, patients with the outcome before the anemia event were excluded. A full list of the ICD 10th revision, current procedural terminology, and other codes used can be found in supplemental Table 1.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Before matching, 1613 patients were identified in the ESA group and 141 439 in the no-ESA group. ESA users were, on average, older (73.3 ± 11.6 vs 69.5 ± 12.8 years; SMD, 0.309) and had significantly higher rates of CKD (57% vs 19%; SMD, 0.841), thrombocytopenia (15% vs 7%; SMD, 0.243), and neutropenia (13% vs 5%; SMD, 0.297; Table 1). Several cancer types were imbalanced, including higher rates of kidney cancer in ESA users. ESA recipients were more likely to have previous chemotherapy encounters (27% vs 10%; SMD, 0.454) and showed lower hemoglobin, hematocrit, and erythrocyte counts than those in the no-ESA group. Discussion

Cancer is a prothrombotic state given tumor biology and treatment exposures that activate all components of the Virchow triad through tumor- and treatment-driven inflammation, cytokine release, immobility, surgery, and the presence of indwelling central venous catheters.8, 9, 10 Chemotherapy can also lead to CIA, which typically manifests as fatigue, dyspnea, and deconditioning, thereby contributing to poor functional status and treatment delays.11, 12, 13 Typically, red blood cell transfusions were used for the management of CIA. However, certain observational analyses demonstrated an increased risk of VTE with this approach in certain populations with cancer.14 The role of ESAs in managing CIA by improving hemoglobin, reducing transfusions, and preventing treatment delays provided a potential alternative in this regard.11, 12, 13, 14

The utility of these agents, however, was rapidly tempered by concerns for increased VTE and possibly decreased survival. A series of meta-analyses showed that although ESAs increase the risk of thromboembolic events in patients with cancer.2,6,15, 16, 17, 18 A meta-analysis by Gao et al of 51 randomized controlled trials including >12 000 patients reported that ESA use was associated with a significantly higher risk of VTE, with relative risks ranging from ∼1.5 to 1.7. The authors also surmised that the magnitude of risk varied by tumor type, with particularly high risk seen in ovarian cancer, cervical cancers, and hematologic malignancies.17 Tonia et al synthesized “10 years of meta-analyses” and found that although ESAs consistently reduced transfusion need, their use was associated with an increased incidence of VTE events in most analyses. Interestingly, the authors also found that the apparent effects on survival shifted over time.2,6 Earlier meta-analyses conducted before ∼2002 had hinted at possible survival benefit with ESAs, but later pooled analyses showed worse overall survival in certain settings, especially when ESAs were used with curative intent, at higher hemoglobin targets, or used off-label.2,6

Similar findings were noted in disease-specific trials exploring the utility of ESA use.18, 19, 20, 21 Randomized studies of epoetin and darbepoetin in patients with early breast cancer receiving adjuvant or dose-dense chemotherapy found worse overall survival in the ESA cohort despite a hematologic benefit.18,19 These data precipitated a paradigm shift in ESA use in oncology marked by US Food and Drug Administration black box warnings and updated professional society guidelines on ESA use. These changes narrowed ESA use predominantly to patients receiving palliative chemotherapy; discouraged ESA use in curative settings; and recommended lower, more conservative hemoglobin targets.2,6 Use studies in Medicare and Veterans Administration populations documented sharp declines in ESA prescribing, shifts toward lower doses and stricter indications, and a more nuanced use of these agents.

Our study includes patients treated in the “post–black box” era of ESA use and aims to determine whether initiating ESA in patients who are VTE-naïve and have CIA increases the short-term risk of incident VTE. The patients treated with ESA were matched on measured demographics, comorbidities, and cancer types, with most SMDs of <0.1.

There was no significant increase in incident VTE within 12 weeks of the index anemia event among patients treated with ESA compared with those in the matched cohort. It is also interesting to note that new anticoagulant initiation within 12 weeks, which would indicate clinically recognized thrombosis, was significantly lower in patients treated with ESA (6.3% vs 10.2%; risk ratio, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.49-0.78; P < .001). This consistent lower rate of DVT/PE events seen in the ESA cohort argues against large amounts of unmeasured VTE burden being missed. At 1 year, survival was modestly but significantly higher in patients treated with ESA (73% vs 69%), with an 18% lower hazard of death (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.72-0.94). Given the observational design and the lack of granular data on disease stage, performance status, chemotherapy intent, and transfusion exposure, it is unclear as to what this survival benefit represents. It may be the result of appropriate patient selection and the possibility that well-managed anemia may support treatment continuity and functional status. What can be inferred safely from the results is that there was no clear signal of harm in short-term survival, which had previously been reported in earlier trials and subsequent meta-analyses.

Notably, the ESA-treated cohort had a higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus and CKD, both of which are independently associated with increased thrombotic risk through endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and procoagulant states. Despite this unfavorable baseline risk profile, no increase in incident VTE was observed among patients treated with ESAs. This finding further supports the possibility that thrombotic risk associated with ESAs may be context dependent and modified by patient selection, hemoglobin thresholds, treatment intent, and contemporary prescribing practices. These observations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

This study had several limitations inherent to the use of real-world deidentified electronic health record data. First, TriNetX currently provides only mean laboratory values for matching rather than medians, which are less sensitive to skewed data; therefore, balance could not be optimized for variables with highly nonnormal distributions, such as ferritin, and some residual laboratory-related imbalances may remain. Additionally, key clinical measures of patient performance status, such as the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score, are not available within TriNetX. As a result, we relied on proxy indicators, which may not fully capture functional status. Finally, as an observational study, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded, and the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

It is important to emphasize that this study does not mitigate the prothrombotic risk of ESAs in patients with cancer because the early trials and meta-analyses clearly identified real safety signals.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Our findings do however suggest is that the real-world manifestation of that risk looks different when ESAs are used with nuance and in keeping with the guidelines.

Conclusions

In this large, real-world, propensity score–matched cohort study of patients with CIA treated in the post–black box era, ESA use was not associated with an increased short-term risk of VTE compared with matched patients who did not receive ESAs. Despite a higher baseline prevalence of comorbidities known to increase thrombotic risk, including CKD and diabetes mellitus, patients treated with ESAs demonstrated similar rates of DVT and PE and a lower rate of new anticoagulant initiation within 12 weeks of anemia onset. These findings suggest that contemporary, guideline-concordant ESA use does not appear to confer the magnitude of thrombotic risk previously observed in earlier randomized trials.

In addition, patients treated with ESA exhibited modestly improved 1-year survival compared with matched controls, although the observational nature of this study and the absence of granular data on disease stage, performance status, treatment intent, and transfusion exposure preclude causal inference. This survival difference may reflect appropriate patient selection and the potential benefits of improved anemia management on treatment continuity and functional status rather than a direct survival effect of ESA therapy.

Overall, our findings do not negate the established prothrombotic risks of ESAs identified in earlier trials and meta-analyses; rather, they suggest that the real-world risk profile of ESAs may be substantially modified by careful patient selection, conservative hemoglobin targets, and adherence to modern prescribing guidelines. Further prospective studies are warranted to better define patient subgroups most likely to benefit from ESA therapy while minimizing thrombotic risk and to clarify the long-term safety implications of ESA use in contemporary oncology practice.
