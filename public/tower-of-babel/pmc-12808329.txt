Biomass valorization using 3D-printed catalysts
Authors: Sathiyamoorthy Murugesan, Vlad Andrei Neacșu, Minodora Maria Marin, Ionuț-Cristian Radu, Derniza-Elena Cozorici, Erika Blânzeanu, Cătălin Zaharia, Kelvin Adrian Sanoja-Lopez, Rafael Luque, Marian Nicolae Verziu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Catalytic biomass valorization into value-added chemicals is crucial to reduce reliance on fossil resources and mitigate environmental impacts. However, more than 90% of chemical manufacturing still relies on conventional catalysts, underscoring the need for sustainable alternatives. Three-dimensional (3D) printing offers unique opportunities to design catalysts with tailored geometries and pore architectures, enhancing mass transfer, active site accessibility, and reusability. Most reviews of 3D-printed catalysts focus on small molecule reactions such as ammonia decomposition or methanol dehydration. In contrast, this review was conceived to provide a comprehensive overview on the design and application of 3D-printed catalysts in biomass conversion. Recent advances in metal-based, acid/base-mediated, enzymatic and photocatalytic systems are discussed, while assessing the benefits and drawbacks of 3D printing compared to traditional catalyst fabrication. By addressing current challenges and outlining future directions, this review highlights the capacity of 3D-printed catalysts to bridge laboratory innovation and industrial application, paving the way for scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable green chemical manufacturing in the emerging circular bioeconomy.

Introduction

The application of 3D printing, or additive manufacturing, in catalyst design has gained increasing attention in recent years due to its potential to improve catalytic performance, process efficiency, and overall sustainability1–4. This technique provides high precision, enabling catalysts to be fabricated with tailored chemical compositions and finely tuned structures5,6. Such optimized geometries enhance heat and mass transfer, promoting more effective interactions between reactants and catalytic active sites, and ultimately improving catalytic efficiency7. In addition, the flexibility of 3D printing allows rapid prototyping and systematic testing of diverse catalyst designs, significantly accelerating the development of optimized catalytic systems. This versatility is particularly advantageous in chemical engineering, where precisely engineered catalyst architectures can lead to substantial improvements in reaction rates and selectivity1.

Many reports regard 3D-printed catalysts as proof-of-concept, research-scale, or pilot-scale rather than full high-volume commercial deployment. This technology is suitable for prototyping and specialized applications, but scaling remains a challenge8. Nevertheless, since 2019, BASF has employed the sulfuric acid catalyst O4-115 for the conversion of SO₂ to SO₃, developed using X3D® technology9. These newly designed catalyst shapes have been implemented in industrial plants, delivering notable improvements in material and energy efficiency10. Replacing a 1.2 m³ catalyst bed with the X3D® catalyst has resulted in significant benefits, including caustic soda savings of 20.5 kg⋅h⁻¹ (equivalent to €58,000 per annum), total annual savings of approximately €100,000, and a 1% increase in yield (equivalent to 1 MTPD or €25,000 per annum).

A further benefit lies in the possibility of directly integrating catalytic functions into reactor components during fabrication. Several 3D printing methods have been employed to produce functional catalysts and reactors, including direct ink writing (DIW)11–15 or robocasting4,16, fused deposition modeling (FDM)17,18, stereolithography (SLA)19, digital light processing (DLP)20, selective laser sintering (SLS)21, selective laser melting (SLM)22,23, bioprinting24, and binder jetting25,26. This approach eliminates the need for separate catalytic materials and supports, streamlining system design by integrating reactor and catalyst functions into a single structure. Notable examples include the direct conversion of C₁ molecules into liquid fuels27,28 and styrene hydrogenation4, demonstrating the potential of 3D-printed systems for more efficient, economical, and sustainable catalysis. Beyond these cases, 3D-printed materials have been applied to a wide range of reactions, including water splitting29,30, hydrogen evolution31, oxygen evolution32,33, ammonia synthesis34,35, CO₂ methanation26,36–44, methane steam reforming22,45, and CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol46, propylene carbonate14, formate47, or dimethyl ether48. Other reported applications include methanol conversion to olefins49, NH₃ decomposition to H₂50, NO oxidation51, H₂O₂ decomposition52, propane oxidation53, CO₂-assisted propane conversion to propylene54, oxidative coupling of methane55, cross-coupling reactions15,17,56,57, nitrobenzene hydrogenation58,59, phenol hydroxylation60, alkyne semihydrogenation61, H₂ release from storage materials62, dehydrogenation of perhydro-dibenzyltoluene63,64 and formic acid65, hydrolysis of alkali borohydrides23, environmental remediation8, cracking of polyethylene pyrolysis oils66 and biomass valorization67–74.

Biomass valorization provides a sustainable pathway for waste management, energy production, and industrial development. Converting renewable organic matter into fuels, chemicals, and materials delivers significant environmental and economic benefits. Conventional catalytic methods are well established for producing a wide range of value-added products, including propylene, ethylene, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, levulinic acid (LevA), succinic acid, maleic acid, fumaric acid, lactic acid, alcohols, phenols, styrene, benzene, toluene, xylene, and other aromatics75–79. Despite growing attention to 3D printing, the application of these technologies for catalyst design in biomass valorization remains underexplored. A scientometric study based on the papers published within the last five years featuring 3D printing and catalysis show that “biomass” occupies a peripheral position with sparse linkages, confirming that research on this topic remains largely disconnected (Fig. 1). This gap presents a promising opportunity to exploit the structural tunability of 3D printing to create catalysts with enhanced functionality, selectivity, and reusability. 3D printing technologies for the preparation of functional materials

Several 3D printing techniques are employed in the fabrication of 3D-printed catalysts. This chapter focuses on four main 3D printing classes: extrusion-based printing, powder-based fusion, vat photopolymerization (VP), and binder jetting, illustrated in Fig. 2, highlighting the materials and processes used in each case. Extrusion based 3D-printing technologies

Material extrusion is one of the most widely adopted 3D printing techniques due to its cost-effectiveness and operational simplicity. It involves selectively dispensing material through a nozzle to build 3D structures layer by layer, following a digital model. This category is mainly divided into two methods: Fused Deposition Modeling and Direct Ink Writing, which differ in the materials used, extrusion mechanisms, solidification processes, and post-processing requirements80. For both FDM and DIW, achieving printability and functionality for 3D-printed catalysts requires balancing material formulation, integration of active components, optimized structural design and printing parameters, and suitable post-processing.

In FDM, thermoplastic polymers are supplied as filament spools, fed through a heated nozzle, melted at a polymer-specific temperature, and deposited layer by layer, cooling and solidifying into the target structure. The quality of the construct depends on material properties and process parameters81–83. The printing speed must balance fabrication time and part quality and is correlated with printing temperature, which is specific to each polymer and filament composition. The temperature directly influences material behavior during extrusion, layer adhesion, and the overall structural integrity of the 3D-printed catalyst. Deposition orientation influences surface finish and mechanical performance, and infill pattern and density determine load-bearing capacity. Nozzle diameter (typically 0.4 mm) controls material flow, build time, and feature resolution by dictating layer height and line width84.

In FDM, one common strategy for fabricating 3D-printed catalysts is to incorporate the active phase into a thermoplastic matrix, producing printable composite filaments. Metal salts such as nickel nitrate85 or metal oxides, including calcium oxide86, iron oxide, zinc oxide, and titanium dioxide87,88 have been embedded into commodity polymers such as poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene, either directly or as nanoparticles, to enhance both mechanical properties and catalytic functionality. Another approach involves printing a precursor structure, followed by deposition of the catalytic phase. For example, alumina-loaded filaments were processed into substrates that were debound and sintered to remove the polymer and densify the ceramic, after which they were impregnated with metal precursors, dried, and calcined to form active catalysts52. Similarly, filaments containing steel nanoparticles were fabricated and used to print the substrate. After thermal removal of the polymer and sintering of the steel, NiO–CeO₂ nanoparticles were deposited by impregnation89. A further strategy was reported by Smith et al.90, who printed polypropylene filaments and then sulfonated the structures to introduce binding sites. The substrates were subsequently soaked in metal nitrate solutions and pyrolyzed, generating carbon frameworks with embedded metal nanoparticles.

By contrast, DIW (also known as robocasting) uses printable formulations such as inks, slurries, pastes, or hydrogels based on polymers, ceramics, or metals91. These formulations require specific rheological properties to enable proper 3D printing under mechanically or pneumatically driven pressure. For structural integrity, the material should have a high initial viscosity (10²–10⁶ mPa⋅s at a low shear rate of ~ 0.1 s⁻¹) and a yield stress of 10–1000 Pa to prevent unintended flow or collapse. During 3D printing, the material should exhibit pronounced shear-thinning, quantified by a shear-thinning index of 0.3–0.6, enabling it to flow smoothly through the nozzle under applied pressure. Once deposited, the material must rapidly regain its structure. A solid-dominant viscoelastic profile (storage modulus G′ >loss modulus G″, G′ = 10²–10⁴ Pa) ensures shape retention, while a recovery time of less than 10 s after shear cessation is essential to preserve fine features and prevent spreading92,93. Solidification is then achieved through processes such as gelation, UV curing, solvent evaporation, or thermal treatment, depending on the material94–96.

For catalyst 3D printing, ceramic-based materials such as alumina97–100, zirconia101,102, and silica103 are frequently used, with the active phase introduced either directly into the formulation before or post-printing through impregnation methods such as vacuum self-assembly or dip coating. Other studies investigated alloying-dealloying strategies to generate robust hierarchical nanoporous catalysts following DIW-based 3D printing of metal-based formulations44,104,105. Among the diverse DIW approaches, the use of zeolites is particularly noteworthy106–108, alongside methods involving active phase incorporation into polymer-derived ceramics109 or integration of metal organic frameworks110. Further, polymer-based 3D-printed carriers designed for enzyme immobilization offer an effective strategy for the development of biocatalysts24,111.

Powder bed fusion

Powder Bed Fusion (PBF) is a class of 3D printing techniques that employ high-energy sources, such as lasers or electron beams, to selectively fuse powder materials according to a digital computer-aided design model. Fusion can occur through complete melting, as in SLM, typically used for metals, or through solid-state sintering, as in SLS, better suited for polymers and ceramics112,113. During printing, a thin powder layer is spread on the build platform and selectively fused, then covered by a new layer, with the process repeating until the object is formed. After printing, the part is extracted, and unfused powder is removed, usually by vacuum114,115. PBF is compatible with a wide range of materials and offers high precision through laser processing. Compared to DIW, which relies on organic solvents, or SLA, which uses hazardous photopolymer resins, PBF is generally more sustainable. Unlike FDM, it does not require filament extrusion, binders, or support structures, and allows recovery and reuse of unfused powder, improving cost efficiency and reducing waste116,117. Despite their advantages, powder-based 3D-printing methods face several limitations. These include the high energy demand required to preheat and maintain powder temperature, health risks from airborne particles during handling, the large powder volumes needed, and the complexity of optimizing numerous process parameters such as powder characteristics, laser settings, and post-processing conditions which, if poorly controlled, can lead to pores, cracks, or other structural defects118–120.

In catalytic applications, PBF is typically followed by post-printing treatments to load or activate the catalyst. For example, Ni/CeO₂ was washcoated onto SLS-printed stainless steel, thermally treated for strong adhesion, and tested for CO₂ methanation121. Similarly, SLM was used to print stainless steel 316 L, copper-coated stainless steel, and AlSi₁₀Mg alloy supports for hydrogen production, with catalysts deposited by impregnation followed by drying122. Among post-printing strategies, dealloying has gained prominence for creating high porosity in 3D-printed structures. For instance, FeCoNiCu powders processed via SLM were fabricated into triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) architectures, then chemically dealloyed in nitric acid under varying conditions to yield porous catalysts for Rhodamine B degradation123. Metallic glass-based TPMS structures were likewise produced through SLM and subsequently electrochemically dealloyed to enhance porosity for azo dye degradation124. Another study used Co–Al alloy powders to print TPMS geometries, which were later treated by KOH dealloying and annealing to improve stability, ultimately serving as catalysts in methanol steam reforming125.

To date, the use of PBF in catalysis has focused primarily on metallic materials. Reported examples include brass for CO₂ cycloaddition with epichlorohydrin126, MoS₂-coated stainless steel for florfenicol degradation127, stainless steel for hydrogen128 and hydrogen peroxide generation129, and Fe-based amorphous alloys for methylorange degradation130. These materials are typically manufactured as monoliths or TPMS structures to increase surface area, enhance heat transfer, and reduce pressure drop131. However, the application of polymeric- or ceramic-based materials in PBF for catalysis remains largely unexplored, revealing a significant gap in current research.

Vat photopolymerization

Vat photopolymerization is a 3D printing technique that uses a vat of liquid photopolymer resin, selectively cured layer by layer by a light source. The two main methods are stereolithography, which employs a UV laser to trace and cure the resin surface point by point, and digital light processing, which projects a full image of each layer onto the resin surface using a digital micromirror device, curing it simultaneously132–134. A major advantage of VP printing is its precision. Both SLA and DLP deliver high resolution, supporting layer thicknesses of ~50–150 µm and feature sizes down to tens of microns, making them suitable for detailed geometries and exceptional surface finish135. DLP often achieves finer results for smaller builds due to voxel curing, whereas SLA’s point-by-point scanning limits resolution over larger areas136,137. DLP also provides higher throughput by curing complete layers in a single exposure, significantly reducing print times compared to SLA138. VP supports diverse resin formulations, including ceramic-filled suspensions, metal powder-doped resins, and bio-based resins such as poly(ethylene glycol), chitosan, gelatin, and methacrylate139. This versatility in material formulation makes VP especially attractive for catalytic applications140.

Recent studies have explored composite resins embedded with metal oxides or catalytic species such as TiO₂ nanoparticles. These functional resins enable the direct fabrication of catalyst supports or active structures from the printer, minimizing post-processing steps140,141. For example, SLA-printed reactors incorporating TiO2 nanoparticles achieved 80% photodegradation of Rhodamine B after four hours, demonstrating the potential for low-cost and reproducible photocatalytic devices140. Using SLA of active slurry combined with pyrolysis, Wu et al.142 developed a flexible method for creating 3D-printed monolithic catalysts with high structural complexity. Ball-milled active precursors were blended with photosensitive resins and auxiliaries, and pyrolysis generated additional pores while producing MoO₃ as the active phase. The resulting structures also exhibited strong mechanical properties and dimensional stability, two critical attributes for monolithic catalysts143.

DLP printing has also been used to fabricate porous γ-Al2O3 scaffolds, which serve as high surface area support for catalytic metals. These structures demonstrated improved mass transport and stability under reaction conditions, outperforming conventional catalysts in alcohol oxidation144. Similarly, Chen et al.141 formulated a 3D-printing slurry containing up to 50% TiO₂ nanoparticles, showing favorable shear thinning behavior, good printability, and stable dispersion. The printed TiO2 monoliths exhibited excellent structural fidelity and homogeneous nanoparticle distribution. Photocatalytic performance tests confirmed their activity, with structures containing 50% TiO₂ achieving the highest degradation efficiency, underscoring the positive correlation between solid content and catalytic performance.

In summary, SLA and DLP techniques offer precise control over geometry and chemistry, combining digital reproducibility, material flexibility, and design complexity. These advantages make them powerful tools for developing next-generation catalytic reactors.

Binder jetting

Binder jetting is a key 3D printing technology in which a binder or liquid bonding agent joins powder particles to form a 3D object. This method can produce parts from different materials, including metals (aluminum, titanium, steel), ceramics (zirconia, alumina, oxides), polymers (nylon, polyolefins), and even natural compounds such as sand. Similar to sintering processes, the unbound powder acts as a support for the printed material during fabrication. The choice of binder depends on the material type and printing parameters. Solid polymer binders are typically used for ceramics and polymer powders, while liquid-based binders are preferred for metals. In general, binders can be classified as liquid or solid, and as organic or inorganic. Examples include water-based or water–alcohol solutions, polymer solutions, solid silicate binders, polymers, and waxes144–148.

One of the main advantages of binder jetting is the ability to reuse unbonded powder, which enhances both economic efficiency and sustainability. Depending on the material type and object design, certain components can be produced as finished parts without requiring post-processing. For metallic and ceramic materials, however, additional heat treatments such as annealing are often applied to improve mechanical strength and durability. Nonetheless, several drawbacks remain. Components may deform during the sintering process, and predicting the extent of deformation is challenging. Other limitations arise from binder powder interactions, powder layer thickness, printing speed, and drying time. These factors directly affect the strength, density, and overall quality of the final printed objects149,150. Despite these challenges, binder jetting has found widespread applications across industries, including aerospace, healthcare, jewelry, engineering, construction, chemistry, and automotive, where it is used to produce structural parts, functional components, and decorative elements.

Chemical engineering, particularly in the field of catalysis, has shown strong interest in binder jetting for designing and developing 3D-printed catalysts. Numerous studies demonstrate its application in catalytic systems. For instance, inorganic 3D-printed Al₂O₃ porous supports for heterogeneous catalysis were fabricated and subsequently calcined at 600 °C for enhanced consolidation, a step not achievable directly through binder jetting. Nonetheless, the printing process can be optimized to produce catalyst supports with larger surface areas and porosities. This research paves the way for further impregnation of supports with increasingly complex geometrical structures25.

Another example involves the fabrication of spherical Ni–Al methanation catalysts via binder jetting from coprecipitated precursor powders, where the Ni/Al ratio was varied to achieve high activity. Tested in a single pellet string reactor, these catalysts reached CO₂ conversion levels of up to 99% with high selectivity toward methane26. In a different study, alumina supports were impregnated in situ with Ni(NO₃)₂-containing ink. Compared with traditional wet impregnation, the binder jetting approach produced catalysts with superior CO₂ hydrogenation activity. Furthermore, the study confirmed that binder jetting produced mechanically stable Ni/Al₂O₃ catalysts151.

In conclusion, binder jetting offers significant flexibility in designing catalyst supports and impregnation strategies, while enabling coprecipitation methods that allow for high loading and superior dispersion of active catalytic phases. Catalytic applications of 3D-printed catalysts in biomass and biochemical conversion

Metal-based catalysis

The integration of 3D printing into metal-based catalysis offers several key advantages on geometry control, mass transfer, as well as the reduction of its deactivation, which improves the performance, stability, and longevity of catalytic systems.

One of the earliest demonstrations of 3D-printed metal-based catalysts was reported by Danaci et al.40, who developed a structured copper support later functionalized with an active catalytic layer. The engineered morphology enhanced heat and mass transfer, effectively minimizing hot spot formation, a recurring issue in packed bed reactors. Applied to CO₂ methanation, the catalyst showed slightly improved productivity compared with its powdered counterpart, along with a more uniform reactor temperature profile. Building on this concept, Middelkoop et al.42 manufactured a Ni/Al₂O₃-based catalyst with a periodic tetragonal structure. This system achieved excellent CO₂ conversion and methane selectivity. Compared to conventional pelletized or spherical forms, the 3D-printed catalyst delivered significantly higher activity and selectivity, underscoring the benefits of optimized macroscopic geometry in catalytic performance.

A further advancement is the concept of self-catalytic reactors, in which the reactor body itself is composed of catalytically active metal. Wang et al.37 reported a 3D-printed nickel-based self-catalytic reactor that served both as the structural framework and as an active methanation site for COₓ. This design exhibited unique surface properties and superior catalytic performance. Related work expanded the versatility of self-catalytic reactors using Fe, Ni, and Co systems for liquid fuel synthesis, syngas production, and CO₂ hydrogenation (Fig. 3a)27. More recently, a 3D-printed Ni/Al₂O₃/FeCrAl monolithic catalyst achieved hydrogen yields of up to 38 mmol per gram of biomass67. This performance was attributed to enhanced heat and flow properties resulting from the tailored catalyst geometry. The yield is comparable to the modeled value of 47.17 mmol⋅g⁻¹ recently reported for rice straw via supercritical water gasification152. Beyond gasification and methanation, 3D printing has also been applied in microreactor technology. A 3D-printed microfluidic device incorporating Pd/Co₃O₄ as a catalyst enabled the continuous flow oxidation of flavonoids, achieving 98% efficiency in only 8 min, a fivefold reduction in reaction time compared with a batch reactor153. Solid biomass can be efficiently converted into hydrogen-rich biogas using a two-stage reaction system. In such setups, the secondary reactor promotes further cracking of released volatiles, encouraging the breakdown of large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller ones. Another advantage is the separation of solid–gas interactions, which helps overcome the incomplete pyrolysis reactions typically observed in single-stage systems154. In this context, Bolívar Caballero et al. developed a one-step process combining biomass pyrolysis at 550 °C with steam catalytic reforming (SCR) of volatiles at 800 °C, using a geometry-enhanced 3D-printed Ni/Al₂O₃/FeCrAl monolithic catalyst67. The catalyst featured a periodic open cellular structure (POCS), which significantly improved mass and heat transfer during SCR, yielding approximately 7.6 wt.% H₂ from the biomass feedstock. The synthesis of the active catalyst followed three steps: (i) a FeCrAl lattice monolith was 3D-printed, (ii) coated with Al₂O₃, and (iii) impregnated with nickel nitrate, followed by NiO reduction to metallic Ni. A schematic representation of the 3D-printed Ni/Al₂O₃/FeCrAl-based monolithic catalyst is illustrated in Fig. 3b. For comparison, steam reforming was also tested with a conventional powdered Ni/Al₂O₃ catalyst (10 wt.%). Under identical conditions, this material produced only 3.16 mmol H₂ per gram of biomass. By contrast, the 3D-printed Ni/Al₂O₃/FeCrAl catalyst achieved ~ 22 mmol H₂⋅g⁻¹, despite containing only 1.6 wt.% Ni. Computational simulations confirmed that the POCS geometry enhanced heat and mass transfer, promoting the reforming of pyrolysis volatiles such as tars and bio-oils. Importantly, the 3D-printed POCS monolith outperformed conventional single-channel monoliths, underscoring the advantages of geometry-driven catalytic design.

Using ink-based printing, Popova et al.71 reported 3D-printed Ni–Cu sodalite zeolite catalysts. For comparison, metal incorporation was carried out either before or after the 3D printing step. Levulinic acid, derived from lignocellulosic biomass, was converted into γ-valerolactone (GVL). The conversion of LevA to GVL, a valuable platform chemical, was evaluated using both powdered and 3D-printed catalysts. Among the powdered formulations, the Ni₅Cu₂.₅/sodalite zeolite catalyst exhibited the best performance, achieving 72% LevA conversion and 59% GVL yield in aqueous medium at 150 °C under a hydrogen atmosphere. Based on this result, the same composition was selected for 3D structuring. However, under similar conditions, the 3D printed Ni₅Cu₁.₅/sodalite zeolite catalyst performed significantly worse, producing only 19% GVL at 21% LevA conversion. TEM micrographs showed that both catalysts had well-dispersed Ni and Cu nanoparticles. However, the 3D printed catalysts had smaller metal particles (7–11 nm) than the powdered materials ( ~20 nm), indicating that intrinsic dispersion of active metals was not a limitation. Despite the better dispersion, BET tests revealed a significant drop in accessible surface area from ~27 m²⋅g⁻¹ for the powdered catalyst to ~10⁻⁴ m²⋅g⁻¹ for the 3D printed catalysts. This reduction of surface accessibility is closely associated with decreased catalytic efficacy, as the bulk of active sites remains inaccessible to reactant molecules under liquid-phase reaction conditions. Mechanistically, LevA is hydrogenated to 4-hydroxyvaleric acid, which subsequently undergoes intramolecular esterification to yield GVL. The differences in performance between powdered and 3D-printed catalysts may also relate to the fact that hydrogenation is thermodynamically favored at lower temperatures than the esterification step155. Across the tested range, byproduct selectivity remained negligible. By contrast, hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching of α-Al₂O₃ monoliths (20 and 40 wt.% HF) revealed a different trend. Higher fluorine content accelerated the decline in acetaldehyde selectivity, promoting secondary reactions that formed ethylene and ethane, as evidenced in Fig. 3c. This suggests that fluorine introduced during the etching process facilitates undesired side reactions. HF treatment increased surface roughness and porosity; catalytic activity improvements were marginal: ethanol conversion and contact time showed only slight gains. Residual fluorine appeared to reduce overall catalyst efficacy, suggesting that HF etching does not enhance, and may even hinder, α-Al₂O₃-based catalyst performance. The catalytic performance of Ni–Cu sodalite zeolite catalysts is primarily determined by the balance and strength of two types of active sites: metallic sites, which drive hydrogenation, and acidic sites, which promote dehydration. The concentration and synergy of these sites are critical for achieving high activity and selectivity. In this system, reducing the copper content from 5 wt.% to 1.5 wt.% led to a marked decline in performance, underscoring the essential role of copper in the hydrogenation step. Moreover, the study found that whether metal impregnation was performed before or after the 3D printing step had no significant impact on catalytic activity. This result highlights the robustness of 3D-printed catalysts for biomass conversion, demonstrating that the printing process does not impair the chemical accessibility of the active sites.

A porous zeolite catalyst was specifically designed to meet both catalytic and DIW printing requirements. Its printing quality, surface structure, strength, and catalytic performance were tested in a xylene isomerization reaction107. Optimal conditions were found to be 46 wt. % ink solid content, 600 μm nozzle diameter, and 0.35 MPa extrusion pressure, which yielded excellent catalytic activity and mechanical strength. Before the reaction, the catalyst was activated under hydrogen for 2 h at 480 °C. The reaction was then carried out at 360 °C with 0.5 MPa backpressure. A hydrogen to hydrocarbon molar ratio of 4.51 was maintained using a mass flow controller. Under these conditions, ethylbenzene conversion reached 51.31%, representing a 71% improvement compared to conventional extrusion-formed catalysts. Additionally, the printed catalyst exhibited a compressive strength of up to 11 MPa. Based on the catalyst’s characteristics, a custom-made reactor was designed (Fig. 3d). Reactants enter the reactor through the inlet and are evaporated via electric auxiliary heating. The vaporized reactants then diffuse into the catalyst loading zone, where the reaction takes place. Finally, the reaction products together with any unreacted reactants are evacuated through the outlet.

Additionally, a 3D-printed Pt/zeolite bifunctional catalyst was evaluated for xylene isomerization under hydrogen at 360 °C in a fixed-bed flow reactor73. Compared with extrudates, the 3D-printed catalysts exhibited superior mechanical strength and higher catalytic performance, attributed to improved diffusivity and surface area. Among various structural designs, the combined linear staggered/wave vertical geometry achieved the best results, with para-xylene concentration reaching 22.89% and ethylbenzene conversion of 36.70%, about 21.68% higher than the extrudates. In related work, Alimi et al.153 fabricated a low-cost 3D-printed microreactor using transparent biodegradable PLA, successfully embedding Pd/Co₃O₄ nanocatalysts for continuous flow reactions. In a carbonate buffer solution (pH 9) with morin and H₂O₂, the microreactor achieved >95% morin conversion within 8 min, compared with 40 min in a batch reactor, demonstrating the significant efficiency and recyclability advantages of 3D-printed flow systems.

Salitra et al.156 demonstrated that compatibility issues in one-pot chemoenzymatic processes can be resolved by 3D printing heterogeneous ceramic materials containing copper as a key catalyst. Using robocasting, they fabricated Cu ceramics composed of Al₂O₃/SiO₂/graphite powder. This approach addressed the challenge that direct Cu²⁺ contact, necessary for Wacker oxidation, typically leads to enzyme deactivation. By spatially integrating two generally incompatible steps (Wacker oxidation and enzymatic reduction), they achieved efficient conversion of styrene to (R)-1-phenylethanol with 80% overall conversion and 99.0% ee enantioselectivity. Symes et al.4 extended this concept by fabricating a 3D-printed reactor chamber containing a Pd–C/silicone polymer catalyst via robocasting. In this setup, one equivalent of styrene with 15 equivalents of triethylsilane in d₄-MeOD produced ethylbenzene in quantitative yield within 30 min, demonstrating the feasibility of embedding conventional hydrogenation chemistry into 3D-printed catalytic reactors.

Quintanilla and Vega developed 3D-printed catalysts of different compositions using robocasting and evaluated them in the hydroxylation of phenol with H₂O₂ to produce dihydroxybenzenes60,157–159. Interestingly, one of their studies investigated 3D-printed Fe/SiC monoliths with parallel interconnected channels of varying geometries (triangular, square, troncoconical) for the same reaction, under the following conditions: Cphenol,0 = CH₂O₂,0 = 0.33 M, catalyst weight = 3.7 g; T = 80–90 °C; residence time τ = 0–254 gcat h⋅L⁻¹ (flow rate 0–0.25 mL⋅min⁻¹), using water as the solvent60. Among the tested geometries, triangular cell monoliths outperformed the others, showing higher phenol hydroxylation rates, greater dihydroxybenzene formation, and more efficient H₂O₂ utilization. Computational fluid dynamics simulations validated these findings. The velocity profiles (in a plane center of the channel) shown that the reduction of the velocity with respect to the maximum reached (umax ≈ 0.11 mm⋅s⁻¹), once the flow was fully developed, is much more noticeable for the triangular channel (97%) compared to the troncoconical (44%) and square (30%). Moreover, in the triangular geometry, the velocity values clearly fluctuated, which means the fluid did not follow a straight path as for square and troncoconical but moves along colliding with the channel wall, and this results in an oscillating flow. As a result, the triangular design accommodates increased macrochannel tortuosity, which causes the fluid to deviate inside the channel.

Acid- and base-mediated catalysis

Solid acid and base catalysts have demonstrated an extensive applicability in a wide range of acid-catalyzed transformations160. Their utilization has been especially prominent in continuous-flow systems incorporating packed-bed reactor configurations, where they contribute to improving catalytic performance. On the other hand, the emergence of 3D-printing technologies, allowing for accurate and tailored creation of reaction environments, offers new possibilities for enhancing the design and performance of these catalytic systems161.

A notable development is the fabrication of zeolite-based catalysts for acid-catalyzed reactions such as xylene isomerization. Chu et al.107 and Yang et al.73 used DIW to produce structured zeolite/Al₂O₃ monoliths that demonstrated excellent mechanical and thermal stability (Fig. 4a). These printed catalysts showed higher diffusivity and reduced reactant flow resistance, resulting in superior catalytic performance. Expanding acid-mediated applications, Hock et al.74 employed FDM to create acidic monolithic catalysts from high- impact polystyrene (HIPS) (Fig. 4b). After sulfonation, the printed material featured sulfonic acid groups with high accessibility in pore channels, significantly enhancing catalytic activity in sucrose hydrolysis. When tested at 80 °C, these monoliths acted as acid catalysts, producing α-D-glucose and β-D-fructose. Compared with the commercial benchmark Amberlyst-15 (a macroporous sulfonic acid ion exchange resin), the 3D-printed catalysts exhibited superior catalytic performance under both batch and continuous flow conditions. This enhancement was attributed to the greater accessibility of functional groups within the pore channels, which alleviated the mass transfer limitations inherent to macroporous resin catalysts. Moreover, the ability to tailor pore topology provided additional hydrodynamic benefits for reactive fluids, underscoring the potential of FDM as a fast, adaptable method for fabricating high-performance acid catalysts.

In another study, Radogna et al.162 used DIW to fabricate α-Al₂O₃ monoliths that served as reference catalysts for ethanol dehydrogenation to hydrogen. Surface modification was carried out through HF etching at concentrations of 5, 20, and 40 wt.% with optimized exposure times of 48, 24, and 5 h, respectively. Although the HF treatments increased surface porosity by up to 5% for the 40 wt.% sample, the etched monoliths consistently produced less hydrogen than the unetched reference across all reaction temperatures (200–600 °C). This reduction in performance was attributed to fluorine poisoning, which promoted secondary side reactions that suppressed the primary dehydrogenation pathway. Selectivity analysis showed that both the reference and HF 5 wt. % catalysts followed similar trends, with acetaldehyde selectivity decreasing and hydrogen selectivity increasing with rising temperature. The DIW printed α-Al₂O₃ monoliths by Radogna et al.162 represent an important reference system for exploring how surface modifications influence catalytic activity. Despite the expected benefits of enhanced porosity, the findings highlight the detrimental effect of residual fluorine on catalytic selectivity and hydrogen yield.

While base-mediated catalysis is less represented in the 3D-printing literature, a notable contribution is from Du Preez et al.163, who employed FDM to construct a continuous flow system for determining the fatty acid content of edible oils (Fig. 4c). The system performed automated transesterification of triacylglycerols at room temperature using sodium methoxide as a catalyst. Based on the room temperature base-catalyzed transesterification of triacylglycerols, the system demonstrated high precision and accuracy when benchmarked against certified fatty acid contents in sunflower seed oil. It was further applied to commercial oils such as avocado, canola, extra virgin olive, and blended canola–olive oil.

Finally, Borges et al.164 reported the fabrication of a 3D-printed bifunctional catalyst containing both acidic and basic sites, specifically designed for biodiesel production from low-quality oils (Fig. 4d). By enabling simultaneous esterification and transesterification, the catalyst proved particularly effective for feedstocks with high free fatty acid content.

Enzymatic catalysis

In biotechnology, the integration of 3D printing and biocatalysis creates numerous innovative opportunities that combine the high fabrication accuracy of the former with the targeted and specific characteristics of the latter, making biocatalytic processes more economically attractive in the production of value-added compounds such as those derived from biomass165. The rigid structure of lignocellulosic biomass requires the action of multiple enzymes, primarily cellulases, hemicellulases, and ligninases166. However, their industrial application is often constrained by poor activity in hydrophobic media, low thermal stability, and limited tolerance to high pH conditions167,168. These drawbacks significantly reduce their efficiency in large-scale processes.

A promising alternative is the immobilization of enzymes on solid supports such as chitosan, collagen, cellulose, silica, activated carbon, or magnetic nanomaterials169,170. The magnetic properties of these supports eliminate the need for labor-intensive recovery techniques like filtration or centrifugation. For instance, magnetic nanoparticles functionalized with chitosan and cross-linked with tripolyphosphate were successfully used as supports for ruthenium. In one study, cellulase immobilized on these nanoparticles catalyzed the conversion of carboxymethyl cellulose to D-sorbitol with a remarkable yield of 83.2% under optimized conditions171. Furthermore, immobilization on alternative magnetic composites, such as Fe₃O₄/GO/CS nanomaterials, enhanced cellulase stability, maintaining activity even at pH 8 and 80 °C172. These results demonstrate that enzyme immobilization not only improves catalytic performance but also expands the operational window, increasing their feasibility for industrial biomass valorization.

Recently, 3D printing has opened new avenues for optimizing enzyme immobilization and activity. By tailoring geometries, surface areas, and porosities, 3D printing enhances the attachment and performance of enzymes on supports (Fig. 5a)173–175. Cost-effective methods such as FDM are particularly useful for fabricating scaffolds that facilitate enzyme entrapment or covalent binding. Printable materials include hydrogels and polymers such as PLA176, which can be chemically modified to create reactive sites for enzyme anchoring. Notably, carbon-reinforced PLA modified with piranha solution significantly improved the catalytic performance of immobilized glycosidase for lactosucrose production (Fig. 5a)175. Currently, the valorization of lignocellulosic biomass or biomass-derived materials using 3D-printed immobilized enzymes is still in its early stages, with only a few studies reported. 3D printing shows strong promise as an enabling technology. For example, immobilization of xylanase by 3D printing produced an excellent catalyst for corn cob hydrolysis, achieving high reusability over 10 cycles while retaining 40% of its initial activity177. By contrast, magnetic immobilization of xylanase for wheat hydrolysis showed a greater decline, with residual activity dropping to 23% after 10 reuse cycles178. A notable advance represented the combination of electrospinning and 3D printing for xylanase immobilization, which enhanced catalytic activity under harsh conditions (pH, temperature) compared with the free enzyme. In this approach, 3D-printed scaffolds provided mechanical stability and design flexibility, while electrospun nanofibrous membranes offered finer, more uniform structures that improved surface area, enzyme retention, and mass transfer. This synergy resulted in a remarkable xylan conversion of 96.3% within 30 h (Fig. 5b)179.

Recently, Büscher et al.180 reported the decarboxylation of ferulic acid to 2-methoxy-4-vinylphenol, a flavoring agent, using POCS that acted both as a support for phenolic acid decarboxylase and as a distributor for the liquid extraction phase. This design improved reaction efficiency by optimizing mass transfer and enzyme immobilization. Similarly, the one-step immobilization of glucose oxidase and catalase by extrusion-based hydrogel bioprinting using a hybrid interpenetrating polymer network preserved enzyme activity up to fourfold during glucose conversion, achieving >97% yield of gluconic acid from glucose181. Compared with other 3D printing techniques for enzyme immobilization, extrusion-based hydrogel bioprinting resulted in higher enzyme loading.

The advantages of 3D-printed microreactors, including minimized dead volume and leakage prevention, combined with dual surface glucose dehydrogenase immobilization, enhanced catalytic performance in the oxidation of glucose to gluconolactone, achieving 95.76% conversion in 2 h182. In agreement with these findings, magnetic enzyme immobilization facilitates easy separation and reuse, while 3D printing enables the fabrication of tailored support structures that improve enzyme stability.

The immobilization of the photocatalysts, as well as their recovery, a challenge in the industry, can be solved by 3D-printing technology, allowing porosity control in order to increase mass transfer and selectivity alike. Borges et al.183 immobilized exfoliated graphitic carbon nitride onto a 3D-printed PLA scaffold for H₂O₂ generation from saccharide-rich feedstocks (Fig. 6a). The highest efficiency was achieved with cellobiose as the substrate, and chloride ions doubled the activity. Expanding design innovation, Zhakeyev et al.184 incorporated a benzothiadiazole derived photosensitizer into a commercial DLP resin, directly fabricating photocatalytic components for flow reactors (Fig. 6b). This approach not only improved photocatalytic efficiency but also allowed regulation of fluid dynamics in fixed bed reactors (Fig. 6c). Four monolith geometries (cylindrical, parallel channel, Voronoi, and gyroid lattice) were tested under visible light (420 nm) in continuous flow. All generated singlet oxygen in water, with the Voronoi structure achieving the highest space time yield (2.34 mmol⋅m⁻²⋅h⁻¹), efficiently converting furoic acid to 5-hydroxy-2(5H)-furanone (γ-lactone).

The combination of titanium dioxide and gold nanoparticles is one of the most widely studied systems for photocatalytic hydrogen production from ethanol–water mixtures at ambient temperature, as it enhances UV absorption, reduces energy loss, and improves overall efficiency185. Elkoro et al.186 fabricated TiO₂-based monoliths by additive deposition of microfilaments, which were subsequently impregnated with gold nanoparticles (Fig. 6d). They emphasized the use of pre-formed gold nanoparticles with uniform particle size ( ~4 nm), enabling a precise evaluation of how 3D-printed titania monolith design affects photocatalytic activity. The distribution of Au nanoparticles proved critical: pre-impregnated monoliths exhibited a homogeneous gold distribution throughout the microfilaments, whereas post-impregnated samples showed an asymmetric distribution, with high gold concentration near the surface but lower levels in the inner regions. XPS analysis confirmed that the gold concentration of the loading suspension in post-impregnated samples was reduced nearly tenfold to ensure comparable gold content at the surface. Interestingly, post-impregnated samples with much lower gold loading (0.005 wt.%) outperformed their pre-impregnated counterparts. Nonetheless, pre-impregnated samples improved after calcination at 400 °C, due to stronger Au–support interactions. The resulting Au/TiO₂ catalyst enabled photogeneration of hydrogen from water–ethanol mixtures under UV irradiation. The exposed surface area, dictated by filament diameter, correlated linearly with H₂ production, while increasing the number of layers did not proportionally enhance activity due to light attenuation, highlighting the need for geometry irradiation cooptimization. Among all designs, a post impregnated titania monolith with 200 µm filaments delivered the best performance, achieving a hydrogen yield of 0.24 molH₂⋅min⁻¹⋅gAu⁻¹.

Outlook

This review highlights the rapidly expanding field of 3D-printed catalysts for biomass conversion, with emphasis on metal-based, acid- and base-mediated, enzymatic, and photocatalytic systems. Across these diverse platforms, 3D printing has proven to be a versatile tool for designing catalysts with tailored structures and functionalities that enhance biomass valorization. Key advantages include structural tunability, which improves heat and mass transfer; enhanced catalytic efficiency; and greater stability and reusability. Their inherent compatibility with continuous flow systems also positions them as strong enablers of process intensification.

Compared with traditional fabrication methods, 3D printing provides distinct benefits such as rapid prototyping, customizable geometries, and flexible designs that can markedly improve performance. However, conventional methods remain more established for scalability and reproducibility, suggesting the two approaches currently play complementary roles. Persistent challenges include limitations in printable formulations, the need for post-processing to ensure mechanical integrity, constraints in large-scale manufacturing, and the absence of standardized testing protocols, which complicates comparisons and slows industrial adoption.

Emerging directions point toward advanced printable inks, hybrid catalytic architectures, and digitally guided designs optimized through artificial intelligence. Computer-aided design and generative design software (e.g., KATALYST 3D, Creo GDX, Magics) can be used to develop print-ready lattice catalyst supports with adjustable porosity and geometry187. Machine learning and physics-based simulation tools (e.g., HP/NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo surrogate models) can also be used to optimize printing factors such as porosity, mechanical stability, and flow or mass transfer characteristics188–190. Machine learning materials modeling (e.g., MLatom) can forecast catalyst material composition and performance, including active site dispersion and support interactions191,192. In parallel, adding techno-economic and life-cycle assessment perspectives will be crucial for determining the cost-effectiveness and environmental footprint of 3D-printed catalysts in comparison to conventional systems. Such evaluations can help drive material selection, process optimization, and scale-up strategies, ensuring that additive manufacturing offers concrete sustainability benefits on an industrial scale.

These developments are expected to expand the role of 3D printed catalysts in green chemistry, particularly within sustainable biorefineries where efficiency and flexibility are crucial. Successful industrial deployment will require collaborative efforts among academia, industry, and technology developers. Realizing the full potential of 3D-printed catalysts will depend not only on technical innovation but also on interdisciplinary partnerships that integrate materials science, chemical engineering, and digital manufacturing. With such combined efforts, 3D printed catalysts can become transformative tools for sustainable biomass valorization at scale.
