Testing the Missing Completely at Random Assumption for Functional Data
Maximilian Ofner, Siegfried Hörmann, David Kraus, Dominik Liebl

Abstract
We consider functional data which have only been observed on a subset of their domain. This paper aims to develop statistical tests to determine whether the function and the domain over which it is observed are independent. The assumption that data are missing completely at random (MCAR) is essential for many functional data methods handling incomplete observations. However, no general testing procedures have been established to validate this assumption. We address this critical gap by introducing a testing framework which is generally based on a partition of the observation patterns. Besides deterministic partitions, we also consider a systematic approach based on clustering. We establish asymptotic results for our tests and illustrate the methodology in several real data applications.



Keywords:
clustering, hypothesis testing, partially observed, random sets, stochastic processes.

1
Introduction

Over the past decades, functional data analysis (FDA) has garnered significant attention in statistical research and in various fields of applications. FDA provides a statistical framework for analyzing samples of functions varying over a continuous domain. For an introduction to methods in FDA, we refer to the monographs
Ramsay and Silverman, (2005)
,
Ferraty and Vieu, (2006)
,
Horváth and Kokoszka, (2012)
or
Hsing and Eubank, (2015)
. Depending on the design and structure of the data, one considers functions which are observed in one of three ways: (1) continuously, (2) on a dense grid, or (3) sparsely. In each case, the measurements are generally supposed to be adequately distributed over a common domain
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.

More recently, efforts have been directed toward developing techniques for functional data samples with varying domains, where each function is observable only on a subset
O\subset I
and missing on
I\setminus O
. Corresponding data has often been termed
partially observed functional data
,
functional fragments
or
functional snippets
. An early example is
Kraus, (2015)
, where incomplete heart rate profiles are analyzed. A subsample of the original data is depicted in Figure
1
(left); see also Section
6.2
for more information. The plot shows heart rate profiles (values in beats per minute) of
n=50
participants during the time interval 8 pm and 2 am. While some profiles were measured on the full domain, several profiles are only partially observed due to a removal or failure of the measuring device.

Figure 1
:
Subsample of
n=50
heart rate profiles with complete functions colored gray and incomplete functions colored black (left). The conditional means of complete and incomplete groups estimated over 878 subjects do not reveal a significant difference; the dashed lines refer to a 95% simultaneous confidence band (right).

The foremost research question which has been addressed to date for the above and related data sets is how to recover the missing information. Several reconstruction procedures have been proposed in the literature
(
Delaigle and Hall,, 2016
;
Descary and Panaretos,, 2019
;
Kneip and Liebl,, 2020
;
Kraus and Stefanucci,, 2020
;
Delaigle et al.,, 2021
;
Lin and Wang,, 2022
;
Elías et al., 2023b,
;
Ofner and Hörmann,, 2025
, see)
. In this work, we focus on another crucial question which has not yet received much attention: what is the missingness mechanism, and how does it depend on the observed data? Specifically, we propose several tests to assess the hypothesis that the missingness mechanism is independent of the data, commonly known as
missing completely at random
(MCAR). The importance of this hypothesis is underlined by the fact that all of the above references and many related papers are crucially based on this assumption
(see
Liebl,, 2013
;
Delaigle and Hall,, 2013
;
Liebl,, 2019
;
Kraus,, 2019
;
Kraus and Stefanucci,, 2019
;
Lin et al.,, 2021
;
Elías et al., 2023a,
;
Elías and Nagy,, 2024
, among many others)
. As we are going to demonstrate, our methodology provides several new insights into the analysis of real data:

•

in an example of heart rate profiles, our tests do not reveal evidence against the MCAR assumption which confirms the opinion of epidemiologists;

•

in an example of electricity price curves, our tests detect clear violations of the MCAR assumption which is in line with the structure of the electricity market;

•

in an example of temperature data, our tests reveal a potential defect of the temperature sensor and thus help to locate the technical cause of missingness.

Further details are discussed in Sections
6.2
-
6.4
and Figure
5
.

To the best of our knowledge, the current literature on MCAR testing in the context of FDA is limited to
Liebl and Rameseder, (2019)
. However, their approach only considers specific violations of the MCAR assumption that can be expressed using a low-dimensional monomial basis representation, enabling the use of a classical multivariate testing method. In contrast, our work explores a much broader range of MCAR violations, extending beyond those in
Liebl and Rameseder, (2019)
. Our approach accounts for violations that affect the entire distribution of the functional data, not just its low-dimensional components.

To explain our core idea, consider again the heart rate data in Figure
1
. If the data were MCAR, the distribution of
X
does not depend on
O
and the curves in the complete and incomplete group should thus reveal similar characteristics. Indeed, Figure
1
(right) shows that the groups cannot be distinguished by their means, which provides evidence for the MCAR assumption. More generally, we describe our testing methodology by the following principle:

1.

Assign each curve to one of two groups. This assignment is determined solely by
O
. An intuitive example is the ad hoc partitioning of data into complete and incomplete functions (see also Figure
1
), though a systematic clustering approach will also be discussed.

2.

Compare specific characteristics of the curves between these two groups. Under MCAR, both subsamples should share the same characteristics, as the partition is independent of the data.

The contributions of the underlying work are as follows. We propose a novel framework for testing MCAR based on a partition of the observation sets and discuss a systematic procedure for the selection of the corresponding groups. Then, we propose two different test statistics for the comparison of the groups and derive the asymptotic distributions. To this end, we also establish a theoretical framework for the analysis of càdlàg-valued (discontinuous) functional data that lie in the Skorokhod space
D[0,1]
instead of the commonly used
L^{2}[0,1]
or
C[0,1]
function spaces. Such a viewpoint is useful when studying uniform properties of empirical mean functions which naturally contain jumps in the case of partial observations. In addition, we introduce a method for constructing simultaneous confidence bands for the difference in mean functions of the groups discussed above. These bands provide an attractive graphical tool for detecting potential violations of MCAR. Moreover, we comment on the consistency of our proposed tests under general alternatives. The testing framework is finally illustrated in a simulation study and three real data examples.

X(t)

X

\mathcal{F}=\sigma(X(t)\colon t\in[0,1])

\sigma

\mathcal{F}_{S}=\sigma(X(t)\colon t\in S)
to be the
\sigma

. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the space of measurable subsets of
[0,1]
. We henceforth assume that the realized sample paths lie in the space
D[0,1]
of càdlàg functions, that is, functions which are right-continuous with left limits. Equipped with the Skorokhod metric, this space is Polish. As pointed out by
(

X\colon\Omega\to D[0,1]

\mathcal{K}
of non-empty compact subsets of
[0,1]
. Equipped with the Hausdorff metric,
\mathcal{K}
is a compact metric space and therefore Polish
(
Molchanov,, 2017
)
. Associated with

is the

\xi_{O}\colon t\mapsto\mathbbm{1}\{t\in O\}
which can be regarded as a random function. Note that the information in our case is twofold as it includes both the processes

\xi_{O}

\xi_{O}(t)

X(t)
is unobserved from

being equal to zero. An illustration is given in Figure

2.1
Hypothesis

We consider the
missing completely at random
assumption

H_{0}\colon\text{$X$ and $O$ are independent.}

(MCAR)

This assumption concerns a probabilistic property of the underlying missingness mechanism and is equivalent to
P(O\subset K|\mathcal{F})=P(O\subset K)
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}
. The quantity
P(O\subset K)
is called
containment functional
and uniquely determines the distribution of
O

(
Molchanov,, 2017
, p. 23)
. A weaker condition is
missing at random
(MAR), which can be defined similarly to
Farewell et al., (2022)
as

P\left(O\subset K|\mathcal{F}\right)=P\left(O\subset K|\mathcal{F}_{K}\right),\qquad\text{for all $K\in\mathcal{K}$.}

(MAR)

Whereas MCAR is a statement about independence of two random variables, MAR should not be confused with conditional independence between random variables. Related discussions can be found in
Seaman et al., (2013)
,
Mealli and Rubin, (2015)
, and
Farewell et al., (2022)
. Historically, a first thorough treatment of missing data was given by the seminal work of
Rubin, (1976)
. Data that are neither MCAR nor MAR are called
missing not at random
(MNAR). We will discuss different examples in the simulation study in Section
6
.

Aside from the fact that we want to test independence between two functional objects, which is a delicate problem on its own
(
Deb et al.,, 2020
, see, for instance)
, the crucial difficulty we face in this context is that
X
is not observable; we only observe
X
on
O
. Hence, existing approaches for complete data are not applicable here. For multivariate data with missing values, the arguably most prominent test for MCAR is due to
Little, (1988)
. Recent contributions to this topic include
Zhang et al., (2019)
,
Berrett and Samworth, (2023)
,
Aleksić, (2024)
, and
Bordino and Berrett, (2025)
. In the case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the number of distinct observation patterns is bounded by
2^{d}
. It is therefore possible to group data according to these patterns and consider an asymptotic scenario where the number of occurrences of each missingness pattern tends to infinity.
The main idea is then to verify whether the subdistributions estimated from these observation patterns are coherent.
This approach is generally infeasible for functional data since, in practice, all observation patterns are distinct. At the population level, we even have an uncountably infinite number of distinct observation sets. Hence, we propose to partition the observation patterns into two systematically chosen sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
and test if the distribution of
X
depends on whether
O\in\mathcal{A}
or
O\in\mathcal{B}
.

2.2
Testing framework

The random set
O
takes values in the space
\mathcal{K}
of non-empty compact subsets of
[0,1]
. We then consider a partition

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\mathcal{B}
for given non-empty subsets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
denotes the disjoint set union. A straightforward approach is to divide the data into complete and incomplete cases, or, more generally, to classify observations based on whether the Lebesgue measure of their observation set falls above or below a chosen threshold. A systematic approach based on clustering is discussed in Section
3
. We impose the following general assumptions on the partition.

Assumption 1
.

Let
A=\{O\in\mathcal{A}\}
and
B=\{O\in\mathcal{B}\}
. The partition satisfies

(i)

P(A)>0
and
P(B)>0
,

(ii)

\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\{P(t\in O|A),P(t\in O|B)\}>0
.

Condition (i) excludes the trivial case where all
O
are attributed to the same subset. Condition (ii) considers group-specific
coverage functions

t\mapsto P(t\in O|\cdot)
and assumes that the information in both subsamples covers the domain
[0,1]
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
1
(ii) is not always satisfied in practice. In such a case, one could restrict the analysis to a closed set
I\subset[0,1]
such that
\inf_{t\in I}\{P(t\in O|A),P(t\in O|B)\}>0
. Proposition
1
in Section
3.2
discusses a generic choice of
I
which guarantees (ii) for a systematic partition.

Now since
\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{K}
is a Polish space, the regular conditional distributions
P(X(t)\leq z|A)
and
P(X(t)\leq z|B)
exist
(
Dudley,, 2002
, see, for example, Theorem 10.2.2 in)
. For our testing problem, we then focus on directional alternatives which violate

H_{0}^{F}\colon\quad P(X(t)\leq z|A)=P(X(t)\leq z|B),\qquad\text{for all $z\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,1]$},

where
H_{0}^{F}\supset H_{0}
. Hypothesis
H_{0}^{F}
states that, for all
t\in[0,1]
, the conditional distribution of
X(t)
does not depend on whether
\{O\in\mathcal{A}\}
or
\{O\in\mathcal{B}\}
. This hypothesis is clearly satisfied under MCAR, where
X(t)
is independent of
O
and the violation of
H_{0}^{F}
covers many practically important MCAR violations (see Section
5
). A further comparison of the hypotheses
H_{0}
and
H_{0}^{F}
is given in the supplementary material. Note that it is in general impossible to
make inference about
P(X(t)\leq z|A)
and
P(X(t)\leq z|B)
as these terms are not estimable due
to the missing data. However, if MCAR holds true, these quantities are equal to
P(X(t)\leq z|t\in O,A)
and
P(X(t)\leq z|t\in O,B)
, respectively, which are estimable from the available data. The general steps of our testing framework are summarized in Algorithm
1
below.

Algorithm 1

Description of MCAR testing framework

1

Choose a partition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\mathcal{B}
;

2

Reject MCAR if

H_{0}^{F}\colon\quad P(X(t)\leq z|O\in\mathcal{A})=P(X(t)\leq z|O\in\mathcal{B}),\qquad\text{for all $z\in\mathbb{R}$, $t\in[0,1],$}

is violated;

Remark 2
.

Two questions naturally arise: why do we partition the observation patterns into only two groups, and why do we restrict the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{F}
to marginal distributions? The main motivation in both cases is the limited coverage of the partially observed data and, in particular, the resulting constraints on practical feasibility. Introducing additional groups would further reduce the available joint observation domain (see Remark
1
), thereby exacerbating data sparsity. This effect is particularly pronounced for finite-dimensional distributions (compare the bivariate case, where the joint domain is a subset of
[0,1]^{2}
), resulting in an even more substantial loss of information.

3
Clustering of observation sets

As outlined in Step 1 of Algorithm
1
, our testing methodology requires a partition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\mathcal{B}
of the observation sets. In this section, we propose a systematic procedure for the choice of this partition. The basic idea is to divide the observation sets into two clusters such that sets within a single cluster are more closely related than sets from different clusters. If a dependence exists between observation sets and functions, then similar (or dissimilar) observation sets are expected to correspond to similar (or dissimilar) functions. In this case, functions from different clusters will exhibit distinct characteristics and MCAR is rejected. While our methodology can be combined with any clustering algorithm, we restrict ourselves to a centroid-based clustering approach, which aligns well with our theoretical framework.

3.1
Distance between sets

To discuss a clustering procedure for set-valued random variables, we introduce a distance first. For two sets
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
, define
S_{1}\,\triangle\,S_{2}=(S_{1}\setminus S_{2})\cup(S_{2}\setminus S_{1})
to be the symmetric difference. Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
and define
d(S_{1},S_{2})=\lambda(S_{1}\,\triangle\,S_{2})
which is sometimes referred to as
Fréchet-Nikodym-Aronszajn
distance
(
Marczewski and Steinhaus,, 1958
)
. This yields a Polish space
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}/{\sim}
that consists of equivalence classes of sets in
\mathcal{S}
where
S_{1}\sim S_{2}
if and only if
\lambda(S_{1}\,\triangle\,S_{2})=0
. It is important to note that
S_{1}\,\triangle\,S_{2}=([0,1]\setminus S_{1})\,\triangle\,([0,1]\setminus S_{2})
which implies that any clustering based on
d
will yield equivalent results, regardless of whether the clustering is applied to observation or missingness patterns. This is a desirable property which does not hold if we chose
d
to be the Hausdorff distance. Moreover, we observe that

d(S_{1},S_{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\xi_{S_{1}}(t)-\xi_{S_{2}}(t)\rvert\,\textup{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\xi_{S_{1}}(t)-\xi_{S_{2}}(t)\rvert^{2}\,\textup{d}t,

and hence the Fréchet-Nikodym-Aronszajn distance between sets corresponds to an
L^{1}
-distance or squared
L^{2}
-distance of indicator functions
(
Marczewski and Steinhaus,, 1958
)
. Different notions of centers exist for set-valued random variables and we consider a particular definition which is due to Vorob’ev. The
Vorob’ev median

M_{0}
of
O
is defined as

M_{0}=\left\{t\in[0,1]\colon P(t\in O)\geq 1/2\right\}

and can be simply computed from the coverage function
t\mapsto P(t\in O)
. It satisfies
E[d(O,M_{0})]\leq E[d(O,S)]
for any measurable set
S\in\mathcal{S}
. For a general introduction to set-valued random variables, the interested reader is referred to
Molchanov, (2017)
.

3.2
Centroid-based clustering

Using the metric
d(S_{1},S_{2})=\lambda(S_{1}\,\triangle\,S_{2})
, we can define two clusters by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\left\{O\in\mathcal{K}\colon d(O,M_{A})\leq d(O,M_{B})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\left\{O\in\mathcal{K}\colon d(O,M_{A})>d(O,M_{B})\right\},

(1)

where
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
are fixed choices of set-valued centers. For a systematic choice, consider a subspace
\mathcal{M}_{0}\subset\mathcal{M}
and define

\{M_{A},M_{B}\}=\argmin_{\{M_{1},M_{2}\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{0}}E\left[\min\{d(O,M_{1}),d(O,M_{2})\}\right],

(2)

whose elements are called
optimal centers
of
O
. We assume that the minimizer
\{M_{A},M_{B}\}
of (
2
) is unique which automatically ensures that
M_{A}\neq M_{B}
. The optimal centers motivate a generic choice of the domain
I\subset[0,1]
that satisfies the condition in Remark
1
.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a centroid-based clustering which is induced by the optimal centers
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
defined in (
2
) for
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\mathcal{M}
. Let
I=M_{A}\cap M_{B}\neq\emptyset
. Then it holds that

\inf_{t\in I}\,\{P(t\in O|A),P(t\in O|B)\}\geq 1/2.

The minimization problem in (
2
) relates to the population version of a 2-medians clustering problem and
M_{A}
as well as
M_{B}
define the centers of related Voronoi cells. In simple cases, they can be computed explicitly as the following example demonstrates.

Example 1

(Monotone missingness pattern)
.

Let
D
be a random variable on
[0,1]
with cdf
F_{D}
and define
O=[0,D]
which realizes in the space
\{[0,d]\colon d\in[0,1]\}
. In this case, the centers are of the form
M_{1}=[0,m_{1}]
and
M_{2}=[0,m_{2}]
and it holds that

E\left[\min\{d(O,M_{1}),d(O,M_{2})\}\right]=E\left[\min\{\lvert D-m_{1}\rvert,\lvert D-m_{2}\rvert\}\right].

(3)

Minimizing the right-hand side of (
3
) in
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
relates to the quantization of a univariate probability distribution
F_{D}
. Following
Graf and Luschgy, (2000, p. 66)
, optimal
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
need to satisfy the following conditions,

\displaystyle 2F_{D}(m_{1})=F_{D}((m_{1}+m_{2})/2),

\displaystyle 2F_{D}(m_{2})=1+F_{D}((m_{1}+m_{2})/2).

For example, if
D\sim U[0,1]
, we obtain
m_{A}=1/4
and
m_{B}=3/4
which finally yields the optimal centers
M_{A}=[0,1/4]
and
M_{B}=[0,3/4]
. By Proposition
1
, the choice
I=M_{A}\cap M_{B}=[0,1/4]
then guarantees
\inf_{t\in I}\{P(t\in O|A),P(t\in O|B)\}\geq 1/2
and hence the condition in Remark
1
holds.

The partition in (
1
) clusters the observation intervals of Example
1
according to their length. Such sets also appear in the real data application of Section
6.3
.

3.3
Estimation of optimal centers

The optimal centers are generally unknown and need to be estimated from the data. To this end, define the
M
-estimator

\{\widehat{M}_{A},\widehat{M}_{B}\}=\argmin_{\{M_{1},M_{2}\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{0}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{d(O_{i},M_{1}),d(O_{i},M_{2})\}.

We tacitly exclude the case
\widehat{M}_{A}=\widehat{M}_{B}
, which occurs in the degenerate case where all
O_{i}
are identical. The minimum of the above function can be computed by a conventional
2
-median clustering algorithm using the Vorob’ev median to approximate the centers. Our hypothesis tests are invariant to the labeling of
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
, and since these centers are not separately identifiable, we assume without loss of generality that
d(\widehat{M}_{A},M_{A})\leq d(\widehat{M}_{B},M_{A})
. The following proposition yields consistency of the empirical centers in our case.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is compact with respect to
d
and the minimizer
\{M_{A},M_{B}\}
of (
2
) is unique. Then,

d(\widehat{M}_{A},M_{A})+d(\widehat{M}_{B},M_{B})=o_{p}(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Consistency of
k
-means in the multivariate case has been established by
Pollard, (1981)
. Our proposition is related to a work of
Jiang and Arias-Castro, (2021)
who derived the consistency of
k
-medoids in general metric and non-metric spaces. An example that satisfies the conditions of Proposition
2
is given by the observation patterns in Example
1
. We finally obtain the data-adaptive partitions

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\left\{O\in\mathcal{K}\colon d(O,\widehat{M}_{A})\leq d(O,\widehat{M}_{B})\right\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{O\in\mathcal{K}\colon d(O,\widehat{M}_{A})>d(O,\widehat{M}_{B})\right\}.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
P(d(O,M_{A})=d(O,M_{B}))=0
and
d(\widehat{M}_{A},M_{A})+d(\widehat{M}_{B},M_{B})=o_{p}(1)
. For any
i
, it holds that
P(O_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\,\triangle\,\widehat{\mathcal{A}})=P(O_{i}\in\mathcal{B}\,\triangle\,\widehat{\mathcal{B}})=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

For ease of reference, we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

For any
i
, it holds that
P(O_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\,\triangle\,\widehat{\mathcal{A}})=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

4
Testing methodology

We now discuss two approaches for testing the hypothesis
H_{0}^{F}
in Step 2 of Algorithm
1
. First, we consider a direct comparison of means and second, we examine a more general comparison of one-dimensional distributions.

4.1
Comparison of means

Let
\mu(t)=E[X(t)]
and consider the hypothesis

H_{0}^{\mu}\colon\quad E\left[X(t)|A\right]=E\left[X(t)|B\right],\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,1]$,}

where
H_{0}^{\mu}\supset H_{0}^{F}\supset H_{0}
. Hypothesis
H_{0}^{\mu}
states that the mean function of
X
is independent of whether
\{O\in\mathcal{A}\}
or
\{O\in\mathcal{B}\}
. This provides a useful benchmark for the more general distribution test of the next section. Under MCAR,
H_{0}^{\mu}
is clearly satisfied, and we can estimate the unknown quantities by the cross-sectionally available data,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{t\in O_{i},O_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\},

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{t\in O_{i},O_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\},

\displaystyle t\in[0,1],

and likewise
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}(t)
and
\widehat{p}_{\widehat{B}}(t)
for
\mathcal{B}
. Assumption
1
(ii) guarantees that the available information in both subsamples covers the domain
[0,1]
. Thus, the denominator
\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}
will be nonzero if the sample size is sufficiently large.

It follows under our assumptions that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}(t)\quad\xrightarrow{p}\quad\mu_{A}(t)=E\left[X(t)|t\in O,A\right],

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}(t)\quad\xrightarrow{p}\quad\mu_{B}(t)=E\left[X(t)|t\in O,B\right],

both pointwise and uniformly, as
n\to\infty
. Under MCAR, it holds that
\mu_{A}(t)=E[X(t)]
, as well as
\mu_{B}(t)=E[X(t)]
, and
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}
should thus be close to
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}
. This motivates a test statistic of the form

T_{\mu}=\sqrt{n}\,\lVert\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}-\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}\rVert_{\infty},

where
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\lvert f(t)\rvert
. It is important to note that the empirical means
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}
generally contain jumps and we thus utilize a central limit theorem for càdlàg-valued random functions. Whereas the central limit theorem requires only mild conditions in Hilbert spaces, more assumptions are required for the Skorokhod space
D[0,1]
. To apply corresponding results in the proof of Theorem
1
formulated below, we introduce additional assumptions. To this end, let
C>1
be some absolute constant which may have a different value at each appearance. The following assumption is adapted from
Hahn, (1978)
.

Assumption 3
.

The generic process
Y=(Y(t)\colon t\in[0,1])
satisfies
E[Y^{4}(t)]<C
and there exists some
\alpha>1
such that for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq 1
,

(i)

E[(Y(s)-Y(t))^{2}]\leq C\lvert t-s\rvert^{\alpha/2}
,

(ii)

E[(Y(s)-Y(t))^{2}(Y(t)-Y(u))^{2}]\leq C\lvert u-s\rvert^{\alpha}
.

The Brownian motion and Poisson process satisfy Assumption
3
. Indeed, since both processes satisfy condition (i) with
\alpha=2
, condition (ii) then directly results from the property of independent increments. An example which satisfies (i) and (ii) with
\alpha=4
is discussed in the following.

Example 2
.

Let
X=(X(t)\colon t\in[0,1])
be a stochastic process with continuously differentiable sample paths such that
E[\lVert X^{\prime}\rVert^{4}_{\infty}]<C
. For any
0\leq s\leq t\leq u\leq 1
, a pathwise application of the mean value theorem then yields

E\left[(X(s)-X(t))^{2}\right]\leq E[\lVert X^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}](t-s)^{2}
,
and
E\left[(X(s)-X(t))^{2}(X(t)-X(u))^{2}\right]\leq E[\lVert X^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{4}](u-s)^{4}
.

The next example illustrates a case of an indicator process
\xi_{O}=(\xi_{O}(t)\colon t\in[0,1])
that satisfies Assumption
3
.

Example 3
.

Consider independent random variables
U_{1},U_{2}
on
[0,1]
with continuously differentiable cdf
F
and define
O=[\min\{U_{1},U_{2}\},\max\{U_{1},U_{2}\}]\subset[0,1]
. Then, it can be shown that
P(\xi_{O}(t)\neq\xi_{O}(u))=2(F(u)-F(t))(1+F(t)-F(u))\leq C(u-t)
and
P(\xi_{O}(s)\neq\xi_{O}(t),\xi_{O}(t)\neq\xi_{O}(u))\leq 2(F(t)-F(s))(F(u)-F(t))\leq C(u-s)^{2}
. Hence, Assumption
3
holds with
\alpha=2
.

Equipped with the above assumptions, we are able to derive the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic based on the sup-norm.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
be satisfied and suppose that both
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
(\xi_{O_{i}})_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d.
D[0,1]
-valued random variables satisfying Assumption
3
with
E{[\lVert X\rVert_{\infty}^{2}]}<\infty
. Under MCAR,

T_{\mu}\xrightarrow{d}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\,{\lvert Z(t)\rvert},

as
n\to\infty
, where
Z
denotes a Gaussian process with continuous sample paths, zero mean, and covariance

\displaystyle k(s,t)=P(A)\,\frac{E[X^{c}(s)X^{c}(t)\xi_{O}(s)\xi_{O}(t)|A]}{P(s\in O,A)P(t\in O,A)}+P(B)\,\frac{E[X^{c}(s)X^{c}(t)\xi_{O}(s)\xi_{O}(t)|B]}{P(s\in O,B)P(t\in O,B)},

(4)

where
X^{c}(t)=X(t)-\left(\mu_{A}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{A\}+\mu_{B}(t)\mathbbm{1}\{B\}\right)
.

Theorem
1
utilizes a connection between partially observed functions and càdlàg processes. We believe that it is an interesting result in its own right, which has not been established in the literature to the best of our knowledge.

For approximating the distribution of
T_{\mu}
, one needs to estimate the covariance function. To this end, note that under MCAR, it actually holds that

\displaystyle k(s,t)=\mathbb{C}\textup{ov}(X(s),X(t))\left(\frac{P(s\in O,t\in O,A)}{P(s\in O,A)P(t\in O,A)}+\frac{P(s\in O,t\in O,B)}{P(s\in O,B)P(t\in O,B)}\right).

(5)

Consequently, estimators of
k
could be constructed from separate estimators for the factors involving
X
and
O
, respectively. In this case, it actually suffices to estimate
\mathbb{C}\textup{ov}(X(s),X(t))
at pairs
(s,t)
for which
P(s\in O,t\in O,A)+P(s\in O,t\in O,B)>0
; see also Theorem 1 in
Kraus, (2019)
for a discussion of covariance estimation in case of partially observed data. However, it is important to remark that the choice between (
4
) and (
5
) will ultimately affect the power of the resulting test. In the supplementary file, we define an estimator based on (
4
) which consistently estimates the covariance of
\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}-\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}})
even if MCAR is violated.

Algorithm
2
in the supplementary file summarizes the required steps for approximating the asymptotic distribution. A slight adaptation actually yields a construction method for simultaneous confidence bands of mean differences
\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{A}}-\widehat{\mu}_{\widehat{B}}
. This is shown in Algorithm
3
in the supplementary file where a confidence band of constant width is constructed. The procedure could be extended to more general shape functions or fairness constraints; see also
Liebl and Reimherr, (2023)
.

4.2
Comparison of distributions

We now opt for a more general approach and consider a simultaneous comparison of one dimensional distributions to test hypothesis
H_{0}^{F}
. To this end, let us define the available data estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\widehat{A}}(t,z)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}(t)\leq z,t\in O_{i},O_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\},

\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,1],

and define
\widehat{F}_{\widehat{B}}
in an analogous way. For
t
and
z
, it follows, under our assumptions, that

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\widehat{A}}(t,z)\xrightarrow{p}F_{A}(t,z)=P(X(t)\leq z|t\in O,A),

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{\widehat{B}}(t,z)\xrightarrow{p}F_{B}(t,z)=P(X(t)\leq z|t\in O,B),

as
n\to\infty
. Under MCAR,
F_{A}(t,z)=P(X(t)\leq z)
and
F_{B}(t,z)=P(X(t)\leq z)
. We therefore base a test on the comparison of
\widehat{F}_{\widehat{A}}
and
\widehat{F}_{\widehat{B}}
. To this end, consider the product measure
\lambda\otimes\nu
where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
and
\nu
some specific probability measure supported on
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}
. We consider the following Cramér–von Mises type test statistic

T_{F}=n\int_{[0,1]\times\Xi}\left(\widehat{F}_{\widehat{A}}(t,z)-\widehat{F}_{\widehat{B}}(t,z)\right)^{2}\,\textup{d}(\lambda\otimes\nu)(t,z).

Related tests have been explored by
Hall and Van Keilegom, (2007)
,
Bugni et al., (2009)
, and
Bugni, (2012)
.
The performance of the resulting test depends on the probability measure
\nu
. It can be chosen by the practitioner or in a data-adaptive way. A particular choice of
\nu
is discussed in Section
6
. To study stochastic properties of
T_{F}
, we consider the separable Hilbert space
H=L^{2}([0,1]\times\Xi,\lambda\otimes\nu)
. Then,
\widehat{F}_{\widehat{A}}
and
\widehat{F}_{\widehat{B}}
are viewed as
H
-valued random variables
\mathcal{X}
which satisfy
E[\lVert\mathcal{X}\rVert_{H}^{2}]<\infty
. Here,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H}
denotes the induced norm on
H
, that is to say,
\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{H}=\int_{[0,1]\times\Xi}f(t,z)^{2}\,\textup{d}(\lambda\otimes\nu)(t,z)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
be satisfied. Under MCAR,

T_{F}\quad\xrightarrow{d}\quad\int_{[0,1]\times\Xi}Z(t,z)^{2}\,\textup{d}(\lambda\otimes\nu)(t,z),

as
n\to\infty
, where
Z
is an
H
-valued Gaussian element with mean zero and covariance function

\displaystyle\rho(s,t,z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=P(A)\,\frac{E[\mathbbm{1}^{c}\{X(s)\leq z_{1}\}\mathbbm{1}^{c}\{X(t)\leq z_{2}\}\xi_{O}(s)\xi_{O}(t)|A]}{P(s\in O,A)P(t\in O,A)}

(6)

\displaystyle+P(B)\,\frac{E[\mathbbm{1}^{c}\{X(s)\leq z_{1}\}\mathbbm{1}^{c}\{X(t)\leq z_{2}\}\xi_{O}(s)\xi_{O}(t)|B]}{P(s\in O,B)P(t\in O,B)},

where
\mathbbm{1}^{c}\{X(t)\leq z\}=\mathbbm{1}\{X(t)\leq z\}-\left(F_{A}(t,z)\mathbbm{1}\{A\}+F_{B}(t,z)\mathbbm{1}\{B\}\right)
.

The approximation of the limiting distribution in Theorem
2
is discussed in the supplementary file.

5
Consistency

To discuss consistency of our tests, suppose first that
(X,O)
satisfies MCAR. Define a new process by
Y(t)=X(t)+\xi_{[0,1]\setminus O}(t)
for
t\in[0,1]
. Clearly
(Y,O)
violates MCAR but
(Y\xi_{O},O)=(X\xi_{O},O)
. This shows that it is generally impossible to distinguish all alternatives from the null based on the available data. In fact, any test has trivial power against all
compatible
alternatives. For a related discussion, the interested reader is referred to
(
Berrett and Samworth,, 2023
)
. In the following, we discuss practical conditions under which our tests are consistent.

Theorem 3
.

It holds that

(i)

if
\lVert\mu_{A}-\mu_{B}\rVert_{\infty}>0
and the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold,
T_{\mu}\xrightarrow{p}\infty
as
n\to\infty
;

(ii)

if
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
and the assumptions of Theorem
2
hold,
T_{F}\xrightarrow{p}\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

The theorem establishes general consistency results for our hypothesis tests. In case of a deterministic clustering, it can be shown that the tests have power against local alternatives of the form
\lVert\mu_{A,n}-\mu_{B,n}\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}
and
\lVert F_{A,n}-F_{B,n}\rVert_{H}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}
, for
c>0
; see also the remark after the proof of Theorem
3
. We now discuss general conditions which yield
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that there exists some
(t_{0},z_{0})\in\textup{int}\left([0,1]\times\Xi\right)
such that

(i)

(t,z)\mapsto F_{A}(t,z)-F_{B}(t,z)
is continuous at
(t_{0},z_{0})
;

(ii)

\{O\in\mathcal{A}\}\not\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\{X(t_{0})\leq z_{0}\}\mid\{t_{0}\in O\}
.

Then it holds that
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
.

The crucial condition is (ii) which states that, conditional on
\{t_{0}\in O\}
, the event
\{O\in\mathcal{A}\}
depends on
\{X(t_{0})\leq z\}
. For this class of alternatives we obtain
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
and Theorem
3
then implies that our distribution test is consistent. An example which satisfies (ii) is given in Example
4
below.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds, that
\mu_{A}(t)-\mu_{B}(t)
is continuous and that
\lVert\mu_{A}-\mu_{B}\rVert_{\infty}>0
. If
\nu
is supported on
\Xi
and for each
t\in[0,1]
it holds that
X(t)\in\textup{int}(\Xi)
almost surely, then
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
.

The above proposition shows that the distribution test is more general in the sense that it also detects differences in means provided that the measure
\nu
is chosen properly. To obtain further insights, consider the following example.

Example 4
.

Let
X=(B(t)\colon[0,1])
be a Brownian motion and assume the censoring mechanism
O=\{t\in[0,1]\colon X(t)\in[a,b]\}
, for some
a<0<b
. Consider the partition given by
\mathcal{A}=\{[0,1]\}
which corresponds to complete versus incomplete sets. It is easy to see for
a=-b
, the censoring does not affect the mean and hence we have
\lVert\mu_{A}-\mu_{B}\rVert_{\infty}=0
. However, using Proposition
3
, it can be shown that
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
.

Note that in Example
4
,
\lVert F_{A}-F_{B}\rVert_{H}>0
actually holds for arbitrary
a<0<b
. Together with Theorem
2
, it shows that a simple clustering of complete versus incomplete sets is sufficient for
T_{F}
to detect the violation of MCAR. Moving towards general
a\neq-b
, it is also possible to get consistency of
T_{\mu}
. An explicit computation of
\lVert\mu_{A}-\mu_{B}\rVert_{\infty}
seems out of reach for the general case; different types of arguments are needed for different pairs
a
and
b
. We focus here on the case, which presumably is the most difficult to detect for
T_{\mu}
, namely where
a=-b+\varepsilon
, with small
\varepsilon
(almost symmetric case) and where
b
is large (
\mu_{A}
and
\mu_{B}
are very close to zero).

Proposition 5
.

Take the same scenario as in Example
4
. Then there exist
b_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, such that if
a=-b+\varepsilon
for
b>b_{0}
and
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, then
\lVert\mu_{A}-\mu_{B}\rVert_{\infty}>0
.

The results will be further illustrated in the numerical simulations of the next section. Figure
4
shows censored paths along with the difference
\widehat{\mu}_{A}-\widehat{\mu}_{B}
in empirical means for the considered case of a Brownian motion and
(a,b)=(-1,2)
.

Case 2: Censoring.
Motivated by the theoretical results in Section
5
, we now examine the performance of the tests for data where MCAR is violated. We consider the following scenarios:

(MNAR)

O=\{t\in[0,1]\colon X(t)\in[a,b]\}

(MAR)

should reveal lower power as it is based on a comparison of means. Higher values of

7
Discussion

We have proposed a novel framework for testing MCAR in case of functional data which is an intricate problem as the number of different observation patterns is generally unbounded. Our method relies on a partition of the observation sets into two groups. These groups can either be chosen systematically or ad hoc by the practitioner. Simulation results indicate that the methodology is reasonably insensitive to the selection of groups provided that the sample size is large enough. For smaller sample sizes, employing an expert-guided deterministic selection approach could improve the performance. Concerning the comparison of groups, we considered approaches based on means and distributions. While the mean comparison allows for informative plots, the distribution test is generally recommended as both theoretical and numerical results suggest that it is consistent against a larger class of alternatives.
Another promising extension of our methodology is to investigate supervised classification methods, rather than unsupervised approaches such as
k
-medians. When suitable training data are available, supervised classification has the potential to increase the power of our testing procedure.

\mu_{C}(1)=E[B(1)|B(1)\in(a,b)]=\frac{\varphi(a)-\varphi(b)}{\Phi(b)+\Phi(-a)-1}.

We need to prove

E[B(\tau)|\tau\geq 1]\neq\frac{\varphi(a)-\varphi(b)}{\Phi(b)+\Phi(-a)-1}.

Set
a=-b+\varepsilon
for
b
large and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
sufficiently small. To prove the above inequality, we will show that

E[B(\tau)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau\geq 1\}]>\frac{\varphi(a)-\varphi(b)}{\Phi(b)+\Phi(-a)-1}.

(6)

•

Regarding the lhs of (
6
), Lemma
5
implies,

\displaystyle E[B(\tau)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau\geq 1\}]

\displaystyle\quad=aP(B(\tau)=a,\tau\geq 1)+bP(B(\tau)=b,\tau\geq 1)

\displaystyle\quad=-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big\{a(\Phi(a+2k(b-a))-\Phi(-a+2(k-1)(b-a)))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+b(\Phi(-b+2k(b-a))-\Phi(b+2(k-1)(b-a)))\Big\}.

For
a=-b+\varepsilon
, we get

\displaystyle E[B(\tau)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau\geq 1\}]

\displaystyle>2b(\Phi(3b-\varepsilon)-\Phi(b-\varepsilon)-\Phi(3b-2\varepsilon)+\Phi(b))

\displaystyle\qquad-2\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+\varepsilon+2k(2b-\varepsilon))-\Phi(b-\varepsilon+2(k-1)(2b-\varepsilon))\right\}.

A Taylor expansion yields

\displaystyle E[B(\tau)\mathbbm{1}\{\tau\geq 1\}]

\displaystyle>2\varepsilon b(\varphi(b)+\varphi(3b))-2\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+4kb)-\Phi(b+4(k-1)b)\right\}+o(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle>2\varepsilon\left(b\varphi(b)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+4kb)-\Phi(b+4(k-1)b)\right\}\right)+o(\varepsilon).

•

Regarding the rhs of (
6
), let
a=-b+\varepsilon
and note that
\Phi(b)+\Phi(b-\varepsilon)\to 2
as
b\to\infty
. Therefore,

\frac{\varphi(b-\varepsilon)-\varphi(b)}{\Phi(b)+\Phi(b-\varepsilon)-1}<\frac{3}{2}\,\left(\varphi(b-\varepsilon)-\varphi(b)\right),

provided that
b
is sufficiently large. Another Taylor expansion yields

\frac{\varphi(b-\varepsilon)-\varphi(b)}{\Phi(b)+\Phi(b-\varepsilon)-1}<\frac{3}{2}\,\varepsilon b\varphi(b)+o(\varepsilon).

To show (
6
), we thus need to prove

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\left(b\varphi(b)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+4kb)-\Phi(b+4(k-1)b)\right\}\right)-\frac{3}{2}\,\varepsilon b\varphi(b)+o(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\varepsilon b\varphi(b)\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+4kb)-\Phi(b+4(k-1)b)\right\}}{b\varphi(b)}\right)+o(\varepsilon)>0.

Since
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\Phi(-b+4kb)-\Phi(b+4(k-1)b)\right\}}{b\varphi(b)}\leq\frac{1-\Phi(b)}{b\varphi(b)}\to 0
as
b\to\infty
, the above inequality is true provided that
b
is sufficiently large. This finishes the proof of Proposition
5
.
∎

Z(t,z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{j}\psi_{j}(t,z),\qquad t\in[0,1],z\in\Xi.

refers to the eigenfunctions of the integral operator on
H=L^{2}([0,1]\times\Xi,\lambda\otimes\nu)
with kernel
\rho(s,t,z_{1},z_{2})=\mathbb{C}\textup{ov}(Z(s,z_{1}),Z(t,z_{2}))

\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\rangle_{H}=\delta_{jk}
where
\langle f,g\rangle_{H}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Xi}f(t,z)g(t,z)\,\text{d}(\lambda\otimes\nu)(t,z)
for elements
f,g\in H

(\zeta_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
are independent Gaussian random variables given by the projections
\zeta_{j}=\langle Z,\psi_{j}\rangle_{H}
. The
j

\zeta_{j}
has mean zero and variance
\kappa_{j}
where
(\kappa_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
refers to the sequence of eigenvalues of the corresponding integral operator. See also
Zhou and Pan, (2014)
for further details on principal components analysis of two-dimensional functional data. We obtain

\int_{[0,1]\times\Xi}Z(t,z)^{2}\,\textup{d}(\lambda\otimes\nu)(t,z)\quad\overset{d}{=}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{j}\chi_{1;j}^{2},

for a sequence
(\chi_{1;j}^{2})_{j=1}^{\infty}
of independent chi-square distributed random variables. To estimate the eigenvalues, we draw Monte Carlo points
\{(t_{k},z_{k})\colon k=1,\dots,K\}
from the distribution
\lambda\otimes\nu
. We then estimate

by the eigenvalues

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}(t_{k},t_{\ell},z_{k},z_{\ell})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{\tilde{\mathbbm{1}}_{i}(t_{k},z_{k})\tilde{\mathbbm{1}}_{i}(t_{\ell},z_{\ell})\xi_{O_{i}}(t_{k})\xi_{O_{i}}(t_{\ell})\mathbbm{1}\{O_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\}}{\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}(t_{k})\widehat{p}_{\widehat{A}}(t_{\ell})}

S2.2

Another option to approximate the distribution of our test statistics is to consider a bootstrap procedure; see also
Efron and Tibshirani, (1994)
for a general introduction. Bootstrap hypothesis tests for two samples of functional data have been considered in
Paparoditis and Sapatinas, (2016)
and
Kraus, (2019)
in a setting similar to ours. In case of the mean comparison, the bootstrap resamples are usually drawn groupwise under the null hypothesis of equal means. A corresponding adaption of this approach to our case is depicted in Algorithm
5
. In contrast, for a comparison of distributions, the resamples are typically drawn from a pooled sample under the null hypothesis of equal distributions (see also
Paparoditis and Sapatinas, (2016)
and
Kraus, (2019)
). However, a direct adaption to our case of MCAR testing seems impossible since the characteristics of observation patterns in the subgroups will generally be different under MCAR. For this reason, we also draw groupwise resamples for the distributional test and provide the details in Algorithm

S4
Comparison of hypotheses

To gain further insights, we compare the two main hypotheses:

\displaystyle H_{0}\colon

X
and
O
are independent,

\displaystyle H_{0}^{F}\colon

\displaystyle\quad P(X(t)\leq z|O\in\mathcal{A})=P(X(t)\leq z|O\in\mathcal{B})\text{ for all $z\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,1]$}.

Clearly,
H_{0}
implies
H_{0}^{F}
for any choice of the partition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\mathcal{B}
. The following examples show that the reverse implication is true under specific conditions but not in general.

S4.1
Example:
H_{0}
is implied by
H_{0}^{F}

Let
Y
and
W
be two independent random functions which are independent of some observation set
O
. Define X(t) = Y(t) +
δ
W(t)
1
{O ∈
A
},   t ∈[0,1], where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
. Here,
X
relates to a contaminated version of
Y
and the parameter
\delta
measures the degree of contamination. If
H_{0}^{F}
holds, then it follows in particular that

\textup{Var}(X(t)|O\in\mathcal{A})=\textup{Var}(X(t)|O\in\mathcal{B})
which entails Var(Y(t)) +
δ
^2 Var(W(t)) = Var(Y(t)), and thus
\delta=0
. Since
\delta=0
,
X=Y
which is independent of
O
, so
H_{0}
is satisfied.

S4.2
Example:
H_{0}
is false but
H_{0}^{F}
true

Let
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
be two independent random variables with standard normal distribution. Define

\displaystyle X(t)=\begin{cases}\zeta_{1},\qquad&\text{for $t\in[0,0.5)$},\\
\zeta_{2},\qquad&\text{for $t\in[0.5,1]$,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle O=\begin{cases}[0,1],\qquad&\text{if $\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}>0$},\\
[0,0.5],\qquad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Take
t_{1}\in[0,0.5)
and
t_{2}\in[0.5,1]
. The joint distribution of
(X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}))
clearly depends on whether
\{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}>0\}
or
\{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\leq 0\}
and
H_{0}
is violated. On the other hand, the marginal distributions of
X(t_{1})
and
X(t_{2})
are standard normal, both on the event
\{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}>0\}
and on the event
\{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\leq 0\}
. Therefore,
H_{0}^{F}
holds as it is based on a comparison of univariate marginal characteristics.
