Regional variations in prevalence and factors associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation in Nigeria
Authors: Funmilola Folasade Oyinlola, Kwala Adline Okorafor, Joseph Ayodeji Kupoluyi, Gbemisola Bolanle Ogbeye, Leopold Ouedraogo, Laila Umar, Immanuel Oluwadare Shittu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe utilisation of maternal healthcare services remains low in Nigeria despite government efforts to enhance access through the establishment of maternal and child health programs. Nigeria is a multiethnic country, and most national policies and programmes aimed at improving utilisation of maternal healthcare services were erroneously implemented equally across different geopolitical zones with little success. Thus, this study examines regional variations in the prevalence and factors associated with maternal healthcare service utilisation in Nigeria.MethodsThe 2021 Nigeria Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) was employed for the study. A weighted sample size of 25,062 women of reproductive age was analysed using STATA SE Version 17. Descriptive statistics (frequency distribution table and chart) were used to gain an understanding of the characteristics of the study population. Inferential statistics (Pearson’s Chi-square test of association) were performed to establish the association between the socio-demographic characteristics of respondents and maternal healthcare service utilisation. All explanatory variables with significant evidence of no collinearity were selected and used in the multilevel mixed-effects binary logistic regression models to examine the factors influencing regional variation in maternal healthcare service utilisation at a p < 0.05 level of significance.ResultsThe study found persistently low utilisation of maternal healthcare services (antenatal care, delivery care, and postnatal care services) across all regions, with prevalence rates ranging from 30.24% in the South South to 38.27% in the North West. The odds of utilising maternal healthcare services were significantly higher among women with secondary level of education and above, currently in union, wealthier and who desire more children than their counterparts who are not. For instance, women who have primary, secondary, and tertiary education had higher odds of utilising maternal healthcare services than their counterparts with no formal education.ConclusionThis study provides compelling evidence of low utilisation of maternal healthcare services across Nigeria’s regions. This underscores the imperative for targeted policy interventions for each region. Therefore, a one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be effective, and policymakers must consider the distinct characteristics and challenges of each region when designing interventions aimed at improving maternal healthcare service utilisation in Nigeria.Keywords: Utilisation, Healthcare, Maternal, Regional, Nigeria

Background

Maternal mortality continues to pose a significant public health challenge in many developing countries, including Nigeria. In 2023, approximately 260,000 mothers worldwide died from pregnancy-related complications, which averages to around 712 maternal deaths per day [1, 2]. The majority of these deaths (9 in 10) took place in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in rural areas, highlighting a critical need for urgent action to address this imbalance [2, 3]. Additionally, 1 in 30 mothers in these regions’ experiences pregnancy-related complications [3]. Every day, about 800 women lose their lives due to preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth, which equates to 1 maternal death every 2 min [3–5].

The prevalence of maternal mortality can be seen mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it accounts for more than two-thirds (70%) of global maternal deaths [6]. This high rate is largely due to the inadequate utilisation of maternal healthcare services. Additionally, the failure to properly utilise these services increases the risk of neonatal morbidity and mortality, especially in developing countries, where the rate of neonatal mortality is particularly alarming, with 92 deaths per 1,000 live births, approximately 15 times higher than in developed countries, which are primarily urban areas [3, 4]. Studies have shown that nearly 80% of maternal mortality and approximately two-thirds of neonatal mortality could be prevented if effective maternal healthcare is utilised throughout pregnancy, delivery, and postnatal care [2, 4, 7].

In Nigeria, the utilisation of maternal healthcare services remains low despite government efforts to enhance access through the establishment of maternal and child health programs [8, 9]. Launched in 2010, this program aims to improve the uptake of routine services, increase attendance at antenatal care, strengthen the healthcare system, enhance healthcare workers’ skills in providing maternal, newborn, and child interventions, and improve the use of health information management systems. Nevertheless, evidence indicates that only 52% of women attended at least four antenatal visits, 38% delivered in healthcare facilities, and 37% accessed postnatal care, which exposes them to a high risk of maternal and neonatal mortality [8]. This indicates a significant decrease in the percentage of women who accessed antenatal care during their pregnancies, subsequently delivered in healthcare facilities and an even more pronounced decline in the rates of postnatal care utilisation. Several factors have been linked to the insufficient utilisation of health services among women, including age, education level, socioeconomic status, distance to healthcare facilities, place of delivery, residential location, exposure to media, birth order of the child, family structure, and women autonomy which also differs across rural and urban areas as well as geopolitical regions [9, 10].

The utilisation of maternal healthcare services varies between rural and urban areas, shaped by socio-demographic and socio-economic factors. In Nigeria, women residing in rural regions frequently experience considerable deprivation and are less likely to access maternal healthcare services than those living in urban settings [11]. In 2018, only 26% of births in rural areas took place in health facilities, in contrast to 61% of births in urban settings [12]. As a result, women in rural regions experience a significantly higher risk of adverse health outcomes, including maternal mortality and morbidity, compared to their urban counterparts. Also, Women living in rural areas of Nigeria often do not utilise maternal healthcare services due to limited availability, structural factors, and the influence of a patriarchal society where men serve as the primary decision-makers in their communities. This situation contrasts with urban areas where access and decision-making dynamics differ [13]. Additionally, a study conducted in Ethiopia found that the employment status of pregnant women is associated with both the non-use and underutilisation of skilled maternal healthcare services, which is primarily due to time constraints arising from their work and household responsibilities [14].

Numerous studies have investigated regional variations in the utilisation of maternal healthcare services and the associated risk factors. For example, a study [14] conducted on the underutilisation of skilled maternal healthcare in Edo State revealed that a significant shortage of skilled healthcare personnel, a lack of respectful care, and insufficient medical supplies, equipment, and infrastructure contributed to the limited use of maternal health services in rural areas. Another study [13] conducted to examine the relationship between maternal healthcare utilisation and women’s empowerment in Bangladesh revealed that women in urban areas tend to be more empowered, possess greater autonomy, and exert more influence within their households and communities. As a result, they are more likely to seek healthcare services for themselves and their children compared to their rural counterparts. Additionally, research [10] conducted to understand trends in maternal health facility utilisation and women’s autonomy in Nigeria highlighted that women’s decision-making autonomy is a crucial factor in maternal health and well-being. It also indicated that maternal health service utilisation is significantly lower among women in rural areas compared to those in urban settings, primarily due to the reduced autonomy experienced by rural women. Furthermore, a study [15] explored maternal healthcare service utilisation and its associated risk factors across 37 low- and middle-income countries. The findings identified wealth as a significant factor influencing maternal health service utilisation in countries such as India, Indonesia, and Nigeria. Specifically, financially well-off women, who are predominantly located in urban areas, have better access to healthcare resources and prefer higher-quality hospital facilities. The study also noted that women with higher education levels living in urban areas are more likely to access healthcare information through media exposure, which can enhance their utilisation of healthcare services.

While these studies provide valuable insights into the differences in the utilisation of maternal healthcare services across rural and urban areas in Nigeria, they typically examine individual regions separately. Additionally, they primarily focused on maternal and neonatal mortality as the risk factors related to inadequate use of maternal healthcare services, without thoroughly exploring these issues. Moreover, these studies rely solely on data from the National Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) conducted between 2005 and 2015. They do not investigate broader factors influencing women’s empowerment, overlook other relevant Nigerian data sources beyond the NDHS, and fail to comprehensively analyse the risk factors associated with the underutilisation of maternal healthcare services and their regional variations.

Therefore, this study aims to enhance the existing body of knowledge by examining regional variations in the prevalence of maternal healthcare service utilisation and its associated risk factors in Nigeria, utilizing data from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) which is a reliable source of health and demographic data and is more recent, being from 2021, compared to the most recent Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS), which was conducted in 2018.

Rationale for investigating regional variations

From the theoretical standpoint, this study rests on the Cosmopolitan-Success and Conservative-Failure Hypothesis (CSCFH) [16] to explain the regional variation in maternal health service utilisation in Nigeria. The CSCFH hypothesis explained the differences between the cosmopolitan and conservative communities in embracing new ideas, such as the utilisation of maternal health services. The hypothesis submits that more cosmopolitan societies are more likely to experience success in health policies, programmes and innovation than in conservative societies [16–18]. This is because cosmopolitan societies embrace new ideas, technologies, social and cultural changes in various aspects of life more than conservative societies, which are characterised by a strong compliance to uphold traditional institutions, customs and values, and resistance to alteration in the socio-cultural, political philosophy and ideology of the community.

Following this theory, in Nigeria, national policies and programmes aimed to improve utilisation of maternal healthcare services have been erroneously implemented homogeneously across different geopolitical zones. Meanwhile, Nigeria is a multiethnic country with more than 250 ethnic groups, among which the Yoruba, Hausa/Fulani and the Igbo are the dominant groups. Administratively, the country was grouped into six geopolitical zones and 774 constitutionally recognised local government areas (LGAs) [19]. The six geopolitical zones are further categorised into two regions: North (North Central, North East, and North West) and South (South East, South South, and South West). Of great concern is the socio-cultural, religious and economic differences across the regions, worthy of investigating in the utilisation of maternal healthcare services [20]. In addition to this, the federal government of Nigeria provides more infrastructure and services within major cities, particularly in the Northern parts of the country, than in the Southern parts of the country. Thus, it is expected that states, communities, and residents in the Northern part of the country (with conservative values) will be hostile to new ideas and innovation in maternal healthcare services utilisation and thus, less likely to use maternal health services compared to their counterparts in the Southern part of the country with cosmopolitan values.

Methods

Data source

Data from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2021, a nationally representative household sample of 38,806 households residing in non-institutional dwelling units in rural and urban Nigeria, was used for this study. The list of enumeration areas (EAs) for the 2006 census, updated in February 2021, was the sampling frame of the 2021 MICS survey. The primary sampling units (PSUs), which were considered as clusters in the 2021 MICS, were defined based on Enumeration Areas (EAs) from the 2006 census EA frames [21]. A total of 25,063 women who have ever given birth among women of reproductive age were eligible and interviewed for the survey. Thus, the data on women were obtained and analysed from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey dataset. The survey provided estimates of the key health indicators at the national and regional levels, as well as for urban and rural areas.

Survey design

The Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) conducted in 2021 was used for this study. The MICS 2021 estimates the situation of women from all six geopolitical zones in Nigeria. It utilised a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling to select the survey sample, where the list of the enumeration areas (EAs) from the 2006 census updated in February 2021 was used for the sampling frame of the 2021 survey [21]. The frame was first stratified into 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) in the country, and the sample of households was selected in two stages. Each stratum had a specified number of census enumerations selected systematically with probability proportional to size, while 20 households were systematically sampled at the second stage. Within each household, all women aged 15–49 years are included in the survey. A random systematic sampling procedure was used to select both the enumeration areas and the households for the survey. Additionally, a total sample of 25,063 was utilised after the sample was weighed, forming the basis for reporting the survey result.

Measurement of variables

Independent variables

The variables used in this study were selected because of their association with the outcome variables from previous studies [11, 22–24] as well as their availability in the MICS Dataset. To validate the extent to which the independent variables influence the maternal healthcare services utilisation among women of reproductive age in Nigeria, the independent variables were grouped into socio-demographic factors (such as age, highest level of education, marital status, place of residence, ethnicity, and wealth index) and mode of delivery. For the socio-demographic variables, maternal age was coded from the dataset as “15–19”, “20–24”, “25–29”, “30–34”, “35–39”, “40–44”, and “45–49” years, respectively. The highest level of education was recoded as “No education”, “Primary”, “Secondary,” and “Higher”. Marital Status was recoded as “Never in Union”, Formerly in Union” and “Currently in Union”. Ethnicity was recoded as “Hausa”, “Igbo”, “Yoruba” and “Others”. Wealth Index was recoded as “Poor”, “Middle” and “Rich”. Mode of delivery was also recoded as “Normal Delivery” and “Caesarean Section”. Place of residence was categorised into Urban and Rural. More so, desire for children was measured using wanted the last child then and wanted child later or did not want more children. Those with “Wanted the previous child” was measured dichotomously using No as “0” and yes as “1”; and the other variable was wanted child later or did not want more children later as “1” and no more as “2”. Also, health insurance coverage was measured dichotomously using No as “0” and yes as “1”.

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed using Stata SE Version 17, a statistical analysis program incorporating the complex survey design used in the MICS. Since the complex survey design effect is essential in estimating the precision of survey estimates, household sampling weight was used in analysing the women’s dataset. Enumeration areas were used as our Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) or clusters to incorporate the design features into Stata for the analysis. Stratification was done with regions as stratum identifiers. In checking errors, we examine the distribution of each of the variables. We checked that all observations relate to allowed categories for categorical variables and that frequencies in each category make sense.

The socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents (women of reproductive age) were examined to gain an understanding of the characteristics of the study population, to organise and check the frequency distribution of the respondents, respectively and which was subject to the weight as stated above. Graphical illustrations of dependent categorical variables were presented with multiple charts. Univariate and bivariate levels of analysis between the independent variables and the outcome variable were conducted to determine the association. The associated factors in the study were determined using the Chi-square test of association. Before the last stage of the analysis (multivariable analysis), a multicollinearity test was performed among the explanatory variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5). All explanatory variables with no significant evidence of collinearity (mean VIF = 1.23, maximum = 1.87 and minimum VIF = 1.01) were selected and used in the multilevel mixed-effects binary logistic regression model to examine the relationship between selected explanatory variables and the outcome variable. In estimating the models, three models were constructed. The first model (Model 0) was fitted with no explanatory variable. The second model (Model 1) was fitted with only the region variable. The third model (Model 2) was fitted with explanatory variables holding region as a random intercept. The fixed effects were expressed as the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at 95% confidence intervals (CI) and were calculated in the model to estimate the strength of the association between explanatory variables and the outcome variable. The random effects were expressed as the Inter-Class Correlation (ICC) Coefficient, and Proportional Change in Variance (PCV), and were calculated to measure the random effects or variation within or between clusters. The ICC was calculated as: ICC = VA / (VA + π2/3) × 100, where VA denotes the estimated variance and π2/3 = 3.29. The PCV was calculated as: PCV = [(VA – VB) / VA] × 100, where VA is the variance in utilisation of antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care in the empty model; and VB is the variance in successive models. The Bayesian Deviance Information Criterion (BDIC) and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) were used for statistical model selection. The tools help in comparing multiple models fitted to the same dataset by balancing the model’s goodness of fit with its complexity, and selecting the most parsimonious model that best explains the data without overfitting. Thus, the model with the lower value of AIC or BIC is considered the better fit regression model (Boco, 2010).

Outcome variables

The outcome variable in the present study was maternal healthcare services utilisation. This variable has three main components: Antenatal Care Service (ANC), Skilled Delivery Service, and Postnatal Care Service (PNC). Regarding the utilisation of ANC, three indicators (number of antenatal visits, timing of first ANC visit, and the type of person seen for antenatal care during pregnancy) were used to generate a composite for ANC. Firstly, the women were asked about the number of antenatal visits they made during their recent pregnancy. The responses were recoded as 0–7 visits = 0 “No” and 8 and above visits = 1 “Yes” [25]. Secondly, women were asked about the timing of the first ANC visit, whether the first visit occurred within the first trimester, which was considered ideal (i.e. conception to 12 weeks). The responses were recoded as: before 12 weeks = 1 “Yes” and after 12 weeks = 0 “No”. Lastly, women were asked the type of person they saw for antenatal care during pregnancy. The responses were recoded as skilled birth attendant (doctor, nurse, or midwife or auxiliary midwives) = 1 “yes” and not skilled birth attendant = 0 “No”. For skilled delivery services, two indicators were used to generate a composite. The place of delivery and the skilled birth attendance at the place of delivery. The place of delivery was determined based on the place of birth, with “0” representing those who gave birth outside of a medical facility and “1” representing those who gave birth either in public or private medical sectors [22, 26]. In addition, skilled attendance at health facility delivery was determined based on a dichotomous variable where respondents were coded as “1” if women had a skilled birth attendant (doctor, nurse, midwife or auxiliary midwives) and otherwise “0”.

The third component of maternal healthcare service utilisation is the postnatal care service. This is the care received by mother and child immediately after childbirth. Four indicators, namely: whether the mother was checked after the delivery was over by a skilled health provider, the place where the mother’s health was checked, number of hours/ days after delivery for the first check of mother happened, and number of hours/ days after delivery for the first check of baby happened were used to generate composite for postnatal services. Responses to these indicators were measured dichotomously and coded as “1” if yes and otherwise “0”.

Ethical consideration

This study analysed survey data from UNICEF, where all participants’ personally identifiable information had been removed. The National Statistical Office, the National Bureau of Statistics, and UNICEF obtained informed consent from survey participants before participating. In addition, upon completing the registration process, the authors were granted permission to download and use the datasets. The data are available online: https://mics.unicef.org/surveys.

Frequency distribution of the socio-demographic characteristics of respondents across regions

Educational attainment varies significantly across regions. The North East and North West have the highest percentages of women with no formal education (64.25% and 65.79%, respectively), pointing to substantial educational disparities in these regions. In contrast, the South West, South East and South South have the highest percentages of women with secondary education (50.26%, 56.42%, and 57.57% respectively), reflecting better access to education. Tertiary education is most prevalent in the South East (26.94%) and South West (20.48%), while it is very low in the North West (3.75%) and North East (4.17%). More so, the result shows that the majority of women across all regions are formerly married, with percentages ranging from 79.10% in the South-South to 95.91% in the North West. This could indicate high rates of widowhood, divorce, or separation, particularly in the North West. The South-South has the highest percentage of never-married women (6.16%), while the North West has the lowest (0.35%), suggesting regional differences in marriage patterns and cultural norms.

Furthermore, considering the wealth index, the North East and North West have the highest percentages of women in the poor wealth category (68.08% and 61.90%, respectively), highlighting higher poverty levels in these regions. Conversely, the South West and South East have the highest percentages of women in the rich wealth category (68.36% and 67.21%, respectively). The result also revealed a strong regional concentration of some ethnic groups. The Hausa ethnic group dominates the North West (78.98%) and North East (26.99%), while the Igbo ethnic group is highly concentrated in the South East (97.67%). The Yoruba ethnic group is most prevalent in the South West (74.44%). The South-South region is the most ethnically diverse, with “Others” making up 88.06% of women.

In a nutshell, the place of residence revealed that the South West has the highest percentage of urban women (76.08%), while the North East has the lowest (19.19%). The North East and North West are predominantly rural (80.81% and 74.82%, respectively), suggesting lower levels of infrastructure development and access to services in these regions.

Frequency distribution of desire for children, mode of delivery and insurance coverage of respondents across regions

Table 2 presents a frequency distribution of desire for children, mode of delivery, and health insurance coverage among women of reproductive age across six regions in Nigeria. The result provides insights into regional differences in reproductive health preferences and healthcare access across all regions. Regarding the desire for children, the result shows that most women across all regions reported that their last child was unwanted. However, there are notable regional variations in the wantedness of the last child. For instance, the South-South region has the highest percentage of women (81.20%) who did not want their last child, while the North West has the lowest (61.24%). Conversely, the North East has the highest percentage of women (36.11%) who wanted their last child. When it comes to wanting more children, most women across all regions do not desire additional children, with percentages ranging from 90.06% in the South-South to 95.62% in the North Central. The South-South also stands out for having the highest percentage of women (9.94%) who want more children later, while the South East has the lowest (4.48%). Furthermore, regarding the mode of delivery, normal delivery is the most common method across all regions, with percentages ranging from 97.71% in the South West to 99.21% in the North West. Caesarean section rates are relatively low, with the highest percentage in the South West (2.29%) and the lowest in the North West (0.79%). Finally, the result on health insurance coverage reveals that a significant majority of women across all regions do not have health insurance. Coverage rates are particularly low, ranging from 95.14% in the South West to 98.85% in the North East. The South West has the highest percentage of women with health insurance (4.86%), while the North East has the lowest (1.15%).

Proportion of maternal healthcare service utilisation of respondents across the region

The results presented in Fig. 1 highlight significant disparities in the utilisation of maternal healthcare services (such as antenatal care (ANC), skilled delivery, postnatal care (PNC) and maternal healthcare services utilisation) among women of reproductive age across six regions. Overall, the findings reveal low utilisation rates for all services, with regional variations that underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve maternal healthcare access and outcomes. The result shows that most women across all regions did not utilise ANC services. The proportion of women who did not access ANC ranged from 61.73% in the South West to 69.76% in the South South. The South East had the highest percentage of women who utilised ANC (33.23%), while the South South had the lowest (26.96%). Also, for skilled delivery services, the proportion of women who did not use skilled delivery services ranged from 67.22% in the South East to 87.54% in the North West. The South East had the highest percentage of women who utilised skilled delivery services (32.78%), while the North West had the lowest (12.46%). More so, postnatal care (PNC) utilisation is the lowest among the three services, with most regions reporting utilisation rates below 20%. The proportion of women who did not access PNC ranged from 79.37% in the North West to 87.65% in the North Central. The North West had the highest percentage of women who utilised PNC (20.63%), while the North Central had the lowest (12.35%). Also, the maternal healthcare services utilisation rates are notably low, ranging from approximately 28.49% in the North East to 33.8% in the South East. Conversely, non-utilisation rates are high, ranging from 69.17% in the South West to 71.51% in the North East.

Bivariable analysis

Association between socio-demographic characteristics and maternal healthcare utilisation of respondents across regions

Table 3 presents the association between socio-demographics and maternal healthcare utilisation of respondents across regions. Significant association was determined using the chi-square (χ²) test at p < 0.05. Maternal healthcare services in the study were categorised into antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care. The results showed that region, age, educational level, marital status and wealth index were significantly associated with antenatal care services (p < 0.05). The proportion of antenatal care utilisation was highest in the South East (33.23%), followed by North West (32.83%), North East (32.07%), and South South (27%) with the lowest. There existed a significant association between region and antenatal care utilisation (χ² = 51.08, p < 0.001). Age of respondents showed a significant association with antenatal care services (χ² = 2920.16, p < 0.001). More than half of the women 15–19 years (55.21%), and 20–24 years (51.17%) utilised ANC services than those in the age group 25–29 years (45.09%), 30–34 years (37.08%), and 45–49 years, with the lowest utilisation rate (5.48%). Similarly, educational level also showed that women with secondary education (35.94%) and tertiary (33.65%) used antenatal care services more than those with primary (26.98%) and respondents with no formal education (23.6%). Also, marital status was significantly associated with antenatal care services (χ² = 383.09, p < 0.001). Currently married respondents have the highest utilisation rates of antenatal care services (32.58%), followed by those not in a union (30.34%). In the same vein, wealth index as a socio-demographic factor was also significantly associated with antenatal care services (χ² = 31.42, p < 0.01), with those in middle and rich households reporting 32.5% and 32.12% respectively. Furthermore, the results show a statistically significant association between desire for children and utilisation of ANC services (χ2 = 1786.53, p < 0.001). Respondents who wanted children now or later (77.9%) use ANC services than those who wanted no more children (27.67%).

Association between socio-demographic characteristics and maternal healthcare services utilisation of respondents across regions

For delivery care, the results revealed that region, age, educational level, place of residence, marital status, ethnicity and wealth index were significantly associated with delivery care among women of reproductive age (p < 0.05). There are significant variations in the use of delivery care services across regions, with women in South East, South West and North Central showing high use with 32.78%, 27.30% and 23.54% respectively (χ² = 673.35, p < 0.001). Considering age, there exist a significant association with delivery care (χ² = 1653, p < 0.001). For instance, respondents in the age group 20–24 years (34.43%), 15–19 years (30.58%), 25–29 years (29.56%) and 30–34 years (26.96%) utilised delivery care services. Similarly, there was a significant association between respondents’ educational level and delivery of care (χ² = 1358.70, p < 0.001). Women with secondary and higher education showed a high rate of delivery care utilisation, with 32.61% and 30.03% respectively. Residential areas also showed that women from urban areas (27.37%) use delivery care services more than those from rural areas (16.69%), with a significant association (p < 0.05). Marital status and ethnicity were also significantly associated with delivery care services (p < 0.05). Slightly over one-fourth (25.52%) and one-fifth (22.07%) of respondents who never married and currently in union used delivery care services more than those formerly in union. Also, there exist a positive significant association between wealth index and the delivery care services (χ2 = 664.52, p < 0.001). Respondents from high wealth index (28.43%) households utilised delivery care services more than middle (21.8%) or poor (13.74%) households. The results also show a statistically significant association between desire for children and utilisation of delivery care services (χ2 = 1786.53, p < 0.001). Respondents who wanted children now or later (55.87%) use delivery care services than those who wanted no more children (18.74%). Similarly, there is a statistically significantly association between health insurance coverage and utilisation of delivery care services.

Concerning postnatal care services, results showed a statistically significant association between region and postnatal care services (χ² = 136.19, p < 0.001). Age of respondents was also identified as a significant determinant of postnatal care service utilisation (χ² = 1593.01, p < 0.001). Also, respondents’ educational level was significantly associated with postnatal care service utilisation (χ² = 22.38, p < 0.001), while marital status and ethnicity were significantly associated with postnatal care service utilisation with p-value 0.001 and 0.01 respectively. Furthermore, the results show a statistically significant association between desire for children and utilisation of postnatal care services (χ2 = 1112.56, p < 0.001).

Multivariable analysis

Tables 4, 5 and 6 present the fixed and random effects results of the multivariable multilevel analysis of factors associated with antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care services among women of reproductive age, with the adjusted odds ratios (AOR), 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) and p-value. The results showed an inverse relationship in the odds of utilising antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care services with the age of the respondents. The higher the age of respondents, the lower the utilisation of antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care services. There was a strong positive relationship between the educational level of respondents and utilisation of antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care services. The results showed that the likelihood of utilising ANC services was higher for respondents who had attained tertiary education (OR = 1.75, 95%CI [1.49, 2.05], p < 0.001), secondary education (OR = 1.65, 95%CI [1.48, 1.84], p < 0.001), and primary education (OR = 1.15, 95%CI [1.15, 1.44], p < 0.001) than for respondents with no formal education [RC]. Also, that the likelihood of utilising delivery services was higher for respondents who had attained tertiary education (OR = 3.43, 95%CI [2.87, 4.10], p < 0.001), secondary education (OR = 2.88, 95%CI [2.53, 3.27], p < 0.001), and primary education (OR = 1.73, 95%CI [1.51, 1.98], p < 0.001) than for respondents with no formal education [RC]. On the other hand, the results also showed that the likelihood of utilising postnatal services was higher for respondents who had attained secondary education (OR = 1.13, 95%CI [1.00, 1.29], p < 0.05) than for respondents with no formal education [RC]. Furthermore, the likelihood of utilising antenatal care, delivery care and postnatal care services was lower among respondents who are in rural areas than in urban areas. For example, the likelihood of utilising antenatal care and delivery care services was lower for respondents who were in rural areas (OR = 0.91, 95%CI [0.80, 1.03], p < 0.001), (OR = 0.80, 95%CI [0.70, 0.92], p < 0.01) respectively than respondents who had never married [RC].

Discussion

This study examined the regional variations in prevalence and factors associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation in Nigeria. The study showed a higher representation of young women of reproductive age (20–29 years) across all the regions except the Southeastern region, which has a record of a high proportion of older women [27, 28]. Over time, the Southeastern and Southwestern regions have been identified as the educationally oriented states while the Northern region has been known as the least least-educationally informed state, which is consistent with the result of this study [27]. Most of the women were formerly married, which may reflect cultural norms, such as divorce or high widowhood rates, particularly in the Northwest and South-south regions [29]. There was wealth disparity between the regions, with the Northeast and Northwest having a high proportion of poverty, while the Southeast and Southwest had a high proportion of wealth index [28]. The Hausa ethnic group predominantly populates the Northwest and Northeast, with the Igbo ethnic group dominating the Southeast, the Southwest inhabited by the Yoruba ethnic group, and there was ethnic diversity in the South-south region [29]. The Southwest region had a high proportion of urban dwellers, with the Northeast and Northwest having a high proportion of rural dwellers [8].

The study also showed the variations in the desire for children, mode of delivery and health insurance coverage across the region. The desire for children varied among women of reproductive age across the region, with most of the women not wanting to have additional children. This non-desire can be associated with the experiences of childbirth they have had, individual and household factors, etc [30]. Women rarely desire to go under the knife for delivery; thus, normal delivery is rampant among women of reproductive age. Hence, the report of a high proportion of normal delivery among women across the region with little variation indicates a homogenous mode of delivery among the women [31]. Health insurance is one of the systems built to provide for financial incapacity in order to cater for the health of an individual. However, women, especially pregnant women, rarely make use of this system, which can be due to a lack of knowledge or awareness, unavailability of the healthcare insurance system, etc [7].

The proportion of utilisation of antenatal care service, skilled delivery service, and postnatal care services was low among the women across the regions. Antenatal care services utilisation among women of reproductive age is a compounding issue in Nigeria. As revealed in this study, women across the regions rarely utilise these services. This underutilisation could be associated with the inefficiency of these services, a sense of incompetence of the health professionals, limited education, household wealth, and exposure to media [9, 32]. In addition to this, skilled delivery service utilisation fosters safe birth and preserves both the mother and child. However, women across the regions, particularly the Southeast and Northwest, rarely utilise the service as reported by this study. This underutilisation can be associated with individual, community, and state factors, affordability, accessibility, and the availability of services [8, 14, 16–18]. Similarly, postnatal care also suffers from non-utilisation by women across the regions. This is associated with employment status, monthly income, health insurance status, etc [5].

At the bivariate level, the findings of the study revealed that age, education, place of residence, marital status, and wealth index are significantly associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation among women across the Northern regions, except for ethnicity, which does not share significance. This finding highlights the significance of age, education, place of residence, marital status, and wealth index in influencing the decision to make use of maternal healthcare services. Notably, women who are younger in age have a high tendency to make use of these services compared to older women who have the belief that they don’t need such services. More so, women who have higher educational attainment have a tendency to use these services, hence the association. The rurality or urbanity of the area a woman finds herself in at any point in time will affect her decision to utilise maternal healthcare services. The marital status of the woman at a point in time is expected to shape her decision to utilise maternal healthcare services, and also the wealth capacity of the woman and household is expected to affect the utilisation of maternal healthcare services [11, 24, 33].

Similarly, age and marital status play a major role in the utilisation of maternal healthcare services among women in the Southern regions as opposed to the Northern regions. Notably, age and marital status are identified as key factors influencing the utilisation of maternal healthcare services among women in this region, amidst other known factors, and this can be related to the premium that is placed on age and marital status in the region [5, 27]. Also, desire for children and mode of delivery were significant factors associated with maternal healthcare services among women across the Northern regions, while health insurance coverage was not significant in the determination of utilisation. Significantly, women who still desire or do not desire to have more children will at one point in time seek maternal healthcare services like the family planning services, counselling, etc., hence, the influence of desire for children on utilisation of maternal healthcare services. More so, the dimension that delivery will take influences the decision to utilise or not utilise maternal healthcare services [15, 34].

Likewise, in the Southern regions, desire for children and mode of delivery were significant factors associated with maternal healthcare services among women, while health insurance coverage was not significant in the determination of utilisation. Significantly, women with the desire to or without desire to have more children are expected to, at some period in time, seek the use of maternal healthcare services like counselling, contraceptive use, etc., thus, the influence of desire for children on utilisation of maternal healthcare services. More so, the mode of delivery influences the decision to utilise or not utilise maternal healthcare services [4, 29].

The analysis of multiple relationships revealed that the age of the respondents, educational level, marital status, wealth status, and desire for more children are significantly associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation. Significantly, there was an inverse relationship between the age of respondents and utilisation of maternal healthcare services. In addition, there was a positive relationship between the education of respondents and the utilisation of maternal healthcare services.

This finding makes a reflection of the transformational power of education amongst women in the Northern regions [29]. Married women have a high likelihood of utilising maternal healthcare services. Women in the middle index were more likely to utilise maternal healthcare services compared to their counterparts who were not. This indicates the power of wealth in influencing the strength of making healthy decisions for women [9]. Notably, when the desire to have children is no more, the tendency to go for certain healthcare services will surely reduce; hence, women who do not want more children will definitely have a low tendency to utilise maternal healthcare services [32].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study leveraged the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) dataset, a robust, nationally representative household survey employing a stratified cluster sampling technique. By harnessing this large-scale national survey, the research provides a dependable source of information on maternal healthcare services and utilisation. The MICS dataset is acclaimed for its rigorous methodology and representative sampling, enhancing the study’s generalizability and reliability. Furthermore, the study’s focus on regional variations across the six geopolitical zones offers nuanced insights into the complex dynamics of each region and their health outcomes. By examining regional variations in prevalence and factors associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation, the study addresses a crucial aspect of women’s reproductive health. The study’s methodological adeptness is substantiated through the analysis of secondary data using appropriate statistical methods. Overall, this study adds to the growing body of research on maternal health and maternal healthcare services utilisation, particularly in the Nigerian context.

Notwithstanding the study’s methodological strengths, the reliance on secondary data from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) dataset constrains the study’s capacity for granular control over variables and measurement instruments. The predefined survey questions and response categories may not comprehensively capture the nuances of regional variations and maternal healthcare services utilisation, potentially introducing measurement biases that could compromise the accuracy of the findings. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of the MICS dataset precludes the establishment of causal relationships between regional variations and maternal healthcare services utilisation, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies to elucidate the temporal dynamics between these variables. Consequently, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the results to diverse populations or environments, highlighting the need for replication studies in other contexts to enhance the external validity of the research.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence of pronounced regional disparities in the prevalence and determinants of maternal healthcare services utilisation among women of reproductive age in Nigeria. The findings unequivocally demonstrate that regional variations exist in the prevalence and factors associated with maternal healthcare services utilisation, corroborating existing literature that highlights the importance of considering individual, household, and community-level variables. The persistently low utilisation of maternal healthcare services across Nigeria’s regions, underscores the imperative for targeted policy interventions. The study’s results have significant implications for health policy and practice, emphasising the need for regionally-focused health interventions tailored to the unique needs of each region. The findings suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach is unlikely to be effective and that policymakers must consider the distinct characteristics and challenges of each region when designing interventions aimed at improving maternal healthcare service utilisation.
