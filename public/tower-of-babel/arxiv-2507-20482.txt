Cutoff for the Swendsen–Wang dynamics on the complete graph
Antonio Blanca, Zhezheng Song

Abstract
We study the speed of convergence of the Swendsen–Wang (SW) dynamics for the q q -state ferromagnetic Potts model on the n n -vertex complete graph, known as the mean-field model. The SW dynamics was introduced as an attractive alternative to the local Glauber dynamics, often offering faster convergence rates to stationarity in a variety of settings. A series of works have characterized the asymptotic behavior of the speed of convergence of the mean-field SW dynamics for all q ≥ 2 q\geq 2 and all values of the inverse temperature parameter β > 0 \beta>0 . In particular, it is known that when β > q \beta>q the mixing time of the SW dynamics is Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Theta(\log n) . We strengthen this result by showing that for all β > q \beta>q , there exists a constant c ⁡ ( β , q ) > 0 c(\beta,q)>0 such that the mixing time of the SW dynamics is c ⁡ ( β , q ) ​ log ⁡ n + Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) c(\beta,q)\log n+\Theta(1) . This implies that the mean-field SW dynamics exhibits the cutoff phenomenon in this temperature regime, demonstrating that this Markov chain undergoes a sharp transition from “far from stationarity” to “well-mixed” within a narrow Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \Theta(1) time window. The presence of cutoff is algorithmically significant, as simulating the chain for fewer steps than its mixing time could lead to highly biased samples.



1
Introduction

The
q
-state ferromagnetic Potts model is a classical spin system model central in statistical physics, theoretical computer science, and discrete probability.
Given a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, a set of spins or colors
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,q\}
, and an edge interaction parameter
\beta>0
, it is defined as a probability distribution over the configurations in
\Omega=\{1,\dots,q\}^{V}
, which correspond to the spin assignments from
\mathcal{S}
to the vertices of
G
.
Formally, the probability of a configuration
\sigma\in\Omega
is given by:

\mu(\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z}\cdot e^{\beta M(\sigma)},

(1)

where
M(\sigma)
denotes the number of edges of
G
with the same spin in both of its endpoints in
\sigma
(i.e., the monochromatic edges of
G
in
\sigma
), and
Z
is the normalizing constant or partition function.
The edge interaction parameter
\beta
is proportional to the inverse temperature in physical applications; the special case
q=2
corresponds to the classical ferromagnetic Ising model.

The algorithmic problem of efficiently generating approximate samples from

\mu
has attracted much attention in theoretical computer science. For the Ising model (i.e., for
q=2
), seminal results from the 1990s
[
JS93
,
RW99
]
provide a polynomial-time approximate sampling algorithm for
\mu
. Additional approximate-sampling algorithms
have been discovered since then for the ferromagnetic Ising setting
[
CGHT16
,
GJ17
]
,
but all have a large running time of at least
O(n^{10})
where
n=|V|
. For
q\geq 3
, the sampling problem is computationally hard, specifically #BIS-hard
[
GJ12
,
GŠVY16
]
, but efficient approximate sampling algorithms are still possible for many interesting families of graphs; see
[
HPR20
,
JKP20
,
CDF+22
,
CDK24
,
BG23a
]
.

An algorithm with the potential of providing both faster algorithms for the
q=2
case and a technology for understanding the feasibility boundary of approximate sampling for
q\geq 3
is the
Swendsen–Wang (SW) dynamics

[
SW87
]
.
This sophisticated Markov chain exploits the connection between the ferromagnetic Potts model and the random-cluster model
[
ES88
]
and was designed to bypass some of the key difficulties associated with sampling from
\mu
at low temperatures (large
\beta
).
In particular,
the SW dynamics
is conjectured to converge to
\mu
in
O(n^{1/4})
steps when
q=2
on any graph
G
for any
\beta>0
. This Markov chain was also recently used to the expand the graph families for which approximately sampling can be efficiently done for
q\geq 3
and
\beta
large
[
BG23a
]
.

The SW dynamics transitions from a configuration
\sigma_{t}\in\Omega
to
\sigma_{t+1}\in\Omega
as follows:

1.

Independently, for every
e=\{u,v\}\in E
if
\sigma_{t}(u)=\sigma_{t}(v)
include
e
in
M_{t}
with probability
p=1-e^{-\beta}
;

2.

Independently, for every connected component
\mathcal{C}
in
(V,M_{t})
, draw a spin
s\in\{1,\dots,q\}
uniformly at random, and set
\sigma_{t+1}(v)=s
for all
v\in\mathcal{C}
.

The SW dynamics is reversible with respect to
\mu
and thus converges to it
[
SW87
,
ES88
]
. We refer to Step 1 as the percolation step and to Step 2 as the coloring step.

The SW dynamics is a highly non-local Markov chain, updating and potentially changing the spin of each vertex in each step, making its analysis quite difficult in most settings. Still,
significant progress has been made in understanding the speed of convergence of this dynamics in various geometries, including the complete graph
[
CF99
,
GJ97
,
LNNP14
,
GŠV19
,
GLP19
]
,
finite subsets of the
d
-dimensional integer lattice graph
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

[
Ull14
,
BS17
,
BCSV18
,
NS19
,
BCP+21
]
,
trees
[
Hub04
,
BCŠV22
]
,
random graphs
[
BG21
,
COGG+23
,
BG23b
]
,
locally-tree-like graphs
[
BG23a
]
,
graphs with sub-exponential growth
[
BG23a
]
,
graphs of bounded
[
BCV20
,
BCC+22
]
or unbounded degree
[
BZ23
]
,
as well as general graphs when
q=2

[
GJ17
]
. A setting that has attracted particular interest and that will be the focus of this work is the complete graph, also known as the
mean-field model
.

On the complete graph, it is natural to re-parametrize the model and use
\beta/n
instead of
\beta
; i.e.,
\mu(\sigma)\propto\exp(\frac{\beta}{n}\cdot M(\sigma))
.
A detailed connection between the phase transition of the mean-field model, which we describe in detail in Remark
1.2
, and
the
mixing time

of the SW dynamics emerged from
[
LNNP14
,
GŠV19
,
GLP19
]
.
The mixing time of a Markov chain
is defined as the number of steps until the Markov chain is close in total variation distance to
its stationary distribution starting from the worst possible initial configuration, and it is the most standard notion of speed of convergence to stationarity. Formally, if
P
is the transition matrix of the chain,
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\tau_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)=\max_{\sigma\in\Omega}\min_{t\geq 0}\{\|P^{t}(\sigma,\cdot)-\mu(\cdot)\|_{\textsc{tv}}\leq\varepsilon\},

and by convention
\tau_{\rm mix}:=\tau_{\rm mix}(1/4)
.

When
q\geq 3
, the mixing time
\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}
of the SW dynamics satisfies:

\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}=\begin{cases}\Theta(1)&\textrm{if}~\beta<\beta_{\textsc{l}};\\
\Theta(n^{1/3})&\textrm{if}~\beta=\beta_{\textsc{l}};\\
e^{\Omega({n})}&\textrm{if}~\beta\in(\beta_{\textsc{l}},\beta_{\textsc{r}});\\
\Theta(\log n)&\textrm{if}~\beta\geq\beta_{\textsc{r}};\end{cases}

(2)

see
[
GŠV19
,
GLP19
]
.
It is known that
\beta_{\textsc{r}}=q
, but
\beta_{\textsc{l}}
does not have a closed form, and it is given instead implicitly as the root of a certain equation
[
GŠV19
,
LL06
]
.
When
q=2
,
\beta_{\textsc{l}}=\beta_{\textsc{r}}=q
and there is no slow-mixing window; in particular,
the mixing time of the mean-field SW dynamics for
q=2
is
\Theta(1)
for
\beta<q
,
\Theta(n^{1/4})
at the critical point
\beta=q
, and
\Theta(\log n)
for
\beta>q

[
LNNP14
]
.

In this work, we provide sharper results for the mixing time of the SW dynamics in the
\beta>\beta_{\textsc{r}}=q
regime, further refining our understanding of the SW dynamics in the mean-field model. We show that there exists a constant
c(\beta,q)>0
such that
\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}=c(\beta,q)\log n+\Theta(1)
,
which implies that this Markov chain exhibits
cutoff
. A Markov chain exhibits cutoff
if there is a sharp drop in the total variation distance from close to
1
to close
0
in an interval of time of smaller order than
\tau_{\rm mix}
which is called the
cutoff window
; i.e., we say that there is cutoff if as
n\to\infty
, for any fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\tau_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)-\tau_{\rm mix}(1-\varepsilon)=o(\tau_{\rm mix}).

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
and
\beta>\beta_{\textsc{r}}=q
. Then, there exists a constant

c(\beta,q)>0

such that SW dynamics for the

q
-state mean-field ferromagnetic Potts model exhibits cutoff at mixing time

\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}=c(\beta,q)\log n
with cutoff window
\Theta(1)
; that is,
\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}=c(\beta,q)\log n+\Theta(1)
.

We provide the expression for the constant
c(\beta,q)
in (
4
).

The only previously known mixing time cutoff result for the SW dynamics
was on the integer lattice graph for sufficiently small enough
\beta

[
NS19
]
; as such, ours is the first such result in the low-temperature SW dynamics.
For the much simpler (local) Glauber dynamics, which updates a single randomly chosen vertex in each step, cutoff is known in the mean-field through the high-temperature
\beta<\beta_{\textsc{l}}
regime
[
LLP10
,
CDL+12
]
. For the special case of the Ising model,
cutoff has also been established for the Glauber dynamics for several graph families; see, e.g.,
[
LS13
,
LS17
,
Kim22
,
vdHK+21
]
.

Recall from (
2
) that the mixing time of the SW dynamics is
\Theta(1)
when
\beta<\beta_{\textsc{l}}
and exponentially slow when
\beta\in(\beta_{\textsc{l}},\beta_{\textsc{r}})
, so whether the chain exhibits cutoff is of limited significance in those parameter regimes.
It is an interesting question whether the SW dynamics exhibits cutoff at the dynamical critical points
\beta=\beta_{\textsc{l}}
or
\beta=\beta_{\textsc{r}}
. In the former, mixing is not entirely governed by a “strong drift” towards a typical spin count distribution and relies on the variance of the dynamics as well; as such, it is unlikely that the dynamics exhibits cutoff at
\beta=\beta_{\textsc{l}}
. For
\beta=\beta_{\textsc{r}}
, the SW dynamics exhibits a first-order drift, but the percolation step is critical, which significantly complicates even the asymptotic mixing time analysis.

From an algorithmic perspective, for a Markov chain like the SW dynamics
that exhibits cutoff at
c(\beta,q)\log n
, knowing only the asymptotic mixing time, e.g., that
\tau_{\rm mix}^{\mathrm{SW}}=\Theta(\log n)
, is insufficient in practice, since simulating the dynamics for say
\frac{c(\beta,q)}{2}\log n
steps, is guaranteed to result in a sample with total variation distance close to
1
.

We conclude this introduction with some brief remarks about our proof and techniques. We construct a refined, multi-phase coupling that converges to a pair of configurations with roughly the correct (expected) number of vertices in each spin class. (In the mean-field
setting
when
\beta>\beta_{\textsc{r}}
, under the stationary distribution
\mu
, there is one dominant spin and all other spins are assigned to roughly the same number of vertices.)
Our coupling gradually contracts the distance to this type of configuration over two phases: first to a linear distance and then to within
O(\sqrt{n})
distance. The second phase determines the leading order of the mixing time. Earlier analyses (e.g.,
[
GŠV19
]
) used simpler arguments
that yield the
O(\log n)
mixing time bound; such arguments treat step deviations “pessimistically” and cannot provide the sharper bound we derive here.
Our analysis carefully accounts for how deviations are amortized over time (i.e., we show that early fluctuations do not matter as much).

An innovation of the present argument is the use of a
q\times q
projection to a partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{q}\}
of
V
at
low temperatures
. This projection contains
information about the number of vertices assigned each spin in each
V_{i}
and its convergence is known to imply that of the SW dynamics due to the symmetry of the mean-field model. This idea
was used before in
[
CDL+12
,
LNNP14
,
GŠV19
]
but in the high-temperature
\beta<\beta_{\textsc{l}}
setting where the percolation steps of the SW dynamics result in sub-critical random (sub)graphs with trivial connected component structures. In the
\beta>\beta_{\textsc{r}}
regime, we must contend with the existence of a linear-sized connected component and how it is distributed in the partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{q}\}
. To address this, we have an additional step in our analysis in which we argue that the
fraction of vertices assigned each spin in each
V_{i}
coalesces to the overall fraction of vertices with that spin in the full configuration before the full configuration reaches a typical spin count (i.e., before the end of second phase of the coupling). This additional guarantee allows us to design the required coupling.

Remark 1.2
.

As mentioned earlier, the mixing behavior of the SW dynamics described in (
2
) is tightly connected to the order-disorder phase transition of the mean-field model which we describe next for completeness. This phase transition
occurs at the critical value
\beta=\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
, where
\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)=q
when
q=2

and

\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)=2\Big(\frac{q-1}{q-2}\Big)\log(q-1)

for
q\geq 3
. When
\beta<\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
, the number of vertices assigned each spin is roughly the same with high probability up to lower-order fluctuations. In contrast, when

\beta>\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
there is a dominant spin in the configuration with high probability.
For
q\geq 3
, the model exhibits
phase coexistence
at the critical threshold
\beta=\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
; this means that at this point, the set of configurations with no dominant spin and the
set of configurations with a dominant spin (with all other non-dominant spins assigned to roughly the same number of vertices), contribute each a constant fraction of the probability mass with all other configurations contributing 0 mass (in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
). The effects of the phase coexistence phenomenon extends to the window
(\beta_{\textsc{l}},\beta_{\textsc{r}})
around
\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
; this window is known as the metastability window and coincides with slow-mixing regime for the SW dynamics. For
q=2
, on the other hand, there is no phase coexistence at
\beta_{\textnormal{{cr}}}(q)
or metastability, so there is no slow mixing.

Preliminaries

In this section, we gather a number of standard definitions and results that we will use in our proofs.

2.1
Couplings

A one step coupling of the Markov chain
with state space
\Omega

specifies
for every pair of states
(X_{t},Y_{t})
a probability distribution over
(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})
such that the processes
\{X_{t}\}
and
\{Y_{t}\}
are each faithful realizations of the chain, and if
X_{t}=Y_{t}
then
X_{t+1}=Y_{t+1}.
The
coupling time

is defined by

T_{\mathrm{coup}}=\max_{x,y\in\Omega}\min\limits_{t}\{X_{t}=Y_{t}\mid X_{0}=x,Y_{0}=y\}.

It is a standard fact that if
\Pr(T_{\mathrm{coup}}>t)\leq\varepsilon
, then

\tau_{\rm mix}(\varepsilon)\leq t
; see, e.g.,
[
LP17
]
.
In addition, to bound
\tau_{\rm mix}=\tau_{\rm mix}(1/4)
, it suffices to bound
\Pr(T_{\mathrm{coup}}>t)
by any constant
\varepsilon<1
, as
the bound can then be boosted by repeating the coupling a constant number of times.
For establishing cutoff,
however, this approach is not feasible, as it would increase the mixing time bound by a multiplicative factor. As such, throughout our proofs, we must track the probability of success of our multi-stage couplings; in particular, our overall coupling must be designed such that
\Pr(T_{\mathrm{coup}}>t)\leq 1/4
.

Let

denote the fraction of vertices of
V_{i}
assigned the dominant in
X_{t}
; i.e., there are
\alpha_{1,i}(X_{t})|V_{i}|
such vertices.
The following lemma ensures that during the second phase, in parallel to making progress towards
m
, at some time
T\leq T_{1}+T_{2}
, the fraction of vertices assigned the spin from the dominant spin class in each
V_{i}
is roughly the same fraction as that from the full configuration
X_{t}
in
V
.
This additional property will allow us to couple
the two process in
O(1)
additional steps in the third phase.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X_{0}
be an arbitrary initial configuration and let
\mathcal{V}
be a
\lambda
-partition of
V
with constant
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exist a constant
C>0
such that with probability at least
\delta
when
T\geq\frac{\log n}{2\log\big(\frac{q}{q-1}\big)}+\frac{\log(2/C)}{\log\big(\frac{q}{q-1}\big)}
, we have

\max_{k\in[q]}|\alpha_{1}(X_{T})-\alpha_{1,k}(X_{T})|\leq{C}/{\sqrt{n}}.

We note that
(2\log\big(\frac{q}{q-1}\big))^{-1}<c(\beta,q)
by Fact (
2.8
), and so at time
T_{1}+T_{2}
we reached two configurations that are close to
m
, i.e., satisfy (
5
), but that also have
\approx\!a|V_{i}|
vertices assigned the dominant spin in each partition set
V_{i}
.

For the third phase, we consider another lower-dimensional projection of the SW dynamics; in particular, we zoom into a projection of dimension
q\times q
. For
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, let
A_{t}:=\{A_{ij,t}\}_{i,j\in[q]},

with
A_{ij,t}
denoting the number of vertices in
V_{i}
assigned color
j
in
X_{t}
.
Similarly, define
A_{t}^{\prime}
for
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

We have shown that after the first two phases of the coupling, for any
constant
\hat{\delta}\in(0,1)
there is a suitable constant
\hat{C}>0
such that at time
T=T_{1}+T_{2}
with probability at least
\hat{\delta}
the following holds:

A1.

\|\alpha(X_{T})-m\|_{\infty}\leq\hat{C}/\sqrt{n}
and
\|\alpha(Y_{T})-m\|_{\infty}\leq\hat{C}/\sqrt{n}
;

A2.

|\alpha_{1,i}(X_{T})-\alpha_{1,i}(Y_{T})|\leq\hat{C}/\sqrt{V_{i}}
for all
i\in[q]
.

Under these assumptions, we design a coupling such that
A_{T+1}=A_{T+1}^{\prime}
with probability
\Omega(1)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
be a constant independent of
n
.
Let
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{1},...,V_{q}\}
be a
\lambda
-partition and let
A_{t}
and
A_{t}^{\prime}
be two copies of the projection chain such that
A1
and
A2
above hold.
Then, there exists a coupling of
A_{t+1}
and
A_{t+1}^{\prime}
such that with probability
\Omega(1)
, it holds that
A_{t+1}=A_{t+1}^{\prime}
.

This lemma implies that we can obtain two configurations such that
A_{T+1}=A_{T+1}^{\prime}
with probability
\Omega(1)
. Similar couplings were also
designed in
[

,
GŠV19
]
but for the high-temperature

setting where all the
spin classes have roughly the same number of vertices and the percolation step is sub-critical in all classes. Here, we have to contend with the presence of a dominant spin class and a super-critical percolation step; this is the reason for the additional step in our analysis (Lemma
3.4
) that guarantees that the extra assumption A2 holds after the first two phases.

The final step of our coupling is a simple boosting phase so that the overall probability of success is at least

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X_{0}
and

be configurations such that

and

In summary, we have shown that there exists a

It was already noted in

which completes the proof of the upper bound in Theorem

.
The lower bound in this theorem follows straightforwardly from

We proceed with the proof of Lemma

Let

Let

k

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose

\varepsilon>0
. Then, for any
t\geq 0
and any constant
\delta\in(0,1)

\delta

\|\alpha(X_{t})-\kappa(F^{t}(\alpha_{1}(X_{0})))\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose

. Then, if

.

Follows from Lemmas

and

∎

We proceed with the proof of Lemma
4.1
for which we will need the following concentration of the fluctuations.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
X_{t}
is such that

\|\alpha(X_{t})-m\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exists
\gamma_{0}(\beta)
such that for any

\Pr\Big(\|\alpha(X_{t+1})-\kappa(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t})))\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\leq e^{-\Omega(r)}+O(r^{-2}).

Proof.

Since
\beta>q
and
a>1/q
by Fact
2.5
,
a\beta>1
and
b\beta<1
. By assumption

\|\alpha(X_{t})-m\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, so when
\varepsilon
is small enough, exactly one color class in the percolation step of the SW dynamics is supercritical, and the remaining
q-1
color classes are subcritical.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
denote the connected components after the percolation step from
X_{t}
, sorted in decreasing order by size and let
R=\sum_{i\geq 2}|C_{i}|^{2}
.
By Lemma
2.4
there exist constants
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}>0
such that
\mathrm{E}[R]=\gamma_{0}n+O(1)
and

\mathrm{Var}[R]\leq\gamma_{1}n.

Then, by Chebyshev’s inequality, for any
\gamma_{2}\geq 1
and
n
large we have

\Pr\left(\left|R-\gamma_{0}n\right|\geq\gamma_{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}n}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\left|R-\mathrm{E}[R]\right|\geq\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}n}\right)\leq\frac{4}{\gamma_{2}^{2}}.

(6)

For
j\geq 2
and
i\in[q]
,
consider the random variables
Q_{j}^{(i)}
where

Q_{j}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}|C_{j}|&\text{if }C_{j}\text{ colored }i,~\text{and}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Let
Z_{i}=\sum_{j\geq 2}Q_{j}^{(i)}
be the number of vertices colored
i
in the coloring step. By Hoeffding’s inequality, for any
\gamma_{3}\geq 0

\Pr\Big(|Z_{i}-E[Z_{i}]|\geq\gamma_{3}\sqrt{n}\mid R\leq\gamma_{0}n+\gamma_{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}n}\Big)\leq 2\exp\Big(\frac{-2\gamma_{3}^{2}}{\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}/n}}\Big),

and combined with (
6
) we deduce by a union bound that

\Pr\big[|Z_{i}-E[Z_{i}]|\geq\gamma_{3}\sqrt{n}\big]\leq 2\exp\Big(\frac{-\gamma_{3}^{2}}{\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}/n}}\Big)+\frac{4}{\gamma_{2}^{2}}.

Turning our attention to
|C_{1}|
, by Lemma
2.2
, there are exists constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\Pr(||C_{1}|-\theta(\beta\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))\alpha_{1}(X_{t})n|\geq\gamma_{4}\sqrt{n})\leq c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}\gamma_{4}^{2}).

(7)

If

||C_{1}|-\theta(\beta\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))\alpha_{1}(X_{t})n|<\gamma_{4}\sqrt{n}
, and
|Z_{j}-E[Z_{j}]|<\gamma_{3}\sqrt{n}
for all
i\in[q]
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(X_{t+1})n

\displaystyle\leq\theta(\beta\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))\alpha_{1}(X_{t})n+\frac{n-\theta(\beta\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))\alpha_{1}(X_{t})n}{q}+(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n}=F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))n+(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n},

and similarly

\alpha_{1}(X_{t+1})n\geq F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))n-(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n}.

In addition, for
j\neq 1

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}(X_{t+1})n

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-\theta(\beta\alpha_{1}(X_{t}))\alpha_{1}(X_{t})n}{q}+(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n}\leq\frac{(1-F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t})))n}{q-1}+(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n},

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}(X_{t+1})n\geq\frac{(1-F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t})))n}{q-1}-(\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{4})\sqrt{n}.

Combining these facts and setting
\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{3}=\gamma_{4}=r/2
, it follows that for
r\geq\gamma_{0}
and
n
large enough that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big(\|\alpha(X_{t+1})-\kappa(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t})))\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}\gamma_{4}^{2})+2\exp\Big(\frac{-\gamma_{3}^{2}}{\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{2}\sqrt{\gamma_{1}/n}}\Big)+\frac{4}{\gamma_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\Omega(r))+O(r^{-2})

as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to provide the proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Let

\xi_{t}=\|\alpha_{j}(X_{t})-\kappa_{j}(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1})))\|_{\infty};

this quantity controls the fluctuation of all color classes at time
t
from their expected value.
Then,

\displaystyle F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1}))-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{1}(X_{t})\leq F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1}))+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}.

Similarly,

F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))-\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\leq\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1})\leq F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))+\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n},

(8)

and since
F
is increasing in
[1/q,1]
(see Fact
2.6
), we have

F\Big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))-\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\Big)-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{1}(X_{t})\leq F\Big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))+\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\Big)+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}.

By Fact
2.5
,
|F^{\prime}(a)|<1
, so for small enough
\varepsilon
,
\max_{|x-a|<\varepsilon}|F^{\prime}(x)|<1
.
Also, since
\alpha_{1}(X_{0})\in(a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon)
by assumption,
Facts
2.5
and
2.6
imply that so does
F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))
. Hence, the Taylor expansion of the function
F
about
F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))
yields that for a suitable
\rho\in(0,1)

F\Big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))+\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\Big)\leq F(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2})))+\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{n},

(9)

and

F\Big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))-\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\Big)\geq F(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2})))-\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{n}.

(10)

Therefore,

F(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2})))-\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{n}-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{1}(X_{t})\leq F(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2})))+\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{n}+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}.

Iterating this argument, we deduce that

F^{t}(\alpha_{1}(X_{0}))-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i}\leq\alpha_{1}(X_{t})\leq F^{t}(\alpha_{1}(X_{0}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i},

(11)

so that

|\alpha_{1}(X_{t})-F^{t}(\alpha_{1}(X_{0}))|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i}.

(12)

In a similar fashion, for
j\neq 1

\displaystyle\frac{1-F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1}))}{q-1}-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{j}(X_{t})\leq\frac{1-F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-1}))}{q-1}+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n},

and from (
8
) since
F
is increasing

\frac{1-F\big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))+\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\big)}{q-1}-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{j}(X_{t})\leq\frac{1-F\big(F(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))+\frac{\xi_{t-1}}{n}\big)}{q-1}+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}.

From (
9
) and (
10
), we then deduce that

\frac{1-F^{2}\big(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))}{q-1}-\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{(q-1)n}-\frac{\xi_{t}}{n}\leq\alpha_{j}(X_{t})\leq\frac{1-F^{2}\big(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))}{q-1}+\frac{(1-\rho)\xi_{t-1}}{(q-1)n}+\frac{\xi_{t}}{n},

and iterating as in (
11
):

\left|\alpha_{j}(X_{t})-\frac{1-F^{t}\big(\alpha_{1}(X_{t-2}))}{q-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(q-1)n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i}.

(13)

From (
12
) and (
13
), it suffices to show that
for a suitable constant
C>0
, with probability at least
\delta\in(0,1)

\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i}\leq C\sqrt{n}.

For this, let
K>0
and let us define the sequence

r_{i}=K\big(1-{\rho}/{2}\big)^{t-i}\big(1+\rho\big)^{t-i}

(14)

By Lemma
4.3
, we can choose
K
large enough such that

\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Pr(\xi_{i}\geq r_{i}\sqrt{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}O(r_{i}^{-2})\leq\delta.

If
\xi_{i}<r_{i}\sqrt{n}
for all
i\leq t
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\xi_{i}\leq K\sqrt{n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\big(1-{\rho}/{2}\big)^{t-i}\big(1+\rho\big)^{t-i}\leq C\sqrt{n}

for large enough
C
. It then follows from a union bound that

\Pr\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}(1-\rho)^{t-i}\geq C\sqrt{n}\Big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Pr(\xi_{i}>r_{i}\sqrt{n})\leq\delta,

as claimed.
∎

.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

For ease of notation, set
|F^{\prime}(a)|=\rho<1
. By Fact
2.7
, there exists
\Delta\geq|F^{\prime\prime}(x)|
for all
x\in(a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon)
.
Let
d_{t}=|a-F^{t}(x_{0})|
. The Taylor expansion of
F
about

\rho d_{t-1}-\frac{\Delta}{2}d_{t-1}^{2}\leq d_{t}\leq\rho d_{t-1}+\frac{\Delta}{2}d_{t-1}^{2}.

(15)

Iterating,

\rho^{T}d_{0}-\frac{\Delta}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\rho^{i}d_{T-i}^{2}\leq d_{T}\leq\rho^{T}d_{0}+\frac{\Delta}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\rho^{i}d_{T-i}^{2}.

Since
d_{t}\leq\varepsilon
for
t\geq 0
, for
\varepsilon
small enough, we can deduce from (
15
), that there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}\leq\varepsilon_{0}<1-\rho
such that

\displaystyle d_{T}\leq\frac{|x_{0}-a|\rho^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\Delta(\rho+\varepsilon_{0})^{2T}}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\Big(\frac{\rho}{(\rho+\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}\Big)^{i}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\rho^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\Delta(\rho+\varepsilon_{0})^{2T}}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\Big(\frac{\rho}{(\rho+\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}\Big)^{i}.

We can choose
\varepsilon_{0}
so that
\rho<(\rho+\varepsilon_{0})^{2}
. Then,

and

, and since

, we obtain

\frac{d_{0}\rho^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}-o\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\leq d_{T}\leq\frac{d_{0}\rho^{\gamma}}{\sqrt{n}}+o\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big).

∎

\hat{M}=\sum_{i\geq 2}L_{i}(A)^{2}\xi_{i}
where the
\xi_{i}
’s are independent
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/q)
random variables.
Observe that
\mathrm{E}[M]q=\mathrm{E}[\hat{M}]
. We derive a bound for
\mathrm{E}[\hat{M}]
and obtain the probabilistic bound for
M
via Markov’s inequality. Let
J=[\frac{1-\theta}{q}n-\delta n^{8/9},\frac{1-\theta}{q}n+\delta n^{8/9}]
for a suitably large constant
\delta>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\hat{M}]

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\Pr(R\not\in J)+\sum_{r\in J}\mathrm{E}[M\mid R=r]\Pr(R=r)

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}(\Pr(R\not\in J\mid\mathcal{B})+\Pr(\neg\mathcal{B}))+\sum_{r\in J}O(r)\Pr(R=r)

\displaystyle\leq O(n)+\mathrm{E}[R]=O(n),

where in the second inequality we used Lemma 3.1 from
[
BGJ96
]
and Lemma
2.4
,
and in third that by Hoeffding’s inequality
\Pr(R\not\in J\mid\mathcal{B})=O(n^{-2})
. Then,
\mathrm{E}[M]=O(n)
and by Markov’s inequality there exists a large enough constant
\gamma_{1}>0
such that
\Pr(M\leq\gamma_{1}n)\geq 0.95
.

Finally, the local limit theorem in Theorem 14 from
[
LL06
]
implies that for a suitable constant
\gamma_{0}>0
, we have

|L_{1}(A)-\theta n|\leq\gamma_{0}\sqrt{n}

with probability at least
0.95
; the result then follows by a union bound.
∎

4.4
using the well-known connection between the random-cluster and the ferromagnetic Potts distributions. In particular, if
A\sim\pi
and we perform a coloring step of the SW dynamics, the resulting configuration has distribution
\mu

[
ES88
]
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Let
A\sim\pi
and obtain
X\in\Omega
by
assigning spins uniformly at random from
\{1,\dots,q\}
to each connected component of
A
independently; then,
X
has distribution
\mu
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the event hat
|L_{1}(A)-\theta n|\leq\gamma_{0}\sqrt{n}
and
\sum_{i\geq 2}L_{i}(A)^{2}\leq\gamma_{1}n
.
The largest spin class will be
the one assigned to the largest component of
A
and will have expected size
\theta+\frac{1-\theta}{q}=a
; all other colors classes will have expected size
\frac{1-\theta}{q}=\frac{1-a}{q-1}
. Under the assumption that
\sum_{i\geq 2}L_{i}(A)^{2}\leq\gamma_{1}n
, we can prove the concentration of
\alpha(X)
about
m
via Hoeffding’s inequality. In particular, for
\ell>0
:

\Pr\Big(\|\alpha(X)-m\|_{\infty}\geq\ell\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}}{n}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{A}\Big)\leq 2e^{-2\ell^{2}},

and for a suitable
\ell>0
we can bound this probability by
0.1
. Since by Lemma
B.1
,
\Pr(\mathcal{A})\geq 0.9
, we obtain that

\Pr\Big(\|\alpha(X)-m\|_{\infty}\leq\ell\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}}{n}}\Big)\geq 3/4

as desired.
∎
