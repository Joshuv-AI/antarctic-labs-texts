Mediation of the association between sedentary behavior and complex multimorbidity by the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio
Authors: Hongcun Sun, Jiandao Hu, Aijuan Qian, Wenbo Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the association between sedentary behavior and complex multimorbidity among American adults, verify the mediating role of the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (hs-CRP/HDL-C) ratio in this relationship, and provide a theoretical basis for the prevention and control of chronic diseases. A total of 8246 adults aged ≥20 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2015 to 2020 were enrolled as study participants. Sedentary time was collected via questionnaire surveys, while hs-CRP and HDL-C levels were obtained through laboratory tests. Complex multimorbidity (defined as the coexistence of ≥3 chronic diseases) was determined based on clinical diagnoses. Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the association between sedentary behavior and complex multimorbidity. A mediation effect model was applied to quantify the mediating effect of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore population heterogeneity. The prevalence of complex multimorbidity in the study population was 25.72%, with a mean daily sedentary time of 5.87 hours. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, each 1-hour increase in daily sedentary time was associated with a 3.46% higher risk of complex multimorbidity (odds ratio = 1.03, 95% confidence interval: 1.02–1.05, P < .001). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a linear association between sedentary time and complex multimorbidity. Mediation effect analysis indicated that the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio exerted a partial mediating effect on the association, accounting for 5.17% of the total effect (95% confidence interval: 0.0001–0.0004, P < .001). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that this mediating effect was more pronounced in individuals aged 45 to 64 years and in males, with mediating proportions of 2.67% and 5.86%, respectively (both P < .05). Sedentary behavior may be positively associated with the risk of complex multimorbidity among American adults, and the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio partially explain this association.

1. Introduction

Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of 2 or more chronic conditions (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, stroke, and tumors) in the same individual.[1]It has become a major global public health concern. A meta-analysis revealed that the global pooled prevalence of chronic disease multimorbidity is 33.1%, with a significant disparity between high-income countries (37.9%) and low- and middle-income countries (29.7%).[2] Other studies have shown that 34.5% of patients have complex multimorbidity (defined as the presence of 3 or more chronic diseases), which becomes more prevalent with increasing age.[3,4] In particular, older adults aged ≧65 years are more vulnerable to complex multimorbidity. Compared with patients with a single chronic disease, those with complex multimorbidity experience poorer quality of life, higher mortality risk, and greater healthcare costs.[5,6] Prevention and control of complex multimorbidity has thus become a core component of public health strategies worldwide.

Sedentary behavior (SB) was defined as the total daily time spent in sedentary activities, including sitting for work, watching television, using computers and other electronic devices, and reading, with energy expenditure ≤1.5 metabolic equivalents.[7] It is one of the main lifestyle characteristics of modern populations. A nationally representative survey of the U.S. population found that the sedentary time of American adults showed an increasing trend from 2007 to 2016: total daily sitting time increased from 5.5 hours to 6.5 hours among young adults and from 5.3 hours to 6.1 hours among older adults.[8]

In recent years, a growing body of research has confirmed that SB is closely associated with a single chronic disease (e.g., coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes).[9] For example, a prospective study demonstrated that maintaining low levels of SB was associated with a 25% to 29% lower risk of total cardiac events and major adverse cardiovascular events compared with sustained high SB.[10,11] Elevated SB is also linked to an increased risk of various cancer types and higher cancer mortality risk.[12] Therefore, reducing and breaking up SB can be regarded as a strategy for the prevention and management of chronic diseases.[13,14] The World Health Organization has identified SB as one of the key risk factors affecting human health, and calls on people to reduce SB to alleviate the burden of chronic diseases.[15]

SB can lead to visceral fat accumulation, vascular endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and chronic low-grade inflammation. These pathological changes may simultaneously impair multiple systems including the cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune systems, thereby creating a “pathogenic milieu” for the development of complex multimorbidity. Therefore, SB can increase the risk of complex multimorbidity. Although studies have demonstrated an association between SB and multimorbidity,[16] research on the dose-response relationship and underlying mechanisms between SB and complex multimorbidity remains scarce, which limits the formulation of targeted prevention and control strategies.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is an acute-phase reactant synthesized by the liver and a classic biomarker reflecting systemic chronic inflammation. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) exerts protective effects such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, and reverse cholesterol transport, and its reduced levels are closely associated with lipid metabolism disorders. As a composite index integrating inflammation and lipid metabolism, the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio can more comprehensively reflect the dual status of the body’s “pro-inflammatory–anti-inflammatory balance” and “lipid metabolism homeostasis” than a single index, and has been proven to be closely related to the occurrence and development of various chronic diseases. Studies have shown that an elevated hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio is significantly associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and other conditions.[17] Specifically, each 1-unit increase in the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio is associated with a 13.3% higher risk of prediabetes and a 26.9% higher risk of diabetes.[18] Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis confirmed that the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio exhibits superior predictive value for predicting prediabetes and diabetes, compared with hs-CRP or HDL-C alone. Additionally, accumulating evidence has indicated that hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio is a critical risk factor for cardiovascular diseases.[19] Mechanistically, an elevated hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio essentially reflects the synergistic effect of inflammatory activation and lipid metabolism disorders: hs-CRP inhibits the synthesis and function of HDL-C, while reduced HDL-C levels further impair anti-inflammatory capacity, forming a “inflammation-lipid metabolism disorder” vicious cycle that ultimately leads to multisystem damage.

Based on existing research evidence, we propose the core hypothesis that SB may increase the risk of complex multimorbidity by elevating the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, with the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio playing a mediating role in this process. The results of this study will provide new evidence for revealing the biological mechanisms underlying the effect of SB on complex multimorbidity and offer theoretical support for formulating precise prevention and control strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This was a cross-sectional analytical study using data derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2015 to 2020. Conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics) of the United States, NHANES is a continuous, nationally representative survey employing a stratified multistage probability sampling method. It collects health and nutrition-related information of U.S. residents through questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests, with the data publicly available (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). Participants included adults aged ≥20 years who completed the SB survey, had complete laboratory test data (hs-CRP, HDL-C), intact chronic disease diagnosis information, and complete covariates information. Individuals with key missing data were excluded from the final analytical sample. Exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnant women; hs-CRP >10 mg/L. A total of 8246 participants were finally included for analysis (Fig. 1). All participants signed a written informed consent form before participation. The National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES study and authorized public dissemination of the data.

2.2. Definition and assessment of complex multimorbidity

Referring to internationally recognized definitions of multimorbidity and combined with NHANES data characteristics, the following common chronic diseases were included: hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, coronary heart disease, stroke, diabetes, asthma, arthritis, lower respiratory tract inflammatory diseases (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, emphysema, and chronic bronchitis), and malignant tumors. Diagnostic criteria for these chronic diseases were based on self-reported medical history. Complex multimorbidity was defined as the concurrent presence of 3 or more of the aforementioned 9 diseases.

2.3. Assessment of SB

SB information was collected through the standardized questionnaire of NHANES, with the core question: “How much time do you usually spend sitting at school, at home, getting to and from places, or with friends including time spent sitting at a desk, traveling in a car or bus, reading, playing cards, watching television, or using a computer on a typical day?.” Responses were recorded in minutes and converted to hours per day for analysis. To ensure data accuracy, NHANES adopted standardized questionnaire instructions, collected information through face-to-face interviews, and all interviewers received professional training to conduct data collection in strict accordance with the operation manual. Information regarding SB and history of chronic diseases was obtained via participant self-report questionnaires instead of objective physical examination or medical record verification. Therefore, recall bias and reporting bias were inevitable in these variables.

2.4. Calculation of hs-CRP/HDL-C

Hs-CRP was measured using the Beckman Coulter UniCel DxC 600 Synchron and DxC 660i Synchron Access chemistry analyzers. HDL-C was determined via laboratory blood biochemistry tests. The hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio (the core mediating variable of this study) was calculated by dividing the hs-CRP level (mg/L) by the HDL-C level (mg/dL/1000). All laboratory tests were performed in accordance with NHANES standardized operating procedures, with qualified quality control.

2.5. Covariates

Covariates were selected on the basis of previous relevant studies, which demonstrated these factors could confound the association between SB and complex multimorbidity.[20,21] Some potential confounding factors were included as follows: age (years), gender (male and female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, and other race), educational level (less than high school, high school, and more than high school), poverty income ratio (<1.300, 1.301–3.500, and >3.500), and marital status (married/living with a partner and living alone). Body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), was classified into 3 categories: <25, 25–29.9, and ≥30. Additional binary variables included smoking status (having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life), alcohol use (consuming 5 or more drinks daily), vigorous activity (large increases in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 minutes continuously), and moderate activity (a small increase in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 minutes continuously).

2.6. Statistical analysis

In our study, participants were divided into 2 groups (no complex multimorbidity group and complex multimorbidity group) to describe their baseline characteristics. Continuous data were described using mean ± standard deviation (for normal distribution), while categorical data were presented as constituent ratio (%). A multivariate logistic regression model was used to analyze the association between SB and complex multimorbidity, with 3 models constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for demographic characteristics: age, gender, race, educational level, marital status, and poverty income ratio); Model 3 (fully adjusted model, adjusted for smoking, alcohol use, vigorous activities, moderate activities, and BMI on the basis of Model 2). Meanwhile, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was employed to examine the dose-response relationship between sedentary time and the risk of complex multimorbidity.

The stepwise method combined with Bootstrap sampling (1000 repetitions) was used to verify the mediating effect of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, with the steps as follows: Test the total effect of SB on complex multimorbidity (results of Model 3); Test the effect of SB on the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio; Simultaneously include SB and the mediating variable to test their independent effects on complex multimorbidity; Calculate the mediating effect value and mediating ratio (mediating effect/ total effect × 100%). If the Bootstrap 95% confidence interval does not contain 0, the mediating effect is significant. Subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying by age (20–44 years, 45–64 years, ≥65 years) and gender (male, female) to explore the population heterogeneity in the correlation between SB and complex multimorbidity.

Sensitivity analyses were used to verify the robustness of the results using the following approaches: Re-analyzing the data after excluding extreme values (individuals with sedentary time >16 hours/day); Repeating the analysis with an alternative grouping method for SB (dichotomization: <6 hours/day and ≥6 hours/day).

Data processing and analysis were performed using R version 4.3.3 (2024-02-29), along with Zstats 1.0 (www.zstats.net). A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

The primary objective of this study was to explore the association between SB and complex multimorbidity among American adults, and to further verify the potential mediating role of hs-CRP/HDL-C in this relationship. Accordingly, the key findings derived from the statistical analyses are detailed in the following sections.

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 8246 participants were included, with a complex multimorbidity prevalence of 25.72%. The mean daily sedentary time was 5.87 ± 3.35 hours, the mean age was 50.11 ± 17.35 years, and the mean hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio was 52.91 ± 54.12. Among the participants, 53.23% were male, 39.36% were non-Hispanic white, 59.84% were married or living with a partner, and 59.77% had more than a high school education (Table 1). Compared with participants without complex multimorbidity, those with complex multimorbidity had significantly higher age, hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, and daily sedentary time (all P < .001). Additionally, the complex multimorbidity group had higher proportions of non-Hispanic white individuals, those with BMI ≥ 30, current smokers, and current drinkers. Conversely, the proportions of individuals with poverty income ratio >3.50, more than a high school education, married or living with a partner, and engaging in vigorous or moderate activities were lower in the complex multimorbidity group (all P < .001). No significant difference was observed in the proportion of males between the 2 groups (P = .358).

3.2. Association between SB and complex multimorbidity

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that after adjusting for confounders in Models 2 and 3, SB was significantly associated with the risk of complex multimorbidity (Table 2). Results from Model 3 indicated that for each 1-hour increase in daily sedentary time, the risk of complex multimorbidity increased by 3.46% (odds ratio [OR] = 1.03, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.05).

3.3. Mediation effect analysis

Fig. 3 presents the results of the mediation effect analysis of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio on the association between SB and complex multimorbidity. Both the direct and indirect effects of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio were significant (P < .001). The proportion of mediation was 5.17%, indicating that 5.17% of the effect of SB on complex multimorbidity was mediated by an elevated hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, with a significant partial mediation effect (P < .001).

Subgroup analysis revealed significant population heterogeneity in the mediation effect. Specifically, a partial mediation effect was observed in middle-aged individuals (45–64 years) and males, with mediation proportions of 2.67% and 5.86%, respectively (P = .048, 0.012, respectively). No significant mediation effect was found in older adults (≥ 65 years), young adults (18–44 years), or females.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the study results. First, after excluding 29 extreme cases with sedentary time >16 hours/day, multivariate logistic regression analysis (Model 3) showed that SB remained significantly associated with the risk of complex multimorbidity (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.06). The mediation proportion of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio was 4.71% (95% CI: 0.0001–0.0004), consistent with the original results. Second, when sedentary time was dichotomized into 2 groups (<6 hours/day and ≥6 hours/day), SB was still significantly associated with the risk of complex multimorbidity (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05), and the mediation proportion of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio was 3.62% (95% CI: 0.0004–0.0021), which remained significant. These results indicate that the conclusions regarding the mediation effect in this study were less affected by potential confounding factors and exhibited good stability.

Collectively, the current study presented a linear relationship between SB and complex multimorbidity. Notably, the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio exerted a partial mediating effect on the association between SB and complex multimorbidity, accounting for 5.17% of the total effect. Furthermore, this mediating effect was more pronounced among individuals aged 45 to 64 years and male participants.

4. Discussion

Based on the large-scale data from NHANES 2015 to 2020, this study systematically investigated, for the first time, the association between SB and complex multimorbidity, as well as the mediating role of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio in this association. The key findings are as follows: SB exhibited a significant dose-response relationship with the risk of complex multimorbidity; The hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio may play a partial mediating role in the association between the 2; The mediating effect of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio showed population heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings put forward a tentative hypothesis regarding potential pathways linking SB to chronic multimorbidity and may offer preliminary clues for future prevention and intervention research on complex multimorbidity.

This study showed a significant dose-response relationship between SB and the risk of complex multimorbidity, with each 1-hour increase in daily sedentary time associated with a 3.46% higher risk of complex multimorbidity. Previous studies have reported a significant correlation between SB and complex multimorbidity in specific populations. For instance, a cohort study of adults aged ≥50 years in Ireland found that SB increased with the number of chronic physical conditions, and individuals with ≥4 diseases had a significantly higher risk of sedentary time.[22] Another Mendelian randomization study indicated that SB plays an important role in the development of cardiometabolic disease among individuals with hypertension,[23] supporting the link between SB and cardiometabolic disease accumulation. SB is therefore an important factor influencing complex multimorbidity. Furthermore, RCS analysis revealed a linear characteristic of this association, suggesting that reducing sedentary time may serve as a key intervention target for decreasing the risk of complex multimorbidity.

From a biological mechanism perspective, the core of SB leading to complex multimorbidity lies in the synergistic damage to multiple systems.[24] Prolonged SB first inhibits skeletal muscle contraction, reducing lipoprotein lipase activity and causing abnormal triglyceride metabolism, which in turn triggers lipid metabolism disorders.[25] Meanwhile, visceral fat accumulation induced by SB stimulates adipose tissue to secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, activating systemic chronic inflammation.[26] The interaction between inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders not only impairs vascular endothelial function, accelerates atherosclerosis, and increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases but also inhibits insulin receptor activity, induces insulin resistance, and promotes the development of type 2 diabetes. Additionally, it may affect renal function, bone metabolism, and other processes, ultimately leading to the coexistence of multisystem diseases.

This study firstly identifies that the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio may exert a partial mediating role in the correlative link between SB and complex multimorbidity. These findings put forward a hypothesis that inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders might serve as potential linking pathways possibly relevant to the observed association. As a composite indicator integrating inflammation and lipid metabolism, the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio may reflect systemic pathological conditions more comprehensively than individual biomarkers. Elevated hs-CRP is indicative of inflammatory activation, while decreased HDL-C may mirror dysregulated lipid metabolism and compromised anti-inflammatory function; accordingly, a higher hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio could imply concurrent imbalance in pro-/anti-inflammatory status and lipid homeostasis.

Subgroup analysis revealed that the mediating effect of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio was more pronounced in middle-aged individuals and males. Such heterogeneity may be attributed to differences in physiological status and sensitivity to SB across populations. Middle-aged men represent a period of high incidence of chronic diseases, during which physiological functions begin to decline, and the cumulative effects of inflammation and metabolic disorders gradually manifest. Meanwhile, middle-aged men are mostly in their prime career stage, with a high proportion of work-related SB (e.g., office work, meetings), resulting in longer and more continuous sedentary time that exerts a more significant impact on inflammation and lipid metabolism. Additionally, lifestyle factors of middle-aged men (e.g., lack of physical activity, irregular diet) may synergize with SB to further exacerbate the elevation of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, leading to a more prominent mediating effect.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it utilized large-sample data from NHANES 2015 to 2020, which has national representativeness and strong external validity. Second, it systematically investigated the association between SB and complex multimorbidity, verifying the dose-response relationship and providing evidence for identifying intervention targets. Third, it is the first study to focus on the mediating role of the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, revealing the critical mechanism of inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders in SB-induced complex multimorbidity and enriching biological evidence for related research. Fourth, comprehensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify result robustness and reduce confounding bias.

However, this study also has certain limitations. First, owing to the cross-sectional design, temporal sequence between SB and complex multimorbidity cannot be determined, and causal inference is not feasible. Second, relevant indicators were collected via self-reporting, which inevitably brings potential recall and reporting bias and may affect data accuracy. Although NHANES uses standardized questionnaires to reduce such bias, it cannot be completely eliminated. Third, the study did not distinguish between types of SB (e.g., work-related SB, recreational screen time), which may exert varying health effects and warrant further investigation. Fourth, information on participants’ medication use (e.g., statins, anti-inflammatory drugs) was not collected, which may affect the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio and complex multimorbidity status, potentially influencing results. Fifth, the 5.17% mediation proportion indicates that other mechanisms (e.g., insulin resistance, oxidative stress) may also be involved in the association between SB and complex multimorbidity, and future studies should explore multi-path mediation models.

Based on the study findings, the following prevention and control recommendations are proposed: Limit daily sedentary time, especially for high-risk groups such as middle-aged men, with a recommended limit of <6 hours per day. Promote the concept of “micro-exercise,” such as sitting-standing alternations every 20 minutes.[27] Implement targeted interventions (e.g., lifestyle guidance, pharmacological regulation) for individuals with daily sedentary time ≥6 hours and an elevated hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio. Strengthen health education to raise public awareness of the association between SB, inflammation, and complex multimorbidity. Promote improvements in workplace and community environments (e.g., popularizing height-adjustable office desks and building accessible fitness facilities) to create a health-promoting environment that encourages reducing sedentary time and increasing physical activity.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this study found that SB was significantly positively correlated with the risk of complex multimorbidity among American adults, with a linear relationship observed between the 2. The hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio showed a possible partial mediating role in this correlative link, which appeared more pronounced in middle-aged individuals and males. Reducing sedentary time and regulating the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio is hypothesized to be a promising direction for easing the burden of complex multimorbidity. Future prospective cohort and interventional studies are required to validate the potential causal association and formulate targeted prevention strategies.
