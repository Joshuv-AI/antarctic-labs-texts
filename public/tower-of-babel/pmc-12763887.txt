The pattern of gene amplification of members of the Plasmodium vivax erythrocyte binding-like proteins family across the Amazon rainforest
Authors: Karla S. Guedes, Gabriela M. Fernandes, Bruno A. M. Sanchez, Francis B. Ntumngia, John H. Adams, Flora S. Kano, Cor J. F. Fontes, Tais N. de Sousa, Luzia H. Carvalho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Plasmodium vivax malaria, the Duffy Binding Protein (DBP), a key erythrocyte binding-like (EBL) protein, enables invasion of DARC (Duffy Antigen/Receptor for Chemokines) positive reticulocytes. Another EBL member, the erythrocyte binding protein 2 (EBP2, also known as EBP/ DBP2), shares structural features with DBP, suggesting a role in erythrocyte invasion. P. vivax genomes may harbour multiple ebl gene copies, though their functional role is unclear. Considering DBP and EBP2 as key vaccine candidates, this study investigates copy number variation (CNV) in these two ebl genes across Amazonian populations. Furthermore, the relationship between CNV and host DARC genotypes was investigated, as DARC is the best-characterized host receptor associated with susceptibility or resistance to P. vivax.MethodsA total of 191 P. vivax isolates from three malaria-endemic sites of the Amazon region (eastern, western, and southwestern) were analysed. DARC polymorphisms associated with functional (FY*A, FY*B) and non-functional (FY*BES) alleles were genotyped by real-time PCR with allele-specific oligonucleotides. CNV at dbp and ebp2 loci was estimated by quantitative PCR (qPCR), using the β-tubulin gene as an internal reference. Gene copy numbers were stratified by geographical origin and host DARC genotype.ResultsAmplification of dbp and ebp2 genes varied across Amazon regions. In the western region, 25% of P. vivax isolates showed dbp gene amplification (up to 8 copies), compared to 2–9% in the southwestern and eastern regions (2–3 copies). Overall, ebp2 amplification was less frequent, detected in 15% of P. vivax isolates from the western and in 1–4% from other regions. In the study areas, all individuals were DARC positives, and no association was observed between gene CNVs and DARC genotypes.ConclusionsThis study identifies dbp and ebp2 gene amplifications in P. vivax isolates from the Amazon rainforest, with regional CNV variation but no association with any DARC-positive genotype. These findings support further investigation into gene amplifications to elucidate their biological and immunological significance in DARC-positive populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05690-2.

Background

The invasion of red blood cells by Plasmodium spp. is a complex biological process that requires the coordinated engagement of multiple parasite ligands with specific receptors on the erythrocyte membrane [1, 2]. In the case of Plasmodium vivax, the most widespread human malaria parasite [3], there are distinct ligand-receptor interactions that provide strict selectivity for reticulocytes [4–6]. The process of reticulocytes invasion by P. vivax depends on the Erythrocyte Binding-Like (EBL) proteins family [7] whose best-characterized member is the Duffy binding protein (DBP), which binds to the human atypical chemokine receptor 1 (ACKR1 also named Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines, DARC) [8]. The interaction between the ligand domain of DBP (region II, DBPII) and DARC receptor is central to blood-stage infection, as individuals lacking DARC on the surface of the RBCs (DARC-negative) exhibit significantly reduced susceptibility to P. vivax infection [9]. Thus, a vaccine that elicits antibodies able to block the DBPII/DARC interface would be expected to block parasite invasion (reviewed by Reddy et al. [10]).

The long-standing hypothesis that P. vivax invades reticulocytes exclusively through the DBPII-DARC interaction has been increasingly challenged by numerous reports of P. vivax infections in DARC-negative individuals [11]. Indeed, P. vivax infections are generally less severe and exhibit lower parasitaemia in DARC-negative than in DARC-positive individuals [12] [13]. Towards understanding the mechanisms of P. vivax invasion in DARC-negative individuals, numerous studies have investigated other parasite ligands and alternative receptors (reviewed by [14]). Among the newly described host receptors suggested to be involved in P. vivax invasion include transferrin receptor protein 1 (CD71) [15], complement receptor 1 (CR1) [16], Band 3 [17], Basigin [18], and CD98 heavy chain [19]. In 2013, a notable study by Hester et al. [20], involving de novo assembly of a field isolate genome, revealed novel P. vivax erythrocyte invasion genes. One of these genes coding a novel P. vivax DBP homolog, erythrocyte binding protein 2 (EBP2 also termed EBP/DBP2). The primary structure of EBP2 exhibits key conserved domain features characteristic of EBL protein family, including a DBL ligand domain (region II), which is considered essential for receptor recognition and merozoite invasion [7]. While some studies suggest that EBP2 binds preferentially to immature DARC-positive reticulocytes [21], others suggest that it binds to both reticulocytes and normocytes, independently of the DARC receptor [22]. Although it remains unclear the role of EBP2 in P. vivax reticulocyte invasion, the combined antibody response to DBP and EBP2 appears to have an additive effect in reducing the risk of P. vivax malaria in children from Papua New Guinea [23, 24]. These findings further strengthen the rationale for prioritize both antigens for inclusion in a multicomponent P. vivax vaccine.

The detection of gene copy number variation (CNV) in DBP and EBP2 added additional genetic complexity to P. vivax infections. In 2013, Menard et al. reported the first evidence of dbp gene amplification in P. vivax isolates from Madagascar, where infections occur in both DARC-positive and DARC-negative individuals [25]. The same authors detected dbp duplication in P. vivax infected blood samples from travelers returning from America, Africa, Asia and Melanesia. Subsequent studies confirmed that P. vivax strains carrying multiple copies of the dbp gene have a global distribution [26–31] and do not seem to have any association with the DARC negativity barrier [32, 33]. At this point, there is still no consensus about the role of dbp gene amplification in the P. vivax infection [14]. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including the possibility that gene amplification enables the parasite to evade the host immune response [33, 34]. Consequently, vaccines targeting exclusively DBP neutralizing antibodies should consider the potential for immune escape.

In contrast to DBP, few studies have explored the genetic diversity of ebp2 gene. Most available data is from Asian and, to a lesser extent, African populations [32, 35]. Recently, P. vivax isolates from the Brazilian Amazon region were analysed, and it was found that the DBL domain of EBP2 is significantly less polymorphic than of DBP [36], a finding consistent with reports from other regions [20, 32, 35, 37]. Although studies on ebp2 gene amplification remain scarce, available data suggest that gene multiplication is common in African populations, in both DARC-positive and DARC-negative individuals [29, 30, 32].

At present, it has been speculated that P. vivax merozoites may utilize both DBP and EBP to invade DARC-positive erythrocytes [22]. Consequently, assessing the variability of dbp and ebp2 genes in the same samples appears critical. To gain insight into the gene amplification of these two main EBL proteins, this study assessed the copy number variation of the dbp and ebp2 genes in P. vivax isolates from different endemic areas of the Amazon region, where malaria transmission ranges from hypoendemic to mesoendemic [38]. Considering that malaria transmission in the Amazon rainforest is associated with DARC-positive carriers, the present study further explored the correlation between gene copy number and DARC genotypes.

Methods

Study sites and sample collection

A total of 191 Plasmodium vivax field isolates were collected from patients across three regions of the Amazon, grouped as follows (Fig. 1, on the inset map): eastern Amazon—Amapá (AP, n = 4) and Pará (PA, n = 19); western Amazon—Amazonas (AM, n = 32), Roraima (RR, n = 38), and Guyana (GUY, formerly British Guiana, n = 5); and southwestern Amazon—Rondônia (RO, n = 61), Mato Grosso (MT, n = 29), and Acre (AC, n = 3). The samples were collected between 2017 and 2023. Malaria diagnosis

Individuals with clinical suspicion of malaria who sought care at malaria outpatient clinics in the Amazon region were submitted to a finger-prick for malaria diagnosis by light microscopy. The Giemsa-stained thick blood smears were prepared and examined by experienced local microscopists, according to the malaria diagnosis guidelines of the Brazilian Ministry of Health [39]. For this study, it was included only individuals whose thick blood smear were positive for P. vivax (n = 191). Genomic DNA was extracted from 1 mL of EDTA blood using the Puregene Blood Core Kit B (Qiagen, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and eluted in 100 µL of buffer, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Species-specific real-time PCR assays using different targets (ribosomal and non-ribosomal) were performed as previously described [40].

DARC genotyping

Commercial TaqMan SNP Genotyping Assays (Applied Biosystems, USA) were used to genotype major DARC polymorphisms (Table S1). The assays targeted the T-33C substitution in the RBC-specific GATA1 transcription factor binding motif (rs2814778; Assay ID: C-15769614–10) and the G125A polymorphism in exon 2 (rs12075; Assay ID: C-2493442–20). The amplification reactions were carried out on a ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA), using 2.5 µL of TaqMan® 2 × Universal PCR Master Mix, 0.25 µL of the specific TaqMan® SNP Genotyping Assay, 1.25 µL of DNase and RNase-free water, and 1 µL of genomic DNA (~ 10 ng/µL). Each assay included both negative controls (water in place of DNA) and positive controls, consisting of previously genotyped samples, as previously described [41]. Data analysis was performed using QuantStudio Real-Time PCR Software v1.3.7 (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Relative quantification of dbp and ebp2 copy number

Gene copy number was estimated by quantitative PCR (qPCR), following the optimized protocol of Roesch et al. [32]. The β-tubulin gene was used as an internal single-copy reference in this study, as it is well established in the literature as a stable single-copy gene for P. vivax [42]. qPCR reactions were performed in 10 μL, containing 5 µL of GoTaq® (SYBR Green) qPCR Master Mix 2X (Promega), 2.2 µL of DNase and RNase-free water, and 1 µL of template DNA. Primer’s concentrations were as follows: 900 nM for dbp (CN-DBP-F and CN-DBP-R); 300 nM for ebp2 (CN-EBP2-F and CN-EBP2-R) and β-tubulin (CN- β-tubulin-F, and CN- β-tubulin -R) (Table S2). All primers exhibited amplification efficiencies within the acceptable range (90–110%), allowing the use of the 2^-ΔΔCt method without efficiency correction.

Amplification and fluorescence detection were carried out on a ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA), using the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min, with a final step of 95 °C for 15 s.

All reactions were run in triplicate on 96- or 384-well plates. Two commercial plasmids, pIDTSMART-AMP, also synthesized by IDT, were used for optimization: one containing dbp and β-tubulin inserts (1:1), and the other with ebp2 and β-tubulin (1:1). A positive control consisting of an isolate previously validated to carry one copy of each gene (confirmed by independent methods) and a negative control (water in place of DNA) were included in all runs. A CT threshold of 32 was applied, as values exceeding this limit approached the assay’s limit of quantification. No-template controls consistently yielded CT values above 38 or produced undetectable amplification signals. Samples showing copy number variation were re-tested independently for confirmation. Samples with estimated copy numbers below 0.5 were excluded due to insufficient reliable data (e.g., low copy numbers or a poor signal-to-noise ratio). Final copy number values were rounded as follows: 0.5–1.4 to 1 copy, ≥ 1.5–2.4 to 2 copies, ≥ 2.5–3.4 to 3 copies, ≥ 3.5–4.4 to 4 copies, ≥ 4.5–5.4 to 5 copies, ≥ 5.5–6.4 to 6 copies, ≥ 6.5–7.4 to 7 copies, and ≥ 7.5–8.4 to 8 copies. Reactions were analysed using QuantStudio Real-Time PCR Software v1.3.7 (Applied Biosystems, USA).

Statistical analysis

A database was created using EpiData software (www.epidata.dk). The graphics and the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and Stata software version 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons of non-parametric continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. Categorical variables were analysed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All data necessary to replicate the study’s findings are openly available (Table S3).

Results

Molecular confirmation of P. vivax infection and DARC genotypes in the study population

Of the 191 P. vivax infections detected by microscopy in this study, 181 (95%) were confirmed as P. vivax monoinfections by a species-specific PCR assay, with 10 (5%) of them identified as mixed infections with P. vivax and Plasmodium falciparum (Table S3). Most P. vivax patients included in the study were male (n = 146, 77%), with a median age of 34 years (IQR 27–45 years). In the study area, DARC genotyping was successfully obtained for 179 out of 191 (94%) P. vivax isolates. All individuals were DARC-positive, with a predominance of the FY*A/FY*B genotype across all Amazon regions (32%–38%) (Fig. 1). DARC positive homozygous (FY*A/FY*A and FY*B/FY*B) and DARC-null positive genotypes (FY*A/FY*BES and FY*B/FY*BES) were also detected in high frequencies in the study area (Fig. 1). Except for the genotype FY*B/FY*B, there were no significant differences between the Amazon regions (Table S4).

dbp and ebp2 gene copy number variation in the Amazon

Successful amplification was achieved for 189 P. vivax isolates for dbp and 185 isolates for ebp2. Of these, the copy number variation (CNV) in the genes among P. vivax isolates showed differences across the distinct regions of the Amazon. The western region showed higher CNV frequencies, with 25% (19 of 75) and 15% (11 of 73) of isolates harbouring CNVs in dbp and ebp2, respectively (Fig. 2A). In contrast, in the southwestern region, gene duplication frequencies were detected at much lower numbers, ranging from 2 to 1% for dbp (2 of 91) and ebp2 (single isolate), respectively. Discussion

In different Amazon endemic settings, this study investigated copy number variation (CNV) in two P. vivax genes encoding proteins implicated in blood-stage invasion: DBP, a well characterized ligand that mediates invasion of DARC positive reticulocytes, and EBP2, a paralog with putative functional redundancy. Gene amplification results showed significant variation across Amazon endemic sites, with the western region harbouring most parasites carrying multiple copies of dbp or ebp2. However, the limited availability of data on dbp CNV and the absence of ebp2 data from the Brazilian Amazon preclude direct comparisons with previous studies. Despite this, multiple copies of DBP were detected in 10% of P. vivax isolates from urban areas of Acre State in the southwestern Amazon. [27]. Taken together, these results from the Amazon support the current view that copy number variation in genes associated with P. vivax reticulocyte invasion seems to be a common phenomenon occurring on a worldwide scale [25, 27, 28, 30–32, 43].

In the current study, it is unlikely that variation in the host DARC gene accounts for the observed regional differences in gene duplication. Although the distribution of FY*B/FY*B genotype showed slight variation between the three regions, the overall DARC genotype frequencies were similar across the three Amazonian regions, with a predominance of the FY*A/FY*B genotype; this pattern reflects a population structure influenced by both Amerindian and European ancestry components [44]. Notably, most P. vivax isolates from the western Amazon originated from a long-term P. vivax-exposed population (Rio Pardo, AM, Table S3) formed mainly by Amazonian natives previously characterized by high contribution of Native Amerindian ancestry [44–46]. In contrast, P. vivax isolates from the southwestern and eastern Amazon are mainly derived from migrants originating in non-endemic regions of Brazil. In these regions, malaria is predominantly an occupational disease, strongly associated with activities such as deforestation and illegal mining [47]. Consequently, multiple, not-mutually exclusive, factors may have contributed to the difference in the CNV profiles between the western and southwestern/eastern regions, including differences in the intensity of malaria transmission, historical parasite population dynamics and host genetics [28]. Future studies with more presentative samples of the Amazon basin should address this topic.

Given that previous findings have shown that susceptibility to P. vivax clinical malaria is influenced by DARC genotypes [44, 48, 49], gene copy number variation was further investigated in relation to DARC genotype. In this DARC positive population, no association was found between dbp or ebp2 gene amplifications and DARC homozygous or heterozygous genotypes. Similar findings were reported in P. vivax isolates from Cambodia and Ethiopia, where DBP duplication was not linked to any specific DARC-positive genotype [27, 33]. Despite this, in populations where DARC-positive and DARC-negative individuals coexist, it has been suggested that the proportion of single- versus multicopy variants of DBP or EBP2 may differ between these groups [28, 29, 43]. There seems to be a consensus that larger studies are needed to further elucidate the role (if any) of DARC in DBP/EBP2 copy number variations, including in both symptomatic and asymptomatic P. vivax infections.

While DBP duplication has been extensively studied in parasites from diverse geographical regions (reviewed by [14]), EBP2 amplification has received far less attention [29, 30, 32]. Here, ebp2 duplication was detected across all three surveyed regions in the Amazon (1–15%), albeit at a lower frequency than dbp amplification (2–25%). Duplication events in P. vivax isolates were predominantly gene-specific, with 62% (16 of 26) of samples displaying amplification in only one of the two erythrocyte-binding genes. Compared to isolates from the Amazon region, P. vivax isolates from Asia and Africa typically exhibit much higher frequencies of EBP2 gene duplications (19 to 56%) [29, 32]. In Ethiopia, where DBP and EBP2 duplications are common, EBP2 amplification (but not DBP) was more prevalent in DARC-positive (46%) than in DARC-negative individuals (21%) [29]. This observation is further supported by recent evidence from Central Africa, where most of P. vivax isolates from DARC-negative individuals exhibited more than two dbp copies in the absence of ebp2 amplification [30]. This is of particular interest as previous studies have demonstrated that EBP2 can bind strongly to DARC-positive reticulocytes and moderately to DARC-negative reticulocytes [21, 26].

To date, very few studies have investigated whether a high copy number of DBP or EBP2 leads to increased protein expression. In 2024, Pestana and colleagues [29] showed that CNVs in various ebl genes (dbp, ebp2, and rbp2b) were not significantly correlated with their respective expression levels, suggesting that increased gene dosage does not necessarily result in higher transcription or protein production. Nonetheless, multiple mechanisms may underlie the lack of correlation between gene duplications and transcriptomic or proteomic data, including (i) the presence of posttranscriptional regulatory pathways that alter mRNA stability or involve microRNAs [50], and (ii) epigenetic regulatory mechanisms that enhance the parasite’s adaptability to the host erythrocyte receptors [51]. Nevertheless, it seems to be a consensus that more transcriptomic data need to be obtained in single- and multicopy DBP infections to determine whether a change in CNV is associated with gene expression levels.

The current study has limitations that should be consider on data interpretation. First, quantitative measurements of P. vivax parasitaemia could not be obtained from Giemsa-stained thick blood smears, since in Brazil slides are routinely examined in public health facilities using the semi-quantitative “cross method” [39], which is highly variable and does not provide a reliable estimation of parasite density. Consequently, it was not possible to evaluate whether levels of parasitaemia would influence the gene copy variation, as it has been the subject of considerable speculation [27, 29, 30]; to proper address this topic and avoid discrepant results, it would be more appropriate to evaluate P. vivax biomass, as it is underrepresented in parasite counts obtained from peripheral blood smears [52].

Secondly, the relatively small sample size per site, combined with a cross-sectional design and substantial variability in the timing of data collection, precluded any attempt to conduct a temporal analysis of dbp/ebp2 CNVs over time. Longitudinal studies should address this topic. Finally, the qPCR approach used in this study estimates dbp and ebp2 gene copy numbers based on the dominant clone in each isolate. Notwithstanding, qPCR assay has been a more feasible protocol that has been used in different studies [32, 43]. Although outside the scope of the current manuscript, it is agreed that next-generation sequencing or a digital PCR approach should be able to overcome this issue of multiclonal infections [30, 32].

Conclusion

This study confirms the low but appreciable presence of dbp and ebp2 gene amplification in P. vivax isolates across the Amazon rainforest where DARC-negativity is rare. The results of these gene amplifications showed significant differences among the Amazon endemic settings, but with no association with any DARC positive genotype. Although the functional significance of dbp and ebp2 amplifications requires further investigation, this pioneering study examining amplification at these loci in the Amazon provides important insights that may support the development of vaccines targeting P. vivax parasites harbouring multiple copies of these key invasion ligands.
