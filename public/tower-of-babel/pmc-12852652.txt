Notum1a Inhibition promotes neurogenesis in the adult zebrafish brain
Authors: Yigit Kocagöz, Nuray Sogunmez Erdogan, Sevval Ozdinc, Dogac Ipekgil, Esra Katkat, Gunes Ozhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNotum is a carboxylesterase enzyme that modulates extracellular signaling by hydrolyzing palmitoleoyl residues from proteins, thereby influencing key pathways involved in cell differentiation, survival, and proliferation. While notum1 expression has been identified in the brain, its role in adult neurogenesis remains poorly understood. Using the adult zebrafish brain as a model system, we demonstrate that the notum1a homolog is broadly expressed across various brain cell types but is absent in undifferentiated radial glial cells. Pharmacological inhibition of Notum activity with the small molecule inhibitor ABC99 stimulates activation of radial glial cells, leading to increased neurogenesis. A BrdU pulse-chase assay confirms that ABC99-induced proliferation enhances the production of mature neurons. Despite Notum’s established role in Wnt signaling, transcriptional analysis following ABC99 treatment reveals no sustained impact on Wnt pathway targets, suggesting that Notum may regulate neurogenesis through alternative mechanisms. Our findings highlight notum1a as a potential modulator of neural progenitor cell dynamics in the adult brain and suggest that targeting Notum could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative conditions characterized by impaired neurogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33883-x.Keywords: Zebrafish, Adult neurogenesis, Notum, Canonical wnt signaling, ABC99Subject terms: Developmental biology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Adult neurogenesis refers to the sustained and coordinated production of new neurons from progenitor cells in specific regions of the nervous system after developmental stages have concluded. This process is essential for maintaining a healthy nervous system, as newborn neurons compensate for cellular loss due to aging and injury, while contributing to cognitive functions such as learning and memory1,2. Neurogenesis, both during development and adulthood, is tightly regulated and requires precise communication between progenitor cells and the surrounding extracellular environment3,4. Disruptions in this coordination can impair synaptic and cellular plasticity, leading to cognitive deficits. Notably, deficits in hippocampal neurogenesis are frequently associated with neurodegenerative disorders including Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s diseases5–9.

Cells in regenerative tissues regulate proliferation in response to environmental changes by secreting ligands that stimulate stem cell activation. The availability and modulation of these activatory ligands are essential for maintaining the balance between stem cell activation and quiescence. This regulation is primarily controlled by a network of extracellular enzymes, among which the palmitoleoyl-protein carboxylesterase Notum plays a critical role in modulating key signaling pathways involved in tissue homeostasis and regeneration10–12.

Notum functions by cleaving membrane-anchored core proteins, mainly through its carboxylesterase activity13,14. It is primarily recognized as a negative regulator of the Wingless-related integration site (Wnt)-signaling pathway, hydrolyzing palmitoleoyl residues from Wnt ligands in the extracellular matrix14. This modification renders Wnt ligands inactive, preventing their interaction with the Frizzled receptor and subsequently attenuating downstream signaling15. Notum has also been directly linked to stem cell quiescence, with high expression correlating with reduced proliferation in aging intestinal tissue, suggesting a role in age-related regenerative decline16. In the neural context, Notum is expressed in the mammalian brain and associated with a regulatory role in the adult ventricular-subventricular zone17,18. It is also essential for neural fate determination during embryogenesis12. However, the function of Notum in the adult brain remains poorly understood, necessitating further investigation into its role in progenitor cell dynamics.

In this study, we investigated the expression of notum1a and its association with adult neurogenesis using the adult zebrafish brain as a model system. Teleosts exhibit lifelong proliferative capacity across multiple tissues, making them an excellent model organism for studying neural progenitors and the regulation of constitutive neurogenic zones19–21. Moreover, the adult zebrafish brain provides an ideal platform for pharmacological experiments and neurogenic pattern analysis. We demonstrate here that notum1a, one of two zebrafish notum1 homologs, is expressed in the telencephalon pallium but is largely absent in actively proliferating cells at the periphery. Direct administration of the Notum inhibitor ABC9922 into the zebrafish brain significantly increased the proliferation of radial glial cells. A BrdU pulse-chase assay further revealed that the drug-induced proliferation led to a higher count of mature neurons, enhancing neurogenesis. Given Notum’s established link to canonical Wnt signaling and its integral role in the maintenance of the nervous system23, we examined the transcriptional effects of ABC99 treatment but found no sustained impact on Wnt pathway targets. These findings indicate that the adult zebrafish brain remains neurogenically responsive to ABC99 but suggest that Notum’s role in regulating adult neurogenesis may involve alternative molecular pathways beyond Wnt signaling.

Results

Notum1a is expressed in the adult zebrafish telencephalon

Two homologs of notum exist in the zebrafish genome. To examine their expression profiles in the telencephalon, we performed in situ hybridization for notum1a and notum1b separately. While notum1a was widely expressed in the adult telencephalon, notum1b expression was undetectable, which is consistent with another literature finding24 (Fig. 1A). notum1a was primarily observed within the central parenchymal regions, which are enriched with neuronal populations. Additionally, it was detected in two neurogenic zones: the midline cell layer in contact with the T-shaped ventricle (named central ventricular region hereafter), and the ventricular-peripheral edge cell line surrounding the pallium and the outer surface of subpallium (peripallium), where stem cell populations predominantly reside25.

The spatial distribution of notum1a-positive cells within the central parenchyma overlaps with neuronal populations of the telencephalon. To determine the cellular identity of notum1a-expressing cells, we conducted fluorescent in situ hybridization combined with NeuroD1 immunostaining, a marker of early neuronal progenitors26. NeuroD1-positive cells often co-expressed notum1a (Fig. 1B). In contrast, notum1a expression was largely absent from the peripheral margins of the telencephalon, where undifferentiated radial-glia like progenitors reside. Consistent with this, GFAP-positive radial glial cells cells, which occupy these peripheral domains, did not show detectable notum1a expression (Fig. 1C).

To further assess notum1a expression within differentiated neurons, we combined in situ hybridization with HuC/D immunostaining. Approximately two-thirds of HuC/D-positive neurons in the pallium expressed notum1a (66%), whereas the proportion was lower in the peripallium (41%). When total cells were irrespective of cell type, notum1a–expressing cells accounted for only 16% of all cells in the peripallial region, which has a broader and multicellular architecture (2–3 cell layers) (Fig. 1D).

To examine the relationship between notum1 expression and radial glial proliferation, we performed fluorescent in situ hybridization for notum1 together with immunostaining for S100B (a radial glia marker associated with stemness) and PCNA (a proliferation marker). In the peripallium, S100B/PCNA double-positive cells were consistently positioned at least one cell layer away from notum1a-expressing clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1A, top and middle, filled arrows). Region-specific expression patterns were apparent: notum1a followed a pattern excluding the outer layer in the peripallium but was broadly expressed in the central ventricular zone. Despite these regional differences, proliferating radial glia did not co-express notum1a. Furthermore, notum1a expression did not appear to restrict proliferation, as S100B-negative/PCNA-positive cells were readily detected within notum1a-positive territories (Supplementary Fig. 1A, bottom, blank arrows).

To assess notum1a expression under non-physiological conditions, we examined its pattern after physical injury induced by piercing the telencephalon. At 3 days post-injury (3 dpi), the notum1a expression domain remained unchanged compared to intact tissue. In the peripallial region, notum1a expression terminated within 1–2 cell layers from the peripheral margin, similar to uninjured controls. Most PCNA-positive cells were localized in the deeper pallial layers; nevertheless, a small yet noticeable number appeared at the peripheral surface, where notum1a expression was absent (Supplementary Fig. 1B, top).

Following injury, the number of PCNA-S100B double-positive radial glia increased in the affected tissue. Unlike in intact brains, some radial glial cells in the central ventricular region of injured tissue expressed notum1a (Supplementary Fig. 1B middle). Although rare, these cells indicate that, in response to severe tissue disruption, dividing radial glial cells can incorporate notum1a into their transcriptional program. This finding raises the possibility that notum1a contributes to the regulation of proliferation dynamics within the highly stimulated regenerative niche.

In Silico analysis confirms conservation of the ABC99-binding pocket in zebrafish notum1a

To further investigate the role of notum1a in the zebrafish brain, we employed a pharmacological approach targeting its activity in adult zebrafish. As a first step, we performed in silico docking to evaluate whether the Notum inhibitor ABC99 could bind zebrafish Notum1a. ABC99, a selective N-hydroxyhydantoin carbamate inhibitor, blocks Wnt signaling by competitively occupying the serine hydrolase active site and preventing Wnt ligand depalmitoleoylation22. While its binding to human NOTUM has been demonstrated18, interactions with zebrafish Notum paralogs remain uncharacterized. To address this, we modeled ABC99 binding to zebrafish Notum1a, while excluding Notum1b due to its lack of expression in the adult brain. We also examined Notum2, another homolog that shares 51% amino acid identity with human NOTUM27 and thus represents a potential alternative ABC99 target.

To explore the evolutionary and structural relationships among zebrafish Notum1a, (UniProt ID: C5H5C4), Notum2 (UniProt ID: E7F0Z8), and human NOTUM (UniProt ID: Q6P988), we compared their amino acid identities and structural similarities. Notum1a shares 72.1% sequence identity and 80.6% similarity with human NOTUM, indicating a strong conservation that likely preserves its function (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Notum2 exhibits only 49% identity and 63% similarity, reflecting greater evolutionary divergence (Fig. 2B). Consistently, a phylogenetic tree derived from a multiple sequence alignment of human NOTUM, zebrafish Notum1a, and zebrafish Notum2 places human NOTUM closer to Notum1a than to Notum2 (Fig. 2C)28. Structural analysis further supported these findings: the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between Notum1a (Swiss Model: C5H5C4) and human NOTUM (PDB ID: 4UYZ) was 16.6 Å, whereas Notum2 (Swiss Model: E7F0Z8) showed a higher deviation of 17.37 Å. These data suggest that Notum1a is structurally more similar to human NOTUM than Notum2.

ABC99 binding to human NOTUM was first validated by comparing its docking conformation with that of palmitoleic acid (PAM) (PDB ID: 8TZR), a known ligand12 (Fig. 2D). Both molecules occupied the same binding pocket with overlapping conformations (Fig. 2E and F). Binding interactions were further supported by DiffDock confidence scores29,30, which showed comparable binding patterns for PAM and ABC99, reinforcing the reliability of the predicted ABC99 binding site. In zebrafish, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis revealed differences between the best ABC99 docking poses on Notum1a and Notum2 relative to human NOTUM: 0.88 Å for Notum1a and 1.949 Å for Notum2, indicating that Notum1a retains a more conserved binding pocket conformation than Notum2 (Fig. 2G and H). Biologically, the sub-Å deviation for Notum1a suggests that ABC99 adopts nearly the same orientation as in human NOTUM, engaging the conserved catalytic groove and key contacts consistent with on-target inhibition. By contrast, the ~ 2 Å deviation for Notum2 reflects a reoriented pose with weakened active-site interactions, suggesting reduced binding efficiency and selectivity.

To further validate the stability of ABC99 binding, we conducted 100 ns-long molecular dynamics simulations for human NOTUM, zebrafish Notum1a, and Notum2 in complex with ABC99. In human NOTUM, ABC99 remained bound for 60 ns before transiently dissociating, then re-establishing its binding to the pocket preserving its initial orientation (Fig. 2I and L). Notum1a exhibited the most stable interaction, with ABC99 remaining bound throughout the 100 ns simulation (Fig. 2J and L), reinforcing its compatibility as a potential drug target. In contrast, ABC99 rapidly dissociated from Notum2 and failed to rebind, instead interacted transiently with peripheral loops, reflecting its instability in this binding site (Fig. 2K and L).

These findings indicate that zebrafish Notum1a shares significant sequence and structural similarity with human NOTUM, particularly in its predicted ability to stably bind ABC99. Unlike Notum2, which lacks a stable binding pocket, Notum1a appears as a strong candidate for studying Notum function in zebrafish and pharmacological targeting with ABC99.

Pharmacological modulation of notum activity induces progenitor cell proliferation and neurogenesis

After validating the binding of ABC99 to Notum1a in silico, we proceeded with experimental analysis. Early embryonic experiments revealed severe morphological abnormalities, including body axis malformations and impaired head formation, leading to smaller brains (approximately 17% decrease in whole-brain size) and absence of eyes (Supplementary Fig. 1). In adult zebrafish, we utilized cerebroventricular microinjection (CVMI) for drug delivery- a widely used method for functional drug studies in the zebrafish brain31. 500 µM ABC99 was injected through glass capillary needles into a skull incision for three consecutive days. To analyze proliferative activity, PCNA-positive cells were quantified in both central ventricular and peripallial regions.

ABC99 administration significantly increased the number of PCNA-positive proliferating cells in the adult zebrafish brain (Fig. 3A). Because progenitor cell activity varies by location, we analyzed proliferation within specific brain regions. In the central ventricular region, drug-treated animals exhibited a 1.8-fold increase in proliferating cell numbers (DMSO: 11.93 ± 1.00, ABC99: 21.15 ± 2.90 cells, mean ± SEM; Welch’s t-test, p = 0.0441). A less significant yet comparable 1.9-fold increase was observed in the peripallial region (DMSO: 19.08 ± 6.53, ABC99: 36.20 ± 1.16 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.0766). To determine the activation of progenitor pools, we quantified PCNA/S100B double-positive cells separately in each region. ABC99 administration led to a 2.5-fold increase in active progenitor cells in the ventricular region (DMSO: 3.45 ± 0.44, ABC99: 8.52 ± 0.76 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.0012). Similarly, the peripallial region showed a 2.6-fold increase in active progenitors (DMSO: 6.00 ± 1.27, ABC99: 15.40 ± 1.34 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p < 0.0022). These results suggest ABC99 enhances cellular proliferation and drives radial glial cells out of quiescence.

To examine the impact of ABC99-induced proliferation on neurogenesis, zebrafish received an intraperitoneal injection of 10 mM BrdU/PBS following the third day of drug administration (Fig. 3B). Label-retaining mature neurons were quantified a week later based on HuC/D (pan-neuronal marker) and BrdU immunoreactivity. In the peripallial region, drug-treated animals displayed a 2.1-fold increase in BrdU-retaining cells (DMSO: 10.59 ± 1.20, ABC99: 22.64 ± 1.90 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.0017) and a 2.5-fold increase in newborn neurons (DMSO: 6.54 ± 1.41, ABC99: 16.37 ± 2.93 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.0235). These changes correspond to an increase in neurogenic commitment, with the ratio of newborn neurons rising from 62% to 78%. However, in the central ventricular region, neither the overall BrdU-retaining cell count nor the newborn HuC/D-positive neuron count was altered following ABC99 administration (BrdU-retaining cells = DMSO: 16,07 ± 2.97, ABC99: 16.50 ± 2.90, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p > 0.9422) (BrdU-retaining HuC/D-positive neurons = DMSO: 13.99 ± 2.54, ABC99: 14.75 ± 4,53, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.887). These results indicate that ABC99-induced proliferation selectively contributes to neurogenesis in the peripallial region.

Given the proliferative effects of ABC99, we examined the influence of GW6471, a small-molecule drug known to upregulate notum expression. GW6471 inhibits neurogenesis by suppressing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha, leading to increased notum production16,32. To assess its impact on proliferation, adult zebrafish were subjected to stab injuries to elicit a robust proliferative response and subsequently received 750 µM GW6471 via CVMI for three consecutive days (Supplementary Fig. 3). GW6471 administration significantly reduced S100B/PCNA double-positive progenitor activation (peripallial = DMSO: 21,98 ± 2,26, GW6471: 12,06 ± 1.31 cells, mean ± SEM; unpaired t-test, p = 0.0193; central ventricular = DMSO: 20,30 ± 3,89, GW6471: 7,13 ± 143 cells, mean ± SEM; Welch’s t-test, p = 0,0364) (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Similarly, GW6471 suppressed BrdU-retaining newborn neuron generation in the entire telencephalon (whole tissue = DMSO: 127.80 ± 7,72, GW6471: 66,53 ± 3,98 cells, mean ± SEM, Welch’s t-test, p = 0,0059) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). To avoid potential confounding from injury-induced cell migration, progenitor cell counts were performed across the entire pallium rather than within specific subregions. Our findings reveal a regulatory role for Notum in progenitor proliferation and neurogenesis in the adult zebrafish brain. Pharmacological inhibition with ABC99 enhanced both processes, while GW6471 suppressed them, highlighting Notum as a potential target for modulating adult neurogenesis.

ABC99 administration does not affect canonical Wnt signaling robustly

Notum is known to inhibit canonical Wnt signaling by targeting Wnt proteins in a feedback loop14. Given the increased proliferation observed following ABC99 administration, we investigated whether this effect was associated with Wnt signaling activity at mRNA level. To assess Wnt pathway involvement, we quantified the expression of key canonical Wnt signaling target genes lef1, axin2, myc, and cdnn1, using qPCR. we noted a significant upregulation of lef1; however, no significant differences were detected between drug-treated and control groups for the other target genes (Fig. 4). Our findings do not determine a strong association between the functional effects of ABC99 and canonical Wnt signaling, suggesting an alternative mechanism driving progenitor cell activation in the adult zebrafish telencephalon.

Discussion

This study investigates the role of the carboxylesterase enzyme Notum in regulating adult neurogenesis by analyzing its expression and functional impact in the zebrafish brain. Using pharmacological inhibition and transcriptional analyses, we aimed to uncover both canonical and potential non-canonical mechanisms of Notum-mediated regulation. Our data show that notum1a is predominantly expressed in neuroD1-positive early neurons and HuC/D-positive maturing neurons rather than in progenitor cells. This pattern suggests that notum1a may function during the neuronal transition from progenitors to differentiated neurons or by contributing to metabolic processes within neuronal populations. Consistent with this, single cell RNA-sequencing of the mouse brain has identified Notum expression in a subset of neural stem cell intermediates17, pointing to an evolutionarily conserved mechanism. Alternatively, the expression observed in neuronal populations may reflect the metabolic remodeling demands that arise during neuronal differentiation, as Notum has also been implicated in energy-intensive metabolic pathways such as fatty acid metabolism33. Although rarely emphasized in previous studies34–36, analysis of available transcriptomic datasets indicates that per-cell expression is generally low. Nonetheless, its persistent presence in (inter)neuronal populations suggests a broader physiological significance. This persistence also complicates cell-type-specific analyses, particularly in central regions with densely packed layers. Interestingly, the spatial expression of notum may provide more information about neighboring transcript-silent cells than about the transcript-producing clusters themselves.

In the mouse brain, activated neural stem cells produce Notum to suppress the expansion of neighboring stem cells in neurogenic regions, and Notum inhibition results in aberrant neurogenesis17. The expression pattern of notum1a in the zebrafish brain may serve a comparable role, maintaining molecular balance by promoting differentiation while limiting excessive symmetrical divisions among transit amplifying cells. Supporting this model, pharmacological inhibition of Notum with ABC99 significantly increased telencephalic proliferation and stem cell activation, as demonstrated by elevated numbers of PCNA-positive cells and PCNA/S100B double positive cells. However, BrdU pulse-chase assays revealed that this activation is only partially sustained over time. The number of newborn neurons significantly increased in the peripallial region, whereas no comparable increase was detected in the central ventricular region, despite a transient early rise in radial glia activation.

Neural stem cells in the adult zebrafish brain display region-specific characteristics37. In our study, we analyzed the proliferation behavior across the whole telencephalon. The dorsal part of the telencephalon was referred to as peripallial in the text, reflecting the positioning of progenitor cells in the thin ependymal layer surrounding the pallium. The midline region along the ventricular cavity (termed “central ventricular region” in this study) displays substantial anatomical variations and progenitor heterogeneity along the anterior-posterior axis, which affects proliferation behavior. To account for potential spatial differences that may mask effects in the peripallial region, this region was analyzed separately. The temporal pattern of drug-induced stem cell activation suggests that progenitors in these regions respond differently, particularly in the persistence of proliferation rather than its onset.

The selective contribution of stem cell activation to mature neuron generation in the peripallial region but not in the ventricular region suggests distinct neurogenic regulatory mechanisms in these areas. The densely packed cellular organization of the central ventricular region likely forces notum1a-positive cells into close contact with radial glial cells, creating a highly compact extracellular environment. This structural arrangement may enforce stricter notum1a-mediated cell cycle control in the central ventricular region than in the peripallial region, thereby suppressing drug-induced proliferation before it leads to increased neurogenesis. Additionally, the compact nature of the ventricular region may hinder drug penetration, potentially limiting ABC99’s long-term effects in this region.

Notum’s role in regulating cell proliferation extends beyond the nervous system16,17,38. This regulatory role is closely linked to its function as a direct target of the canonical Wnt pathway14. Our findings indicate that Notum’s regulatory role is conserved in the zebrafish brain; however, we did not observe a direct correlation between increased proliferation following Notum inhibition and enhanced Wnt signaling in the intact tissue. We did not observe a notable spatial change in notum1a expression during regeneration. Regenerative neurogenesis recruits various other components beyond those involved in maintenance neurogenesis during the response and given that essential immune-origin cells are recruited in the process of reactive proliferation response39, it is important to acknowledge that Notum and its inhibition may have other regulatory functions beyond stem cell activation in CNS regeneration.

Our findings suggest that Notum’s role may extend beyond its established role in the canonical Wnt pathway during neurogenesis. Instead, they raise questions about alternative signaling mechanisms regulated by Notum in the adult brain, potentially through interactions with other ECM components. While the observed changes were not consistent across all canonical Wnt targets, we noted a significant upregulation of lef1, suggesting a more nuanced involvement of Wnt signaling that warrants deeper investigation. Our data underscore the need for advanced enrichment strategies targeting specific non-neuronal subpopulations and proliferative cells to more accurately characterize these subtle transcriptional shifts. Furthermore, our findings open new avenues for exploring Notum’s broader role and its potential interacting partners in telencephalic neurogenesis.

A promising direction for future research involves molecular regulators of membrane organization, such as glypicans, which act as scaffolds in multiple signaling pathways, including those influenced by Notum14,40–42. How Notum inhibition alters glypican interactions and signaling capacity remains an open question. While zebrafish possess ten glypican homologs, their roles in tissue homeostasis remain largely unexplored43. Their expression in highly proliferative tissues, such as regenerating fins44, suggests that ABC99-mediated Notum inhibition may broadly impact proteoglycans beyond those directly associated with Wnt signaling in the adult brain.

This study provides evidence for notum1a expression in the adult zebrafish brain and suggests that Notum inhibition can shift the neurogenic balance. Previous work has proposed that pharmacological inhibition of Notum may exert restorative effects, such as enhancing neurogenic proliferation and modulating signaling pathways involved in stem cell activation under neurodegenerative conditions18. Our findings are consistent with this idea but further suggest that Notum’s functional network extends beyond the Wnt signaling. In particular, it is conceivable that Notum’s lipid-modifying activity influences both paracrine signaling and direct-contact pathways, such as Notch, especially in brain regions where progenitor and differentiated cells are densely packed. Defining additional Notum interaction partners and their roles in neurogenesis will be critical for understanding how proliferating and quiescent cells are dynamically regulated within the adult progenitor niche.

While ABC99 provides valuable insight into the consequences of Notum inhibition, the absence of widespread modulation of canonical Wnt targets does not diminish Notum’s functional importance. Rather, it underscores the need for refined investigative tools, such as Wnt-reporter lines, to monitor target gene activity and cell-type-specific enrichment strategies to uncover the context-dependent roles of Notum. Our findings support a broader perspective on Notum’s molecular functions in the adult brain, encouraging further exploration of its signaling interactions, particularly within the telencephalon, where neurogenic mechanisms remain incompletely characterized. To more precisely define notum1a’s role in adult neurogenesis and assess its therapeutic potential, future studies should incorporate more selective inhibitors or genetic approaches, such as CRISPR-based knockouts. These strategies will be critical for delineating the specific contributions of Notum in distinct cellular populations and neurogenic niches of the adult brain.

Limitations of the study

The results reported here have several limitations. First, broad expression of notum1a across the pallium makes cell type–specific analyses challenging, and the lack of a zebrafish-specific Notum antibody prevents direct protein-level assessment, restricting us to observational ISH/IHC approaches. Quantitative analyses relied primarily on PCNA/S100B labeling, which captures only a subset of progenitor populations; additional markers such as BLBP, GFAP, or nestin could provide a more comprehensive profile. Anatomical heterogeneity and the compact organization of the central ventricular region may influence proliferation behavior and drug penetration, affecting regional responses.

qPCR analysis revealed subtle upregulation of lef1, suggesting nuanced Wnt regulation that warrants further investigation. However, assessment of canonical Wnt signaling using bulk qPCR may lack sensitivity for subtle or cell type–specific changes, and potential involvement of Wnt-independent or non-canonical pathways remains untested. These limitations highlight the need for high resolution strategies, including single-cell RNA sequencing and FACS, to better define Notum’s role and its interacting partners in adult zebrafish neurogenesis.

Materials and methods

Fish husbandry

Wildtype fish were maintained in the İzmir Biomedicine and Genome Center Vivarium-Zebrafish Core Facility under standard husbandry conditions (temperature at 28 °C, pH at 7.4, conductivity at 400 µS/cm, and a 14/10-hour light-dark cycle). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the live animal handling and research regulations under protocols approved by the animal welfare committee of Izmir Genome and Biomedicine Center, Türkiye.

Stab injury

Adult fish between 9 and 15 months were anesthetized with Tricaine 0.02% of Tricaine (MS-222, Sigma-Aldrich). Telencephalic injury was performed as described in45,46. A 30-gauge needle was inserted through the left nostril until the end of the telencephalon. Fish were immediately put back to freshwater tank upon the injury application.

Drug administration

Wild-type (AB) zebrafish embryos were treated with ABC99 (125 µM or 250 µM) or DMSO from 1 to 24 h post-fertilization (hpf). Following treatment, the embryos were rinsed with E3 medium to remove the drug, anesthetized using 1 mg/mL tricaine and transferred to a 6-well glass-bottom plate. Bright-field live imaging was then performed using a stereo microscope.

ABC99, GW6471 and DMSO administrations to adult fish brain were performed as described in31. Briefly, adult fish were initially anesthetized with Tricaine in freshwater tank as above and placed on a wet sponge onto the stage of a stereomicroscope. A slit through the skull close to the midline above the left optic tectum was opened using a barbed-end insulin needle. Approximately 1 µl solution was injected directly into the slit using glass microcapillaries (Final concentration for ABC99: 500 µM, for GW6471: 750 µM; stock solutions for both drugs were prepared in DMSO). An animal was excluded from the experiment if the injected solution leaked back from the slit. Animals that received the solution successfully were put back to freshwater. Because the opening did not heal itself in a single day, no new slit was opened for the following daily injections. For experiments including multiple procedures (i.e. stab injury + drug administration), the stab injury was followed by drug administration with a 15-minute recovery period in between to minimize the operational stress on the animals.

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Zebrafish were anesthetized using 0.02% Tricaine-S solution and sacrificed after receiving daily doses of drugs for three days. Their skulls were removed, and intact telencephalons were collected. RNA isolation was performed from individual telencephalons using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Experiments were carried out in triplicate for each group. Total RNA samples were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). All RNA samples were then converted to cDNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions47.

Tissue Preparation

To collect brain samples, adult fish were euthanized through rapid-chilling immersion into ice water bath and were decapitated with a sharp blade. Telencephalons were dissected in ice-cold PBS solution and fixated with 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Fixed samples were then incubated in %30 sucrose solution in 0.1 M Phosphate buffer for 2–3 days at 4 °C for the cryoprotection. For cryosectioning, samples were mounted in Tissue-Plus OCT Compound (Ted Pella, 27050, Redding, CA, USA), and sections of 12 μm thickness were taken on a Leica CM1950 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Germany). Slides were dried at 62°Cfor a minimum 2 h and kept at −80 °C for later use.

To minimize animal-to-animal variation, samples were collected from tanks containing animals of the same age group, and specimens with extreme body or brain sizes were excluded. To reduce section-to-section variation, images were obtained from adjacent sections on the same slide (12 μm apart), and sections that were disproportionately large or small for the scanning area were excluded.

In situ hybridization

Probes were transcribed from cDNA-amplified PCR products with flanking T7 promoters using DIG-labeled NTPs (Roche) as described in47. Primers for the probes used in this study are as follows:

notum1a-F

TATTTCTTGAGGGCGGCTGG;

notum1a-R

CCAAGCTTCTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGAGTCTTGTAGACTGCGGTCCC48;

notum1b-F

CTTCATGGCGCAGGTGAAGA;

notum1b-R

CCAAGCTTCTAATACGACTCACTATAGGGAGATCCGCTGATCTGCTGATGAT.

(underlined sequences contain T7 promoter region). In situ hybridization was performed as described in49 with several changes. Briefly, tissue sections were re-hydrated with 1 x PBS three times for 5 min, followed by 10 µg/ml Proteinase K treatment in 0.1% PBST (1× PBS/0.1% Tween20) for 6 min at 37 °C. Remaining Proteinase K activity was ceased by post-fixation with 4% PFA at room temperature for 20 min, and the slides were washed with 1 x PBS for 5 min three times. This step was followed by a pre-hybridization step for 1 h. The slides were finally hybridized with antisense probe (concentration: 0.5–1 ng/µl) overnight. The probes were gradually washed away by (1) 20 min hybridization buffer once, (2) 30 min 1x Sodium Citrate Buffer (SSC) twice, (3) 30 min 2x Sodium Citrate Buffer (SSC) twice, 3) 30 min 0.2x Sodium Citrate Buffer (SSC) twice, (4) 30 min 0.1x Sodium Citrate Buffer (SSC) twice. Pre-hybridization, hybridization and washing steps were all performed in a humidity chamber containing 50% formamide/water and at 65 °C. The slides were then blocked with 10 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Amresco, Solon, OH, USA) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with anti-DIG-AP antibody overnight at 4 °C. Alkaline phosphatase substrates nitro blue tetrazolium/5-bromo−4-chloro−3-indolyl-phosphate (NBT-BCIP) and Fast-Red (Sigma-Aldrich, F4648, St. Louis, MO, USA) were used for chromogenic and fluorimetric signal detection, respectively. For combined assays, in situ hybridization is firstly performed, immediately followed by immunohistochemistry as described below.

Immunohistochemistry and BrdU incorporation assay

In pulse-chase experiments, animals were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mM thymidine analog 5-bromo-2’-deoxyuridine (BrdU; Chem-Impex, 00045, Wood Dale, IL, USA) dissolved in water or PBS at scheduled time points. Brain samples were collected as described above.

For immunostaining, frozen sections were washed twice with PBST for 10 min followed by an incubation with 10 mM sodium citrate for 15 min at 85 °C. For BrdU assays, sections were additionally incubated with 4 N HCl for 15 min and washed with 1 × PBS twice for 5 min. They were then incubated in 10 mg/ml BSA dissolved in PBST for 1 h at room temperature to reduce non-specific antigen binding. This step was followed by the overnight incubation with primary antibodies in 2 mg/ml bovine serum albumin dissolved in PBST at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: mouse anti-PCNA (1:300, M0879, Dako, Agilent, CA, United States), rabbit anti-S100β (1:300, Z0311, Dako, Agilent, CA, United States), mouse anti-GFAP (1:500, ab154474, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), mouse anti-NeuroD1, (1:300, ab60704, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), mouse anti-HuC/D (1:300, A-21271, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), rat anti-BrdU (1:300, MCA2060GA, Bio-Rad, Kidlington, UK). The secondary antibodies are as follows: Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, #4412S, Cell Signaling Technology, Leiden, The Netherlands), rhodamine (TRITC) AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, 711-025-152, Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories, PA, United States), Cy5 AffiniPure donkey anti-rat IgG (H + L) (1:400, 712-175-150, Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories), Cy3 AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:400, 715-165-150, Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories), Cy5 AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:400, 715-175-150, Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories). Nuclear staining was performed with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 4083 S, Cell Signaling Technology).

Statistical significance was evaluated using an unpaired t test was used for statistical calculations. The values are the mean ± SEM (standard error of mean) of three samples. Imaging

Fluorescent images were obtained using an LSM 880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) and a Leica SP5-AOBS confocal 1292 microscopes (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at 1024 × 1024 and 2048 × 2048 pixel resolution with 20x immersion lenses. For all experimental groups, images were acquired under identical laser, gain, and exposure settings to ensure comparability. Image stacks were processed using ImageJ software52 for brightness/contrast adjustment, and cropping of relevant regions. No nonlinear adjustments (e.g. gamma), or selective removal, combination, or repositioning of image features were applied.

Brightfield images were obtained with an Olympus SZX10 stereomicroscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistics

For PCNA and BrdU analysis, marker positive cells were counted per section. Average values of cell count/section per fish is considered a single data point. Sections were obtained from at least 3 animals (biological replicates) per condition. Quantitative data were statistically analyzed with unpaired Student’s t-test (equal SD) and Welch’s t-test (unequal SD) and graphed in GraphPad Prism 10. Plots and other images were exported to vector graphics editor Adobe Illustrator 2024 for figure preparation.

Molecular Docking

Three-dimensional structural models for Danio rerio Notum1a and Notum2 were extracted from the SwissModel53,54 repository. The Notum2 model (SwissModel ID: A0AB13A6U2) and the Notum1a model (SwissModel ID: C5H5C4) were used. For human NOTUM, the crystal structure was obtained from the PDB database (PDB ID: 4UYZ, Chain D)14.

The ligands ABC99 and Palmitoleic Acid (PAM) were prepared for docking. The molecular structure of ABC99 was retrieved from PubChem (CID: 134817170)55 and visualized using MolView56. The structure was converted to MOL2 format using OpenBabel57. The PAM structure was extracted directly from the PDB entry 8TZR.

Molecular docking simulations were performed using the NVIDIA DiffDock platform29,30. PAM and ABC99 were docked to human NOTUM , while ABC99 was also docked into the zebrafish Notum1a and Notum2 models. Docking simulations identified the best-posed ligand conformations based on binding affinity and molecular interactions, which were retained for further molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The force field parameters for ABC99 were generated using LigParGen9. Systems for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were prepared using the CHARMM-GUI input generator58–60. Each protein-ligand complex was solvated with TIP3P water molecules and neutralized with Na + and Cl- ions. The systems were composed as; human NOTUM : 51,749 atoms, zebrafish Notum1a : 51,595 atoms, and zebrafish Notum2 : 51,518 atoms.

MD simulations were performed using NAMD v3.0.1 with CUDA acceleration61,62. The CHARMM36 force field (CHARMM36m for proteins)63 was used to describe residue pair interactions. Simulations were conducted under periodic boundary conditions in an isothermal, isobaric NPT ensemble at a constant temperature of 310 K for human NOTUM and 301.15 K for zebrafish Notum1a and Notum2 , with a constant pressure of 1 bar. Temperature and pressure were controlled using the Langevin thermostat and Langevin piston barostat, respectively64.

The equations of motion were integrated using a 2 fs time step, and the SHAKE algorithm was employed to constrain covalent bonds involving non-water hydrogen atoms. A non-bonded cutoff value of 12 Å was applied. 1,000 steps of minimization was applied to the systems to stabilize initial structures. 200 ns equilibration simulations were run with restrained motions on protein atoms. Equilibration progress was monitored by observing the energy decrease and root mean square deviation (RMSD) changes in the total system. Finally, a 100 ns production simulation was conducted for each system to evaluate ligand binding stability. Data from the production runs, including RMSD plots and energy profiles, were analyzed to assess the dynamic stability of the ligand-protein complexes via VMD61 and PyMOL65.
