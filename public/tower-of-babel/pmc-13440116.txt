Comprehensive genomic characterization of extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli isolated from neonates: multiple center insights into virulence, resistance, and transmission dynamics
Authors: Dongmiao Zhang, Yijun Ding, Wenqing Kang, Xueping Zhu, Zheng Cao, Yangfang Li, Jidong Lai, Jinxing Feng, Xiaoyun Wang, Guoqiang Hou, Yanyan Wang, Xianghong Li, Yang Wang, Xiao Liang, Linhui Hao, Peicen Zou, Juntao Li, Ruiqi Xiao, Hengliang Wang, Chao Pan, Yajuan Wang

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC), which can cause severe long-term sequelae by systemic infections, is gradually becoming the primary pathogen threatening neonatal health. The lack of large-scale genomic epidemiological investigation hinders further understanding of neonatal ExPEC. We conducted this nationwide multicenter study to support further strategies for improving neonatal ExPEC management.MethodsThe neonatal ExPEC strains and clinical information, including antimicrobial resistance phenotype, were collected from nine centers within 7 provinces across China between 2018 and 2023. Whole-genome sequencing was performed. Sequence types (ST) and serotypes were acquired to characterize the strains. Phylogenetic analysis and pan-genomic analysis were conducted to identify the population structure. Bioinformatics analysis associated with virulence factors, antimicrobial resistance genes, and mobile genetic elements were conducted. To characterize the situation of horizontal gene transfer, we developed a computational tool for identifying horizontal evolutionary patterns from large-scale genomic draft assemblies. Co-occurrence and co-localization metrics were used to describe the synergistic effects and transmission mechanism of genes.ResultsA total of 411 neonatal ExPEC strains were included. ST1193 (18·0%) was the main ST, while O75 (15·8%) was the most common serotype. Virulence factors and antimicrobial resistance genes were widely distributed across various STs, provinces, years, and isolation sites. Co-occurrence analysis revealed multiple clusters of virulence factors and antimicrobial resistance genes, suggesting co-transmission or co-evolution. Multiple kinds of mobile genetic elements were widely distributed throughout the country. The predicted plasmid-derived contig, genome islands, prophages, and transposons carry different pathogenic genes, respectively. Multiple pathogenic genes exhibited co-occurrence with a specific plasmid replicon, suggesting the critical role of plasmids in the evolution of ExPEC.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that neonatal ExPEC had a shared phylogenetic spectrum with adult ExPEC isolates, but distinct dominant subtypes. Multiple virulence factors and drug resistance genes form a complex network that enhances pathogenicity. The formation of these gene clusters is associated with both the inherent genetic factors of ExPEC and the involvement of complex mobile genetic elements. These data accelerate the understanding of neonatal ExPEC, revealing the distribution of STs, serotypes, pathogenic genes, and transmission dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01717-8.

Background

Neonates represent one of the most vulnerable populations [1], with infectious diseases accounting for one-fourth of neonatal mortality worldwide [2]. Despite improved pathogen surveillance and sanitation, the rising incidence of extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC) [3] infections is reversing progress in neonatal health, posing an urgent clinical challenge [4]. According to 2023 data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET) [5], Escherichia coli (E. coli) consistently ranks as the predominant clinical bacterial pathogen, with persistently high antimicrobial resistance (AMR) rates, causing the most cases of neonatal early-onset infection with severe symptoms [3].

Regarding microbial pathogenesis in hosts, virulence factors (VFs) and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs) play essential roles, which facilitate pathogen colonization, persistence, and host immune evasion [6]. Both of them collectively drive persistent infections and complicate clinical management. However, most E. coli are symbiotic bacteria that are non-pathogenic. The presence of VFs and ARGs results from horizontal gene transfer (HGT) caused by mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including plasmids, pathogenic islands, prophages, integrons, insertion sequences, and transposons [7].

Existing ExPEC research has predominantly focused on adult populations, with limited data available for children, especially neonates (Additional file 1: Supplementary information 1) [8, 9]. However, the divergent clinical profiles of neonatal versus adult ExPEC infections strongly indicate phylogenetic differentiation between these populations. This differentiation is particularly pronounced in pathogenesis: the vertical acquisition of maternal pathogens, entry point related to birth trauma, underdeveloped physiological barriers, compounded by immature immune systems. Moreover, the clinical management of neonatal ExPEC infections needs more caution, given that some systemic infections may lead to long-term or even lifelong sequelae. Our previous study has shown the unique prevalent AMR spectrum, particularly against cephalosporins [10]. It was concerning given that current guidelines still recommend these agents as first-line therapy [11].

In addition, neonatal intestinal microflora development is disproportionately affected by broad-spectrum antibiotics compared to the traditional antibiotics [12], potentially creating long-term dysbiosis risks [13]. Thus, non-antibiotic approaches warrant consideration, such as improved hygiene conditions and vaccines [14]. For neonatal populations, maternal vaccination demonstrates a promising potential application [15]. While E. coli surface polysaccharide antigens, such as the lipopolysaccharide (O antigen) and capsular polysaccharide (K antigen) [16], represent promising vaccine targets acknowledged in WHO's priority vaccine list [17], research on Chinese serotypes remains limited, especially for the special group of neonates. The secondary serotypes associated with vaccines showed diversity across continents, creating fundamental barriers to effective vaccine development [18]. These well-documented situational variations necessitate the implementation of precision surveillance systems informed by local molecular epidemiology.

In this article, we present a nationwide, retrospective epidemiological study of ExPEC in neonatal infection in China, which represents the largest genome investigation in this topic around the world. To address the above challenges, we integrated whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data with the phenotype of strains and their resistance across nine Chinese neonatal centers. We provide general molecular typing, serotyping, and other data to clarify the basic characteristics of prevalent strains. We also provide comprehensive data on VFs, ARGs, and MGEs and their co-occurrence information to understand the potential cooperative pathogenic and MDR mechanisms. Our findings offer foundational insights for developing targeted infection-control strategies for neonatal ExPEC. To our knowledge, this represents the first large-scale genomic characterization of ExPEC exclusively focused on neonatal isolates worldwide, filling a critical gap in understanding age-specific pathogens.

Methods

Study design and participants

Neonates hospitalized between 2018 and 2023 and aged below 28 days were included in this study. Patient-derived samples, including lower tract respiratory secretions, blood, urine, surface secretions, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) tips, abdominal cavity fluids, and gastric fluid, were continuously collected by nine class A tertiary hospitals located in seven provinces in China. E. coli strains were isolated from the samples initially used for routine clinical diagnostics. The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) clinical suspicion or confirmed diagnosis of infection, (2) positive E. coli culture, and (3) absence of other bacterial species detected at the same anatomical site. The isolated E. coli strains were transferred to the Capital Institute of Pediatrics for comprehensive characterization and WGS. Patient data were compiled by clinical experts from respective departments across participating hospitals, including the following information: age, gender, geographic location, isolation year, isolation site, and clinical diagnosis. After data aggregation, quality control and standardization were performed by another expert. The 411 isolates included in this study represent consecutively collected clinical isolates that met the predefined inclusion criteria and for which viable cultures and whole-genome sequencing data were available. The geographic distribution of participating hospitals and the number of isolates contributed by each center are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1 and Table S1. These isolates were included in downstream genomic analyses, including phylogenetic characterization, antimicrobial resistance and virulence profiling, plasmid analysis, and transmission analysis. No further selection based on clinical presentation or strain characteristics was applied.

All of our research procedures were conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and local ethical regulations and strictly met all ethical requirements. This cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Capital Institute of Pediatrics (approval number SHERLLM2025011). Informed consent for subsequent analysis was waived because this research did not involve any human samples or identifiable data.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted following the broth microdilution method according to the instructions of the Sensititre Gram Negative GNX2F Plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A total of 20 antimicrobial agents were tested, including ticarcillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefepime, aztreonam, ertapenem, doripenem, meropenem, imipenem, tobramycin, gentamicin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, doxycycline, tigecycline, minocycline, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, polymyxin B, and colistin. E. coli ATCC 25922 was utilized as the standard strain. The outcomes of the antimicrobial susceptibility testing were categorized as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant, based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2020 standards [19]. MDR was defined as bacteria being resistant to at least three antimicrobial classes.

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol with single bacterial colonies grown in nutrient agar at 37 °C overnight. WGS was conducted on all E. coli isolates collected with a strict DNA quality control rigorously performed prior to sequencing. DNA concentration was quantified using Quantus Fluorometer (Promega) to ensure ≥ 1 ng/μL. For each sequencing library preparation, a minimum input of 50 ng DNA per sample was required, which ensured optimal performance in downstream whole-genome sequencing applications. Purity was confirmed by A260/A280 (1.8–2.0) and A260/A230 (≥ 2.0) ratios, with visual inspection for particulates and discoloration. Integrity was verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis using GelRed staining, with acceptable samples showing intact bands ≥ 3 Kb without smearing or contamination. DNA samples were stored in nuclease-free ultrapure water at −80 °C with ≤ 3 freeze–thaw cycles.

Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following standard operating procedures. The fluorescence image files generated by the Illumina platform were converted into raw sequencing reads through base calling. The sequencing depth of each strain shall not be less than 100x, and the data volume shall be greater than 1.29 GB. Raw data underwent quality control processing using Trimmomatic [20], which included the following steps: (1) adapter removal; (2) Remove leading low quality or N bases (below quality 3); (3) Remove trailing low quality or N bases (below quality 3); (4) Scan the read with a 4-base wide sliding window, cutting when the average quality per base drops below 15; (5) Drop reads below the 36 bases long. Quality-filtered reads were subsequently assembled using the Shovill pipeline [21], which incorporated SPAdes [22] for de novo assembly, producing final genomic contigs. Genome assemblies were subjected to quality control prior to downstream analyses. Assemblies with genome completeness ≥ 95%, contamination ≤ 5%, and an average sequencing coverage ≥ 30 × were retained. Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM [23], and sequencing coverage was calculated by mapping reads back to the assemblies. Assemblies not meeting these criteria were excluded from further analyses. Genome assembly quality metrics for all included isolates are provided in Additional file 2: Table S2. The raw sequences [24] and genome assemblies [25] generated in this study have been deposited in a public repository, and the corresponding accession numbers and isolate metadata are provided in Additional file 2: Table S3.

Strains typing

The phylogenetic group of neonatal ExPEC isolates was performed using ClermonTyping v24.02 [26]. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using MLST v2.23.0 with the ecoli scheme, which assigns STs based on the allelic profiles of seven housekeeping genes [27]. To have a high-resolution genomics view, cgMLST analysis for each strain was performed using cgMLSTFinder v1.2.0 with the Enterobase Escherichia/coli cgMLST v1 scheme [28].

Serotyping was conducted using SerotypeFinder v2.0.2, which predicts serotypes based on the identification of O-antigen and H-antigen genes against the SerotypeFinder database [29]. If multiple results were found, the O and H serotype with the best identity was chosen.

Pan-genome and phylogenetic analysis

Prokka v1.14.6 was used to get whole-genome annotation with the parameters: –genus Escherichia –species coli –gram neg –usegenus [30]. Pan-genome analysis was conducted using Roary v3.13.0 with the following parameters: -e -n -i 90, using the data from Prokka [31]. To reflect horizontal gene content variation, a presence/absence matrix of genes was extracted from the Roary output and used to generate a gene content-based tree using the neighbor-joining method (Pan-genome tree). The core genome alignment produced by Roary, which represent the single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) distribution, was used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree in IQ-TREE v2.4.0, with the best-fit substitution model selected by ModelFinder and branch support assessed by 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (SNP tree) [32]. The cgMLST allelic profiles from cgMLSTFinder were used to generate another tree using the neighbor-joining method (cgMLST tree). The resultant phylogenetic tree was visualized using ggtree v.3.14.0 [33].

Phylogenetic tree comparisons were performed using the Robinson-Foulds (RF) metric to quantify topological differences between tree constructions. Pairwise topological distances were calculated using the dist.topo() from the ape [34] package to compute the normalized RF distance between each tree pair: SNP tree versus cgMLST tree, SNP tree versus pan-genome tree, and cgMLST tree versus pan-genome tree. The RF distance metric measures the fraction of differing bipartitions between trees, ranging from 0 for identical topologies to 1 for completely distinct topologies, enabling quantitative comparison of phylogenetic signals from different genomic features. These differences were visualized by tanglegram() from package dendextend [35] in R.

Pathogenicity prediction

VFs were identified using Virulencefinder v2.0.4 [36]. ARGs were detected using ResFinder v4.6.0 [37], with the option -s to designate species and -c -d to activate PointFinder [38] and DisinFinder [37]. These tools were mainly based on BLAST [39]. Thus, data were filtered with an identity threshold of ≥ 95% and coverage of ≥ 80%. For the report of prevalence, only the information on the presence or absence of genes is retained. For the co-location analysis with MGEs, the confirmed genes on different contigs are retained. Notably, the selected databases have limited coverage and do not capture all experimentally described genes, but instead focus on curated gene sets relevant to specific biological functions. In particular, VirulenceFinder primarily targets genes associated with clinically relevant pathogenicity and commonly used as conserved markers. Therefore, the VirulenceFinder database includes both classical virulence factors and virulence-associated genes. For simplicity, these genes are collectively referred to as “VFs” throughout this study unless otherwise specified.

Mobile genetic element analysis

For plasmid identification, we analyzed E. coli genome assemblies using PLASMe [40]. The obtained plasmid replicons were then annotated by PlasmidFinder v2.1.6 [41]. Due to the lack of long-read sequencing, the number of plasmids is unknown. Therefore, in the analysis, only attention was paid to whether it was located on the plasmid. Genomic islands were identified using IslandPath-DIMOB [42]. Prophages were identified using Phigaro [43]. Insertion sequences were identified using ISEScan_v1.7.2.1 [44]. Transposons were identified using TransposonPSI [45]. Integrons were identified using Integron_Finder v2.0 [46]. It should be noted that the applied tools identify mobile genetic elements primarily based on characteristic sequence features and gene content. These predictions do not explicitly define complete physical boundaries or chromosomal localization. Therefore, regions detected on plasmid-derived contigs were interpreted as plasmid-borne, mobility-associated gene clusters with genomic island-like features, rather than canonical chromosomal genomic islands.

Co-localization, co-occurrence, and correlation analysis

We developed an integrated tool, Co-occurrence based Horizontal Gene Transfer (CoHGT), to automate the complete analysis workflow (Additional file 1: Supplementary information 2) [47]. The output of VFs, ARGs, and MGEs from the bioinformatic tools first needed to be formatted for the next analysis. For BLAST -outfmt 6 output or similar tabular data, we developed the function formatgene() to organize it into a presence/absence matrix. To process JSON files, we employed functions for each bioinformatic tool to transfer it as long-format data. For the outputs from distinct strains, we employed the function copyfile() and jointsv() to summarize files and data. Hence, we got the presence/absence matrix files of VFs, ARGs, and MGEs and annotations by bioinformatic tools for each gene.

We first examined the positional relationship from the annotation data of VFs, ARGs, and MGEs. The start and end coordinates were acquired from the above bioinformatics analysis tools. Due to incomplete plasmid assembly, we only assessed whether genes on the plasmid and the co-localization of plasmid replicons with each gene were not reported. For integrons, genomic islands, and prophages, the gene shall be in the range. We used the function gene_in() to perform alignments at both the contig and sequence levels, which enabled us to determine the co-localization relationship between genes and MGEs.

To investigate the relationships among genes, we used the binary gene presence/absence matrix for VFs, ARGs, plasmid replicons, insertion sequences, and transposons based on all strains. Subsequently, pairwise calculations (Additional file 1: Supplementary information 3) of co-occurrence number, co-occurrence rate, jaccard index, and phi coefficient (φ) are performed. For the pairwise calculations within one document, the long format results and matrix format results can be acquired by function co_in_1() and co_matrix(), while for mutiple document, such as VFs and MGEs, co_two_in_1 and co_for_two() can be used. Co-occurrence heatmaps were visualized using the Heatmap() function from package ComplexHeatmap [48], while co-occurrence networks were generated through the ggraph() in package ggraph [49].

Statistical analysis

No data were excluded from the analyses. The results of this study were primarily based on descriptive indicators, including counts and ratios. Phylogroups, sequence types (ST), and serotypes were reported. The carriage rates of VFs, ARGs, and MGEs were calculated and reported by STs, region, year, and isolation site, respectively. We classified blood, CSF, abdominal cavity fluids, and PICC as invasive, while others were categorized as non-invasive for further analysis. Comparisons were made between invasive and non-invasive groups, as well as between MDR and non-MDR groups, using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Randomization and blinding were not applicable during data collection because this was an observational genomic study involving naturally occurring bacterial isolates. All bioinformatics methods and parameters are described in detail to ensure reproducibility, and the relevant code has been made publicly available.

Results

ExPEC from neonates exhibits great diversity

Phylogenetic analysis showed that B2 (59.6%, 245/411) was the most common group, followed by phylogroup D (17.5%, 72/411), phylogroup A (7.5%, 31/411), and phylogroup C (7.1%, 29/411). MLST analysis indicated that 400 E. coli isolates could be categorized into 69 STs, with 11 strains representing novel STs. The most common isolates, ST1193 (18.0%, 74/411), and other dominant types (> 5%), including ST95 (12.2%, 50/411), ST69 (9.5%, 39/411), ST131 (8.5%, 35/411), ST410 (5.6%, 23/411), and ST73 (5.4%, 22/411), made up nearly 60% of the whole data collection. The major STs were widely distributed across different geographical regions, years, and clinical sources (Fig. 1A). It is important to note that for groups with very low sample sizes (e.g., Hohhot, n = 2), the observed ST distribution is likely subject to substantial sampling bias and should not be interpreted as representing local epidemiology. The national predominance of ST1193 remained evident when analyzed across the larger, well-sampled groups. Among the main STs, ST1193, ST95, ST131, and ST73 belonged to phylogroup B2, while ST69 belonged to phylogroup D. Subsequently, we assessed serotype diversity with genome prediction. Both the O-serotype and H-serotype were identified with high diversity, with 48 O-serotypes and 29 H-serotypes, respectively (Additional file 2: Table S5). O75 (15.8%, 65/411) was the most prevalent O serotype, followed by O6 (9.2%, 38/411), O25 (8.8%, 36/411), O18 (7.3%, 30/411), O50 (6.8%, 28/411), O15 (6.1%, 25/411), O4 (5.4%, 22/411), O1 (5.1%, 21/411), O16 (3.9%, 16/411), and O17 (2.9%, 12/411). The H serotypes showed relative predominantly distributed in H5 (26.0%, 107/411), H4 (13.4%, 55/411), H7 (11.4%, 47/411), H18 (10.7%, 44/411), H1 (10.5%, 43/411), H9 (7.5%, 31/411), and H31 (4.1%, 17/411). Most STs were associated with certain O-serotypes and H-serotypes. When it comes to serotype combination diversity, 91 serotypes were found. The most common serotype was O75:H5, which was associated with ST1193, accounting for 15.8% (65/411) of the total collection and 87.8% (65/74) of ST1193. All the ST410 (n = 23) were identified as H9 with the absence of O-serotype. In addition, 29 strains identified as O25:H4 were associated with ST131 (n = 17) and ST95 (n = 11). 21 strains identified as O18:H7 were associated with ST95 (n = 17). The remaining 87 serotype combinations included fewer than 20 isolates, and 69 serotypes included fewer than 5 isolates. The relationship among the top 10 STs, O-serotypes, and H-serotypes was shown (Fig. 1B).

We also inspected the metadata of all strains by phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1C). Most ST410 was confined to the same year and hospital, suggesting a possible outbreak or local transmission. Similarly, within the ST1193 lineage, several sub-clusters were observed that were associated with specific time periods and sampling locations, indicating potential independent local transmission events. In contrast, most strains in different contexts were widely distributed across the entire phylogenetic tree, indicating the nationwide dissemination of most ST types.

Pan-genome analysis and phylogenetic tree comparison

Pan-genomic analysis indicated that neonatal ExPEC had a conserved core gene (3.1%, 1658/53435) and a large number of variable genes (96.9%) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A), demonstrating remarkable genomic plasticity in this species and highlighting the necessity to surveil HGT events in neonatal ExPEC. The heatmap of variable genes revealed the loss of core genome components in certain strains (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B).

To further evaluate the impact of horizontal evolution on neonatal ExPEC, three phylogenetic trees were constructed based on core genome SNP, allele numbering from core-genome MLST (cgMLST), and the gene presence-absence matrix derived from pan-genomic analysis, respectively reflecting the patterns of vertical inheritance via point mutations, mutations and losses of reference core genome, and HGT-driven evolution (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). All three phylogenetic trees effectively clustered strains belonging to the same ST. However, topological discordance was observed when comparing different STs, indicating fundamental differences in what each tree represents as evolution (Fig. 2). We quantified topological differences between trees using Robinson-Foulds (RF) distances. Pairwise comparisons revealed normalized Robinson-Foulds distances of 60.8 (raw RF = 496) between the SNP and cgMLST trees, 67.2 (RF = 548) between the SNP and pan-genome trees, and 58.8 (RF = 480) between the cgMLST and pan-genome trees. AMR determines the difficulty and duration of clinical treatment. The clinical management of neonatal ExPEC faces high cephalosporin resistance and carbapenem-associated necrotizing enterocolitis; furthermore, antibiotic options such as aminoglycosides and tetracyclines were restricted due to severe complications and relevant policy, causing a dilemma in therapeutic decision-making. The resistance rate of 20 antibiotics has been summarized (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Cephalosporin resistance has emerged at substantial rates across most regions, warranting the empirical use of β-lactamase inhibitors or carbapenems. For highly resistant strains, colistin may serve as the final option.

Resistance to different categories of antibiotics indicated that 85.4% (351/411) strains were resistant to at least one antibiotic, with 49.4% (203/411) classified as the MDR group (Fig. 3B). The resistance rates to different categories of antibiotics, from high to low, were listed as follows: sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim (62.0%, 254/411), cephalosporins (52.8%, 216/411), quinolones (47.0%, 192/411), tetracycline (35.0%, 143/411), monobactams (29.4%, 120/411), aminoglycosides (29.0%, 118/411), β-lactamase inhibitors (11.3%, 46/411), carbapenems (8.3%, 34/411), and colistin (0.7%, 3/411). Through co-resistance analysis of different strains, diverse resistance profiles were identified among the strains, demonstrating the association between β-lactam antibiotics and MDR.

Furthermore, ST-specific resistance analysis revealed notably varied resistance tendencies (Fig. 3C). ST410 was highly resistant to almost all categories except colistin, ST1193 and ST131 were also highly resistant types, while ST95 and ST73 had a low resistance rate compared to the mean level.

VFs associated with extraintestinal infections are prevalent in neonatal ExPEC

VFs are the primary mechanisms of ExPEC pathogenicity, such as adhesins, toxins, evasion, and invasins. A total of 135 virulence factors and virulence-associated genes were identified in neonatal ExPEC within the VirulenceFinder database (Additional file 2: Table S6). Only two genes of 135 were associated with enterotoxin, astA (3.9%, 16/411) and senB (40.9%, 169/411), suggesting a different pathogenic process from intestinal pathogenic E. coli (IPEC), given the low gene-carrying rate in neonatal ExPEC. Most notably, several genes were found to be conserved across nearly all strains, including fimH, yeh family, terC, and nlpl, suggesting a critical role in colonization and survival. We analyzed the co-occurrence of VFs and clustered the results to identify key gene clusters (Fig. 4B). The filtered network diagram indicated that most VFs have universal connections, while there were three isolated gene clusters that may function independently (Fig. 4C, Additional file 2: Table S7). The conserved genes mentioned above may be omitted because lack of correlation with other genes, although they may serve as the background of pathogenesis. Such a background effect similarly applies to the yeh family, as its three genes form an independent loop without significant interactions with other genes. The largest genetic cluster encompasses 45 genes, forming an intricate interaction network. This suggests that neonatal ExPEC may not possess distinct virulence determinants. Rather, the same pathogenesis could result from multiple combinatorial patterns of these diverse genes. The third network involved six genes, including air, csgA, eilA, gad, hlyE, and lpfA. These virulence factors in this network appear to be associated with intestinal pathogenesis or avian pathogenesis, suggesting they may represent a hybrid virulence repertoire capable of causing mixed pathological manifestations. However, as previously noted, our strains rarely carry classic enteric virulence determinants. The final two genes, dhaK and ibeA, which were interconnected, may be two cooperative genes during the invasion process.

We compared the VFs distribution by invasive and MDR status (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). Twelve VFs were considered to promote invasive infection, especially those involved in immune evasion, including kpsE, kpsMII_K1, neuC, and usp, and iron acquisition, including chuA, iucC, iutA, and sitA. Conversely, over 50 VFs showed a negative correlation with MDR status (Additional file 1: Fig. S5B), suggesting functional differentiation of these strains.

Diverse MGEs were identified in neonatal ExPEC isolates

All strains were identified to have plasmid sequences. A total of 1617 plasmid replicons of 50 types from 388 strains were acquired (Additional file 2: Table S10). Except for a few types, including IncFIB(AP001918), IncFIA, and Col156, most of them were not widely popular. All strains were predicted to contain multiple pathogenic islands and prophages, which were both large chromosomal fragments that can be transferred simultaneously. MGEs exhibit co-localization and co-occurrence relationships with specific pathogenic genes

In co-localization analysis, we analyzed large MGEs that can carry genes, including plasmids, prophages, genomic islands, and integrons. Different pathogenic genes and MGEs showed different plasmid-located ratios (Fig. 6A). Moderate VFs were located on plasmids (38.0%, 4847/12759), while almost all ARGs were located on plasmids (94.8%, 2618/2762). The proportion of different MGEs also varied, including genomic islands (24.6%, 1392/5661), prophages (28.3%, 564/1990), integrons (97.0%, 412/425), insertion sequences (57.2%, 1756/3072), and transposons (53.4%, 2880/5395). For specific genes, an even more skewed plasmid distribution ratio was observed, suggesting their preferential localization. Among the prevalent VFs (strains > 100), senB (100.0%, 169/169), traT (99.7%, 300/301), iucC (99.7%, 286/287), traJ (99.5%, 194/195), iutA (99.3%, 302/304), iroN (98.7%, 157/159), sat (96.9%, 154/159), mchF (92.5%, 111/120), and iha (92.3%, 180/195) had a high proportion of plasmid-located over 90% (Additional file 2: Table S6). Most ARGs preferentially localize to plasmids, with only a few less prevalent genes showing chromosomal preference. Interestingly, some β-lactam resistance genes with moderate carriage rates did not consistently reside on plasmids, including blaCTX-M-14 (40.0%, 16/40), blaCTX-M-15 (12.5%, 6/48), blaCTX-M-27 (11.5%, 7/61), blaCTX-M-3 (60.0%, 9/15), and blaCTX-M-55 (21.2%, 7/33) (Additional file 2: Table S8). Furthermore, all the complete integrons were located on plasmids. No significant plasmid localization preference was observed among the prevalent insertion sequence types, which was similar to integrons, except DDE-1 (98.5%, 135/137) and Isb (97.7%, 501/513).

Pathogenic islands were associated with diverse pathogenic genes, especially VFs (Additional file 2: Table S15). The preferred genes included vat (73.1%, 190/261), fimH (27.9%, 142/509), iss (25.5%, 97/381), kpsE (22.3%, 78/350), and sitA (13.2%, 52/393), which were all associated with extraintestinal infections. In addition, several ARGs were also located on pathogenic islands, including aph(3'')-Ib, sul2, aph(6)-Id, and tet(A). The prophages carried three VFs, including iss (37.8%, 144/269), irp2 (3/359), and sitA (2/348) (Additional file 2: Table S16). Integrons were related to ARGs (Additional file 2: Table S17), including dfrA17 (93.8%, 195/208), aadA5 (98.9%, 178/180), dfrA12 (97.3%, 36/37), aadA2 (97.1%, 33/34), aac(6')-Ib-cr (100.0%, 31/31), and blaOXA-1 (100.0%, 29/29). These ARGs were mainly associated with trimethoprim and aminoglycosides, and blaOXA-1 may cause broad antibiotic resistance to β-lactams.

The co-occurrence analysis of MGEs and pathogenic genes revealed the potential transmission mechanism of pathogenic genes (Fig. 6C). The results indicated that most of the inserted sequences have co-occurrence relationships with multiple pathogenic genes, but there were few clustered correlations. Although transposons had obvious co-occurrence gene clusters, their correlation is not strong. The plasmid replicons showed the strongest co-occurrence with pathogenic genes, suggesting statistical association and potential mobility rather than confirmed plasmid-borne transmission.

Multiple MGEs have been identified as key mechanisms for the transmission of VFs and ARGs

When further integrating and reviewing the gene distribution patterns, co-occurrence networks, correlation analyses, and their associations with MGEs, numerous significant findings of transmission dynamics were revealed. iutA and iucC were strongly correlated in VFs co-occurrence in the main network (Fig. 4C), had a high plasmid-located proportion (Fig. 6B), and co-occurred with IncFIB(AP001918) and TY1_Copia (Fig. 6C), suggesting the plasmid-driven transmission mechanism and potentially facilitated by TY1_Copia. sul1, dfrA17, mph(A), and aadA5 were the elements of ARGs co-occurrence in the left main network, also highly located in plasmid (Fig. 6B), and associated with Col(BS512), Col156, IncFIA, and ISb (Fig. 6C), suggesting multiple plasmid-driven transmission and potentially facilitated by ISb. A more typical example was the gene cluster including dfrA12, aadA2, blaCMY-2, aac(6’)-lb-cr, blaOXA-1, and blaNDM-5, which were all plasmid-located and associated with IncFII(pAMA1167-NDM-5). The relationship between genes and STs explained the MDR mechanism in ST410. Additionally, these genes were also located on integrons (Additional file 2: Table S17), revealing multiple transmission mechanisms and the reasons for their strong transmission ability. Another noticeable plasmid replicon was IncFIC(FII), which had a clear and strongly correlated VFs cluster, including anr, hlyF, cvaC, and etsC (Fig. 6C). What's more, this plasmid would often obtain various other VFs, such as iss, traJ, mchF, dhaK, ibeA, focC/sfaE, papA_F13, cma, and tsh. The transmission of these pathogenic genes may represent the most critical pathways requiring screening and containment measures.

Discussion

Neonatal ExPEC infection represents a distinct clinical entity that differs markedly from adult cases in both pathogenesis and outcomes. The immature immune system and unique microbiota of neonates render them exceptionally vulnerable to invasive ExPEC infections, which frequently progress to life-threatening sepsis or meningitis. Despite these alarming patterns, large-scale genomic epidemiological studies focusing on neonatal ExPEC remain strikingly scarce, leaving key questions unanswered regarding population structure, prevalence of pathogenicity genes, and acquisition of pathogenicity genes. This study presents a nationwide retrospective investigation of neonatal ExPEC in China. Our findings provide a comprehensive understanding of neonatal ExPEC, offering valuable data to support research on understanding infection mechanisms, antimicrobial stewardship, and the development of novel control strategies.

E. coli is universally classified into eight genetically distinct phylogroups: A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F, and G [50]. It is generally accepted that groups A, B1, C, and E are predominantly intestinal commensals, whereas groups B2, D, and F are primarily associated with ExPEC. This study revealed that neonatal ExPEC strains exhibited a similar distribution to adult ExPEC strains, with most strains predominantly belonging to groups B2 and D, accompanied by a few strains scattered in other groups [51, 52]. However, regarding the molecular typing and serotyping of bacterial strains, although the top-ranked types are shared, their distribution patterns differ significantly [53, 54]. Multiple studies have established that ST131, ST69, ST73, and ST95 predominant STs among adults [55, 56], were associated with invasive infections. Our findings revealed that ST1193 emerged as the predominant lineage in Chinese neonates, particularly in sepsis and meningitis [57, 58]. In contrast, ST131, while remaining a main cause, occupied a secondary position in invasive neonatal infections. Compared with a previous large-scale global multicenter study of ExPEC serotypes [18], we found that O75 predominates in Chinese neonates. Although serotype O25 also ranks high, its proportion differs considerably. Considering that the ExPEC vaccine currently in clinical trials includes O75 and O25 [59, 60], our study provides additional data on serotype distribution specifically in Chinese neonates, which could inform future neonatal vaccine development. Consistent with previous studies, K1 was more frequently detected among isolates associated with invasive infection. However, no single K-type showed a markedly high prevalence across the cohort, highlighting the heterogeneity of capsule types in neonatal ExPEC and the challenges this poses for capsule-based vaccine development.

Within the pan-genomic and phylogenetic analysis, we revealed the diversity of genes carried by neonatal ExPEC, demonstrating the strong ability of E. coli to horizontally transfer genes. SNP accumulation predominantly follows a neutral evolutionary pattern, being stochastic and typically non-functional, though non-synonymous mutations may undergo selective pressures. Branch lengths reflect nucleotide substitution rates, which often correlate with temporal divergence but may vary due to inter-strain mutation rate differences or natural selection. The cgMLST approach constructs phylogenetic trees by analyzing allelic variations in conserved core genes, which include both pathogenic and non-pathogenic strains across diverse clinical contexts. Branch lengths quantify allelic divergence and facilitate clonal relationship assessment among strains. Pan-genome trees are built from gene presence/absence matrices, with their topological structures being primarily shaped by horizontal gene transfer events and gene loss. The branching patterns reveal genetic foundations of niche adaptation, particularly through the acquisition of virulence factors or ARGs. The observed phylogenetic incongruities demonstrate that natural mutation, homologous recombination, and horizontal transfer all play substantial roles in E. coli evolution. The pronounced topological discordance between the pan-genome and SNP trees demonstrates that pathogenic evolution is predominantly driven by HGT of niche-adaptive virulence determinants, including pathogenicity islands, toxin genes, and secretion systems, rather than SNP accumulation.

In the analysis of resistance phenotype and pathogenic genes, we focused on different combination patterns, reflecting the synergistic pathogenicity of VFs [61] and the factors of MDR [62], indicating that VFs and ARGs have widely emerged. Clinically approved antibiotics for neonates are fewer than for adults [63, 64]. β-lactams have high systemic blood concentrations and fewer side effects, making them the ideal choice for neonates. However, our research indicates that a considerable number of strains are already resistant to this type of antibiotic, often accompanied by MDR, further narrowing the range of antibiotic choices. Consistent with the worldwide dissemination [65], blaNDM-5 emerged predictably in our strains, mediating carbapenem resistance that complicates treatment options. The higher disease burden caused by neonatal diseases for individuals must be taken into consideration [66] because the death of any neonate is unacceptable for hospitals, medical staff, and family members. A small number of highly resistant STs, which may cause serious consequences, should be given attention [67].

Different MGEs drive HGT through distinct yet interconnected mechanisms [68]. Collectively, these MGEs form a dynamic gene pool that accelerates E. coli adaptation to host niches, blurring the line between commensalism and pathogenicity [69]. Plasmids are autonomous circular DNA molecules, replicating independently of the bacterial chromosome, which can rapidly spread between strains through conjugation, transformation, or transduction [70, 71]. Unlike other MGEs, plasmids are not only carriers of pathogenic genes, but can also serve as carriers for other MGEs. Our study revealed the distribution ratios of different genes and MGEs on plasmids, suggesting the driving roles of plasmids in pathogenic gene transmission. This study predicted the pathogenicity islands, which are large chromosomal regions acquired via site-specific recombination and can integrate virulence genes into the host genome [72]. Prophages are phage fragments integrated into the host bacterium, which can carry pathogenic genes, with the most notorious being shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC) [73]. In this study, prophages mainly carries the iss, which can increase the serum survival rate of the strain [74]. The strong association between integrons and ARGs, particularly the dfr and aad families, highlights their critical role in disseminating resistance to trimethoprim and aminoglycosides [75]. Additionally, the link between integrons and the blaOXA gene underscores their contribution to β-lactamase inhibitor resistance, further exacerbating challenges in treating bacterial infections. These findings emphasize the need for surveillance of integron-mediated resistance mechanisms to guide effective antimicrobial strategies. The co-occurrence analysis revealed that insertion sequences and transposons frequently associate with pathogenicity genes, and these genes are usually distributed in clusters, suggesting their pivotal role in HGT. Different insertion sequences and transposons have different related gene clusters, and specific high-risk transposon factors should be monitored.

A limitation of this study is that all strains were collected from grade-A tertiary hospitals, which may not reflect the situation in smaller community healthcare settings. Asymptomatic colonization or mild community-managed infections were not captured. Furthermore, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the samples were unevenly distributed over time. The uneven distribution of infection sites may affect conclusions in other groups due to the absence of adjusting for confounding. Next-generation sequencing cannot achieve complete genome assembly, making it impossible to determine the accurate relative positional relationships between gene fragments and plasmids. Some analyses were solely based on gene prediction and may be limited by sequencing accuracy and gene expression. Additionally, detailed clinical data were not analyzed; thus, the impact of the immune status of neonates during the infection process was not considered. However, these genomic-level predictions will allow us to monitor trends in these clinically associated genes and their movement between strains.

Conclusions

This study presents multicenter data on ExPEC infections in Chinese neonates, comprehensively characterizing molecular typing, evolutionary patterns, key virulence factors, and antibiotic resistance genes. By analyzing mobile genetic elements, we explore their potential transmission mechanisms, providing valuable insights to inform future prevention and control strategies.
