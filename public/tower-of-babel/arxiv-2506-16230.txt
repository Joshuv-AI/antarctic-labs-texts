On Design of Representative Distributionally Robust Formulations for Evaluation of Tail Risk Measures
Anand Deo

Abstract
. Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) is a risk measure widely used to quantify the impact of extreme losses. Owing to the lack of representative samples CVaR is sensitive to the tails of the underlying distribution. In order to combat this sensitivity, Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO), which evaluates the worst-case CVaR measure over a set of plausible data distributions is often deployed. Unfortunately, an improper choice of the DRO formulation can lead to a severe underestimation of tail risk. This paper aims at leveraging extreme value theory to arrive at a DRO formulation which leads to representative worst-case CVaR evaluations in that the above pitfall is avoided while simultaneously, the worst case evaluation is not a gross over-estimate of the true CVaR. We demonstrate theoretically that even when there is paucity of samples in the tail of the distribution, our formulation is readily implementable from data, only requiring calibration of a single scalar parameter. We showcase that our formulation can be easily extended to provide robustness to tail risk in multivariate applications as well as in the evaluation of other commonly used risk measures. Numerical illustrations on synthetic and real-world data showcase the practical utility of our approach. Keywords Conditional Value at Risk; Distributionally Robust Optimization; Extreme Value Theory; Distortion Risk Measures



1.
Introduction

Distributional uncertainty is inevitable in risk management problems which involve
evaluation of a stochastic objective function.
Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) (see
[
51
]
) is a popular tool which
attempts
to hedge against such uncertainty by
computing the worst case evaluation of the desired stochastic objective over a set of plausible models.
This set of models, referred to as the uncertainty set in literature is typically constructed by considering
perturbations of
a nominal distribution (for instance, proposed by a regulator).
A centerpiece of DRO literature is to arrive at a suitable choice of the uncertainty set from available data samples.
Different
measures of ”similarity” between distributions are used to this end. These include
moment constraints (see
[
67
,
23
,
37
]
), likelihood based metrics (see
[
9
,
44
,
27
]
) and optimal transport distances (see
[
14
,
48
,
33
]
).

In several applications, it is natural to consider risk measures which are evaluated on distribution tails: for instance, in an insurance setting, one may desire to quantify the impact of excessively large claims.
Owing to a paucity of tail-samples, estimation of such tail risk measures directly from data becomes difficult. One way to combat this is to propose that the data has been realized from a family of distributions (such as Gaussian), and evaluate the objective under the member of this family whose parameters match those of the data.
This can lead to significant model risk,
as the underlying data distribution may differ substantially from the proposed family and one may end up underestimating the tail objective.
Such errors are considered to hazardous, since one may potentially end up being over-optimistic about the extent of outliers present in data.

Use of DRO becomes a natural step towards combating such model risk.
In order to ensure that the formulation is rich enough to the above underestimation,
recent works such as
[
43
,
39
,
42
]
advocate for use of
DRO with
uncertainty sets which contain a large number of plausible distributions.
However, the flip side
is that the resulting formulation may be too conservative, and one may end up with
evaluations which are excessively large.
Consequently, one strives to balance robustness (not severely under-representing outliers) against conservativeness (excessively large risk evaluations).
In this paper,
considering a class of stochastic objectives evaluated only on the largest
\beta
fraction of data outcomes, we (i) precisely characterize
how the choice of uncertainty set affects the worst case risk as the tail level
\beta
is made smaller (that is, the objective is evaluated further and further into the tail) and (ii)
use these characterizations to arrive at a data-driven DRO formulation which while not being overly conservative still ensures robustness against distributional ambiguity.

1.1.
CVaR as a measure of tail risk

A popularly used measure of tail risk is conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) (see
[
47
]
and references therein).
Given a distribution
P
, and a risk level
\beta
, define the value at risk associated with
P
as

(1)

v_{1-\beta}(P)=\inf\{u:P(Z>u)\leq\beta\}.

Define the CVaR associated with a measure
P
evaluated at a risk level
\beta
as
C_{1-\beta}(P)=E_{P}[Z\mid Z\geq v_{1-\beta}(P)]
. Note that in the previous expression, the random variable
Z
is assumed to have a distribution
P
.
Axiomatically, CVaR is a coherent risk measure: it is monotonic, convex and homogeneous (see
[
5
,
32
,
2
]
for precise definitions) and has the following optimization formulation due to
[
53
]
, all of which make it amenable to data-driven computation:

(2)

C_{1-\beta}(P)=\inf_{u}\left\{u+\frac{1}{\beta}E_{P}\left[Z-u\right]^{+}\right\},\text{ where }[Z-u]^{+}=\max\{(Z-u),0\}.

These desirable properties have lead to CVaR becoming a preferred vehicle for introducing risk aversion in problems in finance, operations research, planning machine learning etc. (see
[
41
,
3
,
8
,
59
,
11
,
58
]
). In addition, unlike VaR, which only quantifies the extent of loss at a particular risk level
\beta
, CVaR, captures losses in the
entire
tail region beyond risk level
\beta
. Consequently, CVaR has become a favored tool for quantifying extreme risks. For instance, in calculating minimum capital requirements, the Basel III accords advocate the use of CVaR over VaR since it provides a more
accurate representation of downside risks faced by banks.

Despite possessing properties that make it attractive from a modeling standpoint,
evaluation of CVaR is highly sensitive to the tails of the data owing to a lack of representative samples. This difficulty is only exacerbated when the data has heavy tails (see
[
46
,
16
]
). Using DRO to tackle distributional uncertainty
therefore becomes a natural solution to hedging against model risk.
Distributionally Robust CVaR evaluation
has received a fair bit of attention in recent literature:
for instance,
[
66
,
35
]
attempt to evaluate the worst-case CVaR of a portfolio loss, while

[
49
]
derive a model for minimizing the worst case CVaR of regret in a combinatorial optimization problem. In the context of distributionally robust CVaR constrained programs,
[
67
,
38
,
18
]
provide tractable reformulations.
Finally,
[
50
,
10
,
19
,
62
,
17
]
study evaluation of a more general class of robust risk measures with a focus on developing tractable reformulations.

1.2.
Main Contributions

While previous works provide reformulations to ensure computational tractability, the question of how the choice of uncertainty set affects the worst case CVaR evaluation still remains unanswered. To illustrate in a real context, consider the following question: ”If a financial regulator uses DRO to combat model uncertainty, how would the excess
minimum capital requirements, incurred due to robustification
depend on the choice of uncertainty set?”.
In mathematical terms: given a set of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}
which consist of perturbations of a nominal model
P_{0}
, how does
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}C_{1-\beta}(P)
compare with
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
?
To answer this question, diverging away from the computational aspects of robust CVaR evaluation, in this paper we aim to quantify how much faster
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})
grows as compared to
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
as
\beta\to 0
as a function of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
.

A follow-up question is following: is it possible to carefully tailor the uncertainty set so that resulting DRO formulation is robust, yet not overly conservative?
In the context of the previous example:
”if the true distribution of data were
Q
,
can a regulator carefully identify
a set of plausible models

\mathcal{P}
for which
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})
is readily estimable from available data samples
and

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})\approx C_{1-\beta}(Q)
(in a sense to be made precise later), so that the despite model uncertainty the minimum capital requirement proposed by the regulator is representative of the (unknown) ground truth?”
In light of recent financial, supply chain and energy market disruptions (among several others), it has become increasingly pertinent to tackle such questions. This paper undertakes the task of attempting to provide an answer to this important, yet relatively less studied problem.
Our main contributions are below:

i) Characterization of worst case CVaR:
We show the
the existence of a scaling function
g_{\beta}
which depends on (i) the quantile function of of
P_{0}
and (ii) measure of discrepancy between distributions used to define
\mathcal{P}
such that

(3)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})\sim g_{\beta}.

Using (
3
),
we characterize the extent of conservativeness over the baseline evaluation
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
for two popular classes of uncertainty sets:

a)
If
\mathcal{P}
were a
p
-Wasserstein ball around
P_{0}
(defined in (
9
))
we demonstrate the existence of a
constant
c\in(1,\infty]
such that

\frac{\log g_{\beta}}{\log C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})}\to c.

If
P_{0}
has a power-law with tail index
\gamma
, then
c=\gamma/p
is finite.
If instead if
P_{0}
were Weibullian, then
c=\infty
.
This observation suggests that that while computationally tractable, Wasserstein uncertainty sets can lead to significant conservativeness, which worsens if the nominal distribution itself had lighter tails.

b)
If
\mathcal{P}
was defined using a polynomial-divergence ball around
P_{0}
then

\frac{\log g_{\beta}}{\log C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})}\to c\in(1,\infty)\text{ as }\beta\to 0

when
P_{0}
has
a power-law with a suitable tail index
\gamma
. Here
c
depends
only
the type of divergence measure. Thus, in the presence of heavy tails, polynomial divergences are highly conservative. If
P_{0}
had Weibullian tails, we show that

{g_{\beta}}\sim K{C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})}\quad\text{where }K\in(1,\infty).

where we identify the precise dependence of
K
on both the type of divergence used, as well as the tail of
P_{0}
.
This shows that divergence based formulations also lead to conservativeness for Weibullian nominal distributions, albeit by only a constant factor.

ii) Design of representative DRO formulations:

The conservativeness of Wasserstein (or) divergence based DRO arises because the uncertainty set contains distributions with tails significantly heavier than that of
P_{0}
.
To resolve this issue there are two aspects that must be taken care of
: (i) design of
an uncertainty set which
only
contains distributions whose tails are identical in strength to that of
P_{0}
and (ii) ensure that the tails of
P_{0}
match those of
Q
. The first of these ensures that the extent of conservativeness is controlled, while the second results in a nominal model which captures the scale of outliers in data accurately. We propose a
class of uncertainty sets of the form

(4)

\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}=\left\{P:\int\phi\left(\frac{dP}{dQ_{\beta}}\right)dQ_{\beta}\leq\delta\right\},

where we allow
Q_{\beta}
to depend on the risk level at which CVaR is to be evaluated.
In most DRO literature, the uncertainty set is constructed around (i) the empirical distribution of data (call it
\hat{P}_{n})
or (ii) a specific family of distributions whose parameters are readily estimable from data.
Owing to lack of representative samples, the empirical distribution does not capture tail properties of the data, while if one uses a parametric family of distributions in (ii) above whose tail properties don’t resemble those of the data, it may lead to severe under-estimation of risk.

In contrast, the proposed nominal distribution
Q_{\beta}
is constructed by
extrapolating the distributional information available in samples from a baseline risk level
\beta_{0}\gg\beta
. In turn
\beta_{0}
is carefully selected so that the largest
\beta_{0}
fraction of data samples are representative of the distribution tail, and it is possible to accurately estimate quantiles at level
\beta_{0}
from
these samples.
Our choice is rooted in literature on extreme value theory (see
[
21
]
and references therein), where similar extrapolations
are used to estimate quantiles at risk levels at which there are often very few or no data samples. The result is that the tail properties of
Q_{\beta}
become increasingly representative of those of
Q
as
\beta\to 0
. Carefully selecting
\phi(\cdot)
in (
4
) further
results in a DRO formulation which does not severely underestimate tail-risk
and
is not overly conservative. In particular, we show that for any
c\in(0,1)
, if
\beta_{0}=\beta^{c}
then

\displaystyle\log C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\beta})

\displaystyle\sim\log C_{1-\beta}(Q)\text{ if }Q\text{ were heavy tailed and}

\displaystyle C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\beta})

\displaystyle\sim C_{1-\beta}(Q)\text{ if }Q\text{ were light tailed}.

Our result demonstrates that the proposed DRO is far less conservative than both Wasserstein and polynomial-divergence based DRO formulations irrespective of the nature of the tail of the data.
From a practical standpoint, the implication of the above result is the following:
given
n
samples of data, setting
\beta_{0}(n)=n^{-\kappa}
for a hyper-parameter
\kappa\in(0,1)
, the proposed formulation
is able to robustly infer tail risk in the statistically challenging regime where
\beta(n)=c_{0}n^{-q}
for
q\geq 1
. This is in contrast to methods using
\hat{P}_{n}
as the nominal distribution, which can only produce reliable risk estimates at levels
\beta(n)\gg 1/n
.

iii) Extensions to distortion risk measures:

In many instances, agents making decisions under uncertainty end up attributing non-linear weights to low probability events. Expected utility frameworks which include measures such as CVaR fail to capture such a behavior.
To remedy this, the notion of distortion risk measures was introduced in
[
63
,
61
]
,
where the authors propose weighing tail probabilities by a ”dual-utility” function to capture non-linearity in an agent’s perception of distribution tails. Further,

[
65
]
derives the following representation of such risk measures:
Let

g_{\beta}(x)=\begin{cases}g\left(\frac{x}{\beta}\right)\quad\quad\quad 0\leq x\leq\beta\\
1\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ \ \ \text{ $x>\beta$}\end{cases}

where
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is an increasing function.
Define a risk measure

(5)

\rho_{g,1-\beta}(P_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{\beta}(P_{0}(X\geq x))dx.

The formulation in (
5
) allows a modeler to ”weigh” different regions of the distribution tail by the function
g(\cdot)
in assessment of risk: setting
g(x)=x
(that is assigns linear weights to the entire tail) leads to CVaR.
Recent works have studied distributionally robust formulations for these risk measures (see
[
50
,
10
]
). We demonstrate that both the asymptotics for
worst case risk evaluation extended to this generality
and that the tailored uncertainty set in (
4
) continues to produce representative risk estimates.

iv) Application to evaluation of systemic risk:
We present an application of the previous ideas to distributionally robust evaluation of contagion in a financial system.
Since the 2008 crisis, this problem has received widespread attention (see
[
1
,
36
,
4
]
and references therein).
A popular framework to capture contagion is to model the system as a (bipartite) graph where one side of the graph represents the various financial entities and the other represents common asset holdings (see
[
40
,
29
]
).
When one or more of these assets are distressed, it may lead to a cascading effect and cause wide-spread losses to the financial entities.
Considering a somewhat stylized model of a banking network, we demonstrate that the proposed formulation may be used to
robustly
evaluate the magnitude of losses caused by such a cascade.

v) Experimental Validation:
To establish practical credibility for the developed methods, we conduct an extensive simulation study. We observe that consistent with theory, the proposed DRO framework leads to worst-case evaluations which are far more representative of tail risk than existing techniques.
As a further application we show that in a financial setting where a manager wishes to re-balance her portfolio
at discrete intervals of time,
that of the proposed formulation may be used to arrive at a re-balancing frequency whose hedging error is small.
Short case studies on the Danish Fire Insurance data set and the FAMA French data set further serve to illustrate the utility of our methods in real-world applications.

1.3.
DRO formulations involving tail risks

We conclude by giving a brief review of literature which considers DRO problems involving distribution tails: In the setting of evaluating rare event probabilities
[
45
,
7
]
study a robust optimization approach which imposes shape constraints on the tail of the underlying probability densities.
However, their formulations become intractable beyond the case where the
objective is the expected value of a random variable.
Kullback-Liebler divergences have been used to measure model risk in various financial contexts by
[
34
,
55
]
. The papers

[
13
,
12
,
64
]
leverage extreme value theory and derive asymptotics for worst case tail probabilities and show the dependence of the worst case evaluation on the tails of the nominal distribution and the uncertainty set.
Specifically, the first two works show how the tail strength of the nominal distribution affects the worst case probability evaluation while

[
13
]
goes further and arrives at a procedure to compute worst case quantiles
using a simple line search.
In the multivariate context,
[
64
]
demonstrates bound on the worst case Value-at-Risk in situations where there in ambiguity about the joint distribution of the data.
The paper
[
31
]
provides asymptotic bounds on the worst case probability evaluation in a multivariate setting, considering a divergence based uncertainty
set with additional moment constraints.
In related work, the recent paper
[
26
]
provides asymptotics to understand the scaling behavior for the optimal cost of a distributionally robust chance constrained problem for several popular ambiguity sets.
Similarly,
[
42
,
43
]
study bounds on the strength of tails of distributions which are permissible within different
\phi-
divergence neighborhoods of a nominal distribution. In particular, they derive conditions on
P_{0}
and the growth rate of
\phi
in (
4
) under which these neighborhoods contain distributions with infinite mean.
Building on this, the recent work
[
39
]
derives conditions on
\phi-
divergence in (
4
) that ensure a finite worst case CVaR. Our paper is more comprehensive, demonstrating that several of the choices proposed by
[
39
]
, while leading to finite worst-case CVaR are excessively conservative.

2.
Problem Setup and Modeling Assumptions

2.1.
Background on Extreme Value Theory

Analysis of distribution tails is ubiquitous in applications across risk management.
Extreme Value Theory (EVT) (see
[
21
,
30
]
) provides a structured framework using which one can model and statistically estimate tail quantiles. To achieve this, one places mild regularity assumptions on the scaled behavior of maxima: given i.i.d. samples
Z_{1},\ldots Z_{n}
of a random variable
Z
with distribution
P
, one assumes that there exist scaling constants
\{(a_{n},b_{n}):n\geq 1\}
such that

(6)

\max_{i=1}^{n}\frac{Z_{n}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\xrightarrow{d}H_{\alpha},

where
H_{\alpha}
has the distribution function

H_{\alpha}(x)=\begin{cases}\exp\left\{-(1+\alpha x)^{-1/\alpha}\right\},\quad 1+\alpha x\geq 0\text{ if }\alpha>0\\
\exp(-\exp(-x))\quad\quad\quad\ \ \quad\quad\ \ \quad\quad\ \text{ if }\alpha=0.\end{cases}

This condition is called the maximum domain of attraction (MDA) condition in literature and will be henceforth denoted by
P\in{\tt MDA}(\alpha)
.
The parameter
\alpha
governs the rate of decay of tails of
P
. The
\alpha>0
case corresponds to
P
having power-law tails. This includes Pareto, Cauchy, student-t and Logisitc distributions among others. The
\alpha=0
case corresponds to distributions whose tails decay slower than a power-law. This includes Gaussian, Exponential, Gamma and Weibull distributions, among others.
An equivalent characterization of (
6
) can be obtained in terms of the tail cdf of
P
. The notion of regular variation is central to this endeavor:

Definition 1
.

A function
f
is regularly varying with index
\rho
if for any
x>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(nx)}{f(n)}=x^{\rho}.

We denote this by
f\in{\tt RV}(\rho)
or
f\in{\tt RV}
if there is no explicit need to specify the index of regular variation. If
f\in{\tt RV}(0)
, we say that
f
is slowly varying.

Regular variation offers a structured approach to studying functions with polynomial-like growth/decay.
Examples of regularly varying functions include
x^{2}
,
x^{k}\log^{p}(x)
and
x^{k}\exp(\log^{q}(x))
for
q\in(0,1)
. We refer the reader to
[
21
]
for more examples.
The following characterization of the condition
P\in{\tt MDA}
in terms of the tail cdf
\bar{F}_{P}
serves to significantly ease our analysis (see
[
30
]
, pg. 132): whenever
\alpha>0
,

P\in{\tt MDA}(\alpha)\iff\bar{F}_{P}\in{\tt RV}(-\gamma),\,\ \ \text{ where }\gamma=1/\alpha.

2.2.
Problem Formulation and Modeling Assumptions

Consider evaluating the worst-case (largest) CVaR over a set of pluasible data distributions:

{\tt CVaR-Rob}(\beta):\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}C_{1-\beta}(P).

The set
\mathcal{P}
is chosen as a perturbation of some nominal model
P_{0}
. More concretely, let
\mathcal{D}
denote a discrepancy measure between two probability distributions, where
\mathcal{D}(P,P_{0})=0
if and only if
P_{0}=P
- commonly used examples include
\phi-
divergences and Wasserstein distance.
Note that nowhere do we require
\mathcal{D}
to be a proper distance between probability distributions.
We consider a class of plausible models around
P_{0}
by considering uncertainty sets of the form

\mathcal{P}=\{P:\mathcal{D}(P,P_{0})\leq\delta\}.

Here
\delta
is referred to the budget parameter, and specifies the extent of perturbation permissible. Throughout this paper, we will assume that
\delta
is fixed. Whenever
\mathcal{P}
is defined through either Wasserstein distance (see (
9
)) or by setting

(7)

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})=\left\{P:\int\phi\left(\frac{dP}{dP_{0}}\right)dP_{0}\leq\delta\right\},

for some convex
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
is a convex set of probability distributions. To see this, if
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is defined via Wasserstein distance then
\mathcal{P}
is a metric ball in the space of probability measures (see
[
60
]
, Chapter 6), and is therefore convex.
So long as
\phi(\cdot)
is convex,

P\to\int\phi\left(\frac{dP}{dP_{0}}\right)dP_{0}

is a convex function (see
[
20
]
pg. 194). Since
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
is the lower level set of a convex function it is a convex set. With
all our probability measures being defined on
\mathbb{R}
(a closed and convex set),
an application of Lemma 1 of
[
49
]

suggests that in either of these cases

(8)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})=\inf_{u}\left\{u+\frac{1}{\beta}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}E_{P}\left[Z-u\right]^{+}\right\}.

The representation (
8
) is especially useful, since it permits us to use well known duality results for worst case expectation in order to examine
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})
and facilitates numerical evaluation.

Assumptions on Tail Behavior:

We impose some minimal regularity assumptions on
P_{0}
discussed which are standard in literature on modeling distribution tails.

Assumption 1

(Heavy tailed nominal distribution)
.

There exists a
\gamma>0
such that
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}(-\gamma)
.

Assumption 2

(Nominal Distributions with Weibullian Tails)
.

There exists a
\gamma>0
such that
\Lambda_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}(\gamma)
.

Assumption
2
supplements Assumption
1
and captures several distributions whose tails decay at a rate faster than power law. To see this,
\Lambda_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}
implies that
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}(x)=\exp(-x^{\gamma+o(1)})
, while under Assumption
1
,
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}(x)=x^{-\gamma+o(1)}
. Examples of such light tailed distributions include
Exponential, Gamma, Weibull and Gaussian among others.
When Assumption
2
holds, it can be further shown that

P_{0}\in{\tt MDA}(0)
(see
[
22
]
, Theorem 2).
While it is possible that
P_{0}\in{\tt MDA}(0)
but
\Lambda_{P_{0}}\not\in{\tt RV}
,
in this paper we will only consider the light-tailed distributions which satisfy Assumption
2
.
We point out that crucially that Assumptions
1
-
2

are semi-parametric in nature, and
do not require (i)
P_{0}
to be a member of a parametric family (such as Gaussian or student-
t
), and (ii)
P_{0}
to have a density.

Interestingly, the above assumptions translate to regularity on CVaR. We collect these observations in Lemmas
2.1
-
2.2
below. We point out that Lemma
2.1
is known in literature (see
[
65
]
), and refer the reader to the appendix for the proof of Lemma
2.2
. For a distribution
P
, define the function
U_{P}(t)=v_{1-1/t}(P)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that a distribution
P
satisfies Assumption
1
with
\gamma>1
. Then,
C_{1-\beta}(P)\sim\gamma/(\gamma-1)U_{P}(1/\beta)
. As a result,
C_{1-\beta}(P)=\beta^{-1/\gamma+o(1)}
as
\beta\to 0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that a distribution
P
satisfies Assumption
2
. Then,
C_{1-\beta}(P)\sim U_{P}(1/\beta).
As a result,
C_{1-\beta}(P)=\log^{1/\gamma+o(1)}(1/\beta)
as
\beta\to 0
.

The above two results imply the following:
for distributions with a power law type tail, CVaR grows polynomially, while for those with Weibullian tails, it grows poly-logarithmically in
(1/\beta)
. These observations serve as a starting point for quantifying the extent of conservativeness incurred upon the
use of DRO.

3.
Conservativeness of Wasserstein DRO

Recall the following definition of Wasserstein distance between probability measures.

Definition 2
.

Given a distance metric
d
, and
p\geq 1
, the
p-
th order Wasserstein between
P
and
Q
is given by

W_{p}(P,Q)=\inf\left\{E_{\pi}\left[d^{p}(X,Y)\right]^{1/p}:\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)\right\}.

The
p
th order Wasserstein distance defines a metric on the space of probability distributions with finite
p
th moments (see
[
60
]
, Theorem 6.9), and therefore is a discrepancy measure as in Section
2
. Consider an uncertainty set is defined by the metric ball

(9)

\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}=\{P:W_{p}(P,P_{0})\leq\delta\}.

To gain intuition on asymptotic rates of
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})
we first analyze the contents of

\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

There does not exist a finite
\delta>0
for which there exists a
P\in\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
such that

\liminf_{x\to\infty}x^{p}\bar{F}_{P}(x)>0.

Further suppose that
P_{0}
satisfies either Assumption
1
with
p<\gamma
or Assumption
2
. Then, for any
(\kappa,\delta)>0
such that
(p+\kappa)<\gamma
there exists
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
such that
\bar{F}_{\tilde{P}}\in{\tt RV}(-p-\kappa)
.

Proposition
3.1
suggests that distributions in a
p
-Wasserstein ball around
P_{0}
can at most have a tail decaying as
x^{-p}
.
This suggests that

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})
can at most grow at a rate
(1/\beta)^{1/p}
. Alternately, the presence of a distribution with tail index
(p+\kappa)
for any
\kappa>0
in
\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
suggests that the worst case CVaR is
of order
(1/\beta)^{1/p+o(1)}
. Since
\gamma>p
,
\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}

always
contains distributions whose tail is significantly heavier than that of the nominal distribution.
In what follows, we demonstrate that the above upper and lower bounds are in fact tight, in that
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})\sim(\beta/\delta)^{-1/p}
, and therefore Wasserstein DRO is excessively conservative when used to evaluate CVaR.

3.1.
Exact Characterization of worst case risk and tail asymptotics

The following well-known duality result (see
[
14
,
33
]
) provides a starting point for the subsequent analysis: for a measurable function
f
,

(10)

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}}E_{P}[f(Z)]=\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{\lambda\delta^{p}+E_{P_{0}}\left[\sup_{y}\left\{f(y)-\lambda|y-Z|^{p}\right\}\right]\right\}.

Note that in our application,
f(y)=(y-u)^{+}
. It turns out that the inner maximization in (
10
) can be computed in closed form with this choice of
f(\cdot)
.
Define

\vartheta(u,\lambda)=u-\frac{1-1/p}{(p\lambda)^{1/(p-1)}}.

Note that as
p\downarrow 1
,
\vartheta(u,\lambda)\to-\infty
if
\lambda<1
,
\vartheta(u,\lambda)\to u
if
\lambda\geq 1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
(u,\lambda)
, the inner expectation in (
10
) evaluates to

E_{P_{0}}\left[\sup_{y}\left\{(y-u)^{+}-\lambda|y-Z|^{p}\right\}\right]=E_{P_{0}}\left[(Z-\vartheta(u,\lambda))^{+}\right],

where if
p=1
,
\nu(u,\lambda)
is to be understood as its limit as
p\downarrow 1
.
Consequently, the worst case CVaR can be written as the value of following joint optimization problem in
(u,\lambda)
:

(11)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})=\inf_{u,\lambda\geq 0}\left\{u+\beta^{-1}\lambda\delta^{p}+\beta^{-1}E_{P_{0}}[Z-\vartheta(u,\lambda)]^{+}\right\}=\inf_{u,\lambda\geq 0}\varphi_{\beta}(u,\lambda).

The optimization problem in (
11
) can be solved by setting the derivatives of
\varphi_{\beta}(u,\lambda)
to
0
. This leads to an
exact
characterization of worst case CVaR under the Wasserstein metric.

Theorem 3.1
.

The worst case CVaR under Wasserstein ambiguity of order
p\geq 1
has the form

(12)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})=C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})+\delta/\beta^{1/p}.

Suppose further that
P_{0}
satisfies Assumption
1
with
\gamma>p
. Then,
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})\sim\delta/\beta^{1/p}
. Consequently,

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\left[\frac{\log C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})}{\log C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})}\right]=\frac{\gamma}{p}.

Suppose
P_{0}
satisfies Assumption
2
, then one has the stronger condition that

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\left[\frac{\log C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})}{\log C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})}\right]=\infty.

Upon inspection of (
12
), the worst case CVaR evaluation can be seen as a ”penalized” version of CVaR under the nominal distribution.
The extent of this penalty term grows as (i)
\beta\to 0
and (ii) the
\delta
is made larger (therefore permitting more distributions to lie in
\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
).

Remark 1
.

The recent paper
[
62
]
also demonstrates that the distributionally robust CVaR optimization problem with Wasserstein uncertainty can be evaluated as the CVaR under the nominal distribution plus a penalty term. However, an important aspect of our work is to understand structurally why the Wasserstein DRO leads to excessive conservativeness. Indeed unlike
[
62
]
, the use of a duality argument above allows
for a constructive proof of Theorem
3.1
, and allows
us to clearly identify
the distribution leading to the worst case CVaR evaluation.

Remark 2
.

Recall that existence of
p
-th moments is a requirement for defining Wasserstein distances. If
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}(-\gamma)
, then
P_{0}
has
p
th moment for all
p<\gamma
. The added condition
p<\gamma
therefore ensures that
P_{0}
lies in the space of all distributions with finite
p-
th moments.

3.2.
Identifying the worst case distribution

The growth rate for
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta})
proposed by
Theorem
3.1
corresponds to the CVaR of a random variable whose tail cdf decays at a rate
x^{-p}
.
Due to Proposition
3.1
,
\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
does not contain any such distribution.
In order to understand the cause of this apparent discrepancy,
we explicitly identify the optimal transport plan which leads to the worst case CVaR evaluation in the result below:

Proposition 3.3
.

The distribution which results in the worst case CVaR evaluation when

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{W}_{p,\delta}
satisfies

P_{\beta,\tt wc}=P_{0}\circ T_{\beta,\delta}^{-1},\text{ where }T_{\beta,\delta}(x)=x\bm{1}(x\leq v_{1-\beta}(P_{0}))+(x+\delta/\beta^{1/p})\bm{1}(x>v_{1-\beta}(P_{0}))

if
p>1
. If
p=1
, then the worst case distribution is supported on the
region

\bigcup_{c\geq 0}\left\{(x,y):y=x\bm{1}(x\leq v_{1-\beta}(P_{0}))+(x+c)\bm{1}(x>v_{1-\beta}(P_{0}))\right\}.

Proposition
3.3
demonstrates that the worst case distribution in fact translates ”tail” samples of
Z
further out, while the samples from the distribution meat remain unchanged.
Interestingly, both
P_{0}
and the worst case distribution
P_{\beta,\tt wc}

have the
same
tail behavior:
if
P_{0}
satisfies Assumption
1
(or
2
) with tail parameter
\gamma>0
, so does
P_{\beta,\tt wc}
.
To illustrate this, let us consider the case where
p>1
and fix

\beta
. Since the map
T_{\beta,\delta}(\cdot)
is invertible,
\bar{F}_{P_{\beta,\tt wc}}(t)=\bar{F}_{P_{0}}(T^{\leftarrow}_{\beta,\delta}(t))
(resp.
\Lambda_{P_{\beta,\tt wc}}(t)=\Lambda_{P_{0}}(T^{\leftarrow}_{\beta,\delta}(t))
.
Taking
t>v_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
, the right hand side of the previous display becomes equal to
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}(t-\delta/\beta^{1/p})
.
Since regular variation is invariant to translations,
\bar{F}_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}
(resp.
\Lambda_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}
) implies that
\bar{F}_{P_{\beta,\tt wc}}\in{\tt RV}
(resp.
\Lambda_{P_{\beta,\tt wc}}\in{\tt RV})
.
The intuition behind the rather surprising observation above can be derived from
Proposition
3.1
which shows that it is too costly to transport samples of
P_{0}
to a distribution whose tail decays exactly at the rate
x^{-p}
.
However, it is feasible to translate the samples in the tail region
just
enough so that the resulting CVaR
appears
as though it is being evaluated from a distribution whose tail decays as
x^{-p}
. We illustrate this intuition through Numerical Example

below:

4.
Conservativeness of divergence based DRO

In this section we derive asymptotics for
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))
, where
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
is defined in (
7
). We make the following standard assumptions on
\phi(\cdot)
:

Assumption 3

(Smoothness of
\phi
-function)
.

Suppose
\phi
is a smooth, convex function such that
\phi^{\prime}(1)=\phi(1)=0
and that
\phi(x)/x\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
.

Assumption
3
captures popular cases such as KL divergence,
\chi^{2}
divergence and the various Cressie-Read divergences (see
[
44
,
27
]
).
In order to gain intuition behind the extent of conservativeness incurred by the DRO formulation with a
\phi-
divergence based uncertainty set, we analyze what all distributions are included
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
. A crucial difference is that, unlike the previous case,
both
the tail strength of
P_{0}
as well as the rate of increase of
\phi(\cdot)
influence whether or not a distribution lies in
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
. We point out that a similar result has been established in
[
42
]
(see Proposition 4 therein). However, our result is derived under significantly milder assumptions: we do not require the existence of densities for the reference model, nor do we place any asymptotic restriction of the tail cdf beyond that imposed by regular variation.
In addition, we provide a constructive proof to showcase how the reference model may be exponentially twisted by a suitable amount
to arrive at distributions with heavier tails.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds.

(i)

There exists
P_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
with
E_{P_{1}}[X]=\infty
, if
\phi(x)\sim x\log x
and either Assumption
1
holds, or Assumption
2
holds with
\gamma<1
. If instead
\phi\in{\tt RV}(p)
and
P_{0}
satisfies Assumption
1

with
\gamma<\frac{p}{p-1}
, there exists
P_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
with
E_{P_{1}}[X]=\infty
.

(ii)

Suppose that for some
p>1
,
\phi\in{\tt RV}(p)
, and let
P_{0}
satisfy Assumption
1
. Then, for any
\gamma^{\prime}>\frac{p-1}{p}\gamma
there exists
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
for which
\bar{F}_{\tilde{P}}\in{\tt RV}(-\gamma^{\prime})
.

(iii)

Suppose the setup of part (ii) holds, but instead

P_{0}
satisfies Assumption
2
. Then, for any
\kappa>0
there exists
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{\tilde{P}}(t)}{\Lambda_{P_{0}}(t)}=\left(\frac{p-1}{p}\right)+\kappa.

Note that whenever
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
,
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))\geq C_{1-\beta}(\tilde{P})
.
Proposition
4.1
can therefore be used to lower bound the worst case risk as follows:

i)
First, suppose that
\phi(x)=x\log x-x+1
(corresponding to Kullback-Liebler divergence). Since
C_{1-\beta}(P)\geq E_{P}[Z]
for all
\beta>0
, part (i) of the above result suggests that
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))=\infty
when either (a) the distribution
P_{0}
is heavy tailed, or (b) it is Weibullian with tails decaying slower than exponential. Similarly, if
P_{0}
had sufficiently heavy tails (that is
\gamma
is smaller than a threshold),
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))=\infty
.
The above observation echos the findings in
[
39
]
, who argue that KL (or) polynomial divergences may not suited for DRO involving tail-risks, since these may lead to unreasonably large worst-case evaluations.

ii)
Next, suppose that
\phi\in{\tt RV}(p)
(this class includes all polynomial divergences) and
P_{0}
is heavy tailed, such that (ii) above holds. Then, there exists
P_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
with
\bar{F}_{P_{1}}\in{\tt RV}(\gamma^{\prime})
for
\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}\gamma-\kappa
. Then,
C_{\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))\geq C_{1-\beta}(P_{1})
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
for all small enough choices of
\kappa
, Lemma
2.1
suggests that the worst case CVaR is orders of magnitude greater than
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
.

iii)
Suppose now that
P_{0}
is light tailed, so that (iii) above holds.
Then, fixing a
\varepsilon>0
, note that there exists a distribution
\tilde{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
for which

\frac{\Lambda_{\tilde{P}}(t)}{\Lambda_{P_{0}}(t)}<\frac{p-1}{p}+\varepsilon,\ \forall t>t_{0}.

Suppose
\beta
is chosen small enough such that
t_{1}=\Lambda_{\tilde{P}}^{\leftarrow}\log(1/\beta)>t_{0}
. Then,
\log(1/\beta)<\Lambda_{P_{0}}(t_{1})(\frac{p-1}{p}+\varepsilon)
. Further, since
\Lambda_{P_{0}}\in{\tt RV}(1/\gamma)
and is increasing, given any
\delta>0
, whenever
\beta
is sufficiently small,

v_{1-\beta}(\tilde{P})>\left(\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)-\delta\right)^{1/\gamma}v_{1-\beta}(P_{0}).

With
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})\sim v_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
(resp.
C_{1-\beta}(\tilde{P})\sim v_{1-\beta}(\tilde{P})
), Lemma
2.2
implies the following: for every
\kappa>0
, there exists a
\beta_{0}
such that for all
\beta<\beta_{0}
,

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))>\left(\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)-\kappa\right)^{1/\gamma}C_{1-\beta}(P_{0}).

Therefore if nominal distribution had light tails,
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))
is (at least) a constant away from the evaluation under the nominal distribution. Further, this constant critically depends on both
p
as well as the tail index of
P_{0}
.

The next (somewhat technical) result which follows from a representation of CVaR due to
[
3
]
helps derive a matching upper bound:

Lemma 4.1
.

For any uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
and
\beta>0
,

(14)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{P})\leq\beta^{-1}\int_{0}^{\beta}v_{1-t}(\mathcal{P})dt.

Lemma
4.1
suggests that the tail behavior of worst-case VaR influences the upper bound on worst case CVaR. In turn, evaluation of worst case VaR for
\phi-
divergence based uncertainty sets has been well studied (see
[
34
,
13
]
). Applying these results leads to the following asymptotic characterization of worst case CVaR:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds, and further
\phi\in{\tt RV}(p)
for some
p>1
.

(i)

If in addition Assumption
1
holds with
\gamma>\frac{p}{p-1}
, then

\log C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))\sim\frac{p}{p-1}\log C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})\text{ as $\beta\to 0$}.

(ii)

If Assumption
2
holds, then

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))\sim\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{1/\gamma}C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})\text{ as $\beta\to 0$}.

Let us use Theorem
4.1
to compare the worst case and nominal evaluations of CVaR.
Consider two separate cases:

i) When
P_{0}
is heavy tailed:
Theorem
4.1
implies that
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))=[C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})]^{p/(p-1)+o(1)}
. This suggests that the worst case CVaR grows at a significantly faster rate than
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
.
A crucial difference from the case where uncertainty sets are defined using Wasserstein distance is that rate at which
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))
grows depends on the tail of
P_{0}
via
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
. The intuition here is that the contents of
\phi-
divergence based uncertainty sets critically depend on both the tail of
P_{0}
, and as a result, so does the rate at which the worst case evaluation grows.

ii) When
P_{0}
is Weibullian:
Here,
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))=(p/(p-1)+o(1))^{1/\gamma}C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
. This suggests that the worst case CVaR is a constant times
C_{1-\beta}(P_{0})
.
The reason is that when
P_{0}
is Weibullian,
\phi
-divergence balls only contain distributions whose hazard functions are at most a constant away from the hazard function of
P_{0}
. This implies that while the
tail of the worst-case distribution
\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})
and that of
P_{0}
decay at the same rate, albeit differing by a constant.
Note also how
\gamma
influences the extent of conservativeness: the fatter the tail (small
\gamma
) the larger the gap between CVaR evaluation under the nominal and worst case distributions.

Numerical Illustration 2
.

In order to show the implications of the asymptotics from Theorem

, we numerically compute
C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))
and compare it with

. The following duality result from

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0})}E[Z-u]^{+}=\inf_{\lambda\geq 0,\eta}\left\{\eta+\delta\lambda+\lambda E_{P_{0}}\left[\phi^{*}\left(\frac{(Z-u)^{+}-\eta}{\lambda}\right)\right]\right\},

where
\phi^{*}(s)=\sup_{t}\{st-\phi(t)\}
. Therefore, from (

),

(15)

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{\phi}(P_{0}))=\inf_{(u,\lambda)\geq 0,\eta}\left\{u+\beta^{-1}\left(\eta+\delta\lambda+\lambda E_{P_{0}}\left[\phi^{*}\left(\frac{(Z-u)^{+}-\eta}{\lambda}\right)\right]\right)\right\}.

is a known distribution. In Figure

.

5.
Design of Representative DRO Formulations

It is desirable that the uncertainty set be designed such that the worst-case evaluation resembles
C_{1-\beta}(Q)
as closely as possible. We introduce the notion of a rate-preserving uncertainty set to capture this
scenario:

Definition 3

(Rate-Preserving uncertainty set)
.

Given a discrepancy measure
\mathcal{D}
, nominal distributions
Q_{\beta}
and budget parameter
\delta
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}=\{P:\mathcal{D}(Q_{\beta},P)\leq\delta\}
denote a sequence of uncertainty sets, and let
Q\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
be a fixed distribution. We say that
\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}
is
Q
-weakly rate-preserving if as
\beta\to 0
,

\log C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\beta})\sim\log C_{1-\beta}(Q).

We say that
\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}
is
Q
-strongly rate-preserving if

C_{1-\beta}(\mathcal{Q}_{\beta})\sim C_{1-\beta}(Q).

Definition
3
attempts to
capture DRO formulations
which
lead to worst-case risk evaluations that are robust, but at the same time, not too conservative. Indeed, when Wasserstein distances (or) polynomial divergences are used to
construct uncertainty sets, the resulting worst case evaluation is significantly larger than the true risk.
Instead, uncertainty sets
\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}
which are
Q-
strongly (resp. weakly) rate preserving lead to less conservative risk evaluations than each of these two cases for heavy (resp. light) tailed
Q
.
On the other hand, the extent of underestimation for such a formulation is also relatively small.
We point out that Definition
3
is tight in that

.

Let

be constructed as in Definition

6.2.

Propositions

(that is, when

.

10.
Conclusion and Future Directions

We arrive at a DRO formulation for a wide class of tail risk measures that is robust, while not being too conservative. We demonstrate that even in regimes where there is a severe paucity of representative tail samples and sample averages fails to produce a good uncertainty set, the proposed method continues to produce accurate risk estimates. There are two directions which merit investigation: first, several works derive conditions on the budget parameter
\delta
that ensure the output of the DRO formulation acts as a lower bound for the true risk with a required level of confidence. A follow up question to ask is whether under one can impose minimal additional regularity assumptions on
Q
beyond those given by regular variation to arrive at such a
\delta
while estimating tail risk measures. Second, one may ask whether extreme value theory based DRO formulations may be naturally incorporated into stochastic optimization formulations, so as to be able to robustly solve decision making problems involving distribution tails. We intend to pursue both these directions as follow-up works.

Case ii)
\Lambda_{Q}\in{\tt RV}
: i) Lower Bound:
On the set
\Omega_{0}
,
v_{1-\beta_{0}}(\hat{Q}_{\beta}^{(n)})=v_{1-\beta_{0}}(Q_{\beta})

5.1

\kappa>1

\displaystyle v_{1-t}(\hat{Q}_{\beta}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=v_{1-\beta_{0}}(Q_{\beta})(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=v_{1-t}(Q)\left(\frac{\log(1/t)}{\log(1/\beta_{0})}\right)^{1/\gamma}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=v_{1-t}(Q)(1+o(1))

where the last statement follows from the regular variation of
\Lambda^{\leftarrow}_{Q}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))
