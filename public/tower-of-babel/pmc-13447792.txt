Safety and Efficacy of GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists in Adults With Epilepsy, Obesity, and Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Hyoshin Son, Inyoung Hwang, Sang Won Lee, Yun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveManaging obesity in patients with epilepsy is complicated by the weight‐gaining properties of essential antiseizure medications (ASMs) such as valproate and pregabalin. We evaluated the safety and efficacy of initiating glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) in this population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the NIH All of Us Research Program database, identifying adults with comorbid epilepsy, type 2 diabetes, and obesity. New initiators of GLP‐1 RAs were compared with matched controls using 1:1 propensity score matching that included baseline weight‐gaining ASM use. The primary safety outcome was a composite of seizure‐related hospitalization or new ASM addition. Secondary efficacy endpoints (weight and HbA1c change at 12 months) were stratified by concomitant weight‐gaining ASM use.ResultsThe propensity‐matched cohort included 610 patients with well‐balanced baseline characteristics. GLP‐1 RA initiation was not associated with failure to control seizure compared with standard care (hazard ratio 0.92, 95% CI 0.68–1.23; p = 0.57); given the observed events, the study could detect a hazard ratio of 1.53 or greater. GLP‐1 RA users achieved a mean adjusted weight loss of −5.73% (p = 0.001) and an HbA1c reduction of −0.71% (p = 0.018). Significant weight reduction was achieved even among patients concomitantly treated with weight‐gaining ASMs (adjusted difference −4.15%, p = 0.003).InterpretationGLP‐1 RAs showed no evidence of seizure worsening and remained effective for weight loss even among patients receiving weight‐gaining ASMs, supporting their use for metabolic benefit in patients with epilepsy.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a prevalent neurological condition affecting over 70 million people worldwide, carrying a substantial burden of medical comorbidity [1, 2]. Approximately 50% of adults with active epilepsy manage at least one additional chronic disease, with obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) occurring at significantly higher rates than in the general population [3, 4, 5, 6]. This relationship is increasingly recognized as bidirectional and complex: metabolic dysregulation may exacerbate seizure susceptibility via mechanisms such as oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, while epilepsy itself and its pharmacological management can drive metabolic deterioration [7, 8, 9]. Crucially, obesity in patients with epilepsy is associated with poorer quality of life, increased seizure frequency, and higher rates of pharmacoresistance [10, 11, 12]. Consequently, the effective management of metabolic health is a critical component of comprehensive epilepsy care.

A major challenge in treating this population is the adverse metabolic profile of many essential antiseizure medications (ASMs) [13]. Agents such as valproate, carbamazepine, pregabalin, and gabapentin are widely prescribed for their robust efficacy but are strongly implicated in iatrogenic weight gain and insulin resistance [14]. This creates a therapeutic dilemma for neurologists: the medications required to maintain seizure stability often compromise metabolic health, which may, in turn, negatively influence long‐term cardiovascular and neurological outcomes [15]. Importantly, switching from effective but weight‐promoting ASMs to metabolically neutral alternatives is not always feasible, as it may increase the risk of breakthrough seizures—particularly in patients with long‐standing or drug‐resistant epilepsy. Clinicians are frequently forced to prioritize seizure control at the expense of metabolic parameters, resulting in treatment regimens that manage epilepsy but neglect the patient's systemic health.

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have emerged as the standard of care for T2DM and obesity, demonstrating profound weight loss and cardiovascular benefits [16]. Despite their widespread use, data regarding their neurological safety in patients with epilepsy remain limited. While emerging preclinical evidence suggests that GLP‐1 RAs may possess neuroprotective properties, concerns regarding their potential to alter the absorption kinetics of oral ASMs via delayed gastric emptying remain a clinical consideration [17, 18]. Although a recent retrospective study suggested a reduced risk of seizure recurrence with GLP‐1 RA use, these findings require validation in diverse cohorts to definitively establish the safety of these agents regarding seizure thresholds [19].

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using the All of Us Research Program database to evaluate the safety and efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs in a nationwide cohort of adults with comorbid epilepsy, T2DM, and obesity. The primary objective was to determine whether GLP‐1 RA initiation is associated with failure to control seizure, defined as seizure‐related hospitalization or the addition of a new ASM. Secondarily, we sought to assess the metabolic efficacy of these agents, specifically testing the hypothesis that GLP‐1 RAs can provide metabolic benefit by attenuating or reversing weight gain in patients treated with concomitant weight‐gaining ASMs.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us Research Program. Initiated in May 2018, the program aims to enroll at least one million individuals from diverse populations across the United States [20]. This study utilized the Curated Data Repository (CDR) version 8, which includes 633,547 participants with data available through October 1, 2023 [21]. The database integrates diverse data streams, including participant‐provided surveys, electronic health records (EHRs), physical measurements, digital health technology data, and genomic data [22]. For EHR data, the All of Us Research Program employs the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) to harmonize clinical information across multiple domains, including conditions, drug exposures, procedures, and measurements, by mapping them to standard OMOP concept IDs [23]. A comprehensive list of the specific OMOP concept IDs utilized to define study cohorts, comorbidities, medications, and outcomes is provided in Table S1.

All analyses were performed using the All of Us Researcher Workbench, a cloud‐based platform providing registered researchers access to de‐identified, individual‐level participant data [24]. To ensure participant privacy, all data were de‐identified in accordance with the program's security protocols. This study complied with the All of Us Research Program's Data and Research Use Policy. The study protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hanyang University Seoul Hospital and was deemed exempt from ethical review (IRB No. HYUH‐2025‐12‐005). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design and Participants

This study employed a new‐user design with a matched control group to evaluate the safety and efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs. The study population consisted of adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with established diagnoses of T2DM, obesity, and epilepsy or seizure disorders. To ensure the cohort represented patients with active epilepsy requiring pharmacological management, all participants were required to have a documented history of antiseizure medication (ASM) use.

The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription for a GLP‐1 RA for the exposure group. For the control group, index dates were randomly assigned based on the distribution of index dates observed in the exposure group, contingent upon an active ASM prescription window. To support valid new‐user ascertainment and baseline characterization, all participants were required to have at least 180 days of continuous observation before the index date. Because the exposure group comprised first‐ever GLP‐1 RA initiators, exposed patients were GLP‐1 RA–naïve by construction. Patients were excluded if their index date occurred before January 1, 2006, or after December 31, 2022. Additional exclusion criteria were: (1) the absence of an active ASM prescription on the index date; or (2) the lack of confirmed T2DM and obesity diagnoses active on the index date.

Exposure and Control Groups

The exposure group consisted of new initiators of GLP‐1 RAs, defined as patients with a first prescription for any FDA‐approved agent in the class, including semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, or tirzepatide. The control group comprised matched patients with established diagnoses of T2DM and obesity who were receiving active ASM therapy but had no record of GLP‐1 RA exposure.

Covariates and ASM Classification

Baseline characteristics, including demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity), were assessed at the index date. Comorbidities, including hypertension, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and conditions comprising the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), were identified using diagnosis codes recorded on or before the index date. Clinical measurements (body weight, BMI, HbA1c) and co‐medications (insulin, metformin, and other oral antidiabetic drugs) were assessed based on records within the 180 days preceding the index date.

To adjust for seizure severity, we included the number of distinct ASMs prescribed at baseline and the history of seizure‐related hospitalizations or emergency room visits in the year prior to the index date. Furthermore, ASMs were categorized based on their known metabolic side effect profiles. Patients were classified as using “weight‐gaining ASMs” if they had an active prescription for valproate, carbamazepine, gabapentin, pregabalin, or vigabatrin at the index date. All other ASMs were classified as “non‐weight‐gaining ASMs.”

Outcomes

The primary safety outcome was a composite seizure endpoint, defined as the time to the first occurrence of either: (1) a seizure‐related hospitalization (inpatient admission, intensive care unit admission, or emergency department visit with a primary diagnosis of seizure or epilepsy); or (2) the addition of a new ASM class with no recorded exposure during the 365 days preceding the index date. This composite definition was adopted from proxies for uncontrolled epilepsy utilized in previous claims database research [25]. Secondary safety outcomes comprised the individual components of this composite endpoint. Efficacy outcomes were evaluated as the percentage change in body weight and the absolute change in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) from baseline to 12 months, allowing for a 3‐month window preceding each time point.

Statistical Analysis

To minimize confounding bias, we performed 1:1 nearest‐neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. The propensity score model included age, sex, race, ethnicity, baseline weight, BMI, HbA1c, number of ASMs, prior seizure hospitalizations, CCI, comorbidities, baseline antidiabetic medications, and indicators for baseline weight‐gaining ASM use (an overall indicator together with separate indicators for valproate, carbamazepine, gabapentin, and pregabalin; vigabatrin had no users in the cohort and was omitted as a zero‐variance covariate) to address potential confounding by indication arising from the preferential prescribing of GLP‐1 RAs to patients receiving weight‐promoting ASMs.

For time‐to‐event safety outcomes, patients were followed for a maximum of 12 months from the index date. Patients who did not experience an outcome event were administratively censored at 365 days. We estimated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using Cox proportional hazards models. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to visualize event‐free survival probabilities. Continuous efficacy outcomes (weight and HbA1c change) were compared using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for the respective baseline values.

Subgroup analyses evaluated the consistency of the treatment effect across key clinical strata, including age, BMI, and prior seizure history. A specific interaction analysis was performed to assess whether the weight‐lowering efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs differed based on the concomitant use of weight‐gaining ASMs. To verify the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses including an as‐treated analysis (censoring patients at treatment discontinuation plus a 30‐day grace period) and a per‐protocol analysis (restricted to patients with sustained adherence for ≥ 12 months). We estimated the post hoc minimum detectable hazard ratio for the primary composite endpoint (80% power, two‐sided α = 0.05) from the observed number of events. Because comparator index dates lack a clinical anchor, we compared the per‐person‐year frequency of clinical encounters, body‐weight measurements, and HbA1c measurements between groups to assess differential ascertainment. We additionally derived ASM dose escalation from OMOP drug‐exposure and drug‐strength records and incorporated it into an augmented safety composite. Efficacy analyses of weight and HbA1c change were conducted on a complete‐case basis under a missing‐at‐random assumption; the proportion of patients with available follow‐up measurements and the comparability of missingness between groups were examined. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python within the All of Us Researcher Workbench.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1121 eligible patients with epilepsy receiving ASMs, comorbid T2DM, and obesity were identified from the All of Us database (Figure 1). This initial cohort included 446 new initiators of GLP‐1 RAs (exposure group) and 675 controls. Prior to matching, the groups exhibited significant imbalances in baseline characteristics, particularly regarding BMI, HbA1c, and insulin usage (Table S2). Following 1:1 propensity score matching, the final analysis cohort comprised 610 patients, with 305 in the exposure group and 305 in the control group.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the matched groups, with all standardized mean differences (SMDs) below 0.1 (Table 1). Prevalent comorbidities included hypertension (90.2%) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (40.0%).

Safety Outcomes

The primary composite seizure outcome occurred at a similar rate in both the exposure and control groups (Table 2). We observed no statistically significant difference in the composite seizure outcome, defined as seizure‐related hospitalization or the addition of a new ASM, between the GLP‐1 RA and control groups (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.68–1.23; p = 0.57). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated overlapping event‐free probability curves throughout the 12‐month follow‐up period, indicating a comparable safety profile for GLP‐1 RAs (Figure 2A). Given the observed number of composite events (n = 174), the study had 80% power to detect a hazard ratio of 1.53 or greater, and we therefore cannot exclude smaller increases in risk within the confidence interval, the upper bound of which (1.23) corresponds to an approximately 23% relative increase in composite events.

Analysis of the individual components of the composite endpoint yielded consistent findings (Table 2; Figure 2B,C). The risk of seizure‐related hospitalization did not differ significantly between the groups (HR 0.80; 95% CI 0.53–1.23; p = 0.31). Similarly, the initiation of GLP‐1 RAs was not associated with an increased requirement for additional ASMs compared to standard care (HR 0.97; 95% CI 0.68–1.39; p = 0.86). These safety findings remained robust across all subgroups, including strata defined by age, baseline BMI, HbA1c levels, and prior history of seizure‐related hospitalizations (Table S3).

Metabolic Efficacy and Interaction With Weight‐Gaining ASMs

GLP‐1 RA treatment resulted in significant metabolic improvements compared to the control group (Table 3). At 12 months, patients treated with GLP‐1 RAs achieved a significantly greater reduction in body weight, with an adjusted treatment difference of −5.73% (95% CI −9.15 to −2.31; p = 0.001). Glycemic control also improved, demonstrating a mean adjusted reduction in HbA1c of −0.71% (95% CI −1.30 to −0.12; p = 0.018) relative to the control group.

Sensitivity Analyses

To verify the robustness of our primary safety findings, we performed two sensitivity analyses (Table S3). In an as‐treated analysis censoring patients at treatment discontinuation, the composite seizure outcome was comparable between groups (HR 0.88; 95% CI 0.65–1.20; p = 0.43). A per‐protocol analysis restricted to patients with sustained adherence for at least 12 months (N = 411) yielded consistent results (HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.73–1.48; p = 0.82).

Additional Analyses

We performed several additional analyses to assess outcome ascertainment and the completeness of the efficacy data. Follow‐up intensity was broadly comparable but not identical: body‐weight measurement frequency did not differ (9.4 vs. 9.1 per person‐year; p = 0.67), whereas clinical encounters and HbA1c measurements were modestly more frequent among GLP‐1 RA users (any encounter, 73.4 vs. 69.8 per person‐year, p = 0.005; HbA1c, 2.2 vs. 1.8 per person‐year, p = 0.001; Table S4). In an exploratory analysis incorporating ASM dose escalation into the composite endpoint, the result was unchanged (HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.80–1.36; p = 0.74), although a daily dose could be parsed for only 33.6% of ASM records (Table S5). Finally, twelve‐month weight data were available for the analysis in a similar proportion of each group (109/305 [35.7%] GLP‐1 RA vs. 115/305 [37.7%] Control; Table S6), and patients with versus without follow‐up weight were generally comparable (Table S7).

Discussion

In this propensity score‐matched cohort study utilizing the All of Us Research Program database, we evaluated the safety and efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs in adults with comorbid epilepsy, T2DM, and obesity. Our analysis demonstrated that GLP‐1 RA initiation was not associated with an increased risk of the composite seizure outcome, defined as seizure‐related hospitalization or the addition of a new ASM, compared to standard care. Beyond this favorable safety profile, GLP‐1 RAs conferred significant metabolic benefits, resulting in substantial weight loss and improved glycemic control. Crucially, this efficacy was preserved even among patients concomitantly treated with weight‐gaining ASMs. These findings suggest that GLP‐1 RAs can produce significant weight loss even among patients receiving these essential weight‐gaining medications, offering a viable therapeutic strategy for metabolic benefit.

A primary safety concern regarding GLP‐1 RA use in epilepsy is the potential for altered drug absorption due to delayed gastric emptying [16, 18]. Theoretically, this delay could shift the pharmacokinetics of co‐administered oral ASMs, raising concerns about subtherapeutic levels, particularly for drugs with a narrow therapeutic index [26]. However, pharmacokinetic studies indicate that while GLP‐1 RAs may slow the rate of absorption, they do not significantly alter the total extent of drug exposure [27]. This pharmacokinetic stability provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for our clinical findings: as long as systemic ASM exposure is not affected by concomitant medications related to gastric emptying, intrinsic seizure control of ASM is preserved. Consequently, our observation that GLP‐1 RA initiation did not lead to increased hospitalization or medication intensification supports the conclusion that these agents can be safely co‐administered with standard ASM regimens, though vigilance during the titration phase remains prudent.

Our safety findings align with and refine recent epidemiological data. A large‐scale study utilizing the TriNetX network reported a reduced risk of seizure recurrence with GLP‐1 RA use [19]. While our analysis did not replicate this statistical reduction, our study offers distinct clinical novelty by strictly focusing on patients receiving active ASM therapy. Unlike broader analyses that may rely solely on diagnostic codes, we isolated a population actively managed with pharmacotherapy and utilized propensity score matching to rigorously adjust for surrogates of seizure severity, including baseline ASM burden and prior hospitalization history. This granular focus ensures that our findings are directly applicable to the complex real‐world population managing both epilepsy and metabolic disease. Despite differences in design, both studies converge on the conclusion that GLP‐1 RAs do not compromise seizure control, providing reassurance for their use in this vulnerable population.

The metabolic burden imposed by essential ASMs, such as valproate, pregabalin, and carbamazepine, represents a critical barrier to optimal epilepsy management. This iatrogenic weight gain is not merely a cosmetic concern but a significant risk factor for treatment failure; medication‐induced obesity is a well‐documented driver of non‐adherence, leading to breakthrough seizures [9, 28, 29]. Furthermore, the relationship between metabolic health and epilepsy is increasingly recognized as bidirectional [30]. Obesity is associated with a chronic pro‐inflammatory state and hormonal dysregulation that may theoretically lower seizure thresholds or exacerbate pharmacoresistance [31]. Consequently, the effective management of metabolic health is not peripheral but central to comprehensive epilepsy care.

Our findings are consistent with GLP‐1 RAs counteracting the weight gain associated with these ASMs. By centrally promoting satiety and enhancing peripheral insulin sensitivity, GLP‐1 RAs target the precise pathophysiological pathways dysregulated by weight‐gaining ASMs [16]. This mechanistic counter‐regulation is consistent with our finding that GLP‐1 RAs induced significant weight loss (−4.15%) even in patients treated with obesogenic medications. Although the point estimate was larger in the non‐weight‐gaining ASM group (−10.61%), this subgroup was small and imprecise and the interaction was not statistically significant; the two estimates should be regarded as consistent rather than meaningfully different. We describe the clinically meaningful weight loss achieved in the weight‐gaining ASM group as a potential “metabolic rescue.” We emphasize, however, that because we did not characterize the temporal relationship between ASM initiation and the onset of obesity, this interpretation rests on an assumption that the obesity in this subgroup was, at least in part, iatrogenically driven; the data are temporally compatible with but cannot confirm such an origin. With that caveat, the finding suggests that the potent anorexigenic effects of GLP‐1 RAs are sufficient to overcome the orexigenic drive of these ASMs. By mitigating this challenging side effect without necessitating the discontinuation of effective seizure therapy, GLP‐1 RAs may contribute to long‐term epilepsy management through improved treatment adherence, reduced cardiovascular risk, and enhanced patient quality of life [32, 33].

These findings address a pervasive therapeutic dilemma in the comprehensive care of patients with epilepsy. When a patient stable on a weight‐gaining ASM develops obesity or T2DM, clinicians face a choice to maintain the current regimen at the cost of metabolic deterioration, or to switch to a weight‐neutral agent which could be associated with a significant risk of seizure recurrence [34]. Our data suggest that the addition of a GLP‐1 RA offers a strategic alternative that allows clinicians to preserve seizure stability while actively managing weight and glycemic control. This adjunctive strategy is particularly valuable for patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy who have achieved control on specific weight‐promoting agents. Furthermore, given that cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of premature mortality in this population, the established cardiovascular benefits of GLP‐1 RAs offer long‐term prognostic value beyond weight management alone [15].

Our study has several key strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically evaluate the safety and efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs in a tri‐morbid population of patients with epilepsy, T2DM, and obesity who are actively receiving ASMs. By utilizing the All of Us Research Program database, we leveraged a diverse, nationwide cohort that enhances the generalizability of our findings to real‐world clinical practice in the United States. Furthermore, the use of a new‐user design with rigorous propensity score matching allowed us to balance key prognostic factors, including seizure severity and baseline ASM burden, thereby minimizing confounding by indication.

However, these findings must be interpreted in the context of certain limitations. First, by design we required the concurrent presence of epilepsy, T2DM, and obesity, which selects a metabolically extreme population; our findings therefore apply to people with epilepsy who have both obesity and established T2DM, with limited generalizability to the broader epilepsy population. Second, consistent with the inherent constraints of observational EHR‐based research, we relied on surrogate markers for seizure control, specifically hospitalizations and medication intensification, rather than granular seizure frequency counts or EEG data, which were not available. While these composite endpoints robustly capture clinically significant failure to control seizure, they may fail to detect subtle fluctuations in seizure frequency that do not necessitate acute care or prescription adjustment, and the addition of a new ASM is an imperfect proxy that may reflect off‐label use (e.g., for neuropathic pain or psychiatric indications) rather than loss of seizure control. Third, the study was powered to detect moderate‐to‐large effects but not small ones: given the observed event count, the minimum detectable hazard ratio for the primary endpoint was 1.53, and the confidence interval cannot exclude a meaningful increase in risk; the safety conclusion is therefore best framed as the absence of a moderate‐to‐large increase in seizure‐related events. Fourth, the comparator index date lacks the biological anchor of treatment initiation, raising the possibility of surveillance bias; although body‐weight measurement frequency was comparable between groups, clinical encounters and HbA1c testing were modestly more frequent among GLP‐1 RA users (Table S4), and because more intensive contact would tend to increase the detection of new ASM additions in the exposed group, this differential ascertainment renders the null safety result conservative. The more frequent HbA1c testing among GLP‐1 RA users is also a potential source of differential ascertainment for the HbA1c efficacy endpoint specifically, mitigated but not eliminated by our prespecified ±3‐month measurement window. Fifth, beyond the dietary, physical‐activity, and socioeconomic factors we could not capture, GLP‐1 RA prescribing is strongly influenced by insurance coverage and access; unmeasured differences in healthcare access, provider engagement, and structured follow‐up therefore represent a residual confounder that is distinct from lifestyle factors, and the modestly higher encounter intensity among GLP‐1 RA users (Table S4) is consistent with such access differences. Sixth, EHR‐based weight measurement is subject to substantial missingness; 12‐month follow‐up weight was available for just over a third (~37%) of the matched cohort, and although the proportion was similar between groups and patients with versus without follow‐up weight were broadly comparable (Table S7), informative missingness cannot be fully excluded, and the efficacy estimates rest on complete cases under a missing‐at‐random assumption. Seventh, while the overall cohort was sufficient for the primary safety analysis, the sample size for subgroup comparisons, particularly the interaction between GLP‐1 RAs and ASM class, limited statistical power; in addition, the HbA1c reduction, though statistically significant, was modest in absolute terms (−0.71%) and based on a smaller sample (n = 145), and should be interpreted with corresponding caution. Eighth, we did not characterize the temporal relationship between ASM initiation and obesity onset; consequently, our “metabolic rescue” interpretation assumes, but cannot confirm, that the obesity in the weight‐gaining ASM subgroup was iatrogenically driven rather than pre‐existing. Only 26 of the 150 weight‐gaining ASM patients had a first exposure that preceded the earliest recorded obesity (fewer than 20 per arm). A robust temporally restricted estimate is therefore not feasible, and we frame the weight loss in this subgroup as temporally compatible with, rather than confirmatory of, an iatrogenic origin. Finally, our analysis employed a fixed 12‐month follow‐up window; while sensitivity analyses accounting for treatment discontinuation confirmed the robustness of our results, longer‐term studies are needed to assess the durability of these benefits beyond one year.

In conclusion, GLP‐1 RAs demonstrated a favorable safety profile in patients with epilepsy, showing no association with seizure exacerbation or increased seizure‐related healthcare utilization. Beyond establishing safety, this study highlights the significant therapeutic value of GLP‐1 RAs in addressing the metabolic burden of epilepsy. Most notably, these agents produced significant weight loss even in patients treated with weight‐gaining antiseizure medications such as valproate and pregabalin. Taken together, these findings suggest that GLP‐1 RAs can address metabolic comorbidity in this population without evidence of worsened seizure control and warrant confirmation in prospective studies.
