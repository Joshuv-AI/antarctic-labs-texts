A case of anti-nephrin antibody-positive, steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome with successful discontinuation of immunosuppressants following rituximab
Authors: Kota Shinzato, Norifumi Hayashi, Ryoko Akai, Yu Kagaya, Yutaka Hiratuji, Sho Kumano, Kazuaki Okino, Keiji Fujimoto, Takao Iwawaki, Kengo Furuichi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimal change nephrotic syndrome (MCNS) is a major cause of nephrotic syndrome in children and adults. Recent studies identified circulating anti-nephrin antibodies in 30%–50% of patients, linking them to severe proteinuria and frequent relapses. We describe a Japanese woman in her 40s with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (SDNS). Anti-nephrin antibodies were identified in serum obtained at disease onset by immunoprecipitation and ELISA, and kidney biopsy showed punctate IgG colocalizing with nephrin. Before rituximab (RTX), she experienced relapses accompanied by increased anti-nephrin antibody reactivity. A low-dose RTX regimen (200 mg every 6 months for seven doses) enabled maintenance of complete remission and discontinuation of prednisolone and cyclosporine, with B-cell depletion confirmed by CD19 monitoring. She has maintained remission for two years after RTX discontinuation, without re-elevation of anti-nephrin antibody reactivity. This case supports a possible role of anti-nephrin antibodies in disease activity and suggests that low-dose RTX may contribute to durable remission.

Introduction

Minimal change nephrotic syndrome (MCNS) is a major cause of nephrotic syndrome in both children and adults. It presents with heavy proteinuria accompanied by edema and is pathologically characterized by minimal changes on both light microscopy and immunofluorescence (IF) and diffuse podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy. Although its pathogenesis has long been considered immune-mediated, specific antigens had remained elusive until recent reports identified circulating anti-nephrin antibodies [1]. Positivity rates for anti-nephrin antibodies vary across studies but are generally around 30%–50% in patients with MCNS [2]. Anti-nephrin antibodies have also been detected in other glomerular diseases at lower frequencies, and relatively high positivity rates have been reported in children with idiopathic nephrotic syndrome [2, 3]. Reported positivity rates may vary depending on the assay method, antigen preparation, cutoff definition, and timing of sample collection in relation to disease activity [2]. Patients with anti-nephrin antibodies tend to present with more severe proteinuria, a higher relapse rate, and a shorter relapse-free interval compared to those without these antibodies [4].

Rituximab (RTX), a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B cells, has emerged as an effective therapy for steroid-dependent and frequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome (SDNS/FRNS), not only through B-cell depletion but also by modulating immune regulation [5].

Here, we report a case of anti-nephrin antibody–positive SDNS. Before the initiation of RTX, the patient experienced repeated relapses associated with anti-nephrin antibody reactivity; however, after RTX therapy, the patient has remained in remission without re-elevation of anti-nephrin antibody reactivity, even after discontinuation of RTX.

Case report

A Japanese woman in her 40s was previously admitted to our hospital at disease onset with the sudden onset of lower extremity edema and weight gain. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, hyperlipidemia, and kidney dysfunction in the setting of severe nephrotic syndrome (Table 1). The abrupt onset of nephrotic syndrome together with a low selectivity index score (urinary IgG clearance/transferrin clearance < 0.2) supported minimal change nephrotic syndrome (MCNS). Immunosuppressive therapy with prednisolone (PSL) and cyclosporine (CyA) was initiated.

Initial therapy was effective and she achieved complete remission (reduction of proteinuria to < 0.3 g/d, stable serum creatinine and serum albumin > 3.5 g/dl) within 69 days. However, she experienced multiple relapses during tapering of PSL and CyA and subsequently developed SDNS (Fig. 2). Although remission could be re-induced with increased doses of PSL and CyA, repeated relapse during tapering made discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy difficult. Nine years after disease onset, low-dose rituximab (RTX; 200 mg/body every 6 months) was added with the aim of maintaining remission and discontinuing PSL and CyA. RTX was administered seven times over approximately 3 years. Thereafter, complete remission was maintained without additional immunosuppressive therapy. Peripheral B-cell depletion was monitored by flow cytometry using peripheral blood CD19-positive B-cell counts. Effective B-cell depletion was defined as a CD19+ cell count of < 5 cells/μL, in accordance with previous studies [6, 7]. No significant infectious complications were observed during repeated RTX administration or during follow-up. She has since maintained long-term remission for 2 years, even after discontinuation of RTX, and remains free of immunosuppressive therapy.

Antibodies against nephrin were retrospectively analyzed in serum samples that had been collected and stored from disease onset through follow-up. Anti-nephrin antibody reactivity was identified in the serum obtained at disease onset by both immunoprecipitation using His-tagged recombinant human nephrin as the bait antigen and ELISA. Immunoprecipitation was used to confirm specific reactivity against recombinant human nephrin (Fig. 3), whereas ELISA was used for longitudinal assessment in serial serum samples. In the ELISA, antibody reactivity was defined as the difference in absorbance between nephrin-coated and non-coated wells. All serial samples from disease onset through follow-up were measured on the same plate, and the results are presented as relative reactivity normalized to the onset sample (Fig. 2).

Immunoprecipitation using his-tagged recombinant human nephrin as bait. Sheep anti-human nephrin antibody was used as a positive control. Immunoblotting with anti-his antibody showed that the patient’s serum at disease onset produced the same band as the positive control, whereas the band disappeared in the serum obtained during remission.

Double immunofluorescence staining for nephrin and IgG demonstrated co-localization of nephrin and IgG (Fig. 1D). Longitudinal measurement of anti-nephrin antibodies by ELISA revealed that anti-nephrin antibody reactivity increased at the time of relapse episodes before the RTX therapy. After RTX therapy, anti-nephrin antibody reactivity remained low without re-elevation, and sustained clinical remission was maintained even in the absence of any immunosuppressive therapy, with urinary protein excretion of 0.03 g/gCr, serum albumin of 4.1 g/dl, and serum creatinine of 0.71 mg/dl at the most recent follow-up (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our case developed SDNS during longitudinal follow-up, with anti-nephrin antibody reactivity identified in serum obtained at disease onset and subsequently varying in parallel with disease activity. According to recent reports, patients with anti-nephrin antibodies exhibit more active nephrotic syndrome, more frequent relapses, and are more prone to steroid dependence [1, 4]. The pathogenic roles of anti-nephrin antibodies in podocytopathy have not been fully elucidated. However, they have been reported to induce nephrin phosphorylation in kidney transplant recipients with recurrent FSGS and in rabbits injected with human anti-nephrin antibodies [8, 9]. Before the initiation of RTX, the patient experienced multiple relapses associated with increased anti-nephrin antibody reactivity. This finding supports a possible pathogenic role of anti-nephrin antibodies in this case and suggests that B-cell depletion induced by RTX may have contributed to suppression of their production.

In this case, a low-dose rituximab regimen (200 mg/body every 6 months) was introduced, allowing the discontinuation of PSL and CyA. We previously reported the efficacy of this regimen in the treatment of SDNS [10]. This low-dose regimen was selected in consideration of prior reports suggesting that higher cumulative rituximab exposure may increase the risk of infectious complications in patients with glomerular disease [11], whereas lower-dose regimens have also been reported to be effective [12]. In the present case, successful B-cell depletion was confirmed by careful monitoring of CD19+ B cells. Previous studies in patients with nephrotic syndrome have demonstrated that a single dose of rituximab induces adequate B-cell depletion, which typically persists for 4–6 months [13, 14]. For sustained remission, repeated administration with a total of 2–4 doses is usually recommended [5].

It is noteworthy that our case maintained sustained clinical remission together with persistent disappearance of detectable anti-nephrin antibody reactivity even after discontinuation of all immunosuppressive agents, including RTX. In this context, clinical remission refers to sustained resolution of nephrotic syndrome, whereas immunological remission refers to the disappearance of detectable anti-nephrin antibody reactivity. In our patient, these two features closely paralleled each other, suggesting that serial measurement of anti-nephrin antibody reactivity may be useful for monitoring disease activity in selected cases. This favorable course may reflect not only transient B-cell depletion but also broader immunologic effects of RTX, although the precise mechanism remains uncertain. Beyond B-cell depletion, RTX reduces antigen presentation, thereby attenuating T-cell activation and suppressing cytokine release that could increase glomerular permeability. Furthermore, RTX has been reported to restore regulatory T- and B-cell populations and to enhance their functions, which may contribute to immune reconstitution and the stabilization of the immune system [5]. It should also be noted that response to immunotherapy is not universal, and treatment response may vary depending on the underlying disease context, including secondary or paraneoplastic settings [15].

In conclusion, we reported the case of a woman in her 40s with SDNS who achieved long-term remission after low-dose RTX therapy, accompanied by disappearance of anti-nephrin antibody reactivity. This case supports a possible role of anti-nephrin antibodies in disease activity and suggests that RTX may contribute to durable remission, although mechanistic causality cannot be established from a single case.
