Letter to the editor: Gene expression profiling of mouse cavernous endothelial cells for diagnostic targets in diabetes-induced erectile dysfunction
Authors: Jun-Kyu Suh
Published: 2023

To the editor:

I appreciate, as an author of the paper [1], the opportunity to write a letter to the editor to talk about our paper in association with current research trends to develop future therapeutics for erectile dysfunction (ED).

Even though phosphodiesterse5 (PDE5) inhibitors are widely used as a first-line therapy for ED, we are confronted with the problem that considerable numbers of patients drop out of PDE5 inhibitor treatment because of its poor effectiveness in certain pathological conditions, nonnegligible adverse effects, and irrelevance to the treatment of cavernous pathology. Research and development (R&D) for new therapeutics, including regenerative therapies, is required to respond to the current clinical situation [2]. To this end, we need more basic knowledge of physiological and pathological conditions, including diabetic ED, neurogenic ED, and other specific disease-induced ED.

As one avenue of basic research to this end, we have investigated whether and how a pathologic condition, diabetes-induced ED, alters cavernous gene profiles. Here, RNA sequencing technology with immunohistochemical study showed that mouse cavernous endothelial cells have different gene profiles in normal-glucose and high-glucose (diabetic) conditions. Among the significantly changed genes, aging-related and angiogenesis-related genes of 14 major gene categories were changed the most. Furthermore, 13 genes from 2 of the gene categories showed consistent changes on the RNA sequencing, and these genes play a critical role in diabetes-induced ED through aging and angiogenesis signaling [1].

We, moreover, expanded the gene expression profile assay in mouse cavernous pericytes and found that the gene expression profiles of mouse cavernous pericytes exposed to the normal-glucose condition or high-glucose condition differed [3]. A total of 2,523 genes with significantly altered expression were classified into 15 major gene categories. It seems to be of significance that Hebp1 gene expression was greatly diminished in the diabetic condition. This gene, thus, is suggested to be a good therapeutic target for diabetic ED.

To provide in-depth insight into the cavernous cellular microenvironment and the mechanisms underlying pathogenesis, a group led by Dr. Lue group developed a single cell atlas of the human corpus cavernosum and described the communication network within this microenvironment [4]. In their study, seven major cavernosal cell types, including subclusters of each cell type, were identified with distinct transcriptome profiles. Each subcluster’s heterogeneity, including the spatial location, biological function, and regulatory pathway, was also identified between normal and ED cavernosal cells.

To better understand the cellular landscape of cavernous tissues and cell-type-specific transcriptional changes in diabetic ED, we also performed single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse corpus cavernosa. Here, we found decreased expression of genes associated with collagen or extracellular matrix organization and angiogenesis in major cells, including fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, valve-related lymphatic endothelial cells, and pericytes, from mouse corpus cavernosum. Cell–cell interaction analysis revealed that pericytes are involved in angiogenesis, adhesion, and migration by communicating with other cell types in the corpus cavernosum; however, these interactions were highly reduced under diabetic conditions. These findings illustrate the importance of altered cellular interaction between cells in the corpus cavernosum in the diabetic condition.

I hope that our paper above may provide an impulse to future corpus cavernosum research to further elucidate the molecular mechanism of the physiological and various pathological conditions responsible for ED. This gene profiling study from cavernous endothelium and pericytes may actually provide new potential targets responsible for diabetic ED. Moreover, recent studies of ours and others also indicate that it is of great importance for R&D of future therapeutics to deeply understand the pathogenesis of ED at the cellular, genetic, and molecular levels.

I believe that continuous research for more knowledge of these issues may hasten the development of new therapeutics, such as regenerative medicines, that precisely correspond to the specific cavernous pathology of patients with ED of different causes.
