Scoped MSO, Register Automata, and Expressions: Equivalence over Data Words
Radosław Piórkowski

Abstract
This paper establishes logical and expression-based characterizations for the class of languages recognized by nondeterministic register automata with guessing (NRA) over infinite alphabets. We introduce Scoped MSO, a logic featuring a novel segment modality and syntactic restrictions on data comparisons. We prove this logic is expressively equivalent to NRA over data domains where “strong guessing” can be eliminated. Furthermore, we define Data-Regular Expressions, a minimalist regular-expression calculus built from quantifier-free regions and equipped with k k -contracting concatenation, and demonstrate its equivalence to NRA over arbitrary relational structures. Together, these formalisms provide a robust descriptive theory for register automata, bridging the gap between automata, logic, and expressions.

Introduction

A remarkable property of the class of
regular languages
is its striking
robustness
: one and the same class of languages admits several radically different, yet equivalent, descriptions. Over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, finite automata, regular expressions, and monadic second-order logic over word structures (MSO) define exactly the regular languages. This three-way equivalence (and its numerous refinements via algebra, temporal logics, varieties, etc.) forms the backbone of classical language theory and explains why “regularity” is such a stable and reusable concept.

When moving from finite alphabets to
infinite alphabets
—modelling systems with process IDs, database keys, large alphabets like Unicode or data values in XML—this robustness generally vanishes. The standard abstraction for such traces is
data words
, sequences over
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
where
\Sigma
is a finite set of labels and
\mathbb{A}
is a data domain called
atoms
modelled as a relational structure with countably-infinite universe. The intended access to elements of
\mathbb{A}
(called
data values
) is limited to testing relations from the signature of
\mathbb{A}
; common examples of atoms include equality atoms
\mathbb{A}_{=}=(\mathbb{N},=)
and dense order atoms
\mathbb{A}_{<}=(\mathbb{Q},<,=)
. The landscape of formalisms for data words is fragmented, featuring a wide array of automata types and logics whose expressive powers are often incomparable and whose decision problems’ complexity and decidability vary wildly
[
15
,
17
,
1
,
5
,
14
]
. Unlike the finite-alphabet case, there is no single notion that plays the role of “
the
” regular languages for data words.

Register Automata.
A natural candidate for a finite-state model over data words is the
register automaton
, introduced by Kaminski and Francez
[
12
]
.
These automata extend finite control with a fixed set of registers capable of storing data values and comparing them for equality.
The model was subsequently extended to arbitrary atoms
\mathbb{A}

[
3
]
.
In this paper, we target the class of
nondeterministic register automata with guessing
(NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
). The “guessing” capability is the most permissive out of three possible variants of nondeterminism in register automata. While NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
are a standard baseline, they have historically lacked a MSO-style logical characterisation.

The MSO Logic for NRAs.
Establishing a correspondence between NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
and logic requires careful calibration of the expressive power.
Simply adding a data atomic formulas to
\textrm{MSO}[<]
leads to undecidability
[
15
,
2
]
, even for the first-order (FO) fragment with three variables and the simplest variant of atoms
\mathbb{A}_{=}
: one may encode grid-like structures of arbitrary dimension in data words and simulate runs of Turing machines on them.
Conversely, restricting the logic too aggressively (e.g., to rigid guards, or to the two-variable FO fragment) yields decidability but fails to capture the full expressive power of NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}

[
8
,
4
,
2
]
.
The lack of closure of NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
under language complement
[
14
]
poses yet another challenge—any logic of the same expressive power must impose limitations on negation operation.

Expressions for NRAs.
Regarding expression formalisms, Brunet and Silva define expressions featuring explicit binders for name allocation and deallocation
[
6
]
and prove an analogue of the Kleene theorem: that these expressions define exactly languages of NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
.
Their formalism provides a versatile
specification
language, allowing concise and readable expressions at the cost of more involved definition of semantics.
We aim for a complementary goal: a
minimalist
expression formalism whose syntax is close in spirit to classical regular expressions.

Contributions.
In this paper, we provide two new formalisms for languages of data words over
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
: Scoped MSO logic (
\textrm{MSO}_{\textrm{S}}{[<,\Sigma,\mathbb{A}]}
) and Data-Regular Expressions (
{\mathrm{DRE}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
), recovering the classical trinity for NRAs:

\displaystyle\text{Automata (${\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]$)}\;\equiv\;\text{Expressions (${\mathrm{DRE}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]$)}\;\equiv\;\text{Logic ($\textrm{MSO}_{\textrm{S}}{[<,\Sigma,\mathbb{A}]}$)}\,.

Data-Regular Expressions:
We introduce a compact expression formalism over
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
.

The calculus relies on a parameterized operation on languages called
k

Preliminaries

We use standard notations to denote natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
, positive natural numbers
\mathbb{N}_{+}
or rationals
\mathbb{Q}
.
We denote ranges of natural numbers by
[{i},{j}]_{\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cap[i,j]
and
[{j}]_{\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq[{1},{j}]_{\mathbb{N}}
.

Words.
Fix an alphabet
\Sigma
and a word
w=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
.
We write
w[i]\coloneqq a_{i}
and, for an interval
I=[{i},{j}]_{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
w[I]
for the infix
a_{i}a_{i+1}\cdots a_{j}
.
The reversed word
a_{n}\cdots a_{1}
is denoted as
w^{R}
.
The length
n
of
w
is denoted as
\lvert w\rvert
, and the set of
positions
of
w
is
\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)=[{n}]_{\mathbb{N}}
.
The unique word of length
0
is called an
empty word
and denoted by
\varepsilon
.
We identify the set of families of words
(Y^{*})^{X}
with the function space
X\to Y^{*}
.
Accordingly, we introduce families as mappings
\bm{u}\colon X\to Y^{*}
but retain the subscript notation
u_{x}
for components.
We lift concatenation and infix extraction operations to families of words.

Word structures.

Given a
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, a
word structure

\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
is the relational structure defined as

\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)=(\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right),<,({\sigma})_{\sigma\in\Sigma})
where “
<
” is interpreted as the natural linear ordering on
\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
,
and for each letter
\sigma\in\Sigma
we have a unary relation symbol
\sigma=\left\{p\in\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}w[p]=\sigma\right\}
interpreted the set of positions in
w
labelled with
\sigma
.
While we use
\sigma
both for letters of
\Sigma
and position predicates, the intended meaning is clear from the context.

2.1
Formalisms for regular languages

This paper’s starting point is the equivalence of the following three formalisms defining regular languages.
Fix a finite alphabet
\Sigma
.

Nondeterministic finite automata.
A
Nondeterministic Finite Automaton
(NFA) is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,Q_{\mathrm{ini}},Q_{\mathrm{fin}},\delta)
, where
Q
is a finite set of states,
Q_{\mathrm{ini}}
and
Q_{\mathrm{fin}}
its initial and accepting subsets, and
\delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q
.
We call the elements of
\delta

transitions
and denote
(q,a,r)\in\delta
using the arrow notation
q\xrightarrow{a}r
.
A
run
of
\mathcal{A}
of length
n
over
w=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}
is a
w
-labelled path
\pi=t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n}\in\delta^{*}
in the labelled graph
(Q,\delta)
, i.e., a sequence such that
t_{i}
has the form
(\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu,a_{i},\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu)
for every
i
, and for any two consecutive transitions
p\xrightarrow{a}q
and
r\xrightarrow{b}s
we have
q=r
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}

accepts

w\in\Sigma^{*}
if there exists a run of
\mathcal{A}
over
w
that begins in some initial state
q_{\mathrm{ini}}\in Q_{\mathrm{ini}}
and ends in an accepting state
q_{\mathrm{fin}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fin}}
.
The language of
\mathcal{A}
is
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\left\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\text{$\mathcal{A}$ accepts $w$}\right\}
.
We write
{\mathrm{NFA}}\left[\Sigma\right]
for the family of all NFA over
\Sigma
.

Regular expressions.

Regular expressions
over
\Sigma
(
{\mathrm{RE}}\left[\Sigma\right]
) are terms generated by the grammar

\displaystyle E,F\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{\equiv}}\emptyset\mid\epsilon\mid a\in\Sigma\mid E+F\mid E\cdot F\mid E^{*}\,.

The language
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E\right)
of an expression
E
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle=\emptyset

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(a\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{a\right\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E+F\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E\right)\cup\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(F\right)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E\cdot F\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E\right)\cdot\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(F\right)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E\right)^{n}

where the language concatenation is
K\cdot L\coloneqq\left\{uv\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}u\in K,v\in L\right\}
for
K,L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, and
K^{n}
denotes
n
-fold concatenation of
K
with itself;
K^{0}\coloneqq\left\{\varepsilon\right\}
.

Theorem 1

(Kleene
[
13
]
)
.

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left({\mathrm{NFA}}\left[\Sigma\right]\right)=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left({\mathrm{RE}}\left[\Sigma\right]\right)
for any finite
\Sigma
.

Syntax of MSO.

The syntax of
Monadic Second Order Logic over Word Structures
(
\textrm{MSO}[<,\Sigma]
) features first-order variables
x,y,\ldots
, second-order variables
X,Y,\ldots
and is given by the grammar

\displaystyle\varphi,\psi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{\equiv}}{}

\displaystyle{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux\;}\varphi\mid{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muX\;}\varphi\mid\varphi\land\psi\mid\varphi\lor\psi\mid\neg\varphi\mid x<y\mid X(x)\mid a(x)\,,

where
a\in\Sigma
.
We write
\varphi\in\textrm{MSO}[<,\Sigma]
when
\varphi
is generated by the above grammar.
A variable in
\varphi
is free, if it is not bound by any quantifier.
We write
\operatorname{\mathrm{free}}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)
(or
\operatorname{\mathrm{free}_{1}\mkern-2.0mu}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)
) for the set of free variables (of first-order variables, respectively) of
\varphi
.
A formula is a
sentence
, if it has no free variables.

Semantics of MSO.
The truth value of
\textrm{MSO}[<,\Sigma]
formulas depends on the interpretation
\mathfrak{I}=(\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right),\nu)
consisting of the word structure
\mathbb{W}=\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
and valuation
\nu\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{free}}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)\to\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\cup\operatorname{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\left(\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\right)
.
Boolean connectives and atomic formulas (
x<y,X(y),a(x)
) are interpreted in a standard way over
\mathbb{W}
.
In particular,
\mathfrak{I}\models a(x)
iff
\nu(x)\in a^{\mathbb{W}}
.
Quantifiers range over
\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
:

•

\mathfrak{I}\models{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux\;}\varphi
iff there is a position
p\in\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right),\nu[x\mapsto p]\models\varphi
,

•

\mathfrak{I}\models{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muX\;}\varphi
iff there is a set
P\subseteq\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right),\nu[X\mapsto P]\models\varphi
.

For a sentence
\varphi\in\textrm{MSO}[<,\Sigma]
, we write
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\models\varphi
whenever
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right),\nu_{0}\models\varphi
, where
\nu_{0}
is the empty valuation.
The language of
\varphi
is defined as
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)=\left\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\models\varphi\right\}
.

Theorem 2

(Büchi, Elgot, Trakhtenbrot
[
7
,
11
,
18
]
)
.

For every finite
\Sigma
, the languages definable by
\textrm{MSO}[<,\Sigma]
are exactly the regular languages over

.

Syntactic sugar.
We use
{{\forall}\mskip 1.5mu\xi\;}\varphi
as a shortcut for
\neg{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mu\xi\;}(\neg\varphi)
; position equality is defined as
x=y\equiv\neg(x<y\lor y<x)
; Boolean constants can be defined as
\top\equiv{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux\;}x=x\lor\neg{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux\;}x=x
and
\bot\equiv\neg\top
.
Additionally, we add easily MSO-definable relations like subset relation
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
.

2.2
Words over infinite alphabets

While the definitions of a word and a language do not require
finiteness of the alphabet
, all the basic language-defining formalisms we have recalled in
section

2.1
critically depend on that assumption.
For instance, a finite automaton for the language
\Sigma
consisting of one-letter words has to contain a transition for every
a\in\Sigma
, and making
\Sigma
infinite would cause the transition relation to become infinite as well.
One solution to this problem is to add more structure to the alphabet and allow the model to make less precise queries about the letters.
This gives rise to the realm of
atoms
and
register automata
.

Atoms.
We call atoms any relational structure
\mathbb{A}=(U,R_{1},\ldots,R_{\ell})
with a countably infinite universe
U
and a finite number of relational symbols in its signature.
We assume that that one of
R_{i}
is the equality of universe elements.
We deliberately confuse
\mathbb{A}
and its universe
U
and write
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
.

Data words.
Data words are words over a
two-track
alphabet
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
, consisting of a finite label from
\Sigma
and a data value from
\mathbb{A}
.
It is often convenient to decompose multi-track words into separate tracks using
convolution
.
For alphabets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
, the convolution of words
w_{1}\in A_{1}^{*},\dots,w_{k}\in A_{k}^{*}
of equal length is the word
\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\right)\in(A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{k})^{*}
defined by
\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\right)[i]\coloneqq(w_{1}[i],\dots,w_{k}[i])
.
Thus, a data word can be viewed as the convolution of a label word over
\Sigma
and a data sequence over
\mathbb{A}
.

.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(\mathbb{N},=)
and
\Sigma=\left\{a,b\right\}
.
A two-track word
\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right)
over
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
is a convolution of two tracks
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
\varpi\in\mathbb{A}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w&=\text{abaab}\\[-2.84526pt]
\varpi&=10,42,5,97,42\end{aligned}

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\text{a}\\

Quantifier-free formulas and regions over
\bm{\mathbb{A}}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite set of variables.
The set of
atomic formulas
over
\mathbb{A}
using variables
\mathcal{X}
consists of all expressions

A

over
\mathbb{A}
using variables
\mathcal{X}
is a Boolean combination of atomic formulas:

\displaystyle\varPhi,\Psi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{\equiv}}\top\mid\neg\varPhi\mid\varPhi\land\Psi\mid R(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\,.

The formulas are interpreted in a standard way over a pair

, where
\nu\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{A}
.

For a word

of

, denoted

, is the set of all literals (atomic formulas or their negations) using variables

that are satisfied when variables are interpreted as data values in

. Formally,
\operatorname{\mathrm{type}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)\coloneqq\left\{\phi\in\text{Lit}(\mathcal{X}_{k})\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathbb{A},\nu\models\phi\right\}
where
\nu(x_{i})=a_{i}
.

of a word

, is the set of all words that have the same quantifier-free type as

.
Intuitively, words in
\operatorname{\mathrm{region}}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
are not distinguishable from

by quantifier free formulas.
We lift
\operatorname{\mathrm{region}}_{\mathbb{A}}
to data words in a natural way.
It is easy to see that
\mathbb{A}^{k}
is a finite union of quantifier-free regions for any

is the expansion of

has arity

2.3
Register automata—a formalism for data word languages

One of the common formalisms for defining languages of data words is a nondeterministic register automaton (
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
).
Fix atoms
\mathbb{A}=(U,R_{1},\ldots,R_{\ell})
and a finite
\mathcal{R}
whose elements we call
registers
.
Let
k\coloneqq\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert
and let
\mathbb{A}_{\textup{nil}}\coloneqq\mathbb{A}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\left\{\underline{\textup{nil}}\right\}
, where
nil
is a fresh element modelling an empty register, and the signature of
\mathbb{A}_{\textup{nil}}
is extended with a unary symbol
nil
interpreted as
\left\{\underline{\textup{nil}}\right\}
.
A register valuation is a function
\mu\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{\textup{nil}}
.

Register constraints.
Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{R}}\coloneqq\bigcup_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\big\{\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}},\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}\big\}
be the variables representing register values before and after a transition, and let
\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangledown$}}{y}}
represent the current input symbol.
A
register constraint

\varPhi
is a quantifier-free formula over
\mathbb{A}_{\textup{nil}}
with the variable set
\left\{\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangledown$}}{y}}\right\}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
We denote the set of all register constraints for
\mathcal{R}
as
\operatorname{\mathrm{constr}}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)
.
To evaluate a constraint, consider two valuations
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{A}\cup\{\textup{nil}\}
and an input symbol
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
.
These induce a combined valuation
\nu
on the variables
\left\{\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangledown$}}{y}}\right\}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{R}}
defined by
\nu(\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}})=\mu(r)
,
\nu(\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}})=\mu^{\prime}(r)
, and
\nu(\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangledown$}}{y}})=\alpha
.

Register automata.
A
nondeterministic register automaton with guessing
(
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
) is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathbb{A},\mathcal{R},Q_{\mathrm{ini}},Q_{\mathrm{fin}},\delta)
, comprising a finite set of states
Q
, a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, initial states
Q_{\mathrm{ini}}\subset Q
, accepting states
Q_{\mathrm{fin}}\subseteq Q
and a set of
transition rules

\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\times\operatorname{\mathrm{constr}}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{R}\right)\times Q
.
A transition rule
(p,\sigma,\varPhi,q)\in\delta
is written using double arrow notation
p\xRightarrow{\sigma,\varPhi}q
.
The semantics is defined by an infinite transition system
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket\coloneqq(C_{\mathcal{A}},\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
over configurations
C_{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq Q\times(\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{\textup{nil}})
, where
\Delta\subseteq C_{\mathcal{A}}\times\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}\times C_{\mathcal{A}}
.
A transition
(p,\mu)\xrightarrow{\sigma,\alpha}(q,\mu^{\prime})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
exists iff there is a rule
p\xRightarrow{\sigma,\varphi}q
in
\delta
such that
\mathbb{A},\nu(\mu,\alpha,\mu^{\prime})\models\varPhi
.
A configuration
(q,\mu)
is initial if
q\in Q_{\mathrm{ini}}
and
\mu(r)=\textup{nil}
for every
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
A configuration
(q,\mu)
is
accepting
when
q\in Q_{\mathrm{fin}}
.
The notion of a run is analogous as in NFA; it is accepting if it starts in some initial configuration and ends in some accepting one.
The language
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
consists of data words labelling runs of
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket
.
Every run
\pi
of
\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket
has an associated sequence
t_{1}\cdots t_{n}\in\delta^{*}
of transition rules.
We denote the family of
k
-register NRA over
\Sigma\times\mathbb{A}
by
{\mathrm{NRA}}_{k}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
.

Variants of nondeterminism in register automata.
Fix
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}_{k}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
and its run
\pi=(q_{0},\mu_{0})\xrightarrow{\sigma_{1},\alpha_{1}}(q_{1},\mu_{1})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(q_{n-1},\mu_{n-1})\xrightarrow{\sigma_{n},\alpha_{n}}(q_{n},\mu_{n})
over
w\in(A\times\Sigma)^{n}
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
guessing
the value of
j
th register in valuation
\mu_{a}
, if
\xi=\mu_{a}(r_{j})
is different from all previous register values
\mu_{a-1}(\mathcal{R})
and from the data value
\alpha_{a}
.
The guess can be either
weak
or
strong
, depending on the run from configuration
(q_{a},\mu_{a})
th onwards.
We say that the
live interval
of
\xi
is
[{a},{b}]_{\mathbb{N}}
whenever
\xi\in\mu_{m}(\mathcal{R})
for all
m\in[{a},{b}]_{\mathbb{N}}
and
\xi\notin\mu_{b+1}(\mathcal{R})
.
The guess of the value of
j
th register in valuation
\mu_{a}
is strong whenever
\alpha_{m}\neq\xi
for every
m
in the live interval
[{i},{j}]_{\mathbb{N}}
.

We distinguish two restricted subclasses of
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
:

weakly-guessing nondeterministic register automata
(
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{w}}}
) and
deterministic register automata without guessing
(
{\mathrm{NRA}}
), which arise as syntactic limitations on the transition constraints
\varPhi
and the determinism of
\delta
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
is weakly guessing, if
\mathcal{A}
is not guessing strongly in its accepting runs.
It is without guessing, if it is never guessing a value in any of its runs.

Definition 4

(Elimination of strong guessing.)
.

We say that atoms
\mathbb{A}
have elimination of strong guessing, whenever for every
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
there exists a weakly guessing
\mathcal{B}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
that recognises the same language.

of transition rules.
By construction, it belongs to
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E_{\mathcal{B}}\right)
, and for every

\displaystyle t_{i}=(q_{i-1}\xRightarrow{\sigma_{i},\varPhi_{i}}q_{i})\quad\text{with}\quad\nu(\mu_{i-1},\alpha_{i},\mu_{i})\models\varPhi_{i}.

Consider, for each

, the

-block

\displaystyle B_{i}\coloneqq\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(\#^{k}\,\sigma_{i}\,\#^{k},\ \ \mu_{i-1}(r_{k})\cdots\mu_{i-1}(r_{1})\ \alpha_{i}\ \mu_{i}(r_{1})\cdots\mu_{i}(r_{k})\right)\,.

.
Moreover, the

-contracting concatenation precisely matches post- and pre-valuations:

symbols of

are

\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left((\#^{k},\mu_{i}(r_{1})\cdots\mu_{i}(r_{k}))\right)
,
which, after reversal, equals the first

.
Hence the word

A direct computation of

-contractions yields

for some
\varpi^{\prime},\varpi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{A}^{k}
.

Direction
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(E(\mathcal{A})\right)\subseteq\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

Let

.
By the semantics of
\cdot_{k}
, there exist words

and

such that

\displaystyle W=u_{0}^{R}a_{1}u_{1}\ \ \cdot_{k}\ \ u_{1}^{R}a_{2}u_{2}\ \ \cdot_{k}\ \ u_{2}^{R}\cdots u_{n-1}\ \ \cdot_{k}\ \ u_{n-1}^{R}a_{n}u_{n}

for

and some sequence
t_{1}\cdots t_{n}\in\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
of transition rules beginning in some initial

and ending in some accepting

Each block

determines (by its data track) a triple of register valuations and a current datum

(\mu_{i-1},\alpha_{i},\mu_{i})
such that

for the constraint of

\varphi_{0}
and an interpretation

\mathfrak{I}=(\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu,I)
.
Fix a family of super-scopes
(I_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}(\varphi)}
such that

I\subseteq I_{x}
and
\nu(y)\in I_{x}
for every

x\in\mathcal{X}(\varphi)
and
y\in\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortdownarrow}\cap\operatorname{\mathrm{free}_{1}\mkern-2.0mu}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)
.
We proceed by induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
There are ten main cases, corresponding to the grammar rules of
\textrm{MSO}_{\textrm{S}}{[<,\Sigma,\mathbb{A}]}
.
In each case we prove

items

(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Case 1
(
\varphi\equiv R(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r})
)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}\right\}
.
Recall that
\lvert\mathcal{V}\cap\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortdownarrow}\rvert\leq 1
.

Case 1A
(
\mathcal{V}\cap\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortdownarrow}=\left\{z\right\}
for some
z
)
.

Let
p=\nu(z)
.

(C1)

Assume
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.
By definition,
(\varpi[\nu(x_{1})],\ldots,\varpi[\nu(x_{r})])\in R
.
Note that
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
.
Let
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
be the unique empty families.
Then
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
holds vacuously.
Take any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
.
These families are trivially consistent with
\bm{\check{u}}
and
\bm{\check{v}}
on
I
.
It remains to show that, for every
i
, the term
t_{i,z}
in
G(\varphi,S)

evaluates to
\varpi[\nu(x_{i})]
.
This is immediate when
x_{i}\equiv z
.
Otherwise,
t_{i,z}
evaluates to
v_{x_{i}}[p]
.
Since
p\in I_{x_{i}}
, property
P_{\mathcal{X}}
yields

v_{x_{i}}[p]=\varpi[\nu(x_{i})]
, as required.

(C2)

Fix
\bm{u},\bm{v}
such that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
,

P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
, and
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.
As in
item

(C2)
, we obtain

\varpi[\nu(x_{i})]=v_{x_{i}}[p]
for each
i
, and hence
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.

Case 1B
(
\mathcal{V}\cap\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortdownarrow}=\emptyset
)
.

(C1)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.
Let
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
be the unique empty infix families indexed by

\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)
, and let
\bm{u},\bm{v}
be any word families that are consistent
with them on
I
and satisfy
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
.
Then
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
holds vacuously.
We must show that

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muz\;}\,(z\in S\land R(t_{1,z},\ldots,t_{r,z}))
.
Choose any
p\in I
(recall that
I\neq\emptyset
).
By the same argument as in Case 1A, we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[z\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I]\models z\in S\land R(t_{1,z},\ldots,t_{r,z})
.
Hence
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.

(C2)

Fix
\bm{u},\bm{v}
such that
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
and the
properties
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and

P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
hold.
By definition, there exists
p\in I
such that

\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[z\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I]\models R(t_{1,z},\ldots,t_{r,z})
.
By the same argument as in Case 1A, we conclude that
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.

Case 2
(
\varphi\equiv x<y
)
.

Immediate.

(C1)

If
\mathfrak{I}\models x<y
, then
\nu(x)<\nu(y)
, and thus

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models x<y
for any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with the unique
trivial infix families
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
indexed by
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
.

(C2)

If
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models x<y
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
, then
\mathfrak{I}\models x<y
.

Case 3
(
\varphi\equiv a(x)
)
.

Immediate.

(C1)

If
\mathfrak{I}\models a(x)
, then
w[\nu(x)]=a
, and thus

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models w[x]=a
for any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with the
unique trivial infix families
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
indexed by
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
.

(C2)

If
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models w[x]=a
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
, then
\mathfrak{I}\models a(x)
.

Case 4
(
\varphi\equiv X(y)
)
.

Immediate.

(C1)

If
\mathfrak{I}\models X(y)
, then
\nu(y)\in\nu(X)
, and thus

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models X(y)
for any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with the unique
trivial infix families
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
indexed by
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
.

(C2)

If
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models X(y)
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
, then
\mathfrak{I}\models X(y)
.

Case 5
(
\varphi\equiv\psi\land\gamma
)
.

Note that
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\mathcal{Y}(\psi)\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\mathcal{Y}(\gamma)
.

(C1)

Assume
\mathfrak{I}\models\psi\land\gamma
. Then
\mathfrak{I}\models\psi
and

\mathfrak{I}\models\gamma
.
By
item

(C1)
of the induction hypothesis, there exist infix
families
\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}})
such that, for
every
\bm{u^{\prime}},\bm{v^{\prime}}
consistent with them on
I
and satisfying

P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u^{\prime}},\bm{v^{\prime}})
, we have
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u^{\prime}},\bm{v^{\prime}}}\models G(\psi,S)
.
Likewise, there exist
\bm{\check{u}^{\prime\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime\prime}}
witnessing the analogous statement
for
G(\gamma,S)
.
Let
\bm{\check{u}}=\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\bm{\check{u}^{\prime\prime}}
and
\bm{\check{v}}=\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\bm{\check{v}^{\prime\prime}}
.
Then
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
holds.
Take any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and consistent with

\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
on
I
.
Since
\bm{u},\bm{v}
are also consistent with
\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}}
on
I
, and satisfy

P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
, we obtain

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\psi,S)
.
Similarly,
\bm{u},\bm{v}
are consistent with
\bm{\check{u}^{\prime\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime\prime}}
on
I
and satisfy

P_{\mathcal{X}}(\gamma,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\gamma,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
, so

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\gamma,S)
.
Hence
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.

(C2)

Let
\bm{u},\bm{v}
be such that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and

P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
hold and
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.
Then
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\psi,S)
and
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\gamma,S)
.
Moreover,
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
hold, and
similarly for
\gamma
.
By
item

(C2)
of the induction hypothesis, we have

\mathfrak{I}\models\psi
and
\mathfrak{I}\models\gamma
, hence
\mathfrak{I}\models\psi\land\gamma
.

Case 6
(
\varphi\equiv\psi\lor\gamma
)
.

The proof is analogous to Case 5, with
\land
replaced by
\lor
.

Case 7
(
\varphi\equiv\neg\psi
)
.

Observe that
\mathcal{Y}(\psi)=\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
, since no top-level
variables can be quantified under negation.

(C1)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}\models\neg\psi
.
Let
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
be the unique empty infix families; then

P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
holds vacuously.
Towards a contradiction, suppose that

\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\psi,S)
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
satisfying

P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and trivially consistent with
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
.
Then
item

(C2)
yields
\mathfrak{I}\models\psi
, contradicting the
assumption.

(C2)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models\neg G(\psi,S)
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
such
that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
hold.
Towards a contradiction, suppose that
\mathfrak{I}\models\psi
.
By
item

(C1)
of the induction hypothesis, for the unique
empty families
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
(which are consistent with
\bm{u},\bm{v}
), we would
have
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\psi,S)
, contradicting the assumption.

Case 8
(
\varphi\equiv{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muX\;}\psi
)
.

Immediate.

(C1)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}\models{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muX\;}\psi
.
Then there exists
P\subseteq I
such that

\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[X\leftarrow P],I\models\psi
.
By
item

(C1)
of the induction hypothesis, there are some infix families
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
such that for every
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with them on
I
and satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
, satisfaction relation
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[X\leftarrow P,S\leftarrow I]\models G(\psi,S)
holds.
We reuse
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
; fix arbitrary
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with them on
I
and such that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
.
Observe that
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
and
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
hold, and therefore
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[X\leftarrow P,S\leftarrow I]\models X\subseteq S
.
By definition,
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
holds.

(C2)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)\equiv{{\exists}\mskip 1.5muX.\thinspace\;}X\subseteq S\land G(\psi,S)
for some
\bm{u},\bm{v}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
.
By definition, there is some
P\subseteq\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[X\leftarrow P,S\leftarrow I]\models X\subseteq S\land G(\psi,S)
.
It is easy to see that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
.
Observe that
P\subseteq I
.
By
item

(C2)
of the induction hypothesis we get
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[X\leftarrow P],I\models\psi
, and, consequently,
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.

Case 9
(
\varphi\equiv{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux\;}\psi
)
.

Case 9A
(
x\not\in\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortuparrow}
)
.

Immediate.
Note that
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\emptyset
, otherwise
x
would be top-level.

(C1)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
; by definition there is
p\in I
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p],I\models\psi
.
By
item

(C1)
of the induction hypothesis, there are
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
such that for every
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with them on
I
and satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
the satisfaction relation
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I]\models G(\psi,S)
holds, so by definition
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
; this is the required claim.

(C2)

Fix
\bm{u},\bm{v}
such that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
and
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.
By definition, there is some
p\in\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I]\models x\in S\land G(\psi,S)
.
Note that this implies
p\in I
.
By
item

(C2)
of the induction hypothesis, we get that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p],I\models\psi
, and by definition,
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
, as required.

Case 9B
(
x\in\,\mathcal{U}_{\shortuparrow}
)
.

Note
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\mathcal{Y}(\psi)\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\left\{x\right\}
.
Here,
G(\varphi,S)\equiv{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mux.\thinspace\;}x\in S\land G(\psi,S)\land\theta_{x,S}
.

(C1)

Assume that
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.
Then there exists
p\in I
such that
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p],I\models\psi
.
By
item

(C1)
of the induction hypothesis with
I_{x}=I
, fix
\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}},\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}})
such that for all
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with them on
I
and satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
, we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I]\models G(\psi,S)
.
Let
\bm{\check{u}}=\bm{\check{u}^{\prime}}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\left\{u_{x}\mapsto\mathord{\raisebox{-0.3pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\bullet$}}}\mathord{\raisebox{-0.3pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\circ$}}}^{\lvert I\rvert-1}\right\}
and

\bm{\check{v}}=\bm{\check{v}^{\prime}}\mathbin{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\cup$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{0.35558pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}\left\{v_{x}\mapsto\varpi[p]^{\lvert I\rvert}\right\}
.
Then
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
holds.
Fix any
\bm{u},\bm{v}
consistent with
\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
.
Let
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}=(\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I])
.
By construction of the infix tracks
\check{u}_{x},\check{v}_{x}
, properties
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{\check{u}},\bm{\check{v}})
hold as well.
Hence
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\psi,S)
.
Moreover, by the choice of
\check{u}_{x}
and
\check{v}_{x}
, we also have
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models\theta_{x,S}
.
Therefore
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
.

(C2)

Fix
\bm{u},\bm{v}
such that
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\varphi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
,
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\varphi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
and
\mathfrak{I}_{\bm{u},\bm{v}}\models G(\varphi,S)
hold.
Let
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v},p}=(\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\bm{u},\varpi,\bm{v}\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p,S\leftarrow I])
.
By definition, there exists
p\in\operatorname{\mathrm{pos}}\mathopen{}\left(w\right)
such that
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v},p}\models x\in S\land G(\psi,S)\land\theta_{x,S}
.
In particular
p\in S
.
Since
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{\bm{u},\bm{v},p}\models\theta_{x,S}
, we have
v_{x}[p]=\varpi[p]
and
u_{x}[I]\in\mathord{\raisebox{-0.3pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\bullet$}}}\mathord{\raisebox{-0.3pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\circ$}}}^{*}
.
Using the assumption
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I]\right)\models\eta_{x}
, a simple induction shows that
v_{x}[q]=\varpi[p]
for every
q\in I
.
Hence
P_{\mathcal{X}}(\psi,\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
P_{\mathcal{Y}}(\psi,\bm{u}[I],\bm{v}[I])
hold.
By
item

(C2)
of the induction hypothesis, we obtain
\operatorname{\mathbb{W}}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right),\nu[x\leftarrow p],I\models\psi
. Since
p\in I
, we conclude that
\mathfrak{I}\models\varphi
.

B.2
From automata to formulas

In this section we prove the remaining direction of
theorem

9
, as formalised by the following:

Lemma 26
.

Fix finite
\Sigma
and atoms
\mathbb{A}
.
For every weakly guessing
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
there exists a formula
\varphi\in\textrm{MSO}_{\textrm{S}}{[<,\Sigma,\mathbb{A}]}
such that
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\varphi\right)
.

Proof roadmap.
We split the argument into:

•

an introduction of simple
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
, a subclass equally expressive as full
{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
,

•

a standard MSO encoding of a run skeleton, and

•

a geometric recursion guided by the live intervals of registers that allows us to check all register constraints in
\textrm{MSO}_{\textrm{S}}{[<,\Sigma,\mathbb{A}]}
, making use of the

{\exists}

modality.

Simple NRAs.
We first define a class of
simple NRA
which enforces injective register valuations and no register shuffling.
This is a well-known construction already present in literature concerning other kinds of register machines
[
10
, Thm. 1]
, and can be adapted to NRA
{}^{\!\textup{{g}}}
in a straightforward way. Therefore, we state the lemma without the proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathbb{A},R,Q_{\mathrm{ini}},Q_{\mathrm{fin}},\delta)\in{\mathrm{NRA}}_{k}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]
.
We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
simple
, if every transition rule
(p,\sigma,\varPhi,q)\in\delta
has a register constraint of the form
\varPhi\equiv\varPhi^{\prime}\land\textstyle\bigwedge_{i\neq j}\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}}_{j}\neq\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}}_{i}\neq\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}_{j}\neq\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}_{i}\land\bigwedge_{i}\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}}_{i}\mathbin{\sim_{i}}\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}_{i}
where
{\sim_{i}}\in\left\{=,\neq\right\}
.

Intuitively, a simple NRA does not
store the same data value in two different registers at the same time,
and never moves a data value between registers, and explicitly says whether
\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}}_{i}=\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}_{i}
or
\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleleft$}}{r}}_{i}\neq\smash{\overset{\raisebox{-1.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\triangleright$}}{r}}_{i}
for every
i
.

For every
\mathcal{A}\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is so, too.

For the remainder of the section, let us fix
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\mathbb{A},\mathcal{R},Q_{\mathrm{ini}},Q_{\mathrm{fin}},\delta)\in{\mathrm{NRA}}^{\!\textup{{g}}}\left[\Sigma,\mathbb{A}\right]

Fix an enumeration of
\mathcal{R}=\left\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\right\}

Run skeleton.
Let
m=\lvert\delta\rvert
and fix an enumeration of transitions

We introduce second-order variables

•

We specify these properties in a standard way:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Run}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\ldots,\Delta_{m})\coloneqq{{\forall}\mskip 1.5mui,j.\thinspace\;}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!\!\!\underbrace{\Big[\textstyle\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=(\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu,\sigma,\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu,\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu)\in\delta\end{subarray}}\Delta_{\kappa(t)}(i)\land\sigma(i)\land\textstyle\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}\in\delta\\

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!\!\!\underbrace{\Big[\makebox[42.67912pt][r]{$\operatorname{\mathrm{succ}}\mathopen{}\left(i,j\right)$}\rightarrow\textstyle\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=(q,\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu,\mkern 2.0mu\cdot\mkern 2.0mu,r)\in\delta\\

\displaystyle\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right)\models{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mu\Delta_{1}\;}\cdots{{\exists}\mskip 1.5mu\Delta_{m}\;}\Big(\mathrm{Run}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{m})\land\mathrm{RegOK}(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{m})\Big)\text{ iff }\operatorname{\otimes}\mathopen{}\left(w,\varpi\right)\in\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

This would be straightforward if we had unrestricted access to the universal quantifier.

\displaystyle J(I_{x},I_{y})\;\coloneqq\;I_{x}\cup I_{y}\;=\;[{\min I_{x}},{\max I_{y}}]_{\mathbb{N}}.

(Alternating disjointness)
.

and
\min I_{y}^{i}
