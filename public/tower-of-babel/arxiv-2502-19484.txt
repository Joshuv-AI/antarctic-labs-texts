A Tentative Detection of Molecular Hydrogen (H 2 ) Emission Lines at Cosmic Dawn
Madisen Johnson, Blakesley Burkhart, Francesco D’Eugenio, Jacques Le Bourlot, Shmuel Bialy, Sandro Tacchella, Roberto Maiolino, Evelyne Roueff, Franck Le Petit, Emeric Bron, Hervé Abgrall, Erica Nelson, Shyam Menon, Matthew E. Orr

Abstract
Using data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES), we tentatively detect two molecular hydrogen (H 2 ) fluorescent emission features in high-redshift galaxies at 2.3 and 3.1 σ \sigma . These features consist of many blended emission lines that result from the de-excitation cascade of H 2 molecules that have absorbed Lyman-Werner band photons. Our study targets galaxies at redshifts z ≥ 7 z\geq 7 galaxies of the early Universe, as they host some of the most extreme conditions in terms of star formation, molecular gas content, and the possible presence of outflows driven by starbursts and active galactic nuclei. To enhance the signal-to-noise ratio of H 2 emission features in the rest-frame wavelength range of 155-191 nm, we stack JWST/NIRSpec spectra from z = 7.0 − 13.4 z=7.0-13.4 . These stacked spectra also exhibit atomic emission features, such as CIV emission with a P-Cygni profile, as well as CIII], OIII], and HeII. The presence of these features and the slightly blue-shifted fluorescent H 2 lines suggest active multiphase atomic and molecular outflows may be common at these redshifts. Our results suggest that it is possible to search for FUV fluorescent H 2 lines in high redshift galaxies using JWST, which would enable the characterization of interstellar radiation fields, densities, and temperatures in extreme photodissociation environments.



I
Introduction

Molecular hydrogen (H
2
), the most abundant molecule in the Universe, plays a crucial role in the baryonic lifecycle throughout cosmic history. H
2
serves as a vital coolant during the Universe’s early stages
(
Bromm 2013
;
Klessen 2019
;
Bialy & Sternberg 2019
;
Tacconi et al. 2020
;
Nakazato et al. 2022
)
and is often essential for star and planet formation processes
(
Chevance et al. 2022
)
. Additionally, H
2
is critical for forming complex molecules such as CO, OH, HCN, and H
2
O, which cool dense gas
(
Herbst & Klemperer 1973
;
Sternberg & Dalgarno 1995
;
Tielens 2013
;
Bialy & Sternberg 2015
, e.g.,)
. The inferred H
2
mass in galaxies strongly correlates with star formation rates and is a key diagnostic for star formation efficiency and gas depletion timescales
(
Lada et al. 2012
;
Krumholz 2014
)
.

Direct detection of H
2
is challenging because it is a symmetrical molecule without a dipole moment. Therefore, only weak electric quadrupole emission may take place from its first excited rotational state,
J=2
, that requires temperatures of
T=511
K, while most star-forming clouds have temperatures around
T\sim 10
K. Consequently, H
2
is observable in warmer regions through infrared emissions, such as those captured by JWST MIRI-MRS at low-redshift. However, detecting colder H
2
typically requires relying on less abundant proxies, such as CO, which emits brightly at low temperatures. The CO
J=1\rightarrow 0
transition, with a critical temperature of 5.5 K, is often used to estimate H
2
masses via a conversion factor (
X_{CO}
,
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Sandstrom et al. 2013
;
Correia et al. 2014
;
Hu et al. 2022
;
Ganguly et al. 2022
). However, variations in
X_{CO}
due to metallicity, opacity, and cosmic ray ionization rates introduce significant uncertainties, especially in extreme environments and at high redshifts
(
Narayanan et al. 2011
;
Gaches et al. 2019
;
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Imara & Burkhart 2016
;
Pingel et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Seifried et al. 2020
;
Borchert et al. 2022
)
.

Detecting CO at
z>4
is further complicated by its faintness and the observational preference for higher-J transitions, increasing uncertainties in
X_{CO}
. Alternative tracers, such as CII, CI, and dust continuum emission, have been used to estimate molecular gas mass at high redshifts
(
Walter et al. 2003
;
Bolatto et al. 2013
;
Venemans et al. 2017
;
Tacconi et al. 2020
;
Ono et al. 2022
;
Hashimoto et al. 2023
;
Jones et al. 2023
)
. Notably,
Novak et al. 2019
reported stacked CO observations at
z=7.5
using ALMA.

Direct detection of H
2
is feasible through fluorescence and collisional excitation (e.g., shock-excited warm H
2
emitting in the IR). Relevant to this work, H
2
absorbs far-ultraviolet (FUV) photons in the Lyman-Werner band (912–1110 Å), exciting the molecule to electronic states (
B^{1}\Sigma^{+}_{u}
,
C^{1}\Pi_{u}
). Subsequent de-excitation produces FUV fluorescent emission lines, with approximately 85% of transitions leading to a vibrational-rotational cascade
(
Stephens & Dalgarno 1972
;
Abgrall et al. 2000
)
. These FUV intensities depend on gas density, UV radiation intensity, molecular formation rates, and dust absorption cross-sections, while relative line intensities remain fairly insensitive to these parameters
(
Sternberg 1989
)
. Other excitation sources, such as cosmic rays, X-rays, and secondary electrons, can also induce fluorescent emission
(
Sternberg 1988
;
Tiné et al. 1997
;
Bialy 2020
;
Gaches et al. 2022
;
Padovani et al. 2024
)
.

At low redshifts, space-based telescopes or stratospheric balloons are required for FUV H
2
observations due to atmospheric absorption. FUV fluorescent H
2
has been detected in nearby molecular clouds, superbubbles, and emission nebulae
(
France et al. 2004
;
France & McCandliss 2005
;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Ryu et al. 2006
;
Lim et al. 2015
;
Jo et al. 2011
;
Jo et al. 2015
;
Jo et al. 2017
)
. Proposed missions like Hyperion and Eos aim to study H
2
fluorescence in the Milky Way
(
Hamden et al. 2024
;
Hamden et al. 2022
)
.

At
z=6-13
, JWST’s infrared coverage (0.6–5 µm) enables observations of rest-frame FUV H
2
and ionized atomic lines. The JADES collaboration has reported high-redshift FUV and optical atomic lines
(
Tacchella et al. 2023
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
;
Robertson et al. 2023
;
Bunker et al. 2023
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
)
, complementing studies by other surveys such as GLASS
(
Treu et al. 2022
)
, CEERS
(
Finkelstein et al. 2023
)
, and UNCOVER
(
Bezanson et al. 2024
)
.

II
Data and Methodology

We utilize data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES), which provides extensive infrared imaging and spectroscopic observations in the GOODS-South (GOODS-S) and GOODS-North (GOODS-N) deep fields
(
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
;
Bunker et al. 2023
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
)
. This survey is designed to investigate galaxy evolution from the epoch of reionization to cosmic noon. JADES draws on approximately 770 hours of Cycle 1 guaranteed observation time from the Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) and the Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec), offering a rich dataset ideal for our analysis. All data used in this study are publicly available via the JADES website and the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST).

Our analysis specifically focuses on the publicly available JADES dataset, which includes medium-depth NIRSpec multi-object spectroscopy. This spectroscopy covers the wavelength range of 0.6 to 5.3
\mu
m using five dispersers. The dataset builds on the extensive legacy of the GOODS-S and GOODS-N fields, developed over the past 30 years, now further enhanced by the capabilities of JWST.

The NIRSpec spectroscopic data were reduced using the standard JWST pipeline. The reduction process included detector-level corrections (e.g., bias subtraction and flat-fielding), wavelength and flux calibration, and the extraction of one-dimensional spectra. Additional post-processing steps were applied to refine background subtraction and mitigate contamination from overlapping spectra in crowded regions. A detailed description of the data reduction procedure is provided in
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
.

The Meudon PDR code considers a stationary plane-parallel slab of
gas and dust illuminated by a radiation field extending from the ultraviolet
to the microwave (seen in Figure
1
), coming
from one or both sides (where the two intensities can be different).
The code then solves radiative transfer over the full wavelength range
at each point in the cloud, taking into account absorption in the
continuum by dust and gas and in the discrete transitions of
\mathrm{H}

and
\mathrm{H}_{2}
and possibly
\mathrm{CO}
and their isotopes.
The thermal balance is then computed, taking into account heating
processes such as the photoelectric effect on dust, chemistry, cosmic
rays, etc., and cooling from line emission of the abundant ions, atoms,
and/or molecules and coupling between gas and dust. Chemistry computation
is strongly coupled to radiative transfer and thermal balance since
reaction rates depend on the gas and grain temperatures, the UV flux
at each position, the charge of grains, etc. This chemistry computation
is then used to calculate the abundance of each species at each point.
This allows the program to calculate column densities, emissivities,
and intensities.

To attempt to detect molecular hydrogen fluorescence signatures, we began by stacking spectra from numerous high-redshift galaxies to enhance faint signals. This process involved shifting all galaxies to their rest frames and regridding the spectra to match the resolution of the JWST instruments. Each spectrum was normalized using its maximum intensity within the UV range (140-170 nm) before stacking. Stacking was performed by summing the flux values at each wavelength. The resulting stacked spectrum was then compared to our high redshift H
2
Meudon model, as described in the previous section.

We analyzed 71 galaxies (7 in GOODS-N and 64 in GOODS-S) from the JADES catalog with
z\geq 7
, yielding a mean redshift of 8.13, as shown in Figure
6
(spectra in black). In addition to the stacked spectra, we plotted the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) using a moving boxcar approach (middle panel) and examined the residuals between the observed data and the model (bottom panel).
We first note a significant P-Cygni-like profile of the CIV emission line, which we show a zoom-in in the Figure
6
bottom inset. This profile is indicative of outflowing material and corresponds to an average outflow velocity range of 100–500 km/s. Ionized atomic outflows have been shown to be common at redshifts around cosmic dawn, and this spectra provide additional evidence for the ubiquity of galactic outflows in the early universe.

We overplot the Meudon PDR model as a blue line in Figure
6
, and show a zoom-in around the most significant H
2
features (marked with vertical red lines) in the top inset. To achieve the best fit between the model and the stacked data, the H
2
model was blue-shifted by 0.15 nm, corresponding to an outflow velocity of approximately 300 km/s, which is in agreement with the observed outflow predicted by the P-Cygni-like CIV line.
This blueshift indicates that the observed H
2
emission is predominantly from gas moving toward the observer, consistent with an outflow scenario where the receding component is obscured, likely due to dust extinction. Far-ultraviolet (FUV) fluorescence lines of H
2
are particularly susceptible to dust attenuation, which may suppress signals from the receding side of the outflow.
While stacking enhances faint signals, some galaxies in the sample may not significantly contribute to the outflow signal due to orientation effects, such as an outflow perpendicular to the line of sight, where the projected radial velocity is minimal.

These limitations underscore the importance of follow-up analyses with individual spectra to disentangle contributions from different galaxies and confirm the presence of outflows. Previous studies, such as those investigating [CII] outflows
(
Stanley et al. 2019
)
, have employed stacking to identify outflows, supporting the validity of this approach.

V
Discussion

V.1
Direct Molecular Gas Observations at Cosmic Dawn

This paper presents the first tentative direct detection of H
2
fluorescent emission features at high redshifts, and suggests JWST can be a powerful probe of molecular hydrogen gas in galaxies during the epoch of reionization. Our findings underscore the potential capability of JWST NIRSpec in detecting molecular hydrogen gas in emission at ultrahigh redshifts.
The rest-frame wavelength range of H
2
lines spans 91.1–164.4 nm
(
Sternberg 1989
)
, while NIRSpec covers a wavelength range of 0.6–5 microns. This enables the possible characterization of warm fluorescent H
2
emission features in galaxies across a redshift range from
z=5.6
to beyond
z=15
. Given the faintness of CO emission at such redshifts, our results offer a novel method for directly probing the molecular interstellar medium in galaxies that lie beyond ALMA’s observational capabilities. For
z<7.5
, these observations complement studies of cold gas traced by dust, [CII], and CO.

The H
2
far-ultraviolet (FUV) emission lines serve as powerful probes to constrain the physical properties of the interstellar medium (ISM) in galaxies and to identify excitation sources. As discussed in
Field et al. 1966
;
Stecher & Williams 1967
;
Dalgarno et al. 1970b
;
Sternberg 1989
, when molecular or partially molecular neutral gas is irradiated by FUV radiation, H
2
molecules are excited to electronic states. These states rapidly decay back to the ground electronic state, producing FUV line emission. The intensities of these emission lines are directly proportional to the flux of the incident FUV radiation. Consequently, the H
2
FUV lines can be used to constrain the interstellar FUV radiation field in galaxies. Since the FUV radiation is predominantly produced by massive, short-lived stars, these measurements can be further connected to constrain the population of massive stars. For a known initial mass function, this enables estimates of the star-formation rate (SFR). Additionally, the H
2
line intensities may provide insights into dynamical timescales within the galactic ISM.

As discussed in
Bialy et al. 2024
, the H
2
FUV lines are generated by the Lyman-Werner (LW) radiation field, which is also responsible for H
2
photodissociation. Measuring the H
2
FUV line intensity therefore allows one to determine the integrated H
2
photodissociation rate (see their Eq. 9; see also
Burkhart et al. 2024
). Comparing this quantity to other timescales, such as the Hubble time, the star-formation timescale, or the dynamical timescale, can offer valuable insights into the evolution of the galactic ISM, the bottlenecks in its evolution, and the processes governing the buildup of stellar mass.

V.2
Ultra-High Redshift Molecular Outflows

An interesting aspect of the CIV and H
2
fluorescence features reported here is that they are consistent with evidence of multiphase outflows. These outflows are potentially driven by supermassive black holes or intense star formation in these galaxies. For instance, in GN-z11
(
Maiolino et al. 2023
)
, a shifted CIV line with a P Cygni profile has been linked to a strong AGN-driven outflow at
z=10.6

(but see
Álvarez-Márquez et al. 2024
, for a different perspective)
.

Our findings suggest that the molecular hydrogen emission features in the stacked JADES galaxies trace molecular outflows, while the CIV lines trace ionized outflows. Both the H
2
and CIV emission features are blueshifted from their expected wavelengths, with H
2
exhibiting a velocity shift of
\sim 300
km/s, slightly lower than that of CIV, which is typical of outflows in lower redshift systems
(
Richings & Faucher-Giguere 2017
;
Fluetsch et al. 2019
;
Morganti et al. 2015
)
.

The ejection of molecular gas into the circumgalactic medium (CGM) has profound implications for the metal enrichment and thermal properties of the surrounding environment. Galactic outflows serve as key mechanisms for transporting metals into the intergalactic medium (IGM) and facilitating cosmic reionization by enabling radiation to escape from host galaxies
(
Giallongo et al. 2015
;
Grazian et al. 2018
;
Seiler et al. 2018
;
Bosch-Ramon 2018
)
.
The tentative detection of molecular hydrogen in these outflows, traced by H
2
fluorescence, suggests that some fraction of the gas remains molecular after ejection or forms in situ once sufficiently far from radiation sources. This aligns with observations of outflows in the local universe
(
Richings & Faucher-Giguere 2017
;
Fluetsch et al. 2019
;
Morganti et al. 2015
)
and simulations
(
Steinwandel et al. 2024
;
Porter et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2020
)
.

VI
Conclusions

Through an analysis of spectral stacking from the JADES catalog, encompassing the GOODS-S and GOODS-N fields, we have identified the first candidates of blended molecular hydrogen (H
2
) fluorescent emission lines at z
>7
, as presented in Table
2
. Our analysis leads to the following conclusions:

•

With the aid of a Meudon PDR model for fluorescent FUV H
2
emission lines, we tentatively detect two molecular hydrogen (H
2
) fluorescent emission features in high-redshift galaxies at 2.3
\sigma
and 3.1
\sigma
. The molecular emission features reported here represent one of the highest redshift detections of molecular gas in emission to date, with our stacked galaxies spanning
z=7.0-13.4
which has a mean redshift of 8.13.

•

In addition, these stacked spectra exhibit ionized emission features, such as CIV emission with a P-Cygni profile, as well as CIII], OIII], and HeII.

•

The emission line features suggest that both CIV and the detected H
2
emission lines originate from high-redshift multiphase outflows.

•

Our results suggest that JWST can be used to detect FUV molecular hydrogen fluorescence at high redshifts and we propose future searches in the range of
z=7-13.4
.

In future work, we also aim to conduct more detailed modeling and analyze other emission lines (e.g., Fe
+
, HeII, OIII], CIII]) to better constrain the physical properties of the gas within multiphase outflows, such as average radiation field strength and mass loading factors. As JWST observations continue to expand and deepen, they will provide more robust insights and detections of these lines in individual sources.
