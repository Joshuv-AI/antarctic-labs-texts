Recognition and control gaps in advanced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome among United States adults
Authors: Menghao Zhou, Fen Zhang, Bingluo Zhou, Yiming Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) stages and condition-specific indicators among adults in the United States, with exploratory state profiles.MethodsWe analyzed 5006 United States adults aged ≥20 years in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, August 2021–August 2023. Survey weights and condition-specific denominators were used. A stage-3 model developed among adults without reported cardiovascular disease was applied to the 2023 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System.ResultsAdvanced CKM (stages 3–4) affected 13.0% (95% confidence interval, 11.6%, 14.4%) of adults. Among advanced-CKM adults with a single-visit kidney abnormality, 80.7% did not report weak or failing kidneys. Within applicable subgroups, 58.7% had blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mm Hg and 56.0% had glycated hemoglobin ≥7%. Concurrent elevation affected 34.1% (95% confidence interval, 24.7%, 44.4%) of the fixed hypertension-plus-diabetes subgroup (91/277). Across 49 jurisdictions, the age-standardized modeled stage-3 contribution ranged from 4.4% to 6.5%; directly reported cardiovascular disease was presented separately.ConclusionsNational estimates describe frequent condition-specific abnormalities within advanced CKM, while the separate geographic projections provide exploratory surveillance context. Fixed thresholds do not establish individual treatment failure, and state projections require external validation.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome links metabolic risk, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) across a staged prevention continuum (Ndumele et al., 2023). A 2026 multisociety guideline further operationalized prevention, detection, evaluation, and management across CKM stages (Ndumele et al., 2026).

National studies have established CKM stage prevalence and its social gradients in United States adults (Aggarwal et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Minhas et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). Risk-factor control in CKM-related populations is also well documented. Ostrominski et al. (2025) examined comprehensive control among adults with at least two of CVD, CKD, and type 2 diabetes. Lee et al. (2025) reported joint treatment and control among adults with hypertension and concurrent diabetes or hyperlipidemia through 2023. Gong et al. (2026a) assessed treatment and control in CKM stage ≥2 across cardiovascular-risk strata and cardiometabolic condition counts.

Kidney reporting, blood pressure, glycemia, and lipid-medication reporting require different clinical denominators. Assessing blood pressure and glycemic elevation in the same adults with hypertension and diabetes describes how often these abnormalities occur together. Access to care and food security provide social context for the clinical indicators.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) provides measured national data, whereas the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a telephone health survey, supports state estimates but lacks full staging measurements. Both represent the noninstitutionalized population of the United States. Cross-survey models can explore geographic patterns but require validation against measured state outcomes. Their 2021–2023 and 2023 data are contemporaneous (Terry et al., 2024). Previous state and neighborhood analyses provide context (Chang et al., 2025; Krishnan et al., 2026).

We used NHANES 2021–2023 to estimate CKM stages and characterize recognition, control, access, and food-security indicators within advanced CKM. BRFSS 2023 supported exploratory state profiles alongside cardiometabolic, social, and access descriptors. Joint indicators describe overlapping abnormalities; threshold, definition and selection sensitivities assess dependence on surveillance choices. Secondary comparisons assessed how differences in clinical indicators between stages 3 and 4 varied with demographic adjustment and restriction to groups represented in both stages.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a cross-sectional surveillance study with a secondary exploratory state application. Participant-level records were not pooled. NHANES August 2021–August 2023 provided measured national staging data and the development sample for a BRFSS-compatible bridge model (Terry et al., 2024). Among 5792 adults aged ≥20 years with positive phlebotomy weights and observed survey design variables, 786 lacked primary staging variables and were excluded, leaving 5006 complete cases (Supplementary Table S1). Four retained participants had unresolved risk-based staging and remained in the national distribution denominator. BRFSS 2023 supplied the state-representative application sample (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). Kentucky and Pennsylvania were absent from the public-use file and excluded from state profiles.

NHANES interview and examination response rates were 34.6% and 25.7%, respectively (Terry et al., 2024). Across 48 states and the District of Columbia in BRFSS 2023, the median weighted American Association for Public Opinion Research Response Rate 4 was 44.6% (range, 31.0%–63.1%) (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). Predictor harmonization appears in Supplementary Methods M4.

Survey-recorded sex (female or male) was used as available in the public-use datasets; gender identity was not included in the harmonized analyses.

CKM staging

We operationalized the American Heart Association CKM framework using measured body size, blood pressure, glycemia, lipid-related information, kidney markers, Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) risk, and CVD history (Khan et al., 2024; Ndumele et al., 2023). The highest captured stage was assigned. Mutually exclusive stages ranged from no captured risk factors (stage 0) to adiposity or prediabetes (stage 1), metabolic risk factors or a kidney abnormality (stage 2), very-high-risk kidney markers or PREVENT 10-year total CVD risk ≥20% without captured clinical CVD (stage 3), and reported clinical CVD (stage 4). PREVENT risk was used at ages 30–79 years without clinical CVD; outside this range, stage 3 required very-high-risk kidney markers. Advanced CKM comprised stages 3–4.

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the 2021 race-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation (Inker et al., 2021). Kidney abnormality meant single-visit eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥30 mg/g. The primary analysis used phlebotomy weights to avoid mixing fasting and nonfasting subsample weights. PREVENT used available optional glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and UACR inputs, without a social deprivation index. Required continuous risk inputs outside permitted ranges were bounded to the nearest American Heart Association-supported values for risk calculation alone; raw clinical measurements were preserved. This boundary convention was introduced during revision and can overestimate or underestimate risk (American Heart Association, 2026). Remaining uncomputable risks were not treated as low risk. Definitions and alternative specifications appear in Supplementary Methods M1 and Table S2.

Recognition, control, access, and social-risk indicators

Among adults with advanced CKM, we estimated: the proportion not reporting weak or failing kidneys (KIQ022) among those with a single-visit kidney abnormality; blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mm Hg (systolic ≥130 or diastolic ≥80) among adults with diagnosed or treated hypertension; HbA1c ≥7% among adults with diagnosed diabetes; and no reported lipid-lowering medication among the high-cholesterol proxy (total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL or reported high cholesterol). Contextual indicators were no usual place for care, no health insurance, and low or very low food security among household adults (FSDAD). Each estimate required an observed indicator response; unknown kidney and food-security responses were excluded. Definitions and denominators appear in Supplementary Methods M1. Equivalent international-system units are provided in Supplementary Methods M1.

Concurrent elevation required both blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mm Hg and HbA1c ≥7% in the fixed subgroup with diagnosed/treated hypertension and diagnosed diabetes. Higher-threshold sensitivities used blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mm Hg (systolic ≥140 or diastolic ≥90) and HbA1c ≥8%, separately and jointly (Supplementary Fig. S1). Fixed thresholds support population surveillance (Fryar et al., 2024; Inoue et al., 2025); individual treatment targets vary with age, comorbidity, frailty, and treatment context.

Exploratory state-level bridge analysis and contextual profiles

The survey-weighted logistic bridge modeled operational stage 3 among NHANES adults without any captured clinical CVD, including heart failure. Predictors were age (natural spline, two degrees of freedom), an indicator for ages 30–79, sex, harmonized race/ethnicity, reported hypertension, diabetes, kidney disease, and current smoking. CVD was not a predictor. Training-fit spline bases were preserved for new predictions (Supplementary Methods M4).

Internal validation used 20 repetitions of five-fold primary sampling unit (PSU) cross-validation and 200 stratified PSU bootstrap optimism corrections. Discrimination, calibration, and reduced-model diagnostics appear in Supplementary Fig. S2 and Methods M4. Predictor-completeness inverse-probability weighting (IPW) used age, sex, race/ethnicity, and jurisdiction, with normalized predicted means within CVD-negative respondents (Supplementary Table S1).

BRFSS common CVD meant a report of myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease/angina, or stroke; any yes indicated CVD, all explicit no responses indicated no common CVD, and other patterns were unresolved. Within respondents with known common CVD, reported CVD prevalence and the modeled stage-3 contribution, (1 − reported CVD prevalence) × normalized mean prediction among common-CVD-negative respondents, were reported separately. Age standardization used the fixed pooled 49-jurisdiction BRFSS distribution. Intervals used delta-method variance propagation and a normal critical value of 1.96, combining BRFSS design covariance and NHANES coefficient variance. A finite-degree-of-freedom sensitivity assessed this approximation; both methods condition on response weights and the age reference (Supplementary Methods M5; Fig. S2).

Contextual indicators were obesity, diabetes, hypertension, reported kidney disease, poverty, insurance, rurality, primary-care physician density, food insecurity, and social vulnerability. Sources comprised federal public-health and census statistics, America's Health Rankings, and workforce and food-security reports (Flanagan et al., 2011; Greenlund et al., 2022; Health Resources and Services Administration, 2025; Rabbitt et al., 2024). Source units, periods and denominators remain distinct; no composite score or policy ranking is assigned (Supplementary Methods M6).

Equity analysis

Descriptive associations with advanced CKM were estimated using survey-weighted modified Poisson regression. A full model included age group, sex, race/ethnicity, education, family income relative to the federal poverty threshold, insurance, and food security. Comparison models used the same complete cases and included age (natural spline, two degrees of freedom), sex, race/ethnicity, and one social factor. They differ in both age specification and social-factor adjustment. Individual-coefficient intervals used design-degree-of-freedom t critical values consistently across both model sets. The full model provides supplementary description under more extensive adjustment; interpretation emphasizes patterns across specifications (Supplementary Table S3; Methods M3).

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated survey weights, strata, and PSUs. National proportions used Korn–Graubard beta 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusted for each domain's design degrees of freedom; imprecise estimates carry reliability flags (Parker et al., 2017; Supplementary Methods M2). During revision, the NHANES analytic-selection model was expanded and refitted. IPW divided phlebotomy weights by completeness probabilities truncated at the complete-case 1st and 99th percentiles (Supplementary Table S1; Methods M2).

Analyses added during revision included alternative clinical thresholds, fixed-subgroup joint indicators, matched fasting definitions with fasting weights, PREVENT without optional HbA1c/UACR, and supporting adjusted comparisons. Fasting-defined diabetes was propagated into PREVENT. Secondary stage comparisons used age, sex, and race/ethnicity adjustment with explicitly defined standardization populations and each fitted model's residual design degrees of freedom. These conservative contrast intervals and the design-degree-of-freedom coefficient intervals address different inferential summaries; their rationale is specified in Supplementary Methods M3. Analyses were descriptive, without multiplicity correction (Supplementary Tables S2–S3; Fig. S1). Analyses used R 4.5.3 and the survey package (Lumley, 2004; R Core Team, 2026).

Ethics and protection of participants

NHANES received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board and obtained participant consent (Terry et al., 2024). The corresponding author's institution granted an exemption from ethics review for this secondary analysis of public, deidentified survey data. Analyses involved no participant contact or reidentification and reported aggregate results to protect participant safety and privacy.

Recognition, control, access, and social-risk indicators among adults with advanced CKM

Among adults with advanced CKM and a single-visit kidney abnormality, 80.7% (95% CI, 75.7%, 85.1%) did not report weak or failing kidneys (Table 2; Fig. 1C). Of those with diagnosed or treated hypertension, 58.7% (95% CI, 54.9%, 62.5%) had blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mm Hg; of those with diagnosed diabetes, 56.0% (95% CI, 47.6%, 64.1%) had HbA1c ≥7%. Lipid-lowering medication was not reported by 18.6% (95% CI, 14.7%, 23.0%) of the high-cholesterol proxy group. Low or very low adult food security was present in 18.8% (95% CI, 14.8%, 23.2%) of those with observed responses. No usual place for care and no health insurance were reported by 4.0% and 3.3%, respectively. Exploratory state-level profiles

The CVD-free training sample contained 4395 participants and 293 stage-3 cases. After excluding 601 stage-4 participants, four had unresolved staging and six additional participants lacked a predictor, including one stage-3 case (Supplementary Table S1). Cross-validated discrimination, calibration and bootstrap-corrected performance are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. BRFSS excluded 4718 respondents with unknown common CVD (1.1% weighted). Among 366,062 with negative common CVD history, 343,715 had complete predictors (92.8% weighted). The largest IPW-versus-complete-case change in the crude modeled contribution was <0.1 percentage points.

Across 49 jurisdictions, reported common-CVD prevalence ranged from 5.5% to 14.7% crude and 6.8% to 13.5% age standardized. The modeled stage-3 contribution ranged from 3.4% to 6.8% crude and 4.4% to 6.5% age standardized (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S4). Socioeconomic gradients

Lower education and income were associated with higher advanced CKM prevalence in both demographic-adjusted and full models, with different magnitudes. Insurance and food-security associations varied with adjustment. Paired estimates and model diagnostics appear in Supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

Among adults with advanced CKM and a single-visit kidney abnormality, 80.7% did not report weak or failing kidneys. Elevated blood pressure and HbA1c were common in their respective clinical subgroups. Approximately one third of adults with advanced CKM, diagnosed or treated hypertension, and diagnosed diabetes had elevations in both measures. Advanced CKM affected about one in eight United States adults, while stage 2 accounted for nearly half. Exploratory state profiles separated directly reported CVD, modeled stage-3 contribution, and contextual descriptors.

These findings build on established evidence of CKM prevalence, multimorbidity, and inadequate risk-factor control. Ostrominski et al. (2025) examined comprehensive control in adults with at least two CKM conditions. Lee et al. (2025) assessed joint control in hypertension with concurrent diabetes or hyperlipidemia, including the 2021–2023 cycle. Gong et al. (2026a) assessed treatment rates in CKM stage ≥2 and risk-factor control among treated adults, including risk and condition-count strata. We describe kidney reporting and clinical indicators among adults with advanced CKM and quantify simultaneous blood pressure and glycemic elevation in those with diagnosed or treated hypertension and diagnosed diabetes. Differences in eligibility, thresholds, and standardization preclude direct numerical comparisons. Joint elevation is also distinct from failure to achieve control of all conditions: a person with only one elevated measure meets the latter but not the former.

Stage 2 represents a broad upstream population for risk-factor prevention. Within advanced CKM, condition-specific denominators distinguish kidney reporting, blood pressure and glycemic measurements, and lipid-medication reports. The observed patterns are consistent with national reports (Fryar et al., 2024; Gong et al., 2026b; Inoue et al., 2025). Yim et al. (2025) provide related international context on CKM trends and socioeconomic disparities in South Korea.

Routine eGFR and UACR testing can identify abnormalities that warrant confirmatory evaluation, consistent with kidney-disease guidance (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) CKD Work Group, 2024). Standardized blood pressure protocols, medication review, home monitoring, and team-based follow-up are established program components (Wall et al., 2023), but this study did not evaluate their effectiveness or delivery. Food insecurity, insurance, and usual-care indicators provide context for surveillance; they do not measure local implementation capacity.

Stage-specific interpretation is constrained by the staging definition. PREVENT incorporates blood pressure, diabetes, eGFR, and optional HbA1c/UACR, so selection into stage 3 enriches several markers being described. A fixed hypertension-plus-diabetes subgroup does not remove this dependence. Omitting optional inputs reduced stage-3 HbA1c elevation from 65.3% to 52.6%; stage 4 remained at 50.1%. After restricting the comparison to adults aged 45–79 in sex-by-race/ethnicity groups represented in both stages, the adjusted difference in joint elevation (stage 3 minus stage 4) was 17.83 percentage points (95% CI, −0.22, 35.87). These descriptive comparisons therefore provide limited evidence about independent management-quality differences.

The state profiles extend the geographic description of CKM-related burden through separate reported-CVD and modeled stage-3 components. They do not estimate state-specific kidney nonreporting, blood pressure control, or glycemic control. Cardiometabolic and social/access descriptors provide context in their source units. Their different age ranges, source years, and measurement methods limit direct comparison; some also draw on BRFSS and cannot independently validate the bridge.

Socioeconomic findings describe the context in which these clinical indicators occur (Havranek et al., 2015; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017). Education and income associations were directionally consistent across the reported models, whereas insurance and food-security associations depended on specification. Limited PSUs constrain model inference, and the full model does not support a joint test of all coefficients. The models are descriptive, differ in age specification and adjustment, and do not establish causal effects. The inverse association between being uninsured and advanced CKM should not be interpreted as a protective effect of lacking insurance.

Several measurement limitations warrant emphasis. Operational CKM staging cannot reproduce a full clinical assessment. Single-visit eGFR and UACR cannot establish persistent CKD, and KIQ022 asks about weak or failing kidneys rather than all CKD diagnoses. Nonreporting may therefore reflect question wording or communication as well as lack of recognition. Blood pressure and HbA1c thresholds are fixed surveillance markers; the study cannot determine whether each value exceeds an appropriate individual target. Detailed medication and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol information was not incorporated in the primary analytic dataset, limiting the lipid indicator to medication reporting. Access and food-security reports also do not measure the quality or adequacy of care.

State projections depend on the transportability of operational stage-3 relationships, including their strong age structure. BRFSS does not capture heart failure, so its common-CVD-negative population differs from the training population. The model has 12 slopes, 15 survey design degrees of freedom, and three residual degrees of freedom. Internal validation cannot substitute for external measured-state validation. The normal interval approximation is conditional; finite-degree-of-freedom alternatives widen intervals, and neither captures structural transport error or response-weight estimation uncertainty.

Selection and classification also limit generalizability. Complete cases represented 87.1% of weighted phlebotomy eligibility; low survey response and indicator-specific missing responses may produce additional selection. IPW addresses observed analytic inclusion under its response-model assumptions, not survey nonparticipation or unmeasured nonrandom selection. The PREVENT boundary convention approximates risk while preserving clinical measurements and can alter stage membership. Outside ages 30–79, risk-based stage 3 is not assigned, potentially undercapturing stage 3 among older adults. Finally, the small fixed subgroup, particularly stage 3, limits precision, and all revision-stage analyses are exploratory.

These cross-sectional data cannot establish causal effects or whether specific programs improve outcomes. Local measured-data validation and assessment of service capacity are required before using the state projections in planning.

Conclusions

Advanced CKM affected about one in eight United States adults, with frequent kidney nonreporting and condition-specific clinical abnormalities. Approximately one third of adults with both hypertension and diabetes in this group had simultaneous blood pressure and glycemic elevation. These national findings and the separate exploratory geographic profiles provide complementary information for surveillance and hypothesis generation.
