Prevalence of unreported and uncontrolled dyslipidemia in a food insecure population
Authors: Ethan Belnap, Camden Richter, William Cohen, Abigail McIntosh, Ishan Khosla, Roberto Garcia, Daniel Luger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dyslipidemia is a major contributor to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, yet it remains underdiagnosed and undertreated in food insecure populations. This study describes the prevalence of unreported and uncontrolled dyslipidemia in food insecure populations in Chicago, Illinois. The study population comprised individuals attending food pantry health screenings in West Chicago from August 2023-October 2025. Demographic information, lipid panel results, and vital measurements obtained during screening were analyzed. Participants were stratified by self-reported history of dyslipidemia. Of 389 participants included, 69.2% met criteria for dyslipidemia at screening, with 49.1% exhibiting mixed dyslipidemia. Over 72.5% individuals did not self-report dyslipidemia, of which 67.7% were found to have dyslipidemia corresponding to a 49.1% prevalence of unreported dyslipidemia. Of the patients who self-reported dyslipidemia, 72.9% demonstrated uncontrolled dyslipidemia. Compared to national estimates, there was a higher prevalence of elevated triglycerides, non-high-density lipoprotein-C, and low high-density-lipoprotein. However, there was a lower prevalence of low-density lipoprotein and total cholesterol elevation. This data highlights high dyslipidemia prevalence within a food insecure population with substantial unreported and uncontrolled disease. It also reflects a unique pattern of lipid abnormalities indicative of a high carbohydrate, low nutrient quality diet. These findings support expanded access to community-based cardiometabolic screening and prevention services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52226-y.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is the abnormal concentration of blood lipids or lipoproteins which can increase the risk of cardiometabolic disease1. Clinically, it is most commonly identified by elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL), elevated triglycerides (TG), elevated total cholesterol (TC), elevated non-high density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), or reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL). At the population level, there is a high prevalence of dyslipidemia in the United States (US), with over 53% of the population having dyslipidemia2. This widespread burden also carries major economic implications, with an estimated 26.4 billion dollars in healthcare expenditure in 20163–5.

Given its widespread prevalence and economic impact, dyslipidemia represents a major public health concern, primarily due to its role as a leading modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) which includes coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, and stroke6,7. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality globally and has been the leading cause of death in the US since 19218,9. This extensive disease burden reflects established biological mechanisms, as longer exposure to elevated LDL levels has been associated with an increase in cardiovascular events and a higher incidence of CAD10–13. Additionally, 1% reductions in LDL or non-HDL-C have been associated with 1% reductions in cardiac events over 5 years12,13.

Although the biological mechanisms impacting dyslipidemia are well established, its prevalence and management are also influenced by socioeconomic status (SES)14,15. Low SES has been associated with a higher risk of developing dyslipidemia, and one manifestation of socioeconomic disadvantage is food insecurity14,15. Food insecurity, defined as the lack of consistent access to enough food for a healthy lifestyle, affects over 10% American adults16. It has been linked to increased prevalence of chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus and CVD, and higher mortality rate from acute coronary syndrome, which is especially relevant in food insecure populations14,15,17,18.

Despite the significant health consequences of dyslipidemia, there remains a paucity in the diagnosis and control of this condition, particularly among food insecure populations. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999-2000 revealed that only 43.7% of treatment-eligible adults reported a prior dyslipidemia diagnosis19. This suggests a large percentage of the American population is affected by dyslipidemia and yet remain undiagnosed. Even among diagnosed individuals, lipid control remains suboptimal. Among adults with elevated TC, LDL, and TG on lipid-lowering therapy, 79.5% are able to meet their TC goals (<200 mg/dL), 63.6% are able to control their LDL (<100mg/dL for high risk, <130 mg/dL for moderate-risk and <160mg/dL for low risk), and 72.3% have controlled TG (<150 mg/dL), respectively20–22.

These challenges associated with recognizing individuals with abnormal blood lipids and improving lipid control may be exacerbated in food insecure populations. However, existing evidence on this relationship remains inconclusive. Previous studies exploring the association between food insecurity and dyslipidemia saw inconsistent results or null findings23–25. However, these studies were largely survey based and did not examine whether patients were accurately diagnosed with dyslipidemia, and if diagnosed individuals were able to achieve lipid control. Additionally, there is a paucity in research on the rates of undiagnosed dyslipidemia within the US. This study seeks to address these gaps by examining screening data from food-insecure populations at food pantries in West Chicago to characterize the prevalence of both unreported and uncontrolled dyslipidemia.

Methods

Study design and the Cardiometabolic Health Initiative (CHI)

This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed lipid panel results obtained during routine clinical care among adult patients. Participants were recruited through Cardiometabolic Health Initiative (CHI) screening events at food pantries in West Chicago from August 2023-October 2025. CHI is a community-based screening program affiliated with Rush University that provides cardiometabolic health screenings to underserved populations in West Chicago. Individuals attending CHI screening events at local food pantries were evaluated for cardiometabolic disease by a multidisciplinary team consisting of physicians, medical students, nurses, and community health workers. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Population

Adult participants (≥18 years) attending a CHI screening events at food pantries in West Chicago between August 2023 and October 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Participants were included if they had complete demographic data, a lipid panel, and vital measurements obtained during screening. Individuals missing one or more of these components were excluded.

Sample size

A total of 576 participants underwent CHI screening during the study period, of which 389 had complete data and were included in the final analysis.

Sampling method

The study sample represented a convenience sample derived from individuals attending CHI screening events.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Rush University Institutional Review Board and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Screening protocol

Cardiometabolic screenings were conducted at food pantries by trained clinical staff as part of CHI. As part of routine clinical care, participants provided informed consent for medical evaluation. Cardiometabolic histories were obtained, including demographic information, prior diagnoses of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, current medication use, and self-reported lifestyle factors.

Biochemical testing

Lipid measurements were done using an Cholestech LDX Analyzer (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA). Prior to use, the analyzer was calibrated according to manufacturer specifications and internal quality controls were performed. Capillary blood samples were collected via fingerstick and immediately analyzed using Abbott lipid test cassettes. The analyzer directly measured triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and total cholesterol (TC). Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) was calculated by the analyzer using the Friedewald equation (LDL = TC – HDL-C – (TG/5) with all values reported in mg/dL) per manufacturer specifications26. Non-HDL was calculated with the equation non-HDL-C=TC-HDL26. Data analysis

Participants were stratified into two groups based on self-reported dyslipidemia status obtained during history taking. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared utilizing chi-square testing. Statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York). A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was used to denote significance.

Results

Demographics

The study population included 389 participants, 257 (66.1%) of which were female (Table 2). Self-reported dyslipidemia status was not significantly different among genders (p=0.58) (Table 2). Age distribution showed a marked gradient with 23.7% of the population representing individuals under 40 years old, 37.3% 60 years or older, and the remaining 39.1% between 40 and 59 years of age (Table 2). Self-reported dyslipidemia prevalence increased significantly with age, rising from 5.65% among individuals <40 years to 40.2% in those ages 60–69 and 26.2% among participants age 70 and older (P<0.001) (Table 2). Racial composition was primarily Black/African American (152, 39.1%) and individuals categorized as “other” (189, 48.6%) with smaller proportions of White (37, 9.5%) and Asian (8, 2.1%) (Table 2). Race was not associated with dyslipidemia (p=0.192) (Table 2). Anthropometric measures revealed 44.2% of participants were obese (BMI ≥30.0 kg/m2) and self-reported dyslipidemia prevalence rose with increasing BMI (p=0.037) (Table 2). Similarly, 63% of individuals had elevated central obesity (waist circumference ≥102cm for men, ≥88cm for women) which was strongly associated with self-reported dyslipidemia (p<0.001) (Table 2). Obesity prevalence varied across age groups and was generally higher among females than males though this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.073) (Supplementary Table 1). Regarding cardiometabolic risk factors, 38% of individuals had hypertension and 18.5% had diabetes mellitus - both strongly associated with self-reported dyslipidemia status (p<0.001) (Table 2). Coronary artery disease was less prevalent (1.8%) but also was associated with self-reported dyslipidemia status (p=0.009) (Table 2). Smoking status was not significant related (p=0.436) (Table 2). Finally, access to a primary care provider (46.4%) was associated with self-reported dyslipidemia (p<0.001) (Table 2).

Among the 282 (72.5%) participants who did not report a history of dyslipidemia, 191 (67.7%) had dyslipidemia upon screening (Fig. 1). The overall prevalence of unreported dyslipidemia within this food insecure population was 49.1% (Fig. 1). Discussion

Dyslipidemia is a largely asymptomatic condition that contributes to progressive atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease over time, suggesting the importance of early detection and management. In this cross-sectional analysis of food-insecure adults undergoing cardiometabolic screening on the West side of Chicago, there was a high prevalence of dyslipidemia including substantial rates of both unreported and uncontrolled disease. This study intentionally compared self-reported history with objectively measured lipid values to quantify the gap between perceived and actual disease burden. Although self-reporting may underestimate prior diagnoses, this discrepancy highlights clinically relevant gaps in awareness and access to care. Over 69.2% of individuals screened met criteria for dyslipidemia, and almost half were unaware of their condition at the time of screening (Fig. 1). These findings highlight a significant burden of previously unrecognized cardiometabolic risk in a population that historically has limited access to healthcare.

Unreported dyslipidemia

In this population, 49.1% of participants met guideline-based criteria for dyslipidemia despite not-self reporting a prior diagnosis (Fig. 1). While national estimates of unreported dyslipidemia are variable and lack consensus, the prevalence observed in this study suggest a meaningful gap in awareness among food-insecure individuals. Prior studies have found that persistent dyslipidemia confers an increased incidence of coronary heart disease26,29. Thus, the elevated prevalence of unreported dyslipidemia shown in this study, may put food insecure individuals at increased cardiovascular risk. In a population that is underserved and under-researched, this study’s findings are important in gaining an understanding of how to improve the health of food insecure individuals.

Contrary to this study’s findings, prior research has shown conflicting evidence when looking at the correlation between food insecurity and dyslipidemia23–25. Some studies suggest there to be an association between food insecurity and self-reported dyslipidemia or hyperlipidemia whereas others suggest when directly measuring blood lipid levels in these populations, there is no significant difference24,25,30. This study, measured food insecure patients directly at food pantries and showed high rates of dyslipidemia as well as unreported dyslipidemia. This suggests food insecurity to be an important social determinant of health and factor affecting blood lipid levels.

Patterns of lipid abnormalities

Compared with national estimates, this food insecure population demonstrated a distinct pattern of lipid abnormalities. The national prevalence estimates for lipid abnormalities include 27% with elevated LDL, 13% with elevated non-HDL-C in those with TG ≥200, 30% with elevated TG, 19–23% with low HDL, and 43% with elevated TC2,31–33. In contrast, the corresponding prevalence in the present study were 16.5%, 37.8%, 32.2%, 42.6%, 25.8%, respectively (Fig. 2). Additionally, over 24.7% of individuals in this study exhibited mixed dyslipidemia, compared to 21% nationally (Supplemental figure S4)2. Interestingly, despite increased abnormalities in TG, non-HDL-C, and HDL, the prevalence of elevated LDL and TC was lower in the food insecure population. These lipid patterns may reflect dietary and socioeconomic factors disproportionately affecting food-insecure populations. Prior research on individuals with lower SES found these populations tend to rely on low-cost, energy-dense diets high in refined carbohydrates but low in nutrient quality34,35. One study on Mexican mothers found this type of diet tends to preferentially elevate TG, lower HDL, and can manifest with lower LDL34. This corresponds with the findings in this study, lower prevalence of LDL elevations but increased prevalence of TG, low HDL, and non-HDL-C. The demographic composition of this study, with a high proportion of Hispanic women, may also partially explain the observed lipid patterns (Table 1).

However, non-HDL-C, a measure of all atherogenic lipoprotein such as LDL, very low-density lipoprotein, and intermediate-density lipoprotein was significantly increased in this same populations. Non-HDL-C is more sensitive to the metabolic effects of high-carbohydrate, low quality diets than LDL and is an established therapeutic target associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes12,13,27,36–38. Additionally, changes in non-HDL-C, TG, and lipid ratios are all independent predictors of coronary events29. The high prevalence of elevated non-HDL-C (37.8%) observed in this study in tandem with frequent abnormalities in TG and HDL suggest this food insecure population may have elevated risk of coronary events.

Uncontrolled dyslipidemia

This study also suggests that food insecure populations face significant challenges controlling blood lipid levels following a dyslipidemia diagnosis. Prior studies have shown that among adults on statin therapy, the age-adjusted control rates are around 79.5%20. While food insecure individuals on lipid-lowering therapy exhibited improved lipid control compared to those not receiving pharmacologic treatment, only 32.1% possessed normal blood lipid concentrations (Supplemental Figure S3). These findings suggest prescribed pharmacologic therapy alone may be insufficient in obtaining optimal lipid control in food-insecure populations.

Several factors may contribute to these findings. Medication nonadherence driven by financial constraints, inconsistent access to insurance, and competing basic needs are all possible contributors. However, among participants not receiving lipid-lowering therapy, the prevalence of abnormal lipid concentrations was higher, highlighting the limitations of lifestyle-based lipid management in food insecure populations. The healthiest diet costs $1.5 more per day to consume10. For food insecure individuals who have additional financial burdens, this daily increase in expenditure may not be feasible. Their diminished control over their diet can result in nutritional insufficiency and thus difficulty controlling modifiable risk factors for CVD like dyslipidemia39. Additionally, consistent exercise is known to demonstrate benefit in lipid control40. The American College of Cardiology recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-vigorous intensity aerobic exercise acknowledging a dose-response relationship between exercise and lipid control40. However, food-insecure individuals may face structural barriers to engaging in regular physical activity, including limited time, competing priorities, and limited access to safe and supportive environments for exercise. With challenges in accessing adequate nutrition and engaging in regular physical activity, lifestyle-based lipid management may be particularly difficult to achieve in food-insecure communities. These findings collectively illustrate that lipid management in food insecure populations is constrained by structural barriers which may contribute to higher rates of uncontrolled dyslipidemia.

Obesity in the food-insecure

Within this food-insecure population 44.2% of individuals met criteria for obesity (BMI over 30 kg/m2), and 63% had elevated waist circumferences indicated a high burden of central adiposity (Table 2). This prevalence exceeds US adults overall (41.9% with BMI over 30 kg/m2) suggesting food-insecure individuals may face nutritional deficiencies rather than true caloric insufficiency10,35. To meet caloric needs within financial constraints, individuals experiencing food-insecurity may rely on low-cost, energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods, including ultra-processed items, and decrease consumption of fruits and vegetables35,39. Additionally, cyclical patterns of food availability characterized by relative adequacy earlier in the month followed by reduced quality or quantity later in the pay cycle, may contribute to irregular dietary intake17,35. This pattern in combination with poor diet quality has been physiologically associated with systemic inflammation, gut microbiome disruption, visceral fat accumulation, and insulin resistance17,35. Also, in our cohort both increased BMI and central adiposity were associated with a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia suggesting a metabolic component (Supplemental Figure 2,3) Together, these factors support the concept that food-insecurity is associated with poor dietary quality and increased metabolic risk, rather than caloric deprivation.

Health literacy and access to healthcare

Over 53% of study participants reported not having access to a primary care physician (Table 1). Individuals of lower SES and those with food insecurity face numerous barriers to accessing healthcare driven by financial constraints and limited health literacy41. Prior analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found individuals with lower income and educational attainment are more likely to be exposed to adverse lifestyle factors including tobacco use, nutritionally deficient diets, and physical inactivity42. These factors are each independently associated with unfavorable lipid profiles including lower HDL, and higher LDL, TG, and TC43–46 The clustering of these exposures may amplify dyslipidemia risk as well as cardiovascular disease in food insecure populations43–46 Additionally, individuals without health insurance are more likely than insured adults to be unaware of dyslipidemia and regular screening among insured populations has been associated with improved lipid management47,48. Limited access to primary care and lower health literacy in this population may partially explain the high prevalence of previously unrecognized dyslipidemia. Reduced opportunities for routine screening in combination with challenges understanding screening results, may contribute to diminished awareness of dyslipidemia diagnosis. These findings collectively underscore the critical need for interventions expanding access to preventative healthcare, nutrition resources, and health literacy support in food-insecure communities.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Reliance on individuals self-reporting a prior dyslipidemia or elevated cholesterol diagnosis is subject to recall bias, misclassification, and variability in health literacy. Participants were asked whether they had ever been told by a healthcare provider that they had high cholesterol or dyslipidemia, a commonly used approach in population-based studies. However, a formally validated questionnaire was not employed. These limitations may be amplified in food-insecure populations with reduced access to longitudinal healthcare and variable health literacy potentially leading to under recognition or misunderstanding of prior diagnoses. As a result, the prevalence of unreported dyslipidemia observed in this study may partially reflect lack of awareness rather than true absence of disease.

Lipid measurements were obtained under non-fasting conditions, reflecting the nature of opportunistic community-based screening. While non-fasting lipid assessment is supported by current guidelines, particularly for evaluation of total cholesterol and HDL, triglyceride levels can be influenced by recent food consumption which may affect LDL values26,40. Additionally, LDL was calculated using the Friedewald equation as implemented by the point-of-care device used in this study. More contemporary methods such as the Martin-Hopkins or Sampson equations may improve accuracy in certain settings but were not applied40.

Dietary characteristics and food pantry utilizations patterns were not systematically measured limiting our ability to characterize dietary patterns in this population. Additionally, data on certain cardiovascular risk modifiers including family history of premature ASCVD, physical activity, and advanced lipid markers (e.g., apolipoprotein B and lipoprotein (a)), were not collected which may limit more detailed risk stratification.

This studies cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. As this study was conducted at food pantries on the West side of Chicago, its convenience sample may limit generalizability. Also, recruitment at community screening events introduces the potential for selection bias. Given these limitations, additional research is needed to further characterize the cardiometabolic risk and longitudinal outcomes in food-insecure populations.

Conclusion

In this community-based screening study, food insecure participants demonstrated a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia compared with national estimates. Substantial proportions of participants were either unaware of their condition or unable to adequately control it at the time of screening. This study also observes a unique pattern of lipid abnormalities with elevations in TG, non-HDL-C, and low HDL, but lower rates of elevated LDL and TC. This study highlights the importance of community-based screening initiatives in identifying unrecognized and uncontrolled dyslipidemia in food insecure populations. Expanding access to cardiometabolic screening, preventive services, and targeted interventions may play a vital role in mitigating cardiovascular risk in food insecure communities.
