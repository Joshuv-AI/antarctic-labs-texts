Innovative Approach to Sustainable Fertilizer Production: Leveraging Electrically Assisted Conversion of Sewage Sludge for Nutrient Recovery
Authors: Gerardine G. Botte, Dayana Donneys-Victoria, Christian E. Alvarez-Pugliese, Jedidian Adjei, Selin Sahin, Nathan W. Wilson, Kayleigh Millerick, Amy Hardberger, Ariel L. Furst, Nicole Hu, Andrew J. Medford
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Efforts addressing sludge management, food security, and resource recovery have led to novel approaches in these areas. Electrically assisted conversion of sludge stands out as a promising technology for sewage sludge valorization, producing nitrogen and phosphorus-based fertilizers. The adoption of this technology, which could lead to a fertilizer circular economy, holds the potential to catalyze a transformative change in wastewater treatment facilities toward process intensification, innovation, and sustainability. This paper provides insights into the economic aspects of the technology, policy considerations, and challenges involved in realizing the potential of electrified processes for sludge valorization. To demonstrate the impact of the technology, a case study for its implementation in the United States assuming the municipal wastewater treatment plants market is discussed. It was found that electrically assisted sludge conversion could enable the recovery of nitrogen and phosphorus from waste, representing up to 9% of the nitrogen and 32% of the phosphorus consumption of the U.S. for fertilizer use. This technology also enables full electrification and modularization of the process, thereby presenting significant economic and environmental opportunities.

Introduction

Increasing urbanization has caused municipal solid waste and wastewater to emerge as primary sources of materials that would otherwise be discarded. A byproduct of wastewater treatment is sewage sludge, a nutrient-rich organic material that contains nitrogen (N) concentrations between 2.4 and 8% and phosphorus (P) concentrations ranging from 1.4 to 3% of the total solids.1−4 The volume of municipal sludge feedstock has been underestimated due to the incorrect interchangeability of the terms “sewage sludge” and “biosolids”.5 For example, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and North East Biosolids and Residuals Association (NEBRA) estimate that between 4.5 and 6 million t (dry weight) of biosolids are produced in the U.S. annually.6,7 In contrast, the most recent estimation of municipal sludge feedstock production in the U.S. is 13.8 million t dry sludge annually.5 The European Union (EU) produced between 3.7 and 6 million t of dry sludge annually (observed period 2018–2022),8 although the sludge production varies significantly among member states.

The current usage of sludge is mainly driven by the need to remove it from wastewater treatment sites, leading to significant amounts of this product being either landfilled or incinerated, a linear process resulting in the generation of greenhouse gases (GHG). In the United States, the U.S. EPA reported that 43% of the sewage sludge is treated and recycled for land application, 14% undergoes incineration, 42% is disposed of in landfills, and the remaining 1% is used in other ways.6 In contrast, in the UK, the disposal pathways for biosolids are as follows: 78% land application, 12% incineration, 5% land reclamation, and 5% other methods.9

One key challenge in determining the impacts of sludge production is the distribution of the wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), which vary widely, ranging from large, centralized facilities to smaller, decentralized systems. This challenge is exacerbated by rapid annual increases in the amount of sludge due to the growth of the urban population, intensified industrial activities, initiation of new plants, and upgrades to existing facilities.5,10 These factors underscore the importance of accurately assessing the total volume and compositional variation of sewage sludge production to inform effective waste management strategies. In addition, regulatory requirements aimed at minimizing potential risks of biosolids to human health highlight the need for proper handling and accurate sludge management and distribution reporting.5

The need for sufficient food production is directly linked with the rapidly growing and increased urbanization of people worldwide. Our ability to produce enough food to sustain the current population, over eight billion people, is primarily credited to agrochemical innovations, namely chemical fertilizers.11 A projected 70% increase in food demand is predicted as the global population surpasses 10 billion by 2050.12 Addressing this demand requires increased cultivation efforts and a reduction in the impacts of input-intensive agricultural practices. Agrochemicals have known significant drawbacks;13 their overapplication has led to soil degradation and water eutrophication, and nitrogen-based fertilizers (NBFs) produced through the Haber-Bosch process is responsible for more than 1% of global CO2 emissions.14 Overall, 11.2% of U.S. GHG emissions are due to agriculture,15 while in Europe, this value reaches 13.2%.16 Despite the critical role of nutrients like N and P in plant growth, current agricultural processes lead to the wasting of most of the applied active N. Agriculture accounts for 132 million t of ammonia (NH3) consumption; however only 20% of NBFs produced directly contribute to food production.17 The remaining 80% is lost to the environment every year, resulting in negative ecological and socioeconomic impacts at an estimated cost of US$200 billion.18 In contrast, phosphorus cannot be generated, so limited resources such as phosphate rock are used to produce phosphorus-based fertilizer (PBF),19 P loss to water bodies is a recurring problem, causing groundwater contamination and freshwater eutrophication.20 High production costs, including energy consumption, transportation expenses, and carbon emissions associated with NH3 produced through the Haber-Bosch process,21 coupled with the decreasing reserves of phosphate rock, geopolitical disruptions, and the volatility of natural gas prices, pose a significant risk of instability in fertilizer prices.1,22,23 In the past two years, fertilizer prices in the U.S. have tripled, climbing from US$94 to US$232 per acre for corn and from US$46 to US$106 per acre for soybeans.24 This sharp increase directly impacts food and energy costs for the general population but disproportionately affects low-income countries where fertilizer constitutes a substantial proportion of the overall cost of food production.1

A review of current state-of-the-art technologies and processes for sewage sludge treatment (Supporting Information Table S1 and Figure S1) demonstrate that biological processes are the most prevalent treatment approach published in the SciFinder database (40%), followed by hydrothermal processes (34%), other processes (22%), and electrochemical processes (4%). These technologies are discussed in greater detail in the Supporting Information, where the specific methodologies and limitations of each treatment approach are elaborated. Fifteen to 29% of articles within each treatment category further discuss nutrient recovery. These results underscore that different approaches for wastewater treatment still primarily focus on contaminant reduction, which ignores the density and values of nutrients within sludge and limits the potential for nutrient circularity.

The field of Electrochemical Valorization of Organic Waste (EVOW) encompasses sustainable and innovative methodologies that leverage electrochemical processes to convert organic waste materials into valuable products.25 One emerging technology in this area is the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge through sludge electrolysis and nutrient recovery processes.26−28 This technology can address both sludge management and food security concerns. To demonstrate the potential of electrochemically assisted valorization of sewage sludge, this paper provides insights into the economics, policy considerations, and challenges associated with realizing the potential of electrified processes for sludge valorization. To display the impact of the technology, a techno-economic analysis (TEA) case study was conducted. This analysis includes insights into potential technical, regulatory, and policy challenges associated with adopting sludge electrolysis as a waste-to-value technology. This approach represents a fundamental shift in the goals of wastewater treatment from mitigating the impact of linear processes to truly circularizing the sludge treatment process.

Materials and Methods

The techno-economic analysis was conducted over a 15 year time horizon to evaluate the project’s financial viability and key performance indicators. The analysis considered operational and capital expenses, alongside revenue generation from the sale of recovered products. In this analysis, the process converts 50% of the organic N in the sludge into inorganic N, and 80% of the P was recovered from the sludge. The pH of the sludge (5% solids) was controlled in a 0.1 M NaOH solution. Electricity was priced at US$0.05 per kW h,29 and the electrolysis process consumed 0.2 kW h per kilogram of dry sludge.26 Sodium hydroxide was priced at US$300 per dry t.30 The operational cost for the centrifuge was 1 kW h per cubic meter of sludge processed,31 and the drying process required 700 kW h per t of water removed,32 using natural gas priced at US$4 per MBTU.33

Revenue was generated from the sale of recovered products, with the following prices: CASFER super compost (CSC) at US$30 per t (50% wet basis),34 triple superphosphate (TSP) at US$4286 per t of P,35 and N at US$500 per t of N. The concentrations in raw sludge dry solids were 7% N and 3% phosphorus by weight solids.1−4 Financial assumptions included an inflation rate of 3% per y, a discount rate of 10% per y, a tax rate of 25.74%, and an annual maintenance cost of 10% of the CAPEX.

The economic performance was assessed using key metrics such as Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) for three different levels of production rates of solids under 21 scenarios (Supporting Information Data S2), which ranged between 0.4 to 1.4 dry t solids/MG of wastewater treated.2,5,36 NPV was calculated to determine the present value of cash flows over the project duration, discounted at 10%. IRR was computed to evaluate the profitability of the project. Total revenue was generated from the tipping fees and the sale of recovered CSC, TSP, and N, while operating costs included the costs of electricity, NaOH, centrifuge operation, drying, and maintenance. Capital costs covered the initial investment required for the setup and infrastructure of the processing plant. All calculations are presented in the Supporting Information Data S2.

Results

Novel Electrically Assisted Conversion of Sewage Sludge for Nutrient Extraction

Contemporary WWTPs operate with advanced technologies and efficient processes to ensure the effective purification of effluent waters. Biological sludge is generated by biological wastewater treatment processes such as activated sludge with its numerous variations, and the solids removed during primary and secondary sedimentation are called “Sewage Sludge”.2,37 Wastewater treatment facilities usually produce between 0.4 to 1.36 dry t of solids/MG of wastewater treated.2,5,36 “Biosolids” is the term used for the product derived from properly treating sewage sludge to meet standards for use or disposal.5 The substantial moisture content in sewage sludge, often surpassing 80 wt %, poses significant logistical and energetic barriers for disposal, specifically for transportation and combustion. Several established methods are available for handling, treating, disposing, and utilizing sludge waste. These processes include thickening, stabilization, digestion, composting, dewatering, incineration, and heat drying.5 Nevertheless, most of these approaches focus on minimizing sludge disposal volume or stabilizing the material and account for up to 30–50% of the overall expenses associated with municipal wastewater treatment processes.38

Waste activated sludge (WAS) removed during secondary clarification typically presents solids concentrations ranging from 0.4 to 1.5% in terms of total dry solids. The protein content of WAS falls within 32–41% of the total solids content, while alkalinity levels ranging from 580 to 1100 mg/L as CaCO3. WAS may or may not be mixed with solids recovered from primary clarification prior to treatment; even mixed, sludges rarely exceed 4% total dry solids prior to sludge treatment. However, once treated, sludge is dewatered to up to 20% dry solids by separating solids from the reject water (centrate or side stream), which is recirculated. The ammonium (N–NH4+) concentration in the centrate typically ranges between 600–800 mg N–NH4/L.39 Recent efforts have explored alternative valorization approaches for sludge, focusing on recovering energy, valuable chemicals, and nutrients. Most of the studies prioritize converting the chemical energy available in the sludge into a useable form (heat or fuel) through biological or thermal processes.40

The electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge (sludge electrolysis and nutrient recovery processes) approach has shown potential for recovering nutrients and expanding waste-to-energy alternatives for sludge valorization. A schematic of the envisioned process is shown in Figure 1 using WAS as the raw material input. Process 1 in Figure 1 is sludge electrolysis.28 Sludge electrolysis begins with a preparation tank to adjust the pH of the stabilized sludge by introducing sodium hydroxide (potassium hydroxide is also an option). The slurry, with solids content ranging between 2% and 10%, enters a membrane-free electrochemical reactor. Transition metal electrodes (nickel, copper, or stainless steel) act as electrocatalysts to oxidize organic matter under mild temperatures, low cell voltage, and oscillating potentials within the reactor. This process directly converts organic N and P into soluble inorganic forms in the aqueous media. The efficacy of this process is hypothesized to be due to electrochemically enhanced hydrolysis, as reported by Lu and Botte.41

Vision of the sludge electrolysis process integrated into a wastewater treatment facility. Products include nitrogen-based fertilizer (e.g., NH3), phosphorus base fertilizer (PBF), and an advanced soil amendment (CSC).

Sludge electrolysis has been demonstrated with transition metal catalysts like copper, stainless steel, and nickel at <2.5 V.26 These electrocatalysts facilitate direct recovery of N as NH3 and P from WAS. Importantly, this sludge treatment also yielded a highly porous soil amendment byproduct that is free of Escherichia coli.27 The achieved porosity of the solid carbon is vital for enhancing soil properties and optimizing agricultural effects. Moreover, the chemical transformations promoted with this approach can generate valuable products such as hydrogen and volatile fatty acids (acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, and isovaleric acid), with mild energy consumption (up to 0.37 kW h kg–1 dry solids).26,42 These findings suggest that electrochemical processes can be energetically competitive when compared to, for example, anaerobic digestion processes, which, despite decades of optimization, continue to require up to 0.2 kW h kg–1 dry solids.43 Overall, the shift from high-voltage to low-voltage electrochemistry decreases the energy requirements and increases the specificity of the chemical processing of sludge. Still, the low-voltage approaches enable nutrient recovery and efficient wastewater valorization to necessary fertilizer components.

In the second step, the liquid from the electrochemical treatment is centrifuged to separate the solid from the liquid fraction, rich in soluble inorganic N and P. The separated liquid is then directed to a crystallization unit (process 2 in Figure 1), where quicklime (CaO) is added to precipitate calcium phosphates (CaP), the primary component of PBF like TSP. Following the crystallization unit, the remaining liquid fraction undergoes a separation process for NH3 recovery, utilizing carriers to concentrate it in the gas phase (process 3 in Figure 1). The alkaline stream exiting the N separator, with low NH3 content, is returned to the preparation tank and wastewater treatment plant. The solid stream from the centrifuge is dried to 50% moisture content to produce a solid fertilizer called CASFER super compost (CSC) (process 4).

A further advantage of the electrically assisted conversion of sludge compared to thermal techniques is integrating this unit operation with existing wastewater treatment workflows. Thermal processes require high capital investment due to the extreme operating conditions, and they demand extensive optimization before integration into a full workflow, which can disrupt the operation of wastewater treatment plants. In contrast, electrochemical approaches support more accessible assessment during testing and scale-up, facilitating technology adoption.

Techno-Economic Analysis

The study considered critical factors such as operating costs, energy efficiency, and potential revenue streams. The financial viability of the project under different cost scenarios was measured as NPV in U.S. million dollars (US$ MM) and IRR. The case study was performed using typical costs scenarios in the U.S.

Tipping fees play a significant role in the profitability of the project with more than half of the potential revenue coming from this source (Figure 2). The tipping fee assumed for the base case (US$80 per t of wet sludge or cake sludge) represents a lower value to what WWTP are typically paying to dispose of the sludge, which includes landfilling gate fees and hauling costs. For example, total disposal costs average US$172 per t of wet sludge, with projected increases up to of US$210–276 by 2028 in the state of Massachusetts.44 In 2023, the average landfilling gate fee (not including hauling) in the U.S. was US$56.80 per t, with regional variations including a maximum average of US$84.44 per t in the northeast and a minimum of US$43.18 per t in the southeast.45 In the EU the cost of landfilling (gate fee and landfill tax) of nonhazardous municipal waste varies considerably by country. For example, it can reach as high as €200 per t in the United Kingdom, while countries like Sweden, Denmark, and Finland have costs around €110 per t.46

Extending the results for the aggregated wastewater treatment plants in the U.S. (15,014) shows that the electrically assisted conversion of sludge could potentially recover up to 1.1 Mt–N and 0.5 Mt–P as TSP. The fertilizer production for this model is equivalent to 9% of the N and 32% of the P fertilizer consumption of the U.S. The profitability of the technology is supported by the growth of the NH3 global market size and the phosphate fertilizer market size, which are projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 6.51% from 2022 to 2030 and 5.7% from 2022 to 2040, respectively.48,49 Additionally, the biosolids global market size is predicted to reach US$2.7 Billion at a growth rate of 4.6% by 2033.50

These findings underscore the substantial potential of the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge to generate more than US$2–12 billion in revenues annually from 15,014 facilities (34,500 MGD of wastewater)5 and provide insights about the targets and capabilities that must be developed to realize its full industrial application. Furthermore, integrating additional wastewater treatment plant streams into the process could amplify the influence on fertilizer production and economics. A potentially more profitable framework involves recovering nutrients using the influent to the treatment plant. Notably, the N content in the influent can be up to six times greater than that found in WAS.3 Additionally, the economic viability of the proposed technology can be further enhanced by offsetting costs associated with traditional waste treatment methods, employing biofuels such as hydrogen, recovering metals, and volatile fatty acids.37 The substantial economic potential of metals, often categorized as toxic and subject to regulation in biosolids, has already been demonstrated. High value elements found in sewage sludges, such as silver, copper, gold, phosphorus, iron, palladium, manganese, zinc, iridium, aluminum, cadmium, titanium, gallium, and chromium, have been assessed at US$480 per dry t.37

The conversion of sludge into NBFs and PBFs through the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge reduces the load of conventional disposal, aligns with a circular and sustainable approach to wastewater treatment, and enables resilient and sustainable food production through decarbonized fertilizers’ capture, recycling, and production. Electrolysis offers several advantages over conventional treatments like incineration or anaerobic digestion, such as selective contaminant removal, valuable resource recovery, environmental sustainability, and energy efficiency. Its scalability and automation make it adaptable to varying sludge composition, volumes, and management needs. The potential of this technology can also be extrapolated to other significant feedstock streams, such as food waste and concentrated animal feeding operations.26,51

Discussion

Opportunities Related to the Electrically Assisted Conversion of Sewage Sludge for the Recovery of Nutrients

Examining gaps related to the recovery of resources and developing the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge as a modular and distributed nutrient recovery technology from organic waste to produce NBF and PBF has prompted various research and development endeavors. One major bottleneck relates to existing inventories of sewage sludge production, which often include out-of-date figures. A better estimate of the sewage sludge produced annually is crucial for accurately assessing the extent of planned resource recovery initiatives and strengthening action plans. Although technologies focusing on the recovery of N and P are well described in the literature, few pilot projects and commercial-scale operations have been developed.52 Overcoming the limitations of the current technologies demands innovative solutions that address existing technology gaps and prompt the development of new and advanced technologies within the circular economy and food security framework. Opportunities for the recovery of P and N are described below.

Process 1: Electrocatalysis and Computational Modeling

The optimization of the electrocatalytic processes for the extraction of nutrients during sludge electrolysis opens new avenues for research. Specifically, developing novel catalysts and enhancements in electrode stability and lifespan hold the potential for achieving more effective, efficient, and compact processes. Additionally, reactor design emerges as a critical factor in overcoming limitations arising from the thermodynamic and rheological properties of sludge.25 Research efforts on designing new configurations and materials for sludge electrolysis can be significantly supported by leveraging computational modeling techniques.

At present, computational models are not widely used in the field of sludge treatment. At a molecular level, WAS is composed of a wide range of molecules, with compositions that vary based on numerous factors related to processing history and location, making it difficult to model at a molecular level. Moreover, process models for electrochemical systems are often dynamic and fall outside the capabilities of standard software like ASPEN.53 Nonetheless, computational modeling has proven helpful in the design and discovery of electrocatalytic materials for reactions ranging from hydrogen evolution to CO2 reduction,54 and electrochemical process models are being increasingly used to provide techno-economic insights into the scale-up of nascent electrochemical technologies such as CO2 reduction.55,56 This suggests that overcoming the challenges associated with modeling electrochemical sludge conversion may enable accelerated discovery of new catalytic materials and more efficient optimization of process efficiency and economics.

To effectively navigate the complexities of translating WAS into computationally manageable molecular compositions, a structured hierarchy is proposed, as illustrated in Figure 4. This hierarchy delineates five stages, ranging from the complex and varied chemical makeup of real sludge to fundamental chemical bonds in the sludge system. It serves as a conceptual framework for incrementally understanding and modeling electrochemical reactions within actual sludge matrices. Although the goal is to establish computational models, careful fundamental experiments will be critical to navigating the hierarchy of chemical complexity. At the pinnacle of this hierarchy lies real sludge (1), embodying the entirety of chemical and practical complexity due to its diverse real-world compositions. The next level, model sludge (2), aims to capture all the chemical complexity of real sludge but has a fixed composition that facilitates reproducibility compared to computational models. This composition is based on a ratio of model components (3), which aim to capture the full physicochemical properties of the individual components of model sludge but have well-defined chemical structures. Many model components are macromolecules, such as proteins or lipids, which can be isolated and used to develop physicochemical models that can be directly compared to experiments. These molecules are still too large to be treated with quantum mechanical methods. However, these model components generally comprise model compounds (4) representing smaller molecules, typically monomers of the model components, and simplify the molecular complexity and physical properties while retaining representative chemical bonds. This level is particularly amenable to cheminformatics approaches and is often small enough to be modeled with quantum mechanical methods. At the base of the hierarchy are the model bonds (5), which include individual elementary steps across all model compounds, capturing the nature of bonds between different functional groups. These model bonds are particularly relevant to catalyst design and discovery, where “descriptor-based analysis” and “volcano plots” are commonly used to identify promising materials based on adsorption and transition-state energies. Ultimately, the goal of this hierarchy is to aid in developing both process models that require knowledge of the physicochemical properties of sludge and catalyst discovery approaches that leverage molecular-scale insight to identify new catalyst materials.

Illustration of proposed complexity hierarchy to facilitate the development of process and atomistic models for electrocatalytic sludge conversion.

In addition to the inherent challenges of sewage sludge, variability in incoming feedstocks to the WWTP and the final product (sewage sludge) can further complicate subsequent treatment processes. This upstream and downstream variability, characterized by fluctuations in composition and properties, could motivate the development of models that dynamically adjust to these changing conditions. Artificial intelligence and machine learning offer significant promise in this area, as they have the potential to analyze complex data streams, identify patterns in variability, and ultimately optimize treatment processes in real-time.57 Although machine learning models have recently been used to predict sludge yield in WWTP, the amount of chemicals added during treatment, and pollutant removals, the accuracy of these predictions can be enhanced by employing deep learning algorithms.57,58 New treatments may be modeled, but the method will require accumulating relevant operating data.

Process 2: Phosphorus Recovery

Process 2 in Figure 1 encompasses the units necessary for P recovery in the form of CaP after mixing the liquid fraction coming from the centrifuge with CaO. This stage entails a series of operations that transform dissolved P into hydroxyapatites or CaP, which can be used as fertilizers. Furthermore, CaP boasts a broader range of applications in various industries and can be obtained through chemical precipitation, although competition with the precipitation of calcium carbonate may arise.59 Despite struvite (MgNH4PO4·6H2O) having a higher fertilizer value than CaPs, the costs associated with chemical additions for P recovery through CaPs precipitation are estimated to be approximately 1 order of magnitude lower than those for struvite production.60

Calcium phosphate precipitation is influenced by factors such as ion concentrations, supersaturation, ionic strength, temperature, pH, and residence time.61 Precipitation starts with the production of octocalcium phosphate (Ca8H2(PO4)6·5H2O), dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (CaHPO4·2H2O), and dicalcium phosphate anhydrate (CaHPO4) at ∼pH = 7. Subsequently, amorphous calcium phosphate (Cax(PO4)y·nH2O) and tricalcium phosphate (Ca3(PO4)2) precipitate. These phases eventually transform into the most stable and common form of Ca–P precipitate: hydroxyapatite (Ca5(PO4)3OH) at high pH.59−61 Maintaining an optimal pH between 7 and 9.5 is crucial for achieving high P recovery and preventing excessive calcium carbonate formation.60 Before large-scale implementation of P recovery through CaP precipitation, certain features need to be addressed, such as examining the P crystallization kinetics, assessing the influence of soluble calcium recycling, and managing particle size distribution during precipitation and ion concentration to avoid struvite precipitation.

As waste valorization aims to employ recovered nutrients for agriculture, the efficiency of recovery is essential, along with the physicochemical form of the nutrients, to ensure that they can be effectively used in agriculture. It is important to highlight that the solubility, type, dose, application method, exposure period, and structure of these calcium phosphates are essential for their bioavailability to plants. For instance, the application of hydroxyapatite nanoparticles at concentrations of 50 and 100 mg/L was found to enhance the growth characteristics, nutrient and mineral content, and overall yield and production of the sweet corn crop, but this formulation can also result in nanotoxicity to other crops.62 Understanding the release and bioavailability of recycled CaP products must be approached on a case-by-case basis since the effectiveness of the fertilizer depends on several soil-related factors.59

Another promising technology for future evaluation in wastewater phosphorus recovery in the form of phosphates involves aluminum-doped magnetic nanoparticles as adsorbents. These particles have demonstrated a maximum adsorption capacity of 102.15 (mg PO4/g) at pH levels ranging from 4 to 9, surpassing significantly the values reported for other adsorbents such as polyacrylamide-coated Fe3O4, magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, and core–shell Fe3O4-layered double hydroxides composite. Additionally, they possess a high surface area and are easy to recover through solid–liquid separation, being recyclable and highly selective even in the presence of other anions and organic materials such as fats and proteins.63 Along with controlling aluminum concentration limits and optimizing the separation process, the bioavailability of the recovered phosphorus for plant uptake should also be evaluated to ensure the effectiveness of this technique for phosphorus-based fertilizer production.

Process 3: Nitrogen Recovery

Recovering dissolved NH3 in the N-separator unit (process 3 in Figure 1) represents an additional challenge in the nutrient recovery from the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge. Current processes involve struvite precipitation, biological treatment, air stripping, ion exchange, membrane processes, and other conventional recovery strategies. However, many challenges have restricted their application at scale to date. Air stripping is a well-established technique already employed in treating concentrated wastewater effluents such as municipal solid waste, landfill leachate, swine manure, and anaerobic digestion.64 This approach efficiently separates and recovers NH3 as ammonium sulfate, a valuable fertilizer.65 The process involves the application of alkali substances like NaOH, Ca(OH)2, or CaO to maintain a critical pH in the range of 8.5–12.66,67 These components are then mixed with acid for the absorption of NH3 and the production of ammonium sulfate. Despite the high recovery efficiency of this process (85.5–98%), it can be energy-intensive due to the associated costs of air blowers and sample heating (20–85 °C).64,66 Further drawbacks include considerable operational costs associated with using sulfuric acid (71%), significant space requirements, sensitivity to water quality, and foaming, fouling or scaling of stripping towers.64−67

One emerging alternative for NH3 enrichment is membrane separation technology. Electrochemical stripping is a new approach to recover N that incorporates electrodialysis and membrane stripping in a three-chamber reactor. It employs a cation exchange membrane to separate N based on charge, while a gas-permeable membrane facilitates volatility. Hydroxyl ions generated in the cathode chamber eliminate the need for alkaline addition and aid in volatilization and transport through the gas-permeable membrane. NH3 is collected as liquid fertilizer, specifically ammonium sulfate, in the trap chamber. Electrochemical stripping demonstrates N removal and recovery efficiencies >73% and >65%, respectively, across N concentrations ranging from 30 to 3000 mg N/L. Furthermore, recovery efficiency improves with temperatures between 15 and 35 °C.68 Conversely, membrane distillation (MD) allows volatile compounds to pass through a hydrophobic microporous membrane while nonvolatile components are selectively retained.69 Factors such as temperature, pH, feed velocity, liquid entry pressure, feed composition, membrane type and thickness, and pore size influence the membrane removal and recovery.69 MD of municipal wastewater and synthetic sludge concentrate using polypropylene and polytetrafluoroethylene membranes at pH 8.5 and temperatures between 60 and 85 °C has achieved NH3 removal ranging from 21.34 to 99.8%.69

Electrothermally driven MD can be employed to reduce MD energy consumption. This process uses conductive films for faster and more efficient heating, enabling localized heating and uniform temperature distribution.70 Recently, cost-effectively reduced graphene oxide substrates with Joule heating capabilities have been developed, holding potential for electrothermally driven NH3 distillation.71 Integrating sludge electrolysis with electrothermally driven MD makes it feasible to extract N as NH3 from the pre-existing alkaline liquid stream (obtained after P recovery), transforming N into a gaseous state capable of permeating hydrophobic membranes. This process offers a more environmentally friendly way to recover and purify NH3. It can be an alternative to traditional methods, making it an attractive option. Nonetheless, several challenges emerge for large-scale applications that can hinder operational efficiency and escalate costs. Among these challenges are the deterioration of process performance due to wetting in long-term operations, membrane fouling, and expenses associated with temperature adjustments.

The capture and storage of NH3 through adsorption under mild conditions in refillable cartridges is the complement for electrothermally driven MD (process 3 in Figure 1). The adsorption of NH3 using porous materials such as activated carbon, metal halides (CuCl2, MnCl2, MgCl2, and CaCl2), and metal–organic frameworks presents a promising alternative to traditional methods like large tanks or pressurized containers. Adsorption offers several advantages, including high removal efficiency, ease of operation, and low energy consumption.72 CuCl2 and MnCl2 have an NH3 adsorption capacity of 551 mg N–NH3/g adsorbent and 418 mg N–NH3/g adsorbent at 25 °C, respectively.73 When selecting carriers or adsorbents, it is crucial to consider that certain materials can be utilized as slow-release fertilizers after the last cycle of adsorption and desorption. The use of these materials can enhance soil physicochemical properties, microbial capacity, and soil fertility.72 In contrast, refillable cartridges offer advantages in terms of waste reduction and environmental impact since they can be regenerated and reused once saturated. Refillable cartridges simplify logistics and handling across the supply chain while eliminating the need for disposable fertilizer bags. Furthermore, allowing farmers to refill cartridges as needed reduces storage requirements and may eliminate the necessity for bulk purchases. The successful implementation of this technology depends on addressing the logistical challenges and safety concerns that the distribution of fertilizers or cartridges presents for decentralized operations among farmers. It is imperative to address these obstacles to achieve widespread adoption.

Factors affecting NH3 capture, such as adsorbent material, pore size, effective surface area, dosage, pH, pressure, and contact time, play a crucial role in the adsorption mechanisms (physisorption or chemisorption) and, therefore, the separation performance.72,74−76 The influence of moisture in the cycling capabilities and chemical and thermal stability of adsorbents has not been well studied, and competitive adsorption between H2O and NH3 molecules can impair selective adsorption.72,74 Addressing the development of cost-effective adsorbents viable for regeneration is also essential.

Just as was described for P delivery, the physicochemical form of N is also critical for the delivery of NH3. Thus, as new ammonium salts and materials for NH3 adsorption are generated, their application directly to agricultural soils must be studied. The short-term and long-term impacts of these materials on both soil composition and crop production will be critical metrics for their efficacy. Similarly, the energy intensity and difficulty in generating materials for NH3 capture must be considered in techno-economic assessments and life cycle assessments for NH3 recovery.

Other Potential Streams of Interest

Liquid streams derived from sewage sludge dewatering, a byproduct known as centrate, present a promising opportunity for NH3 recovery due to its high N concentration. Centrate can be introduced directly into process 3 to increase the amount of NH3 recovered. Importantly, for recovery of any target from an aqueous stream, technologies related to liquid extraction and recovery are generally better studied and more mature than complementary soil extraction approaches. Thus, the overall complexity of integrating N recovery from the centrate is expected to be lower than that from sludge. In addition to N recovery, potential byproducts of sludge electrolysis, such as VFAs, can be separated using techniques similar to those used for NH3 capture. While capturing short-chain acids is relatively straightforward, isolating individual acids from this mixture is challenging due to the similar properties of VFAs,77 their low concentrations (1–100 g/L), and the potential formation of water-acid azeotropes in the solution.78−81 Thus, the energy requirements necessary for different levels of isolation and purity for these compounds must be considered as a factor in the degree of target separation. In many applications (including agriculture), short-chain acids can be used by microbes in the soil or as symbiotes with plants. In contrast, a high valuable utilization of VFAs involves conversion into esters or polymerizing to produce bioplastics.

Process 4: Biosolids as Fertilizers

Process 4 in Figure 1 involves the implementation of electrolyzed sludge as CSC for land application. The electrolyzed sludge is dried to achieve 50% moisture content and packaged to ensure plant nutrient bioavailability. This process is crucial to ensure that the nutrients essential for plant growth in the sludge are readily available. It is important that soil scientists investigate the impact of the fertilizer derived from electrolyzed sludge on soil health, but those results are beyond the scope of this article. CSC can be a valuable soil additive but can only be used on soil if it complies with Class A or B regulations according to the U.S. EPA or the Sludge Directive for the Biosolid land application in the EU or the EU member individual regulations.9 These regulations ensure the safety of the final product by limiting pathogens and pollutants.

Biological Considerations

In the U.S., Subpart D of the Biosolids Rule details mandatory requirements for pathogen (40 CFR §503.32) and vector attraction reduction (VAR, §503.33)82 in sludges applied to land or placed on a surface disposal site. Biosolids are classified as either Class A or Class B, depending upon the extent of microbial inactivation. Class A Biosolids are considered free of the four major types of human pathogenic organisms (bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and helminths). They can be distributed and land-applied without restrictions to the public. Class B Biosolids must be treated or tested for microbial pathogens; pathogen loads are reduced, but solids are not pathogen-free. Land application of Class B biosolids is highly regulated, use is restricted to specific sites, and site monitoring and testing are required after application. In contrast, Class A Biosolids are suitable for unrestricted sale and use when of “exceptional quality”83 and, therefore, appeal to a larger potential market segment.

Class A Biosolid candidates must demonstrate sufficient reduction of bacterial indicator species (either fecal coliform or Salmonella), pathogen reduction must be accomplished prior or simultaneously to VAR, and pathogen reduction must adhere to one of six outlined treatment alternatives (Alternatives 1–6, 40 CFR §503.32).82 In contrast, in most of the EU member states, the standard limit for pathogens like Salmonella or, E. coli in biosolids is less or equal to 1000 colony forming unit (cfu) per gram dry matter.84 The requirements to reduce bacterial indicator species and to achieve pathogen reduction and VAR simultaneously are not likely to be significant barriers to entry for emerging technologies, provided the technologies inactivate bacteria and reduce biodegradable organic carbon.

For an emerging technology such as the electrically assisted conversion of sludge, it may initially seem challenging to meet the requirements of Alternatives 1–6 for pathogen reduction in U.S. The electrochemical treatment does not follow the compositing, heat drying/treatment, thermophilic aerobic digestion, irradiation, or pasteurization regimes described in Alternative 5. At the same time, Alternative 4 (intended for natural treatment processes) is not applicable, while the 72 h contact time detailed in Alternative 2 (alkaline hydrolysis) will substantially increase reaction time,85,86 and Alternative One thermal requirements are not met. On the other hand, electrochemical treatment could meet federal guidelines in US by performing comprehensive pathogen monitoring (alternative 3) or “equivalent” per U.S. EPA’s Pathogen Equivalency Committee (alternative 683). To meet equivalency, electrochemical treatment must consistently and reliably reduce pathogen prevalence in sewage sludge to the same levels achievable in approved technologies, and this must be demonstrated to the regulatory agencies. Results reported by Jafari and Botte87, demonstrate the disinfection capacity of the process at bench-scale. However, compliant data is still needed to demonstrate that the specific conditions for sludge electrolysis support sufficient microbial disinfection. The inactivation mechanisms require further investigation and clarification since the temperature regimes cause by the electrical heat production varies with the solids content, electrode material, inorganic and organic salt concentration in the sludge, electrolyte, and the current type applied.87 The alkaline pH used for the electrically assisted conversion of sludge is not likely to inactivate helminth eggs, which are stable in alkaline environments below temperatures of 55 °C.83,88,89 The fate of helminth eggs in electrified environments is poorly understood,90 which must be investigated if equivalency is to be pursued.

Non-Biological Micropollutants

The EU and the U.S. EPA stringently regulate metals in sewage sludge for agricultural use. The main heavy metals in sewage are cadmium, copper, nickel, lead, mercury, and chromium.82,91 While upstream treatment processes influence the metallic composition of biosolids, metals in influent wastewater generally concentrate within sludges. The removal of metals from the solid phase usually results in a metal-laden liquid phase that may also be subject to regulatory limits, and wastewater treatment technologies do not typically target metals. However, metals are increasingly important recycling targets, as many sustainable technologies rely on metals like rare earth elements for batteries and photovoltaics to power them. Thus, targeting specific metal removal and recycling in sludge could support clean energy technologies while ensuring sludge complies with heavy metal regulatory levels.

Trace organic contaminants that have been detected in biosolids but are not regulated in U.S. include hydrophobic (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, polychlorinated biphenyls, organohalogens, and pesticides/herbicides), hydrophilic (surfactants, hormones, and pharmaceutically active compounds) and colloidal (micro- and nano plastics) substances. In contrast, the Sludge Directive in the EU presents the standard limits for polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins and furan, absorbable organic halogens, nonylphenols, polychlorinated biphenyls, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, among others.9 All have the potential to contaminate the products intended for sale.52,93−95 Consumer concerns regarding organic pollutants in biosolids-derived products are persistent and could potentially hamper retail markets for these fertilizers.92 Commercial products made from recycled or organic materials that are intended to be sold in the European internal market are regulated by the European Parliament and the Council of the EU.96

Pharmaceutically active compounds have been detected in treated sludge and, consequently, in soil following sludge application.97 Strongly sorbing pharmaceuticals tend to accumulate in the soil, while others leach into groundwater. For instance, antibiotics such as oxytetracycline (100.9 ng/g), tetracycline (63.8 ng/g), and sulfamethoxazole (47.9 ng/g) have been detected in soil after sludge application, and substances like ibuprofen and diclofenac have been identified in the leachates of soil amended with urban sludge.97 Perfluorooctane sulfonic acid and perfluorooctanoic acid, members of the per- and poly fluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) family, are frequently detected in biosolids95,98,99 and are highly recalcitrant,100,101 suggesting that a PFAS biosolid regulation, would be highly disruptive to existing biosolid treatment processes. It is therefore critical that the fate of PFAS be considered when developing novel sludge treatment technologies. Even if PFAS are not ultimately regulated, the negative public perception associated with PFAS, pharmaceuticals, and other organics naturally present in wastewater suggests that fertilizer technologies that demonstrate consistent organic pollutant removal or destruction are valuable from a marketing perspective.

Integrating Biological Advances with Electrochemical Waste Valorization

Biological processes are some of the most well-established wastewater treatment systems, including both anaerobic digestion and aerobic composting described previously. Challenges with these approaches remain, though, as they are nonspecific and often degrade components of wastewater input carbon sources to GHGs such as methane and carbon dioxide. The versatility and diversity of ecological microbes support their potential for more targeted applications in tandem with emerging electrochemical approaches. The range of these targeted strategies is broad, but the focus here will be on microbial technologies compatible with electrochemical systems.

As described above, many microbes are incompatible with electrochemical systems, with many bacteria succumbing to lysis and cell death even under low-potential conditions. Thus, all emergent biological approaches must be evaluated in pilot-scale systems to confirm that they remain viable even in the presence of electrodes.

Because most soil nutrients in wastewater and sludge are trapped in organic compounds, mineralization of these compounds is of significant interest. Nontargeted degradation is most often employed for biodegradation in sludge, which is generally uncharacterized, so complex microbial consortia are most often used. Microbes from wastewater treatment plant activated sludge,102,103 soil,104−107 and marine sediment106,108 have been evaluated for degradation of N and P-containing compounds and environmental contaminants such as drug molecules and pesticides. Yet knowledge of the composition of these consortia and the degradation pathways in them remains limited.109 Of individual microbes, two genuses are most often cited for energy-efficient sludge conversions: Shewanella and Geobacter. These microbes are exoelectrogens, meaning they can respire extracellularly under anaerobic conditions. In native niches, they apply this pathway to reduce insoluble metal oxides, solubilizing the metal cations. In engineered environments, these microbes can either degrade organic compounds in wastewater while generating current at an electrode or can use those electrons to solubilize insoluble sludge components. Because these microbes are frequently grown in the presence of electrodes, there is little concern with their deployment in EVOW systems. These results are promising, but microbial methods remain limited due to the necessity of maintaining microbial viability despite the potential toxicity of the chemicals targeted for degradation and the resultant degradation products.

In addition to complete microbe-based degradation, enzymes offer targeted degradation of particular chemical bonds. Because enzymes are protein catalysts, they do not have the same metabolic burden as whole-cell systems, making them attractive for less costly and energy-intensive biodegradations. However, enzymes are often unstable under variable temperatures, high or low pHs, and applied electrochemical potentials. The significance of emergent technologies such as those described here is that they are expected to readily translate from lab-scale observations to field-deployable technologies. Herein, we encounter three critical challenges in bioremediation: (1) the requirement of a stable, biological culture for continuous expression of enzymes; (2) the potential undesired escape of these engineered organisms into natural ecosystems; and (3) the instability of enzymatic degradation in environments outside carefully controlled biological buffers. Surface-expression technologies share a common motif: an artificially fused transmembrane element that extends into the hydrophobic space of the cell membrane, while the fused protein is exposed to the extracellular space. Prevalent surface expression systems include the adhesin involved in diffuse adherence-I (AIDA-I), a chimeric fusion of Braun’s lipoprotein to membrane-spanning segments of the OmpA porin (Lpp’OmpA), and an ice nucleation protein from Pseudomonas syringae with a hydrophobic N-terminal domain (INPNC).110 Applications of this tag to date have focused on environmental contaminants, with surface-expressed human hormone receptors to capture xenoestrogenic contaminants and organophosphate hydrolase enzymes to degrade pesticides. Critically, these proteins remain active even following freeze-drying of the microbes and cost less than US$10 per dried kg of cells. Such technologies are expected to support precise degradation of targets, and as the microbes are not viable following freeze-drying, issues with electrochemical compatibility are circumvented.

Policy

WWTPs worldwide are facing the growing challenge of sludge management and disposal. At the same time, farmers continue to depend on synthetic fertilizers that frequently pollute waterways and air. Targeted policies can be implemented to solve both problems together. Regulations regarding the constituent limits, sludge testing, and reporting obligations for land application, surface disposal, and incineration of sewage sludge can vary significantly depending on the internal policies within states or nations. These regulations often reflect the local environmental, health, and safety priorities of each jurisdiction. For land application and surface disposal, constituents of concern focus on pathogens, organic pollutants, heavy metals111 and incineration standards, including air emission requirements. Additionally, offsite disposal in landfills or incineration facilities is also bound by transportation regulations, including chain of custody records and spill reporting requirements. Even when land application complies with regulations, it can still produce high values of N and P, contributing to eutrophication. To address this issue and promote sustainable soil management, the European Commission has adopted several initiatives, including the EU Soil Biodiversity Strategy 2030, which aims to monitor and improve soil health across the EU, the Zero Pollution Action Plan, the EU Climate Adaptation Strategy, and the Soil Monitoring Law.112

The willingness of a WWTP to transition from traditional sludge management practices to newly engineered solutions will likely be determined by whether the new methodologies reduce regulatory compliance burdens, decrease required treatment or disposal costs, and provide new revenue streams. Even lower cost strategies, like land application, are challenging when the volume of product outpaces areas that can receive waste, transport distances increase, or regulatory standards become more stringent.

Compliance with the tapestry of regulatory obligations can be costly and time-consuming for WWTPs. Linear sludge management overlooks the potential to recover critical nutrients that can be utilized by other industries while also reducing pollutant emissions. Conversion of sewage sludge to a useable fertilizer alleviates some financial burdens through avoided disposal costs and the reduction of compliance obligations while also solving the concern of surging sludge volumes. The ability to sell the resulting product would further offset costs. Financial incentives for recovered products, reduction or elimination of environmental impacts, and offsets of harmful treatment methods are essential for the economic competitiveness of new technologies with a specific focus on sustainability and circular economy models.

Conclusions

In the dynamic landscape of wastewater management, current challenges are opportunities waiting to be transformed. Although a portion of sewage sludge has already been used for land application, it has limited economic and environmental benefits.113 Among several technologies for the recovery of nutrients from sludge, the electrically assisted conversion of sludge, a nascent and circular economy-focused approach, stands out as a promising solution for sewage sludge valorization by commercializing NBFs and PBFs with high demand and value in the market. The global fertilizer market, valued at US$171.76 billion in 2020, is projected to surpass US$240 billion by 2030.114 The ongoing research and development in this field has the potential to catalyze transformative changes in wastewater treatment facilities in the long run, promoting process intensification, innovation, and sustainability since its implementation aligns directly with the principles of a circular economy and three main sustainable development goals: SDG2 (Zero hunger), SDG6 (clean water and sanitation), and SDG13 (Climate action). The economic benefits of the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge are significant, encompassing revenue generated from marketable products such as solid organic fertilizer (CSC), NH3, and P. Additionally, other revenue streams have yet to be calculated.

To unlock the full potential of this technology for commercial-level applications, it is crucial to align research efforts with critical barriers related to modeling, electrocatalysis optimization, and selective separation processes. Future work for the widespread practice of the technology should target the design of new cell configurations and electrocatalysts intended for high solid concentration, investigation of the feedstock variability on the electrochemical performance, electrocatalytic mechanisms, electrode scaling, and microbial-enhanced electrocatalysis. The effectiveness of fertilizers depends on soil type, crop variety, growth stage, management practices, and specific characteristics of the amendment, such as slow-release nutrient properties, porosity, and microstructure.115 Therefore, understanding the influence of these factors on N and P fixation in plants would contribute to the design of strategies for sustainable agricultural crop production and would lead to the optimization of operational conditions. Particular attention should be given to formulating fertilizers and designing flexible and cost-effective methods for drying, storing, and transporting fertilizers.

Life Cycle Analysis is a fundamental tool for determining the most cost-effective separation technologies that, when combined with sludge electrolysis, enable the sustainable production of fertilizers on a large scale. This method meticulously evaluates and compares net energy consumption while quantifying environmental benefits such as emissions, land usage, and water footprints.37 Furthermore, it is vital to quantify other services crucial to society, like safeguarding and enhancing water and air quality, optimizing wastewater treatment plant operations and facility life extension, and fostering social equity. These efforts will translate into improved prices for farmers, heightened crop productivity, and enhanced food security. Life cycle assessment of the electrically assisted conversion of sewage sludge could potentially advocate for the reinvention of the entire wastewater treatment facility production, particularly in the disposal of sewage sludge and biosolids.

Additionally, inventory updates are critical to assessing the scale of planned resource recovery at national levels and reinforcing national action plans. Although the regulatory framework aims to reduce carbon emissions and exert pressure on adopting sustainable technologies, it is still necessary to have policy frameworks or guidelines for promoting the production and use of fertilizers derived from recovered products. This can drive the integration of sludge electrolysis into existing treatment processes, offering both economic and environmental benefits. Likewise, the rationale for generating economic and financial benefits through the revenues of electrochemical sludge treatment could drive the establishment of public-private partnerships. These partnerships would involve raising capital and overseeing technology operations, facilitating project financing, and enabling the introduction of recovered nutrient products to the agriculture sector in the US.
