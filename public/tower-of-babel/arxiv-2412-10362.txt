OP-LoRA: The Blessing of Dimensionality
Piotr Teterwak, Kate Saenko, Bryan A. Plummer, Ser-Nam Lim, University of Central Florida, sernam@ucf.edu

Abstract
Low-rank adapters enable fine-tuning of large models with only a small number of parameters, thus reducing storage costs and minimizing the risk of catastrophic forgetting. However, they often pose optimization challenges, with poor convergence. To overcome these challenges, we introduce an over-parameterized approach that accelerates training without increasing inference costs. This method reparameterizes low-rank adaptation by employing a separate MLP and learned embedding for each layer. The learned embedding is input to the MLP, which generates the adapter parameters. Such overparamaterization has been shown to implicitly function as an adaptive learning rate and momentum, accelerating optimization. At inference time, the MLP can be discarded, leaving behind a standard low-rank adapter. To study the effect of MLP overparameterization on a small yet difficult proxy task, we implement it for matrix factorization, and find it achieves faster convergence and lower final loss. Extending this approach to larger-scale tasks, we observe consistent performance gains across domains. We achieve improvements in vision-language tasks and especially notable increases in image generation, with CMMD scores improving by up to 15 points.

Introduction

Despite their impressive zero-shot and few-shot capabilities
Radford et al. 2021
;
OpenAI 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Dubey et al. 2024
, fine-tuning large foundation models for specific tasks can provide significant performance gains but is often compute- and memory-intensive, with risks of catastrophic forgetting
Ruiz et al. 2024
;
Cho et al. 2021a
;
Biderman et al. 2024
. Methods utilizing low-rank adapters
Hu et al. 2022
;
Hayou et al.
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al.
;
Nikdan et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
mitigate these challenges by introducing compact, low-rank parameter updates, enabling efficient task adaptation without additional inference costs or large memory demands (
Fig.

1
a). Despite their advantages, low-rank adaptation methods pose significant optimization challenges. These optimization difficulties can result in suboptimal convergence and, potentially, poor task performance. This is observed in practice by
Biderman et al. 2024
, where the authors show a large sensitivity to learning rates in LoRA.

Prior attempts to address this issue include LoRA+
Hayou et al.
, which proposes using separate learning rates for the up-projection and down-projection matrices, though this approach requires careful tuning of both rates. PiSSA
Meng et al. 2024
initializes the LoRA weights to the principal components of the weight matrix and removes them from the frozen base weights; however, this breaks the intuition that LoRA serves as a low-rank update to a strong base model, making it instead an update to a weaker model and thus more prone to overfitting. Consequently, there is a need for alternative approaches that retain the benefits of low-rank adaptation while easing optimization.

We propose a overparameterization of low-rank adapters using a compact multi-layer perceptron (OP-LoRA). Instead of directly learning low-rank matrices, we introduce an MLP that predicts parameter matrices from a learned input vector (
Fig.

1
b). At first glance, this is a surprising solution. After all, any solution reached with reparameterization could, theoretically, be reached without it. Further, conventional wisdom equates more parameters with more optimization difficulty. However,
Arora et al. 2018
show the surprising result that over-parametrization acts as a particularly effective implicit gradient acceleration method in cases where all learned matrices can be collapsed together after training by computing their product.

With OP-LoRA, we aim to leverage this theoretical insight by over-parameterizing the low-rank adaptation process in a way that accelerates convergence without altering the expressivity of the learned function. A practical side effect of this over-parametrization is that the MLP’s can be discarded after training, making over-parameterized low-rank adapters just as efficient at inference time as their standard counterparts.

We start by studying MLP reparameterization in an intentionally difficult small-scale matrix factorization setting, where we observe faster convergence and reduced sensitivity to hyperparameters. Notably, MLP-overparameterized matrix factorization (OP-MF) often converges to the Singular Value Decomposition(SVD) solution with SGD, a result that standard matrix factorization frequently fails to achieve. Interestingly, overparameterization composes very well with accelerated gradient descent methods such as SGD with Momentum and Adam, indicating acceleration even for the most performant optimizers. Overall, we observe much accelerated convergence even on poorly conditioned problems.

Inspired by these initial findings, we apply MLP reparameterization to a diverse range of applications, including image generation, vision-language tasks, and pure language processing. We also re-parameterize DoRA
Liu et al.
, creating OP-DoRA. Our approach delivers consistently high performance. For example, fine-tuning Stable Diffusion for image generation yields a 15-point ClipMMD
Jayasumana et al. 2024
Score improvement on two datasets. These results establish MLP reparameterization as an effective method for fine-tuning, offering substantial gains while remaining lightweight at inference.

2
Related Work

Convergence Properties of Neural Networks:
Understanding the convergence behavior of neural networks has been a subject of significant research interest.
Du et al. 2019
find that gradient descent can find global optima in ResNets.
Nguyen and Hein 2017
show that under certain conditions, all local minima are actually also globally optimal.
Huang et al. 2020
explore the geometry of loss landscapes through visualization, and find that optimizers are biased to flat minima, which generalize well, due to the volume disparity between wide minima and sharp minima. They reason that in larger dimensional parameter space this disparity is larger, and coin the term the “blessing of dimensionality”. Most relevant to OP-LoRA, though, is
Arora et al. 2018
’s work showing that overparameterization can function as an implicit acceleration mechanism in gradient descent. We leverage
Arora et al. 2018
’s findings in our method design.

Low-rank Fine-Tuning:
Low-rank fine-tuning, specifically with LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
Huh et al. 2023
, has emerged as a powerful approach for adapting pre-trained models with minimal additional parameters. A number of follow ups have emerged, improving performance. AdaLoRA
Zhang et al. 2023
prunes weights during training. DoRA
Liu et al.
adds a magnitude scaling vector to the updated matrix. RoSA
Nikdan et al. 2024
adds a sparse weight update to the low-rank update, but requires a full-finetuning pass to compute the weight mask. OLoRA
Büyükakyüz 2024
and PiSSA
Meng et al. 2024
ease optimization by initializing LoRA orthogonally. However, they are prone to overfitting due to removing important components from the frozen base weights. We find PiSSA to give lower performance than standard LoRA for Stable Diffusion finetuning in Section
5.1
. In this work we focus mostly on over-parameterizing LoRA, since it is a primary shared component of all follow ups, and demonstrate that overparameterization improves performance on the recent highly performant variant DoRA.

Reparameterization with neural networks:

Ha et al. 2017
generate weights of an LSTM and CNN from a neural network. However, their focus is relaxing weight sharing in LSTMs and reducing parameter count in convolutional networks for image classification. In contrast, we leverage over-parameterization for improved convergence. HyperDreamBooth
Ruiz et al. 2024
generates initializations for LoRA parameters from an input image. In contrast, we learn our parameter-generating MLP from scratch. Most relevant to our work is Residual Prompt Tuning
Razdaibiedina et al. 2023
, which shows that re-parameterizing learned prompts for transformers as an MLP speeds up convergence and speeds up training. This provides additional evidence for the generality of MLP-based reparameterizations.

3
Preliminaries

Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA):
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has become a popular strategy for fine-tuning large models, allowing adaptation to new tasks by learning a low-rank matrix factorization of weight updates. With LoRA, fine-tuning a model layer’s weight matrix
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is achieved by learning an additive low-rank update
\Delta W
such that the adapted weights
W
are given by:

W=W_{0}+\Delta W=W_{0}+BA,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
are learned low-rank matrices, with
r\ll d
, reducing the number of parameters to learn. In an ideal scenario, the matrix factorization
\Delta W=BA
perfectly reconstructs the gradient update of full fine-tuning. Because neural networks weights have themselves been shown to be rank-deficient
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Huh et al. 2023
, such an approximation is reasonable. Though parameter-efficient, matrix factorization methods like LoRA are difficult to optimize. They are non-convex, and the update matrix may not be well conditioned. These concerns are born out in practice, as practitioners have found very high sensitivities of LoRA to learning rates
Biderman et al. 2024
.

Optimization Benefits of Overparameterization:

Arora et al. 2018
proves that replacing linear layers with products of overparameterized matrices, which has the same expressive power as a single matrix due to the linear nature of the transformation, leads to faster convergence. They motivate their work with a simple case study on linear regression, showing that by reparameterizing

L({\mathbf{w}})=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\left[\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}-y)^{2}\right]

as

L({\mathbf{w}}_{1},w_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\left[\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}w_{2}-y)^{2}\right]

where
{\mathbf{w}}
is the learned parameter vector, and
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}{w_{2}}
is a reparamterization of
{\mathbf{w}}
with learned vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
and learned scalar
{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
. Then the update becomes:

{\mathbf{w}}^{(t+1)}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\mapsto$}}{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}-\rho^{(t)}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}}-\sum\nolimits_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\mu^{(t,\tau)}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}^{(\tau)}}

where (
\rho^{(t)}
) is an adaptive learning rate and (
\mu^{(t,\tau)}
) is an adaptive momentum term. This implies that even the addition of a single scaling parameter can speed up learning by adding implicit adaptive learning rates and momentum. They go on to prove that for the linear neural network case, overparameterization accelerates training by easing learning in directions already over the course of optimization. OP-LoRA takes this intuition and overparameterizes further. We more completely review the mathematics of
Arora et al. 2018
in Appendix
1

4
OP-LoRA

Directly inspired by optimization benefits of
Arora et al. 2018
, we avoid directly optimizing
A
and
B
by introducing a two-layer MLP which takes as input
z
and predicts the entries of
A
and
B
. More formally,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=W_{2}(\text{ReLU}(W_{1}z+b_{1}))+b_{2}

where
z
is the learned input vector to the MLP,
W
and
b
correspond to learned weights biases, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
are the generated low-rank matrices.

Once fine-tuning is complete, the MLP can be discarded, retaining only the low-rank matrices
A
and
B
for inference. Furthermore , as in standard LoRA,
A
and
B
can be merged with the pre-trained model’s weights by adding
\Delta W=BA
to the relevant layer weights. Although the MLP is compact in depth, it predicts a high-dimensional output — the size of the LoRA parameters — which makes it overparameterized. For instance, an MLP with a hidden dimension of 32 scales the number of trainable parameters by approximately 32. This makes OP-LoRA particularly advantageous in settings where inference resources are constrained, but sufficient memory is available during training.

4.1
A Matrix Factorization Case Study

To study MLP overparameterization, we design a controlled matrix factorization experiment comparing MLP-generated low-rank matrices
A
and
B
with freely learned parameter matrices. This experimental setup allows us to systematically investigate the convergence behavior, gradient stability, and sensitivity to hyperparameters, offering insights into the practical advantages of OP-LoRA.

Matrix factorization decomposes a target matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
into two lower-dimensional matrices,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
, where
r
is the latent dimension or rank. This decomposition allows us to approximate
M
by
BA
. It can be solved exactly with SVD, or as in our study, one can use gradient descent to minimize the reconstruction error:

\|M-BA\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. This resembles LoRA-tuning, where the pre-trained base weights are set to all zeros and the target matrix
M
is the full-tuning gradient matrix, making it an interesting proxy problem to study.

Experimental setup and training protocol:
We construct a synthetic target matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{100\times 100}
with entries initialized uniformly at random from 0 to 1. The resulting matrix has a poor condition number, defined as the ratio between the largest singular value and lowest, making the optimization difficult and therefore a good test for OP-LoRA. We train for 1000 steps with SGD, with linear warmup for 50 steps and linear learning rate decay.

OP-MF Model:
The OP-MF model generates matrices
A
and
B
through two separate MLPs. We enforce both matrices to be of rank 8. Each MLP receives a learned input vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{128}
and processes it through two fully connected layers with 32 hidden units and ReLU activations, outputting the entries for either
A
or
B
. The second layer is heavily overparameterized; the parameter count is number of hidden units in the MLP by the size of the parameter matrix
A
or
B
. To align with LoRA’s initialization strategy, the MLP for matrix
B
is initialized to output zeros, setting the model close to a pre-trained state.

Matrix Factorization(MF) Model:
We train a MF model with freely learnable matrices
A
and
B
, initialized with random values for
A
and zeros for
B
. Again, both matrices have rank 8.

Research Questions:
Using this easily controlled environment, we investigate key questions to understand the comparative performance of these methods, including the effectiveness of OP-MF in reaching the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) solution, differences in gradient behavior, changes in rank during training, and potential improvements from using advanced optimizers.

Is OP-MF more effective at reaching the SVD solution than standard MF?
In
Fig.

2
, we study the loss curves for both the MF model and the OP-MF model. The plot tracks MSE loss over 1000 iterations, highlighting how the models approach the target matrix under varied learning rates. The red line represents the SVD solution as a baseline. Initially, both MF and OP-MF converge for most learning rates. However, across learning rates, OP-MF achieves lower loss than MF. Furthermore, four OP-MF solutions reach the reconstruction error achieved with SVD, while none of the standard MF ones do. This indicates that OP-MF is both less sensitive to hyperparameters than MF and can achieve a better solution in the best cases.

Observing the loss curves more closely, we see that the loss values initially decrease at a generally similar rate for both OP-MF and MF. However, after a number of steps, OP-MF experiences a sudden acceleration in the loss reduction. This pattern aligns with
Arora et al. 2018
’s theoretical analysis that over-parameterization of linear transforms functions as an acceleration method. Interestingly, the OP-MF loss curves seem to temporarily stall near the same reconstruction error as standard MF, only to then make slow but steady progress towards the SVD solution. We investigate the reason behind this from the perspective of gradients and predicted rank below.

How do the gradients behave in MF and OP-MF?
To offer clues into how OP-MF can improve performance, we examine the gradient norm of parameters and the gradient consistency between optimization steps, both shown in
Fig.

3
. Erratic gradient norms can signal learning instability, while gradient consistency indicates how similar the gradient direction is between steps.

Our results reveal an intriguing pattern: initially the gradient norms for both MF and OP-MF are closely aligned, followed by a divergence. Specifically, the gradient norm in OP-MF experiences a sharp spike, followed by a collapse and a subsequent phase of gradual growth. These fluctuations in gradient norms align with distinct phases in OP-MF’s loss curve. In the initial phase, where gradient norms are similar, both methods’ loss curves follow each other closely. In the next phase, OP-MF’s loss decreases faster, consistent with larger gradients in a stable direction.

Fig.

3
illustrates gradient consistency, measured as the cosine similarity between gradients at 10-step intervals. Before the gradient norm collapse, gradients for both methods remain consistent, suggesting steady optimization. However, following the collapse, OP-MF’s gradients undergo a sharp direction change before again becoming consistent. In contrast, MF’s gradients remain consistently aligned throughout. This shift in OP-MF’s optimization path indicates a phase transition, where it adapts and begins progressing in a new direction—demonstrating a flexibility that MF lacks. This flexibility may allow OP-MF to make progress even in poorly conditioned loss landscapes.

How does the rank of
BA
change over the course of training?
In addition to reconstruction error, another way to track progress towards a solution in matrix factorization is plotting the effective rank of the predicted matrix
BA
over the course of training. Effective rank
\rho
is defined as

\rho=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}\log\sigma_{i}\right)

where
\sigma_{i}
represents the normalized singular values of
BA
, and
r
is the rank of the matrix. A higher effective rank indicates a more full-rank or distributed representation, and a lower effective rank suggesting a more compressed, low-rank structure. One would expect the effective rank to converge towards the effective rank of the ground-truth SVD solution.

In
Fig.

3
d, we observe the behavior of effective rank across iterations for both MF and OP-MF. We find that the overall effective rank shares a lower-bound for both MF and OP-MF, but that OP-MF achieves this lower bound faster. This is consistent with the idea that overparameterization acts as an acceleration scheme. Interestingly, OP-MF is able to increase the rank to something close to that of the SVD-solution in a sudden change of behavior after hitting the lower bound. In contrast, MF does not increase rank. This phase change corresponds to both the spike in gradient norms and where OP-MF starts outperforming MF in terms of reconstruction error (
Fig.

3
). This suggests that part of OP-MF’s success in achieving low reconstruction error is its ability to learn more minor singular components of the target. Neural networks have been shown to have a low-rank bias
Huh et al. 2023
, and our observations suggest that MLP re-parameterization is able to overcome that bias if necessary to minimize the loss.

Can using SGD with Momentum or Adam help?
Arora et al. 2018
connects MLP reparameterization to acceleration mechanisms such as momentum-based optimization. Therefore, a natural question is if using standard acceleration methods like SGD with Momentum or Adam is enough. We experiment by adding Momentum to SGD and replacing it entirely with Adam, an optimizer that combines adaptive learning rates with momentum. As shown in
Fig.

4
, both Adam and SGD with Momentum improve reconstruction error for MF, but OP-MF retains its significant advantage. This shows that the advantages of OP-MF compose with even best-case, advanced, optimizers.

Case-study takeaways:
Taken all together, OP-MF achieves lower reconstruction errors and adapts better to poorly conditioned optimization landscapes than standard MF. This advantage persists even with advanced optimizers. This suggests that MLP reparameterization is effective, and we demonstrate this in the larger-scale experiments in the next section.

5
Experimental Results

In this section, we conduct a series of experiments to evaluate the performance of overparameterized low-rank tuning across diverse applications, including image generation, vision-language tasks, and commonsense reasoning. Our primary baselines are standard LoRA and DoRA, both of which we overparameterize by predicting their parameters using a 2 layer MLP; for DoRA, we also predict the magnitude scaling vector. We call the resulting method OP-DoRA. We begin by testing fine-tuning on Stable Diffusion XL, finding improved image generation performance (Section
5.1
). Next, we assess OP-LoRA and OP-DoRA on vision-language tasks through visual question-answering, captioning, and classification benchmarks (Section
5.2
and
5.3
). We also evaluate the advantages of MLP-based overparameterization in large language models for commonsense reasoning, where external knowledge is required(Section
5.4
). Together, these experiments reveal the consistent performance gains of MLP-enhanced LoRA variants across various model architectures and tasks, reinforcing the benefits of overparameterization in low-rank fine-tuning. Finally, we investigate how varying the number of MLP parameters affects performance(Section
5.5
).

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce OP-LoRA, an MLP-based reparameterization of LoRA designed to tackle the optimization challenges typically associated with low-rank matrix adaptation. By leveraging over-parameterization we accelerate training while maintaining compact inference. Our experiments in matrix factorization and across diverse fine-tuning tasks—including image generation, vision-language, and pure language tasks—demonstrate that OP-LoRA consistently improves performance. These findings underscore the potential of MLP-based reparameterization as an effective approach to low-rank fine-tuning.

1
Gradient Updates in Overparameterized Regression

Arora et al. 2018
motivate their work with a simple case study on linear regression, showing that by reparameterizing

L({\mathbf{w}})=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\left[\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}-y)^{2}\right]

as

L({\mathbf{w}}_{1},w_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\left[\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}w_{2}-y)^{2}\right]

where
{\mathbf{w}}
is the learned parameter vector, and
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}{w_{2}}
is a reparamterization of
{\mathbf{w}}
with learned vector
{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
and learned scalar
{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
, one can accelerate gradient descent. In Section
3
, we use this idea to motivate OP-LoRA. Here we review how this results in implicit acceleration, with derivation and notation as presented in
Arora et al. 2018
. We start with the gradients of
L({\mathbf{w}})
and
L({\mathbf{w}}_{1},w_{2})
:

\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\big[({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}-y)^{t}{\mathbf{x}}\big]

\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}_{1}}=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\big[({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}w_{2}-y)^{2}w_{2}{\mathbf{x}}\big],

\nabla_{w_{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y)}\big[({\mathbf{x}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}w_{2}-y)^{2}{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}\big].

Gradient descent on
L({\mathbf{w}}_{1},w_{2})
with step size
\eta>0
updates the parameters as:

{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}},\quad w_{2}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow w_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{w_{2}^{(t)}}.

The parameter
{\mathbf{w}}={\mathbf{w}}_{1}w_{2}
is then updated as:

{\mathbf{w}}^{(t+1)}=({\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}})(w_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla_{w_{2}^{(t)}}),

which expands to:

{\mathbf{w}}^{(t+1)}={\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}-\eta w_{2}^{(t)}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}}-\eta{\mathbf{w}}_{1}^{(t)}\nabla_{w_{2}^{(t)}}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

This then becomes

{\mathbf{w}}^{(t+1)}={\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}-\eta(w_{2}^{(t)})^{2}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}}-\eta(w_{2}^{(t)})^{-1}\nabla_{w_{2}^{(t)}}{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}+{\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{2})

The last term is assumed to be small and
{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}
is an accumulation of all gradients, so then the update becomes:

{\mathbf{w}}^{(t+1)}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\mapsto$}}{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}-\rho^{(t)}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}}-\sum\nolimits_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\mu^{(t,\tau)}\nabla_{{\mathbf{w}}^{(\tau)}}

where (
\rho^{(t)}
) is an adaptive learning rate and (
\mu^{(t,\tau)}
) is an adaptive momentum term. Such implicit acceleration is the motivation for over-parameterizing LoRA.
