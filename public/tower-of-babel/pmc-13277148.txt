Feelings, thoughts, and attitudes of pregnant women towards newborn heel blood screening test: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Cemile Say, Refika Genç, Mustafa Kılavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to determine the feelings, thoughts, and attitudes of pregnant women towards the newborn heel blood screening test. The research has a mixed-methods design, where quantitative and qualitative data are evaluated together. The study was conducted between December 14, 2023, and May 5, 2024, and included 288 women whose pregnancies were confirmed biochemically, clinically, and radiologically. Data were collected through a questionnaire consisting of multiple-choice and open-ended questions, prepared based on a literature review and developed with expert opinion. Quantitative findings showed that marital status was significantly associated with selected newborn heel blood screening test–related statements. Chi-square analysis indicated that single pregnant women were significantly more likely than married women to report reading about the newborn heel blood screening test and to state that they had not encountered children developing allergic reactions following the test (χ2, p < 0.05). No statistically significant associations were observed between marital status and other test-related statements (p > 0.05). As a result of the qualitative analysis, it was determined that pregnant women’s hesitations regarding the newborn heel blood screening test were related to lack of information, misinformation from social media, lack of communication with healthcare professionals, and trust issues. While the majority of participants found the test necessary for early diagnosis, some approached the application cautiously due to lack of information, fear, and distrust. The findings indicate that pregnant women need to be informed more comprehensively and accurately about newborn screening tests. The role of healthcare professionals in education and counseling processes is critically important.

Introduction

The newborn period, encompassing the first 28 days after birth, is considered one of the most critical stages in a baby’s life. During this period, hereditary and metabolic diseases that might be detected in the baby can lead to permanent health problems, significantly affecting quality of life if not diagnosed early. Therefore, newborn screening tests are vital preventive health services applied in the first days of life to detect serious health problems before symptoms appear [10, 12]).

Today, newborn screening programs have become a standard public health practice in many developed countries. These programs are implemented within the framework of national public health policies and government regulations [19]. In Türkiye, the National Newborn Screening Program is carried out under the authority of the Ministry of Health and regulated through national public health legislation, ensuring that heel blood screening is routinely offered to all newborns as part of standard postnatal care services [14]. These screenings are provided free of charge and are integrated into maternal and child health services to promote early diagnosis and prevention of serious congenital and metabolic diseases. The screening of diseases such as phenylketonuria (PKU), congenital hypothyroidism (CH), biotinidase deficiency (BD), cystic fibrosis (CF), congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) forms the basis of these programs [6]. In Türkiye, this process began in 1983 with PKU, expanded in 2006 with the addition of CH, in 2015 with CF, and most recently in 2022 with SMA [16].

The newborn heel blood screening test is a method within this program that enables early diagnosis of many metabolic and hereditary diseases using a single drop of blood taken from the baby’s heel. However, great care must be taken regarding the hygiene of the materials used in the test, the timing of the procedure, and the proper transportation of the blood sample to the laboratory [3]. Otherwise, false negative or false positive results can occur, delaying treatment.

In recent years, with the increased use of social media, misinformation in healthcare has become a significant problem. Especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, distrust in medical practices increased parallel to vaccine hesitancy; this also negatively affected the perception of preventive health services such as newborn screening tests [13, 17]. Studies indicate that misinformation spread on social media negatively impacts individuals’ health-seeking behaviors, leading to the refusal of important tests due to reasons like fear, doubt, and lack of information [20].

The attitudes of pregnant women towards the newborn heel blood screening test play a critical role not only for their babies’ health but also for the protection of public health. The newborn heel blood screening program contributes to the early diagnosis of hereditary and metabolic diseases, preventing lifelong disabilities and even serious health outcomes. Through this program, timely treatment becomes possible, reducing both the financial and emotional burden on families. Moreover, early diagnosis enhances the quality of life of children and ensures their healthy development. These benefits attract the interest of pregnant women and facilitate the acceptance of the screening program. However, the literature shows that pregnant women’s knowledge levels and perceptions of these tests vary considerably. While some pregnant women accept the importance of the test for early diagnosis and prevention, others state that they are not sufficiently informed, don’t know why the test is performed, or believe it might harm the child [1, 4, 9, 11, 19].

Understanding the underlying reasons for these prevalent hesitations in society will contribute to the development of safe health policies. In Türkiye, the Ministry of Health has implemented a National Newborn Screening Program, which routinely offers heel blood testing for all newborns regardless of maternal obstetric status. This national program, first initiated with phenylketonuria (PKU) in 1986, has since been expanded to include congenital hypothyroidism, biotinidase deficiency, cystic fibrosis, and most recently spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) [14, 16]. In this context, it is necessary to strengthen the information processes provided by healthcare professionals to pregnant women, combat misinformation from social media, and raise awareness about the importance of these tests.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the feelings, thoughts, and attitudes of pregnant women towards the newborn heel blood screening test.

Method

Research design

This study is a mixed-methods research that aims to determine pregnant women’s feelings, thoughts, and attitudes towards the newborn heel blood screening test, analyzing both quantitative and qualitative data. The study adopted a phenomenological design, meticulously examining individuals’ experiences and the meanings they ascribed to these experiences. Quantitative data were obtained from multiple-choice questions and analyzed with descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, while qualitative data were derived from open-ended questions.

Research time and location

The data collection process was conducted online between December 14, 2023, and May 5, 2024. Participants were accessed via the internet, allowing for the inclusion of a broad range of participants in the study.

Population and sample

The study’s population consisted of pregnant women aged 18 and over living in Türkiye, whose pregnancies were biochemically and clinically-radiologically confirmed. Power analysis determined that a minimum of 282 participants were required; taking into account potential data loss, 288 pregnant women were included in the sample.

Data collection instrument

The data collection instrument was developed by the researchers based on a comprehensive literature review of previous studies [1, 8, 15] and refined through the opinions of physicians and faculty members specialized in women’s health and obstetrics. Content validity was ensured through these expert evaluations. The form included 29 multiple-choice and 15 open-ended questions. The questions were structured to assess participants’ demographic characteristics, their knowledge and attitudes towards the newborn heel blood screening test, and their individual experiences.

Implementation process

The research announcement was disseminated to pregnant women meeting the eligibility criteria through obstetrics and gynecology polyclinics and online platforms. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose digitally, and consent forms were obtained online. The questionnaire was accessible via the “Google Forms” (https://docs.google.com/forms/d/10wWKbVZxDA1X30WJ4iaC76iwsJAECqrA3s2S-0q41xk/edit) platform and remained open for participation for a total of 45 days.

Ethical principles

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Istinye University (Istinye University Human Research Ethics Committee, Date: December 14, 2023, Protocol No: 23–227). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Participation in the research was voluntary; written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All data were anonymized and evaluated in line with the principles of confidentiality and privacy.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software. Categorical variables were summarized with percentages and frequencies, while numerical variables were summarized with means and standard deviations. In addition, chi-square tests were applied to examine the relationships between categorical variables and to perform inter-group comparisons. Qualitative data obtained from open-ended questions were analyzed using content analysis. The responses were read several times to ensure familiarity with the data, meaningful expressions were identified, and then classified according to their similarities. These classifications were combined into categories, and broader themes were derived from these categories.

For instance, the agreement rate among single women for the statement “I have not encountered a child who developed an allergy in their heel after the newborn heel blood test” was significantly higher compared to married women (32.14%) (Table 2).

Similarly, single pregnant women’s positive response rate to the statement “I read about newborn heel blood tests” was markedly higher than that of married women. This finding suggests that single participants are more willing to seek information about the test (Table 2).

Among those who viewed it as a personal choice, 21.2% found it natural for these individuals not to have the test, 13.5% respected their decision, and 15% believed everyone could make their own choices.

Discussion

This study examined the feelings, thoughts, and attitudes of pregnant women towards the newborn heel blood screening test using a mixed-methods approach. The findings demonstrated that although a substantial proportion of participants were aware that the test is a routine practice implemented within the national health system, many lacked sufficient information regarding its content, process, and outcomes. Specifically, 62.14% of the participants stated that they knew the test was a routine application implemented by the state, whereas only 17.82% reported knowing which diseases are screened. Additionally, 48.95% of the participants indicated that they were not adequately informed about the scope and content of the test. These findings point to significant gaps in healthcare communication and highlight deficiencies in information delivery during the postpartum period.

Consistent with these results, previous studies have reported that mothers frequently experience unmet information needs during the postpartum period. Balkaya [2] emphasized that insufficient information provision remains a common concern among mothers, while Gürsoy and Zeren [7] highlighted that, although informing mothers is a core responsibility of healthcare professionals as recommended by the World Health Organization, this responsibility is often inadequately fulfilled in clinical practice. Similarly, Massetti et al. [11] found that parents are frequently provided with limited explanations regarding the purpose, procedure, and results of newborn screening tests, which may contribute to uncertainty and reduced trust in preventive healthcare practices.

The present study also revealed that social media plays a notable role in shaping perceptions of the newborn heel blood screening test. A considerable proportion of participants reported that content encountered on social media influenced their knowledge levels and contributed to doubts regarding the reliability of the test. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating that misinformation disseminated through digital platforms can negatively affect individuals’ health-related decision-making processes [5, 18]. Exposure to unverified or conflicting information may increase anxiety, foster distrust, and lead to hesitation towards preventive health services such as newborn screening programs.

In addition, marital status was found to significantly influence responses to certain newborn heel blood screening test–related statements. Quantitative analysis showed that single pregnant women were more likely than married women to report reading about the test and to state that they had not encountered children developing allergic reactions following the test (p < 0.05). These differences may be associated with variations in life circumstances, information-seeking behaviors, and health literacy levels. Supporting this interpretation, Yildiz and Akgün [16] demonstrated that demographic characteristics, including marital status, play a significant role in shaping individuals’ healthcare utilization patterns and information-seeking behaviors.

The mixed-methods design of the study enabled a deeper understanding of these quantitative findings through qualitative insights. Qualitative analysis revealed that many participants experienced uncertainty regarding the purpose, process, and outcomes of the newborn heel blood screening test. Concerns related to genetic data collection, confidentiality of test results, and unclear use of collected samples were frequently expressed, reflecting limited awareness of data protection measures and ethical safeguards. These concerns indicate that insufficient transparency regarding data security may undermine trust in preventive healthcare services.

Furthermore, qualitative findings highlighted that ineffective communication with healthcare professionals was a major contributor to participants’ uncertainty. Many participants reported that the test was administered without adequate explanation, limiting opportunities to ask questions or receive reassurance. In addition, exposure to conflicting information on social media further exacerbated confusion and mistrust. Conversely, participants who reported positive prior experiences with the test and trust in healthcare professionals expressed greater acceptance of the screening process, while still emphasizing respect for individual preferences.

Nearly half of the participants stated that they respected the decisions of individuals who chose not to undergo the newborn heel blood screening test, whereas a considerable proportion associated such decisions with lack of information and insufficient awareness. This finding suggests that health-related decisions are evaluated within a broader social and cultural context and may be influenced by societal norms, shared experiences, and collective judgments. Similar observations have been reported in the literature, indicating that mothers’ attitudes towards newborn screening tests are shaped by their social environment, knowledge levels, and trust in healthcare systems [9].

Overall, the findings of this study underscore the importance of transparent, comprehensive, and trust-based communication in the implementation of newborn heel blood screening programs. Information provided to pregnant women should not be limited to the technical aspects of the procedure but should also address the purpose of the test, interpretation of results, and the handling and protection of collected data. Strengthening communication strategies and ensuring effective informed consent processes may enhance trust in preventive healthcare services and improve acceptance of newborn screening programs.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study was conducted to evaluate the feelings and thoughts of pregnant women regarding the newborn heel blood screening test, revealing how the test is perceived in society and which factors shape attitudes. According to the results, a significant portion of pregnant women are aware of the test’s mandatory nature but lack sufficient information about its purpose, content, and how its results are evaluated. The administration of the test by healthcare professionals without explanation, misinformation from social media, and trust issues developed by some individuals are the main factors preventing the test from being fully accepted in society.

The qualitative findings of the study indicate that pregnant women experience apprehension regarding the test’s reliability, privacy, data usage, and genetic information sharing. Furthermore, ineffective communication with healthcare personnel and insufficient information provision negatively impact the understanding and adoption of the test. Nevertheless, the majority of participants are aware that the test is a beneficial practice for public health and respect personal preferences.

Based on the research results, the following recommendations can be made:Information processes should be strengthened. Before newborn screening tests are administered in the postpartum period, mothers should be clearly and simply informed about the test’s purpose, process, and results.Healthcare professionals’ communication skills should be developed. Nurses and midwives should receive training to establish brief but effective communication during the test, ensuring adequate information provision.Accurate information should be disseminated on social media and digital platforms. To reduce misinformation and prevent trust issues, verified content should be prepared and presented to the public by the Ministry of Health and expert organizations.Data security and ethical consent processes should be made transparent. Pregnant women should be informed about where, how, and for what purpose samples taken during the test are stored, thereby strengthening individuals’ sense of trust.New studies should be conducted. Similar qualitative studies in different regions and socio-cultural groups will more clearly reveal how the test is perceived throughout society and contribute to the development of health policies.
