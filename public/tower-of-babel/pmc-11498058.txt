Systematic Review of the Profunda Artery Perforator Free Flap for Head and Neck Reconstruction
Authors: Renita Wilson, Taylor Cave, Payam Entezami, Erin Ware, Brent A. Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe profunda artery perforator (PAP) flap has gained popularity in head and neck reconstruction with a favorable donor site providing a relatively hidden scar and the ability to harvest a large amount of pliable tissue with consistent vascular anatomy. The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the safety and efficacy of this PAP flap in head and neck reconstruction.Data SourcesPUBMED, EMBASE, Web of Science, Google Scholar (January 1948‐February 2022).Review MethodsA systematic review of the English language literature was conducted for studies with at least 3 patients 18 years or older undergoing head and neck reconstruction utilizing the PAP. Study quality and risk of bias were evaluated using the MINORS scoring system. Main analysis endpoints were flap failure rate, donor site morbidity, and complication rate.ResultsNine articles and 206 total PAP flaps were included. The rate of flap‐related and medical complications was 33%, with only 2 (0.97%) instances of complete flap failure. Other complications included partial flap failure (10, 4.86%) and donor site wound complications (12, 5.83%). A total of 16 flaps (7.77%) required subsequent revision in the operating room. Average MINORS score of the studies suggested a moderate to high risk of bias.ConclusionBased on limited quality evidence, this review suggests that the PAP flap is a safe and feasible tool for head and neck reconstruction, with comparable complication and success rates as other free flaps. Further large‐scale studies are warranted.

Flaps are routinely utilized in reconstructive surgeries following trauma, oncologic resection, or for aesthetic procedures. Immediate reconstruction with vascularized tissue minimizes postoperative complications that arise from open defects. 1 Flap selection is based on several factors to minimize donor site morbidity and functional loss while maximizing flap survival and recipient site functional restoration.

Several free flaps are mainstays in a surgeon's toolbox for soft tissue reconstruction, including the deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap and anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap of the lower extremity. In comparison to pedicled flaps that are limited by location and length of vasculature, free flaps allow for more freedom in selecting appropriate donor sites and have revolutionized reconstructive procedures. While regional pedicled flaps are often utilized in head and neck reconstructions, free tissue transfers, such as the DIEP flap in particular, have rapidly gained traction. 1 The ALT flap is often designed as a fasciocutaneous flap but is versatile to be modified to myocutaneous and myofascial flaps. The popularity of the ALT flap stems from the abundance of donor site tissue, versatility, and limited donor‐site morbidity. The ability to modulate the ALT flap as needed makes it highly suitable for head and neck reconstructions. 2 Both the ALT and radial forearm free flap (RFFF) are commonly used flaps for pharyngeal reconstruction with similar outcomes. 3 Although the ALT flap provides more donor site tissue, better cosmetic appearance, and a lower donor site morbidity, 2 the RFFF can provide an alternative for patients with significant adipose tissue in the thigh. 3

Soft tissue reconstruction in the head and neck has important functional and cosmetic implications. Additionally, wound healing is also an important consideration, with additional flap bulk sometimes desired for this purpose. 2 Therefore, it is vital for the reconstructive surgeon to select an appropriate flap to use and tailor it to the specific patient, balancing cosmesis versus function in order to achieve a favorable outcome. Donor sites that provide versatility in the tissue harvested are of highest interest. As such, the profunda artery perforator (PAP) flap has recently gained popularity as a lower extremity flap that affords several advantages for reconstruction. The PAP flap is a myocutaneous posterior thigh flap based on a perforator supplied by the profunda femoris artery and typically provides a large amount of tissue and consistent vascular anatomy. 4 The upper posterior thigh or posteromedial thigh provides a favorable donor site with a well‐hidden scar in most women, making the PAP flap a strong contender for autologous breast reconstructions. 5 The harvested tissue is pliable and can be contoured to provide coverage to 3‐dimensional defects. Although the PAP flap is routinely considered for breast reconstructions, it has recently gained attention for head and neck defects. However, the existing literature on the PAP flap specifically for head and neck reconstruction is fairly limited. The PAP flap was specifically focused on here given its potential as an attractive option for soft tissue reconstruction with low donor site morbidity. Therefore, this review aims to highlight the applications and outcomes of the PAP flap to provide and additional tool in the reconstructive tool box of head and neck reconstructive surgeons to supplement current workhorse flaps such as the ALT and RFFF flaps.

Methods

This review was completed based on the Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta‐analysis (PRISMA) checklist. Randomized controlled trials, comparative studies, observational studies, and case series with at least 3 patients reporting patients of any gender and at least 18 years of age undergoing a head and neck reconstructive procedure using a profunda artery perforator flap were included. Animal studies and non‐English manuscripts without an available translation were excluded.

The primary outcomes extracted from the included studies were flap failure (partial or complete), donor site morbidity, and procedural complications. Secondary outcomes such as comparison to other flaps (if present), flap size, and recipient vein and artery were collected. Reported patient demographic information was also collected.

Electronic search of PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar was conducted with different search terms to identify papers discussing a PAP flap from January 1948 to February 2022. Search terms included profunda artery flap OR profunda femoris artery perforator flap OR profunda artery free flap OR profunda artery flap OR PAP flap AND (reconstruction OR cancer). Two authors independently screened the articles for eligibility based on titles and abstracts retrieved in the literature search. Reviewers were not blinded to authors, institutions, journal of publication, or study results. Disagreement concerning inclusion/exclusion of studies was resolved by discussion, and when needed, a third reviewer. Following refinement of the duplicates, the authors reviewed the complete texts of the selected articles. The authors performed a manual search of the reference lists of all identified studies to ensure all relevant studies were captured for a total of 9 included articles.

Data were extracted from studies meeting inclusion criteria. Two authors independently extracted information and cross‐checked for accuracy. Disagreement about extracted data was resolved by discussion.

Data were analyzed descriptively or as percentages when appropriate. If a specified outcome was reported in all papers meeting inclusion criteria, a collective average was calculated and reported. No comparative data calculations were required in the analysis of the presented data. Quality of the selected studies was analyzed with the Methodological Index for Non‐randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria to evaluate bias. Two authors independently assigned MINORS scores and the average, calculated by a third author, is reported.

Summary of Overall Flap Outcomes in Included Articles

MINORS criteria evaluation demonstrated a moderate to high risk of bias in all studies. The reduced number of included articles may contribute to a publication bias in reporting only successful PAP flaps. Additionally, the assessment of other flaps in some of the included studies resulted in a lack of standardization in the description and outcomes of the PAP flap.

A total of 206 PAP flaps across 205 patients used for head and neck reconstruction were identified across the included articles. The indications for reconstruction varied considerably, with the most frequent indication being squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck region, including the tongue. Other indications included non‐cancer defects, plate exposure, tumor ablation, scalp soft tissue, and bone defects due to trauma and scar contracture release.

The mean age of patients was 57.3 years with 167 males (81.5%) and 38 females (18.5%). Average flap size was 115.94 cm2. Only 2 complete flap failures were reported (0.97%), with 1 due to vasospasm and 1 due to pedicle thrombosis. A total of 10 (4.85%) partial flap failures were reported. Of 206 total flaps, 22 flaps (11%) returned to the OR for complications which were largely unspecified. Complications were overall infrequent with the most common being infection and hematoma (see Table 1). A total of 16 flaps (7.77%) required subsequent revision procedures, and 2 of these underwent additional flap reconstructions with ALT free flaps. The overall complication rate of all aforementioned complications was 33%.

Statistical meta‐analytic comparison was not performed given the heterogeneity, inconsistently reported data, moderate to high risk of bias identified, and lack of a consistent comparison group in the included studies.

Discussion

Perforator flaps have largely gained popularity in the reconstructive sphere for their ability to preserve the underlying muscle and minimize donor‐site morbidity. 6 The PAP flap is a perforator flap that consists of skin and fat from the upper medial and posterior thigh, utilizing a perforator branching off the profunda femoris artery for vascular supply. 7 The harvested tissue often includes the upper third of the posterior thigh between the gluteal crease and the popliteal fossa with a small to medium caliber artery provided by the profunda femoris artery perforator. 7 The PAP flap was first described for lower extremity use and burn contracture reconstruction, but has since grown to become a mainstay for breast reconstructions, 7 pressure sores, 8 , 9 and ischial and perineal wounds. 9 More recently, the PAP flap has been utilized in a variety of head and neck reconstructions as an alternative to abdominal donor sites as well as ALT, transverse upper gracilis, 7 and other workhorse lower extremity flaps. As described by Scaglioni et al, the transverse PAP flap is best suited for small to moderate defects (buccal, oropharyngeal, palate, partial tongue, and hemitongue) while the vertical orientation is more advantageous for moderate to large defects (total glossectomy, through‐and‐through buccal defects, and hypopharyngeal defects), flap splitting, or chimeric flaps. 6 Although its popularity is certainly growing, the PAP flap (along with other thigh flaps) is used less frequently in Western countries than in Asian countries for tongue reconstructions. 10 Heredero et al speculate that this global discrepancy may be due to the greater thickness and larger bulk of thigh flaps that correlate with the higher BMI observed primarily in Western populations. 10 As a result, the radial forearm flap is more commonly used in the West for tongue reconstructions. 10

Our literature review yielded a total of 206 PAP flaps across 205 patients for a wide variety of head and neck reconstructions. Although the rate of both flap‐related and medical complications was 33%, only 0.97% of flaps were categorized as complete flap failures. Across all articles, authors consistently reported successful outcomes. Several studies showcase potential modifications of the PAP flap to further improve surgical ease and patient outcomes. However, the lack of standardization in outcome collection posed challenges in analyzing the overall efficacy of the PAP flap.

The PAP flap presents certain advantages and disadvantages in comparison to other well‐established lower extremity reconstructive flaps. While the gracilis flap is often small with an unpredictable skin paddle, Angrigiani et al note that the PAP flap provides a larger and more reliable skin territory which allows it to be transferred as either a pedicle or as a free flap. 9 Ultimately, the hallmark comparison for the PAP flap stems from the commonly favored ALT flap. Mayo et al highlight that the ALT flap includes significant soft‐tissue territory which provides great versatility. 11 However, the anatomy of the ALT vasculature can be quite variable. 11 Meanwhile, the PAP flap may have a more constant vascular anatomy. 12 , 13 Additionally, the increased laxity of the posteromedial thigh skin compared to the anterolateral thigh facilitates the closure of the PAP flap donor site. 8 Functionally, Fernandez et al note that these characteristics of the PAP flap donor site allow the flap to be more easily moved by the residual tongue in reconstructions of glossectomy defects, facilitating patient rehabilitation. 13 The softer nature of the medial thigh skin also improves the flexibility of the flap following postsurgical radiation to maintain a more functional neo‐tongue. 13 In comparison to the ALT, Mayo et al note that the PAP flap has a lower morbidity profile and the harvest site typically has less hair (more suitable for head and neck reconstructions). 11 From a surgical perspective, Wu et al believe that the learning curve for elevation of the PAP flap is less steep than that of the ALT flap. 8 However, it is important to note that the PAP flap tends to have a pedicle that is smaller in caliber and may be shorter than the pedicle of the ALT or other workhorse flaps. 14 Additionally, as the deep fascia overlying the medial compartment of the thigh is thin, it is less suitable for procedures requiring an oral sling. 6 Utilization of the PAP flap in such cases would require the additional harvest of the ipsilateral plantar tendon. 6 Overall, complications arising from harvesting PAP flaps occur at low and acceptable rates, comparable to other flap donor sites. Many of the complications in this patient population likely are a result of the head and neck reconstructive site, as opposed to the donor site. Though direct comparative data is limited in our study, the rates of complications in the posterior thigh donor site including seroma, hematoma, fat necrosis, wound dehiscence, and infection appear similar to those of other flaps in the literature. 15

As the PAP flap continues to become a favorable option for head and neck reconstructions, there are several advantages to consider. Aesthetically, the posterior thigh provides a more pleasing scar site 8 , 14 , 16 with abundant hairless skin and soft tissue 5 , 14 , 16 better suited to the head and neck region. Harvesting the PAP flap does not significantly change the contour of the medial thigh or the integrity of the inferior gluteal fold. 8 Surgically, the PAP flap can be reattached intraoperatively if no sizable perforator is identified or successfully dissection. 6 Due to the location of the donor site, the PAP flap can often be harvested simultaneously with the preparation of the recipient site (ie, cancer resection) to minimize operative time. 6 , 16 The numerous sizable perforators available at a single donor site increase the versatility of flap design. 5 The flap orientation can be tailored between an upper transverse incision (primarily utilized for breast reconstruction) and a longitudinal or vertical incision that is more ideal for head and neck defects, minimizes the pull of gravity on the incision, and maintains the choice of perforators. 6 , 11 Additionally, the PAP flap provides substantial thickness and volume without the need to harvest muscle, which is particularly beneficial for patients who require greater tissue volume at the time of reconstruction and/or anticipate adjuvant radiation therapy post‐operatively. 12 The ability to mold the PAP flap as needed for each patient also makes this flap amenable to chimeric flaps. 12 Wu et al highlight the combination of a long vascular pedicle and a large skin paddle as ideal for head and neck reconstructions. 8 Since most head and neck reconstruction patients exhibit suboptimal nutritional status and consequent subcutaneous fat losses, the moderate thickness of the PAP flap is well‐suited for complex insets of the head and neck with the option to trim the flap as necessary. 8 Furthermore, the potential for a dual venous anastomosis with the PAP flap establishes a fail‐safe drainage procedure to reduce the risk of flap failure. 14

However, as any other flap, the PAP flap is not without its disadvantages. Often, preoperative studies may be necessary to identify the dominant perforator. 7 , 17 The ergonomics of flap harvest in the frog‐leg position are challenging and patient position subsequently restricts the number of recipient sites. 16 Li et al indicate that some vascular pedicle harvests of the PAP flap may be shorter, and should a longer pedicle be required, the procedure becomes time‐consuming and may obstruct blood supply to the donor muscle. 16 Although not commonly reported, damage to the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh can result in sensory deficiencies. 16 The PAP flap does not typically involve nerve autografts; thus, patients with extensive parotidectomies with facial nerve sacrifice should consider a different flap. 12 Furthermore, since profunda femoris perforators arise independently, the PAP flap does not lend itself well to a multiple‐skin island design. 12 Prior to attachment, the PAP flap may require thinning or defatting procedures, particularly in obese patients with shallow defects. 6 , 9 The small caliber of veins poses some difficulty in anastomosis; however, Iida proposes the use of the posterior accessory saphenous vein as an alternative. 14

Our study does have certain limitations. The lack of standardization in reporting outcomes, small overall sample size, and quality of the included studies all are important to consider when interpreting the results of this study. Additionally, the possibility of publication bias is also an important consideration that may affect generalizability of these results. Complications and difficulties could certainly be underestimated as a result of publication bias.

Conclusion

Although the studies reviewed in this article are limited in quality and quantity, the PAP flap appears to be a safe, feasible, and favorable option for various head and neck reconstruction procedures with reasonable complication rates. With an aesthetically pleasing donor scar site and high pliability of posteromedial thigh skin, the consistent anatomy of the vasculature eases the surgical harvest and anastomosis of the flap. Furthermore, utilization of the PAP flap in primary reconstructions leaves other lower extremity flap options, such as the ALT site, intact for complex future reconstructions, particularly in high‐risk patients. 13 Further research is needed to evaluate the efficacy of the PAP flap for specific head and neck reconstructions in comparison to other lower extremity and abdominal flap options.
