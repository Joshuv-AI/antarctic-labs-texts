EEG complexity analysis using enhanced entropy features for depression detection and severity classification
Authors: Dorsa Mosleh Shirazi, Maryam Mohebbi, Yashar Abolfathi, Reza Rostami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a psychological disorder characterized by persistent feelings of sadness, and questionnaires are commonly employed for the diagnose this condition and its associated symptoms. The identification of depression is critically important due to its potential to induce significant socioeconomic issues and long-lasting effects on the economy. However, utilizing questionnaires allow room for human error in their findings. This study proposes an objective method for detection depression and its severity using EEG signals. Novel entropy based features, including dispersion entropy (DE), permutation entropy (PE) and their enhanced variants, were extracted to quantify EEG complexity across different brain regions and frequency bands. Following that, the number of dimensions in the feature space was reduced utilizing the minimum-redundancy maximum-relevancy (mRMR) feature selection approach. Ultimately, support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), and k-nearest neighbor (KNN) were implemented as classifiers, and their performances were assessed through 5-fold cross-validation.ResultsThe results indicated that the SVM classifier had an average accuracy of 97% in differentiating individuals in the normal and depressed categories. Moreover, SVM classifier was able to grade the severity of depression with a 71% accuracy.ConclusionThese findings indicate that entropy-based EEG analysis can serve as a powerful and non-invasive tool for both diagnosis and severity assessment of depression, particularly reflecting functional alterations in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes.Keywords: EEG, Depression severity, Dispersion entropy, Permutation entropy, SVM

Background

Depression, a prevalent mood condition, is characterized by persistent feelings of melancholy, discontentment, cognitive impairment, and alterations in sleep patterns [1]. According to the world health organization (WHO), there are around 322 million individuals globally who suffer from depression. By 2030, it is expected that depression will be recognized as a substantial cause of disability on a global scale [2]. Depression prompt identification is of the utmost importance and could induce significant socioeconomic issues [3, 4], despite the fact that questionnaires are typically the primary method of diagnosis. Currently, the dominant diagnostic tests for depression mostly depend on the Beck Depression Inventory [5], the Hamilton [6], the Montgomery-Asberg [7], or the patient’s self-reports. Nevertheless, the accuracy of the diagnosis might be influenced by additional factors such as the doctor’s expertise and the patient’s adherence to medical recommendations. Therefore, it is essential to establish objective and efficient methods for detecting depression and avoid relying on conditions that individuals use to describe depression. Given the widespread utilization of electroencephalogram (EEG) data in previous research to detect depression, it can be considered a highly effective approach for identifying and diagnosing this disorder [8–11]. EEG patterns of depressed individuals and those without any mental disorder have been found to exhibit disparities in these investigations. Hosseinifard et al. achieved 90% accuracy by using a logistic regression (LR) classifier with nonlinear features, including deterended fluctuation analysis (DFA), fractal dimension (FD), correlation dimension (CD), and Lyapunov power exponent (LPE), extracted from 90 participants (45 patients and 45 controls) [8]. Mahato et al. achieved an accuracy of 88.33% by utilizing power spectral density (PSD) and asymmetry features [9]. Mahato et al. conducted a study including 24 depressed individuals and 20 subjects in a normal state. The study examined 72 different linear and non-linear features, which included attributes like asymmetry and paired asymmetry of sub-bands, sample entropy, and DFA. The findings demonstrated that utilizing paired asymmetries and asymmetry resulted in a remarkable accuracy of 98% [10]. Cai et al. employed a three-electrode EEG apparatus to record EEG signals from a cohort of 213 subjects, comprising 92 patients and 121 normal individuals. The minimum-redundancy maximum-relevancy (mRMR) feature selection approach was used to reduce the dimensionality of the feature space. By employing ten-fold cross-validation and executing the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm, 79.27% accuracy was attained [11]. Despite the extensive research conducted on the diagnosis of depression using EEG signals, there is a relatively low number of studies that concentrate on the severity of depression. Given that mild depression can escalate into severe depression over time, and severe depression carries the risk of suicide, it is crucial to investigate the depression severity. In order to assess the severity of depression, Mahato et al. conducted a study that involved a total of 98 participants and obtained 79.19% accuracy by applying support vector machine (SVM) to the most significant 19 sample entropy features [10]. Zhao et al. conducted a study that included a total of 83 participants in order to evaluate the severity of depression. Compared to normal individuals, the EEG complexity of depressed patients demonstrates a substantial increase in all parameters when utilizing the Lempel-Ziv complexity method and sample entropy. Moreover, this complexity increases as the severity of depression increases [12]. Mohammadi et al. conducted a study on a sample of 60 depressed subjects. The purpose of the study was to assess depression score using two metrics derived from graph theory, namely clustering coefficient (CC) and degree, in addition to features such as Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC) and fuzzy entropy. The findings indicated a robust inverse correlation between degree, CC, and the intensity of depression in the alpha band, along with a significant direct correlation between LZC attributes and fuzzy entropy in the alpha and delta bands [13].

Several methods, including fractal dimension, Lyapunov exponent, LZC, and entropy-based methods, can be implemented to conduct dynamic analysis on nonlinear signals (e.g., EEG). To analyze nonlinear dynamical systems, entropy-based methods have emerged as a feasible alternative [14]. In comparison to previous nonlinear studies, these methods demonstrate superior performance when applied to short-time series and signals that are influenced by noise. Time series dynamics encompass a wide range of entropy categories, each of which demonstrates a unique concept of complexity [15–18].

Permutation entropy (PE) is a popular choice due to its minimal computational cost and high performance speed. It is important to note that dispersion entropy (DE) was introduced to address the PE issues, including the disregard of mean amplitude and the disparity between amplitude values [15]. In current research, PE, DE, and their enhanced models were employed to determine whether these characteristics vary across various depression severity levels.

The proposed procedure for data preparation and the database that was employed in this research are detailed in part 2. In addition, the relevant explanations for feature extraction and classification will be provided. The results that have been accumulated and the subsequent discussion are presented in Sects. 3 through 4. The conclusion is ultimately presented in the final section.

Materials and methods

Participants

150 depressed patients and 50 normal controls, aged 17–65 years, participated in this study. The depressed individuals who did not meet any of the exclusionary criteria were eligible to take part in the investigation. Conditions such as mental retardation, substance abuse, unstable mental health, unstable medical conditions, implanted devices, cardiac arrhythmia, suicidal ideation, head injuries (current or past), seizures, epilepsy, neurological disorders, and unstable medical conditions were all grounds for exclusion. Ultimately, informed consent was obtained from all patients for the study.

The EEG data were collected at the Atieh Clinic Neuroscience Center in Tehran, Iran. The participants verbally consented to participate in the study. Depression was diagnosed by a psychiatrist prior to the EEG registration. Furthermore, two distinct criteria, the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, 4th edition (DMS-IV) and the Beck depression inventory (BDI), were employed to evaluate the diagnosis of depression and the severity of depression. Data acquisition

The Mitsar-EEG-201 equipment was used to record the participants’ EEG data for a period of 5 min while they were in an eyes-closed resting state. 19 surface electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, F7, F8, C3, C4, T3, T4, P3, P4, T5, T6, O1, O2, Fz, Cz, and Pz) were placed on the scalp in accordance with the international electrode placement system 10–20, with an impedance of less than 5 kΩ and a sampling frequency of 500 Hz.

Preprocessing

The EEG signals undergo preprocessing using the MATLAB environment and the EEGLAB toolbox. A band-pass filter with a frequency range of 0.5 to 45 Hz and a 50-Hz notch filter were used to remove dc-drift, high frequency noise, and power line interference, respectively. Furthermore, independent component analysis (ICA) was employed to remove stereotype artifacts, such as eye blinking, eye movements, muscle and electrocardiogram (ECG) artifact [19]. Afterwards, a 6-level wavelet decomposition was used to split the EEG data into five frequency bands. Ultimately, the EEG signal and its sub bands were employed to extract features.

Feature extraction

Research has demonstrated that the power of various frequency bands has a considerable ability to differentiate between normal and depressed individuals. Consequently, it is consistently utilized as a critical and influential attribute in the classification of depression [8, 9, 11]. Additionally, entropy features exhibit higher performance against noisy data in comparison to other non-linear features, including the correlation dimension and Lyapunov exponent [14, 20]. PE, DE, and their enhanced variations in entropy calculation were utilized in this research. These entropies offer faster computation and are less affected by noise compared to sample entropy. Additionally, they are robust against short signals and do not yield undefined results. Noteworthy, DE was introduced for the first time in the literature as a diagnostic tool for depression. Eventually, the band power and entropy features were extracted from the original signal and five frequency sub-bands in this investigation.

Power spectral density (PSD)

The EEG data was partitioned into segments that had a 50% overlap. In order to calculate the PSD, the Welch periodogram method with the Hanning window was implemented on the EEG signal [21]. The features were determined based on the power of five frequency bands: delta (0.5 to 4 Hz), theta (4 to 8 Hz), alpha (8 to 13 Hz), beta (13 to 30 Hz), and gamma (30 to 45 Hz). Furthermore, the PSD of 0.5 to 45 Hz was selected as the main band.

Permutation entropy (PE)

Bandt et al. introduced the PE as a metric for evaluating the complexity of nonlinear time series [16]. When signals contain noise, PE is more resilient than sample entropy [22]. Improved multiscale permutation entropy (IMPE)

Multiscale permutation entropy (MPE) is a robust entropy measure that can be applied to a variety of signals, such as nonlinear and non-stationary signals. An improved version of MPE called IMPE was introduced to increase its stability. The original time series is transformed into a new time series by implementing a scale factor τ in the context of multiscale permutation entropy (IMPE) [24].

Fluctuation-based dispersion entropy (FDE)

In specific contexts, it is imperative to eradicate data trends. The initial stage in the commonly employed detrended fluctuation analysis approach entails the removal of the local pattern present in the signal. The dispersion patterns {1,3,4} and {2,4,5}, as well as {1,1,1} and {3,3,3}, are indistinguishable from one another when only the signal’s variance is affected or the local time pattern remains unaffected. Therefore, the implementation of fluctuation-based dispersion entropy (FDE) was carried out [16]. FDE accounts for the difference between adjacent particles in the dispersion model and is considered a model change that depends on this difference.

Reverse dispersion entropy (RDE)

In 2017, Bandt et al. introduced the concept of reverse PE (RPE) and described it as the measurement of the distance from white noise [25]. Following that, a new complexity measure called reverse dispersion entropy (RDE) was implemented to enhance the performance of PE and combine the benefits of DE and RPE [17]. RDE incorporates the amplitude information from DE and the distance information from RPE to analyze time series.

Feature reduction

It is imperative to evaluate the discriminative power of the extracted features in distinguishing between various groups. To determine whether features are separable, many techniques are used. The mRMR approach was implemented in this study to reduce the feature’s dimensions. Optimal efficiency by minimizing redundancy among subsets of features and maximizing their relevance, the maximum relevance algorithm prioritizes a set of features. The goal is to minimize the duplication within the selected set by applying the importance criterion [28]. In this study, 20 selected features are used to distinguish between normal and depressed participants, as well as to differentiate between different depression severities.

Validation

Validation is a method of assessing the performance of the model with respect to the specified dataset. To achieve this, the 5-fold validation method was implemented and the data matrix was partitioned into training and testing sets, so as to ensure the independence of the two sets. It is important to note that the procedures of feature extraction and feature selection were executed independently on the test and training sets. The selection of features from the testing set was based solely on the indices of the most significant features learned from the training set, thereby preventing leakage between the two sets. Ultimately, the 5-fold cross-validation was executed 100 times to guarantee the validity of the testing results. Finally, the confusion matrix was utilized in order to calculate the final values of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score. Consequently, the weighted average method was applied to prevent false results in the group with more subjects.

Results

Initially, the mRMR technique was evaluated for its ability to differentiate between normal and depressed individuals, as well as the severity of their depression. A total of 798 features were initially extracted from the EEG signals by combining 19 channels, 6 frequency bands, and 7 entropy and spectral measures (19 × 6 × 7). To reduce the risk of overfitting and enhance computational efficiency, the mRMR method was employed for selecting 20 features. Subsequently, the box plots were utilized to investigate the associations between the severity of depression and each feature.

The most effective features for distinguishing between normal and depressed individuals were PE, RDE, and IMPE. Moreover, the gamma, alpha, and beta frequency bands were more effective in the diagnosis of depression. In addition, Fp2, F8, Fp1, C3, C4, T8, P4, and P8, were generally the most effective channels, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Discussion

The current research achieved more favorable results by employing methodologies that were comparable to those of earlier studies that applied alternative entropies, including Shannon, Rényi and sample entropy [8, 11, 29, 30], to differentiate between the normal and depression groups. In comparison to other attributes, features of current research (i.e. PE, IMPE, and RDE), which were introduced for the first time in depression diagnosis field, exhibit outstanding performance. Consequently, this approach can be effectively implemented in the field of depression diagnosis. In classifying normal and depressed individuals, simpler features like DE and PE were significant factors. These features reflect the overall differences in signal complexity between normal and depressed individuals [15, 18]. Conversely, in the classification of depression severity, advanced features such as FDE and RDE were more prominent. These features demonstrate increased sensitivity to local fluctuations and multiscale irregularities, thereby revealing more nuanced distinctions among different levels of depression (mild, moderate, and severe) [16, 17]. Additionally, these advanced entropy features are computationally more efficient and more robust to noise than classical entropy measures [20].

Several studies have been conducted thus far in the field of diagnosing the severity of depression. Zhao et al. have incorporated attributes that increase with the severity of depression; however, these features were not employed to categorize the severity of depression [12]. Moreover, Mohammadi et al. have just forecasted depression scores applying graph theory indices such as CC and degree, as well as features such as the Lempel-Ziv index and fuzzy entropy [13]. The other study addressing this topic has conducted exclusively with a statistical population of 44 males and segmented the EEG data into numerous epochs [10]. One potential issue with this study is that it is possible that the epochs of a particular participant be included in both the training and testing phases; therefore, their results are invalid. In contrast, in this study each individual’s signal was examined individually and categorized into three groups: mild, moderate, and severe depression. Furthermore, new individuals were tested in the evaluation portion after the network was trained entirely independently.

Our findings indicate that depression disorder impacts the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions. This is in accordance with the prior study, which demonstrated that the function of neurons in the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal regions is impacted by the onset of depression and an increase in cortisol levels [31]. Furthermore, the amygdala and hippocampus are situated in the temporal lobe and are associated with cognitive functions, affective states, attention, and environmental perception [32–34]. Additionally, the prefrontal cortex, which is located in the frontal lobe, is associated with the regulation of emotions [35]. Individuals who are suffering from depression also experience symptoms such as isolation, increased negative emotions, and decreased attention and alertness [36]. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect EEG data modifications in these regions. In classifying normal and depressed individuals, mid-frontal region channels, particularly Fz, exhibited the highest significance, as diminished prefrontal activity is a key indicator of depression [37]. However, in classifying depression severity, parietal and temporal channels, including F8 and T8, emerged as significant alongside the frontal channels [38, 39]. This indicates the participation of wider networks in advanced stages of depression [40].

This research has revealed that the gamma frequency band is a critical factor in the differentiation of the normal from depressed individuals, as well as the depression severity. Additionally, the connection between gamma waves and alterations in emotion is underscored by the previous investigations [41]. As a result, the gamma frequency band is considered one of the key frequency bands in the field of depression severity diagnosis. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated the significance of the alpha wave in the diagnosis of depression [42, 43], and prior research has established a relationship between these waves and the level of attention and awareness [8], 44–46]. In accordance with these studies, theta and alpha waves are the subsequent critical bands for the diagnosis of depression and its severity, as indicated by the results. The results show that the features of beta band do not change across different levels of depression, whereas beta waves are known to be more associated with anxiety disorders [47].

Depression is characterized by increased neural irregularity and reduced coherence [40, 48], which manifest in EEG signals as scattered, nonlinear, and unstable patterns. Entropy-based features such as RDE and FDE effectively capture these dynamics—RDE by measuring divergence from structured patterns and similarity to white noise [17], and FDE by detecting rapid, local fluctuations in brain activity [16]. These features are particularly relevant in severe depression, where synchronization between brain networks is often disrupted. This aligns with known dysfunction in the prefrontal–limbic network, including reduced pre-frontal cortex activity and amygdala hyperactivity, which contribute to emotional instability [37]. Therefore, the consistent selection of RDE and FDE by the mRMR feature selection method not only supports their statistical value but also reflects meaningful neurophysiological differences, highlighting their potential as reliable markers for both depression detection and severity classification.

According to the Table 5, previous studies have primarily relied on band power or classical entropy measures (such as Shannon, Sample, and Rényi entropies). In contrast, the present study, for the first time, employs enhanced entropy features including FDE, RDE, PE, and IMPE, along with power spectral density. These features demonstrate superior performance due to their higher robustness to noise, greater capability in capturing EEG signal complexity, and reduced computational time compared to classical methods [20]. The combination of these enhanced features with the SVM classifier resulted in an accuracy of 97% for differentiating normal individuals from those with depression, and 71% for classifying the severity of depression, surpassing all comparative studies. The results demonstrate that enhanced entropy measures provide a distinct advantage compared to prior methods and can function as a reliable tool in depression diagnosis.

In the light of the confirmation of numerous articles regarding the distinct effects of depression on the hemispheres of the brain [10, 12], we recommend that future research utilize the differential of the mentioned features between the two hemispheres, such as FDE asymmetry. Additionally, this study encountered various limitations that must be taken into account when interpreting the findings. The limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, certain patients exhibited borderline BDI scores (e.g., a score of 20 categorized by the clinician as mild depression, while the EEG signal may correspond more closely with moderate depression), indicating a possibility of clinical misclassification. Future studies ought to utilize multiple psychometric tests concurrently to improve the reliability of depression severity assessments. Third, certain EEG data exhibited elevated noise levels, potentially impacting the stability of the results. Finally, computations were conducted on the entire EEG signal without segmentation, with the objective of treating each subject’s data as a singular sample and ensuring full separation between training and test sets. The utilization of entropy features, while requiring less computational resources, remains time-consuming and may present difficulties for real-time applications.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to accurately identify depression and its intensity through the analysis of EEG data. To accomplish this, a variety of features were extracted from the EEG data, including PE, IMPE, DE, FDE, RDE, and PSD. Noteworthy, these advanced entropy features were implemented, which had not been previously employed in the diagnosis of depression and its severity. This study suggests that depression affect the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, which leads to an increase in the complexity and irregularity of the EEG signal. Eventually, by utilizing features extracted from EEG data, a 97% and 71% accuracy rate were achieved for depression diagnosis and depression severity classification, respectively.
