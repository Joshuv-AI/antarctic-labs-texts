Experiences of the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster among patients with lung cancer: A descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Qianlin Lai, Wenwen Lu, Lin Xiao, Yuanyuan Luo, Zhihui Yang, Jingxia Miao, Peijuan Chen, Lili Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore how patients with lung cancer perceive and cope with the fatigue–pain–sleep disturbance symptom cluster.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted using individual semi-structured interviews with 20 patients with lung cancer recruited from the medical and radiation oncology departments of a tertiary hospital in China in January 2025. Data were analyzed following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis approach.ResultsThree overarching themes and ten subthemes were identified. (1) Symptom Awareness and Bodily Perception: individual differences in bodily sensitivity, temporal recognition of symptom progression within the cluster, and distress that cannot be easily articulated. (2) How the Symptom Cluster Disrupts Life: erosion of physical function, heightened emotional volatility, guilt associated with being a burden, and involuntary social withdrawal. (3) Learning to Live with the Cluster: reflective attribution, active adaptation, and passive acceptance.ConclusionsThe fatigue–pain–sleep disturbance symptom cluster presents in varying patterns and exerts profound multidimensional impacts on patients with lung cancer. Patients recognize the cluster by perceiving the sequential and interconnected onset of symptoms. Coping strategies span cognitive reframing, proactive self-management, and passive endurance. Future research should integrate subjective experiences with objective assessments to refine core symptom cluster identification, tailor individualized intervention targets, and develop effective management strategies. Empowerment-based approaches may support patients who engage actively in coping, while knowledge-based guidance and emotional support are essential for those who adopt more passive strategies.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading global health concern.1 In China, lung cancer poses a major disease burden.2 It is estimated that by 2050, the projected lung cancer burden in China will reach 1.12 million new cases in men and 680,000 new cases in women.3 Lung cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, and patients typically experience more complex physical and psychological symptoms.4 Although advances in medical technology have improved survival rates, this has also led to a prolonged burden of symptoms and challenges for patients with lung cancer. Severe symptoms can systematically reduce patients’ functional status, increase psychological and economic burdens, and significantly impair their quality of life. Fatigue (95.2%), pain (78.2%), and sleep disturbance (82.3%) are among the quite frequently reported symptoms in patients with lung cancer.5 These symptoms often present as interrelated clusters rather than isolated occurrences. Symptom cluster is defined as a stable group of two or more concurrent symptoms that are interrelated and relatively independent of other symptom clusters, and may share a common underlying mechanism.6 76.6% of patients undergoing lung cancer surgery experienced the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster.7

At the biological level, this symptom cluster shares common pathophysiological mechanisms, supporting its characterization as a coherent cluster. Research indicates that systemic chronic inflammation triggered by cancer and its treatments serves as the vital mechanism linking these symptoms.8 Furthermore, the field of symptom science is evolving from a generalized approach targeting individual symptoms toward precise care focused on core symptoms.9 Research on core symptom clusters supports this new direction, offering a practical method to implement precision nursing. Empirically, several studies have established its central role among symptom clusters in patients with lung cancer. Fatigue, pain and sleep disturbances constitute a core symptom cluster across the disease trajectory in patients with lung cancer.10 Teng et al.11 also identified the sickness behavior symptom cluster (difficulty sleeping, feeling drowsy, pain, and lack of energy) through principal component analysis (PCA), with network analysis subsequently pinpointing its central role. Core symptom clusters play a central role among multiple symptoms, and their effective management can lead to overall symptom improvement. Moreover, the synergistic and cumulative effects of symptom clusters have a greater negative impact on patients’ quality of life and psychological well-being than single symptom. Fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster exerts substantial negative effects on health outcomes, functional performance, and quality of life.12,13

While quantitative research on symptom clusters has identified patterns of symptom co-occurrence, it falls short in capturing patients' subjective experiences and coping processes related to these intertwined symptoms. Expert consensus on advancing symptom science through symptom cluster research highlights qualitative studies as a crucial method for identifying and defining the characteristics of symptom clusters.9 Patients are not merely carriers of symptoms, but also active experiencers and interpreters of their conditions. As the medical model shifts from a disease-centered to a patient-centered approach, greater attention to patients' symptom experiences has become increasingly important. But to date, qualitative studies focusing on symptom clusters in patients with lung cancer remain scarce. Current qualitative research predominantly focuses on individual symptoms or broad symptom clusters,14, 15, 16 with few studies specifically addressing specific symptom cluster. There remains a lack of in-depth analysis of patients' subjective experiences with the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster, which has been identified as a core symptom cluster in individuals with lung cancer. Consequently, little is known about how patients with lung cancer perceive, interpret, and manage this symptom cluster in their daily lives. Qualitative research on fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster in patients with lung cancer can explore the interrelationships and internal structures of symptom cluster from patients’ experiences, identify key issues in unquantified symptom clusters, and reveal potential evaluation and intervention points. This study aimed to explored how patients with lung cancer experienced and coped with fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative research design was employed in this study. Phenomenology emphasizes the description and explanation of events or phenomena from the perspective of those who experienced, its essence is to explore the “lived experiences” of the participants.17 The findings of the study were reported in adherence to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ).18

Theoretical framework

Guided by Armstrong’s symptom experience model,19 this study developed the research interview guide primarily. The symptoms experience model builds upon and extends the symptoms management model and the theory of unpleasant symptoms, further elucidating the subjective dimensions of symptom perception. The concept of symptom experience involves the attributes of symptom occurrence and distress, the situational and existential meaning of individual symptoms, and the combined impact of multiple symptoms.

Study setting and participants

This study was conducted at the medical oncology and radiation oncology departments of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University in Guangdong Province from China on January 2025. Abundant medical resources have attracted many cancer patients that ensure the smooth progress of the research. A purposive sampling method was used to recruit the participants who met the following criteria: (1) ≥ 18 years of age, (2) be pathologically diagnosed with lung cancer, (3) be informed about their illness, and (4) with the concurrent symptoms of fatigue, pain, and sleep disturbances (numerical rating scale ≥ 3). The following subjects were excluded: with hearing or speaking impairments.

Data collection

With participants’ informed consent, two researchers (Author 1 and 2, both were postgraduate students majoring in nursing) with qualitative research training conducted semi-structured interviews in a private and quiet room. The interviews lasted from 10 to 45 min. The Short interview times were observed among a minority of patients with greater physical burden. To ensure data quality and depth in shorter interviews, interviewers received targeted training to prioritize core questions and use active listening with follow-up techniques to elicit specific narratives and examples from participants.

Semi-structured interviews balance structure and flexibility by using a core questions framework to guide the research while allowing follow-up questions to adapt based on responses. The whole interview process was audio-recorded, and the researcher provided supplementary observations and took notes. The audio data were transcribed verbatim, and the interview texts were cross-checked by the interviewers within 24 hours. When there is no new content in the interview, it is considered that the sample size has reached saturation. Grounded in the Symptom Experience Model and existing literature on symptom clusters, the interview guidelines was developed through iterative team discussions to ensure its content validity (Table 1). To ensure the content validity and appropriateness of the interview guideline, we conducted two rigorous internal reviews within the research team. The team consisted of a professor specializing in cancer symptom management, an experienced qualitative researcher, a clinical expert in oncology nursing, and two postgraduate students in nursing. This iterative internal process involved four main revisions: (1) Broad questions (“Please describe your symptom experience …”) were replaced with ones focused specifically on fatigue, pain, and sleep disturbance; (2) Closed-ended questions (“Did you experience … ?”) were converted to open-ended prompts (“Please describe …”); (3) Instead of asking about fatigue, pain, and sleep disturbance as separately, questions were reframed to explore them as a collective experience, aligning with the core research focus on symptom cluster; and (4) Questions with overlapping meanings were removed to streamline the interview. Before formal data collection, three pilot interviews were conducted with participants who met the study’s inclusion criteria. Based on feedback from pilot interviews, we found that the initial order of questions was not logically fluid enough for participants. Data analysis

The verbatim transcripts were analyzed following Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis framework in Microsoft Word.20 By reading and re-reading the interview transcripts, and if necessary, listening back to the audio recordings, the researchers gained a deep understanding of the data and recorded the initial impression. The data were systematically annotated to identify meaningful segments, with short codes generated as the minimal units of analysis. Similar codes were grouped into higher-level themes through comparison and generalization. In Table 2, an example of the data analysis process was reported. Rigour and reflexivity

Researcher triangulation was conducted: The data analysis systematically integrated field notes with interview transcripts, and involved iterative cycles of cross-coding and validation, complemented by regular team discussions to ensure analytical rigour. All authors possessed relevant clinical and research experience in oncology nursing practice. The collective knowledge and experience ensured comprehensive perspectives in study interpretation.

Theme 1 symptom awareness and bodily perception

This theme describes how patients subjectively perceive and experience the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster, including their understanding and meaning-making of the core symptom clusters.

Subtheme 1 individual differences in bodily sensitivity

The shared experience of fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster was characterized by individual differences in patients' perceptions and responses. When describing fatigue, they tended to characterize it as a state of exhaustion, using phrases like “just want to sleep, feel tired,” “want to lie down, feel nothing,” or “not clear-headed.” Some individuals emphasized physical fatigue, while others were more affected by cognitive fatigue. Patients used metaphoric expressions to describe their pain, such as “being clawed,” “heart being dug out,” “worse than having fingers smashed,” or “a single tendon being pulled.” Sleep disturbances varied among patients, including insomnia, short sleep duration, frequent awakenings, and vivid dreaming. Additionally, some sensitive patients could perceive and describe the dynamic changes within their symptom clusters over time. One participant said: “The pain keeps changing, sometimes here, sometimes there. I don’t even know where it hurts anymore.” (N17). Each patient prioritized symptoms within the core symptom cluster differently, based on individual criteria. “Can’t sleep! Ugh, can’t sleep is the most annoying.” (N3). “Honestly, the worst part is the pain, right?” (N16).

Subtheme 2 temporal recognition of symptom progression within the cluster

Although the concept of a symptom cluster was absent in the cognition of most patients, some patients perceived the clustered nature of core symptom at an unconscious level. These patients identified core symptom clusters based on the temporal sequence in which symptoms occurred. This reflected an intuitive understanding of bodily states, grounded in their lived experience. Their temporal perception of symptom cluster reflected not only the bidirectional relationships between symptoms but also more complex triadic interactions.“Well, when there’s no pain in the body, your spirit feels better. But if the pain keeps going, your energy and spirit definitely get worse, you know?” (N16)“When I sleep, especially in the later part of the night, if I sleep like this (on his side), the pain wakes me up. If there’s pain, I just can’t sleep well.” (N18)“Especially just after inserting the tube (chest drainage tube), I’m tired because it hurts, I can’t sleep well, is it tired?” (N3)

Subtheme 3 distress that cannot be easily articulated

Patients tended to view symptom cluster as an inherent part of their illness and described it as “That’s just how it is.” Actually, they focused more on the subjective distress these symptom cluster brought than on their objective characteristics. Patients longed for others to resonate with their symptom cluster experiences, yet often thought that the distress they endured was difficult for others to fully understand. One participant yelled: “You have no idea how it feels to be sleepless”! (N15). Another participant said: “How to say it … Those who haven’t experienced it just won’t understand. But, I’m not wishing this on anyone … It’s just … I really don’t want anyone to go through it, but since I did, I gotta tell you. It’s really hard to bear. I mean, I’m a tough man, but I just … I couldn’t take it.” (N8).

Theme 2 how the symptom cluster disrupts life

The comprehensive impacts of symptom cluster on individuals’ daily functioning, emotional distress, psychological state, and social roles were described.

Subtheme 1 erosion of physical function

The symptom cluster significantly impaired patients’ physical functioning, leading to marked reductions in mobility and self-care capacity. Dysfunction in daily activities contributes to a perceived loss of control over the body and daily life, resulting in a transition from independence to dependence on others for assistance.“The shoulder pain is troublesome because when I go to the bathroom, I can’t reach the spot I want to clean. It’s pretty annoying. My hand just can’t stretch out far enough. I bend, and I tries to bend. But I can’t move it into the right position. So, it’s not really convenient, and that is how it affects things … It’s my right hand. It’s really messing with my life. Like, I can’t even stir-fry with my right hand. I don’t have (enough) strength. Haha (gave a wry smile), if I can’t even handle my own daily stuff, how am I supposed to get anything else done?” (N16)“Walking, you know. It’s about this foot. This foot can’t bend well, the right side. It just won’t bend. When I put on shoes, I can’t even place my foot down properly. I need help. It just won’t bend. What can I do? It affects my movement.” (N14)

Subtheme 2 heightened emotional volatility

Symptom cluster could lead to emotional instability and a reduced capacity for self-regulation in patients. Patients’ emotional profiles were often dominated by two extremes, including pronounced irritability and depressive moods. This not only affected the patients themselves, but also strained their relationships with family members and health care providers due to the externalization of negative emotions.“When the pain hits, it’s easy to get irritable and lose your temper. It doesn’t just affect a little, it affects your whole life, your family, everything … But when I’m in pain, I don’t even want to talk to anyone. Because if I do, I will just end up snapping at them.” (N8)“It’s something that I can handle … I do feel a bit more emotionally down, though. I’m not entirely sure why, but I just find myself easily slipping into a low mood.” (N16)

Subtheme 3 guilt associated with being a burden

Symptom cluster led to a reversal of roles within the family, whereby the patient transitions from being a “giver” to a “receiver” of care. This change could lead to a significant decline in self-worth and a profound sense of guilt. Patients frequently perceived themselves as imposing emotional and financial burdens on their families, which they felt were undeserved. In some cases, this guilt may intensify to the point of generating passive suicidal ideation.“If I can afford it and won’t burden my children, then I’ll go for the treatment. Subtheme 4 involuntary social withdrawal

Physical limitations hindered patients from participating in previous high-intensity social activities. Despite a continued desire for emotional connection, their condition often necessitated social withdrawal. The patient identity becomes pronounced in social settings, this role alienation contributed to their deliberate distancing from prior social networks. Some patients also avoided interaction to prevent burdening others with their emotional distress, opting instead for isolation and silence.“I really want to play basketball, but all these activities are kind of limited … I even had to cancel the basketball game I planned for the New Year. Theme 3 learning to live with the cluster

This theme describes the patient’s adaptive process in which they move from cognitive appraisal to active or passive behavioral adjustments during prolonged coexistence with the core symptom cluster.

Subtheme 1 reflective attribution

Many participants reflected on the causes of their symptom cluster, attributing them to therapeutic, psychological, and environmental factors. The majority identified treatment side effects as the primary cause, describing the symptom cluster as a “normal reaction to chemotherapy” and believing that treatment damages not only “bad cells” but also “good cells”. Just like one participant said: “With chemotherapy, it attacks all the cells in your body, not just the bad ones. It doesn’t care. It just goes in and kills everything, including your own resistance.” (N5) Some patients also perceived a link between symptom cluster and psychological states, highlighting the significance of maintaining a positive attitude in experiencing their symptom cluster. One participant noted that he would often “talk” to the cancer cells within his body, reflecting the perceived influence of psychological mindset on symptom experience. He said: “So now I always talk to it (cancer cells) like this. I say, ‘You’re already a part of me.’ I mean, I just tell it, don’t make me suffer so much pain. If you are going to stay with me, let’s both live for another 20 years. If you want me to die tomorrow, then you will die with me. It feels like it understands. And then, it doesn’t hurt so much anymore … It’s not really the cancer cells that decide. It’s actually the mindset that makes the difference.” (N8).

Environmental factors, as described by participants, encompassed both physical and emotional dimensions. The physical environment referred to external conditions such as noise, lighting, and so on, which participants believed could trigger or aggravate symptoms. The emotional environment referred to the emotional atmosphere perceived by patients. Patients emphasized that prolonged exposure to negative energy could exacerbate their symptoms. One participant said: “This place, how should I put it … The negative energy here is too much. Everyone feels so down, you know? I guess I’m the odd one out. I actually don’t care much about it (the disease). But for others, some nights they might be in pain or something. They hit their heads against objects. You can see all sorts of human conditions here, but none of the good kinds, only the worst and most painful experiences people went through. Humans are emotional beings, after all. I must be impacted. So, I couldn’t sleep.” (N8).

Some modified body posture to alleviate pain based on their lived experience with symptoms, while some adjusted their daily routines to cope with fatigue. Discussion

Patients differed in how they perceived and experienced the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance cluster. Recognizing this individual variation highlighted the need to tailor interventions and identify effective entry points to improve the management of symptom cluster. Most patients lacked a clear understanding of the concept of symptom clusters and tended to interpret the relationships among symptoms primarily based on their chronological order of appearance. A qualitative study on symptom clusters in postoperative pancreatic cancer patients reported similar findings, indicating that patients paid attention to symptoms only when their severity exceeded a personal threshold, and that they associated symptoms based on the order in which they changed.21 Patients' perception and understanding of their symptom cluster could significantly influence the coping strategies they adopted.22 Limited awareness may lead to ineffective or delayed responses. Symptom clusters that were not promptly recognized and addressed could interact and reinforce one another, forming a vicious cycle that exacerbated patient distress.23 Patients’ temporal perceptions revealed the dynamic evolution of symptom clusters, a cognitive pattern that aligns closely with the concept of sentinel symptom. Sentinel symptom, which emerge before the formation or deterioration of symptom clusters, serve as early warning signals for predicting cluster development.24,25 Previous studies have identified fatigue or pain as sentinel symptoms in patients with lung cancer, findings corroborated by our qualitative data where these symptoms emerged as the earliest indicators of symptom cluster.25,26 Participants also identified core symptom that they perceived as playing a dominant role within symptom cluster, viewing it as the most impactful and warranting the greatest attention. These findings inform two proposed strategies: one designed to enable earlier intervention by targeting sentinel symptom, and another aimed at core symptom to produce cascading improvements in symptom cluster control. The former adopts a forward-oriented approach by monitoring and addressing early fluctuations to reduce the risk of the symptom cluster emerging or worsening, whereas the latter adopts an inward-focused approach by intensifying intervention on the most influential primary symptom to generate cascading effects and thereby promote overall improvement of the entire symptom cluster.

Current research on symptom cluster largely relies on quantitative assessments through standardized scales. However, this approach possibly overlooks patients' subjective experiences of their symptoms, thereby missing critical information from the patient’s perspective. Evaluating symptom cluster solely based on objective dimensions is insufficient to achieve a comprehensive understanding of symptom clusters.9 As the direct experiencers of these symptoms, patients' perceptions, interpretations, and emotional responses constitute an essential part of the symptom experience. Patients' coping strategies are largely shaped by how they interpret their symptoms. When patients interpret their symptoms in negative terms, patients are more likely to experience helplessness and negative emotions, and to engage less in active coping behaviors. It is crucial to listen to patients' voices and strive to understand their suffering from their own standpoint. Such suffering should not be regarded as inevitable or unchangeable; instead, it can be alleviated and better managed through proactive, patient-centered symptom management strategies (such as early detection and reporting of symptom changes, systematic monitoring, and tailored self-management plans) and ongoing clinical and psychosocial support from health care professionals and family caregivers. Building on this perspective, future studies could systematically integrate patient narratives into symptom management.27 Approaches such as symptom-narrative diaries may help capture patients' subjective experiences of symptom cluster, and translate these accounts into clinically actionable information to support individualized and precise symptom management. In clinical practice, narrative-based interviews, explanatory communication, and personalized education support patients in redefining the significance of their symptoms, improving their sense of control, and fostering the development and maintenance of positive health behaviors.

Our findings indicated that symptom cluster affected patients with lung cancer on multiple levels, including physical, psychological, and social domains. It is essential to move beyond the traditional view of symptoms as purely physiological phenomena and consider their potential multidimensional impacts more comprehensively. Physical impairment was the most immediate impact in this study, specifically in self-care ability and mobility. Evidence suggested that patients experiencing a lower burden within this symptom cluster tended to demonstrate superior physical functioning relative to those with a higher symptom burden.28 Personalized exercise prescriptions may help prevent functional decline and enhance daily functioning. Traditional Chinese exercises such as Baduanjin and Tai Chi have been shown to improve this symptom cluster and functional status in patients with lung cancer.29,30 Psychological and biological stress pathways as common mechanisms underlying a psycho-neurological symptom cluster in cancer patients.31 Grumpiness and depression experienced by patients caused by symptom cluster was driven by complex psychobiological mechanisms acting in concert. Furthermore, the psychological resources patients need to maintain emotional stability are gradually depleted, leading to reduced emotion regulation. Nurse-delivered brief psychological interventions, such as brief cognitive-behavioral therapy, can effectively enhance patients’ sense of control and help stabilize emotional states.32 The experience of symptom cluster led patients to perceive a sudden shift into the role of the cared-for. This transition prompted them to frame familial emotional capital within a market exchange logic, ultimately fostering a sense of guilt for becoming a burden. A mixed-methods study on the experiences of symptom clusters in oral cancer also noted that participants sought to protect their families from physical and psychological burdens, expressing a preference not to cause them additional distress.33 Clinically, providing support for family education and communication skills was crucial for helping patients rebuild self-worth during the disease trajectory and reduce the psychological burden associated with role discrepancies.34 The burden of symptom clusters in patients with lung cancer significantly limited their engagement in social activities, while role alienation often placed themselves in an “isolated” social position. Similar conclusions have been drawn in other studies on symptom cluster experience.34,35 Because patients may have difficulty maintaining traditional face-to-face interactions, it is advisable to use alternative low-demand forms of social contact, such as online communication, to help maintain social interaction within a tolerable energy range.

Patients' reflective attribution regarding the occurrence of symptom cluster often involved treatment-related, psychological, and environmental factors, many of which are modifiable through targeted interventions. For instance, more careful monitoring of patients’ responses to treatment and timely collection of feedback on adverse effects may support the development of more precise and individualized therapeutic strategies to alleviate the symptom cluster.36 Implementing low-stimulation nighttime care and optimizing ward routines help reduce sleep disruption and its downstream impact on fatigue and pain.37 Negative emotions may contribute to “emotional contamination”. Conversely, this also highlights the potential benefits of positive emotional influence. Peer-support approaches such as group discussions, guided emotional expression, and mentoring facilitated more open expression and reduced symptom escalation.38

Patients who actively adapted to symptom cluster adopted derived strategies grounded in their own experiences, although these approaches were relatively fragmented and lack a systematic framework. Patients focused on single symptom rather than addressing the synergistic effects in symptom cluster management. The expert seminar on “Advancing Symptom Science Through Symptom Cluster Research” underscored the importance of developing and validating collaborative, patient-inclusive management strategies for specific symptom clusters in future.9 Recent studies have systematically summarized management interventions targeting the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster.39 Our team also has previously synthesized the best available evidence for non-pharmacological interventions across six dimensions specific to the core symptom cluster in patients with lung cancer.40 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), patient empowerment is both a prerequisite for health and a self-care strategy that improves outcomes and quality of life in individuals with chronic diseases.41,42 Therefore, proactive patients should be further empowered to engage in the self-management. Specifically, empowerment strategies such as providing self-management knowledge and education enabled patients to independently identify problems and optimize coping strategies, thereby achieving more sustainable symptom management.

However, some patients adopted a passive stance when managing symptom clusters, exhibiting a strong reliance on medical authority. This reliance essentially reflects a transfer of responsibility for decision-making and self-management to health care providers. The physician-dominated model of care is not conducive to long-term patient outcomes. These patients often exhibited low confidence and limited capacity in managing symptom clusters. It is essential to adopt a more humanistic perspective when engaging with these “silent” patients. For passive patients, clinical interventions should start with simple and achievable self-management tasks to gradually build confidence.43 Using clear and intuitive explanations of symptom cluster could help patients develop an overall understanding of their condition. Regular follow-ups and educational support allowed patients to strengthen their self-management skills in a controlled setting and reduce unnecessary reliance on health care providers. Additionally, digital tools could support patients by providing automated monitoring, timely reminders, and personalized feedback, which help improve self-management and adherence to healthy behaviors.

Strengths and limitations

This study highlighted the manifestation, impact, and coping strategies related to the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster from the experience of patients with lung cancer. Focusing on a common core symptom cluster in patients with lung cancer, this study refined our understanding of symptom clusters by moving from broader patterns to specific experiences. The study also had some limitations. The findings' transferability to populations with different cultural or organizational contexts may be limited due to the single-center setting. While efforts were made to ensure accurate translation, the inherent cultural nuances in participants’ original Chinese expressions may not have been fully conveyed in English. The temporal dynamics of the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster could not fully capture by the qualitative study conducted at a single timepoint. This study primarily focused on the symptom cluster, with insufficient exploration of coping strategies for the associated challenges in self-care and social functioning. Future research should adopt a more comprehensive approach to address this aspect.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

The findings highlighted the individual variability and multidimensional impact of the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster in patients with lung cancer. Patients identified this core symptom cluster based on the sequential onset of symptoms and subjectively perceived their experience as an incomprehensible suffering. Study also revealed patients’ coping strategies, ranging from cognitive processing to active or passive actions. These insights provide a deeper understanding of this core symptom cluster. The qualitative study design offered novel patient-centered perspectives on symptom cluster experiences that meaningfully expanded current understanding in symptom cluster field where existing evidence predominantly derived from quantitative research. Future research should validate the findings of this study across more diverse cultural and environmental contexts. Furthermore, a mixed-methods approach could be employed to integrate quantitative and qualitative findings, which would help capture the nuanced manifestations of core symptom clusters. It is also suggestive to identify individualized intervention targets and develop coordinated, efficient symptom cluster management strategies. For patients actively adapting to symptom cluster, empowerment approaches should be enhanced to promote self-management, whereas those passively accepting symptom cluster may benefit more from knowledge guidance and emotional support.

Conclusions

This study indicated significant individual variability and multidimensional impact in the fatigue-pain-sleep disturbance symptom cluster among patients with lung cancer. Patients identified this core symptom cluster by recognizing the sequential onset of symptoms within the cluster. Subjectively, they perceived the experience as an incomprehensible suffering. In coping with the symptom cluster, patients adopted management strategies ranging from cognitive processing to active or passive actions. This study contributed novel evidence for understanding the core symptom clusters in patients with lung cancer.
