Mindfulness-based and mindfulness-informed interventions for internet use disorders in adolescents and young adults: A three-level random-effects meta-analysis
Authors: Han Li, Yihui Wang, Xinyun Li, Christian Montag, Juan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pervasive integration of the internet into daily life has rendered Internet Use Disorders (IUDs) a particular threat to adolescents and young adults, whose vulnerability is amplified by a developmental stage characterized by ongoing neurodevelopment and self-exploration. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy of mindfulness-based and mindfulness-informed interventions (MBIIs) for this population.MethodsSeven databases were searched from inception to April 2026 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Risk of bias was assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), data were synthesized via three-level random-effects models, and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.ResultsFrom 28 included RCTs (26 for final analysis; 88 effect sizes), MBIIs yielded a significant overall reduction in IUDs (d = −1.32, 95% CI [−1.73, −0.91], p < .001). Moderator analyses identified three significant factors influencing treatment efficacy: geographical region, measurement tool, and comparator condition.Discussion and conclusionsOur findings indicate that MBIIs represent an effective intervention for IUDs in youth, offering both therapeutic benefits and preventive potential. These results provide empirical evidence to inform clinicians and policymakers in developing targeted intervention strategies for IUDs.Keywords: mindfulness-based interventions, mindfulness-informed interventions, internet use disorders, adolescents, young adults, meta-analysis, moderating variables

Introduction

Internet Use Disorders (IUDs) refer to a range of predominantly online addictive behaviors (Montag, Wegmann, Sariyska, Demetrovics, & Brand, 2021). Conceptualized as an umbrella term, IUDs encompass various specific disorders where the key diagnostic consideration is a pattern of harmful use causing clinical impairment (Small et al., 2020). We acknowledge that the technology itself is neutral; this framework therefore avoids overpathologizing commonplace behaviors by focusing squarely on the specific context, pattern, and negative consequences that constitute clinical impairment (Billieux, Maurage, Lopez-Fernandez, Kuss, & Griffiths, 2015). A key taxonomic advance in IUDs research is the distinction based on the primary access device, dividing the concept into two branches: IUD, predominantly mobile, and IUD, predominantly non-mobile (Montag et al., 2021). The former is often operationalized as Smartphone Use Disorder (SmUD), referring to generalized overuse via portable devices such as smartphones and tablets (Montag et al., 2021). The latter typically involves excessive engagement through stationary devices such as desktop computers. Beyond the device level, IUDs are further classified by the primary online content involved. A prominent example is Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), which is identified in the DSM-5 as a condition warranting further study. It is proposed as a condition involving persistent and recurrent use of the Internet to engage in games, leading to clinically significant impairment or distress (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The World Health Organization (WHO, 2019)'s ICD-11 formally recognizes “Gaming Disorder”, specifying a “predominantly online” subtype and emphasizing the behavioral pattern over the medium. Another notable IUD in this category is Social Networking Use Disorder (SNUD), which focuses on the compulsive use of online communication platforms (Fournier et al., 2023; Montag et al., 2018). To consistently assess these disorders based on ICD-11 criteria, instruments such as the Assessment of Criteria for Specific Internet-use Disorders (ACSID-11) have been developed, enabling a unified approach to studying their etiology and impact (Müller et al., 2022). Understanding the nuances of these disorders, including their developmental pathways and effective treatments, is crucial.

Internet use disorders

The development of IUDs can be understood as a trajectory where functional motivational processes escalate into a pathological cycle. According to self-determination theory (SDT), initial engagement with Internet platforms is often driven by the pursuit of basic psychological needs, namely autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ntoumanis et al., 2021; Ryan & Deci, 2017). While individuals may turn to online environments to compensate for unmet offline needs, such engagement is also frequently driven by enhancement-oriented motives (i.e., supplementary satisfaction even when offline needs are sufficiently met) or situational affordances, where the convenience of digital platforms facilitates the establishment of routine-based usage patterns (Orben, Meier, Dalgleish, & Blakemore, 2024; Smith, Leonis, & Anandavalli, 2021). Regardless of the initial impetus, these platforms offer immediate rewards that reinforce specific usage behaviors, catalyzing the transition from goal-directed need-fulfillment to the maladaptive pathological cycles delineated in the I-PACE model (Brand, Young, Laier, Wölfling, & Potenza, 2016, 2019, 2025). The I-PACE model posits that IUDs develop through a sequential interaction between predisposing person factors, mediating affective and cognitive responses, and compromised executive functions. Within this framework, individual vulnerabilities (e.g., psychopathology and personality traits) sensitize individuals to internal and external triggers (Li, Yu, Wang, Wong, & Yang, 2025; Melodia, Canale, & Griffiths, 2022); these factors heighten cue-reactivity and craving, which in turn diminish inhibitory control and facilitate online addictive behaviors (Brand, Young, & Laier, 2014; Throuvala, Griffiths, Rennoldson, & Kuss, 2020). Building upon this foundation, Brand et al. (2025) clarify that the progression of these behaviors involves wave-like fluctuations in positive (e.g., experiencing pleasure and fun while using a game) and negative (e.g., alleviating negative mood) reinforcement, characterized by a fused “feels-better” pathway where reward-seeking and stress-reduction are inextricably linked (Brand et al., 2022; Di Blasi et al., 2019). As the disorder progresses toward more advanced stages, it is increasingly driven by a “must-do” (e.g., the expectation that only specific behaviors can effectively help avoid or reduce negative mental conditions, such as using a game) pathway. Brand et al. (2025) emphasize that even seemingly habitual and compulsive behaviors in the later stages remain goal-directed tendencies, frequently serving to mitigate negative affective states (Hommel & Wiers, 2017; Schmid et al., 2024). This self-reinforcing loop, coupled with context-specific executive dysfunction and punishment insensitivity, serves to solidify the disorder within the specific online applications (Brand et al., 2025; Jean-Richard-dit-Bressel et al., 2023).

Internet use disorders among adolescents and young adults

Adolescents and young adults demonstrate a pronounced vulnerability to IUDs, which arises from their distinct neurocognitive and socio-emotional developmental characteristics (Cerniglia et al., 2017; Orben et al., 2024). This vulnerability is primarily driven by a complex interplay of individual predispositions, specific use motives, and passive consumption patterns, rather than merely by the total time spent online (Montag et al., 2024a, 2024b). From a neurodevelopmental perspective, asynchronous maturation, characterized by early maturation of the limbic reward system relative to delayed prefrontal cortex development, results in diminished capacity for impulse control (Bickham, 2021; Casey & Caudle, 2013; Young & Brand, 2017). Furthermore, pubertal hormonal fluctuations contribute to emotional dysregulation and heightened susceptibility to risk-taking behaviors, including problematic internet use (Hutton et al., 2024). For instance, Marciano, Ostroumova, Schulz, and Camerini (2022) demonstrated direct associations between elevated emotional distress and increased problematic internet use among adolescents globally. From a psychological standpoint, this developmental period coincides with critical identity formation processes (Montag et al., 2024a, 2024b). Within the framework of the current interpretations of I-PACE model (Brand et al., 2025), the “feels-better” pathway in youth is substantially reinforced by social affordances; specifically, the instantaneous feedback (e.g., likes or rankings) provided by social media and gaming acts as a dual-reinforcement mechanism, providing positive social validation while simultaneously alleviating interpersonal distress, such as loneliness and the Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) among this population (Fioravant et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2025). Peer influence further exacerbates this cycle, as perceived peer usage patterns are positively associated with an individual's own engagement (Marino et al., 2020). According to Brand et al. (2025), difficulties in emotion regulation represent critical factors that push individuals toward the “feels-better” and “must-do” reinforcement pathways. Given that adolescents' self-regulatory networks are still undergoing substantial maturation and remain sensitive to environment influences, this perspective suggests that interventions for youth may transcend traditional inhibitory control training toward establishing emotion regulation strategies, assisting adolescents in identifying non-digital “feels-better” alternatives to restore affective stability (Chamarro et al., 2024; McAlister, Beatty, Smith-Caswell, Yourell, & Huberty, 2024). Given these multifaceted vulnerabilities, mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) and mindfulness-informed interventions (MIIs) have emerged as a particularly promising therapeutic and preventive approach for this demographic (Renshaw, Fischer, & Klingbeil, 2017; Samanta et al., 2024).

Mindfulness-based and mindfulness-informed interventions and internet use disorders

Mindfulness refers to “the awareness that arises through paying attention, on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgmentally” (Kabat-Zinn, 2003, p. 145). Within the therapeutic landscape, MBIs represent therapeutic approaches that prioritize the cultivation of present-moment awareness through systematic attention training via meditation practices (Baer, 2003). While MIIs integrate mindfulness as a functional component within a broader treatment framework, utilizing non-meditation-based techniques like stories, metaphors and experiential exercises to promote mindfulness skills (Shapero, Greenberg, Pedrelli, de Jong, & Desbordes, 2018). These interventions incorporate various techniques, including formal practices (e.g., body scan, sitting meditation, walking meditation, mindful yoga) and informal daily-life applications, as well as non-meditation strategies to enhance stress management, emotional regulation, and psychological well-being (Baer et al., 2003; Bajaj & Pande, 2016; Michaelsen et al., 2023). Within the context of IUDs, recent meta-analytic findings have identified a significant negative correlation between mindfulness and problematic smartphone use (Ru, Norlizah, Burhanuddin, Liu, & Dong, 2025), and a small negative correlation between trait mindfulness and screen time (Fendel, Bürkle, & Schmidt, 2025). Furthermore, research has demonstrated the efficacy of MBIIs in treating behavioral addictions, with accumulating evidence supporting their application to IUDs (Samanta et al., 2024; Zhang, Lee, Mak, Ho, & Wong, 2021). Mechanistically, MBIIs enhance emotional awareness and metacognitive insight in individuals with maladaptive reward processing, thereby restructuring reward-learning processes (Garland, 2016). Donald et al. (2020) proposed that mindfulness enhances intrinsic motivation within the self-determination theory (SDT) framework by fostering core psychological needs (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) that facilitate sustained behavioral change. Collectively, these findings underscore mindfulness's role in facilitating the transition from extrinsic, compulsive rewards to intrinsically meaningful experiences through enhanced self-regulation and value reappraisal. Given the proliferation of MBIIs targeting IUDs, systematic evaluation of their effectiveness is warranted.

The present study

The clinical landscape of mindfulness-based treatments for technology-based addictive behaviors has been extensively mapped in recent years. Fendel, Vogt, Brandtner, and Schmidt (2024) conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis that validated the overall efficacy of mindfulness programs across diverse study designs and various forms of problematic usage of the Internet. Brandtner et al. (2022) offered critical insights into the affective and cognitive processes targeted by these interventions. Additionally, Thomas et al. (2025) provided a broad scoping of the contemporary mindfulness-oriented approaches and their varied applications in problematic technology use. The present study builds upon this body of work by seeking to refine the evidence base through a specific focus on Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) involving adolescents and young adults. By prioritizing the inclusion of RCT design, we aim to provide a more nuanced and controlled estimation of treatment effects within this specific developmental bracket. Furthermore, this research utilizes a three-level meta-analytic framework to account for dependency among nested effect sizes, which allows for a comprehensive use of the available data. Incorporating the recent scholarly publications alongside earlier foundational studies, this updated synthesis intends to provide more current clinical perspectives for MBIIs in the management of IUDs.

Accordingly, this meta-analysis aims to synthesize existing evidence examining MBII effectiveness for IUDs in adolescents and young adults. We hypothesize that: (1) MBIIs will significantly alleviate IUD symptoms in this population; and (2) this treatment effect will be influenced by moderating factors, the exploration of which serves as our secondary objective.

Methods

This review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) checklist (see Supplementary Table S1, Moher et al., 2015). The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/YN49X).

Study selection

A systematic search was carried out to identify relevant empirical studies published up to April 6, 2026. Multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, PsycInfo, MEDLINE, PsycArticles, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global, Scopus, and Web of Science, were consulted using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms (see Supplementary Table S2). Screening of studies was performed independently by two reviewers. To ensure exhaustive retrieval, reference lists from eligible studies underwent manual inspection, supplemented by Google Scholar verification.

Eligibility criteria

According to the PICOS acronym, the following inclusion criteria were utilized: (1) Participants (P): individuals categorized as adolescents or young adults (typically aged 12–29 years); (2) Intervention (I): interventions that were either mindfulness-based (e.g., MBSR, MBCT) or mindfulness-informed (e.g., ACE, DBT, or integrated mindfulness training); (3) Comparison (C): active or inactive, parallel or waitlist control group; (4) Outcomes (O): at least one outcome related to addictive symptoms or usage patterns of IUDs (e.g., general IUD, SmUD, SNUD, IGD); (5) Study design (S): an RCT design was employed, and information on effect sizes or data suitable for calculating effect size (Cohen's d) was provided; and (6) only peer-reviewed articles or doctoral dissertations in English were included.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) Participants (P): studies involving adult samples with a reported mean age exceeding 29 years; (2) Intervention (I): interventions that did not incorporate mindfulness practice or mindful awareness as a functional component; (3) Comparison (C): studies where the control group did not receive a comparable intervention for IUDs, such as those comparing a clinical group with a healthy control group; (4) Outcomes (O): studies that did not report outcomes specifically related to the reduction of addictive behaviors or excessive usage patterns associated with IUDs; (5) Study Design (S): non-randomized study designs, including quasi-experimental studies where randomization occurred at the cluster level (e.g., classes or groups) rather than at the individual level; (6) full text or final results remained unavailable despite efforts to reach the authors; and (7) review articles (e.g., systematic reviews and meta-analyses), qualitative studies, research protocols and case reports.

Data extraction

Extracted studies were characterized for the following variables: (1) name of the first author; (2) publication year; (3) mean age; (4) geographical region (i.e., eastern, western); (5) clinical status (i.e., non-clinical, clinical); (6) percentage of males; (7) comparator condition (i.e., active control, non-active control); (8) intervention method (i.e., MBIs, MIIs); (9) measurement tool; (10) intervention duration (i.e., less than 4 weeks, 4–8 weeks, and more than 8 weeks); (11) setting (i.e., in-person, online); (12) specific IUD type (i.e., general IUD, SmUD, SNUD, and IGD); (13) the group difference (d) of IUD symptoms under MBIIs.

Coding accuracy was verified through dual-rater procedures. Two independent evaluators initially examined three randomly selected studies (from 28 total) across iterative rounds until acceptable inter-rater concordance was achieved. Subsequently, evaluators independently coded the remaining studies, achieving 95% agreement. Disagreements were resolved through collaborative discussion to establish final classifications.

Quality appraisal

The Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) was utilized to evaluate the risk of bias. Five types of bias were examined, and each domain was graded as “low”, “high, or “some concerns” risk of bias based on the details of each study (Sterne et al., 2019). The overall bias rating was determined by the worst score obtained by each study in any domain. The Grading of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) profiler Guideline Development Tool (Balshem et al., 2011) was utilized to assess the quality of the outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low. The outcome was initially rated as high quality. This rating was downgraded if any of the five factors were compromised (Higgins, 2008). RoB 2 and GRADE ratings were independently assessed by XX and XX, with any discrepancies discussed and resolved.

Data synthesis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.0.0 (R Core Team, 2020), with the “metafor” package (Viechtbauer, 2010). To account for the dependency among nested effect sizes within individual studies (e.g., multiple outcome measures reported within a single trial), a three-level random-effects meta-analytic framework was implemented to calculate the overall estimated effect size of the MBII efficacy on the reduction of IUDs (Van den Noortgate, López-López, Marín-Martínez, & Sánchez-Meca, 2013). This hierarchical model partitioned the total variance into three distinct components: (a) Level 1: sampling variance of individual effect sizes; (b) Level 2: within-study variance among multiple effect sizes; and (c) Level 3: between-study variance in true effects. All relevant outcomes from each study were extracted to preserve data richness and nested within the study ID for model fitting. The primary summary measure was Cohen's d, representing the standardized mean difference (SMD) between the experimental and control groups. Following the recommendations of Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, and Rothstein (2021), effect sizes were primarily calculated using the means and standard deviations (SDs) of post-test scores. In instances where these descriptive statistics were not directly reported, d was converted from t-statistics or F-values using standard formulas (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). For the pooled overall effect, a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated, and a p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. The results of the meta-analysis, including individual study estimates and the overall pooled effect size, were presented using forest plots.

Heterogeneity was assessed via the Cochrane Q-test (Higgins, Thompson, Deeks, & Altman, 2003), along with I2 and τ2 statistics. Following the methodology by Borenstein (2024), a 95% prediction interval was calculated when substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 > 0%) to estimate the range within which the true effects of future, similar studies are expected to fall. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, moderator analyses were conducted via three-level meta-regression to investigate the impact of categorical and continuous study characteristics on the pooled effect sizes. Categorical variables were examined through omnibus tests of moderators to identify group differences (i.e., geographical region, clinical status, comparator condition, intervention method, measurement tool, intervention duration, setting, specific IUD type), while continuous variables were assessed using meta-regression slopes (i.e., publication year, mean age, percentage of males). Following established protocols, a minimum of ten studies was required for each meta-regression model. Additionally, subgroups were formed only when they contained at least three studies to ensure stable estimates (Higgins, 2008). Statistical significance for moderation was evaluated using the F statistic.

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analysis was conducted and presented using Baujat plots and Influence plots. Potential outliers were identified and excluded using the “meta-outliers” function from the altmeta package, applying a threshold of standardized residuals exceeded 3 (Lin, Chu, & Hodges, 2017; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). Potential publication bias was evaluated using Begg's rank correlation test (Egger, Smith, Schneider, & Minder, 1997) and funnel plot symmetry. In cases where evidence of asymmetry or bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method (Duval & Tweedie, 2000) was subsequently applied to estimate the number of potential missing studies and calculate an adjusted pooled estimate.

Results

Description of included studies

Figure 1 illustrates the systematic selection process for this meta-analysis. The initial database search across seven databases yielded 648 records, of which 233 duplicate entries were eliminated. Following title and abstract screening, 415 articles were screened, leading to the exclusion of 364 records after screening. Subsequently, 51 articles underwent full-text evaluation. Of these, 24 studies were excluded for specific reasons (see Supplementary Table S3), resulting in 27 studies initially retained from the database search. An additional 1 study was identified through manual searching of reference lists. Ultimately, a total of 28 RCTs fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final meta-analysis.

PRISMA Flow diagram of the study selection process

The final selected articles (Table 1) comprised 28 independent studies (n = 28) contributing 91 effect sizes (k = 91) published between 2016 and 2026, with a mean participant age of 17.7 (SD = 4.1) years (range 14.1–25). Geographically, 72 effect sizes originated from Eastern populations (n = 24), while 19 derived from Western populations (n = 4), with 58 effect sizes (64%; n = 16) involving clinical samples. Intervention modalities included MBIs (n = 16; k = 47) and MIIs (n = 12; k = 44), administered via in-person group sessions (n = 24; k = 80) or online platforms (n = 4; k = 11). Regarding IUD type, the analysis addressed general IUD (n = 10; k = 16), SmUD (n = 8; k = 26), SNUD (n = 5; k = 15), and IGD (n = 12; k = 34). Notably, several studies employed multi-arm designs, including three-arm (Hussain, Ferreira, & Kuss, 2023; Liang, Yu, Liu, Song, & Song, 2025) and four-arm (Güngör, 2026) RCTs, which allowed for multiple comparisons within a single study. Comparator conditions consisted of active controls (k = 33; Fallah, Mohajerani, Omidi, & Ghaderi, 2025; Fernandez-Crespo et al., 2025; Güngör, 2026; Hussain et al., 2023; Li, 2016; Li et al., 2026; Li, Jiang et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2025; Liu, Xi et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025; Ni et al., 2024; Tadpatrikar et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024, 2026) and non-active controls (k = 58), with the latter further specified as treatment as usual (Li, Wang, et al., 2025; Liu, Jiang, & Zhang, 2021), no-treatment (Ilanloo, Ahmadi, Zahrakar, & Cicognani, 2022; Kochuchakkalackal Kuriala & Reyes, 2023; Liang et al., 2025; Rao, Bhat, Sharma, & TL, 2025, 2026; Shadbad, 2017; Thapliyal, Kushwaha, & Thakur, 2025; Throuvala et al., 2020; Wang & Chen, 2023; Xiong, Yu, & Chai, 2025; Zhang, Wu, Lu, & Guan, 2025), and waitlist (Güngör, 2026; Liu, Yang et al., 2024; Yang, Na, & Rui, 2023). Follow-up assessments indicated sustained therapeutic benefits at 6-month (Liang et al., 2025), 3-month (Li et al., 2017; Xiong et al., 2025), 2-month (Ilanloo et al., 2022; Liu, Yang et al., 2024; Thapliyal et al., 2025), and 1 month (Güngör, 2026; Liang et al., 2025) intervals; however, non-significant long-term effects were observed in other samples (Fernandez-Crespo et al., 2025; Hussain et al., 2023).

Overall analysis and sensitivity analysis

The initial meta-analysis was performed on a dataset comprising 28 studies with 91 effect sizes (N = 1889; N (experiment) = 903; N (control) = 986). The results showed a significant overall effect of MBIIs on IUD symptoms (d = −1.32, 95% CI [−1.64, −1.01], p < .001). However, a high degree of heterogeneity was observed, as indicated by the Q-statistic (Q(90) = 1179.42, p < .001) and the I2 (97.24%), with a residual variance τ2 of 2.24.

To ensure the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. Following the inspection of Baujat plot and influence plot (Figs 2 and 3), three effect sizes from two studies (Kochuchakkalackal Kuriala & Reyes, 2023; Li, Jiang, et al., 2025) were identified as outliers and subsequently excluded.

The refined dataset, consisting of 88 effect sizes from 26 studies (N = 1798; N(experiment) = 858; N(control) = 940), was then analyzed using a three-level random-effects model. The adjusted overall estimate remained significant at d = −1.32 (95% CI [−1.73, −0.91], p < .001), with a 95% prediction interval ranging from −3.44 to 0.8 (Fig. 4). The distribution of variance across the three levels was as follows: 5.33% at Level 1 (sampling error), 86.9% at Level 2 (within-study), and 7.77% at Level 3 (between-study). Regrading geographic region, subgroup estimate indicated that MBIIs in Eastern regions yielded a significant effect (d = −1.52, 95% CI [−1.91, −1.13], p < .001), whereas those in Western regions did not reach statistical significance (d = −0.2, 95% CI [−1.1, 0.68], p = .66).

The measurement tool also emerged as a significant moderator, indicating that the magnitude of treatment effects differed significantly across various measurement tools. Subgroup estimates revealed that the most pronounced effects were found in studies utilizing DSM-5 criteria (d = −2.01, 95% CI [−2.72, −1.3], p < .001), followed by Young's Internet Addiction Test (d = −1.86, 95% CI [−2.45, −1.27], p < .001), Internet Gaming Disorder Test (d = −1.56, 95% CI [−2.34, −0.78], p < .001), Chen's Internet addiction Scale (d = −1.51, 95% CI [−2.65, −0.36], p = .01), and Others (d = −1.1, 95% CI [−1.53, −0.66, p < .001]).

Comparator condition also functioned as a significant moderator of effect size. Subgroup estimates displayed that while significant reductions were observed in comparisons against non-active control comparator conditions (d = −2.04, 95% CI [−2.68, −1.39], p < .001), effects were non-significant in studies employing active control group (d = −0.61, 95% CI [−1.28, 0.05], p = .07).

Regarding IUD type, while the overall moderating effect did not reach statistical significance (F(3, 84) = 2.58, p = .06), specific subtype comparisons provided exploratory insights. According to the model coefficients (Table 2), the treatment effect for SmUD (d = −0.8, 95% CI [−1.38, −0.23], p < .01) was significantly lower than the intercept represented by General IUD (d = −1.83, 95% CI [−2.38, −1.28], p < .001). Furthermore, estimates for SNUD and IGD were −1.16 (95% CI [−1.76, −0.56, p < .001]) and −1.49 (95% CI [−1.99, −0.98, p < .001]), respectively.

Discussion

Using a three-level random-effects meta-analysis of 26 RCT studies (88 effect sizes), this study aims to investigate MBII effectiveness for IUDs in adolescents and young adults. Our investigation tested two hypotheses: (1) MBIIs will significantly alleviate IUD symptoms in this population; and (2) the treatment effect will be influenced by moderating factors.

Overall effect

The primary analysis of this study revealed a significant effect size for MBIIs in reducing IUD symptoms in adolescents and young adults. This finding is consistent with the evidence base established by Fendel et al. (2024), who reported large reduction in symptoms following mindfulness programs across a diverse spectrum of problematic Internet usage within a population spanning all age groups. By focusing exclusively on RCTs, our results lend further support to the argument that mindfulness interventions are effective across multiple subtypes of IUDs, including general IUD, SmUD, SNUD, and IGD. This alignment suggests that the therapeutic benefits of mindfulness remain evident even when evaluated under more stringent methodological criteria. Evidence from several recent network meta-analyses further corroborates these findings, situating mindfulness within a broader range of intervention modalities evaluated through controlled clinical trials involving adolescent and young adult samples across specific IUD subtypes. For “Internet addiction”, mindfulness meditation exhibited the strongest improvement effect among various physical and mental exercises, significantly alleviating symptoms among young adults compared to placebo (JIa, Du, 2025). This is consistent with evidence concerning “mobile phone addiction”, where mindfulness-based therapy has been shown to be more effective than alternative exercise interventions for teenagers (Li, Zhang, et al., 2025). Moreover, when addressing “problematic Internet use”, mindfulness therapy was also identified as an effective non-pharmacological measure (Tian, He, & Guo, 2025). Taken together, these recent scholarly contributions establish mindfulness-oriented interventions as a preferred clinical approach for managing diverse manifestations of IUDs in the modern digital era.

In term of therapeutic mechanisms, Thomas et al. (2025) noted that mindfulness-based approaches targeting problematic technology use commonly function by improving emotion regulation. This is further elucidated by the work of Brandtner et al. (2022), who described how mindfulness targets the core affective and cognitive processes (e.g., craving and inhibitory control) that underpin addictive behaviors. The significant effect size observed in the current youth demographic suggests that these cognitive and emotional pathways may play a particularly vital role during this developmental stage. Consequently, an exploration of how mindfulness modulates these specific processes in adolescents and young adults is discussed in the following sections.

At a cognitive level, MBIIs enhance metacognitive monitoring, directly addressing neurocognitive vulnerabilities in online addictive behaviors. While the I-PACE model (Brand et al., 2025; Mak, Whittingham, Cunnington, & Boyd, 2018) suggests that individuals with addictive tendencies favor immediate gratification over negative consequences (Müller et al., 2023), mindfulness fosters metacognitive awareness of long-term functional impairments (e.g., academic underperformance, feeling of shame and guilt, or the reduction of positive experiences) that are typically ignored during intense craving. This recalibration addresses the imbalance between diminished punishment sensitivity and heightened reward sensitivity (Calvete, Gámez-Guadix, & Cortazar, 2017; Gámez-Guadix & Calvete, 2016). Neuroimaging reveals that mindfulness modulates neural activation patterns during reward processing (Kirk, Brown, & Downar, 2015), while clinical evidence indicates it restores cognitive control over automated behaviors (Garland et al., 2016). Furthermore, although executive failures in IUDs often peak under emotional stress (Brand et al., 2025), which is a vulnerability exacerbated by the developing adolescent prefrontal cortex, the concurrent window of heightened neuroplasticity allows youth to derive structural and functional benefits from mindfulness-induced neural enhancements (Ding & Li, 2023; Farrant & Uddin, 2015; Kaunhoven & Dorjee, 2017; Throuvala et al., 2020). Empirical evidence has demonstrated a consistently robust efficacy of mindfulness interventions targeting cognitive functions specifically within adolescent populations (Dunning et al., 2019; Sanger & Dorjee, 2015; Zoogman, Goldberg, Hoyt, & Miller, 2015). By strengthening “present moment” awareness, mindfulness functions similarly to “brain fitness”, providing a cognitive buffer that stabilizes self-regulation during these high-risk states (Brown, Creswell, & Ryan, 2015; Creswell, 2017; Visted, Vøllestad, Nielsen, & Nielsen, 2015), facilitating deliberate decision-making precisely when inhibitory control is most likely to collapse. This neuroplastic window, also noted by Fendel et al. (2024), suggests that MBIIs may both alleviate current IUD symptoms and act as a preventive buffer, intercepting the progression toward more ingrained addictive patterns.

Regarding affective mechanisms, MBIIs manage the internal tension driving compulsive use. As behavior transitions from risky to seemly habitual, the urge shifts from seeking pleasure to a “must-do” requirement to alleviate emotional discomfort (Melodia et al., 2022). For adolescents, this urge is often driven by a need to escape stressors such as academic pressures, peer conflict, loneliness, or the FoMO characteristic of digital environments (Dam et al., 2023; Zsido et al., 2021). Mindfulness training enhances emotional awareness, decoupling negative affect from addictive digital engagement (Cooper, Yap, & Batalha, 2018). By improving distress tolerance without immediate digital relief (Apaolaza, Hartmann, D’Souza, & Gilsanz, 2019), MBIIs enable youth to develop a resilient emotional system (Liu et al., 2023; O’Connor et al., 2023), effectively weakening the reinforcement loops that solidify IUDs over time. Establishing such emotional resilience early on may proactively reduce the likelihood of these behaviors developing into clinical-level disorders.

Moderating effect

Significant moderation was observed for geographical region, measurement tool, and comparator condition. Regarding IUD type, while the overall moderating effect was non-significant (p = .06), model coefficients revealed that SmUD effects were significantly smaller than general IUD; thus, subtype differences remain exploratory.

First, regarding geographical region, MBIIs yielded substantial effects in Eastern contexts but not in Western settings. This discrepancy might stem from the limited number of Western studies (n = 4, Fernandez-Crespo et al., 2025; Hussain et al., 2023; Li, 2016; Throuvala et al., 2020), which likely reduced statistical power. Similarly, the non-significant result of MBII effectiveness aligns with the findings of Odgers, Dargue, Creswell, Jones, and Hudson (2020), who noted that amongst Western youth populations, the most likely outcome from RCTs to date is that MBIs produce no beneficial effect in anxiety reduction. Furthermore, from the perspective of reconstructed Individualism-Collectivism (I-C) Theory, the higher efficacy in Eastern regions (representing low I-C societies such as China, Iran, Turkey, India, and the Philippines) might reflect a cultural inclination toward “conformity to traditional group norms”, whereas the “personal freedom” prioritized in Western contexts (high I-C societies including the UK, USA, and Spain) may offer less inherent cultural pressure for such behavioral change (Akaliyski, Vignoles, Welzel, & Minkov, 2025). This suggests that the perceived necessity of self-control through mindfulness might be amplified in cultures where fulfilling social roles is paramount. Notably, Fendel et al. (2024) observed that significant differences also exist within world regions, specifically finding that effects in the Middle East were larger than those in East Asia. Therefore, the moderating role of region warrants a more granular classification in future research.

Second, measurement tool emerged as a significant moderator, suggesting that the magnitude of treatment effects is sensitive to the assessment tools employed. It is plausible that different measurements capture distinct facets of the IUD construct (Lortie & Guitton, 2013); for instance, instruments focusing on symptomatic severity and functional impairment (e.g., DSM-5 diagnostic criteria) may be inherently more sensitive to the cognitive-emotional shifts targeted by MBIIs than scales primarily tracking behavioral frequency (Gentile et al., 2017; Maynard, Solis, Miller, & Brendel, 2017; Petry et al., 2014). However, such nuance implies that the reported moderation effect remains somewhat exploratory, particularly as the categorical comparisons relied upon the predominance of specific scales within the current evidence base. Therefore, while these variations provide critical insights, they should be interpreted as a tentative signal rather than a definitive conclusion regarding measurement-based efficacy.

Third, mirroring Fendel et al. (2024), comparator condition significantly moderated effect size. Significant reductions were observed against non-active controls (i.e., treatment as usual, waitlist or no treatment), but not in active controls. This aligns with the perspective that active controls represent a more stringent test of an intervention's efficacy, as they account for non-specific therapeutic factors such as group cohesion, therapist attention, and participant expectations (Goldberg et al., 2018). According to Goldberg, Riordan, Sun, and Davidson (2022), While MBIs typically yield smaller effects (less frequently reach statistical significance) against active controls, they are generally comparable, and occasionally superior to other evidence-based treatments. Therefore, the lack of significant difference here suggests that MBIIs may represent as a viable alternative rather than an ineffective one. These findings underscore that while MBIIs are robust options, further head-to-head trials are needed to determine their specific long-term advantages over traditional psychological therapies.

While IUD type did not emerge as a statistically significant moderator overall, the specific observation that the effect size for SmUD was significantly lower than for general IUD warrants tentative consideration. This discrepancy might be explored through the lens regarding whether users are “on the Internet” or “using the screen-based devices” (Demetrovics et al., 2026). Unlike IGD or SNUD, which are primarily driven by specific digital content, SmUD may involve an attachment to the device itself as a multifunctional “utility tool” essential for daily social and professional functions (Montag & Walla, 2016). Evidence indicated that SmUD exhibits unique risk factor profiles compared to IUD (Jin Jeong, Suh, & Gweon, 2020). Consequently, such utilitarian necessity might create a complex reinforcement loop that is relatively less amenable to mindfulness approaches (Panova & Carbonell, 2018). However, given the non-significant overall moderation effect, these interpretations regarding remain exploratory and require further empirical verification.

Limitations and future research directions

Several methodological constraints should be acknowledged in this meta-analysis. First, the search was restricted to English-language literature, potentially introducing language bias and excluding relevant non-English findings. Second, the omission of gray literature, such as unpublished dissertations, might have resulted in the loss of additional primary data. Third, while focusing on four primary IUD types ensures clinical relevance for adolescents, it limits the generalizability to other specific IUDs (e.g., online shopping). Finally, a further concern involves the lack of sex-disaggregated data in the primary studies, which precluded a formal comparison of effect sizes between male and female participants. While gender was not examined as a categorical moderator due to this data scarcity, existing evidence suggests that specific IUD subtypes may manifest differently across genders (e.g., SNUD, Ogundele et al., 2023). Consequently, it remains unclear whether the efficacy of MBIIs for specific IUD type similarly varies by gender. Future research should prioritize reporting separate effect sizes for male and female participants to enable a more nuanced understanding of gender-specific intervention outcomes.

Conclusion and implications

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a synthesis of current evidence regarding the therapeutic efficacy of MBIIs for IUDs, revealing a significant overall effect in reducing IUD symptoms among adolescents and young adults. Beyond the main effect, moderator analyses identified geographical region, measurement tool, and comparator condition as significant moderators. Clinically, these findings highlight the potential of MBIIs to target the underlying drivers of IUDs, such as emotional dysregulation. By potentially fostering both emotional resilience and better inhibitory control, MBIIs might serve to decouple the link between affective distress and compulsive Internet use. This mechanism provides a theoretical basis for considering mindfulness as a proactive strategy to mitigate symptom escalation. Accordingly, policymakers might consider integrating these programs into broader youth mental health and educational frameworks.
