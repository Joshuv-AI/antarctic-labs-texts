Relationship Between Cytokine Level and Viral Load in Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome
Authors: Lili Guo, Hao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a viral hemorrhagic fever caused by the SFTS virus (SFTSV), a tick-borne Phlebovirus. This study investigates the association between viral load and cytokine levels in patients with SFTS.MethodsViral RNA was isolated from plasma collected in EDTA and quantified by real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Cytokines in the sera of SFTS patients were measured using cytometric bead array (CBA).ResultsViral loads ranged from 2.3 × 103 copies/mL to 7.9 × 1011 copies/mL. Of the 46 patients, 10 (21.7%) died during treatment. Viral loads were ≥1 × 108 copies/mL in the patients who died. The concentrations of G-CSF, IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, MCP-1, and IP-10 were higher in patients with high viral loads. The levels of IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, MCP-1, and IP-10 positively correlated with SFTSV viral load.ConclusionsSFTSV RNA load was significantly correlated with the concentration of IFN-α, as well as other cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-6, MIP-1α, and IP-10).Keywords: cytokine, severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), viral load, virus

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), caused by a tick-borne Phlebovirus, is an emerging infectious viral disease in East Asia [1]. The primary manifestations include fever, thrombocytopenia, leukocytopenia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and multiple organ dysfunctions, with a high case fatality rate of 12–30% [2, 3]. Due to its high mortality risk, SFTS remains a serious emerging public health problem.

Detection of the viral RNA genome and specific IgM antibodies are usual methods for SFTS diagnosis. Several studies have shown that a cytokine storm may be related to the development of SFTS [4–6]. Chen et al. demonstrated that cytokine levels could reflect the severity of the disease and its outcome [7]. In a mouse model of SFTS, SFTSV RNA was primarily enriched in the spleen [8]. Virus infection plays a role in the destruction of platelets via FcγRIIA [9]. Viral load in the blood of SFTS patients may serve as a useful indicator of outcome [10]. Cytokines might also be related to platelet destruction [11]. However, the relationship among cytokines and viral load in SFTS is still not clear. The aim of this study was to determine the relationship between SFTSV load and cytokine levels in patients with SFTS.

METHODS

Participants

Between May 2023 and October 2024, 46 patients who sought treatment at Nanjing Second Hospital for SFTS were retrospectively included in this cohort. Blood samples originally slated for discard after routine laboratory testing on admission day were then frozen and stored at −80°C, awaiting retrospective analysis. We also conducted a comprehensive medical record review, systematically extracted key clinical data, including demographics, presenting symptoms and signs, laboratory findings, and treatment regimens from the electronic medical record system. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Second Hospital, and the need for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research (2024-LS-ky056).

Detection of Virus

The infection was detected by positive real-time RT-PCR for SFTSV. Specific primers were designed to target the highly conserved segments of the L, M, and S genes of SFTSV [12]. RNA was extracted from plasma samples, reverse transcribed, and PCR amplified as described previously [13].

Measurement of Plasma Cytokines

Serum concentrations of cytokines were determined by cytometric bead array (CBA), according to the manufacturer's protocol (BD Biosciences and Sage Biotech). The following cytokines were tested: granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon (IFN)-α, IFN-γ, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, monocyte chemotactic protein (MCP-1), regulated on activation and normally T-cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), and IFN-γ-induced protein (IP-10).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package, version 23 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). Data are shown as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical and ordinal data, and Student's t-test was used to analyze continuous data. The correlation analysis of two variables among virus load and serum cytokines was calculated using the Pearson test. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < .05 was interpreted as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient Information and Viral Load Analysis

SFTSV loads ranged from 2.3 × 103 copies/mL through 7.9 × 1011 copies/mL. 10 (21.7%) of 46 patients died during the treatment. Viral loads were >1.0 × 108 copies/mL in SFTS -related deaths; in 35 of the 36 patients that survived, viral loads were <1.0 × 108 copies/mL. The mean peak titer of patients who died was 3.2 × 107 copies/mL, whereas for patients who survived, the mean titer was 1.3 × 1011 copies/mL (Figure 1). According to the viral loads, the patients were divided into two groups: low virus load (<1.0 × 108 copies/mL, 35 cases) and high virus load (≥1.0 × 108 copies/mL, 11 cases). The mean age of individuals with low viral load was 40.6 years, and 20 (57%) of them were female. There was no difference in clinical characteristics between the two groups except for hemorrhagic symptoms (ecchymosis and gastrointestinal hemorrhage) and levels of AST and creatinine (Table 1). A noticeable trend surfaced in symptom duration among patients with differing viral loads (P = .08); however, no statistically significant difference emerged in symptom duration at admission between survivors and non-survivors (P > .05).

Serum concentrations of G-CSF, IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, MCP-1, and IP-10 were higher in the high viral load group (Table 2). We also assessed the relationships between the expression levels of cytokines and the SFTSV RNA load. DISCUSSION

SFTS is an emerging viral hemorrhagic fever with a high fatality rate. SFTS was first discovered in China in 2010 [14]. The high fatality of SFTS has caused increasing attention and fear in China. Exact knowledge of SFTS may help healthcare providers identify this life-threatening disease as early as possible. Although thrombocytopenia is a key clinical manifestation in SFTS, platelet counts were very similar between deceased patients and survivors in our study. Our study showed that deceased patients had higher viral loads compared with survivors. Our finding that the SFTSV load is related to the severity of the disease is consistent with several other reports [13, 15].

Our study showed that hemorrhagic manifestations, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum creatinine were significantly different between the two groups with different viral loads. Xu et al. also showed that hemorrhagic manifestations and serum creatinine are risk factors for death in SFTS [16]. Because of the similar clinical manifestations, SFTS could be misdiagnosed as hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) [17]. This misdiagnosis may increase the risk of person-to-person transmission and death of SFTS patients.

SFTSV infection induced a cytokine storm with highly elevated levels of various cytokines. Among the cytokines measured in our study, G-CSF, IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, MCP-1, and IP-10 levels in plasma specimens were higher in patients with high viral loads. The finding that a cytokine storm occurred during the early stage of SFTS is consistent with those of previous studies [5, 6]. Moreover, there was a significant positive relationship between the viral load and IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, MCP-1, and IP-10 levels. Type I IFN, secreted by lymphocytes and mononuclear leukocytes in response to the virus, enhances the antiviral capacity of nearby cells. The high susceptibility of SFTSV infection in IFNAR-deficient mice suggests the critical role of IFN in resistance to SFTSV infection [18]. IL-6 plays a vital role in host defense through the stimulation of acute phase responses, hematopoiesis, and immune reactions [19]. MCP-1 and IP-10 act as chemotactic agents to guide the migration of immune cells [20].

Overall, our study has several limitations. First, we only included a subset of cytokines; thus, it was not possible to identify other cytokines and chemokines associated with SFTS. Second, we did not examine the kinetics of viral load and cytokines over the course of the disease. Kinetic data are essential for understanding the pathophysiology of SFTS and predicting disease progression. Third, since this was a retrospective study, we could not ascertain the precise date of the tick bite from the electronic medical record system.

Our results, which show that SFTSV RNA load is significantly correlated with the concentration of IFN-α, as well as other cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-6, MIP-1α, and IP-10), may provide insights into the pathogenesis of SFTS.
