Heat stress and maternal complications in India: A nationwide analysis using the universal thermal climate index
Authors: Shalu R Chaudhary, Medha Wadhwa, Anish Sinha, Deepak B Saxena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAmbient heat is rising in India and may affect maternal health during pregnancy. Physiological adaptations in pregnancy heighten heat strain, yet national evidence examining exposure using Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) is limited. We linked National Family Health Survey-5 records for the most recent live birth (2019–2021), with district-level UTCI. Maternal Complications were using four self-reported pregnancy complication indicators. Heat exposures metrics included mean UTCI and mean of daily maximum UTCI calculated for full gestational period and for trimester-specific windows. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) per 1°C (1 K) increase in UTCI, adjusting for maternal age, parity, household wealth quintile, and place of residence, with state fixed effects. Analyses incorporated sampling weights and robust standard errors. The analytic sample comprised 176,193 women; 56.6% reported at least one maternal complication. Higher mean UTCI across pregnancy was associated with increased odds of maternal complications (aOR 1.004; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.000–1.008; p = 0.042), with the strongest effect in the second trimester (aOR 1.006; 95% CI: 1.002–1.010; p = 0.007); first and third trimesters were not significant. In contrast, the mean of daily maximum UTCI showed an inverse association (full pregnancy aOR 0.993; 95% CI: 0.988–0.998; p = 0.005), which was significant in the first and second trimesters. Higher parity was associated with lower odds of maternal complications, while urban residence showed marginally higher odds. Sustained gestational heat load measured with UTCI was modestly associated with maternal complications in India with a possible mid pregnancy vulnerability. Antenatal services and Heat Action Plans should incorporate heat risk screening, scheduling, cooling, and hydration, and future studies should test non-linear exposure relationships and extreme heat events.Keywords: universal thermal climate index, heat stress, maternal complications, pregnancy, India

Introduction

Climate change, a major global challenge driven largely by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, is known to contribute to a wide range of health problems, directly due to increased heat exposure and indirectly by exacerbating the challenges related to food and water security. The increase in heat health events across the globe, as highlighted by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), indicates a pervasive and intensifying threat to public health that extends beyond localized impacts.1

Heat stress is commonly assessed using the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI). This integrates air temperature, radiant temperature, wind speed, and humidity to determine feel like temperature and the resulting physiological response of human body. It is applicable across seasons and climates and provides a more physiologically relevant measure of heat stress than temperature alone.2,3

India, located largely within the tropical and subtropical climatic zones, experiences frequent and intensified heat waves across its geographical regions. Emerging research from India also highlights the association between occupational heat stress and increased risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage and stillbirth.3,4,5 At the same time, maternal health outcomes remain uneven across states. States such as Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Maharashtra are close to achieving the sustainable development goal (SDG) target of 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, while states like Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Assam, and Madhya Pradesh continue to report high maternal mortality ratios.4,5 These disparities reflect differences in health system capacity and access to obstetric care and may also shape vulnerability to environmental stressors such as heat.

Maternal mortality and morbidity remain a global health challenge, especially in the context of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Sub-Saharan Africa reports 542 per 100,000 live births compared to 12 per 100,000 live births in high-income countries.6 While India has made significant progress in reducing its maternal mortality, maternal complications (MCs) during pregnancy and childbirth continue to pose an important public health problem.7

MCs during pregnancy and childbirth represent important indicators of maternal health and the functioning of health systems. The World Health Organization defines maternal near miss as survival following a life-threatening complication during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days after termination of pregnancy. However, population-based surveys rarely capture the clinical criteria required to identify maternal near-miss or severe maternal morbidity events. Instead, they typically rely on self-reported symptoms or complications experienced during pregnancy and delivery. In this context, MC indicators derived from household surveys provide useful population-level insights into pregnancy-related health problems and care needs, although they do not necessarily represent clinically confirmed severe maternal morbidity.7,8

A cross-sectional study conducted in Tamil Nadu revealed that heat exposure during pregnancy was associated with poorer pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriages, still/preterm births, and low birth weight, compared with women not exposed to heat. This study also revealed a 3-fold higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes in heat-exposed pregnant women during the first trimester and a 2-fold higher risk during the third trimester. Pregnant women working in the informal sector were particularly vulnerable.9

There is a growing body of epidemiological evidences that demonstrate the exposure to heat during pregnancy elicits a range of adverse health complications for both the mother and the fetus. A systematic review in 2023 highlighted that vulnerability to heat varies according to the gestational age. Early pregnancy may be particularly sensitive to heat exposure, while prolonged exposure later in pregnancy has been associated with preterm birth, stillbirth, low birth weight, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and neonatal stress. Recent evidence also suggests that even a one-degree increase in temperature may be associated with increased risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes, indicating possible critical windows of vulnerability during gestation.10,11

Given this background, a nationwide analysis is justified in India for three reasons. First, exposure heterogeneity is substantial due to coastal humidity, inland heat, high-altitude cold, and urban heat-island effects, which create diverse thermal environments that UTCI can unify on a physiologic scale.2,12,13 Second, inter-state differences in healthcare capacity, referral pathways, and emergency readiness may modify heat related MC relationships.4,5,13 Third, policy relevance is immediate: Heat Action Plans and disaster-preparedness frameworks seldom include specific provisions for maternity and neonatal care despite growing evidence of heat vulnerability in pregnancy.11,14

To address these gaps, we undertook this study to examine the association of trimester-specific and pregnancy-long exposure to heat stress with MC indicators captured in National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5) using WHO-aligned proxy variables available in the survey.2,7,12 This enables identification of potential critical windows of vulnerability and geographic heterogeneity, strengthening the evidence base for integrating maternal health into India’s heat-health policy and practice.

Results

The final analytic sample comprised 1,76,193 women aged 15 to 49 years with complete data on complications, assisting computation of MC, and temporally aligned gestational thermal exposures. The mean maternal age was 27.5 ± 5.2 years, and most women (78.5%) resided in rural areas. Socioeconomic representation was broad: 25% of women belonged to the poorest household wealth quintile, and 14.6% to the richest quintile.

More than half of the women (56.6%, n = 99,665) reported at least one MC symptom during their most recent pregnancy. The prevalence of complications varied across age groups, peaking among women aged 20–24 years (57.7%) followed by 56.7% among those aged 25–29 years. A modest socioeconomic gradient was observed, with higher prevalence among the wealthiest quintile (59.2%) compared to the poorest quintile (54.7%). Urban residents exhibited slightly higher prevalence (57.9%) than rural residents (56.2%). These descriptive characteristics are available in Table S1.

Marked geographic variation in burden of complications was also evident. Certain states and union territories reported rates well above the national average, including Goa (75.5%), Ladakh (72.2%), and Punjab (71.6%). In contrast, lower prevalence rates were recorded in Nagaland (36.0%) and Manipur (39.5%). (Table S2).

Substantial variation in gestational thermal exposures was observed across geographic regions in India. The UTCI, which integrates air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and mean radiant temperature to model physiologically perceived heat stress, demonstrated wide exposure gradients across the study population. According to UTCI classification thresholds, values between 26°C and 32°C indicate moderate heat stress, while values exceeding 32°C represent strong to very strong and extreme heat stress. Across the study sample, mean UTCI ranged from 5.9°C in Ladakh and 6.9°C in Uttarakhand to above 29°C in several eastern and coastal states. Peak UTCI values exceeded 36 °C in states such as Bihar, Odisha, Gujarat, Tripura, and West Bengal, indicating periods of strong to very strong heat stress.

Association between gestational UTCI and MCs

To investigate the association between ambient heat stress and MCs, we estimated a series of multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for maternal age, parity, household wealth, residence, and state fixed effects. All models used UTCI exposure in its original units (Kelvin), as obtained from ERA5-HEAT. Because UTCI is formally defined in degrees Celsius, and a change of 1 K equals a 1°C difference, results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) per 1-degree increase in UTCI for consistency with established UTCI thresholds and interpretability.

In the full pregnancy models using mean UTCI, higher average gestational thermal exposure was associated with increased odds of MC. Specifically, each 1-degree increase in mean UTCI across the pregnancy window was associated with a 0.4% increase in the odds of MC (aOR 1.004; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.000–1.008; p = 0.042) (Table 1). The association persisted after adjustment for all covariates and state-level heterogeneity.

Adjusted effects of covariates

Parity was inversely associated with MCs across all models. In the full pregnancy models using mean UTCI, the aOR for parity was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.925–0.969; p < 0.001), with similar estimates observed across trimester-specific and maximum UTCI models.

Household wealth and place of residence showed modest associations with MCs, although statistical significance was marginal in most models. Urban residence was associated with slightly higher odds of MCs compared with rural residence in the full pregnancy model (aOR 1.061; 95% CI: 0.994–1.134; p = 0.071).

Maternal age was not significantly associated with MCs after adjustment for other covariates.

State fixed effects were included in all models to account for regional heterogeneity.

Discussion

This study examined the association between ambient heat stress measured by UTCI and MCs in India. The analysis contributes to a growing body of research examining the health implications of increasing heat exposure, particularly among physiologically vulnerable populations such as pregnant women. Although the relationship between heat exposure and adverse pregnancy outcomes has been increasingly documented, evidence focusing specifically on MCs remains limited.

In adjusted models, each 1°C rise in mean UTCI led to a 0.4%–0.6% rise in the odds of MCs, with strongest association seen in second trimester, whereas higher mean maximum UTCI was associated with lower odds of MCs. This suggests that sustained heat exposure, rather than single heat exposure, may pose a significant threat to maternal health. The reason for this can be attributed to the physiological changes in the pregnant woman’s body, because of additional stress on the cardiovascular system, thus making thermoregulation difficult. The second trimester is a period of rapid fetal growth and difficulty in maternal adaptations due to thermal change can be overwhelming for the female’s body and may lead to severe MCs.15

In contrast, the heat exposure during the third trimester showed no significant association. One possible explanation is that behavioral modifications such as reduced outdoor activity or increased rest during late pregnancy, may reduce heat exposure during this period. Additionally, pregnancies experiencing severe complications earlier in gestation may not progress to term and therefore may not be captured in analyses restricted to live births. Previous studies examining heat exposure during pregnancy have focused on adverse birth outcomes like stillbirth, low birth weight, pregnancy-induced hypertension, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, neonatal stress, and emergency hospital admissions. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizing such outcomes reported an increase in obstetric complications during extreme heat exposure.11 Our analysis focused on MCs among those with live birth; hence, the pattern of association observed across trimesters may differ from studies focusing on fetal or neonatal outcomes. Evidence from South India also suggests that occupational heat exposure during pregnancy has three times higher risk in first trimester and two times in third trimester of occurrence of adverse events such as miscarriage and preterm births.9 While that study examined fetal outcomes, the present analysis suggests that MCs may represent another dimension through which heat exposure affects pregnancy health, and may have a different temporal association.

A notable finding of this study was the substantial geographic variation in both MC prevalence and gestational heat exposure across India. Some states with high thermal exposure also showed high prevalence of MCs, whereas others did not. Likewise, some states with relatively lower UTCI profiles showed higher reported complication prevalence. This heterogeneity suggests that the relationship between heat stress and MCs is likely shaped by contextual factors beyond ambient exposure alone. These may include differences in health system capacity, referral pathways, care-seeking patterns, awareness and reporting of complications, occupational exposure, hydration practices, housing conditions, and other region-specific adaptive mechanisms.1,13 For this reason, state fixed effects were included in the models to account for unmeasured regional heterogeneity.

Previous evidence shows that climate vulnerability often intersects with socioeconomic disadvantages, influencing the capacity of community to cope with environmental stressors. Areas lacking access to clean water, electricity supply, cooling appliances, and obstetric care are less equipped to protect pregnant women during extreme heat.13 Differences in health system capacity and service availability may therefore partially explain the geographic heterogeneity observed in MC prevalence across states. States with stronger healthcare systems and better antenatal care coverage may be better positioned to detect, manage, and prevent complications during pregnancy. The higher prevalence of MCs observed in more urbanized settings should also be interpreted cautiously. The pregnant women in urban areas have better access to healthcare facilities as compared to rural areas, which may increase the likelihood that complications are recognized and reported. In contrast, women in rural or resource-constrained settings may experience barriers to accessing care, resulting in potential underreporting of pregnancy-related complications. This differential may partly explain the higher observed prevalence in urban populations despite potentially comparable or even greater underlying vulnerability in rural areas.

Additionally, the role of cultural and behavioral factors that vary regionally also contributes to variability in heat-related effects in pregnancy. In several parts of India, pregnant women remain actively engaged in agricultural or manual labor activities, which may involve prolonged exposure to high temperatures with limited access to shade, rest, or hydration.9,13 Such occupational patterns may increase susceptibility to heat-related physiological stress during pregnancy and may contribute to the regional variability observed in MC patterns.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between heat exposure and MCs is shaped not only by environmental conditions but also by social, occupational, and health system factors. The present analysis assumed a linear exposure-response relationship; however, potential non-linear effects and threshold responses to heat exposure could not be examined and warrant further investigation. As heat exposure intensifies in many regions due to ongoing climate change, these intersecting vulnerabilities may contribute to widening health inequities, particularly among populations with limited adaptive capacity.1,13 Identifying geographic areas where high heat exposure coincides with limited health system capacity may therefore help prioritize adaptive interventions and resource allocation.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is among the first analyses to examine MCs in relation to UTCI in India. Another strength is the utilization of large-nation representative data of NFHS-5 enables examination of these relationships across diverse climatic and socioeconomic contexts in India. In addition, the use of trimester-specific exposure windows allowed identification of potential gestational periods of vulnerability that may not be apparent in analyses using only full-pregnancy averages. Adjustment for key maternal and socioeconomic variables, along with the inclusion of state fixed effects, helped account for regional heterogeneity in health systems, climate conditions, and population characteristics.

Public health implications and recommendations

Integrating maternal health complications into heat-health preparedness strategies. Pregnant women represent a physiologically vulnerable population, yet many heat action plans do not explicitly address maternal and newborn health. Identifying pregnant women as a priority group in heat early-warning systems and climate adaptation programs may help reduce vulnerability during extreme heat events. Potential interventions include provision of shaded rest areas and hydration support, targeted public advisories for pregnant women during heat waves, and adjustments to antenatal outreach schedules or clinic hours to minimize exposure during peak temperature periods.

Health facilities providing maternity services should also be prepared to operate safely during periods of extreme heat. Ensuring reliable cooling infrastructure, water availability, and backup power supply may be important for maintaining safe maternal care environments. Training healthcare providers to recognize and manage heat-related illness during pregnancy may further improve preparedness and response. A recent scoping review found that relatively few heat health action plans globally have incorporated maternal, newborn, and child health considerations into preparedness frameworks.14 Integrating environmental exposure monitoring with maternal health surveillance systems may therefore strengthen early detection of emerging risks and support more responsive public health planning.14

The study provides evidence that ambient heat stress is associated with MCs in India. The sustained heat exposure during pregnancy, particularly mid-gestation, may influence the risk of MCs. Although the observed associations were modest, they are relevant in a setting where large numbers of pregnancies occur and where heat exposure affects wide populations. The possibility of non-linear exposure-response relationships and threshold effects of heat exposure could not be examined in the present analysis and warrants further investigation. As heat exposure intensifies in many regions due to climate change, strengthening climate-sensitive maternal health planning will become increasingly important. Addressing this challenge will require coordinated efforts across public health systems, clinical care, and environmental monitoring frameworks to ensure that maternal health is protected in a warming climate.

Limitations of the study

Despite the strengths of this study, we would like to highlight certain limitations. Firstly, cross-sectional, retrospective study design limits the causal inference. Second, MCs were identified using self-reported survey indicators rather than clinically verified diagnoses and may therefore be subject to recall bias or misclassification. The use of a five-year recall period may further introduce recall inaccuracy, and differential reporting across population subgroups cannot be ruled out. Thirdly, our exposure assessment was ecological, as district UTCI values were assigned to pregnancies, which may not reflect each individual’s true exposure. Fourth, the analysis included only pregnancies resulting in live births and therefore did not capture pregnancies ending in miscarriage, stillbirth, or maternal death. In addition, gestational timing was approximated using month-level data, which may have introduced imprecision in trimester-specific exposure classification. Season-specific analyses were not conducted, and potential variation in exposure by season of pregnancy or delivery could not be explicitly examined. Finally, the data used in this analysis correspond to the period 2019–2021; heat exposure in recent years has intensified in several parts of India, suggesting that future risks may differ from those observed in this dataset.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study population, data sources and variables

Individual-level data were obtained from the NFHS-5 (2019 -2021), part of the global Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program, implemented in India by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India. The survey used a stratified, two-stage sampling design to ensure representativeness at national, state, and district levels.17,18

Women aged 15–49 years were interviewed regarding their reproductive history, obstetric complications, household characteristics, and access to care. For this analysis, we restricted the sample to women who reported at least one live birth in the five years preceding the survey. We analyzed information related to their most recent live birth only, for which complete reproductive, and demographic data were available. The required variables were obtained from Individual Recode (IR) file.

The primary maternal health variables were extracted from the IR file, including the respondent’s age (v012), parity (v201), household wealth index (v190), place of residence (v025), and state of residence (v024). Gestational age at birth (m17_1, in months), date of interview (v008, century month code) and child’s age in months at interview (b19_01), were used to reconstruct the estimated conception dates. Because NFHS records gestational age and child age in months, trimester-specific exposure windows were approximated at the monthly level. Additionally, obstetric complications related to the most recent birth were obtained from variables s443_1, s434_1, s435_1, and m47_1 and used construct a composite indicator of self-reported maternal complications during pregnancy.

Ethical considerations

This study used publicly available, anonymized secondary datasets, hence no ethical approval was required for this analysis.

Method details

Study design

This cross-sectional, population-based study examined the association between ambient heat stress, measured via the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), and maternal complications (MC) among women of reproductive age in India. The analysis integrated spatially and temporally matched meteorological reanalysis data with individual-level health and demographic data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5).

Climate data and UTCI computation

Ambient thermal exposure during pregnancy was quantified using the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), a biometeorological indicator expressed in degrees Celsius (°C). The UTCI represents the equivalent air temperature of a standardized outdoor environment for someone walking at 4 km/h, wearing light clothing, in shade, that would elicit the same physiological responses (e.g., skin blood flow, sweat rate, core temperature) as actual environmental conditions. It synthesizes four meteorological parameters: 2 m air temperature, 2 m relative humidity (or dew point), 10 m wind speed, and mean radiant temperature (MRT) through the Fiala multi-node thermoregulation model.2

Hourly district-level UTCI data comprising of mean, maximum, and minimum values were acquired from ERA5-HEAT, a derivative dataset of European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecast’s (ECMWF) ERA5 reanalysis, available via Copernicus Climate Data Store API. ERA5-HEAT provides global, gridded hourly UTCI at a 0.25 ° × 0.25 ° spatial resolution (∼27–31 km grid cells).2,12 For analysis, UTCI values were used in Kelvin as provided in the ERA5 HEAT dataset; for interpretative clarity and reporting, values are presented here in equivalent °C.

To align UTCI exposure with individual pregnancies, we calculated estimated gestational windows as follows: the estimated conception month was determined by subtracting the reported gestational age at birth (variable m17_1, in months) from the child’s birth month which was obtained by subtracting child’s age in months at interview from the date of interview (variable v008- variable b19_01). We then defined four exposure periods: full pregnancy (conception to delivery), first trimester (0–13 weeks), second trimester (14–26 weeks), and third trimester (27 weeks to delivery). For each woman, district-level hourly UTCI series were aggregated to daily mean and daily maximum, and these daily values were averaged within each gestational window to obtain mean UTCI and mean of daily maximum UTCI exposures.

Spatial mapping used GADM version 4.1 national shapefiles, with NFHS cluster GPS coordinates linked to administrative districts via R’s sf package. Clusters near district borders were assigned based on proximity to district centroids. UTCI values for each district were computed by averaging across all intersecting ERA5-HEAT grid cells using raster and data.table.

Temporal alignment was achieved by matching daily UTCI values to each woman’s estimated gestational timeline, enabling precise calculation of both average and peak thermal exposure metrics for full pregnancy and trimester-specific models.

Definition of outcome and exposure variables

The primary outcome, Maternal Complications (MC), was defined as a binary variable (1 = presence of any maternal complication, 0 = none). As NFHS does not include clinical indicators required to identify severe maternal morbidity or near miss events, the outcome was constructed using seld-reported pregnancy-related symptoms captured in the survey.

A woman was classified as have experienced maternal complications if she reported any of the following during her most recent pregnancy: excessive vaginal bleeding (s443_1), convulsions not caused by fever (s434_1), swelling of the legs, face, or body (s435_1), or vision problems in daylight (m47_1). Respondents who answered “No” to all four indicators were coded as MC = 0; those missing responses for all four indicators were excluded.

Exposure variables were constructed using the UTCI. For each participant, two primary exposure metrics were calculated across the full estimated gestational period: the arithmetic mean of daily UTCI values, and the arithmetic mean of daily maximum UTCI values. To assess the timing of exposure effects, both metrics were also computed for each trimester defined as 0–13, 14–26, and 27 weeks until delivery. All UTCI variables were treated as continuous measures. Separate regression models were estimated for full-pregnancy exposure and trimester-specific exposures.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Covariates and confounder adjustment

All multivariable models included potential confounders identified a priori: maternal age (continuous), parity (continuous), household wealth quintile (categorical, 1 = poorest to 5 = richest), and place of residence (urban versus rural). State fixed effects (NFHS variable v024) were included to adjust for unobserved regional heterogeneity in healthcare infrastructure, climate adaptation capacity, socioeconomic development, and survey implementation quality.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine associations between gestational thermal exposure and maternal complications. Multivariable logistic regression models were specified as: logit{P(MCi=1)} = β0+β1UTCIi + covariates + state fixed effects, where MCiindicates maternal complications for woman i and UTCIi indicates the relevant gestational heat exposure metric. Separate models were estimated for mean UTCI and mean of daily maximum UTCI, each for the full pregnancy and trimester-specific exposure windows. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Two sets of models were estimated: the first using mean UTCI (in Kelvin), and the second using the mean of daily maximum UTCI (in Kelvin). Each exposure metric was evaluated for the full pregnancy window and separately for trimester-specific exposure periods. Robust standard errors were applied to account for potential heteroskedasticity. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed for all covariates to assess multicollinearity; all VIFs were < 2.0, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

Descriptive statistics incorporated DHS sample weights to ensure representativeness, whereas regression models were unweighted due to the inclusion of state fixed effects, which serve as an internal control for regional sampling variability. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 using the glm, broom, lme4, and survey packages, along with custom scripts.
