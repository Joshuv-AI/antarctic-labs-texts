A rare case of left eye Purtscher-like retinopathy following history of right eye endogenous panophthalmitis- a case report
Authors: Azzahra Muhamad Asri, Tan Kuan Sze, Norlaila Talib, Othmaliza Othman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe report a rare case of left eye Purtscher-like retinopathy following history of right endogenous panophthalmitis.Case presentationA 61-year-old male with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented with a 5-day history of right eye redness, swelling, and progressive vision loss. Systemic symptoms included vomiting, diarrhea, and left lower limb redness. On examination, the right eye had no light perception with signs of panophthalmitis, while the left eye had reduced vision (6/18), with early fundus changes. B-scan of the right eye revealed vitritis, choroidal abscess, retinal detachment, and scleral thickening. Vitreous cultures confirmed Staphylococcus aureus. The patient was treated with intravitreal vancomycin and ceftazidime, followed by enucleation. He received intravenous antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, cefazolin, later switched to cloxacillin) for one month as per infectious disease advice. Subsequently on day 7 of admission, the left eye showed worsening cotton wool spots without vitritis or choroiditis. Intravitreal antibiotics were administered prophylactically; vitreous cultures were negative.ConclusionThe natural history of Purtscher retinopathy which should not be ignored in complex clinical contexts even when there is no history of trauma. Hence, all cases with cotton wool spots with central and paracentral involvement and with few haemorrhages should be viewed with high suspicion of Purtscher-like retinopathy. Nevertheless, a meticulous follow-up is mandatory to avoid its severe complications.

Introduction

Purtscher retinopathy is a rare condition that is noted in cases related to various types of trauma. The characteristic finding in the fundus is the presence of multiple Purtscher flecken. Purtscher-like retinopathy has a similar presentation in the fundus, but without an association with trauma. Purpose of this case report to report a rare case of left eye Purtscher-like retinopathy following history of right endogenous panophthalmitis.

Method

Case report.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old gentleman with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented with right eye redness and blurred vision for 5 days, preceded by left lower limb redness. There was no history of ocular trauma. Systemic examination revealed signs of lower limb cellulitis with sepsis.

Visual acuity in the right eye was light perception, and 6/9 in the left eye. He was diagnosed with right eye endogenous panophthalmitis secondary to Staphylococcus aureus, with associated choroidal abscess, and subsequently underwent enucleation.

He was initially treated with intravenous ciprofloxacin and cefazolin for 7 days, which was then changed to intravenous cloxacillin based on vitreous culture and sensitivity results confirming Staphylococcus aureus. Antibiotic therapy continued for a total of one month, as advised by the infectious disease team.

On day 7 of admission, the left eye fundus examination revealed multiple whitish superficial retinal lesions at the posterior pole, but there was no vitritis, retinitis, or choroiditis. Anterior segment of left eye showed no anterior chamber inflammation. A Doppler ultrasound of the lower limb was normal.

The case was reviewed by the medical retina team. Initially, the patient was treated for presumed left eye endogenous endophthalmitis. However, vitreous tap showed no growth, and the retinal lesions increased in number and size despite multiple intravitreal antibiotic injections, raising suspicion of an alternative diagnosis.

Upon re-evaluation, the retina team identified the lesions as cotton wool spots, with no signs of vitritis, retinitis, or choroiditis. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the macula revealed hyperreflective foci in the inner retina, with no subretinal or intraretinal fluid. Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed masking over the cotton wool spots without evidence of vasculitis or capillary dropout.

The final diagnosis was revised to Purtscher-like retinopathy in the left eye, likely secondary to systemic sepsis, with no evidence of endophthalmitis.

At two months post-right eye enucleation, the left eye visual acuity improved to 6/12, and the number of cotton wool spots had decreased.

Discussion

Purtscher-like retinopathy is a rare retinal condition marked by Purtscher flecken, retinal hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, and retinal edema. It is commonly linked to systemic conditions like acute pancreatitis, vasculitis, or infections without trauma [1]. Its occurrence after endogenous panophthalmitis, a severe ocular infection, is very rare.

Pathogenesis of Purtscher retinopathy has been a field of intense research and controversy spanning decades. The most accepted theory centers on microembolization of retinal vasculature, resulting in arteriolar precapillary occlusion and microvascular infarct of retinal nerve fiber layer (resulting in cotton-wool spots) [2]. Embolization can occur from a fat emboli (as described in long bone fractures) or as a result of disseminated pancreatic proteases in the systemic circulation, as occurs in acute pancreatitis. It has also been proposed that microembolization derives from leukoembolization by leukocytary aggregation. C5 and complement activation would play a crucial role with secondary lymph extravasation. In addition, in cases of chest compression (or weight-lifting or battered baby syndrome), impaired venous return and acute expansion of retinal veins may produce the clinical fundoscopic appearance of Purtscher retinopathy, such as cotton-wool spots, retinal hemorrhages and, less frequently, lesions resembling Purtscher flecken. Disseminated lipase after systemic injury can also result in a retinal vasculitis that leads to thrombosis and vascular occlusion.

In this case, the patient developed Purtscher-like retinopathy in the left eye after a history of right eye panophthalmitis, suggesting that systemic inflammation or immune response from the panophthalmitis may have contributed to the retinopathy [3].

The treatment for Purtscher-like retinopathy focuses on managing the underlying infection, optimizing systemic conditions, monitoring retinal health, and preventing complications like retinal ischemia or macular edema. Early intervention and close monitoring are key to reducing the risk of long-term vision loss.

The case highlights the need for increased awareness of Purtscher-like retinopathy in severe ocular infections like endogenous panophthalmitis and emphasizes the importance of differential diagnosis in patients with retinal changes after significant systemic infections. More research is needed to understand the pathophysiology and long-term visual outcomes of this rare condition.

Conclusion

The natural history of Purtscher retinopathy which should not be ignored in complex clinical contexts even when there is no history of trauma. Hence, all cases with cotton wool spots with central and paracentral involvement and with few haemorrhages should be viewed with suspicion of Purtscher retinopathy after ruling out infective causes.
