Discrimination and intersecting inequities to healthcare access among refugee newcomers in the United States
Authors: Hyojin Im, Gashaye Melaku Tefera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefugee newcomers in the United States experience pronounced disparities in healthcare access, shaped not only by individual-level or material constraints but also by systemic and structural inequities. Perceived discrimination, particularly when compounded by intersecting identities such as race and sex, may significantly influence these access barriers. This study investigates the relationship between perceived discrimination and healthcare access barriers among resettled refugees, examining whether this relationship is statistically associated with biological sex and moderated by race and sex.MethodsData were drawn from the nationally representative Annual Survey of Refugees (2020–2022), comprising 4,246 respondents. Healthcare access barriers were operationalized as a continuous index of self-reported obstacles to care. Negative binomial regression was used to estimate the association between perceived discrimination and healthcare barriers, adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates. Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition quantified the explained and unexplained components of disparity in barriers by discrimination status. A mediation model assessed whether perceived discrimination functioned as an intermediate variable linking sex to healthcare barriers, and a three-way interaction model (Discrimination × Race × Sex) evaluated intersectional moderation effects.ResultsPerceived discrimination emerged as the strongest independent predictor of healthcare barriers, even after adjusting for English proficiency, financial hardship, and physical health. Refugees reporting discrimination faced significantly higher barriers (mean difference = 0.56, p < .001). Notably, 57.7% of the disparity in access remained unexplained by observable covariates, reflecting potential structural and institutional inequities. Mediation analysis indicated an indirect association between sex and healthcare barriers through perceived discrimination (indirect effect B = 0.061, 95% CI [0.030, 0.094]), representing about 35% of the total association (B = 0.176, p < .001). Discrimination’s effects varied significantly across racial and sex groups (F(18, 3585) = 2.31, p < .01), with Black, Middle Eastern/North African, and Asian women reporting the highest access barriers under high discrimination, while White men reported the lowest.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the structurally embedded nature of refugee healthcare inequities. Discrimination functions as both a pathway and amplifier of access disparities, particularly for multiply marginalized groups. Addressing these inequities requires intersectional, structurally competent strategies that treat discrimination as a modifiable determinant of health and prioritize institutional accountability in refugee health policy and practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01770-6.Keywords: Refugee resettlement, Discrimination, Intersectionality, Healthcare access, Gender, Race, Structural inequality, Decomposition analysis, Annual Survey of Refugees (ASR)

Introduction

Healthcare access is a fundamental determinant of population health. Yet for many refugees resettled in the United States, equitable access remains elusive [55]. Although available data specific to refugees is limited, higher percentage of immigrants in general, including refugees, do not have health insurance. For example, uninsured rates for undocumented immigrants are 50%, 18% for lawfully present immigrants, compared to 6% for naturalized citizens and 8% US-born citizens [46]. Refugees have lower rates of using emergency services [29], and only 15% of refugees have regular source of care [67]. Despite policy efforts aimed at facilitating integration, refugees often face a constellation of barriers that restrict their ability to secure timely and adequate healthcare [16, 33]. These challenges extend beyond individual and material barriers —such as limited English proficiency, lack of insurance, or culturally rooted beliefs— to structural forces embedded in institutional systems and sociopolitical contexts [8, 70]. Some of these structural factors include discrimination, fragmented care coordination and systemic underinvestment in safety-net services, rendering healthcare access particularly precarious for newly arrived refugee populations [4, 11]. Rather than operating in isolation, these factors interact and compound one another, producing unique and intensified barriers to healthcare access among refugees [33].

Discrimination intersects with other axes of identity such as gender and race, compounding barriers to healthcare access across both institutional and interpersonal domains [28, 43]. Structural discrimination refers to the systemic privileging of some groups over others through organizational practices, public policies, and societal norms [26, 85], often operating without overt intent. This dynamic is effectively captured by the concept of intersectionality [19], a framework that reveals how overlapping systems of oppression create unique forms of disadvantage for individuals with multiple marginalized identities [60, 85]. As theorized by Crenshaw, intersectionality posits that systems of power such as racism, sexism, xenophobia, and patriarchy do not act independently but interlock to produce unique forms of disadvantage and oppression [19]. This framework is especially relevant in the refugee context, where individuals often hold multiple marginalized identities and face exclusion not only based on who they are, but how they are socially positioned within interlocking hierarchies of inequality [17, 79]. For example, refugee women may face discrimination not only on the basis of their migration status but also due to racialization and gender, exposing them to layered forms of exclusion within healthcare systems [33, 41].

Discrimination, conceptualized broadly as unfair treatment based on race, sex, ethnicity, nationality, language, or immigration status, operates both within healthcare settings and across societal domains [5, 10]. Although often framed as of individual bias, discrimination is inherently structural [34], manifesting through policy exclusions, bureaucratic complexity, and institutional mistrust that marginalize already underprivileged populations [60, 85]. For refugees, discrimination operates across multiple dimensions, including race, immigration status, language, and gender, and is both a lived experience and a barrier to accessing equitable healthcare [20, 41]. Discrimination extends beyond interpersonal interactions to systemic processes, such as restricted eligibility for public insurance programs, linguistic isolation in service provision, and racialized patterns of healthcare distrust [51, 84]. It shapes who receives care, the quality of interactions with providers, and whether individuals feel safe and welcome in healthcare settings. Discrimination further entrenches refugees’ vulnerability by eroding trust in institutions and discouraging engagement with healthcare providers [63]. While discrimination refers to actual acts of unfair treatment, perceived discrimination is an individual’s subjective experience, or stressor, stemming from exclusion or perceived threats of unfair treatment based on characteristics such as cultural background, race, ethnicity, gender, immigration status, or beliefs [2, 69, 87]. Varied but concerning rates of perceived discrimination were documented across different refugee groups in the U.S. A national survey showed that one in five (22%) of refugees reported experiencing discrimination with prevalence of 12% at work place, and 3.4% in health-related situations [71]. In Ohio, over one-quarter of refugees reported discrimination, with 52.1% treated with less respect and 33.5% experiencing racial discrimination [27]. Similarly, more than 32% of Vietnamese refugees reported perceived discrimination [57]. Perceived discrimination is associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, including increased healthcare avoidance, delays in seeking treatment, lower quality of patient-provider interactions, and worse self-rated health [4, 41]. Among refugee and immigrant populations specifically, perceived discrimination has been linked to psychological distress, reduced mental health service use, and impaired chronic disease management [20, 21].

Although the adverse effects of discrimination on healthcare access are increasingly recognized, important knowledge gaps remain in the refugee health literature. Most studies frame discrimination as a matter of individual biases, such as providers’ attitudes and stereotypes toward refugees, instead of its deep-rooted systemic and structural [62, 73]. This framing individualizes discrimination and diverts attention from systemic causes and places undue burden on individuals to navigate inequities [34]. In addition, research rarely explores how discrimination intersects with other social identities and mechanisms of oppression, specifically gender and ethnicity, to produce compounded barriers to healthcare [23, 43]. For instance, refugee women report specific barriers tied to gendered expectations, such as discomfort with male healthcare providers, lack of culturally appropriate reproductive health services, and childcare burdens that restrict appointment attendance [6, 9]. Refugee and immigrant women also report feeling devalued, unheard, and having their concerns dismissed or their pain minimized [25, 40]. Simultaneously, racialized refugees, particularly those from African, Middle Eastern, and Latinx backgrounds, face heightened risks of racial profiling, language discrimination, and overt xenophobia [6, 70]. Although emerging evidence suggests that these intersectional experiences are linked to both increased healthcare barriers and poor health outcomes over time [14], much of the existing literature still treats race and gender as independent axes of inequality, rather than examining their interaction [17, 79]. Furthermore, quantitative studies that parse the mechanisms linking discrimination to healthcare barriers, such as differential access to resources or cumulative psychological stress, remain scarce in refugee health research (see [2]). Decomposition techniques, which distinguish between disparities attributable to differences in individual characteristics versus differences in structural or systemic factors, are rarely employed in this context, limiting understanding of how discrimination becomes embodied in access inequalities.

Addressing these gaps is urgent amid a shifting policy environment in the United States. Recent restrictions on immigration and refugee resettlement have heightened exposure to discrimination among refugee communities, with potentially lasting implications for health equity [4, 6, 72]. A clearer understanding of how discrimination shapes healthcare access is essential for designing interventions that address the root causes of health disparities, rather than their symptoms. Research also revealed that the COCIV-19 pandemic exacerbated existing discrimination and inequalities, with refugees experiencing difficulties of accessing healthcare [56]. Refugees suffered disproportionately from poorly managed chronic diseases, isolation, economic hardship and systemic inattention that increased their vulnerabilities [65]. Increased discrimination during the pandemic worsened mental health conditions among refugees, especially among those with insecure housing and legal status [52]. This study draws on intersectionality theory and national survey data to examine how perceived discrimination is associated with reported healthcare barriers, accounting for a range of sociodemographic, linguistic, economic, and health-related characteristics. Using a Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition approach [15, 58], the study examines disparities in healthcare barriers between refugees who report discrimination and those who do not, separating disparities attributable to observable characteristics from those suggestive of systemic inequities. The study also investigates whether perceived discrimination mediates the relationship between biological sex and healthcare barriers, assessing whether gender disparities arise from differential exposure to discrimination in healthcare access. It further explores three-way interactions among discrimination, race, and sex, identifying intersectional subgroups that experience the most pronounced healthcare access challenges.

Methods

This study uses data drawn from the Annual Survey of Refugees (ASR) between 2020 and 2022, a nationally representative survey administered by the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR). The ASR collects information on refugees’ socioeconomic and integration outcomes within the first five years after resettlement. Detailed survey design, instrumentation, and administration procedures are available in ORR’s annual technical reports [75–77]. For this analysis, we combined the 2020, 2021, and 2022 ASR datasets to increase statistical power and capture variation across arrival cohorts (FY2015–FY2021). The integration of three consecutive survey years provides a methodologically consistent, linguistically inclusive, and nationally representative sample, enabling robust analysis of healthcare barriers across diverse arrival cohorts and resettlement contexts. We restricted the analytic sample to principal applicants aged 18 and older with complete data on key study variables, resulting in a final weighted sample of 4,246 refugees. Table 1 presents the distribution of total refugee arrivals by fiscal year alongside the corresponding ASR sample targets for each wave. As shown, each wave achieved the intended total of 1,500 completed household interviews, with strategic allocation across arrival years to support both cohort-specific and pooled analyses. The cumulative target sample across all three waves (N = 4,500) provides balanced coverage of early, mid-, and recent-arrival cohorts within the FY2015–FY2021 refugee population. Each ASR wave is cross-sectional and samples new households independently, meaning that participants differ across years rather than being followed longitudinally. Because arrival cohorts overlap across waves, time since arrival may partially confound with the year of data collection. To address this, time since arrival was included as a continuous control variable in all models.

Key variables and analytical constructs

This study focused on constructs theoretically and empirically linked to healthcare access among resettled refugees, using variables available in the ASR dataset. Guided by prior literature on structural and psychosocial determinants of health, we identified measures reflecting individual, social, and systemic dimensions of access. Variables were selected based on their conceptual relevance, data completeness across the 2020–2022 waves, and consistency in question wording. All items were derived directly from respondent or household-level responses in the ASR public-use files. To ensure consistency across waves, only items that were identically worded and available in all three datasets (2020–2022) were retained. Items that were revised or added in later waves were excluded from the pooled analysis.

Healthcare access barriers

The primary outcome variable was healthcare access barriers, measured using a composite variable. This outcome was operationalized as a continuous index, derived from a set of multiple-response items capturing self-reported difficulties encountered when seeking medical care. Respondents were asked whether, in the past year, they had experienced difficulty accessing healthcare for any of the following reasons, recorded as binary responses: (a) cost of care, (b) transportation challenges, (c) language or communication barriers, (d) not knowing where to seek care, and (e) difficulty obtaining an appointment. The outcome represents the total number of distinct barriers each respondent reported. Higher values on the index indicate a greater cumulative burden of perceived access challenges. This measure was treated as a continuous outcome in all regression models.

Perceived discrimination

Perceived discrimination was assessed using the categorical variable, which grouped respondents based on their reported experiences of discrimination across multiple domains, including employment, policing, housing, and healthcare. Responses were coded as: no discrimination (1), some discrimination (2), and a lot of discrimination (3). For analytic purposes, particularly in mediation and decomposition models, this variable was recoded into a binary format (0 = no perceived discrimination; 1 = any perceived discrimination) to facilitate binary group comparisons and improve model interpretability. Exploratory factor analysis of the four discrimination items indicated a single dominant factor (first eigenvalue = 2.83; all loadings > 0.65), supporting unidimensional treatment as a composite variable.

Sociodemographic predictors: biological sex and race/ethnicity

Biological sex was coded as a binary variable based on self-identification, with male assigned a value of 0 and female assigned a value of 1. Racial and ethnic identity was categorized into five mutually exclusive groups using the self-reported race5 variable: White, Black, Asian, Middle Eastern/North African (MENA), and Latinx. For regression analyses involving interaction terms or group comparisons, race/ethnicity was dummy coded with White respondents serving as the reference group.

Covariates

Several individual- and household-level covariates were included to account for potential confounding factors associated with healthcare access. Age was grouped into ordered categories, and education level was captured by an ordinal variable reflecting six levels of educational attainment. English proficiency at arrival was assessed using a four-point scale ranging from “not at all proficient” to “very proficient.” Native language literacy was assessed as a binary indicator of the respondent’s ability to read in their primary language, and current employment status was also coded as a binary variable. Additional structural factors included possession of a driver’s license and time since arrival in the U.S., the latter treated as a continuous measure.

Health-related covariates included self-rated physical health, assessed on a five-point scale from excellent to poor, and mental health status, based on reported frequency of sadness in the past month. Household size captured the number of individuals residing in the household. Economic stressors were measured through self-reported financial difficulty, receipt of cash assistance, and other public benefits. Health insurance coverage was operationalized as the number of months insured in the past year. Finally, the initial region of resettlement was included as a categorical variable to adjust for geographic variation in service environments.

Data analysis

Analyses proceeded in several stages to comprehensively examine the association between perceived discrimination and healthcare access barriers. Person-level survey weights from the ASR were used to adjust for the complex sampling design and produce estimates representative of the U.S. refugee population. Given that the dependent variable (number of healthcare barriers, 0–5) was a limited count and exhibited right-skew with a large proportion of zeros, we first estimated Poisson regression models and tested for overdispersion. The overdispersion test was statistically significant (z = 11.01, p < 0.001), indicating variance exceeding the mean. Accordingly, we employed negative binomial regression as the primary specification, which appropriately accommodates overdispersed count data [39]. Vuong tests comparing negative binomial and zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) models suggested minor evidence of excess zeros (AIC-corrected z = –5.02, p < 0.001; BIC-corrected z = 3.99, p < 0.001). However, the standard negative binomial model was retained as the main analytic specification due to its parsimony and interpretability, with zero-inflated models used as sensitivity checks. Ordinary least squares (OLS) and Poisson regressions were additionally estimated for robustness, yielding substantively similar coefficients and significance patterns. Model fit indices (AIC, pseudo-R2) improved under count specifications, confirming that the main findings are robust to alternative estimators (see Appendix A). All categorical predictors were dummy-coded, and continuous variables were standardized when appropriate to facilitate interpretation and reduce multicollinearity [48]. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 5 for all predictors, indicating acceptable specification. Robust standard errors were applied to address minor homoscedasticity.

To decompose observed disparities, a Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition analysis was performed to partition the total mean difference in healthcare barriers between individuals reporting no discrimination and those reporting some or a lot of discrimination [15, 58] This decomposition quantified the "explained" component (due to covariate differences) and the "unexplained" component (reflecting differential effects or unmeasured structural inequities). The decomposition was conducted using R (v4.2) with the oaxaca package [44], specifying a linear decomposition based on OLS estimation, which is appropriate for partitioning mean differences in outcomes [44]. Standard errors for decomposition components were obtained through 1,000 bootstrap replications to enhance the precision and reliability of inferential tests.

Causal mediation models were subsequently specified to examine whether perceived discrimination mediated the relationship between biological sex and healthcare barriers. Mediation analysis was conducted using Python’s statsmodels and mediation packages. Indirect, direct, and total effects were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap procedures with 5,000 iterations to derive robust 95% confidence intervals [36]. Model specifications included logistic regression for the mediator (perceived discrimination) and linear regression for the continuous outcome variable (i.e., number of barriers), ensuring appropriate link functions given variable distributions. To assess whether the relationship between discrimination and barriers varied across intersectional identities, a three-way interaction model was specified, testing the joint moderation of sex and race/ethnicity on the association between perceived discrimination and healthcare access barriers. This model included all lower-order main effects and two-way interactions, in addition to the critical three-way interaction term (Discrimination × Sex × Race) [78]. Interaction effects were probed using marginal effects at representative values, and simple slopes were plotted for interpretive clarity. All models adjusted for a comprehensive set of sociodemographic, economic, and health-related covariates.

Missing data were handled via listwise deletion (i.e., complete case analysis), retaining only participants with non-missing values across the predictors, outcomes, and key covariates included in each specific model. This approach was deemed appropriate given the relatively low proportion of missingness across analytic variables (generally < 5%) and the assumption that data were missing completely at random (MCAR) or missing at random (MAR), which supports unbiased estimation in regression contexts under listwise deletion [3, 24]. Effect estimates, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were reported for all models. For decomposition and mediation models, bootstrapped standard errors and confidence intervals were prioritized over asymptotic values. Model fit statistics, including R2 and adjusted R2, were provided for each regression model. All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2) and Python (v3.11) within JupyterLab, leveraging packages including pandas [53], numpy [31], statsmodels [66], matplotlib [35], seaborn [83], mediation, and oaxaca.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Table 2 presents the sociodemographic and health characteristics of the refugee sample (N = 4,246), stratified by perceived discrimination level. Approximately 49% reported no discrimination, 28% reported some, and 23% a lot. The sample comprises of 57% men and 43% women, with a racially diverse composition: Black (31%), White (29%), Asian (19%), Latinx (12%), and MENA (10%). Most respondents were under age 40, with moderate to high educational attainment and limited English proficiency at arrival. The majority were employed (67%) and reported no recent financial difficulty (86%). Self-rated health was generally moderate, with 60% describing their physical health as good or better, and nearly half reporting sadness at least “some of the time”. Most had driver’s licenses, lived in larger households, and were distributed across all major U.S. regions.

Notable differences emerged across discrimination levels. Healthcare access barriers were significantly more common among those reporting higher discrimination, with only 34% of the “a lot” group experiencing no barriers, compared to 61% of those with no discrimination. Women and Latinx individuals were more represented in the high-discrimination group (50% and 27%, respectively), compared to 42% and 8% among those reporting no discrimination. This group also reported higher financial hardship (17% vs. 11%) and unemployment (10% vs. 5.9%), as well as poorer mental health, with 18% reporting sadness most or all of the time compared to 9% in the no-discrimination group. Racial disparities in perceived discrimination were statistically significant (χ2(8) = 371.45, p < 0.001), with Latinx and MENA refugees showing the highest proportions of reported discrimination (27% and 30%), whereas White and Asian refugees were more represented among those reporting none or low levels (35% and 18%). Notable regional differences were also observed, with those in the South overrepresented among those reporting high discrimination. English proficiency and driver’s license possession varied little across discrimination levels.

Predictors of healthcare access barriers

Diagnostic tests confirmed overdispersion in the Poisson model (z = 11.01, p < 0.001), justifying the use of negative binomial regression as the primary specification. Zero-inflated models were estimated for sensitivity; while they achieved slightly better AIC, BIC favored the simpler negative binomial model (AIC = 9265, BIC = 9468). Perceived discrimination emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of healthcare access barriers. In the negative binomial model, reporting any discrimination was associated with a 47% higher expected count of healthcare barriers (IRR = 1.47, 95% CI [1.34, 1.62], p < 0.001). The Poisson model produced a comparable effect (IRR = 1.45, 95% CI [1.34, 1.57], p < 0.001), confirming robustness across count specifications. Lower English proficiency (IRR = 1.10, 95% CI [1.05, 1.16]), financial hardship (IRR = 1.09, 95% CI [1.02, 1.17]), and poorer physical health (IRR = 1.45, 95% CI [1.34, 1.57]) also predicted significantly higher expected counts of barriers. In contrast, demographic factors such as gender, race/ethnicity, and years in the U.S. were not independently significant after adjusting for covariates. The overall model fit was satisfactory (pseudo-R2 = 0.15, AIC = 9265), and the likelihood-ratio test confirmed the joint significance of predictors (χ2(37) = 444.02, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Decomposition of healthcare barrier disparities by perceived discrimination

To disentangle the components of access disparities by perceived discrimination status, a Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition analysis was conducted to assess the mean difference in reported healthcare access barriers between individuals who reported no perceived discrimination and those who reported experiencing discrimination. The mean difference in reported healthcare barriers between groups with and without perceived discrimination was 0.56 points (SE = 0.10, p < 0.001), with the discrimination group reporting significantly greater difficulty accessing healthcare services. Of this total disparity, 42.3% of the gap (Δ = 0.24) was explained by differences in observed covariates, including self-rated physical health (20.2%), English proficiency at arrival (10.8%), financial hardship (8.9%), employment status (7.2%), education level (5.4%), and length of stay in the U.S. The remaining 57.7% (Δ = 0.32) was unexplained, suggesting the presence of structural factors not captured by measured individual-level characteristics. Mediation analysis and three-way interaction model on discrimination effects

Informed by prior literature on intersectional inequities and the centrality of discrimination, a focused analysis examined how sex and race modify the effects of perceived discrimination. First, a mediation model was estimated to assess whether perceived discrimination statistically operated as an intermediate variable in the relationship between sex and healthcare barriers. Results demonstrated a significant indirect effect: women were more likely to experience discrimination (a = 0.10, p < 0.001), which in turn was associated with greater barriers (b = 0.61, p < 0.001). The indirect effect (a × b = 0.061, 95% CI [0.030, 0.094]) represented approximately 35% of the total effect of sex on healthcare barriers (B = 0.176, p < 0.001). The model accounted for 7.2% of the variance in healthcare barriers (R2 = 0.072), indicating that perceived discrimination is statistically linked to observed sex differences in healthcare access barriers.

To further explore how perceived discrimination is statistically associated with the relationship between racial group membership and healthcare barriers, we estimated a second mediation model with racial group membership as the predictor. Results indicated a significant indirect pathway: racialized groups, particularly Black and MENA respondents, reported greater experiences of discrimination, which were in turn associated with elevated healthcare barriers. Although total effects varied by racial category, indirect effects were strongest among respondents from groups historically subject to structural racism, indicating that perceived discrimination helps explain the observed association between race and healthcare access difficulty.

To assess intersectional variation in the effects of discrimination, a three-way interaction model was estimated (Discrimination × Race × Sex). The interaction was statistically significant, F(18, 3585) = 2.31, p < 0.01, indicating that the relationship between discrimination and healthcare barriers differed significantly across racial/ethnicity and biological sex. Because the estimates are conditional on high perceived discrimination across all groups, the discrimination level held constant and not included as a separate column. These values capture the extent to which healthcare barriers are amplified for specific social positions when discrimination is present at its most elevated level. The estimates reveal that Black females (M = 1.68, SE = 0.24), MENA females (M = 1.63, SE = 0.21), and Asian females (M = 1.54, SE = 0.23) experienced the highest levels of healthcare barriers under high discrimination, while White males consistently reported the lowest (M = 0.88, SE = 0.17). These results highlight that racialized refugee women, particularly Black, MENA, and Asian women, report the highest number of access barriers under conditions of elevated discrimination. Given the cross-sectional nature of the ASR data, however, these mediation results should be interpreted as indicative of statistical associations rather than causal pathways. Discussion

This study offers one of the most comprehensive analyses to date of how perceived discrimination shapes healthcare barriers among resettled refugees in the United States. Descriptive findings revealed systematic variation in reported discrimination and its co-occurrence with other markers of vulnerability. Nearly half the sample reported experiencing no discrimination, while roughly one in four reported “a lot.” Multidimensional disadvantage clustered among respondents reporting high levels of discrimination: they were more likely to encounter multiple healthcare barriers, experience frequent sadness, face financial hardship, and reside in larger households. Women and Latinx refugees were disproportionately represented in the highest-discrimination group, while regional patterns indicated that respondents in the South were more likely to report exclusionary treatment. By contrast, factors such as English proficiency and driver’s license possession showed minimal variation across groups, suggesting that the relationship between discrimination and access is not reducible to individual-level integration markers alone.

Building on this empirical ground, the multivariate models highlight the role of perceived discrimination as a critical determinant of healthcare access, revealing how this influence is patterned along intersecting axes of social marginalization. The association between discrimination and increased healthcare barriers held across a diverse sample, net of demographic, economic, linguistic, and health-related covariates. These findings align with prior evidence that discriminatory treatment, both interpersonal or institutional, functions as a key mechanism by which health inequities are produced and sustained [21, 47, 85]. The persistence of these effects, net of conventional integration indicators, underscores the limitations of individual adaptation in the face of structurally embedded exclusion. These exclusionary processes are systemic, often invisible, and rooted in written and unwritten policies, health beliefs and practices [18], and a major driving force of the growing health inequity in the U.S. [88]. These findings also resonate with the Candidacy Framework [38], which views healthcare access as a dynamic and negotiated process shaped by institutional recognition of who is seen as a “legitimate candidate” for care. Within this framework, refugees’ experiences of discrimination can be interpreted as moments when their candidacy is contested or denied through bureaucratic, linguistic, or attitudinal barriers. This aligns with our observation that the unexplained component of disparities likely reflects institutional gatekeeping and perceived legitimacy within the healthcare system. Recent applications of the framework, such as among persons with multiple sclerosis [37], similarly highlight how structural judgments about deservingness and belonging shape access trajectories, offering a useful theoretical lens for interpreting our findings.

The Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition further disentangles the sources of disparity by separating variation attributable to observed characteristics from unexplained components that may reflect structural inequities. While factors such as poorer health, limited English proficiency, and financial hardship contributed to the gap in healthcare barriers between those who did and did not report discrimination, a substantial share of the disparity remained unaccounted for. These factors likely capture not only individual circumstances but also the downstream consequences of structural inequality. Limited English proficiency, for instance, is not merely a personal attribute but a reflection of restricted access to adult language education and linguistically inclusive healthcare systems [74]. Similarly, poorer self-rated health may represent the cumulative effects of resettlement stressors, underemployment, and constrained access to preventive care that systematically disadvantage refugees long before clinical encounters occur [30, 49]. Financial hardship, in turn, reflects broader exclusion from stable labor markets and social protection. Together, these “explained” factors point to the ways in which social and institutional arrangements shape refugees’ readiness to access care, while the remaining unexplained disparity underscores the operation of more diffuse, institutionalized barriers within healthcare systems themselves.

In the meantime, the unexplained portion likely reflects institutional processes that are not easily reduced to individual-level predictors, such as systemic racism in healthcare delivery, immigration-related restrictions, neighborhood-level inequities, racially biased administrative practices, provider gatekeeping, or uneven enforcement of eligibility rules [26, 59]. In this way, the unexplained component underscores the role of unmeasured, but powerful, structural forces in shaping refugee health inequities. The findings amplify previous calls for adopting a structural approach in understanding health disparities among racialized and minoritized groups [26]. Mediation analysis revealed that sex differences in access were partially explained by differential exposure to discrimination. While women in the sample did not uniformly face more barriers than men, those who experienced discrimination reported significantly higher levels of difficulty accessing healthcare. These findings suggest that gender-based disparities should not be interpreted as innate or behavioral, but as outcomes of differential positioning within systems of power. Beyond individual experiences, these gendered patterns reflect broader structural inequities in care systems. Refugee women often face compounded vulnerabilities arising from caregiving burdens, economic dependency, and gendered expectations within both household and institutional contexts, which can limit their ability to navigate health systems [86, 89]. Moreover, the intersection of gender with race and legal precarity can amplify institutional bias and invisibility in health policy and service delivery [80, 82]. Addressing these disparities requires moving beyond gender-neutral frameworks toward policies that explicitly recognize and respond to the gendered organization of care and access. Prior refugee studies have documented gender disparities in service access [9, 22, 68], but have rarely unpacked the mechanisms by which these emerge. The present findings imply that discrimination likely operates not only as a direct constraint but also as a pathway through which social hierarchies translate into healthcare exclusion. A recent study also confirmed a pathway that healthcare discrimination is associated with healthcare avoidance among Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC) directly as well as mediated by healthcare institutional betrayal [64].

The three-way interaction model provides a more refined understanding of how discrimination differentially constrains healthcare access across intersecting social identities. The relationship between perceived discrimination and healthcare barriers was not uniform but varied significantly by both race and sex, confirming the socially contingent nature of exclusion. Disparities in reported barriers under high discrimination were most pronounced among women of color, especially Black, MENA, and Asian women, highlighting a nearly one-point gap from White men. This gap underscores how discrimination compounds existing inequities in access to care. Furthermore, even within racial groups, sex shaped the severity of the impact. For example, MENA women reported substantially more barriers than MENA men (i.g., 1.63 vs. 1.21), highlighting that risk is not additive but multiplied at the intersection of marginalized identities. These patterns reinforce intersectionality theory’s claim that power operates through relational structures, wherein access to care is mediated not simply by individual traits but by one's positionality within overlapping systems of racial, gendered, and geographic exclusion [12, 19]. Similar patterns have been observed in U.S.-born populations, where compounded forms of marginalization are associated with reduced access to care and poorer health outcomes [13, 18].

Although regional variation was not the primary analytic focus, patterns observed across geographic contexts suggest that local resettlement environments matter. The U.S. refugee resettlement program operates through decentralized partnerships with states and voluntary agencies, creating significant differences in the availability and accessibility of healthcare support by location [45]. For example, a study reported that 40% of refugees were resettled in states without Medicaid expansion, which can substantially affect insurance options and disparities in healthcare access among refugees [1]. Our findings align with work showing that place-based factors, from political climates to healthcare infrastructure, can shape immigrant and refugee health trajectories in ways that intersect with individual characteristics [32]. These regional disparities likely reflect structural inequities embedded in state-level policy environments, particularly differences in Medicaid expansion, investment in safety-net healthcare, and local refugee support infrastructure [1, 81]. States with more restrictive eligibility policies and lower public health investment have been shown to widen access gaps for immigrants and refugees, underscoring that geographic variation in healthcare barriers reflects structural, not merely contextual, inequities. This regional dimension merits further exploration, particularly through multi-level or policy-sensitive approaches.

These findings provide new insight into how healthcare access barriers among refugees are shaped not only by who individuals are, but also by how they are positioned within intersecting systems of power and exclusion. Discrimination emerges here as a key mechanism by which gendered and racialized inequalities are activated and maintained. That these patterns are observable even within a relatively short post-resettlement period suggests that institutional factors, not just pre-migration trauma or transitional stress, play a central role in shaping health-relevant outcomes. In moving beyond additive models and isolated predictors, this study contributes to a more layered and empirically grounded understanding of refugee health disparities. It also raises important questions about how healthcare systems, social policy, and resettlement infrastructure may be restructured to mitigate, rather than reinforce, inequity.

Study limitations

While this study offers a robust examination of healthcare access among resettled refugees through multiple analytical lenses, several limitations must be considered in interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the pathways through which discrimination shapes healthcare access. Although the mediation analysis identified plausible mechanisms linking sex and perceived discrimination to healthcare barriers, these relationships remain associational. It is possible that difficulties in accessing care also heighten perceptions of exclusion or dissatisfaction with health systems. Longitudinal data would allow for a more precise examination of temporal ordering and could capture how repeated exposure to discrimination accumulates to affect access over time [61, 85]. Second, discrimination was measured through self-reports, which capture the affective and interpersonal dimensions of exclusion but may not reflect more implicit or institutional forms of discrimination that shape healthcare outcomes without being recognized as such [47, 59]. These self-reports are also sensitive to variation in cultural framing, social desirability, and levels of trust in formal institutions, all of which may influence disclosure across different refugee subgroups. Additionally, the variable for perceived discrimination was recoded into a binary format to allow for clearer comparisons in decomposition and mediation models. While this recoding facilitated interpretability, it may obscure variation in the intensity and context of discrimination experiences, such as distinctions between healthcare-specific and general discrimination. These constraints underscore the need for future research to employ more granular, multi-domain discrimination measures and to validate findings through qualitative or mixed-methods approaches.

While the models adjust for a wide range of individual-level characteristics, the absence of critical structural and contextual variables limits the explanatory depth of the analysis. Legal status distinctions, such as between asylum seekers and refugees with permanent residency, are known to shape both vulnerability to discrimination and access to health-related entitlements [61], yet such distinctions are not captured in the ASR data. Similarly, neighborhood-level healthcare availability, local policy environments, and proximity to culturally and linguistically appropriate services likely influence outcomes in ways that remain unmeasured. These omitted variables constrain the ability to fully disentangle individual from institutional drivers of disparity. Moreover, although the ASR provides the most comprehensive national dataset on resettled refugees, its sampling frame may systematically underrepresent refugees with high mobility or unstable housing, who are also likely to face the most severe barriers to healthcare access. While post-stratification weights and high cooperation rates lend confidence to the reliability of the data, the potential for selection bias remains. Although each ASR wave samples new households independently, minor overlap across waves cannot be entirely excluded. This potential duplication, if present, is minimal and unlikely to bias results given the independent sampling and weighting design. Finally, the timing of data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic introduces temporal confounding, as emergency policies may have altered access patterns. Furthermore, the rotating, overlapping cohort design means that "time since arrival" may be confounded with the "time of data collection" across the three merged waves, potentially biasing estimates. Temporary expansions of healthcare coverage and disruptions to service delivery may have shaped patterns of access and discrimination in ways not generalizable to other periods. These contextual conditions underscore the importance of situating findings within a specific policy and historical moment, and of validating them through longitudinal and cross-national research.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings from this study call for a shift in refugee health policy from models emphasizing individual adaptation toward frameworks that explicitly address structural inequality. Discrimination emerged not only as a barrier to care but as a mechanism through which broader social hierarchies are reproduced within healthcare systems. Addressing these disparities requires moving beyond cultural competence frameworks toward structural competency and institutional accountability mechanisms. This includes the implementation of equity audits, community oversight boards, and anti-discrimination enforcement within healthcare systems [54]. Policies should also recognize that discrimination is not an incidental barrier but a systemic condition that interacts with race, sex, and geography to create stratified access to care. State and local health departments should be resourced to conduct localized assessments that identify community-specific disparities, particularly in regions where healthcare access is shaped by restrictive policies, fragmented infrastructure, or politicized public attitudes toward immigration [7].

Equity-driven interventions should be intersectionally designed, with explicit attention to the compounded marginalization faced by racialized refugee women. Service delivery models should incorporate trauma-informed communication, culturally concordant care, and flexible outreach strategies that reflect diverse refugee experiences. In tandem, navigation support led by peers from affected communities may enhance trust and mitigate institutional mistrust by redistributing responsibility from individual burden-bearing to shared, community-based task sharing [42]. Program evaluation must include indicators sensitive to gendered and racialized disparities rather than aggregate metrics that obscure subgroup vulnerabilities [13, 17]. At the national level, refugee health surveillance systems should routinely collect and disaggregate data on both perceived and institutional discrimination, linked to healthcare utilization indicators. The portion of disparities unexplained by individual-level factors in this study further reinforces the need for policy approaches that treat discrimination as a measurable, policy-relevant determinant of health equity. Without deliberate structural responses, equity frameworks risk remaining symbolic. A rights-based approach [50] to refugee health must conceptualize healthcare access not as an individual accomplishment but as a collective responsibility embedded in institutional design and public accountability.

Conclusion

This study provides national evidence that perceived discrimination is potentially a key factor and mechanism underlying healthcare inequities among resettled refugees in the United States. Discrimination appears to function both as a marker and amplifier of structural disadvantage, particularly among women and racialized refugee groups. These associations persist beyond individual characteristics, underscoring the limits of integration-focused explanations and highlighting the embeddedness of inequity within institutional contexts. By combining decomposition, mediation, and intersectional analyses, the study reveals that much of the disparity in healthcare access is patterned by unmeasured structural and systemic processes. Addressing these inequities requires a shift toward structurally competent, intersectional, and rights-based approaches that promote institutional accountability and equitable conditions of care.
