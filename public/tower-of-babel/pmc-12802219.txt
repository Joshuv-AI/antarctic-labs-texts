Comparison of the accuracy of toric IOL power in cataract eyes with irregular astigmatism using two keratometry devices
Authors: Yinuo Huang, Min Hou, Yujie Ding, Liangping Liu, Ao Sun, Si Li, Mingxing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare the accuracy of toric intraocular lens (IOL) calculation using simulated keratometry (SimK) obtained from two different devices in cataract eyes with central or paracentral preoperative corneal irregular astigmatism.MethodsPatients with cataract and irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism who underwent uneventful cataract phacoemulsification with toric IOL implantation were retrospectively enrolled. Irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism was confirmed by corneal topography. SimK was obtained using swept-source optical biometry (IOLMaster700, SimKM) and Scheimpflug photography (Pentacam HR, SimKP). Eyes were divided into central (within 3-mm zone) and paracentral (3 to 7-mm zone) irregular astigmatism groups, with subgroup analyses performed accordingly.ResultsA total of 111 eyes (92 patients) were included, including 52 eyes with central and 59 eyes with paracentral irregular astigmatism. Postoperative astigmatism significantly decreased in both groups (both P < .01). Patients in the central irregular astigmatism group had significantly greater postoperative refractive astigmatism than those in the paracentral group. Comparison of SimK-based predictions between the two devices revealed that KM had lower postoperative refractive astigmatism prediction error in the central irregular astigmatism group.ConclusionsToric IOL implantation effectively reduces corneal irregular astigmatism. SimK obtained from optical biometry (IOLMaster 700) demonstrated superior accuracy in predicting postoperative refractive astigmatism compared to Scheimpflug topography (Pentacam HR) in eyes with central irregular astigmatism; this advantage was not observed in paracentral irregularity and should not be generalized to all irregular corneas.Keywords: Toric IOL calculation, Corneal irregular astigmatism, Simulated keratometry, Scheimpflug photography

Background

Residual refractive astigmatism is one of the main causes of poor uncorrected visual acuity after cataract surgery [1]. Preoperative corneal astigmatism exceeding 1.0 diopter (D) has been reported in approximately 23%~47% of cataract eyes [2, 3]. Typically, preoperative corneal astigmatism is regular, and toric intraocular lens (IOL) have been widely used in modern cataract surgery due to their efficacy in correcting regular astigmatism.

However, preoperative corneal astigmatism can be irregular, often stemming from various conditions prevalent in clinical practice. Common causes include keratoconus, corneal ectasia, corneal scarring or aging [4, 5]. While correction outcomes for irregular astigmatism often remain suboptimal, a growing body of evidence suggests that toric IOL implantation can offer partial benefit for certain types of irregular astigmatism. Specifically, studies have reported successful outcomes with toric IOLs in cases of mild-to-moderate keratoconus, corneal ectasia, and post-keratoplasty astigmatism [6–9]. In these instances, toric IOLs are believed to primarily correct the regular component embedded within the overall irregular astigmatism. Nevertheless, the broad application of toric IOLs in eyes with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism remains limited and presents significant challenges.

With the advent of computerized corneal topography systems, corneal astigmatism can now be defined with greater precision. Despite this advancement, accurately assessing astigmatism in irregular corneas is still challenging. Several approaches have been proposed to optimize toric IOL outcomes in eyes with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism. Gao et al. utilized an average preoperative corneal astigmatism measurement derived from three devices, including a Placido-based corneal topographer, an auto keratometer and the IOLMaster700 [10]. Achiron et al. compared toric IOL calculation outcomes using the Scheimpflug-based equivalent keratometry readings (EKR) from the central 2-mm zone, demonstrating refractive prediction performance comparable to that of IOLMaster 500 [11]. However, there remains no consensus regarding the most accurate method for assessing astigmatism and predicting refractive outcomes in eyes with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism.

Currently, swept-source optical biometry (e.g., IOLMaster 700) and Scheimpflug-based corneal topography (e.g., Pentacam HR) are two advanced technologies commonly used in clinical practice for evaluating corneal curvature. The IOLMaster700 has been reported to yield approximately 0.50 D lower monofocal IOL power recommendations in eyes with corneal cylinder ≥ 1.0 D compared to devices such as Eyestar and Anterion [12]. But few studies have systematically investigated the refractive outcomes and accuracy of toric IOL power calculations specifically in cataract patients with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the accuracy of toric IOL power calculation using SimK, obtained by IOLMaster700 and Scheimpflug-based Pentacam, in cataract eyes with central versus paracentral corneal irregular astigmatism.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (No. 2019KYPJ124).

Patients who underwent uneventful cataract surgery with toric IOL implantation at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (Guangzhou, China) from June 2018 to June 2022 were retrospectively enrolled following the recommendations outlined by Abulafia et al. [13]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: visually significant cataract, corneal astigmatism greater than 1.0 D, and mild-to-moderate irregular corneal astigmatism. Irregular astigmatism was defined as meridional inconsistency in refractive power that could not be corrected with a single cylindrical lens, accompanied by asymmetric topographic patterns (according to Bogan’s criteria), and classified as mild-to-moderate when total corneal irregularity was less than 0.5 μm on corneal topography [14, 15]. The exclusion criteria included: progressive corneal disease such as keratoconus, zonular weakness, retinal disease impacting visual acuity or axial length (AL) measurement (e.g., epiretinal membrane, diabetic maculopathy, macular hole), history of ocular trauma, history of corneal or intraocular surgery, postoperative corrected distance visual acuity worse than 6/9, intraoperative complications, case requiring postoperative IOL religment, invalid biometric or incomplete follow-up data [16].

Biometry and astigmatism evaluation

Preoperative ocular biometry was performed using IOLMaster700 (software v.1.80, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) and SimK values were obtained with both Pentacam HR (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany) and IOLMaster 700. The axis deviation between paired hemimeridians was quantified by measuring their angular positions on the Pentacam HR topographic map using a digital on-screen protractor (Windows Snip & Sketch, version 10.1907, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). The magnitude difference was calculated as the absolute difference between the dioptric power values provided directly by the Pentacam HR software for the corresponding hemimeridians. Irregular astigmatism was defined by at least one of the following three conditions evaluated by Scheimpflug topography: (1) non-bowtie corneal shape; (2) significant asymmetry between the two steep hemimeridians (axis deviation >20° or magnitude difference >1.0 D); (3) significant asymmetry between the two flat hemimeridians (axis deviation >20° or magnitude difference >1.0 D) [14, 17, 18]. The enrolled eyes were classified into central and paracentral irregular astigmatism groups according to corneal topographic map (Fig. 1). The central zone encompassed the 3-mm diameter region, while the paracentral zone covered the annular region from 3 to 7 mm diameter. Eyes exhibiting regular astigmatism in the central zone and irregularity only in the paracentral zone were categorized into the paracentral group. If irregular astigmatism appeared in both zones, eyes were classified into the central group due to the greater clinical impact of central irregularities.

Astigmatism classification zone and hemimeridians based on corneal topography. The white area (diameter ≤ 3-mm) represents the central zone; The grey area (3 mm < diameter ≤ 7 mm) represents the paracentral zone. The red lines indicate the steep hemimeridians (S1&S2, S3&S4, S5&S6) and the blue lines represent the flat hemimeridians (F1&F2, F3&F4, F5&F6). Deviation and differences are calculated between the two paired hemimeridians

Manifest refraction was performed 1 to 3 months postoperatively, a window during which postoperative refraction is considered clinically stable, to derive the actual postoperative refraction. Toric IOL alignment was evaluated using slit-lamp microscope and the Nidek OPD-Scan III (Nidek Technologies, Gamagori, Japan). Predicted refraction was calculated using the Barrett toric calculator (https://ascrs.org/tools/barrett-toric-calculator/). Cylinder power was calculated by SimK from IOLMaster700(SimKM) and Pentacam(SimKP) respectively. The prediction error (PE) of postoperative refractive astigmatism is the vector difference between actual postoperative refractive astigmatism and predicted residual astigmatism adjusted by the postoperative toric IOLs axis. The magnitude and vector components of PE, the centroid of PE, the standard deviation (SD) of PE vectors, the SD of the centroid, and the percentage of eyes with PE magnitudes within ± 0.50 D, ± 0.75 D, and ± 1.00 D were calculated using the ASTIGMATISM DOUBLE ANGLE PLOT TOOL (https://ascrs.org/tools/astigmatism-double-angle-plot-tool). In these figures, each point represents a per-eye PE vector (magnitude and axis), the black square indicates the vector centroid, and the ellipse depicts the 95% confidence region; Cartesian coordinates for analysis with both axes in diopters (D). Statistical inference for centroid differences was obtained with paired Hotelling’s T² in double-angle coordinates (see Statistical Analysis) [13, 19].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of Abulafia A [13], Holladay [19] and Næser’s [20]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistical software (version 25.0, IBM Corp).

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Distribution was assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in gender and eye (left/right) between groups were analyzed using the Chi-squared test. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare preoperative corneal astigmatism, age and AL between groups. Paired-samples Wilcoxon test was used to compare measured preoperative corneal astigmatism and PE between the two devices in both groups. Bivariate Hotelling’s T-squared test was used for centroid data.

Cochran’s Q test was used to analyze the percentage of eyes with postoperative refractive astigmatism PE within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.5 D, ± 0.75 D and ± 1.00 D. Because the axis of astigmatism was not simple linear quantitative data, we calculated the percentage of eyes with a deviation greater than 5° to represent a clinically significant difference [20]. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For all statistical analysis, astigmatism data from the left eye were mirrored about 90 degrees to maintain correct nasal/temporal orientation. The Kendall’s Tau test was used to assess the correlation between our grouping and postoperative refractive astigmatism, as well as our grouping and Pentacam’s postoperative refractive astigmatism prediction error, because it is a rank-based, tie-aware non-parametric coefficient that is appropriate for ordinal groupings and non-normally distributed outcomes [21].

Results

A total of 111 eyes (92 patients) with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism were included in this study. Among these, 52 eyes had central irregular astigmatism and 59 eyes had paracentral irregular astigmatism. Demographic and baseline characteristics for each group are presented in Table 1. The AL of the central irregular astigmatism group was shorter than the paracentral irregular astigmatism group (P < .05). There were no significant differences in preoperative corneal astigmatism between the two subgroups (P = .3709 of KM, P = .6268 of KP). Both groups demonstrated a significant reduction in astigmatism following toric IOL implantation (P < .001). However, the postoperative refractive astigmatism of the central group was significantly higher than that of the paracentral group (P = .026) (Table 2).

In the central irregular group, the Bland-Altman plots demonstrated good agreement between the preoperative anterior corneal astigmatism obtained from the two devices (95% limits of agreement, -1.144 ~ 1.147, Bivariate Hotelling’s T-squared test, P = .834), as illustrated in Fig. 2. However, axis deviation greater than 5° between devices was observed in 32.7% of cases (17 of 52 eyes). The mean postoperative refractive astigmatism prediction error (PE) calculated using SimKP was 0.78 ± 0.67 D (0.20 D @ 129° ± 1.01), which was higher than that calculated using SimKM at 0.53 ± 0.33 D (0.05 D @ 174° ± 0.63). The bivariate Hotelling’s T-squared test revealed a statistically significant difference between these centroid values (P = .0422) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our study compared the accuracy of toric IOL power calculation using SimK obtained from optical biometry (IOLMaster 700) and Scheimpflug-based topography (Pentacam HR), focusing on eyes with either central or paracentral preoperative corneal irregular astigmatism. The results demonstrated that SimK obtained from IOLMaster 700 provided superior accuracy in predicting postoperative refractive astigmatism, particularly for eyes with central irregular astigmatism located within the 3-mm measurement zone.

Toric IOL implantation is generally effective for eyes with irregular astigmatism. However, the evaluation of preoperative keratometry remains challenging and limit the application of toric IOL in such cases. Gao et al. reported that astigmatism had reduced from 1.15 to 6.97 D (1.99 ± 1.26 D) to 0-2.75 D (0.65 ± 0.57 D) after toric IOL implantation in 23 eyes with irregular astigmatism [10]. Similar reductions have been described in cases of keratoconus or following posterior segment surgery, supporting the concept that toric IOLs may primarily correct the regular component of irregular astigmatism [22]. In our study, astigmatism was significantly reduced in both the central and paracentral following toric IOL implantation. Collectively, these findings reinforce that toric IOLs can benefit cataract patients with irregular preoperative corneal astigmatism.

Although central SimK is typically used for IOL power calculation, its representativeness may be compromised in cases of irregular astigmatism, where corneal refractive power is distributed asymmetrically. To address this, our study compared the accuracy of toric IOL power calculation for eyes with irregular astigmatism distributed in different corneal zones. Bogan has described the shape of the astigmatism topographic map and proposed the concept of “bowtie” with a mathematical description [14]. On this basis, Karabatsas et al. [17] and Alpins et al. [18] proposed that a difference of more than 1.0 D or a skew larger than 20° between the two hemimeridians within the range of 3-mm diameter indicated significant irregularity and asymmetry. In this study, we proposed a novel grouping method for the studied eyes by integrating both irregularity and asymmetry [21]. Previous research typically focused on only one of these aspects. For instance, when analyzing 4-mm zone high-order aberrations (HOAs), only irregularity was considered [23, 24]. Similarly, studies using the inferior-superior (I-S), superior-inferior (S-I), or skewed radial axis (SRAX) indices exclusively addressed asymmetry [25]. While Meng et al. did consider both irregularity and asymmetry, their analysis of HOAs was limited to the 4-mm range, and they did not include I-S, S-I, or SRAX values from the 5-mm ring [15]. Our grouping method, therefore, offers a significant analytical advantage by integrating both indicators within the same corneal area.

Importantly, Kendall’s Tau-b correlation analysis confirmed a significant association between the grouping and both postoperative refractive astigmatism and prediction error calculated with SimKP. The grouping method was proposed to address this issue, as our indicators can be analyzed within the same region. It is noteworthy that Kendall’s Tau-b Correlation Analysis showed the grouping had a good correlation with the postoperative refractive astigmatism and postoperative refractive astigmatism PE calculated with SimKP. There was no significant difference between the central and paracentral irregular astigmatism groups in terms of gender, laterality, lens opacity, toric IOL type, or preoperative corneal astigmatism. Patients in the central irregular group were older and demonstrated a greater tendency toward against-the-rule astigmatism, which was consistent with prior research [26, 27]. The residual postoperative astigmatism was also higher in the central irregular astigmatism group. This is consistent with the study of Hwang et al., which used hemimeridians deviation degree as an indicator. They proposed that postoperative astigmatism increased significantly when the steep-steep hemimeridians deviation was greater than 10° and believed that more postoperative astigmatism might occur when the deviation reached 20° [28].

In the central irregular astigmatism group, SimKM provided more accurate and precise toric IOL calculations than SimKP, as shown by a lower mean absolute error (0.53 D vs. 0.78 D; P < .05) and reduced variability, represented by a smaller 95% confidence ellipse in the double-angle plot of the postoperative astigmatism PE in our study (Fig. 2). Lender et al. found that toric IOL power recommendations increased by 0.75 D when using SimKP instead of SimKM in patients with preoperative corneal astigmatism >1.00 D [12]. Li et al. also noted that the MAE for the IOLMaster and Pentacam was 0.53 ± 0.40 D and 0.56 ± 0.47 D, respectively, in eyes with asymmetric bowtie astigmatism [25].

Several device-related factors plausibly explain the lower astigmatism prediction error we observed with IOLMaster 700 in eyes with central irregularity. First, the two instruments sample different corneal zones: Pentacam analyzes the entire 3-mm area, potentially incorporating more irregularities, while IOLMaster 700 keratometry samples six points on a 2.5-mm ring—thereby focusing on the more regular portion of the cornea. Second, acquisition dynamics differ: Pentacam tomographic scans require roughly 2 s, while IOLMaster keratometry is acquired almost instantaneously. On irregular or tear-film-unstable surfaces, longer acquisition increases susceptibility to microfluctuations in the ocular surface, which can amplify noise in cylinder magnitude and axis. Consistent with these mechanisms, in our central-irregular group we found more frequent inter-device axis discrepancies (> 5° in 32.7% of eyes), and the SimKP-based predictions showed a wider PE distribution than SimKM (0.78 ± 0.67 D vs. 0.53 ± 0.33 D). Given that small keratometry differences can disproportionately affect IOL power and postoperative refractive astigmatism, even modest increases in measurement variability may translate into clinically meaningful PE with Pentacam-based inputs in this phenotype. These device- and phenotype-specific differences likely underlie the superiority of IOLMaster 700 for toric planning in centrally irregular astigmatism.

Bland-Altman analysis indicated overall good inter-device agreement in the magnitude of preoperative corneal astigmatism in both the central and paracentral irregular astigmatism groups (bivariate Hotelling’s T-squared test, both P >.05). Nevertheless, only the central irregular group showed a significant inter-device difference in postoperative refractive astigmatism PE (bivariate Hotelling’s T-squared test, P < .05). Shajari et al. and Tana-Rivero et al. have reported no statistical difference in corneal measurements between IOLMaster700 and Pentacam, yet emphasized their wide limits of agreement, and noting that a keratometry difference of 1.00 D can alter IOL power by about 1.40 D [29, 30]. And Achiron et al. suggested that this effect might be more pronounced in eyes with irregular astigmatism [11]. Consistent with these observations, central irregular astigmatism group in our cohort exhibited a higher proportion of preoperative inter-device SimK axis discrepancies >5°, as well as broader 95% limits of agreement for preoperative astigmatism(-1.144 ~ 1.147 D) than those in paracentral group(-0.740 ~ 0.820 D).Such greater dispersion and axis discrepancies provide a plausible explanation for why small inter-device discrepancies in the central group were magnified into clinically meaningful differences in postoperative PE, whereas this effect was not observed in the paracentral group.

There are some limitations of this study. First, although our sample size is relatively large, additional data and extreme cases would further strengthen the analysis. Secondly, we did not standardize pre-capture tear-film optimization, which could differentially affect longer acquisitions and may partially contribute to the observed device differences. Finally, the effect of posterior corneal astigmatism was not directly addressed. Although we calculated total corneal keratometry with both devices, the accuracy of predictions was lower when posterior keratometry was considered (data not shown). In irregular corneas, posterior-surface measurements exhibit poorer repeatability and greater axis jitter, which can disproportionately inflate vector-form PE when combined with anterior data; additionally, total keratometry (TK) from the IOLMaster 700 is harder to obtain reliably in such corneas, leading to missing data. Taken together, these factors likely underlie the reduced predictive performance when posterior keratometry is included.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that toric IOL implantation is effective in correcting irregular corneal astigmatism in cataract patients, particularly when irregular astigmatism is distributed in the 3-mm measurement zone. SimK derived from IOLMaster 700 was more accurate in predicting postoperative refractive astigmatism than that from Pentacam for eyes with central irregular astigmatism.
