Abstract
. We classify all Mal’cev clones over a three-element set up to minion homomorphisms. This is another step toward the complete classification of three-element relational structures up to pp-constructability. We furthermore provide an alternative proof of Bulatov’s result that all Mal’cev clones over a three-element set have an at most 4-ary relational basis.

1.
Introduction

This article is dedicated to the study of clones over finite sets, a central topic in universal algebra that traces its origins back to Post’s classification of clones on a two-element set
[
36
]
. It is well known that clones on a finite set with
n
elements form a lattice when ordered by inclusion. In the following, we will denote this lattice by
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
.

By Post’s result, we know that
\mathfrak{L}_{2}
is countable; however, Janov and Muchnik proved that on every finite set with at least three elements there are continuum many clones
[
42
]
. This finding suggests that a classification of
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
à la Post becomes significantly more challenging for
n\geq 3
. In fact, up to date, no complete description of
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
is known. Nevertheless, some remarkable progress has been made on the description of several significant parts of
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
, such as the classification of all 18 maximal
[
29
]
and 84 minimal clones
[
22
]
. Notably, these results were obtained prior to Rosenberg’s groundbreaking classification results on maximal respectively minimal clones on general finite sets
[
37
]
and
[
38
]
. One of the challenges in achieving a full description of
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
lies in the fact that, with the exception of the clone of linear operations, every maximal clone generates an uncountable ideal
[
23
,
31
]
. In 2015, Zhuk provided a classification of all clones of self-dual operations on a three-element set
[
43
]
, which, to date, remains the only complete description of such an uncountable maximal ideal.

In this paper, we are interested in the subset of
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
consisting of all
Mal’cev clones
. A clone is said to be a Mal’cev clone if it contains a
Mal’cev operation
, i.e., a ternary operation
m
satisfying the identity
y\approx m(y,x,x)\approx m(x,x,y)
. In
[
19
]
Bulatov provided a complete classification of all 1129 Mal’cev clones in
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
and described each of them by an at most 4-ary relational basis. In
[
30
]
Kearnes and Szendrei observed that, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there are also only finitely many clones in
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
with a
k
-edge term (although, without explicitly classifying them). Our first contribution is a simplified proof of Bulatov’s result that uses the methods introduced in
[
30
]
.

For many purposes, it is however convenient to study clones up to a coarser order than inclusion. In particular, many properties of a clone are determined by the identities it satisfies. This is captured by the (pre-)ordering of clones by the existence of a
clone homomorphism
, that is, a map between clones that preserve identities. This order is of particular interest in classic universal algebra, as, for two algebraic structures
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
(without constant symbols), there is a clone homomorphism from the term clone
\Clo(\mathbf{A})
to
\Clo(\mathbf{B})
if and only if the variety generated by
\mathbf{A}
has an interpretation in the variety generated by
\mathbf{B}
. Thus, studying clones up to clone homomorphism essentially corresponds to classifications of varieties in the interpretability lattice introduced by Garcia and Taylor
[
26
]
.

Motivated by the study of fixed-template constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs), an even coarser order was introduced by Barto, Opršal, and Pinsker via the notion of
pp-constructability

[
7
]
. Pp-constructions form a unifying framework that describes several different types of reductions between CSPs. More precisely, if a finite structure
\mathbb{A}
pp-constructs a finite structure
\mathbb{B}
, then there exists a log-space reduction from
\CSP(\mathbb{B})
to
\CSP(\mathbb{A})
.

The algebraic counterpart of pp-constructability is a weakened version of clone homomorphism, referred to in the literature as
minion homomorphism
, or
minor-preserving map
. For any pair of finite relational structures
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{A}
pp-constructs
\mathbb{B}
if and only if there exists a minion homomorphism from

\Pol(\mathbb{A})
to
\Pol(\mathbb{B})

[
7
]
. Here
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
denotes the
polymorphism clone
of
\mathbb{A}
, that is, the clone consisting of all operations that preserve the relations of
\mathbb{A}
. Thus, the polymorphism clone, up to minion homomorphism, completely determines the complexity of
\CSP(\mathbb{A})
. This is a fundamental fact used in the “universal-algebraic approach” to CSPs, which leads to numerous results, among which the most famous is probably the P/NP-c dichotomy for all finite CSPs, independently proved by Bulatov
[
20
]
and Zhuk
[
44
,
45
]
. More recently, pp-constructions were also observed to describe reductions between
promise constraint satisfaction problems,
\PCSP(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
; also in this settings, pp-constructions between finite PCSP-templates correspond to minion homomorphisms between their
polymorphism minons

\Pol(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
, hence the name
[
5
]
.

The main difference between clone and minion homomorphism lies in the fact that clone homomorphisms preserve
all
identities, while minion homomorphisms only preserve so-called
minor identities
or
height 1 identities
, i.e., identities that can be stated without using composition and projections. We will define this notion precisely in Definition
2.2
.

If we preorder the class of all clones over some finite set by the existence of minion homomorphisms and factor by the induced equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}
, we obtain the poset
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
. In particular, we say that two clones
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are
minor-equivalent
if
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
. If we restrict ourselves to clones over
n
-element sets, we obtain
\mathfrak{P}_{n}=\mathfrak{L}_{n}/_{\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}}
. A systematic study of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
and
\mathfrak{P}_{n}
was initiated in the Ph.D. theses of Starke
[
39
]
and the fourth author
[
40
]
. Although we do not know whether
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is a lattice, we know that it is a
\wedge
-semilattice without atoms and a unique coatom
\overline{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
. Moreover
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
contains infinite descending chains and infinite antichains (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). A full description of all submaximal elements of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
was recently provided in
[
33
]
, generalizing two previous results that were limited to
\mathfrak{P}_{3}

[
41
]
and polymorphism clones of finite digraphs
[
12
]
. A complete description of
\mathfrak{P}_{2}
was provided in
[
14
]
. This was also used as a basis for the classification of multisorted Boolean clones determined by binary relations
[
6
]
and products of Boolean clones
[
34
]
up to minion homomorphism.

The complete structure of the poset
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
remains yet unknown; only partial results have been proven so far. By
[
41
]
, there are exactly three submaximal elements, which are the equivalence classes of the clones
\mathcal{B}_{2}=\Pol(\mathbb{B}_{2})
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\Pol(\mathbb{C}_{2})
, and
\mathcal{C}_{3}=\Pol(\mathbb{C}_{3})
. Here,
\mathbb{C}_{n}
denotes the cyclic digraph of
n
-vertices with constants, and
\mathbb{B}_{2}=(\{0,1\};\{0,1\}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\},\{0\},\{1\})
.

Furthermore, the ideal of clones of self-dual operations in
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
, i.e., the ideal generated by
\mathcal{C}_{3}
in
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
, was completely described up to minion homomorphisms in
[
15
]
.

The main result of our paper, Theorem
8.2
, is a classification of all Mal’cev clones on a three-element set up to minor-equivalence. In fact, our classification applies more generally to all clones with a
quasi Mal’cev operations

m(x,x,y)\approx m(y,x,x)\approx m(y,y,y)
. We show that there are exactly 10 equivalence classes, their order is depicted in Figure
4
.

Our result can be seen as a significant step towards a full description of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
. Indeed, as was observed in
[
41
, Theorem 2.13]
, every element in
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
that does not have a quasi-Malcev operation lies below
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. In particular, this is also true in
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
. Therefore, by our results, it would suffice to describe the downset of
\mathcal{B}_{2}
in order to complete the classification of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some necessary notation, key definitions, and foundational results from the literature. For the basic notions on clones we refer to
[
35
,
10
]
, for the a more comprehensive
presentation of the notions and results in universal algebra that we use, we refer to
[

]
and
[

.

Basic notation

Tuples are represented in boldface, with their individual components denoted as in, e.g.,
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. A tuple is
injective
if all its components are distinct, i.e., if
|\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}|=n
. We write
[n]
for the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. We are going to denote algebraic structures by capital boldface characters (e.g.,
\mathbf{A}=(A;f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
) and relational structures by capital ‘blackboard’ characters (e.g.,
\mathbb{A}=(A;R_{1},R_{2})
).

We note that blackboard characters used for relational structures may overlap with standard notation for the field of integers modulo a prime
p
, denoted by
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. To avoid ambiguity, we will explicitly clarify when the latter interpretation is intended.

2.1.
Operation clones

For a set
A
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define
{\mathcal{O}}^{(n)}_{A}=\{f\mid f\colon A^{n}\to A\}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{A}=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathcal{O}}^{(n)}_{A}
. For each
n\geq 1
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the
n
-ary
projection
to the
i
-th coordinate is the
operation
\pr^{n}_{i}
defined for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
by
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\mapsto a_{i}
.
For an
n
-ary operation
f
on
A
and

m
-ary operations
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
on
A
, we call
the composition of

f
with
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
the operation
f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
defined for every
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in A
by

(f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}))(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=f(g_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}),\dots,g_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})).

A
clone over
A
is a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{A}
which is closed under composition of operations and contains all projections. If
F\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{A}
, then
\langle F\rangle
or
\Clo(F)
denotes the
clone generated by
F
, i.e., the smallest clone that contains
F
. For an algebraic structure
\mathbf{A}
, we also write
\Clo(\mathbf{A})
for the clone generated by the basic operations of
\mathbf{A}
. This is commonly referred to as the
term clone
of
\mathbf{A}
.

A clone
\mathcal{C}
is called
idempotent
if all its operations are idempotent, i.e.,
f(x,x,\ldots,x)\approx x
holds for all
f\in\mathcal{C}
.
We say that a clone is a
Mal’cev clone
if it contains a Mal’cev operation.

2.2.
Algebraic structures

Every clone
\mathcal{C}
on a set
A
can be written as the term clone of an algebraic structure
\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{C}}
, whose basic operations are some enumeration of the elements in
\mathcal{C}
. As some parts of this paper are more readily explained for algebraic structures than for clones, we will switch between these two languages repeatedly.

We assume that the reader is familiar with standard notions in universal algebra, in particular
subalgebras

\mathbf{S}\leq\mathbf{A}
,
congruences

\theta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
,
quotient algebras

\mathbf{A}/\theta
, and
products

\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
. As usual, we denote by
\HSP(\mathbf{A})
the
variety
generated by an algebra
\mathbf{A}
, i.e., the closure of
\mathbf{A}
under products, quotients, and subalgebras and assume that the reader is familiar with Birkhoff’s HSP Theorem
[
9
]
. For more background on universal algebra we refer to
[
21
]
.

2.3.
Relational structures and cores

A
\tau
-structure is a relational structure over some signature
\tau
. Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be two relational
\tau
-structures. A map
h\colon A\to B
is a
homomorphism
if for every
R\in\tau
and each
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in A^{n}
we have

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in R^{\mathbb{A}}\Rightarrow(h(a_{1}),\dots,h(a_{n}))\in R^{\mathbb{B}}.

We also use the notation
h\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
if
h
is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
.
We say that
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are
homomorphically equivalent
if there is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
and one from
\mathbb{B}
to
\mathbb{A}
.
An
endomorphism of

\mathbb{A}
is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}
to itself. An
isomorphism
between
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
is a bijective homomorphism
h
such that its inverse is also a homomorphism. An
automorphism of

\mathbb{A}
is an isomorphism from
\mathbb{A}
onto itself. A finite structure
\mathbb{A}
is a
core
if every endomorphism of
\mathbb{A}
is also an automorphism. We say that
\mathbb{C}
is a
core of

\mathbb{A}
if
\mathbb{C}
is a core and is homomorphically equivalent to
\mathbb{A}
. It is well established that every finite relational structure has a core that is unique up to isomorphism, thus it makes sense to refer to
the
core of a relational structure.

2.4.
The Inv-Pol Galois connection and pp-formulas

We say that an operation
f\colon A^{n}\to A

preserves a relation

R\subseteq A^{k}
if, for any
\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{n}\in R
, it follows that
f(\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{n})\in R
. Here,
f(\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{n})\in A^{k}
is computed by coordinate-wise evaluation. Equivalently, we also say that
R
is
invariant under

f
. If
f
preserves every relation from a set
\Gamma
, then
f
is called a
polymorphism of

\Gamma
. We denote the set of all polymorphisms of
\Gamma
by
\Pol(\Gamma)
. As
\Pol(\Gamma)
forms a clone (cf. eg.
[
35
, Chapter E.]
), it is commonly referred to as the
polymorphism clone of

\Gamma
. When working with relational structures, we also use the notation
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
for
\Pol(\Gamma)
, where
\mathbb{A}=(A;\Gamma)
. Note that
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
is the set consisting of all of the homomorphisms
f\colon\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}
, for each
n\geq 1
.

Analogously, for any set
F
of operations over
A
, we denote by
\Inv(F)
the set of all relations that are invariant under every
f\in F
; and for an algebraic structure
\mathbf{A}=(A;F)
we write
\Inv(\mathbf{A})=\Inv(F)
. Note that
\Inv(F)
consists of all subuniverses
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
, for each
n\geq 1
. We say that
\Gamma
is a
relational basis
of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
, if
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\Pol(\Gamma)
.

If
A
is a
finite
set, then
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\Pol(\Inv(\mathbf{A}))
holds, hence every clone on
A
is the polymorphism clone of some relational structure
[
16
,
27
]
. There also exists a characterization of
\Inv(\mathbb{A})
in terms of pp-definitions.
A
primitive positive (pp-) formula over
\tau
is a first-order formula which only uses relation symbols in
\tau
, equality, conjunction, and existential quantification. If
\mathbb{A}
is a
\tau
-structure over a set
A
and
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a
\tau
-formula with free-variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, then

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in A^{n}\mid\mathbb{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\}

is called
the relation defined by
\phi
in
\mathbb{A}
.
Any relation defined by some primitive positive formula over
\mathbb{A}
is said to be
pp-definable
in
\mathbb{A}
. If
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are relational structures on the same domain, we say that
\mathbb{A}

pp-defines

\mathbb{B}
if every relation in
\mathbb{B}
is pp-definable in
\mathbb{A}
. It easy to check that
\Inv(\Pol(\mathbb{A}))
contains the set of all relations that are pp-definable from
\mathbb{A}
. The following result shows that if
\mathbb{A}
has a finite domain, then

\Inv(\Pol(\mathbb{A}))
is equal to the set of pp-definable relations:

Theorem 2.1
.

[
16
,
27
]
]
Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be structures on the same finite set
A
. Then
\mathbb{A}
pp-defines
\mathbb{B}
if and only if
\Pol(\mathbb{A})\subseteq\Pol(\mathbb{B})
.

2.5.
Minors and minion homomorphisms

In this subsection we introduce the notions of minor and minion homomorphism.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\colon A^{n}\to A
be an
n
-ary operation and let
\sigma\colon[n]\to[r]
. We denote by
f_{\sigma}
the following
r
-ary operation

f_{\sigma}(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=f(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}).

Any operation of the form
f_{\sigma}
, for some
\sigma\colon[n]\to[r]
, is called a
minor
of
f
.

In other words, the minors of an operation
f
are exactly those functions that can be obtained by permuting/identifying variables and adding dummy variables.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be clones over some finite (possibly distinct) sets, and let
\xi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
be a mapping that preserves arities. We say that
\xi
is a
minion homomorphism
if

\xi(f_{\sigma})=\xi(f)_{\sigma}

for any
n
-ary operation
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
\sigma\colon[n]\to[r]
.

We write
\mathcal{C}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
if there exists a minion homomorphism
from
\mathcal{C}

to
\mathcal{D}
.
If
\mathcal{C}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
and

\mathcal{D}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{C}
,
we say that
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are

minor-equivalent
and write

\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
. For any clone
\mathcal{C}
, we also write
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
to denote the
\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}
-class of
\mathcal{C}
.

Clearly
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
implies
\mathcal{C}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
. Let us remark that minion homomorphisms can also be defined for
minions
as defined in
[
5
]
, and that the image of a clone under a minion homomorphism does not need to be a clone.

A
minor identity
or
height 1 identity
is a formal expression of the form

f(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})\approx g(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(m)}),

for some function symbols
f
,
g
, and some
\sigma\colon[n]\to[r]
and
\pi\colon[m]\to[r]
. A
minor condition

\Sigma
is a finite set of minor identities.
We say that a set of operations
F
satisfies a minor condition
\Sigma
(and write
F\models\Sigma
), if we can assign each function symbol in
\Sigma
to an operation in
F
, in such a way that all the (universally quantified) identities in
\Sigma
are satisfied. For instance, the minor condition
\Sigma=\{f(x,y)\approx f(y,x)\}
is satisfied by
F
, if
F
contains a binary commutative operation.
Every minion homomorphism preserves minor conditions. By a compactness argument outlined in
[
7
]
, there is a minion homomorphism from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{D}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}\models\Sigma\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}\models\Sigma
holds for every minor condition
\Sigma
.

We refer to the partially ordered set of classes of clones over a finite set as
minion homomorphism poset
and denote it by
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
. We also consider the subposet of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
that consists of classes containing a representative on an
n
element set, which we denote by
\mathfrak{P}_{n}
. More precisely:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}

\displaystyle=(\{\overline{\mathcal{C}}\mid\mathcal{C}\text{ is a clone over some finite set}\};\leq_{\mathrm{m}}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\{\overline{\mathcal{C}}\mid\mathcal{C}\text{ is a clone over }\{0,\dots,n-1\}\};\leq_{\mathrm{m}}),

where
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\overline{\mathcal{D}}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
.

Note that the top element of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is the equivalence class of any clone that contains a constant operation; the bottom element of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is the equivalence class containing the clone of projections on any set with at least two elements. These two classes are also the top and bottom elements of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
.

2.6.
Pp-constructions and important minor conditions

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be finite relational structures. We say that
\mathbb{B}
is a
pp-power
of
\mathbb{A}
if
\mathbb{B}
is isomorphic to a structure
\mathbb{P}=(P;\Gamma)
such that

(1)

P=A^{n}
, for some
n\geq 1
,

(2)

\Gamma
is a list of relations on
P
which are pp-definable from
\mathbb{A}
.

To be more precise, in (2), we require that any
k
-ary relation
R\subseteq(A^{n})^{k}
in
\Gamma
is pp-definable from
\mathbb{A}
, when regarded as a
kn
-ary relation on
A
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be finite relational structures. We say that
\mathbb{A}

pp-constructs

\mathbb{B}
if
\mathbb{B}
is homomorphically equivalent to a pp-power of
\mathbb{A}
.

In analogy to Theorem
2.1
, it was shown in
[
7
]
that pp-constructability between finite relational structures translates into the notion of
minion homomorphism
between the corresponding polymorphism clones:

Theorem 2.5

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
be finite relational structures. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{A}
pp-constructs
\mathbb{B}
,

(2)

\Pol(\mathbb{A})\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{B})
,

(3)

If
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
satisfies a minor condition
\Sigma
, then
\Pol(\mathbb{B})
satisfies
\Sigma
.

Thus, the order
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is anti-isomorphic to finite relational structures ordered by pp-constructability (in fact, this is how
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is defined in
[
14
]
).

The next proposition shows that, in the context of classifying clones on finite sets up to minor-equivalence, we can restrict our focus to idempotent clones:

Proposition 2.6

(
[
7
]
)
.

For a finite relational structure
\mathbb{A}
, let
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
denote its core, extended by all constants. Then
\mathbb{A}
pp-constructs
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
and vice-versa. As a consequence,
\mathcal{C}=\Pol(\mathbb{A})
is minor-equivalent to the idempotent clone
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\Pol(\mathbb{A})^{\prime}
.

By Theorem
2.5
(3), whenever
\mathbb{B}
is not pp-constructable from
\mathbb{A}
, this is witnessed by a minor condition
\Sigma
. In the following definition, we introduce some minor conditions that are well known in the literature and that, together with those in Definition
6.6
, will be used to prove inequalities of the form
\mathcal{C}\nleq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{D}
in Section
8
(see Figure
4
).

Definition 2.7
.

We define the following minor conditions:

•

A
n
-ary operation
c
is called a
n
-cyclic operation
if it satisfies

c(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\approx c(x_{2},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}).

•

A
n
-ary operation
f
is a
symmetric operation
if it satisfies

f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\approx f(x_{\pi(1)},x_{\pi(2)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)}),

for every permutation
\pi
of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

•

A ternary operation
m
is a
quasi minority operation
if it satisfies

m(x,y,y)\approx m(y,x,y)\approx m(y,y,x)\approx m(x,x,x).

•

A ternary operation
m
is a
quasi majority operation
if it satisfies

m(x,y,y)\approx m(y,x,y)\approx m(y,y,x)\approx m(y,y,y).

As usual in the literature, a
minority (majority) operation
is an idempotent quasi minority (majority) operation.

3.
Mal’cev algebras and commutator theory

We recall some well-known facts about Mal’cev algebras and their congruences.

Definition 3.1
.

A binary relation
R\subseteq A\times B
has the
parallelogram property
if, for all
(a,c),(a,d),(b,c)\in R
, we have that
(b,d)\in R
. An
n
-ary relation
R\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
has the
parallelogram property
if, for each subset
\emptyset\neq I\subset[n]
,
R
has the parallelogram property considered as a binary relation between
\prod_{i\in I}A_{i}
and
\prod_{j\notin I}A_{j}
.

Whenever
R\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}
for some algebras
\mathbf{A}_{i}
from a variety with a Mal’cev term, then
R
has the parallelogram property. In fact, varieties with a Mal’cev term can be characterized by this property (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 3.7.]
). In particular, relations
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
for a Mal’cev algebra
\mathbf{A}
always have the parallelogram property.

Next, we introduce some notions from commutator theory:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra with Mal’cev term
d(x,y,z)
, and let
\alpha,\beta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
. We say:

•

\alpha
centralizes
\beta
, if
\mathbf{A}
preserves the 4-ary relation

T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha,\beta)=\{(x,y,z,d(x,y,z))\mid x\,\alpha\,y\,\beta\,z)\},

•

\alpha
is
Abelian
if
\alpha
centralizes itself, i.e., it preserves

T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha)=\{(x,y,z,d(x,y,z))\mid x\,\alpha\,y\,\alpha\,z)\},

•

\mathbf{A}
is
Abelian
, if the full congruence
1_{A}
is Abelian, i.e., it preserve the function graph of
d
:

T^{\mathbf{A}}=\{(x,y,z,d(x,y,z))\in A^{4}\mid x,y,z\in A\},

•

the
centralizer
(0_{A}:\alpha)
is the largest
\delta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
such that
\alpha
centralizes
\delta
.

These notions are usually defined in a different way in the literature, which is based on the so-called ‘term-condition’
[
25
, Chapter 4]
. However, in the special case of Mal’cev algebras, they all agree with Definition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra with Mal’cev term
d
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Con(\mathbf{A})
. Then:

(1)

All notions introduced in Definition
3.2
are well-defined and equivalent to the corresponding definitions in
[
25
]
.

(2)

If
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
, then
T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha,\beta)
does not depend on the choice of the Mal’cev term
d\in\Clo(\mathbf{A})
.

(3)

Assume that
\alpha\leq\beta
and
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
, and let
a\in A
. Then
([a]_{\alpha},d)=([a]_{\alpha},x-y+z)
, for some Abelian group
+
on
[a]_{\alpha}
, and all algebras
([b]_{\alpha},d)
with
b\,\beta\,a
are isomorphic to
([a]_{\alpha},d)
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
25
, Exercise 6.7]
that our definition of
\alpha
centralizes
\beta
is equivalent to the standard definition based on the term condition; for a proof, see, e.g.,
[
1
, Lemma 2.4.]
. As a consequence, also all other notions in Definition
3.2
are equivalent to the standard definitions.
For (2), note that
(x,y,y,x),(y,y,y,y),(y,y,z,z)\in T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha,\beta)
, for all
x\,\alpha\,y\,\beta\,z
. But then also their image under any Mal’cev term
d^{\prime}
must be in
T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha,\beta)
, i.e.,
(x,y,z,d^{\prime}(x,y,z))\in T^{\mathbf{A}}(\alpha,\beta)
. Thus,
d^{\prime}
agrees with
d
on such tuples.
Point (3) follows from
[
25
, Lemma 5.6 and Exercise 5.5]
.
∎

We next define a few useful notions for relations with the parallelogram property:

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{A}_{n}
be algebras and let
R\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}
be a relation with the parallelogram property. Then

x\ \theta_{i}\ y\Leftrightarrow\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}R(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},x,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})\land R(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})

is a congruence of
\pr_{i}(R)\leq\mathbf{A}_{i}
. Following
[
30
]
, we call
\theta_{i}
the
i
-th coordinate kernel
of
R
. We remark that this sometimes also referred to as
i
-th
link/linkedness congruence of
R

[
17
]
.

The map
\nu\colon(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{1}/{\theta_{1}},\ldots,x_{n}/{\theta_{n}})
is clearly homomorphism from
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pr_{i}(R)
to
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\prime}=\pr_{i}(R)/\theta_{i}
. We denote the image of
R
under
\nu
by
\overline{R}
and call it
reduced representation
of
R
.

Note that, by the parallelogram property,
R=\nu^{-1}(\overline{R})
and that all
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\prime}
are quotients of subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
. In the following, let us write
\HS(\mathbf{A})
for the set of all quotients of subalgebras of an algebra
\mathbf{A}
. We are further going to need the following definition:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra. An
n
-ary relation
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
is called
critical
if it is
\land
-irreducible
in the lattice of subuniverses of
\mathbf{A}^{n}
(i.e., it cannot be written as the intersection of strictly bigger relations
Q\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
) and it has
no dummy variables
, i.e., it depends on all of its inputs.

Clearly
\Gamma
is a relational basis of
\mathbf{A}
if and only if all critical relations can be pp-defined from
\Gamma
. Thus, it is enough to consider only critical relations in our analysis. Theorem 2.5 in
[
30
]
discusses the properties of critical relations with respect to algebras from congruence modular varieties. In the special case of Mal’cev algebras, the result directly implies the following (compare also with Theorem 2.3.10 in
[
17
]
):

Theorem 3.6

(Consequence of Theorem 2.5. in
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’cev algebra,
n\geq 3
and
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
be a critical relation. Further, let
\overline{R}\leq_{sd}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}
the reduced representation of
R
. Then
\overline{R}
is also critical (as a multi-sorted relation) and

(1)

\mathbf{A}_{i}
is subdirectly irreducible for every
i
,

(2)

the monolith
\mu_{i}
of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
is Abelian for every
i
,

(3)

for every pair of indices
i\neq j
, the quotient of
\pr_{i,j}\overline{R}
by the centralizers
(0_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}:\mu_{i})\times(0_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}:\mu_{j})
is the graph of an isomorphism

\mathbf{A}_{i}/(0_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}:\mu_{i})\cong\mathbf{A}_{i}/(0_{\mathbf{A}_{j}}:\mu_{j}).

Note that Theorem
3.6
only applies to critical relations of arity 3 or higher. However, classifying the invariant relations of arity 2 is also an easy task: Let
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
and
\overline{R}\leq\mathbf{A}_{1}\times\mathbf{A}_{2}
be its reduced representation. Then
\overline{R}
is an isomorphism between
\mathbf{A}_{1}
and
\mathbf{A}_{2}
. Thus, for Mal’cev algebras, there is a one-to-one correspondence of binary invariant relations and the isomorphism between elements from
\HS(\mathbf{A})
.

Mal’cev algebras that only have binary critical relations can be characterized as follows.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’cev algebra. The following are equivalent:

(1)

All critical relations
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
are at most binary.

(2)

\mathbf{A}
has a majority term.

(3)

there is no subdirectly irreducible
\mathbf{B}\in\HS(\mathbf{A})
with Abelian monolith.

Proof.

By the Baker-Pixley Theorem
[
3
]
, (1) is equivalent to (2) (even for non-Mal’cev algebras). For (2)
\Rightarrow
(3), note that the relation
T^{\mathbf{B}}(\mu)
, for the monolith
\mu\neq 0_{B}
of
\mathbf{B}
cannot be invariant under any majority operation
f
(otherwise evaluating
f
at the tuples
(x,y,y,x),(y,y,y,y),(y,y,x,x)\in T^{\mathbf{A}}(\mu)
would imply
(y,y,y,x)\in T^{\mathbf{A}}(\mu)
, so
x=m(y,y,y)
for the Mal’cev term
m
and all
(x,y)\in\mu
- contradiction). The implication (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) clearly follows from Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Varieties that have both a Mal’cev and majority operation are also called
arithmetic
, and are well-studied in the literature. In addition to Theorem
3.7
, several equivalent characterizations are known (e.g. by Pixley terms
[
8
, Theorem 4.70]
, or as those Mal’cev varieties that are congruence neutral
[
8
, Theorem 7.50]
).

We are further going to use the well-known fact that Abelian Mal’cev algebras of prime size are related by at most
4
-ary relations
[
24
]
.

For better reference, we include a short proof sketch:

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an Abelian algebra with Mal’cev term
d
, and assume that
|A|=p
is prime. A relational basis of
\mathbf{A}
is given by
T^{\mathbf{A}}\leq\mathbf{A}^{4}
, together with at most binary invariant relations.

Proof.

By Definition
3.2
,
T^{\mathbf{A}}
, the graph of the Mal’cev operation, is invariant under
\mathbf{A}
. Up to isomorphism there is only one Abelian group of order
p
. Thus, by Lemma
3.3
(3), we can assume without loss of generality that
A=\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
and
T^{\mathbf{A}}=\{(x,y,z,x-y+z)\mid x,y,z\in A\}
, where
+
and
-
denote addition and subtraction modulo
p
.

It is easy to see that
T^{\mathbf{A}}
pp-defines every
n+1
-ary relation of the form
\{(\bm{x},z)\mid\mathbf{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}x_{i}=z\}
, for
\mathbb{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}=1
- these relations are exactly the graphs of term operation of
\mathbf{Z}_{p}=(\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\},x-y+z\bmod p)
.

Now, let
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{n}
be an arbitrary invariant relation. As
R
is invariant under
x-y+z
, it must be an affine subspace of the vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
(of some codimension
k
). Therefore, we can describe it by a system of linear equations
\mathbf{\alpha}_{j}^{T}\bm{x}=c_{j}
,
for
j=1,\ldots,k
(for linearly independent
\mathbf{\alpha}_{j}
). By the observation above,
T^{\mathbf{A}}
pp-defines every
(n+2)
-ary relations
Q_{j}=\{(\bm{x},y,z)\mid\mathbf{\alpha}_{j}^{T}\bm{x}+(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j})y=z\}
, for every
j
.

It is now straightforward to see that the binary relation
S_{j}(y,z)
, defined by
\exists\bm{x}(R(\bm{x})\land Q_{j}(\bm{x},y,z))
, is equivalent to
z-(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j})y=c_{j}
. Thus,
R
can be pp-defined by the formula

\exists y_{1},z_{1},\ldots,y_{k},z_{k}\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}Q_{j}(\bm{x},y_{j},z_{j})\land S_{j}(y_{j},z_{j}).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Note that (up to term equivalence, and isomorphism) there is in particular only one idempotent Abelian Mal’cev algebra on a set of prime cardinality. In the rest of the paper we are going to use the following notation for it:

Definition 3.9
.

Let
p
be a prime. Then we denote by
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
the algebra
\mathbf{Z}_{p}=(\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\},x-y+z\bmod p)
, and by
\mathcal{Z}_{p}=\Clo(\mathbf{Z}_{p})
, the clone of idempotent affine operations modulo
p
. Following the standard convention for relational structures, we further define the relational structure
\mathbb{Z}_{p}=(\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\},T^{\mathbf{Z}_{p}},\{0\},\ldots\{p-1\})
(note that
\mathcal{Z}_{p}=\Pol(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
).

4.
Bulatov’s relational bases

In this section we provide a self-contained proof that the at most 4-ary invariant relations of a three-element Mal’cev algebra
\mathbf{A}
form a relational basis of
\mathbf{A}
.
More to that, we are going to show that each three-element Mal’cev algebra has a relational basis that is a subset of the very set of at most 4-ary relations given in
[
19
]
.
We remark that the proof given in
[
19
]

is written in the language of tame congruence theory
[
28
]
,
while we will use the language of commutator theory introduced in Section
3
.
To help the reader, we provide a key to a translation between the two languages:
a pair of congruences
\alpha\prec\beta
(i.e., that
\beta
is a cover of
\alpha
) is of type
\mathbf{2}
, if
\beta/\alpha
is abelian in
\mathbf{A}/\alpha
, and of type
\mathbf{3}
otherwise.

We start our analysis of Mal’cev algebras on
\{0,1,2\}
with two well-known facts about their congruence lattices.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a Mal’cev algebra over
\{0,1,2\}
. If
\mu
is a nontrivial congruence, then it is unique.

Proof.

Seeking a contradiction, let us assume
that both the equivalence relation
\mu_{i}
with blocks
\{i\}
and
\{0,1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
, and
\mu_{j}
with blocks
\{j\}
and
\{0,1,2\}\setminus\{j\}
are congruences of
\mathbf{A}
, for
i\neq j
. Then formula
\exists y\mu_{i}(x_{1},y)\land\mu_{j}(y,x_{2})
pp-defines the relation
\{0,1,2\}^{2}\setminus\{(i,j)\}
. This relation does not have the parallelogram property, hence
\mathbf{A}
cannot be Mal’cev.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a three-element Mal’cev algebra with an Abelian monolith
\mu
. Then
(0_{\mathbf{A}}:\mu)=\mu
.

Proof.

The statement is trivially true if
\mathbf{A}
is simple. So let us assume that
\mathbf{A}
has a non trivial congruence
\mu
.
As
\mu
is Abelian,
(0_{\mathbf{A}}:\mu)\geq\mu
.
Then
\mu
has one block of size 2, and one of size 1. If
(0_{\mathbf{A}}:\mu)=1_{\mathbf{A}}
, then by Lemma
3.3
(2) all
\mu
-classes
[a]_{\mu}
need to have the same size, which is a contradiction. Thus,
(0_{\mathbf{A}}:\mu)=\mu
.
∎

We first study the at most binary invariant relations of
\mathbf{A}
. We recall from the previous section that these correspond exactly to the isomorphisms between elements of
\HS(\mathbf{A})
. In particular, this includes all isomorphisms between subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}
, which we are going to call
partial isomorphisms
.

Definition 4.3
.

We are going to use the following notation for binary relations on
\{0,1,2\}
:

(1)

By
\mu_{i}
we denote the equivalence relation on
\{0,1,2\}
with classes
\{i\}
and
\{0,1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
.

(2)

By
\rho_{i}=\{0,1,2\}^{2}\setminus\mu_{i}
we denote the complement of
\mu_{i}
.

(3)

The graphs of the transposition fixing
i
are denoted by
\psi_{i}
. Moreover,
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
denotes its restriction to
\{0,1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
. For example,

\psi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
1&0&2\end{pmatrix},\ \psi_{2}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

The graph of the cyclic permutation
(012)
of
\{0,1,2\}
is denoted by
\varphi
, and its restriction to
\{0,1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
by
\varphi_{i}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
1&2&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\varphi_{0}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
2&0\end{pmatrix},\ \varphi_{1}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \varphi_{2}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

We further define the partial bijections

\varphi_{3}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2&0\end{pmatrix},\ \varphi_{4}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
2&1\end{pmatrix},\ \varphi_{5}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

Lastly, let us define the two following relations that describe maps from
\{0,1,2\}
to
\{0,1\}
:

\tau_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \tau_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2\\
1&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

We remark that Definition
4.3
follows roughly the same notational conventions as
[
19
]
. However, we changed the way the relations are indexed for better readability. Note that every partial bijection that is not the identity restricted to some subset is equal to
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
,
\varphi_{i}^{\prime}
or
(\varphi_{i}^{\prime})^{-1}
for some
i
.

We can narrow down the number of binary relations we need to consider, by only constructing relational bases up to isomorphisms of
\mathbf{A}
, i.e., up to renaming the elements of
\mathbf{A}
. By Lemma
4.1
and some easy observations we then obtain the following.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be a Mal’cev algebra on a three-element set. Then
\mathbf{B}
is isomorphic to an algebra
\mathbf{A}
on
\{0,1,2\}
with monolith
\mu
, such that one of the following is true:

(1)

\mu=1_{\mathbf{A}}
and every binary
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
is pp-definable from the unary invariant relations together with

\{\varphi,\psi_{2},\psi_{0}^{\prime},\psi_{1}^{\prime},\psi_{2}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\varphi_{5}^{\prime}\}\cap\Inv(\mathbf{A}),

(2)

\mu=\mu_{2}
and every binary
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
is pp-definable from the unary invariant relations together with

\{\mu_{2},\rho_{2},\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{2}^{\prime}\}\cap\Inv(\mathbf{A}).

Proof.

Let
B=\{a,b,c\}
be the universe of
\mathbf{B}
. Let us first assume that
\mathbf{B}
is simple; we show that we fall within the scope of Case (1). Recall that we can regard binary invariant relations as isomorphism between elements of
\HS(\mathbf{B})
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is simple, all binary invariant relations that are not pp-definable from unary invariant relations must be partial isomorphisms.
If
\mathbf{B}
has exactly one automorphism
(ab)
of order
2
, then we obtain
\mathbf{A}
as the isomorphic copy under the map
a\mapsto 0,\ b\mapsto 1,\ c\mapsto 2
. In this case,
\psi_{2}
is clearly invariant under
\mathbf{A}
. Otherwise, we map
B
to
\{0,1,2\}
in an arbitrary way. In this case, the automorphism group of
\mathbf{A}
is either trivial or full; every corresponding binary relation is pp-definable from the list. Furthermore, the graph of every partial isomorphism of
\mathbf{A}
is equal to one of the given list or to one of its inverses. Thus, the given list of relations pp-defines every invariant
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
.

Next, we show that if
\mathbf{A}
is not simple, then we fall within the scope of Case (2).
Let
\mu
be the non-trivial congruence of
\mathbf{B}
, and let
(a,b)\in\mu
be the non-trivial pair of
\mu
.
Furthermore, let
\mathbf{A}
be the isomorphic copy of
\mathbf{B}
under
a\mapsto 0
,
b\mapsto 1
,
c\mapsto 2
.
Clearly
\mu_{2}
is a non-trivial congruence of
\mathbf{A}
, and by Lemma
4.1
it is unique.

If
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
describes a homomorphism of
\mathbf{A}
to a proper subalgebra, it must have kernel
\mu_{2}
. If the image of the homomorphism is equal to
\{0,1\}
, then
R=\tau_{0}
or
R=\tau_{1}
; in all other cases
R
can be obtained by the restriction of
\mu_{2}
or
\rho_{2}
to some 2-element invariant subset (in the latter case,
\rho_{2}(x,y)\leftrightarrow\exists z_{1},z_{2}\mu_{2}(x,z_{1})\land R(z_{1},z_{2})\land\mu_{2}(z_{2},y)
is pp-definable from
R
, and therefore also invariant under
\mathbf{A}
).

Next, observe that
\mathbf{A}
cannot have any other automorphism than
\psi_{2}
(as composing it with the quotient map
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}/\mu_{2}
, would give rise to a homomorphism with other kernel than
\mu_{2}
).

Last, assume that
R\leq\mathbf{A}^{2}
is the graph of a partial isomorphism that is not equal to
\psi_{2}^{\prime}
. If its domain and codomain intersect with both
\mu_{2}
-classes, then
R
is a restriction of
\mu_{2}
or
\rho_{2}
to two 2-element subuniverses, and therefore pp-definable from them (and in the latter case,
\rho_{2}
is also invariant under
\mathbf{A}
).

If
R
is a partial isomorphism between
\{0,1\}

m(0,2,1)
are in the same
\mu_{2}
-class as
m(0,2,0)=0
, i.e.,
m(1,2,0),m(0,2,1)\in\{0,1\}
. On the other hand, by preservation of
\psi_{2}
, clearly
(m(1,2,0),m(0,2,1))\in\,\psi_{2}
holds, thus
m(1,2,0)\neq m(0,2,1)
. So
m
cannot satisfy the identity
m(x,y,z)\approx m(z,y,x)
, hence
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\not\models\Sigma_{1}
. On the other hand, the Boolean majority operation on
\{0,1\}
is an element of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
that clearly witnesses
\mathcal{C}_{2}\models\Sigma_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})\not\models\Sigma_{2}
, but
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\models\Sigma_{2}
.

Proof.

For contradiction, let us assume that there is a 5-ary
f\in\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})
that satisfies
\Sigma_{2}
. Since
f
satisfies
\Sigma_{2}
,
f(0,0,1,2,2)=f(0,0,2,2,1)=f(0,2,2,1,1)=f(1,1,0,2,2)
. Furthermore, since
f
preserves
\psi_{2}
we obtain

\begin{pmatrix}f(0,0,1,2,2)\\
f(1,1,0,2,2)\end{pmatrix}\in\psi_{2}

and therefore
f(0,0,1,2,2)=f(1,1,0,2,2)=2
. Since
f
preserves
\mu_{2}
, this implies that
f(a,b,c,2,2)=2
, for every
a,b,c\in\{0,1\}
. Moreover,

\begin{pmatrix}f(a,b,c,2,2)\\
f(2,2,2,0,1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\
d\end{pmatrix}\in\rho_{2}

implies that
d\in\{0,1\}
. But then we would obtain

\begin{pmatrix}f(2,2,2,0,1)\\
f(2,2,2,1,0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d\\
d\end{pmatrix}\in\psi_{2},

which is a contradiction.

For the second part, let us define the 5-ary map

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{maj}_{5}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})&\text{ if all }x_{i}\in\{0,1\},\\
2&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Here
\mathrm{maj}_{5}
denotes the operation on
\{0,1\}
that returns the value that appears at least 3 times. It is not difficult to see that
f\models\Sigma_{2}
. As it is idempotent and preserves both
\psi_{2}
and
\mu_{2}
it is a polymorphism of
\mathbb{M}_{1}
.
∎

Let us summarize the results of this subsection.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an idempotent Mal’cev clone on
\{0,1,2\}
that has a majority operation and preserves
\mu_{2}
.

(1)

If
\psi_{2}\notin\Inv(\mathcal{C})
, then either
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{C}_{2}
or
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{I}_{2}
,

(2)

if
\psi_{2}\in\Inv(\mathcal{C})
and
\rho_{2}\notin\Inv(\mathcal{C})
, then
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})
,

(3)

if
\psi_{2},\rho_{2}\in\Inv(\mathcal{C})
, then
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})
.

Furthermore,
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{C}_{2}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{I}_{2}
are pairwise not minor-equivalent.

Proof.

Statement (1), (2), and (3) follow from Lemma
6.3
,
6.4
, and
6.5
, respectively. Since
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})\subseteq\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\subseteq\Pol(\mathbb{H})
, we get that
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Moreover,
\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})\nleq_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{0})
by Lemma
6.8
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}\nleq_{\mathrm{m}}\Pol(\mathbb{M}_{1})
by Lemma
6.7
, and
\mathcal{C}_{2}\leq\mathcal{I}_{2}
by Theorem
5.1
.
∎

7.
Algebras without a majority term

We are left with analyzing all idempotent Mal’cev clones that do not have a majority operation, and do not preserve
\varphi
(in particular this also excludes the only idempotent Abelian Mal’cev clone on
\{0,1,2\}
, namely
\mathcal{Z}_{3}
, and the clone
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\Pol(\varphi,\psi_{2},T_{2})
, both of which are part of the classification result for clones of self-dual operations in Theorem
5.2
).

We will show that all such clones are minor-equivalent to
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
. By the following lemma, we already see it for the minimal Mal’cev clones satisfying our assumptions.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
i\in\{0,1,2\}
and let
d_{i}
be as in (
4.3
).
Then
\mathcal{Z}_{2}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\Clo(d_{i})
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\Clo(d_{i})\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\Clo(d_{2})
.
Moreover, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Clo(d_{2})
contains the operation
f_{k}\colon\{0,1,2\}^{2k+1}\to\{0,1,2\}
given for
all
x_{1},\dots x_{2k+1}\in\{0,1,2\}
by

f_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k+1})=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }|\{l\colon x_{l}=2\}|\text{ is odd},\\
\sum_{x_{j}\neq 2}x_{j}&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where the sum is computed modulo 2.
It is easy to verify that
f_{1}=d_{2}
, and that

f_{k+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k+3})=d_{2}(f_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k+1}),x_{2(k+1)},x_{2(k+1)+1})

for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{2k+3}\in\{0,1,2\}
.
The map that sends
x_{1}+\dots+x_{2k+1}
to
f_{k}
can easily be extended to a minion homomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
to
\Clo(d_{2})
. Conversely, the restriction of function to
\{0,1\}
is a minion homomorphism from
\Clo(d_{2})
to
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.
∎

By the next lemma, any other Mal’cev clone satisfying our assumptions, must contain a minimal Mal’cev clone of the form
\Clo(d_{i})
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an idempotent Mal’cev clone on
\{0,1,2\}
such that
\mathcal{C}
has no majority operation and does not preserve
\varphi
. There exists
i\in\{0,1,2\}
such that
d_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\mathcal{C}
preserves a nontrivial equivalence relation
\mu
, without loss of generality,
\mu=\mu_{2}
. Then, by Theorem
4.8
, there exists a relational basis of
\mathcal{C}
that consists of a subset of

\{\mu_{2},\rho_{2},\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{2}^{\prime},T_{2},T_{2}^{\prime},T^{\mu_{2}},S_{01}\}
together with unary relations. Since
d_{2}
preserves all such relations, we get
d_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
.

If
\mathcal{C}
preserves no nontrivial equivalence relation, then (up to renaming elements) by Theorem
4.8
, there exists a relational basis consisting of a subset of

\{\varphi,\psi_{2},\psi_{0}^{\prime},\psi_{1}^{\prime},\psi_{2}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\varphi_{5}^{\prime},

T,T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime},T_{3}^{\prime}\}
and unary relations.
Since
\mathcal{C}
does not preserve
\varphi
, it also cannot preserve
T
, as
(x,y)\in\varphi\Leftrightarrow T(x,1,y,0)
. Thus,
a relational basis exists that is a subset of
\{\psi_{2},\psi_{0}^{\prime},\psi_{1}^{\prime},\psi_{2}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,\varphi_{5}^{\prime},T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime},T_{3}^{\prime}\}
and unary relations. All such relations are preserved by
d_{2}
, thus
d_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Lemma
7.2
already implies that all clones satisfying our assumption lie above
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
. To show equivalence, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an idempotent Mal’cev algebra on
\{0,1,2\}
such that
\varphi\notin\Inv(\mathbf{A})
, and
\mathbf{A}
does not have a majority term. Then
\mathbf{A}
has a two-element Abelian subalgebra or a two-element Abelian proper quotient.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.7
, there needs to be an algebra
\mathbf{B}\in\HS(\mathbf{A})
, such that
\mathbf{B}
has an Abelian monolith. If
\mathbf{B}
is a 2-element algebra, it needs to be Abelian itself, so we are done. Thus, let us assume that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}
has Abelian monolith. Note that
\mathbf{A}
cannot be simple, as then
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{Z}_{3}
, which preserves
\varphi
. Thus,
\mathbf{A}
has a proper Abelian monolith
\mu
. Since
\mathbf{A}
is idempotent, every equivalence class of
\mu
is also a subalgebra; this gives us an Abelian 2-element subalgebra.
∎

We conclude:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an idempotent Mal’cev clone on
\{0,1,2\}
such that
\mathcal{C}
has no majority operation and
\varphi\notin\Inv(\mathcal{C})
. Then
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra with
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}
. By Lemma
7.3
,
\mathbf{A}
has either a two-element Abelian subalgebra or a two-element Abelian proper quotient
\mathbf{B}
. Since
\mathbf{B}
is idempotent,
\Clo(\mathbf{B})
needs to be equal to
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
, the clone of idempotent affine operations modulo
2
. The map
t^{\mathbf{A}}\mapsto t^{\mathbf{B}}
naturally yields a clone homomorphism from
\Clo(\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{C}
to
\Clo(\mathbf{B})=\mathcal{Z}_{2}
, thus
\mathcal{C}\leq_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.

On the other hand, Lemma
7.2
together with Lemma
7.1
, implies that
\mathcal{Z}_{2}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\Clo(d_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, thus
\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}\mathcal{Z}_{2}
.
∎

9.
Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

We conclude this article with some open problems. As already mentioned in the introduction, in order to obtain a complete description of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
, it remains to describe the downset of
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
:

Problem 9.1
.

Describe all the elements of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
that are smaller than
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
, with respect to
\leq_{\mathrm{m}}
.

A major challenge in solving problem
9.1
is that there is no explicit description of the downset of
\mathcal{B}_{2}
in
\mathfrak{L}_{3}
(in contrast to the ideal of self-dual clones described in
[
43
]
). To date, the cardinality of
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
remains unknown, and the same holds for
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
. From
[
41
, Theorem 2.13]
and the main result from
[
2
]
it follows that if
\mathfrak{P}_{3}
(or
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
) does indeed have continuum many elements, then these must necessarily belong to the ideal generated by
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
.

Another promising direction would be to extend the work done in this article to larger finite domains or, more ambitiously, to all finite sets. More precisely, one could aim to classify Mal’cev clones on arbitrary finite sets up to minion homomorphisms.

Problem 9.2
.

Describe all Mal’cev clones over some finite set up to minion homomorphisms.

To our current knowledge, we do not know any constructive way to obtain a relational basis for a given Mal’cev algebra, thus Problem
9.2
might be a too ambitious problem. However, in the following, we outline a few natural starting points.

As we discussed in Theorem
3.7
, a Pixley clone, i.e., a clone that has both a Mal’cev and a majority operation, has always a binary relational basis. This leads to the question:

Problem 9.3
.

Describe all Pixley clones over some finite set up to minion homomorphisms.

Significant progress on Problem
9.3
was already made in
[
33
]
, where Meyer and Starke classify all the submaximal elements of
\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}
. From their analysis it follows that, besides
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{2}}
, all such submaximal elements are the (
\equiv_{\mathrm{m}}
-classes of) polymorphism clones of the Cayley graphs of finite simple groups
\mathbf{G}
. In particular, all such clones are Pixley clones.

In a recent publication of Bodirsky and Moorhead
[
11
]
also conservative Mal’cev clones were discussed. Such clones are also more feasible to work with, as they always have an most 4-ary relational basis. Although
[
11
]
does not give a full classification of conservative Mal’cev clones up to
\leq_{\mathrm{m}}
, it describes minimal elements and, based on this analysis, shows that all corresponding CSPs are in the complexity class
\bigoplus L
.

A natural class, which subsumes all 3-element Mal’cev clones, Pixley clones, and conservative Mal’cev clones, are Mal’cev clones, whose associated algebras generate residually finite varieties. Thus, we ask:

Problem 9.4
.

Describe
\leq_{\mathrm{m}}
for all clones of finite Mal’cev algebras that generate a residually finite variety. What are the minimal elements, and what is the computational complexity of the corresponding CSPs?

In Theorem
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
, Kearnes and Szendrei gave finite upper bounds on the relational basis of such algebras. However, in contrast to arity 4 that suffices for all mentioned examples, their upper bound is exponential in the size of the domain of the clone. Thus, any progress on Problem
9.4
would probably require a more in-depth analysis of the relational bases in this case.
