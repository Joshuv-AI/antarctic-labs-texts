Multi-omics characterization of microbial and metabolite profiles of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit cow-based bioformulations used in sustainable agriculture
Authors: Kartik Gajjar, Devarsh Panchal, Mahendra Chaudhary, Ishan Raval, Doongar Chaudhary, C. K. Patel, Snehal Bagatharia, Chaitanya Joshi, Amrutlal Patel, Darshan Dharajiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jeevamrit (JV) and Ghanjeevamrit (GH) are traditional cow-based bioformulations used in natural farming practices, and this study provides a comprehensive characterization of their microbial profiles via 16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics and metabolite profiles via GC-MS and LC-MS analysis, with two different groups of samples: experimental preparation (EP) and farmer preparation (FP). JV and GH harbored diverse and functionally rich microbial communities, including Lactiplantibacillus, Arcobacter, Comamonas, Planifilum, Pseudomonas, Gp6, etc., associated with nutrient cycling, microbial activity, and plant growth promotion. Untargeted metabolomics revealed ~ 222 (GC-MS) and ~ 1049 (LC-MS) metabolites in Jeevamrit and ~ 96 (GC-MS) and ~ 1208 (LC-MS) metabolites in Ghanjeevamrit. These metabolites were primarily classified as organoheterocyclic compounds, organic acids, lipids, benzenoids, and organic oxygen/nitrogen compounds, and are functionally associated with nutrient solubilization, microbial metabolism, regulation of plant growth, and enhancement of stress tolerance. Multi-omics analysis revealed a clear separation of EP and FP groups with high inter-omics correlations (Jeevamrit up to r = 0.92; Ghanjeevamrit up to r = 0.91). Jeevamrit exhibited dense connectivity with predominance of positive microbial-metabolite associations, while Ghanjeevamrit displayed fewer and more balanced positive and negative correlations. Overall, the study demonstrates that Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit are microbially diverse and metabolically rich bioformulations, reinforcing their roles in enhancing soil health and plant growth. Future works on strain-level diversity, functional pathways analysis, and field trials across different crops and soil types are needed for the standardization and optimization of natural farming inputs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50831-5.

Introduction

Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit are traditional, low-cost, cow-based bioformulations widely used in zero-budget natural farming (ZBNF). They are considered as alternatives to synthetic fertilizers, as they supply nutrients through diverse microbial communities that enhance soil health and crop productivity1–3. These bioformulations are prepared by fermenting cow dung, cow urine, jaggery, pulse flour, soil, and water in liquid (Jeevamrit) or solid (Ghanjeevamrit) form4, reported to improve soil biological activity, nutrient cycling, and plant growth across a range of crops under organic and natural farming5. The extensive use of chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and herbicides has led to adverse effects on soil health and human well-being. It causes soil degradation, reduced soil biodiversity and organic matter by disrupting the native microbial communities essential for nutrient cycling and soil fertility6,7. The increasing concerns over ecological degradation and chemical resistance in pests underscore the need for sustainable alternatives like Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit, which offer eco-friendly options that enrich soil microbial diversity, strengthen nutrient cycling, and support plant health without any harmful side effects8.

Previous studies on natural farming bioformulations, organic manures, and commercial biofertilizer consortia reported the presence of complex bacterial communities. Such microbial communities contain microbes involved in nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization, organic matter decomposition, and production of bioactive metabolites that promote plant growth and stress tolerance9,10. Recent metagenomic studies on Jeevamrit have linked preparation variability such as mixing regimes and oxygen availability, strongly influences microbial community composition. Oxic conditions favored taxa such as Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas, and anoxic conditions enriched Clostridium and Lactobacillus. These shifts are linked to differences in nutrient solubilization and plant growth-promoting potential; however, integrated multi-omics studies remain scarce11. Characterization of Ghanjeevamrit is similarly limited, with most studies focusing on microbial counts or basic functional assays and lacking metabolomic profiling or methodological standardization12. A recent multi-omics study on Beejamrit combining microbial profiling and metabolite analysis identified diverse beneficial bacteria and bioactive compounds supporting its role as a sustainable seed treatment bioformulation13. However, only a few studies on multi-omics characterization of cow-based fermented inputs, and mostly limited to a single sample or lack comparison between standardized experimental preparation (EP) and farmer preparation (FP) formulations. Such EP-FP comparisons are critical because farmer practices introduce variability in ingredients, fermentation conditions (e.g., mixing intensity, incubation), and environmental factors, which can alter microbial communities and metabolite profiles, potentially leading to inconsistent field performance. Such comparisons help address known gaps in the standardization of natural farming inputs. Despite the widespread adoption of natural farming in India and other countries, the systematic characterization of natural farming inputs (Beejamrit, Jeevamrit, Ghanjeevamrit, etc.) for their microbiome and metabolome is still limited, and the mechanistic basis of their function remains incompletely understood.

To bridge this gap, we used high-throughput 16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics and untargeted metabolomics (GC-MS and LC-MS) approach to comprehensively characterize microbial and metabolite composition of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit, with two different preparations (farmer and experimental preparation). Our objectives are to characterize and compare the metabolites and microbial taxa present in each bioformulation. Correlations between key microbial genera and chemical features were also explored to provide a scientific basis for function and rational of traditional bioformulations used in sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

Two sample groups for Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit were collected for the study: experimental preparation (EP) and farmer preparation (FP). EP samples were prepared following a standardized protocol4 intended for application across different crop experiments and were collected across different cropping seasons, depending on the experimental schedule. In contrast, FP samples were prepared by farmers themselves and collected directly from farmers’ fields with their consent during the Kharif 2023, which may involve slight variations in ingredients and preparation practices. The fermentation duration of FP samples was not standardized and varied according to farmer practices, typically ranging between 24 and 72 h. Detailed information on all samples is given in Table 1. For EP samples, Jeevamrit was prepared by mixing 200 L of water, 10 kg of cow dung, 5–10 L of cow urine, 2 kg of jaggery, 2 kg of pulse flour, and one handful of bund soil thoroughly in a barrel, and then kept for 48 h to allow the fermentation before its application. Similarly, preparation of Ghanjeevamrit includes 100 kg of cow dung, 2 kg of jaggery, 2 kg of pulse flour, and one handful of bund soil are mixed with a little amount of cow urine. The mixture is dried in dark conditions and then used for application4. Each collected sample represents an independent biological replicate (Jeevamrit: n = 9; Ghanjeevamrit: n = 11), and all downstream analysis were performed in triplicate as technical replicates to ensure analytical reproducibility. All samples were transported on ice from the collection site and stored at – 20 °C before processing in triplicate.

Bacterial community profiling via 16S rRNA amplicon metagenomics

Genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with minor optimization. The hypervariable V3-V4 regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using Illumina-adapted barcoded primers as described by14. The PCR amplicons were cleaned using QIAseq beads (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) to remove primer dimers and unwanted fragments. Index PCR was performed using IDT-compatible indices (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) to incorporate sequencing adapters and multiplexing indices. The resulting library was subjected to quality control checks using QIAxcel advanced system (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and normalized to equimolar concentrations (4 nM). Libraries were then multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument, and sequencing was performed using a 2 × 250 paired-end configuration according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis of 16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics data

The raw paired-end reads were exported in FASTQ format, and their quality was assessed with FASTQC. Analysis was performed on the Illumina BaseSpace platform (https://basespace.illumina.com). The RDP Classifier v2.14 was employed for taxonomic classification of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Low-quality and outlier samples were excluded, and read counts were normalized by scaling all samples to the minimum library size. To understand the microbial diversity within samples, alpha diversity was calculated by Observed, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Additionally, beta diversity was calculated by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity distances matrix and plotted using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) plot to check the differences in microbial community between different samples. Pairwise comparisons between EP and FP samples were performed using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity to statistically assess differences in community composition. The Microeco R package was used for calculating alpha and beta diversities15. A sample prevalence of ≥ 50% and a relative abundance of ≥ 1% were considered for the core microbiome analysis in MicrobiomeAnalyst 2.016.

Metabolite extraction

Sample preparation for Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS)

Sample preparation for GC-MS profiling was performed as described by17,18. 200 µL of each sample was extracted using 1000 µL of extraction solvent (methanol and ethyl acetate (1:1). The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and then sonicated for 15 min. Following centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, supernatants were evaporated using a SpeedvacTM vacuum concentrator until the complete dryness was achieved. Derivatization of samples was done using BSTFA (N, O-Bis(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide) with 1% TMCS (Trimethylchlorosilane) at 70 °C for 1 h.

Sample preparation for Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS)

For LC-MS, the protocol outlined by10 was followed for sample processing. 200 µL of each sample was blended with 1000 µL of a multi-solvent system of methanol: acetonitrile: water (2:2:1), then sonicated for 20 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. A 0.22 μm syringe filter was used to filter the supernatant. A diluted quantity of 50 µL filtered sample + 450 µL acetonitrile was utilized for LC-MS analysis.

Metabolite profiling

GC-MS analysis

For GC-MS analysis, a PerkinElmer Clarus 680 GC coupled with a Clarus SQ8C MS instrument was used. An Elite-5 ms column was adopted to separate the derivatives. The initial oven temperature was held at 60 °C for 2 min before it was ramped up to 300 °C (with an increase of 10 °C/min) and then held at 300 °C for 6 min. A 1 µL sample was injected at a temperature of 250 °C with a 1:50 split mode, and MS scanned between 5 and 32 min from 50 to 600 m/z.

LC-MS analysis

The LC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC attached with an AdvanceBio 6545XT Q-TOF-MS (Agilent Technologies Inc., USA). The column used was an Agilent Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (3.0 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with a 0.3 ml/min flow rate. The mobile phase A comprised 0.1% formic acid in water, and the mobile phase B comprised 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The following gradient was used for UHPLC run: 0–1 min, 2% B; 2–10 min, 30% B; 11 min, 60% B; 13–25 min, 95% B, followed by a post-run wash for 1 min. Before each injection, the flush port was activated for 15 s, and the needle was washed for 15 min. The optimal conditions of the electrospray interface were as follows: gas temperature 250 °C, drying gas 10 L/min, nebulizer 35 psi, sheath gas temperature 350 °C; sheath gas flow 10 L/min; capillary voltage 3000 V; and nozzle voltage 1000 V. Spectra were acquired in the m/z range 50-1200 m/z in positive ion mode.

Statistical analysis of metabolomics data

Raw files were converted to .mzML format using ProteoWizard19. The GC-MS and LC-MS data were processed with MS-DIAL and XCMS online to generate a comprehensive data matrix, respectively20,21. The NIST14 spectral library and METLIN database were used for the GC-MS and LC-MS peak annotation. The blank subtraction was performed to remove contaminants, and other parameters used in MS-DIAL and XCMS online are provided in Table 2. Unannotated peaks were removed, and in the case of duplicate metabolites, the one with the higher score value was retained. To normalize peak intensities, sum normalization, log transformation, and autoscaling were applied using MetaboAnalyst (v6.0)22. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to explore the metabolic differences between samples. UC-Davis batch compound ClassyFire23 was used for the classification of metabolites at the superclass level, and classification pie charts were prepared in MS Excel.

Correlation analysis of microbial genera and metabolite profiles

Correlation analysis of microbial (16 S rRNA genus abundance) and metabolite (GC-MS and LC-MS metabolite peak intensity) with normalized datasets was performed using sPLS-DA (sparse partial least square discriminant analysis) within the DIABLO (data integration analysis for biomarker discovery using latent components) framework implemented in mixOmics R package24. N-integration was used, and features with near-zero variance and present in < 20% of samples were filtered out. Pairwise associations (16 S rRNA vs. GC-MS and 16 S rRNA vs. LC-MS) were explored, each retaining 25 features per component to identify strongly correlated taxa-metabolite pairs. For multi-omics discrimination of EP and FP, a DIABLO model with a 5-fold cross-validation was used to tune components and feature selection. The final model was fitted with one component to generate sample discrimination plots, correlation structures between components, and circos plot for microbe-metabolite association networks (≥ 0.7).

Results

16S rRNA amplicon metagenomics of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing data revealed insights into microbial diversity of bioconsortia (Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit). In Jeevamrit samples total of 8,729,335 reads were passed filter, with 8,192,922 reads (93.86%) classified at the genus level into 2,650 features (Table S1). Similarly, in Ghanjeevamrit samples a total of 19,162,747 reads passed filtering, with 1,640,6058 reads (85.61%) classified at the genus level into 3,267 features (Table S2). Data were normalized to the minimum read counts of 1,15,682 and 85,625 for Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit, respectively (Fig. S1 and S2), for comparability and to minimize biases due to varying sequencing depth.

The bacterial community composition of Jeevamrit samples (JV) at genus level revealed substantial variation in their relative abundance among samples (Fig. 1a), with major contributions from Lactiplantibacillus, Arcobacter, Prevotella, Clostridium sensu stricto, Bifidobacterium, Megasphaera, and Comamonas. JV-1 showed a higher proportion of Arcobacter (11.11%) and Comamonas (7.63%). JV-2 and JV-3 were strongly dominated by Lactiplantibacillus (34.68% and 54.95%, respectively). JV-5 showed elevated levels of Arcobacter (12.38%) followed by Prevotella (8.03%). JV-6 showed higher levels of Arcobacter (18.91%) followed by Lactiplantibacillus (16.21%). Farmer field samples (JV-7 to JV-9) were enriched with different genera such as Prevotella, Streptococcus, Phocaeicola, and Bacteroides. Overall, JV-1 to JV-9 exhibited a diverse bacterial community without dominance of a single genus. The roles of the most abundant and functionally important genera identified in the Jeevamrit samples are summarized in Table S3.

Alpha diversity indices revealed clear variation in bacterial diversity across Jeevamrit samples. JV-1 recorded the highest richness (Observed index: 1137, Chao1 index: 1319), followed by JV-2 (Observed index: 1051, Chao1 index: 1281) and JV-9 (Observed index: 929, Chao1 index: 1200), whereas JV-4 (Observed index: 533, Chao1 index: 650) showed the lowest richness (Fig. 1b and c). Similar patterns were reflected in the richness and evenness estimator (Fig. 1d and e), JV-1 (Shannon index: 4.61, Simpson index: 0.97) showed significantly higher diversity, followed by JV-9 (Shannon index: 3.88, Simpson index: 0.95), whereas the lowest Shannon index was recorded in JV-7 (1.93) and Simpson index in JV-8 (0.62). PCoA plot based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix showed distinct clustering across Jeevamrit samples (Fig. 1f), with 27.1% (PCo1) and 22.2% (PCo2) of the total variance explained by the first two axes. PERMANOVA analysis confirmed that the separation between preparation types was statistically significant (F = 4.3275, R² = 0.1476, p = 0.001), although some overlap suggests within-group variability (Fig. S3). Jeevamrit samples exhibited a distinct set of core bacterial genera for two groups (Experimental Preparation (EP): JV-1 to JV-6; Farmer Preparation (FP): JV-7 to JV-9). Acinetobacter, Comamonas, and Romboustia were specific to EP, whereas Aeromonas and Prevotella were specific to FP (Fig. 1g).

Taxonomic profiles of Ghanjeevamrit samples (GH) at the genus level showed differences among samples collected from EP and FP (Fig. 2a). GH-01 to GH-03 were dominated by Planifilum (6.65% to 15.70%) and some other genera, such as Povalibacter (GH-01: 8.46%), Lysinibacillus (GH-03: 8.06%), and Saccharomonospora (GH-03: 11.24%). GH-04 to GH-06 showed higher abundance of Pseudomonas (0.72% to 7.48%), Corynebacterium (4.59% to 11.99%), Salinicoccus (4.69% to 9.78%), Acinetobacter (0.14% to 5.33%), and Mammaliicoccus (GH-05: 21.59%). The farmer field samples (GH-07 to GH-11) showed high variability in bacterial community composition. GH-07 contained moderate proportions of Pseudomonas, Devosia, Luteimonas, Pseudorhizobium, and Brevundimonas (~ 5% each). GH-08 and GH-09 showed higher proportions of Gp6 (~ 6–8%) and Ohtaekwangia (~ 5%). GH-10 exhibited strong enrichment of Planococcus (47.69%), and GH-11 showed a mixed profile with Pseudorhizobium, Ohtaekwangia, and Devosia each contributing ~ 3–5% of the community. Metabolomic analysis of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit

Untargeted metabolomic profiling identified a wide spectrum of metabolites present in both Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit samples. The roles of functionally important and most abundant metabolites based on peak intensity for GC-MS and LC-MS analysis of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit are presented in Tables S4 and S5, respectively. PCA plot of Jeevamrit GC-MS analysis (Fig. 3a) showed clustering of FP (JV-7 to JV-9) along with JV-1 and JV-2 samples, while JV-3 to JV-6 formed a distinct cluster. PC1 and PC2 together explained 33.3% and 14% of the total variance, respectively. GC-MS based metabolite classification of Jeevamrit (Fig. 3b) highlighted that organoheterocyclic compounds (19%) were most abundant, followed by organic acids (17%), organometallic compounds (16%), organic oxygen compounds (14%), and benzenoids (13%). Overall, GC-MS analysis identified ~ 222 total metabolites per sample. Several core and functionally important metabolites were present in all samples. These included organic acids such as organic acids such as benzoic acid, 2-methylbutanoic acid, crotonic acid, butanoic acid, propanoic acid, pentaonic acid, formic acid, 4-aminobenzoic acid, and dihydroxymalonic acid. Carbohydrates such as L-fucose, D-xylose, L-glycine, L-alanine, and inositol were also detected. Heterocyclic compounds including 1,2,4-trioxolane and 1,3-oxathiane were present as well. Other metabolites such as 4-iminobarbituric acid and paromomycin were also identified (Table 3).

Microbe (16 S rRNA) and metabolite (GC-MS and LC-MS) correlation analysis

The mixOmics DIABLO analysis of Jeevamrit showed clear discrimination between EP and FP across 16 S rRNA amplicon, GC-MS, and LC-MS datasets. In sample plots, EP and FP formed distinct, non-overlapping clusters, indicating compositional differences between preparation methods (Fig. 5a). The correlations between latent components were high, with highest in correlation between 16 S rRNA and LC-MS (r = 0.92). It was followed by correlations between 16 S rRNA and GC-MS (r = 0.88), and GC-MS and LC-MS (r = 0.85), demonstrating robust inter-omics agreement. It showed the strong alignment among microbial profiles and LC-MS metabolites. In the 16 S rRNA vs. GC-MS plot (Fig. 5b), bacterial genera clustered tightly on the positive side of component 1 and component 2. In contrast, GC-MS metabolites were broadly dispersed across both components. This pattern reflects higher chemical diversity and limited overlap with microbial variation. The 16 S rRNA vs. LC-MS integration (Fig. 5c) showed a more cohesive pattern, with bacterial genera and LC-MS metabolites aligned closely on both axes. LC-MS metabolites displayed a tighter spread than GC-MS, indicating stronger microbe-metabolite associations, evident by previously observed high correlation (r = 0.92). The circos plot (Fig. 5d) further revealed numerous strong cross-omics interactions (> 0.7) among 16 S rRNA, GC-MS, and LC-MS datasets. Positive correlations were higher compared to negative correlations, indicating synergistic microbial-metabolite relationships. Bacterial genera (1–30) showed extensive positive connectivity with both GC-MS (30–40) and LC-MS (41–50) metabolites. LC-MS metabolites showed broader and denser interactions, whereas GC-MS metabolites formed more compact and targeted associations. The outer expression profiles showed the EP and FP groups remained clearly separated across all omics layers. It also confirmed preparation-specific differences, with most bacterial genera displayed higher expression in the FP group samples than EP. Discussion

Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit are key cow-based inputs in zero-budget natural farming (ZBNF). They are applied as low-cost, microbially enriched bioformulations that enhance overall soil health and can reduce or replace synthetic fertilizers while sustaining crop productivity25. They are typically prepared by fermenting cow dung, cow urine, jaggery, pulse flour, soil, and water, which supply organic carbon, macro- and micronutrients, and diverse beneficial microorganisms. When applied to soil/crop, they improve soil structure, nutrient availability, and plant growth when applied to crops26. Characterization of these bioformulations’ microbiome through 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing and metabolome through GC-MS and LC-MS analyses provides an important basis for the use of these traditional inputs in sustainable agricultural practices.

16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit

The high bacterial diversity, as indicated by richness and evenness indices, observed in several Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit samples, is consistent with previous studies on commercial biofertilizer consortia, which reported diverse bacterial genera. The broad microbial diversity in such bioformulations is considered beneficial. It supports multiple plant- and soil-beneficial functions, including nitrogen fixation and phosphate and potassium solubilization. It also aids in organic matter decomposition and the production of bioactive metabolites that promote plant growth and enhance stress tolerance8,9. Similar to the present findings, diverse bacterial communities in a biofertilizer consortium demonstrated plant growth-promoting activity. This highlights that functional robustness often arises from taxonomically complex groups rather than from a single strain27.

Jeevamrit samples were dominated by different genera such as Lactiplantibacillus, Arcobacter, Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, Megasphaera, Comamonas, Levilactobacillus, Klebsiella, Alcaligenes, Azotobacter, etc. These microbes have well documented plant-beneficial traits (Table S3), supporting their use in sustainable agricultural practices. Lactiplantibacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Lactobacillus, and Levilactobacillus produce phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and gibberellins, synthesize siderophores and ammonia. They also solubilize nutrients like phosphorus (P), potassium (K), zinc (Zn), and silicon (Si). These activities enhance nutrient availability, suppress phytopathogenic fungi, and improve plant tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses28–32. Genera such as Comamonas, Alcaligenes, Klebsiella, Acinetobacter, Azotobacter, Arcobacter, and Acetobacter contribute to nitrogen fixation, solubilization of P, K, and Zn, and production of ACC deaminase and siderophore. They are also involved in degradation of organic compounds and pollutants. These activities support the role of Jeevamrit as a source of nutrients and plant growth-promoting activities 33–43.

Ghanjeevamrit was characterized by high relative abundances of Planifilum, Povalibacter, Pseudomonas, Corynebacterium, Gp6, Salinicoccus, Acinetobacter, Planococcus, Devosia, etc. These microbes contribute to specific roles related to nutrient cycling, plant growth promotion, and stress mitigation (Table S3). Planifilum and Saccharomonospora are involved in carbon cycling by decomposing organic matter. Planococcus and Povalibacter enhance phosphorus solubilization and N-C cycling, which improves nutrient availability from the Ghanjeevamrit44–46. Pseudomonas, Devosia, Luteimonas, Brevundimonas, Ohtaekwangia, Paracoccus, Salinicoccus, Pseudorhizobium, Mammaliicoccus, Acinetobacter, and Corynebacterium showed a wide range of PGPR traits. They possessed traits such as nitrogen fixation, siderophore and IAA production, phosphate solubilization, enhancement of mycorrhizal colonization and antioxidant defenses, inhibition of fungal hyphae, and detoxification of metals and other soil contaminants. Overall, they confer strong potential for improving soil quality and plant resilience under both optimal and stress conditions40,47–54.

Some core bacterial genera were unique to experimental preparation e.g. Acinetobacter, Comamonas, and Romboustia for Jeevamrit and Acinetobacter, Corynebacterium, Haplopseudomonas, Nitratireductor, Planifilum, Pseudomonas, Romboustia, and Salinicoccus for Ghanjeevamrit). Some core bacterial genera were specific to farmer preparation e.g. Aeromonas and Prevotella for Jeevamrit and Gp6, Saccharibacteria, Brevundimonas, Devosia, and Ohtaekwangia for Ghanjeevamrit. This suggests that preparation method, ingredients used, and other environmental factors strongly shape community composition. Some bacterial genera detected in the present study, including Escherichia, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Corynebacterium, Pseudomonas, and Acinetobacter, are known to contain species with opportunistic pathogenic potential55–57.

Metabolomic analysis of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit

An untargeted metabolomic approach (GC-MS and LC-MS) was used to reveal the metabolite profiles of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit. These bioformulations contain metabolically rich compositions due to the high microbial activities. Major metabolite classes identified in GC-MS and LC-MS analysis include organoheterocyclic compounds, organic acids and derivatives, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic oxygen and nitrogen compounds, benzenoids, and phenylpropanoids/polyketides. These classes of metabolites were reported to contribute significantly to the plant and soil ecosystem. Organoheterocyclic compounds boost the carbon utilization of soil microbes and regulate plant growth and stress tolerance58–60. Lipids and lipid-like molecules play vital roles in plant physiology, stress adaptations, and plant-pathogen interactions61. Organic acids are extensively produced by soil microbes and plant roots in the rhizosphere, where they contribute to microbial interaction, nutrient solubilization, pH maintenance, and mitigation of plant stress62,63. Benzenoids generally occur as volatile organic compounds in plants and soil microbes, where they play important roles in plant defense, stress tolerance, and interaction with microbes64. Phenylpropanoids and polyketides are major secondary metabolites of plants, with significant roles in plant growth, defense against stress conditions, and interaction with soil microbes65,66.

GC-MS based metabolite profiling of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit revealed variety of common and unique metabolites which contribute to plant growth, defense, and soil health (Table S4). L-valine, L-glycine, and L-alanine are important amino acids for plant growth and facilitate nutrient enrichment by enhancing carbon and nitrogen accumulation in plants60,67–69. Compounds like 2-propanone, butanoic acid, and propionic acid were also detected, which have been reported for their role in plant defense against various plant pathogens70–72. Similarly, LC-MS analysis also detected various metabolites in Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit (Table S5). Such metabolites included phosphohydroxypyruvic acid and 3,4-dihydroxy-2-butanone 4-phosphate, which are key intermediates in serine and riboflavin biosynthesis, respectively73–75. Compounds like behenoyl ethanolamines, L-isoleucine, and rutin are important plant signaling molecules that regulate plant growth and responses to stress conditions76–78. Phenyllactic acid is an important metabolite that promotes the growth of rice seedlings by increasing water and nutrient absorption79.

GC-MS analysis of Jeevamrit showed the presence of metabolites having diverse roles in plant and soil. Such metabolites included benzoic acid (increases tolerance against abiotic stress), 2-methylbutanoic acid (biocontrol activity against root-knot nematodes and Alternaria alternata), D-xylose (carbon source for soil microbes), and inositol, which supports microbial activity and phosphate cycling80–84. Furthermore, LC-MS analysis identified camptothecin and diosflavone as key metabolites in Jeevamrit. These metabolites play a role in protecting plants from herbivores and pathogens during the early developmental stage and contribute to plant responses against environmental stress and interactions with soil microbes31,85. Similarly, GC-MS profiling of Ghanjeevamrit revealed the presence of lactic acid and glycolic acid, key compounds in the rhizosphere. Lactic acid is produced by many soil bacteria, which regulates soil organic matter, nutrient cycling, and activates plants’ defense via jasmonate and salicylic acid pathways32,86. Glycolic acid, reported in root exudates involved in plant tolerance to heavy metals like Pb87. In LC-MS analysis of Ghanjeevamrit, various unique metabolites like isodomedin, glutathione, and quercetin derivatives were detected. Antioxidant compounds like glutathione and quercetin play important roles in detoxifying the reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhancing the plant tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses88,89. Glutathione also regulates various cellular processes and interactions with microbes, while quercetin supports seed germination and overall plant development88,89. Furthermore, isodomedin possesses antibacterial and cytotoxic activities against plant pathogens90. Overall, the presence of these metabolites supports the role of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit in improving soil health, microbial diversity, and plant growth. The detected metabolite profiles represent the overall chemical composition of the bioformulations rather than exclusively fermentation-derived microbial products and future targeted metabolomics studies will be necessary to better resolve the origin and dynamics of specific metabolites.

Microbe (16 S rRNA) and metabolite (GC-MS and LC-MS) correlation analysis

The multi-omics integration of 16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics, GC-MS, and LC-MS datasets provided insights into how bacterial genera and metabolites co-vary across EP and FP methods of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit. The observed differences between EP and FP groups are likely influenced by variations in fermentation conditions, substrate composition, and inoculum origin. However, key factors such as seasonal variation, temporal dynamics, batch effects, and differences in fermentation duration were not fully controlled and may have significantly contributed to the observed microbial and metabolite profiles. Therefore, the observed EP-FP differences likely reflect a combination of preparation related and environmental factors rather than preparation type alone. Devakumar et al.,8, Fu,91. The high correlations observed in Jeevamrit (up to r = 0.92) and Ghanjeevamrit (up to r = 0.91) among latent components highlight strong cross-omics interaction. Integration of microbial (16 S rRNA) vs. metabolite (GC-MS or LC-MS) networks revealed tight coupling between several bacterial communities and biochemical signatures. This suggested that strong microbe-metabolite associations play a key role in the functional complexity of the formulations92. The circos plot showed predominance of positive correlations between bacterial genera and metabolites, especially in Jeevamrit. LC‑MS features show dense connectivity to many taxa, suggesting extensive synergistic or co-metabolic relationships. In contrast, Ghanjeevamrit displayed fewer strong (> 0.7) links and more negative correlations, indicating possible competitive or inhibitory relationships and a functionally diverse microbe-metabolite network93,94. Positive correlations were observed between L-alanine and genera like Microvirga, Siccibacter, and Pseudoxanthomonas, all of which contribute to nitrogen fixation or nitrogen cycling. These genera utilize L‑alanine as a nitrogen carrier and regulate nitrogenase activity, consistent with the observed association in Jeevamrit95–98. Similarly, Pseudorhizobium showed a strong association with 1-monopalmitin and 2-keto-3-methylvaleric acid, metabolites linked with lipid turnover and branched-chain amino acid metabolism that are commonly enriched in microbially active organic amendments99. In Ghanjeevamrit, fusidic acid is positively correlated with Lacipirellula, and Methylosinus, involved in decomposition of organic matter and nutrient cycling in soil100,101. Clear separation between EP and FP groups was observed in the multi-omics integration, which focuses on discriminative features; however, this pattern was not evident in GC-MS PCA of Ghanjeevamrit, where the full metabolite dataset was considered. This difference highlights that unsupervised analyses capture overall variance, whereas supervised integration methods emphasize features that contribute most to group discrimination. The observed components and interactions likely contribute to the potential application of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit in sustainable agriculture.

This study provides an integrated view of the microbiome and metabolome profiles of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit, but with several limitations. First, although sampling time, season, and location were documented, seasonal effects, temporal dynamics, and batch-to-batch variability were not fully controlled and may substantially influence microbial and metabolite profiles, potentially confounding preparation specific (EP vs. FP) interpretations. In particular, the experimental preparation (EP) samples were derived from multiple cropping seasons and therefore cannot be considered a homogeneous entity, while farmer-prepared (FP) samples reflect real-world variability in preparation and environmental conditions. Second, bioformulations were analyzed at a single fermentation stage with a limited number of samples, which restricted assessment of fermentation dynamics and microbial succession; moreover, while EP samples were fermented under a standardized 48 h protocol, the fermentation duration of FP samples was not controlled and varied according to farmer practices (typically 24–72 h). Given the dynamic nature of microbial fermentation, such variation may substantially influence microbial succession. Third, although the use of “one handful of bund soil” as an inoculum follows traditional preparation protocols, this approach lacks quantitative standardization and represents a limitation for experimental reproducibility. Finally, the use of 16 S rRNA amplicon metagenomics provided genus-level taxonomic resolution only, and other microbial groups were not profiled, while multi-omics correlation-based networks indicate associations but do not establish causality or directionality. Future studies should include time-series sampling across seasons, controlled batch replicates, standardized or characterized inocula, strain-level and functional metagenomics, and targeted metabolomics, coupled with field trials across different crops and soil types. Such efforts will be essential for disentangling seasonal effects from preparation dependent signatures and for standardizing Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit as reliable, field-ready bioformulations for sustainable agriculture.

Conclusion

Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit harbor diverse and functional bacterial communities, with higher diversity and distinct core genera in the experimental and farmer preparations. Dominant genera were associated with multiple plant growth-promoting traits, supporting their role as biologically active inputs for sustainable agriculture. Untargeted GC-MS and LC-MS profiling revealed a broad report on metabolites, including organic acids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organoheterocyclic compounds, organic nitrogen/oxygen compounds, and benzenoids, which are associated with nutrient mobilization, plant defense, and stress tolerance. Multi-omics integration suggested that the preparation type (EP and FP) may influences microbial and metabolite profiles, with high inter-omics correlation, especially in Jeevamrit, dense and predominantly positive networks were formed. However, confounding factors such as seasonal variation, batch effects, and differences in fermentation duration limit the ability to attribute these differences solely to preparation method. Overall, these findings provide a comprehensive characterization of the microbial and metabolite composition of Jeevamrit and Ghanjeevamrit, highlighting their potential relevance for improving soil health and crop productivity. The profiles identified in this study may support future efforts toward standardization, quality improvement, and optimization of natural farming inputs for more consistent field performance.
