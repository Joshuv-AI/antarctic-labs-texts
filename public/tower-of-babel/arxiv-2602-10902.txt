Reevaluating Thermal Instability in a Uniform Plasma: An Extended Analysis of Instability Domains
Varsha Felsy, Ramon Oliver, Jaume Terradas, Amanda Stricklan, Timothy Waters, James A. Klimchuk

Abstract
Thermal instability plays a crucial role in the dynamics of astrophysical plasmas. Building upon the foundational work of 15 and the subsequent analysis by 38 , this study revisits thermal instability in a uniform, non-magnetic medium. We aim to reevaluate and expand the understanding of instability domains, focusing on the classification and characteristics of thermal and acoustic modes in the presence of heating, radiative cooling, and thermal conduction. Except for Spitzer’s expression for parallel thermal conductivity, heating and cooling processes are unspecified. Additionally, we investigate the existence of isobaric and isochoric thermal modes across the extreme limits of very short and very long wavelengths, as well as at intermediate wavelengths; we address a common misconception about the existence of purely adiabatic perturbations. We also perform an in-depth analysis of the dispersion relation for an infinite, uniform hydrodynamic medium, as derived by 15 . This enables the generation of growth rate and dispersion diagrams, providing insight into thermal instability across different wavelength ranges. With the inclusion of thermal conduction, our study refines the classification of the instability regions previously outlined by 38 . Our findings confirm that their classification holds when the Field length is smaller than or comparable to the thermal wavelength, c s ​ t cool c_{\mathrm{s}}t_{\mathrm{cool}} . For larger Field lengths, a simplified classification becomes impractical. Furthermore, we discuss the potential implications of the catastrophic cooling instability ( 39 ) in coronal rain formation.

I
Introduction

Thermal instability is a fundamental mechanism that affects the physical states and evolutionary pathways of astrophysical plasmas across a wide range of cosmic environments. The concept was first introduced by
27
, who proposed that, in an infinite homogeneous medium in thermal equilibrium, instability can arise when radiative losses increase with decreasing temperature, leading to runaway cooling and the formation of cool condensations—–early insights that laid the groundwork for subsequent theoretical developments. Later, in 1965, Field established a rigorous formalism and derived the local criteria for thermal instability, providing a foundational analytical framework
(
15
)
. His formulation elucidated the conditions under which small perturbations in temperature and density grow, forming the basis for understanding multi-phase structure formation in astrophysical plasmas. This seminal contribution was further generalized by
2
;
3
to include the effects of dynamical systems and complex physical processes. Additionally, foundational studies by
8
and
24
expanded the understanding of thermal instability in different astrophysical contexts, emphasizing the role of radiative cooling in various plasma environments.

Since these pioneering works, a vast body of research has sought to understand the complex interplay of radiative cooling, heating processes, and additional physical factors that contribute to the formation of multi-phase structures such as cold clouds and filaments in astrophysical plasmas. Many studies have extended the foundational analyses by exploring the behavior of thermal instability in more realistic configurations, including self-gravity, non-uniform backgrounds, magnetic fields, and external perturbations
(
17
;
32
;
25
;
5
;
16
;
12
;
6
, to name just a few works)
. These investigations have revealed how various physical mechanisms influence the onset, growth, and saturation of thermal instability, often moving beyond the idealized, homogeneous models to better reflect conditions encountered in actual astrophysical environments. Over recent decades, a large body of research on thermal instability in more complex astrophysical settings has been amassed. While a comprehensive review is beyond the scope of this Introduction, interested readers are referred to extensive reviews
(
1
;
19
;
10
;
13
;
18
;
34
;
29
, e.g.,)
that collectively cover a variety of environments and spatial scales, ranging from the solar corona to the intracluster medium, including molecular clouds and the circumgalactic medium. Furthermore, thermal instability in uniform media also continues to attract significant attention from researchers nowadays
(
26
;
14
;
20
;
28
;
31
;
30
, e.g.,)
. Given this sustained interest and ongoing exploration across various astrophysical regimes, the physical insights derived from our work are particularly timely.

Thermal instability fundamentally influences the morphology and dynamics of the interstellar medium, galaxy clusters, and accretion environments. It underpins the formation of cold clouds from hot, diffuse media and plays a key role in the feedback mechanisms that regulate star formation and galaxy evolution
(
25
;
37
)
. These phenomena are often observed as multi-phase systems, where cold, dense filaments coexist within hotter plasma, driven by the destabilization of thermal equilibrium
(
21
;
32
)
. Understanding the precise conditions under which the thermal instability develops and saturates remains a scientific challenge, requiring advanced theoretical models and detailed numerical simulations.

Beyond extragalactic and galactic contexts, thermal instability has significant applications in solar physics. In the solar atmosphere, particularly within the chromosphere and corona, thermal instability mechanisms are believed to contribute to the formation of prominences, coronal rain, and other fine-structured cool plasmas embedded within hot surroundings
(
36
;
35
;
23
)
. These structures often originate from localized cooling regions triggered by radiative losses, with magnetic fields playing an essential role in stabilizing or destabilizing these condensations. Investigating thermal instability within this context not only enhances our understanding of solar activity and energetic events, but also provides fundamental insights into plasma processes that are applicable across astrophysics.

This study aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the different regimes of thermal instability by exploring all possible modes within the same homogeneous hydrodynamical system initially investigated by

II
Main equations

We study thermal instability in an infinite, homogeneous, non-magnetized plasma in mechanical and thermal equilibrium. The equilibrium density, pressure, temperature, and velocity are
\rho_{\mathrm{0}}
,
p_{\mathrm{0}}
,
T_{\mathrm{0}}
,
\vec{v}_{\mathrm{0}}=\vec{0}
. In a plasma element, heat exchange is governed by the heat-loss function,
\mathscr{L}
, and the thermal conduction heat flux,
-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)
, where
\mathscr{L}=\Lambda-H
is equal to the heat losses,
\Lambda
, minus heat gains,
H
, per unit mass and unit time and
\kappa(T)=\kappa_{0}T^{5/2}
. The definition of the non-adiabatic terms follows the convention adopted by
15
.

Perturbations of a quantity
f
about its equilibrium state,
f_{0}
, are expressed as

f(\vec{r},t)=f_{0}+\delta f(x,t),

(1)

where plane-wave solutions propagating in the
x
-direction are assumed,

\delta f(x,t)=A_{f}\exp(nt+ikx).

(2)

Following
15
, we will refer to
n
as the growth rate; sometimes we also refer to the real part of
n
as the growth rate. Small amplitude perturbations are considered in what follows.

II.1
Dispersion relation

The dispersion relation in the form given by
15
is

n^{3}+n^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}\left(k_{T}+\frac{k^{2}}{k_{\kappa}}\right)+nc_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}k^{2}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}^{3}k^{2}}{\gamma}\left(k_{T}-k_{\rho}+\frac{k^{2}}{k_{\kappa}}\right)=0,

(3)

where
\gamma
is the ratio of specific heats and
c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}=\gamma p_{\mathrm{0}}/\rho_{\mathrm{0}}
is the square of the adiabatic sound speed. Furthermore, the following three wavenumbers are defined

k_{T}=\frac{\mathscr{L}_{T}}{c_{\mathrm{v}}c_{\mathrm{s}}},\hskip 28.45274ptk_{\rho}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}{T_{\mathrm{0}}}\frac{\mathscr{L}_{\rho}}{c_{\mathrm{v}}c_{\mathrm{s}}},\hskip 28.45274ptk_{\kappa}=\rho_{\mathrm{0}}\frac{c_{\mathrm{v}}c_{\mathrm{s}}}{\kappa},

(4)

with
c_{\mathrm{v}}
the specific heat at constant volume.
The derivatives of the heat-loss function,

\mathscr{L}_{T}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{\rho},\hskip 56.9055pt\mathscr{L}_{\rho}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\rho}\right)_{T},

(5)

are evaluated in the equilibrium state.

It is customary to express the quantity
n
as
n=n_{\mathrm{R}}+in_{\mathrm{I}}
, where
n_{\mathrm{R}},n_{\mathrm{I}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Given that Equation (
3
) is a third degree polynomial with real coefficients, it can either have three real solutions (
n_{\mathrm{I}}=0
), or a real solution and a pair of complex conjugate solutions. Real solutions correspond to exponentially growing (
n_{\mathrm{R}}>0
) or damped (
n_{\mathrm{R}}<0
), non-propagating thermal modes, while the pair of complex conjugate solutions corresponds to two overstable (
n_{\mathrm{R}}>0
) or stable (
n_{\mathrm{R}}\leq 0
) acoustic waves with frequency
n_{\mathrm{I}}
.

38
chose to cast the dispersion relation as follows

n^{3}+\frac{N_{\rho}}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}n^{2}+k^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}n+\frac{N_{\mathrm{p}}}{\gamma t_{\mathrm{cool}}}k^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}=0.

(6)

The dimensionless quantities
N_{\mathrm{p}}
and
N_{\rho}
are given by

N_{\rho}=N_{\rho 0}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}\right)^{2},\hskip 56.9055ptN_{\mathrm{p}}=N_{\mathrm{p}0}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}\right)^{2},

(7)

with

N_{\rho 0}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{0}}}{\Lambda_{0}}\mathscr{L}_{T},\hskip 5.69046ptN_{\mathrm{p}0}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{0}}}{\Lambda_{0}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{p}\equiv\frac{T_{\mathrm{0}}}{\Lambda_{0}}\left(\mathscr{L}_{T}-\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}{T_{\mathrm{0}}}\mathscr{L}_{\rho}\right),

(8)

and
\Lambda_{0}
the heat losses in the equilibrium. In addition,
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Field length as defined by
38
, based on
4
,

\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\kappa T_{\mathrm{0}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}\Lambda_{0}}}.

(9)

In this definition, the Field length is the characteristic length scale for which cooling and conduction balance. We note that this is not the only definition of this quantity. In some works, e.g.
22
, the factor
2\pi
is omitted. In other works,
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}
is given as the critical wavelength below which the thermal mode is stabilized by heat conduction
(
15
;
12
)
. Here we refer to this length scale as
\lambda_{\mathrm{therm}}
, see Equation (
18
), in which the term
N_{\mathrm{p}0}
tells us how sensitive cooling is to small changes in pressure.

The cooling time scale is defined as follows,

t_{\mathrm{cool}}=\frac{c_{\mathrm{v}}T_{\mathrm{0}}}{\Lambda_{0}}.

(10)

Furthermore,
38
defined the parameter

R=\frac{N_{\mathrm{p}0}}{\gamma N_{\rho 0}},

(11)

whose relevance will be highlighted later. Although Equation (8) of
38
does not include the subscript “0” in both
N_{\mathrm{p}0}
and
N_{\rho 0}
, it must be understood that the parameter
R
is independent of
k
and that the definition of
R
must be properly written as in our Equation (
11
).

A comparison of the two forms of the dispersion relation leads to the following identities:

k_{T}=\frac{N_{\rho 0}}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}},\hskip 5.69046ptk_{\rho}=\frac{N_{\rho 0}-N_{\mathrm{p}0}}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}},\hskip 5.69046ptk_{\kappa}=t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(12)

with

k_{F}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}.

(13)

The analytical solutions of cubic polynomials (
3
) and (
6
) are well known, but they are rather long and not very useful to describe the nature of solutions and the instability domains of a given plasma. As we discuss later, Figure 1 of
15
provides a complex discussion of this point, while

II.2
Instability criteria

From the physical analysis of the energy exchange between a plasma element and its surroundings in a uniform medium,
15
obtained the isochoric, isobaric, and isentropic instability criteria
(
12
, for an alternative derivation, see)
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{T}<0,\hskip 5.69046pt\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{p}<0,\hskip 5.69046pt\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial T}\right)_{S}\equiv\mathscr{L}_{T}+\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}{T_{\mathrm{0}}}\mathscr{L}_{\rho}<0.

(14)

These three criteria, that strictly apply in the absence of thermal conduction, can be rewritten as

N_{\rho 0}<0,\hskip 56.9055ptN_{\mathrm{p}0}<0,\hskip 56.9055ptN_{\mathrm{S}0}<0,

(15)

where the new dimensionless parameter
N_{\mathrm{S}0}
has been defined as

N_{\mathrm{S}0}=\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\left(\gamma N_{\rho 0}-N_{\mathrm{p}0}\right)\equiv-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(R-1)N_{\rho 0}.

(16)

Regarding the regions of instability of the acoustic modes, see the discussion in Section 3.4 of
38
.

It is now obvious that one reason for using the quantities
N_{\mathrm{p}0}
and
N_{\rho 0}
for the mathematical formulation of the thermal instability problem is the simplicity of the isochoric and isobaric instability criteria; something similar can be said about
N_{\mathrm{S}0}
and the isentropic criterion.

When conduction is taken into account, Equations (
14
) or (
15
) no longer provide the instability criteria and must be substituted by
(
15
, see Equations (25) in)

N_{\rho 0}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}<0,\hskip 5.69046ptN_{\mathrm{p}0}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}<0,\hskip 5.69046ptN_{\mathrm{S}0}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}<0.

(17)

In view of these formulas, statements about a plasma satisfying some instability criterion should, in principle, reflect whether they refer to infinite (Equations (
15
)) or finite wavelength (Equations (
17
)); see
33
, where a detailed treatment is given. Anyway, a relevant question is whether a system that is isobarically unstable for some wavelength has a mode with this wavelength that evolves at constant pressure in the linear phase. We address this question in Section
VII
.

15
acknowledged the stabilizing effect of thermal conduction at short wavelengths. From the isobaric and isentropic instability criteria, the wavenumber for which the thermal (acoustic) mode transitions from unstable (overstable) to stable is

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{therm}}=\sqrt{-N_{\mathrm{p}0}}k_{\mathrm{F}},\hskip 5.69046pt\lambda_{\mathrm{therm}}=\frac{2\pi}{k_{\mathrm{therm}}},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{(thermal mode)}

(18)

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{ac}}=\left[\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(R-1)N_{\rho 0}\right]^{1/2}k_{\mathrm{F}},\hskip 5.69046pt\lambda_{\mathrm{ac}}=\frac{2\pi}{k_{\mathrm{ac}}},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{(acoustic modes)}

(19)

see Equations (26) of
15

II.4
Linear theory perturbations

We express the density, pressure, and temperature perturbations as a fraction of their equilibrium value. Denoting the initial amplitude of the density perturbation by
A_{\rho}=A\rho_{0}
for some dimensionless constant
A
, we have, upon plugging Equation (
2
) into the linearized equations of gas dynamics
(
15
, see)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\rho(x,t)}{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}

\displaystyle=A\exp(nt+ikx),

(20)

\displaystyle\delta v(x,t)

\displaystyle=iA\frac{n}{k}\exp(nt+ikx),

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta p(x,t)}{p_{\mathrm{0}}}

\displaystyle=-A\frac{1}{c_{\mathrm{si}}^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{2}\exp(nt+ikx),

(22)

with
c_{\mathrm{si}}^{2}=p_{\mathrm{0}}/\rho_{\mathrm{0}}
the square of the isothermal sound speed.

To investigate the limit
k\rightarrow 0
one needs to write these equations in terms of the initial amplitude of the pressure perturbation,
A_{\mathrm{p}}=\tilde{A}p_{0}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\rho(x,t)}{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{A}c_{\mathrm{si}}^{2}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{2}\exp(nt+ikx),

(23)

\displaystyle\delta v(x,t)

\displaystyle=-i\tilde{A}\frac{k}{n}c_{\mathrm{si}}^{2}\exp(nt+ikx),

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta p(x,t)}{p_{\mathrm{0}}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}\exp(nt+ikx),

(25)

so that

In both formulations, the temperature perturbation is

\frac{\delta T}{T_{\mathrm{0}}}=\frac{\delta p}{p_{\mathrm{0}}}-\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{\mathrm{0}}}.

III
Short wavelength solutions (
\lambda\rightarrow 0
)

Regardless of the value of
R
, when
\lambda\rightarrow 0
the system supports one thermal mode and a pair of acoustic modes. All these modes are stable, and the dominant terms in their growth rates are independent of
N_{\rho 0}
,
N_{\mathrm{p}0}
, and
N_{\mathrm{S}0}
and depend only on
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}
, which is an indication of their stability being determined by thermal conduction alone, as expected. The growth rates given here and in Section
IV
have been obtained analytically or with the help of a computer algebra system.

III.1
Thermal mode

The growth rate of the thermal mode has real and imaginary parts given by

n_{\mathrm{R}}=-\frac{N_{\rho 0}}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}\left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)^{2},\hskip 5.69046ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=0,

(27)

where the third term in
n_{\mathrm{R}}
is the dominant one in the short wavelength limit. Now, the factor
n/k
in Equations (
21
) and (
22
) is proportional to
k
, so it diverges as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, to study this solution, we must use Equations (
23
)–(
26
) rather than Equations (
20
)–(
22
); in the limit
k\rightarrow\infty
we obtain,

\delta\rho=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta p\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta T\neq 0.

(28)

We thus see that this is an isochoric mode with no mass motions and whose pressure and temperature perturbations damp in time. It is worth emphasizing that this mode is attenuated by the effect of thermal conduction alone, yet it has no density perturbations regardless of whether the system is isochorically unstable or not for
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, that is, regardless of the sign of
N_{\rho 0}
.

III.2
Acoustic modes

For very short wavelengths, the acoustic modes have

n_{\mathrm{R}}=-\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},\hskip 56.9055ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=\pm kc_{\mathrm{si}}.

(29)

After inserting this solution into Equations (
20
)–(
22
) or (
23
)–(
25
) and Equation (
26
) we obtain

\delta\rho\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta p\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta T=0.

(30)

For
\gamma>1
, this is a pair of damped isothermal acoustic waves.

\lambda\rightarrow\infty
)

When

IV.1
Thermal modes

For all values of

n_{\mathrm{R}}=-\frac{N_{\rho 0}}{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}-\left[\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}t_{\mathrm{cool}}}+\frac{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}{N_{\rho 0}}(R-1)c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\right]k^{2},\hskip 28.45274ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=0.

(31)

When dealing with perturbations, here we face the same situation we found in Section
III.1
: for this growth rate, the factor

in Equations (
21
) and (
22
) diverges as

and we are forced to use Equations (
23
)–(
26

\delta\rho=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta p\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta T\neq 0.

(32)

Just like the thermal mode for
\lambda\rightarrow 0

) and is stable otherwise.

39

The other two thermal modes (

(33)

hence, one of these modes is unstable and the other is stable, their growth/damping rate tending to zero as

\delta\rho\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta p\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta T\neq 0.

(34)

IV.2
Acoustic modes

The two acoustic modes, that are present when
R>0
, have

n_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{cool}}}{2N_{\rho 0}}(R-1)k^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2},\hskip 5.69046ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=\pm\sqrt{R}kc_{\mathrm{s}},\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{if}\;R>0.

(35)

As noted in

R<1
and
N_{\rho 0}<0
or
R>1
and
N_{\rho 0}>0
; and they are stable otherwise. Unlike adiabatic sound waves, their phase speed is not

but
\sqrt{R}c_{\mathrm{s}}
. Furthermore, in this case, all perturbations are non-zero:

\delta\rho\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta p\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta v\neq 0,\hskip 28.45274pt\delta T\neq 0.

(36)

VII
Do truly isobaric perturbations exist?

In Sections
III
and
IV
we have seen that, under certain circumstances, truly isochoric perturbations are allowed: this happens for the thermal mode when either
\lambda\rightarrow 0
or
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. This leads us to wonder whether purely isobaric perturbations exist. According to Equation (
22
), this requires
n/k=0
. Of course, whenever
k
is finite and the growth rate of the thermal mode vanishes, the perturbations
\delta p
and
\delta v
also vanish, and the system has sinusoidal density and temperature perturbations that do not change in time. This takes place at the wavenumber
k_{\mathrm{therm}}
, given by Equation (
18
), at which the thermal mode changes from being evanescent to being amplified.

Apart from this trivial solution, all other wavelengths produce pressure perturbations. Nevertheless, a truly isobaric thermal mode exists in the absence of conduction. Indeed, if
\kappa=0
and
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, the thermal mode has the growth rate

n_{\mathrm{R}}=-\frac{N_{\mathrm{p}0}}{\gamma t_{\mathrm{cool}}},\hskip 56.9055ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=0.

(40)

Given that
n/k\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, this mode only gives rise to density and temperature perturbations, but in this case they grow in time if the system is isobarically unstable (
N_{\mathrm{p}0}<0
), otherwise they are damped. Equation (
40
) corresponds to Equation (31) of
15
. For completeness, we also give the growth rate of acoustic modes for
\kappa=0
and
\lambda\rightarrow 0
,

n_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{N_{\rho 0}}{2t_{\mathrm{cool}}}(R-1),\hskip 56.9055ptn_{\mathrm{I}}=\pm kc_{\mathrm{s}},

(41)

which corresponds to Equation (32) of
15
.

In spite of these results, a uniform and infinite plasma with thermal conduction can support a thermal mode with an
almost isobaric
perturbation. For each of the seven representative parameter values used in Figures
4
–
10
, there is a wavelength range, roughly between
0.01\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
and
0.1\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
, for which the pressure perturbation is much smaller than that of the density and temperature (all normalized to their equilibrium values). This wavelength range is the smallest in rows 3 and 7 of Table
1
, that are the two cases in which the system is isobarically stable. For shorter and longer wavelengths than those mentioned in this paragraph, the thermal mode transitions to an isochoric behavior. In general, for all seven cases of Table
1
, the thermal mode is essentially isochoric for
\lambda\lesssim 10^{-3}\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
and for
\lambda\gtrsim\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
. Of course, it is always stable if
\lambda<\lambda_{\mathrm{therm}}
and is unstable for
\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{therm}}
in all instability domains but the last in Table
1
.

VIII
Conclusions

We have revisited the linear modes of an infinite and homogeneous hydrodynamic medium, investigated by
15
and
38
. In this work we have imposed no particular form of the heating and radiative cooling processes and have only assumed Spitzer’s expression for parallel thermal conductivity. We find that the presentation of the different regimes of linear thermal instability given in Figure 2 of
38
is easier to understand than in Figure 1 of
15
. Nevertheless, the former contains an error that has been corrected (namely the relevance of the value
R=1/3
) and some necessary additions that have been included in our Table
1
(namely incorporating the wavelengths
\lambda_{\mathrm{therm}}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{ac}}
and thermal conduction). This table gives a comprehensive summary of all domains of thermal instability, making clear which modes are present in different wavelength ranges and whether they are unstable or damped. The domains of instability are separated by critical values of
R
that were derived by
38
under the assumption of vanishing Field length (
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\rightarrow 0
), or equivalently, in the limit of no thermal conduction. The physical meaning of
R
comes from its definition as the quotient
N_{\mathrm{p}0}/(\gamma N_{\rho 0})
, where
N_{\mathrm{p}0}<0
and
N_{\rho 0}<0
are a dimensionless form of the isobaric and isochoric instability criteria obtained by
15
. Including thermal conduction in the analysis implies that the critical values of
R
can shift slightly and we have shown that Table
1
and the auxiliary Figures
4
–
10
remain an excellent guide to the domains of instability of a hydrodynamic plasma for which
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\lesssim\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
. For larger values of the Field length, however, the instability domains have no simple description in terms of
R
.

Furthermore, we have presented a detailed analysis of the modes of the homogeneous hydrodynamical system studied by
15
and
38
for very short and very long wavelengths. We have also shown that, to study the perturbations caused by modes with an isochoric evolution, it is essential to express all perturbations in terms of the amplitude of the pressure perturbation instead of the amplitude of the density perturbation.
When
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, the thermal mode becomes isochoric. It has no velocity perturbation, and the temperature and pressure perturbations damp by heat exchange via thermal conduction. Moreover, there is a pair of damped isothermal acoustic modes that propagate in opposite directions at the isothermal sound speed.

Equation (16) of
31
provides an approximation to the asymptotic behavior of the growth rate of the thermal mode for small wavelength. In terms of the parameters used in this paper, this equation can be written as

n_{\mathrm{R}}=-\frac{N_{\rho 0}}{\gamma t_{\mathrm{cool}}}+\frac{c_{\mathrm{s}}}{\gamma}-\frac{1}{\gamma t_{\mathrm{cool}}}\left(\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)^{2}.

(42)

There is a clear discrepancy between this expression and our Equation (
27
), the reason being that, to derive it,
31
incorrectly assumed that the thermal mode is isobaric when
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. We have plotted the growth rate of Equation (
42
) in Figures
4
–
10
and have verified that
31
’s approximation is not too far from the red curve of the top panel for
\lambda\simeq 10^{-2}\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
, but it departs from the actual behavior of
n_{\mathrm{R}}
for smaller wavelengths. Hence, Equation (
42
) does not provide a valid estimate of
n_{\mathrm{R}}
for
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, while Equation (
27
) does.

When
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
another isochoric thermal mode is present, its stability being governed by the isochoric stability criterion. Thermal conduction plays no role in the exponential damping/growth of this mode. When the parameter
R
is negative, this thermal mode is accompanied by two additional thermal modes with growth rate
\pm\sqrt{R}c_{\mathrm{s}}

(
38
, see)
. When
R>0
, these two thermal modes are replaced by a pair of acoustic solutions with phase speed
\pm\sqrt{R}c_{\mathrm{s}}
that are stable either if
R>1
and the isochoric stability criterion is fulfilled or if
R<1
and the isochoric stability criterion is not fulfilled.

The amplitudes of the perturbed variables in the linear regime (see Equations (

26

\lambda\rightarrow\infty
(
N_{\mathrm{p}0}<0
or
N_{\rho 0}<0
) or not. On the other hand, the medium supports a thermal mode with isochoric perturbation both for
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Isochoric perturbations were discarded by
15
on the basis that “pressure variations accompanying isochoric temperature
variations will in general drive motions which destroy the constancy of density”. This sentence is concluded as follows, “so that
this criterion is incompatible with the force equation.” We have shown that this incompatibility does not exist for very small and very long wavelengths. Furthermore, if we relax the condition on the thermal mode to be
purely
isobaric or isochoric, we find that
it is
almost
isochoric for very small or very large wavelengths and is
almost
isobaric for intermediate wavelengths. For example, for
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=0.2\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
, the thermal mode is almost isochoric for
\lambda\lesssim 10^{-3}\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
and for
\lambda\gtrsim\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
and almost isobaric for
0.01\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}\lesssim\lambda\lesssim 0.1\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
. We also find that increasing
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}
results in a reduction of the range of
\lambda
for which the thermal mode is almost isobaric, together with a shift to higher wavelengths. For example, for
\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
, this range becomes
0.1\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}\lesssim\lambda\lesssim\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
for all rows of Table
1
, except rows 2 and 7, for which the thermal mode behaves almost isochorically for all wavelengths.

In a follow-up of this paper we plan to investigate the non-linear saturation regime of one-dimensional hydrodynamics in the presence of a source term in the energy equation, incorporating constant heating, radiative losses, and temperature-dependent thermal conduction. The aim is to understand the behavior that follows the excitation of the thermal mode or the acoustic modes beyond the linear phase. In addition, although thermal instability in a homogeneous plasma threaded by a uniform magnetic field has already been explored

We end his work with a discussion of the catastrophic cooling (CC) instability recently proposed by

\lambda\rightarrow\infty

Obviously, a comparison between linear thermal instability in a homogeneous medium and coronal rain formation must be taken with a grain of salt because thermal conduction plays a major role in the unperturbed, non-uniform coronal loop equilibrium and this can affect perturbations in ways that are not captured by the Field length.

Appendix A
Power-law cooling function

In the optically thin solar corona, the heat loss rate per unit volume is

\rho\Lambda(T)=n_{\mathrm{e}}n_{\mathrm{H}}Q(T),

(A1)

where
n_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron density and
n_{\mathrm{H}}
is the density of protons and hydrogen atoms. Let us assume that
Q(T)
can be expressed as a power-law function of temperature. Then,

Q(T)=\alpha T^{-\beta}.

(A2)

Assuming a fully ionized hydrogen plasma, with
n=n_{\mathrm{e}}=n_{\mathrm{H}}
,
\rho\simeq m_{\mathrm{p}}n
, and
m_{\mathrm{p}}
the proton mass, the following expressions apply,

N_{\rho 0}=-\beta,\hskip 28.45274ptN_{\mathrm{p}0}=-(\beta+2),\hskip 28.45274ptR=\frac{\beta+2}{\gamma\beta}.

(A3)

Other relevant quantities are

k_{\mathrm{F}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\kappa_{0}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{n_{0}}{T_{0}^{\beta/2+7/4}},\hskip 28.45274ptt_{\mathrm{cool}}=\frac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}}{(\gamma-1)\alpha}\frac{T_{0}^{\beta+1}}{n_{0}},

(A4)

\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=t_{\mathrm{cool}}c_{\mathrm{s}}=\left(\frac{2\gamma k_{\mathrm{B}}}{m_{\mathrm{p}}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}}{(\gamma-1)\alpha}\frac{T_{0}^{\beta+3/2}}{n_{0}},

(A5)

where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant and
T_{0}
,
n_{0}
are the unperturbed temperature and number density.
