The Lehmer complex of a Bruhat interval
Davide Bolognini, Paolo Sentinelli

Abstract
We introduce Lehmer codes for several finite Coxeter groups, including all classical Weyl groups. These codes correspond to embed products of chains in the Bruhat order, which allow us to associate to each lower Bruhat interval of these groups a multicomplex whose f f -polynomial is the Poincaré polynomial of the interval. Via a general construction, we prove that these polynomials are h h -polynomials of vertex-decomposable simplicial complexes, which we call Lehmer complexes. Moreover we provide a classification, in terms of unimodal permutations, of Poincaré polynomials of smooth Schubert varieties in flag manifolds.

Introduction

In this paper we introduce the
Lehmer complex
of a lower Bruhat interval in a finite Coxeter system. This is a shellable simplicial complex whose
h
-polynomial is the rank-generating function of the interval. For Weyl groups, Björner and Ekedahl in
[
9
]
studied these polynomials by exploiting the geometry of the underlying Schubert varieties. We develop purely combinatorial tools to deal with the shape of lower Bruhat intervals, which in principle could be applied to any finite Coxeter group.

Our definition of Lehmer complexes relies on the notion of
Lehmer code
for a finite Coxeter group and on a general construction associating to a multicomplex a shellable simplicial complex whose
h
-vector is the
f
-vector of the multicomplex. Let
(W,S)
be a finite Coxeter system and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
its exponents. We define a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
as a bijection

L:W\rightarrow[e_{1}+1]\times\ldots\times[e_{n}+1]

such that
L^{-1}
is a poset morphism between the direct product of chains
[e_{1}+1]\times\ldots\times[e_{n}+1]
and the Bruhat order
(W,\leqslant)
, see Definition
3.2
. Given a lower Bruhat interval, a Lehmer code realizes a bijection between it and an order ideal of
[e_{1}+1]\times\ldots\times[e_{n}+1]
. Since order ideals of
\N^{k}
are precisely multicomplexes, this allows us to consider lower Bruhat intervals as multicomplexes.

For a finite multicomplex
J
, Björner, Frankl and Stanley in
[
10
]
introduced a shellable simplicial complex
M(J)
, which we call
M
-complex
, such that the
h
-polynomial of
M(J)
equals the
f
-polynomial of
J
, see our Definition
2.3
. Given a Lehmer code
L
for
(W,S)
and
w\in W
, the Lehmer complex
L_{w}(W,S)
is the
M
-complex of the multicomplex
\{L(v):v\leqslant w\}
, see Definition
3.4
.
The
h
-polynomial of
L_{w}(W,S)
equals the rank-generating function of the interval
[e,w]:=\{v\in W:v\leqslant w\}
, where
e
is the identity of the group; this is stated in Theorem
3.5
. In Definition
3.1
we introduce a Lehmer complex for any finite Coxeter group; indeed, to realize such a complex for the whole group we do not need a Lehmer code. With respect to the length statistic, this is the analog of the Coxeter complex, whose
h
-polynomial is the generating function of the descent statistic.

In Theorem
5.5
we provide the following formula for the rank-generating function
h_{w}(q)
of a lower Bruhat interval
[e,w]
in terms of
q
-analogs depending on the maxima of the associated multicomplex:

\sum_{v\leqslant w}q^{\ell(v)}=\sum\limits_{\varnothing\neq X\subseteq\max\{L(v):v\leqslant w\}}(-1)^{|X|+1}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{|S|}[(\wedge_{x\in X}\,x)_{i}+1]_{q}.

By this result, we believe it is an important task to compute the maxima of the order ideal
\{L(v):v\leqslant w\}
directly from the element
w
, without complete knowledge of the interval.

The previous results hold whenever a Lehmer code for a finite Coxeter system is available. The Lehmer code for a symmetric group
S_{n}
is a well-known bijection between the group and
[2]\times\ldots\times[n]
described in terms of inversions and it is involved in the combinatorics of Schubert polynomials and their generalizations, see e.g. the paper of Fulton
[
15
]
. This notion is compatible with the Bruhat order, in the sense of Definition
3.2
and this motivates our nomenclature. For groups of type
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
, Lehmer codes were foreseen, although not explicitly, by Stanley in
[
29
, Section 7]
. In Section
4
we realize explicit Lehmer codes for types
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
D_{n}
and
H_{3}
by exploiting the Coxeter structure of the groups of these types. For these classes of Coxeter systems, by the fact that
M
-sequences coincide with
f
-vectors of multicomplexes, in Proposition
3.3
we recover part of
[
9
, Theorem E]
, extending it to type
H_{3}
. The existence of Lehmer codes for other finite irreducible Coxeter systems remains an open problem.

In Section
2.1
we study
M
-complexes, collecting the tools we need to prove the previous results. In Corollary
2.4
, we prove that every linear extension of the order ideal
J
provides a shelling of
M_{d}(J)
, recovering the fact that the rank-generating function of the order ideal
J
coincides with the
h
-polynomial of
M_{d}(J)
. Along the way (see Proposition
2.9
), we obtain that

\{\mbox{$h$-vectors of flag $M$-complexes}\}=\{\mbox{$f$-vectors of flag simplicial complexes}\}.

Since flag
M
-complexes are completely balanced and vertex-decomposable, this equality provides further evidence for a conjecture due to Costantinescu and Varbaro
[
13
, Conjecture 1.4]
.

The notion of Lehmer code
L
for a Coxeter system
(W,S)
leads us to consider a distinguished set of elements of
W
, which in Definition
5.1
we call
L
-principal elements
. Principal elements for our Lehmer codes in type
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
were already considered under the name of Kempf elements. They arise in relation to desingularizations of Schubert varieties in Grassmannians
[
22
]
and to toric degenerations of Schubert varieties in flag varieties
[
19
]
. In Definition
5.7
, we introduce
L
-unimodal elements
. Proposition
6.11
states that the unimodal elements of the Lehmer code
L_{A_{n}}
of Section
4.2
are exactly unimodal permutations. In Theorem
6.12
we prove that unimodal permutations classify Poincaré polynomials of smooth Schubert varieties in flag manifolds, namely in
S_{n}
we have

\left\{\sum_{v\leqslant w}q^{\ell(v)}:\mbox{$w$ is smooth}\right\}=\left\{\sum_{v\leqslant w}q^{\ell(v)}:\mbox{$w$ is unimodal}\right\}.

Proposition
6.13
provides an alternative classification in terms of partitions into distinct parts. Similarly, the palindromic rank-generating functions of lower Bruhat intervals in type
H_{3}
are classified by unimodal elements, specifically those associated to the Lehmer code
L_{H_{3}}
defined in Section
4.5
, see Example
5.9
.

Notation and preliminaries

In this section we fix notation and recall some definitions useful for the rest of the paper. We drop the number
0
from the set of natural numbers, i.e.
\N:=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
[n]:=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
. For a set
X
, we denote by
|X|
its cardinality, by
\mathcal{P}(X)
its power set and by
X^{n}
its
n
-th power under Cartesian product. If
x\in X^{n}
, we denote by
x_{i}
the projection of
x
onto the
i
-th factor.
The
q
-analog of
n
is a polynomial defined by
[n]_{q}:=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}q^{i}
.

Posets
. Let
(P,\leqslant)
be a finite poset. Given
x,y\in P
such that
x\leqslant y
, define the
interval

[x,y]:=\{z\in P:x\leqslant z\leqslant y\}
. A
linear extension
of
P
is a total order
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
of the elements of
P
such that
x_{i}\leqslant x_{j}
implies
i\leqslant j
, for all
i,j\in[r]
. Given two posets
(P,\leqslant_{P})
and
(Q,\leqslant_{Q})
, a function
f:P\rightarrow Q
is a
poset morphism
if
x\leqslant_{P}y
implies
f(x)\leqslant_{Q}f(y)
. The poset
(P,\leqslant)
is a
lattice
if the induced subposets
\{z\in P:z\leqslant x,z\leqslant y\}
and
\{z\in P:z\geqslant x,z\geqslant y\}
have a unique maximum
x\wedge y
and a unique minimum
x\vee y
, respectively. Recall that the Cartesian product of posets is a poset under the componentwise order. A set
J\subseteq P
is an
order ideal
of
P
if
x\in J
and
y\leqslant x
implies
y\in J
. We say that
x\leqslant y
is a
covering relation

x\vartriangleleft y
if
|[x,y]|=2
. A
chain

x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}
of rank
n
in
P
is
saturated
if
x_{0}\vartriangleleft x_{1}\vartriangleleft\ldots\vartriangleleft x_{n}
. A saturated chain
x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}
not contained in other chains is called
maximal
. If all maximal chains have the same rank
n
, the poset
P
is said to be
graded
of rank
n
. In a graded poset
P
, the
rank function

\rho:P\rightarrow\N\cup\{0\}
is defined by setting
\rho(x)
equal to the rank of the order ideal
\{y\in P:y\leqslant x\}
, for every
x\in P
. The
rank-generating function
or
Poincaré polynomial
of
P
is the polynomial
\sum_{x\in P}q^{\rho(x)}
.

Simplicial complexes and multicomplexes
. Let
V
be a finite set. A
simplicial complex on a set of vertices
V
is an order ideal
X\neq\varnothing
of
(\mathcal{P}(V),\subseteq)
, whose elements are called
faces
. The maximal elements of
X
are called
facets
. It is clear that we can identify a simplicial complex with the set of its facets. The
dimension
of
X
is
\mathrm{max}\{|F|-1:F\in X\}
. A simplicial complex is
pure of dimension
d
if all its facets have the same cardinality
d+1
.

The
f
-vector

(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d},f_{d+1})
of a
d
-dimensional simplicial complex
X
is defined by

f_{i}:=|\{F\in X:|F|=i\}|,

for every
0\leqslant i\leqslant d+1
. The
h
-polynomial

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}h_{i}x^{i}
of a
d
-dimensional simplicial complex
X
is defined by the equality

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}h_{i}x^{d+1-i}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+1}f_{i}(x-1)^{d+1-i}.

The rank-generating function
\sum_{F\in X}q^{|F|}
is the
f
-polynomial
of
X
. The
h
-vector
of
X
is
(h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{d+1})
.

Given a vertex
v
of
X
, recall that the
link
of
v
is the simplicial complex
\mathrm{link}_{X}(v):=\{F\setminus\{v\}:F\in X,v\in F\}
and the
deletion
of
v
is the simplicial complex
\mathrm{del}_{X}(v):=\{F\in X:v\notin F\}.

A pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex is

•

thin
if every face of cardinality
d
is contained in exactly two facets. It is
subthin
if it is not thin and every face of cardinality
d
is contained in at most two facets.

•

shellable
if there exists a
shelling order
, i.e. a linear order
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}
on its facets such that for every
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r
there exist
v\in F_{j}\setminus F_{i}
and
h<j
such that
F_{j}=(F_{h}\cap F_{j})\cup\{v\}
. The
restriction
of a facet
F_{i}
is defined by
\mathcal{R}(F_{i}):=\{v\in F_{i}:F_{i}\setminus\{v\}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathcal{P}(F_{j})\}.
Recall that for a shellable complex the
h
-vector satisfies
h_{j}=|\{i\in[r]:|\mathcal{R}(F_{i})|=j\}|
, for all
0\leqslant j\leqslant d+1
. In particular the
h
-vector of a shellable complex is non-negative.

•

vertex-decomposable
if it is a simplex (possibly empty) or if there exists a vertex
v\in X
satisfying the following properties:

1.

\mathrm{del}_{X}(v)
is pure of dimension
d
, i.e.
v
is a
shedding vertex
;

2.

\mathrm{link}_{X}(v)
is vertex-decomposable;

3.

\mathrm{del}_{X}(v)
is vertex-decomposable.

•

balanced of type
a\in(\N\cup\{0\})^{k}
, where
k\in\N
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=d+1
, if there exists a partition
V=S_{1}\cup\ldots\cup S_{k}
such that for every facet
F
of
X
we have
|F\cap S_{i}|=a_{i}
, for every
i\in[k]
. Notice that our definition is slightly different from the definition given in
[
32
, Chapter 3]
, because we allow the case
a_{i}=0
. It is
completely balanced
if it is balanced of type
(1,\ldots,1)
.

Let
k\in\N
. A
multicomplex
is an order ideal of
\N^{k}
. A simplicial complex is a finite multicomplex, considered as order ideal of
[2]^{k}
. The
f
-polynomial
(or
f
-series if the multicomplex is infinite) of a multicomplex is its rank-generating function. The
f
-
vector
of a multicomplex is the tuple of coefficients of its
f
-polynomial and it is called
M
-sequence
. A numerical characterization of
M
-sequences, which coincide with Hilbert functions of a standard graded
K
-algebra, was proved by Macaulay, see
[
32
, Theorem 2.2]
. Moreover, finite
M
-sequences coincide with
h
-vectors of pure shellable simplicial complexes, see
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Finite Coxeter systems
. A finite
Coxeter system

(W,S)
is a finite group
W
generated by a set
S
of involutions, and relations
(st)^{m(s,t)}=e
, for all
s,t\in S
, where
e
is the identity in
W
and
m:S\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is the so-called Coxeter matrix. For the classification of finite Coxeter systems we refer to
[
8
, Appendix A1]
.

Given
X,Y\subseteq W
and
w\in W
, we let
XY:=\{xy:x\in X,\,y\in Y\}\subseteq W
and
wX:=\{wx:x\in X\}\subseteq W
. Analogously we define
Xw
.

We denote by
\ell:W\rightarrow\N\cup\{0\}
the
length function
. For
X\subseteq W
, we let
X(q):=\sum_{x\in X}q^{\ell(x)}
. For any
J\subseteq S
, the subgroup generated by
J
is denoted by
W_{J}
and we set

{}^{J}W:=\left\{w\in W:\ell(sw)>\ell(w),\,\forall\,s\in J\right\}.

Any element
w\in W
factorizes uniquely as
w=w_{J}({{}^{J}w})
, where
{{}^{J}w}\in{{}^{J}W}
,
w_{J}\in W_{J}
and
\ell(w)=\ell(w_{J})+\ell({{}^{J}w})
. Given
I\subseteq J\subseteq S
, we set

{}^{I}W_{J}:=\left\{w\in W_{J}:\ell(sw)>\ell(w),\,\forall\,s\in I\right\}.

One of the most important features of a Coxeter group is a natural partial order
\leqslant
on it, called
Bruhat order
. It can be defined by the subword property (see
[
8
, Chapter 2]
). The Bruhat order is a refinement of the so-called
weak orders
, see
[
8
, Chapter 3]
. We denote by
\leqslant_{L}
the left weak order, and by
\leqslant_{R}
the right one. The posets
(W,\leqslant_{L})
,
(W,\leqslant_{R})
and
(W,\leqslant)
are graded with rank function
\ell
. Their minimum is the identity
e
. We denote by
w_{0}
their maximum and by
w_{0}(J)
the maximum of
W_{J}
. The
exponents
of a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
are the uniquely determined positive integers
e_{1},\ldots,e_{|S|}
such that
W(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{|S|}[e_{i}+1]_{q}
.

The
symmetric group
of permutations of
n
objects is denoted by
S_{n}
. A permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
can be written in one line notation as
\sigma(1)\sigma(2)\ldots\sigma(n)
. The group
S_{n}
is a Coxeter group; its standard Coxeter presentation has generators
S=\left\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\right\}
, where
s_{i}
is the permutation
12\ldots(i+1)i\ldots n
, for all
i\in[n-1]
. The element of maximal length is
w_{0}=n(n-1)\ldots 21
. We say that a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}

avoids the pattern

\tau\in S_{k}
, with
k\leqslant n
, if there are no
1\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}\leqslant n
such that
\sigma(i_{\tau^{-1}(1)})<\sigma(i_{\tau^{-1}(2)})<\ldots<\sigma(i_{\tau^{-1}(k)})
. Recall that the Bruhat order on
S_{n}
represents the inclusions between Schubert varieties in the flag variety. It is well-known that smooth Schubert varieties correspond to permutations avoiding the patterns
3412
and
4231
.

2.1
Multicomplexes and M-complexes

In this section, given a finite multicomplex, we study a vertex-decomposable simplicial complex, which we call
M
-complex, whose
h
-vector equals the
f
-vector of the multicomplex. This construction is a special case of those introduced by Björner, Frankl and Stanley in
[
10
, Section 5]
to prove the equivalence between finite
M
-sequences and
h
-vectors of shellable simplicial complexes. The same construction was further explored by Murai in
[
25
]
. Our presentation is different from the previous ones and more convenient for our purposes.
M
-complexes, together with a Lehmer code when available, provide Lehmer complexes, as we show in subsequent sections. Our approach highlights the fact that the
h
-polynomial of a Lehmer complex realizes the generating function of the length statistic of a lower Bruhat interval similarly to the way in which
h
-polynomials of Coxeter complexes realize the generating functions of the descent statistic of an interval in the weak order.

Let
k\in\N
and
d:=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\N^{k}
. We shall define a pure simplicial complex
M_{d}
of dimension

\mathrm{dim}(M_{d})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}-k-1

with number of facets equal to
\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
.
For
x\in[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
we define a set

F_{x}:=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left([d_{i}]\setminus\{d_{i}+1-x_{i}\}\right)\times\{i\}.

(1)

For example, if
k=3
,
d_{1}=d_{2}=3
and
d_{3}=4
, we have that

F_{(1,2,2)}=\{(1,1),(2,1),(1,2),(3,2),(1,3),(2,3),(4,3)\}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k\in\N
and
d\in\N^{k}
. We call the simplicial complex

M_{d}:=\left\{F_{x}:x\in[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]\right\}

the
M
-complex of degrees

d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
.

Notice that if
d=(1,1,\ldots,1)
then
M_{d}=\{\varnothing\}
.
We call the numbers
e_{i}:=d_{i}-1
the
exponents
of
M_{d}
, for all
i\in[k]
. Clearly the simplicial complex of Definition
2.1
is pure, balanced of type
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})
, and it has the announced dimension and number of facets.

We consider the componentwise order on the set
[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
. Its rank function is denoted by
\tilde{\rho}:[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]\rightarrow\N\cup\{0\}
, i.e.
x\mapsto\tilde{\rho}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}-1)
. Similarly to Coxeter complexes, in the next result we prove that all linear extensions of this poset are shellings for the
M
-complex
M_{d}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
M_{d}
be the
M
-complex of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
and
\omega:=\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
the number of facets of
M_{d}
. If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\omega}
is a linear extension of
[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
, then
F_{x_{1}},\ldots,F_{x_{\omega}}
is a shelling order for
M_{d}
.

Proof.

If
k=1
the result is straightforward from the definition of
M_{d}
. So assume
k>1
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\omega}
be a linear extension of
[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
; in particular
x_{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
. Let
i,j\in[\omega]
be such that
1\leqslant i<j
.
Then we have that either
x_{i}<x_{j}
or there exist
a,b\in[k]
such that
(x_{i})_{a}>(x_{j})_{a}
and
(x_{i})_{b}<(x_{j})_{b}
. In both cases there exists
h\in[k]
such that
(x_{i})_{h}<(x_{j})_{h}
. Notice that
(d_{h}+1-(x_{i})_{h},h)\in F_{x_{j}}\setminus F_{x_{i}}
. Let
z:=((x_{j})_{1},\ldots,(x_{j})_{h-1},(x_{i})_{h},(x_{j})_{h+1},\ldots,(x_{j})_{k})
. Since
(x_{i})_{h}<(x_{j})_{h}
, we have that

; hence there exists

such that

.
Moreover
F_{x_{j}}\setminus F_{x_{r}}=\{(d_{h}+1-(x_{i})_{h},h)\}
. This proves our statement.
∎

The simplicial complexes of the next definition are, mutatis mutandis, the ones introduced in
[

, Section 5]

Definition 2.3
.

Let
k\in\N
and

. For any subset
\varnothing\neq J\subseteq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
, the

-complex of

, where the facet

is defined in (

).

Recall that multicomplexes are precisely order ideals of

, for some

. Hence, finite multicomplexes are order ideals of
[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
, for some
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. In the next result we prove that the

-polynomial of

is the rank-generating function of the order ideal
J
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\varnothing\neq J\subseteq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
be an order ideal. Then any linear extension of
J
provides a shelling order of

. Moreover, the polynomial

\sum_{x\in J}q^{\tilde{\rho}(x)}
is the

.

Proof.

Since order ideals provide partial linear extensions, the first part is an immediate consequence of Theorem

. The second part of the statement is essentially

, Theorem 1.14 (i)]
.∎

Let

. It is easy to see that the complex

is thin if and only if

Corollary 2.5

Let

is

, if

;

2.

the ball

It is well-known that a vertex-decomposable simplicial complex is shellable. If

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\varnothing\neq J\subseteq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
be an order ideal. Then
M_{d}(J)
is vertex-decomposable.

By the previous results and the characterization of
M
-sequences as
f
-vectors of multicomplexes, non-zero finite
M
-sequences are exactly the
h
-vectors of
M
-complexes. More precisely, the following refinement of the well-known equivalence between finite
M
-sequences and
h
-vectors of shellable simplicial complexes holds (see
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
). Notice that this refinement is implicit in Murai’s work
[
25
]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Given a polynomial
P\neq 0
, the following are equivalent:

\mathrm{1)}

the coefficients of
P
form an
M
-sequence.

\mathrm{2)}

P
is the
h
-polynomial of a vertex-decomposable simplicial ball.

Proof.

If
P=1
the result is trivial. So assume
P\neq 1
.
It is clear that
\mathrm{2)}
implies
\mathrm{1)}
, since a vertex-decomposable simplicial complex is shellable.
Now we prove the other implication. Let
P
be a polynomial whose coefficients form an
M
-sequence. It is the
f
-polynomial of a finite multicomplex, i.e. the rank-generating function of an order ideal
J\subsetneq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
for some
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
. Consider the
M
-complex
M_{d}(J)
. By Corollary
2.4
, the
h
-polynomial of
M_{d}(J)
is
P
. By construction
M_{d}(J)
is balanced of type
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})
and by Proposition
2.6
it is vertex-decomposable. Since
P\neq 1
,
|J|>1
. By Corollary
2.5
,
M_{d}(J)
is a simplicial ball, because
J\neq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
.
∎

From the proof of the previous proposition, it is clear that in the case of simplicial complexes, i.e. order ideals in
[2]^{k}
,
M_{d}(J)
is a completely balanced simplicial complex.

Corollary 2.8
.

The
f
-polynomial of a simplicial complex is the
h
-polynomial of a
completely balanced vertex-decomposable simplicial ball.

Recall that a simplicial complex, i.e. an order ideal
J\subseteq[2]^{k}
, is
flag
if either
J=[2]^{k}
or
\tilde{\rho}(x)\leqslant 2
for all
x\in\min([2]^{k}\setminus J)
. We recover
[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
thanks to Corollary
2.8
and the following proposition.

Proposition 2.9
.

The simplicial complex
M_{d}(J)
is flag if and only if
J
is a flag simplicial complex.

Proof.

First of all we prove that if
J
is a flag simplicial complex then
M_{d}(J)
is flag. By our assumption we have that
J\subseteq[2]^{k}
is an order ideal. Let
G:=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{h},j_{h})\}
be a non-face of
M_{d}(J)
, where
j_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant j_{h}
and
i_{t}\in[2]
for all
t\in[h]
and
h\geqslant 3
. For every
j\in[k]
the set
X_{j}:=\{(1,j),(2,j)\}
is a non-face of
M_{d}(J)
by construction. If
X_{j}\subseteq G
for some
j\in[k]
, then
G
is not minimal. So assume
X_{j}\not\subseteq G
for all
j\in[k]
; this implies
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{h}
. Let
x\in[2]^{k}
such that
x_{j_{t}}=i_{t}
if
t\in[h]
and
x_{j}=1
for
j\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{h}\}
. Since
G\subseteq F_{x}
, then
F_{x}
is not a facet of
M_{d}(J)
, i.e.
x\notin J
. Since
J
is flag, there exists
y\leqslant x
,
y\notin J
such that the set
A:=\{i\in[k]:y_{i}=2\}
satisfies
1\leqslant|A|\leqslant 2
. It is clear that the set
\{(2,t):t\in A\}
is contained in
G
and it is not a face of
M_{d}(J)
, because otherwise there exists
z\in J
such that
\{(2,t):t\in A\}\subseteq F_{z}
and this implies
y\leqslant z
and
y\in J
. It follows that every non-face of
M_{d}(J)
of cardinality
\geqslant 3
is not minimal, i.e.
M_{d}(J)
is flag.

Let
\varnothing\neq J\subseteq[d_{1}]\times\ldots\times[d_{k}]
. We prove now that if
M_{d}(J)
is flag then
J
is a flag simplicial complex. Consider
m_{i}:=\max\{x_{i}:x\in J\}
. Notice that, for all
i\in[k]
, the set
\{(d_{i}+1-m_{i},i),\ldots,(d_{i},i)\}
is a minimal non-face of
M_{d}(J)
by construction. Hence, since
M_{d}(J)
is flag, we have that
m_{i}\leqslant 2
, for all
i\in[k]
, i.e.
J
is a simplicial complex. We need only to prove that
J
is flag. If
\max(J)=\{(2,\ldots,2)\}
we have nothing to prove. Then assume that
\max(J)\neq\{(2,\ldots,2)\}
. Let
x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
be a minimal non-face of
J
and let
h:=|\{i\in[k]:x_{i}=2\}|
. Notice that
G:=\{(d_{i_{1}},i_{1}),\ldots,(d_{i_{h}},i_{h})\}
is a non-face of
M_{d}(J)
, where
\{i\in[k]:x_{i}=2\}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{h}\}
and
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{h}
. Indeed, if it is a face, there exists
z\in J
such that
G\subseteq F_{z}
and then
z\geqslant x
, but
x\notin J
. It is also clear that
G
is minimal, by the minimality of
x
. It follows that
h\leqslant 2
and this concludes the proof.
∎

By Proposition
2.9
we obtain the following equality, which supports
[
13
, Conjecture 1.4]
:

\{\mbox{$h$-vectors of flag $M$-complexes}\}=\{\mbox{$f$-vectors of flag simplicial complexes}\}.

3
Lehmer complexes of lower Bruhat intervals

In this section we introduce Lehmer codes for finite Coxeter groups and we study the shape of their lower Bruhat intervals whenever a Lehmer code is available.

By taking advantage of Definition
2.1
, for a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
we introduce a shellable simplicial complex whose
h
-polynomial coincides with the rank-generating function of the group
W
with the Bruhat order.

Definition 3.1
.

The Lehmer complex
L(W,S)
of a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
with exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is the simplicial complex
M_{d}
, where
d:=(e_{1}+1,\ldots,e_{n}+1)
.

It is well-known that the rank-generating function of the poset
(W,\leqslant)
is
\prod_{i=1}^{n}[e_{i}+1]_{q}
. Then, by Corollary
2.4
, it equals the
h
-polynomial of the Lehmer complex of
(W,S)
.

It is worth to mention that Lehmer complexes are the analogues of Coxeter complexes with respect to the length statistic: both are shellable complexes and all the linear extensions of their underlying poset (the weak order in the case of the Coxeter complex) provide shelling orders, see
[
7
]
.

The Lehmer codes of permutations provide a bijection between
S_{n}
and
[1]\times\ldots\times[n]
. Such a bijective function is a poset morphism between
S_{n}
with the left weak order and
[1]\times\ldots\times[n]
ordered componentwise, see
[
14
, Lemma 2.4]
. Moreover, it holds also that the inverse of this function is a poset morphism, whenever
S_{n}
is endowed with the Bruhat order. This is stated in Corollary
4.3
. In this section we extend the notion of Lehmer code to finite Coxeter groups. We recall that the relation
\leqslant
on
W
is the Bruhat order.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(W,S)
be a finite Coxeter system with exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
. A bijective function
L:W\rightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{0,1,\ldots,e_{i}\}
is a
Lehmer code
if

L^{-1}:\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\{0,1,\ldots,e_{i}\}\rightarrow(W,\leqslant)

is order preserving.

If
L
is a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
and
n:=|S|
, then necessarily

\ell(w)=\rho(L(w))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(w)_{i},

where
\rho
is the rank function of the poset
(\N\cup\{0\})^{n}
.
For
w\in W
we define

h_{w}(q):=\sum_{v\leqslant w}q^{\ell(v)},

the rank-generating function of the interval
[e,w]
, and

J_{w}:=\{L(v)+(1,\ldots,1):v\leqslant w\}.

For all finite Coxeter groups with a Lehmer code we recover in a purely combinatorial way a result due to Björner and Ekedahl, see
[
9
, Theorem E]
. They prove it for finite Weyl groups as a consequence of the geometry of their Schubert varieties.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
L
be a Lehmer code for a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
. Then
the coefficients of
h_{w}(q)
form an
M
-sequence, for all
w\in W
.

Proof.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be the exponents of
(W,S)
.
Since the inverse of a Lehmer code is a poset morphism,
the set
J_{w}
is an order ideal of
[e_{1}+1]\times\ldots\times[e_{n}+1]
, i.e. a multicomplex. Clearly
h_{w}(q)
equals the
f
-polynomial of
J_{w}
and then its coefficients form an
M
-sequence.
∎

In view of the next section, the previous result applies to Coxeter systems of types
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
D_{n}
and
H_{3}
.

Now we introduce a simplicial complex whose
h
-polynomial equals the rank-generating function of the lower Bruhat interval
[e,w]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
L
be a Lehmer code for a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
with exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
; for
w\in W
the Lehmer complex
L_{w}(W,S)
of
[e,w]
is the simplicial complex
M_{d}(J_{w})
, where
d:=(e_{1}+1,\ldots,e_{n}+1)
.

In view of the analogy between the complex
L(W,S)
and the Coxeter complex of
(W,S)
, the shellable complex
L_{w}(W,S)
is an analogue of a Coxeter cone as introduced in
[
33
]
. The next result is an immediate consequence of all the machinery developed up to this point.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
L
be a Lehmer code for a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
and
w\in W
. Then the
h
-polynomial of the simplicial complex
L_{w}(W,S)
is equal to
h_{w}(q)
.

Proof.

Since
\ell(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(w)_{i}=\rho(L(w))
, for all
w\in W
, by Corollary
2.4
the result follows.
∎

4
Lehmer codes for some finite Coxeter groups

In this section we construct several Lehmer codes for some finite irreducible Coxeter systems. In general the reducible case can be traced back to the irreducible one in view of the following observation. If
(W,S)\simeq(W_{1},S_{1})\times(W_{2},S_{2})
, and
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
are Lehmer codes for
(W_{1},S_{1})
and
(W_{2},S_{2})
, respectively, then the assignment

w\mapsto L(w)=(L_{1}(w_{1})_{1},\ldots,L_{1}(w_{1})_{|S_{1}|},L_{2}(w_{2})_{1},\ldots,L_{2}(w_{2})_{|S_{2}|})

defines a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
, where
w=w_{1}w_{2}\in W
,
w_{1}\in W_{1}
and
w_{2}\in W_{2}
.

While a rank-preserving bijection
W\simeq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{0,1,\ldots,e_{i}\}
exists for all finite Coxeter systems, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
are the exponents of
(W,S)
, the additional condition to be a poset morphism is not fulfilled by all such bijections.

Remark 4.1
.

The existence of Lehmer codes for classical Weyl groups, although in different terms, was already foreseen by R. Stanley in
[
29
, Section 7]
.
In
[
6
]
, S. Billey has proved that, in type
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
, any lower Bruhat interval with palindromic Poincaré polynomial admits an immersed product of chains, i.e. it has a “Lehmer code”, see
[
6
, Corollary 1.3]
. We checked that this property holds also in type
H_{3}
.

Given a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
, one can construct directly several other Lehmer codes. Indeed, it is clear that if
L
is a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
and
f
is an automorphism of the poset
(W,\leqslant)
, then
L\circ f
is a Lehmer code for
(W,S)
. In the dihedral system
I_{2}(m)
, the automorphism group of the Bruhat order is isomorphic to
\Z_{2}^{m-1}
. In all other cases the automorphism group of
(W,\leqslant)
can be described in terms of the automorphism group of the Coxeter graph of
(W,S)
and the inversion automorphism
w\mapsto w^{-1}
, see
[
9
, Section 2.3]
and references therein. Denote by
L^{*}
the
dual of the Lehmer code
L
, which we define by setting

L^{*}(w):=L(w^{-1}),

for all
w\in W
. This is a Lehmer code by the previous discussion.

One of the main tools for our constructions is the machinery described in
[
8
, Section 7.1]
to factorize the Poincaré polynomial of
(W,S)
. We establish here some useful notation. Let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and
w\in W
. As proved in
[
8
, Corollary 2.4.6]
(see also
[
8
, Proposition 3.4.2]
)
we have a factorization

w=w^{(1)}w^{(2)}\cdots w^{(n)},

such that
w^{(i)}\in{{}^{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1}\}}W_{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i}\}}}
, for all
i\in[n]
, and
\ell(w)=\ell(w^{(1)})+\ldots+\ell(w^{(n)})
. Then the Poincaré polynomial of
(W,S)
factorizes as

W(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{{}^{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1}\}}W_{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i}\}}}(q).

For the notation on irreducible Coxeter systems we follow
[
8
, Appendix A1]
, sometimes with a different linear order on the generators. We also observe that for every
u,v\in W
, if
u^{(i)}\leqslant v^{(i)}
for all
i\in[n]
then, by the subword property of the Bruhat order,
u=u^{(1)}\cdots u^{(n)}\leqslant v^{(1)}\cdots v^{(n)}=v
. This is crucial for the construction of some Lehmer codes. In the following subsections, for sake of clarity, we adopt the same notation for the type of a Coxeter system and the group defining it.

4.1
Lehmer codes for type
I_{2}(m)

Let
m\in\{3,4,\ldots\}
.
The Coxeter system
(W,S)=I_{2}(m)
has two generators
s,t
which satisfy the relation
(st)^{m}=e
. The Poincaré polynomial factorizes as
W(q)=W_{\{s\}}(q)\cdot{{}^{\{s\}}W}(q)=[2]_{q}[m]_{q}
.
Let

L_{I_{2}(m)}:W\rightarrow\{0,1\}\times\{0,\ldots,m-1\}

be defined by setting
L_{I_{2}(m)}(w)=(\ell(w^{(1)}),\ell(w^{(2)}))
, for all
w\in W
.
Then the function
L_{I_{2}(m)}
is a Lehmer code for
I_{2}(m)
.
It is straightforward to check that there are
2^{m-1}
distinct Lehmer codes for
I_{2}(m)
and they are all obtained by composing
L_{I_{2}(m)}
with an automorphism of
(W,\leqslant)
.

4.2
A Lehmer code for type
A_{n}

The Poincaré polynomial for Coxeter systems
(A_{n},S)
of type
A_{n}
factorizes as
A_{n}(q)=[2]_{q}[3]_{q}\cdots[n+1]_{q}
. The quotients
{{}^{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1}\}}(A_{n})_{\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{i}\}}}
with the Bruhat order are chains, for all
i\in[n]
. Let

L_{A_{n}}:A_{n}\rightarrow\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\{0,\ldots,i\},

be defined by
L_{A_{n}}(w)=(\ell(w^{(1)}),\ldots,\ell(w^{(n)}))
, for all
w\in A_{n}
. It is clear that
the function
L_{A_{n}}
is a Lehmer code for type
A_{n}
. For example, if
w=s_{2}s_{1}s_{3}s_{2}
then
L_{A_{3}}(w)=(0,2,2)
; indeed
w^{(1)}=e
,
w^{(2)}=s_{2}s_{1}
and
w^{(3)}=s_{3}s_{2}
.

We now describe the Lehmer code
L_{A_{n-1}}
in terms of inversions. Consider the symmetric group
S_{n}
as a Coxeter group of type
A_{n-1}
.
Let

L_{n}(w):=(0,L_{A_{n-1}}(w)_{1},\ldots,L_{A_{n-1}}(w)_{n-1}),

(2)

for all
w\in S_{n}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Then

L_{n}(w)_{k}=|\{i\in[n]:i<k,\,w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)\}|,

for all
k\in[n]
,
w\in S_{n}
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
n
. If
n=2
the result is trivial. Assume
n>2
. If
w(n)=n
, we have that
L_{n}(w)_{n}=0=|\{i\in[n-1]:w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(n)\}|
and, by our inductive hypothesis,
L_{n}(w)_{k}=|\{i\in[k-1]:w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)\}|
, for all
k<n
.

Let
a:=w^{-1}(n)<n
, and
\widetilde{w}:=w^{(1)}\cdots w^{(n-2)}
. Then, for
k<n
, by the inductive hypothesis we have that

\displaystyle L_{n}(w)_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{n-1}(\widetilde{w})_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{i\in[k-1]:\widetilde{w}^{-1}(i)>\widetilde{w}^{-1}(k)\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{i\in[k-1]:w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(k)\}|,

since
w^{(n-1)}=s_{n-1}s_{n-2}\cdots s_{a}
and then

w=\widetilde{w}(1)\ldots\widetilde{w}(a-1)\,n\,\widetilde{w}(a)\ldots\widetilde{w}(n-1).

Moreover we have that

\displaystyle L_{n}(w)_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell(w)-\ell(\widetilde{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{i\in[n-1]:w^{-1}(i)>w^{-1}(n)\}|

and this concludes the proof.
∎

For
n\geqslant 3
, we denote by
\iota_{n}
the non-trivial automorphism of the Coxeter graph of
A_{n-1}
. If
n=2
,
\iota_{n}
is the identity. If
w_{0}
is the element of maximal length in
S_{n}
, then
\iota_{n}(w)=w_{0}ww_{0}
, for all
w\in S_{n}
.
In the following corollary we recover what is known as
Lehmer code of a permutation
, see e.g.
[
23
, Section 11.3]
and
[
24
, Chapter 2]
. It is worth to mention that the Lehmer code of a permutation provides the leading term of the corresponding Schubert polynomial, see
[
15
]
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
.
Then

|\{j\in[n]:j>i,\,w(j)<w(i)\}|=(L_{n}\circ\iota_{n})^{*}(w)_{n-i+1},

for all
i\in[n]
,
w\in S_{n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
we obtain

\displaystyle L_{n}(w_{0}w^{-1}w_{0})_{n-i+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{j\in[n-i]:(w_{0}ww_{0})(j)>(w_{0}ww_{0})(n-i+1)\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{j\in[n-i]:w(i)>w(n-j+1)\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{j\in[n]:j>i,\,w(i)>w(j)\}|.

We have that

(L_{n}\circ\iota_{n})^{*}(w)=(L_{n}\circ\iota_{n})(w^{-1})=L_{n}(w_{0}w^{-1}w_{0})
and the result follows.
∎

By Corollary
4.3
, what is known as the Lehmer code satisfies the condition of Definition
3.2
and then it is a Lehmer code in our sense.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

. The function

5

Given a Lehmer code
L
of a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
, we
introduce a partial order
\Lh

W
by setting

u\Lh v\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,L(u)\leqslant L(v),

for all
u,v\in W
. For
u\Lh v
, we let
[u,v]_{\mathrm{Lh}}:=\{z\in W:u\Lh z\Lh v\}
.
We have that
[u,v]_{\mathrm{Lh}}
is a subposet of
[u,v]
. It is natural to study the elements
w\in W
for which
[e,w]_{\mathrm{Lh}}=[e,w]
as sets. For these elements, it is clear that the rank-generating function of
[e,w]
factorizes as a product of
q
-analogs involving the entries of their Lehmer codes. Hence we define a distinguished set of elements of
W
, which we call
L
-principal
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
L
be a Lehmer code for a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
. An element
w
is
L
-principal
if

[e,w]_{\mathrm{Lh}}=[e,w]
as sets.

We denote by
\Pr(L)
the set of
L
-principal elements in
W
. Notice that the poset
(W,\Lh)
is a lattice. The lattice operations are the ones induced by the lattice
\imm(L)
, i.e.

u\curlywedge v=L^{-1}(L(u)\wedge L(v)),\,\,\,u\curlyvee v=L^{-1}(L(u)\vee L(v)),

for all
u,v\in W
. It turns out that for any Lehmer code,
\Pr(L)
is also a lattice.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(W,S)
be a finite Coxeter system with a Lehmer code

. Then the poset
\Pr(L)
is a lattice.

Proof.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}\in\Pr(L)
,

a:=a_{1}\curlywedge a_{2}
and
w\leqslant a
.
We have that
a\Lh a_{i}
, for all

. Since
a_{i}\in\Pr(L)
, we have that
a\leqslant a_{i}
, for all
i\in[2]
. Hence
w\leqslant a_{i}
, for all
i\in[2]
, i.e.
w\Lh a_{i}
, for all
i\in[2]

L
-principality of

. We have proved that
w\Lh a
for all
w\leqslant a
, i.e.

, and this concludes the proof.
∎

In this section we consider the symmetric group
S_{n}
with its standard Coxeter presentation. For the Lehmer code
L_{A_{n-1}}
, principal elements are exactly Kempf elements in type
A_{n-1}
and these are the permutations in

avoiding the pattern

is the set of unimodal permutations in

. The main result of this section is the classification of Poincaré polynomials of smooth Schubert varieties in complex flag manifolds. These are realized by the rank-generating functions for lower Bruhat intervals of unimodal permutations, as we prove in Theorem
6.12
.

Let
P(n)
be the set of partitions of a non-negative integer
n
. To write a partition we adopt French notation. Then an element
\lambda\in P(n)
is either
(\,)
, if
n=0
, or a tuple
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
, where
0<\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{r}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}=n
, if
n>0
. Given a partition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})\in P(n)
, we define
[\lambda]:=[r]
and, given
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
m_{k}(\lambda):=|\{i\in[r]:\lambda_{i}=k\}|
.
We denote by
\lambda^{*}
the
dual partition
(or conjugate partition), i.e. the unique partition determined by the condition
m_{k}(\lambda^{*}):=\lambda_{r-k+1}-\lambda_{r-k}
, for all
k\in[r]
, where
\lambda_{0}:=0
. The partition
(\,)\in P(0)
is self-dual.

The set of
smooth permutations
in
S_{n}
is defined by setting

\Sm_{n}:=\{w\in S_{n}:\mbox{$w$ avoids $3412$ and $4231$}\}.

It is known that
w\in\Sm_{n}
implies

h_{w}(q)=\sum\limits_{v\leqslant w}q^{\ell(v)}=\prod\limits_{i\in[E(w)]}[E(w)_{i}+1]_{q},

where
E:\Sm_{n}\rightarrow\bigcup_{k\geqslant 0}P(k)
is the function which assigns to any element
w\in\Sm_{n}
its
exponents
(see
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
). On other hand, if
h_{w}(q)
is product of
q
-analogs then
w
is smooth.
To define
E(w)
, following
[

, Remark 2.8]
, we need some preparation.

For

define a poset
P_{w}=([n],\leqslant_{w})
by setting
i<_{w}j
if and only if
i<j
and
w^{-1}(j)<w^{-1}(i)
, for all
i,j\in[n]
. The comparability graph of the poset

is known as a

. For a finite poset

of saturated chains of

its rank function and define

, for all

, and

. Proposition

allows us to define a partition into distinct parts for any smooth permutation. Let

be the poset on

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
P
be a poset whose Hasse diagram is a forest. If
P
avoids
Q
as induced subposet then
\hat{\rho}_{h}>\hat{\rho}_{h+1}
, for all
0\leqslant h\leqslant m_{P}

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the result for trees. If
m_{P}=0
the result is trivial. The condition
m_{P}>0
implies
|P|>1
.
We proceed by induction on
|P|
. If
|P|=2
then
m_{P}=1
and
\hat{\rho}_{0}=2>1=\hat{\rho}_{1}
. Let
|P|>2
. We claim that there exists a leaf
x\in P
belonging to a unique maximal saturated chain.
For
p\in P
define
p^{\vartriangleleft}:=\{z\in P:p\vartriangleleft z\}
and
p^{\vartriangleright}:=\{z\in P:p\vartriangleright z\}
. Notice that, since
P
avoids
Q
,
\{p\in P:|p^{\vartriangleleft}|>1\}\cap\{p\in P:|p^{\vartriangleright}|>1\}=\varnothing
.
Consider the induced subposet of
P
on the set
X=\{p\in P:|p^{\vartriangleleft}|>1\}\uplus\{p\in P:|p^{\vartriangleright}|>1\}

X=\varnothing
then the claim is clearly true because in this case
P
is a chain. Assume

. Then
X
is a forest, being an induced subposet of a tree which avoids
Q
. Let
y\in X
be a leaf of the forest
X
. Since
y
is a leaf of the forest
X
, there is at most one element in
X
that is adjacent to
y
in
X
. Because
|y^{\vartriangleleft}|>1
,
y
has at least two covers in
P
, which define at least two disjoint upward branches. At most one of these branches can contain elements of
X
. Therefore, there exists a cover
w
of

such that the entire upward branch from
w
contains no elements of
X
. Let
c
be a maximal saturated chain passing through
y
and
w
. By our choice of
w
, the intersection
c\cap X
contains
y
and can contain only elements
z\in X
such that
|z^{\vartriangleleft}|>1
, because
P
avoids
Q
. Thus we have
|v^{\vartriangleright}|\leqslant 1
, for all
v\in c
. Hence, the element
x=\max c
belongs to a unique maximal saturated chain. Similarly we obtain the claim in the case
|y^{\vartriangleright}|>1
.

Let
P^{\prime}:=P\setminus\{x\}
. Then
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{j}\in\{\hat{\rho}_{j}-1,\hat{\rho}_{j}\}
, for all

. If

then
\hat{\rho}_{m_{P}-1}>1=\hat{\rho}_{m_{P}}
because there are at least two saturated chains of rank

in

, one containing

and one not containing

. Moreover
\hat{\rho}_{h}=\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h}+1>\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h+1}+1=\hat{\rho}_{h+1}
, for all

, by our inductive hypothesis. Assume then

.
We have that

\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{a}=\hat{\rho}_{a}
implies
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{b}=\hat{\rho}_{b}
, for all

.
Let

. Hence, if
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h}=\hat{\rho}_{h}
, by our inductive hypothesis we have

\hat{\rho}_{h}=\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h}>\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h+1}=\hat{\rho}_{h+1}
.
If
\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h}=\hat{\rho}_{h}-1

\hat{\rho}_{h}=\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h}+1>\hat{\rho}^{\prime}_{h+1}+1\geqslant\hat{\rho}_{h+1}

∎

We proceed now to define

Definition 6.4
.

Let

is a

if
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}=\{0,\ldots,\max(x)\}

As ordered set partitions, Fubini words index bases of generalized coinvariant algebras, as treated in
[

Fubini words have a geometric interest as they realize indexing sets for stratifications of spaces of line configurations, see
[

Since the dual of a partition into distinct parts, when a zero prefix is added, is a Fubini word, we obtain the following corollary of Proposition

Corollary 6.5
.

Let

. Then the dual partition

, with a

The next definition introduces the notion of lazy Fubini word.

Definition 6.6

An element

and

, for all

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
n\in\N
and

. The following are equivalent:

\mathrm{1)}

w
is

-principal;

\mathrm{2)}

L_{n}(w)
is a lazy Fubini word;

\mathrm{3)}

w
avoids the pattern

.

In particular
|\Pr(L_{n})|=C_{n}
, where
C_{n}
is the

-th Catalan number.

Proof.

1)\Rightarrow 2)
: by contradiction, assume
L_{n}(w)
is not lazy Fubini. Hence by Definition
6.6
there exists
i\in[n-1]
such that
L_{n}(w)_{i}<L_{n}(w)_{i+1}-1
. Then there exists
a<i
such that
w^{-1}(i+1)<w^{-1}(a)<w^{-1}(i)
.
We have that
s_{i}w<w
in the Bruhat order. But

and then

, i.e.

, a contradiction.

2)\Rightarrow 3)
: by contradiction, assume that
w
has the pattern

b<c<a
with

such that

such that

implies that

for all

Hence

3)\Rightarrow 1)

in
S_{n}
are in bijection with partitions
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
of
k
such that
\lambda_{1}<\ldots<\lambda_{r}\leqslant n-1
, see
[
2
, Theorem 10]
. We denote by
\Lambda
this bijection and then by
\Lambda(u)
the partition associated to
u\in U_{n}
. We recall how this bijection works. We let
\Lambda(e):=(\,)
, the partition with
0
parts. Given
u\in U_{n}\setminus\{e\}
, let
j:=u^{-1}(n)
. Then

\Lambda(u)=(n-u(j+1),\ldots,n-u(n)).

Conversely, given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
of
k
, such that
\lambda_{1}<\ldots<\lambda_{r}\leqslant n-1
, consider the set
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-r}\}:=[n]\setminus\{n-\lambda_{1},\ldots,n-\lambda_{r}\}
and assume that
t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n-r}
. Then

\Lambda^{-1}(\lambda):=t_{1}\ldots t_{n-r}\,\,n-\lambda_{1}\ldots n-\lambda_{r}.

The last result of the paper allows us to obtain the partition in (
4
), for unimodal permutations, in a considerably simplified way by using the bijection
\Lambda
.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
u\in U_{n}
. Then
\Lambda(u)=\lambda_{u}
and

(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{u(n)},\Lambda(u)^{*}_{1},\ldots,\Lambda(u)^{*}_{s})=L_{n}(u),

where
s=n-u(n)
is the number of parts of
\Lambda(u)^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda(u)=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
and
\Lambda(u)^{*}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s})
.
We prove that
(0,\ldots,0,\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{s})
is equal to
L_{n}(u)
. If
\ell(u)=0
we have nothing to show. Let
\ell(u)>0
.
It is clear that
L_{n}(u)_{i}=0
for all
i\leqslant u(n)
and
[L_{n}(u)]_{u(n)+1}\neq 0
. We have that
s=\lambda_{r}=n-u(n)
and the number of zeros in
L_{n}(u)
is
u(n)=n-s
.

Let
0<k\leqslant n-u^{-1}(n)=r
; since
u
is unimodal, we have that

|\{i\in[n]:L_{n}(u)_{i}=k\}|=u(n-k)-u(n-k+1).

Hence, by defining
\lambda_{0}:=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle u(n-k)-u(n-k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n-u(n-k+1)-(n-u(n-k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{r-k+1}-\lambda_{r-k}

and this concludes the proof of the second equality by definition of dual partition.

By
[
17
, Remark 2.8]
we have that
\lambda_{u}^{*}
provides the exponents of
u
. By Proposition
6.3
also the Lehmer code
L_{n}(u)
provides the exponents of
u
. Hence, by the equality proved above,

\Lambda(u)^{*}=\lambda_{u}^{*}
and then
\Lambda(u)=\lambda_{u}
.
∎

Remark 6.15
.

By
