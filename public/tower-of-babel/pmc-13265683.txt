Arthroscopic treatment of osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum in an early adolescent swimmer: a case report
Authors: Kyle B. Christy, Zoe A. Tashjian, Brian R. Wolf, Robert Z. Tashjian
Published: 2026

Introduction

Osteochondrosis of the capitellum was first described in 1929 and was believed to be analogous to Perthes disease of the hip.2,16 This has since been divided into Panner's disease, which affects mainly preadolescent males, resolving with rest, and osteochondritis dissecans (OCD), which is progressive, affecting mainly adolescent overhead athletes.1,2,5, 6, 7,16 Osteochondritis dissecans is the result of the underlying subchondral bone losing its vitality from a lack of blood flow, potentially causing the separation and fragmentation of the articular surface from the bone.1,8,9 Additionally, repetitive microtrauma and compressive forces to the radiocapitellar joint from valgus stress are believed to play a role.1,5,6,8,12,16,21,22 Furthermore, in a biomechanical study, Schenck et al reported the central radial head is significantly stiffer than the lateral capitellum, leading to a higher strain across the capitellum with radiocapitellar joint stress.16

Capitellar OCD is most prevalent in adolescent overhead athletes, specifically baseball players and gymnasts, from the high amounts of valgus stress placed onto the elbow.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9,16,21 It is suggested that the most at-risk age range for capitellar OCD is 10-19 years old, with the average of males being 15.1 years old and females 12.5 years old.10,20 Stable, early-stage lesions can be treated nonoperatively with rest, physical therapy, and analgesics.1,3,4,8,11,16,21,22 Although, reported healing rates with nonoperative treatment have ranged from 40% to 84%,13,18,19 Takahara et al reported that no patients had fully resumed their sports activity after nonoperative treatment.19 When nonoperative treatment fails or when the lesion is unstable, operative treatment is necessary and includes débridement, drilling, microfracture, fragment fixation, osteochondral autograft transfer system (OATS), and autologous chondrocyte implantation.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9,11,12,14,17,21,22 In athletes undergoing operative treatment, full return to sports at pre-operative levels range from 49% to 100%.1,3,4,13,19,21 However, there are no reports on swimmers who underwent treatment for capitellar OCD lesions.

Here, we present a case report detailing an early adolescent swimmer that failed nonoperative treatment, ultimately undergoing an arthroscopic débridement and microfracture for OCD of the capitellum.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old, right-hand-dominant girl presented to the author's clinic (B.W.) with pain primarily along the posterior and lateral aspect of her left elbow. She stated the pain began suddenly while swimming approximately 2 months prior but denied any trauma. She also reported painful clicking, popping, and locking. She is a competitive swimmer, competing in butterfly, freestyle, and backstroke, and trains approximately 10 hours per week. Her pain was present pulling back the water while swimming, opening doors, and lifting her backpack. Prior to presenting to the clinic, she was initially managed conservatively with rest and a splint. However, after 1 month of nonoperative treatment, her symptoms worsened.

Physical exam findings revealed the patient had 0°-150° of elbow motion from extension to flexion on the affected side and −10° to 150° on the contralateral elbow. Upon palpation, there was tenderness over the soft spot and capitellar region and subluxation of the ulnar nerve. With valgus stress, there was no gapping.

Plain radiographs of the affected elbow displayed flattening and lucency of the capitellum consistent with an osteochondral lesion of the capitellum and open physes at the epicondyles, trochlea, capitellum, olecranon, and radial head (Fig. 1). Having previously failed nonoperative treatment and the presence of an unstable OCD lesion, an operative treatment plan was chosen. Given the patient was an early adolescent with a small, <10 mm lesion and intact lateral capitellar wall on imaging, elbow arthroscopic débridement and possible microfracture were chosen over OATS.

Surgical technique

A left elbow arthroscopy was performed with the patient in a prone position. The proximal anteromedial portal was made and the anterior compartment examined with no loose bodies. There was moderate synovitis of the radiocapitellar joint and the anterior portion of an osteochondral defect of the capitellum could be seen with elbow extension (Fig. 3). After a posterior lateral portal was made, and immediately upon entering the olecranon fossa, a ∼1 cm cartilage loose body was encountered (Fig. 4A). A trans-triceps working portal was then made, and the loose body was removed (Fig. 4B). A second, smaller loose body attached to the olecranon fossa was encountered and subsequently removed (Fig. 4B).Figure 3Intraoperative view of the OCD lesion of the capitellum. Next, a direct posterior portal was made, and the posterior radiocapitellar joint and synovial plica were débrided. The OCD lesion was immediately present, and fibrous debris at the base of the lesion, which measured ∼9 mm from medial to lateral and ∼11 mm anterior to posterior, was débrided with a small curette. A 70° arthroscope was used to optimize visualization. At the posterolateral portion of the base, a subchondral cyst was encountered and subsequently débrided. The edges of the lesion were then stabilized and squared. Post-operative follow-up

The patient reported that her mechanical symptoms mostly resolved by approximately 4 months post-operatively which at that point she returned to swimming. At 5 and a half years post-operatively, she continues to compete in the same strokes with a goal of swimming in college. During the backstroke and fly, she reported that she rarely experiences clicking in her elbow, though this does not impact her swimming. Additionally, she reported that her elbow is very sensitive in spots, and aggravation can cause numbness/tingling down the forearm and into the hand. The range of motion of the affected elbow is 0°-150° from extension to flexion, never returning to −10°-150°, the equivalent on the contralateral elbow. The patient was very satisfied with the procedure, with a visual analog pain score of 0 of 10, Mayo Elbow Performance Index score of 100, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score of 3.4 with a sports subscore of 0, and a Kerlan-Jobe Orthopedic Clinic Shoulder and Elbow score of 96.0.

Discussion

Capitellar OCD affects adolescent overhead athletes primarily, with cyclic valgus stress across the elbow believed to be a key contributor.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9,12,16,21,22 However, there continues to be limited literature on the management of capitellar OCD in the early adolescent, skeletally immature athlete. Additionally, there are no reports on the management of capitellar OCD in swimmers. The patient presented in this case was an early adolescent swimmer who underwent arthroscopic débridement and microfracture for capitellar OCD after failed nonoperative treatment.

Risk factors for capitellar OCD include adolescents who participate in overhead sports, with the most common age range being 10-19 years old and an average female age of 12.5 years old.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10,16,20,21 This is consistent with the presented case, where the patient described was an 11-year-old swimmer. Although not a traditional overhead sport such as baseball or gymnastics, swimming, specifically the freestyle and butterfly strokes, subject the athlete's elbows to a cyclic valgus load. The resulting repetitive compressive forces to the radiocapitellar joint are thought to play a role in the development of OCD lesions of the capitellum.1,5,6,8,12,16,21,22

In the case presented, the OCD lesion occurred in the patient's nondominant elbow. However, this is unsurprising since during the pull phase of a swimming stroke, the swimmer's elbow is subjected to valgus stress. Unlike throwing athletes that only use their dominant arm in a repetitive motion subjecting the elbow to valgus stress, swimmers do this bilaterally. Additionally, the patient's pre-operative radiographs showed open physes at the epicondyles, capitellum, trochlea, olecranon, and radial head. Current literature is conflicting with regard to outcomes in patients with open physes who underwent operative treatment. In patients with large capitellar OCD lesions and an open radial head physis, Miyake et al reported worse outcomes after arthroscopic débridement and drilling.15 On the other hand, Bexkens et al reported that an open physis was correlated with improved clinical outcomes after arthroscopic débridement and microfracture.4 Although, in a separate study, Bexkens et al reported that there was no association with an open capitellar physis and improved radiographic healing after microfracture.3

There is currently no consensus on the treatment plan for adolescent athletes with capitellar OCD lesions. While current literature suggests that stable, early-stage lesions can be treated nonoperatively,1,3,4,8,11,16,21,22 Takahara et al found that zero athletes fully returned to their previous level of sports, and only 15% of baseball players reported improved throwing ability after nonoperative treatment.19 In this case, the patient failed nonoperative treatment prior to receiving arthroscopic treatment. Furthermore, Bexkens et al reported that decreased duration of pre-operative symptoms correlated with better clinical outcomes.4 The question then arises, how often should adolescent athletes be treated nonoperatively, if at all? Additionally, there is no consensus on the optimal surgical procedure to treat capitellar OCD. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Westermann et al found that 94% of patients who underwent OATS returned to their previous level of sports, which was significantly higher than the 71% of patients who underwent microfracture.21 However, as an arthroscopic treatment, microfracture is inherently less invasive than OATS, which is an open procedure. Additionally, for OATS, there is a risk for donor site morbidity. The early adolescent patient highlighted underwent arthroscopic débridement and microfracture for a small OCD lesion with an intact lateral capitellar wall. Although range of motion was not fully restored and symptoms happen rarely, the patient is fully satisfied with her outcome. The patient continues to swim and compete in the same strokes as she did pre-operatively, and her ability has not been negatively affected, as highlighted by the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand sports subscore of 0 and Kerlan-Jobe Orthopedic Clinic Shoulder and Elbow score of 96.0.

Conclusion

This case report describes an adolescent swimmer who underwent arthroscopic débridement and microfracture for symptomatic capitellar OCD, resulting in a significant improvement in pain relief and function and a return to preinjury-level sport. Awareness that swimming may result in OCD lesions of the capitellum and treatment with débridement can result in full return to sport will assist others in evaluating adolescent swimmers with elbow pain.
