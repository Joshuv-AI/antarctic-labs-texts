Clinical utility of ‘Shaken’ biopsies for whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Rachel Nelan, Martina Mijuskovic, Martina Hughes, Jennifer Becq, Zoya Kingsbury, Eleni Tsogka, Miao He, Dunja Vucenovic, Clare Craig, Greg Elgar, Pauline Levey, Tamara Suaris, Emma Walsh, Mark Ross, J Louise Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWhole-genome sequencing (WGS) is beginning to be applied to cancer samples in the clinical setting. This ideally requires high-quality, minimally degraded DNA of high tumour cell content, while retaining sufficient tissue with excellent morphology for histopathological diagnosis and immunohistochemistry. The aim of this study was to investigate alternative ways of handling cancer samples to fulfil both diagnostic and molecular requirements.MethodsEx vivo biopsies were taken to investigate the feasibility of using cancer cells ‘shaken’ from the surface of a biopsy for WGS, while maintaining the tissue biopsy for histological diagnosis. WGS from the shaken cells was compared with the gold standard of a fresh-frozen (FF) biopsy. The procedure was piloted in the real-world setting for breast cancer samples.ResultsCells shaken from ex vivo biopsies can yield DNA of sufficient quantity and quality for WGS, while having no discernible impact on quality of tissue morphology. WGS data showed good coverage, comparable variant calls and generally higher tumour content in shaken cell samples compared with the control FF samples. For real-world biopsies, DNA yields were lower, but WGS data were of excellent quality for the cases analysed.ConclusionsShaken biopsy sampling allows genomic sequencing from patients with cancer who may otherwise not receive a genome sequence due to limited sample availability. It represents a way of overcoming the logistics of obtaining and storing FF tissue making it a suitable technique for wider scale implementation in the clinical setting.Keywords: CANCER, BREAST, DNA, Methods

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study shows that it is possible to combine gold-standard histopathological assessment with comprehensive, high-quality genomic analysis using the same tissue biopsy. Cells shaken from the biopsy are used as the source of material for whole-genome sequencing, while the rest of the biopsy is fixed for histopathological evaluation. Our study shows there is no detriment to either.

Introduction

The discovery of driver mutations and their differential sensitivity to specific therapies have allowed precision medicine to become a reality for many cancers. Targetable mutations identify which patients ought to benefit from a specific treatment, preventing unnecessary intervention and toxic side effects for others.1 However, this increases demand on diagnostic tissue samples for additional molecular testing to supplement morphological analysis.

In many cases, testing is most appropriately done using biopsy samples: for example, over 67% of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are at an advanced stage at diagnosis, where surgery is not an option.2 For most of these patients, the available tissues for diagnosis and molecular profiling are small biopsies or cytology specimens with few cells, and additional biopsies may be required for presurgical molecular analysis.2 Also, increasing numbers of patients (especially those with breast cancer) are undergoing neoadjuvant therapy; therefore, therapeutic decisions may well depend on diagnostic sample analysis, requiring pathways that combine histological diagnosis and genomic testing on the same sample.

The UK’s 100,000 Genomes Project was the first national whole-genome sequencing (WGS) programme, whose key aim was to create the infrastructure for a new Genomic Medicine Service, transforming care and bringing advanced diagnosis and precision treatments to patients. Recruitment of patients with cancer or rare diseases was completed in 2018, with the 100 000th sequence accomplished in December 2018.3 WGS is now commissioned for selected tumour types,4 eliminating the need for separate, targeted diagnostic tests and providing additional biomarker information such as mutational signatures. At scale it is cost-efficient and has the potential to enhance histopathological diagnostic and prognostic information and to aid treatment. However, WGS using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue is compromised, since formalin and downstream processing induce DNA fragmentation, DNA-DNA and DNA-protein crosslinking, and deamination of cytosine bases to uracil. Tissue processing contributes to DNA damage with high temperature leading to fragmentation and denaturation. Also, low yields of DNA may demand PCR amplification at sequencing library preparation. These factors produce unevenness of coverage, particularly in regions of high-adenine-thymine (AT) or high-guanine-cytosine (GC) content, and an increased level of false positive somatic variant calls.5 6 Therefore, submission of fresh-frozen (FF) tissue is recommended for WGS. However, this is challenging in a clinical setting as it requires changes in the way hospitals transfer samples to laboratories and how laboratories and staff work7; furthermore, FF material cannot compete with the superior morphological preservation of FFPE; and, most laboratories have established additional tissue analytics such as immunohistochemistry on FFPE. This would naturally drive the need for two biopsies: a formalin-fixed specimen for morphological assessment (and standard immunohistochemistry) and an FF sample for WGS. This places an additional burden on both the patient and the clinical team and creates a concern that intra tumour heterogeneity will render the two biopsies distinct from each other.

Based on the long-used practice of imprint cytology,8 we hypothesised that tumour cells would be preferentially shed from the surface of a tissue biopsy. We proposed that gently shaking a fresh biopsy in sterile fluid would yield a tumour-rich cell sample for WGS while preserving the tissue biopsy for parallel histopathological diagnosis. Here we report the results of analysing ex vivo and real-world biopsies from patients with breast cancer to test this hypothesis.

Methods

Briefly, fresh ex vivo ‘biopsy’ samples and real-world biopsy samples were inverted 45 times over 60 s in sterile phosphate-buffered saline to generate suspended tumour cells. Each cell sample underwent WGS in parallel with its FF biopsy counterpart. Tumour content, coverage and variant calling were compared between shaken cell samples and matched FF biopsies. Details are found in the online supplemental methods.

Results

Morphology (ex vivo cases)

Initially, we evaluated the shaken biopsy approach using ex vivo ‘biopsies’ from surgical resections of patients diagnosed with breast cancer (online supplemental methods). Cells were dislodged by shaking from multiple biopsies for each tumour (figure 1). There was no discernible difference between shaken and unshaken FFPE tissues, with excellent cellular and architectural preservation. Similarly, shaken FF tissue retained excellent morphology (figure 2a–c).

All cytological preparations contained tumour cells (recognised on morphological features), though the cellularity and morphological preservation of samples varied (figure 2d). Apart from one case, which was very haemorrhagic, cytospin preparations typically contained >60% tumour cells.

DNA yield and quality (ex vivo cases)

For biopsy B (and C where available), DNA was extracted from tissue and matched cells and assessed for its suitability for WGS.

A260/A280 ratios were comparable between DNA from FF biopsies and matched cytospin samples (range 1.74–2.04), indicating that neither the shaking process nor the DNA extraction method affected purity.

DNA Integrity Number (DIN) values for tissue and matched cells were also comparable, indicating that shaking had no discernible impact on DNA quality (). All samples met the DIN value threshold (≥7) for sequencing.

DNA yields from cell samples and matched tissue were variable (figure 3a). In two cases, yields from tissue were substantially higher; however, yields were otherwise similar between the two sample types, suggesting that a substantial fraction of cells were dislodged by shaking. Sufficient DNA for sequencing was obtained from all samples.

DNA yields (µg) obtained from ex vivo and diagnostic tissue and matched shaken cell samples. Graph showing DNA yields (μg) obtained from ex vivo (a) and diagnostic (b) tissue and matched shaken cell samples.

There was no apparent detriment to DNA quality or yield from keeping cell pellets for 72 hours at 4°C before extraction.

DNA yield (diagnostic biopsies)

Shaken cell samples were obtained from 76 real-world, diagnostic tissue biopsies (online supplemental methods). DNA yields ranged from 18 to 6270 ng (). 13 samples with the highest yields were chosen for WGS (). For these, DNA yield was lower for most cell samples compared with matched tissue (figure 3b).

WGS analysis

Sample selection and sequencing metrics

WGS data were compared between each tissue biopsy and the cells shaken from that biopsy. Five ex vivo pairs were selected for WGS, including cells that had been extracted within 12 hours or following storage for 72 hours at 4°C. Following encouraging findings from these, we analysed real-world biopsies from 13 patients with breast cancer.

Tumour and germline samples were sequenced to mean aligned depths of 103–131× and 37–68×, respectively. Paired cell and tissue samples from ex vivo and diagnostic biopsies produced excellent sequence quality metrics ().

Tumour content

In 16/18 (89%) cases, WGS data indicated that tumour content in cells was ~20% higher on average than matching tissue (range −6% to 59%) (figure 4). This may indicate that tumour cells shed preferentially during the shaking process.

Genome-wide features

Copy-number profiles were highly reproducible between cells and matching tissue (figure 5). Small somatic variants showed good concordance of calls; and concordant variant allele frequencies (VAFs) follow tumour purity predictions (figure 5c,f). This is also apparent from depth plots and B-allele frequencies (figure 5a,b,d,e).

Three cell/tissue pairs showed evidence of tumour heterogeneity, manifesting as non-integer copy-number changes and/or subsets of small somatic variants at unexpectedly low VAFs in the cell or tissue sample following tumour content correction (SBIOPSY013, SBIOPSY015 and SBIOPSY020). This might indicate that cells closer to the biopsy surface in these cases have a different representation of cellular subpopulations than those in the core.

Tumour mutational burden (TMB) was higher in most cell samples than in the matching tissue, reflecting the improved power to call somatic variants in samples with higher tumour content (). An exception was SBIOPSY020, where significant heterogeneity was observed between cells and tissue (). Here, the tissue sample had higher TMB than the cell sample despite having lower tumour content. In addition to differences in copy-number profiles and subsets of small VAFs, the tissue sample showed an elevation in the proportion of the APOBEC mutational signature.

Clinically and biologically important features

Considering only 136 ‘Actionable genes in solid tumour v1.9’ (online supplemental methods), 40/41 (97.6%) small variants and 40/46 (87%) high-confidence structural variants (SVs) were concordant between cells and matching tissue. A splice region variant in ERBB2 in SBIOPSY001 was the only discordant small variant; this had a VAF of only 3% in the cell sample but was not called in the tissue, which had lower tumour content. All discordant SVs had low VAFs in the sample where the call was made (≤5%). Half of these cases (3/6) had evidence of the variant in the matching sample, but this was insufficient for a variant call.

In the HER2+ tumours (SBIOPSY001 and SBIOPSY017), amplification of ERBB2 was called in cells and matching tissue (figure 6), while HER2− tumours showed no significant ERBB2 copy-number increases (>4×, or >2× the baseline).

All small mutations in genes most frequently mutated in breast cancer9 10 were concordant between cells and matching tissue (figure 6). These included clinically actionable PIK3CA hotspot mutations (n=3), and PTEN, RB1 and MAP3K1 loss-of-function mutations. Some heterogeneity was observed among copy-number alterations (CNAs), with small differences in some genes’ predicted copy-number states (figure 6). However, most CNAs were either concordant or of the same type (gain vs loss) in cells and tissue.

The most abundant mutational signatures were concordant between paired samples (figure 6), except for heterogeneous tumour SBIOPSY020. Signatures of homologous recombination DNA repair deficiency (HRD), an important breast cancer biomarker, were observed in both tissue and matched cells for three tumours (SBIOPSY002, SBIOPSY008 and SBIOPSY012), manifesting as elevated microhomology-related indel signature ID6 in all cell/tissue pairs, and, in 2/3 cases, single base substitution signature SBS3.11 DNA mismatch repair and microsatellite instability (MSI) signatures (SBS44, ID2) were supported by a high-fitted mutation count in SBIOPSY016 in the matching samples (figure 6). This case also showed concordance in high TMB, consistent with the MSI phenotype. APOBEC deaminase-related signatures SBS2 and SBS1312 were present in cells and matching tissue of two tumours (SBIOPSY001, SBIOPSY006) and in the tissue sample only of heterogeneous tumour SBIOPSY020, along with elevated TMB, as expected.

Discussion

A growing battery of molecular tests on solid tumours is needed to aid diagnosis and guide treatment. This must be carefully balanced with the need for morphological diagnosis when tissue is limited. WGS is being indicated for an increasing number of solid tumour types as the most efficient platform to cover multiple tests. However, DNA quantity and quality requirements for WGS can be challenging in the clinic, particularly when the sample type with maximum clinical utility is a biopsy, further limiting the available material and posing significant logistical challenges.

Generally, histological samples are placed in formalin at the point of tissue removal from the patient. This allows fixation to start immediately and, particularly for biopsies, results in excellent morphological preservation. However, formalin fixation and downstream processing cause DNA damage and denaturation, which can compromise somatic variant calling from Next-generation sequencing (NGS) data.5 Indeed, the impact of formalin fixation has led to erroneous conclusions from NGS previously.13 14 In contrast, FF tissue provides excellent quality nucleic acid for sequencing; but there are significant resource implications in establishing an additional pathway to transport fresh or frozen tissue rapidly from the clinic to the laboratory for storage and extraction. Furthermore, it is imperative that allocating biopsies to genomic analysis does not compromise diagnostic accuracy. Thus, all biopsies need to be assessed for the presence and subtyping of the tumour, further complicating this tissue pathway.

Here, we evaluated a simplified pathway by exploring the utility of tumour-enriched cells dislodged from the surface of tissue for WGS, while retaining the remaining tissue sample to align diagnostic and analytical assessment. Our goal was to evaluate DNA suitability for genomic analysis while maintaining excellent morphology for diagnosis.

The utility of molecular analysis using cytological material from solid tumours has been widely demonstrated.15 16 High-quality nucleic acid is derived from cytological material, though yields are limited compared with tissue samples.16 Despite this, NGS panels have been successfully applied to cytology samples in several tumour types including NSCLC17 and thyroid cancer.18 In lung cancer, previous studies demonstrated that, if FFPE samples are unavailable, a cytology sample can be appropriate for accurate diagnosis and molecular testing, with detection rates of EGFR mutations seemingly as high as those with tissue samples.19,21 Also, a recent study demonstrated that tumour cells selectively scraped from smeared slides yielded high-quality nucleic acid for molecular analysis22 and showed significantly higher overall tumour cell numbers and tumour cellularity than cell block specimens.22 23

Extrapolating from studies that estimate cell counts in cancer tissues, an average 14-gauge breast core needle biopsy (10×10×1 mm), including non-tumour components and stroma, may have 1–6 million cells.24 With efficient DNA extraction of 5 pg (picograms) per cell, 400 000 cells would yield 2 µg of DNA.25 Therefore, we hypothesised that sufficient cells for WGS could be retrieved from the periphery of a biopsy with minimal impact on cells available for histology.

Using ex vivo biopsies to compare shaken and non-shaken tissues, we confirmed generation of a tumour-rich cytology sample with no demonstrable impact on tissue morphology. The quality of DNA from the dislodged cells was equivalent to that from FF tissue and was unchanged after refrigeration of cells for 72 hours. This would allow time for samples to be stored and transported between the clinic and the DNA extraction laboratory.

Absolute yields of DNA from shaken cell samples were generally much lower for diagnostic biopsies than ex vivo biopsies (). The larger volume and surface area of ex vivo biopsies are likely to be important in this regard. Furthermore, DNA yields from cells shaken from diagnostic biopsies were generally lower relative to their matched tissue samples (figure 3), in keeping with previous reports.26 A change to the time or vigorousness of the shaking may be needed, therefore, to dislodge more cells from the biopsy. What are the implications for the suitability of the shaken biopsy method for WGS? DNA requirements for WGS have fallen considerably: currently, PCR-free library preparation can start from 50 ng of DNA. Only six of the 76 diagnostic biopsies delivered less than 50 ng of DNA from the shaken cells (). In clinical applications at scale, demands are somewhat different: automated library preparation usually requires excess material, and there is often a requirement to allow for repeated attempts. Therefore, further optimisation of the shaking procedure to harvest more cells, alongside adoption of improved library preparation, will be needed to develop a clinical assay. In cases where more than one diagnostic biopsy is taken, all could be shaken to increase DNA yield.

Submission of samples for genomic testing requires a tumour cellularity estimate, so results can be interpreted in context. For ex vivo biopsies, we generated cytospin preparations from the shaken sample to evaluate presence of tumour cells, observing a definite correlation between the biopsy and the cytospin preparation (very cellular biopsies generated cellular cytospins; sclerotic biopsies gave paucicellular cytospins). We did not do this for the ‘real-world’ biopsies because of the limited material available. However, going forward, we suggest that with reduced DNA required for library preparation, aliquots of the ‘shaken’ sample could be used for cytospin preparation and assessment of tumour cellularity.

Among samples that underwent WGS, we found higher tumour content in the shaken cell sample in 89% of cases compared with the tissue biopsy; in many cases, this difference was substantial. This suggests that tumour cells are preferentially dislodged during the shaking step. Higher tumour content results in increased evidence for calling somatic variants in the cell-based WGS data. This tumour cell enrichment may make the approach particularly useful for tumours characteristically associated with a desmoplastic stroma, such as pancreatic and prostate cancers, where achieving sufficient tumour cell cellularity can be challenging.

A key requirement for the shaken biopsy is that the genomic profile of the tumour in the tissue is accurately reflected in the cell sample. Karnes et al26 reported that despite lower DNA input, cytology samples and matching FFPE tissue samples showed 99.5% concordance on NGS. Here, we evaluated somatic variant concordance across 136 genes of recognised clinical importance in solid tumours. Over 97% of small variants and ~87% of structural variants were concordant between cells and tissues. All discordant variants manifested low allele frequencies (≤5%, indicating subclonality). Furthermore, discordant structural variants were observed in genomic regions harbouring repetitive elements, where detection can be challenging particularly at low VAF. Importantly, mutational signatures relating to actionable phenotypes such as HRD and MSI were concordant between cells and tissues. Intra tumour heterogeneity is a potential challenge in genomic analysis of biopsy material, as biopsies from different areas of a single tumour can exhibit as much genomic variation as tumour biopsies from different patients.27 Matched cell and tissue samples demonstrated high concordance and, critically, all clinically actionable variants were detected in the cell samples, further supporting the analytical validity and emphasising the potential clinical utility of this approach. One sample (SBIOPSY020) showed marked heterogeneity between the cell and tissue sample. This is likely due to the expansion of a subpopulation of cells within the tissue sample that was reflected differently in the tissue and the cell sample.28

In conclusion, this study supports the validity of the shaken biopsy approach for WGS, with no evidence to suggest that clinically important alterations would be missed. The method could be particularly useful when tissue availability for combined histological diagnosis and genomic analysis is very limited; in such cases, histological diagnosis may take priority over molecular analysis. The stability of the shaken biopsy over 72 hours further enhances the clinical utility of this method. Clearly, this study has been limited to breast cancer samples and will need to be validated in other sample types. However, we provide evidence that the clinical application of the shaken biopsy approach should be pursued.
