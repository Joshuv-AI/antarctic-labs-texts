CD276-directed supramolecular nanoplatform with pH-triggered gemcitabine release for potent tumor stromal and vascular suppression
Authors: Jiayi Li, Hao Liu, Zhijun Li, Zhihuan Zheng, Lanzhu Luo, Liqing Lin, Jizhen Lin, Gang Liu, Xinhua Lin, Bing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGemcitabine (Gem) remains a cornerstone chemotherapy for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, but its clinical efficacy is limited by poor pharmacokinetics, dense fibrotic stroma, and hypovascularization. While pH-responsive liposomes can enhance circulation and targeted drug release, their clinical application is hindered by low drug loading capacity and premature leakage of hydrophilic drugs. To address these challenges, we exploited the high and specific expression of CD276 on pancreatic cancer cells, tumor vasculature, and fibroblasts. We engineered a high-specificity and high-affinity anti-CD276 scFv, and developed a novel nanoplatform (Gem@CPL) that integrates pH-responsive and supramolecular assembly strategies with Gem, achieving 4-fold higher drug loading, improved stability, and tumor-specific release. Cellular and animal studies confirmed that Gem@CPL facilitates tumor-specific accumulation and CD276-mediated internalization, resulting in improved intracellular delivery and therapeutic efficacy. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed a 2.26-fold prolongation of half-life (t₁/₂) and a significant reduction in volume of distribution (Vd) to 0.11-fold compared to free Gem, indicating superior systemic exposure and minimized off-target distribution. Gem@CPL increased anti-tumor activity by 1.77-fold, demonstrating its enhanced efficacy via sustained circulation and targeted delivery. By specifically targeting CD276, this platform minimizes systemic toxicity and potentially improving patient tolerability, offering promising prospects for clinical translation and better outcomes in pancreatic cancer treatment.Keywords: CD276, pH-triggered, Supramolecular assembly, Stromal vascular depletion, Pancreatic cancer

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies of the digestive system, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10 %, making it the deadliest among cancers and earning the designation “the king of cancers” (Zhai et al., 2023). Globally, both incidence and new case rates are on the rise, driven by aging populations and inadequate early detection strategies (Wu et al., 2024a). Characterized by late diagnosis, limited eligibility for surgical resection (approximately 20 % of patients), and suboptimal response to current therapeutic modalities, PDAC is associated with high rates of recurrence and mortality (Wu et al., 2024b). The principal challenges encompass the absence of distinctive early symptoms and reliable biomarkers, which impede timely intervention. The tumor microenvironment (TME) in pancreatic cancer is notably formidable, with over 70 % composed of dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and abundant cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Niu et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024). This dense stroma forms a nearly impenetrable barrier, severely restricting drug penetration and immune cell infiltration (Meng et al., 2024). Additionally, the disorganized and poorly permeable vasculature further impairs effective delivery of therapeutics and immune components. These features collectively contribute to the limited efficacy of existing treatments (Li et al., 2024). Addressing these obstacles necessitates comprehensive characterization of the TME to identify key therapeutic targets, along with the development of innovative drug delivery systems, to promote the advancement of more effective and selective treatment strategies.

Comprehensive analysis of the pancreatic cancer (PC) tumor microenvironment has identified CD276 (also known as B7H3) as a prominent member of the B7 family of immune checkpoint proteins, sharing sequence and structural similarities with PD-L1, as a Type I transmembrane protein (Guo et al., 2025; Kontos et al., 2021). CD276 is broadly expressed across various malignancies, with elevated levels in tumor tissues correlating with poor prognosis, while its expression in normal tissues remains limited (Du et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2024). Notably, CD276 is highly expressed within the pancreatic TME—on tumor cells, stromal fibroblasts, and neovascular endothelial cells—rendering it a promising biomarker for early detection and disease monitoring (Luo et al., 2024; Seaman et al., 2017). Functionally, CD276 exhibits a dual immunoregulatory role: it has been identified as a co-stimulatory molecule facilitating T-cell activation; however, emerging evidence also suggests it acts as a co-inhibitory molecule, suppressing tumor-infiltrating T-cell proliferation and promoting immune evasion (Xiong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2020). Despite ongoing investigations into its complete spectrum of functions and ligands, accumulating data associate CD276 overexpression with tumor progression and adverse prognosis in pancreatic cancer, positioning it as a compelling target for therapeutic intervention (Timpanaro et al., 2023).

Given the physiologically complex barriers characteristic of pancreatic tumors—including poor vascularization, dense extracellular matrix (ECM), and hypovascularity—effective drug delivery and tumor penetration remain significant challenges (Wu et al., 2024a). dditionally, metabolic reprogramming within pancreatic tumor cells leads to an acidified, weakly basic tumor microenvironment, which favors tumor growth, suppresses immune responses, and facilitates invasion and metastasis (Qi et al., 2023). Exploiting this acidic microenvironment with pH-responsive drug delivery systems presents a promising strategy to enhance tumor-specific drug accumulation and controlled release, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Liposomes are among the most extensively employed delivery systems in both clinical and translational research (Wang et al., 2025a). Clinically approved formulations, such as doxorubicin liposomes and amphotericin B liposomes, along with numerous nucleic acid-based liposomal candidates in clinical trials, exemplify their safety and adaptability (Chaudhary et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2021). By leveraging differences in membrane stability under varying pH conditions, pH-responsive liposomes have been engineered for environment-triggered drug release (Lin et al., 2024). Rational formulation design and meticulous process control are essential to optimize pH sensitivity and stability—factors that critically influence therapeutic efficacy (Chen et al., 2023a). In this context, we propose employing response surface methodology (RSM) to systematically optimize formulation parameters, ensuring maximum efficacy and safety within the tumor microenvironment (Kaur et al., 2024).

Gemcitabine (Gem), a nucleoside analog that inhibits DNA synthesis, effectively impedes tumor cell proliferation and remains the cornerstone chemotherapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer (Hu and O'Reilly, 2024). As the first FDA-approved drug for this indication, Gem-based neoadjuvant and combination therapeutic regimens are recommended across all disease stages. Nonetheless, due to its small hydrophilic nature, encapsulating Gem within liposomes poses significant challenges; typical loading capacities are generally below 4 wt%, which restricts its therapeutic efficacy (Wang et al., 2024). Supramolecular self-assembly offers an innovative strategy that utilizes hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic bonds to promote nanocomplex formation, thereby improving drug solubility, stability, and co-delivery performance (Chen et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025). To enhance encapsulation efficiency, we have designed a novel supramolecular complex of Gem with copper(II), which can be incorporated into pH-responsive liposomes to significantly increase drug loading, stability, and controlled release profiles (Tucci et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025b).

Building upon these principles, we developed a CD276-based targeted nanoplatforms incorporating pH-responsive and Gem-based supramolecular self-assembly (Gem@CPL) to achieve high selectivity, effective tumor accumulation, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy against pancreatic cancer. Elevated CD276 expression is independently associated with poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer, with levels significantly higher than in non-cancerous or normal pancreatic tissues (Chen et al., 2023b; Dong et al., 2024). Although the complete spectrum of CD276's functions and ligands remains under investigation, its potential as a therapeutic target is promising. As illustrated in Scheme 1, Gem@CPL employs tumor-specific versatile targeting of CD276-positive tumor cells, vasculature, and associated stromal elements, facilitating accumulation within the tumor microenvironment. Following localization, CD276-mediated endocytosis accelerates cellular internalization, and disassembly of the self-assembled nanocomplex in the mildly acidic lysosomal environment promotes localized Gem release (Liu et al., 2025). This design enhances intracellular stability and allows for controlled, sustained drug release, maximizing anti-tumor activity while minimizing adverse effects on normal tissues (Zhang et al., 2023). These advances in nanoplatform technology demonstrate significant potential for clinical translation, offering a promising avenue for improving precision therapy in pancreatic cancer and addressing current limitations in drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Distearoyl phosphatidylcholine (DSPC), dioleoyl phosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE), and cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS) were acquired from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, Alabama, USA). Methoxy polyethyleneglycol - polyethylene glycol 2000 - distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine (mPEG2000- DSPE) and Maleimide - polyethylene glycol 2000 – distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine (MAL-PEG2000-DSPE) were purchased from AVT Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cholesterol was purchased from Ponsure Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Gemcitabine hydrochloride (Gem) and 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu) were obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Traut's agent was obtained Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Triton-100 was purchased from Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The fetal bovine serum was acquired from Umedium Anhui Gezhe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). All remaining chemical reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Corporation. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of Gem@CPL

Intelligent pH-responsive liposomes (PSL) and PSL with CD276 tumor-specific versatile targeting (CPL) were prepared using the reverse evaporation, thin lipid film hydration, freezing-thawed method, and a combination of thin lipid film hydration with the freezing-thawed method. The results were analyzed using the polydispersity index (PDI) and encapsulation efficiency to select the optimal preparation method. The lysolipid-containing PSL were composed DPPC, DOPE, CHEMS, cholesterol (Chol), and mPEG2000-DSPE. In this study, we optimized the formulation and preparation process using artificial intelligence-assisted response surface methodology (RSM) based on the previously selected best preparation method. We conducted a three-factor, four-level investigation using RSM, with the concentration ratio of phospholipids (DOPE/CHEMS) to DPPC set within the range of 20 to 60, the drug-to-lipid ratio ranging from 0.6 to 2.25, and the temperature varying between 50 and 70 °C (Table S1). We assessed the effects of these three critical factors—the drug-to-lipid ratio, phospholipid ratio, and temperature—on drug loading, particle size, zeta potential, and PDI.

Briefly, the lipids were precisely weighed in specific ratios and dissolved in chloroform, which was then slowly evaporated under a rotary vacuum system at 40 °C to form a dried lipid film. Gemcitabine (Gem) at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 300, and 400 mM was mixed with 50 mM Cu(II) gluconate and 270 mM triethanolamine (TEOA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The mixture was gently vortexed every 15 mins until the solutions became visibly transparent to prepare the Gem supramolecular self-assembled. The dried lipid film was then hydrated with the Gem supramolecular self-assembled solutions at 60 °C at least 1 h to form intelligent pH-responsive liposomes loaded with Gem supramolecular self-assembled (Gem@PSL).

Gem@CPL was prepared using the same protocol, except that DPPC, DOPE, CHEMS and Chol were combined with DSPE-PEG2000-Mal to form the thin lipid film. After thin lipid film hydration and liposome homogenization, thiolated anti-B7H3-scFv was added to the liposome surface to attach to the maleimide group, corresponding to 500 μg of anti-B7H3-scFv per gram of lipids. This anti-B7H3-scFv-liposome mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C to form Gem@CPL.

Characterization of Gem@CPL

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) are key indicators for assessing liposomes quality, as conventional liposomes loaded with hydrophilic drugs like Gemcitabine (Gem) often exhibit low drug loading, which affects the drug's efficacy and impacts the preparation process and cost (Sha et al., 2025). To evaluate the EE and DL of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL, as well as the pH-responsive drug release, this study established an HPLC quantification method for Gem using 5-Fu as an internal standard. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the preparation formulation and process, and we assessed the drug release behaviors of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 under room temperature conditions. For the release study, 1 mL of the treated liposomes was plased in regenerated cellulose dialysis bags (molecular weight cut-off was 2 kDa, Millipore Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) and immersed into 10 mL of release medium at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 100 rpm. At predetermined time interval, 500 μL samples were withdrawn and replaced with fresh buffer solution. The collected samples were filtered with 0.45 μm filter and centrifuged at 14000g for 10 min. The concentration of released Gem was determined by correlating the results to standard curves using HPLC.

The size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (Micromeritics, Atlanta, USA). The morphologies of Gem@CPL was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (FEI, Oregon, USA). The storage stability and physiological activity of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL were assessed by monitoring change in particle size, zeta potential and drug leakage over a one-month period at 4 °C following liposome fabrication.

CD276 expression assessment and Gem@CPL uptake analysis

Panc-1 and BXPC-3 human pancreatic cancer cell lines in logarithmic growth phase were harvested and assessed for CD276 surface expression by flow cytometry. Cells were incubated with either an anti-human CD276-APC antibody as experimental group, or a Kappa Isotype Control-APC as control group for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, washed twice with flow buffer to remove unbound antibody, and then resuspended in staining buffer. CD276 surface expression was subsequently analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACSVerse, BD, Germany).

The cellular uptake and retention of PSL and CPL nanoplatforms were evaluated using flow cytometry and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). For flow cytometry, Panc-1 or BXPC-3 cells (1× 105 cells/mL) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight until they reached 80 % confluency. The cells were then incubated with FITC@PSL or FITC@B7H3-PSL (FITC equivalent) for 0.5 h, 4 h and 8 h, respectively. After incubation, the cells were trypsinized, washed three times with cold PBS, and resuspended in 0.4 mL of PBS. The cells were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry, with data collected for 10,000 gated events across three independent experiments.

For CLSM, the cells were then incubated with FITC@PSL or FITC@B7H3-PSL (FITC equivalent) for 0.5 h, 2 h and 4 h, respectively. At the specified time points, the cells were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at 4 °C and then rinsed twice with PBS. Following fixation, the cells were permeabilized with 0.2 % polysorbate 20 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature and subsequently incubated with Anti-6× His tag®antibody (Dylight®550) diluted 1:500 v/v for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, cell nuclei were stained with two drops of DAPI Molecular Probes per milliliter of medium for 15 min and observed with CLSM (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Endocytosis mechanism of CPL study

To investigate the endocytosis mechanism of CPL, Panc-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight until they reached 80 % confluency. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium, and five different endocytosis inhibitors—chlorpromazine, genistein, wortmannin, and amiloride—were added individually at concentrations of 50 μM, 200 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM, respectively. After a 60-min pre-incubation with the inhibitors, the cells were treated with FITC@PSL or FITC@CPL in the presence of the inhibitors for an additional 2 h. Following treatment, the cells were trypsinized and isolated by centrifugation at 1000g for 5 mins, and washed three times with cold PBS. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 0.4 mL of PBS. The cells were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry, with data collected for 10,000 gated events per sample. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell cycle assay of Gem@CPL

Panc-1 cells (5× 105 cells/mL) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight until they reached 80 % confluency. PBS, free Gem, Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL (all at 100 nM Gem equivalent) were then added to the culture medium, and the cells were cultured for an additional 12 h at 37 °C. At the end of the incubation period, both adherent and non-adherent cells were trypsinized. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and then fixed in 70 % cold ethanol and stored at 4 °C for 12 h. After fixation, the cells were centrifuged again and washed twice with cold PBS. They were then incubated with RNase A for 30 min at 37 °C and stained with propidium iodide (PI) in a total volume of 400 μL for 30 min in the dark. The DNA content of the samples was measured using flow cytometry (FACSVerse, BD, Germany).

Apoptosis and cytotoxicity analysis of Gem@CPL

For the apoptosis assay, Panc-1 and BXPC-3 cells (3× 104 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight. The cells were then exposed to PBS, Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL for 24 h. At the end of the incubation period, both suspended and adherent cells from each group were collected, stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide according to the manufacturer's protocol, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

The cytotoxicity of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL on human pancreatic cancer cell lines was assessed using the MTT assay following a modified manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, Panc-1 and BXPC-3 cells (5× 103 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight until they reached 50 % confluency. Fresh medium (used as a control) or medium containing different formulations (Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL) at various Gem concentrations (0.1 to 1000 nM) was added. After an additional 48 h incubation, cytotoxicity was determined using the MTT method.

Biocompatibility and pharmacokinetic evaluation of Gem@CPL in vivo

All animal experiments received approval from the Experimental Animal Center of Fujian Medical University. These experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines established by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Certificate number: FJMU IACUC 2021–0380). Blood was collected from three BALB/c mice into a 10 mL EP tube containing heparin sodium to prevent clotting. To assess hemolysis, 500 μL of the erythrocyte suspension was mixed with 500 μL of saline containing varying concentrations of Cu2+ TEOA/Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL (1 mg/mL), and incubated at 450 rpm and 37 °C for 1 h. Saline served as a negative control (0 % lysis) and distilled water as a positive control (100 % lysis). Intact erythrocytes were removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm.

The pharmacokinetic behavior of Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL was studied using Panc-1 tumor-bearing BALB/c nude mice. When tumor volumes reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to three treatment groups (n = 12 per group) and administered Gem, Gem@PSL, or Gem@CPL (5 mg/kg Gem equivalent dose, n = 12) intravenously via the tail vein. Blood samples (200 μL per mouse) were collected from the orbital sinus using heparinized tubes at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h post-administration (n = 3 per time point per group). The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 mins to obtain the plasma, which was then stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Following blood collection, the corresponding mice (n = 3 per time point per group) were euthanized by cranio-cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Tissue samples (including the heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, spleens and tumors) were harvested at each designated time point 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h. Every tissue sample was thoroughly rinsed in ice-cold saline to eliminate blood and other contaminants, then blotted dry with filter paper. An accurately weighted amount of each tissue samples was homogenized in PBS, then mixed with pre-cooled normal saline and homogenized at 65 Hz for 3 to 5 mins. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Gem concentrations in biological samples were determined using LC-MS method. 200 μL plasma and tissue homogenate samples were spiked with 10 μL of a 5-Fu working solution (internal standard, IS) and vortex-mixed with 400 μL of a water-acetonitrile solution (v/v, 4:1) for 3 mins. The samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 5 mins to precipitate proteins. Subsequently, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore organic phase filter membrane, and 5 μL was injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

HPLC separations were performed using an ODS C18 (100 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 3.5 μm). The isocratic mobile phase consisted of water and acetate in a 1:1 v/v ratio, running at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. For positive ion mode, MRM transitions were set at m/z 263.2 → 110.0 for gemcitabine and m/z 130.0 → 40.0 for the internal standard (5-Fu). Gem exhibited a retention time of 6 min, with a recovery rate exceeding 95 % and a standard curve demonstrating an r2 value of 0.999. Pharmacokinetic variables were calculated using DAS 2.0 software from the National Drug Evaluation Center of China. For further details regarding LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis, please refer to the supplementary materials and methods.

Antitumor efficacy of Gem@CPL in vivo

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the developed formulations was evaluated in BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) bearing subcutaneous pancreatic tumors. BALB/c athymic nude mice harboring Panc-1 xenograft tumors were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5): PBS, Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL. The specified formulations were administered intravenously via tail vein injection every two days for a total of seven times (Gem-equivalent dose: 25 mg/kg). Mice weight and tumor volume were monitored every other day throughout the 15-day treatment. Following euthanasia on day 15, tumors were excised and weighed. Liver, heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested, formalin-fixed, and processed for histological analysis. Overall health was monitored throughout the experiment. The treatment's effects on tumor tissue were evaluated using histological (H&E), apoptosis (TUNEL), immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence staining to assess morphology, apoptosis, and protein expression.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out a minimum of three times, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6 (San Diego, USA). To assess statistical significance between groups, a two-tailed Student's t-test was employed. Significance levels are denoted as follows: ⁎p < 0.05, ⁎⁎p < 0.01, and ⁎⁎⁎p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Preparation of Gem@CPL

This study investigates the comparative changes in size and polydispersity index (PDI) of PSL prepared using four different methods: reverse evaporation, thin lipid film hydration, freezing-thawed method, and a combination of thin lipid film hydration with the freezing-thawed method. As shown in Fig. S1, the results indicate that liposomes prepared using the hydration and freezing-thawed method exhibit a size of 121 ± 0.88 nm with a narrow distribution over a 15-day period.

Based on this preparation method, we employed AI-assisted RSM to optimize the formulation and preparation process of PSL. Based on this preparation method, we employed AI-assisted Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to optimize the formulation and preparation process of pH-responsive liposomes (PSL). As shown in Fig. 1A–B, the investigated factors—drug-to-lipid ratio, temperature, and phospholipid composition—significantly affected the drug loading capacity and zeta potential. The results from the AI-RSM indicated that the optimal concentration ratio of the phospholipids (DPPC, DOPE, CHEMS, Chol, and mPEG2000-DSPE) is 55:27:18:20:5 (molar ratio), while the optimal drug-to-lipid ratio is 2.42 (w/w). As shown in Fig. 1C, the optimal ratio between CD276 scFv and the traut's agent is 1:5 and the best linking ratio for the modified CD276 and MAL-PEG2000-DSPE is 1:100, resulting in a coupling efficiency of approximately 95 %, with 90 % of the linked units contributing to the total amount (Zhong et al., 2025).

As shown in Fig. S2A, increasing the Gem concentration results in a continuous decrease in the absorbance of Cu2+ at 650 nm in a 50 mM copper gluconate solution. When the molar ratio of Gem to Cu2+ reaches 4:1, the absorbance drops to half of that in the blank control group. Further increases in concentration do not significantly change the absorbance compared to the 200 mM complexed state. Therefore, the optimal gemcitabine ratio in the Gem supramolecular self-assembled nanoparticles with 270 mM TEOA is 4:1 with Cu2+. This formulation was subsequently used to prepare Gem@PCL and Gem@CPL for further experiments.

Characterization of Gem@CPL

The Gem@CPL was preparation using a combination of thin lipid film hydration with the freezing-thawed method. As shown in Fig. 1D–G and Fig. S2B, Gem@CPL exhibited a yellow-brown opaque color, with HPLC results indicating a DL capacity of 14.56 ± 1.16 %, which is over 4 times that of CPL loaded with free Gem. Moreover, the particle size and zeta potential of the Gem@CPL were comparable to those of the blank CPL, measuring 170 nm and − 39.67 ± 2.08 mV, respectively, suggesting that the properties of the Gem@CPL are similar to those of the blank CPL. TEM analysis confirmed that the particle size was consistent with DLS results, showing a nearly spherical shape and uniform dispersion without aggregation, indicating good stability. Over a 60-day stability assessment, the particle size of the blank group ranged from 150 to 180 nm, with a PDI of 15 % to 25 %. The Gem@PSL group showed a particle size range of 180 to 200 nm and a PDI of 18 % to 26 %. The Gem@CPL group had a particle size of 170 to 200 nm and a PDI range of 13 % to 25 %. These results indicate that both the Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL exhibit particle sizes and PDI values comparable to those of the blank group, demonstrating good stability (Fig. S3).

In this study, we used pH 7.4, pH 6.5, and pH 5.5 to simulate the normal physiological environment, tumor microenvironment (TEM), and acidic environment of tumor cell lysosomes, respectively, to investigate the in vitro drug release behaviors of Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL. As shown in Fig. 2D, Gem release varies significantly under different pH conditions. However, the release profiles of Gem in Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL are quite similar, indicating that the modification with the CD276 tumor-specific versatile antibody does not affect the pH-responsive drug release characteristics of PSL. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), both Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL displayed a cumulative release of approximately 60 % over 72 h, suggesting that the liposomes can extend the drug's stability in the body. At pH 6.5, Gem release reached about 60 % within 12 h, indicating that the carrier can rapidly release the drug when it accumulates in the TEM. Upon targeting and being taken up by tumor cells, the acidic conditions in lysosomes (pH 5.5) accelerate gemcitabine release. Both formulations released around 80 % of Gem within approximately 2 h, with nearly 100 % release at 48 h. This demonstrates that the Gem@CPL can quickly release the drug upon entering the cells, effectively targeting and killing tumor cells (Guo et al., 2024).

CD276 expression assessment and Gem@CPL uptake analysis

To determine the surface expression of CD276 (B7-H3) on human pancreatic cancer cell lines (Panc-1 and BXPC-3) using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 2A–B, the expression levels of the CD276 antigen in BXPC-3 and Panc-1 human pancreatic cancer cells reached 99.0 %, while the positive rate for the isotype control group was only 0.12 %. These results indicate that both BXPC-3 and Panc-1 human pancreatic cancer cells specifically express the CD276 protein.

To verify the drug uptake of PSL and CD276-scFv modified CPL in pancreatic cancer cells, we employed FACS and CLSM to monitor the uptake of FITC@PSL and FITC@CPL by BXPC-3 and Panc-1 cells over different time points. As indicated in Fig. 2C–F, the uptake of both formulations increased over 0.5 to 8 h. At each time point, the fluorescence intensity of the CD276-scFv modified FITC@CPL was significantly higher than that of FITC@PSL, suggesting that the CD276-scFv modification enhances the uptake efficiency of FITC@CPL in tumor cells, thereby increasing drug accumulation. Quantitative analysis revealed that the uptake efficiency of CPL was approximately 47 % higher than that of PSL in Panc-1 cells, and about 36 % higher in BxPC-3 cells.

We also assessed cellular uptake after co-culturing for 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 h. As shown in Fig. 3A–B, the fluorescence intensity of green FITC gradually increased over time, indicating notable uptake by BXPC-3 cells. The CD276-modified FITC@CPL exhibited greater cellular accumulation compared to FITC@PSL. Furthermore, the red fluorescence from the CD276 antibody was localized to the tumor cell membrane, indicating active targeting and binding to the CD276 antigen, facilitating enhanced internalization of the nanoparticle delivery system. Additionally, at each time point, the fluorescence intensity of FITC@CPL was greater than that of FITC@PSL in both cell types, demonstrating superior uptake performance and effective targeting capabilities of the CPL. The FACS results were consistent with the qualitative observations from CLSM, confirming that FITC@CPL has a higher capacity for active tumor cell targeting and accumulation, thus enhancing intracellular drug concentration and improving anti-pancreatic cancer efficacy.

Endocytosis mechanism of CPL study

To further investigate the targeting accumulation and internalization mechanisms of PSL and CD276-scFv modified CPL, we explored the endocytosis mechanisms (Kostarnoy et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3C, after incubating FITC@PSL and FITC@CPL at 4 °C for 2 h, the uptake efficiencies were 32.53 ± 2.48 % and 20.80 ± 0.77 %, respectively. Following the addition of amiloride and wortmannin, the ability of Panc-1 cells to uptake both nanoparticles significantly decreased, indicating that the uptake of these nanoparticles primarily relies on ATP-dependent energy and macropinocytosis. In comparison, following the addition of chlorpromazine, a known inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, the uptake of FITC@PSL was 82.36 ± 4.78 %, while that of FITC@CPL was 65.94 ± 6.16 %, confirming that the CD276-scFv modification does enhance cellular uptake of PSL (Table 1). Additionally, when the cells were treated with the caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor, filipin, both nanoparticles exhibited strong fluorescence intensity, suggesting that the inhibitory effect of this agent was weaker than that of the other inhibitors. In summary, macropinocytosis and ATP-dependent mechanisms play significant roles in the internalization of the nanoparticles, and clathrin-mediated endocytosis also contributes to the cellular uptake of FITC@CPL.

Cell cycle assay of Gem@CPL

As demonstrated in Fig. 4A, Free Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL all induced cell cycle arrest, primarily concentrated in the G1 and G2 phases, compared to the control group. This effect was accompanied by a notable decrease in the proportion of cells in the S phase. This shift is likely due to the direct interaction of Free Gem, and Gemcitabine released from the nanodelivery systems, with cellular DNA. This interaction leads to G1/G0 arrest and, consequently, a reduction in the S phase cell population. These results indicate that Free Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL effectively disrupt DNA replication and protein synthesis within tumor cells, contributing to their observed anti-tumor activity.

Apoptosis and cytotoxicity analysis of Gem@CPL

The apoptotic potential of Free Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL on Panc-1 and BXPC-3 cells was evaluated using Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining followed by flow cytometry (Fig. 3D–F). After 12 h of treatment with Gem@CPL, the total percentage of early and late apoptotic Panc-1 and BXPC-3 cells was 20.6 ± 3.44 % and 50.7 ± 1.2 %, respectively (Table 2). These values represent a 3.2-fold and 1.5-fold increase compared to Free Gem, and a 1.5-fold and 1.2-fold increase compared to Gem@PSL. These results indicate that CD276-scFv-modified Gem@CPL effectively induces apoptosis in tumor cells, likely due to its active targeting capability which enhances drug accumulation within the cells.

In cell proliferation inhibition assays, blank liposomes, at lipid concentrations ranging from 0.1 nM to 1000 nM, showed no significant inhibitory effect on either Panc-1 or BXPC-3 cells after 48 h of incubation. Even at the highest concentration (1000 nM), cell viability remained above 80 % for both cell lines. These results indicate that blank liposomes do not interfere with the normal growth of Panc-1 and BXPC-3 cells and exhibit good biocompatibility (as shown in Fig. 4B–C). In contrast, treatment with Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL for 48 h resulted in half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of 80.65 nM and 58.53 nM for BXPC-3 cells, and 206.1 nM and 136.6 nM for Panc-1 cells, respectively. Compared to the IC50 values of free Gemcitabine (116 nM and 249.1 nM, respectively), both nanoformulations demonstrated approximately a 30 % reduction in IC50. Gem@CPL exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect, further suggesting its superior ability to suppress tumor cell proliferation.

Biocompatibility and pharmacokinetic evaluation of Gem@CPL in vivo

The Gem supramolecular self-assembly and directional modification of Gem@CPL with CD276 svFv demonstrate good biocompatibility, which is essential for their further applications. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct hemocompatibility testing to prevent interactions with blood upon entry into the bloodstream, as this may lead to thrombosis (blood clotting), hemolysis (destruction of red blood cells), or other harmful side effects. The results in Fig. S6 show that mouse blood undergoes hemolysis in pure water due to low osmotic pressure, leading to a hemolysis rate of 100 %. In contrast, the hemolysis rate of PSL is 1.21 ± 0.16 %, indicating good stability in blood. The hemolysis rate for Gem supermolecular self-assembly nanoparticles are 6.62 ± 0.31 %. However, when Gem is loaded into PSL (Gem@PSL), the hemolysis rate decreases to 3.04 ± 0.38 %, suggesting that PSL improves biocompatibility and reduces the drug's hemolytic effect. Additionally, when PSL is modified with CD276-scFv and loaded with Gem (Gem@CPL), the hemolysis rate further decreases to 2.70 ± 0.45 %, which is lower than that of PSL alone. These findings indicate that both PSL and CPL have hemolysis rates below 5 %, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility and their potential as effective drug carriers for reducing hemolysis associated with chemotherapy drugs (Table 3). The progressive reduction in hemolysis from the free drug assembly to the encapsulated and targeted forms suggests that the nano-formulation effectively shields the drug's cytotoxic effects from circulating blood components. This protective effect, combined with the active tumor targeting conferred by the CD276-scFv, may potentially divert the drug away from the bone marrow niche, thereby reducing the risk of systemic hematological toxicity commonly associated with free gemcitabine.

The results from Fig. 4C–F confirm that the Gem@CPL significantly enhances drug accumulation in tumor sites while notably reducing concentrations in non-target organs such as the heart, kidneys, and lungs. The considerable accumulation of both Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL in the spleen and liver aligns with the typical in vivo behavior of nanocarriers. This distribution pattern is primarily due to the function of the reticuloendothelial system (RES), in which these organs serve as the primary sites for the biological filtration of nanoparticles from circulation via resident macrophages. Although such sequestration reduces the drug dose reaching the tumor, it is a well-documented pharmacokinetic feature that highlights the complexity of nanoparticle delivery in vivo (Shi et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025b). After 30 mins of administration, the concentrations of Gem, Gem@PSL, and Gem@CPL in the tumor were 440.24 ± 5.69 ng/g, 547.72 ± 10.70 ng/g, and 581.88 ± 37.33 ng/g, respectively. Gem@CPL exhibited a significant increase in tumor accumulation, being approximately 32.2 % higher than free Gem and about 6.2 % greater than Gem@PSL at this early time point. After 4 h, the tumor concentrations for the three groups were 310.22 ± 61.90 ng/g, 430.22 ± 28.07 ng/g, and 449.24 ± 117.80 ng/g, respectively. Throughout this period, Gem@CPL consistently showed the highest tumor concentration, demonstrating a 44.8 % enhancement over free Gem. These quantitative results confirm that CD276-scFv modification effectively promotes the targeted enrichment and sustained retention of Gem@CPL in tumor tissue. The Gem group exhibited a gradual decline in drug concentration due to its short half-life and rapid clearance. In contrast, the decline in concentrations for Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL was significantly slower, indicating that the intelligent pH-responsive nanoplatforms effectively accumulate in tumor sites and rapidly cleared through the liver and kidneys, enhancing anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing side effects.

This study further employed a small animal imaging system to investigate the in vivo biodistribution of the CD276 tumor-specific versatile targeting drug delivery system. As shown in Fig. 4G–H, Panc-1 tumor-bearing BALB/c nude mice were injected with DiR@PSL and DiR@DPL, and fluorescence imaging was performed at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h. Results revealed that the fluorescence intensity in the tumor increased over time for both formulations, with DiR@DPL demonstrating significantly better performance than DiR@PSL. After 24 h, both liposome formulations maintained strong fluorescence intensity in the tumor, indicating prolonged circulation in the mice. Furthermore, DiR@DPL accumulated in tumor sites via CD276-mediated active targeting. These findings confirm that DiR@DPL has excellent targeting capabilities, effectively enriching in the tumor area, which aligns with results from in vitro cell targeting experiments.

Antitumor efficacy of Gem@CPL in vivo

The in vivo assessment of the CD276 tumor-specific versatile targeting nanoplatforms is vital for evaluating its overall therapeutic efficacy and safety, essential steps toward clinical translation. After two weeks of treatment, Gem, a first-line chemotherapeutic for pancreatic cancer, achieved a tumor suppression rate of 48.6 % (Fig. 5A–C). However, mice treated with free Gem experienced significant weight loss, indicating systemic toxicity and reduced health. In contrast, Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL showed higher tumor suppression rates of 76.9 % and 86.2 %, respectively, while maintaining body weights similar to controls. This suggests these nanoformulations extend Gem's half-life in vivo, with Gem@CPL's CD276-mediated active targeting markedly enhancing tumor accumulation and destruction. Histological analysis (Fig. 6B) confirmed the safety advantages: free Gem caused substantial tissue damage and inflammation in the spleen, lungs, liver, and kidneys. Gem@PSL reduced tissue injury, with only localized inflammation, while Gem@CPL exhibited minimal organ damage, demonstrating superior biocompatibility. Overall, this pharmacological evaluation shows that the CD276-targeted system not only improves antitumor efficacy but also significantly reduces systemic toxicity, offering a promising approach for pancreatic cancer treatment.

Pancreatic cancer's unique challenges stem from its deep anatomical location and dense ECM barrier formation during tumor progression, which markedly impede drug delivery and promote resistance. In this study, post-treatment, we isolated primary organs and tumor tissues for analysis. Immunohistochemistry of CD34 assessed neovascularization, while multicolor immunofluorescence of α-SMA and FAP evaluated stromal fibrous barriers. As shown in Fig. 5D, the control group displayed severe ECM fibrosis, reflecting typical pancreatic cancer pathology. After treatment, the free Gem group still exhibited dense stroma with tumor cells tightly encapsulated. In contrast, Gem@PSL partially reduced stromal density, enabling some drug penetration. Most notably, Gem@CPL significantly decreased α-SMA and FAP levels, exposing tumor cells by breaking down the ECM barrier and facilitating deeper infiltration. While the current immunofluorescence approach effectively captured specific stromal cell markers, future studies incorporating non-specific histological stains such as Masson's trichrome would provide complementary morphological evidence of collagen remodeling and further validate the stromal disruption observed in this study. Furthermore, Fig. 6A revealed that treatment with Gem@PSL and Gem@CPL markedly reduced microvessel density, especially in the Gem@CPL group, where vascular density and surrounding tumor cells decreased significantly. This indicates effective inhibition of tumor angiogenesis. These data highlight the high expression of CD276 on pancreatic tumor cells, neovasculature, and stromal fibroblasts—correlating with poor prognosis. This unique expression profile enables our CD276-scFv modified CPL to execute a “pan-tumoral” targeting strategy. Although its full immunomodulatory role remains to be clarified, CD276 is a promising therapeutic target. Overall, Gem@CPL, via CD276-scFv modification, precisely targets and accumulates in tumor tissue, effectively kills tumor cells, and reduces toxicity, underscoring its potential as a pancreatic cancer therapy.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully developed a CD276 tumor-specific versatile targeting nanoplatform (Gem@CPL) for pancreatic cancer therapy, utilizing CD276-scFv modification for precise tumor targeting and improved drug delivery. By exploiting the high expression of CD276 on tumor cells, neovasculature, and stromal fibroblasts, we validated CD276 as a promising immunotherapeutic target to improve treatment outcomes. The nanoplatform effectively penetrates the dense ECM of pancreatic tumors, achieving deeper infiltration and superior antitumor efficacy compared to free Gem and unmodified formulations. It significantly inhibits tumor growth and angiogenesis while reducing systemic toxicity, highlighting its therapeutic potential. Additionally, preliminary evaluations of its in vivo pharmacokinetics and biosafety provide supportive data for potential future translation and clinical application. Despite these promising results, the immunomodulatory role of CD276 remains to be fully elucidated, necessitating further mechanistic studies. Future research should also explore combination therapies to maximize clinical benefits.
