Machine learning-based construction of an oxidative stress-related model reveals molecular subtypes and tumor microenvironment infiltration signatures in colon cancer
Authors: Baizhi Zhai, Yi Zhao, YuXiang Fan, Xiaowei Xia, Yi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColon cancer is a common malignant tumor of digestive system. More and more research has shown that oxidative stress plays an important role in the development and progression of colon cancer. Oxidative stress occurs when redox homeostasis in cells is broken which is usually accompanied by overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS).MethodsIn this study, we constructed a colon cancer prognostic model based on oxidative stress-related (OSR) genes. Lasso regression and multivariable cox proportional hazards were performed to identify the best gene signature. We established the prognostic model through OSR genes. To further explore the function of OSR genes, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were applied to reveal relative mechanisms. External datasets were employed for validation, and molecular biology experiments were conducted to verify the functional roles of key genes in colon cancer.ResultsBy screening out oxidative stress-related genes associated with prognosis, we constructed a prognosis model based on PXDNL, PTGS2, DLAT, GLRX, P4HA1 and P4HA3. Through validation with external datasets, the model accurately predicted the prognosis of colon cancer patients. Moreover, we also utilized single sample GSEA (ssGSEA) to investigate the degree of immune infiltration in the tumor environment. In addition, we conducted cell experiments to confirm siGLRX can induce tumor cell apoptosis by promoting the level of oxidative stress in cells.ConclusionThese results suggest that oxidative stress-related genes may serve as potential biomarkers for the prognosis of colon cancer, providing guidance for clinical treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04308-y.Keywords: Oxidative stress, Machine learning, Colon cancer, Prognosis, ROS, Immune infiltration, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Colon cancer is one of the most common malignant gastrointestinal tumors. It ranks third as the third most common cancer in the world and is the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Despite significant advances in early diagnosis, surgery, chemoradiotherapy, and clinical prognostic prediction in recent decades, the prognosis for colorectal cancer patients remains poor [2]. Currently, the TNM staging system is widely accepted and recognized as the most reliable independent indicator to reflect the progression of malignant tumors and judge the prognosis [3]. It is also the primary basis for determining the scope of surgical resection, surgical method, and reasonable adjuvant therapy [4]. However, the clinical value of tumor histological type and grade remains unclear. Therefore, the development of new prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers for colon cancer is particularly necessary.

As basic research on colon cancer deepens, increasing evidence suggests that the occurrence and progression of this disease are closely linked to oxidative stress [5–7]. Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between the production of oxidants and antioxidants, leading to an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are involved in the onset and development of cancer [8]. Furthermore, ischemia modified albumin (IMA) is a modified form of serum albumin that is produced by ROS and is recognized as a reliable marker of oxidative stress [9]. Some studies have suggested that measuring serum IMA levels can serve as a marker for detecting the postoperative course of colon cancer patients [10]. Additionally, the level of IMA in different TNM stages is closely related to the prognosis of colon cancer patients.

The dynamic and paradoxical role of oxidative stress in the complex landscape of cancer biology represents a fundamental area of research. Oxidative stress arises from an imbalance between the production of ROS and the cell’s antioxidant defense mechanisms [11]. Elevated ROS levels can directly damage nucleic acids, causing single- and double-strand DNA breaks, base modifications, and cross-linking [11]. If not efficiently repaired, this oxidative damage introduces mutations in critical genes, including proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressors. The pathogenic mechanisms of oxidative stress are multifaceted, driving tumor development through the induction of genomic instability, the modulation of key signaling pathways, the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and the regulation of novel cell death modalities like ferroptosis [12].

Furthermore, oxidative stress profoundly remodels the tumor microenvironment (TME) [13]. High ROS levels in the TME can promote the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby increasing the invasive and metastatic potential of cancer cells [13]. Concurrently, ROS suppress the anti-tumor functions of immune cells like cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, while fostering the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [14]. This creates an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates immune evasion. In an adaptive response to their self-generated oxidative milieu, cancer cells frequently upregulate their antioxidant defense systems, most notably the NRF2-KEAP1 pathway [15]. This master regulator of the antioxidant response enhances the expression of enzymes like glutathione peroxidases (GPXs) and glutaredoxins (GLRXs), enabling tumors to maintain redox homeostasis, support their survival, and develop resistance to conventional radio- and chemotherapy [16].

In recent years, high-throughput sequencing technology has rapidly developed, enabling the acquisition of a large amount of genetic and clinical data from cancer patients [17]. By deeply mining these datasets, we can decipher the genetic mechanisms of tumorigenesis and tumor evolution, along with shifts in the immune microenvironment. This molecular-level understanding of cancer progression ultimately paves the way for personalized treatment strategies. Bioinformatics provides a powerful approach to analyze multi-gene expression data from these sources, enabling the screening of key genes and the construction of robust prognostic models.

This study primarily utilized public datasets to develop and validate prognostic features of colon cancer based on oxidative stress-related genes. The verification of these genes effectively predicted the prognosis of colon cancer patients and shed light on the relationship between oxidative stress-related genes and the immune microenvironment. These findings have potential clinical applications.

Materials and methods

TCGA and GEO data download and collection

The mRNA expression data of colon cancer patients and corresponding clinical data were downloaded from the official website of the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). A total of 451 patients’ data were collected, but only 375 cases with complete clinical information and survival data were included in the study. Additionally, the GSE39582 and GSE17536 chip datasets, which included overall survival time, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The GSE39582 dataset consisted of 585 samples and the GSE17536 dataset consisted of 177 samples.

Oxidative stress related genes

The NMF algorithm identifies the molecular subtype

Oxidative stress-related (OSR) genes were combined with a non-negative matrix clustering algorithm (NMF) to cluster COAD queues. The clustering number was determined after 50 iterations using the “brunet” selection criterion. The number of clusters, denoted by the value of k, was set to 2–10 [18].

Lasso Cox regression analysis

The Lasso method is a variable selection technique that uses an L1 penalty term. This approach applies a restriction that restricts the model’s total absolute parameter values [19]. During the process of selecting important features, variables whose coefficients are equal to zero after compression are eliminated to minimize prediction error.

Construction and validation of OSR-related prognostic risk model

The TCGA-COAD and GEO databases were used for training and validation of the model. The resulting risk score was obtained by the following formula: Riskscore = a.Where n is the number of genes, Coefi is the risk coefficient, and xi is the expression level of OSR genes in this risk model [20]. Patient risk is categorized as low or high based on the median risk score, which is determined using a specific formula. To discriminate between patients at high and low risk, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used. The accuracy of risk model prediction was then investigated using time-ROC analysis [21]. Finally, the effect of the risk signature in predicting the prognoses of COAD patients was validated using the GSE39582 and GSE17536.

The construction of nomogram

Nomogram was established in R using the “rms” package to predict the prognosis of patients by combining risk scores and various clinicopathological factors [22]. A calibration curve was developed, and the impact of prognostic factors was assessed using the C-index and Akaike information criteria (AIC) to gauge the model’s predictions’ accuracy [23].

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) analysis was used to compare the possible differentially expressed genes and signaling pathways between different risk groups of OSR genes [24]. “clusterProfiler” and “msigdbr” R package were utilized to perform GSEA analysis. In GSEA analysis, the Molecular Signature Database (MsigDB) provided by GSEA was used as the path annotation source (including KEGG, REACTOME and other common path annotation data) [25, 26].

Characteristic differences of low and high risks

Single sample GSEA (ssGSEA), ESTIMATE and CIBERSORT were used to quantify immune microenvironment [27]. CIBERSORT is a linear support vector regression-based deconvolution technique for transcriptional expression matrices. It can assess the cell composition in various tumor samples using conventional gene expression data [28]. It has been shown in a range of diseases to have good accuracy and the capacity to simultaneously measure numerous types of immune cells. The ESTIMATE algorithm uses gene expression data to infer the ratio of stromal cells to immune cells in a tumor sample [29]. The CIBERSORT algorithm was used to calculate the proportion of 22 types of immune cells in the samples based on a significance threshold of P < 0.05, and the results were visualized.

Cell culture

Cell lines and culture conditions: Colon cancer cell lines HCT116, HT29 and normal colon epithelial cells NCM460 (Cell Bank of Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were used for the experiment. HCT116, HT29 and NCM460 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (GIBCO, Billings, MT, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (1 g, LuKang, China), respectively, at 37 °C and 5% CO2 [30].

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from HCT116, HT29 and NCM460 cells using TRIzol reagent (100mL, Vazyme, China), and the RNA concentration and purity were determined using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (ALLSHENG, China) [31]. For subsequent experiments, only samples having purity values of A260/280 < 2 and A260/230 > 1.8 were taken into consideration. As directed by the manufacturer, a cDNA synthesis kit was used to create cDNA from 1 µg of total RNA [32]. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR-Green (500rxns, Vazyme, China) and the CFX96 Touch real-time PCR assay system (Bio-rad, USA). The primer sequences used can be found in Table 2.

Cell transfection

The cells were treated with GLRX siRNA (5′ UACCUGCAACUGAGGAUUC 3′) at a final concentration of 30 nM according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Biotend, China) [33].

Cell counting Kit-8 assay

Cells were inoculated in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and cultured at 37 ℃. CCK-8 (10 µL/ well) was added and cultured [34]. The absorbance was read at 450 nm by using microplate reader (MOLECULAR DEVICES, USA).

Wound healing assay

HCT116 and HT29 cells were uniformly inoculated in the 6-well plate with 5 × 105 cells per well. Use 20 µL pipette head for scratching. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (GIBCO, Billings, MT, USA) culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum was continued, and images were taken at 0, 24 h time points [35].

Transwell assay

Using a 24-well plate with a 12.0 μm pore size and a hydrophilic polytetrafluoroethylene cell culture chamber, transwell assays were carried out. Each well’s lower chamber received 600 µL of full medium after the transwell chamber had been pre-infiltrated. Serum-free media was used to resuspend the cells, and 200 µL of cell suspension were added to the upper chamber to make 15 × 104 cells per well [35].

Western blotting

For protein extraction, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, China) with protease inhibitors (1:100, Thermo Scientific). Western blotting used the following primary antibodies: GLRX (1:1000; ABclonal, A23246), β-actin (1:100,000; ABclonal, AC038), NRF2 (1:1000; ABclonal, A21176), SOD2 (1:1000; ABclonal, A21805), BCL-2 (1:1000; ABclonal, A19693), BAX (1:1000; ABclonal, A12009), Caspase-3(1:1000; ABclonal, A19664), Cleaved-Caspase-3(1:1000; ABclonal, A28349). Membranes were cut horizontally according to the molecular weight markers prior to antibody incubation.

Statistical analysis

The Wilcox test was used to assess the levels of gene expression in colon cancer and healthy colon cells. The prognostic risk signature of OSR genes was chosen using Cox regression analyses. The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed to compare group differences. Clinical data were collected using Chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. T-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for statistical analysis. The results were expressed as mean ± standard error (SEM), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of molecular subtypes

The complete course of our analysis was depicted in a flow chart (Fig. 1). According to PCA results, colon cancer samples were separated from normal samples, but colon cancer samples were more dispersed (Fig. 2A). We then focused our analysis on colon cancer samples. Global changes in gene expression between colon cancer and normal samples were studied by applying measures of difference in expression profiles, namely Euclidean distance. We can see that the differences between colon cancer samples are much more significant than the differences between normal samples (Fig. 2B). Then, COAD samples were clustered by non-negative matrix clustering algorithm (Fig. 2D). On the basis of the co-phenotype, dispersion and residual, the optimal number of clusters was selected as 2 (Fig. 2C). Two distinct regulatory patterns were found using nonnegative matrix clustering based on the expression levels of prognostic genes (Table 2) in COAD, with 107 cases in the oxidative stress-related cluster 1 and 314 cases in cluster 2 (Fig. 2E). The two patient clusters were also subjected to survival analysis, and the results revealed that patients in cluster 1 had a considerably higher chance of surviving than those in cluster 2 (Fig. 2F). The results of gene expression heat map showed that OSR related genes were significantly different in cluster 1 and cluster 2 subtypes, and combined with clinical staging and age distribution, OSR related genes could play a certain role in most colon cancer patients (Fig. 2E).

Lasso cox analysis

Firstly, the TCGA-COAD cohort was divided into training set and test set in 7:3 ratio. All samples were separated randomly into groups in order to prevent the impact of random allocation on model stability. The lasso-cox regression model was built using 47 OSR genes (Table 3), and 10-fold cross-validation was used to identify the best model. When the model obtained the smallest loss, the penalty parameter = 0.0017 was chosen, and the 22 OSR genes added to the model were derived (Fig. 3A, B). TXNRD1, PTGS2, PHGDH, MPO, GSTM2, NOS2, GLRX5, GLRX, COX4I2, QSOX2, MSRA, ASPHD2, P4HA1, TXNDC12, PHF2, ALOX12, GSR, PYCR2, CYB5R2, P4HA3, PXDNL, and DLAT are the corresponding genes. Finally, for these 22 OSR genes, a multivariate cox regression model was further established according to AIC value (907.4) (Fig. 3C). To further narrow down the range of genes, by analyzing the gene expression and differential multiples of oxidative stress-related genes, we found that PTGS2, P4HA1, PXDNL, DLAT, P4HA3, GLRX played important roles in oxidative stress (Fig. 3E). Meanwhile, according to cox regression analysis, the C index of the model was 0.7, and 6 risk genes which include PTGS2, P4HA1, P4HA3, PXDNL, DLAT and GLRX were screened (Fig. 3D). The TCGA-COAD cohort revealed that there were notable differences in the expression of 6 OSR genes in tumor and para-carcinoma tissue by xiantao academic (https://www.xiantaozi.com). (Fig. 3F–K).

Construction of the prognostic risk model

The TCGA-COAD were used as model training. To further confirm the clustering results, prognostic models for all colon cancer patients were constructed. The 6-selected genes’ potential as prognostic indicators in COAD patients were demonstrated by the multivariable cox regression analysis. The survival status was subsequently evaluated in the Kaplan-Meier Plotter datasets, which showed that all selected genes were included in the prognostic risk model and had good survival prediction performance (Fig. 4A, B). Robust validation of OSR genes-related prognostic model by GEO

TCGA-COAD cohort and the independent GEO datasets, GSE39582 and GSE17536 were used for model testing and verification. The test cohort was split into low and high-risk groups based on the median risk score of the test group. Patients in the low-risk group exhibited clear survival advantages, according to the results of the survival analysis. According to the expression level, we determined the risk score for each sample and showed the distribution of the risk score for the sample (Fig. 6A, D, G). The KM survival analysis have shown that the high-risk patients were less likely to survive (Fig. 6B, E, H). The testing-cohort’s time-ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.65, 0.73, and 0.84, respectively (Fig. 6C). The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year time-ROC curves for GSE39582 were 0.79, 0.93, and 0.88, respectively (Fig. 6F). GSE17536’s time-ROC curves at 1 and 3 years were 0.69 and 0.69, respectively (Fig. 6I). We also compared the models of Wang et al. [36] and Chu et al. [37] and found that our model had a higher C-index. Meanwhile, the AUC results also demonstrated that our model performed better (Fig. 6J, K).

To ascertain the prognostic predictive value of OSR genes-related risk models, multivariate Cox regressions were conducted (Fig. 7A). The nomogram was used to forecast the 3-year and 5-year survival rates (Fig. 7B). The calibration curve demonstrates that the actual and anticipated survival performance are well matched (Fig. 7C). In addition, the predictive accuracy of TCGA-COAD was greatly increased when paired with age, pathological stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and risk score.

The risk model corresponds with clinicopathological characteristics to confirm the prognostic model connected to the OSR genes’ impact on COAD. Given the later clinical stage, greater tumor size, and lymph node metastases, the risk score was significantly higher, confirming the strong concordance between the risk score and prognosis (Fig. 7D–G).

Correlation between risk score and degree of immune infiltration

To further elucidate the intrinsic biological differences leading to high and low risk scores, immune infiltration was assessed by CIBERSORT, ssGSEA and ESTIMATE algorithms. We evaluated the effect of low and high-risk score on stromal score, immune score, and tumor purity using the ESTIMATE algorithm (Fig. 8A–D). The results showed that the levels of immune score and stromal score in the low-risk score group were significantly higher than those in the high-risk score group, while the tumor purity in the high-risk score group was significantly higher than that in the low-risk score group. Furthermore, 22 and 28 immune cells were calculated for each patient using CIBERSORT and ssGSEA algorithm. We observed significant differences in the distribution of 9 types of immune cells (40.9%) (Fig. 8E, F). Among these immune cells, macrophage M0 were highly expressed in the high-risk score group, while the other eight immune cells were highly expressed in the low-risk score group (Fig. 8E). To this end, we evaluated how the expression of 6 OSR genes correlates with immune cell abundance. All 6 genes demonstrated obvious correlations with various immune cell types (Fig. 8G, H). These results suggest that the reason for the better prognosis in the low-risk score group compared with the high-risk score group may be related to the immune microenvironment in the tumor, and the low-risk score group may have a higher level of immune infiltration. Subsequent TIDE analysis of multiple datasets revealed that the high-risk group had significantly elevated TIDE and Exclusion scores, indicating reduced sensitivity to immunotherapy (Fig. 8I–L).

Effect of silencing GLRX on proliferation, migration and mechanism in colon cancer cells

To avoid false positive results from public databases, we further verified the expression of 6 OSR based on RT-PCR results from colon cancer cell line HCT116, HT29 and normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460. The results showed that DLAT, GLRX, P4HA1 were significantly up-regulated in colon cancer cell line HCT116, HT29 and P4HA3, PTGS2, PXDNL were significantly down-regulated in colon cancer cell line (Fig. 9A). These validation results were basically consistent with the TCGA-COAD cohort, further supporting the potential involvement of GLRX in the initiation and development of colon cancer.

Impact of GLRX on colon cancer cells. A The mRNA expression of 6 OSR genes (n = 5). B The verification of transfection efficiency (n = 5). C CCK8 proliferation assay of HCT116 and HT29 (n = 6). D, E Wound healing and transwell assay of HCT116 and HT29 (n = 3). F The protein expression of NRF2, SOD2, BAX, BCL-2, Caspase-3, Cleaved-Caspase-3 and Actin, uncropped scans of all blots are provided in Supplementary Information

We concentrated our study on GLRX since tumor cell lines express it most highly. To validate the biological function of GLRX in CRC cells, siRNA was designed to knock down the expression of GLRX. qRT-PCR was used to verify the efficiency of transfection (Fig. 9B). GLRX silencing dramatically decreased tumor cell viability, according to the CCK8 assay (Fig. 9C). Transwell assay and wound assay results similarly shown that GLRX silencing reduced the ability of tumor cells to migrate after 24 h (Fig. 9D, E). Based on the above results, we continue to explore the mechanism by which GLRX regulates colon cancer cells. NRF2 and SOD2 are important marker of oxidative stress levels. Through Western Blot experiments, it was found that compared with the control group, the protein levels of NRF2 and SOD2 in siGLRX colon cancer cells HCT116 were upregulated, indicating an increase in cellular oxidative stress levels. Considering that the continuous increase in oxidative stress levels eventually leads to apoptosis, we further studied the effect of GLRX on apoptosis. Compared with the control group, the BAX level of colon cancer cells with siGLRX was upregulated, the BCL-2 protein level was downregulated, and the Caspase3 protein level was significantly downregulated. The upregulation of Cleaved-Caspase3 level indicates an elevated level of apoptosis. Overall, siGLRX can induce tumor cell apoptosis by promoting the level of oxidative stress in cells (Fig. 9F).

Discussion

Colon cancer is a common malignant tumor of digestive system, and its prognosis is related to many factors [38]. In recent years, more and more studies have shown that oxidative stress plays a vital role in the occurrence and progression of colon cancer [38–41]. Oxidative stress refers to the excessive production of highly reactive oxidative molecules such as oxygen ions and free radicals in the body, which is associated with the initiation and development of many diseases, including tumors. Oxidative stress causes DNA damage, lipid peroxidation of cell membrane and protein oxidation, which further affects cell proliferation and migration and promotes tumor occurrence [39, 41, 42].

Oxidative stress arises from a disruption of cellular redox homeostasis, theoretically defined as a relative excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or reactive nitrogen species (RNS) over antioxidants [8]. ROS influence both cellular signaling and tumor cell growth. Classic examples include their effects on growth factor signaling, the regulation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) activity [43]. Furthermore, ROS have been reported to promote tumorigenesis by oxidizing Cys-797 on the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). This oxidation enhances EGFR tyrosine phosphorylation and activates downstream pathways such as Ras/MAPK and PI3K/AKT [44].

A central mechanism of oxidative stress in the TIME is the impairment of anti-tumor immunity. For instance, in breast cancer models, oxidative stress within the TIME increases methylation of SIRT1, a protein with tumor-suppressive secretory functions, via the methyltransferase SETD7. This modification inhibits SIRT1 secretion, which in turn upregulates PD-L1 expression on M2 macrophages and fosters CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Restoring extracellular SIRT1 suppresses PD-L1 and rejuvenates T cell function, highlighting an oxidative stress-driven immune evasion axis [45]. Similarly, in lung cancer, tumor-derived factors systemically activate NRF2 in bone marrow myeloid progenitors, inducing epigenetic reprogramming that drives the differentiation of immunosuppressive macrophages. These cells, characterized by high PD-L1 and ARG1 expression, inhibit T and NK cell activity, and targeting NRF2 reverses this immunosuppression [46]. Beyond transcriptional reprogramming, oxidative stress directly compromises effector immune cells. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells undergo disulfidptosis, a novel oxidative stress-induced death triggered by glucose deprivation and cystine uptake via SLC7A11. This process is regulated by the STAT3-LDHB-G6PD axis, which limits NADPH production and disrupts redox balance, exacerbating T cell exhaustion [47]. Concurrently, regulatory T cells (T cells) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) exploit ROS to suppress effector T cells and NK cells, further cementing an immunosuppressive niche [48].

The dual role of oxidative stress in tumorigenesis, acting both as a promoter of genomic instability and a potential barrier to cancer progression which underscores the importance of redox-regulating proteins like glutaredoxins (GLRX) [16]. Among these, GLRX3 has emerged as a critical player in maintaining redox homeostasis and supporting tumor survival [49]. As a type II monothiol glutaredoxin, GLRX3 is highly expressed in various cancers, including breast, lung, and nasopharyngeal carcinomas, where it regulates iron metabolism, antioxidant responses, and anti-apoptotic signaling [50]. For instance, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, GLRX3 upregulation correlates with enhanced iron metabolism and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promoting tumor proliferation and invasion. Knockdown of GLRX3 disrupts these processes, highlighting its role in tumor progression. Similarly, in lung cancer, GLRX3 supports cell survival by modulating the STAT3 pathway and suppressing caspase-3-mediated apoptosis [51]. These findings position GLRX3 as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in multiple cancers.

In this study, we developed a prognostic model for colon cancer using TCGA transcriptome data based on oxidative stress-related genes. The model demonstrated robust prognostic accuracy, which was further validated in an independent GEO cohort. These results underscore the critical role of oxidative stress-related genes in determining colon cancer outcomes.

Complementing the bioinformatic analysis, we performed in vitro functional assays using siRNA to knock down GLRX. This knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation and migration of colon cancer cells. Overall, our findings suggest that siGLRX induces tumor cell apoptosis by elevating intracellular oxidative stress levels, indicating that GLRX plays an important role in colon cancer progression and represents a potential therapeutic target. While the present functional validation focused on gene knockdown that sufficient to establish its necessity for cancer cell survival and progression, while future studies involving gene overexpression, mechanistic investigations, and clinical tissue validation will further solidify its role in driving malignant transformation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study constructed a prognosis model of colon cancer based on oxidative stress-related genes and verified the predictive ability of this model. At the same time, our cell experiment results further confirmed the significant role of these genes in colon cancer, providing a new way to further study the occurrence and development of colon cancer.

Although the prognosis model of colon cancer in this study has high predictive ability, there are still some limitations. First, we studied only six oxidative stress-related genes, and there may be other genes associated with colon cancer prognosis that have not been considered. Secondly, although we have verified the effects of GLRX on the proliferation and migration of colon cancer cells in our experiments, the specific mechanism of action of GLRX in the initiation and progression of colon cancer still needs further study.
