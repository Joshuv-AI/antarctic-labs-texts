Guardians of resistance and virulence: detection of mec, femA, Van, pvl, hlg and spa genes in methicillin and vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus from clinical and food samples in Southwestern Nigeria
Authors: Folasade Muibat Adeyemi, Omotayo Opemipo Oyedara, Nana Aishat Yusuf-Omoloye, Olaoniye Habeebat Ajigbewu, Onyinye Lynda Ndaji, Maryam Kikelomo Adegbite-Badmus, Taiwo Samson Olumakinde, Timilehin Emmanuel Oluokun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaphylococcus aureus strains are highly virulent and associated with an eclectic range of severe nosocomial and community-acquired infections.ObjectivesThis study assessed methicillin- and vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA/VRSA) from clinical and ready-to-eat (RTE) food sources, screened for antibiotic resistance; and molecular determinants of antibiotic and virulence genes.MethodsAltogether, 465 clinical and RTE food samples were analyzed via conventional microbiological techniques and S. aureus identification was confirmed by nuc gene detection. Phenotypic screening for methicillin and vancomycin-resistance was by agar-screen cum micro-broth dilution respectively, while antibiotic susceptibility testing was done by the disc-diffusion technique. VanA/vanB/VanC1, femA, mecA/mecC; pvl/hlg and spa gene detection was via Polymerase chain reaction.ResultsPhenotypically, 211 Staphylococcal isolates were recovered, 138 (65.4%) of them carrying the nuc gene – all 138 (100.0%) were VRSA, while 59/138 (42.8%) were MRSA phenotypically. Overall, 114/138 (82.6%), 7/138 (5.1%), and 6/138 (4.3%) of isolates had the femA, mecA, and mecC genes, while van genes were detected in only 3 (2.2%) isolates, with virulence determinants pvl, hlg, and spa gene carriage in 8 (5.8%), 10 (7.2%), and 77 (55.8%) isolates respectively. In all, 11.6% carried resistance-associated genes, 55.8% carried virulence genes, and co-detection of resistance and virulence genes was observed in 12.3%. Overall, 96/138 (69.6%) were multidrug-resistant (MDR), while one strain was extremely drug-resistant (XDR). MAR Indices ≥ 0.2 was observed in 83.3% of isolates.ConclusionThis study highlights virulence levels of MRSA and VRSA circulating strains in Osogbo, contributing to their sustained surveillance, and improving available data for successive epidemiology investigations. This study also reports the occurrence of the mecC gene in S. aureus isolates from RTE foods and human samples in Southwestern Nigeria.Keywords: Human sample, mecC, MRSA, Osogbo, RTE foods, Virulence, VRSA, femA, Spa, Pvl, Hlg

Introduction

Staphylococcus, a genus presently made up of 81 species and subspecies [1] is of the family Staphylococcaceae and order Bacillales. Staphylococcus aureus, a coagulase-positive member, is generally hosted in the nasal cavity of 25 to 30% of the healthy populace with no apparent clinical indication [2] regardless of public health interventions to curb its spread. It is highly virulent, versatile, and implicated in a wide range of severe nosocomial and community-acquired infections, accounting for about 30% of hospital-acquired infections, and 50% of bloodstream infections [3]. They have been reported to easily acquire simultaneous resistance to numerous classes of antibiotics and encode several resistance and virulence determinants [1]. These determinants are expressed by cell surface constituents, hydrolytic enzymes, toxins, and proteins, and include but are not limited to Panton-Valentine leucocidin (PVL), hemolysins, and staphylococcal surface Ig-binding protein A (spa) (encoded by lukF/lukS-PV, hlg, and spa genes respectively).

PVL, a pore-forming cytotoxin (PFT) is a virulence determinant implicated in skin and soft tissue infections. It consists of two distinct protein entities - LukS-PV and LukF-PV that act in sync to create its toxic effect [4], causing tissue necrosis and destroying polymorphonuclear leukocytes. The Staphylococcal protein A is a unique cell wall protein with five different binding sites (A to E) for IgG excepting IgG3 [5] thus thwarting phagocytosis of the pathogen [2]. It is encoded by the spa gene, whose length differs between strains due to the high variability of the Xr-region, with diverse numbers of 24 nucleotide base-pair tandem repeats [6], recurring between 2 to 16 times [5]. This results in major polymorphisms and sequence variations, and thus is used extensively as a molecular tool in studying the genetic diversity of S. aureus strains [6].

Methicillin resistance by S. aureus was first reported in England in 1961 [7], and subsequently, reports of intermediate resistance to vancomycin, the next alternative for the treatment of Staphylococcal infections ensued in 1996 [8]. In 2002, vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA) was isolated from a 40-year-old diabetic woman in Michigan, USA [9], trailed by reports from Australia, Europe, and across the globe [8]. Methicillin resistance is linked with the presence of the mecA, and occasionally mecB or mecC gene, and observed by in-vitro resistance to cefoxitin. The femA gene, vital for the expression of resistance to methicillin by S. aureus [10, 11], is encoded by the femAB operon [12] which codes for two separate proteins essential for the cross-linking of the pentaglycine bridge during peptidoglycan biosynthesis and serves as a molecular marker in the identification of S. aureus [13].

Vancomycin resistance leads to reduced permeability, thickened and improper crosslinking of the cell wall, and is conferred by 11 van genes, vanA and vanB being the most prevalent, particularly in clinical isolates [9]. Various authors have reported the gradual increase in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of vancomycin, linked with therapeutic failures (termed vancomycin “MIC Creep”) [14]. A recent report estimated that deaths accruing from bacterial AMR in 2021 were roughly 4.71 million, while deaths associated with MRSA rose globally from about 261,000 to approximately 550,000 between 1990 and 2021, the highest recorded amongst other resistant pathogens [15]. In Nigeria, pertinent data exist on the prevalence of methicillin-resistant and vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA and VRSA) as the prevalence of both traits and associated genes have been reported in different studies focusing on the most prevalent van and mec genes. A study in 2018 [16] reported 1 VRSA strain which tested negative for both vanA or vanB genes. Again, a more recent study in 2024 [17] confirmed vanA and vanB genes in 21.43% and 10.71% of S. aureus isolates respectively. However, there is a need for continued surveillance to observe the evolution of these strains within and outside the hospital setting. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the occurrence of multidrug-resistant MRSA and VRSA isolates and detect associated resistance genes (vanA, vanB, VanC1, mecA, mecC), femA and virulence determinants pvl, hlg, and spa genes in isolates from clinical and RTE food sources in Osogbo, Southwest Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and processing

A total of 465 samples were collected and analyzed in this study. These comprise 265 clinical samples (viz skin [50], nasal [50], and wound swabs [165]); and 200 RTE food samples - (cheese [50], coleslaw mix [50], donkunnu sauce (a local delicacy) [50] and meat-pie fillings [50]); all collected according to standard laboratory protocols [7] across Osogbo metropolis in Southwestern Nigeria.

Skin swabs were collected from the armpits of participants (5 cm radius), and wound samples from patients at the surgical wards of the UNIOSUN Teaching Hospital (UTH), Osogbo using sterile swab-sticks. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Health Planning, Research, and Statistics Department of the Osun State Ministry of Health, Osogbo (OSHREC/PRS/569T/366) and the Institutional Ethics Review Committee of UNIOSUN Teaching Hospital (UTH), Osogbo (UTH/REC/2023/01/30/739). Individual consent was voluntarily obtained from participants and parents or wards of children aged ≤ 18 years. RTE food samples were collected aseptically in sterile 250 ml Ziplock bags and labeled appropriately. All collected samples were preserved in cool boxes containing ice packs and transferred to the Osun State University Microbiology laboratory within 4 h of collection for further processing.

Isolation and identification of Staphylococcus aureus isolates

All swabs were inoculated into 10 ml of Tryptone Soy Broth (TSB) (Oxoid) by breaking off the cotton tip of the swab into the broth, while 1 g each of the RTE food samples was inoculated into 9 ml of TSB and incubated overnight. Growth in TSB was streaked out on Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) (Oxoid) and incubated for 24 to 48 h at 37 ± 2 °C. Distinct yellow- or cream-coloured colonies on MSA were characterized using conventional biochemical tests – Gram staining, catalase, slide and tube coagulase, DNAse, blood hemolysis, and oxidase tests for presumptive identification. The isolates were preserved in TSB supplemented with glycerol (15%), frozen for storage at − 20 ± 2ºC, and then used as working cultures in succeeding analyses.

Molecular identification was done as follows: chromosomal DNA was extracted by the thermal lysis method [18] and recovered DNA was quantified for purity and concentration with the Nanodrop-One Spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA). All isolates were screened for the presence of the nuc gene for S. aureus identification. DNA templates were amplified by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) in a 20μL solution comprising 10μL of 2× Master-Mix (Biolabs, England), 1μL each of 10μM forward and reverse primers (Inqaba-Biotec, South Africa), and 5μL of DNA template made up to 20μL with 3μL of DNAse/RNAse-free sterile water (BioConcept, USA) with the Master Cycler Nexus Gradient 230 (Eppendorf, Germany). Amplicons (10μL each) were run on 1.0% SafeView-Classic stained agarose gel at 80 V for 60 min, then viewed using the UV trans-illuminator E-BOX-CX5 TS imaging system (Vilber, France). 1 kb DNA Ladder (Biolabs, England) served as the DNA molecular weight standard.

Phenotypic screening for Methicillin and Vancomycin-resistant Staphylococci

Phenotypic screening for methicillin resistance was carried out with the disc diffusion method [19] using Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) (Oxoid) and cefoxitin (30 μg) discs (Mast diagnostics), while phenotypic resistance to vancomycin was performed with the agar screen test [20] using Brain Heart Infusion Agar (BHIA) (Oxoid) supplemented with 6 μg/ml vancomycin (Oxoid) and the MIC assay. Resistance was indicated by growth on the vancomycin-supplemented BHIA agar at 35 ± 2 °C after 24 h [20]. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay was by broth microdilution [19] in 96-well microtiter plates (Thermo Scientific) using Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) (Oxoid). The MIC value was determined as the lowest concentration of vancomycin at which there was no observable growth. Isolates were classified as vancomycin-susceptible S. aureus (VSSA) at MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL, vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) at MIC between 4 and 8 μg/mL, and VRSA with MIC ≥ 16 μg/mL [19].

Antibiotic sensitivity testing (AST)

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of the confirmed S. aureus isolates was done with the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on MHA, and ten antibiotics (Mast) in ten classes were assessed. The first nine antibiotics, namely - cefoxitin (30 μg), clindamycin (2 μg), erythromycin (15 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), linezolid (10 μg), norfloxacin (10 μg), tetracycline (30 μg), tigecycline (15 μg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 μg) were selected and interpreted using the EUCAST breakpoint guidelines vs. 14 [21]; while the tenth, chloramphenicol (30 μg), was interpreted with the CLSI performance standard for antimicrobial susceptibility testing [19]. The Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) indices of the isolates were also evaluated as earlier described [22].

Molecular detection of resistance and virulence genes

Detection of resistance genes mecA/mecC, femA, and vanA/vanB/vanC1 [23]; and virulence determinants pvl gene luk-PV (to amplify both lukS-PV and lukF-PV) [24] and the γ-hemolysin gene hlg to jointly amplify the hlgC/hlgB genes [24] was done in all isolates. Resistance genes were detected with one monoplex reaction for femA and multiplex reactions for mecA/mecC and vanA/vanB/VanC1. A single multiplex PCR was employed for the simultaneous detection of lukS-PV, lukF-PV, hlgC, and hlgB, while a monoplex reaction was used for the spa gene. Amplification was done in 20μL solutions for all the reactions (except for the van genes amplified in 25μL solutions) as described for the nuc gene above. The PCR mix for vanA/vanB/vanC1 was made up to 25μL solution with 12.5μL of 2× Master-Mix, 1μL of each 10μM primer, 5μL of DNA template and 1.5μL of DNAse/RNAse-free sterile water. Enterococcus faecium ATCC 51559 served as the vanA-positive control, E. faecalis ATCC 51299 vanB-positive control while S. aureus ATCC 29213 served as the van-negative control. The mecA and mecC-positive controls were S. aureus NCTC 12493 and NCTC 13552 respectively. Amplicons (10μL each) were run and viewed as described above. Results

Demographic information of the study participants

Two hundred and sixty-five individuals, comprising 147 (55.5%) males and 118 (44.5%) females were enrolled in this study. All participants were within the age range of 4 to 90 years with an average of 35.7 years. A little less than half of the participants (44.2%) were ≤ 29 years old, while around the same proportion (44.5%) were unmarried.

Frequency of occurrence of VRSA and MRSA isolates

Overall, 211 Staphylococcal isolates were phenotypically recovered from 465 samples (comprising clinical (57.0%), and RTE food (43.0%) samples), and 138 (65.4%) of them were confirmed to possess the nuc gene after genotypic identification. All 138 (100.0%) were vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) as revealed by both the agar screen and MIC assay (Fig. 1). However, 59 of these (42.8%) were methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains, as characterized via phenotypic screening i.e., all MRSA isolates were also VRSA, however, not all the VRSA revealed resistance to methicillin by phenotypic screening (Table 3).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing results

The S. aureus isolates exhibited the highest resistance to erythromycin at 63.0%, closely trailed by clindamycin (62.3%); then chloramphenicol (50.0%) and tetracycline (49.3%). Tigecycline was most effective in-vitro at 14.5%; followed by trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (27.5%) then linezolid (28.3%) (Fig. 2). A total of 96 isolates (69.6%) were multidrug-resistant (resistant to ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics), and these include all isolates from skin swabs. However, the highest number of MDR strains were from meat-pie fillings (20/23), one of them being extremely drug-resistant (XDR) as it was resistant to all ten antibiotics. Seven isolates were however sensitive to all the tested antibiotics, 5 of which were recovered from coleslaw (Table 4). MAR Indices ≥ 0.2 was observed in 83.3% of isolates. Molecular identification and characterization of recovered VRSA and MRSA isolates

Molecular screening for the nuc gene to confirm presumptive phenotypic identification revealed the presence of bands for 138 isolates, confirming their identity as S. aureus. This constitutes about a 29.7% recovery rate from the collected samples (13.1% from clinical and 16.6% from RTE food samples). For the methicillin resistance genes, 7 (5.1%), and 6 (4.3%) isolates had the mecA, and mecC genes respectively. The screening for vancomycin resistance genes vanA, vanB, and vanC1 in the 138 S. aureus isolates revealed bands for only 3 (2.2%) isolates; all from RTE food samples – 1 for vanA and 2 for vanB; none had the vanC1 gene. A total of 114 (82.6%) isolates revealed the femA gene.

The results for the virulence genes (vis pvl, hlg and spa) are as follows. The spa gene was recovered in 77 (55.8%) isolates across all seven sample types, pvl was detected only in wound samples and meat pie fillings while hlg was observed in all sample types except skin swabs in 5.8% (n = 8) and 7.2% (n = 10) of the isolates respectively (Fig. 4). Altogether, 11.6% (16/138) carried either vancomycin or methicillin-resistance-associated genes; 55.8% (77/138) had virulence genes, with dual or multiple carriages observed in 15 of them (19.5%) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The present study assessed the occurrence of S. aureus strains via phenotypic and molecular techniques and screened the isolates for the presence of methicillin and vancomycin resistance genes, and virulence genes pvl, hlg, and spa from clinical and RTE food sources in Osogbo, Southwest Nigeria. These strains have been implicated in nosocomial infections, and are now increasingly present in community settings, including food production units [25]. They are classified as priority pathogens of high risk and severe hazard by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) respectively [26], underscoring their threat to public health.

The proportion of S. aureus from clinical/human samples (13.1%) in the present study is much lower than in previous studies – 19.96% from Nepal, 40.0% from Saudi Arabia, 40.5% from Iran, 45.8% from Egypt and 51.0% from Northern Nigeria [27–31]. Also, varying values of S. aureus prevalence in meat products and RTE meat samples have been reported across the globe – 12.5%, 23.4%, 34.5%, and 50.8% from China, Zagazig City, Egypt, Iran, and Qalubiya Governorate, Egypt (Saad et al., 2019) respectively [29, 30, 32, 33] with S. aureus prevalence in food products within Nigeria ranging from 5.0 to 72.5% across the country [34]. This wide disparity in values within and outside Nigeria has been explained by factors including but not limited to sample types, sample collection, isolation techniques, pre-processing and post-handling of RTE foods, and storage/preservation. Others may include the microbial property of ingredients/flavours used in RTE food preparation, tools, and utensils employed [30].

Contrary to earlier studies [35], in vitro resistance to erythromycin and clindamycin in this study was high. Clindamycin, a lincosamide, is a viable option in the treatment of infections caused by MRSA, but a steady increase in the number of strains gaining resistance to it is being recorded [36]. This resistance can be either constitutive or inducible, however, only constitutive phenotypes exhibit resistance to clindamycin and erythromycin. Dynamics influencing resistance to macrolide/lincosamide antibiotics may include uncontrolled and unwarranted antibiotic use, proximity to a colonized/infected patient, extended or prior hospitalization or catheterization, poor personal hygiene, the incidence of chronic diseases, or even a complete lack of awareness [37]. Other predisposing factors are patients’ family size, recent illness and present hospital admission. Tigecycline, also one of the preferred choices in the treatment of MRSA infections revealed the highest activity as in-vitro resistance to it in this study was 14.5%. This corresponds with a recent review [38] which reports low resistance to tigecycline globally. A high number of isolates (83.3%) had MAR Indices ≥ 0.2, indicative of an antibiotic pressurized environment. This is worrisome as more than half of these (56.5%; 65/115), inclusive of the XDR strain are from food sources.

The observed disparity between the phenotypic screening with the presence of screened resistance genes highlights the essence of confirming phenotypic data which may be subjective with genotypic findings and reinforces molecular methods as the gold standard in bacterial characterization. The detection of resistance genes is confirmatory regardless of the phenotypic screening. These discrepancies may be due to screening methods, antibiotic disc concentration, possible dynamics in breakpoint values, or even peculiarities within strains.

MRSA infections exhibit resistance to practically all β-lactam antibiotics, which attach to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) crucial for the final polymerization and crosslinking (transpeptidation) of the peptidoglycan chain during synthesis, resulting in lysis [39]. This resistance is conferred by mecA on the Staphylococcal Cassette Chromosome mec (SCCmec) encoding PBP2a/PBP2′, which can facilitate cross-linkage even after wild-type PBPs have been deactivated by β-lactam antibiotics. The low proportion of isolates with the mecA and mecC genes, very much at odds with the phenotypic results, is at variance with earlier studies where all tested MRSA isolates had the mecA gene [30]. However, phenotypic MRSA strains are reported as resistant in this study as false negative results for mecA have been previously described [19, 40], and the mec variants screened for (A and C), unusual phenotypes, new or unique resistance mechanisms may also evade detection by mecA PCR [19, 41].

The mecC gene, observed in 4.3% of isolates has 69–70% nucleotide sequence homology with the mecA gene [41] and has been reported in both humans and animals. It is produced by a mutation on the SCCmec and it expresses a transpeptidase enzyme which has a 63% match with the PBP2a [42]. Recently from Pakistan, mecA was detected in 31.7%; mecC in 7.9%; and co-carriage of both genes in 57.1% of MRSA isolates screened [42]. The first report of mecC carriage in Southwest Nigeria was from 54.5% of screened MRSA isolates obtained from environmental/veterinary samples [43]. Therefore, as far as we know, this study is the first report of the mecC gene in isolates from human and RTE food samples in Osogbo, Nigeria. Transmission from the environment, and animal slaughterhouses is a probability as mecC has been found in a variety of livestock, wildlife, and domestic animals. This implies an extensive host range and poses a risk of zoonotic transmission to humans and RTE foods, probably during preparation.

The femA gene has been reported by previous studies to be implicated in the synthesis of the pentaglycine side chain of bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan [13], and it also performs a regulatory role as its expression is required for high-level methicillin resistance [44]. Conflicting with our finding, the complete absence as well as 100% occurrences of the femA gene in S. aureus isolates have been reported [45, 46]. The co-detection of the femA gene with mecA (and/or mecC) is a dependable marker of potential high-level methicillin resistance as its expression in MRSA isolates is associated with enhanced methicillin resistance. In this study, all isolates carrying mec genes except 1 also had the femA gene, albeit an additional 101 isolates had only the femA gene. This suggests the possibility of increased methicillin resistance in these isolates in the case of methicillin resistance gene acquisition by horizontal transfer. The observed phenotypic VRSA prevalence of 29.7% (138/465) in the present study although higher than the 11.1% previously reported [7], correlates with 28.3% VRSA prevalence from clinical and RTE meat in another study. However, van gene carriage was much higher than the 2.2% reported in our study [30]. Interestingly, the 3 van-gene carrying isolates in our study were all from food sources.

Vancomycin, typically a drug of last resort in the therapy of MRSA infections, is gradually becoming ineffectual. VRSA strains possess the vanA operon, capable of modifying peptidoglycan synthesis and adjusting the site to which vancomycin binds - the last two peptides (D-ala-D-ala) [35]. The findings in the present study are however unconventional as VRSA strains are typically found to emerge after MRSA strains acquire the van gene, but here, we report VRSA strains that are not resistant to methicillin via phenotypic screening. However, 2 of the 3 van-carrying isolates also carry mec genes, while the last isolate had the femA gene. The high occurrence of VRSA strains by phenotypic screening was not commensurate with the number of van genes detected. The reason for this, although not quite apparent, could however be due to the genuine absence, or likely occurrence of other van genes that were not screened for in this study, sub-optimal sampling, emergence of new genotypes, mutations or wild-type reversions of resistance targets [19].

Both PVL and γ-hemolysin (encoded by the hlg gene) are PFTs, highly potent, bind to the same receptors on human immune cells, and as such, possibly affect the same types of cells [47]. PVL is implicated in skin, soft tissue, and other invasive and tissue necrotic infections. In line with this, 5 out of 8 isolates carrying the pvl gene in this study were from wound samples. Higher values have been recorded [48], and a steady increase in the pvl gene occurrence in MRSA isolates from 28.6% in 2018 to 50.0% in 2021 was observed in Japan [49]. The hlg gene occurrence was slightly higher than the pvl in this study. The γ-hemolysin, composed of an S (hlgA/hlgC) and F (hlgB) component (both jointly amplified in this study), destroys immune cells by forming pores across the cell membrane, thereby causing lysis [47]. Staphylococcal protein A impairs host defences by binding to antibodies. The spa gene was detected in isolates across all the seven sample types analyzed, which is a strong indication of the virulence level, and immunogenic suppressive trait of S. aureus.

The high levels of carriage of virulence determinants in drug-resistant S. aureus isolates from human and food sources is a major cause for concern and portends severe public health challenges within and outside the hospital milieu, implying increased virulence in the strains, thwarting therapy, and escalating severity of infection.

Conclusion

The level of VRSA isolates in this study is surprisingly higher than that of the MRSA strains via phenotypic screening. This study also revealed the presence of mec genes, and worthy of note is the presence of the mecC gene in S. aureus isolates from RTE foods and human samples, which had hitherto been only reported in environmental samples in Southwestern Nigeria. This research work highlights the virulence levels of currently circulating strains of MRSA and VRSA strains in this environment as the combined presence of resistance and virulence genes in these isolates portend public health threats and possible complications from infections caused by them. This report will contribute relevantly to the sustained monitoring and surveillance of these strains in RTE foods as well as the human population, increasing the current crop of data available about these organisms for successive surveillance and epidemiology investigations. However, key limitations in this research are our incapacity to carry out spa-typing of our recovered isolates, and failure to include primers for detecting more of the previously reported van genes conferring resistance due to budgetary restraints. Again, we did not assess the inducible clindamycin resistance of the isolates to delineate fully resistant strains from strains that can acquire resistance during therapy. Furthermore, the region of sampling was restricted to a particular location, and was not widespread to improve epidemiological findings. These are potential areas of investigation in subsequent research studies.
