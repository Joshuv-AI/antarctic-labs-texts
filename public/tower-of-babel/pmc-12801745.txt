Patterns of flavored e-cigarette use at the time of quitting smoking: comparison between American young adults (18–24 years old) and older adults (> 24 years old)
Authors: Konstantinos Farsalinos, Christopher Russell, Riccardo Polosa, Konstantinos Poulas, George Lagoumintzis, Anastasia Barbouni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlavored e-cigarettes remain a controversial public health topic, with disagreement on their potential benefits and harms. In this study we compared patterns of flavor use in e-cigarettes at the time of quitting smoking between young adults (18–24 years old) with older adults (> 24 years old).MethodsA convenience sample of US adults who formerly smoked and reported consuming e-cigarettes every day or some days during the period of quitting smoking were included in the study (N = 51,641) and responded to an online questionnaire. Participants were divided into young adults (n = 9895) and older adults (n = 41746).ResultsThe most popular flavors used regularly by young adults and older adults were fruit (92.3% and 81.3%, P < 0.001), followed by dessert/pastry/bakery (67.3% and 68.1%, P = 0.054) and candy/chocolate/sweet (49.7% and 43.3%, P < 0.001). Fruit was the most prevalent single most often used flavor for both groups but was more prevalent in young adults (63.4% vs. 46.0% in older adults, P < 0.001), followed by dessert/pastry/bakery which was more prevalent in older adults (32.1% vs. 22.7% in young adults, P < 0.001). Menthol and mint/wintergreen flavors, while the fourth and fifth most popular flavors used regularly, were the single most often used flavors by only a small minority in both groups. Tobacco flavor was the single most often used flavor by 1.1% of young and 6.0% of older adults (P < 0.001). From logistic regression analysis, being a young adult was positively associated with the use of fruit (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.16–2.35), menthol (OR: 1.58, 95% CI 1.09–2.30), mint/wintergreen (OR: 1.51, 95% CI 1.00-2.28) and non-alcoholic/non-coffee drink (OR: 1.83, 95% CI 1.18–2.83) flavors, and negatively associated with the use of tobacco (OR: 0.52, 95% CI 0.34–0.78) flavor at the time of quitting smoking.ConclusionNon-tobacco flavors were popular among the US adult former-smoking e-cigarette consumers at the time of quitting smoking, but different flavor preferences exist between young and older adults. Tobacco flavor use prevalence was low, as were menthol and mint/wintergreen, particularly as the single most often used flavors. Future studies should explore patterns of e-cigarette flavor use in young adults who smoke or used to smoke, in addition to those with no smoking history.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01343-4.

Background

The growing popularity of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) over the past few years has raised concerns over the fact that these products are available in a large number of flavor combinations [1–3]. Flavoring additives are a necessary component of e-cigarette liquids because the rest of the ingredients (usually propylene glycol, glycerol and nicotine) are flavorless. Since the early years of e-cigarette marketing, a large variety of flavors have been available, not limited to tobacco-like flavors but also including other types such as fruits, drinks, sweets, beverages and others. Studies suggest a gradual transition over time since e-cigarette use initiation, from tobacco to non-tobacco flavors, while people who exclusively use e-cigarettes and have quit smoking appear to be more likely to use non-tobacco flavors compared to those who still smoke and use e-cigarettes (dual use) [4–6].

The availability and variability of non-tobacco flavors has been the main reason for criticism by public health scientists and authorities, fearing that such products could be appealing to youth and young adults who represent the main vulnerable groups in becoming dependent on nicotine and even initiate smoking. Some suggest there is a “gateway to smoking” effect for e-cigarettes, which could be attributed to flavors [7–12], although usually such studies do not account for the common liability model of explaining behaviors [13]. At the same time, youth and younger adults are more likely to use non-tobacco flavors compared to older adults [14, 15]. Thus, it has been suggested that flavors may be one of the main drives for e-cigarette use among younger people. The concerns about e-cigarettes being appealing to youth and young adults through their flavor profile has led to discussions or even already-established regulatory initiatives to restrict flavor variability, mainly by allowing only tobacco flavors to be marketed. However, the main focus of research examines e-cigarette flavors use among younger people who have not smoked but has not addressed the issue of flavor choices among those who smoke or who have quit smoking.

Considering the above, the purpose of this study was to examine the patterns of e-cigarette flavor use in a convenient sample of Americans who reported having formerly smoked and having used e-cigarettes at the time of quitting, comparing young adults (18–24 years old) with older adults (> 24 years old).

Methods

Study sample and online platform

The study sample has been presented previously [16]. In brief, individuals aged 18 years and older living in the US who had ever used an e-cigarette were invited to complete an online questionnaire available through Dacima Survey software (Dacima, Montreal, Quebec, Canada). Before entering the main survey questionnaire, participants had to read and agree to an informed consent. The questionnaire was open for participation from April 3rd to May 2nd, 2018. Double entries were excluded by recording the IP addresses of the participants. No financial or other incentive was offered in exchange for participation. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Patras in Greece.

Participants and questionnaire design

All participants were ever e-cigarette consumers. Through a detailed assessment of their past and current smoking status, only participants who reported having formerly smoked, defined as those who had ever smoked (even 1 or 2 puffs) but had not smoked in the past 30 days, were selected for this study. An additional inclusion criterion was that they should have been using e-cigarettes (every day or on some days) at the time of quitting smoking. The specific question was: “Thinking back to when you quit smoking cigarettes completely, were you using an e-cigarette/ vaping device…”, with response options being “Every day”, “Some days” and “Not at all”. In total, 51,641 subjects fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Based on their age, the subjects were divided into two groups: young adults (aged 18–24 years) and older adults (aged > 24 years).

Participants were asked about their past cigarette consumption and time since quitting smoking. The patterns of e-cigarette flavor use at the time quitting smoking was examined by asking the flavor choices used regularly, with the participants having the option to choose more than one flavor, and the single most often used flavor. For all questions on flavor choices, a pre-determined list of 12 flavor types was provided, specifically tobacco, menthol, mint/wintergreen, fruit, dessert/pastry/bakery, candy/chocolate/sweet, spice, coffee, alcohol/cocktail, non-alcoholic/non-coffee drink, unflavored and other. Additionally, they were asked to report which flavors were particularly helpful to avoid relapse to smoking and whether finding an e-cigarette/e-liquid flavor they liked was important in their attempt to quit smoking. The latter question had a response option in the form of a Likert scale, with options being “Extremely important”, “Very important”, “Important”, “Slightly important” and “Not important”. Additional questions were used to examine e-cigarette nicotine concentration and type of device used at the time of quitting smoking.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed with the data presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and proportions (%) and number (n) for categorical variables. Comparisons between the two age groups were performed using cross-tabulations and chi-square tests with z-test to compare column proportions with Bonferroni correction. Mann Whitney U-test was used to compare the duration of quitting smoking and the cigarette consumption when smoking between groups. Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the odds of regular use of each e-cigarette flavor at the time of quitting smoking as well as of each single e-cigarette flavor used most often at the time of quitting smoking. In both regressions, age was the dependent variable, and the models were adjusted for demographics (residence state, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, employment status, education and annual household income), nicotine concentration in e-cigarettes and type of device used at the time of quitting smoking, cigarette consumption when smoking and smoking cessation duration. Two models were prepared, one for regularly used flavors and another for the single most often used flavor. Further sensitivity analysis was performed comparing age group 18–24 years with the slightly older age group of 25–34 years. All analyses were performed with commercially available software (SPSS v. 22, Chicago IL, USA) and a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Participant demographics (n = 51,641)

Participants were consuming a median of 20 (IQR: 15–30) cigarettes per day when smoking, with young adults having lower consumption than older adults (10, IQR: 5–20 vs. 20, IQR: 15–30 respectively, P < 0.001). Additionally, they had quit smoking for a median of 37 (IQR: 23–60) months, with young adults having lower quit duration than older adults (24, IQR: 12–38 vs. 43, IQR: 24–61 respectively, P < 0.001).

E-cigarette consumption patterns, equipment and flavors use by adults who formerly smoked, at the time they quit smoking (n = 51,641)

A median of 4 (IQR: 2–5) different flavors were used at the time of quitting by the study subjects, with no statistically significant differences between groups. The most commonly used flavors in the whole study sample and in each group separately were fruit (83.3% of all participants), followed by dessert/pastry/bakery (68.0%), candy/chocolate/sweet (44.5%), coffee (19.9%) and menthol (18.2%). Fruit, dessert/pastry/bakery, coffee and menthol were more prevalent in young compared to older adults. Tobacco flavor use was reported by 15.0% of the participants, with more than double the prevalence of use in older compared to younger adults. Fruit was the most popular single most often used flavor overall (49.3% of the total sample) and for both groups, but it was more prevalent in younger compared to older adults. Dessert/pastry/bakery was the second most popular flavor (30.3% overall), but was more prevalent in older compared to younger adults. Tobacco was the single most often used flavor for 5.1% of the sample, but use by older adults was almost 6-fold more prevalent (6.0%) compared to younger adults (1.1%). Use of unflavored liquids was rare.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that explored the use of e-cigarette flavors specifically at the time of quitting smoking among US young adults aged 18–24 years compared to older adults. While non-tobacco flavors were quite popular in both groups, statistically significant differences were observed between age groups. Younger adults were more likely to use non-tobacco flavors, particularly menthol, mint/wintergreen and fruit flavors, while they were less likely to use tobacco flavors, although the popularity of tobacco flavors was low in both groups. Similar differences were observed in their perception of the importance of different flavors in helping them quit smoking and in avoiding relapse.

Examining the smoking behavior of young adults is important since establishing a long-term smoking habit usually begins in adolescence but extends into early adulthood [17–19]. A substantial proportion of young adults who were smoking or were experimenting with tobacco cigarettes during adolescence are at risk for future smoking [20]. While exploring the value of tobacco harm reduction strategies among young adults who smoke or at risk for smoking appears to be important, most work on e-cigarettes focuses on general patterns of use and on the potential risk for future smoking among those who never smoked in the past. While the literature suggests that younger adults are more likely to consume non-tobacco flavors and to report higher satisfaction from use of such flavors compared to older adults [14, 15, 21–23], limited research exists on the patterns of e-cigarette consumption as a smoking cessation tool. Ramo et al. reported higher prevalence of e-cigarette consumption among young adults who smoke and were more nicotine dependent and interested in quitting [24]. Mantey et al. followed up young adults who currently or formerly smoked for 6 and 12 months and found that those who reported an attempt to quit as a reason for use had higher odds for future cessation [25]. Gagne et al. found that daily e-cigarette consumption was associated with a lower frequency of smoking and more quitting among young adults [26]. Still, the potential influence of different flavors on smoking cessation has not been explored in this age group.

In contrast, there is substantial evidence among adults that non-tobacco e-cigarette flavors play a role in smoking cessation. Population surveys have shown that adult consumers of non-tobacco flavors are more likely to make a quit attempt, reduce smoking consumption and successfully quit compared to those exclusively using non-flavored or tobacco-flavored e-cigarettes [27–30]. Secondary analyses of clinical trials identified that those who consume non-tobacco flavors had higher odds of reducing or quitting smoking [31, 32]. A systematic review of factors associated with the appeal of e-cigarettes among adult smokers concluded that the availability of a variety of flavors is an important parameter that might facilitate complete substitution for cigarettes [33]. Such data raise the need to explore whether similar associations exist among young adults who smoke. However, our study raises another interesting issue, the difference in flavor choices, as well as in perceptions of the importance of different flavors, during the period of smoking cessation between younger and older adults. This could have regulatory implications, since it raises the possibility that non-tobacco flavor preferences among younger adults are not only confined to those who had never smoked but expand to those who smoke, and could potentially facilitate smoking cessation. Thus, any restrictions on flavor variability could compromise the quitting prospects in this age group. It should be noted, however, that non-tobacco flavors are very popular in all age groups, in agreement with other surveys which reported similar findings, particularly for people who have quit smoking [12, 14]. Thus, regulatory restrictions on flavors might compromise cessation attempts among all age groups. Furthermore, the availability of tobacco flavors only represents a choice that most closely resembles the flavor and experience of tobacco cigarettes. It is plausible to hypothesize that maintaining an experience similar to smoking might increase the odds of future relapse, although there is no conclusive evidence for such an effect. Additionally, while flavor restrictions may reduce e-cigarette use, they could simultaneously increase tobacco cigarette use [34–36], particularly among young people [37], as well as result in the creation of an illicit market. Thus, any regulatory decisions should balance the harm reduction prospects of flavor availability in e-cigarettes with the prevention of unintended use by youth and people who do not smoke. Our study provides some insights on the harm reduction prospect of non-tobacco flavors among young adults who smoke, and this should be further explored in longitudinal studies.

It should be mentioned that while observational data suggest that non-tobacco e-cigarette flavors may facilitate smoking cessation, this has not been verified in randomized controlled trials [38]. This may be due to the complex behavioral patterns of use, which was also observed herein since participants reported using several flavors on a regular basis. There is also substantial heterogeneity between different interventional studies, with some providing flavor options at baseline only while other allowing participants to change flavors over time. Similarly, some studies allowed use of flavors bought by the participants themselves, while others asked subjects to use only the flavors provided by the research group [39, 40]. Also, randomization to different flavors may be contaminated by ad libitum use of non-tobacco flavors by those assigned to the tobacco flavor group [41]. Therefore, while randomized controlled studies are necessary to establish causality, there are inherent weaknesses in such a design related to the complex, dynamic and rapidly evolving patters of use and choices among e-cigarette consumers.

An important limitation of the study is the cross-sectional design and the recruitment of a convenience sample of established e-cigarette consumers. Thus, the study was not designed or intended to estimate the population prevalence or frequency of e-cigarette flavor consumption at the time of quitting smoking. It should be, more appropriately, considered as a proof-of-concept analysis that could drive future research focused on the effects of e-cigarette and flavor use among young people who smoke, in addition to never-smoking populations. The large sample size results in small differences being statistically significant. For example, a statistically significant difference in the popularity of tobacco flavors between was observed, and the relative difference in prevalence was 2-fold to 6-fold between groups, but overall use was low in both groups, particularly as the single most often used flavor. Participants in this study had already quit smoking at the time of survey participation and were reporting their flavor use patterns at a time period in the past, raising the possibility for recall bias. The eligibility criteria were relatively permissive regarding the length of smoking abstinence (minimum of 30 days), and participants who had recently stopped smoking would be more likely to relapse compared to those who had quit for longer periods. Such a discrepancy was not addressed in our study. Other characteristics, such as the use higher performance e-cigarette devices or different nicotine concentrations, may also contribute to a successful quit attempt; however, we adjusted our regression analysis to these potential factors. Furthermore, the survey was performed at a time when open systems were the most popular types of e-cigarette devices in the US. While pod systems were already available at that time, over the years the market evolved with increased availability of prefilled and refillable pod systems, as well as an increased use of nicotine salts at high concentrations. Such changes may affect flavor choices and behaviors, which cannot be addressed in this study. While the study provides descriptive data on flavor preferences, it does not explore the underlying reasons why these preferences exist, particularly in relation to smoking cessation. Further qualitative data and expanded questionnaires in future surveys are needed to explore whether the preferential use of some non-tobacco flavors by young adults is due to being more prone to experiment with more flavors or due to psychological, social or cultural factors. One study reported that young adults were more likely to use multiple flavors compared to older adults [15], but did not examine flavor use at the time of quitting cessation. In our study, no difference was found in the number of flavors used regularly between groups. Finally, future studies should longitudinally examine young adults who smoke, and assess how flavor choices affect their smoking cessation attempts and their future smoking status, while the potential differences in flavor choices in different regions outside the US should be explored with similar studies performed locally.

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study of a large sample of US adults who formerly smoked, identified that young adults have different flavor choices at the time of quitting smoking compared to older adults. This information should be further explored in clinical trials using different flavors specifically in younger adults, and should be considered by regulators in order to avoid unintended adverse effects of over-restrictive regulation on e-cigarette flavors.
