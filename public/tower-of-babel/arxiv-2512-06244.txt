Auto-exploration for online reinforcement learning
Caleb Ju, Guanghui Lan

Abstract
The exploration-exploitation dilemma in reinforcement learning (RL) is a fundamental challenge to efficient RL algorithms. Existing algorithms for finite state and action discounted RL problems address this by assuming sufficient exploration over both state and action spaces. However, this yields non-implementable algorithms and sub-optimal performance. To resolve these limitations, we introduce a new class of methods with auto-exploration, or methods that automatically explore both state and action spaces in a parameter-free way, i.e., without a priori knowledge of problem-dependent parameters. We present two variants: one for the tabular setting and one for linear function approximation. Under algorithm-independent assumptions on the existence of an exploring optimal policy, both methods attain O ⁡ ( ϵ − 2 ) O(\epsilon^{-2}) sample complexity to solve to ϵ \epsilon error. Crucially, these complexities are novel since they are void of algorithm-dependent parameters seen in prior works, which may be arbitrarily large. The methods are also simple to implement because they are parameter-free and do not directly estimate the unknown parameters. These feats are achieved by new algorithmic innovations for RL, including a dynamic mixing time, a discounted state distribution for sampling, a simple robust gradient estimator, and a recent advantage gap function to certify convergence.



Keywords.
reinforcement learning, auto-exploration, parameter-free methods, Markov decision processes, Markov chains, mixing times, last-iterate convergence, gap functions, robust estimation

Introduction

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are a fundamental mathematical framework for decision-making under an uncertain, dynamic environment. MDPs originated in the 1950s, and they have a rich theoretical foundation with efficient solutions
[
9
,
50
]
. Recently, reinforcement learning (RL), which is the data-driven analogue of MDPs where the underlying dynamics are unknown and must be inferred from data, has gained significant attention due to its empirical success in AI strategic gameplay, training large language models, controlling complex processes, and robotics
[
29
,
8
,
41
,
49
]
. This paper focuses on infinite-horizon discounted RL problems, where the goal is obtain a policy that outputs actions (i.e., decision) for an agent (i.e., decision maker) at each step that collectively minimize the expected cumulative discounted cost from the environment, or equivalently maximize the negative cost.

Being a data-driven framework, RL and their solutions are greatly influenced by the quality of data. In general, the data can be generated in an offline, generative (or simulator), or online (or single-trajectory) fashion
[
57
]
. The offline approach assumes a batch of data has already been collected
[
60
,
28
,
22
]
, while the generative approach assumes access to a simulator that can arbitrarily generate fresh independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data
[
18
,
2
,
36
]
.
While these two approaches offer practical advantages when the data is scarce, such as medical applications
[
56
]
, and provide optimal convergence guarantees performance with respect to (w.r.t.) the provided training data, they may yield poor out-of-sample performance due to distribution shifts
[
60
,
28
,
22
,
20
]
.
On the other hand, the online approach uses a single stream of data directly from the data generating process. While online models can learn using data from the target distribution, the data is often correlated across time
[
63
,
55
]
. This prevents the direct application of prior RL methods that assumed i.i.d. data. Therefore, a central challenge in RL is to design practical and theoretically efficient algorithms for the online model.

Unlike generative models, online models cannot directly control the sampling process. Instead, they can only influence it through action selection, and consequently, they face exploration issues or insufficient data for “hard-to-reach” states and actions (recall that states are the configurations of the underlying dynamics).
To avoid poor exploration, two common strategies are
explicit (action) exploration
, i.e., myopic or
\epsilon
-greedy exploration
[
40
,
41
,
45
]
, and function approximation. The former selects an action uniformly at random from time to time to ensure every action is visited with probability greater than zero. The latter utilizes tools such as linear function approximation and neural networks
[
54
,
41
,
17
]
. These functions can possibly exploit low-dimensional structure to accurately estimate the value for hard-to-reach state-action pairs.
While both tools are indispensable in empirical RL
[
41
,
54
]
, their theoretical understanding are limited, such as how they affect the sample complexity of RL algorithms. Even in some value-based methods, their combination may not lead to convergence
[
19
]
.

Our limited theoretical understanding is due to the fact existing results rely on sufficient exploration assumptions, which may not hold in practice. For example, both value-based and policy gradient methods commonly assume sufficient action exploration, or that the sampling policy has a sufficiently large positive support
[
65
,
32
,
37
,
42
,
13
,
31
,
64
]
. This is clearly violated for on-policy sampling methods in unregularized finite state and action RL problems, because the policy converges to an optimal policy, which can be an all zero-ones vector
[
50
]
.
A natural remedy is explicit exploration. However, directly applying explicit exploration in policy gradient methods yields a sub-optimal
O(\epsilon^{-4})
sample complexity
[
4
]
, where
\epsilon>0
is the accuracy tolerance. This can be improved to
O(\epsilon^{-2})
with a carefully timed and controlled explicit exploration schedule
[
38
]
, which also holds holds under linear function approximation.
While this may suggest explicit exploration is necessary for efficient RL,
[
40
]
showed this is not the case. They introduced a new
implicit (action) exploration
phenomena, where under a structural assumption on the existence of a mixing optimal policy,
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity is guaranteed without explicit exploration. The advantage is simpler algorithms and avoiding sub-optimal actions.
However, unlike
[
38
]
, this result only holds for tabular RL, i.e., without function approximation.

While conditions for sufficient action exploration are known, conditions for state exploration are scarce. This is natural, since one can control action selection but not what states are visited.
To quantify the hardness of state exploration, we define a
difficulty of exploration
parameter
D_{\text{expl}}
, which quantifies the cost of sufficient state exploration. This parameter goes under different names and definitions depending on the properties and analysis of the RL algorithm. For example, it can be viewed as the distribution-mismatch
[
2
]
, transfer error
[
3
,
1
]
, concentrability coefficient
[
44
,
43
]
, covering time
[
16
,
7
]
, diameter
[
58
,
33
]
, or the mixing time of a Markov chain
[
30
,
32
,
38
,
13
]
.

Because
D_{\text{expl}}
often depends on the behavior of a stochastic RL algorithm, it is in general an
algorithm-dependent
parameter. Equivalently, it lacks
algorithm-independent
bounds, which only depend on the problem and not an algorithm’s behavior. For instance, both
[
38
,
40
]
establish
O(D_{\text{expl}}\cdot\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity for policy gradient methods, where
D_{\text{expl}}
is related to the mixing rate and stationary distribution (assuming it exists) of every Markov chain induced by an intermediate policy. As a result,
D_{\text{expl}}
can be arbitrarily large.
[
40
, Remark 5.2]
suggested that

can be algorithm-independent through implicit exploration, but no rigorous bounds were provided.
Later, it was shown that under similar structural assumptions as
[

\epsilon
-greedy exploration, then on-policy Q-learning can attain
O(D_{\text{expl}}\cdot\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity

. Here,
D_{\text{expl}}=O(\lambda^{-\bar{b}})
, where

is the minimum action probability for any intermediate policy and

is an algorithm-independent constant (see Proposition

\epsilon
-greedy exploration ensures

has an algorithm-independent bound of
O(\epsilon^{-\bar{b}})
, resulting in a sub-optimal
O(\epsilon^{-(2+\bar{b})})
sample complexity. Therefore, an algorithm-independent
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity remains open. Additionally,
[
45
]
is restricted to the tabular setting. It is unclear how function approximation, which incurs an unknown function approximation error, affects state exploration, c.f.,
[
19
]
.

Another major issue with existing online methods for RL is their practical implementation. Both explicit and implicit exploration require knowledge of the (unknown) mixing rate and stationary distribution of Markov chain to ensure hyperparameters like the number of samples to collect, exploration strength, stepsize (i.e., learning rate), and iteration count lead to convergence
[
38
,
40
,
31
]
.
While these works argue that such parameters can be estimated, current estimation procedure lacks non-asymptotic guarantees when the quantities of interest are unknown
[
61
]
.
On the other hand, there is some progress on developing parameter-free (or prior-free) methods for RL. These are methods that do not need prior knowledge of problem-dependent parameters, such as the mixing rate, to ensure finite-time convergence
[
34
,
58
,
59
]
. However, these results are either limited to the generative model or have algorithm-dependent bounds in the online model.
As a result, these unknown problem-parameters must be tuned heuristically before running the algorithm, which lacks theoretical guarantees and introduces overhead when implementing these RL methods.

1.1
Contributions and paper outline

We introduce a new class of methods with automatic exploration, or
auto-exploration
for short, for solving finite state and action, infinite-horizon discounted RL problems. These are parameter-free methods that efficiently explore both the state and action spaces under algorithm-independent assumptions in the online model, e.g.,
[
40
]
.
In particular, we introduce two variants of auto-exploration: one for the tabular setting and one for linear function approximation.
Our main result is that under the same structural assumption on the existence of a mixing optimal policy as
[
40
]
, then with probability
1-\delta
, both methods solve to
\epsilon
-accuracy with
O(D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)\cdot\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity.
Crucially,
D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)
is algorithm-independent. It has polylogarithmic dependence on
1/\delta
, polynomial dependence on problem-specific parameters, and no dependence on
\epsilon
nor other algorithm-dependent terms. See Theorem
3.5
for an exact definition. This appears to be the first time an algorithm-independent
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity is achieved under weaker exploration assumptions. And because these are parameter-free methods, they are simple to implement.

Before discussing the technical details of these methods, we want to highlight some high-level takeaways.
To start, our result in the tabular setting is achieved using implicit exploration. This suggests that explicit exploration is unnecessary for state exploration under algorithm-independent, structural assumptions
[
40
]
.
In contrast, our result in the linear function approximation setting requires explicit exploration, similar to
[
38
]
, even under the aforementioned structural assumption. In fact, this assumption can be relaxed from the existence of exploring optimal policy to the existence of any exploring policy. Our analysis reveals that explicit exploration is not for the purpose of state exploration, but it is to hedge against the (unknown) function approximation error, which adds an unavoidable bias. Indeed, Corollary
5.3
suggests that when the function approximation error is sufficiently small, then explicit exploration can be mostly avoided.
These insights clearly illustrate the role that explicit exploration and function approximation play in state exploration and delivering efficient sample complexity.

This paper is organized as follows.
Starting in section
2
, we introduce the basics of MDPs and RL.
We then introduce stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD)
[
32
]
, which is a recently developed policy gradient method based on stochastic mirror descent
[
46
]
. Because of SPMD’s success in action exploration
[
38
,
40
]
, our focus is on developing SPMD with auto-exploration. But our developments can be extended to other value-based and policy gradient methods.
The remainder of this section establishes new high-probability, last-iterate convergence of SPMD under weaker estimation assumptions. This development offers a couple benefits. First, many empirical RL algorithms output the last-iterate policy from a few (e.g., one to five) trial runs, so high-probability, last-iterate guarantees provide theoretical support for their efficacy. Second, these new convergence guarantees will later help provide stopping conditions with provably adaptive sample complexity.

Next, we introduce the first SPMD with auto-exploration in Section
3
, which is for the tabular setting. We extend the implicit action exploration phenomena from
[
40
]
to a new implicit state-action exploration phenomena under the same structural assumption therein. See Assumption
3.2
for an exact statement.
When combined with a new dynamic mixing time, SPMD attains an algorithm-independent sample complexity of
{O}(D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)\cdot\epsilon^{-2})
as described in the first paragraph of this subsection. See Theorem
3.5
for more details. Moreover, our algorithm’s use of a new dynamic mixing time removes the need to tune or estimate parameters.

The second SPMD with auto-exploration uses linear function approximation. Starting in Section
4

[
32
,
39
,
40

The resulting SPMD with linear function approximation offers a similar

In Section

1.2
Other related works

Last-iterate convergence of RL methods
.
Much of the existing convergence guarantees for RL are w.r.t. regret (i.e. ergodic convergence) or best-iterate
[
40
]
. Note that the best-iterate is not easy to compute in the data-driven setting. Recently, expected last-iterate convergence has recently been shown for policy gradient and Q-learning
[
26
,
14
]
, but no high-probability results were provided. High-probability, last-iterate convergence has been proven for approximate dynamic programming, where one estimates the state-action value function to high-accuracy and then applies the classic value or policy iteration
[
44
,
27
]
. While this approach delivers
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity, the results will have poor worst-case dependence on the discount factor when extending them to the online setting.
Other works have examined last-iterate convergence in the harder constrained or multi-agent setting
[
15
,
12
]
, but they either require a generative model or the true model.

Exploration in episodic RL
.
Another popular data generation model for RL is the so-called episodic setting. Here, the data generating process can be reset to a fixed initial state or distribution after a set period of time. Compared to the generative model, the episodic setting is less flexible because one cannot arbitrarily choose the starting point. On the other hand, it is stronger than the general online model since the data can be arbitrarily reset to obtain i.i.d. samples. In this setting, sublinear regret bounds — corresponding to
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity — have been developed
[
11
,
23
]
to solve finite-horizon RL problems. Their sample complexities critically avoid exploration constants like
D_{\text{expl}}
by applying bonus terms to explore under-explored actions (as done in multi-armed bandits
[
5
]
) and resetting the state back to a target distribution. Such efficient results compared to the general online setting further cement the challenge in solving RL problems with an online model.

1.3
Notation

Throughout this paper, we denote all logs in base 2 unless otherwise explicitly written.
We denote the probability simplex over
n
elements as
\Delta_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1,x_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i\}
.
Throughout this paper, we assume the norm satisfies
\|x\|\leq 1
for any vector
x\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
that is a probability distribution.
Clearly, this holds for all
\ell_{p}
norms where
p\geq 1
.

For any
p,q\in\Delta_{n}
, we measure their Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence by

\mathrm{KL}(q||p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
.
Observe that the KL divergence can be viewed as a special instance of Bregman’s distance (or prox-function) widely used in the optimization literature.
We define Bregman’s distance associated with a distance generating function
\omega:X\to\mathbb{R}
for some set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
D(q,p):=\omega(p)-\omega(q)-\langle\nabla\omega(q),p-q\rangle,\ \forall p,q\in X
.
The choice of
X=\Delta_{n}
and Shannon entropy
\omega(p):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log p_{i}
results in Bregman’s distance as the KL-divergence. We assume
\omega
is strongly convex with modulus
\mu_{\omega}=1
w.r.t. a user-chosen norm
\|\cdot\|
. This holds w.r.t. the
\ell_{1}
-norm for Shannon entropy.

New convergence guarantees for stochastic policy mirror descent

We start by reviewing the basics of Markov decision processes (MDPs) before discussing our main algorithmic workhorse, stochastic policy mirror descent
[
32
]
.

2.1
Background on MDPs

An infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision process (MDP) is a five-tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,c,\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space, and
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the transition probability matrix (i.e., for any state-action
(s,a)
,
P(s^{\prime}|s,a)
is the probability of the next state being
s^{\prime}
). We assume
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are both finite sets. The cost is
c:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is a discount factor. A feasible policy
\pi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
determines the probability of selecting a particular action at a given state. We denote the space of feasible policies by
\Pi
.
We measure a policy
\pi
’s performance by the action-value function
Q^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\textstyle Q^{\pi}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\big[c(s_{t},a_{t})+h^{\pi(\cdot|s_{t})}(s_{t})]\mid s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})\big],

where the function
h^{\cdot}(s):\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}
is closed strongly convex function with modulus
\mu_{h}\geq 0
w.r.t. the policy
\pi(\cdot|s)
, i.e.,

\textstyle h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)-[h^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s)+\langle(h^{\prime})^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\rangle\geq\mu_{h}D^{\pi}_{\pi^{\prime}}(s),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product over the action space
\mathcal{A}
,
(h^{\prime})^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s,\cdot)
denotes a subgradient of
h^{\cdot}(s)
at
\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)
, and the Bregman’s distance between any two policies at state
s
is

\textstyle D_{\pi}^{\pi^{\prime}}(s):=D(\pi(\cdot|s),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s))=\omega(\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s))-\omega(\pi(\cdot|s))-\langle\nabla\omega(\pi(\cdot|s)),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)-\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle.

The generality of
h
allows modeling of the popular entropy regularization, which can induce safe exploration and learn risk-sensitive policies, as well as barrier functions for constrained MDPs.
The regularization
h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}
is separated from the cost
c(s,a)
to better exploit the strong convexity and achieve faster convergence
[
32
]
.
We assume all costs are bounded, which without loss of generality can be stated as
c(\cdot,\cdot)+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)\in[0,1]
.
We also define the state-value function
V^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
w.r.t.
\pi
to be

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}[c(s_{t},a_{t})+h^{\pi_{t}(\cdot|s_{t})}(\cdot|s_{t})]\mid s_{0}=s,a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})\big].

(1)

It can be easily seen from the definitions of
Q^{\pi}
and
V^{\pi}
that

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s)

\textstyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\langle Q^{\pi}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle

\textstyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\textstyle=c(s,a)+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)V^{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

The main objective in MDP is to find an optimal policy
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the value optimality condition:
V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq V^{\pi}(s),\forall\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Sufficient conditions that guarantee the existence of
\pi^{*}
have been intensively studied (e.g.,
[
10
,
50
]
).
Note that the value optimality condition can be formulated as a nonlinear optimization problem with a single objective function.
Given an initial state distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
, one solves

\textstyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\{f_{\rho}(\pi):=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\rho(s)\cdot V^{\pi}(s)\}.

When
\rho
is strictly positive, then the optimal solution satisfies the value optimality condition.
While the distribution
\rho
can be arbitrarily chosen, prior policy gradient methods typically select
\rho
to be the
stationary state distribution induced by the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, denoted by
\nu^{*}:=\nu^{\pi*}

[
32
,
25
,
39
]
.
As such, the problem reduces to
\min_{\pi\in\Pi}f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi)
.
This aggregated objective function is commonly formulated and solved by nonlinear programming approaches such as policy gradient methods.

More recently, the present authors proposed a stronger notion of approximate optimality called
distribution-free
convergence
[
26
]
. In essense, convergence is measured w.r.t.

\textstyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}}f_{\rho}(\pi).

Clearly, this is stronger than the previous minimization problem. While dynamic programming (e.g., value iteration, Q-learning) and linear programming achieve distribution-free convergence, only a handful of nonlinear programming methods (e.g., policy gradient methods) have distribution-free guarantees
[
2
,
26
]
. In this paper, we again aim for distribution-free convergence.
As a final remark, the guarantees we develop apply for any regularization
h^{\cdot}(s)
. Notably, our result focuses on general convex regularizers, i.e.,
\mu_{h}\geq 0
. Faster convergence rates can be achieved for strongly convex regularizers, i.e.,
\mu_{h}>0
, but we omit them for simplicity and refer to
[
26
]
for details.

Next, we briefly discuss some structural properties of MDP/RLs that will help with convergence.

2.2
Performance difference and advantage function

Given a policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, we define the discounted state visitation distribution
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\textstyle\kappa^{\pi}_{q}(s)

\textstyle:=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}P_{\pi}^{(t)}(q,s),

(2)

where
P_{\pi}^{(t)}(q,s):=\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}\{s_{t}=\cdot|s_{0}=q\}
is the distribution of state
s_{t}
when following policy
\pi
and starting at state
q\in\mathcal{S}
.
In the finite state case, we can also view
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
as a vector.

We now state an important “performance difference” lemma
which tells us the difference on the value functions for two different policies.
We skip the proof, which can be found in
[
25
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be two feasible policies. Then
we have

\textstyle V^{\pi^{\prime}}(s)-V^{\pi}(s)

\textstyle=(1-\gamma)^{-1}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\psi^{\pi}(q,\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|q))\kappa_{s}^{\pi^{\prime}}(q),\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

where for a given
p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, the advantage function is defined as

\textstyle\psi^{\pi}(s,p):=\langle Q^{\pi}(s,\cdot),p\rangle-V^{\pi}(s)+h^{p}(s)-h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s).

(3)

Using these structural properties, one can show the following policy gradient method solves RL problems to global optimality.

2.3
Stochastic policy mirror descent

Recall the stochastic policy mirror descent
[
32
]
(SPMD) is an iterative algorithm, where at iteration
t
it performs the simple update,

\displaystyle\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s)=\mathrm{argmin}_{\upsilon\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\big\{\langle\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot),\upsilon\rangle+h^{\upsilon}(s)+\eta^{-1}_{t}D^{\upsilon}_{\pi_{t}}(s)\big\},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(4)

where
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}
is a stochastic estimate of
Q^{\pi_{t}}
,
\eta_{t}
is the stepsize, and
D^{\cdot}_{\cdot}(s)
is Bregman’s distance at state
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
As pointed out in
[
32
]
, a closed-form solution exists for certain
D
, otherwise one can solve the sub-problem to high-accuracy very efficiently
[
32
]
.
Later in subsection
3.4
, we will choose a Bregman’s distance induced by a negative Tsallis divergence, and the sub-problem can be efficiently solved with bisection search
[
40
]
.

The convergence of SPMD has been shown in terms of average-iterate or regret in both expectation and high-probability
[
32
,
40
]
, and more recently with expected last-iterate
[
26
]
.
We next look to establish new convergence guarantees in both high-probability and last-iterate.

2.4
New high-probability, last-iterate convergence for SPMD

The main technical difficulty of extending last-iterate expected convergence
[
48
]
to high-probability is that the average deviation error between two consecutive policies can not be made small by Bernstein-like inequalities. We instead use Lemma
2.1
with the analysis in
[
21
]
to control the deviation error between groups of iterates.
In what follows below, we require only two assumptions, which will be verified later in the paper. First, (
5
) requires an almost sure boundedness for the Q-function. Second, (
6
) asks for the weighted bias to be small, similar to
[
40
, Lemma 5.2, Theorem 5.1]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
k\geq 4
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, choose a stepsize
\eta_{t}=\alpha/\sqrt{k}
.
In addition, suppose for
t=0,\ldots,k-1
we have almost surely,

\textstyle\|Q^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2},\|\hat{Q}^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2}

\textstyle\leq\bar{Q}^{2}

3
Auto-exploration in the tabular setting

This section develops a parameter-free implementation of SPMD in the tabular setting. We start by reviewing simple Monte Carlo estimators.

3.1
Review of Monte Carlo estimator

In this section, we condense the regularized cost
c(s,a)+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)
into
c(s,a)
for simplicity, similar to
[
38
, Section 4]
. This can be done since
\pi
is a fixed policy during policy evaluation, i.e., estimation of
Q^{\pi}
. We also denote
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
as the state-action pair space.

First, we define the truncated on-policy Monte Carlo (TOMC) estimator
[
40
]
.
At each iteration of SPMD, we use the samples
z_{m_{s}},z_{m_{s}+1},\ldots,
, where
z_{t}:=(s_{t},a_{t})
and
m_{s}\geq 0
is the starting time (e.g., if we sample
M
samples per iteration, then
m_{s}=Mt
at iteration
t
of SPMD).
Without loss of generality by re-indexing the time index, we assume the samples we are given at each iteration are
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\ldots
.
For a mixing time
m\geq 0
, we define
\mathcal{T}_{m}(z):=\{t\in[0,m):Z_{t}=z\}
and

\textstyle\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z):=\begin{cases}\min\mathcal{T}_{m}(z)&:\mathcal{T}_{m}(z)\neq\varnothing\\
m&:\mathcal{T}_{m}(z)=\varnothing\end{cases}.

(10)

In English,
\mathcal{T}_{m}(z)
is a random set containing all the times the state-action pair
z
is encountered between time indices
0
to time
t-1
.
Meanwhile,
\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z)
is the first hitting time of state
z
, and states not visiting within the mixing time are assigned values
m
.
We also define the space of “non-rare” state-action pairs w.r.t. the probability provided by
\pi
,

\textstyle Z^{\pi}(\underline{\pi}):=\{(s,a):\pi(a|s)\geq\underline{\pi}\},

(11)

for some to-be-determined constant
\underline{\pi}>0
.
Later, we will show
Z^{\pi}(\cdot)
contains optimal actions with high probability for properly chosen
\underline{\pi}
.
With these quantities, we define estimator for the state-action value function, i.e., Q-function (the sum over an empty set of indices is 0),

\textstyle\tilde{Q}_{m,\underline{\pi}}(z):=\begin{cases}\sum_{t=\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z)}^{m-1}\gamma^{m-\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z)}\cdot c(z_{t})&:z\in Z^{\pi}(\underline{\pi})\\
(1-\gamma)^{-1}&:z\not\in Z^{\pi}(\underline{\pi})\end{cases}.

(12)

For
z\not\in Z^{\pi_{t}}(\underline{\pi})
, the estimator for
\tilde{Q}_{m,\underline{\pi}}(z)
is set to a large value to de-incentivize future policies from selecting
z
.
Otherwise for
z\in Z^{\pi_{t}}(\underline{\pi})
, then
\tilde{Q}_{m,\underline{\pi}}(z)
is a simple Monte-Carlo estimate, and its bias decreases linearly with the mixing time
m
, but the rate depends on the state-action visitation measure and mixing time ratio
[
40
, Lemma 3.1]
.
These are, respectively,

\textstyle\lambda^{\pi}(s,a)

\textstyle:=\pi(a|s)\cdot\nu(s)

(13)

\textstyle t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)

\textstyle:=\lceil\log_{\rho}(\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2C})\rceil.

Here,
(C,\rho)
are related to the mixing parameters for
\pi
(see Proposition
3.3
). In general, these problem-specific parameters are unknown. The next section proposes a dynamic mixing time to circumvent the need to know these parameters.

3.2
Parameter-free Monte Carlo estimator

We first define a so-called
discounted hitting time
,

\textstyle h_{m}^{\pi}(z):=\gamma^{m-\tau^{\pi}_{m}(z)}.

(14)

This random variable, which lies in between
[0,1]
, can be easily computed since it depends on the observable
\tau^{\pi}_{m}(z)
.
As shown below, this discounted hitting time provides a bound on the bias.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
z\in Z^{\pi}(\underline{\pi})
,

\textstyle\big|\mathbb{E}[\tilde{Q}_{m,\underline{\pi}}(z)-Q^{\pi}(z)|\tau_{m}(z)]\big|

\textstyle\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}h^{\pi}_{m}(z).

Moreover, with
\beta_{\delta}^{\pi}(z):=\min\{1-\sqrt{\gamma},\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)\cdot\log(\frac{4t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)}{\lambda^{\pi}(z)\cdot\delta})}\}
, we have for any fixed
z\in Z
,

\textstyle\Pr\{h^{\pi}_{m}(z)>3(1-\beta_{\delta}^{\pi}(z))^{m}\}

\textstyle\leq\delta.

Proof.

For notional convenience, define
b(z):=\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)\cdot\log(\frac{4t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)}{\lambda^{\pi}(z)\cdot\delta})}
, so that
\beta_{\delta}^{\pi}(z)=\min\{1-\sqrt{\gamma},b(z)\}
. We omit dependence on
\delta
and
\pi
since it is clear from context.

The first inequality is a direct result of the fact
{\tilde{Q}}_{m}
is an
(m-\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z))
-truncated unbiased estimator of
Q^{\pi}
, i.e., the bias is at most
(1-\gamma)^{-1}\gamma^{m-\tau_{m}^{\pi}(z)}
.
For the second bound, it was shown in
[
40
, Lemma 3.1]
that
\Pr\{\tau(z)=i|Z_{0}\}\leq(1-\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2})^{\lceil i/t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)\rceil-1}
.
Therefore,

\textstyle\Pr\{\tau(z)\geq b(z)^{-1}|Z_{0}\}

\textstyle\leq\sum_{i=\lceil b(z)^{-1}\rceil}^{\infty}(1-\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2})^{\lceil i/t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)\rceil-1}

(15)

\textstyle\leq 2\sum_{i=\lceil b(z)^{-1}\rceil}^{\infty}(1-\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2})^{i/t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)}

\textstyle\leq 2\sum_{i=\lceil b(z)^{-1}\rceil}^{\infty}(1-\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)})^{i}=\frac{4t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)}{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}(1-\frac{\lambda^{\pi}(z)}{2t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)})^{\lceil b(z)^{-1}\rceil}\leq\delta,

where the penultimate line uses Bernoulli’s inequality and the last bound is by choice in
b(z)
.

For the remainder of the proof, let us condition on the complement event above, i.e.,
\tau(z)<b(z)
, which holds with probability
1-\delta
.
We consider two phases of policy evaluation, one where the mixing time
m
is small and the other where it is sufficiently large.

Case 1
:
m\leq 2b(z)^{-1}
: Then

\textstyle\gamma^{m-\tau(z)}\leq 1=3(1-\frac{2}{3})\leq 3(1-\frac{b(z)}{3})^{2b(z)^{-1}}\leq 3(1-\frac{b(z)}{3})^{m},

where the second inequality is by Bernoulli’s inequality.

Case 2
:
m\geq 2b(z)^{-1}
: Recalling we condition on
\tau(z)<b(z)^{-1}
, then

\textstyle m-\tau(z)>m-b(z)^{-1}=m/2+(m/2-b(z)^{-1})\geq m/2.

Hence,
\gamma^{m-\tau(z)}\leq\gamma^{m/2}
.
Therefore,
\gamma^{m-\tau(z)}\leq 3\max\{\gamma^{m/2},(1-\frac{b(z)}{3})^{m}\}
.
∎

Similar to
[
40
, Lemma 3.1]
, this result shows with high probability the estimator’s bias decreases linearly with any mixing time
m
.
However, a key difference between the above result and
[
40
, Lemma 3.1]
is the former provides an easy-to-compute bound on the estimator’s bias, while the latter instead relies on unknown algorithm-dependent parameters.
Finally, using the discounted hitting time is very natural.
It measures the waiting time until the state-action pair
z
is “hit”, and from that moment the bias decreases at a geometric rate similar to a generative model that starts at
z

[
57
]
.

To ensure the bias is no larger than
\varsigma>0
, Lemma
3.1
suggests the following
dynamic mixing time
,

\textstyle\tilde{m}^{\pi}(\underline{\pi},\varsigma):=\min_{m\geq 1}\{m:\max_{z\in Z^{\pi}(\underline{\pi})}h^{\pi}_{m}(z)\leq\varsigma\cdot(1-\gamma)\}.

(16)

where
\varsigma,\underline{\pi}>0
are some to-be-chosen constants and
Z^{\pi}(\cdot)
is from (
11
).
Here, the name dynamic indicates this variable is not fixed and depends on real-time observations.
Although Lemma
3.1
provides a high probability bound on the mixing time, the bound can be arbitrarily large when the mixing time
t_{\text{mix}}^{\pi}(z)
is very large or a state-action visitation probability
\lambda^{\pi}(z)\approx 0
is small.
Prior works bypass this by making sufficient exploration assumptions on every generated policy
\pi
.
In the next section, we derive weaker conditions that lead to auto-exploration.

3.3
Implicit action exploration implies state exploration

We introduce the only assumption made in this paper about the underlying MDP, which pertains to the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
.
Note that the existence of the deterministic optimal policy holds for MDPs without regularization
[
50
]
.

Assumption 3.2

There exists a deterministic optimal policy
\pi^{*}
s.t. the Markov chain induced by
\pi^{*}
is irreducible and has a unique stationary distribution of
\nu^{*}
where
\nu^{*}(s)\geq\underline{\nu}^{*}>0

Additionally, there exists constants

\textstyle\|\Pr^{\pi^{*}}(s_{\tau}=\cdot|s_{0})-\nu^{*}\|_{\mathrm{tv}}\leq C^{*}\cdot(\rho^{*})^{\tau},\ \forall s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}.

The most important distinction to be made is this assumption only posits the existence of an exploring policy, while prior works may assume every generated policy
\pi_{t}
or any policy in general is exploring
[

,
40
]

4
Nearly parameter-free RL with function approximation

Function approximation, either through the form of linear models, nonlinear kernels, or neural networks, is a key ingredient in the empirical success of RL towards high-dimensional settings. In this section, we focus on linear function approximation since we can obtain globally optimal solutions. Throughout this section, our goal is to estimate the state-action value function

4.1

4.2
Stochastic operator and its statistical properties

Our remaining goal is to construct a stochastic operator for (
30
). The following proposed sampling scheme is a three-step approach.

Step 1: Space exploration
.
Sample a random state
\tilde{s}_{or}
according to the distribution,

\textstyle\tilde{s}_{or}\sim\begin{cases}\{s_{or}\}&:\text{w.p.}~1-f\\
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{S})&:\text{w.p.}~f\end{cases},

where
f
is the frequency parameter from (
28
) and
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{S})
is the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{S}
.
\tilde{s}_{or}
should be sampled using any random sampler and not from the underlying Markov chain’s trajectory.

Starting at state
s_{0}
, we generate a trajectory
s_{0},\ldots,s_{\tau(\tilde{s}_{or})}
(where
\tau(\cdot)
is the first hitting time of a state) using the
state-exploration policy

{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}
until the trajectory arrives at state
\tilde{s}_{or}
, where for a state-exploration parameter
\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi}\in[0,1]
,

\textstyle{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}(a|s)

\textstyle:=(1-\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi})\pi(a|s)+\frac{\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi}}{|\mathcal{S}|}.

(32)

This perturbed policy is similar to the action-exploration policy
{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{A})}
from (
29
), but the only difference is the perturbation constants are different. The reasons we call
{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}
the state-exploration policy is that it uses action exploration for the purpose of state exploration (i.e., Proposition
3.3
).

Step 2: Geometric mixing
.
Let
m
be a user-defined mixing time.
Sample a random time
\tilde{t}
according to

\textstyle\tilde{t}\sim\mathrm{Geo}(1-\gamma).

Suppose
\tilde{t}<m
, and we will discuss the case
\tilde{t}\geq m
in Step 3 below. At state
\tilde{s}_{or}
, extend the trajectory by length
\tilde{t}
into
s_{\tau(\tilde{s}_{or})},\ldots,s_{\tau(\tilde{s}_{or})+\tilde{t}}
using the original policy
\pi
.

Step 3: Operator construction
.
At state
s_{\tau(\tilde{s}_{or})+\tilde{t}}
, we sample two more actions. For notational simplicity, let
t^{\prime}=\tau(\tilde{s}_{or})+\tilde{t}
. First, sample
a_{t^{\prime}}\sim{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{A})}(\cdot|s_{t^{\prime}})
using the action-exploration policy. Observe the cost
c(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}})
, and let the next state be
s_{t^{\prime}}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}})
. Then sample the second action

using the original policy.
This mixed sampling approach, where two policies are used, is the same as in
[
38
, Section 4.2.2]
, which recall is to induce exploration over the action space.

Grouping these state-action pairs and cost as
\xi_{t^{\prime}}=\{(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}}),(s_{t^{\prime}+1},a_{t^{\prime}+1}),c(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}})\}

{s}_{or}
,
f
,
m
,
\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi}
,
\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{A})}_{\pi}
):

(33)

where for any time index

5
Auto-exploration with linear function approximation

We propose a guess-and-check procedure to adaptively estimate a lower bound
\underline{\kappa}
.
To “check” our guess, we first recall a recently developed advantage gap function.

5.1
Efficient certificate of an accurate policy

In this section, we introduce a certificate to certify when a policy has achieved a sufficiently small error. Define the so-called advantage gap function
[
26
]
,

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s):=\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(s,p)\},

(43)

This estimable gap function provides tight approximations of the (un-computable) optimality gap
[
26
, Proposition 2]
:

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s)\leq V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}g^{\pi}(s^{\prime}),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(44)

Since the advantage function for the last-iterate policy
\pi_{k}
cannot be computed exactly, we will use the Monte-Carlo estimator
\tilde{Q}^{\pi}_{m,\underline{\pi}}
from (
12
). To ensure all state-action pairs are visited, we propose the following two-phase procedure. Fix an action probability threshold
\underline{\pi}>0
and accuracy tolerance
\varsigma>0
. Although any
\underline{\pi}
is allowed, we set it to
\underline{\pi}=\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi}
from (
36
). Recall the set of state-action pairs
Z^{\pi_{k}}(\underline{\pi})
from (
11
) and the dynamic mixing time
\tilde{m}^{\pi_{k}}(\underline{\pi},\varsigma)
from (
16
). We will shorthand the latter by
\tilde{m}
.

1.

Phase I
. Use the Monte-Carlo estimator with a dynamic mixing time
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{k}}_{\tilde{m},\underline{\pi}}
. By its definition,
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{k}}_{\tilde{m},\underline{\pi}}(z)=(1-\gamma)^{-1}
for every “rare” state-action pair, i.e. for
z\not\in Z^{\pi_{k}}(\underline{\pi})
.

2.

Phase II
. For each “rare”
z
, complete the following estimation procedure sequentially. Starting from any beginning state
s_{0}
, use the state exploration policy
{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{k}
from (
32
) with exploration coefficient
\varepsilon^{(\mathcal{S})}_{\pi}
from (
36
) until
z
is visited. Then replace the value of
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{k}}_{\tilde{m},\underline{\pi}}(z)
from Phase I with the finite sum estimator from (
12
) using the original policy
\pi_{k}
for a mixing time
\tilde{m}
.

We make a few remarks. The second phase is off-policy since it uses a different policy
{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{k}
. If only on-policy sampling is used (i.e., only sample with
\pi_{k}
), then the sample complexity to reach the “rare” state-action pairs may be large based on (
24
). Using an argument similar to (
24
), we find the sample complexity of this two-phase procedure is at most, with probability
1-\delta
,

\textstyle\tilde{O}\big(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{S}|(\log\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{\varsigma\delta})^{2}}{(1-\gamma)(1-\rho^{\pi_{k},+})\underline{\nu}_{k,+}}\big)\leq\tilde{O}\big(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{S}|(\log\frac{|\mathcal{Z}|}{\varsigma\delta})^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}\cdot D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)\big),

(45)

where

\rho^{\pi_{k},+}:=\min\{1-\rho^{\pi_{k}},1-\rho^{{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{k}}\}
and
and

\underline{\nu}_{k,+}:=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\min\{\nu^{\pi_{k}}(s),\nu^{{\tilde{\pi}}^{(\mathcal{S})}_{k}}(s)\}
,
and we recall
D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)
from Theorem
3.5
.
Algorithm-dependent variables
\rho^{\pi_{k},+}
and
\underline{\nu}_{k,+}
are defined to capture the two policies used in each phase.
In particular, the sequential visit to each “rare” state-action pair in Phase II incurs the additional
|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{S}|
factor. This sequential sampling is imperative to ensure we estimate the state-action value w.r.t.
\pi_{k}
and not
{\tilde{\pi}}_{k}^{(\mathcal{S})}
.

Since we used the finite sum estimator, the output of this two-phase procedure is a random vector whose
\ell_{\infty}
norm is at most
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
. Furthermore, these outputs are estimators of
Q^{\pi_{k}}
with bias at most
\varsigma
element-wise. To ensure small total error, we will need
M\geq 1
estimators. Let the output of
i

\tilde{Q}_{\varsigma}^{(i)}
. Define the estimated advantage function,
\tilde{\psi}_{\varsigma}^{(i)}(s,a):=\tilde{Q}_{\varsigma}^{(i)}(s,a)-\tilde{V}^{(i)}_{\varsigma}(s)
, where
\tilde{V}^{(i)}_{\varsigma}(s)=\langle\tilde{Q}^{(i)}_{\varsigma}(s,\cdot),\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)\rangle
. Then define

\textstyle\hat{\psi}^{\pi_{k}}_{\varsigma,M}(s,a):=M^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\psi}_{\varsigma,M}^{(i)}(s,a).

We next develop the following Hoeffding-like result.
A proof can be found in
[
40
, Lemma 5.4]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the following estimated advantage gap function,

\textstyle\hat{g}^{\pi_{k}}_{\varsigma,M}(s):=\max_{\upsilon\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\hat{\psi}^{\pi_{k}}_{\varsigma,M}(s,\upsilon).

Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\textstyle\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\exists s~:~|\hat{g}^{\pi_{k}}_{\varsigma,M}(s)-g^{\pi_{k}}(s)|>\frac{2(1-\gamma)^{-1}\sqrt{2\ln(2|\mathcal{Z}|/\delta)}}{\sqrt{M}}+2\varsigma\big\}\leq\delta.

We next combine this advantage gap function with SPMD to obtain a parameter-free algorithm.

\textstyle V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq\max\big\{\epsilon+\big(\frac{36}{1-\gamma}+\epsilon_{\text{app},\infty}\big)\epsilon_{\text{app},\infty},\frac{6\epsilon}{1-\gamma}\big\}\cdot(\log\frac{8k|\mathcal{S}|}{\delta})^{2},\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

\textstyle\underbrace{\tilde{O}\big(\overbrace{\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|^{3-p}|\mathcal{S}|^{4}[\log(|\mathcal{A}|/\delta)]^{3/2}}{(1-\gamma)^{13}(1-\tilde{\bar{\rho}})\tilde{\underline{\nu}}f^{4}\epsilon^{2}}}^{\text{SPMD+CTD}}+\overbrace{\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{S}|[\log(|\mathcal{Z}|/\delta)]^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}(1-\rho^{\pi_{k},+})\underline{\nu}_{k,+}\epsilon^{2}}}^{\text{certificate}}\big)}_{\text{alg-dependent bound}}\leq\underbrace{\tilde{O}\big(\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|^{3-p}|\mathcal{S}|^{4}[\log(|\mathcal{Z}|/\delta)]^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{13}f^{4}\epsilon^{2}}\cdot D_{\text{expl}}(\delta)\big)}_{\text{alg-independent bound}},

where
\tilde{\bar{\rho}}
and

Proof.

The first part can be shown by (

, satisfies the assumption.

(46)

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{=}}\tilde{O}\big(\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|^{3-p}|\mathcal{S}|^{4}[\log(|\mathcal{A}|/\delta)]^{3/2}}{(1-\gamma)^{13}(1-\tilde{\bar{\rho}})\tilde{\underline{\nu}}f^{4}\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{|\mathcal{A}|^{2}|\mathcal{S}|[\log(|\mathcal{Z}|/\delta)]^{2}\log\frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{(1-\gamma)f}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}(1-\rho^{\pi_{k},+})\underline{\nu}_{k,+}\epsilon^{2}}\big)

where (1) is by our choice of

The second part of the corollary is analogous to the tabular setting (Theorem

.

Consider the same setup as Corollary

where recall

Proof.

For notational simplicity, let us denote
\underline{k}:=\min_{0\leq t\leq k-1}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi_{t}}_{{s}_{or}^{(t)},f}(s)

During the doubling trick in Algorithm

The sample complexity uses similar arguments as the proof for Corollary

While there is no general, convenient approach to certify a small

6
Conclusion

We introduce a class of methods with auto-exploration that obtain the first algorithm-independent
O(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity to solve online RL problems in a parameter-free way. Our developments also reveal insights into existing deep RL algorithms. Recall that when using linear function approximation, we introduced a robust estimator (Corollary
4.11
) and sampling according to a discounted state visitation distribution (
28
). Such techniques are similar to previously used gradient clipping and generalized advantage estimator
[
53
]
in state-of-the-art RL algorithms like proximal policy optimization
[
54
]
. Our analysis provides theoretical foundations for these heuristics within the context of RL problems.

\textstyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\cdot(T\cdot 2^{-(K+i+1)}\|Q_{0}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}+\frac{90T}{(1-\gamma)^{2}})\leq\frac{98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}},

(56)

where the first inequality is by Jensen’s inequality, the second is by Proposition
4.8
, and the third is by
\|Q_{0}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}=\|\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}\leq[4(1-\gamma)^{-1}]^{2}
from Proposition
4.1
.

For the set of inner products, we will derive two possible upper bounds.
The first upper bound, which is for “near” terms (i.e.,
i\approx i+j
), we have

\textstyle|\mathbb{E}\langle\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q},\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\rangle_{W}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{E}\|\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2})\leq\frac{98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}},

(57)

which is due to Young’s inequality and (
56
).
For the second upper bound, which is for “far” terms (i.e.,
i\ll i+j
), we start by applying Proposition
4.8
(with
t=1
) to uncover

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\|Q_{N+iT}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}\leq T\cdot 2^{-(K+i)}\|Q_{0}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}+\frac{90T}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\leq\frac{98T}{(1-\gamma)^{2}},

(58)

where the last bound is similar to (
56
).
Let us write
B:=\frac{786\Omega^{2}T^{2}C_{2}(\iota,m)}{(1-\gamma)^{2}\mu}
for notational convenience, which is from Theorem
4.9
.
We then proceed as follows.

\textstyle|\mathbb{E}\langle\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q},\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\rangle_{W}|

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{E}[\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q}|\mathcal{F}^{(i)}]\|_{W}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}]

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(2)}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\frac{2^{-j}\Omega^{2}}{\mu}\|Q_{N+iT}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}+B\big)^{1/2}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}\big]

\textstyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2^{-j}\Omega^{2}}}{\mu^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}[\|Q_{N+iT}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}]+\sqrt{B}\mathbb{E}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(3)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\sqrt{2^{-j}\Omega^{2}}}{2\mu^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}[\frac{\|Q_{N+iT}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}}{T^{1/2}}+T^{1/2}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}]+\sqrt{B}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}}

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(4)}}{{\leq}}\frac{\sqrt{2^{-j}\Omega^{2}}}{2\mu^{1/2}}\cdot(\frac{98\sqrt{T}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+\frac{98\sqrt{T}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}})+\sqrt{B}\sqrt{\frac{98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}}=\frac{98\sqrt{2^{-j}\Omega^{2}T}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}\mu^{1/2}}+\sqrt{\frac{98B}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}}

(59)

where (1) is by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
(2) is by Theorem
4.9
, (3) is by Young’s inequality and Jensen’s inequality,
and (4) is by (
56
) and (
58
).

Bringing everything together back into (
55
) , we have for any integer
\ell\geq 1
,

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{Q}_{N,2N}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}

\textstyle\leq K^{-2}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}\|\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q}\|_{W}^{2}+2K^{-2}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{\ell,K-1-i\}}\mathbb{E}\langle\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q},\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q}\rangle_{W}

\textstyle\hskip 10.0pt+2K^{-2}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\sum_{j=\ell}^{K-1-i}\mathbb{E}\langle\vec{Q}^{(i)}-\bar{Q},\vec{Q}^{(i+j)}-\bar{Q}\rangle_{W}

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\frac{98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}K}+\frac{2\ell\cdot 98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}K}+\frac{2\cdot 98\sqrt{\Omega^{2}T}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}K^{2}\mu^{1/2}}\cdot\big(\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\sum_{j=\ell}^{K-1-i}2^{-j/2}\big)+\sqrt{\frac{98B}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}}

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{98}{(1-\gamma)^{2}K}(1+2\ell+\frac{8\cdot 2^{-\ell/2}\sqrt{\Omega^{2}T}}{\mu^{1/2}})+\sqrt{\frac{786\cdot 98\Omega^{2}T^{2}\cdot C_{2}(\iota,m)}{(1-\gamma)^{4}\mu}},

where the three summands in (i) are bounded by, respectively (
56
), (
57
), and (
59
).
The double summation in (ii) is bounded above by
\frac{K}{2^{\ell/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}2^{-j/2}=\frac{K(1-2^{-K/2})}{2^{\ell/2}\cdot(1-2^{-1/2})}\leq\frac{4K}{2^{\ell/2}}
.
The proof for
N=KT
is complete upon choosing
\ell=\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{T\Omega^{2}}{\mu})\rceil
.

For the case
N\neq KT
, the proof is almost identical, except we modify the definition of the
(K-1)
-th epoch by adding iterates
N+KT,N+KT+1,\ldots,2N-1
:

\textstyle\vec{Q}^{(K-1)}:=(N-(K-1)T+1)^{-1}\sum_{t=N+(K-1)T}^{2N}Q_{t}.

The final result is the same, except the upper bound for the theorem we proving increases by a factor of 2 since the number of summands in
\vec{Q}^{(K-1)}
increases by at most 2.
∎
