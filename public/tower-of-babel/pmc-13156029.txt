Rapid temporal processing in the olfactory bulb underlies concentration-invariant odor identification and signal decorrelation
Authors: Mursel Karadas, Jonathan V. Gill, Sebastian Ceballo, Shy Shoham, Dmitry Rinberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In a dynamic environment, sensory systems must filter out irrelevant information to construct a stable percept. Animals who rely on smell need to identify and discriminate odors despite fluctuations in concentration, yet odor receptor activation is strongly concentration dependent. Here we explored how odor signals are transformed within the mouse olfactory bulb (OB) by developing an all-optical approach to identify the connectivity between odor receptor channels (glomeruli) and the mitral and tufted cells (MTCs), while monitoring their odor responses. We found that the glomeruli and MTCs activated earliest in a sniff robustly represented odor identity across concentrations, whereas MTCs connected to later activated glomeruli were concentration dependent. Furthermore, probing the responsiveness of MTCs to glomerular input found a short temporal window of excitability at a sniff’s onset, followed by prolonged odor-evoked inhibition. Our findings demonstrate, in awake animals, that the OB implements a rapid temporal filter, which is responsible for stabilizing identity across concentrations while decorrelating responses between odors.

Main

Sensation begins when stimuli from the external world interact with sensory receptors. The initial layers of neural processing transform and reformat receptor information before conveying it to downstream brain areas. This transformation is not arbitrary, but serves specific computational functions and lays the foundation for subsequent processing. Although cortical computations are thought to play key roles in object recognition and the extraction of complex features like faces1, early processing in areas like the retina performs crucial tasks like edge detection2, background motion subtraction3 and intensity normalization4. Learning the hierarchy and logic of these transformations is crucial for understanding sensory processing.

The olfactory system provides a good model for studying peripheral sensory processing due to its well-defined anatomical organization and genetic accessibility. In mouse olfaction, the panoply of chemical odorants is sensed by ~5 million olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs), the axons of which are segregated into ~2,000 glomeruli based on the receptor’s genetic identity in the olfactory bulb (OB). Odorants bind to unique sets of olfactory receptors and elicit specific, highly reproducible, spatiotemporal patterns of activity across glomeruli5–8, which in turn activate the next layer of neurons, the mitral and tufted cells (MTCs)9–12. Although the MTCs each receive their primary input from a single glomerulus, they are embedded in a vast inhibitory network of interneurons mediating lateral inhibition13–16 and exhibit complex temporal dynamics through which odor information is encoded on both fast10,12 and slow timescales17,18. MTCs are the only cells that send their output from the OB to downstream brain areas, thus observing their dynamics provides important clues for understanding the precortical computations of olfactory processing.

Early olfactory signal transformations (that is, from glomeruli to MTCs) have been proposed to underlie multiple computational roles, including lateral inhibition for contrast enhancement19, divisive normalization19–22 for concentration-invariant odor encoding23 and pattern decorrelation for enhancing odor discriminability18,24,25. However, there is little direct evidence for the role that these computations play in odor identification26. Furthermore, these proposed computations generally require relatively long time intervals (hundreds of milliseconds to seconds), which are substantially longer than the time required for odor recognition observed in behavioral experiments (<100 ms)8,27,28. This leaves open the question of how the OB transforms odor activity patterns so that they can be rapidly identified across a range of concentrations while keeping their identities distinct8,26–28.

To understand the logic and purpose of computations performed by the OB, as well as their underlying neural mechanisms, we require the ability to (1) track the temporal dynamics of a large number of glomeruli and MTCs in response to many odor stimuli, (2) establish connectivity between glomeruli and MTCs and (3) perturb neural processing with high temporal precision. In this study, we took advantage of an all-optical approach, which combines two-photon (2P) calcium (Ca2+) imaging of glomeruli and MTCs with glomerular optogenetic pattern stimulation. This approach allowed us to establish functional connectivity between glomeruli and MTCs, as well as characterize MTC responses to highly controllable synthetic and natural stimuli at timescales relevant for odor recognition.

Our results highlighted a crucial computational role of the OB network: temporal filtering, by which early, strongly odor-activated glomeruli can effectively drive responses in their recipient MTCs, whereas later (and typically weaker) activated glomeruli cannot29. We demonstrated that temporal filtering has profound consequences, preventing excitatory transmission except within a short time window, effectively decoupling MTC responses from the activity of their input glomeruli. These results led us to an integrative model connecting the odor affinity of OSNs to lateral interactions between glomerular channels. We further showed that these interactions are highly temporally dependent, as anticipated by previous studies29. Finally, this model and a series of experiments helped resolve previously ambiguous findings30–32 about the stereotypy and diversity of tuning across MTCs sharing the same glomerular input and tested predictions for more complex scenarios, such as nonadditive responses of MTCs to odor mixtures.

Results

To study the transformation of information from glomeruli to MTCs, we developed an all-optical approach. We used a transgenic mouse model expressing the fast activity indicator GCaMP6f in MTCs and Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in the OSNs (cross between OMP-ChR2-EYFP33 and Thy1–GCaMP6f (GP.511)34). For the numbers of animals in each experiment, see Supplementary Table 1. Our all-optical system combines a digital mirror device (DMD) capable of one-photon (1P) pattern stimulation31,35 of the bulbar surface with 2P Ca2+ imaging of the glomerular and MTC layers (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1). Our customized 2P microscope is based on the Janelia MIMMS 2.0 design, with an extended field of view (~2 × 2 mm2). This allowed us to image almost the entire dorsal surface of the OB and record postsynaptic glomerular activity at 60 Hz (Fig. 1b), as well as many MTCs (>100) at 30 Hz in the same preparation (Fig. 1c). Shifting the focal plane of 1P stimulation ~150 µm above the 2P imaging plane let us image the MTC layer (~150-µm deep) while stimulating individual glomeruli at the dorsal surface with ~3–4 µm lateral resolution. By aligning glomerular and MTC activity to the first inhalation after odor delivery and averaging across trials, we identified their response latencies and ranked them by their activation order (Fig. 1b,c and Methods). To find MTCs receiving direct excitatory input from individual glomeruli, we imaged MTC activity during glomerular optogenetic stimulation (Fig. 1a,d). We first identified a region of interest (ROI) corresponding to an individual glomerulus using 2P imaging and generated a stimulation pattern matching the shape of the glomerular ROI using the DMD-based projector system. This approach allowed us to achieve precise stimulation, with much less excitation of adjacent glomeruli compared to a previously used grid-based square stimulation strategy (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2)27,31,37. Stimulation of individual glomerular ROIs (10 ms, 15–30 mW mm−2) evoked rapid, reliable fluorescence changes in a few MTCs (2–6 cells) ~150 µm below the glomerular layer (Fig. 1d). These cells were classified as ‘daughter MTCs’ (D-MTCs) functionally connected to the targeted glomerulus.

Our method of D-MTC identification may be affected by off-target stimulation, mostly due to activation of axons of passage crossing above and beside a targeted glomerulus before terminating in more posterior glomeruli (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). To minimize this effect, we matched the stimulation patterns to the shape of individual glomerular ROIs and used the minimal stimulation power sufficient to evoke MTC responses (Extended Data Fig. 2c). An MTC responding to the stimulation of multiple glomeruli was assigned to the glomerulus, which evoked the stronger response (Extended Data Fig. 2d). If the response amplitude criterion was ambiguous, we assigned the MTC to the more posterior glomerulus (Methods).

Response characteristics of daughter and nondaughter MTCs

One open question is the extent to which the output of MTCs is shaped by direct input from their ‘parent’ glomerulus (intraglomerular signaling) versus lateral signals originating from other glomeruli in the OB (interglomerular signaling; Fig. 2a). To examine this, we recorded MTC responses to 12 odors at 2 concentrations in awake animals (n = 3 OBs in 2 animals). We observed that activity patterns were diverse across D-MTCs connected to different glomeruli during odor presentations, whereas D-MTCs receiving input from the same glomerulus were much more consistent (Fig. 2b; example traces for 14 D-MTCs from 4 glomeruli in 1 mouse, 7 odors, mean of 10 trials). To quantify the statistics of these responses, we compared their pairwise correlations across odors for MTCs from the same and different glomeruli, using glomerulus–glomerulus correlations as a reference (Fig. 2c,d). We observed that intraglomerular processing imposes high stereotypy on D-MTC responses (Fig. 2d), yielding very high pairwise correlations between sister cells (r = 0.88). At the same time, correlations across MTCs from different glomeruli were much lower (r = 0.13), effectively decorrelating odor representations at this level (Cohen’s d = 0.98, Mann–Whitney U-tests, P < 10−3).

To quantify the striking difference between signal correlations among D-MTCs and other MTCs from different glomeruli, we examined the time course of correlations within each trial epoch (Fig. 2e; pre-odor: −1: 0 s; odor: 0: 1 s; and post-odor: 1: 3 s). Intraglomerular D-MTCs exhibited higher stereotypy than interglomerular D-MTCs across all odor epochs (n = 71 intraglomerular and 221 interglomerular cell pairs from 15 glomeruli). Mean correlations (pre-odor, odor and post-odor) were 0.46, 0.88 and 0.83 for intraglomerular pairs and 0.02, 0.06 and −0.05 for interglomerular pairs. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for intraglomerular versus interglomerular comparisons were 2.19, 2.85 and 3.44 for the pre-odor, odor and post-odor periods, respectively; corresponding Mann–Whitney U-tests yielded P < 10−6, P < 10−36 and P < 10−5. Notably, intraglomerular D-MTCs were maximally similar during the odor presentation period (Fig. 2d,e and Supplementary Fig. 1). This suggests that processing within each glomerulus leads to highly similar, although not identical, activity in the connected D-MTCs, and this is preserved during odor responses as well as outside of odor response periods.

The early responding glomeruli and MTCs are preserved across concentrations

For glomerular recordings (Fig. 3a,b), we ranked the glomeruli based on their activation latencies and found that, although the set of responsive glomeruli changed substantially across concentrations, the subset responsive to all concentrations largely retained their rank ordering across latencies (Fig. 3e). For MTCs, we found that increasing the concentration of an odorant changed the set of responsive cells, but the earliest responses were largely preserved across concentrations (Fig. 3c,d; example data shown from MTCs expressing the fast indicator jGCaMP8f; subsequent analyses were performed on mice expressing GCaMP6f). To quantify this effect, we divided responsive MTCs into two groups: consistent, responding to both high and low concentrations, and inconsistent, responding to only one concentration. The latencies of consistent MTC responses were strongly biased toward the beginning of the sniff cycle, whereas inconsistent responses generally had longer latencies (consistent: median = 150 ms, n = 349; inconsistent: median = 210 ms, n = 601 MTC–odor pairs from 8 bulbs in 7 animals; P < 1 × 10−4; two-sided Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test) (Fig. 3f).

These findings provide strong evidence that both early glomerular and MTC responses carry concentration-invariant information about odor identity.

The transformation of odor responses from glomeruli to MTCs

As shown in Figs. 1b and 3a, an odor activates a set of glomeruli in a unique temporal sequence6, presumably due to the different binding affinities of their corresponding receptors, with high-affinity receptors activating earlier after inhalation and low-affinity receptors activating later (‘Discussion’)38,39. These glomeruli provide direct excitatory input to their D-MTCs, but it is unknown whether the timing of glomerular activation impacts the nature of responses in their D-MTCs. To address this question, we identified D-MTCs for a subset of glomeruli (Fig. 1d) and analyzed MTC odor responses in relation to the response properties of the parent glomeruli. Using 2P imaging, we measured responses of 58 glomeruli to 2 odors (5 repetitions): methyl valerate (MVT) and benzaldehyde (BENZ) (Fig. 1b and Methods), including two parent glomeruli with identified D-MTCs (G31 and G44). Across all conditions, we found that D-MTCs connected to parent glomeruli with low ranks (5.3 ± 4.8, mean ± s.d.) exhibited excitatory responses, whereas D-MTCs exhibiting inhibitory responses were connected to parental glomeruli with broadly distributed rankings (20.3 ± 11.7, P < 1 × 10−4, one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) (Fig. 4d). Response signs were determined based on initial responses after odor delivery. A similar picture emerged for the distribution of parent glomerular response latencies: D-MTCs with excitatory responses were connected to parent glomeruli that were almost all activated within 20 ms after the first activated glomerulus (median latency: 10 ms; 80% range: 30 ms) (Fig. 4e).

We also observed that, on average, glomerular response amplitude decreased with increasing response latency (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). Accordingly, the D-MTCs connected to parent glomeruli with the highest response amplitude exhibited predominantly excitatory responses (P < 1 × 10−4, one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) (Extended Data Fig. 4d). To rule out that amplitude–latency dependence was a feature of postsynaptic glomerular activation, we tested this relationship in mice expressing GCaMP6f in the OSNs (OMP-Cre × GCaMP6f). We found the same amplitude–latency relationship in log(ΔF/F0) versus time coordinates across three concentrations, closely matching postsynaptic measurements (postsynaptic versus presynaptic: low concentration: slope = −6.9 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.98 versus slope = −5.9 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.86; mid-concentration: slope = −6.8 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.97 versus slope = −4.7 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.82; high concentration: slope = −3.8 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.71 versus slope = −5.9 × 10−3 ms−1, R2 = 0.90; 121 glomeruli from 2 bulbs). Our findings thus support the amplitude and latency of individual glomerular responses being tightly coupled (see below and ‘Discussion’).

We next investigated how the population activity of glomeruli and MTCs encodes odor identity across different concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 5). Correlations between different odors were significantly lower than correlations between the same odors at different concentrations (glomerular level: within odor: 0.44 ± 0.19, across odor: 0.17 ± 0.17; two-sample Student’s t-test: t = 5.42, P < 1 × 10−4, Cohen’s d = 1.62). This difference was amplified through the transition from the glomerular to the MTC level (MTC level: within odor: 0.49 ± 0.23, across odor: 0.09 ± 0.22; two-sample Student’s t-test: t = 6.43, P < 1 × 10−4, Cohen’s d = 1.82). Thus, although the population responses across different odors strongly decorrelated from the glomerular level to the MTC level (two-sample Student’s t-test comparing layers: t = 4.61, P < 1 × 10−4), similar to Fig. 2c–e, the correlation between the same odors at different concentrations slightly increased at the MTC level, highlighting concentration-invariant odor representations.

Temporal interactions between different glomerular units shape odor representations

Based on odor responses in defined glomerular units (parent glomeruli and connected D-MTCs), we hypothesized that an initial excitatory drive was followed by recruitment of an inhibitory network creating a temporal window during which the MTCs receive primarily excitation from their parent glomeruli. To test how parent glomerular activation timing affects the amplitude of D-MTC responses, we probed D-MTC excitability using brief optogenetic stimulation of a parent glomerulus at different pulse latencies relative to inhalation onset. We delivered these pulses both in isolation and in the presence of odors that elicited broad glomerular activity patterns. We expected that, in the presence of a background odor, only early pulses would effectively activate D-MTCs, whereas later pulses would be suppressed by an inhibitory network recruited by earlier odor-activated glomeruli. Using this optogenetic approach allowed us to clamp the activation amplitude of the targeted glomerulus and, thus, selectively examine the role of timing in D-MTC excitability.

For this experiment, we used a mouse line in which the M72 receptor and ChR2 genes were inserted into the S50 receptor locus (M72S50-ChR2-EYFP)35,40 (Fig. 5a). In this line, the ChR2-EYFP-expressing M72 glomerulus is positioned near the center of the dorsal OB. To image postsynaptic glomerular and MTC activity, we crossed this line with the Thy1-GCaMP6f mouse line (strain 5.11)34. As expected, 2HA evoked strong excitatory responses in the M72 parent glomerulus and its connected D-MTCs at both high (data not shown) and low concentrations (Fig. 5e,f; n = 11 D-MTCs, 4 mice). When we presented an optogenetic pulse (10 ms, 20–25 mW) without an odor, D-MTC response amplitude was nearly independent of pulse latency (11 neurons in 4 mice) (Fig. 5c,g). Pairing pulse presentation with 2HA increased D-MTC response amplitude; however, the increase was small likely due to response saturation. In the presence of low and high concentrations of BENZ, D-MTC responsiveness to light stimulation of the M72 parent glomerulus decreased sharply after a brief interval, ~30 ms (Fig. 5g). This could be explained by inhibition of the M72 D-MTCs from other glomeruli activated by BENZ. This suppression lasted for >180 ms, which is consistent with the time course of lateral inhibition measured in in vitro studies29,41.

Is the result observed for the M72 glomerulus generalizable to other glomeruli? To address this question, we used the OMP-ChR2-EYFP × Thy1-GCaMP6f mouse line introduced previously. We identified the D-MTCs of a specific glomerulus and performed odor imaging in the glomerular layer using a battery of odors at different concentrations (Fig. 5h). We identified five glomerulus–ligand pairs (n = 5 mice), so that (1) the chosen ligand activated the glomerulus only at a high concentration and (2) we could find D-MTCs for that specific glomerulus in the field of view. For example, one such glomerulus responded to ethyl butyrate (EB) at high, but not at low, concentrations (Fig. 5i) and its response latency to high concentrations of EB was delayed compared to other glomeruli. Thus, EB could be considered a weak ligand of the target glomerulus (Fig. 5i,j). We probed the responsiveness of its D-MTCs to an optogenetic pulse (10 ms, ~20 mW mm−2) with and without presentation of EB. As observed for the M72 glomerulus, D-MTC responsiveness decreased sharply for light pulses presented at 60 ms or later after inhalation onset (Fig. 5j,k). These findings strongly support the existence of a time-dependent filter, putatively mediated by glomerulus–glomerulus inhibitory interactions, as a general computational principle in the OB network. In this scheme, the excitatory drive from early activated glomeruli precedes the inhibitory drive, thus creating a brief temporal window during which MTCs can transmit parent glomerulus input with minimal interference.

Having established and characterized the excitatory temporal window using our optogenetic approach, we then tested this phenomenon using odorant stimuli. We observed that the presentation of 2HA at a low concentration (2 µl of 2HA in 5 ml of mineral oil, 100× air dilution) activated only the M72 glomerulus in the field of view and evoked robust activation of all M72 D-MTCs (Fig. 6a, left, b, top). Next, we presented a mixture of 2HA and BENZ, which activated additional glomeruli besides M72; depending on the relative concentration of 2HA and BENZ, the most sensitive BENZ-responsive glomerulus was activated either before or after M72. When we mixed 2HA with BENZ at a low concentration, D-MTC responses decreased only slightly, presumably because activation of the M72 glomerulus by 2HA preceded activation of most of the BENZ-recruited glomeruli (Fig. 6a, middle, b, second row). However, increasing BENZ concentration evoked stronger and earlier responses in these glomeruli. As glomerular activation onset and amplitude are coupled (Extended Data Fig. 4), we observed that adding a high BENZ concentration increased the responses of these other glomeruli (Fig. 6a, last row, c) and shortened their activation latency. Crucially, the M72 glomerulus signal itself remained unchanged by the addition of BENZ (t-statistic <1.4 with a P > 0.1, paired Student’s t-test), whereas the M72 D-MTC responses were significantly reduced (Fig. 6c; with a t-statistic = 3.17 and P < 0.008 for 2HA + low BENZ; a t-statistic = 4.08 and P < 0.003 for 2HA + high BENZ, paired Student’s t-test; 8 D-MTCs in 2 mice). This result further supports our conclusions from the optogenetic probing experiments that the MTCs of later activated glomeruli are suppressed by earlier activated glomeruli. To further investigate the influence of other glomeruli on D-MTC responses, we tested multiple concentrations of strong (2HA) and weak ligands (BENZ and menthone (MEN)). Discussion

Before cortical processing, odor information from receptor neurons is integrated in glomeruli42 and then transformed into MTC activity. We examined this transformation within single glomerular units in awake, freely breathing animals. Employing an optogenetic targeting strategy, we identified MTCs connected to specific glomeruli and measured odor-evoked and light-evoked responses in parent glomeruli and their corresponding D-MTCs. Earlier, but not later, glomerular and MTC responses were preserved across concentrations of the same odor. Moreover, MTCs linked to early activated glomeruli displayed stereotypical excitatory responses, whereas those connected to later activated glomeruli exhibited variable and diverse responses. To understand this phenomenon, we probed MTC responsiveness to pulsed glomerular optogenetic activation during odor presentation. MTCs could effectively transmit glomerular signals to cortex only in a short temporal window at the beginning of the sniff cycle. Beyond this window, the number of MTCs responding to excitatory input from their parent glomeruli was limited, due to odor-evoked lateral inhibition from early responding glomeruli. Furthermore, analogous odor-mixture-based experiments confirmed that odor processing is governed by the same rules. These observations were made possible by technological advances that allowed us to monitor and manipulate networks at relevant spatial and temporal scales. We developed an optical system for patterned optogenetic stimulation at the brain surface combined with fast 2P Ca2+ imaging of deeper layers. We also employed an odor delivery system capable of presenting a diverse panel of odors spanning an 8,000-fold concentration range, with precisely controlled timing synchronized to the animal’s breathing (Extended Data Fig. 9). These tools permitted identification of glomeruli and their functionally connected MTCs, and imaging their responses to odor and optogenetic stimuli.

What are the underlying neural mechanisms of temporal filtering?

Odors evoke multi-glomerular sequences (Fig. 3a) and we found that their early portions were mostly preserved across different concentrations (Fig. 3e). One potential mechanism for such sequential activation is that odor dynamics within the nose produce a rising instantaneous odor concentration at the olfactory epithelium during inhalation, resulting in affinity-dependent receptor activation latencies, with more sensitive receptors activating earlier (Fig. 7a). Although simplistic, this biophysical picture provides a plausible framework for understanding the observed sequential glomerular activation, which plays a central role in shaping neural dynamics at the next processing level. The earlier activated glomeruli evoke excitatory responses in their D-MTCs. Concurrently, they drive inhibitory cells in the OB network, which in turn initiate a delayed inhibitory drive. Which specific cells could shape the inhibition-mediated temporal filtering of MTC responses? The OB’s lateral inhibition is mediated by a diverse array of neurons, at both the glomerular and the mitral cell (MC) layers. Within the glomerular layer, short axon cells can inhibit external tufted cells (eTCs) in glomeruli located millimeters away29,41,43,44. As eTCs initiate excitation within glomeruli, inhibiting them would naturally suppress odor-driven spiking in MCs45,46. In the MC layer, the interactions between MTCs are mediated by parvalbumin (PV) interneurons and granule cells (GCs). PV interneurons provide spatially broad inhibition across the MC layer, whereas GCs exert a more focal impact20,47. The similarity of the inhibitory responses across D-MTCs of later activated glomeruli (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 5) suggests a shared inhibitory source acting on their parent glomeruli, potentially via short axon cells in the glomerular layer. Additional insight can be gained by comparing our multi spot-size presynaptic stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 10) with postsynaptic glomerular stimulation48–50. Previous studies stimulating MTC dendrites (using mice expressing ChR2 in MTCs) found that increasing the stimulation spot size from 100 µm to 300 µm did not change the effect on MTC firing48, although wide-field stimulation evoked long lasting responses48–50. In these experiments, the optogenetic stimulation bypassed the intraglomerular inhibitory network and directly activated MTCs. Conversely, in our paradigm, we stimulated OSN axons in the glomerular layer and observed strong MTC response suppression with an increase of the size of the stimulated area, which is consistent with recruitment of the intrabulbar inhibitory network, potentially mediated by short axon cells29,41,43,44. However, we cannot rule out structured inhibition of MCs via GCs and PV interneurons. The former mechanism, which requires further investigation, is thought to shape MTC responses in behaving animals30,51–54.

How do our findings on temporal filtering relate to prior models of the OB?

Our all-optical interrogation of OB circuits aligns with, synthesizes and extends prior computational and experimental studies on temporal processing. Early imaging studies showed that spatiotemporal patterns of input activation carry odor identity information across concentrations and that glomeruli receiving stronger sensory input are activated slightly earlier than those receiving weaker input5,6. Studies of lateral inhibition29,41,55 demonstrated that inhibition rapidly and potently suppresses later inputs, finding that ‘the MTC output of less activated glomeruli is truncated by inhibitory interglomerular circuit input from more strongly activated ones’, concluding that the temporal sequence of glomerular activation by sensory input determines which stimulus features are transmitted to downstream olfactory networks and which are filtered out.

Yet, these early studies did not capture the full complexity and precision of awake-state olfaction, leading Liu et al.29 to speculate that this mechanism only matters for high-frequency exploratory sniffing. Computational models of OB temporal processing likewise generally omit key aspects of the circuit that we describe. Schaefer and Margrie’s simulation-based model of latency-based odor coding56 omits circuit interactions. Similarly, Kaplan and Lansner’s interval coding model57, in which MTCs are tuned to specific concentration ranges, lacks experimental support in mammalian olfaction. Cavarretta et al.58 proposed a two-stage model of spatial and temporal processing via periglomerular and mitral–granule cell circuits which supports our data. However, our work further identifies interglomerular interactions, likely mediated by short axon cells, as a key mechanism for temporal filtering, responsible for concentration invariance and odor discrimination in naturalistic patterns. Similar to us, Chae et al.59 observed complex, non-monotonic MTC responses and Economo et al.60 showed that odorant-specific and glomerulus-specific inhibition suppresses sparse glomerular ensembles. We extended these ideas by demonstrating how such inhibition drives temporal filtering for decorrelation and concentration invariance across odor concentrations.

What is the functional significance of temporal filtering?

A tight temporal window, when MTCs receive mostly excitatory inputs from their parent glomeruli, may confer multiple computational benefits for odor processing. First, it could implement primacy coding-based concentration-invariant odor recognition27,61,62. According to the primacy model, the earliest activated glomeruli encode odor identity; consistent early activation of the most sensitive glomeruli at low and high concentrations of the same odors (Fig. 3a,b,e) provides the conditions for concentration-invariant odor encoding. Remarkably, the consistency across concentrations for the set of MTCs activated within a brief temporal window early in the inhalation phase (Fig. 3c,d,f) enables propagation of concentration-invariant odor identity to higher brain areas. In contrast, the diverse MTC odor responses later in the sniff cycle vary strongly with concentration and, thus, are likely to encode complex olfactory attributes rather than invariant odor identity.

Where is the concentration-invariant odor representation formed? Our results highlight temporal filtering via delayed inhibition within the OB as a potential neural mechanism (Fig. 7). Previously, a temporal filtering mechanism was identified in the piriform cortex50, where earlier activity of piriform neurons was concentration invariant and recurrent inhibition provided a mechanism for filtering out other signals. Our observations complement these findings and suggest that concentration-invariant odor representations actually start forming in the OB; the cortex receives pre-processed signals and amplifies the temporal filtering effect initiated in the OB.

Temporal filtering may play another role in odor information processing. Olfactory receptor neurons and their corresponding glomeruli usually respond to many odors and the responses to different odors may overlap, especially for chemically similar odors. Amplification of earlier responses and partial suppression of later responses at the next processing stage may be responsible for decorrelating signals from different odors (Fig. 7b,c). This conceptual framework is strongly supported by our experimental observations of interchannel decorrelation as signals propagate from glomeruli to MTCs (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 5). The impact of inhibition effectively amplifies earlier signals and suppresses later responses, thus increasing the contrast between spatially overlapping, but temporally distinct, odor-evoked glomerular patterns. Decorrelation due to temporal filtering occurs on a rapid timescale, exerting its influence immediately after an initial temporal window of 30–40 ms, which is much faster than previously proposed timescales of decorrelation18. Such a neural computation is compatible with behavioral results demonstrating rapid decisions about odor identity63 and masking experiments limiting the temporal window of odor information available for odor judgments to <100 ms (ref. 28). Further, temporal filtering can help explain how rodents are able to discriminate between different ratios of a binary odor mixture and generalize this discrimination criterion across concentrations64.

Amplitude versus timing

In sniffing animals, the amplitude and timing of activation onsets are tightly coupled at the OB input level, as evidenced by our data (for example, Extended Data Fig. 4). Extended Data Figs. 4f, 5 and 6b further confirm the similarity of amplitude-based and time-based ranking in this context. Given the pervasiveness of this coupling, decoupling amplitude and timing may not have functional relevance. Even if firing rates were considered to be more critical for circuit computation, response timing confers robust explanatory power, particularly in the context of reaction times. Nevertheless, our study has generated several observations that provide valuable insights into this complex and multifaceted issue, which will require further investigation.

Limitations of the optogenetic approach

Our conclusions rely on a new ‘functional optogenetic connectivity’ strategy to establish glomerulus–MTC connectivity. Although our experiments targeting the M72 glomerulus clearly demonstrate the utility of this technique for identifying D-MTCs when only a single glomerulus expresses ChR2, generalizing it to a preparation where many glomeruli can be stimulated (for example, OMP-ChR2-EYFP mouse lines) presents challenges. First, ‘false positives’ may arise from off-target stimulation of axons of passage or from indirect modulation via lateral connections in the OB. Although axon-of-passage effects can be mitigated and likely do not substantially bias our results (for details on mitigation strategies, see Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2), lateral activation may reflect an intrinsic property of the circuit and cannot be fully avoided. Nevertheless, such modulation effects appeared to be weak and our strict D-MTC identification criteria (Methods), together with the stereotypy of odor responses among MTCs assigned to the same glomerulus (Fig. 2), allowed us to confidently separate direct versus indirect responses. Second, ‘false negatives’ are also unlikely, because we never observed MTCs sharing a parent glomerulus’s odor tuning that failed to respond when that glomerulus was stimulated. Overall, although these potential discrepancies remain practical challenges, they are unlikely to affect the study’s conclusions.

Off-target photostimulation could, in principle, confound latency-dependent suppression, but several controls argue against this. First, in OMP-ChR2 experiments, the light pulse amplitude was fixed whereas timing varied, so any scattered-light activation should have been constant across latencies and identical with or without odor, yet suppression emerged only with odor and strengthened at longer latencies, implicating odor-driven network inhibition rather than optical spillover. Second, in M72-ChR2 mice, where ChR2 is restricted to a single glomerulus, we observed the same temporal window. Third, enlarging the stimulated area (positive control) reduced transmission only for large radii, providing an upper bound on spillover effects that far exceeds any in our main assays. Finally, odor-only mixture experiments, free of optical confounds, produced the same suppression whereas the parent glomerulus signal remained unchanged, further supporting a network-mediated mechanism.

Limitations of postsynaptic latency measurements as proxies for OSN activity onset

Our analysis of transformations within the OB relies on postsynaptic glomerular imaging, which reflects mixed activity from different cell types defined by genetic labeling. By using the Thy1-GCaMP6f line, we predominantly labeled eTCs that closely follow OSN activity, rather than the broader excitatory population (mainly MTCs) targeted by Tbet-Cre. Prior studies36 have demonstrated that periglomerular cells and eTCs closely follow presynaptic OSN activity, whereas MTCs exhibit slightly longer latencies than OSNs. Overall, our latency measurements likely provide an upper bound on presynaptic latencies and this limitation does not alter the study’s conclusions.

Broad context and open questions

One of our key observations is that MTC activity is not a simple copy of glomerular activation. The MTC responses are not fully defined by their apical dendritic signal, but are modulated by the inhibitory network60. This phenomenon has been characterized in zebrafish65,66 and flies67–69, but remains largely unexplored in mammals, where it can be connected to neural dynamics and behavior.

Although our results generalized across the OB’s output neurons, both MCs and TCs, most of our recordings were from cells in the MC layer. These two types of neurons differ in their odor-evoked activity53,59,70 and projection targets71. In our recordings, TCs tended to follow their glomerular input more closely than MCs (Extended Data Fig. 8a), resulting in less temporal filtering overall (Extended Data Fig. 8b).

Our findings resolve earlier ambiguities about the diversity and stereotypy of odor-evoked activity across intraglomerular D-MTCs and clarify the relationship between odor-evoked responses of parent glomeruli and their D-MTCs. For the first time in awake mice, we simultaneously measured odor responses in identified parent glomeruli and multiple D-MTCs, showing that D-MTC responses were highly consistent with each other in sign and magnitude (Fig. 2) and yet could vary dramatically from the response of their parent glomeruli (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 3). Furthermore, the odor response similarity between a parent glomerulus and D-MTCs was dependent on the timing of the parent glomerulus response. This followed a general rule: for a given odor, early responding glomeruli passed their activation to their D-MTCs, whereas later responding glomeruli did not consistently do so, a mechanism described here as temporal filtering.

Our model (Fig. 7) synthesizes these findings and settles ongoing controversy about the nature of the interactions between parent glomeruli and D-MTCs. Previous results in anesthetized mice showed either high stereotypy31 or high diversity32,72 in the odor response rates among intraglomerular D-MTCs. These studies proposed two views: (1) D-MTCs closely follow the response magnitude of their input glomerulus, but differ in their precise response phase31 or (2) D-MTCs differ greatly in the response rate, from both their input glomerulus and each other32. Given that anesthesia strongly alters odor responses and neural dynamics73,74, these findings may not generalize to the awake OB. In awake mice, odor responses in D-MTCs connected to the M72 glomerulus appeared to be highly stereotyped for high-affinity ligands but diverse for weaker ligands30. Our observations reproduced these highly stereotypical responses, supporting a new model: in awake mice, a parent glomerulus’s ability to drive its connected D-MTCs depends on the timing of its response and intraglomerular D-MTCs are highly stereotyped relative to each other, but not always to their parent glomerulus. In this view, the apparent diversity in ref. 30 likely resulted from pooling MTC responses across different animals—M72 D-MTCs may share tuning within each animal, but may differ in their tuning to low-affinity ligands across mice.

In our head-fixed mice (2–3 Hz breathing), a brief stimulus evokes a consistent inhibitory window of ~200 ms. At these low frequencies, inhibition likely resolves within one sniff cycle, allowing the circuit to reset and process each inhalation as a discrete event. However, during active sensing at higher frequencies (5–8 Hz), inhibition would carry over to subsequent sniffs, with the net effect on neural responses depending on the complex summation of inhibition from multiple channels. This hypothesis provides a circuit-level explanation for the diverse, odor-specific or cell-specific effects of high-frequency sniffing on MTCs reported in ref. 75. How the olfactory system uses these dynamically altered responses across sniffs to guide behavior during active sensing remains a critical open question.

Here our observations lead us to propose that the signal transformation from glomeruli to MTCs serves two key functions: (1) for shaping concentration-invariant odor representations and (2) for rapidly decorrelating glomerular patterns.

Methods

Animals

In all experiments we used adult mice (both females and males), aged 8–10 weeks, at the start of the experiment. For experiments combining imaging and stimulation of multiple glomeruli, we used mice expressing GCaMP6f in MTCs and Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in OSNs. This expression was achieved through a cross between the OMP-ChR2-EYFP33 and Thy1-GCaMP6f (line no. 5.11)34 mouse lines. For a specific subset of experiments targeting the M72 glomerulus, we employed adult homozygous M72S50-ChR2-EYFP mice (Olfr545 tm3(Olfr160)Mom)76 crossed with Thy1-GCaMP6f. In addition, we used Ai148 (strain no.: 030328)77 × Tbet-Cre (strain no. 024507)37 animals instead of Thy1-GCaMP6f for some experiments (Supplementary Table 1). For OSN imaging we used the GCaMP6f reporter line Ai93D (JAX, strain no. 024103)78 crossed with OMP-Cre (JAX, strain no. 006668). Animals were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h reverse light-to-dark cycle (lights on at 20:00). Animals were group housed until surgical implantation and then kept in isolated cages in a temperature-controlled and humidity-controlled animal facility. All animal care and experimental procedures strictly adhered to protocols approved by the New York University Langone Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees.

Surgical preparation

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane during surgical implantation (2.0% during induction, 1.5% during surgery). A circular craniotomy (~3 mm diameter) was performed to expose both hemispheres of the dorsal OB, extending from the rostral rhinal vein to the nasofrontal suture, centered on the midline, using an air-driven dental drill (Midwest Tradition, FG 1/8 drill bit). A cranial window, a glass 3-mm diameter coverslip, was implanted and secured using a combination of self-curing resin (Orthojet, Lang Dental) and cyanoacrylate glue (Krazy Glue). In addition, a customized, three-dimensionally printed, diamond-shape headpost79 was securely affixed to the skull using C&B Metabond dental cement (Parkell). To ensure adequate recovery, each animal was allowed a minimum of 10 d before the start of the experiments.

Imaging and photostimulation

For all-optical imaging and photostimulation experiments, we built a two-photon (2P) microscope based on the MIMMS 2.0 design (HHMI Janelia Research Campus, Ashburn, VA) and added an arm for patterned photostimulation (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The 2P fluorescence of GCaMP6f was excited at 930 nm using a mode-locked, pulsed Ti:Sapphire laser with an 80-MHz repetition rate, ~120-fs pulse duration and dispersion compensation (InSightX3, SpectraPhysics). The beam was relayed and magnified by a telescope (scan lens, f = 50 mm and tube lens, f = 200 mm) to the back-aperture of a ×16 and 0.8 numerical aperture water immersion objective lens (Nikon). Images were acquired at 30 Hz using resonant-galvanometer raster scanning (Cambridge Research) for MTC somata and 60 Hz for the glomerular layer. Emitted photons were reflected by a dichroic mirror (customized, laser bandpass reflective dichroic, Chroma, cat. no. zt575/140dcrb-uf1) and separated to either green or red channels via a dichroic mirror (DM2, Chroma, cat. no. 565dcxr) and fluorescence was detected using GaAsP photomultiplier tubes (Hamamatsu, cat. no. H10770PB-40). Images were digitized and recorded using ScanImage 2019 software80 (Vidrio Technologies). The stability of the imaging and head-fixation system permitted imaging of the same field of view (FOV) over multiple sessions. Estimated average power at the objective front aperture ranged from 50 mW to 100 mW.

One-photon (1P) photostimulation was performed using a DMD projector system (Vialux, cat. no. ALP-4.3). The output (timing and power) of a 473-nm diode-pumped, solid-state laser (CNI Laser, cat. no. MBL-N-473, 1.5 W) was controlled by an acousto-optic modulator (AA Opto-Electronic, cat. no. MTS110-A3-VIS) and coupled to an optical fiber via a coupler (Thorlabs, cat. no. PAF-X-11-PC-A). The fiber output was first passed through a piezo-driven homogenizer (Mightex) to remove laser speckle, then collimated, expanded (~10 mm in diameter) and projected to the DMD. The DMD-generated light patterns were relayed and magnified by two additional telescopes, 3:2 (75 mm and 50 mm) and 5:3 (100 mm and 60 mm), and were combined with the imaging path using a dichroic mirror (Semrock, cat. no. MGP01-350-700) at the conjugate image plane. The 1P stimulation patterns were projected ~150 µm shallower than the 2P imaging plane, which focused the patterns on to the glomerular layer imaging in the MTC layer. To compensate for the variable distance between MTCs and the glomerular layer, the 1P stimulation beam was designed with a lower numerical aperture, so that the lateral resolution of the light excitation patterns remained stable along the axial direction for ~100 µm (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c).

The point spread function of the 1P photostimulation was characterized using a wide-field microscope (Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). A thin (<5 µm) fluorescent layer was illuminated with a pattern and the excited fluorescence signal was collected through the objective lens, the tube lens and a mirror (Semrock, cat. no. MGP01-350-700) on to a CMOS camera (Allied vision Prosilica, cat. no. GT1920). The axial dimension of the photostimulation was measured by an inverted scope configuration. Calculations of full-width at half-maximum for the lateral (x and y) and axial (x and z) dimensions were performed after correcting for a slight tilt (100) along the (x and z) plane.

The DMD allowed for generation of spatiotemporal patterns at the sample plane with <3-µm spatial and <1-ms temporal resolution. The axial spread of the beam was ~100 µm for patterns of glomerular size. This approach ensured the activation of glomeruli at various depths, the position of which could vary due to a nonzero angle between the head-fixation clamp and the cranial window (Extended Data Fig. 1d). However, better axial confinement of the photostimulation pattern can be achieved by placing a holographic diffuser in the optical path to break the laser’s spatial coherence, as described in the ADePT method81.

Odor delivery

A three-cassette air-dilution olfactometer was used to prepare and deliver odors with specific concentrations (slight modification from ref. 82). Total clean airflow was maintained at 1 l min−1 by a mass flow controller (MFC) (Alicat, cat. no. MC-1SLPM-D/5M/5IN). Each olfactometer cassette consisted of an MFC for each odor line (0–100 ml min−1; Alicat, cat. no. MC-100SCCMD/5M/5IN), two inline Teflon four-valve manifolds (NResearch, cat. no. 225T082), one on–off three-port bypass valve (NResearch, cat. no. TI1403270) and eight odor vials. Odors were prepared as neat or diluted in mineral oil and stored in amber volatile organic analysis vials (Restek, cat. no. 21797). Although all odor valves were closed, air from the carrier MFC and the odor line MFC of each cassette flowed through a bypass valve and the combined flow equaled a total of 1,000 ml min−1. This airflow was passed through a ‘final valve’ (two three-way valves; NResearch, cat. no. SH360T042) to an exhaust line. Clean air was delivered through the final valve to the odor port via a separate clean air line controlled by another MFC at ~1,000 ml min−1. The flow at the entrance of the nose port was reduced to ~500 ml min−1 by vacuuming half of it. To deliver an odor, the bypass valve on one of the cassettes was closed while a pair of odor valves was opened simultaneously. Air flow passed through the vial headspace and merged with the clean air line, thus providing air dilution to the odorized headspace.

To present a mixture of odors, both bypass valves were closed and two pairs of odor vials from two cassettes were opened simultaneously. Concentrations of an individual odor, or components of an odor mixture, were controlled by changing the flow rate of the MFCs in the odor line. For example, to deliver a high concentration of an odor, an odor-line MFC was set at 100 ml min−1. After flow was stabilized (at least 2 s), the final valve was switched from clean air flow to the odorized flow and odorized air was delivered to the odor port with a <40-ms latency. At the end of the stimulus presentation, the final valve switched back to delivering clean air to the odor port. As liquid dilution ratios do not accurately predict the headspace (gas phase) concentration for many odors83, liquid dilutions were assayed using a photo-ionization detector (Aurora Scientific) to determine relative concentrations of the odorant in the gas phase. For concentration series experiments, all dilutions were made in the air phase by diluting odorized air with clean air using a serial diluter, a combination of two MFCs28. For instance, to achieve a tenfold reduction in the concentration of an odor, the flow rates through both a vacuum MFC and an air MFC were adjusted to 900 ml min−1 (https://github.com/olfa-lab/olfactometry/tree/master/device_firmware/serial_repeater_teensy).

All odors used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Closed-loop system

Timing of photostimulation delivery was controlled in a closed-loop manner relative to the sniff phase. Respiration was monitored using a pressure transducer coupled to a customized Teflon ‘odor port’ which continuously passed filtered air over the mouse’s nostrils at a rate of 0.5 l min−1 (ref. 61). The pressure change relative to the atmospheric pressure was measured using an externally mounted pressure sensor (Honeywell, cat. no. 24PCEFJ6G) positioned in front of the animal’s nares. This pressure signal was amplified (Analog Devices, cat. no. AD620) and a Schmitt (dual-threshold) trigger was used to define inhalation and exhalation onsets in real-time on an Arduino microcontroller. Once a trial was initiated, after flow was stabilized (2 s), the final valve was switched by exhalation onset and odor was delivered to the odor port. The system then detected the first inhalation onset and delivered photostimulation at a variable ∆T delay after inhalation onset by triggering a transistor–transistor logic (TTL) pulse from the pulse generator. This TTL pulse was used to time three additional TTL pulses from a stimulator: photomultiplier tube (PMT) gating, DMD frame trigger and ON state of an acousto-optic modulator (AOM). In most of our experiments, the DMD frame trigger and AOM on-state pulse durations were set to 10 ms. The PMT gating signal started 2 ms earlier and ended 2 ms later to ensure gating.

Online motion correction

To facilitate imaging the same FOV within and across days, we implemented a previously described online motion correction method84. The FOV was first aligned to a reference image manually, then the position was fine-tuned automatically using a custom-designed closed-loop algorithm, implemented as a module within the ScanImage software package80 (Vidrio Technologies). This algorithm attempted to minimize the difference between the reference image and the FOV by iteratively moving the microscope stage (Thorlabs, cat. no. PLS-XY) to reduce the residual displacement computed using a rigid motion correction package (NoRMCorre, Flatiron Institute)85. The optimization typically converged within 10–15 s once the residual displacement vector was reduced to <0.5 mm in magnitude. Apart from aligning the FOV across multiple days, we also performed this motion correction routine between consecutive blocks of imaging. This allowed us to maintain precise alignment and compensate for any potential drift in the FOV during the experiment.

Segmentation of glomeruli

ROIs were manually selected based on the mean or maximum intensity projections. However, this method was insufficient to detect overlapping glomeruli. Furthermore, glomeruli can vary in size and shape, making it unreliable to solely depend on maximal fluorescence projections. Therefore, following the method described in ref. 86, we obtained a low-dimensional representation of odor responses across glomeruli using a two-stage singular value decomposition (SVD). First, for each trial i (10 s duration), we computed the SVD of the fluorescence matrix Fi = USVT and kept the top 20 components to form matrix Gi. Image processing and analysis

Data analysis was performed using custom-written software in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health (NIH)), MATLAB (MathWorks) and the Anaconda Python distribution. Images were aligned to a session-averaged template image using a nonrigid motion correction package85. Cellular ROIs were manually drawn using both mean and maximum intensity projections from three to five blocks of trials per session (5–10 min per block). As neurons could be centered in higher or lower z planes, to ensure segmentation of cell somas we also used a z-stack of at least 100 µm in depth centered on the focal plane for ROI selection. During pattern projection, each photostimulation trial exhibited either a trough (due to PMT gating) or a peak (due to high EYFP expression during large pattern projection). To identify the frames in which stimulation occurred for individual ROIs, we calculated the histogram of the fluorescence signal around the onset of stimulation. The value with the most distinct deviation from the mean value was considered to be the stimulation frame. Once the photostimulation frames were identified, they were removed and replaced with an average of the pre-stimulation and post-stimulation frames. To reduce noise, we applied a recursive prediction or correction algorithm based on the Kalman filter to trial image stacks (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/plugins/kalman.html). For GCaMP6f data, we used a gain value of 0.5 and a noise variance of 0.5. For odor and photostimulation trials, we calculated ∆F/F0, with F0 defined as the average fluorescence 1 s before inhalation onset.

MTC responses to glomerulus stimulation and identifying functional connections

To identify MTCs functionally connected to individual glomeruli, we used a mouse model that combined ChR2 expressed in OSNs (OMP-ChR2-EYFP) and GCaMP6f in MTCs (GP.511). First, we identified the anatomical structure of the glomeruli through 2P imaging of the glomerular layer. Then, we stimulated small areas (~70–80% of each glomerulus) targeting individual glomeruli and imaged MTC activity (150–200 µm deeper with respect to the glomerular layer) (Fig. 1d). Photostimulation parameters of 10-ms duration and 15–30 mW mm−2 elicited rapid and reliable changes in the fluorescence signal of sister MTCs. Our stimulation duration and spot size were more conservative than previous studies27,31, which reduced the number of false positives caused by unwanted excitation through axons of passage and overlapping glomeruli. We explored the response to off-target photostimulation, targeting a glomerulus as well as several surrounding positions using parameters of 10-ms duration and 20 mW mm−2, and determined that unwanted excitation generated smaller responses than direct excitation (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Furthermore, we observed that each glomerulus had a distinct light excitation threshold and that stimulation of anterior glomeruli could potentially excite some distant posterior glomeruli even at lower light powers (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Consequently, we adopted the following criteria to identify the parent glomerulus of MTCs: MTCs were assigned to the glomerulus eliciting the largest excitation (mean ∆F/F0) or the most posterior glomerulus eliciting a response if there was no significant difference in excitation level (paired Student’s t-test). This approach ultimately enabled us to precisely identify sister MTCs.

Activation-onset latency

To determine the glomerular activation-onset latency, we first defined the baseline fluorescence (F0) as the mean raw fluorescence signal acquired during the 0.45-s period (500 ms to 50 ms) preceding odor inhalation. Glomerular responses were expressed as ΔF/F0, calculated from trial-averaged fluorescence signals (five trials for glomeruli, ten trials for MCs). A glomerulus was considered responsive if its mean ΔF/F0 response during the first 0.5 s post-inhalation was positive and exceeded 4 s.d. above the baseline and its maximum ΔF/F0 response in the same window exceeded 6 s.d.(4 s.d. for MCs) above the baseline, with the peak occurring at least >100 ms (considering the slow-onset kinetics of GCaMP6f) after inhalation onset. For responsive glomeruli, onset latency was defined as the first time point at which the ΔF/F0 signal exceeded 4 s.d. (3 s.d. for MCs) above its baseline. For glomeruli with negative responses, latency was calculated as the first time point where the ΔF/F0 signal fell below −3 s.d. from the baseline and 2,000 ms was added to ensure that they ranked after positive and nonresponsive glomeruli. The s.d. was computed independently for each glomerulus based on its pre-stimulus baseline fluorescence fluctuations and responsiveness criteria were applied uniformly across all glomeruli. These criteria were thoroughly checked for each session to ensure that glomerular responses were not inadvertently filtered out. To ensure distinct response latency values when ranking glomeruli and to resolve ties among glomeruli with identical calculated latencies, a small adjustment was added to the determined latency: latency (ms) + 1 × 10−3/Fmax (ms), where Fmax represents the maximum response amplitude. This approach for onset latency determination aligns with methods used in previous GCaMP6f imaging studies36,60,87,88. For MC responses, the same strategy was employed, but with a lower responsiveness threshold of 4 s.d. from baseline. We have validated our method through simulations that demonstrated reduced amplitude bias. We further tested the method’s robustness across optogenetic and odor-evoked response paradigms, confirming consistent latency estimates (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Response classification and ranking

Based on the criteria defined above, each glomerular response was classified as excitatory, inhibitory or nonresponsive. These classifications were used to establish a definitive rank order for temporal analysis. Excitatory glomeruli were ranked first according to their calculated onset latencies, followed by nonresponsive glomeruli and, finally, inhibitory glomeruli, which were placed last due to the analytical time penalty applied to their onsets. A similar methodology was applied to MTCs, where the response sign was used solely to categorize the cell’s overall response type based on its respective thresholds. For both glomeruli and MTCs, the negative responses may reflect inhibitory mechanisms, such as lateral inhibition within the OB. In our analyses of onset ranking and its relationship to MTC responses, we focused on excitatory glomerular input. For odor sets with multiple concentrations, each concentration was treated as an independent condition, and onset latencies and the resulting rank order were recalculated separately for each concentration. Quantitative analyses of how glomerular ranking and onset latency relate to MC responses (for example, Fig. 4d,e) were restricted to glomeruli exhibiting significant excitatory responses. In the ranking–correlation analysis, glomeruli that did not show a positive response (including negative or inhibitory or nonresponsive cases) were assigned the same rank (greater than or equal to the total number of glomeruli in the FOV). In OMP-ChR2 × Thy1–GCaMP6f static YFP fluorescence significantly reduced odor-evoked ΔF/F, making inhibitory responses very difficult to resolve above noise and therefore very unlikely to be detected at the glomerular level.

Odor similarity

To determine odor similarity at the glomerular level, we used Spearman’s rank correlation (SciPy package). Nonresponsive glomeruli were assigned an identical value equal to the total number of glomeruli imaged, ensuring their contribution to the ranking was uniform. Within-odor similarity (Spearman’s rank correlation) was calculated by correlating responses across different concentrations of the same odor. Across-odor similarity is determined by correlating responses between distinct odor sets, irrespective of concentration. For MTC odor–odor correlations, we used the mean MTC response during odor presentation (Pearson’s correlation). Final odor–odor correlations were averaged across multiple animals.

Cell similarity

To determine cell similarity, we used cosine or Pearson’s r similarity. Responses were sampled at ~30 Hz (corresponding to 33-ms bins) over a 6-s duration, resulting in a vector of length 180 for each cell. This approach ensures that the time resolution is sufficient to capture fast timescale differences in responses.

Quantification and statistics

Data were analyzed using custom-written MATLAB and Python scripts. Statistical analysis of physiological data was performed using standard functions from the Python SciPy library (such as the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Student’s t-test, correlation coefficient, regression and Cohen’s d). Specific analyses are described alongside experiments in the text and figure captions.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on standard practices in the field for functional imaging and optogenetic experiments. Data exclusion criteria were established as: mice were included in the analysis only if they exhibited sufficient cranial window clarity, robust GCaMP expression and measurable glomerular and MC responses to optogenetic and odor stimulation. Mice that did not satisfy these criteria (>80% reported sample size) were excluded from the analysis.
