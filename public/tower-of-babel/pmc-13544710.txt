Cardiac Dysfunction in Blunt Traumatic Brain Injury Patients
Authors: Harry Kuperstein, Sam Tierney, Shabnam Parsa, Yoli Meydan, Benjamin Krauss, Ambika Mukhi, Jeffrey Stahl, Daniel Rutigliano, James Vosswinkel, Sahar Ahmad, Randeep Jawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IMPORTANCE:Cardiac dysfunction following blunt traumatic brain injury (TBI) has been commonly defined as new decreased ejection fraction (EF) or new regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMAs).OBJECTIVES:We assessed cardiac dysfunction after blunt TBI by echocardiographic signatures, association with intracranial abnormalities, and clinical findings.DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS:Retrospective cohort study of adult blunt TBI patients hospitalized at an American College of Surgeons verified Level 1 Trauma Center during 2022–2023 who underwent echocardiography (echo) within 48 hours of admission. Patients with preexisting cardiac comorbidities were excluded.MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES:The trauma registry at a Level 1 Trauma Center and corresponding electronic medical record were reviewed for TBI patients. Echo reports were evaluated for cardiac dysfunction as measured by EF, RWMAs, and fractional shortening (FS). FS is a simple M-mode measure predictive of systolic function defined as the percentage change in left ventricular diameter between the end of diastole and systole.RESULTS:Of 105 blunt TBI patients meeting inclusion criteria, 81 had normal FS, 23 patients had decreased FS below 30%, and 1 had increased FS on echo. All 81 patients with normal FS had preserved EF, but 19 of 23 (83%) patients with decreased FS also had preserved EF. Patients with decreased FS had increased ICU length of stay, inpatient mortality, and craniotomy/craniectomy rates. Subarachnoid hemorrhage was the most frequent intracranial abnormality in patients with decreased FS.CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE:FS was depressed in a subset of TBI patients with above adverse in-hospital outcomes. Our data suggest FS below 30% may be a useful echocardiographic parameter associated with clinically significant cardiac dysfunction in blunt TBI patients.

KEY POINTS

Question: What are the echocardiographic signatures and clinical consequences of cardiac dysfunction in blunt traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients?

Findings: We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult blunt TBI patients and identified 105 patients meeting inclusion criteria. Twenty-three of these patients (22.1%) had decreased fractional shortening (FS) on echocardiography with this cohort showing statistically significant increases in in-hospital mortality, ICU length of stay, and rates of craniotomy/craniectomy.

Meaning: FS below 30% is a possible indicator of clinically significant cardiac dysfunction in blunt TBI patients, irrespective of measured ejection fraction.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality after trauma worldwide (1–3). Patients with TBI have been noted to experience multiple organ dysfunction with inconsistent, temporally variable phenotypes (4–8). A sympathetic catecholamine surge in response to TBI in a minority of patients leads to a phenomenon termed paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity, with features including tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnea, and fever occurring for several days after the insult (9). Another complication is hypotension, which may follow in patients with systolic dysfunction after TBI. Hypotension after TBI more broadly is estimated at an incidence of 18% and is associated with increased mortality (8, 10, 11). Proposed causes for hypotension after TBI include a loss of inotropy secondary to a catecholamine surge, sympathetic overdrive, neurogenic damage to autonomic regulatory systems, and activation of the systemic inflammatory response leading to vasodilation (11–13). The variability in patient presentation after TBI prompts further investigation for mechanistic and prognostic factors for cardiac dysfunction, which is known to be affected by elevated catecholamines after TBI.

A practical, noninvasive clinical evaluation for cardiac dysfunction is echocardiography (echo) (14, 15). Previous studies of echo findings in TBI patients, inclusive of retrospective and prospective study designs, have demonstrated a varied incidence of cardiac dysfunction and an inconsistent relationship of TBI-related cardiac dysfunction and mortality (4, 10, 16–21). A meta-analysis of blunt and penetrating adult TBI estimated that the incidence of myocardial injury, defined as elevated troponin levels, after moderate-to-severe TBI was 33% and the incidence of cardiac dysfunction after TBI was 16% (4). Abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG) findings after TBI, including prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc), have also been demonstrated (20, 22). Possible explanations for the variance in findings between studies include the mechanism of trauma, penetrating or blunt intracranial injury, and medical comorbidities, injury severity, among others.

Within existing literature exploring cardiac dysfunction after TBI, findings on echo reports include increased incidence of reduced ejection fraction (EF), left ventricular regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMAs), decreased basal fractional shortening (FS), and decreased early (E) mitral inflow deceleration time (DT) (8, 10, 16, 17, 19). However, not all studies examined all these parameters, and at least one study found a preserved rather than reduced EF (19). EF and basal FS both represent common measures of systolic function. EF is defined as the stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume, commonly estimated through echo using Simpson biplane method of discs from an apical approach (23). Basal FS is another echocardiographic parameter defined as the percentage change in left ventricular diameter between the end of diastole and systole, typically measured by M-mode in parasternal long-axis view just beyond the tip of the mitral valve leaflets, a mid-ventricular position (23). FS has been considered as a possible/alternate estimate of systolic function in prior studies since it can reliably be obtained in the parasternal long-axis view while the patient is supine, whereas EF is obtained best when the patient is in the left lateral decubitus position, which is not always feasible in TBI patients due to variable intracranial pressure with movement (8, 10).

We aimed to characterize the incidence and nature of cardiac dysfunction after blunt TBI with intracranial abnormalities on radiographic imaging.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective review of the trauma registry at an American College of Surgeons verified Level 1 Trauma Center and medical records for all blunt trauma patients admitted to the hospital with TBI, as noted on CT and/or MRI, with hospitalization during 2022–2023, with echo performed within about 48 hours of hospital admission. This convenience sample was selected as a representative sample of TBI patients admitted to our hospital. As such, our study is at least comparably powered as compared with others that studied echocardiographic findings in TBI, as identified in a meta-analysis (4). Comorbidities in the trauma registry are coded in accordance with the annually updated National Trauma Data Standard (NTDS) (24). As the NTDS cardiac comorbidity criteria are very strict, we also used chart review to determine if patients met inclusion criteria.

Patients with known significant cardiac comorbidities such as cardiac arrest on presentation, any previous cardiac surgery, pacemaker or other implanted cardiac device, heart failure, arrhythmia, ischemic heart disease identified on registry and/or chart review were excluded from the study. Patients with conduction blocks were excluded if it was determined by manual review of the echo images that the block affected left ventricular contractility at the mid-ventricle. Similarly, patients were also excluded from review if echo was conducted outside of a 48-hour window from time of hospital admission. Patients with a history of epilepsy were excluded as altered cardiac conduction and function in these patients have been reported in human and animal studies (25–27).

Data collected from the trauma registry included demographic information, mechanism of injury, disposition following initial presentation, and hospital outcomes. Data collected from the electronic medical record included the index CT of the head report and any repeat CT scan reports within 48 hours of admission, ECG reports preceding the echo, vasopressor and inotrope use within 2 hours of echo, beta blocker or calcium channel blocker use on the day of echo, medical history including cardiac history and cardiology evaluation, and echo report.

Specific variables extracted from written echo reports included FS measured at the mid-left ventricular chamber in parasternal long-axis view, left ventricular EF measured by Simpson method or much less frequently visual assessment, presence of RWMAs, and mitral inflow E-wave DT. We defined a decreased FS at below 30% in accordance with our institutional standard. Of note, the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) defines normal FS as 25% or higher; although there is some heterogeneity in the literature (vide infra) (23, 28). In accordance with ASE, a normal EF was defined as 52% or higher in males and 54% or higher in females (23). Echo images of patients with incomplete information in the echo report were reviewed by National Board of Echocardiography testamurs with special Competence in Adult Echocardiography or Critical Care Echocardiography.

Injury severity in each body region was measured by the abbreviated injury score (AIS) (29). All patients had head injury with AIS greater than or equal to 2. Data analysis included univariate statistics reported as median with interquartile ranges with Mann-Whitney U test performed to detect a difference in median values and either chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher exact test performed for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined at α equals to 0.05 for all statistical tests employed.

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board at our University (IRB Number 2024-00421, TBI Retrospective ECHO, Approved September 16, 2024). All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible institutional committee on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 (30).

RESULTS

Of 434 blunt TBI patients screened, 105 patients met inclusion criteria, having no significant cardiac comorbidities recorded and undergoing an echo performed within 48 hours of admission. Patients were divided into two cohorts: those with decreased FS and those with normal FS (Fig. 1). One patient with increased FS was excluded from this grouped analysis. All patients but one were admitted through the emergency department (ED), with 17.4% of decreased FS and 29.6% of normal FS patients being transferred into our hospital, p values of equals to 0.30.

Echocardiographic findings were subsequently reviewed. The EF was preserved in 83% (19/23) of the decreased FS cohort and in all normal FS cohort, with median values of 61% and 68%, respectively (Table 3). Among the decreased FS cohort, four patients had a reduced EF (i.e., 39%, 40%, 47%, and 53%), whereby the EF was negligibly reduced in the latter patient using the gender specific criteria. The association between FS and EF was subsequently assessed for each group. Within the decreased FS group, EF and FS had negligible correlation (r = 0.12) (Fig. 2). Within the normal FS group, EF and FS were positively, albeit not highly, correlated (r = 0.37). The groups did not reach a statistically significant difference in RWMAs (13.0% vs. 2.5%, p = 0.07). The mitral valve DT was similar between groups. There was no significant difference in the frequency of at least mild-moderate or more severe valvular abnormalities (17.4% vs. 17.3%) and/or diastolic dysfunction (8.7% vs. 8.6%) between the FS decreased and FS normal groups. In addition, 1 patient in the normal FS group had a moderate effusion. Only one patient with decreased FS underwent a repeat echo during hospitalization, where the FS remained decreased.

DISCUSSION

This study identified two distinct cohorts of blunt TBI patients, those with decreased FS (< 30%) and those with normal FS (≥= 30%) on echo performed within 48 hours of admission. The decreased FS group, with preserved EF in 83% of patients, demonstrated longer ICU LOS, higher inpatient mortality rates, and higher rates of craniotomy/craniectomy.

This study adds to the limited literature on cardiac dysfunction after TBI, the association of depressed FS with worse outcomes, and the association of cardiac dysfunction with lower Glasgow Coma Scale and more severe head injury (4, 10, 16, 21). However, our study contrasts with existing literature in that only a few of our patients had decreased EF; both decreased EF and RWMA have been proposed as definitions for cardiac dysfunction after TBI (4, 22). Some research has identified depressed systolic function following TBI as an FS less than 25, which is also the standard proposed by the ASE (10, 23). The above study was excluded from the above meta-analysis (4, 22). However, in other cardiac literature, FS above 30% has been associated with normal EF (31). We used the institutional cutoff of 30% for decreased FS. Despite using a higher cutoff value for considering decreased FS, we observed significantly worse clinical outcomes in these patients, suggesting that a cutoff for FS of 30% may be sensitive for clinically significant cardiac dysfunction after TBI, even without concomitant reduction in EF.

EF remains the most widely used measure of global systolic performance. It reflects a volumetric endpoint and may fail to detect more subtle abnormalities in myocardial mechanics, especially those that occur early in the course of neurocardiac injury (32). Only four TBI patients demonstrated a reduced EF, and all were in the decreased FS group. The observation of reduced FS despite preserved EF in 83% of decreased FS patients suggests that regional or directional components of ventricular contraction, particularly radial shortening, may be impaired even when overall chamber emptying remains apparently normal. In support of this notion, the data demonstrated a modest correlation between EF when FS was normal, but a negligible correlation between the two when FS was reduced. Conceivably, the discrepancy between EF and FS may also be associated with compensatory hypercontractility in other regions of the left ventricle, which enables these patients to preserve stroke volume and thereby EF (33).

Acute TBI is associated with profound sympathetic activation, catecholamine-mediated myocardial stress, and rapid shifts in preload and afterload, all of which can alter ventricular mechanics and function (34, 35). Because FS is derived from linear measurements of ventricular diameter, it is particularly sensitive to these loading conditions and to changes in ventricular geometry. In contrast, EF reflects the integrated contributions of longitudinal, circumferential, and torsional myocardial contraction, allowing compensatory mechanisms to preserve overall global ejection even when one component of contraction is impaired. As a result, a low-FS/preserved-EF phenotype may represent transient neurogenic myocardial stunning, loading-related mechanical alterations, or early myocardial injury not yet detectable by conventional EF assessment.

Similar phenomena are seen in certain patients with aneurysmal SAH known as “neurogenic stunned myocardium,” which leads to cardiac systolic dysfunction due to a catecholamine surge and its after effects (5). Myocardial injury (i.e., troponin elevation) has also been suggested as a downstream sign of these processes and, for TBI specifically, has been estimated at an incidence of 33% (4, 36). Contrary to a previous review, in our study, troponin levels were not elevated overall and were not significantly different between groups with and without decreased FS (20). We also evaluated ECG findings for myocardial ischemia/injury and did not identify any; however, a substantial number of patients in both groups had nonspecific ST/T changes. ECG abnormalities have been previously identified in 20–54% of TBI cases (20). Theoretically, a more limited change in loading conditions as indicated by a depressed FS without RWMA or decreased EF would not result in myocardial necrosis and consequently elevated troponins or ECG changes. The only arrhythmias identified in both groups on the index ECG were sinus bradycardia/tachycardia, which occurred significantly more often in the decreased FS group. Tachycardia would be a part of the catecholamine surge (20). We noted a significantly higher rate of IVH in the decreased FS group. Although the reason for this is unclear, it may represent an anatomical explanation for catecholamine-induced cardiac dysfunction in this group. It is possible that hemorrhage in the ventricles disrupts Reissner’s fiber, a structure within the cerebral aqueduct and fourth ventricle that is involved in the regulation of cerebrospinal fluid concentration of catecholamines (37).

Although transient, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy has been reported after head injury in case reports and cohort studies (38–40). Takotsubo cardiomyopathy has also been suggested to share a similar catecholamine surge mechanism with cardiac dysfunction after TBI (33, 41). Echo in patients afflicted with Takotsubo cardiomyopathy classically demonstrates a reduction in EF at admission with subsequent improvement, as well as left ventricular RWMAs which have been proposed to follow either an apical ballooning or mid-ventricular akinetic morphologic variant (42, 43). To this end, the greater frequency of SAH and IVH in the decreased FS group would correspond with the classic description of intracranial aneurysmal rupture with attendant SAH (38).

Another mechanism for cardiac dysfunction may be a neuroinflammatory process that occurs after blunt trauma or neurologic surgical intervention, such as craniotomy (44). Patients in our decreased FS cohort had significantly higher rates of craniotomy or craniectomy. All patients who underwent either craniotomy or craniectomy had echo performed after the intervention. Neuroinflammation resulting from either this intervention or the TBI itself may have led to secondary cardiac dysfunction, with the release of inflammatory mediators affecting the myocardium via hematogenous spread and in a heterogenous distribution.

It follows that comorbidities which selectively impair longitudinal contractility of the left ventricle (as opposed to short axis shortening which is what is assessed by FS) would not permit such compensation. Mathematical modeling has suggested that mid-ventricular axial shortening of the left ventricle has a greater contribution to stroke volume and EF, but longitudinal shortening can compensate in pathologic states impairing circumferential or axial strain (44). In turn, age-associated heart remodeling, which facilitates enhancing longitudinal shortening would be protective against this form of TBI-related cardiac dysfunction.

Large-scale population studies have informed that increasing age is associated with reduced left ventricular end-diastolic volume, decreased left ventricular length, and decreased short-axis ventricular function, all of which increase the contribution of longitudinal shortening to stroke volume (45). A recent large national trauma database study identified that echo after TBI was only ordered in about 5.3% of patients and is typically ordered in patients who are older or have significant cardiac comorbidities (14). Our study with early echo therefore embodies a much higher percentage and is notable for excluding those with cardiac disease. Indeed, the median age of our decreased FS cohort was younger than the normal FS cohort. The association of decreased age with systolic dysfunction after TBI has been reported in other studies (10). An alternative explanation is that catecholamine responsiveness decreases with age, thus blunting the effect on short-axis contractile dysfunction (46).

Vasoactive catecholamines such as epinephrine and norepinephrine impair myocardial function over time by increasing demand without a corresponding increase in supply. In a similar manner, vasopressor use can contribute to cardiac dysfunction, such as that seen in patients with elevated levels of hematogenous catecholamines (47). In this study, however, only 5 of the 23 patients with decreased FS required vasopressor/inotropic support around the time of the echo. Since the remaining patients with decreased FS were not receiving vasopressors around the time of the echo, this factor alone does not account for the observed systolic dysfunction. Conversely, we evaluated for the presence of agents that could decrease cardiac contractility (i.e., beta-blockers) before echo and did not find any significant difference. Early treatment with beta-blockers has been suggested elsewhere in randomized trials to improve mortality in TBI patients (34). The calcium channel blockers used were peripherally acting in all cases except one case in the decreased FS group, and hence would not account for any reduction in cardiac function in the rest. We are unable to determine how much systolic dysfunction contributed to the worse in-hospital outcomes observed in our decreased FS cohort, given that EF was preserved in the majority. It remains unclear whether systolic dysfunction is a proximal cause of worse clinical outcomes in these patients, or a proxy measure for an underlying systemic process associated with worse clinical outcomes following TBI.

These findings support the need for more comprehensive imaging strategies when evaluating cardiac function in patients with severe neurologic injury, regardless of age or cardiac comorbidities. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial Simpson biplane EF measurements, global longitudinal strain, mitral annular plane systolic excursion, cardiac biomarker trends, neuroinflammatory markers, and detailed hemodynamic data, including vasopressor exposure and fluid status. In addition, 3D echo may provide an even more accurate assessment of ventricular function by directly measuring ventricular volumes without the geometric assumptions inherent to 2D imaging. Three-dimensional volumetric EF measurements have been shown to correlate more closely with cardiac MRI and may therefore offer greater sensitivity for detecting subtle global systolic dysfunction (48).

LIMITATIONS

This is a retrospective trauma review at a single suburban trauma center over a limited time frame. While we excluded patients with cardiac comorbidities identified on either registry review or on chart review, not all patients had an echocardiogram documented in their chart before the traumatic event that prompted initial evaluation. We were therefore unable to account for patients with undiagnosed cardiac dysfunction, and these patients may have been included in this study. The presence of valvular disease and/or diastolic dysfunction in a fraction of patients in both groups would likely support this concept. There is a corresponding lack of baseline EF before injury recorded for our patients. In addition, had the trauma registry been used exclusively to exclude cardiac comorbidities, there would have been a greater number of patients that would have been included in this study. Separately, we included patients with isolated head trauma as well as polytrauma. Although the median ISS was similar between the two groups, the lower frequency of ISS less than 9 in the decreased FS group and the relatively limited frequency of severe injuries to non-head regions in both was relatively limited, the other injuries may be other potential confounders for cardiac dysfunction. The presence of hemorrhagic shock may be another confounder, although the frequency of MTP activation at ED presentation, median index blood pressure in the ED, and median index pulse in the ED were not significantly different. Finally, there was one patient with increased FS with normal EF and moderate diastolic dysfunction, who was not included in either group because FS was neither normal nor decreased.

CONCLUSIONS

The preliminary single trauma center retrospective data demonstrate an association between decreased FS, despite preserved EF, on echo had higher ICU LOS, in-hospital mortality, and more often underwent craniotomy or craniectomy. Further study of FS is needed to determine if it is an artifact or indeed a marker of more severe blunt traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. The higher frequency of SAH and IVH in the decreased FS group, in the absence of significant differences in the frequency of severe injuries or hemorrhagic shock at admission between groups is notable and hypothesis-generating. An integrated approach combining 3D volumetric imaging, myocardial strain analysis, and physiologic data could help determine whether the low-FS phenotype observed in significant TBI represents reversible neurogenic myocardial stunning, transient loading effects, or the early manifestation of a clinically meaningful myocardial injury syndrome. Clarifying this distinction would not only improve our understanding of the brain–heart interaction in neurocritical illness but may also help refine cardiac monitoring and risk stratification strategies in patients with severe TBI.
