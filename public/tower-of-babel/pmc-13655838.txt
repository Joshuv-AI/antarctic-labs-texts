Risk factors for plastic bronchitis complicating severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children and development of a machine learning-based predictive model
Authors: Kun Jiang, Silei Yan, Haoxiang Gu, Liling Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlastic bronchitis (PB) is a rare but life-threatening complication of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP) in children, and effective tools for its early prediction remain limited. This study aimed to identify risk factors for PB complicating SMPP and develop a machine learning-based predictive model.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 166 children (aged 1–14 years) with SMPP admitted between January 2022 and December 2024. Patients were classified into PB (n=51) and non-PB (n=115) groups based on bronchoscopy and randomly split into training and validation sets (7:3 ratio). Eight machine learning algorithms were developed and evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision-recall curve (PRC), decision curve analysis (DCA), accuracy, and F1-score. The optimal model was selected and interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Candidate risk factors were identified and further analyzed using multivariable logistic regression to develop a nomogram. The predictive model was assessed using both sets and further validated by bootstrap resampling.ResultsGeneralized linear model (GLM) achieved the highest performance [AUC: 0.951, cross-validation AUC: 0.915, accuracy 0.880, F1-score: 0.825, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC): 0.856] in the training set, representing the optimal model. Fever duration, serum prealbumin, and D-dimer were the most influential features in SHAP analysis. Multivariable analysis confirmed these as independent risk factors: fever duration [odds ratio (OR): 1.550, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.297–1.908, P<0.001], prealbumin (OR: 0.959, 95% CI: 0.939–0.976, P<0.001), and D-dimer (OR: 1.723, 95% CI: 1.157–2.627, P=0.009). The nomogram exhibited acceptable discriminative ability [C-index: 0.933 (95% CI: 0.887–0.980) in the training set; 0.831 (95% CI: 0.717–0.946) in the validation set; 0.913 (95% CI: 0.869–0.957) in bootstrap validation] and calibration (Brier score: 0.106, 0.142, and 0.103, respectively). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded χ2=5.983 for internal validation [P=0.649 (>0.05)], suggesting acceptable model fit, with DCA confirming clinical utility across 0–90% threshold probabilities.ConclusionsFever duration, serum prealbumin, and D-dimer are independent risk factors for PB in children with SMPP. This internally derived nomogram achieved moderate discrimination and acceptable calibration in our limited single-center cohort. Further external validation in independent patient cohorts is therefore necessary to verify its generalizability and clinical utility.

Highlight box

<bold>Key findings</bold>

• Machine-learning-driven analysis identified prolonged fever duration, decreased serum prealbumin and elevated D-dimer as independent risk factors for plastic bronchitis (PB) in children with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP), and a three-variable nomogram demonstrated acceptable performance via internal hold-out and bootstrap validation.

<bold>What is known and what is new?</bold>

• SMPP in children may be complicated by PB, which can lead to severe respiratory sequelae. Flexible bronchoscopy is the gold-standard diagnostic test for PB but is too invasive for routine screening. Reliable non‑invasive predictive tools for PB remain limited.

• Using eight machine‑learning algorithms, we identified fever duration, prealbumin and D-dimer as three independent predictors for PB among children with SMPP. An internally validated nomogram was constructed based on these three readily available clinical and laboratory indicators.

<bold>What is the implication, and what should change now?</bold>

• The easy-to-use nomogram exhibits acceptable discrimination, calibration and net clinical benefit. It may assist clinicians in stratifying PB risk and reducing unnecessary bronchoscopy, pending further external validation.

Introduction

The outbreaks of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), the most common pathogen responsible for lower respiratory tract infections in children, occur every 3–5 years (1-3). It accounts for 20–40% of all community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) cases. Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) typically presents as self-limiting persistent dry cough and fever (4). However, the emergence of macrolide-resistant MP strains since 2020 has increased the incidence of refractory MPP (RMPP), severe MPP (SMPP), and fulminant MPP (4,5). Compared with uncomplicated MPP, SMPP and RMPP are associated with substantially higher rates of complications (6,7). Specifically, the incidence of complications such as atelectasis, bronchiolitis obliterans, and bronchiectasis increases from 10.5% in uncomplicated MPP to 42% in SMPP and RMPP (8).

Respiratory tract infection represents the leading cause of plastic bronchitis (PB) in Asia, with MP being the predominant responsible pathogen (9-11). MP infection can induce severe airway inflammation, causing mucus plug formation and airway obstruction (12,13). Without prompt intervention, this can progress to bronchial casting, which is characterized by inflammatory bronchial stenosis, obstruction, and granulation tissue hyperplasia. This, in turn, can lead to long-term sequelae such as atelectasis, secondary bronchiectasis, and bronchiolitis obliterans, which significantly compromise pulmonary function and quality of life. Therefore, early diagnosis of PB and prompt bronchoscopic cast removal are critical to prevent life-threatening complications and reduce long-term morbidity (14,15).

Despite being essential for diagnosing and managing PB, the invasive nature and associated risks of flexible bronchoscopy limit its routine use (14,15). Consequently, an accurate, simple, non-invasive predictive model using readily available clinical parameters would assist clinical decision-making and reduce unnecessary bronchoscopic procedures. To address this need, we aimed to (I) identify risk factors for PB in children with SMPP using machine learning, (II) develop and internally validate a predictive model, and (III) construct a clinically applicable nomogram for early PB diagnosis. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0579/rc).

This retrospective cohort study included 166 children (aged 1–14 years) who were hospitalized in the Department of Respiratory Medicine at the Children’s Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine with a discharge diagnosis of SMPP between January 2022 and December 2024. The patients were classified into PB (n=51) and non-PB (n=115) groups based on bronchoscopy and randomly split into training and validation sets (7:3 ratio) using a random number table (Table 1).

Diagnostic criteria

MP infection was diagnosed by either a positive serum-specific MP immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody (acute-phase titer ≥1:160) or positive MP-DNA detected by real-time fluorescent polymerase chain reaction in throat swabs or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). SMPP, diagnosed according to the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2025 Edition) (16), was defined as severe CAP or MPP at risk of sequelae.

PB was diagnosed based on (I) bronchoscopic findings showing hyperemia and edema of the respiratory mucosa, increased mucus secretion, and bronchial luminal occlusion by inflammatory bronchial casts (BCs) that expanded into a branching plastic morphology when removed with biopsy forceps and immersed in normal saline (9 g/L); and (II) histopathological findings demonstrating BCs containing extensive inflammatory cells (predominantly eosinophils and neutrophils) and desquamated epithelial cells, with positive immunohistochemical staining for CD3, CD20, CD68, and myeloperoxidase (MPO). No blinding was performed for outcome assessment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were (I) age ≤14 years; (II) SMPP diagnosis (16); (III) written informed consent obtained from parents or legal guardians; and (IV) complete clinical data.

Exclusion criteria comprised (I) recurrent respiratory tract infections, asthma, chronic pulmonary diseases, malignancies, hepatorenal diseases, connective tissue diseases, previous cardiac surgery, hematological disorders, or immunodeficiency disorders; (II) recovery phase of MPP; (III) concurrent infection with other pathogens or Mycobacterium tuberculosis; (IV) foreign body aspiration; or (V) incomplete clinical data.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (approval No. 2026R084-E01). Written informed consent for the clinical procedures was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants. For this retrospective study, the requirement for additional informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the use of anonymized data.

Statistical analysis and machine learning

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Thirty-one independent variables were processed to address potential multicollinearity using the following criteria: variance inflation factor (VIF) threshold ≤5, high correlation cutoff ≤0.7, and standard deviation threshold for constant variables ≤0.05. Eight machine learning algorithms—random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), generalized linear model (GLM), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), gradient boosting machine (GBM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), decision tree (DT), and naive Bayes (NB)—were developed and evaluated in the training and validation sets. In the preprocessing workflow, feature normalization followed our consistent training-data-only processing strategy used for collinearity and variable screening. Z-score scaling parameters were estimated solely from the training set and reused on the validation cohort to avoid information leakage, while tree algorithms required no scaling.

All preprocessing and feature screening procedures were fitted exclusively on the training dataset to avoid data leakage. Missing value imputation, median-based outlier truncation, one-hot encoding and standardization were trained using only training samples; the calculated median, quantile and scaling parameters were directly applied to the validation set without re-estimation. Variable filtering including variance screening, VIF and Pearson correlation filtering was performed within training data only, and no statistics derived from validation samples leaked into the modeling pipeline.

To mitigate unstable performance evaluation stemming from the small sample size and limited positive PB events, we implemented five repeats of stratified 10-fold cross-validation (CV) exclusively within the 70% training cohort. The 30% hold-out validation set was reserved only for auxiliary external reference and not involved in CV or model screening. We trained eight machine learning algorithms for exploratory performance comparison, CV metrics serve as the primary screening criterion, and hold-out validation results are only used for secondary supplementary reference, and the final interpretable clinical nomogram was built using parsimonious L2-regularized GLM with superior anti-overfitting performance, instead of complex ensemble models.

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons

Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables with normal distribution, expressed as mean ± standard deviation, were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables, presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)], were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Predictive model development

A predictive model for PB was constructed using the rms package in R. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision-recall curve (PRC), decision curve analysis (DCA), accuracy, and F1-score. The optimal model was selected based on the above-mentioned metrics and interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify the most influential features. Candidate risk factors were subsequently analyzed using multivariable logistic regression to develop a nomogram. Then, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) in addition to the hold-out validation set. Afterward, model calibration was evaluated using calibration curves and the Brier score. No differences in study setting, eligibility criteria, outcome definition or predictor variables existed between the validation cohort and development cohort. Statistical significance was set at two-tailed P<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 166 children with SMPP, 51 (30.7%) developed PB, while 115 (69.3%) did not (Figure S1). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics. Compared with the non-PB group, the PB group had longer fever duration {median [IQR]: 11 [10–13] vs. 8 [6–10] days; P<0.001}, higher rates of hypoxemia (37.3% vs. 5.2%; P<0.001), glucocorticoid use (90.2% vs. 47.0%; P<0.001), IVIG administration (31.4% vs. 3.5%; P<0.001), and multilobar atelectasis (≥2 lobes; 51.0% vs. 16.5%; P<0.001). Laboratory markers showed significantly higher D-dimer, CRP, PCT, ALT, LDH, ferritin, IL-6, and IL-8 levels and lower albumin and prealbumin levels in BALF in the PB group (all P<0.05). Age, sex, pleural effusion, MP 2063/2064 mutation rate, ESR, immunoglobulin E (IgE), BALF WBC count and differential count, or IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels showed no significant differences (Table S1).

Machine learning model comparison

Following sequential filtering for constant predictors (standard deviation threshold ≤0.05) and multicollinearity (VIF and Pearson correlation screening), 31 independent variables were retained to construct machine learning prediction models (Figures S2-S4). All screening thresholds were generated from training data to prevent leakage-induced overoptimistic indices. Given its superior performance, GLM was selected for further analysis. The exceptional training performance [AUC: 0.951, CV-AUC: 0.915, area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC): 0.856, CV-AUPRC: 0.730, F1 score: 0.825, CV-F1 score: 0.584] (Table 2, Figure S5) suggests potential overfitting; however, validation set performance (AUC: 0.900) supported model robustness.

GLM model interpretation

The GLM model identified the top 10 predictive features from 31 candidate variables: fever duration, serum prealbumin, serum D-dimer, BALF IL-6, serum albumin, BALF neutrophil percentage, sex, BALF IL-8, MP DNA copy number, and serum PCT (Figures 1,2). SHAP analysis revealed that fever duration, prealbumin, and D-dimer were the three most influential features. Partial dependence plots (Figure 3) showed that fever duration and D-dimer were positively associated with PB probability, whereas prealbumin demonstrated a negative association. SHAP summary plots (Figure 4) further illustrated the direction and magnitude of these associations.

Model performance

The nomogram demonstrated acceptable discrimination [C-index: 0.933 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.887–0.980) in the training set; 0.831 (95% CI: 0.717–0.946) in the validation set; 0.913 (95% CI: 0.869–0.957) in bootstrap validation] and good calibration (Brier score: 0.106, 0.142, and 0.103, respectively) (Figures 7,8). The slight performance decrease from the training to the validation set (maximum ΔC-index: 0.102; maximum ΔBrier score: 0.039) indicates minimal overfitting. Additional performance metrics across the three sets were as follows: 0.846 in the training set, 0.755 in the validation set, and 0.819 in bootstrap validation for accuracy; 0.889 in the training set, 0.800 in the validation set, and 0.863 in bootstrap validation for sensitivity; 0.827 in the training set, 0.735 in the validation set, and 0.800 in bootstrap validation for specificity; and 0.781 in the training set, 0.667 in the validation set, and 0.746 in bootstrap validation for F1-score (Figures 9-11).

Individual risk factor performance

As standalone predictors, fever duration showed the highest discriminatory ability [C-index: 0.809 (95% CI: 0.746–0.872)], followed by D-dimer [C-index: 0.779 (95% CI: 0.710–0.848)] and prealbumin [C-index: 0.771 (95% CI: 0.680–0.861)]. Optimal cutoff values were 8.5 days for fever duration, 93.75 mg/L for prealbumin, and 0.925 mg/L for D-dimer (Tables 4,5, Figure 13).

Discussion

MPP is the leading cause of CAP in Chinese children aged ≥5 years. Direct MP-mediated cytotoxicity and dysregulated host immune responses increase disease severity, with SMPP and RMPP conferring substantial risks of intrapulmonary and extrapulmonary complications (16). PB, a critical complication of SMPP, is associated with significant morbidity and can be fatal. Early identification of PB risk factors is essential for improving clinical outcomes (16).

Key findings and pathophysiological mechanisms

We strictly separated training and validation data during all preprocessing and variable selection steps to avoid information leakage, a common factor causing artificially high predictive performance. Every statistical rule for feature processing was trained on training samples only, which enhanced the credibility of validation-set metrics. This study identified fever duration, serum prealbumin, and D-dimer as independent predictors of PB in children with SMPP. The derived nomogram demonstrated acceptable discrimination (C-index: 0.933) and clinical utility (DCA: 0–90%) using routinely available parameters.

SHAP and DCA were applied to interpret nonlinear risk associations and net clinical benefit, which cannot be captured by conventional simple logistic regression. Multiple machine learning models served only as auxiliary comparisons rather than core clinical prediction models. We performed multi-algorithm screening to validate the stability of the three risk predictors, instead of emphasizing complex machine learning architectures.

Fever duration reflects persistent MP infection and host inflammatory response. Our optimal cutoff (8.5 days) is shorter than the 10 days reported by Gong et al. for RMPP (17), suggesting that PB may precede refractory disease. This aligns with Zhao et al.’s study (18), which demonstrated prolonged fever in PB-complicated RMPP, as well as Huang et al.’s research (19), which identified fever duration as a risk factor for mucus plug formation in adenovirus pneumonia.

Prealbumin, a negative acute-phase reactant with a short half-life (~2 days), has superior sensitivity, compared to albumin, for detecting nutritional compromise and inflammation severity (20). Our cutoff (<93.75 mg/L) is substantially lower than Wang et al.’s 141.6 mg/L for SMPP (21) and Zhang et al.’s 144.5 mg/L for bronchial mucus plugs (14), likely reflecting the more severe inflammatory burden of PB. The inverse relationship between prealbumin and IL-6 (20) supports its role as a proxy for cytokine-driven pathology.

D-dimer elevation indicates fibrinolytic activation secondary to vascular endothelial injury and hypoxia. Our findings corroborate Qiu et al. (22) and Zheng et al.’s results (23), which demonstrated the superiority of D-dimer over conventional markers (CRP and LDH) for predicting MPP severity. Furthermore, the negative correlation with prealbumin suggests the coupled activation of inflammatory and coagulation cascades (Table 6).

In addition, Xu et al. (27) constructed a 5-variable PB nomogram including age, fever peak, atelectasis, albumin and LDH, with internal AUC 0.8768 and external AUC 0.8399. Our model only adopts three routine non-imaging indicators and delivers comparable predictive efficiency for easier bedside use. Besides, we adopted eight machine learning algorithms and SHAP for objective feature screening, which was absent in their study. Our model offers three advantages: (I) objective, routinely available parameters eliminating treatment-related subjectivity; (II) rapid turnaround (all tests available within hours); (III) stability of the results since multicollinearity was assessed among all candidate variables before model construction; and (IV) machine learning–optimized integration with demonstrated calibration and clinical utility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our cohort of 16 children with SMPP was relatively small compared with existing PB prediction models, and the single-center retrospective design inevitably introduced selection bias. A simple 7:3 train-test split may yield unstable predictive metrics given our limited PB events; thus, we conducted five repeats of stratified 10-fold CV on training data to generate more robust performance values, and we avoided overinterpreting optimistic training-set results throughout the Results section. Second, immune function (lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulins) and pulmonary function tests were not assessed, and these may refine risk stratification. Third, external validation in independent cohorts, which was not performed, is essential before clinical implementation. Fourth, the obvious gap between training and cross-validated F1-scores is attributed to the limited number of PB positive events in the small training cohort, indicating mild overfitting risk.

Conclusions

Fever duration, serum prealbumin, and D-dimer are independent, readily available predictors of PB in children with SMPP. The internally validated nomogram demonstrates acceptable discrimination and calibration, supporting its potential for clinical decision-making, yet pending external validation. Children with fever lasting >8.5 days, prealbumin <93.75 mg/L, and D-dimer >0.925 mg/L warrant heightened surveillance for PB and early bronchoscopy.
