Optimizing diagnosis of Bacillus cereus bacteremia using time to positivity: a retrospective observational study to differentiate true bacteremia from contamination
Authors: Kohei Hasegawa, Kumiko Suzuki, Mitsuyo Nakano, Kenya Murata, Yoshihiko Ogawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBacillus cereus can occasionally cause invasive infections, irrespective of the host’s immune status. However, the presence of B. cereus in a single blood culture is typically considered a contaminant, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true incidence of B. cereus bacteremia. This study aimed to evaluate the cut-off value for time to positivity (TTP) to distinguish true bacteremia from contamination.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included patients with positive blood cultures for B. cereus between November 2011 and February 2025. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated to evaluate the diagnostic performance of TTP, which was achieved by plotting the sensitivity (the true-positive rate) on the y-axis against 1 – specificity (the false-positive rate) on the x-axis for a series of potential TTP cut-off points.ResultsDuring the study period, 49 patients were enrolled, of whom 18 were classified into the true bacteremia group and 31 into the contamination group. All patients had at least two sets of blood cultures collected. The median TTP was significantly shorter among patients in the true bacteremia group (7.8 h; interquartile range [IQR], 6.5–8.9) than in the contamination group (11.2 h; IQR, 9.9–13) (p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis indicated that a cutoff value of 9.0 h achieved the highest sensitivity (77.8%) and specificity (90.3%) for predicting true bacteremia, while a more lenient threshold of TTP > 13.0 h yielded 100% sensitivity for identifying contamination.ConclusionsA cut-off value of 9.0 h can differentiate true B. cereus bacteremia from contamination, whereas a TTP > 13 h is indicative of contamination. Given its suboptimal diagnostic accuracy, TTP can help differentiate true B. cereus bacteremia from contamination but should be interpreted with clinical and laboratory data for the diagnosis of sepsis.

Background

Bacillus cereus is a spore-forming, aerobic, facultative, Gram-positive rod commonly found in the environment, including soil, marine habitats, vegetables, invertebrate intestinal tracts, and human skin [1]. B. cereus shares many behavioral and genetic traits with other Bacillus species, particularly B. anthracis [2]. B. cereus is a major cause of food poisoning [3]. Additionally, B. cereus can cause invasive human infections, including traumatic or surgical wound infections, prosthesis-related infections, and, most notably, catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs). These occur across all age groups, from neonates to older adults [1, 4].

B. cereus is typically regarded as a contaminant unless isolated from multiple sequential blood cultures [5, 6]. However, a retrospective study conducted in Switzerland revealed that 37.5% of episodes in which B. cereus was isolated from blood cultures were diagnosed as true bacteremia, defined as more than one positive blood culture bottle with signs of sepsis or localized infection [7]. Clinically, fatal or complicated courses have been documented—particularly in patients with hematologic diseases—underscoring that a “contaminant” result may still represent true sepsis [1]. Therefore, the incidence of true B. cereus bacteremia may be underrecognized, and any positive B. cereus blood culture result should prompt closer scrutiny of the true infection.

Previous studies have shown that the time to positivity (TTP), which is the duration from the initiation of a blood culture to a positive result, can help differentiate true bacteremia from contamination [8, 9]. Although the TTP for true B. cereus bacteremia may be shorter than that for contamination in patients with hematological diseases [10], no previous study has evaluated the efficacy of TTP in other patient populations. This study aimed to determine the optimal TTP cutoff value for differentiating true B. cereus bacteremia from contamination.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, retrospective, observational study was conducted at the Sakai City Medical Center in Osaka, Japan, a 487-bed tertiary care facility. The study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (IRB approval number: 24–487).

Data collection and patient characteristics

We identified patients with positive blood cultures for B. cereus between November 2011 and February 2025. Consecutive positive blood cultures were considered as a single episode. Patients with bacteremia caused by multiple bacterial species were excluded because the presence of additional species could affect the TTP. Pediatric cases were also excluded from the study. Data on age, sex, collection site (outpatient or inpatient), and the number of positive blood culture bottles were extracted from electronic medical records.

We compared the TTP between the true bacteremia and contamination groups to evaluate its usefulness as a distinguishing factor.

Definition

TTP was defined as the time from the start of culture to the first bottle test positive. Cases were classified as true bacteremia if they met at least one of the following criteria: (1) at least two sets of positive blood cultures obtained within 24 h of each other; (2) at least one set of positive blood cultures accompanied by a positive specimen from the suspected infection site (if the positive specimen is a catheter tip, it must be accompanied by a positive peripheral blood culture); (3) at least one positive blood culture set with signs of inflammation at the catheter insertion site, and either a culture of exit-site exudate yielding the same organism or clinical evidence of suppurative thrombophlebitis; or (4) persistent bacteremia, defined as two blood culture collections at least 24 h apart with growth in ≥ 1 set at each collection (whether a single set or 1 of 2 sets) [6, 11]. Cases that did not meet any of these criteria were classified as contaminated.

Microbiological examinations

At our institution, blood cultures are performed using hospital-specified methods [6] for both inpatients and outpatients. After collection, the samples were promptly transported to the hospital’s microbiology laboratory. Samples are accepted 24 h a day, and culture is promptly initiated after submission, regardless of whether the sample was collected from an inpatient or an outpatient.

Blood samples were processed using the BacT/ALERT® system (bioMérieux, France) until July 31, 2015, and the BACTEC® system (Becton Dickinson, US) from August 1, 2015, onwards. Blood culture collection methods adhered to the microbiology laboratory guidelines [12]. B. cereus was identified using the BD BBL Crystal RGP Identification System (Becton Dickinson, US) (https://www.bdj.co.jp/pdf/111–245150 N-01.pdf) and egg yolk reactions until April 30, 2022. From May 1, 2022, B. cereus identification was performed using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (Bruker Japan, Kanagawa, Japan).

Statistical analysis

We analyzed categorical variables using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. We used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Additionally, Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied when applicable. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. We constructed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve by plotting the true-positive rate (sensitivity) against the false-positive rate (1 – specificity) across a range of TTP cutoff values, identifying the point at which the sum of sensitivity and specificity was maximized. The area under the curve (AUC) was estimated using parametric procedures. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated based on each determined TTP cutoff value. For comparison, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated using the conventional diagnostic criterion of two or more positive blood culture sets. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [13].

Results

During the study period, blood cultures from 60 patients were positive for B. cereus. After excluding 11 pediatric cases and those with polymicrobial bacteremia, 49 patients were included in the analysis (18 in the true bacteremia group and 31 in the contamination group) (Fig. 1). Table 1 presents the characteristics of both groups. All patients had blood cultures drawn from at least two samples. In the true bacteremia group, only two patients had a single positive blood culture set. The true bacteremia group had more hospitalized patients than the contamination group (p = 0.003), whereas the contamination group included a higher proportion of outpatients (p = 0.003).

The TTP values for both groups were less than 20 h in all cases except one, which was 118 h. The median TTP in the whole cohort was 10.0 h (interquartile range [IQR], 8.3–12.1) and was significantly shorter in the true bacteremia group (7.8 h; IQR, 6.5–8.9) than in the contamination group (11.2 h; IQR, 9.9–13; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The ROC curve was used to evaluate different TTP thresholds to classify true bacteremia (Fig. 3). The AUC of the ROC curve was 0.863 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.752–0.975). A cut-off value of 9.0 h provided the highest sensitivity (77.8%; 95% CI, 52.4–93.7) and specificity (90.3%; 95% CI, 74.2–98.0). When a stricter threshold of TTP ≤ 5.0 h was applied, the sensitivity decreased to 22.2% (95% CI, 6.4–47.6), while the specificity increased to 96.8% (95% CI, 83.3–99.9). In contrast, a more lenient threshold of TTP ≤ 13.0 h resulted in 100% sensitivity (95% CI, 81.5–100) but reduced specificity to 22.6% (95% CI, 9.6–41.1) (Table 2).

For comparison, the diagnostic performance of using two or more positive blood culture sets as a criterion was evaluated. This yielded a sensitivity of 88.9% (95% CI, 64.0–98.6), a specificity of 100% (95% CI, 90.5–100), a PPV of 100% (95% CI, 80.5–100), and an NPV of 93.9% (95% CI, 79.8–99.3).

Among patients with CRBSI, 81.8% (9/11) had an inpatient onset, representing half of the patients in the true bacteremia group. Discussion

Previous studies have shown that the average TTP of B. cereus in blood cultures was significantly shorter than that of other contaminant Gram-positive organisms (approximately 5.3–7.2 h vs. more than 20 h) [10, 14]. Although the utility of TTP in microbial estimation for B. cereus bacteremia has been reported [10], there are few reports of TTP distinguishing true bacteremia from contamination. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that the median TTP for true B. cereus bacteremia is significantly shorter than that for contamination in a heterogeneous cohort. In addition, our study adopted a rigorous methodology to differentiate true bacteremia from contamination. Recognizing that a consensus clinical definition for B. cereus bacteremia is lacking in the literature [7–10], which complicates cross-study comparisons, we established and strictly applied a set of objective criteria. Because CRBSI is a common manifestation of B. cereus infection [1, 3, 4], we based our definitions on the consensus guidelines of the Infectious Diseases Society of America [11], thereby minimizing the risk of misclassification and strengthening the internal validity and reliability of our findings.

Since B. cereus has been identified as a causative organism of invasive infections in hosts with varying degrees of immune function [15, 16], it is important to accurately identify true bacteremia. When more than two sets of blood cultures are obtained at a given time, distinguishing between true B. cereus bacteremia and contamination may be more reliable. Between July and September 2024, a shortage of BACTEC® (Becton Dickinson, US) blood culture bottles led some institutions to limit blood culture collection to a single set [17, 18]. During this supply-restriction period at our institution, B. cereus was not incidentally isolated from blood cultures, and all patients had at least two sets of blood cultures. Although our findings are informative for cases with two or more blood culture sets, their extrapolation to single-set positivity remains uncertain, and TTP should be interpreted with caution in this context.

TTP may be partially affected by the initial quantity of organisms introduced into blood culture bottles [19]. Contamination typically occurs when a small number of bacteria are introduced through the skin, making it reasonable to assume that the TTP is longer than that in cases of true bacteremia. In our study, the median TTP for contamination was 11.2 h, and TTPs exceeding 13 h were classified as contaminated based on the optimal ROC threshold. However, several contamination cases had a TTP shorter than 9.0 h. Since B. cereus can exhibit rapid growth [10, 14], leading to a short TTP even in contamination, reliance on this marker alone is insufficient. Although a TTP cut-off of 9.0 h was identified as a reasonable threshold in our analysis, its sensitivity of 77% for detecting true bacteremia might not meet the level of diagnostic accuracy typically required for standalone clinical decision-making. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation incorporating other clinical and laboratory findings is essential for an accurate diagnosis.

In our study, we also evaluated the diagnostic performance of the conventional criterion of two or more positive blood culture sets for identifying true bacteremia. This criterion demonstrated a specificity of 100% (95% CI, 90.5–100), and a positive predictive value of 100% (95% CI, 80.5–100). However, its sensitivity was 88.9% (95% CI, 64.0–98.6), and the negative predictive value was 93.9% (95% CI, 79.8–99.3) because two cases of true bacteremia were positive in only a single set. This indicates that while positivity in multiple sets is a strong confirmation of true bacteremia, relying on this rule alone may lead to missed or delayed diagnoses in a subset of patients. In contrast, our proposed TTP cutoff (< 9.0 h) showed a sensitivity of 77%. Although this is lower than that of the multiple-set criterion, TTP offers a distinct advantage as an early warning sign that can be applied to every positive bottle, including those from a single positive set. Therefore, TTP should not be considered a replacement for conventional criteria but as a valuable complementary tool to help clinicians promptly identify patients at high risk for true bacteremia, particularly when only one culture set becomes positive.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. The relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power to detect differences or associations, although we reviewed all the available databases at our institution. Second, although we applied established clinical definitions for true bacteremia and contamination using consensus guidelines [11], the possibility of misclassification remains. Additionally, while TTP may be influenced by the volume of blood collected and the time before culture initiation [20], these factors were not assessed and may have affected our results. Third, because only two patients in the true bacteremia group had a single positive blood culture set, a direct comparison of the TTP between groups for such cases was not feasible. Fourth, the equipment used for blood cultures was changed during the study period, which may have affected TTP. Because B. cereus was only isolated in true bacteremia cases during the BacT/ALERT® period, it was not possible to directly compare the TTP with that of the BACTEC period. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate our findings and evaluate the utility of TTP in patients with a single positive blood culture set.

Conclusions

True B. cereus bacteremia tends to show shorter TTP than contamination in blood culture. A TTP cut-off of 9.0 h may aid in distinguishing true B. cereus bacteremia from contamination, whereas a TTP exceeding 13 h indicates sample contamination. However, the diagnostic accuracy of TTP alone may be insufficient to distinguish true bacteremia from contamination. Therefore, while TTP can be a useful adjunct for differentiating true B. cereus bacteremia from contamination, it should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and laboratory findings to achieve an accurate diagnosis of sepsis.
