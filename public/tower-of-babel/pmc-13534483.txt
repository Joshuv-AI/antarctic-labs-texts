In vivo CRISPR screening identifies metastasis suppressors in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Soaad Galal, Leslie Chaltel Lima, Ni Wang, Cléo Moury, Gang Yan, Meiou Dai, Suhad Ali, Jean-Jacques Lebrun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metastatic cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, yet tumor cell–intrinsic mechanisms restraining metastatic dissemination remain incompletely defined. Here, we perform an unbiased in vivo genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screen in a breast cancer xenograft model to identify regulators of metastatic progression. This approach uncovers clinically relevant metastasis suppressor genes (MSGs), including VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2, whose loss enhances lung colonization. Functional validation demonstrates that depletion of these genes promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, migration, invasion, intravasation, and angiogenesis, whereas CRISPR-mediated activation suppresses metastatic spread. Integration with patient datasets reveals reduced expression in tumors and associations with advanced disease, with higher expression trending toward improved outcomes. Notably, CMTR2 loss induces vascular remodeling and intratumoral heterogeneity, supporting a role in tumor–vascular interactions. Collectively, this study identifies a network of MSGs that constrain tumor dissemination and highlights the power of in vivo CRISPR functional genomics to uncover regulators of metastatic disease.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed cancer and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths in women worldwide, with an increasing rate of mortality1. According to global cancer statistics, each year 2.3 million women are diagnosed with BC, with about 666,000 deaths2. One of the most aggressive subtypes is the triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which represents around 15% of all BC cases and is characterized by the lack of expression of progesterone receptor (PR), estrogen receptor (ER), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). As a result, TNBC tumors don’t respond to classic hormonal therapy against PR or ER, or targeted HER2 therapies and lack effective targeted therapies3–5.

TNBC patients are at a higher risk of developing distant metastases compared to non-TNBC patients. Metastasis, a process leading to the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor to distant secondary sites, is a complex multistep process. Following detachment from the primary tumor site, breast cancer cells adapt to their microenvironment, lose their epithelial architecture (undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to acquire a mesenchymal phenotype)6, migrate and invade the surrounding stroma and extracellular matrix (ECM), intravasate into the bloodstream and extravasate to colonize distant sites and organs, such as the lymph nodes, lungs, bone, liver, and brain7–12. Despite advancements in the molecular and genetic analysis of TNBC, the 5-year survival rate of metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) remains very low, at an estimated 12% rate.

While oncogenic genes and pathways that promote breast cancer metastasis have been well characterized, their metastasis suppressor genes (MSGs) counterparts have remained elusive, with few candidates identified so far13. Among those are NM23, through inhibition of MAPK/ERK pathway14; BRMS1 and BRMS1L, which regulate PI3K15 and Wnt16 signaling, respectively; DLC-1, a negative regulator of Rho signaling and cytoskeletal remodeling17; miR-146a/b through downregulation of NFκB and EGFR pathway18; SDPR, through inducing apoptosis19, RKIP, through inhibition of EMT and MAPK/ERK signaling20, and PRL, through regulation of apicobasal polarity and terminal differentiation of mammary epithelial cells21–24. Since conventional therapies are largely ineffective against metastatic TNBC, gaining a comprehensive understanding of MSGs and their underlying molecular mechanisms will be critical for developing new anti-metastatic strategies.

Using an unbiased, genome-wide in vivo CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (KO) screening approach in a TNBC xenograft model, we identified several clinically relevant MSGs (VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2), whose loss of expression induces lung colonization, primary tumor growth, intravasation, and spontaneous metastasis. Functional and mechanistic validation revealed that KO of these genes promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, cell migration, invasion, intravasation, and angiogenesis, whereas CRISPR activation of their endogenous promoters markedly suppresses metastatic dissemination in preclinical TNBC models. Importantly, the metastasis-suppressive functions of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 are preserved across both immunodeficient and immunocompetent settings. Notably, we found that CMTR2 deletion also led to intratumoral heterogeneity and vascular remodeling, highlighting a critical role for this MSG in regulating tumor angiogenesis and circulating tumor cell dissemination. Collectively, these findings identify a previously unrecognized network of MSGs that constrain metastatic progression in TNBC and underscore the utility of in vivo CRISPR functional genomics for uncovering therapeutic vulnerabilities in aggressive breast cancers.

Results

Unbiased in vivo genome-wide metastasis suppressor screen

To identify clinically relevant MSGs in TNBC, we performed a whole-genome loss-of-function CRISPR screen using the SUM159PT xenograft TNBC model. The SUM159PT model is a well-established tumorigenic and metastatic model derived from anaplastic carcinoma of a breast cancer patient. SUM159PT cells are mesenchymal-basal-like and harbor clinically relevant PIK3CA (p.H1047L), and TP53 (p.R158L) mutations, (Cellosaurus accession: CVCL_5423), the most frequently observed mutations in TNBC, and as such is a good representative model of the TNBC disease25–34. SUM159PT cancer cells were transduced with the human lentiviral pooled genome-scale CRISPR/Cas9 KO GeCKOv2 library; the screening workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The GeCKOv2 library includes 65,383 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) which cover the whole genome of 19,050 coding genes (3 sgRNAs/mRNA), 1,864 miRNAs (4 sgRNAs/miRNA), and 1000 non-targeting control sgRNAs35,36. A low multiplicity of infection (MOI ~ 0.3) was chosen to ensure the integration of only one sgRNA per cell, as we previously showed29,31,32,37. For the screen, pooled KO cells (10 × 106 cells) were injected intravenously (tail vein) into five severely immunodeficient NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice at a 500-fold library coverage, in two separate experiments. For in vitro cell representation of the pooled library sgRNAs, a separate pool of 10 × 106 cells was snap-frozen for subsequent sequencing and used for data analysis (referred to as Cell Rep 1&2). Two weeks post-injection, animals were sacrificed, lungs resected, and metastatic lung tumors (referred to as Tumor GeCKO 1&2) were collected for next-generation sequencing, using Illumina HiSeq 2500 to identify those sgRNAs that were dropped out or enriched in the metastatic tumors. Depleted sgRNAs indicate genes that promote metastasis, whereas enriched sgRNAs correspond to genes with metastasis-suppressing activity. Datasets were analyzed by quantifying the sgRNAs read counts before and after selective pressure (Cell Rep 1&2 vs. metastatic tumor samples GeCKO 1&2) using the MAGeCK Robust Rank Aggregation Algorithm (RRA)38 and applying a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of <0.05, as we previously showed31,32. As shown in Fig. 1b, screen results show high library coverage for both the Cell Rep and metastatic tumor samples of an average 99.96% and 85%, respectively. Quality of the screen is further supported by the low Gini index in the Cell Rep samples with an average of 0.09, indicative of a uniform sgRNA distribution prior to in vivo selection (Fig. 1c), ensuring that subsequent changes in individual sgRNA abundance are due to the selective pressure during in vivo metastatic colonization rather than unequal initial distribution. Bioinformatics analysis identified multiple sgRNA hits with significant enrichment or depletion (p < 0.05) across replicates, corresponding to 1,030 positively selected and 1189 negatively selected genes (Fig. 1d, e). The top positively enriched hits (LFC > 4) and the most depleted hits (LFC <−2) identified in the screen are presented in Supplementary Data 1. A ranked plot of CRISPR screen gene enrichment based on −log10 RRA score is shown in Fig. 1f, with >19,000 genes ordered from most enriched to most depleted. Positively selected genes represent candidates with anti-metastatic potential, as their specific KOs were enriched in lung tumors. From the 1,030 positive hits, the top four candidates were prioritized for validation based on highly significant enrichment (FDR < 0.05) and a strong increase in sgRNA abundance after injection (log fold change [LFC] > 7.6) (Fig. 1g, h). These include VPS45 (vacuolar protein sorting 45 homolog), CMTR2 (cap methyltransferase 2, its synonym FTSJD1), ZFYVE20 (zinc finger domain-containing protein 20 or its synonym RBSN), and NF2 (neurofibromatosis type 2, or moesin-ezrin-radixin-like (MERLIN)) (Fig. 1d, e; red highlights, f). The enriched sgRNA list also included several genes that were previously reported as tumor and metastasis suppressors, such as KEAP139–42, PTPN2343,44, and STK1145. By contrast, negatively selected genes are likely to exert pro-metastatic functions. As a proof of concept and further validation of our screen, several known prometastatic genes were identified among the negative hits, including SUMO246, DCAF1347, PKM48, and TLN149,50 (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 1d, e; blue highlights). To further validate our screen, we cross-referenced the top 82 depleted genes (FDR < 0.25) with the Sanger CRISPR essentiality dataset from the DepMap portal51, which includes 1,244 essential genes, and found that 44 of the 82 genes were classified as essential, thereby supporting the robustness of our screening results (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Pathway enrichment analysis using Enrichr52,53 (KEGG pathways) revealed that the top 20 positively selected genes regulate pathways whose dysfunction contributes to cancer progression and metastasis, including Hippo signaling pathway54, tight junction55, endocytosis56 and cysteine and methionine metabolic pathways57–59, (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 1b). On the other hand, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the top 20 negatively selected genes showed that central carbon metabolism and cell cycle pathways, which are essential during cancer development60,61 are the most dysregulated pathways (Fig. 1i). Collectively, these results underscore the power of in vivo genome-wide CRISPR screening approaches in uncovering genes that restrain the metastatic progression in TNBC and identify VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2 as potential MSGs.

Loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, NF2 promotes TNBC metastasis

To further evaluate the efficacy of our 4 identified MSG candidates in regulating tumor metastasis in vivo, we engineered individual CRISPR knockouts (KOs) in SUM159PT cell line, as described previously29,30. KO efficiency was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 2a), and insertion of proper indel mutations was assessed using Surveyor nuclease assays (Fig. 2b). To overcome the qualitative limitation of the Surveyor nuclease assay, we performed targeted long-read amplicon sequencing followed by CRISPResso262 analysis to quantitatively define genome editing outcomes at single-base resolution in VPS45, RBSN, NF2, and CMTR2 KO SUM159 cells (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Sequencing reads were aligned to reference amplicons derived from scrambled control SUM159 cells, enabling accurate discrimination of CRISPR-induced variants from baseline sequence noise. This approach demonstrated high editing efficiencies across all loci, with insertions and deletions (INDELs) enriched at the predicted Cas9 cleavage sites (Fig. 2c). Editing efficiencies of 93.17% (VPS45), 97.44% (RBSN), 98.83% (NF2), and 66.30% (CMTR2), defined as the percentage of reads harboring insertions, deletions, or substitutions relative to total aligned reads (Fig. 2d), confirm robust on-target CRISPR activity. Detailed CRISPResso2 profiling further defined the editing landscape, showing localized sequence alterations at the expected cut sites and consistent mutational profiles with reproducible allele distributions across all targets (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3), collectively supporting effective gene disruption. Functional in vivo validation was subsequently conducted using a preclinical model of breast cancer lung metastasis established via tail vein injection, as detailed in the Methods section and illustrated in Fig. 2e. As shown in Fig. 2f, g, KO of each of the four MSG candidates in SUM159 cell line resulted in a marked increase in metastatic lung nodules compared to control animals (overall p < 0.05), observed 15 days post-injection. Notably, VPS45 loss produced the most dramatic phenotype, with lung nodule counts exceeding 400 (p < 0.001), nearly twice those observed in the other three KO groups, consistent with the LFC data from our CRISPR screen (Fig. 1h).

To further investigate the potential roles of the four KOs in regulating metastasis, we utilized a preclinical spontaneous metastasis model in which cancer cells were orthotopically implanted into the mammary fat pads of immunocompromised female mice (Fig. 2h). All 4 knockout groups significantly increased primary tumor burden compared with scrambled controls (Fig. 2i), as evidenced by higher endpoint tumor volumes (Fig. 2j). At experimental endpoint, 25 days post-transplantation, primary tumors and lungs were resected and all 4 KO models showed increased tumor weights across all individual mice, compared to control mice (Fig. 2k, l). Interestingly, while the control animal group showed no or little lung metastasis, the 4 KO models increased the number of metastatic nodules, with the most significant values in the CMTR2KO group (Fig. 2m, n). Intriguingly, resected tumors from the CMTR2KO group displayed a phenotype suggestive of increased vascularization relative to other groups, suggesting that CMTR2 may also regulate angiogenesis (Fig. 2i).

To directly address whether the increased lung tumor burden observed upon gene loss is driven by altered proliferation, we systematically evaluated the intrinsic growth capacity of SUM159KO cells. Across multiple orthogonal assays, including MTT and SRB measurements of cell growth and viability, as well as colony formation assays assessing clonal expansion, we observed no increase in proliferative capacity in any of the KO cells relative to controls. Consistently, expression of the proliferation marker PCNA remained unchanged. In contrast, some KOs exhibited modest reductions in viability and biomass, indicating that loss of these genes does not confer a growth advantage under standard conditions (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). Importantly, the enhanced metastatic phenotype was maintained in experimental metastasis (tail-vein) models, where primary tumor growth is bypassed, further supporting a proliferation-independent mechanism. Collectively, these data indicate that the dominant effect of these perturbations is an increase in metastatic fitness rather than enhanced intrinsic proliferation.

Altogether, these data demonstrate that inactivating gene expression of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 promotes breast cancer cell metastasis to the lungs in both intravenous (tail vein injection) and spontaneous (orthotopic transplantation) preclinical in vivo models of TNBC metastasis, supporting our screen findings and the main hypothesis that these genes function as MSGs in TNBC.

Loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, NF2 promotes metastasis across immunodeficient and immunocompetent TNBC models

To mitigate potential cell line–specific effects, we extended functional validation to the SUM149PT model, a well-established TNBC system characterized by genomic instability and mutations in key tumor suppressors, including CDKN2A, BRCA1, and TP53 (Cellosaurus accession: RRID: CVCL_3422). Efficient gene KO was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 3a). In tail vein xenograft assays, terminated at 44 days post-injection, loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 led to an overall increase in lung metastatic burden (Fig. 3b–d). VPS45KO and RBSNKO exhibited the most pronounced and statistically significant effects (p ≤ 0.01), whereas CMTR2KO showed a consistent but non-significant trend (p = 0.067, two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test), and NF2KO had a modest, non-significant effect (p = 0.56). To provide orthogonal validation of metastatic burden, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on lung sections (n = 3 sections per lung per mouse). Quantification of metastatic nodule area confirmed the increase in metastatic burden across KO conditions, consistent with nodule count–based analyses (Fig. 3e, f). Together, these data demonstrate that the pro-metastatic effects of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 loss are conserved across multiple TNBC models, supporting a context-independent role in regulating metastatic progression.

To determine whether these metastasis suppressor genes (MSGs) retain their function in the presence of an intact immune system, we next employed the 4T1 syngeneic model in immunocompetent BALB/c mice. Efficient gene disruption in 4T1 cells was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. 3g). In experimental metastasis assays (tail vein injection; Fig. 3h), loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, or NF2 resulted in a consistent and statistically significant increase in lung metastatic burden compared to control cells (all p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3i, j). Specifically, metastatic nodules increased from 5.14 ± 2.10 in controls to 12.57 ± 1.49 (VPS45KO), 10.71 ± 1.38 (RBSNKO), 10.75 ± 1.72 (CMTR2KO), and 11.57 ± 3.02 (NF2KO) (mean ± SEM), corresponding to approximately two-fold increases in metastatic colonization efficiency, with VPS45 loss producing the strongest effect. Histopathological analysis further confirmed a significant increase in both the number and area of metastatic lung lesions across all KO groups (Fig. 3k, l).

Consistent results were obtained in a spontaneous metastasis setting using orthotopic mammary fat pad transplantation (Fig. 3m–o), where loss of all four genes significantly enhanced lung metastasis (all p < 0.01). Control tumors exhibited minimal metastatic burden (3.14 ± 1.28 nodules), whereas NF2KO (12.00 ± 1.79), RBSNKO (13.57 ± 2.78), and CMTR2KO (15.29 ± 2.97) showed marked increases. VPS45KO again displayed a striking phenotype (41.14 ± 18.70 nodules), suggesting a dominant role in constraining metastatic dissemination. Notably, primary tumor growth was not consistently affected across groups, as assessed by longitudinal measurements and two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (Fig. 3p), indicating that these genes preferentially regulate metastatic competence rather than primary tumor proliferation. A modest but statistically significant increase in tumor growth was observed at endpoint in the VPS45KO group (one-way ANOVA; Fig. 3q), although this effect was not sustained over time.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the metastasis-suppressive functions of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 are preserved across both immunodeficient and immunocompetent settings. This cross-model consistency strongly supports a tumor-intrinsic role for these genes in restraining metastatic progression while confirming their relevance within a physiologically intact tumor–immune microenvironment.

We next assessed their relative expression levels across molecular subtypes of breast cancer, including the luminal, HER2 + , and TNBC subtypes. As shown in Fig. 4b, CPTAC protein expression showed significantly lower RBSN and NF2 protein expression levels in all breast cancer subtypes, compared with normal breast tissue, particularly in the TNBC subtype. For VPS45, a trend showing lower protein expression levels was observed in the TNBC subtype. Transcriptomic analysis of CMTR2 mRNA levels also showed significantly lower expression in all 3 subclasses compared with the normal mammary tissue (Fig. 4b).

To assess potential association with tumor histology, we examined their expression in infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC), infiltrating lobular carcinoma (ILC), and mixed histology. We found a significant decrease in the protein expression of RBSN in the IDC compared with ILC, which suggests RBSN protein expression is required for the homeostasis of cells that originate from the milk ducts. As expected, NF2 exhibited a significant decrease in IDC, ILC, and mixed histology. Nonetheless, a non-significant decrease in the protein expression of VPS45 was identified in ILC and mixed histology. Besides, CMTR2 mRNA expression significantly reduced across all tumor histology types compared with normal mammary tissue (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Interestingly, CMTR2 expression was most markedly decreased in metaplastic histology, a highly aggressive form of triple-negative breast cancer, known to be resistant to standard chemotherapy and to exhibit the worst prognosis.

Expression patterns were also assessed according to tumor stage. The results showed significantly lower expression levels for RBSN and NF2 in the advanced stages 2 and 3, while there was a non-significant decrease in stage 1. Even though non-significant, a decrease in VPS45 expression was detected in all 3 stages. For CMTR2 mRNA expression, levels were significantly lower in all stages, starting from the early invasive cancer stage 1 to stage 4 (the metastatic stage). Noticeably, the expression level of RBSN, NF2, and CMTR2 is most significantly lower in stage 2, suggesting that their expression may play a role in the early prevention of tumor progression, consistent with a metastasis suppressor role, aiming at curbing invasion and migration of cancer cells to distant sites and organs (Fig. 4c). When we compared the protein or mRNA expression level of our target genes based on patients’ race (Caucasian, African-American, and Asian), we found a significant decrease in the expression of RBSN, NF2, and CMTR2 in all 3 major racial groups including African-American patients, who exhibits high prevalence of metastatic TNBC and poor survival outcomes66. Additionally, VPS45 protein expression showed a non-significant decrease in all 3 racial groups (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

Furthermore, we evaluated the association between candidate MSG expression and patient outcomes using Kaplan–Meier survival analyses. Higher expression of VPS45, RBSN, NF2, and CMTR2 was generally associated with trends toward improved relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) across breast cancer cohorts (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 5c). Among these, RBSN displayed the most consistent and pronounced association with both RFS and OS, whereas VPS45, NF2, and CMTR2 showed more modest and variable trends across datasets. These analyses are exploratory and intended to provide clinical context consistent with the metastasis suppressor functions identified through our in vivo functional screening approach.

Finally, to assess potential genetic alterations, we interrogated cBioPortal datasets (https://www.cbioportal.org/), derived from large-scale cancer genomic projects67,68. By exploring the publicly available datasets (Metastatic Solid Cancers, UMich, Nature 2017), we found that VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2 display frequent mutations in metastatic solid tumors derived from different biopsy sites of a large cohort of 500 adult patients67–69 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Interestingly, in this metastatic solid cancer data, VPS45 and CMTR2 were profiled with nonsense mutations in cutaneous melanoma (p.Y39*) and lung adenocarcinoma (p.L66*), respectively.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that the identified MSG candidates are consistently downregulated in breast cancer, particularly in TNBC, with reduced expression associated with advanced disease features. Survival analyses provide supportive evidence, with RBSN showing the strongest association with patient outcomes, while the remaining genes exhibit more modest trends. These findings are consistent with a tumor-suppressive role in metastatic progression.

VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, NF2 regulate EMT associated genes in TNBC

During metastasis, cancer cells undergo EMT, which facilitates their migratory and invasive capabilities. EMT, together with cell migration and invasion, represents a critical cascade of events that enables breast cancer cells to disseminate from the primary mammary site and colonize distant secondary metastatic sites9,70,71. Metastatic dissemination requires cells not only to migrate from the primary sites but also to be able to invade the surrounding mammary fat pad tissues, to eventually reach blood vessels and distant organs. We therefore assessed the invasive potential of the 4 individual MSG candidates using Matrigel-coated Transwell assays, as described previously72,73. Consistent with the wound-healing results, KO of the target candidates significantly increased cancer cell invasion through Matrigel, overall p < 0.001 (Fig. 6c, d). Collectively, these data demonstrate that VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 genes regulate the EMT transition and angiogenesis processes, as well as cancer cell migration and invasion, further strengthening our hypothesis and screen results highlighting these genes as potential suppressors of metastasis in TNBC.

Following local invasion, metastatic cells must breach vascular barriers to enter the bloodstream, a process termed intravasation. This process represents another critical step during metastasis, allowing cancer cells to further disseminate and reach distant organs74,75. To assess the potential effect of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 on intravasation, we used a TNBC preclinical model, whereby SUM159 CRISPR-KO cancer cells were orthotopically xenografted in immunocompromised NSG female mice. Tumor outgrowth was assessed regularly, and at experimental endpoint, when tumor size reached an average volume of 1 cm3, animals were euthanized and approximately 0.6 mL of circulating blood was collected via cardiac puncture from each mouse in all groups (scrambled, VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 KOs; n = 6 mice per group). Blood samples were cultured in vitro for 14 days, and circulating tumor cells (CTCs) derived from the xenografts were then quantified; the assay workflow is illustrated in Fig. 6e. To standardize across animals, CTC counts were normalized to both tumor volume (cm³) and blood volume (mL) to calculate the tumor blood burden for each group. Interestingly, we found all 4 KOs to significantly promote the number of CTCs and intravasation of SUM159 cells, compared to control scrambled cells (Fig. 6f, g). Of note, CMTR2 gene deletion led to a drastic effect in increasing CTC numbers. These data indicate that all 4 MSG candidates, particularly CMTR2, play a critical role in controlling intravasation and CTC dissemination through the bloodstream.

Together, these findings demonstrate that loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 enhances multiple steps of the metastatic cascades, including EMT, migration, invasion, and intravasation capacity, thereby validating these genes as critical suppressors against metastatic dissemination in TNBC.

To investigate potential functional interactions among the identified metastasis suppressors, we performed systematic pairwise double KO experiments in SUM159 cells. Immunoblot validation confirmed efficient single and double gene disruption (Fig. 6h). Among all combinations tested, only the simultaneous loss of VPS45 and RBSN resulted in a marked increase in cell migration, as assessed by wound-healing assays under serum-free conditions (Fig. 6i). At the molecular level, VPS45/RBSN double KO induced a significant upregulation of MMP1 expression compared to single KOs, p < 0.05 (Fig. 6j), supporting a mechanistic link to enhanced invasive potential. Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed a robust endogenous interaction between VPS45 and RBSN in SUM159 cells (Fig. 6k). Furthermore, VPS45 depletion reduced RBSN protein levels, suggesting that VPS45 contributes to RBSN stability. No interactions were detected among other gene pairs. Together, these results identify a specific VPS45-RBSN functional axis that cooperatively regulates cell motility and invasive gene expression, whereas NF2 and CMTR2 appear to act through independent pathways.

To further validate our findings in another TNBC model, we used the MDA-MB-231 TNBC model, which has been extensively characterized as a robust preclinical model for spontaneous metastasis to the lung. These cells harbor mutations in KRAS (p.G13D), BRAF (p.G464V), and TP53 (p.R280K), and a homozygous deletion of CDKN2A (Cellosaurus accession: CVCL_0062), providing these cancer cells with a high migratory and invasive capacity. We endogenously overexpressed VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 using the CRISPRa approach, described above. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, CRISPRa transduced MDA-MB-231 cells were transplanted orthotopically into female NSG mice. Tumor volume was monitored over time, and at the experimental endpoint, 56 days post-transplantation, animals were euthanized, and the diameters of the lung nodules were assessed. We found that the endogenous activation of VPS45, RBSN, and NF2 decreased the tumor volume compared to the NTC group and decreased the diameter of lung metastatic nodules (30 lung nodules/3 lungs were analyzed), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8b-d. Endogenous activation of the CMTR2 gene promoter did not affect tumor growth or lung nodule diameter in the MDA-MB-231 model, likely due to the fact that the MDA-MB-231 lineage harbors a p.C427LfsTer2 frameshift mutation (DepMap portal, ID: ACH-000768). The mutation leads to a severely truncated CMTR2 protein, disrupting its stability and proper folding, and is therefore expected to produce a non-functional, destabilized protein. Collectively, these results demonstrate that VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 genes exert potent metastasis suppressive activities and can govern both early and late stages breast cancer cells metastasis to the lungs. The study also underscores the efficacy of in vivo genome-wide CRISPR screening approaches in uncovering metastasis suppressor genes in cancer.

To further validate these observations, CD31 immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed on tumor samples and revealed a marked increase in vascular structures in CMTR2KO tumors compared with scrambled tumors, reflected by an increased percentage of CD31-positive area (n = 4 tumors, 6 ROIs per tumor; scrambled, 0.18–1.04% [mean = 0.76%]; CMTR2KO, 1.32–5.71% [mean = 2.90%]; Mann-Whitney U test, p < 0.05), indicative of enhanced angiogenesis (Fig. 8b). Moreover, signs of leaky and abnormal vasculature were also observed with the presence of extravascular erythrocytes (adjacent to or surrounding CD31⁺ endothelial structures) within CMTR2KO tumors, a hallmark of immature and poorly stabilized angiogenic vessels which is absent in the scrambled tumors. Correspondingly, paraffin embedded CMTR2KO tumor tissues and total protein lysates of the grinded tumor appear extensively reddish compared with scrambled controls, suggesting increased blood content (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). These results also suggest enhanced intravasation by CMTR2KO cells into the bloodstream through breaching such poorly structured and destabilized vasculatures. Consistent with this phenotype, RT-qPCR analysis of tumor-derived RNA revealed upregulation of angiogenesis-related genes. On average, CMTR2KO tumors showed > 1.3-fold increases in human-specific CD34 and ANGPT2, along with > 4-fold increases in mouse-specific Vegfa and Ptgs2 expression, compared to the scrambled controls (Fig. 8c). A higher magnification view of the primary tumors shown in Fig. 2i is presented in Fig. 8c for clear visualization of increased vasculature of the CMTR2KO primary tumors. Furthermore, lung tissue sections from SUM149PT cells transplanted via intravenous injection not only showed an increased number of lung nodules but also a notable increase in vascularization surrounding tumor areas (Supplementary Fig. 9c).

Finally, the angiogenic effect of CMTR2KO was further examined by shortwave infrared (SWIR) fluorescence imaging to visualize primary tumor vasculature in vivo using an orthotopic transplantation model of control vs CMTR2KO SUM159 cells. Intravenous administration of indocyanine green dye (ICG; 0.025 mg/mL, 200 μL per mouse) revealed enhanced vascularization in CMTR2KO tumors relative to scrambled controls, as quantified by fluorescence intensity with a maximum signal intensity of 4.2 × 10¹² versus 1.9 × 10¹² arbitrary units (a.u.), respectively (Fig. 8d).

In summary, we show that loss of CMTR2 promotes abnormal blood vessel formation and a pro-angiogenic, metastasis-prone tumor phenotype. As enhanced vascularization facilitates CTC intravasation, our results underscore CMTR2’s role as a metastasis suppressor through angiogenesis regulation. Restoring CMTR2 activity may reduce tumor cell plasticity and normalize neovasculature, both essential for achieving sustained control of metastasis.

Discussion

Although most breast cancers are curable when detected early, metastatic disease remains largely incurable, with over 90% of patient deaths resulting from cancer dissemination rather than primary tumor growth. Using an unbiased in vivo genome-wide CRISPR screen, we identified metastasis suppressor genes (MSGs) in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the most aggressive breast cancer subtype characterized by poor prognosis, high metastatic potential, and limited targeted therapies. Among the top candidates, VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2 emerged as potent metastasis suppressors regulating TNBC cell proliferation, motility, invasion, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and hematogenous spread. Gain-of-function of these genes significantly reduced lung colonization of TNBC cells, underscoring their essential role in restraining TNBC progression and highlighting the power of in vivo CRISPR screening to uncover key metastasis regulators (Fig. 9). Our findings identify VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2 as key regulators of tumor progression and metastasis in TNBC. Analysis of clinical datasets revealed that reduced expression of these four genes is associated with disease progression and poor prognosis in breast cancer patients, particularly those with TNBC. Survival analyses provide limited and exploratory support for clinical association, with RBSN showing the most consistent relationship with patient outcomes, whereas VPS45, NF2, and CMTR2 display more modest and variable trends. These observations are consistent with the metastasis suppressor functions identified here but do not establish these genes as independent prognostic biomarkers.

While our primary screening strategy was designed to identify tumor cell–intrinsic regulators of metastasis using immunodeficient models, it is well established that the tumor microenvironment, particularly immune components, plays a critical role in metastatic progression. To address this, we extended our validation to an immunocompetent setting using the 4T1 syngeneic model. Notably, loss of VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 consistently enhanced metastatic dissemination even in the presence of an intact immune system. These findings indicate that the metastasis-suppressive functions of these genes are predominantly tumor-intrinsic and remain operative despite complex tumor–immune interactions. Importantly, the preservation of these phenotypes across both experimental (tail vein) and spontaneous (orthotopic) metastasis models further underscores their role across multiple steps of the metastatic cascade, including colonization and dissemination. Together with the concordant results observed across independent human TNBC cell lines, these data support a model in which VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 function as core barriers to metastatic competence, with effects that are robust to both genetic background and immune context. This cross-model consistency substantially strengthens the physiological and translational relevance of our findings.

Interestingly, an ongoing investigation using the liposomal CMTR2 inhibitor AT-1965 reported reduced primary tumor growth in orthotopic 4T1 models, an effect attributed to enhanced innate immune sensing of hypomethylated mRNA caps. In contrast, our genetic ablation of CMTR2 did not suppress primary tumor growth but instead promoted metastatic dissemination in both experimental and spontaneous metastasis settings. While these differences may reflect distinct biological consequences of acute pharmacological inhibition versus stable genetic loss, as well as potential immune-mediated effects, highlight the context-dependent roles of CMTR2. These observations suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting CMTR2 will require careful evaluation, particularly with respect to their potential impact on metastatic progression.

The NF2 gene encodes the protein merlin, a well-characterized suppressor of both tumor formation and metastasis in different cell lines78–80, and as such serves as a proof of concept for our screen. NF2 can modulate the activity of GTPases that are involved in actin cytoskeleton organization, and regulate stemness, cell growth, adhesion, adherens junctions, all of which are major events associated with the aggressive clinical behavior seen in TNBC. Previous studies identified VPS45 as a potential tumor suppressor in breast cancer and ovarian cancer81,82. VPS45 knockdown in ovarian cancer was also reported to promote a cancer stem-like phenotype, a feature observed in many malignant tumors82–84, suggesting that VPS45 expression is required for regulating stemness. Furthermore, RBSN (its synonym ZFYVE20), encodes Rabenosyn-5, a small GTPase, is a highly conserved multi-domain protein that interacts with VPS45, and together act as essential regulators for endocytosis. Both VPS45 and RBSN exert tumor suppressive functions in Drosophila, and their loss of function disrupts endocytic traffic and epithelial polarity85. According to TCGA data available in The Human Protein Atlas, RBSN is a favorable prognostic marker in rectum adenocarcinoma and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, and low expression of RBSN is linked to poor prognosis in these carcinomas (p < 0.001). Our results expand on these studies and define VPS45 and RBSN as potent suppressors of metastasis in triple negative breast cancer. Our study also demonstrated that loss of the cap 2′-O-ribose methyltransferase (CMTR2) markedly promoted spontaneous metastatic dissemination and significantly increased the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules in the orthotopic SUM159PT mammary fat pad xenograft model, despite producing the smallest primary tumors among the four gene KOs analyzed. Interestingly, loss of heterozygosity at chromosome 16q22.2 of the human genome, where CMTR2 is located, has been suggested to be a key driver for breast cancer metastasis and recurrence86–88. Consistent with this, we observed that CRISPR activation of the CMTR2 gene led to a substantial reduction in lung metastasis of SUM159 cells, defining CMTR2 as a master metastasis suppressor gene in breast cancer. Notably, our data reveal a dissociation between primary tumor growth and metastatic potential. While NF2 loss preferentially enhanced tumor expansion, CMTR2 deficiency more strongly promoted metastatic dissemination, consistent with evidence that metastatic competence is not dictated by tumor size alone. This divergence likely reflects distinct gene functions, with NF2 primarily regulating proliferation via Hippo signaling, whereas CMTR2 loss may drive broader transcriptomic reprogramming89,90, that, in turn, likely facilitates invasion and colonization. These findings underscore the functional heterogeneity of metastasis suppressor genes and the need to distinguish regulators of tumor growth from those governing metastatic progression. Overall, discovering MSGs is crucial for advancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving TNBC progression, invasion, metastasis, chemoresistance, and relapse. These genes could act as valuable biomarkers for identifying patients at high risk of metastasis and pave the way for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to improve clinical outcomes in metastatic TNBC.

We also found that besides regulating cell motility, invasion, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and lung metastasis, CMTR2 ablation also led to a strong increase in tumor cell intravasation, further leading to a drastic increase in the number of CTCs in the SUM159 TNBC orthotopic model, and suggests that CMTR2 might hinder TNBC metastasis in the early stages. Our data also reveal that this major increase in CTC intravasation observed in the CMTR2KO may result through perturbation of the angiogenesis process. Indeed, newly formed tumor-associated blood vessels are usually structurally abnormal and leaky due to disorganized vascular network and weak endothelial junctions that facilitate the entry of CTCs into the bloodstream and circulation. Importantly, CMTR2 depletion has been reported to induce yolk sac vascularization defects in mice embryos91. This aligns well with our MRI and CD31 IHC data, which show greater vascular heterogeneity and the presence of fluid-filled channels in CMTR2KO tumors, accompanied by an overall increase in microvascular density within CD31-positive regions. In addition, RT-qPCR analysis of RNA from primary CMTR2KO tumors revealed marked upregulation of several key angiogenic drivers and vascular remodeling mediators, including CD34, ANGPT2, Vegfa, and Ptgs2. These results also suggest that restoring CMTR2 activity could simultaneously temper plasticity and normalize neovasculature, two preconditions for durable metastatic control.

The identification of our four MSG candidates as functional suppressors of epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity and vascular remodeling, underscores their relevance to the ongoing revival of differentiation-based therapeutic strategies in solid tumors. Unlike classic therapeutic approaches aiming at eradicating malignant cells through cytotoxicity, differentiation-based therapy aims at re-establishing lineage identity and tissue architecture, thereby diminishing metastatic competence and therapeutic resistance. In breast cancer, and TNBC in particular, cell-state plasticity (EMT/MET cycling, stem-like phenotypes, and stress-adapted “persister” states) is increasingly recognized as a driver of dissemination and relapse. Our data show that loss of each of the four MSGs (CRISPRKO) downregulates epithelial determinants and upregulates mesenchymal and pro-angiogenic programs, while endogenous activation (CRISPRa) restores antimetastatic restraint.

Building on this framework, the identification of VPS45, RBSN, NF2, and CMTR2 as metastasis suppressor genes (MSGs) in TNBC highlights several potential translational avenues for restoring their functional activity. Approaches aimed at reactivating endogenous gene expression, including small activating RNAs (saRNAs)92,93, are under active preclinical investigation and may provide a strategy to reinstate MSG expression. In parallel, post-transcriptional regulation by oncogenic microRNAs may represent an additional layer of control; for instance, CMTR2 is predicted to be a target of hsa-miR-9-5p, a microRNA implicated in breast cancer metastasis94, suggesting that anti-miR-based strategies could relieve repression and restore its function. Furthermore, RNA-based delivery platforms, including lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated mRNA delivery, have demonstrated clinical feasibility and may offer a framework for transient protein restoration. The functional diversity of these genes, spanning vesicular trafficking (VPS45, RBSN), cytoskeletal regulation (NF2), and mRNA cap modification (CMTR2), underscores their involvement in distinct regulatory nodes of metastatic progression. This raises the possibility that restoring their activity could exert broader control over metastatic competence than single-pathway targeting approaches, although such strategies remain to be validated in future studies.

Methods

Ethics declaration

All animals were housed and handled in accordance with the approved guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) “Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals”. All experiments were performed in accordance with the approved McGill University Animal Care protocol (AUP # 7497 to JJL).

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell lines SUM159PT or SUM149PT were cultured in Ham’s F-12 media containing L-glutamine (WISENT Inc.) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), insulin (5 μg/mL; WISENT Inc.) and hydrocortisone (1 μg/mL; WISENT Inc.) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Detailed information for the SUM cell lines is available at (www.sumlineknowledgebase.com). HEK293FT and MDA-MB-231 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing L-glutamine (WISENT Inc.) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), and the cells were maintained under the same incubation conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). The 4T1 cell line was obtained from ATCC and was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. SUM cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. Stephen Ethier. MDA-MB-231 cells were obtained from ATCC, and HEK293FT was obtained from GenHunter. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination by the Diagnostic Laboratory of the Comparative Medicine and Animal Resources Centre (McGill University) and confirmed negative.

Human genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 library (GeCKOv2) lentiviral production and infection

The human genome-wide CRISPR/KO pooled library A (GeCKOv2; Addgene #1000000048) was a gift from Feng Zhang. The GeCKOv2 library amplification and virus production were carried out according to the Addgene protocol95. Two independent biological infections were performed using SUM159 cell line. The library contains 65,383 single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting 19,050 genes, including 3 sgRNAs per mRNA, 4 sgRNAs per miRNA, and 1000 non-targeting control sgRNAs. Briefly, SUM159 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells/well and infected with the optimal virus concentrations (MOI ~ 0.3), and 8 μg/mL of polybrene was added to the complete growth medium prior to virus infection to facilitate viral transduction. The infected SUM159 cells were spin-infected at 800 × g for 2 h at 32 °C, incubated overnight, and then harvested. About 3 × 106 cells were seeded into each T225 flask. After 24 h of infection, 2 μg/mL of puromycin (InvivoGen) was added for selection. After 7 days of puromycin selection, 10 × 106 cells were used for lung metastasis screen, while 10 × 106 cells were frozen at −80 °C to be used for genomic DNA extraction and next-generation sequencing, representing the baseline library population. The genome-wide library infected SUM159 (10 × 106) cells were harvested after 7 days of puromycin selection, and 2 × 106 cells/mouse were intravenously injected into the tail vein of 10 immunodeficient NSG female mice (5 mice per experiment). Two weeks post-injection, mice were euthanized, lungs were resected, and tumor outgrowth was subjected to genomic DNA extraction and deep sequencing to identify those enriched knockout sgRNAs, corresponding to candidate metastasis suppressor genes.

Genomic DNA extraction (GeCKOv2)

For each biological replicate, ground tumor tissue (derived from lung nodules) and 10 ×106 cells (cell representation sample) were subjected to genomic DNA extraction followed by deep sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing system. For genomic DNA extraction, cells or tumor samples were lysed in 6 mL of NK Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris, 50 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, pH 8) and 30 μL of 20 mg/mL Proteinase K (Qiagen). Followed by incubation at 55 °C for 1 h for the cell pellets and overnight for tumor sample. QIAGEN Ribonuclease A (0.05 mg/mL) was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by cooling on ice for 10 min. Then, 2 mL of ice-cold 7.5 M ammonium acetate (Sigma) was added. Then, samples were briefly vortexed at high speed and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min. To precipitate DNA, isopropanol was added to the collected supernatants, followed by centrifugation. The gDNA pellets were washed in 70% cold ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 1×TE Buffer. The gDNA concentration was measured using the Epoch Microplate Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher).

GeCKOv2 library preparation and next-generation sequencing

To perform next-generation sequencing, the gRNA inserts were amplified in two-step PCR reactions. To enhance processivity and PCR yield, Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (Agilent) was used in all PCR reactions. PCR1 reactions contained: Herculase 5×Buffer (20 μL), 100 mM dNTP (1 μL), Adapter Primer F (2.5 μL), Adapter Primer R (2.5 μL), Herculase II Fusion Enzyme (1 μL), extracted gDNA (10 μg), and PCR-grade water was added up to a final volume of 100 μL. For PCR1 amplification, a thermocycler was used as follows: 98 °C for 2 min, 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 2 min and repeated for 18 cycles. Then, all PCR1 amplicons were pooled and stored at −20 °C. PCR2 reactions contained: Herculase 5× Buffer (20 μL), 100 mM dNTP (1 μL), Adapter Primer F (2.5 μL), Adapter Primer R (2.5 μL), Herculase II Fusion Enzyme (1 μL), PCR1 amplicon (5 μL), and PCR-grade water (68 μL), to a final volume of 100 μL. PCR2 amplification was performed using the same conditions as PCR1. Final PCR amplicons were resolved on a 2% gel, excised and purified by the QIAquick PCR & Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen). The purified amplicons were sequenced ( ~ 20 million reads/sample) using the HiSeq® 2500 sequencing system at Genome Québec (https://genomequebec.com/). Then, significantly enriched or depleted sgRNAs were identified from the sequencing data.

GeCKOv2 screening bioinformatics

CRISPR screen data were analyzed using the Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 Knockout (MAGeCK) pipeline (version 0.5.3)96. Sequencing reads were mapped to the corresponding sgRNAs, followed by read-count normalization and quality control. At the sgRNA level, differential abundance of individual sgRNAs was evaluated using a negative binomial model to account for count overdispersion, yielding two-sided p-values and log2 fold changes (LFCs). Gene-level significance was determined using the MAGeCK Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA) algorithm, which aggregates the rankings of sgRNAs targeting the same gene, as described by Li et al. 38,96. RRA applies one-sided statistical tests to evaluate directional selection by assessing the enrichment of targeting sgRNAs at the top of genome-wide ranked lists independently for depletion (negative selection) and enrichment (positive selection). Gene-level p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with statistical significance defined as FDR < 0.05. The top positive hits identified in the screen with LFC > 4 and the top depleted hits with LFC < −2 are listed in the Supplementary Data 1. All sgRNA sequences corresponding to the top four enriched genes that were used in the lung metastasis screen are listed in the Supplementary Table 1.

CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid construct (Individual Gene KO)

Stable KO TNBC cells were generated using the LentiCRISPRv2 vector (Addgene plasmid #52961, gift from Feng Zhang). Cloning was performed as described in the Addgene protocol95. Briefly, 3 sgRNAs for each gene (VPS45, ZFYVE20, CMTR2, and NF2) and a scrambled non-targeting control were designed using the CRISPick algorithm97. Human-specific or mouse-specific sgRNA sequences for individual KOs in SUM cell lines or 4T1 cells, respectively, are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Each sgRNA oligo was phosphorylated and annealed using T4 polynucleotide kinas (PNK) enzyme using a thermocycler (Bio-Rad T100™) as follows: 30 min at 37 °C and 5 min at 95 °C, then cooling to 25 °C. Annealed oligos were diluted 1:10 and ligated into a BsmBI-digested lentiCRISPRv2 via a Golden Gate reaction containing T7 DNA ligase (NEB) and using the thermocycler program: 37 °C for 5 min, 20 °C for 5 min, repeated for 15 cycles. For transformation, Stbl3 bacteria (Invitrogen) were used, followed by overnight incubation at 33 °C on ampicillin LB agar plates for selection. Plasmids were purified using the Qiagen Plasmid Miniprep Kit.

CRISPR activation plasmid construct (Individual Gene Activation)

The CRISPR/dCas9 Synergistic Activation Mediator (SAM) system, including LentiSAMv2 (Addgene plasmid #75112) and LentiMPH v2 (Addgene plasmid #89308) was used98. Briefly, 3 sgRNAs for each gene (VPS45, CMTR2, RBSN, and NF2) were designed using the CRISPick algorithm. The sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Oligo annealing and ligation into the LentiSAM vector were performed as described above and in the SAM protocol98. For transformation, Stbl4 bacteria (Invitrogen) were used, following the same procedure as above. The Qiagen Plasmid Miniprep Kit was used for purification of the cloned vectors.

Lentiviral production and infection

The HEK293FT cell line was used for lentiviral production. Briefly, the cells were transfected with (15 μg of the cloned vector for each gene, 4.5 μg pMD2.G (Addgene #12259), and 12 μg psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), mixed with polyethyleneimine (PEI) 10 mg/mL, and Opti-MEM), followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C for 16 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium. After 48 h of transfection, the supernatant containing viral particles was collected and stored at −80 °C. For CRISPR KO lentiviral infection, TNBC cells were infected with the corresponding virus in the presence of 8 μg/mL of polybrene in the medium to facilitate virus transduction, followed by overnight incubation. Cells were harvested, seeded, and 36 h later subjected to puromycin selection, then were used for functional assays. For generation of VPS45/RBSN double KO SUM159PT cells, lentiviral particles targeting each gene were co-administered at a 1:1 ratio under identical transduction conditions, followed by puromycin selection for downstream functional assays.

For CRISPR activation lentiviral production and infection: the HEK293FT cell line was used for lentiviral production of the LentiSAMv2 vectors for each target gene and LentiMPH v2 vector, and the procedures are the same as mentioned above in the lentiviral production section. For individual targets’ endogenous activation, SUM159 or MDA-MB-231 cells were first transduced with LentiMPH v2 lentivirus encoding the MS2-P65-HSF1 activator helper, and selected using hygromycin (500 μg/mL, InvivoGen), then transduced with LentiSAMv2 containing the sgRNA for each gene, followed by blasticidin selection (10 μg/mL, WISENT Inc.).

Targeted amplicon sequencing and CRISPR editing analysis

To further validate and quantify genome editing outcomes at higher resolution, PCR amplicons generated for the Surveyor nuclease assay were used for targeted amplicon sequencing. These amplicons were derived from genomic DNA of cells transduced with sgRNA corresponding to VPS45, RBSN, NF2, CMTR2 (KO) or a non-targeting scrambled sgRNA control. Purified PCR products from KO cells or scrambled control cells were subjected to long-read sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platform via Plasmidsaurus. This approach enabled full-length sequencing of individual amplicons, providing accurate characterization of CRISPR-induced sequence alterations. Resulting FASTQ files were analyzed using CRISPResso262 with alignment to the corresponding reference amplicon. Insertions and deletions (INDELs) and mutation spectra were quantified at single-base resolution. Editing efficiency in KO samples was assessed relative to the non-targeting scrambled sgRNA control to account for background editing signals.

In vivo lung metastasis studies

To validate CRISPR/Cas9 screening results, individual gene KO cell lines were generated in SUM159PT or SUM149PT cells targeting VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, and NF2 (one sgRNA per gene). Briefly, KO cells were intravenously injected via the lateral tail vein into NSG mice at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per mouse. Experimental groups consisted of n = 8 mice per group for SUM159PT models and n = 6-8 mice per group for SUM149PT models.

After 15 days following injection of SUM159PT cells or 44 days post-injection of SUM149 cells, mice were euthanized and lungs were harvested and stained with Bouin’s solution. Metastatic lung nodules were then quantified manually using a 3D stereoscopic microscope. In addition, one representative lung from each SUM149 KO groups were fixed in formalin and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Then, the total tumor area was quantified using QuPath v0.7.0 software and the average nodule size was determined by dividing the total area of tumor per section by the total counts using three serial lung sections (proximal, middle, and distal sections).

To evaluate metastatic potential in an immunocompetent context, the 4T1 syngeneic model was used for lung metastasis assay. 4T1 TNBC cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mouse, n = 8-9 mice per group) were transplanted into female BALB/c mice (6–7 weeks old) through the tail vein. Animals were euthanized after one month of injection, and metastatic burden was assessed by quantifying lung nodules using a 3D stereoscopic microscope. In addition, one representative lung from each 4T1 KO group was fixed in formalin and processed for H&E staining, followed by quantification of nodules area using QuPath v0.7.0 software.

To test the effect of endogenous activation of the target candidates on TNBC lung metastasis, SUM159 cells transduced with CRISPR lentiSAMv2 constructs targeting VPS45, RBSN, CMTR2, or NF2 (1 sgRNA/gene) were intravenously injected via the tail vein (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse; n = 7 mice/group). Mice were sacrificed after one month post-injection, and lungs were stained with Bouin’s solution and metastatic nodules were examined using a 3D stereoscopic microscope. The diameter (mm) of each visible metastatic lesion was measured following the established methodology for experimental pulmonary metastasis assessment, including the size-based evaluation strategy described by Wexler et al. 77, in which lesion size distribution is recommended when metastatic growth dynamics represent the primary experimental variable. The average metastatic lesion size per mouse was calculated by dividing the sum of individual lesion diameters by the total number of nodules.

For experimental metastasis models generated by tail vein injection, mice were monitored regularly for overall health status, body condition, activity, and signs of distress. Experimental endpoints were predetermined based on previously determined and published kinetics of metastatic progression of the transplanted cell lines to allow reliable detection and quantification of lung metastatic nodules while avoiding excessive disease burden. All procedures and humane endpoints were performed in accordance with McGill University animal care guidelines.

In vivo orthotopic xenograft studies (mammary fat pad transplantation)

Orthotopic xenograft injections into the mammary fat pad (MFP) of NSG mice were performed to address the effect of the target genes KO on both primary tumor growth and spontaneous metastasis of TNBC cells. Briefly, stable SUM159PT KO cells (1 sgRNA/gene) were harvested and diluted in a 1:1 ratio of PBS (WISENT Inc.) and Matrigel (Corning, Cat. #356234), and 1 × 106 cells/mouse were inoculated into the MFP of NSG female mice (n = 7 mice per gene). Primary tumor volumes were assessed three times/week using the formula: [4/3×π × (length/2) × (width/2)2]. The injected mice were euthanized on day 25 post- inoculation. The tumor weight was assessed ex vivo and the lung metastasis nodules were quantified manually using a 3D stereoscopic microscope. To validate metastatic potential in an immunocompetent setting, 4T1 KO cells were orthotopically transplanted into female BALB/c mice (6-7 weeks old; 2 × 10⁵ cells per mouse; n = 8 mice per group) following resuspension in a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel. Tumor growth was monitored over time, and mice were euthanized at endpoint of about one month of post-transplantation. Lungs were collected, stained, and metastatic nodules were quantified.

To test the effect of endogenous activation of the target candidates on the spontaneous metastasis of TNBC, MDA-MB-231 cells transduced with the endogenously activated targets of CRISPR lentiSAMv2 cloned constructs (1 sgRNA/gene) were harvested and diluted at 1:1 ratio of PBS (WISENT Inc.) and Matrigel (Corning, Cat. #356234) and 2 × 106 cells/mouse were inoculated into the MFP of NSG female mice (n = 8-9 mice/group). Primary tumor volumes were assessed regularly using the formula: [4/3 × π × (length/2) × (width/2)2]. The injected mice were euthanized on day 56 of inoculation. Lungs were collected, fixed, and stained. Diameters of 30 lung nodules/3 lungs were quantified using ImageJ software.

For orthotopic mammary fat pad transplantation models, tumor growth was monitored regularly by caliper measurements, and experimental endpoints were predetermined based on the established growth kinetics and metastatic behavior of the transplanted cell lines. Tumor volume was maintained within the limits approved by the McGill University animal care protocol, with a maximum permitted tumor volume of 2000 mm³.

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs)

SUM159 KO cells (scrambled, VPS45KO, RBSNKO, NF2KO, and CMTR2KO) were orthotopically injected into NSG female mice (n = 6 mice per group, 1 × 106 cells/mouse). When tumor volume reached approximately 1 cm3, mice were anesthetized, and about an average of 0.6 mL of the circulating blood was drawn by cardiac puncture using a 1 mL syringe coated with 10 mM EDTA and immediately transferred into Lithium-Heparin tubes (LH, 1.3 mL, SARSTEDT) to prevent coagulation. Then, blood samples were plated directly in Ham’s F-12 medium containing L-glutamine (WISENT Inc.) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1× Mammary Epithelial Growth Supplement (MEGS, Thermo Fisher, Cat. # S0155), and insulin (5 μg/mL, WISENT Inc.). Three days later and every other two days thereafter, the medium was gently replaced with a fresh medium to eliminate blood cells. On day 14 of in vitro culture, tumor cells were harvested and counted. Tumor blood burden was calculated by normalizing the total number of circulating tumor cells to both the corresponding volume of blood drawn (mL) and tumor volume (cm3), to account for differences in primary tumor burden.

Wound-healing assay

Transduced SUM159 KO cells (single or double gene KO) were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/ well) until 90% confluency, then the monolayer was rinsed with PBS and starved using serum-free Ham’s F-12 medium for 24 h. Then, the monolayer was scratched using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, followed by washing once with PBS to remove detached cells due to the scratch. Images of the scratch area were captured immediately (0 h), and again at 24 h using an inverted Nikon Ts2 microscope at 10x magnification. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software, and the wound closure (%) was calculated as the percentage reduction in wound area relative to baseline.

Matrigel invasion assay

Matrigel™ Matrix (Corning, Cat. #354234) was used to coat 12-Transwell inserts of a 24-tranwell invasion plate (8-μm pore size, Corning, Cat. #3422). For coating, Matrigel was diluted to a final concentration of 400 μg/mL in serum-free medium, then 50 or 100 μL was applied to each insert. Followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Transduced SUM159 KO cells (3 × 104 cells/insert) were seeded in the upper chambers and a complete growth medium (750 μL/chamber) containing 5% FBS as a chemoattractant was added to the bottom chambers, followed by incubation for 18–24 h in the cell culture incubator. Then, non-invading cells were removed using a cotton swab, and the invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Followed by washing with PBS,staining with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min, and washed with distilled water. For imaging, an inverted Nikon Ts2 microscope was used at 10 × magnification. Multiple fields per insert were photographed and the number of invaded cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

SUM159PT cells (VPS45KO (gRNA2), RBSNKO (gRNA1), NF2KO (gRNA3), CMTR2KO (gRNA3), and scrambled control cells) were seeded in 96-well plates (2000 cells per well) and allowed to grow for 48 h. MTT reagent (Sigma) was added to each well (final concentration 0.5 mg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to scrambled control cells.

Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay

Transduced SUM159PT cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well and allowed to grow for 72 h. Then, cells were fixed with cold 50% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C, then rinsed with tap water four times, and air-dried overnight. Cells were stained with 0.4% SRB solution for 30 min at room temperature. Unbound dye was removed by washing with 1% acetic acid, and plates were air-dried for 24 h. The bound dye was solubilized using 10 mM Tris base (pH 10.0–10.5) for 5 min on shaking platform, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

Transduced SUM159PT cells were seeded at low density (500 cells/well) in 6-well plates in triplicate and cultured for 7–10 days to allow colony formation of single cells. At the endpoint, cells were washed with PBS, fixed, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature, followed by washing with distilled water to remove excess stain. Plates were air-dried and imaged using a flatbed scanner. Colonies were counted and expressed as the mean number of colonies per condition.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using ice-cold cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl,150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM Na3VO4, and 1× protease inhibitors (1 mM Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 μg/ml Leupeptin hydrochloride, 10 μg/ml Aprotinin and 10 μg/ml Pepstatin A), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche). Protein concentration was assessed using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat. #23227). Prior to loading into the gels, samples were diluted in loading buffer (10% SDS, 0.313 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 50% glycerol, 0.5% Bromophenol Blue, and 0.5 M DL-Dithiothreitol), then boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 1 h. Followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. The membranes were washed and incubated with specific secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, then washed. For imaging, ECL was added to the corresponding bands, then membranes were imaged using ChemiDoc Touch Instrument (Bio-Rad), and data was visualized and analyzed using Image Lab Software. Specific antibodies that were used for western blot analysis are VPS45 Rabbit polyclonal antibody (Proteintech®, 12006-1-AP, 1:2000), VPS45 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Proteintech®, 12616-1-AP), RBSN (Rabenosyn-5) rabbit polyclonal antibody (Proteintech®, 22218-1-AP, 1:2000), CMTR2 (FTSJD1) rabbit polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PAS-70295, 1:1000), NF2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (ABclonal Technology, A2456, 1:1000), β-tubulin mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 3F3-G2, sc53140, 1:4000). Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling, 13110, D3H8P, 1:1000).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed in ice-cold, non-denaturing lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 1 mM EDTA, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (1× final concentration). Lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min and passed through a 31-gauge insulin syringe needle to reduce viscosity by shearing genomic DNA. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the clarified supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay, and equal amounts of protein (1 mg) were used for each immunoprecipitation. Lysates were incubated with 3 µg of the indicated primary antibodies, VPS45 (Proteintech®, 12616-1-AP) or Rabenosyn 5 (Proteintech®, 22218-1-AP), under gentle rotation at 4 °C overnight. Immune complexes were captured using Pierce™ Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by adding 20 µL of beads per sample and incubating for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in Laemmli sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, followed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

TNBC cells were cultured to 80% confluence and lysed using TRIzolTM reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. #15596026). For RNA extraction from SUM159 xenograft tumor tissue, 50 mg of tissue was used. Extraction and purification of total RNA were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using Tecan Infinite 200 PRO Microplate Reader for nucleic acids quantification, and 2 μg was used for reverse transcription using random hexamers and M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Cat. #28025013). Real-time PCR was performed using SsoFastTM EvaGreen® Supermix (Bio-Rad, Cat. # 1725201) on Rotor-Gene 6000 real-time PCR thermocycler (Corbett) as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 20 s. Relative mRNA expression levels were assessed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal normalization control. The specific primer sequences that were used to assess the corresponding mRNA expression levels are listed in the Supplementary Table 5.

Immunohistochemistry

Primary tumors from the CMTR2KO and scrambled control groups of SUM159 xenograft were fixed in 10% formalin, then the tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and mounted on glass slides. For staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using graded alcohol. For antigen retrieval, slides were immersed in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave oven for 20 min. After cooling, slides were washed with TBST, and endogenous peroxidase blocking (3% H2O2) was applied for 15 min at room temperature. Followed by incubation with protein blocking reagent (EMD Millipore Corp., Cat. #20773-M) for 20 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with CD31 (PECAM-1) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #77699 s, 1:100). Sections were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (EnVision+ System-HRP, labelled polymer, anti-rabbit; Dako, Cat. #K4003) for 90 min at room temperature. Then, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was applied as the substrate chromogen for 4 min. Nuclei were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Abcam, Cat. #AB220365). Slides were mounted and digitally scanned with an Aperio AT Turbo Scanner (Leica Biosystems) at the RI-MUHC IHC platform. The microvascular density was quantified by thresholding and calculating the average percentage of CD31-positive area relative to the total area of the regions of interest (ROIs = 6 regions per tumor) using ImageJ software. Statistical significance between the scrambled and CMTR2KO stained tumors (n = 4 tumors per group) was assessed using a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining of lung tissue

Lung tissues from in vivo tail vein transplantation experiments using SUM149 and 4T1 transduced cells were collected at endpoint. A representative subset of lungs (n = 1 mouse per group) was fixed in formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into serial sections (proximal, middle, and distal). Sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and scanned at the RI-MUHC IHC core facility for histological assessment of metastatic lesions.

Ex vivo tumor MRI scanning

The primary tumor tissue from the scrambled and CMTR2KO SUM159 xenografts was resected after 25 days of orthotopic transplantation of transduced SUM159 cells and fixed in formalin. Imaging was performed using a Bruker 7 T MRI system (BioSpec 70/30) equipped with a volume mouse head coil at the RI-MUHC Small Animal Imaging Laboratories (SAIL) Platform. A 3D T2-weighted imaging sequence based on Steady-State Free Precession (SSFP) was employed. The field of view (FOV) was set to 15 × 15 × 6 mm, and the resolution was 0.091 × 0.091 × 0.073 mm. An echo time (TE) of 3.298 ms, a repetition time (TR) of 6.596 ms, and 4 repetitions with 2 averages were applied. The scan was performed without fat suppression to visualize and enhance tumor tissue contrast. The total acquisition time was approximately 60 min.

SWIR optical in vivo imaging

To monitor tumor vasculature, transduced SUM159 cells (scrambled or CMTR2KO) were injected (1 ×106 cells/mouse, a 1:1 ratio of PBS and Matrigel) into the mammary fat pads of NSG female mice (20-25 g each mouse). The tumor volume was assessed regularly, and SWIR imaging was performed at experimental endpoint. Hair overlying the tumor region was removed using a trimmer prior to imaging. Then, mice were transferred to the PRISM SWIR In Vivo Imaging System (MediLumine Inc., Montreal, Quebec, Canada) at the Small Animal Imaging Laboratories (SAIL) Platform. Anesthesia was maintained at 1.5–2.5% isoflurane, and mice were positioned on a 37 °C heated stage. Image acquisition started immediately after intravenous tail vein injection of 200 µL of indocyanine green (ICG) dye (0.025 mg /mL ICG in sterile water, SKU: MDL-CG-121, MediLumine Inc.) using an insulin syringe with a 30-gauge needle. A 52-second acquisition was performed on the PRISM SWIR imaging system using 785 nm laser excitation and a 1300 nm long-pass emission filter. Images were collected with a field of view of 20.9 mm and a working distance of 340 mm. The acquisition was recorded at 100 ms exposure per frame, with two exposures averaged per saved image (effective frame rate: 5 fps).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis of the CRISPR lung metastasis screen is detailed in the Methods section under the “GeCKOv2 screening bioinformatics” subsection.

For comparisons among multiple groups with normally distributed data, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare each experimental group with the control group. For data that did not meet normality assumptions, a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test. Where appropriate, two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used. UALCAN dataset statistics were generated using a two-sided Welch’s t-test according to the database output. All results are presented as the mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.0) was used for plotting graphs. The number and nature of the replicates for all immunoblotting and cleavage assays are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
