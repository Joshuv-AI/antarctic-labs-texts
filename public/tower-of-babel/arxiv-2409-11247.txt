Birth control and turnpike property of Lotka-McKendrick models with diffusion
Marius Bargo, Yacouba Simporé

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we study the turnpike property in age-structured population dynamics with a birth control. These models describe the temporal evolution of one or more populations, incorporating age dependence and spatial structure. To this end, we first establish the null controllability of the system : we prove that, for any
T>A
and any initial datum in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,A))
, the population can be driven to zero using control functions that are spatially localized in
t
but act only at age
a=0
. We then show that, although this control is initially applied only at birth, it can be reformulated as a distributed control, and we demonstrate that the resulting control operator is admissible in the state space. Thus, to prove the turnpike property we combine our null-controllability results with Phillips’ theorem on exponential stability to design a suitable dichotomy transformation based on solutions of the algebraic Riccati and Lyapunov equations. Finally, we present numerical examples that substantiate our analytical findings.

Keywords
: Null-controllability ; exponential stability ; Riccati operator ; turnpike property ; population dynamics.

1.
Introduction

Many models of population dynamics have been proposed, including those developed by Pierre-François Verhulst and Lotka–McKendrick. One of the earliest mathematical models of population growth was presented in
1798
by Thomas R. Malthus, who juxtaposed the exponential increase of populations with the limitations imposed by available resources. In recent decades, the field has witnessed significant advancements.

The growing interest in population dynamics is largely driven by global demographic growth, prompting policymakers to implement control measures. The baby boom, characterized by a surge in births after World War II, profoundly transformed global demographic dynamics. This rapid population increase led to challenges such as overpopulation, depletion of natural resources, and accelerated aging of populations. To address these issues, policies like China’s one-child policy, inspired by Song Jian’s research in 1976, were implemented.

These demographic challenges demand rigorous management to prevent crises related to the workforce, pension systems, and equitable access to resources. Studies in population dynamics aim not only to explain population evolution and address issues like extinction or stability but also to determine an optimal population level, often defined as an ”ideal population,” at minimal cost, within the framework of control theory.

The turnpike theory, developed by three preeminent
xx
th
century economists, Paul Samuelson, Robert Solow and Robert Dorfman, offers a pertinent tool in this context. Inspired by Von Neumann’s maximal growth model, it optimizes dynamic trajectories by rapidly bringing the system close to an ideal stationary state before reaching the final objective. This theory establishes that, in the long term, optimal solutions to a control problem follow a trajectory close to that of an associated stationary problem.

The turnpike phenomenon refers to the tendency of optimal trajectories, over long but finite time horizons, to approach and remain close to a steady state for most of the time. This behavior was first observed by von Neumann in the context of optimal growth strategies toward economic equilibria and was later explicitly named in
[
13
]
.

In modern macroeconomics, the turnpike property implies that when an economic agent aims to transition an economy from one capital level to another, the most efficient strategy, given a sufficiently long time horizon, is to rapidly adjust the capital stock to a level near a stationary optimal path before finally reaching the target. This concept, inherent to resource allocation strategies, has also been demonstrated in partial differential equations arising in mechanics, supporting the idea that for an optimal control problem defined over an extended time horizon, any optimal solution will remain close to the corresponding static optimal solution for most of the interval.

The turnpike phenomenon reveals a quasi-stationary structure in dynamic systems. In particular, when the time horizon
T
is sufficiently long, the optimal trajectory of the dynamic system remains near the steady-state solution of the associated static problem. This insight is particularly significant in population dynamics, where achieving an ideal population structure is crucial, and it provides a robust framework for characterizing population behavior and designing effective stabilization strategies.

We analyze a model of population dynamics to underscore the exponential decay of dynamic solutions toward the steady state. We consider the following system:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}y(x,a,t)+\partial_{a}y(x,a,t)-\triangle y(x,a,t)+\mu(a)y(x,a,t)=0&\text{ in }Q,\\
\\
\partial_{\nu}y(x,a,t)=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\\
y(x,0,t)=\mathds{1}_{\omega}(x)v(x,t)+\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)y(x,a,t)da&\text{ in }Q_{T},\\
\\
y(x,a,0)=y_{0}(x,a)&\text{ in }Q_{A},\end{array}\right.

with
\mathds{1}_{\omega}(x)
the characteristic function of the open subset
\omega\subset\Omega
, where the control

is assumed to act.

In the above model,

•

,
\Sigma=\partial\Omega\times[0,A]\times[0,T]
,
Q_{T}=\Omega\times[0,T]

•

(
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
) the bounded open set with a regular boundary representing the spatial domain occupied by the individuals. The operator

denotes the Laplacian with respect to the spatial variable

, modeling the diffusion or spatial displacement of the population.

•

y(x,a,t)
represents the density of individuals located at
x\in\Omega
, of age

, at time
t\geq 0
.

•

is the initial population distribution.

•

denotes the expected lifespan.

•

are nonnegative functions representing the fertility and mortality rates, respectively, with

•

The penultimate equation, which describes the birth process, is also known as the renewal equation; it provides a nonlocal condition that links the newborns to individuals of reproductive age:
\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)y(x,a,t)da
.

•

We impose homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, reflecting an isolated population in which individuals remain confined to the domain

\partial_{\nu}y(x,a,t)=0
.

In the sequel, we assume that the fertility rate

and the mortality rate

satisfy the demographic property :

\displaystyle(H_{1}):\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mu\geq 0\text{ a.e. in }(0,A),\\
\mu\in L^{1}_{loc}(0,A)\quad\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A}\mu(a)da=+\infty,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle(H_{2}):\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\beta\in C^{1}(\Omega\times[0,A])\text{ and }\beta(x,a)\geq 0\text{ }\forall(x,a)\in\Omega\times[0,A],\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{N}ull_{\beta}):\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\beta(x,a)=0\text{ }\forall a\in(0,a_{b})\text{ where }0<a_{0}\leq a_{b}<A.\\
\end{array}\right.

We introduce also the following function

\pi(a)=e^{-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\mu(s)ds}

which is the probability of survival of an individual from age
0
to
a.

In this paper, we investigate two key aspects of an infinite-dimensional linear system modeling age-structured population dynamics with spatial distribution, with a particular focus on control applied at birth : the null controllability and the turnpike property.

The model under consideration features a boundary control acting at birth (i.e., localized at age zero). As a first step, we study the well-posedness of the system and the admissibility of the control operator, by showing that the boundary control problem can be reformulated as an equivalent system with an internal source term, which admits a well-posed formulation.

Next, we establish the null controllability of the system for any time
T>A
. Finally, we prove that the optimal control problem associated with this system satisfies the exponential turnpike property in the context of infinite-dimensional continuous-time linear-quadratic control, without any constraint on the control input or the initial condition. A numerical simulation is presented to illustrate and support the theoretical results.

Let us introduce the notation used throughout this paper. We denote by

In the context of functional analysis applied to dynamical systems and control theory, let
\mathcal{A}
be a (possibly unbounded) linear operator on

is defined by

\rho(\mathcal{A})=\bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid\lambda I-\mathcal{A}\text{ is invertible on }H\bigr\},

and its resolvent is

R(\lambda;\mathcal{A})=(\lambda I-\mathcal{A})^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(H).

The spectrum

is the complement of

in
\mathbb{C}

Next, choosing any fixed

•

•

\|z\|_{H_{1}}:=\bigl\|(\lambda I-\mathcal{A})\,z\bigr\|_{H},\quad z\in D(\mathcal{A}).

With these definitions, one obtains the continuous embeddings

H_{1}\;\subset\;H\;\subset\;H_{-1}.

In addition, throughout the paper, we refer to the control that drives the state to zero as the “null-control.”

Now, we provide some basic results on the population semigroup for the linear age structured model with diffusion. We give the existence of the semigroup in the Hilbert space
H.
For it, we define the operator

5.
Proof of Exponential Turnpike Property

5.1.
Linear-Quadratic optimal control problems

In this section, we introduce an optimal control system governed by a quadratic cost functional to be minimized. Such problems are commonly referred to as linear–quadratic optimal control problems. By Theorem
3.4
, for every
T>A
there exists a control
v\in L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])
such that the solution
y
of (
1.1
) satisfies

y(x,a,T)=0\quad\text{a.e. }(x,a)\in\Omega\times[0,A],

for all initial data
y_{0}\in H
; this property is equivalent to trajectory controllability. Our goal is to steer the state toward a prescribed trajectory
\bar{y}
so as to track a continuously evolving target
y_{d}\in H
.

We now formulate the corresponding linear–quadratic optimal control problem. Originating from a population dynamics model with age structure and spatial diffusion, this dynamic optimization problem consists in finding
v^{*}\;\in\;L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])

that minimizes the cost functional

(5.1)

\displaystyle J_{1}(v^{*})=\min_{v\in L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}J_{1}(v)

where

(5.2)

\displaystyle J_{1}(v)=\dfrac{N}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y(t)-y_{d}\|^{2}dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\omega)}dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\langle y_{T}(T),p(T)\rangle

and
N>0,

its solution
y(t)=y(x,a,t)
subject to

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t}(x,a,t)=\mathcal{A}_{m}y(x,a,t)+Bv(x,a,t)&\hbox{ in }Q,\\
\\
y\left(x,a,0\right)=y_{0}(x,a)&\hbox{ in }Q_{A}.\end{array}\right.

The system (
1.1
) is reformulated in the form (
5.3
) thanks to the construction from
Section
2
(and Theorem
3.4
)
, with the control operator denoted by
B
. Minimizing the functional (
5.1
) subject to constraint (
5.3
) corresponds to the “linear regulator problem,” or equivalently, the linear-quadratic problem.

To study the turnpike property, we introduce the steady-state versions of (
5.1
) and (
5.3
). We thus consider the associated stationary system :

(5.4)

-\mathcal{A}_{m}y(x,a)=Bv(x,a)\qquad\text{in}\;Q_{1},

and the following minimization problem

(5.5)

J_{2}(\bar{v})=\min_{v\in L^{2}(\omega)}J_{2}(v),

where

(5.6)

J_{2}(v)=\frac{N}{2}\|y-y_{d}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\omega)},

and
y
denotes the solution to (
5.4
). We now state the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists an optimal control minimizing the cost functional
J_{1}
.

To prove that
J_{1}
attains its minimum over
L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])
, we will establish the following properties:

(1)

J_{1}
is convex,

(2)

J_{1}
is weakly lower semicontinuous,

(3)

J_{1}
is coercive, i.e.,

\lim_{\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times(0,T))}\longrightarrow\infty}J_{1}(v)=\infty.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
:

Coercivity of the cost functional
J_{1}

:

Let
y=e^{\alpha t}\tilde{y}
be a solution of

(5.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\tilde{y}(x,a,t)+\partial_{a}\tilde{y}(x,a,t)-\Delta\tilde{y}(x,a,t)+(\mu(a)+\alpha)\tilde{y}(x,a,t)=e^{-\alpha t}Bv(x,a,t)&\text{ in }Q,\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{\nu}\tilde{y}(x,a,t)=0&\text{ on }\Sigma,\\[2.84526pt]
\tilde{y}(x,0,t)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)\tilde{y}(x,a,t)da&\text{ in }Q_{T},\\[2.84526pt]
\tilde{y}(x,a,0)=y_{0}(x,a)&\text{ in }Q_{A},\end{array}\right.

where the parameter
\alpha
will be determined later.

Multiplying the first equation by
\tilde{y}
and integrating the resulting expression over
Q
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{A}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{y}^{2}(x,a,T)\,dx\,da+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{A}\int_{\Omega}2(\mu(a)+\alpha)\tilde{y}^{2}(x,a,t)\,dx\,da\,dt

\leq 2\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}+2A\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{A}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{y}^{2}(x,a,t)\,dx\,da\,dt+\|y_{0}\|^{2}.

Choosing
\alpha=A\|\beta\|^{2}+1
, it follows that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|y(T)\|\leq C^{\prime}\Bigl(\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}+\|y_{0}\|\Bigr).

Hence, we deduce that

\bigl|\langle y_{T}(T),p(T)\rangle\bigr|\leq C^{\prime}\Bigl(\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}+\|y_{0}\|\Bigr)\|p(T)\|.

Moreover, since

\frac{N}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|y(t)-y_{d}\|^{2}\,dt>0,

we obtain

J_{1}(v)\geq\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}-C^{\prime}\|p(T)\|\right)-C^{\prime}\|y^{0}\|\|p(T)\|.

Since the right-hand side tends to infinity as
\|v\|\rightarrow\infty
, we conclude that

\lim_{\|v\|\rightarrow\infty}J_{1}(v)=\infty,

thus establishing the coercivity of the cost in
L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])
.

The Convexity of the Cost
:

The functional
J_{1}
is quadratic, and the dependence of
y
on
v
is affine. Consequently,
J_{1}
is strictly convex.

Continuity

Let
y=y(v,y_{0})
be a solution of (
1.1
). The mapping

(v,y_{0})\longmapsto y(v,y_{0})

defines a continuous function from
L^{2}(\omega\times(0,T))\times H
into
L^{2}(0,T;H)
. To establish this continuity property, consider an arbitrary pair
(v,y_{0})\in L^{2}(\omega\times(0,T))\times H
and set
\bar{y}=y(v,y_{0})
. Then
\bar{y}
satisfies the following problem:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\bar{y}+\partial_{a}\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}+\mu(a)\bar{y}=Bv&\text{in }Q,\\[3.87498pt]
\partial_{\nu}\bar{y}=0&\text{on }\Sigma,\\[3.87498pt]
\bar{y}(x,0,t)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)\bar{y}\,da&\text{in }Q_{T},\\[3.87498pt]
\bar{y}(x,a,0)=y_{0}&\text{in }Q_{A}.\end{cases}

Next, define
z=e^{-rt}\bar{y}
for some
r>0.
Then,
z
is a solution to:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}z+\partial_{a}z-\Delta z+(r+\mu(a))z=e^{-rt}Bv+&\text{in }Q,\\[3.87498pt]
\partial_{\nu}z=0&\text{on }\Sigma,\\[3.87498pt]
z(x,0,t)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)z\,da&\text{in }Q_{T},\\[3.87498pt]
z(x,a,0)=y_{0}&\text{in }Q_{A}.\end{cases}

Multiplying the first equation of this system by
z
and integrating by parts over
Q
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\|z(\cdot,\cdot,T)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|z(\cdot,0,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}^{2}+\|\nabla z\|_{L^{2}((0,T);H)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{A}\int_{\Omega}(r+\mu(a))z^{2}\,dx\,da\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\omega}e^{-rt}vz\,dx\,dt.

From this, we deduce that

\|\nabla z\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,T);H)}+r\|z\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|y_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|z(\cdot,0,t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}+\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|z\|^{2}.

Since

\frac{1}{2}\|z(\cdot,0,t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))}\leq\frac{1}{2}A\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|z\|^{2},

we obtain

\|\nabla z\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,A)\times(0,T))}+\left(r-\frac{A\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+1}{2}\right)\|z\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|y_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}.

Choosing
r
such that

r>\frac{A\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+1}{2},

yields

\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,A)\times(0,T);H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|y_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}.

This implies that

\|\bar{y}\|_{L^{2}((0,A)\times(0,T);H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq e^{rT}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(\|y_{0}\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}\right).

Since
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it follows that
\bar{y}
is strongly continuous in
L^{2}\bigl((0,A)\times(0,T);\,L^{2}(\Omega)\bigr).

J_{1}
is continuous because the state
y
depends continuously on the initial data
(v,y_{0})
. Therefore, by virtue of its continuity and coercivity,
J_{1}
is weakly lower semicontinuous. It follows that
v^{*}\in L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])
satisfies (
5.1
) and therefore
v^{*}
is an optimal control. We denote it by
v_{T}
, corresponding to the optimal state
y_{T}
.

Considering the stationary version of the state equation, the associated steady-state cost
J_{2}
admits an optimal control for problem (
5.4
) by the same standard methods used in Proposition
5.1
. We denote this optimal pair by
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
.

5.2.
Preliminary

In this subsection, we present, on the one hand, the optimality systems associated with the dynamic and steady control problems based on Pontryagin’s maximum principle, and on the other hand, some tools (or definitions) useful for the study of the stabilization of a dynamic system.

Necessary Optimality Conditions for the Dynamic Optimal Control Problem
:
By applying Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle to the optimal pair
(y_{T},v_{T})
associated with (
5.1
), we deduce the existence of an adjoint variable
p_{T}\in L^{2}([0,T];H)
such that

(5.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{\partial y_{T}}{\partial t}(x,a,t)=\mathcal{A}_{m}y_{T}(x,a,t)+Bv_{T}(x,a,t)&\hbox{ in }Q,\\
\\
y_{T}\left(x,a,0\right)=y_{0}(x,a)&\hbox{ in }Q_{A},\\
\\
-\dfrac{\partial p_{T}}{\partial t}(x,a,t)-\mathcal{A}^{*}_{m}p_{T}(x,a,t)=N(y_{T}-y_{d})&\hbox{ in }Q,\\
\\
p_{T}\left(x,a,T\right)=p_{T}(T)&\hbox{ in }Q_{A},\\
\\
v_{T}(x,a,t)=-B^{*}p_{T}(x,a,t)&\hbox{ in }Q.\end{array}\right.

Necessary Optimality Conditions for the Static Optimal Control Problem
: Similarly, by applying the Pontryagin Maximum Principle to any optimal pair
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
of (
5.5
), we deduce the existence of an adjoint state
\bar{p}\in H
such that:

(5.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mathcal{A}_{m}\bar{y}(x,a)=B\bar{v}(x,a)&\hbox{ in }Q_{1},\\
\\
-\mathcal{A}^{*}_{m}\bar{p}(x,a)=N(\bar{y}-y_{d})&\hbox{ in }Q_{1},\\
\\
\bar{v}(x,a)=-B^{*}\bar{p}(x,a)&\hbox{ in }Q_{1}.\end{array}\right.

The notion of stability plays a crucial role in the realization of the turnpike phenomenon. Its characterization in infinite-dimensional settings is significantly more intricate than in the finite-dimensional case. In
[
24
]
, it is already established that if a linear control system is both controllable and observable in finite dimensions, then there exists a linear feedback law
y\mapsto Ky
such that the zero equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable for the equation

\partial_{t}y=\mathcal{A}_{m}y+BKy.

Essentially, there are three main types of conditions that ensure the stabilizability of a system state, such as that in (
5.3
). The first type requires specific structural properties of the generator
K
; the second relies on the concept of controllability; and the third is expressed in terms of a corresponding Riccati operator equation.

System (
5.3
) is said to be exponentially stabilizable if it is possible to design a state feedback law that stabilizes it, as specified in the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

The state equation in (
5.3
) is said to be stabilizable if there exists a feedback matrix
K
such that

\|\exp(t(\mathcal{A}_{m}-BK))y_{0}\|\leq\|y_{0}\|\exp(-t\nu)

for some constants
C,\nu>0
.

In practical applications, the choice of
K
is crucial for achieving optimal performance of system (
5.3
). In general, the objective is to design robust feedback laws suitable for uncertain linear control systems. Various tools are available to tackle this problem. In our approach, we derive a simplified form of optimal control in which the control is expressed as a linear function of the corresponding state, a formulation that is particularly advantageous in engineering applications. Specifically, this feedback is constructed using the Riccati operator associated with the finite-horizon problem. This operator is known to generate a feedback control law that ensures exponential stability over an infinite time horizon, as shown in
[
16
]
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Before proceeding further, we define the following cost functional for the case where the target satisfies

:

(5.11)

J_{1}^{0}(v_{T})=\dfrac{N}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y_{T}(t)\|^{2}dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|v_{T}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\|y_{T}(T)\|^{2},

with

\min\limits_{v\in L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}J_{1}^{0}(v).

The cost functional is well-defined for every

. Indeed, since the

is exponentially stable, and therefore

Since equation

Then there exists a unique, symmetric, positive definite matrix

(5.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{E}_{t}(T-t)=I.N+\mathcal{E}(T-t)\mathcal{A}_{m}+\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}\mathcal{E}(T-t)-\mathcal{E}(T-t)BB^{*}\mathcal{E}(T-t)&\hbox{ in }(0,\infty)\\

with the terminal condition

(ii)

(iv)

There exists a Lyapunov function of the form

Proof of Lemma

:

In the reference case
y_{d}\equiv 0
, we denote by

the cost functional defined in (

\mathcal{E}(T)

) yields an optimal cost given by the nonnegative quadratic form

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},y_{0}\rangle.

For
T>0
, we define the operator

\mathcal{E}(T):H\rightarrow H,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{by}\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0}(x,a):=p_{T}(x,a,0)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }y_{0}\in H.

It is evident that

which implies

(5.18)

v_{T}(x,t)=-B^{*}\mathcal{E}(T-t)y_{T}(x,a,t)\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,t\in[0,T].

The expression (
5.18
) gives the feedback optimal control for the cost functional (
5.11
).

The operator
\mathcal{E}(T)
is obtained by multiplying the first equation of (

and integrating over

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},y_{0}\rangle=N\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y_{T}(t)\|^{2}dt+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|p_{T}(0,t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}dt+\|y_{T}(T)\|^{2},

and

\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},y_{0}\rangle=\inf_{v\in L^{2}(\omega\times[0,T])}J_{1}^{0}(v).

Thus, the minimal value of the cost functional (

\frac{1}{2}\bigl\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},\,y_{0}\bigr\rangle,
and the corresponding optimal control is given by (
5.18
).

Case (i)
: We now prove that there exists a constant
M>0
such that

for all

.

Starting from the estimate

(5.21)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},y_{0}\rangle\leq\dfrac{N}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y(t)\|^{2}dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}dt

. Consequently, there exists a constant

such that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{E}(T)y_{0},y_{0}\rangle\leq M\|y_{0}\|^{2}.

This shows that
\mathcal{E}(T)
is uniformly bounded from above in

, so that
\mathcal{E}(T)\leq M
.

Since the cost functional (

) is nonnegative by definition, we also have

:We now show that the operator

in the cost functional can only increase the minimal cost. Let

and denote by

Case (iii)

Remark 5.4
.

Controllability of the state equation is essential to ensure that the optimal pair
(y_{T},v_{T})
remains uniformly bounded in
T
. Moreover, the gain operator
B^{*}\mathcal{E}(T)
converges to
B^{*}\hat{E}
as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, the infinite-horizon controller
v_{\infty}=-\,B^{*}\,\hat{E}\,y_{\infty}

is asymptotically stabilizing: it confines the state

to a neighborhood of the equilibrium and maintains effective control over the entire infinite time horizon.

We now build on the global asymptotic stability of (

) to show that the closed-loop system is in fact exponentially stable. In finite dimensions, global asymptotic stability (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
26
]
) is closely tied to exponential stability: the eigenvalues of the stability matrix

all have strictly negative real parts; equivalently,
\Re(\sigma(M))\subset(-\infty,0)
. Moreover, by
[
21

, the equilibrium
0
is (Lyapunov) stable if and only if every eigenvalue of

is either strictly negative or, if it has zero real part, is simple. This result follows from the existence of an appropriate Lyapunov function. Consequently, the closed-loop evolution

\displaystyle\partial_{t}y_{\infty}(t)\;=\;M\,y_{\infty}(t),\quad y_{\infty}(0)=y_{0},

is exponentially stable.

In infinite-dimensional settings, however, the operator

]

Lemma 5.5
.

The null-controllable system (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\|y_{\infty}(t)\|\,dt<\infty\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }y_{0}\in H.

From

The turnpike property describes how, over long time horizons, the optimal state, control, and adjoint state stay close to an optimal steady state for the majority of the interval. Our analysis of this phenomenon hinges on diagonalizing the Hamiltonian operator associated with the system, with stability playing a central role. In particular, the extremal triple
(\bar{y},\bar{v},\bar{p})
solving the steady optimal control problem
J_{2}
under constraint (
5.4
) is precisely the equilibrium of the Hamiltonian system (
5.9
).

To analyze exponential decay toward the steady state, define the deviation variables

(5.29)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}=y_{T}-\bar{y},\quad\tilde{p}=p_{T}-\bar{p},\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

By combining systems (
5.9
) and (
5.10
), these deviations satisfy the Hamiltonian system

(5.30)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{y}\\
\tilde{p}\end{bmatrix}=Ham\,\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{y}\\
\tilde{p}\end{bmatrix},

where the Hamiltonian operator
Ham\colon D(\mathcal{A}_{m})\times D(\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*})\rightarrow H\times H
is defined by

Ham=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{m}&-BB^{*}\\
-I.N&-\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

In finite dimensions, absence of imaginary-axis eigenvalues for
Ham
is equivalent to the plant being both stabilizable and detectable (see, e.g.,
[

]
). Here, stabilizability has already been established for system (

). Detectability (the dual concept of stabilizability) means there exists an operator
\mathcal{L}
such that

generates an exponentially stable semigroup (e.g.,
[

. Exponential stabilizability of system (

) implies its exponential detectability
[
42
]
. Moreover, observability of the state system entails null-controllability of its adjoint, which guarantees the existence of an operator
\mathcal{L}
ensuring this exponential detectability. It should be emphasized that observability (and thus detectability) together with stabilizability are two key elements in the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

We next state a Lemma that is pivotal for establishing exponential decay.

Lemma 5.7
.

(1)

There exists a unique symmetric positive definite solution
\hat{E}
to the algebraic Riccati equation

(5.31)

\hat{E}\mathcal{A}_{m}+\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}\hat{E}-\hat{E}BB^{*}\hat{E}+I.N=0,

such that the operator
\mathcal{A}_{m}-BB^{*}\hat{E}
is stable ; that is, it generates a semigroup that is exponentially stable.

(2)

Define

(5.32)

\displaystyle\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}I&S\\[2.84526pt]
\hat{E}&\hat{E}S+I\end{bmatrix},

where
S
is the solution to the Lyapunov equation

S(\mathcal{A}_{m}-BB^{*}\hat{E})+(\mathcal{A}_{m}-BB^{*}\hat{E})S=BB^{*}.

Then,
\Lambda
is invertible and

(5.33)

\displaystyle\Lambda\,Ham\,\Lambda^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{m}-BB^{*}\hat{E}&0\\[2.84526pt]
0&-(\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}-BB^{*}\hat{E})\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently, the operator
Ham
is boundedly invertible and its diagonal blocks are generators of semigroups that are exponentially stable.

Remark 5.8
.

The Hamiltonian operator
Ham
has no purely imaginary eigenvalues; it is thus hyperbolic, meaning all its spectrum lies off the imaginary axis. Consequently,
Ham
admits a block-diagonal decomposition (or spectral splitting) into a “stable” part, whose spectrum has strictly negative real parts, and an “unstable” part, whose spectrum has strictly positive real parts. In infinite dimensions, this corresponds to the generator of an exponentially dichotomous semigroup, a stronger notion than mere hyperbolicity in finite dimensions.

Proof of Lemma
5.7

By Lemmas
5.3
and
5.5

is positive definite and defines a Lyapunov function. The semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}

is exponentially stable, and so is the semigroup generated by its adjoint
M^{*}
.

Consider now the operator Riccati equation for the steady gain
S

S\,M+M^{*}\,S=BB^{*}.

One shows that the block operator

\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}I&S\\[2.84526pt]
\hat{E}&\hat{E}S+I\end{bmatrix},

is invertible, with inverse

\Lambda^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I+SE&-S\\[2.84526pt]
-E&I\end{bmatrix}.

Using the algebraic Riccati equation (
5.31
), one checks directly (
5.31
), which implies that the Hamiltonian operator

is boundedly invertible on
D(\mathcal{A}_{m})\times D(\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*})

Remark 5.9
.

The semigroups

\{e^{t(\mathcal{A}_{m}-BB^{*}\hat{E})}\}_{t\geq 0}

Next, we state the following Lemma.

Lemma 5.10
.

There exists a constant

, independent of the time horizon

\|y_{T}(T)\|\leq C,\qquad\|p_{T}(0)\|\leq C.

Proof of Lemma

For the first estimate,

For the second estimate,

Using Lemmas
5.7
and

Proof of Theorem
3.5
:

Consider equation (
5.30
) and employ the same dichotomy transformation as in the proof of Lemma

:

(5.34)

\begin{pmatrix}z(t)\\[2.84526pt]
q(t)\end{pmatrix}=\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{y}(t)\\[2.84526pt]
\tilde{p}(t)\end{pmatrix},\hskip 18.49988ptt\in(0,T)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{a.e.},

where, as established earlier,

(5.35)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}z(t)\\[2.84526pt]

z(t)=S(t)z(0),\hskip 18.49988ptq(t)=S(T-t)^{*}q(T),\hskip 18.49988ptt\in(0,T)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{a.e.},

where

and by

\displaystyle\|\tilde{p}_{T}(0)\|\leq C\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\|\tilde{y}_{T}(T)\|\leq C,

\|z(t)\|\leq C\,\|\tilde{y}_{T}(0)\|e^{-\nu t},\hskip 9.24994pt\|q(t)\|\leq C^{\prime}\,\|\tilde{p}_{T}(T)\|e^{-\nu(T-t)},\hskip 18.49988ptt\in(0,T)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{a.e.}

Remark 5.11
.

The final estimate (

\bar{y}
while tracking the running target

. In summary, the turnpike property is a powerful tool: qualitatively, it describes the tendency of optimal trajectories to spend most of their time near the steady state ; quantitatively, it provides explicit bounds on the “energy” (or cost) required to maintain this proximity over long horizons.

6.
Integral Turnpike Property

In this section, we study the behavior of the optimal trajectories
(y_{T},v_{T})
for large
T
, in the regime
T\leq A
. Given the equilibrium
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
, we ask whether
(y_{T},v_{T})
remains in a neighborhood of
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
over the entire time interval
[0,T]
when
T\leq A
.

6.1.
Dissipativity

We establish a turnpike property by employing the concept of dissipativity introduced by Willems in
[
46
]
. The following definitions summarize the key notions required for this analysis. Recall that

\mathcal{K}=\bigl\{\alpha:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)\,\bigm|\,\alpha\text{ is continuous, strictly increasing, and }\alpha(0)=0\bigr\}.

Definition 6.1

(Strict dissipation inequality)
.

Let
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
be an optimal stationary point of (
5.5
), and define the supply rate

w(y_{s},v)=\bigl\|y_{s}-y_{d}\bigr\|^{2}\;+\;\bigl\|y_{s}(T)\bigr\|^{2}\;-\;\bigl\|\bar{y}-y_{d}\bigr\|^{2}.

We say that (
5.1
) is
strictly dissipative
at
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
with respect to
w
if there exist

•

a storage function
S\colon H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is locally bounded and bounded below,

•

and a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
,

such that for every
T\in(0,A)
and every
\tau\in(0,T)
, any solution
(y_{s},v_{s})
of (
5.3
) with
v_{s}\equiv\bar{v}
satisfies

(6.1)

\displaystyle S\bigl(y_{s}(\tau)\bigr)\;-\;S\bigl(y_{s}(0)\bigr)\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{\tau}w\bigl(y_{s}(t),\,v_{s}(t)\bigr)\,dt\;-\;\int_{0}^{\tau}\alpha\bigl(\|y_{s}(t)-\bar{y}\|\bigr)\,dt.

Proposition 6.2
.

System (
5.3
) is strictly dissipative in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

Proof of Proposition
6.2
:

Multiply the first equation in (
5.9
) by
\bar{p}
and integrate by parts over
[0,\tau]
. This yields

(6.2)

\displaystyle\langle y_{0},\bar{p}\rangle\;-\;\langle y(\tau),\bar{p}\rangle\;-\;\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle y(t),\,\bar{y}-y_{d}\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle v(t),\,\bar{p}\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle\bar{v},\,\bar{p}\rangle\,dt,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\langle y_{0},\bar{p}\rangle\;-\;\langle y(\tau),\bar{p}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle y(t)-\bar{y},\,\bar{y}-y_{d}\rangle\,dt.

Since

\langle y-\bar{y},\,\bar{y}-y_{d}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\|y-y_{d}\|^{2}-\|\bar{y}-y_{d}\|^{2}-\|y-\bar{y}\|^{2}\bigr),

we obtain

(6.4)

\displaystyle\langle y_{0},\bar{p}\rangle-\langle y(\tau),\bar{p}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\|y(t)-\bar{y}\|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\|y(t)-y_{d}\|^{2}-\|\bar{y}-y_{d}\|^{2}\bigr)\,dt.

Noting that
\|y(\tau)\|^{2}\geq 0
, this is exactly the strict dissipation inequality (6.1) with storage function
S(y)=\langle y,\bar{p}\rangle
and

\alpha(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,s^{2}
. Hence the system is strictly dissipative.

Remark 6.3
.

Under the assumption
R<1
, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{m}
generates an exponentially stable semigroup (i.e., when
v\equiv 0
). As a consequence, the system (
5.3
) is stabilizable. Indeed, from equation (
5.29
), we derive the following coupled evolution system:

(6.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{y}(t)\\
\tilde{p}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{m}&0\\
0&-\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{y}(t)\\
\tilde{p}(t)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}BB^{*}\bar{p}\\
-N\tilde{y}(t)\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T).

The corresponding solutions can be expressed as:

(6.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{A}_{m}}\tilde{y}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{A}_{m}}BB^{*}\bar{p}\,ds,

(6.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(t)=e^{(T-t)\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}}\tilde{p}(T)+N\int_{t}^{T}e^{(s-t)\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}}\tilde{y}(s)\,ds.

We estimate
\tilde{y}(t)
as:

(6.8)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{y}(t)\|\leq Me^{-\nu t}\|\tilde{y}(0)\|+\frac{M\|BB^{*}\bar{p}\|}{\nu}\left(1-e^{-\nu t}\right).

Hence, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that:

(6.9)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{y}(t)\|\leq C_{1}\|\tilde{y}(0)\|e^{-\nu t}.

By injecting the estimate (
6.9
) into the adjoint convolution, we deduce:

(6.10)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{p}(t)\|\leq Me^{-\nu(T-t)}\|\tilde{p}(T)\|+C_{2}\|\tilde{y}(0)\|e^{-\nu t},

for some constant
C_{2}>0.
We thus conclude that there exists
K>0
such that,

(6.11)

\displaystyle\|y_{T}(t)-\bar{y}\|+\|p_{T}(t)-\bar{p}\|\leq K\left(\|y_{0}-\bar{y}\|e^{-\nu t}+\|p_{T}-\bar{p}\|e^{-\nu(T-t)}\right),\quad\text{a.e.}\;t\in(0,T).

Since the system is strictly dissipative, it follows that it is stabilizable, which in turn allows us to establish the integral turnpike property.

6.2.
Integral turnpike

This subsection addresses the integral turnpike property. We establish a weak version of the turnpike and show that the cost-minimizing control remains close to the static optimal control.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
T\leq A
. There exists a constant
M>0
, independent of
T
, such that the optimal solution
(y_{T},v_{T})
of the dynamic control problem and the solution
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
of the static control problem satisfy

(6.12)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|y_{T}-\bar{y}\|+\|v_{T}-\bar{v}\|\right)dt\leq M.

Proof of Theorem
6.4
:

Define the error variables

\tilde{y}(t)=y_{T}(t)-\bar{y},\hskip 9.24994pt\tilde{p}(t)=p_{T}(t)-\bar{p},

with boundary conditions

\tilde{y}(0)=y_{0}-\bar{y},\hskip 9.24994pt\tilde{y}(T)=y_{T}(T)-\bar{y},\hskip 9.24994pt\tilde{p}(0)=p_{T}(0)-\bar{p},\hskip 9.24994pt\tilde{p}(T)=p_{T}(T)-\bar{p}.

Then the state–adjoint system becomes

(6.13)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{y}=\mathcal{A}_{m}\,\tilde{y}+B\,\tilde{v},\\[4.0pt]
-\partial_{t}\tilde{p}=\mathcal{A}_{m}^{*}\,\tilde{p}+N\,\tilde{y}.\end{cases}

Multiply the first equation by
\tilde{p}
, the second by
\tilde{y}
, and subtract to obtain

N\int_{0}^{T}\|\tilde{y}\|^{2}\,dt\;+\;\int_{0}^{T}\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}\,dt=\langle\tilde{y}(0),\,\tilde{p}(0)\rangle\;-\;\langle\tilde{y}(T),\,\tilde{p}(T)\rangle.

By Cauchy–Schwarz and the assumptions

\|\tilde{y}(T)\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
and
\|\tilde{p}(0)\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
, it follows that

(6.14)

\displaystyle N\int_{0}^{T}\|y_{T}-\bar{y}\|^{2}\,dt+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{T}-\bar{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\omega)}\,dt\leq M.

for some constant
M>0
.

Remark 6.5
.

By Theorem
6.4
and the arguments in
[
30
]
, one also obtains the standard estimate for the time-average deviation (turnpike measure) of
(y_{T},v_{T})
from
(\bar{y},\bar{v})
. The realization of the turnpike property in the case
T\leq A
confirms that the population can be stabilized before the next generation (Theorem
6.4
).

Let

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}y_{k}(a,t)+\partial_{a}y_{k}(a,t)+(\mu(a)+\lambda_{k})y_{k}(a,t)=\mathds{1}_{\{0\}}(a)v_{k}(a,t),&\text{ in }[0,A]\times[0,T],\\

In our numerical experiments, we consider the following data

8.
Open Problems and Comments

These population dynamics models serve a dual purpose : first, to explain the evolution of a population over time; and second, to provide insights into birth and death processes by addressing key questions such as: When is the extinction of a population almost certain? and How can we achieve an ideal population at minimal cost? To our knowledge, the turnpike property emerges as a powerful tool for addressing the latter.

For instance, in the context of malaria, various global strategies aim to reduce disease transmission by targeting the population of disease vectors. Mosquito control remains a central concern, particularly through genetic modification techniques intended to limit or suppress vector reproduction. One widely used method consists of irradiating insects to induce sterility prior to release, thereby decreasing the reproductive capacity of the target population. If near-certain extinction of such a population is achievable, then identifying an optimal control strategy that drives the population density toward a desired threshold (e.g., below a critical viability level) becomes both economically viable and ecologically sustainable. As a perspective, we propose the following research directions:

•

In system (
7.2
), the operator
\lambda
acts as a mortality term, influencing the system without affecting the turnpike property. It would be insightful to investigate the impact of time-dependent mortality on this behavior.

•

Remark A.3
.

Let
\varphi\in D(\mathcal{A}_{m})
be such that
\frac{\varphi}{\pi}\in H
. We aim to show that
\varphi\in\mathcal{R}
. For every
\varphi\in D(\mathcal{A}_{m})
, there exists
f\in H
such that

\varphi^{\prime}+\mu\varphi-\Delta\varphi=f.

Then, one can write

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\varphi(x,0)+\int_{0}^{a}\left(\frac{e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)}{\pi(s)}\right)^{\prime}ds,

which, by applying the quotient rule, becomes

(A.19)

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\varphi(x,0)+\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\left(e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)\right)^{\prime}\pi(s)-\pi^{\prime}(s)e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)}{\pi(s)^{2}}ds.

Observing that

\left(e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)\right)^{\prime}\pi(s)=-\Delta\,e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)\,\pi(s)+e^{-s\Delta}\varphi^{\prime}(x,s)\,\pi(s),

and substituting into (
A.19
), we obtain

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\varphi(x,0)+\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\pi(s)e^{-s\Delta}\varphi^{\prime}(x,s)+\mu(s)\pi(s)e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)-\pi(s)\Delta\,e^{-s\Delta}\varphi(x,s)}{\pi(s)^{2}}ds.

Hence, one deduces that

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)\varphi(x,a)da+\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(x,s)+\mu(s)\varphi(x,s)-\Delta\varphi(x,s)\right)}{\pi(s)}ds,

or equivalently,

(A.20)

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)\varphi(x,a)da+\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}f(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds.

Define the functions

I(x)=\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)\varphi(x,a)da,\qquad i(x,a)=\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,z+a)dz.

Then, from (
A.20
), we deduce that

(A.21)

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,z+a)dz}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}dz}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}f(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds+I(x)-i(x,a)}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}dz}.

Our objective is to show that the expression on the left-hand side of (
A.21
) belongs to
H
; that is,
\varphi\in\mathcal{R}
.

On the one hand, since
\frac{e^{-s\Delta}f(x,s)}{\pi(s)}\in H
, it follows from the Hardy inequality
[
18
]
that

\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}f(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds\in H.

On the other hand, we need to establish that

\frac{I(x)-i(x,a)}{a}\in H.

To this end, we compute the derivative of
I(x)-i(x,a)
with respect to
x
. Differentiating under the integral sign (assuming sufficient regularity of
\beta(x,a)
and
\varphi(x,a)
), we have

\frac{\partial I(x)}{\partial x}=\int_{0}^{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(\beta(x,a)\varphi(x,a)\bigr)da=\int_{0}^{A}\left(\frac{\partial\beta(x,a)}{\partial x}\varphi(x,a)+\beta(x,a)\frac{\partial\varphi(x,a)}{\partial x}\right)da.

Similarly, for
i(x,a)
we obtain

\frac{\partial i(x,a)}{\partial x}=\int_{0}^{A-a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,z+a)\right)dz.

By applying Leibniz’s rule to differentiate the product, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(I(x)-i(x,a)\right)=\int_{0}^{A}\left(\frac{\partial\beta(x,a)}{\partial x}\varphi(x,a)+\beta(x,a)\frac{\partial\varphi(x,a)}{\partial x}\right)\,da-\int_{0}^{A-a}\frac{\partial\beta(x,z)}{\partial x}\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}e^{-a\Delta}\varphi(x,z+a)dz

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}e^{-a\Delta}\frac{\partial\varphi(x,z+a)}{\partial x}\,dz.

Given that
\beta(x,a)\in C^{1}(\Omega\times(0,A))
,
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,A;H^{2}(\Omega))
, and that the function
\pi(\cdot)
is bounded, it follows that
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(I(x)-i(x,a)\bigr)\in H
. Consequently, by the Hardy inequality, we have

\frac{I(x)-i(x,a)}{a}\in H.

Thus, the left-hand side of (
A.21
) belongs to
H
, which implies that
\varphi\in\mathcal{R}
.

Hence,
\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}\in H
.

It remains to verify that

(A.24)

\frac{\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}\,e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)dz}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds}\in H.

This is the desired condition that ensures
\bar{y}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Case 1
: For
a\in[\epsilon,A]
(with
\epsilon>0
), consider the expression

(A.25)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}\,e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)\,dz.

This expression can be equivalently rewritten as

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}\,dz.

Noting that for
a
in the appropriate range we have

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}=\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-T)}{\pi(a-T)},

it follows that the expression becomes

\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-T)}{\pi(a-T)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}\,dz.

By decomposing the integral over the intervals
[0,\,T-a]
and
[T-a,\,A-a]
, we obtain

\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-T)}{\pi(a-T)}-\int_{0}^{T-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,b(x,T-(z+a))\,dz-\int_{T-a}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,z+a-T)}{\pi(z+a-T)}\,dz.

After further manipulations, this expression can be recast as

\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-T)}{\pi(a-T)}+\int_{A-a}^{A}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,z+a-T)}{\pi(z+a-T)}\,dz-b(x,T-a),

which ultimately simplifies to

\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(z+a)}\,e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)\,dz.=\int_{A-a}^{A}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,z+a-T)}{\pi(z+a-T)}\,dz.

Hence, the expression in (
A.24
) belongs to
L^{2}(\epsilon,A;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Case 2
: For
a\in[0,\epsilon]
(with
\epsilon>0
), we consider

(A.26)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz.

This expression can be rewritten as

b(x,T-a)-\int_{0}^{T-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz-\int_{T-a}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz.

Since

b(x,T-a)=\int_{0}^{A}\beta(x,a)y(x,a,T-a)\,da,

we can express the above as

b(x,T-a)-\int_{0}^{T-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,b(x,T-(z+a))dz-\int_{T-a}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz.

Thus,

(A.27)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz=b(x,T-a)-\int_{0}^{T-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,b(x,T-(z+a))dz

\displaystyle-\int_{T-a}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,z+a-T)}{\pi(z+a-T)}dz.

By further manipulation, we obtain

(A.28)

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,a)}{\pi(a)}-\int_{0}^{A-a}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\frac{e^{-a\Delta}\bar{y}(x,z+a)}{\pi(z+a)}dz=\int_{A-a}^{A}\beta(x,s)\pi(s)\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,s+a-T)}{\pi(s+a-T)}ds.

We now seek to establish an upper bound for this final integral. Specifically, we have

\frac{\displaystyle\int_{A-a}^{A}\beta(x,s)\pi(s)\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,s+a-T)}{\pi(s+a-T)}ds}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{A-a}^{A}\beta(x,s)\pi(s)\frac{e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,s+a-T)}{\pi(s+a-T)}ds}{a}.

By a change of variable, setting
z=s+a-T
, we rewrite the right-hand side as

\|\beta\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,A])}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\left|e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,s+A-T)\right|ds}{a}.

Then, by an application of the Hardy inequality, the function

\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{a}\left|e^{(T-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,s+A-T)\right|ds

belongs to
L^{2}(0,\epsilon;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Consequently, the entire expression in (
A.27
) belongs to
L^{2}(0,\epsilon;L^{2}(\Omega))
, which implies that
\bar{y}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
:

In accordance with Propositions
A.2
and
A.4
, we deduce that

(A.29)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ran}(\mathcal{T}_{T})\subset\operatorname{Ran}(\Phi_{T}),

which implies the null-controllability of system (
3.1
). Consequently, since (
A.29
) holds, there exists a control
v\in L^{2}([0,T];H)
satisfying

(A.30)

\displaystyle\Phi_{T}v=-\mathcal{T}_{T}y_{0}.

By following an argument analogous to that in Proposition
A.2
, we can construct a control fulfilling (
A.30
).

Specifically, for
t-a<T-A
we have
\Phi_{t}v=0
and

(A.31)

\displaystyle y(x,a,t)=\frac{\pi(a)}{\pi(a-t)}e^{t\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-t).

Next, for
T-A\leq t-a\leq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle y(x,T-(t-a),T)=\frac{\pi(T-(t-a))}{\pi(a-t)}e^{t\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-t)

\displaystyle+u(x,t-a)\int_{a-t}^{T-(t-a)}\frac{\pi(T-(t-a))e^{(T-(t-a)-s)\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds,\;\forall a\in[T,A],\,x\in\Omega.

To ensure that (
A.30
) is satisfied, the control must be defined as

(A.32)

\displaystyle v(x,a,t)=\frac{-\dfrac{e^{(t-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-t)}{\pi(a-t)}}{\displaystyle\int_{a-t}^{T-(t-a)}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}dz},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }T-A\leq t-a\leq 0.

For
0<t-a\leq T
, we have

(A.33)

\displaystyle y(x,a,t)=\pi(a)e^{a\Delta}\eta(x,t-a)+u(x,t-a)\int_{0}^{a}\pi(a)\frac{e^{(a-s)\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds,

and equivalently,

y(x,T-(t-a),T)=\pi(T-(t-a))e^{(T-(t-a))\Delta}\eta(x,t-a)+u(x,t-a)\int_{0}^{T-(t-a)}\frac{\pi(T-(t-a))e^{(T-(t-a)-s)\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}ds,

with

\eta(x,t)=\int_{t+A-T}^{A}\beta(x,a)\pi(a)\frac{e^{t\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-t)}{\pi(a-t)}da.

Thus, (
A.30
) is verified if we define

v(x,a,t)=-\frac{\pi(T-(t-a))\eta(x,t-a)}{\pi(T-(t-a))\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-(t-a)}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}dz}.

In conclusion, we have derived a null control of the form

(A.34)

v(x,a,t)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }t-a<0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,x\in\Omega,\\[2.84526pt]
-\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{t-a}^{A}\beta(x,z)\pi(z)\,\dfrac{e^{(t-a)\Delta}y_{0}(x,z-(t-a))}{\pi(z-(t-a))}\,dz}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A-(t-a)}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}\,dz},&\text{if }t-a\geq 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

with the corresponding controlled state given by

(A.35)

y(x,a,t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi(a)}{\pi(a-t)}e^{t\Delta}y_{0}(x,a-t),&\text{if }t-a<0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,x\in\Omega,\\[2.84526pt]
\pi(a)e^{a\Delta}\eta(x,t-a)\left[1-\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{e^{-s\Delta}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,s)}{\pi(s)}\,ds}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{A-(t-a)}\frac{e^{-z\Delta}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\omega\times[0,a_{0}]\}}(x,z)}{\pi(z)}\,dz}\right],&\text{if }t-a>0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

which satisfies

(A.36)

y(x,a,A)=0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,a\in[0,A].

Taking
v=0
on the time interval
(A,T)
, equation (
4.2
) implies that

y(\cdot,\cdot,T)=\mathcal{T}_{T}y_{A}(\cdot,\cdot),

thus, we obtain the estimate

(A.37)

\|y(\cdot,\cdot,T)\|_{H}\leq C\|y_{A}\|_{H}.

Combining (
A.37
) with (
A.36
), we deduce that for every
T>A-a_{0}
,

(A.38)

y(x,a,T)=0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega,\hskip 9.24994pta\in[0,A].

This establishes the null-controllability of the system (
3.1
).
