The impact of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (abPG-SGA) on postoperative nutritional status in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery
Authors: Tingfang Lv, Lingyu Tang, Zhiwei Xiao, Hongmei Nie, Huanmei Hu, Lu Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the impact of personalized nursing care plans, based on the modified abPG-SGA, on postoperative nutritional status in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on clinical data from colorectal cancer surgical patients admitted to a single center. Patients were divided into a routine nursing care group and a personalized nursing care group assessed under the modified abPG-SGA. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined, and nursing methods, data collection, and statistical analysis were detailed. Various parameters including demographic features, disease characteristics, surgical outcomes, gastrointestinal complications, postoperative complications, nutritional markers, dietary intake, serum electrolyte levels, length of hospital stay, and functional recovery were compared between the two groups. Based on the composite nutritional score, patients in the personalized care group were stratified into three risk grades, guiding the intensity of nutritional support. Clinical response to intensive support was assessed within 7 days post-intervention. Statistical analysis included independent-samples t-test, chi-square test, and multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for age, BMI, sex, ASA score, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), tumor location, and treatment time period.ResultsThe personalized nursing care group showed significantly improved bowel function recovery time, reduced incidence of postoperative wound infection and urinary tract infection, improved nutritional markers and dietary intake, and maintenance of specific serum electrolyte levels compared to the routine nursing care group. Additionally, the personalized nursing care plans were associated with a reduction in the length of hospital stay and improved postoperative recovery parameters. Furthermore, risk stratification using the modified abPG-SGA demonstrated strong alignment with the intensity of nutritional support and predicted clinical response, with higher-risk patients exhibiting greater improvement. Multivariable regression analysis confirmed that personalized nursing care was independently associated with significantly lower odds of surgical site infection (adjusted OR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08–0.85, P = 0.025) and urinary tract infection (adjusted OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.79, P = 0.022), as well as a significant reduction in hospital stay (adjusted β = -1.92 days, 95% CI: -3.31 to -0.53, P = 0.007). Furthermore, personalized nursing care independently predicted improved postoperative prealbumin levels (adjusted β = 29.1 mg/L, 95% CI: 9.5–48.7, P = 0.004) and serum protein levels (adjusted β = 3.9 g/L, 95% CI: 1.1–6.7, P = 0.007) after adjustment for confounders.ConclusionPersonalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA were associated with improved postoperative nutritional status and recovery outcomes in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. However, due to the non-randomized retrospective design, causal inference cannot be made, and findings should be interpreted as associations. Further prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03821-3.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains a significant health burden globally, with substantial implications for patients’ nutritional status and postoperative recovery [1–3]. Surgical intervention forms a cornerstone of curative treatment for colorectal cancer, yet it poses challenges in maintaining adequate nutritional status, promoting recovery, and reducing postoperative complications. As such, there was a growing interest in evaluating strategies to optimize postoperative nutritional status and improve recovery outcomes in this patient population. One such strategy of interest was the implementation of personalized nursing care plans, tailored to individual patients’ nutritional needs, based on comprehensive nutritional assessments such as the modified Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (abPG-SGA) [4–6].

There was a substantial body of evidence supporting the association between malnutrition and adverse outcomes in surgical patients, including those undergoing colorectal cancer surgery [7–9]. Addressing malnutrition and optimizing nutritional status in the postoperative period was therefore of paramount importance in enhancing patient outcomes and reducing the burden on healthcare systems. Traditional approaches to postoperative nutritional support have primarily focused on standardized care protocols, such as generalized dietary advice or broad nutrition interventions. While these approaches have provided some benefit, they often fail to address the individualized nutritional needs of patients, particularly in the context of complex surgical procedures like colorectal cancer resections. A comprehensive yet pragmatic nutritional assessment tool that can swiftly inform tailored interventions is thus critically needed in the fast-paced surgical setting.

While validated tools such as the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) and its abridged version (abPG-SGA) are cornerstones of nutritional assessment in oncology, their primary reliance on patient-reported outcomes may not fully capture the acute, multidimensional risk profile of perioperative patients [10, 11]. Therefore, this study employed a modified composite nutritional assessment tool, strategically structured to integrate three validated components: [1] objective body composition and biochemical parameters for immediate physiological insight; [2] the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), recognized for its sensitivity in detecting undernutrition risk in elderly and hospitalized populations [12]; and [3] the Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS2002), which systematically accounts for disease severity and impaired nutrition status [13]. This composite tool was designed to provide a rapid, holistic, and clinically actionable evaluation, bridging the gap between comprehensive assessment and the timely demands of postoperative care pathways. Personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA have the potential to address specific nutritional challenges, optimize nutrient intake, support digestive processes, and contribute to improved postoperative recovery and nutritional status in colorectal cancer surgical patients [14, 15]. However, the precise impact of these personalized nursing care plans on postoperative nutritional status and recovery outcomes in this patient population remains an area of active investigation.

This retrospective cohort analysis seeks to elucidate the potential impact of personalized nursing care plans based on this modified, composite assessment approach on postoperative nutritional status among patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. By comparing the outcomes of patients receiving personalized nursing care with those receiving routine nursing care, the study aims to provide valuable insights into the efficacy of individualized nutritional support in the postoperative setting.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis of clinical data from colorectal cancer surgical patients admitted to our center from June 2023 to December 2023. During this period, the standard nursing protocol for nutritional management evolved in our department. Initially, routine nutritional care was applied. Subsequently, from approximately September 2023 onward, the modified abPG-SGA was systematically implemented as part of a clinical quality improvement initiative to guide personalized nutritional care. Based on the nursing protocol they received, the patients were categorized into two cohorts: a routine nursing care group (n = 144) and a personalized nursing care group assessed under the modified abPG-SGA (n = 129). The allocation was non-random and reflected the temporal change in clinical practice.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria [16]: (1) clinical diagnosis of colorectal cancer; (2) postoperative pathological examination confirming colorectal cancer; (3) age > 18 years; (4) no preoperative infections; (5) capable of basic reading and writing; (6) complete medical records.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had: (1) other malignant tumors in the body; (2) preoperative diagnosis of impaired glucose tolerance; (3) severe preoperative malnutrition; (4) acute or chronic gastrointestinal diseases; (5) severe acute or chronic conditions affecting the heart, brain, lungs, or kidneys; (6) communication barriers due to language issues; (7) severe cognitive impairments.

Nursing methods

Routine care group

Patients in this group received standard nutritional care. Postoperatively, patients were provided with intravenous nutritional support. Once the passage of flatus occurred, oral intake was resumed, ensuring the patients received their daily energy requirements. Patients were informed about dietary precautions and advised to consume a light diet.

Personalized care group assessed under the modified abPG-SGA (a composite nutritional screening tool)

Upon admission, nurses applied a composite nutritional screening tool, developed for this study based on a modified approach inspired by the abPG-SGA concept. It is important to emphasize that this composite tool is exploratory in nature and has not been externally validated; future studies are needed to establish its reliability, validity, and clinical utility. This tool integrates assessments from three validated components to provide a multidimensional evaluation: body composition assessment, Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), and Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS2002). The first part, body composition assessment, primarily includes weight loss in the past 3 months, arm muscle circumference, triceps skinfold thickness, total lymphocyte count, serum prealbumin, and serum albumin, with a total score of 14. A score < 11 suggests good nutritional status, while a score ≥ 11 indicates poor nutritional status. The second part, the MNA, includes the following components: BMI, weight loss, mobility, neuropsychological problems, and acute disease burden. According to its standard scoring, a score of 12–14 indicates normal nutritional status, 8–11 indicates risk of malnutrition, and 0–7 indicates malnutrition [17]. It is important to note that in the MNA, a higher score reflects better nutritional status, which is opposite in direction to the other two components. The third part, NRS2002, includes disease severity (0–3 points), nutritional status impairment (0–3 points), and age (an additional point if ≥ 70 years). A score ≥ 3 indicates nutritional risk [18].

Score Integration and Patient Stratification: To combine the three components into a single composite score for clinical triage, the MNA score was first reversed so that its direction aligned with the other two components (i.e., higher scores indicating poorer status or greater risk). The reversed MNA score (rMNA) was calculated as: rMNA score = 14 − original MNA score. Subsequently, the total composite score was calculated as the sum of the body composition assessment score, the rMNA score, and the NRS2002 score, yielding a theoretical range of 0–35 points, with higher scores reflecting poorer overall nutritional status or greater risk. It is acknowledged that directly summing scores from scales with different ranges (e.g., 0–14 for body composition assessment and 0–3 for NRS2002) without applying statistical weighting represents a methodological simplification adopted for clinical practicality. This approach may underscore the contribution of components with narrower score ranges. The clinical utility of the composite score lies in its ability to facilitate rapid risk stratification rather than to provide a finely calibrated continuous risk metric. Based on this composite score, patients were classified into three grades for guiding nutritional intervention: Grade A (0–24 points) indicating good nutrition, Grade B (25–27 points) indicating moderate nutritional risk, and Grade C (≥ 28 points) indicating poor nutrition/high risk. These cutoff points were established based on a preliminary internal data review and clinical consensus, aiming to facilitate straightforward categorization within the postoperative care workflow.

The nutritional intervention group, based on abPG-SGA, was led by the head nurse, with three responsible nurses and one nutrition therapist. The group oversees the entire nutritional management process for the patients. The head nurse distributes the abPG-SGA scale to the patients and guides them in filling it out correctly. The nutritional intervention group provides targeted nutritional interventions based on the patients’ abPG-SGA scores. For grade A patients, they were guided to eat according to their usual dietary habits and advised to quit smoking and drinking. Grade B patients were given intravenous nutritional supplements arranged by the nutritionist to ensure a daily calorie intake of at least 42KJ/kg. Grade C patients were guided by the nutritional intervention group to receive intravenous parenteral nutrition (PN) containing lipid emulsions, glucose, and compound amino acids, with a non-protein calorie intake of 42 kJ/kg daily and a protein supply of 1.2 ~ 1.5 g/kg daily. The PN formula was additionally supplemented with compound water-soluble vitamins, fat-soluble vitamins, and multi-trace element preparations, and the dosage of potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphorus was dynamically adjusted according to the patients’ serum electrolyte results. Clinical response to intensive support was evaluated within 7 days post-intervention based on objective markers (≥ 30% increase in prealbumin, ≥ 2 g/dL rise in serum albumin, or ≥ 2% weight gain). A schematic representation of the composite scoring system and patient stratification is provided as Supplementary Fig. 1.

Intervention compliance assessment

Nursing staff conducted daily supervision of nutritional intervention implementation, and clinical nutrition therapists performed regular follow-up evaluations (once every 2 days) to assess patients’ compliance with the personalized nutritional care plan. Patients were required to complete a daily dietary diary to record the intake of nutritional supplements and enteral/parenteral nutrition. Compliance was defined as the completion of ≥ 90% of the prescribed nutritional intervention measures, and the compliance rate was calculated as the number of compliant patients divided by the total number of patients in each grade and the entire personalized care group. The reasons for low compliance were recorded and analyzed in detail.

Data collection

The collection of patients’ demographic characteristics included age, gender, and smoking history. Disease features were recorded, encompassing tumor location, prior abdominal surgeries, ASA score, family history of cancer, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Surgical outcomes such as operation duration, intraoperative blood loss, surgical site infection rate, incision length, and number of harvested lymph nodes were tabulated. Gastrointestinal complications rates were documented, encompassing postoperative nausea, vomiting, constipation, diarrhea, and gastrointestinal function recovery time. Postoperative complications, including wound infection, anastomotic leaks, intestinal obstruction, pneumonia, and urinary tract infection, were also recorded. Nutritional biomarkers such as preoperative weight, postoperative weight, prealbumin levels, serum protein, and lymphocyte count, were collected. Dietary intake data including calorie intake, protein intake, fluid intake, fiber intake, vitamin C intake, vitamin D intake, and iron intake were gathered. Serum electrolyte levels one week postoperatively, including sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate levels, were recorded. Hospital-related time parameters, such as length of stay, abdominal drainage duration, gastric tube placement duration, and frequency of flatus, were documented. Functional recovery data, including time to walk independently, time to first flatus, and time to regular diet, were also collected. Additionally, data on nutritional risk grade assignment and the provision and clinical response to intensive nutritional support were recorded for the personalized care group.

Blood parameters analysis

Upon 7:00 AM postoperatively, 5 ml of fasting venous blood was drawn from the patients for the following analyses. Serum protein and prealbumin levels were measured using the AU5800 Automatic Biochemical Analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Lymphocyte count: EDTA-K2 was used for anticoagulation, and after room temperature transportation and standing, 20 µl of antibodies were added to the Trucount tube. Subsequently, using the backflow method, 50 µl of anticoagulated whole blood was added to the Trucount tube, vortexed, and then the whole blood sample was placed in the dark for 15 min at around 24 °C. The lymphocyte count was then measured using the CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Peripheral blood from the elbow vein was collected at 7 days postoperatively, centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 10 min (centrifuge radius: 10 cm), and the serum was used to measure potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphate levels using an electrolyte analyzer (produced by Nanjing Panshida Electronic Instrument Co., Ltd., Model: PSD-15 A).

Dietary intake

Patients completed a daily dietary diary from the day they resumed oral intake after surgery until the day of discharge, with a mean recording duration of 7.22 ± 1.22 days for all study subjects, recording the types and quantities of food consumed, with solid foods measured in grams and liquid foods in milliliters. Nurses were responsible for reviewing the daily dietary diaries, implementing targeted strategies, providing guidance, and ensuring adherence to the plan. Additionally, they calculated the patients’ calorie intake, protein intake, fluid intake, fiber intake, vitamin C intake, vitamin D intake, and iron intake during meals.

Statistical methods

A post-hoc power analysis (G*Power 3.1, α = 0.05, effect size d = 0.5) indicated a power of 0.98 for the total sample of 273 patients (144 in the routine nursing care group and 129 in the personalized nursing care group), exceeding the 0.80 threshold, supporting adequate sample size for detecting clinically meaningful differences. Data from pre-care and follow-up results were utilized for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were expressed as [n(%)]. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-test, and before-and-after care values were compared using a paired-samples t-test.

To rigorously account for the non-randomized, before-after study design, we further performed multivariable regression analyses for all key outcome variables. For continuous outcomes (e.g., length of hospital stay, nutritional markers, serum electrolytes, recovery parameters), multiple linear regression was applied. For binary outcomes (e.g., postoperative complications, gastrointestinal symptoms), multivariable logistic regression was used. All models were adjusted for a prespecified set of clinically relevant confounders, including: age, BMI, sex, ASA score, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia), tumor location, and treatment time period (categorized as early period [June–August 2023] vs. late period [September–December 2023]) to account for temporal trends in clinical practice. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for binary outcomes, and adjusted β coefficients with 95% CI for continuous outcomes. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Risk stratification based on modified abPG-SGA and response to personalized nutritional intervention

Among the 129 patients in the personalized nursing care group, nutritional risk was stratified using the modified abPG-SGA total score (range: 0–35) into three categories: Grade A (0–24 points, good nutrition), Grade B (25–27 points, moderate nutritional risk), and Grade C (≥ 28 points, poor nutrition). Of these, 48 (37.21%) were classified as Category A, 55 (42.64%) as Category B, and 26 (20.16%) as Category C.

Intensive nutritional support was provided to 2/48 (4.17%) of Category A patients, 27/55 (49.09%) of Category B, and 24/26 (92.31%) of Category C patients, with a clear stepwise implementation based on the recovery of patients’ intestinal function, demonstrating strong alignment between risk level and intervention intensity. The intensive nutritional support included three forms with detailed components: ① high-protein oral supplements (20 g of protein per 100 mL, recommended daily intake of 200 ~ 300 mL); ② enteral nutrition (EN) via nasogastric tube with an intact protein full-nutrition formula (20% of energy from protein, 55% from carbohydrates, 25% from fats, osmotic pressure 300 mOsm/L), containing sufficient vitamins, minerals and dietary fiber; ③ parenteral nutrition (PN) consistent with the nutritional formulation for Grade C patients as described above. Among the 53 patients receiving intensive nutritional support, 29 cases only received high-protein oral supplements, 21 cases received EN combined with oral supplements, and 3 cases received PN followed by EN plus oral supplements (PN was used before the recovery of intestinal flatus, and gradually transitioned to EN combined with oral supplements after flatus until normal oral diet was resumed). No patients received only dietary intervention without nutritional supplements under intensive support.

The personalized care group achieved a high overall intervention compliance rate of 96.12% (124/129). Grade-specific compliance rates showed a slight decrease with the increase of nutritional risk grade: Grade A (good nutrition) had the highest compliance rate of 97.92% (47/48), followed by Grade B (moderate nutritional risk) with 96.36% (53/55), and Grade C (poor nutrition/high risk) with 92.31% (24/26). A total of 5 patients in the personalized care group had low compliance, and all cases were caused by transient postoperative gastrointestinal discomfort (including nausea, abdominal distension, and poor appetite). All low-compliance patients resumed complete adherence to the personalized nutritional care plan after symptomatic intervention and relief of gastrointestinal discomfort, with no persistent non-compliance cases recorded.

Among the 53 patients who received intensive nutritional support, clinical response was assessed by changes in objective markers within 7 days post-intervention. Overall, 41 (77.36%) showed improvement: 19/27 (70.37%) in Category B and 22/24 (91.67%) in Category C achieved at least one of the following: ≥30% increase in prealbumin, ≥ 2 g/dL rise in serum albumin, or ≥ 2% weight gain. None of the two Category A patients met response criteria. These findings indicate that the modified abPG-SGA not only stratifies risk but also predicts responsiveness to tailored nutritional therapy (Table 7).

Multivariable regression analyses for key outcomes

To address potential confounding due to the non-randomized design, multivariable regression analyses were performed adjusting for age, BMI, sex, ASA score, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, tumor location, and treatment time period. Table 8 summarizes the results for postoperative complications, recovery parameters, and gastrointestinal outcomes. As shown in Table 8, after multivariable adjustment, personalized nursing care remained independently associated with significantly lower odds of surgical site infection (adjusted OR [aOR] = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08–0.85, P = 0.025) and urinary tract infection (aOR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.03–0.79, P = 0.022), while no significant independent associations were observed for anastomotic leak, ileus, or pneumonia (all P > 0.05). For length of hospital stay and recovery parameters, personalized nursing care was independently associated with a reduction in hospital stay by 1.92 days (adjusted β = -1.92, 95% CI: -3.31 to -0.53, P = 0.007), shorter abdominal drainage time (β = -1.58 days, 95% CI: -2.62 to -0.54, P = 0.003), shorter gastric tube placement time (β = -1.44 days, 95% CI: -2.38 to -0.50, P = 0.003), and earlier exhaust time (β = -6.13 h, 95% CI: -10.44 to -1.82, P = 0.005). For gastrointestinal and functional recovery, the association between personalized nursing care and improved bowel function recovery time remained significant after adjustment (β = -2.31 h, 95% CI: -4.42 to -0.20, P = 0.032), whereas no significant independent associations were found for time to ambulation, time to first flatus, time to regular diet, nausea, vomiting, constipation, or diarrhea after multivariable adjustment (all P > 0.05). Table 9 summarizes the results for nutritional markers, dietary intake, and serum electrolytes. As shown in Table 9, after adjustment, personalized nursing care remained significantly associated with higher postoperative prealbumin levels (adjusted β = 29.1 mg/L, 95% CI: 9.5–48.7, P = 0.004) and higher serum protein levels (β = 3.9 g/L, 95% CI: 1.1–6.7, P = 0.007), while the associations with postoperative weight (β = 1.21 kg, 95% CI: -0.32 to 2.74, P = 0.121) and lymphocyte count (β = 0.19 × 10⁹/L, 95% CI: -0.05 to 0.43, P = 0.119) did not retain statistical significance after adjustment. For dietary intake, personalized nursing care independently predicted higher postoperative calorie intake (β = 142.3 kcal/day, 95% CI: 28.7–255.9, P = 0.014), protein intake (β = 8.7 g/day, 95% CI: 1.9–15.5, P = 0.012), fluid intake (β = 187.4 mL/day, 95% CI: 48.3–326.5, P = 0.008), and fiber intake (β = 2.4 g/day, 95% CI: 0.5–4.3, P = 0.014) after adjustment, whereas the associations with vitamin C, vitamin D, and iron intake did not retain statistical significance (all P > 0.05). For serum electrolytes, personalized nursing care remained significantly associated with higher serum calcium (β = 0.09 mmol/L, 95% CI: 0.02–0.16, P = 0.015), magnesium (β = 0.06 mmol/L, 95% CI: 0.01–0.11, P = 0.025), and phosphate levels (β = 0.12 mmol/L, 95% CI: 0.03–0.21, P = 0.010) after adjustment, while sodium and potassium showed no significant independent associations (both P > 0.05). These adjusted analyses confirm that the observed benefits of personalized nursing care are unlikely to be fully explained by temporal trends or measured confounders, supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer surgery presents a significant challenge to patients, who often experience postoperative nutritional deficits that can lead to increased morbidity and prolonged recovery [19–21]. In this study, we sought to evaluate the impact of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA on postoperative nutritional status in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.

One of the notable findings from this study was the significant improvement in bowel function recovery time in the personalized nursing care group compared to the routine nursing care group. This was a particularly important result, as it suggests that the implementation of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA may contribute to enhanced gastrointestinal function recovery following colorectal cancer surgery. Theoretically, this improvement in bowel function recovery time could be attributed to the tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients in the personalized care group, which aimed to address individualized nutritional needs based on the abPG-SGA assessment. Nutritional support tailored to the specific needs of patients, as guided by the abPG-SGA scale, may have contributed to improved gastrointestinal function recovery, potentially through the optimization of nutrient intake and support for digestive processes [22, 23]. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that bowel function recovery is multifactorial. Other perioperative factors, such as perioperative fluid management (e.g., restrictive or goal-directed fluid therapy) and the timing and compliance of early postoperative mobilization, may also independently or synergistically influence gastrointestinal recovery. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, these variables were not systematically captured. Future prospective studies are warranted to disentangle the relative contributions of nutritional support, fluid management, and mobilization protocols to bowel function recovery.

Furthermore, the study findings revealed a significant reduction in the incidence of certain postoperative complications, specifically wound infection and urinary tract infection, in the personalized nursing care group compared to the routine nursing care group. This was a significant result with important clinical implications, as it suggests that personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA may play a substantial role in reducing the occurrence of specific postoperative complications in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. The underlying mechanism for this reduction in postoperative complications may be multifactorial, with nutritional optimization likely playing a key role [24–26]. Nutritional status was known to influence immune function and the body’s ability to combat infections, and the tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients in the personalized care group may have contributed to bolstering immune function and reducing the likelihood of postoperative infections.

Moreover, the study findings indicated significant improvements in various nutritional markers and dietary intake parameters in the personalized nursing care group compared to the routine nursing care group. This includes improvements in postoperative weight, prealbumin levels, serum protein levels, lymphocyte count, calorie intake, protein intake, fluid intake, fiber intake, vitamin C intake, iron intake, and vitamin D intake. These findings collectively suggest that personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA have a positive impact on postoperative nutritional status and dietary intake in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. The tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients in the personalized care group, based on their abPG-SGA assessment, likely contributed to these improvements in nutritional parameters. Individualized nutritional support, addressing deficiencies and specific needs identified through the abPG-SGA assessment, may have facilitated the optimization of postoperative nutritional status and dietary intake, thereby positively impacting patients’ recovery and overall well-being [27].

Additionally, the study findings demonstrated significant differences in serum electrolyte levels one week postoperatively between the routine nursing care group and the personalized care plans group, particularly in calcium, magnesium, and phosphate levels. The observed differences in serum electrolyte levels suggest that personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA may contribute to the maintenance of specific electrolyte balances following colorectal cancer surgery. The tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients in the personalized care group may have played a role in supporting electrolyte balance and homeostasis, potentially through the optimization of nutrient intake and support for metabolic processes involved in electrolyte regulation.

Furthermore, the study results indicated a reduction in the length of hospital stay and improved postoperative recovery parameters, such as abdominal cavity drainage time, gastric tube placement time, and exhaust time, in the personalized nursing care group compared to the routine nursing care group. These findings underscore the potential impact of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA on enhancing postoperative recovery and expediting hospital discharge following colorectal cancer surgery. The tailored nutritional interventions, as guided by the abPG-SGA assessment, may have contributed to the improved postoperative recovery parameters and reduced length of hospital stay through the optimization of patients’ nutritional status and overall well-being [28].

The study also demonstrated that the modified abPG-SGA effectively stratified patients into distinct nutritional risk categories, which guided the intensity of nutritional interventions. Notably, the majority of patients classified as moderate or high risk (Grades B and C) received intensive nutritional support, and a high proportion showed positive clinical responses, particularly in the highest risk group. This suggests that the modified abPG-SGA not only identifies nutritional risk but also predicts responsiveness to tailored nutritional therapy, enhancing its utility in clinical decision-making. These findings underscore the importance of risk-stratified approaches in optimizing nutritional care and improving postoperative outcomes.

Compared with previous studies that utilized single nutritional assessment tools such as the PG-SGA, NRS2002, or MNA to guide nutritional interventions in cancer patients, our modified abPG-SGA composite scoring system offers a multidimensional approach that integrates body composition, nutritional risk screening, and geriatric-sensitive indicators. For example, while the abPG-SGA has been validated as an effective malnutrition screening tool in oncology populations, it relies heavily on patient-reported outcomes and lacks objective physiological parameters [11]. Similarly, the NRS2002 effectively captures nutritional risk based on disease severity and reduced intake but does not incorporate body composition or functional measures [29]. The MNA, although sensitive in elderly and hospitalized patients, is not specifically designed for the perioperative cancer setting [30, 31]. By contrast, our composite system enables a more nuanced risk stratification (Grades A, B, and C) that directly informs stepped-care nutritional support, ranging from routine dietary guidance to intensive parenteral nutrition. This integration of multidimensional assessment with intervention allocation represents a practical advancement in postoperative nutritional management. Nevertheless, the methodological simplification of direct score summation without statistical weighting warrants further refinement in future studies.

The potential benefits of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA in improving postoperative nutritional status and recovery outcomes in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery can be attributed to several underlying mechanisms and reasons. The observed improvements in gastrointestinal function recovery, reduced postoperative complications, enhanced nutritional markers and dietary intake, maintenance of electrolyte balances, and expedited postoperative recovery parameters provide compelling evidence for the efficacy of personalized nursing care plans. One major reason for these potential benefits was the individualized approach to nutritional assessment and intervention facilitated by the modified abPG-SGA scale. This scale enables a thorough evaluation of patients’ nutritional status by considering variables such as weight loss, dietary intake, gastrointestinal symptoms, functional capacity, and metabolic stress [11, 32]. The personalized assessment identifies specific nutritional deficiencies, challenges, and requirements unique to each patient. Tailoring nutritional interventions based on the individualized abPG-SGA assessment allows personalized nursing care plans to address specific patient needs, potentially leading to improved nutritional status and enhanced recovery outcomes. Furthermore, personalized nursing care plans may optimize nutrient intake and support digestive processes by targeting deficiencies in macronutrients, micronutrients, and fluids crucial for postoperative recovery [33, 34]. Addressing specific nutritional needs identified through the abPG-SGA assessment can facilitate the optimization of nutrient intake, supporting metabolic processes, immune function, tissue repair, and gastrointestinal function recovery. This may contribute to the observed improvements in postoperative nutritional status, reduced complications, and expedited recovery parameters. Additionally, the reduction in postoperative complications and maintenance of electrolyte balances may be linked to the impact of personalized nursing care plans on immune function and metabolic homeostasis. Nutritional status plays a critical role in immune function, and personalized nutritional support based on the modified abPG-SGA assessment may help bolster immune responses and reduce the risk of postoperative infections and complications [35, 36]. Moreover, the tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients may contribute to maintaining electrolyte balances, supporting metabolic processes and physiological homeostasis following colorectal cancer surgery. These mechanisms could underlie the observed reduction in postoperative complications and the positive impact on electrolyte levels in patients receiving personalized nursing care plans. Furthermore, the expedited postoperative recovery parameters, including reduced hospital stay and improved recovery markers, may be attributed to the comprehensive nature of personalized nursing care plans. By addressing not only nutritional needs but also factors such as functional capacity, metabolic stress, and gastrointestinal symptoms, personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA can support the holistic recovery of patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. The individualized approach may facilitate earlier mobilization, faster resolution of postoperative complications, and improved functional recovery, ultimately contributing to the observed expedited recovery parameters [37, 38]. In conclusion, the potential benefits of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA in improving postoperative nutritional status and recovery outcomes in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery were supported by underlying mechanisms related to individualized nutritional assessment and intervention, optimization of nutrient intake and support for digestive processes, impact on immune function and metabolic homeostasis, and comprehensive support for holistic recovery. These reasons provide a theoretical framework for understanding the observed improvements associated with personalized nursing care plans in this clinical context.

While the findings of this study provide valuable insights into the potential benefits of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA in improving postoperative nutritional status in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery, it was important to acknowledge certain limitations inherent in the study. Firstly, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a broader patient population. Additionally, the sample size of the study was relatively small, and the duration of follow-up was limited. Notably, due to its retrospective design, no prospective sample size calculation was performed, which constitutes a methodological limitation. Future prospective studies are recommended to conduct sample size estimation based on the minimal clinically important difference of core nutritional outcome indicators such as serum prealbumin levels or body weight changes, to further strengthen the robustness of their conclusions. A larger, multicenter study with an extended follow-up period would be beneficial to further elucidate the long-term impact of personalized nursing care plans on postoperative outcomes in this patient population. Furthermore, the study design was non-randomized, which introduces the potential for selection bias. Importantly, the non-randomized, retrospective before-after design based on a temporal change in clinical practice may introduce unmeasured confounding. Specifically, improvements in postoperative outcomes observed in the personalized care group (treated later, from September to December 2023) may partially reflect a “period effect” — concurrent enhancements in perioperative care protocols, increased team experience with colorectal cancer surgeries, refinements in discharge planning and postoperative follow-up processes, or broader institutional quality improvement initiatives — rather than the personalized nutritional intervention alone. Factors such as temporal trends in perioperative care or changes in nursing expertise over time could have contributed to the observed differences. Therefore, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded, and future randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm the causal effect of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA on postoperative nutritional outcomes. Future research employing a randomized controlled trial design would enhance the strength of evidence regarding the impact of personalized nursing care plans on postoperative nutritional status. Moreover, although key demographic and clinical characteristics were balanced between groups, we did not compare the preoperative nutritional status of patients using a standardized tool prior to intervention. Future studies should ensure the assessment and reporting of preoperative nutritional status to strengthen the causal inference regarding the effect of nutritional interventions. Additionally, the retrospective nature of this study precluded systematic collection of certain perioperative factors that may influence postoperative recovery and nutritional outcomes, including detailed anesthesia type (inhalational vs. intravenous), use of regional analgesia, central venous catheter placement, adherence to individual components of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, and specific surgical procedure types (e.g., primary anastomosis vs. stoma formation, Hartmann procedure). Due to the limitations of the original medical record data, these variables were not systematically documented, and therefore between-group comparisons could not be performed. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized documentation of these key perioperative indicators to better control for confounders and enhance the reliability of conclusions. Furthermore, although arm muscle circumference was used as a feasible marker for muscle mass assessment under retrospective conditions, future prospective studies should consider more precise methods, such as handgrip strength testing or CT-based measurement of skeletal muscle area at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) level, to more comprehensively evaluate the impact of sarcopenia on postoperative nutritional status. Fourth, the construction of the modified abPG-SGA composite tool requires critical examination. It should be acknowledged that the modified abPG-SGA composite tool was internally developed for this study and has not been externally validated. Further validation in future prospective, multi-center studies is required. The direct summation of scores from instruments with disparate scales (e.g., 0–3 vs. 0–14) without formal weighting is a methodological simplification. This approach may underscore the contribution of components with narrower score ranges (e.g., NRS2002) to the overall risk assessment. Future research should explore and validate more statistically rigorous methods of integrating multi-component nutritional assessments, such as using standardized Z-scores, employing machine learning-derived weights, or developing risk prediction models that optimally combine these variables. Additionally, given the large number of outcomes analyzed in this study, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. As such, the reported P-values are descriptive and the analyses should be considered exploratory, with an associated risk of type I error. Future confirmatory studies with pre-specified primary endpoints and appropriate multiplicity correction are needed to validate our findings. Lastly, while efforts were made to standardize the delivery of nursing care interventions, variations in individual patient responses and adherence to the care plans could have influenced the outcomes. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of personalized nursing care plans in optimizing postoperative recovery and nutritional status in colorectal cancer patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study support the potential benefit of personalized nursing care plans based on the modified abPG-SGA in improving postoperative nutritional status and recovery outcomes in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery. The observed improvements in gastrointestinal function recovery, reduction in postoperative complications, enhancements in nutritional markers and dietary intake, maintenance of electrolyte balances, and expedited postoperative recovery parameters provide compelling evidence for the efficacy of personalized nursing care plans in the context of colorectal cancer surgery. The tailored nutritional interventions provided to patients based on their abPG-SGA assessment likely underlie these positive outcomes, highlighting the importance of individualized nutritional support in optimizing postoperative recovery and overall well-being. Further research and prospective studies were warranted to confirm these findings and elucidate the long-term impact of personalized nursing care plans on postoperative outcomes in colorectal cancer patients.
