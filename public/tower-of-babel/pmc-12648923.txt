Association between attachment loss and root caries in middle-aged and elderly people in Guizhou Province, China
Authors: Liangsa Zhang, Lin Yang, Taiming Dai, Jiangling Sun, Juanjuan Wu, Liming Chen, Xinyue Chen, Arsy Huda Fathaniard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the relationship between clinical attachment loss (CAL) and its related factors and root caries in middle-aged and elderly people in Guizhou, China, and to provide a theoretical basis for the prevention and treatment of root caries in middle-aged and elderly people.MethodsThe study included a sample of 3960 people aged 35–44 and 65–74 years who underwent oral examinations and questionnaires. The association between CAL and root surface caries was analysed using logistic regression models, adjusting for socio-demographic background, general health behaviour, oral health behaviour, presence of chronic diseases and crown caries. After adjusting for all variables, subgroup analyses were performed by age and sex.ResultsThe prevalence of root caries in the sample population was 22.51% in the age group of 35–44 years and 74.63% in the age group of 65–74 years. CAL ≥ 6 mm was positively associate with root surface caries (OR: 1.99, 95% CI: 1.50–2.63). The caries prevalence rates corresponding to CAL from low to high were 28.67%, 58.22% and 75.63% respectively, and this significance was maintained in the regression analysis model. Increasing age was positively associated with the development of root caries (OR: 4.88, 95% CI: 3.86–6.17). Economic area, years of education, dental cleaning within one year and crown caries were influential factors for root caries.ConclusionsThe prevalence of root caries is high among the elderly in Guizhou, China, and attachment loss is associated with a higher incidence of root caries, so health education on oral caries and loss of periodontal attachment knowledge should be actively carried out among those with high attachment loss to improve their oral hygiene.Keywords: Middle-aged and elderly people, Loss of attachment, Root caries, Influencing factors

Background

Caries and periodontal disease are the most prevalent and severe oral diseases worldwide[1]. Globally, 2.3 billion people are affected by dental caries[2], and periodontal disease is one of the top three oral diseases recommended by the World Health Organisation (WHO)[3]. The high prevalence of these two diseases can lead to a serious oral disease burden[2].

A systematic review showed that periodontitis is associated with root caries and not with crown caries[4]. Adults with periodontal disease are 38% more likely to have caries in the dental roots compared to adults without periodontal disease[5]. Root caries vary widely among age groups but are particularly common in the elderly[6]. According to the analysis of the results of the Fourth National Oral Health Survey in China, the prevalence of root caries in Chinese adults increased substantially from 35 to 44 to 65–74 years of age, and the prevalence rate (number of decayed and filled tooth roots, DFR ≥ 1) increased from 25.4% to 61.9%[6]. This suggests that increasing age may aggravate the increased prevalence of root caries in patients with periodontal disease[7].

Due to differences in research methodology and sample selection, there is no conclusive evidence on the association between the two diseases as of now. Some studies have revealed a negative association between periodontal disease and dental caries[8], In contrast, others have found a strong positive association between the two[9–11], and few studies have pointed out that there is no link between the two diseases[12]. Severe periodontitis is usually accompanied by attachment loss[13], and different CAL thresholds can impact the prevalence of periodontitis in investigations[14]. Indeed, loss of attachment due to periodontitis may lead to an increase in the surface area of the tooth exposed to the oral cavity[15], thereby increasing the risk of developing root caries[10, 16]. This implies that loss of attachment may be an essential factor contributing to the occurrence of root caries. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between the two.

Fewer large-sample studies on the association between the two diseases have been conducted in the Chinese population[17], and more clinical data are needed for in-depth studies. Guizhou province is located in the less developed area of western China[18], which is a typical karst landscape with a humid and rainy climate. In addition, Guizhou province has a large proportion of ethnic minorities, with a total of 49 ethnic minorities, many of whom have different lifestyles from other ethnic groups[19], such as preferring acidic foods and cured meat[20]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the association between CAL and its related factors and root surface caries using a sample of middle-aged and older adults from Guizhou, China, by selecting relevant variables based on the framework of oral health determinants proposed by the International Collaborative Study of Oral Health Outcomes (ICS - II)[21]. We hypothesised that higher levels of attachment loss are associated with a higher prevalence of root caries in middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Study population

According to the age survey group recommended by the World Health Organization, the subjects of this study were the middle-aged group of 35–44 years old and the elderly group of 65–74 years old[22]. Multistage stratified, cluster random sampling method was used, according to the detection rate of periodontal disease of 90.9% in the group of 35–44 years old, and 90.7% in the group of 65–74 years old, with a relative error control of 10%, and a no-response rate of 10%, and the efficiency of the sampling design was 4.5. In the first stage: incorporating economic stratification factors, the 88 counties (districts) of Guizhou Province were categorised into three groups based on the province’s published minimum wage standards. Subsequently, 11 counties (districts) were randomly selected from these groups, taking geographical distribution into full consideration. In the second stage: A list of all townships (subdistricts) within the selected counties (districts) and their respective populations was compiled. Using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, six village (neighbourhood) committees were randomly selected from this list. In the third stage: Employing quota sampling, 30 residents aged 35–44 and 30 residents aged 65–74 were surveyed in each (residential) village committee, maintaining equal representation of males and females. The age distribution was kept balanced to prevent significant age bias. Within sampled neighbourhood committees, the survey begins with ground-floor residents of the building nearest to the committee office, proceeding door-to-door. During the survey, basic information was recorded for residents aged 35–44 and 65–74 in each household. The termination criterion was reaching the required sample size–30 individuals per age group within each village/neighbourhood committee, comprising 15 males and 15 females. Appointment scheduling was completed concurrently with household registration. Based on the sample size formula , the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 143 for middle-aged group and 145 for the elderly group. To strengthen the reliability of our results, we expanded the sample size to improve statistical power and accommodate possible non-responses or missing data. A total of 4063 individuals were randomly selected and 103 were excluded from the loss of attachment exclusion, resulting in 3960 individuals being included in the study. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects before the start of the project. This study was reviewed and agreed by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guiyang Stomatological Hospital on May 24, 2019 (approval number: GYSKLL-KY-20190524-01).

Methods of investigation

Oral examination

The oral examination was performed according to the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey procedures and the methods and standards of the Basic Methods for Oral Health Surveys (5th edition) published by the World Health Organization[22, 23]. Examinations were carried out in the field by uniformly trained dental practitioners who had completed standardized calibration to ensure consistency. Survey sites were centrally located within each grassroots survey unit—such as community centers or village committees—to facilitate participant access. Each site was organized into three functional areas: a registration area (where questionnaires and examination forms were collected), a questionnaire interview area, and a clinical examination area. Examinations were conducted indoors under appropriate lighting conditions (natural or portable artificial light), and site staff were assigned to maintain order and minimize disruptions. The oral examination was based on visual inspection combined with gentle probing using a flat mouth mirror and a Community Periodontal Index (CPI) probe. A total of 32 teeth were examined for each participant. The assessment covered dental conditions (including crown caries and root caries) and periodontal health indicators (such as attachment loss). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the examination.

Questionnaire survey

The questionnaire was developed based on the guidelines of Oral Health Surveys: Basic Methods (5th edition, 2013) by the World Health Organization and the China’s Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey. And it has been previously published in the Oral Health Epidemiological Survey Report in Guizhou Province (2019–2020)[22–24]. As part of its original development, The relevant constructs and their measurements were selected after extensive discussions among Chinese experts in public health, medical statistics and dentistry. This collaborative process ensured the validity and applicability of the constructs assessed in the study. Chinese public health experts, medical statisticians, and stomatologists selected the items and measures through extensive discussions, a collaborative process that ensured the relevance and appropriateness of the constructs assessed in the study. One-on-one and face-to-face interviews were used to conduct the survey, and the questionnaire focused on oral health problems and oral health service utilisation among middle-aged and elderly people.

Quality control

The survey team comprised one technical supervisor, six oral examiners, six recorders, two questionnaire administrators, two registration clerks, and two local coordinators responsible for logistics. The six oral examiners were experienced dentists with over three years of clinical practice, who completed both theoretical and practical training prior to the survey. To qualify for participation, examiners were required to achieve a minimum Kappa value of 0.6 in preliminary calibration tests. During the survey, the examiners demonstrated strong reliability in assessing periodontal pocket depth, with Kappa values ranging from 0.61 to 1.00.[24] Furthermore, examiners engaged in consistency evaluations alongside core examiners from China’s Fourth National Oral Health Survey to ensure uniformity in diagnostic criteria.

Variables

Outcome variable: root caries

The presence or absence of root surface caries in the test subjects was the only outcome variable in this study, and was defined as the caries status based on root caries and the number of fillings, recorded as “yes” or “no”. The presence of caries in the roots was determined after oral examination by uniformly trained examiners, and the Kappa value test for caries status examination was performed between all examiners (including standard examiners), Ten to fifteen respondents were selected and the respondents were given one oral examination each by the examiner and a standard examiner, and then the results of each examiner were compared with the standard examiner on the basis of the same dentition, and a Kappa value of caries status of 0.8 or more was considered to be fully reliable. The Kappa values for all examiners were calculated to be 0.81–1.00.81.00, with good inter-examiner agreement[25].

Exposure variable: CAL

The highest CAL score was used as an indicator to assess the severity of periodontal disease in individuals. The severity of periodontal disease was classified into the following three groups based on the CAL score: 0–3 mm, 4–5 mm and ≥ 6 mm.

Other independent variables

Drawing on previous research, it was decided to include the following variables to adjust the regression model[26, 27]. Socio-demographic background, such as age(Classified as “35–44 years” and “65–74 years”), sex, ethnicity(“Han ethnic” and “ethnic minorities”), economic area (classified by the government into three categories of areas according to the minimum wage: The Category 1 area has the highest minimum wage, The Category 2 area has the second highest, and Category 3 area has the lowest), and years of education (classified according to the level of education received; ≤9 years, minimum compulsory education; 10–15 years, upper secondary education and secondary vocational education; and ≥ 16 years, access to tertiary education); general health behaviours, e.g. years of smoking and liquor consumption. Oral health behaviours, e.g. dental visits in a year, dental cleaning services in a year, frequency of consumption of desserts, frequency of cleaning teeth with toothpicks or floss, frequency of toothbrushing. As well as having a chronic disease (self-reporting, categorised as “don’t have/don’t know” or “yes”) and caries in the crowns. All variables are categorical.

This study employed the variance inflation factor (VIF) to detect icollinearity among independent variables. A VIF exceeding 10 is generally considered indicative of severe collinearity. In this research, all independent variables exhibited VIF values below 5 (range:1.005–2.022), suggesting that multicollinearity did not compromise the stability of the model results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 29.0 statistical analysis software, chi-square test based on the characteristics of the independent variables, and weighted statistics to adjust for potential bias introduced by the complex sampling design. Logistic regression was used to analyse the relationship between CAL and root surface caries. The significance level α was set at 0.05, and the association was expressed in the ratio of ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three logistic regression models were constructed to measure the effect of CAL on the caries status of dental roots. CAL was used as the only independent variable in the model 1, socio-demographic factors were added in the model 2, and general health behaviour, oral health behaviour, having a chronic disease, and caries in the crowns continued to be added in the model 3. After adjusting for all variables, subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex were performed.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

A total of 2040 adults aged 35–44 years and 2023 adults aged 65–74 years completed the survey. Excluding subjects with attachment loss exclusions from oral examinations, 3960 subjects were ultimately included in the analysis (2037 in the 35–44 year-old group and 1923 in the 65–74 year-old group). The overall root caries prevalence in the sample population was 47.53%, with the highest prevalence of CAL ≥ 6 mm (75.63%), followed by CAL = 4–5 mm (58.22%), and the lowest prevalence of CAL = 0–3 mm (28.67%). In addition, there were statistically significant differences in the variables of age, sex, economic area, educational attainment, years of smoking, chronic diseases, dental cleaning within one year, use of toothpicks or floss, frequency of brushing, and caries in the crowns (Table 1).

Association of other independent variables with root surface caries

In the model 3, the prevalence of root caries was significantly higher in the older age group (4.88 times). Females had a higher prevalence of root caries. Category 3 area had a higher prevalence of caries than Category 1 area. The prevalence of root caries was higher in those with less than 10 years of education than in those with good education (≥ 16 years). Root caries were higher among those without a dental cleaning within one year. The prevalence of root caries was significantly higher (826.44 times) in those with crown caries (Table 2).

Discussion

The high prevalence of caries remains a severe public health challenge. China is probably one of the top ten countries with the highest demand for standardised treatment related to oral diseases[2], and caries, as one of the most common oral diseases, requires a multifaceted approach to study its influencing factors. As reported in earlier studies[9, 10, 28], there is an association between periodontal disease and the occurrence of root caries. CAL is an important clinical parameter indicating the progression of periodontal disease. In the present study, we used regression modeling to reveal its association with root surface caries. Also, we found that older age, lower salary level and years of education, not receiving dental cleaning services within one year, and caries in crowns were the main factors for the occurrence of root surface caries, which is significant. Of course, comparisons must be made cautiously due to the differences in the subjects included in different studies and the criteria for caries classification.

The prevalence of caries was significantly higher in subjects with CAL ≥ 6 mm than in the other two classifications. This trend was consistent in both age and sex subgroup analyses, the increased prevalence was not only seen in middle-aged 35–44-year-olds (OR: 2.64, 95% CI: 1.38–5.04) and 65–74-year-olds (OR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.10–2.16), but also in males (OR: 1.99, 95% CI: 1.37–2.90) and females (OR: 1.93, 95% CI: 1.28–2.92). Other studies have looked for an association between periodontal disease and root caries. AlQobaly et al. defined a CAL ≥ 4 mm as having periodontal disease and found that the prevalence of root caries was higher in those with periodontal disease (OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.16–1.30)[28]; Martínez et al. used the Spanish working population and found that the roots of the examined teeth in those with CAL ≥ 6 mm caries and fillings (DFR) indices were significantly higher[29]. This association can be explained from several perspectives; periodontal disease and untreated caries often coexist in the same patient [30], and untreated caries promote plaque accumulation, with the latter being closely associated with its progression as an initiator of periodontal disease[9]. With periodontal attachment loss, the root surface is left with only weak dentin and cementum, which is more susceptible to damage than a crown covered with hard enamel.

Generally, age is a significant influencing factor for root caries[7]. In our study, age was positively associated with root caries occurrence. In the model 1, the prevalence of root caries was significantly higher in older adults aged 65–74 years than in middle-aged adults aged 35–44 years (OR: 5.87, 95% CI: 4.73–7.26), and this prevalence was slightly reduced after adjusting for variables. As previously described, increasing age aggravates the chance of developing root caries in patients with periodontitis. Changes in immune function are a plausible explanation for this result, as there is evidence of changes in immune function with age, which may be associated with increased susceptibility to caries and periodontitis[10]. Stratified analysis by age revealed that at the same level of CAL, middle-aged adults had a higher prevalence of root caries than older adults, (35–44 years: OR = 2.64, 65–74 years: OR = 1.54). This is similar to the findings of Yu et al. (35–44 years: OR = 1.40, 65–74 years: OR = 1.28)[17]. The higher OR in this study may be due to the selection of study population and different classification of root caries (caries were classified as crown, mixed and root caries in Yu et al.‘s study). Differences in caries prevalence by age and by CAL level could explain this phenomenon, the results of the present study showed that the caries prevalence in middle-aged people aged 35–44 years was much lower than that in older people aged 65–74 years (22.51% versus 74.63%), When periodontal disease was present (increased CAL), the caries prevalence was increased substantially in comparison with the normal state, which led to a higher prevalence of caries than that of the older people at the same level of CAL.

Sex also affects the prevalence of root caries, and in this study, overall, females had a slightly higher caries rate than males. Still, based on the findings of existing studies, it seems that adult males are more likely to suffer from root surface caries[26]. However, some studies have found the opposite[31], which may be related to the characteristics of different populations, and the exact reasons for this need to be carried out in a more in-depth study. The results of stratified analyses by sex showed that the increased prevalence of caries in the roots from CAL = 4–5 mm to CAL ≥ 6 mm was higher in males than in females, suggesting that periodontal status deterioration has a greater impact on caries in males. This may be related to a different propensity for dental visits, as women seem to seek dental treatment more often[32]. Oral hygiene practices are worse in males and severe attachment loss tends to occur in males[33], whose gingival recession results in root exposure, thus favouring the development of root surface caries; the sex difference was significant in the model 2, but this significance disappeared in the model 3 as the sex difference was affected by more variables in the model 3, which may have weakened its significance.

The prevalence of root surface caries in middle-aged and elderly people is influenced by multiple factors. Notably, the prevalence of root caries among the elderly in Guizhou, China, was 74.6%, significantly higher than the national average of 61.9%. This may be related to the fact that Guizhou is not economically developed, with low penetration of oral health services[18], consumption of sour and spicy foods by local residents and long-standing oral health problems such as dental fluorosis[20]. However, the prevalence of root caries in middle-aged people was 22.5%, slightly lower than the national average of 25.4%. A reasonable explanation for this is that as the level of economic development in Guizhou increases, the literacy level of the population continues to improve, and the level of oral health literacy also improves[34]. High educational attainment (≥ 16 years of education) is considered to be associated with a lower prevalence of root caries in this study, which is mainly concentrated in the middle-aged group. There were a large number of missing values in the data on subjects’ income, and the variable “economic area”, which reflects the wage level in the area where the participant lives, was included in the analysis. When there is a significant difference in wage levels (i.e., Category 1 area compared to Category 3 area), there may be a difference in access to oral health care services and a significant difference in root caries prevalence. Root caries status was significantly better in subjects who had a dental cleaning within the past year, as past dental visits increased the likelihood of adopting oral health-related behaviours[35]. Root caries tend to occur concurrently with crown caries[26], therefore, the incidence of root caries in subjects with caries in the crowns was much higher than in those without caries in the crowns. In addition, no association has been found for the time being between root surface caries and smoking or the presence of chronic diseases. Dental cleaning with toothbrushes, toothpicks and dental floss were not found to be clearly associated with root caries development, and the inclusion of middle-aged and older adults together in the analysis may have influenced this result[36], as good oral hygiene practices (frequency of brushing) were found to be inconsistent in maintaining good oral health in a study of men under 45 years of age[37].

Based on the results of the analysis, the following recommendations can be made: first, when developing caries intervention strategies, attention should be paid to the factor of CAL, and graded interventions should be implemented according to the level of CAL, and more active interventions are needed for patients with a CAL ≥ 6 mm; second, attention should be paid to the oral hygiene status of the elderly, low-education, and low-income earners, and health education on periodontitis and root planar caries should be actively carried out to promote their health literacy, such as mastering the correct brushing method, regular oral examination, timely cleaning, and so on. The second is to pay attention to the oral hygiene condition of the elderly, low-level education, and low-income people, and actively carry out health education on loss of attachment and root surface caries to improve their health literacy, such as mastering the correct method of brushing teeth, regular oral examination, and timely cleaning.

There are inevitable limitations to this study. Firstly, given the cross-sectional study design used in this study, it was impossible to fully capture the potential time-lagged effects that would establish a causal relationship between the severity of loss of attachment and root caries. Secondly, the findings are only representative of the population in Guizhou, China, and cannot be generalised to the broader area; finally, the population income was not included in the analysis in this study, which, as expressed by the World Health Organization Commission on Social Determinants of Health, may lead to the omission of key information.

Conclusions

We found that CAL levels and their associated factors were related to the prevalence of root surface caries in middle-aged and elderly people in Guizhou, China. This study suggests a potential role of CAL in root caries intervention. Therefore, relevant authorities should develop effective intervention strategies based on the characteristics of patients’ CAL and actively conduct health education on oral caries and attachment loss knowledge to control the prevalence of root caries.
