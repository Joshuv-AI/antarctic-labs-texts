Filtering and Statistical Properties of Unimodal Maps Perturbed by Heteroscedastic Noises
Fabrizio Lillo, Stefano Marmi, Matteo Tanzi, Sandro Vaienti

Abstract
We propose a theory of unimodal maps perturbed by an heteroscedastic Markov chain noise and experiencing another heteroscedastic noise due to uncertain observation. We address and treat the filtering problem showing that by collecting more and more observations, one would predict the same distribution for the state of the underlying Markov chain no matter one’s initial guess. Moreover we give other limit theorems, emphasizing in particular concentration inequalities and extreme value and Poisson distributions. Our results apply to a family of maps arising from a model of systemic risk in finance.

Introduction

The measurement of any quantity is plagued by an observational error that prevents knowing with infinite precision the true value of the variable of interest, which therefore remains latent. In some fields, as for example climate or finance, this measurement error can be very large and the observational error distribution, including its bias and variance, are unknown and not constant in time. In time series analysis, the procedure to obtain an estimate of the true latent process and to provide accurate predictions in the presence of observational noise is termed
filtering
. The seminal Kalman filter provides an optimal filtering procedure when the dynamics of the latent process is linear and the noise driving the latent dynamics and the observational noise are Gaussian distributed.
In many systems, however, the dynamic is strongly non-linear and therefore approximated methods, such as the extended Kalman filter or the particle filter approach, provide solutions that can be used in practice.
From the theoretical perspective, however, the properties of filtering of highly non-linear dynamics are still not fully characterized. In particular, we are interested here in unimodal maps perturbed by an heteroscedastic

It is in fact expected that any observations will have a small uncertainty to the actual state of the Markov chain. 3.2
Filtering via Random Cones

In this subsection we are going to prove that under suitable conditions, the filtering problem defined by the equations (

This corresponds to well-posedeness of the filtering problem; in words, it means that by collecting more and more observations, one would predict the same distribution for the state of the underlying Markov chain no matter one’s initial guess.

To do so, we first express the evolution in equations (

)-(
21

3.2.2
Cone Construction and Equivariant Measure

Now we show that under some conditions on the transition kernel of the Markov chain and on the observational noise, the system admits an equivariant measure. Before that, let’s gather some useful facts about the cones we are going to use.

Cones of positive functions

For

, we define

Let

be the cone of integrable nonnegative functions defined on

Let’s call

the intrinsic Hilbert metric on

. Notice that this cone has the characteristic that, not only its diameter with respect to the Hilbert metric is infinite, but some densities are at infinite distance from each other. Let’s recall the definition of the Hilbert metric on a cone, and write an explicit expression for

on

. First of all, for any
\phi_{1}
,

Then

\Theta_{0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=\log[\beta_{0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\beta_{0}(\phi_{2},\phi_{1})].

Notice that if there is

such that
\phi_{1}(x)=0
, but
\phi_{2}(x)>0

\{t>0:\,t\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}\}

is nonempty, then since there must be

\phi_{1}(x)\neq 0

\beta_{0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})>0
. We can summarize this remark by saying that for every

\beta_{0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\in(0,+\infty]

which implies
\Theta_{0}:\mathcal{V}_{0}\times\mathcal{V}_{0}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]

is a Hilbert metric that can take values on the extended positive semiaxis.

The following result is standard in the case of cones with finite Hilbert metrics, we double check that it still holds for the extended metric on the cone
\mathcal{V}_{0}

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

\Theta_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L}\phi_{1},\mathcal{L}\phi_{2})\leq\Theta_{\mathcal{V}}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

Proof.

\{t>0:\,t\mathcal{L}\phi_{1}-\mathcal{L}\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\}\supset\{t>0:\,t\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{V}\}

and therefore

5
Concentration inequalities

We now give another large deviation result provided by a concentration inequality for our process, which is an interesting general result in itself. We continue to consider
Z_{t}
as the sum of a Markov chain
X_{t}
plus the noise
s(X_{t})\epsilon_{t}
as we did in the filtering section.
For a function

which is separately Lipschitz in each variable

with Lipschitz constant

we need to estimate

\mathfrak{C}_{n}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}\left(e^{K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n}))}\right)

and
\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}.
Following a suggestion in
[

\mathfrak{C}_{n}=\int d\mathbb{P}e^{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n}))}e^{-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n}))}

\cdot\int d\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}e^{K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s(X_{1})\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s(X_{n})\epsilon_{n}))}

Assumption CI.
We now suppose that

Since the variables

On the other hand in the first integral in

we recognize the concentration integral of the separately Lipschitz function

with

Then we use the fact that an exponential concentration inequality holds for Markov chain with exponential convergence to the (unique) stationary measure

see, for instance,
[

such that

\int d\mathbb{P}e^{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s_{M}\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s_{M}\epsilon_{n}))}e^{-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s_{M}\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s_{M}\epsilon_{n}))}\leq e^{C_{X}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\text{Lip}_{j}(K)^{2}}.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let us suppose that our Markov systems verifies

and the modulation term satisfies Assumption

\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}\left(|K(X_{1}+s_{M}\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s_{M}\epsilon_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{P}_{\epsilon}}(K(X_{1}+s_{M}\epsilon_{1},\dots,X_{n}+s_{M}\epsilon_{n}))|>t\right)\leq

2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{4\sum_{j=1}^{n}Lip_{j}(K)^{2}[C_{\epsilon}s_{M}^{2}+C_{X}]}\right).

Application: deviation of the empirical measure.

At this regard,

(27)

Suppose that

is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T^{k}_{\overline{\eta}}x+s(T^{k}_{\overline{\eta}}x)\epsilon_{k})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T^{k}_{\overline{\eta}}x)\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}C\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|s(T^{k}_{\overline{\eta}}x)\epsilon_{k}|.

11

and in a suitable renormalized interval of time. We will argue that such a distribution is a standard Poisson.

The starting point is therefore to compute the following distribution

\rho_{Z}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t^{\prime}}1_{B_{t}}(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}x+s(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}x)\epsilon_{i})=k\right),

(35)

where
t^{\prime}
comes from the scaling (
45
), see also the similar quantity
\Delta^{\prime}_{t}
defined below, and is given by

t^{\prime}:=\left\lfloor{\frac{\tau}{\int\psi((B_{t}-x)/s(x))d\mu(x)}}\right\rfloor.

(36)

Let us call
S_{t^{\prime}}
the quantity inside the parenthesis in (
35
), so we will compute the distribution, for
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},
of

W_{k}:=\rho_{Z}(S_{t^{\prime}}=k).

In particular
we need to compute the characteristic function
\Phi(S_{t}^{\prime})
given by

\Phi_{S_{t^{\prime}}}(\lambda)=\int e^{i\lambda S_{t^{\prime}}}d\rho_{Z}.

Let us now introduce a new operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
defined as, on functions
f
belonging to our Banach space of bounded variation functions:

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(f)(x)=\mathcal{L}(f\int e^{i\lambda(1_{B_{t}}(x+s(x)\epsilon)}d\psi(\epsilon))(x).

Notice again that this operator is a small perturbation of
\mathcal{L}
and therefore we could proceed with the perturbative technique as before. It is now stratightforward to check that

\Phi_{S_{t^{\prime}}}(\lambda)=\int\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{t^{\prime}}(f)(x)d\mu(x).

The top eigenvalue
\upsilon
of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
differs from
1,
which is the top eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
by

1-\upsilon=\l(\lambda)\Delta^{\prime}_{t}+o(\Delta^{\prime}),

where
\Delta^{\prime}_{t}
denotes as before the distance between the two operators and is given by

\Delta_{t}^{\prime}:=\int(\mathcal{L}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}})hdx=(1-e^{i\lambda})\int\psi((B_{t}-x)s(x)^{-1})d\mu(x)=(1-e^{i\lambda})\Delta_{t};

moreover

l(\lambda)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{\prime}_{k}(\lambda),

and

q^{\prime}_{k}(\lambda):=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int(\mathcal{L}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}})\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{k}(\mathcal{L}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}})(h)dx}{\Delta^{\prime}_{t}},

provided the limit exists. In such a case,
since
\Phi_{S_{t^{\prime}}}(\lambda)
grows like
\upsilon^{t^{\prime}},
by the scaling (
36
) on
t^{\prime}
we finally have:

\Phi_{S_{t^{\prime}}}(\lambda)\rightarrow e^{-l(\lambda)(1-e^{i\lambda})\tau},\ t\rightarrow\infty.

Supposing that
l(\lambda)
is continuous at
0,
we see that the right hand side of the previous equation is the characteristic function of a random variable
W
which we could identify with the limit, in distribution, of
W_{k}.
It turns out that
W
is discrete and infinite divisible, so that it is
compound Poisson distributed.
By the same argument used in the preceding section, we could prove that when
s=constant
then all the
q^{\prime}_{k}(\lambda)=0
and therefore

l(\lambda)=1.
We have therefore proved:

Proposition 8.1
.

Let us suppose that our Markov system verifies
Assumption TM
and the observational noise satisfies
Assumptions EI
. Moreover suppose the observable
\phi
enjoys
Assumption B
. Then we have that
l(\lambda)=1,
and
W
has the standard Poisson distribution

\mathcal{P}(\tau):=\frac{\tau^{k}e^{-\tau}}{k!}.

Notice that in this case even the extremal index in the Gumbel’s law is
1.
It would be therefore interesting to explore numerically in future works the distribution of
S_{t^{\prime}}
and see what kind of compound Poisson distribution one gets.

8.2
Poisson point processes

The Poisson distribution could be enriched by defining the
rare event point process
(REPP). Let us first introduce a few objects. Put
I_{l}=[a_{l},b_{l}),l=1,\dots,k,a_{l},b_{l}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
a finite number of semi-open intervals of the non-negative real axis; call
J=\cup_{l=1}^{k}I_{l}
their disjoint union. If
r
is a positive real number, we write
rJ=\cup_{l=1}^{k}rI_{l}=\cup_{l=1}^{k}[ra_{l},rb_{l}).
We denote with
|I_{l}|
the length of the interval
I_{l},
which we also design with its Lebesgue measure
\text{Leb}(I_{l}).
The REPP counts the number of visits to the set
B_{t}
(still satisfying
Assumption B
), during the rescaled time period
v_{t}J:

N_{t}(\cdot,*)(J)=\sum_{l\in v_{t}J\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}}1_{B_{t}}(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}\cdot+s(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}\cdot)\epsilon_{i}(*)),

(37)

where
v_{t}
is taken as

v_{t}=\left\lfloor{\frac{\tau}{\int\psi((B_{t}-x)/s(x))d\mu(x)}}\right\rfloor,\ \tau>0.

(38)

Our REPP belongs to the class of the point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0},
see
[
28
]
for all the properties of point processes quoted below. They are given by any measurable map
N
from our original probability space with probability
\rho_{Z},
into
\mathcal{N}_{p}([0,\infty)),
which denotes the set of counting measures
M_{c}
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}),
which is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra making all evaluation maps
M_{c}\rightarrow M_{c}(B),
from
\mathcal{N}_{p}([0,\infty))\rightarrow[0,\infty]
measurable for all measurable sets
B.
Any counting measure
M_{c}
has the form
M_{c}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{x_{i}},\ x_{i}\in[0,\infty).

The distribution of
N
, denoted
\rho_{N},
is the measure
\rho_{Z}\circ N^{-1}=\rho_{Z}[N\in\cdot],
on
\mathcal{M}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}).

The set
\mathcal{N}_{p}([0,\infty))
becomes a topological space with the vague topology, i.e. the sequence
(M_{c})_{n}
converges to
M_{c^{*}}
whenever
(M_{c})_{n}(\phi)\rightarrow M_{c^{*}}(\phi)
for any continuous function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with compact support. We also say that the sequence of point processes
N_{n}
converges in distribution to the point process
N,
eventually defined on another probability space with probability
\rho^{\prime}{{}_{Z}},
if
(\rho_{Z})_{N_{n}}
converges weakly to
(\rho^{\prime}_{Z})_{N}.
In this case we will write
N_{n}\xrightarrow{\rho_{Z}}N.

If we now return to our REPP (
42
), we will see that a very common result is to get

N_{n}\xrightarrow{\rho_{Z}}\tilde{N},

where

\mu(x,\tilde{N}(x)(I_{l})=k_{l},1\leq l\leq n)=\prod_{l=1}^{n}e^{-\tau\text{Leb}(I_{l})}\frac{\tau^{k_{l}}\text{Leb}(I_{l})^{k_{l}}}{k_{l}!},

(39)

for any disjoint bounded sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{n}
and non-negative integers
k_{1},\dots,k_{n},
which is called the
standard Poisson point process
.
In order to study the convergence of our REPP we should first remind the definition of the
Laplace transform for a general point process
R:(X^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}},\mu^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{p}([0,\infty)):

\psi_{R}(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left(e^{-\sum_{l=1}^{k}y_{l}R(I_{l})}\right),

(40)

for every non negative values
y_{1},\dots,y_{k},
each choice of
k
disjoint intervals
I_{i}=[a_{i},b_{i}),i=1,\dots,k.
In the case of the previous standard Poisson point process we get

\psi_{N}(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=e^{-t\sum_{l=1}^{k}(1-e^{-y_{l}})\text{Leb}(I_{l})},

(41)

Then, in order to establish the convergence in distribution of the REPP
N_{n}
toward the CPP
N
it will be sufficient to show the convergence of the Laplace transforms.

It turns out that this is particularly simple in our case assuming the hypothesis of Proposition
8.1
and using the techniques developed in
[
29
]
which are close to those of characteristic function used in the proof of Proposition
8.1
. In particular we get

Proposition 8.2
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
8.1
, consider the counting measure

N_{t}(\cdot,*)(J)=\sum_{l\in v_{t}J\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}}1_{B_{t}}(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}\cdot+s(T_{\overline{\eta}}^{i}\cdot)\epsilon_{i}(*)),

(42)

where
v_{t}
is taken as in (
38
).

Then the Laplace transform
\psi_{N_{t}}
converge to the right hand side of (
41
) and therefore
N_{t}
converges in distribution to the standard Poisson point process of intensity
\tau
given by (
39
).

Appendix A
How to detect the modulation term

Consider now the observable

\phi(x)=-\log|x-y|,\ x,y\in I,

(43)

which is a standard one for extreme value distributions
[
20
]
.
The set
B_{t}:=\{\phi\geq u_{t}\}
will now be a true ball of radius
r_{t}=e^{-u_{t}}
and centered at
y:
we denote it as

B_{t}(y,r_{t}).
Then
\left(\frac{B_{t}(y,r_{t})^{c}-x}{s(x)}\right)^{c}
will be a ball of radius
r_{t}/s(x)
and centered at the point
y-x.
So we have

\psi\left(\frac{B_{t}(y,r_{t})^{c}-x}{s(x)}\right)^{c}=\psi(B_{t}(y-x,r_{t}/s(x))).

Therefore the scaling (
34
) will tell us how the radius of the ball should behave:

t\int\psi(B_{t}(y-x,r_{t}/s(x)))d\mu(x)\sim\tau.

(44)

or equivalently, as a scaling for
u_{t}:

t\int\psi(B(y-x,e^{-u_{t}}s(x)^{-1}))d\mu(x)\sim\tau.

(45)

Notice that now we removed the subindex
t
from the ball, since its radius will be an explicit function of
t.

It is now known that using EVT numerical theory, the quantity
u_{t}
is from one side related to local behavior of the measures
\mu,\psi,
on the other hand it could be detected by simulations. In this case we could have information on
s(\cdot)
and that would be a useful result. Let us elaborate on this point.
Suppose that
\psi
is smooth with support in
(-\epsilon,\epsilon).
Then
\psi(B(y-x,e^{-u_{t}}s(x)^{-1}))=O(\epsilon^{-1}e^{-u_{t}}s(x)^{-1})
and this value will persist when the ball will move in the support of
\psi.
At the end we get

t\int\psi(B(y-x,e^{-u_{t}}s(x)^{-1}))d\mu(x)\sim te^{-u_{t}}\int s(x)^{-1}d\mu(x).

Therefore

te^{-u_{t}}\int s(x)^{-1}d\mu(x)\sim\tau.

(46)

We should now remind how the scaling exponent
u_{t}
could be accessed numerically.

In extreme value theory, the boundary level
u_{t}
are chosen as the affine function of the real variable
-\log\tau\in\mathbb{R}:

u_{t}=\frac{-\log\tau}{a_{t}}+b_{t},

For practical (numerical) purposes, the distribution function of the form
W_{t}
is modeled for
t
sufficiently large, by the so-called
generalized extreme value (GEV)
distribution, which is a function depending upon three parameters
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, (the tail index),
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, (the location parameter) and
\sigma>0
, (the scale parameter):

\text{GEV}(y,\xi;\kappa,\sigma)=\exp\large\{-[1+\xi(\frac{y-\kappa}{\sigma})]^{-1/\xi}\large\}.

The location parameter
\kappa
and the scale parameter
\sigma
are scaling constants in place of
b_{t}
and
a_{t}
. The idea is now to use a block-maxima approach (see next Appendix B) and fit our unnormalised data to a GEV distribution; for that it will be necessary to find a linkage among
a_{t},b_{t},\kappa
and
\sigma
. For observables producing the Gumbel’s law, it has been shown, that for
t
large we have

a_{t}\sim\frac{1}{\sigma};\ b_{t}\sim\kappa;

(47)

moreover the shape parameter
\xi
tends to zero. We now return to estimate the sequences
a_{t},b_{t}
throughout the scaling (
46
). We have

u_{t}\sim\log\tau+\log(t\int s(x)^{-1}dx),\Rightarrow a_{t}=1;\ b_{t}=\log(t\int s(x)^{-1}dx),

and therefore for large
t:

\sigma=1;\ \kappa=\log(t\int s(x)^{-1}dx).

Hence in principle the average of the reciprocal of
s
could be detected with the GEV numerical technique. Let us better enlighten this point. Suppose we have a system for which we know the extreme value law holds; then
\kappa=\log t.

If instead
\kappa
assumes a different value, this would mean that there is a local observational modulation
s.

Appendix B
The GEV algorithm

We now see how to get the quantities
\sigma
and
\kappa
with the block-maxima technique from the time series.

•

First of all we construct our time series as

Y_{i}=-\log(\text{dist}(Z_{i},y)),

for a given
y\in I
and take
i=1,\dots,K
.

•

We divide the set of integers from
1
to
K
into
m
bins each containing
t=K/m
observations. Then we take the maximum of the
Y_{i}
into each bin. This will produce a vector
V_{j}^{(n)},j=1,\dots,m
, where the superindex
n
means that each component was computed by maximizing
n
random variables. Usually one splits the series in
m=1000
bins each containing
t=1000
(or
t=10000
) observations.

•

For some selected values of
m
, the maxima are normalised and fitted to the GEV distribution by using a maximum likelihood method directly implemented in MATLAB Statistics Toolbox
gevfit
:

https://www.mathworks.com/help/stats/gevfit.html. It will return maximum likelihood estimates of the parameters for the GEV distribution giving
95\%
confidence interval for estimates.

•

We thus estimate
a_{t},b_{t}
.

•

Finally we redo the previous procedure for increasing values of
K.
