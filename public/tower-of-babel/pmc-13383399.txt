Physical function frailty trajectory before and after cardiovascular event among older adults in three multinational cohorts
Authors: Quan Zhou, Zhaoping Wang, Qin Xu, Xue Xia, Meng Gao, Luwen Zhang, Xue Tian, Di He, Anxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo capture temporal pattern of physical function frailty before and after cardiovascular disease (CVD), including heart disease and stroke incidence event, among older adults.MethodsA total of 23,307 participants aged over 50 years from three cohorts: English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were studied. Physical function frailty was assessed by mobility and daily function scores. Higher scores indicated more severe physical function frailty. CVD was ascertained by self-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke. Four subtypes of heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure were also assessed.ResultsIn participants who experienced a CVD event, physical function frailty (measured by the mobility and daily function scores) were already present prior to the event and worsened thereafter. Short-term increase of mobility scores following the CVD diagnosis were observed (ELSA: β = 4.40, 95% CI 3.34, 5.45; HRS: β = 4.73, 95% CI 3.95, 5.50; CHARLS: β = 3.08, 95% CI 1.01, 5.14). The post-annual increase of physical function frailty after the event was significantly faster than that before the event. These patterns were consistent for both heart disease and stroke incidence events, as well as for the four subtypes of heart disease.ConclusionsPrior to CVD onset, participants who developed CVD exhibited higher levels of physical function frailty at baseline compared with CVD-free participants. Following the incidence of CVD, participants experienced a short-term increase and a faster annual rate of increase in physical function frailty.Summary of the paper for patients and the publicWe recommend that integrating routine screening for physical function frailty into primary care for older adults to identify high-risk individuals for targeted CVD prevention. Tailored cardiac rehabilitation programs should be developed for CVD survivors. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death globally, particularly among older adults [1]. Incident CVD, such as heart disease or stroke, event triggered physical functional disability which manifest as difficulty in walking, personal care, and performing household tasks [2], ​​imposing substantial burdens on individuals’ independence, family caregivers, and healthcare systems [3]. ​​In older adults, the aging of multiple physiological systems (skeletal, neural, and muscular) contributed to physical functional frailty, characterized by declines in daily activity and mobility [4, 5]. The muscular and cardiovascular systems interact during the aging process [6]. Therefore, understanding the trajectory of physical function frailty before and after incidence CVD event is crucial for developing strategies to reduce the CVD burden and slow the progression of physical functional frailty in older adults [7].

Previous studies on the physical function frailty-CVD link have yielded inconsistent results. A cohort study of older adults in Amsterdam reported that frail older adults do not have an increased risk of CVD (hazard ratio (HR) 1.47; 95%CI: 0.86, 2.52) [8]. In contrast, another analysis of three cohorts of China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and Health and Retirement Study (HRS) reported robust prticipants who progressed to pre-frail or frail status had increased risks of incident CVD (CHARLS, HR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.54, 2.21; ELSA, HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.25, 1.86; HRS, HR = 1.59, 95% CI:1.31, 1.92), especially for physical functional frailty [9]. Prior studies indicated that inadequate cardiovascular health which was comprised seven health factors and behaviors including smoking status, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), diet quality, blood pressure, total cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose is associated with lower physical function, which was supported in related studies [10–12].

The association between physical function frailty and CVD is supported by shared biological pathways.​ Inflammation plays a significant role in the association between physical function frailty and CVD [13, 14]. Reactive oxygen species (e.g., superoxide radical, hydrogen peroxide) are involved in intracellular signaling cascades of endothelial dysfunction, ischemia-reperfusion, and atherosclerosis various [15, 16]. Elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein, interleukin-6) are consistently associated with frailty and predict future functional decline and disability in individuals with or at risk for CVD [17–19]. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, exacerbated by events Like myocardial infarction or stroke, further damage neuronal repair mechanisms and skeletal muscle homeostasis, accelerating physical decline [20–22]. Additionally, psychosocial factors such as depression and social isolation, which are linked to frailty, may also adversely affect cardiovascular health. Accumulating evidence supported that elevated chronic inflammation in cardiovascular disease survivors accelerates physical functional decline by compromising muscle energy efficiency and promoting muscle loss. Decline in functional status over time associated with high baseline inflammatory biomarker levels including interleukin-6 (IL6) and lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2, was observed in older stroke-free individuals (N = 3,298) in northern Manhattan [23]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) potentially underlying prospective disability in adults with heart disease aged 65 years and older (N = 8,618) in the United States of America (USA) [17]. Growing evidence indicates that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction following heart disease and stroke damage neuronal repair mechanisms and skeletal muscle homeostasis. This multisystem damage accelerates the decline in physical function. Post-stroke neuronal biochemical and molecular alterations activate massive reactive oxygen species generation [24]. Even at the chronic stage (> 6 months post-stroke), markers of inflammation and oxidative stress including hs-CRP, IL-6, protein carbonyls, and oxidized low-density lipoprotein remained elevated in older (> 50 years) stroke survivors (N = 256) in USA. Following treadmill rehabilitation, changes in hs-CRP, protein carbonyls, and oxidized low-density lipoprotein were negatively associated with changes in peak oxygen uptake rate and 6-minute walking distance [25].

Beyond its role as a risk factor, the impact of cardiovascular events on the long-term trajectory of functional decline is increasingly recognized. For instance, the population-based Rotterdam Study (N = 14,712) reported that individuals experienced steeper declines in daily function up to 10 years before their first-ever stroke compared with those who remained stroke-free [26]. Similarly, a pooled analysis of the HRS, SHARE, and ELSA cohorts (N = 33,660) indicated that during the post-stroke chronic phase, functional limitations tend to plateau before increasing again, with the evolution varying by disability severity [27]. However, significant critical gaps remain. Due to methodology and data limitations, these prior studies failed to capture the immediate, short-term functional changes following stroke occurrence or to quantify the dynamic nature of physical function frailty surrounding CVD and specific heart disease subtypes, such as angina or heart attack. Consequently, the critical time window for physical function frailty intervention surrounding a CVD event remains undefined.

The aim of the current study was to capture temporal pattern of physical function frailty before and after CVD including heart disease and stroke incidence event among older adults. We hypothesized that high baseline physical function frailty was associated with CVD incidence risk. The progression of physical function frailty would be steeper after incident CVD and continue to deteriorate over time.

Method

Study design and population

This retrospective analysis utilized data from three prospective, nationally representative aging cohorts: ELSA (United Kingdom), HRS (United States), and CHARLS (China). For this analysis, the assessment of CVD and physical function frailty in ELSA cohort was conducted in 2004 (baseline), with follow-up surveys conducted in 2006, 2008, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, and 2018. For the HRS cohort, the assessment of CVD and physical function frailty was conducted in 2006 (baseline), with follow-up surveys conducted in 2008, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, and 2020. For the CHARLS cohort, the assessment of CVD and physical function frailty assessment was conducted in 2011(baseline), with follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, and 2018. Figure S1 shows the selection process of the study population. Among 40,710 aged ≥ 50 years participants from the ELSA, HRS, and CHARLS, we first excluded 11,928 participants with CVD at baseline or without CVD follow-up information. Moreover, 5,475 participants with missing data on physical function frailty at baseline or less than two physical function frailty measurements during follow-up were excluded. Finally, 23,307 participants were included in the final analyses. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cohort studies.

Assessment of physical function frailty

The physical function frailty was assessed by mobility score and daily function score. The mobility score (range: 0-100) is derived from 7 items [28]. The daily function score (range: 0-200) sums activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL (IADL) scores, each based on 5 items [29]. For all items, 0 indicates “no difficulty” and 1 indicates “some difficulty.” The score of 0 represents no functional impairment, while higher scores indicate greater difficulty, with the maximum score signifying the most severe physical function frailty. For more details, please see Table S1.

Covariates

Covariates considered as potential confounders included age, sex, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and BMI. These variables were harmonized across cohorts. Among the ELSA, HRS, and CHARLS, marital status was divided into two categories: married or partnered and other marital status (separated, divorced, unmarried, or widowed). Education was classified into three levels: below high school, high school, and college or above. Smoking status was categorized as never smokers and ever smokers. Ever smokers included former and current smokers. Similarly, drinking status was categorized as never drinkers and ever drinkers. Physical activity was divided into three categories: vigorous (vigorous activity more than once a week), moderate (moderate activity more than once a week), and light (the rest). The missing rates of covariates were summarized inTables S2. The missing data of covariates were imputed using the multiple imputation with chained equation [30], which was described in our previous study [9].

Outcome ascertainment and follow-up

The primary outcome was incident CVD, defined as a new self-reported, physician-diagnosed heart disease (angina, myocardial infarction, or heart failure) or stroke. In each survey wave, participants were explicitly asked whether they had been told by a doctor that they had heart disease or stroke. In subsequent waves, participants were asked to confirm previously reported events, and any inconsistencies were corrected retrospectively. Those who reported being diagnosed with heart disease, stroke were considered to have CVD. This method of using self-reported diagnoses from these cohorts is well-established [31]. Previous validation studies support the accuracy of self-reported CVD diagnoses. For example, Xie et al. found that self-reported coronary heart disease in ELSA cohort showed 77.5% consistency with medical records [32]. Another analysis of HRS cohort data reported that misreporting of stroke was random and not bias-inducing [33]. We also evaluated major heart disease: angina, heart attack and heart failure in the ELSA and HRS cohorts. Coronary heart disease was defined as a composite of angina and heart attack.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. The physical function frailty trajectories before and after the event among participants were evaluated by the linear mixed models. Both the intercept and the slope were fitted as random effects to account for inter-individual differences at baseline and different rates of change in physical function frailty over the follow-up period [34, 35]. Based on prior literature review and underlying mechanisms [9, 36], all models were adjusted for baseline values of potential confounders that were selected a priori: age, sex, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity and BMI. The main analyses were repeated using the subtype of CVD including heart disease and stroke and four subtypes of heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure with same modeling strategies. The physical function frailty trajectories before and after the CVD event were also analyzed in the different age (Aged < 60 and Aged ≥60), gender (Male and Female) and BMI (BMI < 30 kg/m2 and BMI ≥30 kg/m2) subgroups. Subgroup differences were assessed using the Z-test [37].

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the main analyses among participants: (1) without hip fracture at baseline; (2) without falls at baseline; (3) with normal physical function (the daily function score < 120 and mobility score < 60) at baseline; (4) completing both physical function measures during the study (completing 8 measures for ELSA, completing 8 measures for HRS, completing 5 measures for CHARLS); (5) with complete data on covariates. In addition, we repeated the main analyses after further adjusting for the use of antihypertensive and antidiabetic drugs.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

According to inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 5,285 participants from ELSA (female: 57.1%, mean age: 64.1 years), 10,233 from HRS (female: 61.6%, mean age: 65.6 years) and 7,789 from CHARLS (female: 48.8%, mean age: 61.1 years) were included in this study. Baseline characteristics of these participants are presented in Table 1. Participants with CVD incidence were older, more likely to be female, less likely to be married or partnered, lower physical activity levels and poor daily functioning (scores ≥10) and mobility (scores ≥10) than participants without CVD during follow up, these patterns varied by cohort. The median (IQR) length of follow-up post-CVD event for CHARLS, ELSA, and HRS was 4.5 (4.4-6.4), 8.9 (5.2-11.2) and 9.3 (6.7-11.4) years, respectively. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants were described in the Table S10.

Baseline physical function frailty before cardiovascular disease event

The more severe physical function frailty in the baseline was observed in participants with incidence CVD event compared with those without incidence CVD event during follow-up. These patterns were also observed in two subtypes of CVD, including heart disease and stroke. For mobility, higher mobility scores among incidence-CVD participants were observed (ELSA: β = 5.37, 95% CI 4.02, 6.72; HRS: β = 4.68, 95% CI 3.69, 5.66; CHARLS: β = 7.06, 95% CI 5.63, 8.50), comparing with CVD-free participants (Table 2; Fig. 1). As for daily function, higher daily function scores among incidence-CVD participants were observed (ELSA: β = 3.01, 95% CI 1.66, 4.35; HRS: β = 1.93, 95% CI 0.98, 2.88; CHARLS: β = 6.23 95% CI 4.20, 8.27), comparing with CVD-free participants (Table 3; Fig. 2). The higher mobility scores in the baseline were observed in participants with incidence of four type heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure, compared with those without incidence event during follow-up (Table S3, Figure S4). The higher daily function scores in the baseline were observed in participants with incidence of heart failure, compared with those without incidence event during follow-up (Table 4, Figure S5).

Mean annual rate of physical function frailty before cardiovascular disease event

We found no consistent evidence that mean annual rate of physical function frailty before CVD diagnosis in individuals with incident CVD differed from that of CVD-free individuals. Similar patterns were observed for two subtypes of CVD, including heart disease and stroke (Table 2; Fig. 1; Table 3; Fig. 2). Similarly, there was no consistent evidence that mean annual rate of mobility scores before diagnosis in individuals with incident four subtypes of heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure differed from that of event-free individuals (Table S3, Figure S4). However, lower mean annual rate of daily function scores before diagnosis in individuals with incident heart attack and coronary heart disease, compared with that of event-free individuals was observed (Table 4, Figure S5).

Short-term change in physical function frailty after cardiovascular disease events

Participants diagnosed with CVD experienced short-term increase in physical function frailty following CVD incidence event. Similar patterns were observed after the heart disease or stroke incidence event. The short-term increase in physical function frailty was steeper after stroke compared to heart disease. For mobility, increased mobility scores after incidence-CVD diagnosis were observed (ELSA: β = 4.40, 95% CI 3.34, 5.45; HRS: β = 4.73, 95% CI 3.95, 5.50; CHARLS: β = 3.08, 95% CI 1.01, 5.14; Table 2; Fig. 1). As for daily function, increased daily function scores after incidence-CVD diagnosis were observed (ELSA: β = 4.50, 95% CI 3.22, 5.79; HRS: β = 5.53, 95% CI 4.61, 6.45; CHARLS: β = 3.83 95% CI 0.80, 6.86; Table 3; Fig. 2). Participants diagnosed with angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease or heart failure experienced short-term increase in physical function frailty following incidence event. As for heart failure, increased mobility scores (ELSA: β = 8.88, 95% CI 4.48,13.28; HRS: β = 2.66, 95% CI 0.68, 4.66; Table S3, Figure S4) and daily function scores (ELSA: β = 16.69, 95% CI 11.04, 22.36; HRS: β = 5.18, 95% CI 2.44, 7.94; Table 4, Figure S5) after incidence heart failure were observed.

Post-annual change in physical function frailty after cardiovascular disease events

The post-annual increase of physical function frailty significantly faster than it did before the CVD event, these patterns were observed after the heart disease or stroke incidence event. For mobility, faster post-annual increase rate of mobility scores after incidence-CVD diagnosis were observed (ELSA: β = 0.48, 95% CI 0.22, 0.75; HRS: β = 0.20, 95% CI 0.01, 0.39; CHARLS: β = 1.24, 95% CI 0.36, 2.11; Table 2; Fig. 1). As for daily function, faster post-annual increase rate of daily function scores after incidence-CVD diagnosis were observed (ELSA: β = 2.46, 95% CI 2.08, 2.83; HRS: β = 2.29, 95% CI 2.01, 2.58; CHARLS: β = 1.55, 95% CI 0.24, 2.87; Table 3; Fig. 2). The post-annual increase of daily function scores significantly faster than it did before event four subtypes of heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure event (Table 4, Figure S5). Among subtypes of heart disease, greater effect size of post-annual change in daily function after heart failure onset was observed (HRS: β = 3.62, 95% CI 2.76, 4.47). No consistent and robust evidence supported post-annual increase of mobility scores after any subtypes of heart disease (Table S3, Figure S4).

Subgroup analysis

Combining subgroup analyses across the three multinational cohorts, we consistently found that the trajectories of physical function frailty varied by age group, except for gender and BMI subgroup. In detail, participants aged ≥ 60 years experienced higher baseline physical function frailty and significantly steeper post annual increases in physical function frailty following CVD incidence compared to those aged < 60 years (P for interactions < 0.05; Figure S2, Figure S3).

Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of physical function frailty before and after incident CVD event was confirmed by all sensitivity analyses. Firstly, the trajectories were similar with the main results in analyses among participants without hip fracture (Table S4), without fall event (Table S5) or without abnormal physical function (Table S6). Secondly, consistency was maintained in analyses limited to participants with complete physical function measurement information during follow up survey (Table S7), or with completed covariates data in the baseline survey (Table S8). Lastly, the results of reanalysis additionally adjusted drug information were similar with main results (Table S9).

Discussion

Major findings

In three cross-national cohorts of community-dwelling older adults, the physical function frailty trajectories before and after CVD incidence event were captured. Prior to CVD onset, participants who developed CVD exhibited higher levels of physical function frailty at baseline compared with those who remained CVD-free. Following the incidence of CVD, participants experienced a short-term increase and a faster annual rate of increase in physical function frailty. These patterns were also observed in two subtypes of CVD including heart disease and stroke and four subtypes of heart disease including angina, heart attack, coronary heart disease and heart failure. The participants aged ≥ 60 years experienced higher baseline physical function frailty and significantly steeper post annual increases in physical function frailty following CVD incidence compared to those aged < 60 years.

Existing literature suggests a bidirectional relationship between physical function frailty and CVD, forming a reciprocal feedback loop where each condition exacerbates the other [38, 39]. While previous research has typically examined physical function frailty as either a fixed risk factor for CVD or a consequence of it, our study is the first to utilize three cross-national cohorts to map longitudinal trajectories of physical function frailty both before and after an incident CVD event. Our analysis revealed a nuanced temporal pattern: although the annual rate of change (slope) in physical function did not significantly differ between the two groups prior to CVD onset, a higher baseline level of frailty was associated with increased CVD risk. This suggests that it is the cumulative burden of functional impairment, rather than its pre-event velocity of decline, that serves as a critical predictor for incident CVD. These findings corroborate and extend previous multicohort evidence. For instance, a multicohort study pooled data from three international cohorts (HRS, CHARLS and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement) reported that functional dependency was associated with higher multimorbidity risk [40]. A high risk of stroke associated with ADL limitations was found in CHARLS (N = 46,728) [41].

Our findings demonstrate that the onset of specific CVD events was followed by a distinct “two-phase” decline in physical capacity. Specifically, following a stroke or heart attack, participants experienced an immediate, significant increase in physical function frailty (representing an acute intercept shift), which was followed by a faster annual rate of decline compared to their pre-event trajectory. The association we observed between CVD incidence and subsequent functional impairment aligns with evidence from a retrospective Japanese cohort (N = 7,456), which found that absolute CVD risk was significantly associated with both disability and mortality in adults aged 65 and older [42]. By employing repeated longitudinal assessments, our study extends prior knowledge by calculating specific effect sizes for these acute and long-term changes that were not previously quantified in clinical cohorts.

Among subtypes of heart disease, greater effect size of physical function frailty after heart failure onset was observed in our study. Consistent with our findings, a systematic review encompassing 21 studies with 68,556 heart failure patients reported a combined prevalence of sarcopenia of 31% in this population, which markedly exceeds the 6–12% prevalence observed in the general population [43]. The hallmark of heart failure is a reduction in cardiac output and subsequent systemic hypoperfusion, leading to chronic tissue hypoxia affecting skeletal muscle, cerebral tissue, and other organ systems [44]. In cardiac cachexia, a shift toward type II (fast‑twitch) myofiber predominance is noted, accompanied by intrafibrillar edema, fat infiltration, and fibrosis-changes that may relate to NT‑pro‑BNP secretion [38]. A hypermetabolic state accompanied by significant inflammation is also present. Although sarcopenia can increase risk of heart failure (HF), a plethora of HF-related factors can contribute to sarcopenia pathogenesis and progression. These include the state of physical inactivity seen in HF, malnutrition, and malabsorption, increased oxidative stress, hormonal changes such as increased angiotensin II levels, decline in growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor-1 levels, and myostatin upregulation, as well as decreased muscle blood flow [39].

The finding of our study supported that individuals aged 60 years or older appear to represent vulnerable populations experiencing higher baseline physical function frailty and greater post-annual physical function frailty following CVD event, which could be explained by age-related physiological vulnerability and decreased functional reserve [45]. Subgroup analyses across the three multinational cohorts did not show consistent evidence of a higher CVD risk in women: while women exhibited higher scores in some functional domains before CVD onset in certain cohorts (e.g., baseline mobility in HRS and mean difference in mobility in ELSA), men demonstrated a greater mean difference in daily function before CVD in the CHARLS cohort. This inconsistency suggests that the observed sex differences may not be universal but rather influenced by specific population characteristics or methodological factors. Similarly, large-scale population studies from the European Health Information Survey (N = 316,333) and Canadian Community Health Survey (N = 127,462) have reported more significant impairments in instrumental activities of daily living among female stroke survivors [46], whereas a follow-up study of stroke survivors in Italy (N = 310) found lower rates of walking disability in women [47]. This further supports the notion that the relationship between sex and CVD-related outcomes is highly dependent on study population, assessment methods, and regional context.

Potential mechanisms

Prior to CVD onset, participants who developed CVD exhibited higher levels of physical function frailty at baseline compared with CVD-free participants. High levels of physical function frailty may reflect age-related physiological and pathological processes that collectively increase the risk of CVD. Physical function frailty reflects impairments in muscle strength, coordination, balance, and cardiorespiratory capacity, which collectively lead to reduced exercise tolerance and diminished peak oxygen uptake. These deficits subsequently elevate the risk of developing heart failure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [48]. Accumulating evidence supported that reactive oxygen species (ROS) including superoxide radical and hydrogen peroxide in aging progress play a vital role in the progression of CVDs. These reactive oxygen species involvement in intracellular signaling cascades of endothelial dysfunction, ischemia-reperfusion, and atherosclerosis [15, 16]. In addition to biological mechanisms, psychological factors may also explain the Link between physical function frailty and increased CVD risk. Older adults with disabilities often experience anxiety about their Health, fear of dependency, and social isolation, which can lead to depression and distress [49–51]. These mental health issues are established risk factors for CVD [52].

Following the incidence of CVD, participants experienced a short-term increase and a faster annual rate of increase in physical function frailty. Acute increase in physical function frailty after CVD could attribute to decreasing muscle strength and flexibility as well as joint stiffness. In the short term, reduced cardiac output due to heart disease compromises blood supply to the peripheral vasculature and skeletal muscles. This state of hypoperfusion diminishes oxygen and energy delivery to skeletal muscle tissue, ultimately leading to impaired physical function [53]. Similarly, cerebral ischemia due to stroke damages the neural structures responsible for motor control, thereby disrupting mobility and daily function [54]. Given the physiological interplay between the cardiovascular and muscular systems, the oxidative stress, and inflammation triggered by a cardiovascular event could explain accelerated annual rate of increase in physical function frailty after CVD.

A systematic review of experimental studies indicates that chronic systemic inflammation activates proteolytic catabolism and inhibits myogenesis along with repair leads to muscle loss primarily through sustained and excessive activation of the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription pathway, particularly the IL-6/STAT-3 axis [19]. Post-stroke neuronal biochemical and molecular alterations activate massive ROS generation [24]. Oxidative stress and inflammation play a role in the cascade of ischemic events in the brain area, where ischemic damage leads to neuronal death, brain infarction and disability [55]. In myocardial infarction and heart failure, hypoxia and cellular ATP depletion elevate ROS levels and induce mitochondrial dysfunction [20]. Chronic oxidative stress and impaired quality control could compromise mitochondrial function. This, in turn, damages the mitochondrial-orchestrated pathways governing redox signaling, calcium handling, and apoptosis, ultimately disrupting muscle homeostasis [56]. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction following heart disease and stroke damage neuronal repair mechanisms and skeletal muscle homeostasis. This multisystem damage accelerates the decline in physical function.

Beyond molecular mechanisms after CVD event such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, the accelerated decline in post-CVD physical function is also explained by the cumulative burden of CVD-related comorbidities, polypharmacy, and psychosocial factors. A more rapid increasing rate in the total number of (ADL + IADL) disabilities associated with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (defined as having two or more of diabetes, stroke, or heart disease) was observed in participants (N = 16,754) from CHARLS [57]. Among Chinese older adult stroke survivors (N = 11,743), chronic conditions were associated with prevalence and severity of activity limitation [58]. Gail J. McAvay and colleague reported that antihypertensive drugs have greater adverse effects on mobility in those with established CVD disease (high sedentary time could be a mediating factor) [59]. Cross-sectional analysis of 76 stroke survivors (> 6 months post-stroke) reported that anxiety was an independent predictor of self-perceived ADL/IADL function [60].

Clinical implications

Our findings support integrating physical function frailty assessment into the management of adults aged ≥ 60 years. In primary care, clinicians​ should incorporate brief mobility and daily function questionnaires during annual health checks or routine follow-up visits​ for older adults. This assessment serves as a dynamic vital sign. For risk stratification, individuals with a moderate traditional ASCVD risk score but significant physical frailty should be re-classified as high risk, triggering referral for intensive prevention (e.g., supervised exercise, nutritional counseling). While cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is recommended for CVD survivors to reduce mortality and improve quality of life [61, 62], challenges persist with low participation and adherence rates [63]. Our findings indicate that older CVD survivors experience a short-term increase and an accelerated annual rate of increase in physical function frailty. These older CVD survivors with coexisting physical function frailty may be intolerant of conventional CR exercise regimens, contributing to low participation and adherence rates in CR programs. Therefore, for CVD survivors unable to complete cardiopulmonary exercise testing, physical function frailty assessments are recommended as a practical tool to reflect exercise capacity and functional status, aiding in the initial evaluation of exercise risk and rehabilitation potential. Furthermore, community- or home-based programs focused on physical maintenance and nutritional support must be integrated as a core component of post-discharge healthcare.

Strengths and limitations

This study examines physical function frailty before and after heart disease or stroke, tracking their long-term trajectories over 11 years across three large prospective cohorts (CHARLS, ELSA, HRS). This multi-cohort approach, representing diverse ethnic and geographic groups, enhances the external validity and robustness of our findings. Furthermore, by restricting the study population to individuals with at least three repeated assessments including baseline of physical function frailty, we were able to precisely capture the dynamic transition from pre-event functional status to post-event chronic decline. There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, consistent with previous studies [64, 65], CVD ascertainment relied on participants’ self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce misclassification bias and would likely underestimate the strength of the association. However, earlier evidence suggests that any such bias is generally minor and non-systematic [9, 33]. Future studies should incorporate medical records, clinical laboratory measures, or imaging data to further improve the diagnostic accuracy of CVD. Secondly, despite extensive adjustment for confounders, unmeasured factors, such as genetic predisposition and dietary habits may still contribute residual confounding. Thirdly, comparison of baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants revealed evidence of healthy-participant bias. Fourthly, physical function was assessed using the mobility and daily function scores. Although the 6-minute walk test and the short physical performance battery might serve as preferable alternatives [66, 67], the mobility and daily function measurements are simple and highly practicable, and prior studies have demonstrated their strong predictive capacity for functional decline and mortality [68, 69]. Besides, focusing solely on the physical domain of frailty did not capture the full spectrum of frailty, integrating multidimensional frailty assessments is warrant in the future studies.

Conclusion

Prior to CVD onset, participants who developed CVD exhibited higher levels of physical function frailty at baseline compared with CVD-free participants. Following the incidence of CVD, they experienced a short-term increase and a faster annual rate of increase in physical function frailty. Our findings highlight older adults, particularly those aged ≥ 60 years, as a high-risk population for this detrimental functional trajectory surrounding CVD.​ Future research should focus on the CVD incidence risk in individuals with high physical function frailty and develop precise preventive strategies to delay short-term functional decline and promote long-term recovery in CVD survivors.
