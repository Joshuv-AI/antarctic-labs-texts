Interplay between Netrin-1 and Norrin controls arteriovenous zonation of blood–retina barrier integrity
Authors: Jessica Furtado, Luiz Henrique Geraldo, Felipe Saceanu Leser, Bartlomiej Bartkowiak, Mathilde Poulet, Hyojin Park, Mark Robinson, Laurence Pibouin-Fragner, Anne Eichmann, Kevin Boyé
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe blood–retina barrier (BRB) prevents entry of toxins and tightly controls the movement of ions, nutrients, and cells between the blood and the CNS. Deep understanding of how BRB function develops and is maintained and how it can be permeated for drug delivery in disease states remains a challenge. Here, we uncover an endothelial-specific gene expression program underlying BRB integrity. This program is zonated across arteries, capillaries, and veins and regulated by opposing gradients of the Netrin-1 receptor Unc5b and Lrp5-β-catenin signaling between retinal arterioles and venules, which intersect at the level of the capillaries. Our findings offer insight into the BRB gene expression program and its control by upstream regulators.

The inner cellular lining of blood vessels is formed by endothelial cells (ECs), which are heterogeneous in function and in gene expression patterns across arteries, capillaries, and veins. To unlock the targeted therapeutic potential of the endothelium for treatment of disease, deep understanding of how the different types of blood vessels develop and maintain their functional properties is essential. Blood vessels in the central nervous system (CNS) form the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and blood–retina barrier (BRB) to prevent entry of toxins and tightly control the movement of ions, nutrients, and cells between the blood and the CNS. This ensures neuronal function and protection while supporting high energy demands (1–5).

Unlike the BBB, retina vasculature and the BRB develop after birth, supporting increasing metabolic demands of the retina. The components of the BRB are crucial toward this goal, maintaining homeostasis and accommodating energy changes. As such, BRB components are tightly regulated. Sustained loss of barrier integrity leads to the development of multiple diseases including diabetic retinopathy and retinal inflammatory disease (4–8). Conversely, the impermeability of the BRB impedes drug and treatment delivery in various pathologies (9–12). While many of the components of the BBB are conserved in the BRB, there are key differences, which are reflected in the varying abilities of drugs to permeate these barriers (13). For instance, the BRB exhibits differential expression and activity levels of transporters compared to the BBB (14), with decreased pGP efflux transporter activity (15, 16) and increased expression levels of biogenic amine influx transporters (17). Understanding how BRB function develops, and how the BRB can be targeted in disease states is therefore a critical yet unmet medical need.

The main known pathway that maintains CNS ECs in the barrier-competent state is β-catenin signaling (18–21). Retina and cerebellar ECs mainly rely on Norrin signaling through Fz4, Tspan12, and Lrp5 receptors (18, 19, 22–25). Receptor activation converges on β-catenin accumulation, nuclear translocation and transcriptional regulation of barrier-specific gene expression, including upregulation of the tight junction protein Claudin-5 that suppresses paracellular permeability (26, 27), modulation of the levels of various influx and efflux transporters (28–30), and downregulation of the permeability protein plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein (Plvap), which forms EC fenestrae and transcytotic vesicles (18, 19, 31, 32). The Discs large homologue 1 (Dlg1) intracellular scaffolding protein, enhances Norrin-mediated signaling at the BRB; however, the underlying mechanisms are unclear (33). In addition, we previously identified Netrin-1/Unc5b signaling as a β-catenin-associated mediator of BBB integrity (34). While a role in the BRB has not been established, Netrin-1 signaling in human patients with diabetic retinopathy has been implicated in the regulation of vascular permeability, and the associated downstream mechanisms remain unknown (35).

Within endothelial beds, β-catenin differentially regulates BRB integrity in venous, capillary, and arterial ECs. Loss of Norrin/β-catenin signaling components lead to a zonated Claudin-5 low (−) /Plvap high (+) phenotype associated with BRB leakage from veins and capillaries, while arterial ECs remain in a barrier-competent state (18, 22, 36). Conversely, loss of Sox17 or blocking the Notch signaling ligand Dll4 results in vascular transcytosis and BRB leakage specifically from arteries (37, 38). Zonated expression patterns of brain-enriched transporters and junctional proteins along the arteriovenous axis have been observed previously (39), but the identity of the barrier gene expression programs and how they are controlled remains to be determined.

In this study, we identify a BRB gene expression program that is differentially regulated through the arteriovenous spectrum by Unc5b and by Lrp5. Using a combination of genetic and molecular approaches, we show that loss of Netrin-1-Unc5b signaling in mouse retinas disrupts BRB integrity in a zonated manner, with an increase in permeability in arteriolar and capillary ECs, while venules maintain an intact BRB. This loss of BRB integrity is accompanied by downregulation of multiple BRB genes encoding tight-junction proteins and various nutrient and ion transporters primarily in arterioles and capillaries. In addition, we show that Norrin signaling through Lrp5 regulates BRB integrity in veins and capillaries. We therefore uncover complementary arteriovenous regulation of the BRB expression program between Netrin-1/Unc5b and Norrin/Lrp5 signaling with Unc5b and Lrp5 signaling converging in capillaries.

Overall, our findings provide insight into the BRB gene expression program and its control by upstream regulators. This could have important implications in the understanding and treatment of BRB disease states.

Results

BRB Regulatory Genes Display an Arteriovenous Zonated Expression Pattern.

To understand the spatialization of the BRB gene expression program, we performed scRNA-seq on P12 mouse retinas, which have acquired a functional impermeable BRB (40) (Fig. 1A). To allow for comparison with mutations in genes that control BRB gene expression, we used Cre-negative littermates treated with tamoxifen (TAM) between P6 and P8 (see Materials and Methods). A total of 110,951 cells were sequenced, and after filtering, analysis was conducted on 107,140 cells (see Materials and Methods). Retinal cell annotation and clustering were performed based on gene expression profiles and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots were made for visualization. Clusters were annotated according to well-defined cell type markers (Dataset S1). The PECAM;Cldn5 enriched EC population comprising 3,735 cells was then further subclustered to identify arteriole, capillary, venule, tip cell, and proliferating EC subclusters based on known arteriovenous zonated gene expression profiles (Fig. 1 B and C and Dataset S2).

We queried regulation of a core BBB gene set composed of 141 previously identified brain EC enriched genes, encoding for tight junction proteins, various nutrient and ion transporters, and signaling molecules (41). The core BBB module was conserved in the BRB and demonstrated an arteriovenous zonation from arteriole, capillary to venule ECs (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C). To validate expression and zonation profile of the barrier regulatory genes, we performed immunostaining of mouse WT retinas with antibodies recognizing the protein products of selected genes (Fig. 1E). We observed zonated Unc5b and Cthrc1 protein enrichment in retinal arterioles compared to venules, corresponding to the zonation of mRNA expression (Fig. 1 D and E). Fz4, Tspan12 mRNA and Lef1 and Mfsd2a mRNA and protein displayed the opposite zonation pattern with enrichment in venous capillaries and venules compared to arterioles (Fig. 1 D and E). Hence, known regulators of BBB/BRB function such as Unc5B, Lef1, and Mfsd2a display an arteriovenous zonated expression pattern.

Netrin-1/Unc5b and Norrin/Lrp5 Regulate BRB Integrity Along the Arteriovenous Axis.

Given that Netrin-1 acts in BBB integrity (34, 35, 42), we speculated that it might have a role in the regulation of BRB development. We therefore used inducible Ntn1 global KO mice (hereafter Ntn1iKO) generated by crossing Ntn1fl/fl mice (43) with a ROSA26CreERT2 driver (Jackson laboratories), allowing ubiquitous gene deletion upon TAM injection. TAM injection from P6-P8 and retina analysis at P12 showed that Ntn1iKO converted arterioles and arterial capillaries to a Claudin-5−/Plvap+ phenotype indicative of BRB breakdown, while Ntn1iKO venules and control littermate vessels remained in a Claudin-5+/Plvap− state (Fig. 2A). Using western blotting we confirmed efficient Ntn1 gene deletion, significantly decreased Claudin-5 and increased Plvap protein expression in TAM-injected Ntn1iKO neonates compared to TAM-treated Cre-negative littermate controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Intraperitoneal (i.p.) sulfo-NHS-biotin injection with a 1 h circulation revealed tracer extravasation and BRB disruption in P12 Ntn1iKO retinas (Fig. 2B). As observed for the conversion to Claudin-5−/Plvap+, tracer extravasation appeared stronger in arterioles and capillaries when compared to venules across most retinal petals (Fig. 2B). To ensure that the effects we observed were specific to BRB integrity, we analyzed vascular density in P12 Ntn1iKO and control retinas. Both showed uniform vascular density across the superficial, intermediate, and deep vascular layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D), demonstrating that Netrin-1 controls BRB permeability but not angiogenesis between P6 and P12.

Among the known Netrin-1 receptors, Unc5b is expressed at highest levels in ECs that constitute the BRB (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E) (44). To assess whether Unc5b is involved in Netrin-1 signaling at the BRB, we used TAM-inducible endothelial-specific Unc5b knockout mice (hereafter Unc5biECKO) generated by crossing Unc5bfl/fl mice (34) with Cdh5CreERT2 mice (45). Gene deletion was induced by injecting TAM at P6-P8 and immunostaining at P12 revealed endothelial-specific deletion in Unc5biECKO mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). In concordance with enriched Unc5b expression in arterioles, i.p. sulfo-NHS-biotin injection at P12 following TAM treatment between P6 and P8 revealed a zonated BRB breakdown, with more tracer extravasation from arteries and capillaries that converted to a Claudin-5−/Plvap+ leaky phenotype compared to venules and Cre-negative littermate controls (Fig. 2 C and D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sections showed that Unc5biECKO retina capillaries displayed more disrupted tight junctions and increased presence of transcytotic vesicles compared to TAM-injected Cre-negative littermate controls (Fig. 2 E and F). Western blotting of mouse retina protein lysates confirmed Unc5b deletion along with decreased Claudin-5 and increased Plvap protein expression in Unc5biECKO neonates compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 G and H). As seen in Ntn1iKO mice, Unc5biECKO P12 retinas showed superficial, intermediate, and deep plexus densities that were similar to controls, demonstrating that Unc5b deletion at P6 does not affect angiogenesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 I and J).

To further investigate how arteriovenous zonation of the BRB was established, we turned to the atypical Wnt ligand Norrin, which protects the BRB mainly via the coreceptor Lrp5 (18). We generated TAM-inducible and endothelial-specific Lrp5 KO mice (hereafter Lrp5iECKO) by crossing Lrp5fl/fl mice with a Cdh5CreERT2 driver to induce endothelial gene deletion. TAM injection from P6-8 followed by sulfo-NHS-biotin injection at P12 revealed conversion to a leaky Claudin-5−/Plvap+ phenotype compared to TAM-treated Cre-negative controls (Fig. 2 G and H). As shown previously for retina Norrin signaling (18, 22, 23) the conversion was stronger in venules and capillaries when compared to arterioles, revealing opposite zonation between Ntn1/Unc5b and Lrp5 mutant retinal vessels with respect to BRB integrity. Lrp5iECKO P12 retinas did not show any intermediate or deep plexus vascularization, confirming that Lrp5 deletion at P6 also affects retinal angiogenesis (18, 22, 23) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2K).

Netrin-1 and Unc5b Regulate Arterial and Capillary BRB Gene Expression Programs.

To determine the zonation and signaling pathways induced by Netrin-1/Unc5b that maintain BRB integrity, we performed scRNA-seq on P12 Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO retinas TAM injected between P6 and P8 and compared the EC gene expression signatures to those obtained from their littermate controls shown in Fig. 1. Retinal cell annotation and clustering were performed based on gene expression profiles and clusters were annotated according to well-defined cell type markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A, B, D, and E and Datasets S1 and S2). All clusters present in Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO were also present in corresponding control cells, and vice versa (SI Appendix, Fig. 3 A and D). A volcano plot of differentially expressed genes showed decreased expression of Cldn5 in Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO ECs compared to controls along with decreased expression of other BRB-related genes such as Slc2a1, Abcb1a, and Mfsd2a (Fig. 3 A and D). Next, we queried expression levels of previously published sets of BBB enriched (46) and BRB enriched genes (44) (Dataset S3). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed downregulation of both BBB and BRB enriched genes and downregulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling target genes in Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO ECs (44) (Fig. 3 B and E and Dataset S3). However, except for decreased Tspan12, there was no significant change in WNT/Norrin ligand and receptor expression levels themselves (see “Wnt” in Fig. 3 B, E, and O and list of receptors and ligands in Dataset S3) (Fig. 3 A, B, D, and E and Dataset S3). Other notable expression differences were in pathways related to gap junctions, tight junctions, apical junctions, and Alzheimer’s disease genes (Datasets S4 and S5). DNA replication and cell cycle pathway-associated gene expression were not significantly changed, while apoptosis genes (47, 48) were down-regulated when compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and F). Glycolysis was down-regulated in Ntn1iKO retinas compared to controls, but not statistically changed in Unc5biECKO retinas compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and F). At the EC subcluster level, compared to TAM-treated littermate controls, Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO retina ECs showed decreased expression of WNT signaling components and targets across arterioles, arterial capillaries, venous capillaries, and venules with stronger decreased expression on the arterial side (Fig. 3 C and F). These components include Cldn5, Ctnnb1, Slc2a1 (encoding Glut1), Lef1, Abcb1a (encoding the drug efflux transporter pGP), Slco1c1, Mfsd2a, Tspan12, Apcdd1, and Nkd1 (Fig. 3 C and F). Together, our analyses of Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO retina ECs indicate that Netrin-1 signals through Unc5b to regulate BRB integrity and expression of BRB genes in a zonated manner.

As western blot of protein lysates from Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO retinas confirmed reduced expression levels of β-catenin and of the transcriptional effector Lef1 compared to TAM-treated Cre-negative controls (Fig. 3 G–J), we next asked whether Netrin-1 and Unc5b regulate BRB integrity via β-catenin signaling. TAM-inducible endothelial-specific heterozygous loss of Unc5b (Unc5bfl/WTiECKO) or β-catenin (Ctnnb1fl/WTiECKO) showed a BRB-competent Claudin-5+/Plvap− expression pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 G–J) revealing that a single allele of Unc5b or Ctnnb1 was sufficient to maintain BRB integrity. However, double heterozygous Unc5bfl/WT;Ctnnb1fl/WTiECKO retinas exhibited conversion of arterioles and capillaries to a Claudin-5−/Plvap+ state compared to Cre-negative littermate controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 G–J), demonstrating that Unc5b and Ctnnb1 genetically interact to maintain BRB ECs in a Claudin-5+/Plvap− state. Next, we overexpressed an activated form of β-catenin by crossing Unc5biECKO with Ctnnb1flex3/+ mice (49) which enhances β-catenin signaling in vivo (18, 50). The resulting offspring (Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+) displayed decreased Plvap protein expression, along with increased Claudin-5 protein expression compared to Unc5biECKO retinas at P12 (Fig. 3 K–M). Moreover, i.p. injection of sulfo-NHS-biotin into Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+ mice showed reduced BRB leakage compared to Unc5biECKO mice (Fig. 3 K–M).

To determine to which extent the activated β-catenin overactivation normalized BRB function and gene expression, we performed scRNA-seq of TAM-treated Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+ and Unc5biECKO P12 retinas (Fig. 3 N–P). Indeed, a volcano plot of differentially expressed EC genes and GSEA revealed increased BRB associated gene expression along with increased expression of β-catenin target genes (including Cldn5, Ctnnb1, Slc2a1, Lef1, Abcb1a, Slco1c1, Mfsd2a, Tspan12, Apcdd1, and Nkd1) in Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+ mice as compared to Unc5biECKO littermates (Fig. 3 N–P and Dataset S6). Gene expression in DNA replication and cell cycle pathways was not significantly changed; however, apoptosis genes were up-regulated and glycolysis genes down-regulated in Unc5BiECKO;Ctnnb1flex/3 retinas as compared to Unc5BiECKO (Fig. S3K and Dataset S6).

So far, our genetic and scRNA-seq data are consistent with Netrin-1/Unc5b acting in BRB stabilization in arterioles and capillaries, via a mechanism that can be rescued or compensated by β-catenin overactivation.

Next, we treated serum-starved confluent C57BL/6 mouse primary retina ECs with Netrin-1, Norrin, or with a combination of both ligands (Fig. 4 F and G). Stimulation with either Netrin-1 or Norrin induced Lrp5 phosphorylation with a peak between 5 and 30 min (Fig. 4 F and G). However, combined stimulation with Netrin-1 and Norrin led to increased and sustained Lrp5 phosphorylation levels when compared to either ligand alone (Fig. 4 F and G). Treatment of primary brain ECs revealed similar effects of Netrin-1, Norrin, and combined treatment on Lrp5 phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E). Netrin-1 stimulation also induced transient phosphorylation of Lrp6 (34) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Norrin stimulation induced lower levels of Lrp6 phosphorylation when compared to pLrp5, and combination of both Netrin-1 and Norrin did not show additive effects on Lrp6 phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). However, combining Netrin-1 and Wnt7a did show additive effects on Lrp6-phosphorylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H and I), demonstrating that Netrin-1 enhanced Lrp5 and 6 activation in the presence of Norrin or Wnt7a, respectively.

To determine whether Netrin-1 and Norrin increased β-catenin-mediated transcription, primary retina or brain ECs were transfected with a luciferase construct containing four native TCF/LEF binding sites (TOPflash) or its negative-control counterpart (FOPflash) (Fig. 4H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4J). Treatment with either Netrin-1 or Norrin alone increased luciferase activity with a maximal response after 8-24 h, but combinatory stimulation with Netrin-1 and Norrin doubled luciferase activity compared to each ligand alone, revealing additive activation of both ligands on β-catenin-mediated transcription (Fig. 4H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4J).

Mechanistically, the scRNA-seq data showed that the scaffolding protein Dlg1 was down-regulated in Ntn1iKO, Unc5biECKO and increased in Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+ compared to Unc5biECKO littermates (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). Dlg1 regulates β-catenin-dependent BRB integrity independently of Frizzled receptor interaction (33), so we tested whether Unc5b interacted with Dlg1 at the protein level. Unc5b immunoprecipitation of cultured ECs pulled down Dlg1 (Fig. 4I). Netrin-1 treatment increased the pulldown of Dlg1 with Unc5b with a peak at 30 min (Fig. 4 J and K). Dlg1 siRNA-mediated knockdown reduced the pulldown of Lrp5 with Unc5b compared to control siRNA-treated cells (Fig. 4 L and M). Consistently, silencing of Unc5b or Dlg1 using siRNA prevented Netrin-1-induced Lrp5 and Lrp6 phosphorylation (Fig. 4 N and O), indicating that Netrin-1 binding to Unc5b induced recruitment of Dlg1, which regulated complex formation with Lrp5. To determine which domains of Unc5b mediated this effect, we used HUVECs treated with Unc5b siRNA and infected with adenoviral constructs expressing siRNA-resistant Unc5b full length (FL), a cytoplasmic domain deletion (dCD), and a construct expressing only the cytoplasmic UPA domain (UPA) that mediates interaction with Lrp6 (34). All constructs were GFP tagged to facilitate receptor detection. GFP pulldown and WB analysis showed that Unc5b-FL and Unc5b-UPA coimmunoprecipitated with Lrp5 and Dlg1, while Unc5b-dCD did not (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). These results identify the Unc5b-UPA cytoplasmic domain as the main Dlg1 interacting domain.

To test the interaction between Unc5b and Lrp5 in vivo, we generated TAM-inducible endothelial-specific heterozygous loss of Unc5b and Lrp5 (Unc5bfl/WT;Lrp5fl/wtiECKO). TAM injection from P0-2 followed by sulfo-NHS-biotin injection at P12 led to Claudin-5−/Plvap+ leaky capillaries in Unc5bfl/WT;Lrp5fl/wtiECKO retinas, while single Lrp5fl/wt mutants display an intact BRB, revealing that Unc5b and Lrp5 interact to maintain capillary ECs in a BRB-competent state (Fig. 4 P and Q). These results support Netrin-1/Unc5b interacting with Lrp5 signaling in capillary BRB stabilization. Compared to Lrp5iECKO P12 retinas, which lack the deeper retinal vascular layers, Unc5bfl/WT;Lrp5fl/wtiECKO single heterozygote deletion showed intermediate and deep plexus densities that were similar to TAM-treated Cre-negative littermate controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E).

Netrin-1/Unc5b and Norrin/Lrp5 Regulate BRB Gene Expression Along the Arteriovenous Axis.

So far, our data point to a model where the BRB gene expression program is differentially regulated throughout the arteriovenous spectrum by opposing gradients of the Netrin-1 receptor Unc5b and Lrp5-β-catenin signaling between retinal arterioles and venules. We therefore investigated the zonation and regulation of the core BBB/BRB enriched genes by Netrin-1 and Unc5b. GSEA of Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO versus control cells showed decreased expression of the BBB/BRB enriched gene set, which was restored upon β-catenin gain of function (Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+) (Fig. 5 A–C). Of the 141 BRB genes, 58 genes are down-regulated in Ntn1iKO compared to controls, and 11 genes are up-regulated. 103 are down-regulated in Unc5biECKO compared to controls, while the same 11 are up-regulated. 101 genes are up-regulated in Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+ compared to Unc5biECKO, and 18 are down-regulated (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–H). Both Unc5b and Ntn1 positively regulate this gene set across arterioles, arterial capillaries, venous capillaries, and venules. Overactivation of β-catenin up-regulates BRB genes that are down-regulated by Unc5b deletion (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–H), confirming that β-catenin can rescue or compensate for loss of Netrin-1 and Unc5b to maintain the BRB gene expression program.

To investigate the arteriovenous regulation of BRB genes between Netrin-1/Unc5b and Norrin/Lrp5 signaling, we analyzed expression of arterial-enriched and vein/capillary-enriched BRB proteins in Unc5biECKO and Lrp5iECKO P12 retinas (Fig. 5 F–M). TAM injection from P6-P8 and retina analysis at P12 showed decreased expression of the arterial-enriched Cthrc1 protein in Unc5biECKO arterioles compared to littermate controls, while Lrp5iECKO retinas did not exhibit any significant change (Fig. 5 F–I). Conversely, the vein/capillary-enriched marker Mfsd2a trended downward in Unc5biECKO mutants but was strongly decreased in Lrp5iECKO veins and arteries, compared to their respective littermate controls (Fig. 5 J–M). Finally, the vein-enriched marker Lef1 was decreased in Ntn1iKO, Unc5biECKO, and Lrp5iECKO veins and arteries (Fig. 5 N–S).

Discussion

Our study reveals that BRB integrity is zonated across arterioles, capillaries, and venules. BRB integrity is regulated by Netrin-1/Unc5b on the arterial side, while BRB integrity on the venular side is regulated by Norrin/Lrp5, and both signaling pathways intersect at the level of the capillaries, as shown in double heterozygous Unc5bfl/WT;Lrp5fl/wtiECKO retinas.

Using scRNA-seq of postnatal BRB-competent retina ECs queried against expression of BBB-enriched genes, we uncover an EC-specific gene expression program composed of >100 genes that are expressed in the P12 BRB. These encode Wnt signaling components, tight junction proteins, nutrient and ion transporters, and other genes. Most of these genes exhibit zonated expression across arterioles, arterial capillaries, and venules. Such genes include the lipid transporter Mfsd2a, the glucose transporter Glut1 (Slc2a1), the gene for the multidrug efflux protein pGP (Abcb1a), the Lef1 transcription factor that lies downstream of Norrin signaling and Unc5b, a Netrin-1 receptor that regulates BBB integrity. mRNA and protein expression profiles for Unc5b and Cthrc1 are enriched on the arterial side, and Lef1, Slc16a1, Slc38a5 mRNA and protein are enriched in venules (51, 52). It remains to be determined how well other gene and protein expression profiles correlate at the BRB.

Norrin-mediated β-catenin signaling is known to protect the BRB, with preferential activity on the venous side (18, 22, 23). Netrin-1-Unc5b controls BRB integrity, with regulation of Claudin-5 and Plvap more on the arterial side when compared to the venous one, prompting us to query how pathways intersect during BRB development. Mechanistically, scRNA-seq of Ntn1 and Unc5b KO ECs revealed decreased β-catenin signaling along with down-regulated expression of β-catenin BRB gene targets Cldn5, Lef1, Slc2a1, Mfsd2a, Apcdd1, Nkd1 as well as Abcb1a and the thyroid hormone transporter Slco1c1. Functionally, we show genetic interaction between Unc5b and Ctnnb1 and between Unc5b and Lrp5 in retinal ECs. Heterozygous deletions in Unc5b, Ctnnb1, or Lrp5 displayed BRB-competent Claudin-5+/Plvap− ECs while combined heterozygous deletions converted ECs into a leaky Claudin-5−/Plvap+ phenotype. The leak in Lrp5/Unc5b double heterozygotes was mainly restricted to capillaries, suggesting that genetic pathway interaction mainly protects the capillary BRB. The Plvap conversion and the vascular leakage in Lrp5iECKOs occurred mainly in veins and capillaries, confirming previous reports (18, 22, 23), revealing opposite zonation in Norrin and Netrin-1 mediated BRB regulation.

Despite this opposing zonation, endothelial-specific β-catenin overactivation in Unc5biECKO mice rescued a BRB-competent state, preventing leakage to injected tracers, and increased expression levels of BRB genes when compared to Unc5biECKO ECs across arteries, capillaries, and veins. Hence, β-catenin overactivation in Unc5biECKO could either support an epistatic rescue or act via a compensatory mechanism that remains to be elucidated.

Interaction between β-catenin and Unc5b involves Lrp5, as we show biochemically, and through genetic interaction. Mice that have one floxed allele of Unc5b, Ctnnb1, or Lrp5 do not exhibit a BRB leak, but mice that are heterozygous flox for both Unc5b and Lrp5 or both Unc5b and Ctnnb1 convert capillary ECs to a leaky phenotype. These data are consistent with Netrin-1/Unc5b signaling acting via β-catenin signaling in BRB stabilization in capillaries. Lrp6 is a close homologue of Lrp5 that is also expressed by retinal ECs (53) and likely contributes to retinal angiogenesis and BRB formation (18, 53). Similarly, gene dosage experiments suggest that Frizzled receptors other than Frizzled4 are likely at play. Also, the Gpr124/Reck coreceptors are expressed in retinal ECs, and play a modest functional role that can be detected upon Norrin signaling impairment (22). Interestingly, despite functional redundancies between the molecular composition and genetic determinants of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, phosphorylation of Lrp6 was unaffected by Unc5b deletion in retinas, while its phosphorylation was reduced in brain ECs (34).

Netrin-1 and Norrin additively stimulate β-catenin transcription. In vitro, we observed that combined stimulation of ECs with Netrin-1 and Norrin additively regulated Lrp5 but not Lrp6 phosphorylation while combined stimulation of ECs with Netrin-1 and Wnt7a additively regulated Lrp6 phosphorylation. These data suggest that Netrin-1 can cooperate with Norrin and Wnt7a to induce β-catenin activity above the specific threshold required to maintain CNS ECs in a barrier-competent state (18, 22, 23, 54, 55). Opposing arteriovenous zonation between Unc5b and Lrp5 may confer additional BRB protection in arterial capillaries and arterioles and perhaps ensure tailored nutrient supply to adjacent neuronal cells, but this requires further investigation. Arteriovenous zonation is conserved between mouse and human brain ECs, but the specific gene expression signatures differ between species (39), prompting further investigation.

ScRNA-seq showed that only a few retinal cell types expressed low levels of Ntn1 mRNA, suggesting that Netrin-1 may come from the circulation, from the heart, lungs, trachea, or adipose tissue, all of which have higher Ntn1 mRNA levels as compared to the retina (56).

We demonstrate that phosphorylation of Lrp5 at sites Y1473 and T1492 is required for BRB function wherein Netrin-1 binding to retinal endothelial Unc5b maintains Lrp5 phosphorylation at both phosphosites. The GSK3 and CK1 families are the most prominent kinases that have been implicated in Lrp5 and Lrp6 phosphorylation (57, 58). In particular, CK1 was shown to phosphorylate sites in PPPSPxS and S/T motifs such as Lrp5 T1492, as well as conserved YDRxH regions including Lrp5 Y1473, all enabling Wnt signal transduction (57, 58). PPPSPxS phosphorylation provides a docking site for the adapter protein Axin1, which inhibits the β-catenin destruction complex and promotes nuclear β-catenin translocation, resulting in activation of Norrin-induced-Lef1 signaling and BRB gene expression regulation (22, 59). Unc5b interaction with Lrp5 required the scaffolding protein Dlg1, which was previously shown to maintain the BRB and interact with Tspan12 and Norrin in the cerebellar and hindbrain BBB (33). Loss of endothelial Dlg1 function converted retina and brain ECs to a leaky, Claudin-5−/Plvap+ state, and Dlg1 genetically interacted with Ndp, Fz4, and Tspan12 to regulate BRB and BBB integrity, via interaction with proteins other than Frizzled receptors (33). We could coimmunoprecipitate Unc5b with Dlg1 and show that this interaction is enhanced by Netrin-1 binding to Unc5b. Moreover, we show that siRNA-mediated Dlg1 knockdown in brain ECs decreased Unc5b pulldown of Lrp5 and Lrp6 and phosphorylation by Netrin-1. Hence, our data suggest that Dlg1 enhances both WNT- and Norrin-β-catenin signaling via Unc5b, providing mechanistic insight into pathway convergence. Further experiments will need to clarify how this interaction occurs. Toward this goal, we show that Dlg1 can bind to the intracellular Unc5b-UPA domain, which was already shown to be required for its interaction with Lrp6 (34).

Despite the evidence that BRB gene regulation of Unc5b depended on β-catenin, we observed differences between these two pathways. The arteriovenous zonation differences discussed above deserve further investigation and likely involve β-catenin-independent Unc5b signaling pathways in arteries that remain to be identified. Our data are consistent with two alternative and nonmutually exclusive scenarios, wherein Netrin-1/Unc5b and Norrin/β-catenin signaling may converge on a similar transcriptional signature, via an alternative signal transduction machinery and distinct (co)- transcriptional effectors. Alternatively, BRB opening in capillaries may induce inflammatory signals that further destabilize the arterial BRB gene expression program.

Another interesting difference concerned retinal angiogenesis regulation by Unc5b and Norrin-β-catenin signaling. Unc5b deletion did not affect formation of the deeper vascular layers, while Ndp, Fz4, Lrp5, and Ctnnb1 mutation prevents formation of these vessels (18, 23, 53, 60). Mechanistically, we speculate that Unc5b deletion maintains β-catenin levels sufficiently high to induce vessel diving, but not high enough to maintain BRB gene expression. This supports that endothelial Unc5b signaling levels are carefully controlled during BRB development. In line with this idea, dysregulation of Netrin-1 expression in human patients with diabetic retinopathy was shown to contribute to vascular leak and macular edema (35). Current treatments targeting ocular diseases mostly comprise antiangiogenic/anti-VEGF treatments. Our results uncover therapeutic potential in targeting permeability while keeping angiogenesis intact.

Recent studies showed that engineering of Wnt/Norrin signaling ligands into BBB-specific Wnt activators enables BBB repair in brain tumors and ischemic stroke (61). Other studies have developed agonists that elicit a β-catenin signaling response (62) and that induce BRB stabilization and prevention of diabetic macular edema, including an agonistic antibody that activates the Frizzled4 Norrin receptor (63). Approaches to enhance Netrin-1 binding to endothelial Unc5b could synergize with such therapies and offer more broad application through the regulation of both the BBB and the BRB.

Materials and Methods

Mouse Models.

All protocols and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Unc5bfl/fl mice (34) were bred with Cdh5CreERT2 mice (45). β-catenin GOF Ctnnb1flex/3 mice, Ctnnb1fl/fl mice, and Ntn1fl/fl mice were described previously (43, 49, 63–66). Lrp5flfl mice were a gift from Dr. Julie Goodwin at Yale University. Gene deletion or overactivation was induced by injection of tamoxifen (Sigma T5648) diluted in corn oil (Sigma C8267). Postnatal gene deletion was induced by 3 injections of 100μg of tamoxifen at P6, P7, and P8 for analysis at P12. All mice were on a C57/Bl6J background. Mice from both sexes were used.

Retina Dissociation and Sample Preparation for Single-Cell RNA Sequencing.

Ntn1flfl and Unc5bflfl controls and corresponding littermate Ntn1iKO and Unc5biECKO, as well as Unc5biECKO;Ctnnb1flex3/+and corresponding littermate Unc5biECKO mice were TAM injected from P6 to P8. P12 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized (IACUC guidelines) by cervical dislocation. 4-8 retinas from each group were dissected in cold DPBS and dissociated with the Neural Tissue Dissociation kit (P) (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-092-628). The retinas were digested 5 min in buffer P supplemented with DNase I (2,000 U/mL, Qiagen #79254). The digestion was stopped by adding FBS (10% final) and the cells were immediately filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. After centrifugation (300 g for 3 min), the cells were resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2 mM EDTA and 0.5% BSA. The single-cell suspension was enriched for ECs using CD31 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-097-418) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library preparation, sequencing, and scRNA-seq bioinformatic analysis are described in SI Appendix.

Retina Whole-Mount Immunostaining.

Eyes were collected and placed in 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 to 20 min. Formaldehyde was replaced with PBS, retinas were dissected and were stored in 100% methanol at -20°C overnight and then washed 3 × 10 min with PBS and incubated with specific antibodies in blocking buffer (1% fetal bovine serum, 3% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.01% Na deoxycholate, 0.02% Sodium Azide in PBS at pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. The next day, retinas were washed 3 × 10 min and incubated with secondary antibody in buffer containing PBS pH 6.8, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM MnCl2 at room temperature for 1 h, then washed 3 × 10 min, cut into petals and mounted using fluorescent mounting medium (DAKO). For mice injected with tracers, retinas were dissected in 4% formaldehyde to ensure dye fixation within the tissue during retina dissection. The list of primary and secondary antibodies used in this study is described in SI Appendix.

Cell Culture.

C57BL/6 Mouse Primary Retinal Microvascular Endothelial Cells (C57-6065) and C57BL/6 Mouse Primary Brain Microvascular Endothelial Cells (C57-6023) were purchased from Cell Biologics. HUVECs were from Lonza (C2519A). Cells were cultured in complete mouse endothelial cell medium (Cell biologics—M1168) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 and split when confluent using Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) (Life Technologies, 25300054). Cell stimulation with recombinant protein as well as transfection methods are described in SI Appendix.

Western Blot.

Retinas were dissected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. They were lysed in RIPA buffer (Research products, R26200-250.0) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, 11836170001 and 4906845001) using a Tissue-Lyser (5 × 5 min at 30 shakes/second). For western blot on cell culture, cells were washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. All protein lysates were centrifuged 15 min at 13,200 rpm at 4 °C and supernatants were isolated. Protein concentrations were quantified by the BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, 23225) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 30ug of protein were diluted in Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610747) boiled at 95 °C for 5 min and loaded in 4 to 15% acrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, 5678084). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred on a nitrocellulose membrane and incubated in TBS 0.1% Tween supplemented with 5% BSA for 1 h to block nonspecific binding. The list of primary and secondary antibodies used in this study is described in SI Appendix. Method for immunoprecipitation is described in SI Appendix.

Intravenous and Intraperitoneal Injection of Tracer.

Sulfo-NHS-biotin tracer was injected intraperitoneally in P12 mice at a concentration of 10 mg/mL (300uL per mouse) and left to circulate for 1 h.

Confocal Microscopy and Image Analysis.

Confocal images were acquired on a laser scanning fluorescence microscope (Leica SP8) using the LASX software. 10×, 20×, and 63× immersion objectives were used for acquisition using selective laser excitation (405, 488, 547, or 647 nm). Quantification of vascular density and pixel intensity are described in SI Appendix.

Statistical Analysis.

All in vivo experiments were done on littermates with similar body weight per condition. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. The Mann–Whitney U test was performed for statistical analysis of two groups. ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed for statistical analysis between 3 or more groups.
