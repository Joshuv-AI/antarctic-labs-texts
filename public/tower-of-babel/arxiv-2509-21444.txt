Introduction

The historical evolution of homotopy theory has been fundamentally driven by the pursuit of computing homotopy groups. The endeavor to compute homotopy groups has led to the creation of numerous refined instruments and conceptual frameworks, such as the Adams spectral sequence, the EHP spectral sequence, the motivic homotopy theory, and the Goodwillie calculus. These are not merely of intrinsic importance to topology, but also resonate widely across mathematics, shaping the development of homological algebra, algebraic geometry,
K
-theory, category theory and other fields.

Recent advances, fueled by the motivic homotopy theory and computer science, make great progress on computing the stable homotopy groups of spheres, see Wang and Xu
[
33

90

[

[

settled the last case of the Kervaire invariant one problem; by deploying the motivic homotopy theory in conjunction with their calculations of the stable homotopy groups of spheres, they proved that there exist framed manifolds of Kervaire invariant one in dimension 126.

Yet, it should be stressed that, despite the long-established knowledge of many stable homotopy groups of spheres, their unstable analogues remain largely beyond our grasp; at best, one can probe the metastable range by appealing to their stable behavior and probe some unstable periodic elements by the
