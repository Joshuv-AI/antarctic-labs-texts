A Proliferating Nematic That Collectively Senses an Anisotropic Substrate
Toshi Parmar, Fridtjof Brauns, Yimin Luo, M. Cristina Marchetti

Abstract
Motivated by recent experiments on growing fibroblasts, we examine the development of nematic order in a colony of elongated cells proliferating on a nematic elastomer substrate. After sparse seeding, the cells divide and grow into locally ordered, but randomly oriented, domains that then interact with each other and the substrate. Global alignment with the substrate is only achieved above a critical density, suggesting a collective mechanism for the sensing of substrate anisotropy. The system jams at high density, where both reorientation and proliferation stop. Using a continuum model of a proliferating nematic liquid crystal, we examine the competition between growth-driven alignment and substrate-driven alignment in controlling the density and structure of the final jammed state. We propose that anisotropic traction forces and the tendency of cells to align perpendicular to the direction of density gradients act in concert to provide a mechanism for collective cell alignment.



I
Introduction

Cell proliferation has recently been in the spotlight as a yet relatively unexplored source of activity that engenders large fluctuations and non-linear growth kinetics, which are in turn affected by mechanical stresses
Hallatschek et al. 2023
. For elongated cells that divide along their long axis, growth and division can be thought of as a source of extensile activity
Ranft et al. 2010
. The resulting active stresses are known to provide a mechanism for driving
local
alignment in active nematic liquid crystals even when the system is isotropic when passive
Santhosh et al. 2020
;
Srivastava et al. 2016
. Activity alone, however, is not sufficient to generate
global
alignment. Proliferation generated mechanical stresses have indeed been shown to
locally
align elongated cells like motile bacteria
Wang et al. 2011
;
You et al. 2018
, but global alignment in monolayer of elongated cells has only been observed in the presence of external cues. Examples include vascular endothelial cells aligned by fluid shear flow
Resnick et al. 2003
, fibroblasts that align with the extracellular matrix (ECM) they deposit
Li et al. 2017
;
Duclos et al. 2014
;
Adar and Joanny 2024
;
Guillamat et al. 2025
, non-motile bacteria under spatial confinement
Volfson et al. 2008
;
You et al. 2021
, confined amniserosa cells in drosophila embryo
Ray et al. 2024
, actin fibers in
Hydra
guided morphogen gradients
Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2020
, and various elongated cells guided by micro- or nano-grooves on surfaces
Lacroix et al. 2024
.

Recent experiments by one of us have examined the behavior of human dermal fibroblasts (hdFs) grown on a Liquid Crystal Elastomer (LCE) substrate
Luo et al. 2023
.
In these experiments cells are seeded at low density (
{\sim}50~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
), then grow and divide along their long axis, organizing in locally aligned high density domains separated by low density disordered regions. At intermediate densities (
{\sim}200~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
), cells show significant alignment to one another, but not to the substrate, regardless of whether the LCE substrate is in its isotropic or nematic state. Upon a further increase in density, however, the behavior on isotropic and nematic substrates is distinctly different. On isotropic substrates no further alignment is observed as the density continues to increase. On nematic substrates the ordered domains grow in size and align with the direction of order of the substrate, resulting in alignment with the substrate at the global scale of the entire monolayer.
At even higher densities (
\sim$500\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
) cells jam orientationally and eventually (around
700\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}
) growth ceases. The monolayer then is in an arrested state with jammed topological defects, as previously seen in epithelial cells
Angelini et al. 2011
;
Garcia et al. 2015
;
Puliafito et al. 2012
;
Bi et al. 2016
;
Lawson-Keister and Manning 2021
;
Jacques et al. 2023
;
Sarkar et al. 2023
. Importantly, the degree of nematic order in the jammed state depends on the initial seeding density, with lower seeding density leading to more ordered states.

This system is different from the ones mentioned above that align through external cues because here individual cells do not align with the substrate.
Instead, the alignment only occurs through
collective
sensing,
making it clear that both interaction with the LCE substrate and steric effects are crucial for global alignment.

Two questions then stand out: (i) Why are cells at intermediate densities locally aligned to one another but not to the substrate? (ii) Why does the degree of order of the final jammed state inversely depend on the initial seeding density? An intuition for the second question may come from epithelial monolayers, where sparsely seeded cell layers grow into more correlated monolayers
Garcia et al. 2015
.

To address these questions, we formulate a hydrodynamic theory for a proliferating nematic that aligns with external cues and is capable of jamming. We first present a new analysis of experimental data
Luo et al. 2023
based on a k-nearest neighbor method that allows us to construct continuum fields in systems with strong density fluctuations by averaging over topological instead of metric neighbors
Biau and Devroye 2015
.
This analysis enables us to tease out the role of density gradients in driving cell alignment.
The mechanism through which cells collectively sense the molecular-scale order of the substrate is not known, but a couple of observations provide some cues. First, suppressing focal adhesions impedes the onset of global alignment
Luo et al. 2023
. Second, the substrate is too rigid for substrate-mediated interactions to play a role.
Inspired by these observations, we modify the standard nemato-hydrodynamic equations
Marchetti et al. 2013
to allow for cell growth and alignment with the LCE substrate. We consider two ways of modeling alignment:
first, an explicit “external field” that tends to align cells with the substrate and, second, anisotropic traction forces resulting in anisotropic friction.

We first examine the interplay of alignment due to the external field and due to steric effects in a minimal spatially homogeneous model. The observation that individual cells do not sense the order of the substrate is incorporated by assuming that the alignment to the external field is proportional to cell density. This simple model explains the observation that a low initial seeding density results in a more ordered state at long times as arising from the competition between the rate of cell growth and the rate of cell reorientation that drives nematic order. Essentially, at low seeding density, appreciable order can build up before cells jam due to steric effects.

Guided by our analysis of the experiments which shows a correlation between cell orientation and density gradients (Fig.
2
(d,e)), we then allow for spatial inhomogeneities to tease out the mechanisms that drive collective sensing of substrate order. We formulate a spatially inhomogeneous model where substrate alignment is driven by anisotropic tractions, which we show are as effective as the external field in driving global alignment with the substrate. In addition, anisotropic tractions result in anisotropic density fluctuations, with steeper density gradients in the direction of substrate order. This enhances proliferation along this direction, smoothing out gradients and aligning neighboring cell patches. In other words, anisotropic tractions and pressure gradients together provide a mechanism for collective sensing of substrate order.

Finally, unlike what is observed in dense bacteria colonies
Copenhagen et al. 2020
and in confluent epithelia
Saw et al. 2017
, the cores of the frozen-in topological defects in the jammed state are regions of low density for
both
the
+1/2
and the
-1/2
defects. This arises from our assumption that cells can pack better when aligned, hence density is higher in regions of high nematic order. In contrast, disordered defect cores tend to be depleted of cells. This behavior is consistent with experiments and arises from the fact that adhesive couplings are very weak in fibroblasts, and interactions are dominated by steric effects. It is also useful to note that a similar behavior is observed in active nematics made from microtubule–kinesin mixtures, where defect cores are indeed void of microtubules
Sanchez et al. 2012
.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
II
we present a new analysis of experimental data that demonstrates the correlation between density gradients and cell orientation. In Section
III
we formulate the continuum model of a proliferating nematic capable of jamming. In Section
IV
we examine the competition between cell growth and substrate-driven alignment in the case of a spatially homogeneous system. In Section
V
we consider the spatially extended model and show that anisotropic tractions and density fluctuations together provide a mechanism for collective cell alignment to the substrate. We conclude in Section
VI
with a summary of our results and some comments on open questions. Section
VII
gives details on data analysis and computational methods.

II
Interplay of Ordering and Density in hdF-LCE System

Here we describe a new analysis of previously published experimental data for hdF cells growing on an LCE substrate
Luo et al. 2023
. To construct continuum fields from discrete experimental data we use a
k
-nearest neighbor (kNN) method, which allows us to describe non-confluent monolayers, with large density fluctuations and empty regions between cells. Let the
k
cell nuclei nearest to an arbitrary query point
\mathbf{x}
be labeled by
j=1,2,\cdots,k
. Each of the
k
elongated nuclei is located at a distance
r_{j}
from
\mathbf{x}
and oriented along the angle
\phi_{j}
measured from the positive
x
-axis. For a graphical illustration of the method see Fig.
7
.
The coarse-grained density and nematic order parameter fields are then defined as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\pi(\max[r_{j}])^{2}}\;,

(1)

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}e^{2i\phi_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}}\;,

(2)

where
\max[r_{j}]
is the largest of
r_{j}
among the
k
-neighbors and

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[{-\dfrac{r_{j}^{2}}{\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}^{2}}}\right]\;.

(3)

The complex coarse-grained order parameter field
q(\mathbf{x})
can also be written as
q(\mathbf{x})=S(\mathbf{x})e^{2i\theta(\mathbf{x})}
, where
S(\mathbf{x})=|q(\mathbf{x})|
is the magnitude of the order parameter field and
\theta(\mathbf{x})
the director angle field. In our analysis below we use
k=10
and have verified that our results are robust to variations of
k
.

To quantify nematic order we introduce three average quantities:

\displaystyle\bar{S}

\displaystyle={\langle|q\rho|\rangle}_{\mathbf{x}}/\langle\rho\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}

(4)

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|\langle q\rho\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}|^{2}}/\langle\rho\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}

(5)

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{Re}\,q\rho\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}/\langle\rho\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}\;,

(6)

where the brackets denote a spatial average over the entire system. The quantities are then averaged over ensembles.

Here
\bar{S}
measures average local cell-cell nematic alignment and is finite in a state with large randomly oriented nematic domains with an intermediate size that is much larger than the cell spacing but smaller than the system size.
It will be referred to in the following as the local order parameter.

\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}
quantifies global cell-cell order and
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}
captures global alignment with the substrate’s direction of broken symmetry, chosen by convention as the
x
-axis. The cell-substrate order,
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}
, ranges from
-1
for monolayers aligned with the y-axis to
+1
for monolayers aligned with the x-axis. Note that
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}<\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}<\bar{S}
, i.e. local order is a requisite for global order, and global order is a prerequisite for cell-substrate order.
The distinction between the three order parameters is illustrated in the cartoons shown in Fig.
1
(c–f).
A monolayer globally aligned to the substrate has high values of
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}
,
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}
and
\bar{S}
(see Fig.
1
(a)). A monolayer that is globally ordered but not aligned with the substrate would have finite
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}
and
\bar{S}
, but small
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}
(see Fig.
1
(b)).
A monolayer with aligned but randomly oriented domains has finite local order
\bar{S}
, but vanishing global and cell-substrate order in the limit of large system size (
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}\simeq 0
and
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}\simeq 0
, see Fig.
1
(c)).
Finally, a monolayer that is disordered on the cell scale has
S\approx 0
and thus also vanishing global and cell-substrate order (see Fig.
1
(d)).
Global order clearly also implies local order, but not the opposite. Similarly, a small value of global order necessitates low cell-substrate order, but not vice versa.

The cell-substrate order shows a strong dependence on the density, as can be seen in Fig.
1
(e). Below
300\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}
, there is little-to-no cell-substrate order in the monolayer. Order, however, develops sharply at higher density and plateaus around
550\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}
at
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}\approx 0.7
, where the monolayer jams orientationally. We see a significant dependence of the cell-substrate order at jamming on the initial seeding density, namely smaller seeding densities lead to more aligned monolayers (see Fig.
1
(f)).

Regions of appreciable local cell-cell order consist of aligned domains separated by topological defects (Fig.
2
(a,b)). We find that at intermediate to high mean densities (
500
–
900\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}
), the defect cores are regions of low cell density for both the comet-shaped
+1/2
(Fig.
2
(e)) and the trefoil-shaped
-1/2
(Fig.
2
(f)) defects. This is in contrast to previous studies on different cell types where it was observed that cells accumulate at defect cores (neural progenitor cells
Zhao et al. 2025
and
Myxococcus xanthus
bacteria
Copenhagen et al. 2020
) or are even extruded due to the large compressive stress that develop in this region (epithelia
Saw et al. 2017
). At even higher densities (
\gtrsim$1300\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}$
), however, fibroblasts have been found to form mounds at the core of
+1/2
-defects where the system starts forming a second cell layer
Sarkar et al. 2023
. Details on calculating the density near defect cores are presented in Sec.
VII.1
.

The orientation of individual cells is affected by mechanical stress exerted by neighboring cells, and hence, by the density of cells. We observe that the cells’ orientation tends to be orthogonal to density gradients (see Fig.
2
(a-d)). In other words, cells tend to align parallel to the interface separating high and low density regions. The same behavior has been observed before in proliferating bacteria
Dell’Arciprete et al. 2018
and is consistent with what is expected in extensile active fluids
Blow et al. 2014
. In the following we use the above observations to formulate a continuum nemato-hydrodynamic theory.

III
A Proliferating Nematic Capable of Jamming

To capture the interplay between cell growth and orientation, we consider a minimal continuum model where the steady-state nematic texture minimizes the Landau-de Gennes free energy, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}r\left[f_{\mathrm{LdG}}(\mathbf{Q})+f_{\chi}(\mathbf{Q})+f_{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\right]\;,

(7)

with free energy densities

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{LdG}}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=f_{\mathrm{LdG}}^{0}(\mathbf{Q})+\frac{K\rho}{2\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}}|\mathbf{\nabla Q}|^{2}\;,

(8)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{LdG}}^{0}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{a(\rho)}{2}|\mathbf{Q}^{2}|+\frac{b(\rho)}{4}|\mathbf{Q}^{2}|^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle f_{\chi}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\chi}Q_{ij}(\partial_{i}\rho)(\partial_{j}\rho)\;,

(10)

\displaystyle f_{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=-\tilde{\Pi}\rho P_{ij}Q_{ij}\;.

(11)

The real order parameter field
Q_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
is a symmetric traceless tensor related to the complex order parameter
q(\mathbf{x})
by
q=Q_{xx}+iQ_{xy}
, with
Q_{yy}=-Q_{xx}
and
Q_{yx}=Q_{xy}
. It can also be written in the familiar form
Q_{ij}=S(n_{i}n_{j}-\delta_{ij}/2)
, where
S
measures the degree of nematic order and
\mathbf{n}=\left(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\right)
is the director field that identifies the direction of broken orientational symmetry, with
\mathbf{n}=-\mathbf{n}
and
|{\mathbf{n}}|=1
.
The first two terms in Eq. (
8
) control the equilibrium state in the absence of substrate coupling, with
a(\rho)=a_{0}(1-\rho/\rho_{\mathrm{IN}})
and
b(\rho)=2a_{0}(1+\rho/\rho_{\mathrm{IN}})
. The third term denotes nematic elasticity in a one constant approximation, which we scale with density, as is seen in simulations of hard rods
Revignas and Ferrarini 2023
. When
\tilde{\Pi}=0
the steady state is controlled by the isotropic–nematic transition density
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
. For
\rho<\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
the cell layer is isotropic, with
S=0
. For
\rho>\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
the cell layer is in a nematic state, with
S=\sqrt{(\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{IN}})/(\rho+\rho_{\mathrm{IN}})}
,
which saturates to
1
for
\rho\gg\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
.
In the absence of alignment terms (
\chi=\tilde{\Pi}=0
), the direction of broken symmetry is chosen spontaneously. In the following all densities are measured in units of
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
, unless otherwise indicated.

The free energy density
f_{\chi}
couples orientation to density gradients, favoring alignment of the director normal to density gradients for
\tilde{\chi}>0
(as seen in Fig.
2
(d)). We define a non-dimensional parameter
\chi=\tilde{\chi}\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}^{2}/K
to refer to this coupling strength.
Finally,
f_{\Pi}
describes the interaction with the substrate modeled as an external field
P_{ij}
that aligns cells with the direction of nematic order in the substrate.
In the following we consider a uniform substrate anisotropy aligned, by convention, with the
x
-axis:
P_{xx}=-P_{yy}=1
and
P_{xy}=P_{yx}=0
. The strength of alignment is controlled by the energy scale
\tilde{\Pi}
. Collective sensing of the substrate is incorporated via the factor of cell density in
f_{\Pi}
. We note that such extra-cellular guiding term that is density and order dependent has also been proposed earlier to explain density dependent isotropic–nematic transition in human Melanocytes
Kemkemer et al. 2000
.

To describe the ordering dynamics we assume that cells are seeded at a density
\langle\rho(t=0)\rangle=\rho_{\mathrm{seed}}
and then proliferate, with the density growing logistically up to a characteristic carrying capacity
\rho_{c}(S)
, (defined as the number of cells that can attach to a given substrate area), which depends on the local nematic order. This dependence incorporates the fact that an aligned monolayer can accommodate a larger carrying capacity. The biological motivation for this is the reduced density seen in defect cores which are regions of vanishing nematic order. The nematic order parameter in turn evolves with relaxational dynamics controlled by the free energy
\mathcal{F}
, while getting advected, rotated, and aligned by cellular flows with velocity
\mathbf{v}
. The coupled dynamics of density and nematic order is then given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})=\frac{\rho}{\tau_{g}}\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{c}(S)}\right)\;,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{t}\mathbf{Q}=\lambda(\rho)\bm{E}+\frac{1}{\gamma(\rho)}\mathbf{H}+\Delta(\rho)\,\mathbf{N}\;,

(13)

with
\tau_{g}
the characteristic proliferation time at low density. The material derivative on the right hand side of Eq. (
13
) is given by
\mathcal{D}_{t}\mathbf{Q}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}+\nabla_{k}(v_{k}\mathbf{Q})+\mathbf{Q}\cdot\bm{\Omega}-\bm{\Omega}\cdot\mathbf{Q}
, with
\Omega_{ij}=(\partial_{j}v_{i}-\partial_{i}v_{j})/2
and

E_{ij}=(\partial_{j}v_{i}+\partial_{i}v_{j}-\delta_{ij}\partial_{k}v_{k})/2
.
The molecular field is
\mathbf{H}=\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mathbf{Q}}\right]^{T}
, where
[\cdot]^{T}
denotes the traceless part. Rotational friction
\gamma(\rho)
and flow alignment
\lambda(\rho)
are assumed to depend on density to ensure that the dynamics of
\mathbf{Q}
and cell reorientation cease upon jamming. We choose the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{\gamma}_{0}}~\Theta{\left(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)-\rho\right)}\;,

(14)

\displaystyle\lambda(\rho)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}~H{\left(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)-\rho\right)}\;,

(15)

where
\Theta(x)=xH(x)
, with
H(x)
the Heaviside step function.
The jamming density
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}
is assumed to depend on the degree of nematic order,

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(0)+(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(1)-\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(0))S^{2}\;,

(16)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(0)
and
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(1)
are the jamming densities in the isotropic and nematic state, respectively.
We set
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(1)>\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(0)
because aligned cells can pack better, and thus, jam at a higher density.
Estimates of the various densities in the system are provided in Appendix
A
.
In general the density
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}
where orientational order freezes and the density
\rho_{c}
where cells stop dividing (the carrying capacity) can be different. For simplicity, we choose their ratio to be constant, with

\displaystyle\rho_{c}(S)=r_{\mathrm{J}}\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)\;.

(17)

The ratio
r_{\mathrm{J}}
will be an important parameter of the model. This choice is motivated by the form of the equation of state, where pressure is chosen to increase exponentially as the system reaches jamming
Volfson et al. 2008
,

\displaystyle p(\rho,S)=Be^{\rho/\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)}\;.

(18)

Here,
B
plays the role of a bulk modulus, akin to an inverse compressibility.
As the system reaches carrying capacity
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
, pressure becomes uniform, implying that density inhomogeneities can exist post jamming without creating flows.

Finally, the
Q
-dynamics includes a non-conserved Gaussian white noise
N_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t)
with zero mean, variance
\Delta(\rho)=\eta_{Q}\rho/\gamma(\rho)
and correlations

\displaystyle\langle{N}_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t){N}_{kl}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=J_{ijkl}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;,

(19)

with
J_{ijkl}=\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\right)/2
.

We assume that dissipation is dominated by friction with the substrate. The cell flow velocity is then determined by force balance in the Stokes limit

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}\rho v_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}^{a}-\partial_{i}p\;,

(20)

The first term of the right hand side of Eq. (
20
) is the gradient of the active stress with deviatoric component

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}^{a}=\alpha(\rho)Q_{ij}\;.

(21)

The isotropic part of the active stress has been incorporated in the pressure. The activity is taken of the form

\displaystyle\alpha(\rho)=\alpha\Theta(\rho_{J}(S)-\rho)\;,

(22)

with
\alpha<0
corresponding to extensile activity.
Fibroblast colonies exhibit various active processes that may be relevant on different time and length scales. Individual cells crawl on the substrate at speeds of around
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}

Luo et al. 2023
and reverse their direction of motion roughly every hour
Duclos et al. 2014
. On time scales shorter than the reversal time, cells move by generating contractile force dipoles. At longer times the back-and-forth motion along the cell’s long axis effectively yields extensile stresses. Growth and division also generate extensile stresses on the timescales of cell division. The cell’s doubling time is around 50–
60\text{\,}\mathrm{h}

Luo et al. 2023
. In this study, we focus on the two dominant long-time sources of anisotropic active stresses in the monolayer, both extensile in nature: cell growth and back-and-forth motion of cells along their long axis.
Notably, as we shall see below, these anisotropic active stresses are not essential for the collective alignment of cells on an anisotropic substrate. Rather it is the isotropic proliferation-induced pressure gradient that drives collective alignment.

IV
Homogeneous proliferating nematic

We first consider a spatially homogeneous system to understand the interplay between growth, jamming, and alignment to the substrate modeled as an external field. The equilibrium nematic texture minimizes the Landau–de Gennes free energy, given by
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{A}f(Q_{ij})
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the area of the system and
f(Q_{ij})=f_{LG}^{0}(Q_{ij})+f_{\Pi}(Q_{ij})
. With no spatial gradients and vanishing velocity, the coupled dynamics of density and nematic tensor simply become

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{\tau_{g}}\left(1-\frac{\rho}{r_{\mathrm{J}}\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)}\right)\;,

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{ij}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma(\rho)}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{0}}{\delta Q_{ij}}\;.

(24)

In the following, we rescale times with
\tau_{g}
, densities with
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
, and energies with
a_{0}
, defining
\Pi=\tilde{\Pi}/a_{0}
and
\gamma_{0}=\tilde{\gamma}_{0}/(a_{0}\tau_{g})=\tau_{Q}/\tau_{g}
, where
\tau_{Q}=\tilde{\gamma}_{0}/a_{0}
is the nematic relaxation time. The coupled equations for order parameter, director angle, and density then read

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\Theta{(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)-\rho)}\Big[S(1-\rho+(1+\rho)S^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad-2\Pi\rho\cos{2\theta}\Big],

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}S}\Theta{(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)-\rho)\Pi\rho}\sin{2\theta},

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=\rho\left(1-\frac{\rho}{r_{\mathrm{J}}\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)}\right)\;,

(27)

with initial condition
\rho(t=0)=\rho_{\mathrm{seed}}
,
S(t=0)=0
and randomly chosen
\theta(t=0)
. In the homogeneous system, once the angle variable aligns due to the external field, the average local order parameter (
\bar{S}
), the global order parameter (
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{g}}
), and the cell-substrate order parameter (
\bar{S}_{\mathrm{cs}}
) all coincide and will be simply denoted by
S
.

The equations contain three dimensionless parameters:

\gamma_{0}
, which represents the ratio of the relaxation time of the nematic to the time scale of cell growth; the alignment ratio
\Pi
, which is a ratio of the strengths of alignment to nematic substrate and alignment driven by steric interaction; and the jamming density ratio
r_{\mathrm{J}}
, which is the ratio of the carrying capacity to the density at which orientations jam.

We now examine the steady states of the system and their stability for the three cases discussed in Fig.
3
. The nullclines of the system are
h(\rho,S)=S(1-\rho+(1+\rho)S^{2})-2\Pi\rho=0
,
\sin{(2\theta)}=0
,
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}
, and
\rho=r_{\mathrm{J}}\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
. The angle
\theta
aligns with the nematic direction of the substrate and the equations simplify allowing us to just examine the three nullclines in the
S
-
\rho
plane for
\theta=0
. We find it instructive to divide our analysis into three cases based on the value of
r_{\mathrm{J}}
.

When
r_{\mathrm{J}}<1
, or the carrying capacity of the monolayer is smaller than the jamming density, the only steady state is a stable fixed point (green dot in Fig.
3
(a)) given by:
h(\rho_{c}(S),S)=0
. In this case the late-time behavior of the system is independent of the seeding density. When the system is initialized at high seeding density, however, the dynamics transiently slows down near
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
. Thus the system spends a long time in a low-order state before it eventually reaches the high-order fixed point. On the other hand, trajectories starting from low seeding density rapidly approach the fixed point, since they are far away from
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}
. To illustrate this, we indicate the state of the system at
T=2\tau_{g}
by gray crosses in Fig.
9
(a), showing that the build-up of order is inversely proportional to the seeding density.

To better understand this transient behavior, consider the special case
r_{\mathrm{J}}=1
, where the arrest of cell division and jamming of orientations happen at the same density. The two corresponding nullclines therefore coincide and the jammed state
\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
is a steady state for any value of
S
. This condition identifies a line of fixed points shown as a solid line in Fig.
3
. The stability of this line of fixed points determines the behavior of the trajectories. Stable (green) and unstable (orange) segments are separated by Exceptional Points (EP). In Appendix
B
we derive conditions on the presence and positions of the pair of EPs and create a bifurcation diagram (see Fig.
9
) that clearly shows that a part of the phase-space becomes inaccessible due to the emergence of an unstable segment along the line of fixed points which repels the phase-space flow.

Finally consider the case
r_{\mathrm{J}}>1
, where the carrying capacity of the monolayer is larger than the jamming density. In this case
\rho=r_{\mathrm{J}}\rho_{\mathrm{J}}
is a line of fixed points due to the Heaviside function in Eq. (
IV
). This line of fixed points is always stable and supports the observation that the seeding density inversely determines the degree of final order. The trajectories that start equally spaced from the
x
-axis cluster towards small and large
S
, and become diluted for intermediate
S
even though the entire line is stable. This behavior can be understood as a “ghost” of the segment of unstable fixed points found for
r_{\mathrm{J}}=1
at intermediate
S
. The dynamics in the regime
r_{\mathrm{J}}>1
robust for small noise in both variables and our full spatiotemporal simulations, described in the next section, are carried out in this regime.
The results of the numerical solution of Eqs. (
IV
)-(
27
) with
r_{\mathrm{J}}=1.5
are shown in Fig.
1
(a,b). These show that the model successfully captures the dependence of order at jamming on the seeding density.

We can intuitively understand the observed dynamics as the result of two competitions: a competition of timescales and a competition of alignment mechanisms. The two competing timescales are the rate of growth of density, which drives the density towards its jamming value
\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
, and the rate
h(\rho,S)
at which steric effects and the external field drive nematic order.
To reproduce the experimental observations, ordering must be sufficiently fast for cells to align before jamming occurs, but not too fast such that high seeding density results in jamming before an ordered state has been reached.
The competition between steric alignment and alignment driven by the external field controls the behavior of the ordering rate
h(\rho,S)
.
For small values of
\Pi
the external field selects the direction of broken symmetry, but the onset of order is dominated by steric effects and the isotropic to nematic transition density
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
determines the threshold for the onset of order. For large
\Pi
alignment is controlled by the external field. In this case the system reaches an ordered state for all seeding densities. For intermediate values of
\Pi
the ordering rate
h(\rho,S)
is fast enough to align before jamming. In this regime smaller seeding densities reach more ordered states.

V
Spatially extended model

The minimal ODE model explains how the emergence of order depends on the seeding density. It does not, however, distinguish between local and global order nor does it address the question of
how
cells align with the substrate. In the experiments, the orientation of individual cells seems unaffected by substrate anisotropy
Luo et al. 2023
, suggesting that alignment with the substrate is a collective effect.
However, the substrate is too stiff to be appreciably deformed by the cells
Luo et al. 2023
, suggesting that mechanical interactions of cells through the substrate deformations are likely not involved in collective alignment.
It is also known that inhibiting focal adhesions results in the loss of collective directional sensing
Luo et al. 2023
.
We therefore hypothesize that cell attachment depends on the cell’s orientation relative to the substrate order due to the microscopically anisotropic structure (polymer chains) of the LCE, and cellular sensing on a molecular scale.

Guided by these observations, we have explored two ways of modeling the effect of alignment to the substrate in our spatially extended model. (
i
) An explicit aligning external field
P_{ij}
in the free energy, as introduced in Eq. (
11
). The dependence of the aligning strength on density captures the collective nature of the alignment and allows for spatial variations of the alignment through density fluctuations. (
ii
) Anisotropic friction which enters in the force-balance Eq. (
20
) in the form

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}\rho(\delta_{ij}-\epsilon P_{ij})v_{j}

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}^{a}-\partial_{i}p\;,

(28)

where
P_{ij}
encodes the substrate orientation as defined earlier and
\epsilon
is a dimensionless parameter that controls the friction anisotropy.
The friction coefficients along
x
and
y
are then
\zeta_{xx}=\zeta_{0}(1-\epsilon)
and
\zeta_{yy}=\zeta_{0}(1+\epsilon)
.
In the anisotropic friction variant of our model, we set
\Pi=0
.

We find that both model variants reproduce the growth of local and global order with mean density and the dependence of cell-substrate order on the initial seeding density, as shown in Fig.
4
(a-c) and Videos (
1
–
3
). In particular, both variants capture the experimental observation that at intermediate density (
{\sim}$200\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}$
) there is appreciable local order (see Fig.
4
(b)) but little cell-substrate order (see Fig.
4
(a)). Moreover, the spatially extended model also reproduces the result obtained with the ODE that the cell-substrate order at jamming decreases with increasing seeding density (Fig.
4
(c)).

Both local and cell-substrate order depend strongly on the initial conditions, specifically the initial density, which is highly inhomogeneous in experiments where upon seeding cells are clustered in dense patches surrounded by empty regions. As cells divide, the clusters spread, but away from jamming, cell density continues to exhibit large fluctuations. To capture these effects, we have initiated our numerical simulations with a spatially inhomogeneous initial state consisting of patches of high cell density embedded in a noisy low density background (see Videos (
1
–
3
)). Details on the initial condition are given in Appendix
C
.

V.1
Collective alignment

In experiments on anisotropic substrates, we find that cell density gradients become anisotropic, with steeper gradients perpendicular to the direction of substrate order; see Fig.
11
(b). In agreement with this observation, simulations with anisotropic friction exhibit stronger proliferation-driven flows along the direction of reduced friction (
x
-direction), thus smoothing out density gradients along this direction.

To better understand the correlation between density gradients, cell orientation, and substrate anisotropy we examined in detail the structure of a growing cell cluster.
Starting from a circular cell patch with uniform orientation inside the patch, we find that alignment of cells to density gradients rapidly orients them circumferentially around along the patch boundary, as shown in Fig.
5
(b).
Topology then requires a net topological charge of
+1
inside the patch, which is accommodated by two
+1/2
defects situated diametrically opposite near the boundary of the patch.
As cells proliferate, anisotropic friction favors expansion along the
x
-direction and the patch acquires a spindle shape, where density gradients are smoothed near the poles compared to the flanks, as shown in Fig.
5
(c).
These anisotropic density gradients then cause cells to orient along the spindle axis.
Active-stress-driven propulsion of the
+1/2
defects situated near the poles of the spindle further contributes to stretching the spindle and aligning the cells.

While both cell proliferation and active stress generate flows which are larger in the direction of substrate order due to the lower friction, we find that proliferation plays the dominant role in driving cell alignment with the substrate. This is supported by Fig.
5
(a) which shows that cell substrate alignment increases as a function of the growth-induced pressure (controlled by the bulk modulus
B
) even when both flow alignment
\lambda
and active stress
\alpha
are set to zero.
We conclude that proliferation plays an important role that is not captured by the deviatoric active stress of the commonly used form
\alpha\mathbf{Q}
.

Finally, we note that extensile active stress combined with flow-alignment is known to generate perpendicular anchoring to gradients of nematic order
Blow et al. 2014
. Since order and density follow one another in this system, this mechanism also leads to an effective perpendicular anchoring of cells to density gradients. However, we find in our simulations that this “active anchoring” is much weaker as compared to the explicitly included anchoring to density gradients (
\chi
-term in the free-energy). The active anchoring can give sufficient cell-substrate order in the absence of explicitly included anchoring term but it is weaker than the case with explicit anchoring (see Fig.
11
(b)).

V.2
Defect dynamics

Finally, we investigated the motion of topological defects in more detail.
Topological defects are created in high density patches where anchoring induces a net charge of
+1
.
The compensating negative topological charge is initially distributed across the low density regions where
S\approx 0
. As patches grow into one another, this negative charge localizes in
-1/2
defects along the domain borders where patches with different cell orientation meet.
The negative and positive defects eventually annihilate as the monolayer orders (see Videos (
1
–
3
)). Upon orientational jamming, some defect pairs freeze before they can annihilate.
The number of defect frozen in the jammed state of the monolayer increases with increasing seeding density, as shown in
Fig.
6
(a).
This is expected because starting at higher seeding densities results in a faster approach to jamming, giving defects little time to annihilate.

In the original publication of the experiments
Luo et al. 2023
, it was noted that defects were seen moving in the direction of their head (signature of extensile activity) when they are oriented in the direction of the substrate’s broken symmetry. This was attributed to activity due to proliferation. However, defects were also observed to move in the direction of their tail (signature of contractile activity) when oriented perpendicular to the direction of the substrate’s broken symmetry. In our work activity is always extensile and the role of the deviatoric active stress is to stretch the ordered high density patches into spindles due to extensile defect self-propulsion. At both early and late times in the simulations, we see defects move as expected in extensile systems (see Videos (
1
–
3
)), in agreement with previous calculations
Brézin et al. 2022
.

Finally, we find that cell density is always depleted at the core of both
+1/2
and
-1/2
defects (Fig.
6
(b,c)) in simulations – consistent with the observations from experiments (Fig.
2
(e,f)).
In the simulations, this happens because we have assumed that aligned cells can pack better and will jam at higher density (see Eq. (
16
)). In other words, high density correlates with high nematic order, while the density is lower at the disordered defect cores. This behavior is in contrast to what is observed in bacteria
Copenhagen et al. 2020
and epithelial cells
Saw et al. 2017
, where
+1/2
defects are observed to be sites of cell accumulation.
On the other hand, experiments in active microtubule nematics always observe a depletion of microtubule at defect cores
Sanchez et al. 2012
.

VI
Discussion

Motivated by recent experiments
Luo et al. 2023
, we have developed a hydrodynamic model of a proliferating nematic aligned by external cues and capable of jamming. Our work captures two important observations seen in the system of hdF-cells growing on a LCE-substrate: the order in the final jammed monolayer depends on seeding density and cell ordering is a
collective
phenomenon.

We consider two ways of modeling the alignment to the nematic substrate: (
i
) an external field that aligns the director field of the continuum model to the direction of order of the substrate and (
ii
) anisotropic traction forces exerted by cells on the substrate. Importantly, the strength of each of these couplings is proportional to density to capture the collective nature of the alignment.

We show that a minimal spatially homogeneous model with no activity-driven flows is sufficient to explain the dependence of final order on seeding density. An important assumption is that cell growth will cease at a jamming density that grows with the degree of nematic order, reflecting the fact that cell can pack more efficiently when aligned. The dynamics is controlled by the interplay of growth and alignment rates, with the latter a function of density. A lower seeding density allow cells more time to align before jamming, resulting in a more ordered state, as seen in experiments.

The study of the spatially extended model with anisotropic tractions suggests a mechanism for the observed collective ordering. Density gradients drive flows which extend farther in the direction of substrate order due to the lower friction. These flows in turn smooth out density gradients along the direction of substrate order compared to the orthogonal direction, generating anisotropic density gradients. In addition, the coupling
\chi
tends to align cells normal to density gradients. The net outcome is the stretching and alignment of dense regions along the direction of substrate order. As shown in Fig.
5
(a), this effect occurs even in the absence of explicit active stresses, but requires cell proliferation and density inhomogeneities.
This is a prediction that could be tested in future experiments: when cell proliferation is inhibited (e.g. by arresting the cell cycle) cells should no longer align with an anisotropic substrate.

We have also observed a strong dependence of the final jammed state on the structure of the initial state. Specifically, heterogeneity in the seeding density and clustering of cells are needed to obtain an ordered jammed state. Preliminary results suggests that a more heterogeneous initial state can result in a more ordered jammed state. Exploring systematically this type of “order from disorder” is an interesting direction for future work. The dependence of the continuum model on the amount of heterogeneity in the initial state may be related to the fact the continuum model itself misses the discrete nature of cell division and neglects the associated small scale fluctuations that are amplified at later times, especially near jamming.

Appendix A
Estimates of various densities

The various densities entering our model are summarized in Table
2
. Here we describe how we have estimated the range of values for these densities.

The density
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}
can be estimated assuming the cells behave like ellipsoidal particles of length
{\sim}$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
and aspect ratio of about
6
. Fibroblasts, however, move back and forth along their long axis
Duclos et al. 2014
, which could increase their effective aspect ratio to 12–15. The continuous isotropic to nematic transition for hard ellipsoids in two dimensions has been studied numerically for aspect ratio ranging from 2.4 to 9
Baron et al. 2023
, where the IN transition happens at packing fractions between 0.85 to 0.45 respectively. We estimate that these cells with higher effective aspect ratio than 9 transition at a packing fraction of about
0.25
, which gives
\rho_{\mathrm{IN}}\sim$150\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}$
. For uniform distribution, this density corresponds to 4–5 cells within a circle of radius

r_{J}=1

For sufficiently large values of
\Pi/\gamma_{0}
, we can assume that the director angle
\theta
quickly aligns with the

\rho

system evaluated at the fixed points, given by

\displaystyle J=\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}-ASh(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S),S)&h(\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S),S)\\

(29)

with
A=2(\rho_{\mathrm{NJ}}-\rho_{\mathrm{IJ}})>0

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{0}

\displaystyle=(1,AS)\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=-1-\frac{A}{\gamma_{0}}Sh(\rho_{\mathrm{J}},S)\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{h(\rho_{\mathrm{J}},S)}{\gamma_{0}},1\right)\,.

(31)

S

), hence eigenvectors and eigenvalues are only functions of
S
. The eigenvector
\mathbf{e}_{0}
always points along the tangent to the line of fixed points, implying that perturbations in that direction are marginally stable. The stability of the fixed points in the other direction is controlled by the sign of
\lambda_{1}(S)
. For
\lambda_{1}<0
, trajectories are attracted towards the line of fixed points, which becomes an absorbing boundary as shown in Fig.
3
(green portions). For
\lambda_{1}>0

and correspond to exceptional points.

\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)
implies a change in the behavior of the system as in this region all trajectories are repelled from the line of fixed points and reach a jammed state with same density and value of
S
, regardless of the initial

We can show that the exceptional points, when present, always come in pairs and lie below the point of intersection of the
S

\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{J}}(S)

\lambda_{1}(S)=0
, which requires both the following inequalities to be satisfied

\displaystyle h(\rho_{\mathrm{J}},S)

\displaystyle<0\;,

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}

\displaystyle>\frac{-1}{A\,\min[Sh(\rho_{\mathrm{J}},S)]}\;.

(33)

The first condition is satisfied in the region below the
S
-nullcline for any value of
\Pi

The second condition, Eq. (

Appendix C
Clustering of cells at low densities

In experiments the initial cell density is strongly inhomogeneous, as shown in Fig.
10
. Cells are organized in dense clusters and are aligned with each other within the cluster due to steric effects. The presence of clustering is evident in histograms of local density that exhibit long tails. Local order follows local density. Local order is calculated using an adaptive kNN
Zhao and Lai 2022
with cut-off radius,
a\sim$200\text{\,}~\mu\mathrm{m}$
and cut-off number of neighbors,
n_{c}=2
. Essentially near a point if the number of neighbors within the cut-off radius is less than the cut-off number of neighbors, the order is set to zero. The number of neighbors to average,
k
is chosen adaptively as
k=\lfloor n^{2/3}\rfloor
. This method results in an order field that is consistent with individual cell orientations as seen in Fig.
10
(b).

To replicate the clustering seen in experiments, we initialize our system with circular patches of high density (
\sim 180~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
) embedded in small random background drawn from a uniform distribution between (
0-15~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$
),
, with the above values chosen from experimental data. Both the number of patches and the mean value of the fluctuating background density are assumed to scale linearly with the seeding density. The resulting spatially inhomogeneous density field is normalized so that its mean is the seeding density. The patches are uniformly oriented in randomly chosen directions with scalar order proportional to the local density. We neglect any effect of boundary on the patches and assume uniform orientation. The background region is disordered, with random orientation and infinitesimal scalar order.

Appendix D
Signatures of Collective Alignment Mechanism

Even though asymmetric friction generates asymmetric flow profiles, flow-alignment alone doesn’t seem to drive the ordering to the extent that is seen in experiments. We confirm this in simulations with asymmetric friction where we set activity and density-gradient alignment terms to zero and look at flow-alignment only. We find in Fig.
11
(a) that flow-alignment can drive ordering but it isn’t strong. The error-bars are large due to initial conditions being strongly heterogeneous.

We next look at activity and flow-alignment induced “active anchoring” of cells to density gradients that can also, in principle, drive alignment without any explicit anchoring of cells to density gradients. We find in Fig.
11
(b) that indeed activity and flow alignment can drive ordering but it is not as strong as compared to the case with explicit alignment.

Next we looked for asymmetry in density gradients in x (direction of substrate’s broken symmetry) versus y directions in experiments as well as simulations with asymmetric friction. We find that in experiments on nematic substrates the asymmetry in gradients is consistently present at all densities (see red dots in Fig.
11
(c)). However, in experiments on isotropic substrates the asymmetry averages out to zero across experiments (see one set of blue stars above 1 and another below 1 in Fig.
11
(c)). In simulations with asymmetric friction (
\epsilon=0.4
) the asymmetry gradually increases but is consistently higher than experiments.
