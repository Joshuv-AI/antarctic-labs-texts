Transcriptomic analysis of palmitoylation-related genes reveals a preferential association with BCAA catabolism in adipose tissue from pediatric obesity
Authors: ShiRui Cao, Jing Chen, Chao Liu, PeiHong Zhao, Le Zhang, YuTong Hou, MinKai Cao, Jia Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric obesity is a major public health challenge with adverse effects on health and long-term metabolic outcomes. Although diet and genetic susceptibility contribute to obesity across the life course, pediatric obesity may be particularly influenced by early-life developmental and maternal factors. Protein palmitoylation is linked to lipid metabolism, but whether palmitoylation-related programs differ between pediatric and adult obesity remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed transcriptomic datasets from pediatric and adult obesity using differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Candidate genes were further evaluated in a juvenile high-fat diet-induced obesity mouse model and online obesity databases.ResultsIn both age groups, palmitoylation-related candidate common hub genes were significantly enriched in lipid metabolic pathways. However, pediatric-specific palmitoylation-related candidate common hub genes were prominently enriched in branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolic and catabolic processes. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression prioritized seven candidate genes. In the juvenile obesity model, four genes (Cldn2, Mccc2, Pcca, and Vdac2) were consistently downregulated in adipose tissue from obese offspring, correlating significantly with fat mass. Downregulation was less pronounced in obese adult mice, whereas total protein palmitoylation was markedly elevated in both age groups, with a greater increase in juveniles. Of the four genes, Mccc2 and Pcca were directly involved in BCAA catabolism, suggesting an association between BCAA metabolism and pediatric obesity.ConclusionThese findings provide a basis for future mechanistic studies of age-related differences in adipose tissue metabolism in obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a growing global health concern, and pediatric obesity has become a particularly urgent clinical problem. A 2024 study estimated the pooled prevalence of obesity among children and adolescents at 14.8% (1). Importantly, pediatric obesity is not merely an earlier form of adult obesity but a distinct clinical and biological entity shaped by growth, developmental stage, and early-life influences such as maternal obesity (2, 3). Pediatric obesity often persists into adulthood and is associated with adverse health effects both early in life and over the long term (4, 5, 6). Compared with adult-onset obesity, obesity beginning in childhood is linked to poorer long-term outcomes, including higher risks of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and related comorbidities (7, 8, 9). Therefore, age-specific molecular studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying pediatric obesity.

At the tissue level, obesity is characterized by persistent expansion of adipose tissue and metabolic dysfunction (10, 11, 12). Energy-dense dietary patterns are important contributors to pediatric obesity, although its development is multifactorial and influenced by genetic, developmental, behavioral, and environmental factors. Palmitate, a prevalent saturated fatty acid in many dietary fats, can be converted to palmitoyl-CoA, which serves as the acyl donor for protein palmitoylation (13, 14). However, protein palmitoylation is dynamically regulated by multiple enzymatic and metabolic factors, and the relationship between dietary lipid exposure and adipose tissue palmitoylation remains incompletely understood.

Protein palmitoylation is a reversible post-translational lipid modification that regulates the localization, stability, trafficking, and signaling of target proteins and has been implicated in lipid metabolism and adipocyte function (15, 16). Nevertheless, palmitoylation-associated transcriptional alterations in adipose tissue from children with obesity have not been comprehensively characterized. This issue may be particularly relevant because pediatric and adult adipose tissues differ substantially in cellular composition, metabolic activity, and functional capacity, and these differences may be further amplified under obese conditions. We, therefore, hypothesized that adipose tissue in pediatric obesity would display distinct palmitoylation-related transcriptional signatures compared with adult obesity. To address this question, we performed an integrative analysis of transcriptomic datasets of subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) from children and adults with obesity, with a focus on palmitoylation-related genes, and further assessed selected expression patterns using a juvenile mouse model and publicly available clinical obesity datasets.

Materials and methods

Data collection

A total of 3,599 palmitoylation-related genes were retrieved from GeneCards using the keyword ‘palmitoylation’. The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) was searched using the term ‘obesity’, and datasets were classified according to sample characteristics into pediatric and adult SAT transcriptomic datasets, comprising eight adult SAT datasets (GSE65540 (17), GSE106289 (18), GSE110729 (19), GSE152991 (20), GSE156906 (21), GSE159924 (22), GSE162653 (23), and GSE166047 (24)) and two pediatric SAT datasets (GSE205668 (25) and GSE228892 (26)). Detailed information on the included GSE datasets and sample sizes is provided in Table 1. Raw count matrices and platform annotation files were downloaded from GEO.

Data preprocessing

The R packages limma and sva were used to merge and process pediatric and adult gene expression matrices (27). Batch effects were corrected using ComBat (28). PCA was performed to assess normalization efficiency and batch-effect removal across datasets, using the prcomp function in R.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The limma package was used to identify DEGs between obese and normal-weight adults and between obese and normal-weight children. Genes meeting the criteria of nominal P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.263, corresponding to an absolute FC > 1.2, were considered candidate DEGs. The FC > 1.2 cutoff was selected as a moderate effect-size threshold because obesity-related transcriptional alterations in heterogeneous human samples may be biologically meaningful despite relatively modest fold changes. Palmitoylation-related genes were then extracted from the pediatric and adult DEG sets to obtain palmitoylation-related DEGs for each group. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package (29).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Weighted gene co-expression networks were constructed using the WGCNA package (30). To merge potentially similar modules, the cut-height threshold was set to 0.25. Phenotypic traits (normal weight vs obesity) were incorporated into the network, and module eigengenes (MEs), module membership (MM), and gene significance (GS) were calculated. MEs represented the first principal component of each module. MM reflected the correlation between a gene and the corresponding module eigengene and represented intramodular connectivity, whereas GS reflected the correlation between a gene and the obesity phenotype. Modules with a correlation coefficient ≥ 0.40 and P < 0.05 were considered obesity-related key modules. Within these modules, candidate hub genes were defined as genes with |GS| > 0.20 and |MM| > 0.30, referring to similar research paradigms in a previous study (31). The use of these criteria enabled the identification of genes that were both associated with the obesity phenotype and representative of the co-expression pattern of obesity-related modules.

Selection of candidate common hub genes and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression

Genes that met both DEG and WGCNA hub-gene criteria were retained for subset analysis. Adult DEGs, pediatric DEGs, adult WGCNA hub genes, and pediatric WGCNA hub genes were integrated to generate comparison subsets. Genes present only in the pediatric set were defined as candidate pediatric-specific palmitoylation-related common hub genes; genes present only in the adult set were defined as candidate adult-specific palmitoylation-related common hub genes; and genes present in both were classified as candidate shared palmitoylation-related common hub genes. Venn diagrams were generated using the venn package in R. LASSO regression was then used to prioritize key palmitoylation-related genes in pediatric obesity (32). Based on the candidate pediatric-specific palmitoylation-related common hub genes, a logistic LASSO model was constructed using the glmnet package. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to select the tuning parameter, and the optimal model was determined according to the minimum partial likelihood deviance criterion.

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Changzhou Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (China). Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility. Before experimentation, all mice were acclimated for 1 week on a normal chow diet (10% kcal from fat, Sipeifu, SPF-F02-002, China) and then assigned to the indicated protocols. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Jiangnan University (WXCH2024-03-062).

Mouse models of obesity

Mice were assigned to either a normal chow diet group (10% kcal from fat, Sipeifu, SPF-F02-002, China) or a high-fat diet group (60% kcal from fat, Sipeifu, D12492, China, based on formulation from Research Diets, USA). For the obesity model of adult mice, the number of mice was determined based on previous similar studies (33). Male mice were randomly assigned to a normal chow diet group or a high-fat diet group (n = 5 per group). Dietary intervention began at 6 weeks of age and continued for 10 weeks before tissue collection. For the obesity model of juvenile mice, dams were maintained on chow or high-fat diet throughout mating, pregnancy, and lactation. Male offspring were weaned at 3 weeks of age, housed separately, and maintained on the same diet as their dams until 5 weeks of age (offspring were exposed to the same HFD perinatally through maternal dietary exposure during gestation and lactation and directly post-weaning). Given the lack of prior analogous studies on juvenile mice in our obesity model, and considering the potentially larger inter-individual variability and possible outliers in juvenile animals, the sample size was increased to n = 7 per group to improve the robustness of the analysis and accommodate potential inter-individual variability. Mice were euthanized under general anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation. Subcutaneous adipose tissues (inguinal subcutaneous white adipose tissue), visceral adipose tissues (epididymal white adipose tissue and perirenal white adipose tissue), and subscapular brown adipose tissues were collected, weighed, and stored at −80°C.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from adipose tissues using total RNA isolation reagent (Vazyme, RC113-01, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R333-01, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a Slan96S system using ChamQ SYBR qPCR master mix (Vazyme, Q311-02, China). Primer sequences for detected genes are listed in Table 2. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Detection of total S-palmitoylation levels

Total protein was extracted from tissues. S-palmitoylated proteins were detected using the palmitoyl site detection kit (Aimsmass, AM10315, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the assay consisted of four sequential steps – blocking, reduction, capture, and detection – based on the acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) method (34). Processed protein samples were then analyzed by western blotting to assess overall S-palmitoylation levels.

Western blotting

Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0010S, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010, Germany). Membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with HRP-conjugated streptavidin (Beyotime, A0303, China) for palmitoylated protein detection. PCCA (Proteintech, 21988-1-AP, China) and MCCC2 (Proteintech, 12117-1-AP, China) antibodies were employed to determine the protein expression in various adipose tissues. β-actin (CST, #4970, USA) antibody was employed as internal control. Signals were visualized using a Tanon imaging system, and band intensities were quantified by densitometry with ImageJ (version 1.50b). Data were normalized to β-actin.

Database validation

The Adipose Tissue Knowledge Portal (https://adiposetissue.org/) was used to validate the expression levels of candidate common hub genes in obese adipose tissues, their single-cell localization, and their correlations with clinical parameters related to obesity (35). The STRING online database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used for association analysis and functional enrichment analysis among common candidate hub genes, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Processing and analysis of the GEO dataset were performed using R (version 4.4.3). Relevant R code was available in Supplementary Document 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article). Statistical analysis for animal experiments was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1). Each dot in the dot plot represented one individual mouse. Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test; for non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Unless otherwise indicated, data were presented as mean ± SD. Simple linear regression was used to assess the correlation. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns = no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, or ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Flow chart for identifying palmitoylation-related signature genes in pediatric obesity

The analytical workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Briefly, identification of pediatric obesity-specific palmitoylation-related key genes involved the following steps: i) extraction and normalization of GEO datasets about subcutaneous adipose tissue from pediatric and adult obesity; ii) construction of obesity-related palmitoylation-associated gene profiles; iii) integration of DEG analysis with WGCNA to identify candidate common hub genes; iv) functional annotation and prioritization of these genes; and v) validation of candidate genes in animal models and online databases. Thus, the workflow was designed to move systematically from broad transcriptomic screening to pediatric-specific biological validation.

Identification of palmitoylation-related DEGs in SAT from pediatric and adult obesity

SAT is a major focus of clinical obesity research because of its central role in lipid storage and metabolic regulation. Accordingly, we collected eight SAT datasets from adults with obesity and two SAT datasets from children with obesity from the GEO database. Normal-weight children and adults served as controls for the pediatric and adult cohorts, respectively. Palmitoylation-related genes were retrieved from GeneCards using the keyword ‘palmitoylation’. Following normalization, principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated the distribution of samples across GEO datasets after batch correction (Fig. 2A and D). DEGs were identified using P < 0.05, and the corresponding volcano plots were presented in Fig. 2B and E. Intersecting the DEG sets with the palmitoylation-related gene list yielded 473 palmitoylation-related DEGs in pediatric obesity and 479 in adult obesity (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The top-ranked DEGs for each group were shown in Fig. 2C and F. These results established the initial pool of obesity-associated palmitoylation-related genes for subsequent network analysis.

WGCNA identifies obesity-associated modules

Because differential expression analysis tends to prioritize genes with large fold changes, genes with more modest but coordinated regulatory effects may be overlooked. Therefore, we performed WGCNA to identify obesity-associated co-expression modules. Hierarchical clustering was used to define modules, each represented by a distinct color (Fig. 3A, pediatric obesity; Fig. 3B, adult obesity). Correlations between module eigengenes and obesity status are shown in Fig. 3C for pediatric obesity and Fig. 3D for adult obesity. In pediatric obesity, the MEslateblue module showed the strongest positive correlation with obesity (correlation coefficient, CC = 0.42, P = 0.0003), whereas the MElightgreen module exhibited the strongest negative correlation (CC = −0.44, P = 0.0001). In adult obesity, the MElightskyblue module was strongly positively correlated with obesity (CC = 0.49, P = 1 × 10−16). Candidate hub genes were selected according to the module membership and gene significance criteria. In pediatric obesity, candidate hub genes in the MEslateblue module included SLAMF6, RGS1, MYD88, FCGR2A, BLNK, and LCP2, within a total of 125 genes. Candidate hub genes in the pediatric MElightgreen module included CPT1A, APOB, LPL, and GPAT3, among 182 genes. In adult obesity, candidate hub genes in the MElightskyblue module included CPT1B, APOE, ACOX1, CES1, and DGAT2, among 187 genes. Integration of DEG and hub-gene analyses suggested 48 pediatric-specific candidate common hub genes, 38 adult-specific candidate common hub genes, and 19 shared candidate common hub genes (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Table 3). Having defined these age-stratified candidate hub gene sets, we next asked whether they were associated with distinct biological pathways.

Functional enrichment and LASSO analysis of candidate common hub genes

Gene Ontology-Biological Process (GO-BP) enrichment analysis was performed for the three groups of candidate common hub genes. Although the specific gene sets differed, both pediatric- and adult-specific palmitoylation-related genes were significantly enriched in lipid metabolic processes. By contrast, no significant biological process enrichment was detected among genes shared by pediatric and adult obesity. Among pediatric-specific genes, BCAA metabolic processes were prominently enriched, whereas adult-specific genes were enriched in pathways including ion transport, bile acid signaling, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 4A). BCAAs comprise valine, leucine, and isoleucine. Pediatric obesity-specific candidate common hub genes associated with BCAA catabolism included DLD, DBT, MCCC1, MCCC2, ACAT1, and PCCA (Fig. 4B), representing 12.5% of the pediatric-specific candidate common hub genes (FDR = 3.32 × 10−7 base on GO-BP database). These findings suggest that palmitoylation-related genes in obesity are broadly linked to lipid metabolism, but that the predominant metabolic emphasis differs by age group, with pediatric obesity showing a stronger association with BCAA catabolism-related pathways. To move from pathway-level enrichment to gene-level prioritization, LASSO regression was applied to the 48 pediatric obesity-specific palmitoylation-related genes, reducing the set to seven genes (Fig. 4C and D): CLDN2, MCCC2, PCCA, VDAC2, NOTCH2, TLR5, and CSNK2A2. Notably, MCCC2 and PCCA encode enzymes involved in BCAA catabolic pathways. These seven genes were designated as pediatric obesity-specific palmitoylation-related key genes and were subsequently evaluated in animal models and clinical obesity databases.

Animal model validation identifies prominent alterations in juvenile obesity

To assess adipose tissue expression changes in these candidate genes, we established a juvenile obesity model induced by high-fat feeding. Because maternal diet is a known determinant of pediatric obesity risk, dams were maintained on a high-fat diet throughout pregnancy and lactation, and offspring were subsequently exposed to the same dietary intervention from postnatal weeks 3 to 5 (Fig. 5A). Under these conditions, juvenile mice receiving the high-fat diet exhibited greater body weights (Fig. 5B) and increased white adipose tissue accumulation (Fig. 5C) relative to chow-fed controls. Expression of the seven pediatric obesity-specific palmitoylation-related key genes was then measured in white adipose tissues. All seven genes showed reduced expression to varying extents, consistent with trends observed in pediatric human SAT samples. Using FC < 0.5 and P < 0.05 as thresholds, Cldn2, Mccc2, Pcca, and Vdac2 were significantly downregulated at the mRNA levels (Fig. 5D). Correlation analysis further indicated that Cldn2, Mccc2, Pcca, Vdac2, and Notch2 were moderately associated with adipose tissue mass (Fig. 5E). Together, these results indicate that Cldn2, Mccc2, Pcca, and Vdac2 are the most robust obesity-associated palmitoylation-related candidates in juvenile mice.

Clinical databases support altered BCAA-related gene signatures in obesity

Because pediatric obesity datasets remain limited, we also used the Adipose Tissue Portal to examine gene expression and clinical associations in publicly available datasets. Although these datasets are derived predominantly from adult participants, they nevertheless provided supportive evidence. At both the transcript and protein levels, the seven candidate genes showed varying degrees of association with clinical parameters. Of them, MCCC2, PCCA, and VDAC2 were significantly negatively correlated with BMI in both subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissues at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6A and B). Previous studies have reported reduced VDAC2 expression under high-fat diet conditions, potentially reflecting its involvement in mitochondrial function (36, 37). In contrast, the genes of greatest mechanistic interest in this study were MCCC2 and PCCA, both of which encode components of the BCAA catabolic pathway. As shown in Fig. 6C and D, transcriptomic evidence from multiple studies indicated that these genes are consistently downregulated in adipose tissue under obese conditions and restored following bariatric surgery. Single-cell data further suggested that MCCC2 was predominantly expressed in adipocytes, whereas PCCA was expressed in adipocytes, as well as endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells (Fig. 6E). To exclude the potential effect of puberty on these two molecules, we examined adipose tissue weight and the expression of Mccc2 and Pcca in mice aged 3–5 weeks (Fig. 6F). Despite a rapid increase in white adipose tissue weight over this two-week period (Fig. 6F, top), the mRNA levels of Mccc2 and Pcca remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6F, bottom). To determine whether MCCC2 and PCCA might be more strongly associated with pediatric obesity, adult mice were exposed to a 10-week duration of high-fat feeding (Fig. 6G). High-fat feeding significantly decreased adipose Mccc2 and Pcca mRNA expression in adult obese mice, yet this effect was considerably less pronounced than in their juvenile counterparts (Fig. 5D and 6H). Under control conditions, PCCA and MCCC2 protein levels were higher in juvenile than in adult mouse adipose tissue. HFD exposure was associated with lower protein levels in both age groups, with a larger relative reduction in the juvenile group (Fig. 6I). Consistent with this finding, total protein palmitoylation in adipose tissue increased in both juvenile and adult obese mice after high-fat feeding, but the increase was more pronounced in juvenile animals (Fig. 6J). Taken together, these data indicate that the palmitoylation-related genes PCCA and MCCC2 may serve as relatively sensitive candidates associated with pediatric obesity and further suggest an association between pediatric obesity and altered BCAA-related gene signatures.

Discussion

The prevalence of pediatric obesity has increased substantially in recent years, accompanied by an earlier onset of obesity-related diseases (38). Identifying molecular factors associated with pediatric obesity has, therefore, become an important public health objective. Pediatric obesity is influenced by genetic susceptibility, perinatal factors, and later environmental and behavioral exposures. However, most available evidence remains observational, and omics-based studies are limited. In this study, we compiled publicly available GEO adipose tissue transcriptomic datasets from pediatric obesity and compared them with the corresponding adult obesity datasets. To our knowledge, systematic comparisons of adipose tissue gene expression profiles between pediatric and adult obesity remain scarce.

This study focused on protein palmitoylation, which has received increasing attention because lipids function not only as metabolic substrates but also as regulators of signal transduction and metabolic homeostasis (39). Previous studies have shown that palmitate levels are elevated in the plasma and adipose tissue of individuals with obesity (40). Here, total protein palmitoylation in adipose tissue was significantly increased in obese mice relative to chow-fed controls, with a greater increase in juvenile mice. This finding is consistent with reports that high-fat feeding enhances protein palmitoylation (41), suggesting that obesity-associated palmitate excess may contribute to metabolic dysfunction through altered palmitoylation.

We next examined palmitoylation-related genes between pediatric and adult obesity. These genes were retrieved from GeneCards, which integrates evidence from multiple sources (42). In both pediatric and adult obesity, palmitoylation-associated genes were strongly linked to lipid metabolism, consistent with evidence that palmitoylation regulates key determinants of lipid homeostasis (41, 43, 44, 45, 46). Through LASSO analysis and subsequent validation, we reduced the candidate set to seven genes, of which CLDN2, VDAC2, PCCA, and MCCC2 showed the strongest negative associations with obesity in animal models and public human obesity databases. Of these, PCCA and MCCC2 are of particular interest because they encode enzymes required for BCAA catabolism. PCCA catalyzes the ATP-dependent carboxylation of propionyl-CoA to D-methylmalonyl-CoA and is involved in the catabolism of the branched-chain amino acids isoleucine and valine. MCCC2 catalyzes the carboxylation of 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA to 3-methylglutaconyl-CoA, which is an important enzymatic step in leucine catabolism. Biochemically, decreased expression of PCCA and MCCC2 may shift the metabolic fate of BCAA catabolism from energy production toward lipid storage. The accumulated propionyl-CoA derived from BCAA catabolism serves as a key precursor for the synthesis of odd-chain fatty acids (e.g. C15:0 and C17:0) (47). Excessive accumulation of these substrates may promote heterogeneity in protein palmitoylation (48), thereby modulating protein function.

Elevated circulating BCAA levels have been reported in obesity and are associated with insulin resistance (40). Leucine, isoleucine, and valine are closely associated with obesity, diabetes, and related metabolic disorders (49). Under high-fat or high-calorie dietary conditions, excessive BCAA intake or impaired BCAA catabolism may promote ectopic lipid accumulation, aggravate lipotoxicity, and worsen insulin resistance (49, 50, 51). Thus, enrichment of BCAA-related genes (PCCA and MCCC2) in the pediatric obesity-specific module is biologically plausible and consistent with previous metabolic observations. Nevertheless, because our evidence is based primarily on transcriptomic and expression-level analyses, this interpretation should be viewed as associative rather than causal. Reduced MCCC2 and PCCA expression may contribute to pediatric obesity, but may also reflect secondary changes induced by obesity-associated metabolic stress or inflammation.

In rodent studies, developmental staging is often defined relative to sexual maturation. Because mice enter puberty shortly after weaning, short-term post-weaning dietary intervention alone may not adequately model pediatric obesity. We, therefore, incorporated maternal HFD exposure during pregnancy and lactation, followed by post-weaning HFD exposure in the offspring, thereby covering both the perinatal and prepubertal periods. Mice progress rapidly from weaning at 3 weeks to puberty, with 4- to 6-week-old mice generally considered to be undergoing pubertal development. Accordingly, 5 weeks of age was selected as the pubertal endpoint for our model. Given that puberty is associated with transient insulin resistance and major metabolic changes (52), we assessed Mccc2 and Pcca expression between 3 and 5 weeks of age under control conditions. No significant changes were observed. These findings suggest that normal age-related changes alone may not fully explain the differences observed in obese mice. However, interactions among pubertal development, maternal dietary exposure, and post-weaning HFD cannot be excluded. By 5 weeks of age, offspring displayed increased body weight and greater subcutaneous fat accumulation, indicating that the model captured key features of juvenile obesity.

Our integrated transcriptomic analysis suggested a more pronounced BCAA catabolic abnormality in pediatric obesity. Owing to ethical and practical constraints on adipose tissue sampling from children, we compared juvenile and adult mouse models. In both models, the expression of PCCA and MCCC2 was significantly downregulated under high-fat feeding; however, despite a shorter duration of direct high-fat diet exposure in juvenile mice relative to adult mice, the reduction was substantially greater in the juvenile group. Dysregulated amino acid metabolism in adipose tissue is increasingly recognized as a contributor to obesity and is often characterized by elevated BCAA and reduced glycine levels (53, 54). Developmental metabolic programs may nevertheless produce distinct metabolic kinetics in children. Although BCAA levels are elevated in pediatric obesity, downstream branched-chain keto acids are reduced, consistent with altered BCAA catabolic processing (55, 56). In addition, early-life BCAA supplementation has been reported to exert sexually dimorphic effects and to improve obesity development and insulin sensitivity in adult male mice (57). These findings suggest that elevated BCAAs may not directly cause obesity progression; instead, impaired BCAA catabolism, including reduced PCCA or MCCC2 expression, warrants further investigation.

Continuous high-fat dietary exposure from gestation through the prepubertal period facilitated the development of an early-life obesity phenotype in this study. However, the model does not allow precise identification of the developmental stage at which BCAA metabolism, particularly MCCC2 and PCCA, plays a critical role. Our findings nevertheless support the possibility that BCAA-related metabolic alterations are prominent features of pediatric obesity. They may also be relevant to intergenerational metabolic effects of maternal diet on offspring, although this possibility remained speculative and was not directly examined in this study.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the screening strategy relied on publicly available GEO datasets, and the number of human transcriptomic datasets was relatively limited and unbalanced, potentially introducing selection bias. DEG screening used nominal P values and effect-size criteria without multiple-testing adjustment to retain moderate-effect genes, which may have increased false-positive findings. Subsequent prioritization via palmitoylation-gene overlap, WGCNA, and animal experiments cannot fully eliminate initial false discoveries. Therefore, the identified genes should be considered candidates requiring validation in larger independent cohorts. Second, the study was designed primarily as a systems-level analysis of palmitoylation-related genes in pediatric obesity. Based on whole adipose tissue, the observed reductions in PCCA and MCCC2 expression may partly reflect obesity-associated changes in cellular composition or relative RNA contribution rather than cell-intrinsic transcriptional downregulation. Future studies using single-cell transcriptomic profiling of adipose tissue from obese individuals may address this issue. Given the important role of adipocytes in adipose BCAA metabolism, cell-type-resolved profiling combined with adipocyte-specific manipulation of Mccc2 or Pcca will be needed to clarify their causal roles in obesity and palmitoylation-related metabolic regulation. Such experiments may also determine whether loss of these BCAA catabolic enzymes alters global or site-specific protein palmitoylation patterns. Integrative multi-omics analyses could further identify palmitoylation-modified proteins with distinct functions in pediatric obesity. Third, BCAA concentrations, enzyme activities, and metabolic flux were not directly measured. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as transcriptomic and expression-level evidence of BCAA-related alterations rather than definitive proof of impaired BCAA catabolism. Future studies employing adipose tissue-specific knockout targeting Pcca or Mccc2 may help clarify the causal and mechanistic relationship among BCAA catabolism, protein palmitoylation, and obesity.

In summary, integrated transcriptomic analysis of pediatric and adult obesity identified molecular features associated with pediatric obesity and suggests that an altered BCAA catabolism-related transcriptional signature, reflected partly by reduced MCCC2 and PCCA expression, may represent a candidate metabolic signature of pediatric obesity. These findings may provide a basis for developing age-specific nutritional and therapeutic strategies for pediatric obesity.
