Automated deep learning for real-time focal liver lesions detection in ultrasound videos a multicenter study
Authors: Jionghui Gu, Yanhua Chu, Dengbo Chen, Baohua Wang, Dong Xu, Lingyun Bao, Dong Wang, Xiaoxing Li, Yu Xia, Qiyu Zhao, Tianan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early detection of focal liver lesions (FLLs) is crucial for clinical practice, but ultrasound performance heavily depends on operator experience. We developed Auto-DFLLs, an automated deep learning model based on ResNet and FPN architectures to detect FLLs in ultrasound videos. It was trained and validated on 5258 prospectively collected videos from three hospitals. On internal validation, Auto-DFLLs achieved an AP50 (average precision at IoU = 50%) of 0.7772, Pr70 (precision at 70% recall) of 0.7967, and FP70 (false positives at 70% recall) of 3.4688. Validation study showed that Auto-DFLLs significantly improved junior sonographers’ detection (AFROC-AUC: 79.52 vs. 71.55, P = 0.021) and enhanced senior sonographers’ performance (AFROC-AUC: 78.64 vs. 74.57, P = 0.0366), especially for small lesions (< 10 mm, P = 0.034). Auto-DFLLs maintained stable detection across different lesion size, echogenicity, location, and ultrasound equipment from different manufacturers. Auto-DFLLs reduces operator-dependent variability and offers a reliable assistive tool for real-time FLLs screening, particularly valuable in resource-limited areas.

Introduction

Liver lesions are categorized into diffuse and focal lesions1. Common focal liver lesions (FLLs) include hepatic cysts, hepatic hemangiomas, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), hepatic metastatic carcinoma, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatic abscesses2. Rare types encompass hepatic adenomas and primary hepatic lymphoma3. Given that primary liver cancer ranks as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally and is a global public health problem, early detection is paramount for improving patient outcomes1. Ultrasonography, due to its non-invasive nature, real-time imaging, and widespread availability, is the most widely used and easily deployed imaging examination for liver disease. In Asian countries, it serves a dual clinical purpose: as a key tool for early screening of HCC in high-risk populations, and for the general detection and evaluation of various FLLs in patients with abdominal symptoms or incidental findings, including benign and malignant4. This dual role underscores its central position in both preventive oncology and general diagnostic practice.

Despite widespread use of ultrasound examination, the initial detection rate of any potential lesion in both scenarios is highly dependent on the sonographer’s experience. This operator dependency creates a fundamental bottleneck. Early detection of malignant lesions dramatically improves prognosis; 5-year survival for early-stage HCC can reach 80–90%, compared to just 3% for advanced stages5–7. Conversely, missed diagnoses lead to disease progression and lost therapeutic opportunities8–10. This reality highlights the critical importance of reliable lesion detection, which is a foundational step common to both HCC screening and general FLLs diagnosis. The magnitude of this challenge is evident in practice: despite universal routine screenings in China, the early detection rate for HCC remains as low as 27%11. Studies indicate that the missed detection rate of FLLs smaller than 2 cm is up to 30%, and it is especially difficult to distinguish the lesions in the background of fatty liver12. This highlights a universal challenge in sonographic practice: the reliable identification of lesions during real-time scanning, regardless of their subsequent pathological classification.

There are two independent reasons for missed FLLs in liver ultrasound examinations: (1) the lesion never appears in the scanning field of view; and (2) the lesion appears but goes unrecognized. Innovations in hardware and software, along with standardized scanning protocols and imaging planes, are actively addressing the first issue. The problem of not accurately identifying FLLs in the field of view is more difficult to solve. Ultrasound examination is highly operator-dependent, with significant variability in detection sensitivity among sonographers. Inexperienced sonographers may overlook subtle liver lesions due to cognitive biases. Studies have shown that senior sonographers can improve the FLL detection rate13. Ideally, a real-time automated lesion detection system could serve as an effective second observer, drawing the sonographer’s attention to the lesion in real time, effectively providing an “extra set of eyes” for all aspects of the video data. Therefore, how to accurately capture key frames in the ultrasound video and rapidly improve the sonographer’s ability to recognize FLLs in real time are urgent challenges for liver ultrasound examination, with significant implications for improving outcomes in both cancer screening and general diagnostic workflows.

In recent years, research on the automatic detection of lesions in ultrasound videos has become a hotspot in the field of artificial intelligence (AI)14–16. While promising results have been reported for organs with simpler anatomy such as the thyroid and breast, the application of AI to liver ultrasound remains nascent and largely unexplored11,17,18. The liver is the largest parenchymal organ, and FLLs exhibit vast morphological and echogenic heterogeneity, complicating feature learning and limiting algorithmic generalization19,20. Furthermore, dynamic video analysis is confounded by physiological motion and operator-dependent probe movements. While these challenges have constrained progress, a few pioneering studies have begun to explore AI for liver ultrasound videos (only two studies). Tiyarattanachai et al. developed an AI system using a relatively small dataset of 446 videos from 334 patients, which may limit its robustness and generalizability to diverse clinical settings21. On the other hand, Saibro et al. focused on improving the generalization capability of computer-aided diagnosis systems for video classification tasks—a fundamentally different objective from the precise detection and localization of individual lesions required in clinical practice22. Despite their contributions, constrained by several key limitations: (1) the use of relatively small-scale datasets (hundreds of videos), which limits model robustness; (2) a primary focus on video-level classification rather than frame-level, real-time lesion detection and localization; and (3) a lack of large-scale, multicenter validation of clinical utility, particularly in a real-time setting that mimics the sonographer’s workflow. Consequently, current AI tools lack the robustness and generalizability required for deployment in daily clinical practice.

In this study, to address the fundamental challenge of automated detection of any FLLs that underlies diverse clinical workflows, we developed an automated detection system for focal liver lesions (Auto-DFLLs) based on prospective, large-sample cohort of abdominal ultrasound video data. The AI model was validated in a multicenter patient cohort and compared to the detection performance of sonographers with different levels of experience, thereby further exploring its potential value in assisting sonographers. Results

Clinical characteristics

From January 2022 to December 2023, we prospectively collected liver ultrasound examination videos from 5057 patients at Hospital 1, comprising 3963 FLLs and 1264 normal liver ultrasound videos, which were divided into a training cohort (N = 4000) and an internal validation cohort (N = 1057). Between January 2024 and May 2024, we prospectively collected liver ultrasound videos from 201 patients in Hospital 2 and Hospital 3, including 134 FLLs and 71 normal liver ultrasound videos. Thus, a total of 5258 patients’ liver ultrasonography videos (corresponding to 4097 FLLs) were collected in this study. Of the 4097 FLLs included in the study, 1098 lesions (26.8%) were confirmed by histopathology and 2999 lesions (73.2%) by imaging follow-up or multimodal consensus. The relatively high prevalence of FLLs reflects the specialized clinical setting of our primary center—a national referral hospital for hepatobiliary diseases—where patients are routinely referred for evaluation of known or suspected liver abnormalities. A detailed justification of this cohort composition is provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Patient age and sex were well-matched across all cohorts (all P > 0.05). Lesion location and mean lesion size also showed no significant differences (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). However, lesion size distribution and echogenicity profiles differed significantly between the training and external validation cohorts (P < 0.001). Each video recorded the complete liver ultrasound examination, with an average duration of 58.52 ± 29.18 s (range: 15.67–194.87 s) for each patient. For lesion-level analysis, each annotated FLL was visible across a mean of 111.26 ± 171.57 frames (range: 5–1086 frames). Collectively, we acquired more than 10000 min (> 15 million frames) of ultrasound video data for developing and validating the automated detection model for FLLs.

Automatic detection performance of the model

The proposed deep learning model (Auto-DFLLs) in our study (development details in Supplementary Note 2) demonstrated excellent performance in detecting FLLs on the internal validation cohort. Auto-DFLLs achieved an AP50 of 0.7772, confirming its high accuracy in lesion localization. In frame-level detection evaluation, the model attained a precision of 0.7967 at a recall rate of 0.7 (Pr70). Notably, when targeting a 70% average recall rate, the false-positive detection rate after IoU-based aggregation was controlled at 3.4688 segments per minute—a result that meets clinically acceptable tolerance thresholds (Table 2). These findings strongly support the potential of the model for clinical application in FLLs detection from ultrasound videos. Performance of Auto-DFLLs model in internal validation cohort

To further evaluate the robustness and generalizability of Auto-DFLLs, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by ultrasound device manufacturers. In negative control groups (GE Logiq E9 and Mindray Resona 7), Auto-DFLLs correctly identified lesion-free cases with perfect specificity (mAP50 = 0, Pr70 = 0). Among positive samples, Auto-DFLLs achieved optimal performance on Philips-EPIQ5 and Esaote-MyLab systems, with mAP50 values of 0.8263 (95% CI: 0.812–0.841) and 0.8122 (95% CI: 0.798–0.827), respectively, while maintaining low false-positive rates (FP70: 2.83 ± 0.15 and 2.81 ± 0.13). In contrast, elevated false-positive rates were observed on SAMSUNG R10 and GE-LOGIQ-E20 devices. Notably, sample size significantly impacted performance of Auto-DFLLs. Devices with adequate data (Philips-EPIQ5, n = 82) demonstrated stable results, whereas the single-sample device (SonoScape S60, n = 1) exhibited anomalously high FP70 values of 5.80, which highlights the influence of dataset on performance. The performance of Auto-DFLLs across different manufacturers is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Beyond equipment variability, we also assessed detection performance across clinically relevant lesion characteristics. As summarized in the subgroup analysis table (Table 3), detection rates varied by echogenicity, location, and size. Anechoic lesions showed the highest detection rate (0.826, 95% CI: 0.790–0.848), followed by hyperechoic (0.663, 95% CI: 0.599–0.701) and hypoechoic (0.619, 95% CI: 0.535–0.688) lesions, while isoechoic lesions posed the greatest challenge (detection rate: 0.448, 95% CI: 0.346–0.485). Spatially, lesions in the left lobe of the liver were detected more reliably (0.809, 95% CI: 0.773–0.851) than those in the right lobe (0.645, 95% CI: 0.595–0.676). Furthermore, detection performance improved with lesion size: rates were 0.588 (95% CI: 0.502–0.601) for lesions <10 mm, 0.684 (95% CI: 0.646–0.741) for 10–20 mm, and 0.756 (95% CI: 0.725–0.794) for lesions > 20 mm. The clinical benefits of the Auto-DFLLs

We conducted a two-stage clinical study in the external validation cohort to evaluate the impact of an Auto-DFLLs-assisted system on improving FLLs detection performance among sonographers and its clinical applicability. Four sonographers were enrolled, comprising a junior group (n = 2, 3–5 years of experience) and a senior group (n = 2, 14–20 years of experience), who performed FLLs detection for liver ultrasound videos both independently and with AI assistance. In the junior group, Auto-DFLLs assistance significantly improved AFROC-AUC from 71.55 (95% CI: 66.69–76.40) to 79.52 (95% CI: 74.76–84.28) (P = 0.0021) and WAFROC-AUC from 72.10 (95% CI: 66.33–77.87) to 80.23 (95% CI: 74.89–85.57) (P = 0.0011) with concurrent enhancements in sensitivity and specificity. The senior group also exhibited significant improvements in detection performance (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2). During independent video review, the junior sonographers (sonographers 1 and 2) achieved AFROC-AUCs of 66.54 (95% CI: 60.60–75.95) and 74.70 (95% CI: 69.43–79.96), and WAFROC-AUCs of 67.59 (95% CI: 60.84–74.35) and 74.72 (95% CI: 68.65–80.78), respectively, whereas the senior sonographers (sonographers 3 and 4) demonstrated AFROC-AUCs of 76.55 (95% CI: 71.40–81.71) and 74.43 (95% CI: 69.48–79.39), and WAFROC-AUCs of 76.60 (95% CI: 70.23-82.97) and 73.87 (95% CI: 67.70–80.04). Under Auto-DFLLs assistance, the junior group showed marked performance gains: sonographers 1 and 2 reached AFROC-AUCs of 80.94 (95% CI: 75.95–85.94) (P < 0.001) and 79.22 (95% CI: 74.17–84.26) (P = 0.0741), and WAFROC-AUCs of 80.86 (95% CI: 75.11–86.60) (P < 0.001) and 80.96 (95%CI: 75.38-86.54) (P = 0.0120), respectively (Fig. 3). In contrast, the senior group exhibited only slight, non-significant increases with Auto-DFLLs. Sonographers 3 and 4 achieved AFROC-AUCs of 78.09 (95% CI: 72.68–83.50) (P = 0.5975) and 78.07 (95 CI%: 73.28–82.85) (P = 0.1714), and WAFROC-AUCs of 79.60 (95% CI: 73.92–85.28) (P = 0.3083) and 78.14 (95% CI: 72.42–83.86) (P = 0.1606) (Fig. 4). Impact on diagnostic confidence and reading time

Beyond improving detection performance, we evaluated the effect of Auto-DFLLs assistance on two key sonographer metrics: diagnostic confidence and reading time. Diagnostic confidence was scored on a 1–10 scale for each detected lesion. As illustrated in Fig. 6, Auto-DFLLs assistance led to an increase in confidence scores across all four sonographers. Regarding reading time, our data revealed a differential impact based on experience level. Junior sonographers exhibited improved efficiency, with their average time decreasing from 111.32 to 96.95 s (sonographer 1) and from 151.60 to 116.54 seconds (sonographer 2). These results highlight that while AI can streamline workflow for novice users, its integration into the workflow of experienced operators may involve a different cognitive strategy, balancing speed with verification.

Role of AI assistance in lesions of different size

In the external validation cohort, subgroup analysis based on FLL size revealed differential effects of the Auto-DFLLs model assistance on detection performance (Supplementary Table 2). In the group of tiny lesions ≤ 10 mm, AI assistance significantly improved detection rates (0.29 ± 0.02 vs. 0.18 ± 0.06, t = −3.32, P = 0.034), demonstrating the particular value of Auto-DFLLs in identifying small lesions. For moderate lesions (11–20 mm) group, detection rates remained significantly higher with AI assistance than without (0.71 ± 0.05 vs. 0.60 ± 0.06, t = −2.75, P = 0.033). However, in the group of large lesions > 20 mm, no statistically significant difference was observed between AI-assisted and unaided readings (0.71 ± 0.04 vs. 0.69 ± 0.04, t = −0.69, P = 0.516), which is consistent with clinical experience that large lesions are more easily recognized by the naked eye. These findings confirm that AI assistance provides the most substantial improvement for small lesion detection, with its incremental value diminishing as lesion size increases.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated Auto-DFLLs, an AI model for real-time automated detection of FLLs from abdominal ultrasound videos. Leveraging an ultra-large-scale, prospective database comprising 5258 cases with 4097 FLLs, our model achieved an AP50 score of 0.7772 on an internal validation cohort of 1057 cases. Crucially, in an external validation study, Auto-DFLLs assistance significantly improved detection performance among junior sonographers, enabling them to reach or even surpass the detection levels of senior sonographers.

Our work represents a significant advancement over prior research in this field. Unlike Tiyarattanachai et al.’s model21, which was trained on a small cohort of 446 videos, Auto-DFLLs was developed and validated on over 5000 prospectively collected videos from multiple centers, enhancing its robustness and generalizability. Furthermore, while Saibro et al.22 focused on video-level classification, our approach is designed for precise, frame-by-frame lesion localization—a critical requirement for real-time clinical utility. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate such a large-scale, multicenter, prospective validation of an AI system specifically designed for dynamic liver ultrasound video analysis.

A core technical innovation is the image-level spatiotemporal fusion module. Drawing on clinical observations that true lesions exhibit spatial continuity and temporal persistence across frames, while false positives are often transient, our model analyzes the entire frame sequence to suppress background noise and isolated artifacts. This global contextual modeling significantly improves specificity without compromising sensitivity, enhancing the reliability of lesion detection in complex, dynamic video data. Furthermore, the Auto-DFLLs model achieves true clinical real-time performance, operating at a processing speed of 30.4 frames per second (FPS) with only two RTX 4090 GPUs. This translates to an inference time of 32.9 milliseconds per frame, which meets the widely recognized real-time standard of 25 FPS in computer vision, enabling seamless integration into clinical workflows. This high-speed processing capability ensures that the model can analyze the live video feed concurrently with the sonographer’s scanning maneuvers, dynamically displaying bounding boxes promptly for detected lesions during the examination itself, rather than as a post-hoc analysis. This seamless integration is critical for clinical utility, as it provides instant, actionable feedback without introducing disruptive lag or interrupting the natural workflow. This efficiency, combined with minimal operator input requirements, makes Auto-DFLLs highly compatible with real-world clinical settings.

Subgroup analyses in the internal validation cohort revealed that Auto-DFLLs’ detection performance varied significantly by lesion size, location, and echogenicity. Detection rates improved with increasing lesion size and were highest for anechoic lesions, likely due to their high contrast and well-defined borders, while markedly lower for isoechoic lesions, which blend into the surrounding parenchyma and are notoriously difficult to identify even for experienced sonographers. Notably, lesions in the left liver lobe were detected more accurately than those in the right lobe. This difference may be attributed to the right lobe’s larger anatomical extent and greater susceptibility to image degradation from rib shadowing, bowel gas interference, and suboptimal acoustic windows, all of which contribute to more variable imaging conditions. These findings align with established clinical challenges in liver ultrasound and highlight specific scenarios, such as small, isoechoic, or right-lobe lesions, where the model’s sensitivity remains limited and human oversight continues to be essential.

Our findings confirm that conventional ultrasound performance in FLL detection remains constrained by well-documented operator dependency and limited sensitivity, consistent with current literature benchmarks particularly for sub-2 cm lesions11,12,23. A critical clinical innovation of Auto-DFLLs is its ability to mitigate the profound impact of operator-dependent variability on FLL detection. The validation study demonstrated that the Auto-DFLLs model provides differentiated auxiliary value to sonographers with varying years of experience, reflecting the tiered application characteristics of AI technology in clinical decision support systems. For junior sonographers, the model exhibits a remarkable “competency amplification effect,” enabling them to rapidly approach expert-level performance. For senior sonographers, it offers decision support by reducing cognitive bias and enhancing diagnostic consistency. This pronounced effect on lesion detection underscores that Auto-DFLLs primarily mitigates misses attributable to recognition failure, effectively augmenting the sonographer’s perceptual-cognitive capabilities for lesions that are present within the acquired video data. These findings align closely with the contemporary precision medicine paradigm of “stratified assistance,” where AI intervention strategies should be personalized according to the user’s expertise level. It is important to acknowledge that the current reader study employed a fixed session order with a 1-month washout period, which may not fully eliminate recall bias or learning effects. We are actively designing a randomized cross-over trial for future validation.

The auxiliary role of the Auto-DFLLs model is of great significance to clinical practice. Traditionally, cultivating proficient sonographers requires over a decade of training, whereas the Auto-DFLLs-based intelligent assistance system effectively lowers the diagnostic experience threshold. By integrating Auto-DFLLs into ultrasound workflows, patients in remote areas can now access diagnostic expertise comparable to that of senior specialists. With the global incidence of FLL continuing to rise, widespread adoption of such AI-assisted models carries substantial practical significance.

We acknowledge that the current model showed performance variability across different ultrasound devices, with elevated false positives observed on Samsung R10 and GE LOGIQ E20 models. This is likely attributable to an imbalance in our training data, which was heavily skewed toward Esaote MyLab and Philips EPIQ devices. Acknowledging that cross-device robustness is critical for clinical deployment, we are actively collecting multi-center, multi-device data to retrain a more generalized model with balanced representation across major manufacturers. Despite this limitation, the proposed Auto-DFLLs model exhibited promising overall detection performance. Compared to contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) or MRI-related studies, our approach maintains clear advantages in both data scale and prospective validation. Moreover, ultrasound offers inherent clinical benefits, including real-time visualization, radiation-free operation, cost-effectiveness, and rapid examination time. Building upon the robust detection foundation of Auto-DFLLs, we aim to integrate lesion characterization capabilities (benign vs. malignant, cyst vs. hemangioma) to provide a more comprehensive diagnostic tool. This evolution will transform the system from a detection aid into a holistic diagnostic assistant, further augmenting clinical decision-making and potentially guiding management strategies. Additionally, we are exploring domain adaptation techniques and lightweight calibration modules to enhance cross-platform performance without requiring extensive retraining.

Our study has several limitations. First, the model was trained on data from a single center with heterogeneous ultrasound equipment, warranting multicenter validation. Second, patient-level factors such as BMI and background liver disease (steatosis or cirrhosis) were unavailable, limiting subgroup analyses. Third, the current model performs detection only and does not classify lesion types. Fourth, although the external validation cohort is modest in size, its clinical relevance is reinforced by a prospective reader study showing that AI assistance significantly improved sonographer detection performance, providing real-world evidence of generalizability beyond sample size alone.

In conclusion, based on an ultra-large-scale liver ultrasound video database, the Auto-DFLLs system was developed using advanced AI algorithms to achieve accurate real-time detection of FLLs in clinical validation including a multicenter prospective test. This Auto-DFLLs system can act as a second observer, and its auxiliary role significantly improves the detection performance of sonographers across different experience levels, especially enabling junior sonographers to break through the experience-related limitations. The model exhibits unique advantages in assisting with the detection of small lesions. Additionally, it features compatibility with multiple ultrasound device brands and low computational requirements. These characteristics endow the system with outstanding clinical utility, especially in medical resource-limited regions, where it can effectively alleviate the shortage of experienced sonographers and provide a novel approach to improving early screening for liver diseases.

Methods

Patients

This prospective multicenter study was initiated in January 2022, with the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hospital 1) serving as the primary coordinating center (approved by the institutional ethics committee: IIT20230472B-R2). Two additional participating centers included Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Hospital 2) and Hangzhou First People’s Hospital (Hospital 3). Both two participating centers’ institutional review boards (IRB) approved this study. All patients provided written informed consent before undergoing ultrasound examinations.

Ultrasound examinations were performed for clinically indicated reasons, including abnormal liver function tests, surveillance for chronic liver disease, staging or follow-up of known malignancies, or evaluation of non-specific abdominal discomfort. The patient cohort primarily comprised adults aged 18–85 years, reflecting the typical demographic seen in routine hepatobiliary ultrasound practice. The inclusion criteria were: (1) No prior history of local liver treatment; (2) For patients with FLLs, lesions were confirmed by one of the following definitive criteria: i) Histopathological confirmation, where the ultrasound examination was performed within three days prior to biopsy or surgery, and all pathological diagnoses were made by pathologists with over five years of experience. ii) Imaging confirmation by at least two contrast-enhanced modalities (CEUS, CECT, or CEMRI), with all examinations performed within a one-month interval. Each lesion was independently assessed by two experienced radiologists, with confirmation based on consistent imaging features. iii) Stability confirmation via follow-up imaging demonstrating no significant change for ≥ 6 months; 3) For patients without FLLs: i) Absence of FLLs confirmed by at least two imaging modalities. ii) No FLLs detected during ≥ 6 months of follow-up. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Age less than 18 years; (2) Poor ultrasound video quality (lesions not clearly visible, motion artifacts, shaking, or blurring); (3) Missing clinical information.

To ensure an objective quality screening process, all ultrasound videos were independently evaluated by two additional sonographers who were blinded to pathological and clinical information. Their assessment was based solely on video quality, such as the presence of motion artifacts, shaking, or blurring. In cases of disagreement between the two evaluators, a third senior sonographer was consulted to make a final decision. This approach not only enables systematic exclusion of low-quality ultrasound videos but also helps mitigate the impact of operators’ subjective variability and proficiency differences on the dataset during the initial screening phase.

This study encompassed the following FLL types: hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatic hemangioma, liver cyst, liver abscess, and hepatic metastases. All lesions were confirmed according to the aforementioned diagnostic criteria. A total of 5258 liver ultrasound videos were included to develop the Auto-DFLLs model. To ensure independence between datasets and prevent data leakage, all splits were performed at the patient level: videos from Hospital 1 were randomly allocated to training (80%) and internal validation (20%) cohorts, while those from Hospitals 2 and 3 constituted the independent external validation cohort. US videos acquisition

All liver ultrasound examinations were performed by certified sonographers with ≥ 5 years of abdominal ultrasound experience following a standardized protocol. Prior to the study, all operators completed unified training and quality control assessments to ensure procedural consistency. The scanning protocol included comprehensive 2D evaluation of the liver using multi-angle sector-sweep techniques, systematically covering six key planes: (1) subxiphoid oblique view of the left lobe, (2) sagittal view of the left portal vein’s left margin, (3) sagittal view of the left portal vein’s right margin, (4) subcostal oblique view of the hepatic vein confluence, (5) right intercostal sagittal view of the anterior right lobe, and (6) right intercostal coronal view of the posterior right lobe (Supplementary Fig. 1). For the training/internal validation sets, complete liver scanning was maintained after lesion identification, while the external validation used continuous dynamic scanning without static measurements. All videos were recorded at ≥ 25 fps (720p resolution), excluding cases with quality scores < 4/5 or incomplete visualization, and archived in lossless MPEG-4 format. The study utilized multiple diagnostic ultrasound instruments (Philips EPIQ5/7, GE LOGIQ E9/E20, Mindray Resona 7, Esaote MyLab, Samsung R10, and SonoScape S60) with abdominal convex probes (imaging depth: 8–20 cm; frame rate: 26–35 Hz), employing both fundamental and harmonic imaging modes as clinically indicated. Details of the instruments used in each hospital can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

US videos processing

The ultrasound video preprocessing workflow consisted of three key steps: First, anonymization was performed using digital blurring to obscure all protected health information, including hospital logos and patient identifiers. Second, to improve training efficiency, each patient’s ultrasound video was segmented so that clips containing lesions were preferentially retained. Because lesions usually appear only briefly in a full examination, this step ensured that the model was exposed to sufficient examples of lesion appearances. Importantly, these clips also contained abundant non-lesion frames and background regions, thereby preserving the contextual information necessary for differentiating lesions from normal tissue. Third, a structured annotation system was employed where two senior sonographers (≥10 years’ experience) independently performed frame-by-frame bounding box annotations, documenting lesion location, echogenicity, and size (Supplementary Fig. 2). When there were discrepancies in the annotation results, a third sonographer with more than 10 years of experience would conduct an adjudication review, resulting in an expert consensus annotation. All annotation data were calibrated by an internal quality control system to ensure annotation consistency (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.85).

Automatic detection FLLs model construction

We developed a deep learning model (Auto-DFLLs) for real-time FLLs detection in ultrasound videos using a multi-module architecture. During training, input samples were generated through a sliding window approach, with each sample comprising the current frame and T preceding frames (T + 1 total frames). For real-time inference, the model processed video streams via capture cards while maintaining identical temporal input patterns. The core architecture of Auto-DFLLs incorporated three specialized modules: (1) A lightweight ResNet34-BiFPN backbone for cross-scale feature fusion to enhance small lesion detection24,25; (2) A lesion-level spatiotemporal fusion module employing adaptive feature weighting and multi-round temporal attention to integrate candidate lesion features across frames; (3) An image-level spatiotemporal module utilizing optical flow mapping to suppress false positives through background motion analysis. The ResNet34 backbone was initialized with weights pretrained on the ImageNet dataset to leverage transfer learning. The model was trained end-to-end using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10−4, a batch size of 16, and a total of 5 epochs. The loss function combined classification and regression objectives, augmented by auxiliary supervision to stabilize training. To enhance the generalization capability and robustness of the model, we applied data augmentation techniques including random horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5 and random color jittering implemented via the Pillow library’s ImageEnhance module, with contrast and brightness adjustment factors set to 0.5.

Model evaluation metrics

After the model training was completed, we used an internal validation cohort of videos for model testing and performance evaluation. For the characteristics of the lesion detection task in liver ultrasound video, the following three evaluation metrics with clinical significance were selected in this study. (1) Pr70 (Precision at 70% Recall): This metric is used to evaluate the detection precision of the model when higher sensitivity is guaranteed, which is specifically defined as the detection precision at which the lesion recall at the frame level reaches 70%. This index directly reflects the model’s precision performance in the sensitivity range that is of most concern to the clinic. (2) FP70 (False Positives at 70% Recall): Calculates the false-positive rate by first aggregating consecutive-frame FP detections (using IoU thresholding) into FP segments, and reporting the result as segments per minute. This method better simulates real clinical workflow compared to raw FP counts. (3) AP50 (Average Precision at IoU=50%): Evaluates overall detection performance at a 50% IoU threshold, a clinically validated standard confirmed by expert consensus to satisfy diagnostic localization requirements.

Validation study design

To evaluate the clinical utility of our Auto-DFLLs model in ultrasound videos, we conducted a human-machine comparative study involving four sonographers with varying experience levels (senior group: 14 and 20 years; junior group: 3 and 5 years). The trial comprised two phases using an external validation cohort of 201 liver ultrasound videos. In Phase 1, sonographers independently annotated lesions and provided confidence scores without AI assistance. Following a 1-month washout period, Phase 2 repeated the annotation with real-time Auto-DFLLs assistance, where the system dynamically displayed prompt lesion bounding boxes during video review. Sonographers re-annotated all perceived lesions under identical protocols, maintaining the same annotation methods and confidence scoring. During the whole process, the sonographers were strictly blinded to their own prior annotations, to the clinical information, to each other’s interpretations, and to the reference standard during both phases. The annotation interface was reset between sessions, and no performance feedback was provided after Phase 1. The schematic of the lesion annotation is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. All operations were performed on the Insight iUS platform, with lesion detection defined as ≥ 1 frame overlap between annotations and the reference standard. By comparing the detection rate, IoU and diagnostic confidence of the two stages, the auxiliary value of the AI system for different seniority sonographers was quantitatively evaluated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and Python (version 3.8.10). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Model performance in lesion detection was evaluated using the Intersection over Union (IoU) and Average Precision (AP). Diagnostic performance in the validation study was assessed via the weighted alternative free-response receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (WAFROC-AUC) and the alternative free-response receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (AFROC-AUC). All AFROC and WAFROC analyses were conducted within a multi-reader, multi-case (MRMC) framework. Pairwise comparisons of AUCs were performed using the Obuchowski–Rockette (OR) method, which accounts for both reader and case variability and provides valid confidence intervals and P-values for correlated MRMC data. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
