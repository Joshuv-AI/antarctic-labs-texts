Underperformance in Skin Cancer Practices Despite Good Knowledge and Attitudes Among Primary Care Physicians
Authors: Abdulrahman Alqerafi, Naif Ahmed Alshehri, Khalid M Alghamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary care physicians play an important role in the early recognition, prevention, and referral of suspicious skin lesions. However, data on their preparedness to address skin cancer in Saudi Arabia remain limited.MethodsIn this nationwide cross-sectional study, primary care physicians completed an anonymous online questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceived barriers. Knowledge was assessed using 27 items scored as correct or incorrect (range 0–27), with adequate knowledge defined a priori as ≥17/27 (≥60%). Attitudes were assessed using seven statements rated on a three-point agreement scale, scored 0–2 each (range 0–14), with positive attitudes defined as ≥10/14 (≥70%). Practice was summarised using a 0–5 index representing the number of recommended skin cancer-related behaviours performed, with proper practice defined as ≥3/5. Perceived barriers were assessed as a separate domain using the same three-point agreement format. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with proper practice.ResultsA total of 159 primary care physicians were included. Mean knowledge and attitude scores were 20.9 ± 3.2 and 11.2 ± 1.9, respectively, and 90.6% and 89.3% met thresholds for adequate knowledge and positive attitudes. In contrast, the mean practice index was 2.0 ± 1.4, and only 34.6% met the definition of proper practice. Only 24.5% had performed a full-body skin examination for skin cancer screening, 23.3% routinely counseled patients on sun protection, 20.8% used diagnostic tools for suspicious lesions, and 32.7% had attended skin cancer-related continuing medical education. Training attendance and more years in practice were independently associated with proper practice. The most frequently reported barriers were lack of access to diagnostic tools (74.2%) and absence of clear clinical protocols for skin cancer screening or management (69.2%).ConclusionPrimary care physicians showed good knowledge and positive attitudes regarding skin cancer, but practice remained suboptimal. Improving training, clinical guidance, and access to diagnostic tools may strengthen skin cancer prevention and early detection in primary care.Keywords: skin cancer, primary care physicians, knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers, facilitators, Saudi Arabia, early detection, sun exposure, full skin examination

Introduction

Skin cancer includes melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers, mainly basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. It remains an important public health problem worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, more than 1.5 million cases of skin cancer and more than 120,000 skin cancer-associated deaths were reported globally in 2020.1,2 Ultraviolet radiation is the main modifiable risk factor, and protective measures such as limiting sun exposure and using sunscreen may help reduce disease risk.1,2 Early recognition is also important, particularly because suspicious skin lesions are visible and may be detected before reaching advanced stages.

Although the burden of skin cancer in Saudi Arabia is lower than that reported in some Western populations, it remains clinically relevant. A study from the Eastern Province reported that skin cancer represented 3.2% of all newly diagnosed cancers in Saudi Arabia in 2010.3 Earlier reports from Jeddah and southwestern Saudi Arabia also showed that non-melanoma skin cancers, particularly basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, account for a substantial proportion of diagnosed cases.4,5 More recent data from western Saudi Arabia and southern region have shown a similar pattern, with squamous cell carcinoma and basal cell carcinoma remaining the most frequently reported types.6,7 These findings support the continued importance of prevention, awareness, and early recognition in the local setting. A recent nationwide Saudi survey reported that Fitzpatrick phototypes II and III predominate in the population (34.2% and 40.5%, respectively), with darker phototypes comprising a minority, in a setting of consistently high year-round ultraviolet indices.8

Primary care physicians (PCPs) are well positioned in this area because they are often the first to evaluate patients with new or changing skin lesions and to identify high-risk individuals during routine visits. Their role extends beyond referral and includes recognizing suspicious lesions, providing sun-protection counseling, encouraging appropriate self-examination, and determining when dermatologic assessment is needed.9,10 However, effective skin cancer care in primary care depends not only on knowledge, but also on attitudes, confidence, and routine clinical practice.

Studies from other countries suggest that gaps may persist across these domains. A national survey from the United States found that lack of time, limited training, and low confidence were common barriers to skin cancer examination and prevention counseling in primary care.10 A study from Germany similarly showed that general practitioners reported uncertainty in assessing unclear lesions and dermatoscopy, in addition to organizational barriers affecting skin cancer screening.11 In Turkey, PCPs showed acceptable knowledge of some skin cancer symptoms, but weaker recognition of certain risk factors, limited skin self-examination practices, and low post-graduation training exposure.9 These findings highlight the importance of assessing knowledge, attitudes, practices, and barriers together rather than focusing on knowledge alone.

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to assess skin cancer-related knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceived barriers specifically among PCPs in Saudi Arabia. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate these domains and identify factors associated with proper practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a nationwide cross-sectional survey conducted among PCPs practicing in Saudi Arabia. The study targeted physicians working across the central, western, eastern, northern, and southern regions of the Kingdom. Data collection was conducted from May to October 2025.

Study Population and Sampling

A nationwide convenience sampling strategy was used. The target population comprised physicians practicing in primary care settings in Saudi Arabia. Recruitment was carried out through professional networks, direct invitations sent by Email and messaging platforms, and dissemination through regional primary care contacts and physician stakeholders. Clinic directors and other professional contacts were approached to help circulate the survey link among eligible physicians working in primary care settings. All eligible responses received during the study period were screened for inclusion in the final analysis.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible participants were physicians currently practicing in primary care settings in Saudi Arabia and providing direct patient care. This included general practitioners, family medicine residents, family medicine specialists, and family medicine consultants who were actively involved in patient care at the time of the survey. Physicians not working in primary care roles, those not actively seeing patients, and those in purely administrative positions were excluded. Duplicate entries and questionnaires with substantial missing data in the main study variables were also excluded from the final analysis. An eligible response was defined as a completed questionnaire from a participant meeting all inclusion criteria who provided electronic informed consent. Substantial missing data were defined as the omission of one or more demographic variables required for the regression models. Of 163 responses received, four were excluded on this basis, yielding the final analytic sample of 159.

Questionnaire Development and Data Collection

Data were collected using an anonymous, self-administered online questionnaire in English. Electronic informed consent was obtained at the beginning of the survey, and only participants who agreed were allowed to proceed. The questionnaire was developed by the investigators following review of the relevant literature and adapted to the Saudi primary care context. Adaptations included framing the knowledge item on the most common skin cancer type with specific reference to the Saudi population, adding a barrier item addressing cultural modesty and reluctance to undergo full-body examination, using qualification categories corresponding to the Saudi primary care training pathway, and including referral options reflecting local referral routes. Content relevance and clarity were reviewed by two dermatologists prior to administration. The instrument was investigator-developed and has not undergone formal psychometric validation.

The questionnaire included structured close-ended items and one optional open-ended question for additional comments. It covered five main domains: knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers/facilitators, and demographic characteristics. The knowledge domain assessed awareness of common skin cancer types, mortality, risk factors, warning signs, diagnostic confirmation, and recognition of actinic keratosis as a precancerous lesion. The attitude domain assessed perceptions regarding the public health importance of skin cancer, the role of PCPs in prevention and early detection, confidence in recognizing suspicious lesions, confidence in counseling patients on sun protection, and perceived need for further training. The practice domain evaluated routine skin examination, prior performance of full-body skin examination, number of patients with suspicious lesions seen in the preceding 12 months, usual management or referral patterns, counseling practices, use of diagnostic tools, and prior continuing medical education related to skin cancer. The barriers and facilitators domain explored perceived obstacles such as lack of training, lack of confidence, limited consultation time, lack of diagnostic tools, unclear protocols or referral pathways, and limited educational opportunities, in addition to factors that participants believed would improve skin cancer screening and management in practice. Demographic variables included age, gender, region of practice, qualification, and years in practice.

Study Variables and Scoring

Knowledge was assessed using 27 scored items, with one point assigned for each correct response, yielding a total knowledge score ranging from 0 to 27. Adequate knowledge was defined a priori as a score of ≥17/27 (≥60%). Attitudes were assessed using seven statements rated on a three-point agreement scale (agree, neutral, disagree), each scored from 0 to 2, with negatively worded items reverse-coded before summation, yielding a total attitude score ranging from 0 to 14. Positive attitudes were defined as a score of ≥10/14 (≥70%). Practice was summarized using a 0–5 practice index representing the number of recommended skin cancer-related behaviors performed; this was treated as an index rather than a psychometric scale. Proper practice was defined as a practice index score of ≥3/5. Barrier-related items were also summarized, and a barrier score was used in regression analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).12 Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians and ranges, as appropriate. Knowledge and attitude scores were analyzed as continuous variables, and predefined thresholds were additionally used to classify participants into adequate or inadequate knowledge and positive or non-positive attitudes. Practice was analyzed using the 0–5 practice index, with proper practice defined as a score of ≥3. Associations between variables were assessed using bivariate analyses as appropriate, and multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with proper practice. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Saud University Medical City under Project No. E-25-9824 and Ref. No. 25/0373/IRB. Participation was voluntary, electronic informed consent was obtained before questionnaire completion, and responses were handled confidentially. No directly identifying personal information was disclosed, and study data were stored securely with restricted access.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 159 primary health care physicians were included in the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 40.0 ± 11.5 years. Most participants were male (52.8%, n=84). The most represented regions were the Central region (28.9%, n=46) and the Western region (28.3%, n=45). Regarding qualification, most participants were family medicine specialists (44.7%, n=71) or family medicine consultants (39.0%, n=62). In terms of years in practice, 34.0% (n=54) had 0–5 years of experience, 20.8% (n=33) had 6–10 years, 16.4% (n=26) had 11–15 years, and 28.9% (n=46) had more than 15 years of practice (Table 1).

Knowledge and Attitudes

Overall knowledge scores were high, with a mean score of 20.9 ± 3.2 out of 27, and 90.6% of participants (n=144) met the predefined threshold for adequate knowledge (≥17/27) (Table 2). However, performance varied across individual knowledge items. The lowest-scoring items were recognition of a painful skin lesion as a possible warning sign of skin cancer (23.9%, n=38), identification of the most common type of skin cancer in the Saudi population (45.9%, n=73), and recognition that having many common moles increases skin cancer risk (45.3%, n=72) (Table 3). By contrast, most participants correctly identified excessive ultraviolet exposure as a risk factor (98.7%, n=157), irregular borders as a warning sign (96.9%, n=154), and skin biopsy as the most appropriate step to confirm the diagnosis in a suspicious lesion (95.6%, n=152). In sensitivity analyses, 77.4% of participants met a 70% threshold for knowledge and 44.0% met an 80% threshold.

Attitudes toward skin cancer were generally positive. The mean attitude score was 11.2 ± 1.9 out of 14, and 89.3% of participants (n=142) were classified as having positive attitudes according to the predefined threshold (Table 2).

Practices Related to Skin Cancer Detection and Prevention

Practice scores were modest overall. The mean practice index was 2.0 ± 1.4 out of 5, and only 34.6% of participants (n=55) met the definition of proper practice (practice index ≥3) (Table 2). Because the practice items represented distinct behaviors, item-level results were also examined (Figure 1).

Only 24.5% of participants (n=39) reported ever performing a full-body skin examination specifically to screen for skin cancer. During routine physical examinations, 47.2% (n=75) reported examining the patient’s skin beyond the presenting complaint often or always. Preventive counseling was also limited: 23.3% (n=37) reported routinely counseling patients on sun protection and skin cancer prevention, whereas 50.3% (n=80) did so sometimes and 26.4% (n=42) never did so. Most participants did not use diagnostic tools such as dermatoscopes or magnification when evaluating suspicious lesions (79.2%, n=126); 16.4% (n=26) reported occasional use and 4.4% (n=7) reported regular use. In addition, only 32.7% (n=52) had attended CME or training related to skin cancer within the previous 5 years.

Perceived Barriers and Facilitators

The most frequently reported barriers to skin cancer detection and prevention in practice were lack of access to diagnostic tools (74.2%, n=118), lack of clear clinical protocols for skin cancer screening or management (69.2%, n=110), patient-related factors such as cultural modesty or reluctance to undergo full-body examination (65.4%, n=104), and lack of time during consultations (63.5%, n=101). Limited opportunities for continuing education in skin cancer were also commonly endorsed (60.4%, n=96), followed by lack of training in diagnosing skin cancer (57.9%, n=92). About half of the participants also agreed that low perceived incidence of skin cancer in their patient population (50.3%, n=80) and lack of defined referral pathways (49.7%, n=79) were barriers (Table 4 and Figure 2).

Factors Associated with Proper Practice

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, attendance at CME or training related to skin cancer was strongly associated with proper practice (adjusted OR 7.42, 95% CI 3.04–18.10, p<0.001). In addition, more years in practice were associated with higher odds of proper practice (adjusted OR 1.07 per year, 95% CI 1.01–1.15, p=0.031). Age, sex, region, qualification, and barrier score were not significantly associated with proper practice in the multivariable model (Table 6). In a sensitivity model that additionally included knowledge and attitude scores, higher attitude scores remained independently associated with proper practice (adjusted OR 1.46 per 1-point increase, p=0.016), whereas knowledge score was not independently associated.

Discussion

This nationwide survey showed that primary care physicians generally had good knowledge and positive attitudes regarding skin cancer, but these did not translate into equally strong practice. While most participants met the predefined thresholds for adequate knowledge and positive attitudes, only about one-third met the definition of proper practice. Important gaps were evident in full-body skin examination, routine skin examination beyond the presenting complaint, preventive counseling, use of diagnostic tools, and recent training exposure. Participants also reported several barriers that may help explain this gap, particularly lack of access to diagnostic tools, absence of clear clinical protocols, patient-related factors, limited consultation time, and restricted opportunities for continuing education. Overall, these findings suggest that the main challenge in primary care may not be awareness of skin cancer itself, but the translation of that awareness into consistent clinical practice.

Although the overall knowledge score was high, item-level results showed that knowledge was not uniformly strong across all domains. Participants performed well on core aspects of skin cancer recognition, including ultraviolet exposure as a risk factor, irregular borders as a warning sign, and biopsy as the appropriate step to confirm diagnosis. However, weaker performance was seen in identifying the most common type of skin cancer in the local population, recognizing multiple common nevi as a risk factor, and considering pain as a possible warning feature. These gaps are clinically relevant because they reflect limitations in practical recognition and risk assessment. A study from Turkey similarly found that primary care providers showed acceptable knowledge of some skin cancer symptoms, while recognition of certain risk factors remained less satisfactory.9 An additional noteworthy finding in our study was that nearly one-quarter of participants did not correctly identify cosmetic laser procedures as not being an established skin cancer risk factor, suggesting some uncertainty regarding incorrectly perceived risk factors.

Attitudes toward skin cancer were generally positive, indicating that most physicians recognized its clinical importance and the role of primary care in prevention and early detection. This is encouraging, because it suggests that the low practice scores were not simply due to dismissive views or lack of perceived relevance. Nevertheless, favorable attitudes did not translate into consistently adequate clinical behavior. A similar mismatch between beliefs and behaviors was reported in a 2024 study from Iran, where acceptable knowledge and attitudes were not matched by equally strong preventive practices.13 In addition, a systematic review found that educational interventions for primary care physicians improved at least one skin cancer-related outcome domain in most studies, supporting the value of targeted training.14

Practice was the weakest of the three KAP domains, with only 34.6% of participants meeting the threshold for proper practice. Full-body skin examination for skin cancer screening had been performed by only a minority, routine skin examination beyond the presenting complaint was reported by less than half, and preventive counseling on sun protection was uncommon. Use of diagnostic tools was also low, and only about one-third had attended skin cancer-related continuing medical education within the previous 5 years. These are important deficiencies in a primary care setting, where suspicious lesions may first be encountered incidentally during general visits rather than through dedicated dermatology consultations. A study from the Netherlands likewise reported that only a minority of general practitioners routinely performed comprehensive skin examinations.15 Taken together, these findings support the presence of a meaningful knowledge-practice gap in skin cancer care within primary care.

These findings should be interpreted in light of the realistic scope of primary care. The central role of the primary care physician in skin cancer care is the timely recognition and referral of suspicious lesions rather than definitive diagnosis or management, and it is neither feasible nor appropriate to expect performance equivalent to that of a dermatologist within a time-limited general consultation. The gaps identified here are therefore best understood as indicators of where system-level support is required—clearer referral pathways, defined criteria for specialist assessment, accessible diagnostic tools, and targeted training—rather than as deficiencies attributable to individual physicians. Notably, half of participants identified the absence of defined referral pathways as a barrier (49.7%), and support from dermatologists was among the most strongly endorsed facilitators (87.4%), suggesting that strengthening the interface between primary and specialist care may yield greater benefit than expanding the diagnostic expectations placed on primary care physicians.

The reported barriers further support this interpretation. The most frequently endorsed obstacles were lack of access to diagnostic tools, absence of clear clinical protocols, patient-related factors, limited consultation time, and restricted opportunities for continuing education. This is consistent with evidence from the United States, where lack of time was one of the main barriers to skin cancer examination and prevention counseling in primary care.10 Similarly, a study from Germany found that uncertainty in assessing unclear lesions, dermatoscopy-related challenges, and structural barriers affected implementation of skin cancer screening.11 These findings suggest that practice is influenced not only by physician awareness, but also by workflow, resources, and organizational support. This may help explain why adequate knowledge alone did not translate into stronger clinical practice in the present study.

The reported facilitators point toward practical solutions. Participants strongly supported more training opportunities, clear clinical guidelines, better organizational support, support from dermatologists, more patient education, more consultation time, and improved access to diagnostic tools. This pattern suggests that physicians were not resistant to engaging in skin cancer-related care, but were identifying what would make such care more feasible in routine practice. This interpretation is supported by prior studies showing that primary care clinicians are more willing to participate in skin cancer care when supported by appropriate training and system-level resources.16–18

The regression findings add an important practical dimension. Attendance at skin cancer-related CME or training showed the strongest association with proper practice, and more years in practice were also independently associated with better performance. This is clinically plausible, since skin cancer-related care in primary care depends not only on factual knowledge, but also on repeated clinical exposure, confidence in lesion assessment, and familiarity with examination, counseling, and referral. Notably, in the sensitivity model, higher attitude scores remained independently associated with proper practice, whereas knowledge scores did not. Together, these findings suggest that targeted training and accumulated clinical experience may be more influential than knowledge alone in shaping actual practice.

These findings have practical implications. Improvement efforts should focus less on awareness alone and more on making skin cancer-related care easier to deliver in primary care. The combination of limited practice, low training exposure, and strong support for training, guidelines, organizational support, dermatologist support, and diagnostic tools points to practical gaps rather than lack of interest. In practical terms, this supports targeted educational programs on suspicious lesion recognition, clearer primary care guidance on examination and referral, and greater access to diagnostic tools where feasible.17–19

One of the main strengths of this study is that it focused specifically on primary care physicians, a group central to early recognition, counseling, and referral of suspicious skin lesions but underrepresented in the Saudi literature. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to assess skin cancer-related knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceived barriers specifically among primary care physicians in Saudi Arabia. The study also included physicians from different regions of the Kingdom and assessed both KAP domains and perceived barriers and facilitators, which increases its practical relevance.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Second, the study relied on convenience sampling and an online self-administered questionnaire, which introduces the possibility of selection bias and limits generalizability. Third, practices were self-reported rather than directly observed and may therefore have been overestimated because of recall error or social desirability bias. Fourth, the questionnaire was investigator-developed and has not undergone formal psychometric validation, which limits comparability with studies using validated instruments. Fifth, although lack of consultation time was among the most frequently endorsed barriers, we did not collect data on appointment duration or daily patient volume, and were therefore unable to examine whether practice varies with consultation workload. Finally, the questionnaire did not include items on referral patterns to dermatology, including referral thresholds, waiting times, or ease of access to specialist assessment; as referral is central to the primary care role in skin cancer, this represents an important gap, and future work incorporating referral behaviour and consultation workload would provide a more complete picture.

In summary, primary care physicians showed generally good knowledge (90.6% adequate) and positive attitudes (89.3%), but practice remained suboptimal, with a mean practice index of 2.0 ± 1.4 and only 34.6% meeting the definition of proper practice. Important gaps were seen in full-body skin examination (24.5%), preventive counselling (23.3%), use of diagnostic tools (20.8%), and recent training exposure (32.7%). Attendance at skin cancer-related training was the strongest independent correlate of proper practice (adjusted OR 7.42, 95% CI 3.04–18.10). Improving training, clearer clinical guidance, organisational support, and access to diagnostic resources may help strengthen the role of primary care in the early detection and prevention of skin cancer.
