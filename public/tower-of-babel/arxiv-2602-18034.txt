Separating Non-Interactive Classical Verification of Quantum Computation from Falsifiable Assumptions
Mohammed Barhoush, Tomoyuki Morimae, Ryo Nishimaki, Takashi Yamakawa

Abstract
Mahadev [SIAM J. Comput. 2022] introduced the first protocol for classical verification of quantum computation based on the Learning-with-Errors (LWE) assumption, achieving a 4-message interactive scheme. This breakthrough naturally raised the question of whether fewer messages are possible in the plain model. Despite its importance, this question has remained unresolved. In this work, we prove that there is no quantum black-box reduction of non-interactive classical verification of quantum computation of 𝖰𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA} to any falsifiable assumption. Here, “non-interactive” means that after an instance-independent setup, the protocol consists of a single message. This constitutes a strong negative result given that falsifiable assumptions cover almost all standard assumptions used in cryptography, including LWE. Our separation holds under the existence of a 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ​ - ​ 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA} gap problem. Essentially, these problems require a slightly stronger assumption than 𝖰𝖬𝖠 ≠ 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{QMA}\neq\mathsf{QCMA} . To support the existence of such problems, we present a construction relative to a quantum unitary oracle.

Introduction

Quantum computation and communication promise capabilities that are beyond what is classically achievable. Yet, building and operating quantum devices remains expensive and technologically demanding. A natural and practically important solution is delegated quantum computation: classical users outsource computations to powerful quantum servers, while retaining the ability to verify correctness. Designing such verification protocols is a central challenge in quantum cryptography.

Our main result is a quantum black-box separation:

Theorem 1.1

Let

Falsifiable assumptions encompass essentially all standard cryptographic assumptions, including one-way functions, trapdoor permutations, RSA, and LWE. Intuitively, an assumption is falsifiable if it can be cast as an efficient interactive game between a challenger and an adversary, where the challenger can verify whether the adversary has won the game. 1.2
Limitations and Open Problems

Our separation holds for
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
of
QMA
, and not
BQP
. Indeed, it is not possible to separate
CVQC
for
\mathsf{BQP}
from falsifiable assumptions: the primitive itself is a falsifiable assumption as checking whether an element belongs to a
\mathsf{BQP}
language can be done in QPT.

That said, we expect the proof technique to adapt relatively straightforwardly to yield a conditional impossibility of
classical
reductions of
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
for
\mathsf{BQP}
to falsifiable assumptions with
classical
challengers/verifiers. A natural approach is to use a suitable gap problem that remains secure against
NP
distinguishers—that is, against classical polynomial-time algorithms with access to an
NP
oracle. Provided there exists a
\mathsf{BQP}
language that is hard for such
NP
distinguishers, then an analogous argument should establish the desired separation. Formalizing this approach may be an interesting direction of future work.

Furthermore,
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
are naturally required to satisfy adaptive soundness, where the cheating prover may choose the false statement after seeing the public key
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}
. In contrast, under the weaker static soundness notion, the false statement is chosen before the verifier sends its public key. The latter notion is not covered by our impossibility result. Extending our separation to rule out static soundness remains an interesting open problem (or, alternatively, constructing such arguments would also be significant).

1.3
Related Work

Black-Box Separation for
SNARG
s.

Our separation shares structural similarities with the seminal result of Gentry and Wichs
[
15
]
, who established a black-box separation between succinct non-interactive arguments (
SNARG
s) and falsifiable assumptions. A
SNARG
is a non-interactive proof system for
NP
statements in which the message lengths are
short
–traditionally at most polylogarithmic in the instance and witness length. Gentry and Wichs introduced the notion of
subexponential subset membership problems
for
NP
, requiring an
NP
language with efficiently samplable yes instances (with witnesses) and not-necessarily-efficiently samplable no instances such that the two distributions are indistinguishable against subexponential-time adversaries. Their work showed that no black-box reduction can base the soundness of
SNARG
s on falsifiable assumptions assuming the existence of such problems. This separation was later extended to the quantum setting, ruling out even quantum black-box reductions
[
4
]
.

Separating
\mathsf{QMA}
and
\mathsf{QCMA}
.

The existence of
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problems is closely tied to the broader question of separating
\mathsf{QMA}
from
\mathsf{QCMA}
—a central open problem in quantum complexity theory. Aaronson and Kuperberg
[
1
]
first established a quantum unitary oracle relative to which
\mathsf{QMA}\neq\mathsf{QCMA}
. Ideally, such separations should rely on classical oracles, leading to several follow-up works moving towards this goal
[
17
,
23
,
18
,
31
,
7
]
.

Our construction of a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem is based on a quantum unitary oracle, resembling Aaronson and Kuperberg’s oracle. However, our approach requires additional structure: we need a
\mathsf{QMA}
language where yes and no instances are indistinguishable against
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle-aided distinguishers, rather than merely undecidable by
\mathsf{QCMA}
algorithms. We believe that techniques from more recent oracle separations
[
17
,
23
,
18
,
31
,
7
]
may also be adapted to construct
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problems in alternative oracle models. Exploring this direction is a potential avenue for future research.

1.4
The Gentry-Wichs Separation.

Since our separation bears some similarities to the Gentry-Wichs separation of
SNARG
s from falsifiable assumptions
[
15
]
, we briefly review their construction.

Let
L
be an
NP
language with a subexponential gap problem. Assume that there exists a black-box reduction
\Sigma
from a
SNARG
to a falsifiable assumption. Then, by definition,
\Sigma
must succeed in breaking the assumption when given oracle access to an adversary breaking the
SNARG
.

At a high level, the separation proceeds in four steps:

1.

The Leakage Lemma.
If two distributions
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
are indistinguishable, then even when augmented with auxiliary strings of short length, their extensions remain indistinguishable. The shortness is key: it ensures that brute-force search suffices to recover auxiliary strings, so this information should not allow distinguishing yes and no instances of the language. This result is known as the leakage lemma.

2.

Constructing an adversary.
Define
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
as yes/no instance distributions of
L
. Extend
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{L}^{*}
by pairing instances with their honest
SNARG
proofs. The leakage lemma implies the existence of a distribution
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
over no instances paired with valid-looking proofs that is indistinguishable from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Note that these proofs must pass the verification algorithm of the
SNARG
scheme, otherwise the two distributions,
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
, can easily be distinguished. Let
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
be the possibly-inefficient algorithm that samples from
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
.

3.

Simulating the adversary.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be the algorithm that samples yes instances and honestly proves them. In other words,
\mathcal{S}
samples from the distribution
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Use sample indistinguishability of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
to argue oracle indistinguishability of
\mathcal{S}
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
.

4.

Breaking the reduction.
Given that
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
produces valid proofs for no instances, it constitutes an inefficient attack against
SNARG
soundness. Thus,
\Sigma^{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
constitutes an attack against the assumption. Because
\Sigma
cannot distinguish oracle access to
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
from
\mathcal{S}
, it must succeed in breaking the assumption in both cases. But since
\mathcal{S}
is efficient,
\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}
yields an efficient attack on the assumption—a contradiction.

1.5
Technical Overview of Our Separation.

We now describe our separation of
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
from falsifiable assumptions. Let
L
be a
QMA
language with a subexponential
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem. Assume that there exists a quantum black-box reduction
\Sigma
from a
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
to a falsifiable assumption.

Modified leakage lemma.

Let
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
be yes and no instance distributions of
L
. We aim to establish an analogous result to the leakage lemma, showing that
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
remain indistinguishable in the presence of auxiliary classical information, reflecting the possible proofs in a
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
. However, in our case, the
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
proofs are not necessarily short; so the resulting space of possible proofs is exponentially large. This renders brute-force search computationally intractable, meaning the leakage lemma does not apply.

To address this challenge, we augment the adversary with access to a
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle. This provides a carefully calibrated level of computational power: it is sufficient to facilitate the search without compromising the hardness of the underlying
\mathsf{QMA}
problem. More specifically, the adversary must be able to search through the space of possible proofs (as in the Gentry-Wichs scheme), without gaining the capacity to break the indistinguishability between
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
. The ability to “search through classical proofs” is similar to the ability to solve
\mathsf{QCMA}
problems since this involves finding a classical witness, so this oracle is the natural choice.

Roughly, a
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle takes an input
(V,x)
, consisting of a QPT algorithm
V
and an input
x
, and outputs 1 if there exists a classical witness
w
such that
\Pr[V(x,w)=1]\geq 2/3
and outputs
0
if for any string
\widetilde{w}
of appropriate length,
\Pr[V(x,\widetilde{w})=1]\leq 1/3
. Our key insight is that finding the correct auxiliary information can be modeled as a classical witness search, making it reducible to a
\mathsf{QCMA}
problem. On the other hand, solving a
\mathsf{QMA}
problem requires a
quantum
witness; thus,
\mathsf{QMA}
may remain hard even for an adversary with
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle access. We formalize this by asserting that the
\mathsf{QMA}
language has a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem.

Leveraging this oracle requires a more sophisticated approach than the Gentry-Wichs brute-force method, as casting the search for auxiliary information into a
\mathsf{QCMA}
framework is non-trivial.

With this idea, we establish our version of the leakage lemma.

Theorem 1.3
(Informal of
Theorem

4.1
)

Let
p
be a polynomial and let
L
be a language with a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem
(\mathcal{L},\overline{\mathcal{L}})
(yes, no instance distributions). Assume that there exists a distribution
\mathcal{L}^{*}
on pairs
(x,\pi)
where
x
is distributed according to
\mathcal{L}
and
\pi
is some arbitrary string of length at most
p(\lvert x\rvert)
.
Then there exists a distribution
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
on pairs
(\overline{x},\overline{\pi})
where
\overline{x}
is distributed according to

\overline{\mathcal{L}}
and
\overline{\pi}
has length at most
p(\lvert\overline{x}\rvert)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
are computationally indistinguishable.

Upgrading sample-indistinguishability to oracle indistinguishability.

We apply our modified leakage lemma to the distribution
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, which consists of yes instances of a
\mathsf{QMA}
language alongside honestly generated
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
proofs. The lemma then guarantees the existence of a distribution
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
consisting of pairs
(\overline{x},\overline{\pi})
that is indistinguishable from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

In the Gentry-Wichs framework, the subsequent step involves constructing a prover
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
and a simulator
\mathcal{S}
that sample from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
, respectively, to argue that the reductions
\Sigma^{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
and
\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}
are indistinguishable. However, since we are trying to rule out
quantum
black-box reductions, we must consider reductions
\Sigma
that possess oracle access to unitary versions of
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{S}
. This is problematic since our modified leakage lemma only shows that a classical sample from
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and from
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
are indistinguishable. Therefore, we need to upgrade this sample indistinguishability to ensure that even quantum oracle access is insufficient to distinguish the two distributions.

A similar issue was faced in
[
4
]
when generalizing Gentry-Wichs to rule out quantum black-box reductions for
SNARG
s to falsifiable assumptions. However, their context was simpler: the prover in a
SNARG
is a polynomial-time classical algorithm. This allows for the simulation of quantum oracle access using a set of classical samples. In our case, the honest prover in a
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
is a
quantum
algorithm. Hence, it is not clear how to simulate oracle access to a quantum prover using classical samples. To resolve this issue, we employ the compressed oracle technique from
[
29
,
30
]
, allowing us to build a (stateful) QPT algorithm
\mathcal{S}
that is quantum oracle indistinguishable from
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
.

Separating
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
from falsifiable assumptions.

Finally, we follow quantum version of Gentry-Wichs
[
4
]
to separate
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
from falsifiable assumptions as follows. By the previous argument, we showed that no QPT algorithm can distinguish quantum oracle access to
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{S}
. Since the assumed black-box reduction
\Sigma
is a QPT algorithm, the output of
\Sigma^{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
and
\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}
should be indistinguishable.

On the one hand, since
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
produces valid proofs for no instances, thus breaking
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
soundness,
\Sigma^{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
successfully breaks the falsifiable assumption. On the other hand,
\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}
is indistinguishable from
\Sigma^{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}
and, thus, also breaks the falsifiable assumption by indistinguishability. This gives a contradiction since
\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}
is efficient, given that
\mathcal{S}
is efficient, thus, implying the existence of an efficient attack against the falsifiable assumption.

1.6
Building a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem

Notice that the attack presented above only applies to
\mathsf{QMA}
languages with a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem. In order to support the existence of such a problem, we give a construction relative to a quantum unitary oracle. We use a result from
[
1
]
(
Lemma

5
in our paper), which states that no QPT adversary with the aid of a classical witness can distinguish oracle access to the identity unitary from a unitary that adds a phase
-1
to some random Haar state and acts as the identity on all other orthogonal queries.

We construct a (subexponential)
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem as follows. We sample, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a language
L_{n}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
uniformly at random, and define
L=\cup_{n}L_{n}
. Then, for each element
x
in the language, we sample a Haar random state
{\lvert\psi_{x}\rangle}
. The oracle
U_{L}
is defined to apply a phase of
(-1)
on input states of the form
{\lvert x\rangle}{\lvert\psi_{x}\rangle}
for
x\in L
, while acting as the identity on all orthogonal inputs. Therefore, the only way to detect whether
x\in L
is to possess the specific state
{\lvert\psi_{x}\rangle}
, which intuitively by the Haar-randomness and
Lemma

5
appears completely random to any adversary with classical advice in the form of a
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle.

While the intuition is straightforward, proving it requires addressing some technical hurdles that go beyond the scope of
[
1
]
. Our case deals with a distinguisher that has oracle access to a
\mathsf{QCMA}
solver instead of a classical witness, as well as oracles that sample from yes and no distributions. As such, the adversary potentially has more power than in
[
1
]
. Specifically, the
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle allows for adaptive access to a
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle, which is possibly stronger than obtaining a classical witness as in
[
1
]
. Furthermore, the sampling oracles leak information which may aid in the attack.

To resolve these issues, we first present a result (
Lemma

6
) showing that if yes and no instances of a language are indistinguishable against distinguishers with classical witnesses, then the language remains indistinguishable against distinguishers with
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle access. This allows us to reduce the complexity of the
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle to a more manageable classical witness model. We then employ a hybrid argument to systematically decouple the sampling oracles from the secret states. Finally, by applying the reduced result from
[
1
]
, we prove the existence of a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem relative to our quantum unitary oracle.

We recall a tool used to simulate oracle access to a certain set of classical functions. Consider a set of distributions denoted by

\mathbf{D}:=\bigotimes_{x}\mathbf{D}_{x}

An oracle

2.5

The goal of a

(x_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}},\pi_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}})\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
and set
O_{j}(1^{m},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\coloneqq(x_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}},\pi_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}})
.

Run
\mathcal{D}^{q{O}_{j}}(1^{n})
and output the result.

Lemma 4

For
m<m^{*}
, hybrids
\textbf{H}_{m}
and
\textbf{H}_{m+1}
are indistinguishable.

Proof

This is clear because the output of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
on any input
(1^{m},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})
with
m<m^{*}
is sampled from
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
.
∎

Now we analyze the distinguishing advantage among the rest of the hybrids. For any
j\in[m_{\max}(n)]
, let

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}\coloneqq\left|\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|.

(68)

Set
j^{*}=\textsf{arg\ max}_{j}\epsilon_{j}
. Note that
\textbf{H}_{1}
has the same output distribution as
\mathcal{D}^{{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}}(1^{n})
over the distribution
\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}
, and
\textbf{H}_{m_{\max}}
has the same output distribution as
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{S}_{n}}(1^{n})
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle<\left|\Pr[\textbf{H}_{m_{\max}(n)}=1]-\Pr[\textbf{H}_{1}=1]\right|

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=2}^{m_{\max}(n)}\left|\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|

(70)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=m^{*}(n)+1}^{m_{\max}(n)}\left|\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|

(71)

\displaystyle\leq(m_{\max}(n)-m^{*}(n)+1)\cdot\epsilon_{j^{*}}.

(72)

Note that
Eq.

71
is obtained because the hybrids
\textbf{H}_{j}
and
\textbf{H}_{j+1}
have the same distribution when
j<m^{*}(n)
. This gives
\epsilon_{j^{*}}\geq\frac{\delta}{(m_{\max}(n)-m^{*}(n)+1)}
.

For
m^{*}(n)\leq j\leq m_{\max}(n)
, we define a distinguisher
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{O}(1^{n})
, given oracle access to some oracle
O
, as follows.
\mathcal{D}_{j}
receives as advice a sample
(x_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}},\pi_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}})\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
for each
m<j
and
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}\in\{0,1\}^{m^{d}}
. Then, it constructs the following algorithm:

\displaystyle O_{\mathcal{D}_{j}}(1^{m},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})\coloneqq\begin{cases}(x_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}},\pi_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}})&\ m<j,\\
O(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})&\ m=j,\\
\mathcal{S}_{n}(1^{m},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})&\ m>j.\end{cases}

(73)

\mathcal{D}_{j}^{O}(1^{n})
runs
\mathcal{D}^{O_{\mathcal{D}_{j}}}(1^{n})
and outputs the result.

All in all,
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{O}(1^{n})
simulates hybrid
\textbf{H}_{j+1}
when
O=\mathcal{S}_{n}(1^{j},\cdot)
and simulates
\textbf{H}_{j}
when
O\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{j}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{j}\coloneqq\{\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{j,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}\}_{\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}\in\{0,1\}^{j^{d}}}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left|\Pr[\mathcal{D}_{j}^{\mathcal{S}_{n}(1^{j},\cdot)}(1^{n})=1]-\Pr_{O\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{j}}[\mathcal{D}_{j}^{O}(1^{n})=1]\right|=\left|\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j}=1]-\Pr[\textbf{H}_{j-1}=1]\right|=\epsilon_{j}.

(74)

Regarding the size of
\mathcal{D}_{j}
, taking into account the size of
\mathcal{D}
and the advice, we get that
\lvert\mathcal{D}_{j}\rvert\leq 2^{O(j^{d})}
.

Recall that for
m>m^{*}
and
\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}\in\{0,1\}^{m^{d}}
, we have
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
are
(s^{\prime}(m),\epsilon^{\prime}(m))
-sample-indistinguishable. Notice that
q(n)\in 2^{O(m^{d})}
and the size of the distributions
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}},\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m,\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk}}
is bounded by
2^{2m^{d}}
. Therefore, applying
Lemma

1
, we obtain
(s(m),q(n),\epsilon(m))
-quantum-oracle-indistinguishability with

and
\epsilon(m)\coloneqq O(2^{-m^{d+1}})
between the functions sampled from these distributions. In other words, we have that for any
s(m)
-time,
q(n)
-query quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Pr_{O\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{qO}(1^{m})=1\right]-\Pr_{O\leftarrow{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{qO}(1^{m})=1\right]\right|\leq\epsilon(m).

(75)

By
Theorem

2.1
and the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, for any
q
-query quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{O\leftarrow{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{qO}(1^{n})=1\right]=\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{q\mathcal{S}_{n}(1^{m},\cdot)}(1^{n})=1\right].

(76)

Therefore, we have that for any
s(m)
-time,
q(n)
-query quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Pr_{{O\leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{*}_{m}}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{qO}(1^{m})=1\right]-\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{q\mathcal{S}_{n}(1^{m},\cdot)}(1^{m})=1\right]\right|\leq\epsilon(m).

(77)

However, we have constructed an algorithm
\mathcal{D}_{m}
for some
m\geq m^{*}(n)
of size

, which is smaller than
s(m)
for large enough
n
, that distinguishes oracle access to

and

with advantage satisfying (for large enough

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m}\geq\frac{\delta}{(m_{\max}(n)-m^{*}(n)+1)}=\frac{2^{-\log^{\frac{d+0.5}{d}}(n)}}{(m_{\max}(n)-m^{*}(n)+1)}>\epsilon(m).

(78)

where the last inequality holds because

, so for large enough

,

\displaystyle\epsilon(m)\leq\epsilon(m^{*})<2^{-\log^{(d+1)/d}(n)}<\frac{2^{-\log^{\frac{d+0.5}{d}}(n)}}{\mathsf{poly}\left(n\right)}

(79)

In other words, for some

,
\mathcal{D}_{m}
breaks
(s(m),q(n),\epsilon(m))
-quantum-oracle-indistinguishability, yielding a contradiction. Therefore, we conclude that for any QPT,
q
-query distinguisher
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle\left|\Pr_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}}[\mathcal{D}^{q{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}}(1^{n})=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{D}^{q\mathcal{S}_{n}}(1^{n})=1]\right|<\delta

(80)

for large enough

.

∎

6
Main Result

We are now ready to show the main result of this work about the impossibility of quantum black-box reduction of
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
to falsifiable cryptographic assumptions.

Theorem 6.1

Let
L
be a language in
\mathsf{QMA}
with the verification algorithm
\mathcal{Q}
.
Assume that
L
and
\mathcal{Q}
have a subexponential
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem.
Let
\Pi=(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{V})
be a
\mathsf{NI}\text{-}\mathsf{CVQC}
for
L
and
\mathcal{Q}
. Then, for any falsifiable cryptographic assumption
(\mathcal{C},c)
, one of the following statements hold:

1.

The assumption
(\mathcal{C},c)
is false.

2.

There is no quantum black-box reduction showing the soundness of
\Pi
from the assumption
(\mathcal{C},c)
.

Proof

Since
\Pi
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

5.1
, there exists an (inefficient)
\Pi
-adversary set
\overline{\textbf{P}}
such that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}}\left[\begin{tabular}[]{c|c}\hbox{\multirowsetup$1\leftarrow\mathcal{V}(\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk},\overline{x},\overline{\pi})\wedge\overline{x}\notin L\ $}&$\ (\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}sk})\ \leftarrow\mathcal{G}(1^{n})$\\
&$\ (\overline{x},\overline{\pi})\ \leftarrow\overline{\mathcal{P}}(1^{n},\mathsf{\vphantom{p}pk})$\\
\end{tabular}\right]\geq 1-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right).

Assume that statement
(2)
is not true, i.e. there exists a quantum black-box reduction
\Sigma
that establishes the soundness of
\Pi
based on
(\mathcal{C},c)
. In other words, there exists a polynomial
p
such that,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}}[\langle\mathcal{C}(1^{n}),\Sigma^{{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}}(1^{n})\rangle=1]\geq c+\frac{1}{p(n)}.

(83)

By
Theorem

5.1
, there also exists a (stateful) QPT algorithm
\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
are oracle indistinguishable with respect to QPT adversaries. Given that
\langle\mathcal{C},\Sigma^{(\cdot)}\rangle
is QPT, for large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\langle\mathcal{C}(1^{n}),\Sigma^{{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}(1^{n})\rangle=1]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}\leftarrow\overline{\textbf{P}}}[\langle\mathcal{C}(1^{n}),\Sigma^{{\overline{\mathcal{P}}}}(1^{n})\rangle=1]-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)

(84)

\displaystyle\geq\left(c+\frac{1}{p(n)}\right)-\mathsf{negl}\left(n\right)\geq c+\frac{1}{2p(n)}.

(85)

Therefore, the QPT algorithm
\Sigma^{{\mathcal{S}_{n}}}
breaks
(\mathcal{C},c)
, so statement
(1)
is true.
∎

Theorem 7.1

Let

be a constant and

be a polynomial on the security parameter

\mathsf{QMA}
and a corresponding

-
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}

Proof

We first define the language for the gap problem. For each

, let

be a language of size
\lfloor 2^{n/2}\rfloor
chosen uniformly at random and let

and let

if

L(x)=0

Now we define the quantum unitary oracles used to construct the gap problem for
L
as follows. For simplicity, we let
{\lvert 0\rangle}
denote the state
{\lvert 0^{k}\rangle}

Construction 1

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

n
-qubit state
{\lvert\psi_{x}\rangle}\leftarrow\mu_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}\coloneqq(\textsf{GenYes},\textsf{GenNo},U)

\textsf{GenYes}\coloneqq\{\textsf{GenYes}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\textsf{GenNo}\coloneqq\{\textsf{GenNo}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
and

.
Each of them is defined as follows:

1.

\textsf{GenYes}_{n}:
A unitary on
(t+1)n+1
qubits defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{GenYes}_{n}\coloneqq|\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}\rangle\langle 0|+|0\rangle\langle\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}|+I-|\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}\rangle\langle\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}|-|0\rangle\langle 0|,

(90)

where
{\lvert\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}\rangle}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|L_{n}|}}\sum_{x\in L_{n}}{\lvert x\rangle}{\lvert\psi_{x}\rangle}^{\otimes t(n)}{\lvert 1\rangle}
.
In other words,
\textsf{GenYes}_{n}
is a unitary that “swaps”
|0\rangle
and
|\phi_{n}^{\textsf{Yes}}\rangle
.

2.

\textsf{GenNo}_{n}:
A unitary on
n+1
qubits defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{GenNo}_{n}\coloneqq|\phi_{n}^{\textsf{No}}\rangle\langle 0|+|0\rangle\langle\phi_{n}^{\textsf{No}}|+I-|\phi_{n}^{\textsf{No}}\rangle\langle\phi_{n}^{\textsf{No}}|-|0\rangle\langle 0|,

(91)

where
{\lvert\phi_{n}^{\textsf{No}}\rangle}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\overline{L}_{n}|}}\sum_{x\in\overline{L}_{n}}{\lvert x\rangle}{\lvert 1\rangle}

In other words,
\textsf{GenNo}_{n}
is a unitary that “swaps”

and

3.

U_{n}

\displaystyle U_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\{0,1\}\\

Appendix 0.A
QCMA
Oracle Emulation using Classical Witness

In this section, we show that giving
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle access can be emulated using a classical witness, even in the presence of other quantum unitary oracles. This is used to bound the distinguishing advantage of an adversary that is given a
\mathsf{QCMA}
oracle in the construction of a
\mathsf{QMA}\text{-}\mathsf{QCMA}
gap problem.
We use the following basic algebraic property.

Lemma 7

For any functions
A,B
mapping
\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
, we have

\left|\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}A(w)-\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}B(w)\right|\leq\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|A(w)-B(w)|.

Proof

Let
w_{A}\coloneqq\argmax_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}A(w)
and let
w_{B}\coloneqq\argmax_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}B(w).
Without loss of generality, assume
|A(w_{A})|\geq|B(w_{B})|
. Then,

\displaystyle\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|A(w)-B(w)|

\displaystyle\geq\left|A(w_{A})-B(w_{A})\right|\geq|A(w_{A})-B(w_{B})|

(121)

\displaystyle=\left|\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}A(w)-\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{n}}B(w)\right|.

(122)

∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section, which is also stated as
Lemma

6
.

q
be a function on the security parameter
n

\epsilon\leq 1
be a function on

n

q
-time quantum algorithm
\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}U\leftarrow\mathcal{U}\\
U^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\max_{w\in\{0,1\}^{q}}\left(\left|\Pr[\mathcal{B}^{Q,U}(1^{n},w)=1]-\Pr[\mathcal{B}^{Q,U^{\prime}}(1^{n},w)=1]\right|\right)\right]\leq\epsilon(n,q)
