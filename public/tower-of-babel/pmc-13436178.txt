The potency of native postbiotics and paraprobiotics in modulating inflammation by affecting the gut–kidney axis
Authors: Fatemeh Haririzadeh Jouriani, Mahnaz Torfeh, Fatemeh Ashrafian, Niloofar Rezaie, Mohsen Mohammadi, Shadi Aghamohammad, Mahdi Rohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut's impact on kidney health can be observed through its influence on the gut–kidney axis. However, using paraprobiotic and postbiotic agents may improve gut health and kidney function by affecting signaling pathways. This study aims to assess the anti‐inflammatory and autophagy‐inducing properties of native postbiotics and paraprobiotics to enhance kidney health by targeting the gut–kidney axis.MethodsIn this animal experiment, colitis was induced in male C57Bl/6 mice using dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), and mice were treated with postbiotics and paraprobiotics consisting of four Lactobacillus and two Bifidobacterium strains. A quantitative polymerase chain reaction assay was conducted to evaluate the gene expression involved in the autophagy process in the kidney.ResultsA significant decrease in weight and colon length and an increase in disease activity index and pathological changes were observed in the DSS group. However, the paraprobiotic and postbiotic mixtures, with more emphasis on postbiotics, were effective in preventing these changes caused by DSS. Exposure to DSS resulted in the downregulation of autophagy‐related genes, but this difference was not statistically significant. Both the paraprobiotics and postbiotics increased the expression of genes. The postbiotic mixture had a more pronounced effect in increasing the expression of atg7, atg12, and beclin.ConclusionsOur native postbiotics and paraprobiotics indicated an effective role in reducing inflammation, especially gut and kidney inflammation, through the gut–kidney axis. The administration of these beneficial agents with the least side effects could be considered a suitable complementary treatment for controlling symptoms in patients with inflammatory‐related disease.

INTRODUCTION

The gut–kidney axis has been widely studied in recent years because the gastrointestinal tract can play a major role in the initiation of systemic inflammation in kidney diseases such as acute renal injury, chronic kidney disease (CKD), IgA nephropathy, and the final stage of CKD known as end‐stage renal disease. 1 , 2 , 3 Recent studies have shown that gut inflammation throughout the digestive tract may be associated with weakness of the epithelial tight‐junction barrier in the gut, leading to increased permeability (leaky gut), and allowing bacterial DNA and endotoxin to pass into the bloodstream. 4 Therefore, uncontrolled inflammation in the gut causes detrimental effects on other organs by activating the innate and adaptive immune cells and triggering the inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly the Nuclear Factor kappa‑light‑chain‑enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) Mitogen‑Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), and Janus Kinase – Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK–STAT) pathways, and releasing pro‐inflammatory and inflammatory cytokines. 5 , 6

The different roles of autophagy signaling pathway have also been observed in inflammatory reactions and regulatory responses to inflammation; thus, it may be considered as a beneficial treatment for kidney disorders. 7 Growing evidence has suggested the great potential of autophagy to protect kidneys from various types of kidney inflammation such as acute, chronic, metabolic, and aging‐related kidney disorders. 8 Therefore, it seems that the activation of the autophagy process could reduce the severity of inflammatory diseases by modulating inflammation in different organs, especially kidneys. Autophagy is also known as the physiologic process in which the components of cells can break down directly in the degradation process in lysosomes and autophagosomes. This pathway helps the cells and tissues in different organs to keep their homeostasis balanced. 9 To maintain homeostasis, the autophagy mechanisms can work together to break down intracellular substances and supply degraded products. 10 Disturbances in autophagy can interrupt cellular homeostasis and lead to the development of several infectious diseases and inflammatory‐related disorders. Past studies have reported that autophagy is involved both in inflammatory‐related diseases and in aging and lifespan extension. 11 , 12 The studies have provided an overview of the close relationship between the gut and other organs (gut–organ axis) in inflammation control in numerous inflammatory diseases via triggering different inflammatory responses.

Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are two important groups of probiotics utilized in both human and animal health. According to recent research, there is strong evidence that probiotics may exhibit several therapeutic features, including anti‐inflammatory and immune‐modulatory abilities, through increasing the expression of genes involved in inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly NF‐κB, MAPK, and JAK–STAT pathways. 13 , 14 Moreover, probiotics play an advantageous role in decreasing the level of pro‐inflammatory cytokines efficiently and increasing the level of anti‐inflammatory cytokines. 15 As mentioned in previous studies, probiotics are also known as a natural biotreatment agent due to their numerous health‐promoting features and inherent ability to fight inflammatory‐related diseases, particularly CKD. 16 , 17 Indeed, the advantageous effects of probiotics can assist in decreasing uremic toxin production and improving renal function in in vitro and animal models. In addition, human studies on probiotics in chronic inflammatory kidney disorders have attracted the attention of scientists and encouraged the scientific community in the past decade to comprehensively explore the therapeutic potentials of probiotics. 18 , 19

The emerging global concern regarding the safety of consuming live microorganisms has increased interest in components and metabolites derived from probiotic strains. 20 Presently, cell components and metabolites are replaced with live microbial cells by scientists, which are also known as paraprobiotics and postbiotics derived from probiotic bacteria. Paraprobiotics and postbiotics derived from Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium encompass a wide array of molecules, including proteins, polysaccharides, bacteriocins, and organic acids. These compounds have the potential to exert positive effects on the host, such as modulating the immune system, fighting tumors, defending against microbes, and combating inflammation. 21 , 22 Paraprobiotics and postbiotics offer distinct advantages over probiotics due to their well‐defined chemical structures and established safety dose parameters. 23 , 24 Furthermore, these products offer the added benefit of a longer shelf life, which enhances their convenience and cost‐effectiveness for consumers. These findings suggest a potential for new preventive or pharmacological treatments for inflammatory disorders. Therefore, the utilization of paraprobiotics and postbiotics could serve as a viable and more secure substitute for live probiotic bacteria. 25 , 26 Several studies have demonstrated that paraprobiotics and postbiotics play a significant role in improving mental and physical health through their effects on the gut–organ axis. 2 , 4 The effectiveness of paraprobiotics and postbiotics should be evaluated in other organs, including the liver, heart, and kidneys.

In this study, we evaluated the precise molecular effects of paraprobiotics and postbiotics derived from our native Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium on kidney inflammation that occurs after gut inflammation through the gut–kidney axis. We also investigated the anti‐inflammatory properties of our selected strains on the autophagy signaling pathway as one of the noteworthy mechanisms that lead to inflammation reduction. This study intends to increase our understanding of the benefits of paraprobiotics and probiotics, which may promote their applications in patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial culture

Based on the preceding analysis, six strains were identified in the breast milk and stool specimens of healthy Iranian adults, namely Lactobacillus reuteri 100, Lactobacillus plantarum 42, Lactobacillus plantarum 119, Lactobacillus plantarum 155, Bifidobacterium bifidum 1001, and Bifidobacterium longum 1044. Each of these bacterial strains was cultured on de Man – Rogosa – Sharpe medium (MRS) and incubated in an anaerobic condition at 37°C for 18 h. 27 , 28

It is important to note that the native probiotic strains utilized in this study, namely Lactobacillus reuteri RP100, Lactobacillus plantarum RP42, Lactobacillus plantarum RP119, Lactobacillus plantarum RP155, Bifidobacterium bifidum RP1001, and Bifidobacterium longum RP1044, have been previously characterized for their potent anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Our prior research work demonstrated that these specific strains significantly elevated antioxidant markers (Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT), Glutathione peroxidase (GPX), Reduced glutathione (GSH)) and anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐10) and concurrently reduced pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1β), Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α)) and malondialdehyde in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)–induced colitis model. Furthermore, these beneficial effects were shown to be mediated through the modulation of key signaling pathways, specifically Nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) and NF‐κB, providing robust evidence for their molecular capabilities in combating inflammation and oxidative stress. 29

A bacterial suspension with a concentration of 109 CFU (colony forming unit)/mL was prepared and added to MRS broth at 10% (v/v). The mixture was then incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 96 h. After centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected by passing the suspension from a 0.22‐μm syringe filter (MillexGS, Millipore). The inoculation process followed a single strain preparation method. The resulting supernatant called the postbiotic mixture was aliquoted into separate vials and stored at −80°C.

Postbiotic cocktail preparation

Paraprobiotic cocktail preparation

After 18 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C; then the pellet was washed twice using phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The bacterial suspension, adjusted to a concentration of 109 CFU/mL, was then heated at 100°C for 10 min. The inactivated cells were sonicated in 10‐min intervals, with 1‐min cycles at 40 Hz, all within a sonicated ice water bath. Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, yielding a precipitate that was used as a paraprobiotic. To prepare a paraprobiotic mixture, all six selected strains (adjusted to 109 CFU/mL) were mixed and treated using the aforementioned process.

In vivo study

Twenty C57Bl/6 male mice aged between 4 and 5 weeks were purchased from the Pasteur Institute in Karaj, Iran. They were weighed and divided into four groups randomly. Mice in the first group were administered PBS orally (control group: Ctrl), the second group PBS with 2% DSS simultaneously (DSS group), the third group a mixture of paraprobiotics of our native Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. with 2% DSS (DSS + Para group), and the final group a mixture of postbiotics of our native Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. with 2% DSS (DSS + Post group). The animals were maintained in a room with a 12‐h light–dark cycle, a temperature of 23 ± 1°C, and a humidity of 50% ± 10%. All mice were provided with unlimited access to both drinking water and food throughout the period of the experiment. On the 14th day after acclimatization, designated as day 0, the experiment was initiated as shown in Figure 1. Prior to this, the mice were provided food for a period of 14 days to acclimatize. In the Ctrl and DSS groups, the mice received 0.2 mL of PBS orally. Subsequently, for DSS modeling, the mice were administered 2% DSS for 14 days starting from day 14 until the end of the experiment on day 28. The mice in the DSS + Para and DSS + Post groups received paraprobiotics and postbiotics, respectively, at a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL, in a volume of 0.2 mL daily for 2 weeks.

The animal experiment was approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the Pasteur Institute of Iran (IR.PII.REC.1400.061). The experimental design, shown in Figure 1, provides a clear representation of the methodology employed in the study. The percent change in body weight was obtained by subtracting the body weight at the beginning of the study from that at the end of the study, dividing by the initial body weight, and then multiplying by 100. This formula provides a standard method to measure changes in weight over a given period of time. The various factors involved in the assessment of the disease activity index (DAI) such as weight loss measurement, fecal consistency, and amount of blood in stool are outlined in Zhang et al. 30 Therefore, the combination of these three factors provides a thorough clinical assessment. The assessment was conducted such that the evaluators were blinded to the experimental conditions.

Histopathological examination of the colon

The tissue samples were preserved in 10% formalin before they were embedded in paraffin. Subsequently 3‐μm‐thick paraffin sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for analysis. The slides were scanned and image processed using the ImageJ scanner and viewer software. This software is known for its efficiency and accuracy in capturing and analyzing images. The histopathological variances for each alteration within the examined groups were assessed and analyzed by a neutral observer who was blinded to the study. The colon tissue sections were carefully analyzed and scored for pathological results, with a focus on inflammation. 31

Collection and preparation of samples

The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, the colon of each mouse was carefully removed and cleaned with PBS. The colon length was detected to assess the intensity of the inflammatory response. Additionally, every mouse in the study groups underwent daily assessments for body weight, fecal consistency, and major hemorrhage. It is important to highlight that these evaluations were carried out in a blinded fashion, ensuring unbiased results.

DAI assessment

The DAI assessment evaluates the changes in weight, stool solidity, and discharge of blood in stool. Subsequently, the summation of these three parameters yields an overall clinical score. The assessment was conducted in a manner where the evaluators were blinded to the details.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was prepared after homogenizing and centrifuging the suspension of tissue samples at 12 000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min RNA extraction was carried out using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech Corp., Taiwan). Then, the isolated RNA was reverse transcribed using the complementary DNA synthesis kit (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Co., Iran); 2x SYBR‐Green (RealQ plus Master Mix Green, Amplicon A/S, Denmark) was used for real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR). To normalize the expression of the target gene, the gapdh gene was utilized as a housekeeping gene. 32 The primer sequences employed in the present study are presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for normal data, whereas Kruskal–Wallis test was used for nonnormal data. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Administration of postbiotic mixture had a better protective effect on the DSS‐induced colitis model

To determine the better effects of paraprobiotic or postbiotic mixtures on protection against DSS‐induced colitis, mice were exposed to DSS together with either paraprobiotic or postbiotic mixtures (Figure 1A). In the Ctrl group, there was an increase in body weight on day 14 compared to day 0. On the contrary, a significant decrease in body weight was observed in the DSS group compared to the Ctrl group (p = 0.0004). However, both the paraprobiotic and postbiotic mixtures prevented the weight loss caused by DSS, with the postbiotic mixture causing significantly less weight loss (p = 0.04). Compared to the Ctrl group, the DSS group had a shorter colon length and an increase in DAI and pathological scores (colon length p < 0.0001, DAI score p < 0.0001, and pathological score p < 0.0001, respectively). However, both the DSS + Para (colon length p = 0.0001, DAI score p < 0.0001, and pathological score p < 0.0001, respectively) and DSS + Post (colon length p < 0.0001, DAI score p < 0.0001, and pathological score p < 0.0001, respectively) groups exhibited an increase in colon length and a decrease in DAI and pathological scores (Figure 1B–E). Interestingly, the postbiotic mixture exhibited a better effect compared with the paraprobiotic mixture (colon length p = 0.004, DAI score p = 0.001, and pathological score p = 0.004, respectively). In the DSS group, pathological changes in the colon were observed, characterized by an increase in inflammation, depletion of goblet cells, and the presence of crypt abscesses. On the contrary, protective effects on these colonic alterations were observed in both the DSS + Para and DSS + Post groups, with the postbiotic mixture having a more pronounced effect (Figure 1F). In summary, the administration of a postbiotic mixture of our Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. obviously had a better protective effect on the occurrence of colitis.

Administration of postbiotic mixture had a better upregulating effect on autophagy‐related genes in the kidney of DSS‐induced colitis model

To understand whether the paraprobiotic and postbiotic mixtures had an advantageous effect on DSS‐induced kidney inflammation, we evaluated the impact of these two mixtures on the gene expression related to autophagy in the kidneys of DSS‐treated mice using quantitative PCR. Contrary to the DSS group, both the DSS + Para and DSS + Post groups exhibited an increased expression of the beclin gene, with the postbiotic mixture having a more pronounced effect on this gene (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2A). The messenger RNA level of atg5 decreased in the kidney of the DSS group compared to the Ctrl group, but this difference was not statistically significant. However, both the paraprobiotic and postbiotic mixtures can increase the expression of atg5 (post p ≤ 0.0001) (Figure 2B). Additionally, a 14‐day exposure to DSS resulted in a slight decrease in the expression of atg7 compared to the Ctrl group. However, the administration of the paraprobiotic and postbiotic mixtures significantly increased the expression of this gene (p = 0.0003 and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Figure 2C). The expression of atg12 was downregulated in the DSS group compared to the Ctrl group, but both treatment groups exhibited a significant increase in this expression (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Figure 2D). It is worth noting that the postbiotic mixture had a more profound effect on the expression of atg7 (p = 0.0004) and atg12 (p < 0.0001) than the paraprobiotic mixture. Furthermore, an increase in the expression of atg13 was observed in both the DSS + Para and DSS + Post groups (Figure 2E), whereas DSS treatments led to a decrease in this expression (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively). However, the difference between the upregulating effect of the paraprobiotic and postbiotic groups was not statistically significant (Figure 2D). In summary, the postbiotic mixture demonstrated a superior protective effect against kidney inflammation in DSS colitis mice by increasing the expression of autophagy‐related genes.

DISCUSSION

The gastrointestinal microbes exert a significant impact on various functions of the human body, including digestion, vitamin synthesis, protection against pathogens, regulation of the central nervous system and immune system, and even human behavior. 33 The importance of the gut–organ axis should be highlighted as a key factor in overall human health. It is essential to recognize the impacts of this relationship on human health to prevent the appearance of many diseases. 34 The gut–kidney axis is a considerable connection between the gastrointestinal tracts and the kidneys, and the relationship highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between these two vital organs for modulating inflammatory responses. 6 The presence of inflammation is similar to what is seen in Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), where microorganisms enter the inner lining of the intestines and bloodstream without restriction. As a result, individuals with IBD are at an increased risk of developing inflammation in other organs. 35 Therefore, it is crucial to use treatments with minimal side effects to manage inflammation and prevent further complications. Paraprobiotics and postbiotics that are derived from probiotics are considered beneficial agents with anti‐inflammatory properties with least side effects compared with living microorganisms. 36 Therefore, the impact of paraprobiotics and postbiotics on these pathways could be considered as a reliable approach for managing inflammation. The aim of this study was to investigate the anti‐inflammatory potency of our indigenous probiotic strains by modulating the autophagy signaling pathway through affecting the gut–kidney axis. The findings presented in this study provide valuable insights into the therapeutic potential of paraprobiotics and postbiotics in managing kidney inflammation.

In this study, we evaluated intestinal inflammation using body mass, colon length, DAI, and pathological score. In the Ctrl group, a significant increase was observed in body mass and colon length, and a significant decrease was observed in DAI and histological scores. However, in the DSS group, the result was completely contrary to that the Ctrl group. A significant decline was observed in body mass and colon length, and an elevation was observed in DAI and histological scores, due to the inflammatory effects of DSS on colon wall. Cochran et al. 37 observed a loss of intestinal barrier function; moreover, Hoffmann et al. 38 have reported weight loss, stool consistency, and blood in stool besides strong diarrhea and colonic hemorrhage. In the DSS + Para and DSS + Post groups, a significant upward trend in body weight and colon length was observed in both treatments. Interestingly, postbiotics could demonstrate a stronger effect on the reduction of inflammation of colon compared with paraprobiotics. The DAI and histological scores decreased as a result of paraprobiotic and postbiotic treatments; meanwhile postbiotics has exhibited high potency for inflammation control and promoting the integrity of colon tissues. On the contrary, Zhang et al. 39 compared postbiotics and probiotics to manage ulcerative colitis or other diseases that are caused by inflammation, and postbiotics can be considered for administration as next‐generation biotherapeutics. Recent studies assessed body weight, colon length, and DAI and confirmed that probiotics and postbiotics can have beneficial effects on intestinal inflammation. 40 , 41

Our focus has shifted toward examining the potential benefits of our native paraprobiotics and postbiotics on the gut–organ axis. Chronic inflammatory diseases are serious conditions characterized by a long‐term decline in kidney function, often resulting from underlying health issues. 42 Disruption of autophagy has been linked to conditions like diabetic nephropathy and age‐related decline in kidney function. Studies on atg5‐deficient mice have shown a progressive increase in proteinuria, whereas proximal tubule‐specific atg5‐deficient mice exhibit age‐related renal dysfunction. 43 , 44 , 45 These observations suggest the protective role of autophagy against renal senescence and inflammation.

According to our results, DSS administration resulted in a decrease in the expression of atg5, atg7, and atg12 genes; however, this decrease did not exhibit statistical significance. In atg13 and beclin, no decrease was observed in gene expression. The observed phenomenon may be a result of the necessity to enhance DSS induction to affect the autophagy pathway in the kidney. Additionally, this might be because autophagy and inflammation have a reciprocal connection, acting as two different aspects of the same coin. In specific scenarios, when inflammation arises, autophagy acts as a controller, averting the harmful consequences of excessive inflammatory reactions. 46 Consequently, it may be vital to adequately manage autophagy gene expression to uphold suitable levels of reduction. In the current study, autophagy‐induced agents appear crucial to sustain the expression levels of autophagy after DSS induction. This maintenance is deemed vital for orchestrating immune activation while mitigating the potential adverse consequences associated with DSS administration. The use of our native potential probiotic strains can significantly increase the expression levels of beclin, atg5, atg7, atg12, and atg13. This demonstrates the beneficial and conclusive effects of paraprobiotics and postbiotics in regulating the autophagy pathway. A comparison of paraprobiotics and postbiotics found that both exhibit similar anti‐inflammatory effects. However, postbiotics can exhibit stronger effects than paraprobiotics on the expression of beclin, atg7 and atg12 in reducing inflammatory effects. The administration of both paraprobiotics and postbiotics instead of living organisms is especially beneficial for individuals who may encounter side effects from traditional probiotics.

LIMITATIONS

A limitation of the current study is that although we observed significant modulation of autophagy‐related gene expression in the kidney tissues, the assessment was primarily conducted at the transcriptional level. Although our findings strongly suggest the activation of autophagy pathways, confirming these effects at the protein level, particularly for key autophagy markers such as the LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio and p62/SQSTM1 degradation, using techniques like Western blot, would provide even more definitive evidence. Due to resource and technical constraints during the experimental period, we focused on gene expression analysis. Future studies must aim to incorporate protein‐level analyses to further elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms of autophagy modulated by our native postbiotics and paraprobiotics.

CONCLUSION

The gut–organ axis has become the focus of research in recent years due to its potential implications for overall health. This intricate connection between the gut and various organs has raised important questions on how our digestive system influences our well‐being. As scientists continue to uncover the complexities of this relationship, understanding the signaling pathways related to the gut–organ axis is crucial for promoting optimal health and preventing diseases. Regarding the importance of the autophagy signaling pathway in intestinal inflammation and its impact on other organs, particularly the kidney, it is imperative to prioritize the administration of anti‐inflammatory and autophagy‐modulatory agents, with minimal side effects in patients with inflammatory conditions like IBD. This approach is crucial in mitigating the harmful effects of inflammation on the gut–organ axis and overall patient health. According to the current study, both paraprobiotics and postbiotics, with more emphasis on postbiotics, could have anti‐inflammatory effects via affecting the gut–kidney axis and through increasing the autophagy signaling pathway. Therefore, these beneficial agents, paraprobiotics and postbiotics, can be administered as a safe supplementary form of treatment, which could be advantageous in modulating inflammation instead of living organisms.
