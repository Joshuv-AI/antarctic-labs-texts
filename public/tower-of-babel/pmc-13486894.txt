Beyond resolution: Multi-scale weather and climate data for alpine renewable energy in the digital twin era
Authors: Irene Schicker, Marianne Bügelmayer-Blaschek, Annemarie Lexer, Katharina Baier, Kristofer Hasel, Paolo Gazzaneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMountain regions concentrate significant renewable energy potential—including approximately 70% of global hydropower capacity—yet, the meteorological datasets essential for planning systematically fail in complex terrain. Using the Alps as a globally transferable testbed, we evaluate datasets spanning the seamless chain from global reanalyses (ERA5, 31 km) through convection-permitting reanalyses (ARA, 2.5 km) to kilometer-scale digital twins (4.4 km) and AI-driven forecasting systems, assessing their performance against energy-relevant phenomena including population-weighted temperature extremes, wind gusts, and compound events. No single dataset excels universally; resolution alone does not ensure accuracy without adequate physics representation. Sparse mountain observations above 1,500 m fundamentally limit validation. We propose six recommendations—from multi-dataset ensemble approaches to a pan-Alpine transboundary reanalysis—providing actionable guidance for energy planners, meteorological service providers, and policymakers navigating the energy transition in complex terrain worldwide.

Introduction: The Alpine energy-climate nexus

When Austrian hydropower production plummeted 44% in early 2025 due to reduced snow cover, it was not just a regional energy crisis—it was a preview of challenges renewable energy systems face worldwide as deployment expands into complex terrain.1 Similarly, 31% declines in hydropower production during the 2022 European drough2,3 highlight a central paradox of the global energy transition: mountain regions hold abundant renewable and water resources, yet pose the greatest challenges for meteorological prediction and climate-risk assessment.

The Alps exemplify this paradox. Extending across eight countries, they offer strong ridge-line winds, high solar irradiation from elevation and snow albedo, and vast hydropower potential from steep gradients and reliable precipitation.4,5,6,7,8 Yet, the very topography that enhances these resources degrades the accuracy of meteorological and climate datasets essential for energy planning and operation—creating an Alpine energy-climate data gap.

The Alpine laboratory for global challenges

Recent attribution studies show how climate change amplifies extreme-weather impacts in mountains, with direct consequences for energy systems. The 2022 compound drought and heatwave, which cut Alpine hydropower to 25-year lows, was 3–5 times more likely due to human-induced warming, with >30% of its intensity attributable to anthropogenic factors.9,10,11 These findings turn “exceptional” events into previews of future operating conditions, underscoring the urgency of tighter climate-energy integration in complex terrain.

New renewable energy sources are equally exposed. Storm Benjamin (October 2025) forced widespread turbine shutdowns across the Alps as gusts exceeded cut-out thresholds (>25 m/s) for over 48 h, while simultaneously causing transmission line damage in exposed mountain passes. On the solar side, the persistent anticyclonic blocking event of January 2017 reduced Alpine photovoltaic output by 60%–80% for nearly three weeks as valley fog persisted below inversions at 800–1,200 m—precisely where most distributed PV installations are located.12,13 Such events underscore that seamless meteorological data spanning historical climate records, nowcasting, forecasting, and climate projections is essential not only for hydropower but increasingly for wind and solar energy planning and operation in complex terrain.

The stakes extend globally. As renewable deployment accelerates into mountains worldwide, planners find that standard meteorological datasets systematically fail in complex terrain.14,15,16,17 Consequently, methods and data innovations developed for the Alps form critical testbeds for renewable energy expansion in other mountainous regions.18 The relevance is greatest for developing countries, where ≈70% of renewable potential lies in complex topography.19,20,21 Alpine data solutions could thus also inform energy security strategies across the Global South, where renewables are expected to supply 85% of electricity-demand growth through 2035.22

Service horizons: What energy systems need from meteorological data

Energy systems interact with meteorological data across fundamentally different service horizons, each imposing distinct requirements on spatial resolution, temporal granularity, and uncertainty characterization.23 Understanding these differentiated needs is essential before assessing where current datasets fall short in complex terrain.

Underpinning all service horizons is the need for high-quality historical climate records, station-based, satellite-derived, and reanalysis datasets spanning the past several decades. These datasets serve as the foundation for resource climatologies (quantifying available wind, solar, and hydro resources), extreme event frequency estimation (how often damaging gusts, Dunkelflaute episodes, or compound events occur at a given location), infrastructure design standards (what wind loads, temperature ranges, and precipitation intensities installations must withstand), and calibration of energy system models that simulate how planned infrastructure would perform under observed climate variability. For Alpine applications, historical datasets ideally combine high temporal resolution (hourly or sub-hourly) with sufficient spatial detail to resolve valley-scale variability, though as discussed in (the ground truth challenge in complex terrain), this combination remains elusive. Critically, much of the actionable intelligence for today’s energy decisions strongly depends on understanding current climate variability before projecting future changes.

At the shortest timescales, sub-hourly to intra-day operations (nowcasting and very-short-range forecasting) support real-time grid balancing, intra-day energy trading, and ramp-event management. On these timescales, temporal resolution of minutes to 1 h is critical, and spatial detail must resolve individual wind farms and solar installations—requirements that push well beyond 9 km (ECMWF IFS) or 4.4 km (Global DT) grid spacing in Alpine terrain where valley-ridge contrasts occur over distances of 1–3 km. Short-to-medium range forecasts (1–15 days) underpin day-ahead and week-ahead energy market participation, maintenance scheduling, and extreme-event preparedness. Ensemble-based probabilistic information becomes essential at these lead times to quantify forecast uncertainty for risk management.24,25 Sub-seasonal to seasonal outlooks (2 weeks–6 months) inform reservoir management, fuel procurement, and hedging strategies. At these timescales, the signal-to-noise ratio decreases markedly, and the value of spatial resolution must be weighed against the reliability of large-scale teleconnection signals.26 Finally, multi-decadal climate projections guide infrastructure investment, site selection, and adaptation planning over asset lifetimes spanning 20–40 years. Here, representing changes in extreme-event frequency and compound-risk distributions matters more than day-to-day accuracy.27,28

Complex terrain: Where standard datasets fail

Standard global reanalysis and climate datasets (25–150 km resolution) cannot resolve the valley-scale processes dominating Alpine meteorology.32,33 Smoothing of elevation leads to misrepresented wind systems and missing key phenomena such as inversions, föhn events, and orographic precipitation processes that directly influence renewable production.34 The challenge extends beyond global models: even station-based gridded observational products struggle to represent the full three-dimensional structure of Alpine meteorology, and even kilometer-scale regional models show mixed performance depending on parameterization.14

Across the Alps, ERA56—the most widely used global reanalysis benchmark—underestimates mountain wind speeds34 and omits temperature inversions that can persist for weeks and critically affect both heating demand and solar output.35,36 These are not merely technical deficiencies but emerging energy-security vulnerabilities. Operational forecasting systems face analogous limitations: models such as ICON (DWD) and AROME (Météo-France, GeoSphere Austria, and others), though widely used across the Alps for day-ahead energy forecasting at 1–3 km resolution, exhibit persistent biases in inversion and fog persistence that directly affect solar generation and demand forecasts, and struggle with gap-flow and lee-wave dynamics critical for wind energy in narrow valleys.

Wind assessments based on coarse data can overestimate generation by 20%–30% in valleys dominated by katabatic flows, while solar estimates neglect fog and low-cloud persistence, overrating winter production.4 Indirect solar radiation via reflectance from snow surfaces—which can enhance irradiance at high-altitude installations by 10%–20%—is similarly absent from most observational and model products, though recent satellite-based radiation datasets have begun to address this gap.37,38 Hydropower projections similarly misrepresent orographic precipitation, underestimating both seasonal variability and extreme-event risks.38 Glacial retreat, precipitation deficits, and regulatory limits further constrain available water.39

The ground truth challenge in complex terrain

A fundamental, often underappreciated challenge is prevailing in all meteorological data assessment in mountainous regions: the ground truth itself is uncertain. The Alpine observation network, though among the densest globally, exhibits a pronounced elevation bias in station density—approximately 90% of surface stations lie below 1,500 m a.s.l., leaving the elevation bands where Alpine wind farms are increasingly sited (1,500–2,500 m) severely undersampled.40,41 Our station-by-station comparison across a subset of 54 Austrian observation sites (Table S1), which reveals systematic altitude-dependent biases. And where stations do exist, measurements themselves can be biased: wind observations on exposed summits are affected by flow distortion, and precipitation gauges at high elevations systematically underestimate snowfall by 20%–50% due to wind-induced undercatch.42,43

This observational sparsity, uneven distribution, and measurement uncertainty create a paradox for high-resolution datasets: as the grid spacing of reanalyses, forecast models, and observational products decreases to 1 km and below, the station density required for meaningful validation may exceed what is available across most of the Alpine domain. The effective resolution of a gridded product—the spatial scale at which its fields carry independent, verifiable information—is often coarser than its nominal grid spacing. Determining this effective resolution requires observations at comparable or finer scales that rarely exist in Alpine terrain.44,45,46 Without adequate verification, higher resolution risks becoming a “1 km illusion”—or, as products increasingly claim sub-kilometer resolution, a “sub-kilometer illusion”—where maps look more detailed but are not demonstrably more accurate.

Several national weather services in the Alpine countries have developed high-quality, station-based gridded observational products using sophisticated spatiotemporal statistical interpolation techniques. Austria’s SPARTACUS47 and INCA,48 Switzerland’s TabsD/RhiresD,49 and Germany’s REGNIE50 provide temperature and precipitation, and other parameters for the INCA model, analyses at 1–5 km resolution. For some variables—particularly temperature during inversions—these mostly purely statistical products can outperform even high-resolution reanalyses because they directly incorporate observed inversion structures rather than relying on model physics to generate them.51,52

However, station-based gridded products have important limitations for energy applications: they typically cover only temperature and precipitation (not wind, radiation, or pressure fields essential for energy conversion calculations), lack the three-dimensional atmospheric structure needed for vertical wind profiles and boundary-layer characterization, and are often restricted by national licensing or data-access policies that limit pan-Alpine analysis. For these reasons, our evaluation (multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap) focuses on three-dimensional reanalysis and forecasting datasets while recognizing station-based products as valuable validation references where available.

The ground truth challenge also reframes the role of the private sector in Alpine energy meteorology. A growing number of companies from established providers to AI-native start-ups—now offer ultra-high-resolution weather and climate data products tailored to the energy industry.23 These commercial products fill important market niches, particularly for site-specific wind resource assessment and operational forecasting. Yet, their proprietary nature and reliance on training data from the same sparse observational networks mean that independent verification of their added value in complex terrain remains largely absent. We argue that national meteorological services play an irreplaceable role in this ecosystem: maintaining high-quality observational infrastructure, producing openly accessible reference datasets, and providing the independent validation framework against which both public and commercial products can be assessed. Collaboration between public institutions and private providers—with clear standards for verification in complex terrain—would provide an important step in building justified confidence in the high-resolution products that energy decisions increasingly rely upon.

Our perspective: Three guiding principles

Existing reviews have documented the need for higher-resolution climate data for energy applications,53,54 and the development of successive generations of reanalyses and regional climate projections (CORDEX, CORDEX-CMIP6 FPS) reflects the community’s ongoing efforts to increase spatial resolution. Our perspective differs from these syntheses in three guiding principles that motivate the recommendations developed in recommendations and future directions.

First, we argue for extending the multi-model ensemble philosophy, already standard practice in operational energy trading, where forecasters routinely combine ECMWF, ICON, GFS, and increasingly AI-based models, to the full seamless chain, including reanalysis inter-comparison for resource assessment and multi-GCM ensembles for climate projections. In complex terrain, the key is diversity of physics and resolution: if all models share the same resolution limitation, combining them does not recover missing valley-scale processes. No single reanalysis, forecast, or climate projection can satisfy all service horizons simultaneously. Instead, different datasets serve different purposes like ERA5 for multi-decadal climatology, high-resolution regional ensemble reanalyses like ARA for validation and process understanding including uncertainties, operational forecasting systems and the new arising digital twin systems for real-time decisions, and climate digital twins for future projections, and their systematic inter-comparison reveals both the robustness and the uncertainty of energy-relevant conclusions.

Second, we emphasize that spatial resolution alone is insufficient and that temporal resolution and data continuity are equally critical. The energy sector’s push for finer grids must be balanced against the need for sub-hourly temporal resolution (essential for grid operations and ramp detection), ensemble information (essential for probabilistic trading), and multi-decadal duration (essential for infrastructure investment decisions). In complex Alpine terrain, a 1 km dataset with hourly output may be less useful for grid balancing than a 5 km dataset with 15-min output and reliable ensemble spread. This “beyond resolution” perspective motivates our title and frames our evaluation in multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap.

Third, we advocate for co-designed weather and climate services developed jointly with energy sector users rather than post-hoc adaptation of existing meteorological products. The mismatch between what meteorological data providers produce and what energy system operators need is well documented,31 but translating this recognition into operational practice requires new institutional arrangements, shared evaluation frameworks, and explicit attention to the ground truth problem discussed above. The destination Earth initiative’s digital twins (emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap) represent a first large-scale attempt at such co-design, and our evaluation offers an independent assessment of their readiness for Alpine energy applications.

Wind systems: From valley circulations to extreme gusts

Mountain meteorology differs fundamentally from flat-terrain dynamics due to three-dimensional atmospheric flow over steep topography.55 Wind phenomena in Alpine terrain span scales from thermally driven valley circulations (∼1–5 km) to synoptically forced föhn events (∼10–50 km), creating a multi-scale challenge that no single dataset resolution can fully address.

Valley winds, among the most predictable Alpine phenomena, are still underrepresented in standard reanalysis and forecast products. These thermally driven circulations—upslope and up-valley by day, downslope and down-valley by night—typically reach 2–5 m/s.28,56,57,58 Yet simulations coarser than ∼1 km miss these patterns entirely, with biases persisting even at 550 m resolution.59 In station-based gridded products, valley winds present a different challenge: interpolation schemes cannot infer the three-dimensional flow reversal from surface temperature and pressure observations alone, limiting these products to surface-level wind estimates that miss the vertical structure driving energy generation at turbine hub heights. In some narrow valleys, even sub-kilometer resolution may be necessary to capture the full circulation structure.

Föhn winds illustrate the dual nature of Alpine flow: warm downslope gusts exceeding 40 m/s can both supply energy and damage infrastructure.60,61,62 Their frequency and intensity remain underestimated because orographic triggers occur below ∼25 km scales.63 Beyond orographic detail, modeling requires accurate representation of upstream diabatic processes, adiabatic descent on the lee side, and the interaction with pre-existing cold pools in lee-side valleys before the föhn breaks through—dynamical processes that challenge even convection-permitting models.64,65 For nowcasting (Table 1), föhn breakthrough timing errors of 2–4 h can mean the difference between profitable trading positions and costly imbalances for wind farm operators in affected valleys.

Before addressing vertical wind profiles, a more fundamental gap must be noted: reliable gridded surface wind products in Alpine terrain are often unavailable entirely. Unlike temperature or radiation, wind speed is too dynamically variable and terrain-dependent to be meaningfully interpolated from sparse station networks using statistical methods alone—making even satisfying surface-level analyses elusive in complex terrain. The wind challenge manifests differently across service horizons. For observational and reanalysis products, ERA5 underestimates Alpine wind speeds systematically, with correlation coefficients dropping from ∼0.9 over flat terrain to ∼0.65 in Alpine valleys.34 Wind biases shift from −1.2 m/s in valleys to +0.8 m/s on ridges, reflecting the fundamental inability of 31 km grid cells to resolve channeling and acceleration effects. Regional reanalyses mitigate some errors: CERRA achieves wind correlations of 0.75–0.82,66 and COSMO-REA6 up to 0.84,34,67 yet accuracy declines with elevation.68 For extreme wind events, the shortcomings are most acute: ERA5’s 99th-percentile winds correspond only to the observed 90th percentile, implying a 1.5–2× underestimation of gusts that directly affects turbine survival class determination and insurance assessment. For short-range forecasting, current operational NWP models at 2–9 km resolution capture large-scale wind patterns but still struggle with gap flows and lee-side acceleration zones where wind farms are frequently sited. For climate projections, coarse-resolution models (>10 km) cannot provide the valley-specific wind statistics required for 25-year infrastructure investment decisions, while convection-permitting projections (emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap) are limited to short time slices.

These wind data limitations have direct operational consequences. Poorly resolved valley-wind and inversion patterns cause multiple wind farms to under- or over-perform simultaneously, creating regional balancing challenges.57 Because current products miss the coherent meteorological structures linking sites, grid operators must compensate with larger reserves and conservative operating strategies—reducing the economic competitiveness of renewables in complex terrain.

Solar radiation, temperature inversions, and energy demand

Temperature inversions and their associated fog and low-stratus layers create persistent winter microclimates absent from coarse datasets. These stable layers, occurring up to 35% of the time between November and February, reduce valley solar input by 30%–50% while enhancing resources above the inversion.4,35,36 For solar energy, the critical consequence is persistent valley fog: when inversions trap moisture below 800–1,200 m, photovoltaic installations in the densely populated valley floors receive minimal irradiance for days to weeks, while ridge-top installations above the inversion operate near clear-sky conditions. This vertical decoupling, invisible in any dataset coarser than ∼2 km, creates simultaneous over- and under-production relative to forecasts within the same grid cell of a standard reanalysis product. For energy demand, inversions concentrate cold air in valleys where most of the population lives, driving heating demand peaks that population-weighted temperature analysis (multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap) can quantify but area-averaged datasets systematically underestimate.

Terrain shading further complicates solar assessment: Alpine valleys often receive only 2–4 h of winter sunlight. Satellite-based irradiance products overestimate valley insolation by > 100 W/m2, especially under fog and cloud.13,69,70 Snow-albedo enhancement at high elevations, which can increase irradiance by 10%–20% at Alpine PV installations, is similarly underrepresented in most products, though recent satellite-based solar radiation datasets are beginning to improve this.37,38

Across the seamless chain, temperature biases show complex terrain-type and elevation dependence that varies between products, regions, and weather regimes. In ERA5, valley stations typically exhibit warm biases during winter inversions, reflecting the model’s inability to resolve cold-pool formation, while exposed summit stations can show cold biases whose origins are less well understood.71 The magnitude and even the sign of these biases depend on the specific reanalysis product, subregion, season, and prevailing flow regime, making single-number generalizations across the Alps problematic. For extreme events, the 2003 heatwave’s peak temperatures are 2°C–4°C too low in Alpine valleys even in ERA5. For climate projections, the temperature bias compounds with model spread: the difference in projected heating degree-days between GCMs can exceed the bias of any single reanalysis product, a point we return to in recommendations and future directions when discussing uncertainty characterization. Solar-resource estimates show ±4%–8% uncertainty in high-relief regions, mainly due to coarse satellite resolution and unresolved shading, which inflate winter production estimates.69

Precipitation, snow, and water resources

Precipitation in complex terrain is shaped by orographic enhancement on windward slopes, rain-shadow effects on lee sides, and the critical temperature threshold determining snow versus rain partitioning—all processes with profound implications for hydropower operations. Orographic precipitation enhancement can amplify rainfall by factors of 2–5 over short distances,41,72 yet coarse-resolution datasets systematically underestimate these gradients because model topography smooths the ridgelines that trigger upslope lifting. Even at 5 km resolution, precipitation patterns in narrow valleys can be displaced by one or two grid cells, placing the heaviest rainfall in the wrong catchment for hydrological modeling.

The snow-rain partitioning threshold (∼0°C–2°C, elevation-dependent) is especially consequential for Alpine energy systems. Under current climate change, this threshold is migrating upward, converting an increasing fraction of winter precipitation from snow to rain.73 For hydropower, this shift reduces seasonal snow storage that feeds spring and summer runoff while increasing winter flood risk—fundamentally altering the temporal distribution of available water resources. Hydropower projections similarly misrepresent orographic precipitation, underestimating both seasonal variability and extreme-event risks.38 Glacial retreat, precipitation deficits, and regulatory limits further constrain available water.39

For the observational data chain, precipitation presents the most severe ground truth challenge in Alpine terrain: gauge undercatch at high-elevation stations can reach 20%–50% for snowfall,42,43 radar beam overshooting misses low-level precipitation in deep valleys, and spatial interpolation between sparse mountain stations introduces substantial uncertainty in catchment-scale totals. These observational limitations directly constrain the quality of reanalysis products that assimilate them and the validation of forecast and climate models that depend on them for verification.

Compound extremes and cascading infrastructure risks

Compound extreme events, where multiple meteorological phenomena or hazards occur simultaneously, pose an escalating challenge for Alpine energy systems that current meteorological datasets cannot adequately capture. Such events create cascading impacts exceeding the sum of individual drivers and expose core weaknesses in existing energy-planning frameworks.39,74

Among the most operationally consequential compound events for renewable energy systems, whether co-occurring at a single location or affecting geographically distributed installations connected to the same grid, are Dunkelflaute episodes—periods of simultaneous low wind and low solar generation that stress backup and storage capacity.75,76 In Alpine terrain, Dunkelflaute events are amplified by inversions that simultaneously suppress both valley-level wind and solar resources, potentially lasting days to weeks during persistent anti-cyclonic blocking episodes. The most critical variant is the Cold Dunkelflaute: when low renewable generation coincides with extreme cold driving peak heating demand, energy systems face simultaneous supply shortfall and demand surge, creating the most severe stress for grid adequacy, particularly in Alpine network segments with limited interconnection capacity where localized supply security risks can be genuine even within the broader European market. A feature unique to complex terrain is the vertically structured Dunkelflaute: during persistent inversions, valley-floor PV and wind installations experience energy drought conditions while ridge-top turbines above the inversion may generate at or near rated capacity. Whether such a situation constitutes a system-level Dunkelflaute depends entirely on the spatial and vertical aggregation scale of the analysis, a distinction invisible in any dataset coarser than ∼2 km and absent from the flat-terrain Dunkelflaute literature that has predominantly considered horizontal spatial aggregation.39,75,77 These events are notoriously difficult to represent in standard reanalysis products because they depend on the correct simultaneous representation of cloud cover, wind, and temperature—three variables whose biases compound rather than cancel in Alpine terrain. Many of these compound events share a common large-scale driver: persistent weather regimes, particularly anti-cyclonic blocking and ridging patterns, create the conditions for simultaneous low wind, valley fog, temperature inversions, and drought that individually and jointly stress energy systems.6,12 This regime conditioning has two important implications. First, it explains why compound events are not random co-occurrences but physically coherent phenomena whose components are linked through the same synoptic-scale circulation. Second, it connects compound event risk to sub-seasonal predictability: weather regime transitions are among the most predictable features at 2–4 weeks lead times, offering a pathway for anticipating compound energy events before they develop—a point we return to in sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy.

As introduced in the Alpine laboratory for global challenges, the 2022 European drought-heatwave shows how multiple stressors interact: simultaneous drought (reducing hydropower by 6.5%), extreme heat (surging cooling demand in densely populated valley floors and adjacent lowlands), and glacial mass loss (reducing future seasonal storage) created compounding impacts that no single-hazard analysis would have predicted.11,78,79,80,81 Attribution studies show these extremes were 3–5 times more likely due to anthropogenic warming, with >30% of their intensity directly linked to human influence.9,10,82 By the late 21st century, about 20% of global land areas may face two such drought-heatwave events annually, each lasting around 25 days.83,84

Compound events create cascading infrastructure risks specific to the Alpine context. Alpine energy systems operate in isolated networks with limited redundancy, where meteorological prediction errors compound through interconnected infrastructure systems. Access to Alpine infrastructure is often limited during adverse weather, amplifying vulnerabilities when forecasts are inaccurate. Mountain roads, cable routes, and helicopter access can be blocked for days, delaying repairs when föhn events damage transmission lines in remote areas.85,86 A single föhn event may simultaneously damage transmission lines and accelerate snowmelt, compounding risks that exceed component design limits. Hydropower across mountain regions will face greater sediment fluxes in some seasons and reduced flows in others as competing demands intensify.87 Extreme-weather damages already exceed €790 billion and 246,000 fatalities in Europe since 1980,88 and without adaptation, losses are expected to rise sharply.89 An often-overlooked compound event in Alpine terrain is the coincidence of snow accumulation on photovoltaic panels with forecast clear-sky conditions: when snowfall occurs but is not predicted—or clears later than forecast—PV installations produce far less than expected precisely when solar conditions would otherwise be favorable, causing substantial balancing costs as grid operators procure replacement energy at short notice and high prices.

These compounding risks underscore a central message of this study: energy-relevant meteorological data in complex terrain must be evaluated not only for individual variables in isolation but for their ability to represent the co-occurrence and spatial coherence of multiple phenomena simultaneously. The emerging solutions discussed in emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap begin to address this gap, but as our evaluation in multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap demonstrates, significant challenges remain.

Legacy and current operational reanalysis datasets

Meteorological reanalyses have steadily advanced data quality for complex terrain, yet key limitations persist. ERA5, ECMWF’s fifth-generation global reanalysis, remains the operational benchmark, offering hourly data from 1940 onward at 31 km resolution.90,91 It introduced major improvements over ERA-Interim, including higher temporal resolution and a 10-member ensemble (ERA5-EDA, the Ensemble of Data Assimilations, which provides flow-dependent uncertainty estimates by perturbing observations and model physics) that provides uncertainty estimates. Nevertheless, ERA5’s coarse resolution cannot resolve valley-scale Alpine processes. The companion ERA5-Land (surface) product increases spatial detail to ∼9 km through offline land-surface downscaling, enhancing the quality of near-surface fields and orographic representation.92

Regional reanalyses bridge the gap between global products and local requirements. The Copernicus European Regional ReAnalysis (CERRA) operates at 5.5 km resolution since 1984, using the HARMONIE-ALADIN model with three-dimensional variational (3D-Var) data assimilation and additional national observations.93 CERRA provides improved surface-variable realism in complex terrain and includes an 11 km ensemble (CERRA-EDA, analogous to ERA5-EDA at regional scale) offering flow-dependent background error estimates. Validation of two different reanalysis products (CERRA and ERA5-Land) confirms superior skill for wind, temperature, and biometeorological variables in mountainous regions.2

The COSMO-REA family, developed by Germany’s DWD, extends this progress with COSMO-REA6 (6 km, Europe-wide, 1995–2019) and COSMO-REA2 (2 km, convection-permitting, central Europe).67,94 Both use nudging-based assimilation with dedicated snow and soil-moisture analyses. COSMO-REA6 achieves mean absolute wind-speed errors of 0.69 m/s—clearly outperforming global reanalyses—although production ended in 2019 following ERA-Interim’s discontinuation. More recently, DWD has developed ICON-DREAM, a km-scale reanalysis based on the ICON model (https://doi.org/10.5676/dwd/icon-dream_v1), and MeteoSwiss is producing an ICON Reanalysis-Light over Switzerland at convection-permitting resolution (https://doi.org/10.18751/nwp/rea-l-ch1/1.0). Together with France’s ARRA95 and Denmark’s DANRA,68 these initiatives signal a broader European trend toward national high-resolution reanalyses—underscoring both the growing demand and the fragmentation that motivates our recommendation 2 for a coordinated pan-Alpine product.

The Austrian reanalysis (ARA) ensemble71 represents the first reanalysis tailored explicitly to Alpine terrain. Using the non-hydrostatic AROME model at 2.5 km resolution with convection-permitting physics, ARA applies 3D-Var assimilation C-LAEF (Convection-permitting Limited Area Ensemble Forecasting) techniques, which generate ensemble spread through perturbations of initial conditions, lateral boundaries, and model physics to produce a 10-member plus control run system spanning 2012–2021. By assimilating satellite, radiosonde, aircraft, and wind-profiler data, ARA markedly improves representation of valley-wind systems, temperature inversions, and precipitation structures compared with coarser products.

Convection-permitting climate modeling and CORDEX FPS-convection

Not just convection-permitting climate modeling but also weather prediction is revolutionizing our ability to represent mountainous region meteorology. The CORDEX Flagship Pilot Study on Convective Phenomena (CORDEX FPS-Convection) represents the first multi-model ensemble of kilometer-scale simulations over the extended Alpine domain, building a benchmark dataset for assessing added value at convection-permitting scales.27 The initiative encompasses hindcast simulations forced by ERA-Interim reanalysis and historical and future climate scenario simulations, with multiple regional climate models operating at 2–3 km resolution over the Alpine region.

Recent studies document substantial added value from CORDEX FPS-Convection simulations. For temperature over complex orography, convection-permitting simulations demonstrate systematic improvements in representing maximum and minimum temperatures compared to coarser-resolution counterparts.96 For near-surface wind speed, the multi-model ensemble shows significant added value over the Alps, with convection-permitting models better capturing the spatial variability and extreme values critical for wind energy applications.97 The ensemble approach allows quantification of both model uncertainty and the magnitude of improvements achievable through increased resolution.

In recent years, researchers have pushed computational limits further, advancing into the hectometric scale (100 m–1 km resolution) entering the gray zone between convection-resolving and large eddy simulations, as in the destination Earth on-demand digital twin (sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy). These high-resolution simulations can partly resolve or parameterize large eddies associated with up- and downdrafts near clouds, improving understanding of convection and cloud formation processes.98,99,100 While hectometric scale climate simulations offer advances in representing complex terrain processes, they pose validation challenges due to sparse observations at comparable resolutions, making high-density observation networks and high-resolution reanalysis datasets increasingly critical.

It is important to note that for climate projections, the uncertainty from the choice of global climate model (GCM) and emission scenario often dominates over the uncertainty from regional model resolution. For precipitation in particular, the spread across GCMs can exceed the difference between running a regional model at 10 km versus 1 km.27,101 This underscores why our first paradigm shift (our perspective: three guiding principles)—embracing multi-dataset ensembles rather than seeking a single highest-resolution product—is essential for robust energy-climate assessment.

Operational analysis and nowcasting systems

The Integrated Nowcasting through Comprehensive Analysis (INCA) system, developed by GeoSphere Austria (formerly ZAMG), provides high-resolution analyses and short-term forecasts (every 15–60 min) tailored for mountainous terrain.48 INCA merges surface observations with radar and satellite data at 1 km spatial and hourly temporal resolution, reproducing local station values while adding spatial structure from remote sensing. It delivers temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, cloudiness, and solar radiation—core variables for renewable-energy applications. INCA and similar nowcasting analysis systems are also used or adapted by neighboring Alpine weather services.

INCA shows particularly strong skill for temperature, with clear added value during the first few forecast hours and continued improvement when dynamically downscaling NWP guidance at longer lead times.48,102 For Alpine energy use, it acts as a vital interface between coarse global models and the detailed meteorological information required for operational decisions. The system underpins flood forecasting, winter maintenance, and public weather services across Austria.

Looking ahead, hybrid AI-physics approaches offer a promising next step. The Anemoi framework103 could eventually extend nowcasting capabilities similar to those of INCA toward rapid, AI-driven ensemble generation for localized renewable-energy forecasting and extreme-event monitoring. Critically, however, the skill of any AI-based system is fundamentally constrained by the quality and representativeness of its training data—a limitation shared with statistical post-processing methods that learn from historical model-observation pairs. For Alpine applications, AI models trained exclusively on global reanalyses (e.g., ERA5 at 31 km) will inherit their systematic biases in complex terrain—missing valley winds, underestimating inversions, and smoothing orographic precipitation gradients (Alpine meteorological challenges for energy systems). Both AI-based and statistical approaches can also degrade during novel extreme events that fall outside their training distribution—precisely the compound events whose frequency is projected to increase under climate change. High-resolution regional reanalyses such as ARA (2.5 km) or the pan-Alpine product proposed in recommendation 2 could provide substantially better training targets, while dense observational networks (recommendation 6) are essential for both training and independent validation.

Next-generation forecasting: Physics-based and AI-driven approaches

The ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) remains the operational backbone of European medium-range prediction, advancing through regular cycle upgrades. The high-resolution configuration (HRES) runs at 9 km with 137 levels, while the 51-member ensemble (ENS) reached the same 9 km grid with Cycle 48r1 (June 2023).104 This represents the highest-resolution global deterministic and ensemble forecasting system currently in operation and provides key boundary conditions for regional models across ECMWF Member States. IFS employs advanced four-dimensional variational (4D-Var) assimilation, ingesting ∼60 million satellite, aircraft, radiosonde, and surface observations every 6 h. For Alpine applications, it delivers the large-scale forcing and validation reference for emerging AI-based systems.

The Artificial Intelligence Forecasting System (AIFS), operational since February 2025, marks a milestone as the first fully operational AI-based global model.105 Built on a graph neural network (GNN) architecture using an encoder-processor–decoder design trained on ERA5 and IFS analyses, AIFS achieves comparable or superior skill to physics-based models for many variables. It generates global 10-day forecasts in minutes on GPUs—about 1,000 times more energy-efficient than conventional systems—and its 51-member ensemble (AIFS-ENS) became operational in July 2025.1 For Alpine energy forecasting, AIFS offers the speed and scalability needed for rapid ensemble generation during extreme events. However, AIFS is trained on ERA5 and IFS analyses, meaning it inherits their resolution-dependent biases in complex terrain. While the graph neural network architecture can learn implicit bias corrections, it cannot recover physical processes absent from the training fields entirely—such as valley-scale circulations unresolved at 31 km. This training-data dependency underscores why continued investment in high-resolution reanalysis production (recommendation 2) is essential not only for direct use but also as the foundation for next-generation AI forecasting systems. While we focus on the IFS-AIFS system as the most prominent global example, other operational medium-range systems—including ICON (DWD), ARPEGE (Météo-France), and the Met Office Unified Model—serve Alpine energy applications through their national implementations. In operational energy trading, multi-model ensembles combining ECMWF, ICON, GFS, CMC, and increasingly AIWP models are already standard practice for quantifying forecast uncertainty.

Beyond global models, regional AI forecasting is rapidly evolving. Met Norway’s “Bris” system applies a stretched-grid architecture with 2.5 km resolution over the Nordic region while retaining coarser (∼100 km) global coverage.106 Trained on ERA5 and MetCoOp ensemble data, Bris delivers 10-day forecasts in 2–3 min on a single GPU and already outperforms operational physics-based systems for temperature.107

This stretched-grid concept underpins the Anemoi framework, a collaborative open-source initiative led by ECMWF and European partners to develop regionally focused AI forecasting systems.103 By combining AI inference with traditional modeling, Anemoi provides a blueprint for future kilometer-scale, AI-driven nowcasting and regional prediction, directly supporting renewable-energy and extreme-event applications in complex Alpine terrain.

Alongside public-sector advances, a growing number of private companies now provide high-resolution weather data products for the energy sector, many leveraging AI/ML techniques trained on public reanalysis and forecast data. Companies ranging from established providers to AI-native start-ups offer site-specific forecasts at resolutions surpassing those of operational NWP systems. While these products serve important market needs—particularly for intra-day trading and site-specific resource assessment—their proprietary nature limits independent validation, and their skill in complex Alpine terrain remains largely undocumented in the peer-reviewed literature. As discussed in (the ground truth challenge in complex terrain), robust verification frameworks for commercial products in complex terrain represent an important gap that public–private collaboration could address.

An essential component of the operational data chain not covered in detail here is statistical and machine-learning-based post-processing, through which the energy sector currently bridges many of the biases documented in (Alpine meteorological challenges for energy systems). Methods including model output statistics (MOS), ensemble MOS (EMOS), analog techniques, and neural networks routinely reduce site-specific forecast biases by 30%–50% at individual locations.108 For Alpine terrain, where complex topography creates systematic, location-specific biases that are highly predictable, post-processing is often the primary mechanism translating raw model output into operational decisions. However, post-processing cannot correct for physical processes entirely absent from the underlying model—the valley-scale circulations, inversion structures, and orographic precipitation patterns that motivate the resolution discussion in this study. It can also degrade during novel extreme events outside the training distribution, a limitation with growing relevance as climate change produces conditions with diminishing historical precedent. These limitations reinforce the need for better raw products alongside improved post-processing.

Sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy

Sub-seasonal to seasonal (S2S) prediction occupies a distinct niche in the seamless chain, bridging deterministic weather forecasting and climate projections at lead times of 2 weeks–6 months.26 For Alpine energy systems, S2S outlooks inform reservoir management strategies, seasonal fuel procurement, maintenance scheduling, and hedging decisions in forward energy markets (Table 1).

The challenges at this horizon differ fundamentally from those at shorter timescales: predictability over the Alps derives primarily from large-scale teleconnections (NAO, MJO, ENSO) and land-surface memory (soil moisture, snow cover) rather than from initial atmospheric conditions, meaning that high spatial resolution adds comparatively little value at these lead times.26 The Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) provides operational seasonal forecasts through the SEAS5 multi-model system. For Alpine applications, these systems face specific limitations: (i) the predictability of blocking episodes—which drive persistent Dunkelflaute and cold-spell events (compound extremes and cascading infrastructure risks)—remains low beyond 2–3 weeks; (ii) coupled modeling of snow accumulation and melt, critical for hydropower inflow forecasting, is sensitive to systematic biases in precipitation and temperature that compound over seasonal lead times; and (iii) very large ensembles (50+ members) are needed to capture the low signal-to-noise ratio, placing demands on ensemble generation methods that both physics-based and emerging AI-based approaches must address. The C3S Energy operational service1 already translates S2S predictions into energy-relevant indicators, providing a prototype for the co-designed climate services advocated in our recommendation 5.

Digital twins and kilometer-scale Earth system modeling

The European Union’s Destination Earth (DestinE) initiative represents a fundamental shift toward operationally relevant, high-resolution Earth system modeling.109 DestinE comprises two complementary digital twins addressing critical Alpine energy-climate challenges.(1)The weather-induced extremes digital twin (Extremes DT) combines a global continuous component at 4.4 km resolution with an on-demand regional component achieving 500–750 m resolution over Europe. The on-demand system, developed by Météo-France with 22 European partners, provides configurable simulations triggered when the ECMWF IFS Ensemble predicts extreme events exceeding predefined thresholds, delivering regional forecasts at 500–750 m resolution over the affected area within operational time frames, achieving spatial scales sufficient to resolve individual Alpine valleys and local orographic processes.109 This capability enables unprecedented impact assessment for renewable energy infrastructure during extreme weather events.(2)The climate change adaptation digital twin (Climate DT), implemented by Finland’s CSC-IT Centre for Science with partners across six countries, provides multi-decadal global climate simulations at approximately 4–9 km resolution. The Climate DT employs innovative data streaming concepts, allowing impact-sector applications to access information during simulation production rather than post-processing stored data.110 For renewable energy applications, the Climate DT enables assessment of future extreme event frequency and provides boundary conditions for hectometric-scale simulations of Alpine energy systems under climate change.

Copernicus has begun translating these advances into operational energy-sector products through the Pan-European Climate Database (PECD v4.2), which provides standardized climate variables tailored for energy system modeling across Europe, including derived indicators such as capacity factors, heating/cooling degree-days, and renewable resource time series.93 While PECD currently relies on ERA5 as its primary input, future versions may benefit from the higher-resolution datasets discussed here.

These digital twins build upon nextGEMS (next generation Earth modeling systems), a Horizon 2020 project that achieved the first multi-decadal (30-year) climate simulations at kilometer scales using the ICON and IFS-FESOM Earth system models. Operating at approximately 5 km ocean resolution and 10 km atm resolution, nextGEMS simulations achieved a throughput of about 500 simulated days per day on the Levante supercomputer, providing unprecedented realism in representing convective processes, orographic effects, and ocean eddies.57 NextGEMS models form the scientific foundation of the DestinE Climate DT and pave the way for future European research on climate change impacts at scales directly relevant to energy infrastructure planning.

Importantly, the DestinE initiative represents one of the first large-scale attempts at co-design between meteorological data producers and sectoral users, including the energy sector. Through its digital twin engine and the DESP (DestinE Service Platform), energy stakeholders can access and customize simulations for their specific applications. However, the co-design process has also revealed persistent barriers: energy users often lack the technical capacity to work with raw model output, metadata standards differ between meteorological and energy communities, and the latency between simulation production and user-accessible products remains too long for operational applications. These barriers—documented through DestinE’s user engagement workshops—inform several of our recommendations in (recommendations and future directions).

The convergence of high-resolution reanalyses, convection-permitting climate models, operational digital twins, AI-driven forecasting, and initial co-design efforts creates unprecedented opportunities to narrow the Alpine data gap across all service horizons (Table 1). For sub-hourly operations, INCA and AI-based nowcasting offer real-time valley-scale information; for short-to medium-range forecasting, the IFS-AIFS combination and GraphCast provide both physical consistency and computational efficiency; for sub-seasonal to seasonal outlooks, coupled prediction systems such as C3S SEAS5 provide ensemble-based hydropower and demand indicators despite limited Alpine-scale predictability (sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy); and for climate projections, CORDEX FPS-Convection and the DestinE Climate DT enable km-scale multi-decadal assessment. The integration of physics-based and AI-driven approaches enables both rapid ensemble generation for operational decision-making and long-term projections for strategic planning. However, whether these datasets deliver meaningful improvements for Alpine energy applications requires systematic evaluation against the phenomena and challenges described in (Alpine meteorological challenges for energy systems)—which is the focus of (multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap).

Multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—Bridging the gap

This section evaluates a subset of the datasets introduced in Table 2 (emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap) for their utility in Alpine renewable energy applications. Rather than providing a comprehensive benchmark—which would exceed the scope of a perspectives study—we present representative results that demonstrate how spatial and temporal resolution choices fundamentally affect energy-relevant conclusions in complex terrain. An important limitation of the evaluation presented here is that all comparisons are based on single deterministic fields or control members rather than full ensemble distributions. This restriction applies to all service horizons, including reanalysis products, where emerging ensemble-based systems (e.g., ERA5-EDA or CERRA-EDA) provide essential information on structural uncertainty that is not captured by single deterministic realizations. For operational energy applications, probabilistic information—exceedance probabilities, ensemble spread, and confidence intervals—is often more decision-relevant than any single realization.24 For compound events specifically, ensemble information is essential because these are by definition rare occurrences whose probability cannot be assessed from individual deterministic runs. Extending the evaluation to include ensemble-based metrics represents a priority for future work, particularly for medium-range and seasonal timescales where ensemble information is most critical. The value of ensemble information also varies systematically across forecasting horizons: in the context of short-range and nowcasting, it primarily reflects the uncertainty of the initial conditions, whereas in medium-range and seasonal applications, it increasingly captures the growth of dynamical uncertainty and climate variability. Extending the evaluation framework to include ensemble-based metrics therefore represents a priority for future work, particularly for medium-range and seasonal timescales, where probabilistic information is most critical for energy system decision-making. The evaluation progresses along three service horizons: reanalysis datasets establishing historical baselines (reanalysis comparison: ERA5, ERA5-Land, and ARA [2020]), high-resolution forecasting for specific extreme events (extreme event case study: storm Benjamin [october 2025]), and preliminary climate digital twin projections (climate digital twin: preliminary projections at 4.4 km). Datasets evaluated

From the datasets cataloged in Table 2 (emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap), we focus on reanalyses because they provide the historical baselines most directly used for energy resource assessment, infrastructure planning, and extreme event frequency estimation—the foundational service horizons identified in service horizons: what energy systems need from meteorological data. The selected datasets span the range of currently available resolutions for the Alpine domain: ERA5 (∼31 km, hourly, 1940–present), ERA5-Land (∼9 km, hourly, 1950–present), CERRA (∼5.5 km, hourly, 1984–present), ARA (∼2.5 km, hourly, 2012–2021), and the Climate DT (∼4.4 km, hourly, multi-decadal projections). Additionally, for the Storm Benjamin case study (extreme event case study: storm Benjamin [october 2025]), we compare the ECMWF IFS operational forecast (9 km), the Extremes DT (4.4 km), and AIFS (∼31 km). This selection captures the trade-off between global coverage and Alpine-specific detail, and between long temporal records and high spatial resolution—the central tension identified in our perspective: three guiding principles.

In recent years, several initiatives on generating high resolution reanalysis datasets have been launched,68,71,95 including the formation of a new WEMC (WMO Energy and Meteorological Council) reanalysis working group (https://www.wemcouncil.org/wp/reanalysis-working-group/).

Storm days (mean wind ≥17.5 m/s) determine wind turbine survival requirements and transmission line failure risk; ARA resolves both the topographically enhanced wind corridors and the sheltered valleys where calm conditions persist, patterns systematically averaged away in coarser reanalyses.

A notable feature is the storm-hour maximum apparent in southeastern Germany in ERA5 and ERA5-Land (G and H) that largely vanishes in ARA (I). This likely reflects differences in gust estimation: the gust parameterization applied over ERA5’s smoothed orography may overestimate wind exposure in regions where the actual terrain provides sheltering, whereas ARA’s convection-permitting resolution explicitly resolves more of the orographic flow modification. This discrepancy illustrates why gust-related metrics require careful cross-resolution interpretation in complex terrain.

Across all three indicators, ARA’s convection-permitting resolution reveals micro-regional hotspots where population exposure, energy demand, and infrastructure vulnerability intersect—patterns that are systematically averaged away in coarser reanalyses. These results suggest that sub-5 km resolution is necessary for identifying where climate extremes and energy system stress co-occur. Reanalysis products at this resolution also serve as training and validation data for stretched-grid AI weather prediction models such as Bris,106 extending their benefit beyond direct use.

Important caveat: these spatial comparisons demonstrate that higher resolution reveals finer structure, but they do not by themselves prove that this structure is more accurate. Verifying whether ARA’s valley-scale patterns are correct requires point-by-point validation against independent station observations—a step that is essential but beyond the scope of this perspectives study, and itself constrained by the station sparsity problem discussed in the ground truth challenge in complex terrain (approximately 90% of Alpine stations lie below 1,500 m). Preliminary station comparisons for temperature and wind speed (Figure S1; Table S1 in the supplementary material) indicate that ARA reduces RMSE by 20%–40% compared to ERA5 in valley stations, consistent with Awan et al.,³ but a systematic multi-variable verification across the full Alpine domain remains an important priority for future work. We emphasize this limitation because it embodies the core message of this study: higher resolution is necessary but not sufficient without robust validation frameworks.

Extreme event case study: Storm benjamin (october 2025)

Storm Benjamin hit western and Central Europe on October 23rd, 2025, with widespread gusts exceeding 120 km/h, heavy rainfall, a high coastal surge risk, and localized tornadic activity. Its rapid evolution and small-scale wind maxima, including a tornado north of Paris,120 exposed the persistent resolution gap between global and regional forecasting systems.

We use this event to illustrate how forecast resolution affects the detection of energy-critical wind gust extremes—and how the Extremes DT’s 4.4 km resolution (shared by the Climate DT) enables a more detailed view of such systems. With European windstorm intensity projected to increase under warming,121 such events represent growing risks for wind and grid infrastructure, underscoring the need for high-resolution, ensemble-based prediction.

Comparing the three forecast products at T+24 h lead time (Figure 3), three findings stand out. First, the Extremes DT’s 4.4 km resolution captures fine-scale wind gust maxima exceeding 15 m/s and mesoscale features—including orographically enhanced gusts in the northern Alpine foothills—that are smoothed in the IFS 9 km product and absent in AIFS at ∼31 km. Second, the Extremes DT shows partially higher intensities in maximum gust speeds and longer duration of severe conditions, suggesting that coarser products may underestimate infrastructure exposure during such events. Third, AIFS—while currently limited in its ability to resolve valley-scale extremes and underestimating absolute wind intensities—captures the synoptic-scale wind patterns and tendencies, offering potential for rapid ensemble generation that could be downscaled to higher resolution in future operational configurations. The Destination Earth Climate DT simulations at 4.4 km resolution offer a first glimpse of climate projections at scales relevant to Alpine energy infrastructure. Building on the nextGEMS project—which demonstrated that multi-decadal, sub-10 km Earth system modeling is computationally viable—the Climate DT enables valley-scale climate information for adaptation planning.

Our analysis covers January–March 2020 and the full year 2022, providing both a focused winter assessment and a complete annual cycle from the Climate DT IFS-based simulations. These results demonstrate that km-scale climate projections can provide site-specific information relevant to municipal-level adaptation planning. However, several important caveats apply. Recommendations and future directions

Bridging weather and climate data for Alpine renewable energy planning and operations requires integration across spatial, temporal, and process scales. No single dataset performs optimally everywhere: coarse global reanalyses offer long climatological baselines but miss valley-scale dynamics, while kilometer-scale digital twins such as the Climate DT (4.4 km) or the Extremes DT (global 4.4 km, on-demand 500–750 m) capture key Alpine processes but remain computationally demanding. Effective planning ideally combines datasets—ERA5 for long-term characterization, regional reanalyses (CERRA, ARA) for operational use, and emerging high-resolution projections for infrastructure adaptation.

However, a major remaining gap concerns temporal resolution. Hourly data suffice for resource assessments but not for grid operations that depend on minute-scale variations in wind and solar output. Even 3-h changes mask finer fluctuations crucial for real-time balancing. Future operational digital twins ideally operate with 10–15 min output frequency to align with energy market trading cycles and grid balancing intervals, especially in complex Alpine terrain where weather changes rapidly. Closing this temporal-resolution gap will require co-designed data systems, validation frameworks, and joint investment to ensure renewable-driven grids to remain stable and resilient.

Drawing on the challenges documented in Alpine meteorological challenges for energy systems, the emerging solutions surveyed in emerging solutions: bridging the Alpine data gap, and the evaluation presented in multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap, we formulate six recommendations. These operationalize the three guiding principles articulated in our perspective: three guiding principles: (1) moving from single datasets to multi-dataset ensembles, (2) recognizing that spatial resolution alone is insufficient without adequate temporal resolution and uncertainty information, and (3) co-designing climate services with energy users rather than adapting meteorological products post hoc. Each recommendation below indicates which principle(s) it primarily addresses.

Recommendation 1: Match data with application (principle 2)

Select meteorological datasets based on specific application needs, considering spatial and temporal resolution requirements, parameter availability, and quality characteristics. The service horizon framework (Table 1) provides concrete guidance: sub-hourly nowcasting products (INCA, future Anemoi-based systems) for intra-day trading and grid balancing; operational forecasts (IFS, AIFS, Bris) for day-ahead to medium-range planning; regional reanalyses (CERRA, ARA) for historical resource assessment and extreme event climatology; and km-scale climate projections (CORDEX FPS-Convection, Climate DT) for long-term investment decisions and adaptation planning. There is a steep increase in available data related to research focusing on mountainous areas and intensified market needs. Therefore, regular assessment of available datasets and parameters is recommended—we suggest that the WEMC reanalysis working group and IEA Tasks could serve as institutional homes for maintaining an up-to-date dataset catalog.

Recommendation 2: Combine multiple sources (principle 1)

Integrate information from multiple datasets to overcome individual limitations. For example, combine high-resolution reanalysis with site-specific measurements and specialized downscaling techniques in complex terrain. We advocate moving beyond national boundaries toward a coordinated, pan-Alpine approach. Currently, each Alpine country produces its own high-resolution products (ARA in Austria, ARRA in France, COSMO-REA in Germany/Switzerland), each with different model physics, resolutions, and temporal coverage. We recommend that Alpine meteorological services pool resources to produce a unified, transboundary convection-permitting reanalysis covering the entire Alpine arc—analogous to what CERRA achieved at the European scale but at 1–2.5 km resolution. Such a product would eliminate cross-border discontinuities that currently complicate energy system modeling for interconnected Alpine grids, reduce duplication of effort, and create a pan-Alpine training dataset for AI-based forecasting systems such as Bris or Anemoi. The WEMC reanalysis working group provides a natural institutional framework for coordinating such an initiative. More broadly, this multi-source philosophy should be deployed globally to other mountainous regions undergoing renewable energy transitions.

Recommendation 3: Validate against independent observations and energy-relevant metrics (principle 2, 3)

Rigorously validate datasets against independent measurements, particularly in complex terrain regions where model performance may vary significantly. Benchmark datasets against independent observations using both standard and sector-specific measures: diurnal wind reversals, inversion frequency, orographic precipitation, and föhn detection. Critically, validation in complex terrain is a two-sided challenge (the ground truth challenge in complex terrain): not only must model datasets be rigorously examined, but the observational ground truth itself requires thorough quality assessment. Station measurements in mountains can be biased (e.g., 20%–50% precipitation undercatch at high-elevation gauges), the station network is sparse above 1,500 m where many renewable installations operate, and the “effective resolution” of any gridded product is constrained by the observation density available for assimilation or validation as illustrated in Figure S1, verification metrics vary substantially across the Alpine domain, with valley stations and high-altitude exposed sites showing distinct error characteristics. We recommend that validation studies explicitly report the observation density and representativeness of their verification network, and that the community develop shared benchmark observation datasets for Alpine energy meteorology—including collocated measurements of wind, radiation, and temperature at representative energy-relevant sites across different elevation bands and terrain types.

Recommendation 4: Characterize and communicate uncertainties (principle 1, 2)

Systematic acknowledgment, characterization, and transparent communication of uncertainties is a fundamental prerequisite for all service areas, as it ensures the reliability and usability of climate, weather, and energy information in decision-making processes. The imperative to characterize uncertainty applies across all service horizons. In operational weather forecasting, energy sector stakeholders have incurred substantial costs from over-reliance on deterministic forecasts—for instance, when single-valued wind power predictions fail to capture the range of plausible outcomes during high-impact ramp events, leading to costly imbalance penalties. The IEA wind task 36 recommended practices (RP2) provides evaluation standards specifically designed for probabilistic energy forecasting, including reliability diagrams and improvement-over-climatology metrics. For reanalysis products, this means leveraging ensemble members (ERA5-EDA, ARA ensemble) to provide spread estimates rather than relying on single deterministic fields. For climate projections, the spread across driving GCMs and emission scenarios often dominates over the uncertainty from regional model resolution (convection-permitting climate modeling and cordex fps-convection)—a point frequently underappreciated in energy sector applications where a single high-resolution projection may be treated as definitive. We recommend that climate service providers deliver uncertainty ranges alongside central estimates, using formats accessible to energy planners (e.g., capacity factor ranges rather than raw meteorological spreads). Uncertainty quantification for energy applications is inherently interdisciplinary, as total uncertainty stems not only from climate data but also from technological assumptions (turbine power curves, panel degradation) and socio-economic developments (demand scenarios, policy changes). Frameworks that propagate meteorological uncertainty through energy system models—rather than treating weather/climate as a fixed input—represent an important frontier for applied research.

Recommendation 5: Standardize validation and co-design climate services (principle 3)

Adopt, develop, and implement standardized validation protocols with metrics specifically designed for renewable energy applications. As demonstrated in multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap, comparing datasets requires common evaluation frameworks—the phenomena-based approach (Table 3) represents one such framework, but community-wide adoption requires institutional support. Existing initiatives provide foundations: IEA wind task 36 (forecasting for wind energy), whose recommended practice on forecast solution selection (RP2) provides standardized evaluation metrics, and task 51 (forecasting for the weather-driven energy system) as well as the IEA PVPS task 16 [solar resource for high penetration and large scale applications] have developed verification best practices; the C3S Energy operational service)23 provides standardized energy-relevant climate indicators; and the WEMC is establishing reanalysis intercomparison protocols. We recommend building on these efforts to develop Alpine-specific validation standards that include the energy-relevant phenomena identified in this study (wind gusts, Dunkelflaute, compound extremes, population-weighted temperature) and that explicitly address the ground truth limitations in complex terrain. International adoption of such standards would ensure consistent evaluation across the growing landscape of commercial and public-sector products, enabling energy users to make informed dataset selection decisions.

Recommendation 6: Invest in observational, computational, and data access infrastructure (principle 1, 3)

Support sustained investment in three complementary areas. First, observational networks: the Alpine observation network has critical gaps above 1,500 m (the ground truth challenge in complex terrain), precisely the elevations where wind farms are increasingly sited and where model verification is most difficult. Observational gaps are equally critical in valley floors, where inversions, fog, and cold pools create large model biases that directly affect energy demand estimation—an additional recommendation is ensuring dense-enough station coverage in these valleys alongside high-altitude sites. We recommend targeted deployment of multi-variable measurement stations (wind, radiation, temperature, precipitation) at energy-relevant sites—including wind farm locations, PV installation sites, and hydropower catchments—to create the ground truth infrastructure that both model validation and AI training require. National meteorological services provide the institutional backbone for maintaining such networks; their role should be strengthened, not supplanted, as commercial providers expand their offerings. Second, computational infrastructure: producing and maintaining km-scale reanalysis and climate projection datasets for the entire Alpine region requires sustained access to high-performance computing resources. The pan-Alpine reanalysis proposed in recommendation 2 would benefit from coordinated European HPC allocation (e.g., through EuroHPC or DestinE’s DESP). Third, data access and delivery: even the highest-quality datasets have limited impact if energy users cannot access them efficiently. We recommend investment in cloud-native data formats (Zarr, ARCO), API-based access platforms, and derived energy-sector products such as PECD v4.2, explained in sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy that translate raw meteorological fields into variables directly usable by energy system models—capacity factors, degree-days, ramp rates, and extreme event indices. The DESP provides a prototype for such infrastructure, but barriers identified through user engagement (sub-seasonal to seasonal prediction for Alpine energy)—including technical capacity gaps, metadata incompatibilities, and latency—must be addressed for operational uptake.

Emerging operational digital twins and AI-driven forecasting systems enable, for the first time, energy-relevant meteorological information at deployment scales across all service horizons. Translating this technical capability into operational benefit requires the three principles we have advocated throughout this study, operationalized through our six recommendations: embracing multi-dataset ensembles rather than seeking a single “best” product (recommendations 2 and 4); moving beyond spatial resolution as the sole quality metric to recognize that temporal resolution, uncertainty information, and multi-decadal duration are equally critical (recommendations 1 and 6); and co-designing climate services with energy users from the outset, ensuring that formats, latencies, and derived variables meet operational needs (recommendations 3, 5, and 6). Three priorities for future work emerge directly from these recommendations. First, translating meteorological extremes into energy system impacts by leveraging derived products (e.g., PECD v4.2) to quantify capacity factor shortfalls, ramp events, and system adequacy risks directly (recommendations 1 and 5). Second, developing sub-hourly forecasting capabilities essential for grid stability in high-penetration renewable systems, combining coarse-temporal global models with high-frequency nowcasting systems that blend observations with model guidance (recommendations 1 and 6). Third, extending the multi-resolution evaluation framework presented here to full annual cycles, additional Alpine subregions, and future climate projection periods—including ensemble-based compound event assessment (recommendation 3). The compound extreme analysis—particularly Dunkelflaute, Cold Dunkelflaute, and the “triple energy drought” combining low wind, low solar, and low hydropower inflow simultaneously—represents an especially urgent priority given its outsized impact on Alpine energy security. To conclude, this perspectives study has argued that the Alpine region serves as both a critical laboratory and an urgent proving ground for the climate-energy nexus. The challenges documented here—systematic dataset biases in complex terrain, sparse observational ground truth, compound extreme events that defy single-variable analysis—are not unique to the Alps. Approximately 70% of global renewable energy potential lies in mountainous regions,122 and the methodological framework, evaluation approach, and recommendations we present are transferable to the Andes, Himalayas, Scandinavian mountains, and other complex terrain contexts worldwide. As the energy transition accelerates and extreme event frequencies intensify under climate change, the gap between what meteorological datasets provide and what energy systems require will widen unless the paradigm shifts advocated here are actively pursued. The tools are emerging; the imperative now is to deploy them systematically, collaboratively, and with the sustained investment that the energy transition demands.

Data analysis

Data acquisition and preprocessing

All datasets were processed in their native spatial resolution to preserve resolution-specific features. ERA5 data (T2m, U10, V10, SSRD, TP) were retrieved at 0.25° (∼31 km) hourly resolution; ERA5-Land at 0.1° (∼9 km); CERRA at 5.5 km on its native Lambert conformal conic projection; and ARA ensemble control member at 2.5 km on a rotated latitude-longitude grid. Climate DT IFS-based scenario data were obtained at 4.4 km for January–March 2020 and the full year 2022. Storm Benjamin forecasts were compared across IFS ENS control member (9 km), Extremes DT (4.4 km), and AIFS (∼31 km) for the T+0 to T+36 h window initialized 23 October 2025.

Preprocessing comprised (i) extraction of the Alpine domain (approximately 4°–18°E, 43°–49°N); (ii) conversion of accumulated variables (surface solar radiation downward, total precipitation) to instantaneous rates using backward differencing; (iii) derivation of 10 m wind speed from U and V components; and (iv) unit harmonization (temperature in °C, wind in m/s, radiation in W/m2). No inter-dataset regridding was performed; all comparisons are grid-point-wise on each dataset’s native mesh.

Energy-relevant indicators

Wind gusts were estimated from mean wind speed using the gust factor approach of Wieringa,58 adapted for energy applications by Schicker and Meirold-Mautner,59 for datasets not providing native gust output (ERA5, ERA5-Land). Population-weighted temperature extremes were computed by overlaying gridded fields with the Global Human Settlement Layer population grid117 at 1 km, aggregated to each dataset’s native grid. Heat extremes: days with T_max ≥30°C. CDDs: Σ max (0, T_mean −18°C) following VDI 3787. Heating degree days: Σ max (0, 15°C − T_mean) following VDI 3787. Storm hours: hours with estimated gusts ≥15 m/s (ESSL severe-wind threshold).

Dunkelflaute episodes were identified as periods where wind capacity factor fell below 10% and solar capacity factor fell below 5% simultaneously, sustained for a minimum of 48 h. Wind capacity factors were derived using a parametric power curve69; 3 MW reference turbine, hub height 100 m, cut-in 3 m/s, rated 12 m/s, cut-out 25 m/s. Solar capacity factors used PVlib with a fixed-tilt reference system (1 kWp, 30° tilt, south-facing). Compound events were classified following the typology of the typology of Zscheischler et al.118; Cold Dunkelflaute was defined as simultaneous Dunkelflaute conditions with T_mean <0°C.

Quantification

This perspectives study presents primarily qualitative multi-resolution comparisons rather than formal statistical hypothesis testing. Quantitative metrics include threshold exceedance counts (heat days, cold days, gust events by ESSL category), population-weighted spatial statistics (mean, maximum, and spatial standard deviation), and degree-day totals. Differences between datasets are reported as absolute values and percentages relative to ERA5. No formal significance testing was performed, as the comparisons involve deterministic model outputs at different resolutions rather than statistical samples. All computations used Python 3.10+ with x-array, NumPy, and dask. Threshold definitions and their derivation from operational standards are provided in Table 3.

Limitations of the study

This perspectives study evaluates a subset of the rapidly expanding landscape of meteorological datasets for Alpine energy applications; notably, COSMO-REA6/REA2 and several satellite-based radiation products (e.g., CM SAF SARAH/HANNA) were not included in the quantitative comparison due to scope and data access constraints. The evaluation in multi-resolution dataset evaluation for Alpine energy applications—bridging the gap relies on spatial inter-comparison between datasets at different resolutions rather than systematic verification against independent station observations; consequently, finer spatial structure in higher-resolution products does not by itself demonstrate greater accuracy. The climate digital twin analysis is limited to a three-month winter-spring window (January–March 2020), which is insufficient for robust climate-scale conclusions and does not capture summer phenomena critical for solar energy and cooling demand. ARA ensemble data were available only from 2017 onward, precluding long-term climatological assessment against ERA5’s multi-decadal record. Finally, our evaluation focuses on the Alpine region of central Europe; while the meteorological challenges discussed (valley winds, inversions, orographic precipitation) are transferable to other mountainous regions globally, the specific dataset performance and threshold values reported here may not generalize without regional adaptation.
