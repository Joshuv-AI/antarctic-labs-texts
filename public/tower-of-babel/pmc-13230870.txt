Multi-criteria based stable clustering technique for vehicular ad-hoc networks
Authors: Abdul Waheed Khan, Javed Iqbal Bangash, Shahid Kamal, Zul Hilmi Bin Abdullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vehicular Ad-hoc Network (VANET) plays an important role in the realization of the Intelligent Transportation System (ITS) which leads to a plethora of new applications such as traffic/drivers safety, traffic management, and infotainment. However, the high mobility and dense distribution of the vehicles on the road cause problems for routes management and network scalability. These issues can be effectively resolved by employing clustering techniques which not only supports scalability of network thereby eradicating broadcast storms caused by packets flooding but also simplifies medium access control. Despite its numerous advantages, cluster formation and sustainability of clusters in high mobility scenarios is one of the major challenges in VANETs. To improve stability of the cluster, efficient cluster head selection in accordance with the various traits of VANETs among candidate vehicles is desired. In this paper, a Multi Criteria based Stable Clustering technique (MCSC) for VANETs is proposed. The proposed MCSC technique includes a novel feature of average Past Cluster head Lifetime (PCL) along with relative speed, distance and degree of connectivity for efficient selection of Cluster Head (CH) and elects Substitute Cluster Head (SCH) to maintain the stability of cluster structures. Apart from CH selection, cluster formation, maintenance and clusters merging features are also incorporated. A simulation based performance evaluation is conducted to evaluate efficacy of the proposed technique through different parameters and benchmarking with state-of-the-art. Demonstrated by simulation results, the proposed MCSC technique outperforms existing clustering techniques in VANETs with respect to key stability indicators. Specifically, MCSC improves the cluster-head and cluster-member lifetimes by approximately 16% and 14.5%, respectively, compared to existing approaches. Furthermore, the proposed technique reduces the re-clustering rate by about 13%, indicating enhanced cluster stability under dynamic vehicular conditions.

Introduction

Vehicular Ad-Hoc Network (VANET), a sub-class of Mobile Ad-Hoc Network (MANET), is a self-organized wireless network of vehicles along the road. With the advancements in automobile technology, VANET lays the foundation for the implementation of ITS application. These applications include traffic management, traffic safety and sharing infotainment messages1. In VANETs, vehicles are equipped with on-board units (OBUs) which allows them to communicate with each other, called V2V communication. Likewise, vehicles are also able to communicate with deployed infrastructure e.g. road side unit (RSU), thereby connecting to central traffic management. Figure 1 shows communication architecture of VANET.

Compared to MANETs, vehicles in VANETs are highly mobile and have variable density which causes frequent changes in network’s topology2. Due to the dynamic nature of the network, VANETs pose several challenges, including scalability to support system and application-level services, as well as security and privacy3,4. Researchers have scrutinized the idea of clustering in VANETs which aims to transform flat network into hierarchical network by forming groups of vehicles called clusters via some sort of clustering techniques. Each cluster has number of cluster members (CMs) and a leader, known as cluster head (CH). The CH, acts as an access-point thereby providing a kind of virtual infrastructure, and is responsible for inter and intra cluster(s) communication. In this way, benefits of infrastructure-based network are availed without the cost of infrastructure deployment5. A clustering structure within the network reduces the communication overhead, supports routing6, and security7. However, it is inevitable that the highly dynamic topology of VANETs will interrupt the already established clusters and result in increased communication overhead for clusters maintenance8. One of the important desired attributes of clustering techniques especially in VANETs is to avoid frequent re-clustering thereby reducing the management overhead.

Clustering in VANETs provides a stable architectural framework that facilitates subsequent routing, MAC, and trust management operations. Through hierarchical communication via stable cluster heads, clustering not only reduces control overhead but also enables more reliable and long-lasting routes even under high mobility scenarios. Likewise, clustering greatly assists MAC operations by coordinating channel access and avoiding collisions. In the security domain, clustering offers a framework for localized authentication, misbehavior detection, and trust evaluation. More broadly, clustering enables effective cross-layer optimization in VANETs by jointly enhancing routing stability, improving MAC-layer efficiency, and supporting robust trust management. However, the effectiveness of all these operations fundamentally depends on the stability of the formed clusters. The primary focus of this research is to achieve stable clustering in VANETs without explicitly addressing routing, MAC, or trust management mechanisms, as improved cluster stability inherently supports and enhances these downstream operations.

In this paper, we propose a stable clustering technique for VANETs to construct stable clusters such that to maximize lifetime of both cluster-heads and cluster-members while minimize re-clustering rate. In order to attain stability of clusters, firstly we propose a Connectivity LifeSpan (CLS-based) neighbors filtering scheme to filter out unstable neighboring vehicles. Secondly, an efficient CH selection among stable neighbors is conducted on the basis of relative mobility, degree of connectivity and past cluster life. Thirdly, the selection of substitute CH is incorporated to avoid frequent clustering in case of temporary disconnection of CMs with respective CH due to speed variation. These strategies enable the proposed clustering technique to attain relatively more stability than other state-of-the-art. It is worthwhile to mention that the high-level architecture of our proposed MCSC technique shares functional blocks with existing paradigms, the essential novelty lies in the integrated design, and uniquely unified by stability history i.e. PCL. Unlike existing schemes combining instantaneous metrics, the PCL serves as a historical filter, pre-qualifying inherently stable vehicles before weighted metrics are applied thereby fundamentally altering the CH election outcomes. Furthermore, the backup cluster head is an integral part of our design, working in continuity with the PCL-based primary cluster head to enhance stability and provide focused resilience against transient disconnections. Similarly, clusters merging is conditioned on stability rather than proximity alone, avoiding instability caused by conventional approaches. Thus, while functional blocks appear classic, the design philosophy, component interdependence, and stability-driven metrics collectively distinguish our work as an integrated stability framework.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: “Related work” describes previous work related to cluster formation and maintenance in VANETs. “Materials and methods” presents our proposed technique. “Performance evaluation” includes performance analysis of the proposed technique by taking into account clustering performance metrics and comparison with recent techniques. The "Limitations and future work" section highlights the current constraints of the study and outlines potential directions for further research to address these limitations. Finally, the paper is concluded in “Conclusion” section.

Related Work

Clustering techniques—partitioning nodes into CHs and CMs on the basis of certain criteria are being in use for quite some time. They are particularly applicable in MANETs and dozens of clustering techniques have been proposed by various researchers. However, due to high mobility of vehicles and frequent topological changes, clustering techniques proposed for MANETs are not directly applicable in VANETs. For instance, Fan et al.10,11 attempted to apply MANET techniques in VANETs to form clusters and to improve broadcasting performance in inter vehicle communication, but their approaches could not adapt to traffic dynamics.

Despite great diversity in the literature, clustering techniques nearly follow the same procedural flow. The fundamental clustering procedure includes cluster head selection and cluster maintenance schemes. In VANETs, due to high dynamic nature of vehicles, it is difficult for vehicles to communicate with each other in the form of cluster for extended times and maintain the topology. In this scenario, re-clustering and high cluster maintenance cost will reduce efficiency of network and invalidate the purpose of clustering. Therefore, to form stable clusters and sustain their stability, are the vital issues in clustering techniques for VANETs. Researchers have proposed many clustering techniques based on mobility metrics to form stable clusters. This section presents a critical review of relevant clustering techniques for VANETs.

Some studies aim to achieve stable clustering along with reliable and efficient communication. In Ref.12, proposed a clustering mechanism based on iterative Trimmed and Truncated Mean (iTTM) algorithm and Criteria Importance Through Inter Criteria Correlation (CRITIC) technique aiming to improve the clustering stability. It attains the stated aim in two steps: In the first step, iTTM is used to collect the temporal relationships among the vehicles. In the second step, the CH is selected based on multi-decision CRITIC using consensus trust score, connectivity level, link lifetime and relative speed. It struggles to maintain stable clusters under high mobility because frequent changes in vehicle positions and speeds trigger constant re-elections, reducing cluster stability. Another technique aiming to achieve the goal of the reliable and stable CH selection is proposed in Ref.13. The proposed framework is based on Adaptive Neighboring Behavior-Incentivization (ANB-I), where the CH selection process is based on the Multi-Criteria Multi-Decision (MCMD) using link lifetime, neighborhood degree and relative average speed. Trust-based Stable Clustering (TSC)14 is the mechanism that also aims to select stable CH. The CH selection process is based on multiple parameters including degree, trust value, position, speed, and remaining travel time of the vehicles. These schemes slightly improve the stable clustering but introduces more overheads due to weighing multi-criteria and trust computation.

Some researchers have used the parameter of link lifetime (LLT) to sample surrounding neighbors and form clusters with stable neighbors. MDMAC15 introduced LET (Link Expiration Time) in messages by the vehicles, to find k-hop neighbors and avoid high-speed CH candidates. The main idea is to reduce number of cluster changes and avoid re-clustering to achieve cluster stability. Density based clustering introduced in Ref.16 further extends the LET mechanism thereby adding link quality measurement through SNR to assess the suitability of CH to form stable clusters. Ji et al.17 aims to improve efficiency and reliability by organizing vehicles into clusters and designating cluster heads for data forwarding. While the approach enhances communication performance under moderate mobility, it relies on relatively static CH selection and assumes predictable link durations. In highly dynamic VANET scenarios, where vehicle speeds and positions change rapidly, the already formed clusters quickly become outdated, resulting in frequent re-clustering.

In most of the techniques, there is one CH for each cluster. But, in scenarios where due to speed variation or change in direction, the CH remains unavailable, re-clustering is inevitable. In order to maintain the stability of network and avoid re-clustering, many researchers have proposed different techniques to select secondary cluster head that immediately switches its role as CH once the predecessor leaves the cluster, so that the cluster does not break. A stability-based clustering technique (SBCA)18 aims to decrease the communication overhead for the cluster formation and maintenance and to increase cluster lifetime. While SBCA selects CH based on link stability to reduce re-clustering, it relies heavily on predicted link lifetimes and relative vehicle speeds. In highly dynamic traffic scenarios, where vehicles frequently change speed, direction, or lane, such predictions lead to incorrect decisions thereby causing frequent re-clustering and instability. Additionally, SBCA does not fully account for rapid topology changes in dense or heterogeneous traffic, limiting its effectiveness in real-world high-mobility conditions.

In Ref.19 proposed a clustering technique which considers the behavior of vehicles for efficient cluster head selection and also employs a backup CH to preserve the stability of cluster structures. But, CH selection is purely being made on basis of speed of vehicles which does not yield stable clusters. DHC20 is a clustering technique which uses mobility metrics and communication link quality metrics which includes the SNR and LET for cluster formation. Moreover, secondary CH is selected for cluster maintenance and cluster merging feature is also incorporated to prolong the life of cluster. The drawback of this technique is that it results in increased overhead while evaluating cluster merging options because all CMs are taken into confidence before making final decision. Furthermore, in case of connection of CM with CH and SCH, CM simply joins the next available CH as a member node without assessing whether it may itself qualify to become the CH. Lacking this self-assessment feature contradicts the formation of stable clusters.

Some of the existing techniques in literature make use of threshold and weight based mechanisms for CH selection. TB and UFC21,22 are some examples of threshold based clustering technique. The vehicle that firstly announces itself as a CH wins the competition and turns out to be CH. The drawback of this type of schemes is that it may potentially ignore the most eligible CH which affects stability of cluster. In weight-based method, each vehicle is assessed by different metrics to act as CH, such that the one with high value is selected as CH. In this regard, authors in Ref.23 proposed a clustering scheme to form clusters based on relative distance and velocity of the vehicles and future position by using Gauss Markov Model. But, this probabilistic approach is vulnerable to degraded network performance in case of high variation in mobility. In Ref.24,25 cluster head selection is on account of mobility metrics which include relative velocity, distance and acceleration. But, there is Infrastructure based cluster maintenance and reforming which contradicts with the typical nature of ad-hoc network and increases maintenance overhead.

Some researchers have added reputation and trust as metrics along with mobility metrics for cluster head selection. TACR26 takes into account velocity and position along with trust metrics to calculate cluster head selection metric. Trust metrics ensure capability of cluster head and also provide secure communication within cluster. VWCA27 selects a cluster head on the basis of degree of connectivity, vehicle direction, an entropy value evaluated from the mobility of vehicles, and a distrust level. MOFA28 takes into consideration the direction, position, velocity and degree of vehicles, ID of lane and reputation of each node for stable topology of VANETs. But, reputation score is based on number of times vehicle perform the task of CH, this may increase reputation score of vehicle to be selected even for short period of time. In order to deal with this, we introduced PCL to examine the consistency of CH through its duration. Behura et al.29 proposed a multi-objective cluster-based routing approach aiming to enhance security and routing efficiency in VANETs. While effective in ensuring secure communication, the method does not explicitly address cluster stability under highly dynamic urban traffic conditions. Its evaluation focuses on network performance metrics rather than clusters longevity, making it more suitable for highway or low-mobility scenarios. Kumar et al.1 introduced Enhanced Clustering based Efficient Relay Vehicle (ECERV) selection framework for VANETs. The CH selection is mainly influenced by instantaneous network conditions, which can result in frequent cluster reconfigurations in high mobility scenarios.

Based on literature review, it is revealed that majority of existing clustering techniques for VANETs lead to unstable clustering especially in high mobility scenarios which increases communication overhead. Taking this into account, we aim to develop a stable clustering technique that is not only in accordance with the traits of VANETs but also avoids frequent re-clustering caused by high mobility of vehicles.

Materials and methods

In this section, we propose a cluster-based vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication technique without any need of deployed infrastructure such as RSUs for communication among vehicles. We assume that each vehicle is equipped with On board Unit (OBU) containing a Global Positioning System (GPS), a Dedicated Short-Range Communication (DSRC) wireless unit and a Processing Unit (PU). The vehicle uses the GPS to obtain its real-time position, velocity and moving direction and PU is used to run the clustering technique on each vehicle separately. Vehicles periodically share their information with each other via hello messages. These messages include vehicle’s identity, current velocity, current position, moving direction, and past CH Lifetime (PCL). The vehicle which has not played any CH role in the past, its PCL value is considered as zero. Our technique is applicable for both urban and highway traffic. The roads may have multiple lanes and are bi-directional but with no intersections.

We assume a fully trusted operational environment in this work where vehicles share messages without any malicious intent. The main purpose of this study is to evaluate stability of clusters under realistic mobility conditions and therefore we do not consider adversarial threats such as forgery of messages, Sybil, or spoofing attacks. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that real-world deployment would require integrating lightweight authentication and integrity protection mechanisms to ensure the reliability of the information exchanged during cluster formation and maintenance.

Our proposed model MCSC is a distributed technique. Every vehicle runs this technique independently after getting information of other vehicles through hello messages. In this section, we illustrate the vehicles’ state with and without clustering, neighbors’ filtration strategy and techniques for cluster formation, maintenance and merging. Neighborhood discovery

Initially, every vehicle which joins the road is in the UC state. Any vehicle wishing to become part of VANET, starts sending periodic Hello messages to announce its presence and to share its information with the surrounding neighbors. This information includes the vehicle ID (VID), velocity (Vi), two-dimensional position (x, y), direction (Dir) and average past cluster head lifetime (PCLavg).

Once a Hello message is received from neighboring vehicles, not all of them are supposed to be grouped in the same cluster. Firstly, considering the direction of vehicles, vehicles moving in opposite direction will be removed from the neighbors list. Next CLS based neighbors filtering scheme is employed to classify neighbors into stable and unstable neighbors considering transmission range of the vehicle, relative velocity and distance between the vehicles.

CLS-based neighbor filtration

To improve clusters stability, we introduce CLS-based neighbor filtration scheme to pick out stable neighbor (SN) from neighboring vehicles. Connectivity Lifespan (CLS) represents the predicted time span in which two neighboring vehicles may stay connected. CLS is calculated using Eq. (1). Here, Δdij and Δvij represent the difference of distance and velocity between two vehicles Vi and Vj, respectively. R represents the transmission range of a vehicle. CLS helps to estimate the connection sustainability. Larger CLS value indicates that connection is more sustainable.

The expression for CLS estimation assumes constant relative velocity and linear trajectory over the short prediction interval, a widely adopted approximation in VANET clustering literature that remains reasonably accurate over typical beaconing intervals (100 ms to a few seconds). We acknowledge that real-world maneuvers such as lane changes, sudden acceleration, and turns can affect CLS accuracy. However, our framework incorporates several practical mitigations: (1) directional filtering restricts CLS calculation to same-direction vehicles (Algorithm 1, line 3), eliminating invalid opposite-direction encounters; (2) frequent updates recalculate CLS upon every beacon reception (Algorithm 1, lines 4 and 9), ensuring continuous reflection of current mobility; (3) threshold-based selection (δSN) filters out vehicles with uncertain predictions; and (4) multi-criteria robustness combines CLS with Degree of Connectivity and Past Cluster Lifetime, reducing reliance on any single metric.

Cluster head selection

After filtering stable neighbors, vehicles automatically start CH selection process. We define an eligibility parameter namely Composite Clustering Function (CCF), to assess the fitness of a vehicle to act as a cluster head. CCF is formulated in Eq. (2). CLSij denotes connectivity lifespan with stable neighboring vehicles, Degi denotes the number of stable neighbors of a specific vehicle and PCL gives the value of past cluster head lifetime of vehicle which is a valuable parameter to evaluate its eligibility. A higher PCL value indicates that the vehicle has experience in forming stable clusters, which supports more effective cluster-head selection and improves overall network performance. It is worthwhile to note that in homogeneous traffic conditions where connectivity, density and mobility are fairly uniform, all the three metrics contribute equally toward achieving stable cluster head selection and the overall clustering stability, and therefore equal weightages of 0.333 are assigned to the corresponding coefficients α, β, and γ. For heterogeneous or dynamic traffic conditions, vehicles compute their weightages for α, β, and γ locally based on observed CLS values, neighbors density and PCL history to ensure that stability, connectivity, and experience contribute proportionally to the current network conditions. Cluster formation and maintenance

Due to high mobility of vehicles in VANET, there is frequent change in state of vehicles, which causes maintenance overhead. So, the aim of cluster formation & maintenance is to maintain the cluster topology as stable as possible in different scenarios. A set of procedures are proposed to sustain cluster topology in high dynamic nature of network. Algorithm 3 governs the process considering these different scenarios.

While monitoring connection with CH, when CM misses two consecutive beacon messages from CH, it sends a join request to SCH to make sure cluster remain intact even in the absence of selected CH. After getting response from SCH, CM joins the cluster and monitors connection with SCH as CH. While the SCH mechanism effectively reduces re-clustering events, it is worth mentioning that its performance depends on communication reliability. The proposed MCSC incorporates a tolerance mechanism requiring two consecutive missed beacons before triggering SCH takeover which prevents false transitions due to transient link disconnection. However, if a CM’s join request to the SCH or the SCH’s response is lost during takeover, the CM reverts to UC state, causing SCH takeover to fail and triggering full re-clustering overhead. Additionally, if SCH response delay exceeds the CM’s timeout, the node unnecessarily transitions to UC despite SCH availability, highlighting a trade-off between detection speed and delay tolerance.

Computational complexity and scalability analysis

The proposed MCSC is fully distributed and ensures scalability in large-scale deployments. The decision-making process of each vehicle depends entirely on local information from adjacent vehicles, communicated through the periodic exchange of Hello or beacon messages. The computational overhead for each vehicle is estimated as follows: neighboring filtering and CLS computation incurs O(N); Adaptive weight computation requires O(1); and CCF calculation and CH/SCH selection results in O(N) complexity. For being fully distributed in nature, the proposed MCSC technique results in overall computational complexity of O(N) thereby making it independent of the total number of vehicles. Moreover, the communication overhead of the proposed MCSC technique in the form of control messages exchanges like Hello, CH selection, and SCH notifications are limited to local broadcast. This localized and distributed design enables MCSC to maintain stable clustering performance even in large-scale VANETs scenarios.

Performance evaluation

In this section, we describe simulation setup and results. We used Veins simulator for simulation based performance evaluation of the proposed MCSC technique against ECERV1 and TSC13. Both ECERV and TSC aim to form stable clusters for VANETs thereby making use of multiple parameters.

Simulation Setup

For VANETs simulation in an urban setting, we considered a road length of 30.0 km having bi-directional traffic where each direction is comprised of three lanes. In all our experiments, we ran the simulation for 200 s with different deployments of vehicles. Every experiment was repeated 10 times and the results were averaged out for robustness purposes. Likewise, the total number of vehicles was varied from 50 to 300 having velocity ranging from 10 m/s to 35 m/s. Similarly in different set of experiments, wireless transmission range of vehicles is set in the range from 200 m to 500 m. A summary of the simulation parameters is presented in Table 2.

Performance metrics

Stability index of the proposed MCSC technique is measured and evaluated in terms of average CH duration, average CM duration and re-clustering rate. Furthermore, the performance evaluation in terms of aforementioned metrics is carried out in various network dynamics including the impact of vehicle velocity, the number of vehicles and the vehicles transmission range.

For any clustering technique especially in VANETs, variation in velocity of vehicles has a direct impact on the stability of clusters and therefore it should be taken into consideration for evaluation purposes. Likewise, the stability of network by varying the number of vehicles also needs to be assessed. Similarly, the transmission range of vehicles would also have an impact towards stability of links and thus the clusters. A brief description of performance metrics in context of stable clustering is given as follows:

I.Average CH lifetime.

I.Average CH lifetime.

Average CH lifetime.

CH lifetime is the duration for which vehicle becomes a CH till the time when it acquires a CM role or leaves the cluster’s premises. In this regard, higher average CH lifetime is desired for more stable clusters.

II.Average CM lifetime.

II.Average CM lifetime.

Average CM lifetime.

CM lifetime represents the time period for which a CM remains in the same cluster. Likewise, average CM lifetime represents the time duration that every cluster member remains associated with any CH during simulation time. Like average CH lifetime, it also reflects the stability of clusters for vehicles being part of some clusters and are not left unassociated due to frequent re-clustering.

It indicates how frequently re-clustering is performed during simulation time. This also demonstrates the case when the cluster is completely dissolved and all vehicles acquire the state of UC. Throughout simulations, improvements in re-clustering rate pertains to indirect reduction of network overhead through enhanced clustering stability, rather than a direct measurement of overhead.

Limitations and future work

This study focused on stable clustering for bidirectional road segments without intersections, assuming relatively smooth vehicular mobility and homogeneous communication conditions. Complex urban factors such as intersections, traffic signals, multi-lane turning behavior, and heterogeneous traffic density are not explicitly modeled. In addition, the scheme relies on fixed and uniformly weighted clustering metrics, which may not fully capture rapidly changing network dynamics in dense or highly irregular environments.

Part of future work we plan to extend the proposed approach to urban intersection scenarios by incorporating traffic-light awareness and turn-based mobility models. Further research may also consider heterogeneous vehicles, variable transmission ranges, and realistic channel impairments, as well as large-scale validation using real-world vehicular mobility traces. Another future direction would be to integrate lightweight authentication and conditional privacy-preserving mechanisms (e.g., pseudonym-based or certificate-free schemes) at the CH/SCH level, enabling secure message validation while maintaining user privacy without compromising clustering stability.

Conclusions

This paper proposed a Multi-Criteria-based Stable Clustering (MCSC) technique to enhance clustering stability in VANETs. The key contribution is a stability-aware clustering framework that integrates Connectivity Lifespan (CLS) and a Composite Clustering Function (CCF) incorporating a novel Past Cluster-Head Lifetime (PCL) metric for robust cluster-head selection. The inclusion of a Substitute Cluster Head (SCH) and an adaptive cluster merging mechanism further strengthens cluster continuity under high mobility.

Extensive simulations under varying vehicle speeds, transmission ranges, and network densities quantitatively demonstrate the effectiveness of MCSC. Compared to existing approaches, MCSC achieves approximately 16% and 14.5% improvements in average CH and CM lifetimes, respectively, and reduces the re-clustering rate by about 13%. Supported by simulation results, the proposed technique significantly improves clusters stability in highly dynamic vehicular environments.
