Glutamate- and GABA-targeted drugs for Co-occurring bipolar and alcohol use disorders: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study of N-acetylcysteine, gabapentin, and placebo
Authors: James J Prisciandaro, William H Mellick, Sara Hix, Kaiya Brand, Bryan K Tolliver, Raymond F Anton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForty percent of people with bipolar disorder (BD) develop alcohol use disorder (AUD), with co-occurring AUD substantially worsening the course of BD. Though efficacious treatments remain elusive, frontal glutamate and GABA dysregulation represent promising targets for intervention. This study evaluated the ability of two adjunctive medications, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and gabapentin, to modulate glutamate and GABA (primary) and to reduce heavy drinking and depressive symptoms (exploratory) in people with BD + AUD. Fifty-four individuals meeting DSM-5 criteria for BD + AUD were enrolled in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study, with 3, 1-week conditions (NAC, gabapentin, placebo). Medication effects on dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) glutamate+glutamine (Glx) and GABA levels, via proton MR spectroscopy, and percent heavy drinking days (%HDD) and depressive symptoms were evaluated with linear mixed models. Forty-nine participants (M[SD] age=41.0[12.2], 51% female, 74% white) provided 121–131 posttreatment-MRI scans, depending on analysis. Medications were well-tolerated, and adherence was excellent. DACC Glx (F = 4.76, p = 0.012) but not GABA (F = 0.96, p = 0.388) levels significantly differed across conditions, with only NAC (M[SD] = 21.50[2.55]) demonstrating significantly different(lower) glutamate levels relative to placebo (M[SD] = 22.59[2.56]; p = 0.003; SMD = 0.43). %HDD (F = 3.90, p = 0.025) also significantly differed across conditions, again with only NAC (M[SD] = 31.86[4.60]) demonstrating significantly different(lower) %HDD relative to placebo (M[SD] = 40.94[4.55]; p = 0.015; SMD = 0.33), though neither NAC (p = 0.128) nor gabapentin (p = 0.228) differed from placebo on depressive symptoms. These results support the development of mechanistic clinical trials targeting glutamatergic dysfunction in people with BD + AUD. Introduction

Nearly 40% of individuals with bipolar disorder (BD) develop alcohol use disorder (AUD) over their lifetime [1]. Co-occurring AUD worsens the course of BD via more frequent and severe manic and depressive episodes, increased resistance to treatment, and increased risk for hospitalization and suicide [2, 3]. Nonetheless, treatment research for co-occurring BD and AUD (BD + AUD) remains limited, with only seven double-blind, placebo-controlled studies of medications for BD + AUD published to date [4]. The few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that have been conducted in people with BD + AUD have evaluated medications FDA approved to treat either BD or AUD in hopes that such medications would prove efficacious for BD + AUD. Unfortunately, under this paradigm, only one, nearly 20-year-old study has demonstrated a positive treatment (i.e., valproate) effect on drinking [5] to date. A different approach to developing medications for RCTs would be to target neurobiological dysfunctions characteristic of individuals with BD and/or AUD [6].

Convergent lines of evidence support dysregulation in prefrontal glutamate and γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA), the main excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters [7, 8], respectively, as candidate targets for pharmacological intervention in BD + AUD. Impairments in excitatory-inhibitory (E-I) synaptic development and function, biasing E-I synaptic balance towards more excitation, have been consistently found across animal models of mania [9], with a recent systematic review of postmortem studies of individuals with BD concordantly reporting robust evidence for decreased cortical GABAergic interneuron density [10]. Additional molecular evidence of hyper-excitatory E-I dysfunction in BD is provided by studies linking BD with genes coding for glutamate transporters [11] and ionotropic and metabotropic receptor subunits [12–14], reduced expression of GABA receptor subunits and regulating proteins [15, 16], and differences in GABA receptor genes [17–19]. 1H-MRS studies provide a unique opportunity to better understand these issues in humans and have generally demonstrated elevated frontal glutamate + glutamine (Glx) [20, 21] and GABA [22] levels in people with BD relative to healthy control (HC) participants. With respect to AUD, chronic alcohol drinking produces compensatory downregulation of GABAA receptors and upregulation of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, culminating in the hyper-excitatory state of alcohol withdrawal [23, 24]. These and related E-I adaptations are believed to be largely responsible for craving, compulsive use, and risk of relapse in individuals with AUD [25, 26]. In human 1H-MRS studies, AUD is generally associated with reduced frontal Glx levels in recently drinking individuals [27–30], with Glx levels transiently elevated during acute alcohol withdrawal [31]. The cumulative impact of BD- and AUD-related E-I dysfunctions in people with BD + AUD remains minimally studied. In one early 1H-MRS study, participants with BD + AUD reporting ≥6-months abstinence from alcohol had abnormally low Glx levels in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [32]. We demonstrated that, following ≥7 days of monitored abstinence, individuals with co-occurring BD and current (i.e., within the past 3-months) AUD had significantly lower dorsal ACC (dACC) GABA and glutamate levels [33], relative to individuals with BD alone, AUD alone, or HC participants; these deficits were, in turn, significantly associated with higher levels of self-reported alcohol craving and impulsivity.

Although no studies to date have investigated effects of medications on brain glutamate or GABA levels in people with BD + AUD, 1H-MRS studies in other populations have identified safe and well-tolerated medications that reliably alter brain glutamate and/or GABA levels, including gabapentin and n-acetylcysteine (NAC). Gabapentin modulates GABA and glutamate transmission via selective blockade of presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels that contain the α2δ-1 subunit [34] as well as additional E-I-related mechanisms [35, 36]. In 1H-MRS studies, both acute [37–39] and chronic [38, 39] gabapentin dosing significantly increased occipital GABA levels in healthy volunteers and people with epilepsy in a dose-dependent manner. Though RCTs in treatment-refractory individuals with BD have failed to support the efficacy of gabapentin for resolving acute mood episodes, gabapentin has been shown in some studies to reduce alcohol withdrawal symptoms and relapse drinking in people with AUD [40–42], in part by modulating frontal glutamate levels [43], making it a potentially useful adjunctive treatment strategy for BD + AUD. NAC increases levels of the brain’s primary antioxidant, glutathione, by providing cells with its rate-limiting component, cysteine [44], via System xc, which imports oxidized cysteine in exchange for glutamate [45], thereby activating presynaptic mGlu2 receptors and inhibiting further presynaptic glutamate release [46]. These glutamatergic effects, manifested in vivo as NAC-induced reductions in ACC glutamate levels [47–49], have formed the foundation of numerous studies of NAC for treating SUD [50], producing mixed findings on substance use outcomes but with a meta-analysis finding a large effect of NAC on reducing craving [51] and the first published RCT of NAC for AUD demonstrating decreased drinks per drinking day with 28-days of NAC treatment [52]. NAC has also been regularly evaluated as an adjunctive treatment for BD, with a meta-analysis finding NAC conferred a statistically significant medium-sized effect on reducing depressive symptoms [53].

In sum, convergent lines of evidence suggest abnormal frontal glutamate and GABA levels as candidate targets for pharmacological intervention in people with BD + AUD. The present early-phase, 3-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study aimed to modulate these targets using adjunctive medications shown to normalize cortical GABA (i.e., gabapentin) and glutamate (i.e., NAC) levels in past research and, secondarily, to evaluate medication-related changes in heavy drinking and depressive symptoms across brief follow-up intervals in people with BD + AUD. Our primary hypotheses were that NAC would significantly modulate frontal Glx levels and that gabapentin would significantly modulate frontal GABA+ levels in people with BD + AUD.

Methods

Participants

Participants, aged 18–65 years, were recruited via clinical referral and community advertising. Participants met DSM-5 criteria for current (i.e., within the past 3 months) moderate-to-severe AUD and BD I or II, reported active drinking within the past 14-days, and were prescribed daily use of ≥1 FDA-approved mood-stabilizing medication (i.e., lithium, lamotrigine, divalproex sodium and/or 2nd generation antipsychotic), as restricting the study to medication-naïve individuals would represent a safety hazard, hinder recruitment, and be inconsistent with the intended clinical application of NAC and gabapentin as adjunctive treatments. To minimize the impact of concomitant medications on results, participants agreed to abstain from antioxidant supplements, including those containing riboflavin, during study participation and any participants with additions, discontinuations, or dose changes of psychoactive medications of ≥20%, ≤2-weeks before testing were excluded [54]. Additional exclusion criteria included history of serious medical illness or DSM-5 Psychotic Disorder, clinically significant suicidal and/or homicidal ideation or severe mood disturbance (i.e., MADRS > 35 and/or YMRS > 25) otherwise conferring an acute safety risk, clinically significant alcohol withdrawal symptoms (i.e., CIWA-Ar >10 [55]) or signs of autonomic instability (e.g., tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, hyperreflexia), hepatocellular disease (i.e., as indicated by total bilirubin, alanine [ALT}, and/or aspartate [AST] transaminases >3× normal) or renal insufficiency (i.e., as indicated by creatinine >2× normal), electroconvulsive therapy in the past 3 months, past month use of, or history of allergic reaction to, NAC or gabapentin, use of medications hazardous if taken with NAC (e.g., carbamazepine) or gabapentin (e.g., opioid or benzodiazepine medications), use of antidipsotropic medications (i.e., disulfiram, naltrexone, acamprosate), history of head injury with loss of consciousness >5-min, presence of non-MR-safe material, or significant claustrophobia. Female participants with positive urine pregnancy tests or refusing adequate forms of birth control were also excluded.

Procedures

This study was approved by the MUSC Institutional Review Board (IRB). All procedures were conducted with adherence to the 1964 Helsinki declaration. After providing written informed consent and demographic information, including self-reported sex, race and ethnicity, participants were assessed for eligibility by a licensed psychiatrist or clinical psychologist using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5 [56]), along with a medical history and physical examination with associated lab work conducted by the study physician and the Clinical Neurobiology Lab at MUSC, respectively. At this and each subsequent visit, participants completed the Timeline Followback (TLFB [57]) assessment of daily alcohol consumption, the Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS [58]), Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS [59]), Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment Alcohol Scale Revised (CIWA-Ar [55]), and Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS [60]). Eligible participants completed 3, 1-week experimental conditions (i.e., NAC [2400 mg/day], placebo, gabapentin [1200 mg/day]) in a randomized order following a fully counterbalanced crossover design, followed by a safety visit within 72-h of final medication discontinuation. Each 1-week condition consisted of an in-person visit for assessment and dispensing of medication (Day 1), titration to maximum dose via b.i.d. dosing (Days 1–5), MRI scanning (Day 5), and medication washout (Days 5–7). See Fig. 1 for a study design schematic. Medications were packaged and dispensed by the MUSC Investigational Drug Service, who also oversaw double blinding procedures for the study. NAC was purchased from the Professional Compounding Centers of America (USP, 30-1601, concentration on a dry basis = 99.1%). Study medication was over-encapsulated with riboflavin (50 mg/capsule) for urinary detection by fluorescence spectroscopy at each MRI visit to assess adherence [61]. To promote compliance, participants took their first and final dose (i.e., 1-h before MRI) of each medication in front of study staff. Given the 6–7-h elimination half-lives of both NAC and gabapentin [62, 63], participants reached steady-state concentrations of the maximal dose of each medication well before MRI [64] and underwent approximately 11-half-lives of elimination prior to the start of each subsequent condition. MRI scanning was completed in a 3-T Siemens PrismaFit MR scanner at the Center for Biomedical Imaging at MUSC. Participants were instructed not to consume alcohol or drugs within 12 h of their MRI appointments, and participants who smoked cigarettes were allowed to have their last cigarette approximately 1-h prior to MRI. During localizing, structural (256 sagittal slices; 1 mm thick/50% gap), and 1H-MRS acquisition, participants viewed scenic images via a back-projected mirror mounted to a 32-channel head coil. A 2.5 × 2.5 × 3 cm3 dACC voxel for 1H-MRS was placed on midsagittal T1-weighted images, posterior to the genu of the corpus callosum, with the ventral edge of the voxel aligned with the dorsal edge of the callosum [29, 33]. Following placement of 6 outer-volume-suppression bands ~1-cm away from the voxel faces and auto-shimming via FASTESTMAP, single-voxel water-suppressed (number of averages = 256), immediately followed by water-unsuppressed (number of averages = 16), 1H-MRS spectra, were acquired using a vendor-supplied MEGA-PRESS sequence (TR = 2000ms; TE = 68 ms), with editing-pulse frequencies symmetric to water (1.9 ppm and 7.5 ppm).

Analytic plan

1H-MRS data were processed through the default pipeline of the Osprey toolbox for MATLAB [65]. GABA+ levels were derived from the difference spectrum, whereas Glx levels were derived from the OFF spectrum [66]. Within-voxel tissue fractions of gray (GM) and white (WM) matter and cerebrospinal fluid were calculated based on automated segmentation in Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology) using volume masks generated in Osprey, and metabolite concentrations were normalized to the unsuppressed water signal, corrected for within-voxel tissue fractions, and expressed in mmol/kg [67]. Study hypotheses were evaluated using population average models via the linear mixed model module of IBM SPSS v27 software, with unstructured covariance matrices and restricted maximum likelihood estimation, permitting analysis of all available data. Each statistical model consisted of the main effects of treatment condition (NAC, placebo, gabapentin] and MRI number (sequentially coded), the interaction of treatment condition with MRI number (to test for order effects), and a single, outcome-dependent covariate (GABA+ and Glx: within-voxel GM to total tissue [GM + WM] ratio; MADRS and percent heavy drinking days [%HDD]: baseline MADRS and %HDD, respectively); changes to manic symptoms were not anticipated due to floor effects/restricted range, common to outpatient samples. Given the small number of targeted hypotheses evaluated, statistical tests were each evaluated at a nominal alpha level of 0.05. Effect sizes were estimated as standardized mean differences (i.e., equivalent to Cohen’s D). Pre-planned exploratory moderation analyses were conducted by adding to the models detailed above the main effect and interaction (i.e., with treatment condition) of each moderator of interest (i.e., age, sex, BD subtype, current episode [(hypo)manic or depressive] status, cigarette-smoking status [≥10 cigarettes/day], co-occurring current SUD (any), co-occurring current anxiety disorder [any, including posttraumatic stress disorder], and concomitant medication by class [lithium, anticonvulsive, antipsychotic, antidepressant]).

Results

Participants

Fifty-four individuals with BD + AUD (M[SD] age = 41.0[12.2], 51% female, 73% white) were enrolled in the study. Forty-nine (i.e., 91% of enrolled) participants completed at least one experimental condition (see Table 1 for baseline characteristics), providing a total of 138 completed posttreatment MRI scans (i.e., NAC n = 44, gabapentin n = 46, placebo n = 47; see Fig. S1 for CONSORT Flow Diagram). 1H-MRS data quality was uniformly excellent (via visual inspection, as well as water linewidth M[SD] = 5.72[0.71] FWHM, creatine linewidth M[SD] = 4.93[0.56] FWHM, and creatine SNR M[SD] = 184.30[39.01]), with few (n = 7) scans excluded from analysis due to system errors. See Fig. S2 for 1H-MRS spectra. NAC and gabapentin were very well tolerated, with 19 (38.8%) of 49 participants reporting 26 mild and 9 moderate adverse events total (i.e., none requiring discontinuation) that were distributed relatively equally across conditions (NAC n = 15, placebo n = 10, gabapentin n = 10; see Table S1 for AEs by type). Adherence was excellent, successfully demonstrated via urinary riboflavin detection for 121 (92.4%) of 131 post-treatment visits, and the study blind was successfully maintained, with participant condition (i.e., active vs. placebo) guessing observed at chance levels (i.e., 51.2% correct, not significantly different from 50%, F = 1.63, p = 0.200). There was no evidence of carryover effects (i.e., statistically significant interactions of treatment condition with MRI number [sequentially coded]) across dependent variables (Glx p = 0.305, GABA+ p = 0.672, Glx/GABA+ p = 0.787, MADRS p = 0.156, %HDD p = 0.343). Although findings from adherent conditions(participants) are detailed below (i.e., NAC n = 41, gabapentin n = 39, placebo n = 42), statistical significance (i.e., significant vs. not) only diverged for planned moderation analyses, where moderators were only significant in adherent conditions(participants).

1H-MRS

Treatment condition (NAC, placebo, gabapentin) was significantly associated with Glx levels (F = 4.76, p = 0.012), with post-hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrating that NAC (M[SD] = 21.50[2.55]; p = 0.003; SMD = 0.43), but not gabapentin (M[SD] = 21.89[2.52]; SMD = 0.28), was associated with significantly different (i.e., lower) Glx levels relative to placebo (M[SD] = 22.59[2.56]; Fig. 2). Although condition was not significantly associated with GABA+ levels (F = 0.96, p = 0.388; Fig. S3), it was significantly associated with the experimental ratio of Glx/GABA+ (F = 4.41, p = 0.016), representing E-I balance in line with recent 1H-MRS studies [68], with post-hoc pairwise comparisons finding that both NAC (M[SD] = 5.74[1.27]; p = 0.010; SMD = 0.43) and gabapentin (M[SD] = 5.75[1.26]; p = 0.016; SMD = 0.43) were associated with lower Glx/GABA+ levels relative to placebo (M[SD] = 6.29[1.28]; Fig. 3).

Clinical variables

Although, controlling for baseline scores, treatment condition was significantly associated with MADRS scores (F = 3.71, p = 0.030), post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that MADRS scores did not significantly differ between NAC (M[SD] = 11.36[7.02]; p = 0.128; SMD = 0.25) or gabapentin (M[SD] = 8.21[6.82]; p = 0.228; SMD = 0.20) and placebo (M[SD] = 9.60[6.93]). Conversely, controlling for baseline %HDD, treatment was significantly associated with %HDD (F = 3.90, p = 0.025), with post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealing that NAC (M[SD] = 31.43[30.35]; p = 0.015; SMD = 0.33), but not gabapentin (M[SD] = 40.65[29.66]; p = 0.860; SMD = 0.02), was associated with significantly lower %HDD relative to placebo (M[SD] = 41.36[30.71]; Fig. 4).

Moderation analyses

The effects of treatment on Glx and GABA+ levels, though not on clinical variables, depended on smoking status (≥10 cigarettes/day, 34.7%; F = 4.20, p = 0.019; Fig. S4) and current mood episode status (depressive or [hypo]manic, 61.2%; F = 3.52, p = 0.035; Fig. S5), respectively, with NAC effects on Glx levels significantly stronger in smokers than non-smokers (F = 4.15, p = 0.045) and with both NAC and gabapentin significantly increasing GABA+ levels in participants experiencing a current mood episode (F = 4.29, p = 0.018). In contrast, none of the observed effects of treatment depended on age, sex, BD subtype, current non-alcohol SUD or anxiety diagnoses, or mood stabilizing medication classes; all p-values > 0.1.

Discussion

In this early-phase, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover MRI study of adjunctive treatments for BD + AUD, NAC and gabapentin were both safe and well-tolerated in BD + AUD participants, but only NAC was significantly associated with (i.e., lower) neurochemical (i.e., dACC Glx levels) or clinical outcomes (i.e., heavy drinking days) relative to placebo. Though both NAC and gabapentin were associated with significantly lower levels of an experimental 1H-MRS index of E-I balance (i.e., the ratio of Glx:GABA), neither treatment was significantly associated with dACC GABA levels or depressive symptoms. Pre-planned exploratory moderation analyses found that NAC effects on dACC Glx levels were significantly stronger in cigarette smokers and that NAC and gabapentin were each associated with significantly higher dACC GABA levels in participants experiencing a current mood episode, providing critical guidance for planned follow-up studies. Together, these results provide initial support for the potential clinical utility of adjunctive NAC for treating people with BD + AUD as well as the experimental medication-development paradigm designed for the present study, more broadly.

The fact that NAC was associated with significantly lower frontal Glx levels and heavy drinking days relative to placebo in the present study is consistent with results from several NAC 1H-MRS studies in people with cocaine use disorder [48, 49] and schizophrenia [47] as well as RCTs in people with AUD [52], respectively. Finding association of NAC versus placebo treatment with relatively fewer heavy drinking days in people with BD + AUD (albeit across a brief, 4–5 day follow-up interval) is particularly encouraging, however, as only one RCT published 20 years ago has demonstrated reduced drinking with medication treatment (i.e., valproate) in this clinical population [5]. Planned future studies will evaluate the replicability of this finding across clinically meaningful time intervals. The observation that participants who smoked ≥10 cigarettes/day had an even stronger association of NAC treatment with lower frontal Glx levels may be explained by the additional dysfunction to glutamate systems conferred by smoking (e.g., via chronic activation of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on presynaptic glutamatergic terminals [69]), providing more room for improvement with NAC [70]. Conversely, our failure to find significant effects of NAC on depressive symptoms contrasts with results from prior clinical trials [53], with this discrepancy most likely attributable to marked differences in study design (e.g., in the duration of treatment and/or clinical population evaluated) between this and previous trials. Although gabapentin fared less well than NAC in the present study, failing to modulate primary 1H-MRS or clinical outcomes, exploratory analyses revealed potential paths forward for investigating gabapentin for BD + AUD, including evaluating gabapentin in people with BD + AUD who are experiencing a current mood episode. Though speculative, it’s possible that such individuals suffer from a relatively more severe, state-dependent frontal GABA deficit that is amenable to gabapentin treatment [71].

In contrast with the present study, RCTs for BD + AUD have historically focused on decreasing drinking and mood symptoms. While crucial in determining efficacy, these studies have not examined how or why evaluated medications affect clinical outcomes, greatly limiting their potential impact, particularly when medications prove ineffective, as is far too often the case. The duration of traditional RCTs (12–16 weeks), arguably necessary to evaluate medication effects on clinical outcomes, has resulted in marked difficulties retaining participants from this highly impaired clinical population. The medication-development platform created for the present study, intended to precede and inform subsequent symptom-focused RCTs, was designed to overcome these limitations by explicitly focusing on neurochemical medication targets, thereby allowing for briefer dosing periods. These briefer dosing periods, in turn, allowed us to maximize efficiency and statistical power by having each participant complete three experimental conditions in <1-month, thereby providing equivalent statistical power to a parallel-group RCT with 2.7–6.4 times as many participants (n ~ 132–314) [72]. By all accounts this strategy worked, with 91% of participants completing at least one experimental condition (i.e., making their data analyzable), and with clear results obtained (e.g., free from significant carryover effects) capable of motivating the next planned step in next-stage clinical trials, a longer (i.e., 1-month vs. 4–5 days) randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group evaluation of NAC for BD + AUD, using a cross-lagged panel design to prospectively evaluate the mechanistic role of NAC-related reductions in glutamate on reducing heavy drinking in people with BD + AUD.

The strengths of the present study should be considered in the context of its relative weaknesses. First, although a boon to both feasibility and external validity, inclusion of participants varying along dimensions of psychiatric phenomenology (e.g., anxiety disorders) could be perceived as a limitation. As such, we carefully evaluated the potential impact of each on our primary findings and, like other similar studies [33, 73], showed that most had minimal to no impact. Moreover, embracing the psychiatric heterogeneity of real-world individuals with BD + AUD allowed us to uncover two factors (i.e., smoking and mood episode status) that will be important to consider in planned follow-up studies. Second, although participants were asked not to consume alcohol or drugs within 12 h of MRI, and although participants completed self-report, breath, and urine assessments of recent alcohol or drug use at each MRI, it remains difficult to verify past 12-h alcohol and drug use using available methods. As such, it is possible that some participants used alcohol or drugs within 12 h of MRI. Third, though optimizing our study design for feasibility and efficiency provided marked benefits (e.g., to retention), doing so necessarily limited our ability to fully evaluate the effects of NAC and gabapentin on clinical outcomes. As such, enthusiasm concerning the observed impact of NAC on reducing heavy drinking should be tempered by the brief evaluation window across which drinking was assessed. Fourth, similarly, although our study was adequately powered to detect significant effects in primary analyses, exploratory moderation analyses were likely underpowered and should be interpreted with caution until replicated. Fifth, due to budgetary constraints, participants did not complete pretreatment MRI, potentially limiting statistical power to detect true effects. Sixth, although we were interested in quantifying brain glutamate and GABA levels, limitations inherent to currently available 1H-MRS pulse programs at common clinical magnetic field strengths (e.g., 3-T) [74] constrained us to quantifying Glx and GABA+. Though not ideal, these proxies have been used in most published 1H-MRS studies to date, with Glx containing ~3× higher contribution from glutamate than glutamine, rendering it statistically colinear with glutamate [75]. Finally, additional caution should be taken in interpreting results involving Glx/GABA ratios, as the exact relationship of these ratios, particularly in humans scanned at 3 T [68], to E-I balance is presently unknown.

These limitations notwithstanding, results from the present study support the development of larger mechanistic studies targeting glutamatergic dysfunction in people with BD + AUD by showing modulation of frontal Glx levels and preliminary improvement in heavy drinking. Ultimately, we hope to demonstrate the power and efficiency of the proposed approach for both developing new medications, designed to better engage neurobiological targets of interest, and for advancing to more costly large-scale RCTs medications that have a substantially higher chance of success.
