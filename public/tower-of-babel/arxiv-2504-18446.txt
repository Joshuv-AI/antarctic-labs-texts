Olivier Leblanc, Chung San Chu, Laurent Jacques, Yves Wiaux

Abstract
The emerging generation of radio-interferometric (RI) arrays are set to form images of the sky with a new regime of sensitivity and resolution. This implies a significant increase in visibility data volumes, scaling as 𝒪 ⁡ ( Q 2 ​ B ) \mathcal{O}(Q^{2}B) for Q Q antennas and B B short-time integration intervals (or batches), calling for efficient data dimensionality reduction techniques. This paper proposes a new approach to data compression during acquisition, coined modulated rank-one projection (MROP). MROP compresses the Q × Q Q\times Q batchwise covariance matrix into a smaller number P P of random rank-one projections and compresses across time by trading B B for a smaller number M M of random modulations of the ROP measurement vectors. Firstly, we introduce a dual perspective on the MROP acquisition, which can either be understood as random beamforming, or as a post-correlation compression. Secondly, we analyse the noise statistics of MROPs and demonstrate that the random projections induce a uniform noise level across measurements independently of the visibility-weighting scheme used. Thirdly, we propose a detailed analysis of the memory and computational cost requirements across the data acquisition and image reconstruction stages, with comparison to state-of-the-art dimensionality reduction approaches. Finally, the MROP model is validated in simulation for monochromatic intensity imaging, with comparison to the classical and baseline-dependent averaging (BDA) models, and using the uSARA optimisation algorithm for image formation. An extensive experimental setup is considered, with ground-truth images containing diffuse and faint emission and spanning a wide variety of dynamic ranges, and for a range of u ​ v uv -coverages corresponding to VLA and MeerKAT observation.



1
Introduction

Data acquisition by interferometry in radio astronomy relies on an array of strategically placed antennas measuring, through the signal covariance matrix, a noisy and nonuniform partial coverage of the spatial Fourier domain of the image of interest,
i.e.
, a small portion of the celestial sphere accessible to the instrument.
Radio interferometry (RI) is fundamental in studying a wide range of astronomical phenomena, including star formation, galaxy evolution, and the cosmic microwave background.

The classical model stores in memory all the visibilities—the Fourier samples obtained from the interferometric acquisition.
While current arrays like
Very Large Array
(VLA)
(
Thompson et al. 1980
)
or MeerKAT
(
Asad et al. 2021
)
respectively have 27 and 64 antennas, future arrays such as the
Square Kilometer Array
(SKA) are coming up with an increasing number of antennas.
SKA itself is to form petabyte-scale (
10^{15}
bytes) images from exabyte-scale (
10^{18}
bytes) data volumes
(
Scaife 2020
)
.
Compression is thus emerging as a critical need to reduce the data size and make RI imaging scalable.

Recently,
Leblanc et al. 2024
proposed a compressive sensing technique applied directly at the level of the antenna measurements. It showed that
beamforming
—a common technique of dephasing antenna signals—usually used to focus some region of the sky, is equivalent to sensing a rank-one projection (ROP) of the signal covariance matrix. Random beamforming was combined with random modulations across time to build the modulated rank-one projection (MROP) model that replaces the enormous number of visibilities with a tunable number of MROPs.
In
Leblanc et al. 2024
, the emphasis was made on the mathematical foundations of the random beamforming. Image recovery guarantees were provided for image reconstruction under a simplistic model assuming sparsity in the image domain.
The associated reduced sample complexity, theoretically derived in a simpler case without modulations and numerically obtained in phase transition diagrams, was shown to scale as
\mathcal{O}(K)
where
K
is the image sparsity.

This paper significantly extends the study of the MROP model while still focussing on monochromatic intensity imaging.
Firstly, we discuss two acquisition schemes. The antenna-based
random beamforming
acquisition scales linearly with the number of antennas, while the visibility-based acquisition is closer to the classical correlation computation and facilitates visibility weighting.
Secondly, the noise statistics of MROPs are derived.
Thirdly, the memory requirements and the computational costs of the acquisition and imaging steps are derived and compared with other models in the literature.
Finally, the reconstruction of images with high dynamic range in a realistic numerical setting demonstrates the potential of the MROP model.

1.1
Related Work

Several compression techniques for RI that are connected to our method exist in the literature.
Among them, compressing the observation vector that collects all the visibilities by multiplication with a Gaussian random matrix (with much fewer rows than columns) was discussed in
Kartik et al. 2017
and shown to increase the computational cost of the forward model significantly.
Kartik et al. 2017
proposed an efficient Fourier reduction model approximating the optimal (in the least-squares sense) dimensionality reduction that projects the data with the adjoint of
\bm{U}
which contains the left singular vectors of the SVD of the classical imaging operator
\bm{\Phi}:=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{*}
.
On the other hand,
baseline-dependent averaging
(BDA)
(
Wijnholds et al. 2018
;
Atemkeng et al. 2018
)
offers an effective reduction in data volume by averaging consecutive visibilities corresponding to short baselines,
i.e.
, those with nearly constant frequency locations.
Finally, standard visibility gridding, which consists of storing the
dirty image
, is an efficient data dimensionality reduction technique when the original visibility data size is larger than the image size, as discussed in
Kartik et al. 2017
.
The dirty map can even be computed in an antenna-based way which reduces its acquisition cost
(
Thyagarajan et al. 2017
;
Krishnan et al. 2023
)
.

Random beamforming
for RI has first been suggested in
Öçal et al. 2015
.
Then, the connection of random beamforming to ROPs of the signal covariance matrix has been highlighted in
Leblanc et al. 2024
, leveraging the theory of recovery guarantees via ROP to derive the sample complexity in a simplified case.
This paper studies image reconstruction using the uSARA algorithm
(
Repetti & Wiaux 2020
;
Terris et al. 2022
)
, which is a sophisticated version of the proximal gradient method
(
Beck 2017
)
.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we significantly increase the practicality of the MROP approach proposed by
Leblanc et al. 2024
to RI by providing four novel contributions, all studied in the context of monochromatic intensity imaging.

Acquisition of MROPs.

Two perspectives on acquiring MROPs are discussed in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
on the grounds of their acquisition costs, their compatibilities with the existing correlators, and their compatibilities with visibility weighting.
The first perspective, involving
random beamforming
, scales linearly with the number of antennas but is shown to raise challenges for visibility weighting because the visibilities are never accessed.
A possible solution that decomposes the matrix of weights as a sum of rank-one matrices is proposed in Section
3.2.2
.
The second perspective, coined
visibility-based
acquisition of MROP, applies post-correlation compression so accesses the visibilities, hence enabling their weighting, and is also compatible with the existing correlators.

Noise statistics on MROPs.

In Section
3.2.3
, we conduct a statistical analysis showing that MROP computation preserves the additive i.i.d. Gaussian nature of noise in the visibilities, merely rescaling it, irrespective of the visibility weighting scheme.

Reduced memory requirements.

The MROP model is shown in Section
4
to benefit from reduced intermediate and final memory storage for the acquisition and imaging processes compared to the classical model, and for a similar computational cost.
The computational complexities and memory requirements of multiple models are synthesised in Table
2
for the acquisition and in Table
3
for the imaging.

Numerical validation.

The performance of the MROP imaging model is numerically studied in Section
5
in various regimes, including four ground-truth images set to different values of dynamic range, each coupled with two antenna arrays (namely VLA and MeerKAT) and twenty five different
uv
-coverages, and a range of ratios between the image resolution, the number of visibilities, and the number of MROPs.
The MROP model is compared with both the classical model and the computationally effective BDA model
(
Wijnholds et al. 2018
)
using uniform visibility weighting and the uSARA reconstruction algorithm. It is shown that the MROP model features a high compression factor and good reconstruction performances at a computational cost similar to the one of the classical model.

2.3
Visibility Weighting

Visibility weighting
is a common technique to improve the dynamic range and the ability to adjust the synthesised point spread function associated with the produced image
(
Briggs 1995
)
.
Within each batch

\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{}\mathbf{C}_{b}:=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\odot\bm{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}_{b}

This modifies the classical acquisition and imaging models as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})+\mathbf{W}\bm{\xi},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}\bar{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{W}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(11)

where

is the diagonal visibility weighting operator defined as

\bm{v}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{v}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{VB}\mapsto\mathbf{W}\bm{v}=\begin{bmatrix}\vect(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{1})\odot\bm{v}_{1}\\

3
Compression Models

This section introduces various compression models designed to reduce memory demands for both data storage and image reconstruction. After highlighting the limitations of existing post-correlation compression in Section

3.1
Post-Correlation Compression

The issue with the classical scheme described in Section
2
is that the number of visibilities
VB=Q(Q-1)B/2
scales as the square of the number of antennas
Q
, and it creates challenges in memory requirements and computational cost for image formation with just under a hundred antennas and thousands of batches.
To reduce the memory requirements while still being able to estimate the image, several compression techniques have been considered in the past.

Dirty Image Model.

For instance, applying the adjoint of the forward operator as
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}:=\real{\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}\bm{v}}
and
\bar{\bm{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}:=\real{\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\bar{\bm{v}}}
has the effect of
gridding
the visibilities to form the
dirty image

(
Kartik et al. 2017
)
.
The associated acquisition and imaging models are

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=\real{\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})+\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}\bm{\xi}},

(13)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=\real{\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\bar{\bm{\xi}}},

(14)

with
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}
,
\bar{\bm{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}
and
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}
are the adjoints of the acquisition and imaging operators, respectively.
This model reduces the memory requirements to
N
(real) pixel values while only doubling the computational cost of one iteration of the classical forward model.
The visibility-weighted dirty image noise statistics can be shown to be
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{W}\bar{\bm{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{0},\real{\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\diag(\bm{w}^{2})\mathbf{\Phi}}/2)
.
The noise in the dirty image pixels is not i.i.d. but is correlated, which makes the image recovery more challenging.

BDA.

A second possibility named
baseline-dependent averaging
(BDA)
(
Wijnholds et al. 2018
)
consists in averaging the visibilities associated with low-frequency content over consecutive batches.
This yields fewer measurements
VB_{\rm bda}\ll VB
with an average number of batches per baseline
B_{\rm bda}:={\textstyle\frac{1}{V}}\sum_{i=1}^{V}B_{i}\ll B
where
B_{i}
is the reduced number of batches of the
i
-th pair of antennas.
Applying the
averaging operator

\bm{S}\in\{1,1/2,1/3,1/4,\ldots\}^{VB_{\rm bda}\times VB}
, which contains a single entry per column but may have multiple values per row (summing to one) depending on the number of averaged visibilities, provides

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\rm bda}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})+\mathbf{S}\bm{\xi},

(15)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{v}}_{\rm bda}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm bda}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(16)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm bda}(\bm{\sigma}):=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})=\mathbf{G}_{\rm bda}\mathbf{FZ}\bm{\sigma}
and
\mathbf{G}_{\rm bda}:=\mathbf{SG}\in\mathbb{C}^{VB_{\rm bda}\times 4N}
is the averaged version of
\mathbf{G}
.
An attractive feature of BDA is that the interpolation matrix
\mathbf{G}_{\rm bda}
can be approximated directly from the
uv
-coverage chosen to represent the averaged visibilities, hence reducing the computational cost.

With BDA, the noise on the averaged visibilities is not i.i.d. . Taking the multiplicity of each row of the averaging operator
\mathbf{S}
yields the noise statistics
\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\xi}}~\sim~\mathcal{CN}(\bm{0},\tau^{2}\diag(\bm{s}^{-1}))
, where the
i
-th component
s_{i}:=\lVert\mathbf{S}_{i}\rVert_{0}
of
\bm{s}
counts the number of visibilities averaged in
\bar{v}_{{\rm bda},i}
for all
i\in\llbracket VB_{\rm bda}\rrbracket
.
The noise statistics of the averaged then weighted visibilities, with
\mathbf{W}_{\rm bda}\in\mathbb{R}^{VB_{\rm bda}\times VB_{\rm bda}}
, is

\mathbf{W}_{\rm bda}\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{CN}\big(\bm{0},\tau^{2}\diag(\bm{w}_{\rm bda}^{2}\odot\bm{s}^{-1})\big)
.
For BDA, natural weighting now applies different weights to the averaged visibilities depending on their noise level,
i.e.
,
\bm{w}_{\rm bda,\rm n}=\tau^{-1}\bm{s}^{1/2}
.
A uniform weighting of the averaged visibilities requires

\bm{w}_{\rm bda,\rm u}=\tau^{-1}\bm{\rho}_{\rm bda}^{-1/2}\odot\bm{s}^{1/2}

where
\bm{\rho}_{\rm bda}\in\mathbb{R}^{VB_{\rm bda}}
is the density of the averaged visibilities in the
uv
plane.

BDA is cheap and can reduce the number of data points down to
VB_{\rm bda}/VB=0.1
when also averaging over frequencies
(
Wijnholds et al. 2018
)
, but the resulting compression does not leverage the small amount of information contained in low-complexity models of the image
\bm{\sigma}
.

Random Gaussian Compression.

A third possibility consists of reducing the dimension (and thus the memory footprint) of the forward operator
\mathbf{\Phi}
with
\mathcal{P}
random Gaussian projections and computes
\bm{y}:=\mathbf{A}\bm{v}
and
\bar{\bm{y}}:=\mathbf{A}\bar{\bm{v}}
, which give

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})+\mathbf{A}\bm{\xi},

(17)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{A}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(18)

with
\bm{y}
,
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{P}\times VB}
with
A_{jk}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{CN}(0,1/\mathcal{P})

(
Kartik et al. 2017
)
.
Simple derivations show that, with visibility weighting and in expectation with respect to the coefficients of
\mathbf{A}
, the noise statistics are
\mathbf{AW}\bar{\bm{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{0},\tau^{2}\mathcal{P}^{-1}\lVert\bm{w}\rVert_{2}^{2}\Id)
. In conclusion, whichever the initial visibility weighting choice, natural or uniform, only a global rescaling of the original weights is needed to ensure unit variance of the noise on the final measurements. Under this normalisation constraint, natural visibility weighting follows as
\bm{w}_{\rm n}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}/VB}\,\bm{1}_{VB}
, while uniform visibility weighting reads
\bm{w}_{\rm u}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}\,\bm{\rho}^{-1/2}/\lVert\bm{\rho}^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}
.

Unfortunately, as will be explained in Section
4
,
\mathbf{A}
is dense and makes its application too computationally expensive in practice.
This random Gaussian compression of the classical scheme is illustrated on the right-side of Fig.
2
(a).

3.2
Antenna-based MROP

This section begins by introducing the antenna-based MROP model outlined in
Leblanc et al. 2024
. It then deepens the analysis by exploring the interplay between visibility weighting and random beamforming and examining the noise statistics associated with MROPs.

3.2.1
Revisiting the Sensing Model

The compressive sensing scheme, namely
modulated rank-one projections
(MROP), proposed in
Leblanc et al. 2024
differs from the post-correlation compression schemes described above because the compression is applied at the antenna measurements level.
It consists of combining two compression layers:
(i)

random beamforming
trading the
V
-dependence of the data dimension in (
6
) for a smaller number
P
of ROPs, and
(ii)

random modulations
trading the number of batches
B
for
M
modulations of the ROP vectors obtained for each batch.

Layer 1: random beamforming.

Let us consider a
phased-array
model with two sets of random sketches of the measurement vector
\bm{\mu}(t):=\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\bm{x}(t)
and
\bm{\nu}(t):=\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\bm{x}(t)
using the matrices
\bm{\mathcal{A}}:=(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}_{P})
and
\bm{\mathcal{B}}:=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{P})
where,
\forall p\in\llbracket P\rrbracket
,
\bm{\alpha}_{p}=(\alpha_{p1},\ldots,\alpha_{pQ})^{\top}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\bm{u}
for some random vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{Q}
applying phase delays with
u_{q}\underset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P^{-1/4}\exp(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{U}[0,2\pi))
,
i.e.
,
\mathbb{E}[u_{q}]=0
and
|u_{q}|^{2}=1/\sqrt{P}
,
\forall q\in\llbracket Q\rrbracket
(and similarly for
\bm{\beta}_{p}
), and more specifically their sampling
\bm{\mu}_{b}[i]
and
\bm{\nu}_{b}[i]
obtained in the same way as
\bm{x}_{b}[i]
was obtained in (
2
). Aggregating their elementwise products in time gives

\bm{y}_{b}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\bm{\mu}_{b}[i]\odot\bm{\nu}_{b}^{*}[i],

(19)

whose components write

y_{pb}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\bm{\alpha}_{p}^{*}\bm{x}_{b}[i]\bm{x}_{b}^{*}[i]\bm{\beta}_{p}=\bm{\alpha}_{p}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{b}\bm{\beta}_{p},~~\forall p\in\llbracket P\rrbracket.

(20)

In (
20
),
\bm{\alpha}_{p}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{b}\bm{\beta}_{p}:=\langle\bm{\alpha}_{p}\bm{\beta}_{p}^{*},\mathbf{C}_{b}\rangle_{\rm F}
is called a
rank-one projection
(ROP) of the sample covariance matrix
\bm{C}_{b}
defined in (
3
) because it amounts to projecting it onto the rank-one matrix
\bm{\alpha}_{p}\bm{\beta}_{p}^{*}

(
Cai et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2015
)
.
As clear from (
19
), the computational complexity of the ROP computation scales with the number of antennas
Q
.
This characteristic sets the antenna-based MROP model apart from the visibility-based MROP model discussed in Section
3.3
.

Analogously to the upper triangular selection made for the classical model in Section
2
, a
debiasing
step is necessary. In the ROP measurements of (
20
), debiasing consists of removing the dependency in the diagonal
\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{d}}:=\diag(\diag\mathbf{C}_{b})
including
Q
repetitions of the DC component
\mathcal{F}[\sigma^{\circ}](\bm{0})
and the diagonal covariance matrix of the noise
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\rm n}
.
Both quantities can be estimated from a limited set of additional measurements
(
Leblanc et al. 2024
)
.
Gathering the
B
measurement vectors
(\bm{y}_{b})_{b=1}^{B}
with each
\bm{y}_{b}:=(y_{pb})_{p=1}^{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
containing the
P
debiased ROPs yields the
random beamforming
acquisition model

\bm{y}=(\bm{y}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{y}_{B}^{\top})^{\top}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\mathcal{X})+\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{C}^{PB},

(21)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
is additive noise and with

y_{pb}:=y_{pb}^{\prime}-\bm{\alpha}_{pb}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{d}}(\mathcal{X}_{b})\bm{\beta}_{pb}^{*}+\widetilde{\xi}_{pb},\ \forall p\in\llbracket P\rrbracket,\ \forall b\in\llbracket B\rrbracket.

(22)

The imaging model associated with (
21
) corresponds to a linear transform of the visibility vector and writes

\bar{\bm{y}}_{b}=\mathbf{R}_{b}\bar{\bm{v}}=\mathbf{R}_{b}\mathbf{G}_{b}\mathbf{FZ}\bm{\sigma}+\mathbf{R}_{b}\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{b},

(23)

where each row of the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{b}
computes a single ROP of the (debiased)
hollow
matrix
\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}:=\mathbf{C}_{b}-\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{d}}
. Applied to the upper triangular matrix
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}_{b}
that is vectorised in
\mathbf{G}_{b}\mathbf{FZ}\bm{\sigma}
for the classical model described in Section
2
, where
\mathbf{G}_{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{V\times 4N}
is the convolutional interpolation operator corresponding only to batch
b
,

\mathbf{R}_{b}:\bm{u}\mapsto\mathbf{R}_{b}\bm{u}
can be defined as

(\mathbf{R}_{b}\bm{u})_{p}=\vect(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\bm{\alpha}_{pb}\bm{\beta}_{pb}^{*})^{\top}\bm{u}+(\vect(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\bm{\beta}_{pb}\bm{\alpha}_{pb}^{*})^{\top}\bm{u})^{*},~~\forall p\in\llbracket P\rrbracket.

(24)

The
B
ROPed batches can then be concatenated as

\bar{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{y}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\bm{y}}_{B}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\mathbf{R}_{b}\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{G}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{FZ}\bm{\sigma}+\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{B}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mathbf{DGFZ}\bm{\sigma}+\mathbf{D}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(25)

or, equivalently,

\bar{\bm{y}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{\Phi}}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{D}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(26)

with
\bar{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{PB}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Phi}}:=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\Phi}
.
The imaging model (
26
), coined
concatenated
ROP (CROP), consists of adding the CROP operator
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{PB\times VB}
to the classical model (
8
) sensing the visibilities.
The impact of the CROP operator
\mathbf{D}
on the noise statistics is less trivial than in the previously discussed models and is therefore thoroughly analysed in Section
3.2.3
.

CROP can be considered a compression model of its own. However, one expects a significant redundancy in the information carried by the
B
batches of
P
projections, as these arise from non-independent Fourier data of the image to be formed.
This leaves the opportunity for additional compression and has motivated the introduction of the MROP model.

Layer 2: random modulations.

MROP follows from introducing a second layer of dimensionality reduction, consisting in applying
M
random modulations of the ROP vectors before integrating them across batches,
i.e.
, we compute

\bar{\bm{z}}_{m}:=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\gamma_{mb}\bar{\bm{y}}_{b},

(27)

with the
modulation vectors

\bm{\gamma}_{m}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\bm{\gamma}
,
\forall m\in\llbracket M\rrbracket
, each component
\gamma_{b}
of
\bm{\gamma}
being i.i.d. as the (normalised) Bernoulli distribution
M^{-1/2}\mathcal{U}\{\pm 1\}
.
From the CROP model (
26
), this can also be viewed as

\bar{\bm{z}}_{m}=\big[\gamma_{m1}\Id,\ldots,\gamma_{mB}\Id\big]\,\bar{\bm{y}}=(\bm{\gamma}_{m}^{\top}\otimes\Id_{P})\bar{\bm{y}}.

Moreover, the
M
modulations can be concatenated as

\bar{\bm{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{z}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\bm{z}}_{M}\end{bmatrix}=(\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\otimes\Id_{P})\bar{\bm{y}}=\mathbf{M}\bar{\bm{y}},

(28)

with
\bm{\Gamma}:=[\bm{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\gamma}_{M}]\in\{\pm 1\}^{B\times M}
,
\mathbf{M}:=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\otimes\Id_{P}\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{PM\times PB}
, and
\bar{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{C}^{PM}
.
The resulting acquisition and imaging models respectively write

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{M}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\mathcal{X})+\mathbf{M}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}},

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Phi}}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{MD}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(30)

which turn into simply applying the
modulation operator

\mathbf{M}
to the random beamforming acquisition model (
21
) and to the CROP model (
26
).
The imaging model (
30
) is illustrated in Fig.
2
(d).
The computations of this random beamforming acquisition of MROP (
29
) are illustrated in Fig.
2
(b).
The notion of
batchwise
computation, which is critical to memory efficient implementations and is illustrated in Fig.
2
, is explained in Section
4.1
.
Starting from
\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}_{P\times M}
, the random modulations are summed batchwise as
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}=\mathbf{Z}^{(b-1)}+\bm{y}_{b}\times\bm{\gamma}_{b}
to remain at a fixed data dimension
PM
. The MROP vector
\bm{z}
is the vectorisation of the final matrix
\mathbf{Z}^{(B)}
.

IROP: the asymptotic case.

We also present the asymptotic case where no modulation is applied before time integration,
i.e.
,
\gamma_{mb}=1
for all
b
and a single
m
in (
27
), so that
\bm{z}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bm{y}_{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
. This model is coined
integrated
ROP (IROP). It amounts to replacing
\bm{\Gamma}
with
\bm{1}_{B}
in (
28
), yielding
\mathbf{M}=\bm{1}_{B}^{\top}\otimes\Id_{P}=[\Id,\ldots,\Id]
, hence writing

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\big[\Id,\ldots,\Id\big]\,\widetilde{\mathbf{\Psi}}(\mathcal{X})+\big[\Id,\ldots,\Id\big]\,\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}

(31)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})+\mathbf{R}\bar{\bm{\xi}},

(32)

with
\mathbf{R}:=\big[\Id,\ldots,\Id\big]\,\mathbf{D}=\big[\mathbf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{b}\big]\in\mathbb{C}^{P\times VB}
.

In
Leblanc et al. 2024
, we theoretically demonstrated that the total forward operator
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Phi}
satisfies a variant of the
restricted isometry property
(RIP)
(
Chen et al. 2015
)
, the
\text{RIP}_{\ell_{2}/\ell_{1}}
, a property ensuring the recovery of sparse images from their compressive observations. This method was shown to achieve reduced sample complexities compared to the classical uncompressed scheme of (
8
). However, as this asymptotic IROP case brings the same number of measurements as an MROP approach with
M=1
, one
a priori
expects that the number
P
of IROPs necessary for accurate image estimation is larger than in an MROP approach with
M>1
. A more detailed discussion of relative model complexities is deferred to Section
4
, which will reveal the advantage of MROP.

3.2.2
Compatibility with Visibility Weighting

This section extends the antenna-based MROP acquisition model by showing that visibility weighting is still compatible with random beamforming but at the price of an increased acquisition cost.

One issue with the antenna-based acquisition of MROPs using random beamforming, as clear from the definition of the random beamforming acquisition operator
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Psi}}
in (
21
) and illustrated in Fig.
2
(b), is that there is no access to the visibility matrices
\{\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}\}_{b=1}^{B}
.
The visibilities thus seemingly cannot be weighted before being compressed.
However, the weighting of the visibility matrix
\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}
of batch
b
can be distributed within the sketching vectors by decomposing the weighting matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}
(introduced in Section
2.3
) as a sum of rank-one matrices,
i.e.
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br}^{*}
.
Indeed, the
pb
-th
visibility-weighted
CROP measurement obtained from the random beamforming acquisition can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{y}_{pb,\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}}&=\bm{\alpha}_{pb}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\odot\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}})\bm{\beta}_{pb}\\
&=\bm{\alpha}_{pb}^{*}(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br}^{*}\odot\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}})\bm{\beta}_{pb}\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\bm{\alpha}_{bp}\odot\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br})^{*}\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}(\bm{\beta}_{pb}\odot\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{br}).\end{split}

(33)

where the operation
\odot
is an elementwise multiplication.

Eq. (
33
) shows that visibility weighting comes at the price of multiplying the initial number of ROPs by the rank
R
of the weighting matrix.
This suggests that a trade-off between
(i)
increasing
R
for an improved image recovery by visibility weighting, and
(ii)
decreasing
R
to decrease the acquisition cost, discussed in Section
4
, must be found in order to still benefit from a compressive imaging scheme.
However, this increased acquisition cost does not impact the memory requirements or the image reconstruction process.

A drawback that the MROP model shares with the dirty image and random Gaussian compression models is that the visibility weighting is fixed forever once the weighted MROPs have been acquired.

3.2.3
Noise Statistics on MROPs

The noise statistics play a crucial role in image recovery, as detailed in Section
5

\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\beta,\bar{\bm{\xi}}}\left[\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{{}^{\prime}*}\right]=\frac{\tau^{2}}{P}\begin{bmatrix}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{1}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\Id_{Q}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{B}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\Id_{Q}\end{bmatrix}.

(37)

Defining
\bm{w}_{b}=\vect(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b})
, so that
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}=2\lVert\bm{w}_{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, the covariance matrix of the noise
\mathbf{R}_{b}\mathbf{W}_{b}\bar{\bm{\xi}}_{b}
is thus, in expectation over
\alpha
and
\beta
, the identity matrix up to a normalisation
2\tau^{2}P^{-1}\lVert\bm{w}_{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
In conclusion, whichever the initial visibility weighting choice, natural or uniform, only a global rescaling per batch of the original weights is needed to ensure unit variance of the noise on the final measurements. Under this simple normalisation constraint, natural visibility weighting thus follows as
\bm{w}_{\rm n}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{P/2V}\,\bm{1}_{VB}
, while uniform visibility weighting reads, for each batch
b\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
,
\bm{w}_{{\rm u},b}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{P/2}\bm{\rho}_{b}^{-1/2}/\lVert\bm{\rho}_{b}^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}
, where
\bm{\rho}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is the restriction to batch
b
of the vector of density
\rho
.

Secondly, let us study the impact of the modulation operator
\mathbf{M}
on the noise statistics.
By linearity,
\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime\prime}]=\mathbf{M}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime}]=\bm{0}
.
From the definition of
\mathbf{M}
, the
pm
-th noise element in the MROP model writes as

\bar{\xi}^{\prime\prime}_{pm}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\gamma_{mb}\bar{\xi}_{pb}^{\prime}.

(38)

Re-using (
36
), the element of the covariance matrix of
\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime\prime}
associated with the indices
p,m
and
p^{\prime},m^{\prime}
writes as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\beta,\bar{\bm{\xi}}}\left[\bar{\xi}^{\prime\prime}_{pm}\bar{\xi}_{p^{\prime}m^{\prime}}^{{}^{\prime\prime}*}\right]&=\sum_{b,b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\gamma_{mb}\gamma_{m^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\beta,\bar{\bm{\xi}}}\left[\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{pb}\bar{\xi}_{p^{\prime}b^{\prime}}^{{}^{\prime}*}\right]\\
&=\sum_{b,b^{\prime}=1}^{B}\gamma_{mb}\gamma_{m^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\frac{\tau^{2}}{P}\delta_{bb^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\\
&=\frac{\tau^{2}}{P}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\gamma_{mb}\gamma_{m^{\prime}b}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.\end{split}

(39)

With
\sum_{b=1}^{B}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}=2\lVert\bm{w}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
|\gamma|^{2}=M^{-1}
, taking the expectation with respect to
\gamma
in (
39
) demonstrates that
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\bar{\bm{\xi}}}\left[\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime\prime}\bar{\bm{\xi}}^{\prime\prime*}\right]=2\tau^{2}P^{-1}M^{-1}\lVert\bm{w}\rVert_{2}^{2}\Id_{PM}
.
Normalising the weights by ensuring
\lVert\bm{w}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\tau^{-2}PM/2
also ensures a unit standard deviation.
In conclusion, whichever the initial visibility weighting choice, natural or uniform, only a global rescaling of the original weights is needed to ensure unit variance of the noise on the final measurements. Under this simple normalisation constraint, natural visibility weighting follows as
\bm{w}_{\rm n}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{PM/2VB}\,\bm{1}_{VB}
, while uniform visibility weighting reads
\bm{w}_{\rm u}=\tau^{-1}\sqrt{PM/2}\,\bm{\rho}^{-1/2}/\lVert\bm{\rho}^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}
. We finally note that the IROP case follows by taking
M=1
in the above relations.

3.3
Visibility-based MROP: Back to Post-Correlation

We introduce an alternative acquisition method for MROP and its CROP and IROP variants, based on visibilities rather than antenna-based random beamforming (see Section
3.2
), as shown in Fig.
2
(c).
This approach is of the same nature as the models discussed in Section
3.1
and remains compatible with existing correlators
(
Thompson et al. 2017
)
.
Within each batch
b
, the visibilities
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}_{b}
are weighted from an elementwise multiplication with an upper triangular weighting matrix
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}
as
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{b}=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{b}\odot\bm{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}_{b}
, like in the classical model (
6
).
Next, the Hermitian matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}=\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{}\mathbf{C}_{b}+(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\widetilde{}\mathbf{C}_{b})^{*}
, containing the weighted visibilities and their complex conjugates, is projected onto
P
rank-one matrices
\{\bm{\alpha}_{pb}\bm{\beta}_{pb}^{*}\}_{p=1}^{P}
, yielding the ROP vector
\bm{y}_{b}=\{\bm{\alpha}_{pb}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{b}^{\mathrm{h}}\bm{\beta}_{pb}\}_{p=1}^{P}
.
This ROP vector is distributed over
M
channels with random modulations and integrated with the previous ones as
\mathbf{Z}^{(b)}=\mathbf{Z}^{(b-1)}+\bm{y}_{b}\times\bm{\gamma}_{b}
starting from
\mathbf{Z}^{(0)}=\bm{0}_{P\times M}
for
b\in\llbracket 1,B\rrbracket
, and where
\bm{\gamma}_{b}:=(\gamma_{mb})_{m=1}^{M}
are random signs.
The final vector
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{PM}
follows the acquisition model

\bm{z}=\mathbf{MDW}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X}),

(40)

while the imaging model remains the same as in (
30
).

This method solves the problem of applying visibility weighting to MROPs, which was the case of the antenna-based MROP method as discussed in Sec.
3.2.2
.
As for the antenna-based acquisition of MROPs, the weighting is fixed forever once the weighted MROPs have been acquired.

4.1
Memory Efficient Computations

While minimising the final number of measurements
D
serves the memory requirements for the long-term storage, limiting the memory requirement during acquisition and image reconstruction is equally important.
This section describes the concepts of
batchwise
and
precomputation-free
implementations, both leveraging
on-the-fly
computation to reduce memory requirements.

Batchwise computation at acquisition.

Firstly, for any of the post-correlation compression models described in Section
3
, including the visibility-based MROP and variants, a naive acquisition approach consists in accumulating all the
VB
visibilities first, and then applying compression.
This however implies memory requirements
\mathcal{O}(VB)
, partly defeating the purpose of the compression.
A simple solution is to adopt a batchwise implementation, consisting in compressing the covariance matrix of each batch successively and summing the compressed data on the fly over batches, hence limiting the memory requirement to
\mathcal{O}(\max(V,\alpha D))
with
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=1/2
for models with complex- and real-valued data respectively.
Fig.
2
(c) illustrates the batchwise implementation for the visibility-based MROP approach.
Secondly, in the antenna-based MROP approach, the covariance matrix is never computed as the ROPs are obtained by random beamforming of the antenna measurement vector directly.
A naive accumulation of projection data across batches thus scales as
\mathcal{O}(PB)
and again a batchwise approach with on-the-fly compression will automatically reduce this to
\mathcal{O}(PM)
.
Fig.
2
(b) illustrates the batchwise implementation for the antenna-based MROP approach.

Batchwise computation at reconstruction.

As exemplified in Section
5.4
for the uSARA algorithm, image reconstruction algorithms are iterative, and each iteration involves an update of the image estimate to enforce data fidelity on the one hand and an update enforcing the image model on the other hand. Implementing the computation of the imaging models as the consecutive application of their operators, in particular passing by the
VB
visibilities, implies that a memory requirement
\mathcal{O}(\max(VB,N/2))
, where the factor
1/2
accounts for the fact that, while the
VB
visibilities are complex-valued, the
N
pixel intensities are real-valued.
However, deploying
batchwise
compression and summing the compressed data on the fly over batches, enables reducing the memory requirements, now scaling as
\mathcal{O}(\max(V,\alpha D,N/2))
. This process is very similar to the batchwise approach at acquisition, and is illustrated for the MROP model in Fig.
2
(d).

Precomputation-free forward operators at reconstruction.

Reducing the cost of the forward operator of any model is possible at the price of precomputing the composed forward operator.
However, storing the precomputed operator in matrix form significantly increases memory requirements.
We thus assume here that all computations are performed on the fly, generating the forward operators when needed from a minimal amount of information. In this context, the memory requirements for the forward operators of all sensing models can be neglected compared to the size of the intermediate and final data.
Firstly, there are no memory requirements for the NUFFT operator
\mathbf{GFZ}
.
Indeed, it can be computed on the fly as it is fully determined by the positions of the
Q
antennas and the Earth’s dynamics.
Secondly, the compression operators associated with the random Gaussian compression model and MROP, CROP, and IROP can be retrieved with negligible cost by storing random seeds. This avoids having to store the random vectors in memory.

4.3
Classical Model

Acquisition cost.

To acquire the visibilities
\bm{v}=\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})+\bm{\xi}
, the (upper triangular part of the) outer product of each antenna signal vector
\bm{x}_{b}[i]
, with
i\in\llbracket I\rrbracket
, is taken, costing
\mathcal{O}(V)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{3}}
operations per time instant, hence giving a total cost of
\mathcal{O}(IVB)_{\rightarrow 4~10^{16}}
.

Data size and memory requirement at acquisition.
Both the final data size and intermediate memory requirements are
\mathcal{O}(VB)_{\rightarrow 5~10^{6}}
, driven by the total number of visibilities, which is the very motivation for dimensionality reduction.

Computational cost at reconstruction.
The classical imaging model (
8
) consists of the consecutive application of
\mathbf{Z}
,
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}
, giving a computational cost
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB)_{\rightarrow 3~10^{8}}
, where the
2N\log N
term comes from a real FFT applied on a
4N
-sized vector, and the
JVB
term is for the application of
\mathbf{G}
.
In the illustrative numerical setup, the cost is dominated by the interpolation with
\mathbf{G}
.

Memory requirement at reconstruction.

The memory requirement at reconstruction is
\mathcal{O}\big(\max(VB,N/2)\big)_{\rightarrow 5~10^{6}}
. This value is dominated by
VB
in our numerical setup .

4.4
Dirty Image Model

Acquisition cost.

The dirty image can be acquired in a batchwise manner as
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}=\real{\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbf{\Phi}_{b}^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{b}(\mathcal{X})}
.
The total acquisition cost is given by the computation of the visibilities at each time instant plus their back-projection in each batch,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{O}(IVB+JVB)\simeq\mathcal{O}(IVB)_{\rightarrow 4~10^{16}}
.
An antenna-based method, named EPIC, exists to acquire the dirty image directly from the signal set
\mathcal{X}
as
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}=\real{\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathcal{X})}

(
Thyagarajan et al. 2017
)
.
This acquisition method has the advantage to depend linearly on
Q
.
Indeed, its computation costs
\mathcal{O}(JQ+N_{b}^{\prime}\log N_{b}^{\prime})_{\rightarrow 8~10^{4}}
per time instant and
\mathcal{O}(I(JQ+N_{b}^{\prime}\log N_{b}^{\prime})B)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{18}}
in total, where
N_{b}^{\prime}
is the average number of gridded visibilities per batch (
i.e.
, the number of
uv
cells activated by the visibilities of one batch, corresponding to the number of nonzero columns in the interpolation operator
\mathbf{G}_{b}
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
), which takes a value
6~10^{3}
in our illustrative setup. This alternative approach was proposed for dense arrays,
i.e.
, for large
Q
only, and indeed appears suboptimal in our illustrative numerical setup.

Data size and memory requirement at acquisition.
The memory requirement for the final data of the dirty image model is
\mathcal{O}(N/2)
. This approach represents an efficient data dimensionality reduction as soon as
N/2\ll VB
. Intermediate memory requirements scale as
\mathcal{O}(\max(V,N/2))
, which could also be dominated by
V
in the case of dense arrays.
Alternatively, working with the
N^{\prime}<4N
gridded visibilities obtained as
\mathbf{G}^{*}\bm{v}
may provide further savings if
N^{\prime}<N/2
.
In our numerical setup,
N^{\prime}=6~10^{4}
and the memory requirement is lower for the “dirty image” approach, as opposed to the “gridded visibilities” approach.
For the sake of simplicity, Table
2
provides the memory requirements for the former rather than the latter.

Computational cost at reconstruction.

The cost of computing the imaging model
\bar{\bm{\sigma}}_{\mathrm{d}}=\real{\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}(\bm{\sigma})}
is twice the cost of the classical model, hence having a complexity order
2\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{8}}
.

Memory requirement at reconstruction.

The memory requirement simply reads
\mathcal{O}\big(\max(V,N/2)\big)_{\rightarrow 3~10^{4}}
. In our illustrative setup, the image size is dominant.

4.5
BDA

Acquisition cost.
The acquisition cost is the same as for the classical model, requiring
\mathcal{O}(IVB)_{\rightarrow 4~10^{16}}
operations, as the additional baseline-dependent averaging entails negligible overhead smaller than
\mathcal{O}(VB)
.

Data size and memory requirement at acquisition.

Averaging the visibilities (in a batchwise manner) reduces intermediate memory requirements and data size to
\mathcal{O}(VB_{\rm bda})_{\rightarrow 2~10^{6}}
as opposed to
\mathcal{O}(VB)_{\rightarrow 5~10^{6}}
for the classical model.

Computational cost at reconstruction.

The forward model has the same structure as the classical model, but requires the computation of
B_{\rm bda}<B
visibilities, thus reducing computational cost to
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB_{\rm bda})_{\rightarrow 10^{8}}
operations.

Memory requirement at reconstruction.

During image reconstruction, the memory requirement is given by
\mathcal{O}\big(\max(VB_{\rm bda},N/2)\big)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{6}}
.
Like for the classical model, this value is dominated in our setup by the number of visibilities.

4.6
Random Gaussian compression

Such approach has been exhaustively demonstrated by the theory of compressive sensing to deliver optimal data dimensionality reduction
Foucart & Rauhut 2017
, with the number of projections needed scaling as the sparsity
K
of the image model, and in all instances upper-bounded by the image dimension
\mathcal{P}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(N/2)
.

Acquisition cost.

The sample covariance matrices are acquired for
\mathcal{O}(V)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{3}}
operations per time instant, and a number
\mathcal{P}
of projections of these sample covariance matrices are computed for each batch.
The total acquisition cost thus scales as
\mathcal{O}(IVB+\mathcal{P}VB)\simeq\mathcal{O}(IVB)_{\rightarrow 4~10^{16}}
.

Data size and memory requirement at acquisition.

While the memory requirement
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{P})_{\rightarrow 3~10^{4}}
for the data is optimal, the intermediate memory requirement
\mathcal{O}(\max(V,\mathcal{P}))_{\rightarrow 3~10^{4}}
may well be dominated by the number of baselines for dense arrays, even if it is not the case in our numerical setup.

Computational cost at reconstruction.

The imaging model (
18
) has a total cost
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB+\mathcal{P}VB)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{12}}
.
This will be dominated by the random projection term
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{P}VB)
as soon as
\mathcal{P}>J
. Unless very simple images are considered, with very small sparsity,
\mathcal{P}
will not be far from its upper bound
N/2
, leading to highly unaffordable computational costs. This explains why such approach rapidly becomes impractical and alternative dimensionality reduction techniques are required
(
Kartik et al. 2017
)
.

Memory requirement at reconstruction.

Assuming batchwise computation of the projections, the largest intermediate data size is given by
\mathcal{O}\big(\max(V,N/2)\big)_{\rightarrow 3~10^{4}}
.
In the illustrative setup, the
N/2
term dominates.

4.7
MROP

This section compares the computational costs and memory requirements of both the antenna- and visibility-based ROP models, analysing in detail (i) the general MROP proceeding to random projections followed by random modulations, (ii) the CROP variant where the random projections are not modulated, and (iii) the IROP variant where the random projections are summed up.

Acquisition cost.

As discussed in Section
3.2
and illustrated in Fig.
2
(b), the antenna-based acquisition of MROPs does not compute the covariance matrices.
Instead, it requires
\mathcal{O}(2PQ+P)
operations for the projections with
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
and for the product in (
19
) at each time instant.
The computational cost of the random modulations is
\mathcal{O}(PMB)
.
The resulting total acquisition cost is
\mathcal{O}(2IPQB+PMB)\simeq\mathcal{O}(2IPQB)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{17}}
, which is smaller than the classical sensing scheme if
2PQ\leqslant V
and larger otherwise.
The CROP model will incur the same complexity but with
P\rightarrow P_{\rm crop}
and
M\rightarrow 0
. Acknowledging that
P_{\rm crop}\leqslant P
in general as there is no subsequent compression via random modulation, offers a potential advantage to CROP over MROP. In our illustrative setup,
P_{\rm crop}=0.6P
, confirming the advantage is realised in practice.
In the IROP case,
P\rightarrow P_{\rm irop}
and
M\rightarrow 1
as a simple summation is applied. The results in
Leblanc et al. 2024
suggest that the product
PM
is to some extent the driving factor for the quality of image reconstruction in MROP, rather than
P
and
M
separately. In the asymptotic case where
M\rightarrow 1
, one thus have
P_{\rm irop}\sim\mathcal{O}(PM)
, leading to a highly unaffordable acquisition cost
\mathcal{O}(2IP_{\rm irop}VB)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{19}}
.

The visibility-based acquisition of MROP is a post-correlation scheme, meaning the covariance matrix must be computed batchwise at a cost
\mathcal{O}(V)_{\rightarrow 2~10^{3}}
.
Then, the ROPs must be explicitly computed for each batch in
\mathcal{O}(2PV)
operations.
The computational cost of the random modulations is
\mathcal{O}(PMB)
.
The resulting total acquisition cost is thus
\mathcal{O}(IVB+2PVB+PMB)\simeq\mathcal{O}(IVB)_{\rightarrow 4~10^{16}}
, which is the same as the classical sensing scheme.
As the
O(IVB)
term dominates largely, the CROP and IROP models will be driven by the same computational cost.

In our illustrative numerical setup, the antenna-based approach for MROPs is 5 times more costly than the visibility-based approach and classical model.
However, for dense arrays, if
PM
is indeed the driving factor, the antenna-based setting offers an opportunity to bring the MROP acquisition cost below the requirements of the classical scheme by increasing
M
so that
2PQ\leqslant V
. Validating this statement requires further investigations beyond the scope of the current work.

Data size and memory requirement at acquisition.

The memory requirement for storing the final MROP data is
\mathcal{O}(PM)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{4}}
.
In our numerical setup
PM\simeq N
, so that the MROP model provides a highly efficient data dimensionality reduction with respect to the classical model when the number of visibilities
VB
is much larger than the image size, of the same order of (but slightly suboptimal compared to) the reduction to
O(N/2)
delivered by the dirty image model.
The intermediate memory requirements are
\mathcal{O}\big(\max(V,PM)\big)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{4}}
.
In the illustrative setup, the
PM
term dominates.

With regards to the CROP model, the memory requirement for data storage is
\mathcal{O}(P_{\rm crop}B)_{\rightarrow 1~10^{5}}
.
Whether it compares favourably or not to MROP’s
O(PM)
depends on how much below
P
the value
P_{\rm crop}
can be chosen. In our illustrative setup CROP requires twice more memory than MROP:
P_{\rm crop}B=2PM
.
With regards to the IROP model, the fact that the required
P_{\rm irop}
may still be smaller than MROP’s
PM
is a potential advantage in terms of memory requirement, scaling as
\mathcal{O}(P_{\rm irop})_{\rightarrow 6~10^{4}}
.
In the illustrative setup, IROP is on par with MROP.

Computational cost at reconstruction.

The operators involved in the MROP imaging model (see Section
3.2
) are illustrated in and Fig.
2
and Fig.
3
.
The imaging cost is given by
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB+2PVB+PMB)_{\rightarrow 10^{9}}
.
In general, discarding the FFT term on the assumption of a large enough number of visibilities
VB
, if
2P>J
and
M>V
, the
\mathcal{O}(PMB)
cost of the modulations will dominate.
However, as these modulations imply flipping signs and complex summations in contrast to complex multiplications, their unit cost is generally smaller than the unit cost of the other terms, so that it will take even larger
P
and
M
values for this term to dominate.
If
2P>J
and
M<V
, the
O(2PVB)
cost of ROPs dominates, which is the case in our numerical setup.
Only when
2P<J
and
M<V
would the cost reduce down to that of the classical model.

For the CROP model,
P\rightarrow P_{\rm crop}
and
M\rightarrow 0
, leading to a computational
cost
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB+2P_{\rm crop}VB)_{\rightarrow 8~10^{8}}
and placing CROP in an even better position than MROP to reduce the computational cost down to that of the classical model. In the illustrative setup, CROP indeed entails a slightly smaller complexity as
P_{\rm crop}=0.6P
.
For the IROP model,
P\rightarrow P_{\rm irop}
and
M\rightarrow 1
, leading to the highly unaffordable computational
cost
\mathcal{O}(2N\log N+JVB+2P_{\rm irop}VB)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{12}}
if
P_{\rm irop}\sim\mathcal{O}(PM)
, just as for random Gaussian compression.

Memory requirement at reconstruction.

Assuming batchwise computation, MROP’s memory requirement is set by
\max(V,PM,N/2)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{4}}
.
In the numerical setup, the
PM
term dominates by a factor 2 over the
N/2
term as
PM\simeq N
.
The memory requirement for CROP scales as
\max(V,P_{\rm crop}B,N/2)
, which compares favourably to MROP only if
P_{\rm crop}B\leqslant PM
. This condition is not met in our numerical setup as
P_{\rm crop}B=2PM
, and CROP is suboptimal.
The memory requirement for IROP scales as
\max(V,P_{\rm irop},N/2)_{\rightarrow 6~10^{4}}
, similar to MROP if
P_{\rm irop}\sim\mathcal{O}(PM)
.
In the illustrative setup, IROP is thus on par with MROP.

4.8
First Conclusions on Viable Models

With regards to memory requirement, MROP and CROP offer a great opportunity for a significant reduction in data size and associated memory requirements with respect to the classical and BDA models. With regards to computational cost at acquisition and reconstruction, MROP and CROP can enable imaging at a complexity similar to the classical model.
IROP will be as unaffordable as the pure Gaussian random projection approach.
In this context, neither IROP nor the Gaussian random projection models are considered viable, and they are discarded in the numerical experiments presented in Sections
5
-
6
.

The elephant in the room in this context is the dirty image model, or the related visibility-gridding model.
Section
4.9
discusses why it does not represent the ultimate solution to dimensionality reduction and is therefore also discarded from our numerical experiments, leaving only the classical model and BDA as competitors to MROP and CROP.

4.9
Blessing and Curse of Dirty Image Model

The classical model iterations of gradient-based image reconstruction algorithms, such as uSARA used in this paper and presented in Section
5.4
, only rely on
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}
and the dirty image
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}
for the image reconstruction process.
In such an algorithm-specific context, the acquisition cost, memory requirement, and imaging cost of the classical model naturally boil down to those of the dirty image model.
This represents a distinct advantage of the dirty image model, which brings the memory requirements of the classical model down from data scale
\mathcal{O}(VB)
to image scale
\mathcal{O}(N/2)
.

However, in RI, the problematic of the calibration of antenna-based direction-independent effects (DIEs) and direction-dependent effects (DDEs) is integral to the image formation process.
The general self-calibration approach consists in alternating image estimation and DIE/DDE estimation loops.
This self-calibration process can be formulated as a joint inverse problem for the image and calibration variables, and methods have been developed for a provably convergent process
(
Repetti et al. 2017
;
Dabbech et al. 2021
)
.
In this context, it is paramount to ensure that any data dimensionality reduction approach remain “calibration-friendly”, which can be ascertained if the dimensionality reduction operator is linear and independent of the image and calibration variables themselves.
Under such constraint, DIEs/ DDEs can simply be introduced in the measurement model as additional variables before the dimensionality reduction operator.

All compression schemes considered here do satisfy these constraints with the notable exception of the dirty image approach, which introduces further coupling between the calibration variables by using
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}
to reduce the original data dimension down to image dimension.
As a result, in the wider context of joint self-calibration and imaging, the dirty image approach raises significant challenges, calling for alternative approaches. The dirty image model will thus not be considered in the numerical experiments presented in Sections
5
and
6
.

5.4
Reconstruction with uSARA

Here, we briefly present the gradient-based image reconstruction algorithm used for the experiments of Section
6
. While simple minimisation algorithms such as
CLEAN

(
Högbom 1974
)
and
BPDN

(
Van Ewout Berg & Friedlander 2008
)
exist, we focus our analysis on a recent state-of-the-art method for high dynamic range RI imaging.
The
unconstrained Sparsity Averaging Reweighted Analysis
(uSARA)
(
Repetti & Wiaux 2020
;
Terris et al. 2022
)
was recently demonstrated to deliver state-of-the-art reconstruction results on the classical model (
43
).
Applied to any of the sensing models (
43
)-(
47
) and calling their forward operator
\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}\in\{\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{W}_{\rm sub}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm sub},\mathbf{W}_{\rm bda}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm bda},\mathbf{DW}\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{MDW}\mathbf{\Phi}\}
and their associated data
\bm{b}\in\{\bm{v},\bm{v}_{\rm sub},\bm{v}_{\rm bda},\bm{y},\bm{z}\}
, the minimisation problem associated with uSARA writes

\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}}=\argmin_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}~{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{u}-\bm{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\eta\mathcal{R}(\bm{u}),

(49)

for a measurement data
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
, a
regularisation parameter

\eta>0
, and prior model

\mathcal{R}(\bm{u}):=\theta\sum_{j=1}^{9N}\log\big(\theta^{-1}|(\bm{\Theta}^{*}\bm{u})_{j}|+1\big)+\iota_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}(\bm{u}),

(50)

where
\theta>0
and the
+1
are used to avoid reaching zero values in the argument of the logarithmic terms. The “average sparsity” prior in (
50
) consists of a non-negativity constraint and a log-sum prior (a non-convex approximation of the
\ell_{0}
-norm) promoting average sparsity in an over-complete wavelet dictionary
\bm{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 9N}
. Specifically, the dictionary
\bm{\Theta}
concatenates the first eight Daubechies wavelets
(
Daubechies 1992
)
and the Dirac basis
\Id_{N}
. The mean squared error loss
\lVert\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{u}-\bm{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}/2
is the considered differentiable data-fidelity term, corresponding to the negative log-likelihood of the data under i.i.d. Gaussian random noise assumption.

The uSARA optimisation problem in (
49
) can be solved using a Forward-Backward algorithmic structure
(
Beck 2017
)
, involving at each iteration the data-fidelity term via its gradient
\real{\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{u}-\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bm{b}}
and the prior term via its so-called proximal operator.
While the algorithmic details and internal parameters of uSARA can be found in
Terris et al. 2022
, the only information needed to run it is the forward operator
\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}
and the noise level affecting the data, both driving the choice of the parameters
\eta
and
\theta
.
A heuristic relation was developed and validated in
Terris et al. 2022
to that effect.
When the data are affected by i.i.d. Gaussian noise with unit variance, the heuristic takes its simplest form, with
\eta
and
\theta
set to
3\theta\simeq 2\eta/L\simeq 1/\sqrt{2L}
.
When the noise in the data is made non-i.i.d. due to uniform weighting, a correction factor applies, which is discussed in
Wilber et al. 2023
.
As our simulations focus on uniform weighting, the latter form of the heuristic applies for the classical, subsampled, and BDA sensing models. However, in the case of the CROP and MROP models, we have demonstrated in Section
3.2.3
that the randomness of the projections preserves the i.i.d. nature of the noise even in the presence of uniform weighting, so that the simpler form of the heuristic applies.

In practice, slight adjustments to the heuristic
\theta
, and in turns to the regularisation parameter
\eta
, are necessary to achieve optimal reconstruction quality with a balance between resolution and sensitivity.
In our experiments, a single heuristic correction is used for all
\sim 50
inverse problems within a dynamic range category for a given telescope and a given sensing model.

In a reproducibility perspective, we provide explicit stopping criteria for the uSARA algorithm described in
Terris et al. 2022
.
For all tests, the stopping criteria of uSARA must be fixed to
\xi_{1}=10^{-5}
and
\xi_{2}=10^{-4}
in Algo 1 and 2, respectively, while limiting the maximum number of iterations in the outer and inner loops of Algo 1 to 3000 and 20, and to 200 in Algo 2, respectively.

Implementation

Despite the computational considerations discussed in Section
4
, we numerically implemented the CROP and MROP models by applying each operator successively,
i.e.
, in the same fashion as illustrated in Fig.
3
and not with the batchwise summation scheme shown in Fig.
2
(d).
Indeed, the dimension of our problem did not challenge the storage of all intermediate data sizes. Moreover, challenges related to the parallelism of batchwise computations are beyond the scope of the present work.

For a fair comparison in computational cost, all validations were conducted using a single Nvidia A40 GPU card with 48GB of memory.

5.6
Performance Metrics

The quality of the reconstruction by each model is evaluated through visual examination of the reconstructed and residual dirty images, along with the
signal-to-noise ratio
metric, both in linear scale (SNR) and logarithmic scale (logSNR).
The computational cost will be analysed using the averaged iteration time.

The reconstructed images are compared to their ground truth using the SNR, in dB, defined as

\text{SNR}(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\bm{u})=20\log_{10}\bigg(\frac{\lVert\bm{u}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\bm{u}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{2}}\bigg).

(51)

Given the high dynamic ranges of interest, the estimated images are shown in logarithmic scale, using the mapping

\rlog:\bm{u}\mapsto\frac{\max(\bm{u})}{\log_{10}(d)}\log_{10}\bigg(\frac{d}{\max(\bm{u})}\bm{u}+1\bigg),

(52)

where
d
is the dynamic range of
\bm{u}
.
The logSNR computes the SNR of the images mapped to logarithmic scale as

\text{logSNR}(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\bm{u})=\text{SNR}(\rlog(\widetilde{\bm{u}}),\rlog(\bm{u})).

(53)

For any sensing model, analogously as for the classical operator in (
14
), the dirty image is computed as

\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}:=\real{\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bm{b}}

(54)

where
\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}
and
\bm{b}
are defined above (
49
).
The
residual
dirty image is computed as

\bm{r}:=\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{d}}-\real{\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}}\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}},

(55)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}}
is the reconstructed image. If the reconstruction succeeds, the residual image should only include noise back-projected by
\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}
.

Finally, the
relative error
map
\bm{\sigma}_{\rm r}
gives the relative difference between the ground truth
\bm{\sigma}
and reconstructed image
\widetilde{\bm{\sigma}}
where the pixel intensity of the ground truth
\sigma_{n}
at pixel index
n
is above the assumed noise level
\bar{\tau}
, and 0 elsewhere,
i.e.
,

(\sigma_{\rm r})_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}_{n}}{\sigma_{n}},&\text{if}~\sigma_{n}>\bar{\tau}\\
0,&\text{elsewhere.}\end{array}\right.

(56)

SNR and logSNR curves.

Across all observation settings considered, the classical model, BDA model, CROP model with
P_{\rm crop}B\simeq 2N

PM\simeq N
all converge to very similar SNR and logSNR metrics.
Both the CROP and MROP metrics exhibit a progressive decrease of reconstruction quality when the number of measurements decreases from those values.
Firstly, the alignment of BDA with the classical model is expected as averaging is by construction limited to preserve an accurate Fourier model.
Secondly, the results confirm that compressing the visibilities into CROPs or MROPs is an efficient dimensionality reduction approach, delivering the same reconstruction quality as the classical model while effectively reducing memory requirements.
Thirdly, these results also suggest that the image size is a reference point for the necessary number of projections.
Fourthly, the results also point to an extra efficiency of MROP over CROP, with respective memory requirements
\mathcal{O}(N)
and
\mathcal{O}(2N)

This confirms that MROP’s modulations effectively address the redundancy in the information carried by the
B

Finally, as expected, the reconstruction quality deteriorates significantly when subsampling is used for data dimensionality reduction.

Timing curves.

The average time of one uSARA iteration is reported for all models in the last row of Fig.
6
, left for VLA and right for MeerKAT.
We recall that, as mentioned in Section
5.4
, each uSARA iteration involves the gradient term
\real{\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{u}-\bar{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{*}\bm{b}}
, and the proximal operator of the average sparsity prior.
While the computational cost of the former is directly impacted by the sensing model, the latter is predominantly dominated by the image size.
In this context, different sensing models and data regimes will only imprint significant variations of the average iteration times in regimes where
D>N

For the classical and BDA models, the measurement operator is a NUFFT, whose cost is dominated by the computation of
\bm{G}
or
\bm{G}_{\rm bda}

D
, as
D>N

In this context, the reported average time per iteration is larger for the classical model than for the BDA model, and larger in the high visibility-to-image regime of MeerKat than in the low visibility-to-image regime of VLA. For the subsampled model, the measurement operator is still a NUFFT, whose cost is however dominated by the FFT
\bm{F}

D\leqslant N

Consequently, as observed in the graphs, the average time per iteration for the subsampled model is essentially independent of
D

For the CROP and MROP models, the measurement operator is that of the classical model, followed by the random projections, and modulations in the case of MROP.
The graphs thus naturally reveal that the CROP and MROP iterations are slower than the classical ones.
But overall, the CROP and MROP average iteration always remains less than twice the time of the classical model.
In line with the complexity analysis of Section

7
Conclusion and Perspectives

This paper introduced the MROP model as an efficient data compression framework for RI, detailing its computational cost and memory requirements for both the acquisition and imaging stages.
The study explored antenna- and visibility-based approaches for acquiring MROPs. Following from a detailed theoretical complexity analysis, numerical experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the MROP model by reconstructing realistic high dynamic range images with faint and diffuse emission across the field view in both VLA and MeerKAT observation settings, confirming its advantages over the classical, subsampled, and BDA models, and the simpler CROP model.

Our contributions open several perspectives for future research.
Firstly, future research should study the combination of MROP with weighting schemes beyond uniform weighting, from natural or Briggs
(
Boone 2013
)
weightings, to
adaptive
weighting
(
Yatawatta 2014
)
, to alternative preconditioning methods
(
Onose et al. 2017
)
. Secondly, the theoretical recovery guarantees in
Leblanc et al. 2024
only holds for the IROP model and for a simplistic sparse image model, calling for further theoretical developments to support the MROP approach and for more complex RI image models.
This is key to providing a capability to determine
before observation
the number of MROPs necessary to an optimal image reconstruction quality.
Our simulation results suggest that the image size is a reference point for the necessary number of MROPs, which represents a driver to derive theoretical recovery guarantees.
Thirdly, the option of precomputing the forward operator to reduce its computational cost at the price of increased memory storage opens many perspectives on the complexities discussed in Section
4
. Fourthly, while this paper focused on monochromatic intensity imaging, MROP should be generalised to wideband polarisation imaging, with a significant compression potential expected along the spectral dimension.
Fifthly, addressing the problem of joint self-calibration and imaging in the context of MROP is paramount to its potential adoption.
In this regard, we emphasised that MROP is self-calibration friendly, with DIEs/DDEs to be introduced as additional variables before the random projections in the measurement model, thus opening the door to leveraging the “joint self-calibration and imaging” extension of uSARA
(
Repetti et al. 2017
;
Dabbech et al. 2021
)
.
Sixthly, the MROP approach should be validated for a variety of state-of-the-art image reconstruction algorithms beyond uSARA, from CLEAN to its most recent learned variant R2D2

(
Dabbech et al. 2024
;
Aghabiglou et al. 2024
)
.
Last but not least, real data validation is another important validation step towards the adoption of the MROP model, for which efficient batchwise implementations of the MROP measurement operator are warranted.
