Drain erosion into duodenum after cholecystectomy
Authors: Anna Sater, Steffen Meiler, Whitney Renee Jenson, Kristy Lynn Hawley, William Aaron Marshall
Published: 2025

Case presentation

A patient in their 70s presented for delayed cholecystectomy 6 weeks after admission for treatment of gallstone pancreatitis. They were managed non-operatively during their index admission due to new-onset arrhythmias and acute hypoxic respiratory failure. After discharge, they were medically optimized and reported resolution of symptoms prior to operation. Intraoperatively, however, it was found that the patient’s pancreatitis had evolved considerably, necessitating conversion to open cholecystectomy. There was extensive periduodenal and pericholecystic inflammation due to pancreatic necrosis that significantly distorted the anatomy of the cystic plate, therefore intraoperative esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed to aid in identification and protection of the duodenum. Pancreatic necrosis near the pylorus and portal triad was resected to aid visualization of the gallbladder. The dome of the gallbladder was identified and mobilized in a top-down fashion, but the infundibulum and cystic duct were fused to the duodenum, therefore fenestrated subtotal cholecystectomy was performed, and a 19-French Jackson-Pratt (JP) drain was left within the gallbladder fossa. Pathological findings were negative for malignancy, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 after an uneventful course with JP drain in place. The patient returned to the clinic 2 weeks postdischarge, and the drain was left in place due to continued high purulent output with plans to return to the clinic to monitor drain output. The patient was lost to follow-up.

Three months after their index admission and 7 weeks after operation, the patient presented to the emergency department with intractable vomiting, 30-pound weight loss, and severe hypovolemia. An abdominal CT scan was obtained which demonstrated gastric outlet obstruction with duodenal bulb narrowing from peripancreatic inflammation, without pseudocysts or collections (figure 1). A nasogastric (NG) tube was placed, and parenteral nutrition (PN) was initiated given their weight loss. On hospital day 3, the gastroenterology service was consulted for diagnostic EGD and to attempt placement of postpyloric feeding access. During EGD, a foreign body was noted within the lumen of the duodenum (figure 2); postprocedural sinogram confirmed that the JP drain had, in fact, eroded into the duodenum (figure 1).

What we did and why?

Given the residual periduodenal inflammation resulting from severe pancreatitis and recent laparotomy, along with their recent weight loss and malnutrition, surgical intervention was deemed incredibly high-risk. Therefore, we opted for drain removal from the duodenum under endoscopic visualization. The patient returned to the endoscopy suite, and the drain was withdrawn through the duodenotomy until it disappeared from view (figure 2). Despite attempts, the duodenotomy was not amenable to clipping. The drain was secured to the skin after endoscopy and exchanged the following day by our interventional radiology colleagues for a smaller pigtail drain. This was monitored for enteric contents over the next 3 days. Drain output remained benign, and NG output decreased with bowel function return. An upper GI series was obtained on postprocedure day 4 which was negative for duodenal leak (figure 3). The diet was advanced without issue, PN was discontinued, and the patient discharged on day 19. The drain was removed after reassuring sinogram 1 week after discharge, nearly 4 months after their index admission for gallstone pancreatitis.

Discussion

This case highlights drain-related gastric outlet obstruction and the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration. Each team “thought outside the box” to provide a patient-centric solution to this unique complication. More importantly, this approach spared the patient a repeat operation, which would have been a morbid intervention. Our gastroenterology and interventional radiology colleagues are to be commended for their willingness to assist the surgical team in caring for this patient.

Surgical drains play a critical role in postoperative care by mitigating complications such as fluid collections and abscess formation, yet they also pose inherent risks, including migration and erosion into adjacent organs, which can lead to serious complications. There is an abundance of studies and case reports in the surgical literature highlighting disastrous, unintended consequences of surgical drains.1,3 For this patient, we suspect that the drain fistulized into the duodenum due to the extensive peripancreatic inflammation from severe pancreatitis. Given the risk of biliary and pancreatic leak, the decision to leave a drain in a hostile surgical field at the time of open cholecystectomy was entirely justified, but we acknowledge that the patient’s subsequent presentation with gastric outlet obstruction likely does not occur in absence of drain placement. This case highlights the importance of diligent drain management. As soon as the drain is not clinically warranted, it should be removed, a crucial step not performed in this instance as the patient was lost to follow-up.
