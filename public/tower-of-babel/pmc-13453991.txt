Impact of steam explosion and high-pressure processing on phenolics of beach pea and Dunn pea: a comparative evaluation of their functional benefits
Authors: Sarusha Santhiravel, Fereidoon Shahidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effects of steam explosion (SE) and high-pressure processing (HPP) on the phenolic composition, distribution, and biological activities of beach pea (Lathyrus maritimus L.) and Dunn pea (Pisum sativum var. avense) were investigated. Phenolic fractions were extracted as free (FP), esterified (EP), glycosylated (GP), and insoluble-bound (IBP), and their profiles were characterized using UHPLC–QTOF–MS/MS. In untreated samples, phenolics were predominantly present in the IBP fraction. Both SE and HPP significantly altered phenolic distribution by reducing IBP, EP, and GP fractions and increasing FP content, indicating enhanced release of bound phenolics. SE exhibited a greater effect on phenolic liberation but resulted in their higher overall loss, whereas HPP preserved more total phenolics due to milder processing conditions. A total of twenty phenolic compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, and stilbenes, were identified, with notable redistribution across fractions following treatment. These compositional changes were associated with enhanced biological activities of the FP fraction, including increased radical scavenging (nitric oxide and peroxyl radicals), improved inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase, and greater protection against low-density lipoprotein oxidation and DNA strand scission. The extent of phenolic release and functional enhancement varied between the two legumes, highlighting matrix-dependent responses. Overall, SE and HPP effectively improved the extractability and functionality of phenolics, with implications for developing value-added functional food ingredients from underutilized legumes.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Legumes are a diverse group of plants in the Fabaceae family that play a central role in human nutrition and sustainable agriculture. They are widely cultivated and consumed around the world for their high content of protein, complex carbohydrates, dietary fiber, micronutrients, and biologically active phytochemicals (Singh et al., 2022). Because of this nutrient density, legumes are often promoted as important foods for improving global food security and public health. Among legumes, peas are particularly valued for being both nutritious and versatile in culinary and industrial applications. Peas encompass a range of species and varieties, from common green peas (Pisum sativum) to underutilized species such as beach pea (Lathyrus maritimus L.) and Dunn pea (Pisum sativum sativum var. avense) (Wu et al., 2023). Beach pea, a wild coastal legume distributed across shorelines of temperate regions, has been shown to contain higher crude protein, crude fiber, and total phenolics than some conventional peas, suggesting that it may be a promising source of health-beneficial compounds. Although historically not cultivated on a commercial scale, beach pea has increasingly attracted attention as a potential functional food ingredient due to these compositional attributes (Chavan et al., 2003). Despite these promising compositional traits, it remains underexplored as a food resource. Dunn pea, although agronomically closer to cultivated peas, is also considered an underutilized pulse crop with limited scientific investigation regarding its phenolic composition and biological activities. However, its adaptability to diverse growing conditions, nutritional quality, and potential richness in bioactive compounds make it an attractive candidate for functional food and nutraceutical applications. Therefore, investigating beach pea and Dunn pea may contribute to the diversification and valorization of underutilized legumes with potential health benefits.

A key group of bioactive molecules in peas and other legumes are phenolic compounds, which are secondary plant metabolites with established roles in human health. Phenolics are broadly classified into several major categories based on their chemical structures: phenolic acids (e.g., caffeic and ferulic acids), flavonoids (e.g., flavonols and anthocyanins), stilbenes (e.g., resveratrol), and lignans (Shahidi et al., 2019). These compounds contribute to antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, and metabolic health benefits in humans. However, a substantial portion of phenolics in legume seeds are bound to cell wall components such as cellulose and hemicellulose, forming “insoluble bound phenolics (IBP)” that are poorly extracted during digestion or conventional processing. Soluble phenolics are more extractable form of phenolics, which are present in the plant vacuole and categorize as free phenolics (FP), esterified phenolics (EP), and glycosylated phenolics (GP) (Shahidi & Yeo, 2016). Because bound phenolics are not readily released in the gastrointestinal tract, their bioaccessibility, and potential health benefits are limited, even though their total content is high.

Various physical, chemical, and biological processing methods have been explored to disrupt plant cell wall structures and improve the release of bound phenolics. Conventional techniques such as soaking, cooking, fermentation, germination, enzymatic hydrolysis, and thermal treatments can partially liberate phenolic compounds; however, these methods are often time-consuming, less efficient, or may lead to degradation of heat-sensitive bioactives (He et al., 2026). Therefore, there is growing interest in developing advanced and sustainable processing technologies capable of enhancing phenolic release while preserving their functional properties. Among these, steam explosion (SE) and high-pressure processing (HPP) have emerged as promising approaches due to their effectiveness in modifying plant matrices and improving phenolic bioaccessibility (Wan et al., 2022 and Hossain et al., 2022).

Previous work from our group demonstrated that SE, a thermo-mechanical pretreatment combining high temperature and pressure followed by rapid decompression, is highly effective in disrupting plant cell wall structures and releasing IBP. In Eston lentil hulls, SE significantly increased the proportion of FP and enhanced antioxidant activity, highlighting its potential as a green processing technology for valorizing legume by-products. However, SE may also induce partial degradation of heat-sensitive compounds due to the severity of processing conditions (Santhiravel & Shahidi, 2026). Moreover, the applicability of SE to other legume matrices, particularly underutilized species such as beach pea and dun pea, remains largely unexplored.

In parallel, HPP has emerged as a promising non-thermal technology capable of modifying plant matrices while minimizing thermal degradation (Lu et al., 2026). HPP operates at pressures typically ranging from 100 to 600 MPa, leading to structural disruption of cellular components and weakening of non-covalent interactions without significant temperature elevation. While HPP is widely used in the food industry for microbial inactivation and preservation of sensory and nutritional quality, recent studies have highlighted its potential to enhance the extractability and bioaccessibility of phenolic compounds (Raza et al., 2026). By facilitating the release of phenolics from intracellular compartments and weakly bound states, HPP can enhance their extractability and functional properties while preserving the structural integrity of many phenolic compounds due to the relatively mild processing temperatures employed.

Despite these advantages, limited information is available regarding the comparative effectiveness of SE and HPP in modulating phenolic composition and bioactivity in underutilized legumes. In particular, the redistribution of phenolic compounds among FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions, as well as the resulting changes in antioxidant and biological activities, have not been systematically investigated in beach pea and dun pea.

Therefore, the present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the impact of SE and HPP on the phenolic profile and functional properties of these legumes. Specifically, this work investigates the redistribution of individual phenolic compounds across FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions using UHPLC–QTOF–MS/MS, along with associated changes in antioxidant capacity, radical scavenging activity, enzyme inhibitory potential, and protective effects against lipid oxidation and DNA damage. By directly comparing thermal–mechanical and non-thermal processing approaches, this study provides new insights into optimizing processing strategies to enhance the bioaccessibility and functionality of phenolic compounds in underutilized pulse crops.

Materials and method

Materials and reagent

Mature beach pea (Lathyrus maritimus L.) pods were harvested from Bellevue Beach, Salmon Cove, and Sandy Cove in Avlon region, NL, Canada. Dunn peas were supplied by ADM through Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (Guelph, ON, Canada). Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionamidine) dihydrochloride (AAPH), 4-nitrophenyl β-D-glucopyranoside (PNPG), naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride, sodium nitroprusside, sulfanilic acid, dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), human low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, supercoiled plasmid pBR322 DNA, and a range of phenolic standards were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich Ltd. (Oakville, ON, Canada). The enzymes employed, including α-glucosidase derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (≥ 10 units/mg protein) and α-amylase from porcine pancreas (≥ 5 units/mg protein), were also obtained from the same supplier. All solvents and additional reagents used were of analytical or chromatographic grade and were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Ltd. (Oakville, ON, Canada) and Fisher Scientific Co. (Nepean, ON, Canada).

Steam explosion treatment

SE pretreatment was performed at the University of Sherbrooke using a custom-built system. Prior to processing, beach pea and Dunn pea seeds were soaked in distilled water for 48 h at ambient temperature to ensure adequate moisture uptake. The hydrated samples were then exposed to SE conditions of 190 °C and 1.38 MPa for 2 min. After treatment, the material was pressed at 0.69 MPa for 10 min at room temperature to remove residual moisture. These hydration and processing parameters were selected based on earlier optimization work conducted in the same facility, aimed at maximizing cell wall disruption while preserving structural integrity of phenolic compounds and minimizing microbial and other unintended activities (Damay et al., 2018). The treated samples were subsequently frozen at −20 °C and lyophilized for 72 h using a Labconco FreeZone freeze dryer (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA).

High-pressure processing treatment

For HPP, beach pea and Dunn pea samples were treated in their intact dry form without prior milling. The samples were packed in plastic Ziploc bags, with water added inside the bags to function as the pressure-transmitting medium, and then hermetically sealed. The sealed packages were placed in the HPP chamber and processed using a pilot-scale NC Wave 6000/55 system (Hyperbaric, Burgos, Spain). Processing was conducted at 450 MPa for 5 min, which was selected as the optimized condition, at an ambient temperature of 20 °C. Following treatment, the pressure was rapidly released, and the samples were removed from the vessel. The processed materials were subsequently frozen at −20 °C and freeze-dried for 72 h using a Labconco FreeZone freeze dryer (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA).

Sample preparation

Both SE-treated and HPP-treated samples were milled using a Black & Decker coffee grinder CBG5 series (Black & Decker, Canada Inc., Brockville, ON, Canada) and passed through a sieve to obtain a uniform particle size of ≤ 1 mm (mesh 16, sieve opening = 1 mm; Tylor test sieve, Mentor, OH, USA). For the untreated control, pea samples were not subjected to SE or HPP and were directly ground into flour under the same grinding and sieving conditions.

Extraction of free, esterified, glycosylated, and insoluble-bound phenolics

Phenolic compounds were isolated from SE-treated, HPP–treated, and untreated beach pea and Dunn pea samples using a protocol adapted from Hossain et al. (2022) with minor modifications. In brief, 1 g of sample was extracted with 10 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone and sonicated for 20 min at 30 °C using an ultrasonic bath at a frequency of 40 kHz with an ultrasonic power of 180 W (Model JPS-30A, JELIN, China). The extract was centrifuged (Sorvall™ Legend X1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) at 4000 × g for 5 min, and acetone from the supernatant was removed under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator (R-300, Büchi, Switzerland). The aqueous fraction was adjusted to pH 2.0 with 6 M HCl. FP were recovered by five successive liquid–liquid extractions with diethyl ether/ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) in a separatory funnel. The combined organic phases were evaporated at 40 °C, and the residue was dissolved in 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol.

Following FP recovery, the remaining aqueous phase was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis with an equal volume of 4 M NaOH for 4 h under a nitrogen atmosphere to release EP. After hydrolysis, the mixture was acidified to pH 2.0 with 6 M HCl and centrifuged, and EP were extracted using diethyl ether/ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) as described above. The aqueous fraction remaining after EP extraction was further hydrolyzed with 5 mL of 6 M HCl at 75 °C for 60 min under nitrogen in an incubator shaker (150 rpm; MaxQ 4000, Thermo Scientific, USA) to liberate GP. These compounds were subsequently recovered with equal volumes of diethyl ether/ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v).

Insoluble bound phenolics were isolated from the residual solid material after removal of soluble phenolics. The residue was treated with 4 M sodium hydroxide at a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:20 (w/v) and stirred continuously under nitrogen for 4 h to minimize oxidative degradation. Following alkaline hydrolysis, the suspension was acidified to pH 2.0 with 6 M HCl and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min. The released insoluble bound phenolics were extracted with diethyl ether/ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v), evaporated to dryness, reconstituted in 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol, and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Changes in the phenolic content

Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

The TPC of each phenolic fraction from untreated and HPP-treated pea samples was determined using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu assay based on Singleton and Rossi (1965) and Yeo and Shahidi (2017). In brief, 125 µL of sample was mixed with 2 mL of distilled water and 63 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, followed by the addition of 250 µL of 7.5% sodium carbonate solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was read at 725 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (HP 8452 A, Agilent Technologies, USA). TPC was calculated from a gallic acid calibration curve (0–200 mg/L) and expressed on a dry weight basis as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g of sample.

Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

The TFC of all phenolic fractions was determined using a modified version of the method described by Hossain et al. (2022). Briefly, 250 µL of each extract was combined with 1 mL of distilled water and 75 µL of 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO₂) and left to react for 6 min. Then, 75 µL of 10% aluminum chloride (AlCl₃) was added, followed by 500 µL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and 500 µL of distilled water. After 15 min, absorbance was recorded at 510 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. TFC was calculated on a dry weight basis as mg catechin equivalents (CE) per gram of sample.

Total proanthocyanidin content

Proanthocyanidin content was measured using a modified version of the method described by Chandrasekara and Shahidi (2010). Briefly, 25 µL of each sample was added to a 96-well microplate and mixed with 150 µL of 4% methanolic vanillin solution and 75 µL of concentrated hydrochloric acid. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was recorded at 500 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy Mx, BioTek Instruments, USA). Proanthocyanidin content was determined from a (+)-catechin standard curve and expressed on a dry weight basis as mg catechin equivalents (CE) per gram of sample.

Identification and quantification of Phenolics Using Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography-Quadrupole Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS)

Phenolic compounds in the FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions from SE-treated, HPP-treated, and untreated lentil hulls were identified and quantified using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS), following Santhiravel and Shahidi (2026).

Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a binary pump, multisampler, column compartment, and diode array detector (DAD), controlled via SCIEX OS software. A Synergi Fusion-RP C18 column (50 mm ×2 mm, 4 μm) was used with solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in methanol). The gradient was: 0 min, 90% A; 10 min, 10% A; 12 min, 10% A; 17 min, 90% A; re-equilibration to 20 min. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min at 25 °C. Samples were filtered through 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filters, and 7 μL was injected. Detection wavelengths were 280 nm and 330 nm. The UHPLC was coupled to a ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer (SCIEX) with electrospray ionization in negative mode. Optimized parameters included an m/z range of 100–2000, source temperature of 350 °C, and nebulizer gas pressure of 0.48 MPa, using nitrogen as the drying gas. Data acquisition and processing were carried out with ZenoTOF 7600 Explorer software, which also performed mass calibration and accuracy checks.

Phenolics were identified by comparing retention times, precursor ions, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with authentic standards, including caffeic acid, gallic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, cinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, sinapic acid, ferulic acid, protocatechuic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, chlorogenic acid, ellagic acid, resveratrol, quercetin, and catechin. Compounds without commercial standards were tentatively identified based on their UV–visible spectra, MSⁿ fragmentation, accurate mass measurements, and literature comparisons. Quantification was performed using external calibration curves of the corresponding standards or, when unavailable, the aglycone forms. Concentrations were expressed on a dry weight basis as mg per g of sample.

Effect on scavenging activities promoting cardiovascular health

Nitric Oxide (NO) scavenging activity

NO radical inhibition was measured using a modified Griess–Ilosvory assay according to Lalhminghlui and Jagetia (2018). In this modification, 0.1% (w/v) naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride was used instead of 5% 1-naphthylamine. The reaction mixture (1.5 mL) consisted of 1 mL of 10 mM sodium nitroprusside, 0.25 mL phosphate-buffered saline, and 0.25 mL of phenolic extract, and was incubated at 25 °C for 150 min. After incubation, 0.25 mL of the mixture was combined with 0.5 mL of sulfanilic acid (0.33% in 20% glacial acetic acid) and left for 5 min to complete diazotization. Next, 0.5 mL of naphthylethylenediamine dihydrochloride was added, mixed, and allowed to react for 30 min at 25 °C. Nitrite concentration was measured at 546 nm and calculated against a control containing only sodium nitroprusside under the same conditions. Peroxyl radical scavenging activity

Peroxyl radical scavenging activity was measured using the oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay, following a modified procedure of Chandrasekara and Shahidi (2012). The assay was performed on a Fluostar Optima microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Durham, NC, USA), with all reagents and samples prepared in 75 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). In each well of a black 96-well microplate (Costar® 3695, Corning, NY, USA), 20 µL of sample was combined with 200 µL of fluorescein (0.11 µM) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 75 µL of AAPH solution (17.2 mg/mL). Fluorescence was measured every 210 s at excitation/emission wavelengths of 485/520 nm. Antioxidant capacity was calculated from the area under the fluorescence decay curve (AUC) and quantified against a Trolox standard curve. Results were expressed on a dry weight basis as µmol Trolox equivalents (TE) per gram of sample.

Inhibition of supercoiled DNA strand scission

The inhibitory effect of phenolic extracts on peroxyl- and hydroxyl radical-induced cleavage of supercoiled plasmid DNA was evaluated following Ambigaipalan et al. (2016) with modifications. Supercoiled pBR322 plasmid DNA (50 µg/mL) was dissolved in 0.5 mM PBS (pH 7.4). For peroxyl radical-induced oxidation, 5 µL of DNA, 5 µL PBS, 10 µL of 7 mM AAPH, and 10 µL of phenolic extract were combined. Hydroxyl radicals were generated by mixing 5 µL DNA, 5 µL PBS (0.1 M), 5 µL FeSO₄ (0.5 mM), 5 µL H₂O₂ (0.5 mM), and 5 µL extract. Reaction mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, after which 5 µL of loading dye (0.25% xylene cyanol, 0.25% bromophenol blue, 50% glycerol) was added. Controls (DNA with PBS and radical generators) and blanks (DNA with PBS) were included. Ten microliters of each mixture were loaded onto a 0.7% agarose gel in TAE buffer (40 mM Tris–acetate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.5) containing SYBR Safe (5 µL per 50 mL gel). Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for 90 min, and band intensity was quantified using an α-Imager system under UV light. The percentage retention of supercoiled DNA was calculated using the standard formula.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and figure generation were carried out using RStudio (version 2024.12.1 + 563; Posit Software, PBC) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Comparisons between untreated and treated samples across the four phenolic fractions for each assay were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Changes in phenolic composition induced by SE and HPP

Figure 1 presents the distribution of TPC and TF across FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions in untreated, SE-treated, and HPP-treated beach pea and Dunn pea samples. In the untreated state, phenolics were primarily concentrated in the IBP fraction, with values of approximately 3.0 mg GAE/g for beach pea and 2.2 mg GAE/g for Dunn pea. This was followed by the FP fraction, which showed comparable levels in beach pea (2.5 mg GAE/g) and slightly lower levels in Dunn pea (1.9 mg GAE/g). A notable variation was observed in the distribution of conjugated phenolics between the two legumes. Beach pea exhibited a greater proportion of EP (1.6 mg GAE/g) relative to glycosylated forms (0.3 mg GAE/g), whereas Dunn pea showed the opposite trend, with higher GP (0.8 mg GAE/g) compared to EP (0.4 mg GAE/g). Both SE and HPP treatments significantly modified the distribution of phenolics among these fractions. A marked reduction in the IBP fraction was observed following processing, decreasing by 50.7% (SE) and 28.5% (HPP) in beach pea, and by 49.3% (SE) and 22.0% (HPP) in Dunn pea. This reduction indicates that both treatments effectively promoted the release of bound phenolics from structural components of the cell wall. The greater efficacy of SE compared to HPP can be attributed to its combined application of elevated temperature and pressure, which facilitates the cleavage of strong covalent linkages between phenolics and cell wall polymers. In contrast, HPP, conducted under high pressure but relatively mild temperatures, primarily disrupts non-covalent interactions, resulting in a comparatively lower extent of phenolic release.

In addition to liberating IBP, both treatments also influenced esterified and glycosylated fractions. For example, EP in beach pea decreased by 37.1% and 17.4% following SE and HPP, respectively. Similarly, GP in Dunn pea were reduced by 50.4% (SE) and 42.6% (HPP). These changes suggest that processing conditions can induce the breakdown of ester and glycosidic linkages, contributing to the transformation of conjugated phenolics into their free forms. Correspondingly, the FP fraction increased in both legumes, rising by 30.7% (SE) and 19.1% (HPP) in beach pea, and by 26.1% (SE) and 13.6% (HPP) in Dunn pea.

Despite the observed increase in FP, the reductions in IBP, EP, and GP fractions were not fully compensated, leading to an overall decline in the total of all phenolic fractions quantified by spectrophotometry. Specifically, total phenolics decreased by approximately 16.1% and 7.8% in beach pea following SE and HPP, respectively, and by 18.4% and 11.7% in Dunn pea. This imbalance may be explained by the partial loss of phenolics during processing. Released phenolic compounds may partition into the aqueous phase generated during treatment or may not be fully recovered during subsequent hydrolysis steps (Santhiravel & Shahidi, 2026). Supporting this, analysis of the aqueous fraction collected after SE treatment of beach pea revealed that approximately 10.7% of the initial phenolics were present in this medium. An additional reduction (~5.4%) may be attributed to thermal degradation or irreversible binding of phenolics to other macromolecules under processing conditions (Yeo & Shahidi, 2017).

A comparable trend was evident for TFC. Flavonoids associated with the IBP fraction showed substantial reductions following processing, decreasing by 59.8% and 32.7% in beach pea after SE and HPP, respectively, and by 54.7% and 23.7% in Dunn pea. In parallel, the free flavonoid fraction increased markedly. In beach pea, free flavonoids rose from 2.09 mg CE/g in the untreated sample to 2.85 mg CE/g after SE and 2.47 mg CE/g after HPP. A similar enhancement was observed in Dunn pea, where values increased from 1.18 mg CE/g to 1.82 mg CE/g and 1.48 mg CE/g following SE and HPP, respectively. These findings indicate that both treatments promote the liberation of flavonoids from bound forms, with SE exerting a more pronounced effect due to its combined thermal and mechanical action.

Previous research has highlighted the capacity of SE and HPP to alter phenolic distribution in plant-based systems. For example, SE treatment of Eston lentil hulls resulted in a 47.2% increase in FP, although this was accompanied by a substantial reduction in total phenolic content (31.1%) (Santhiravel & Shahidi, 2026). Similarly, Cui et al. (2023) demonstrated that SE enhanced the release of FP from grape pomace while diminishing bound fractions. In contrast, Hossain et al. (2022) reported that HPP at 600 MPa for 10 min significantly increased TPC and TFC across multiple phenolic fractions in sea cucumber body wall tissues. Collectively, these findings underscore the influence of processing conditions and matrix characteristics on phenolic behavior. Notably, the present study provides a direct comparison of SE and HPP applied to the same legume systems, offering new insights into their relative effectiveness in modulating phenolic composition.

A diverse range of phenolic acids was detected in both legumes, including protocatechuic, gallic, vanillic, syringic, caffeic, ferulic, sinapic, p-coumaric, p-hydroxybenzoic, and ellagic acids, along with conjugated derivatives such as feruloyl tartaric and caftaric acids. Structural assignments were supported by diagnostic fragmentation behavior. For example, a signal at m/z 341 was tentatively assigned to a caffeoyl hexoside based on fragment ions at m/z 179 and 161, corresponding to hexose loss (162 Da) followed by dehydration of the caffeic acid moiety. Caftaric acid was identified from a precursor ion at m/z 311 and fragments at m/z 179, 149, and 135, characteristic of caffeic acid derivatives. Similarly, feruloyl tartaric acid showed a parent ion at m/z 325 with fragment ions at m/z 193 and 149, while p-hydroxybenzoic acid was assigned based on its molecular ion at m/z 137 and fragments at m/z 108 and 119, reflecting neutral losses of CHO and H₂O. Protocatechuic acid was confirmed by its [M–H]⁻ ion at m/z 153 and a fragment at m/z 138 resulting from demethylation.

In addition to phenolic acids, several flavonoids and related compounds were identified in the pea extracts, including catechin, epigallocatechin, quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin, naringenin, and the flavonoid derivative isovitexin. Their structural assignment was supported by typical mass spectrometric features such as Retro-Diels–Alder (RDA) fragmentation and neutral losses associated with glycosidic moieties. For example, quercetin displayed a deprotonated molecular ion at m/z 301 and produced characteristic fragment ions at m/z 179, 151, and 107, indicative of B-ring cleavage. Isovitexin (apigenin-6-C-glucoside) was distinguished by its unique fragmentation pattern associated with flavone C-glycosides, particularly cross-ring cleavages within the sugar unit, allowing differentiation from its positional isomer vitexin. Furthermore, a stilbene compound, resveratrol, was also detected, showing a precursor ion at m/z 227 and fragment ions at m/z 183, corresponding to decarboxylation ([M–H–CO₂]⁻), and at m/z 137, consistent with previously reported spectral data.

Processing induced notable changes in the number and distribution of phenolic compounds across different fractions. In untreated beach pea, 18, 17, 15, and 19 compounds were identified in the FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions, respectively. After SE treatment, these values shifted to 20, 17, 17, and 17 compounds, while HPP treatment resulted in 20, 19, 19, and 18 compounds in the respective fractions. A similar trend was observed in Dunn pea, where untreated samples contained 18, 16, 15, and 19 compounds in FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions, respectively. These numbers changed to 20, 15, 14, and 16 after SE and to 20, 16, 15, and 16 after HPP. Notably, kaempferol and isovitexin were absent in the FP fraction of untreated samples but appeared after both treatments, suggesting their release from bound forms. Conversely, certain compounds originally present in the IBP fraction, such as quercetin and kaempferol in beach pea and quercetin, isovitexin, and epigallocatechin in Dunn pea, were no longer detected after processing, indicating redistribution or transformation.

Quantitative analysis further revealed differences between the two legumes. The total phenolic pool in untreated beach pea (2.59 mg/g) was substantially higher than that of Dunn pea (0.45 mg/g), consistent with overall TPC results. However, gallic acid was more abundant in Dunn pea (12.32 μg/g) than in beach pea (8.11 μg/g), highlighting compound-specific variations.

Both SE and HPP significantly influenced phenolic distribution among fractions (Table 2). In beach pea, phenolic contents in FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions were 0.79, 0.44, 0.09, and 1.27 mg/g in untreated samples. Following SE, these shifted to 1.25, 0.29, 0.06, and 0.64 mg/g, while HPP treatment resulted in 1.03, 0.32, 0.06, and 0.92 mg/g, respectively. Overall, total phenolics decreased to 2.24 mg/g (−13.6%) after SE and 2.33 mg/g (−10.3%) after HPP. Despite this reduction, the FP fraction increased markedly by 36.5% and 23.5% after SE and HPP, respectively, indicating enhanced release of bound phenolics. A similar redistribution pattern was observed in Dunn pea. The FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions contained 0.15, 0.03, 0.06, and 0.22 mg/g in untreated samples, which shifted to 0.22, 0.02, 0.02, and 0.11 mg/g after SE and to 0.18, 0.01, 0.04, and 0.16 mg/g after HPP. Comparatively, SE resulted in greater liberation of phenolics into the free fraction but also caused more pronounced losses in total phenolic content. In contrast, HPP, being a milder process, preserved a larger proportion of total phenolics but achieved a lower degree of release. This distinction reflects the combined thermal and mechanical effects of SE, which enhance cell wall disruption but may also promote degradation of heat-sensitive compounds, whereas HPP primarily induces structural modifications with limited thermal impact.

In beach pea, phenolic compounds were predominantly localized in the IBP fraction, and both SE and HPP treatments markedly altered their distribution. A consistent shift was observed toward the FP fraction, accompanied by reductions in the EP, GP, and IBP fractions. For instance, caftaric acid exhibited notable decreases in the EP, GP, and IBP fractions by 29.1, 32.9, and 55.9%, respectively, following SE, and by 15.2, 52.1, and 29.2% after HPP. In contrast, its concentration in the FP fraction increased by 24.9 and 10.7% after SE and HPP, respectively. This redistribution pattern was consistently observed across most identified phenolic compounds, indicating processing-induced liberation of bound forms.

A similar trend was evident in Dunn pea, where phenolics were also primarily associated with the IBP fraction in untreated samples. Both SE and HPP promoted a shift toward the FP fraction while reducing the concentrations in EP, GP, and IBP fractions. Correspondingly, its FP fraction increased by 25.2 and 14.7% after SE and HPP, respectively. These results further support the role of processing in releasing phenolics from bound matrices into more extractable forms.

Effect on scavenging activities promoting cardiovascular health

The ability of phenolic compounds to modulate radical scavenging activity is closely linked to their role in cardiovascular protection, particularly through the regulation of oxidative stress and vascular function (Torres‐Fuentes et al., 2022). Among reactive species, nitric oxide and peroxyl radicals are of particular relevance due to their involvement in endothelial signaling and lipid peroxidation, respectively. Nitric oxide plays a central role in vasodilation, blood pressure control, and inflammatory regulation; however, excessive NO or its interaction with reactive oxygen species can promote oxidative damage and endothelial dysfunction. Thus, compounds capable of scavenging excess NO or maintaining its balance are essential for vascular homeostasis (Andrabi et al., 2023). In addition, peroxyl radicals act as key drivers of lipid peroxidation, initiating oxidative modification of low-density lipoproteins (LDL), which is a critical event in atherogenesis (Vekic et al., 2023). Therefore, assessing NO and peroxyl radical scavenging activities provides valuable insight into the cardioprotective potential of phenolic fractions.

Impact on the regulation of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes

The modulation of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes represents an important dietary strategy for controlling postprandial glycemic responses and mitigating the risk of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes (Pasmans et al., 2022). Phenolic compounds from plant-based sources have been widely recognized for their capacity to influence the activity of key enzymes involved in carbohydrate digestion, particularly α-amylase and α-glucosidase (Ćorković et al., 2022). While α-amylase catalyzes the initial breakdown of starch into oligosaccharides, α-glucosidase completes the process at the intestinal brush border by converting these intermediates into absorbable glucose. Rapid or excessive activity of these enzymes can result in sharp postprandial glucose spikes. Consequently, their inhibition is considered an effective approach to delay carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption, thereby improving glycemic regulation (Li et al., 2022). Assessing the inhibitory potential of phenolic fractions against these enzymes provides valuable insight into their applicability as functional ingredients for managing hyperglycemia.

The inhibitory effects of different phenolic fractions from untreated, SE-treated, and HPP-treated beach pea and Dunn pea samples are shown in Fig. 4. In untreated beach pea, the IBP exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity against both α-amylase and α-glucosidase, followed by the FP, EP, and GP fractions. Processing significantly altered these activity profiles. Notably, inhibitory activities associated with the IBP and EP fractions decreased markedly after both SE and HPP treatments, whereas the GP fraction remained largely unchanged. In contrast, the FP fraction showed a significant enhancement in inhibitory potential. Specifically, α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition in the FP fraction increased by approximately 17.7 and 7.1%, respectively, following SE, and by 9.4 and 4.3% after HPP. Following processing, the inhibitory activities of IBP, EP, and GP fractions declined substantially. However, in contrast to SE, HPP had minimal impact on the α-amylase inhibitory activity of the EP and GP fractions. The FP fraction, on the other hand, exhibited pronounced increases in inhibitory activity, with α-amylase inhibition rising by approximately 29.4 and 20.9% after SE and HPP, respectively, and α-glucosidase inhibition increasing by 19.8 and 3.8%. The observed enhancements in the FP fraction are likely attributable to the disruption of cell wall structures induced by SE and HPP, facilitating the release of bound phenolics into more accessible forms that can interact more effectively with enzyme active sites. Modulation of cellular and molecular protection against oxidative damage

Oxidative stress at the cellular and molecular levels is a major factor underlying the development of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, cancer, and neurodegenerative conditions. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage essential biomolecules such as lipids and DNA, leading to structural and functional impairments (Jomova et al., 2023). Phenolic compounds are widely recognized for their protective roles against such damage, owing to their radical scavenging capacity, metal-chelating ability, and capacity to interrupt oxidative chain reactions (Brown et al., 2019). In particular, inhibition of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) oxidation is critical in preventing atherogenesis, while protection against DNA strand scission helps preserve genomic stability. Therefore, assessing the effects of phenolic-rich fractions on LDL oxidation and DNA integrity provides valuable insight into their potential in mitigating oxidative stress–related pathologies.

In the present study, LDL oxidation was induced using copper sulfate, and the extent of oxidation was monitored through conjugated diene formation at 234 nm. The reaction reached a steady state after approximately 10 h, and this plateau phase was used for comparative analysis. In untreated beach pea, LDL oxidation inhibitory activity followed the order IBP > FP > EP > GP, whereas in untreated Dunn pea the order was IBP > FP > GP > EP, indicating matrix-specific differences in phenolic distribution (Fig. 5). Following SE and HPP treatments, inhibitory activities of the IBP, EP, and GP fractions declined in both legumes. For instance, inhibition by the IBP fraction in beach pea decreased from 23.6% in untreated samples to 13.7% after SE and 18.1% after HPP, while in Dunn pea it decreased from 14.3 to 8.1% and 10.7%, respectively. Enhanced metal-chelating activity may reduce the availability of Cu2⁺ ions, thereby limiting the initiation of lipid peroxidation. Additionally, structural modifications induced during processing may expose hydroxyl groups that contribute to electron donation and radical stabilization, further improving inhibitory efficacy.

Limited studies have examined the impact of SE and HPP on LDL oxidation. Previous work on Eston lentil hulls demonstrated that SE reduced inhibitory activity in bound fractions while enhancing that of the FP fraction (Santhiravel & Shahidi, 2026). Similarly, Hossain et al. (2022) reported that IBP from sea cucumber exhibited the highest inhibitory activity in untreated samples, with minimal changes following HPP, except for slight improvements in the IBP fraction.

The protective effects of phenolic fractions against oxidative DNA damage were further evaluated using a plasmid DNA model. Oxidative stress can convert supercoiled DNA into open circular or linear forms, which exhibit reduced electrophoretic mobility. In this study, hydroxyl radicals were generated using a hydrogen peroxide–Fe2⁺ system, while peroxyl radicals were produced using AAPH. Across all samples, phenolic fractions demonstrated considerable protective effects against both hydroxyl- and peroxyl radical–induced DNA strand scission (Fig. 6). Following SE and HPP treatments, the inhibitory activity of IBP fractions decreased significantly, while the FP fraction showed notable enhancement. In beach pea, IBP activity declined by 46.5% (SE) and 24.8% (HPP) against hydroxyl radicals and by 44.9 and 27.2% against peroxyl radicals. In Dunn pea, reductions of 42.7 and 21.5% (hydroxyl radicals) and 20.6 and 9.1% (peroxyl radicals) were observed following SE and HPP, respectively. Conversely, the FP fraction exhibited increased protection. In beach pea, inhibition increased by 20.4 and 23.2% (SE) and by 9.5 and 16.9% (HPP) against hydroxyl and peroxyl radicals, respectively. In Dunn pea, increases of 25.5 and 20.6% (SE) and 13.4 and 9.1% (HPP) were observed. While the EP and GP fractions showed significant reductions after SE, changes following HPP were generally not statistically significant.

These findings suggest that both SE and HPP promote the release of phenolic compounds from bound and conjugated forms into free, more bioaccessible fractions. The enhanced activity of the FP fraction is likely due to improved availability of phenolics with strong radical-scavenging and hydrogen-donating properties, enabling more efficient neutralization of ROS and protection against DNA damage. At the same time, the reduction in activity observed in bound fractions appears to reflect a redistribution of phenolics rather than extensive degradation.

To date, only limited studies have investigated the influence of HPP on DNA strand scission, and this study represents the first to evaluate the impact of SE on hydroxyl- and peroxyl radical–induced DNA damage. Hossain et al. (2022) reported no significant changes in DNA protective activity following HPP treatment of sea cucumber-derived phenolics, highlighting the influence of matrix composition on processing outcomes. These findings, together with the present results, emphasize the importance of matrix-dependent behavior in determining the functional response of phenolics to processing and their overall bioactivity.

Although the present study demonstrated that SE and HPP effectively modulated phenolic composition and enhanced the functional properties of beach pea and dun pea, several limitations should be acknowledged. The biological activities were evaluated using in vitro assays, which may not fully represent physiological responses in vivo. In addition, only selected processing conditions were investigated, and the response of phenolic compounds may vary depending on processing severity and legume matrix composition. Furthermore, while UHPLC–QTOF–MS/MS analysis confirmed redistribution of phenolic compounds, the formation of potential degradation products during processing was not comprehensively examined. Therefore, further studies involving in vivo models, digestion and bioavailability assessments, and optimization of processing parameters are required to better understand the functional implications of these treatments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both SE and HPP effectively modified the phenolic composition and enhanced the functional properties of beach pea and dun pea by promoting the redistribution of phenolics from insoluble-bound, esterified, and glycosylated forms into more bioaccessible free forms. This transformation led to improved biological activities, including radical scavenging, enzyme inhibition, and protective effects against LDL oxidation and DNA damage, primarily driven by the increased availability of FP. SE exhibited a greater ability to release bound phenolics, resulting in higher increases in FP content, but was also associated with greater overall losses due to thermal degradation, whereas HPP, as a milder non-thermal process, preserved more total phenolics while achieving moderate release. UHPLC–QTOF–MS/MS analysis confirmed significant changes in the composition and distribution of individual phenolic compounds, highlighting matrix-dependent responses between the two legumes. Overall, this study demonstrates the potential of SE and HPP as effective processing strategies to enhance the bioaccessibility and functionality of phenolic compounds in underutilized legumes, providing valuable insights for their application in functional foods and nutraceutical development.
