LeishVet update and current recommendations on feline leishmaniosis

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In 2015, the LeishVet group published a comprehensive review on feline leishmaniosis (FeL) caused by Leishmania infantum, highlighting the growing interest in the disease despite the limited availability of evidence-based studies at the time. Over the past decade, considerable advances have been made in various aspects of animal leishmaniosis, including FeL. Veterinary awareness has increased, and diagnostic support is more widely available. However, current knowledge on FeL still largely comes from case reports and small case series studies, with fewer analytical studies and a lower evidence level compared to canine leishmaniosis. In response, the LeishVet group reviewed recent progress and provided updated recommendations based on key international publications, clinical expertise and expert consensus. A series of questions was formulated to address the main aspects of FeL most relevant for veterinary practitioners, including etiology, transmission, epidemiology (including geographical distribution and risk factors), immunology, clinical presentations and clinicopathological findings, the role of coinfections and/or comorbidities, diagnosis, prognosis formulation based on clinical staging, treatment, monitoring and prevention of FeL. The answers are largely supported by visual aids (tables, figures, and algorithms). This updated review paper focuses mainly on L. infantum infection and disease in domestic cats as it is the species of Leishmania most frequently diagnosed in cats. Background

In 2015, the LeishVet group published a comprehensive review on feline leishmaniosis (FeL) caused by Leishmania infantum, highlighting the growing interest in the disease despite the limited availability of evidence-based studies at the time [1]. Key knowledge gaps included risk factors, clinical signs, diagnostic protocols, treatment options, prognosis, and prevention strategies in cats. Additionally, awareness among veterinary professionals regarding FeL has remained low. To address these issues, LeishVet developed a set of clinical recommendations based on a thorough review of the relevant international literature, the clinical expertise of its members and expert consensus discussions [1].

Over the past decade, considerable advances have been made in various aspects of animal leishmaniosis, including FeL [2–6]. While most of the data still focuses on canine leishmaniosis (CanL), it is now well recognized that feline infection is common in endemic regions and that infected cats can contribute to sand fly transmission [7]. Veterinary awareness has increased, and diagnostic support is more widely available. However, current knowledge on FeL largely comes from case reports and small case series, with fewer analytical studies and a lower evidence level compared to CanL. In response, the LeishVet group reviewed recent progress and has provided updated recommendations based on key international publications, clinical expertise and expert consensus. A series of questions has been developed on the topics considered most relevant for practitioners and the answers provided have been largely supported by visual aids (tables, figures, algorithms).

The aim of this review was to critically evaluate new data and findings on FeL published since the release of the previous LeishVet guidelines on this topic [1]. The review primarily focuses on infection and disease caused by L. infantum in domestic cats and provides updated LeishVet recommendations accordingly. In addition, specific sections address leishmaniosis in wild felids and infections caused by Leishmania spp. other than L. infantum, to support veterinary specialists working in wildlife and zoo medicine and to consider the emerging posed by globalization.

Methodology

The PubMed database was searched based on the following keywords and Boolean operators: (“Leishmani*”[All Fields]) AND (“cat”[All Fields] OR “cats”[All Fields] OR “catus” [All Fields] OR “feline*”[All Fields] OR “felid*”[All Fields] OR “felis”[All Fields]). Articles published between January 2015 and December 2025 were considered for initial analysis, without distinction between reviews, research articles, clinical trials and case series with confirmed diagnosis. The reference lists of the selected articles were consulted to look for additional references, this turn with no mandatory time limits. Thus, some articles published before 2015 were also included, as well as conference abstracts, online documents and personal communications from some LeishVet members. The LeishVet group reached their final recommendations after several rounds of discussion.

Etiology and transmission

Which species of Leishmania infect cats and what is their geographical distribution?

In total, eight species of the genus Leishmania have been found to infect cats in different locations around the world. Leishmania infantum is the only species present both in the Old (Africa, Asia and Europe) and New (Americas) Worlds and infects cats in areas of endemicity for human and canine disease (Figs. 1 and 2). Leishmania major, Leishmania tropica and Leishmania tarentolae have been found to occur naturally in the Old World only, and Leishmania amazonensis, Leishmania braziliensis, Leishmania guyanensis and Leishmania mexicana in the New World only, where the name Leishmania chagasi was often used previously and is now considered as a synonym of L. infantum. Except for L. tarentolae, all the Leishmania spp. that infect cats have proven a zoonotic character, i.e., they can also infect and cause disease in humans [8], apart from other mammals, including dogs or other felids [7]. In recent years, the most widely used method for characterizing Leishmania spp. has been the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) followed by DNA sequencing. Similar to what has recently been studied in dogs [9, 10], infection by L. tarentolae, a parasite normally hosted by lizards, has also just been described in cats in Italy. Colony or stray cats with a mostly complete outdoor lifestyle may be exposed to L. tarentolae in areas where L. infantum is endemic and some cats harbour both parasites [11]. The real impact of L. tarentolae (particularly in terms of possible serological cross-reactions during diagnosis or possibly repercussions on the immune response) remains to be determined.

How is Leishmania transmitted to and from cats?

Like other trypanosomatids, Leishmania spp. have co-evolved with their arthropod hosts, i.e., sand fly vectors that transmit them to vertebrate hosts [12]. Sand flies can take their blood meal from a wide variety of vertebrates: mammals (including humans), birds, or reptiles [13], depending on the specific vector species feeding preferences [14, 15]. Sand fly species which are competent vectors of Leishmania spp. can thus spontaneously feed on cats, as some studies have documented [16–19]. Xenodiagnostic studies have demonstrated the vector’s ability to complete feeding on cats. These experiments have also proven that cats infected with L. infantum can effectively transmit the parasite to the sand fly as shown for Phlebotomus perniciosus in Europe [20] and for Lutzomyia longipalpis in South America [21–23]. Therefore, cats constitute potential sources of this parasite and may contribute to the global reservoir. No data are available on the infectiousness of healthy infected cats and information on other, non-vectorial transmission pathways in cats is currently very limited. These potential pathways are shown in Fig. 3, which allows for comparison with what is known in dogs. For example, Leishmania DNA can be detected in some tissues and blood of apparently healthy cats, and the risk of parasite transmission by blood transfusion has been suggested [24]. Other transmission modalities, including vertical or horizontal pathways, have been the subject of two investigations with a relatively small number of enrolled cats [25, 26], but they have not been proven in these vertebrate hosts, as is the case in dogs, mice or humans [27–30]. Nevertheless, the presence of DNA of Leishmania was documented in testes and epididymis of two males (the one tested was seropositive) and the ovaries and uterus of a seropositive female cat [26]. A recently published study showed the frequency of genital lesions of epididymitis in male cats, particularly those who were seropositive, as well as endometritis lesions in females. Furthermore, in pregnant female cats, at least one fetus tested positive for PCR, thus suggesting the hypothesis of transplacental transmission [25]. These results are similar to other observations in dogs in which the effectiveness of the venereal and vertical transmission routes has been well established [31], although the percentage of positive newborn kittens was low [25]. Although L. infantum is an inoculable pathogen, other modes of horizontal transmission have not yet been evaluated in cats. The frequency of fights in cats (scratches and bites with blood exchange) could play a role, as is already known for other infectious diseases. Epidemiology including risk factors and geographical distribution

What is the prevalence of L. infantum infection in cats in endemic regions?

The detection of L. infantum infection in cats has been the subject of a growing number of studies over the past 10 years. These studies expand the information previously published [1].

Detection is mainly based on serological techniques (indirect fluorescent antibody test [IFAT], enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA], direct agglutination test [DAT], western blot [WB] and Dual-Path Platform [DPP®] rapid immunochromatographic test) and the use of molecular techniques (including conventional PCR and quantitative real-time PCR [qPCR]) carried out on blood, bone marrow and lymph node aspirates, swabs (conjunctival or oral) or biopsies. The use of cytology (bone marrow and lymph nodes) is occasional and culture is rarely used.

Leishmanial infection has been found in cats in all regions where CanL is endemic. Seroprevalence in cats ranges from 0.0 to 75% and molecular levels of infection range from 0.0 to 60.7% in endemic regions of the Old World. Serological and molecular prevalence values are, respectively, from 0.0 to 74% and from 0.0 to 91.7% in South America.

The results vary greatly among studies, which are often difficult to compare. The measured prevalence values concern very disparate categories of cats, and the terminology used to categorize the cats is variable and not standardized (see Additional files 1 and 2: Tables S1 and S2). Furthermore, the number of cats enrolled in the studies varies from a few to several hundred, so the calculated prevalence cannot be considered representative of local feline populations in general. Finally, there are differences between the diagnostic methods used and, for example, the serological cut-off chosen, which can significantly influence the percentage of infected cats detected. In most studies, very low serological levels are neither sought nor used, which likely leads to a bias in the true infection values. It is interesting to note that the prevalence values obtained by serology (IFAT and/or ELISA) are higher than those obtained by detecting parasite DNA in the cat blood in approximately 75% of the studies using both approaches. Similarly, using IFAT, the prevalence values are higher than those obtained with ELISA in approximately 75% of studies using both techniques. Few studies have validated the serological techniques in cats using feline positive controls obtained from cats with clinical illness confirmed by isolation and negative control sera from a substantial number of cats from non-endemic areas [32–36]. Regarding the prevalence of disease, it is less frequently reported than in their canine counterparts. Just as the infection is often non-clinical in many wildlife species, immune responses that lead to natural feline resistance may account for the observed differences in the prevalence of infection in cats as compared with dogs [1]. Studies evaluating Leishmania specific cellular immunity in cats may be able to better estimate infection, but they are still poorly reported in cats [32, 37, 38].

What is the epidemiological role of L. infantum infected cats?

Available data on the epidemiology of L. infantum infection in cats in endemic areas most often show that, if the same detection criteria are used, the prevalence of feline infection is lower than the prevalence detected in dogs [42, 62–65]. Furthermore, analyses of the contents of sand fly blood meals suggest that cats are usually not frequent hosts [14, 15].

The relative rarity of feline clinical cases compared to the occurrence of the disease in dogs in endemic areas also supports that view. However, infection levels are not negligible and may be locally equivalent [57] or even higher than those detected in dogs [66].

The chronicity of feline infections, combined with the ability to infect vectors, makes them excellent sources, clearly suggesting that the cat contributes to the reservoir pool of Leishmania to other hosts, including humans. Therefore, studies with infected and healthy cats should be extended to identify their role in sand fly infection.

What do we know about the immune response against L. infantum infection in cats?

There is substantial evidence that the differences in the spectrum of immunological responses observed in dogs and humans with leishmaniosis determine the wide range of clinical manifestations associated with the disease [67–69]. Dogs with a strong T helper (Th) 1-mediated immune response tend to show a granulomatous inflammatory pattern with fewer parasites in the skin, while those with a predominant Th2 response exhibit a perivascular to interstitial mixed infiltrate (predominance of macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells) with a higher parasitic load [69]. In contrast, the knowledge of how host immune responses might modulate the clinical and pathological spectrum in cats in response to Leishmania infection is very limited. Some studies suggest that cats may mount an effective immune response that limits parasite replication, resulting in milder or subclinical infections [37, 38, 70]. However, the histopathological cutaneous features of FeL are less well characterized, and it is currently unclear whether the formation of granulomas in cats reflects a similar immunological control of the infection as observed in dogs [69].

There is more information regarding the adaptive humoral response than cell-mediated immune response in cats. Different levels of parasite-specific antibodies and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) are variably detected in infected cats [37]. The detection of antibodies against L. infantum antigen is well documented in cats naturally [71] or experimentally infected with this species [72]. Cats diagnosed with clinical FeL are antibody-positive (IgG) to L. infantum with variable levels of antibodies detected by quantitative methods (IFAT, ELISA and DAT) [1, 36]. Similar to dogs, the antibody level appears positively correlated with parasite load in blood [37, 38]. Cats induce parasite-specific Th1-mediated immune response producing IFN-γ [37] and testing positive by delayed hypersensitivity reaction type IV with a suspension of killed L. infantum promastigotes (Montenegro skin test or leishmanin skin test [70]). Lode et al. [38] detected higher serum concentrations of IFN-γ and interleukin (IL)-12 in apparently healthy infected cats compared to apparently healthy non-infected cats.

The states of L. infantum infection in which a specific Th1 immune response is found are not well established [37, 38, 70]. Again, similarly to dogs, antibody-negative cats are able to produce specific IFN-γ [37]. However, lower levels of adaptive immune response (specific antibodies and IFN-γ) compared to dogs are generally seen [63]. Finally, a correlation between specific IFN-γ production and any clinical manifestation has not been determined, as it has been shown, for instance, in papular dermatitis due to Leishmania infection in dogs [73].

Clinical presentations of FeL caused by L. infantum

Information about the spontaneous disease caused by L. infantum in cats is supported by cases and case series and non-analytical studies. This means that there is no consolidated evidence-based knowledge on FeL. According to current data, FeL remains rare, as a disease, even in areas where leishmaniosis is common in dogs and feline infection is frequent [66]. There is currently no consolidated evidence of risk factors favouring the progression to disease in infected cats. It cannot be excluded that the disease is still underdiagnosed because it is neglected—or even ignored—by many practitioners, and clinical suspicion is challenging when suggestive signs are lacking or the clinical presentation is overlooked or mixed with signs of a concurrent disease. Moreover, in some endemic areas, the level of medical care for cats is generally lower compared to dogs.

FeL appears to be a highly variable disease, similar to its canine counterpart. The infection spreads systemically in cats and the disease can affect multiple organs and tissues. Cats may show either systemic or just regional clinical signs, which may progress over time [74–77].

The course of FeL is generally chronic and progressive, and the disease has been diagnosed in adult cats (age range of 2–21 years) with a median age of 8 years [78]. The incubation period could be long-lasting and possibly influenced by immunosuppressive therapies and other factors [20]. In non-endemic areas, as is the case with dogs, some cats can manifest signs of FeL years after their relocation from endemic countries [79].

What do we know about coinfections and other medical conditions?

A review of 63 clinical cases of cats with L. infantum infection revealed that 66% had co-morbidities [78]. The main coinfection was with FIV and several studies have identified FIV to be associated with feline L. infantum infection [43, 48, 57, 78]. FIV progressively dysregulates the cat’s immune system and CD4+ Th lymphocyte cells are the main target for the viral infection [80]. These cells have a central role in adaptive immune defense mechanisms, and their impairment may enhance susceptibility of FIV-infected cats to L. infantum infection and also promote the progression of infection to disease. Moreover, some immune-mediated disorders (feline chronic gingivostomatitis [FCGS], chronic kidney disease [CKD]) and clinicopathological abnormalities in the complete blood count (CBC; anemia, leukopenia, pancytopenia) and serum proteins (hyperglobulinemia, hypergammaglobulinemia) have in their differential diagnosis lists both FIV and L. infantum. Understanding when these disorders are affected by FIV comorbidity is clinically relevant as they may improve with anti-Leishmania therapy.

Given the clinical overlap of cutaneous lesions between FeL and feline sporotrichosis (Sporothrix brasiliensis), this emerging zoonotic fungal disease should be considered, particularly in endemic regions with high prevalence, such as Brazil [81]. Other infections and medical conditions, also associated with immune suppression, have been sporadically reported as comorbidities in FeL cases. These also include treatment with corticosteroids in approximately 20% of the reported cases with a comorbidity [78]. A cat with chronic L. infantum infection developed clinical disease after cytarabin, an antineoplastic drug, and methylprednisolone were administered [20]. Any other long-term immunosuppressive treatment should be considered a potential risk factor for developing clinical FeL, similarly to what is known for severe feline toxoplasmosis. Acute and lethal toxoplasmosis cases have been associated with cyclosporine treatment in cats under immunosuppressive therapy for atopic dermatitis and immune-mediated hemolytic anemia (IMHA) [82, 83]. Additionally, cats treated with cyclosporine or cytarabin have also been diagnosed with FeL in Spain (personal communications: L. Solano-Gallego, G. Miró). Overall, coinfections or other comorbidities are present in the majority of cats with clinical FeL and are a major contributor to the pathogenesis and clinical outcome of feline Leishmania infection.

What are the most common clinical signs?

The most commonly reported clinical signs of FeL, which constitute the most frequent reason for veterinary consultation in cats, are dermatological (cutaneous and/or mucocutaneous). Additionally, lymphadenomegaly is often detected during physical examination, and these are the two main complaints in cats. Systemic signs (lethargy, anorexia and weight loss), ocular and oral disease are less frequently reported, as well as a list of rarer manifestations (Table 1). It is important to note that published case reports and case series often do not establish a direct association between specific clinical signs and L. infantum infection, so any definitive claim about the prevalence of clinical signs in FeL should be considered with caution. In fact, coinfections and comorbidities may occur and contribute to the disease as a whole. CKD has been reported in cats at the time of FeL diagnosis [74, 77, 84, 85], and signs of CKD (polyuria/polydipsia, anorexia, lethargy, weight loss, vomiting) may be the reason for seeking veterinary consultation. In other cases, CKD develops over time in followed-up cats [74, 84]. Mild forms of CKD (International Renal Interest Society [IRIS] stages 1–2) may be underdiagnosed when urinalysis with urinary protein-to-creatinine (UPC) ratio and serum symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) are not measured [86]. Importantly, apart from possibly inducing an immune-mediated renal disease, L. infantum amastigotes can be directly found in the kidney in association with macrophagic inflammation [87] and cultivated from the kidney of cats with FeL and CKD (M.G. Pennisi: personal communication). What are the most common dermatological patterns seen?

As mentioned above, cutaneous involvement is reported in the majority of cats with leishmaniosis (approximately 70–75%) [78, 88]. The most frequent lesions observed are ulcers and nodules (ulcerated or not), which may occur singly or in combination (Figs. 7 and 6). These lesions may also be located at mucocutaneous junctions, including the eyelids (Fig. 5), nose, lips and anal area [78, 88–92]. In contrast to CanL, alopecia with scaling, footpad hyperkeratosis and onychogryphosis are not often observed in FeL [21, 77, 88, 93].Fig. 5Nodular dermatitis in a Sphynx cat with leishmaniosisFig. 6Ulcerative dermatitis in a Persian cat with leishmaniosis (same patient as in Fig. 7)Fig. 7Nodular blepharitis in a Persian cat with leishmaniosis (same patient as in Fig. 6)

A case initially misdiagnosed in García-Torres et al. [78] as having papular dermatitis involved a cat presenting with three well-defined erythematous and ulcerated plaques, each approximately 1 cm in length and a few millimetres wide, lacking the characteristic “volcano-like” appearance typically observed in papular dermatitis associated with Leishmania infection in dogs [93, 94]. In cats, papular lesions typically evolve into plaques or nodules, particularly along the margins of the pinnae. This distribution could reflect the site of sand fly inoculation, as reported in dogs. Other cutaneous patterns associated with severe CanL, such as pustular or vasculitic ulcerative dermatitis, have not been documented in feline cases [93].

Pruritus is seldom a direct clinical feature of FeL. Although resolution of severe itching following antileishmanial therapy has been reported in one case [95], in most cases pruritus is attributed to concurrent diseases such as flea allergy dermatitis, pemphigus foliaceus or squamous cell carcinoma, as reviewed in Pennisi et al. [1]. Notably, transient pruritus involving the trunk has been described as an adverse reaction to allopurinol (AL) in a case of FeL [75].

Which are the ocular manifestations observed in FeL?

Ocular disease in FeL (unilateral or bilateral) varies greatly and, taken as a whole, is frequently reported in published cases. Signs of anterior uveitis or panuveitis (red eye, iris “bombé”, flare, hyphema, anisocoria, dyscoria and synechiae between iris and the cornea or the lens) are the most commonly observed ocular signs [74, 76, 79, 88, 96]. Conjunctivitis and single or multiple conjunctival nodules [77, 88, 96], keratitis [88, 96] and chorioretinitis [88] have also been reported. When FeL is not promptly diagnosed and treated, the inflammation may lead to panophthalmitis [79, 97]. In some cases, cytological and/or histological confirmation of a causative role of L. infantum in the ocular disease has been well documented [77, 79, 96, 97]. Unilateral conjunctival edema (lower and third eyelids chemosis) was observed in a cat with forehead granulomatous panniculitis caused by L. infantum [98]. The cause of the severe chemosis was not investigated and it could have been a consequence of the severe local swelling.

What are the oral manifestations detected?

Nodules occur in the gingival mucosa, the tongue and lips and amastigotes were seen in lesional biopsies from cats with leishmaniosis [1, 88, 99, 100]. Feline chronic gingivostomatitis is often observed in FeL [88, 96] and it could be representative of a complex interaction among different pathogens. FCGS is a severe and frequent immune-mediated oral disease affecting up to 26% of domestic cats [101]. Retroviral infections are among the risk factors for FCGS [102] and FIV is the more frequent coinfection detected in FeL [1, 57]. This severe chronic oral disease may be caused by the alteration of local and/or systemic immune response due to viral infections (e.g., FIV, feline calicivirus), but the consequences of interactions among viral pathogens and L. infantum have not been investigated. Moreover, other infections (e.g., feline calicivirus) are associated with FCGS and they were not investigated in cases of FeL with oral manifestations. Amastigotes are associated with macrophagic inflammation and have been detected in oral tissue of cats with leishmaniosis and FCGS (personal communication: M.G. Pennisi).

Which rare localizations have been reported?

Rare signs are associated with infrequent detection of the parasite in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and other tissues as summarized below. Also, splenomegaly is sporadically reported, and Leishmania amastigotes were histologically detected in the spleen of necropsied cats [77, 95, 96, 103, 104]. Importantly, the cytological evaluation of fine needle aspirates in cats with splenomegaly may confirm the diagnosis [77, 95, 104].

Chronic upper respiratory tract disease is reported in some FeL cases and a causative role of L. infantum has been confirmed in some cats [74, 105, 106] or suspected because of clinical cure obtained with anti-Leishmania treatment [95]. Nodules were observed in the nasal cavity and the nasopharynx, and an ill-defined invasive mass extended to the rostral cranial cavity in a very severe case [74, 105, 106]. Chronic nasal discharge was the more frequent manifestation, and in severe forms signs of the obstructive syndrome (inspiratory dyspnea and stertor) were the main complaint. Interestingly, amastigotes were found in a bronchoalveolar lavage of an atopic cat with chronic respiratory disease treated with cyclosporine for 2 years (personal communication: G. Miró).

Chronic diarrhea was the main complaint in a cat with granulomatous gastroenteritis caused by L. infantum and the most severe lesions were detected in the duodenum [104].

Mastitis was reported in an old cat with a clinical recurrence of FeL under AL treatment. The diagnosis of Leishmania-associated mastitis was confirmed by cytology and PCR [76].

Regarding other rare signs seen, a clear association of the clinical signs observed and FeL has not been documented. This is the case of fever [88, 107] and icterus [85, 88]. FeL was diagnosed by cytological evaluation of an enlarged lymph node and antibody detection in a cat chronically treated with methylprednisolone and presented with multifocal neurologic signs [88]. The causative role of FeL in the neurological signs was not investigated, but clinical cure with anti-Leishmania treatment was reported [88].

What clinicopathological findings may alert to the possibility of FeL?

Unfortunately, most data on clinicopathological findings in cats are limited to individual case reports where routine clinicopathological evaluation was performed [1, 74–77, 79, 85, 88, 95, 96, 98, 99, 104–109]. The most frequently reported laboratory abnormalities are displayed in Table 1 and include alterations compatible with chronic inflammation such as hyperproteinemia and hyperglobulinemia, typically associated with polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia, and mild-to-moderate, normocytic, normochromic, non-regenerative anemia [74, 76, 88, 95, 96, 98, 99, 104, 105, 107, 108]. In a cat with severe chronic rhinosinusitis, a bi-clonal gammopathy on capillary serum protein electrophoresis was found and in the nasal lesion a high number of plasma cells associated with light chain type amyloid deposition were found [106]. In this severe case, a plasma cell dyscrasia concurrent with FeL was diagnosed, but the occurrence of a mature-type plasma cell tumor could not be totally excluded [106].

However, in some cases no alterations in the CBC were found [74, 96, 107, 108] and other hematological findings such as thrombocytopenia [76, 77, 96, 98], pancytopenia [110, 111], leukocytosis, neutrophilia, monocytosis, lymphocytosis [77, 79, 85, 88, 109], eosinophilia [105], and leukopenia [99] were reported but were less common. Hypoalbuminemia [88, 99, 106] as well as increased alpha-2 globulin levels [79, 88] were also seen. Other inflammatory markers, such as increased serum amyloid A [79, 106] and erythrocyte sedimentation rate [109] were observed. Additionally, less frequent biochemical abnormalities include increased bilirubin and elevated liver enzymes (alanine and aspartate transaminase) [1, 85, 88, 99, 106]. Renal involvement has been observed, both at the time of diagnosis and during follow-up, in some cases [1, 39, 78]. In particular, proteinuria (increased UPC ratio), increased serum SDMA and azotemia were detected [74, 85, 106, 107].

Cats that are candidates for relocation to non-endemic areas should be tested before leaving their country, irrespective of their health status. It is important to remember that seroconversion to L. infantum probably occurs in cats from weeks to months after infection. Cats travelling to endemic areas and those relocated should be tested again by quantitative serology after a reasonable amount of time (4–6 months) has elapsed since leaving the endemic area. Travel and relocation history of cats should always be carefully considered by practitioners at the annual health check-up consultation.

In the case of cats with risk factors for leishmaniosis, quantitative serological tests are recommended. For example, cats positive for antibodies to FIV and/or FeLV antigen, those requiring chronic immunosuppressive therapies (e.g., for FCGS, atopic syndrome, IMHA) and those suffering from malignant neoplasia (Fig. 8).

What are the main diagnostic techniques employed?

The main diagnostic techniques used for the diagnosis of FeL include quantitative serology by IFAT or ELISA carried out in diagnostic and research laboratories, and PCR for the detection of Leishmania spp. DNA [113, 114].

Serological techniques should be validated by reference laboratories, but cut-offs and other thresholds of positivity may differ between methods. The interpretation of results should be clearly explained by the laboratories. It is important to remember that very low to low values are mainly suggestive of infection but not necessarily of disease. Based on current evidence, high levels of antibodies are conclusive of a diagnosis of FeL and can be defined as a fourfold (at least) increase of an established IFAT cut-off titer or high levels already defined by other serological techniques, as quantitative ELISA, from a reference laboratory. To our best knowledge there are currently no commercial serological tests for feline Leishmania infection which have been externally validated and published in a peer-reviewed journal, and rapid tests for dogs are not considered accurate for use in cats [115].

PCR techniques are frequently used on blood, conjunctiva, lymph node, skin and bone marrow aspirates. Blood PCR is considered less sensitive than other sampling locations [52]. Parasitemia in infected cats is typically even lower than in dogs [63, 66], and it is recommended to test lesional tissues when available and several tissues from the same cat by PCR to increase the likelihood of diagnosis [116]. The main PCR techniques used for diagnosis are kinetoplast DNA PCR and the ribosomal internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) PCR [66, 114], with qPCR being widely available and recommended. The combination of multiple diagnostic tools is recommended for the best detection. It is important to identify the species of Leishmania in geographical locations where more than one of these species are endemic.

Cytological specimens on stained preparations from lesional tissues or biological fluids can also be very informative about the type of pathological process, such as the type of inflammation compatible with this infection. Moreover, cytology is the fastest method to differentiate infectious agents, such as Sporothrix spp. and Cryptococcus spp., among others, from Leishmania spp. Therefore, amastigotes can be visualized by cytology; however, cytology might often be unrewarding if the parasite load is low [116, 117].

Histopathology of the skin or other affected organs may also be helpful in the detection of the type of inflammatory response in the evaluated tissue and, in some cases, also for the detection of Leishmania amastigotes, although more difficult to identify than in cytology. However, specific immunohistochemistry (IHC) is usually necessary to detect L. infantum infection in affected tissues [92].

What are the cytological findings that are suggestive of FeL?

The inflammatory infiltrates observed in tissue cytology samples (such as aspirates and impression smears) from organs like the skin, eye, oral and nasal mucosa, liver, spleen and kidney are commonly composed of neutrophils, macrophages or a combination of both (neutrophilic-macrophagic inflammation), but can also include lymphocytes and plasma cells (lympho-plasmacellular inflammation). Macrophages can contain amorphous to fibrillar pinkish cytoplasmic inclusions due to amyloid accumulation [106]. Additionally, lymphoid reactive hyperplasia is frequently seen in lymphoid organs such as lymph nodes and spleen, where variable numbers of Leishmania amastigotes may be detected [1].

How can diagnostic imaging aid in diagnosing and monitoring FeL?

Abdominal ultrasonography is a basic investigation tool useful for completing the diagnostic pathway in suspected cases of FeL, particularly when abnormalities are evident on physical examination of the abdomen (e.g., splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, thickened intestine wall, kidney masses). These clinical findings can be produced by other causes that have to be distinguished from FeL and may also be present as a comorbidity with FeL (see Differential diagnoses, Table 3). In the case of the spleen, apart from increased size, a honeycomb appearance has been observed [74, 95, 104]. Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspirates of spleen, lymph nodes and other organs with focal or diffuse lesions can be obtained for cytological and PCR investigations. In cases of macrophagic and lymphocytic gastroenteritis due to L. infantum infection, abdominal ultrasonography has shown increased intestinal thickness and this finding respectively led to digestive system endoscopy [104] and exploratory laparotomy [118] for taking multiple biopsies in both cases.

In cats with chronic upper respiratory tract disease, advanced diagnostic imaging and nasal endoscopy are required to investigate anatomical abnormalities and to perform guided biopsies of nodular or infiltrative lesions (see Differential diagnoses, Table 3). Computed tomography (CT) of the head was used in two cats with severe dyspnea due to rhinopharyngeal obstructive disease caused by FeL [105, 106]. A nasal cavity with nasopharyngeal invading mass, sinusitis and enlarged regional lymph nodes were found [105, 106]. In one case, the osteolytic destruction of the nasal turbinates, nasal septum and the cribriform plate were very severe, and CT images documented an infiltrative mass in the rostral cranial cavity [105]. CT examination demonstrated impressive improvement obtained by anti-Leishmania treatment in this case [105]. Rhinoscopy-guided biopsies of invasive masses and nodules can provide samples for culture, PCR, cytological and histological assessment needed for diagnosing FeL in these atypical cases [74, 105, 106]. In one case, histopathology evidenced a mixed histiocytic and lymphoplasmacytic rhinitis with a high number of plasma cells associated with lakes of eosinophilic congophilic substance suggestive of light chain type amyloid [106].

What is the diagnostic approach for skin lesions in cats with suspected FeL?

A systematic and evidence-based approach to the diagnosis of FeL with dermatological manifestations should begin with an assessment of its likelihood within the differential diagnosis list (Table 2). This prioritization should be guided by key diagnostic features of the clinical manifestations and the known prevalence of FeL in the relevant geographic area.

Cutaneous cytology represents a simple, rapid and minimally invasive first-line diagnostic tool that should be performed on any skin lesion amenable to sampling. The detection of Leishmania parasites in skin samples via cytology can be effectively achieved through fine-needle aspiration or puncture of papules, plaques or nodules, direct impression smears of ulcerative areas or skin surface underneath a crust [92] or by delicate scraping the raised edges of ulcers (personal communication: L. Ordeix, P. Bourdeau). When cytological findings are suggestive of FeL but do not reveal amastigotes, and clinicopathological abnormalities suggestive of leishmaniosis are present, Leishmania PCR from cytological specimens [119] or histopathological confirmation by skin biopsy plus Leishmania IHC and/or qPCR [120] are necessary to definitively diagnose FeL or to explore other differential diagnoses, whatever the results of serology may be.

Dermatopathological analysis of L. infantum-associated cutaneous lesions in cats typically reveals an inflammatory infiltrate composed of macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells and variable numbers of neutrophils [89, 121–123]. The infiltrate generally follows a nodular (granulomatous) to diffuse pattern in the dermis and/or panniculus [98, 122, 124], although in a few cases a granulomatous perifolliculitis with a high number of lymphocytes and plasma cells and a lichenoid lympho-plasmocytic interface dermatitis at the dermoepidermal junction have been described, like in dogs [89]. Epidermal changes may also include orthokeratotic or parakeratotic hyperkeratosis, particularly in cases of exfoliative dermatosis, along with variable epidermal acanthosis, which may be ulcerated [89, 123]. Interestingly, a case of granulomatous panniculitis clinically characterized by poorly demarcated, painless swelling of the forehead has been described [98]. Macrophages within the infiltrate may contain intracytoplasmic Leishmania amastigotes in cats [92], although in cases with less common dermatopathological patterns such as perifolliculitis or lichenoid interface dermatitis, the parasitic load may be low [89]. In such cases, Leishmania-specific IHC and/or PCR on skin biopsies can be employed to assess the presence of Leishmania in the lesion [92, 120, 125]. Notably, qPCR is considered the most sensitive method for detecting Leishmania in the skin of diseased dogs [126]. Nevertheless, a positive qPCR result may also occur in infected cats presenting with unrelated dermatological conditions [89, 123, 127, 128]. Therefore, for the diagnosis of disease, qPCR findings should always be interpreted in the context of the accompanying clinicopathological data.

Should blood donor cats be tested for L. infantum infection?

We know that blood transfusion-borne transmission of the infection has been documented in recipient dogs and humans in L. infantum-endemic areas [30]. This may occur also in cats. In fact, some epidemiological studies have reported variable percentages of antibody and/or blood PCR positivity in apparently healthy cats from endemic areas [42, 61, 129]. The quality control requirements of some veterinary blood banks operating in endemic areas do not include L. infantum among the infectious agents to be tested in feline blood donors and do not exclude donation from shelter and outdoor cats. However, apart from emergencies, LeishVet recommends testing for L. infantum in clinically healthy cats being screened for blood donation. We have to consider that the transfused blood will be received by fragile individuals whose immunological status could be impaired, and this may be a risk factor for developing FeL. Also, the transfused blood, even in a non-fragile animal, may induce a chronic infection status that, years later, may progress to disease.

A good compromise between costs and safety is to perform tests for excluding L. infantum infection (quantitative serum antibody detection and blood PCR) after confirming the negative status of candidate donors for other worldwide spread blood-transmitted pathogens [130].

Prognosis is related to the clinical stage, with Stage I having a more favourable prognosis, due to the mild/localized clinical signs, the absence of clinicopathological alterations and comorbidities, and negative or low levels of anti-Leishmania antibodies, which suggests a good cellular immune response. The worst prognosis is related to advanced phase disease (Stage III), complicated by severe comorbidities (e.g., CKD, severe non-regenerative anemia, pancytopenia) and progressive retroviral coinfections. Stage II clinical cases have a non-predictable (i.e., good to guarded) prognosis because of the presence of various factors that conditionate the evolution of the disease, independently of the treatment. Cats on Stage IIb have a worse prognosis than cats classified as Stage IIa, because of the occurrence of coinfections and comorbidities.

Treatment and monitoring

What are the most used drugs for FeL treatment?

Treatment efficacy clinical trials in FeL are not yet available, with the most important evidence derived from the description of their use for single clinical cases. To date, the treatment of FeL remains empirical as pharmacokinetic studies of anti-Leishmania drugs in cats are still lacking. A recent review on FeL [131] examined 28 clinical cases reported in the literature, in 21 of which it was possible to have complete data on the clinical outcome after treatment. These clinical cases had been diagnosed from 2009 to 2023, with extremely variable clinical approach and follow-up that makes comparison difficult. Additionally, one-third were affected by concomitant immune suppressive infectious diseases. Fourteen cats were infected by L. infantum, while three hosted cutaneous Leishmania spp. (2 cases: L. mexicana; 1 case: L. amazonensis), the remaining four did not include a confirmative and definitive demonstration of the species of Leishmania. This review confirms that AL as a single treatment is the most used drug in the therapy of FeL, followed by the combination of meglumine antimoniate (MA) and AL [1, 77, 88, 95, 107, 131]. The dose and duration of treatment as well as side effects are summarized in Table 5. MA treatment is considered well tolerated in cats at a dosage of 50 mg/kg, but local and systemic side effects have been reported in dogs for this drug, like pain at the injection site, cellulitis, hepatic, cardiac and renal toxicity, weakness, and pancreatitis [67, 132, 133], which should not be excluded in cats because of the limited numbers of feline cases followed up during treatment. A clinical response to a treatment specifically targeted and exclusively active on Leishmania can provide information on a possible role of the parasite in clinical cases; however, LeishVet does not recommend “ex juvantibus” diagnosis (i.e., making inferences about Leishmania spp. as the cause of suspected clinical FeL based solely on an observed favourable response to treatment). Miltefosine (MIL), a drug that is used in combination with AL for CanL treatment, was employed in one case of FeL, because of adverse effects due to AL treatment. The authors reported the occurrence of acute kidney injury after MIL treatment course, in a cat that was already affected by CKD [74]. In Brazil, a cat with leishmaniosis treated sequentially with marbofloxacin, MIL and AL showed clinical improvement only after 5 months of AL monotherapy, but remained seropositive (IFAT), cytologically and molecularly positive to L. infantum, and also infectious to L. longipalpis [134]. Nevertheless, the administration of MIL using the commercial product licensed for CanL (Milteforan®, Virbac Laboratory, Carros, France) caused the formation of Heinz bodies in red blood cells of two out of three healthy cats treated for 14 days in a safety study, because the formulation contains propylene glycol as excipient [135].

Similar to dogs and CanL, FeL treatment has not demonstrated to provide parasitological cure in the treated cats. Sick cats which received FeL treatment showed a good clinical result in roughly 50% of cases, with a decrease in parasitic load. Relapses and recurrences are always possible, as well as the possible need for euthanasia practiced in about 30% of cases in one study [131].

Should healthy infected cats be treated with anti-Leishmania drugs?

In principle, as in dogs, clinically healthy infected cats demonstrate an effective immune response and therefore leishmanicidal treatments should be limited for use in sick cats. However, these healthy infected cats should be monitored (Fig. 10). There is no evidence-based data on the recommended frequency of monitoring the course of L. infantum infection derived from longitudinal studies. Nevertheless, we recommend to follow up antibody positive and/or PCR positive cats with no compatible signs of FeL, with the aim of early detection of disease progression, by performing clinical and serological monitoring with variable frequency, based on clinical criteria and occurrence of risk factors. In case of healthy cats infected by L. infantum, monitoring will be included in the annual health check. In case of comorbidities (e.g., neoplasia, FeLV) and/or risk factors (e.g., FIV, administration of intense or prolonged immunosuppressive therapy) it is advisable to perform monitoring every 4 to 6 months (i.e., 2–3 times/year), depending on the patient’s clinical status. A fourfold increase (i.e. two titres) of an established IFAT cut-off titer (or high levels already defined by other serological techniques, as quantitative ELISA) should raise suspicion of transitioning from a healthy infected to a sick state, and a thorough evaluation of clinical signs and clinicopathological alterations should be carried out.Fig. 10Management of cats with serological and/or PCR-positive positivity for Leishmania infantum and no clinical signs and clinicopathological abnormalities compatible with feline leishmaniosis (FeL; clinically healthy infected cats). As in dogs, it is necessary to monitor sick cats under treatment by running a CBC, serum biochemical profile, serum protein electrophoresis, and urinalysis with UPC ratio in combination with quantitative serology and PCR as well as any supplementary diagnostic procedure required (e.g., imaging examination). The frequency of clinical check-ups is higher initially, aiming at early recognition of drug adverse events and lack of treatment efficacy, and depends on clinical staging after diagnosis. Which preventive measures are available for FeL and do we need to apply them to all cats living in endemic areas?

Prevention of FeL relies primarily on minimizing exposure to sand flies, as there are currently no drugs or vaccines specifically approved for this purpose in cats. Preventive measures should therefore be recommended for cats living in endemic areas, particularly for those with outdoor access, to reduce the risk of L. infantum transmission. Vector control through the application of topical insecticides with repellent or antifeeding activity represents the most effective strategy. A collar containing a combination of 10% imidacloprid and 4.5% flumethrin has been evaluated successfully in dogs [136–138] and cats [139], significantly decreasing the likelihood of L. infantum infection in cats under field conditions, and it is currently considered the only prophylactic tool supported by evidence for this purpose in this species; however, this flea and tick collar is not specifically registered for the prevention of FeL (off-label use). The efficacy of repellent products is time-dependent and the recommended intervals of application should be respected when evaluating their effectiveness. Apart from the flumethrin collar, pyrethroid-based formulations are generally toxic to cats and products commonly used in dogs must be strictly avoided, as well as close contact with dogs treated with pyrethroid spot-ons during the first 24 h after application [140]. Additional preventive measures aimed at reducing sand fly exposure are recommended in endemic areas, including keeping cats indoors or restricting outdoor activity during peak vector activity (from dusk to dawn) and installing fine-mesh window screens to limit vector entry [141]. Maintaining good general health through adequate nutrition and regular veterinary care, including appropriate endo- and ectoparasite control, may support immune competence and potentially reduce the risk of infection or disease progression. Prevention of retroviral infections such as FIV and FeLV is also important, as these infections may increase susceptibility to progressive L. infantum infection and possibly the severity of clinical leishmaniosis [1, 47, 57, 80, 142].

In Central and South America, leishmaniosis caused in cats by Leishmania spp. other than L. infantum is mostly a dermatological disease. It is caused by species included in the subgenus Leishmania represented by the L. mexicana complex (L. mexicana, L. amazonensis) and in the subgenus Viannia represented by the L. braziliensis complex (L. braziliensis and L. guyanensis) [120]. Due to the limited number of published cases, detailed clinical descriptions of cats infected by these Leishmania spp. is scarce. The majority of cases were caused by L. mexicana [120, 149–153]. The rarity of diagnosis and detailed descriptions in cats means that comprehensive clinical data are not available, and our current knowledge of the disease presentation is based on isolated case reports or few case series [120, 149–160]. The cutaneous lesions observed (mainly nodular-ulcerative dermatitis), diagnostic techniques used and drugs administered are reported in Table 6. Interestingly, no cats tested for FIV and FeLV were found positive. Some cats were followed up, and the clinical course was variable with frequent clinical recurrences and new additional lesions occurring [149–152, 157, 159]. Spontaneous cyclic progression of lesions caused by L. mexicana was described as follows: a small erythematous papule evolves to a brownish edematous nodule resembling a “clown nose” that ulcerates and then may temporarily heal with crusted scaly caps [151]. Life expectancy was, however, good, even in cats where treatment failed or was not given [149, 151, 152]. Excisional biopsy and cryosurgery resulted in curative outcomes in some followed up cats [150, 153, 160]. Severe neutropenia was described as an adverse drug reaction to AL treatment (15 mg/kg, q24h) in a single cat [152]. Recurrence of signs was partly controlled by the oral administration of Artemisia annua (wormwood) extract in this cat [152].

Currently, no studies are available about the preventive efficacy of a collar containing flumethrin and imidacloprid against Leishmania spp. other than L. infantum at individual and population level in cats.

What is the importance of other felid species in the epidemiology of FeL?

Several wild felid species, including the European wildcat, Iberian lynx, lion, ocelot, jaguar, tiger, serval and the sand cat, have been reported as infected or seropositive for Leishmania spp. and could therefore be considered potential hosts or reservoirs [7, 161, 162]. The first reports in this context include detection of Leishmania DNA by qPCR in tissues (liver, spleen and skin) of European wildcats (Felis silvestris silvestris) from northern Spain [163]; and subsequent studies have confirmed additional positive wildcats from the same areas [164, 165]. The published records of Iberian lynx (Lynx pardinus) are from Sobrino et al. [166], who found PCR‑positive spleen and blood samples in one of four sampled lynxes from Spain. Lima et al. [167] detected L. infantum infection by molecular (n = 4) and serological (n = 11; ELISA and IFAT) diagnosis in a sample of 41 lynxes in southern Portugal. Moreover, a large-scale epidemiological study run in the Iberian Peninsula by Barbero-Moyano et al. [168] evaluated the circulation of L. infantum in free-ranging (n = 487) and captive (n = 296) Iberian lynx populations, detecting a prevalence of 10% by quantitative serology (IFAT) and/or qPCR. Cases in captive large felids have also been documented. Libert et al. [169] reported leishmaniosis in a breeding group of Barbary lions (Panthera leo leo) at Montpellier Zoological Park, where one adult animal showed compatible clinical signs (epistaxis, colitis, lameness, paw ulcers) and was PCR‑positive in blood. It improved after a short course of allopurinol combined with marbofloxacin, while another group member was clinically healthy but seropositive (IFAT) and PCR‑positive. Iatta et al. [170] reported that among 20 tigers (Panthera tigris) tested from a wildlife park in Italy, nine were serologically positive (IFAT) and five PCR-positive; infected animals showed dysproteinemia with hypergammaglobulinemia. In Brazil, PCR–RFLP on lymph node and/or blood samples detected Leishmania infection in five pumas (Puma concolor), one jaguar (Panthera onca) and one lion (Panthera leo) at the zoological park of the Federal University of Mato Grosso [171, 172]. Also in Brazil, at the Belo Horizonte Zoological Garden, two ocelots (Leopardus pardalis), one Siberian tiger (Panthera tigris altaica), one jaguar and one lion were found Leishmania-positive in serum samples by a commercial ELISA (Safetest® Diagnostics), an in-house ELISA and/or three commercial rapid tests, i.e. DPP®, rKDDR® immunochromatographic kit and SNAP Leishmania IDEXX® [173]. Nevertheless, these serological tests have not been validated for use in large felines. Finally, it is worth mentioning that earlier reports described infection with Leishmania donovani in sand cats (Felis margarita) from Saudi Arabia [174], and also in servals (Leptailurus serval) [161].

Despite these reports, the epidemiological role of wild felids in Leishmania transmission remains unclear. Most infected individuals appear to be subclinical, and parasite loads reported in felids are often lower and more variable than those typically seen in canid reservoir hosts. While domestic cats have been shown to infect sand fly vectors under xenodiagnosis, there is no information on the capacity of transmission to vectors by wild felids (and hence contributing substantially to wild or zoonotic cycles). Therefore, wild felids may act mainly as spill over or incidental hosts in many settings. Still, in particular ecological contexts (e.g., where sylvatic cycles are intense), they could contribute to maintaining or bridging Leishmania transmission.

Conclusions

These update and current recommendations on FeL are intended to provide useful information for feline practitioners on risk factors, clinical signs, diagnostic protocols, treatment options, prognosis and prevention measures in cats. In areas of endemicity, the prevalence of L. infantum infection in cats is generally lower than in dogs, and leishmaniosis as a disease is also generally less frequent in cats than in dogs. Nevertheless, similar to its canine counterpart, FeL appears to be a highly variable disease, with affected cats potentially showing regional or systemic clinical manifestations. Veterinary awareness has increased, and diagnostic support is more widely available, aimed at confirming the disease in clinically suspected cats or detecting infection in apparently healthy animals. Coinfections or other comorbidities are present in the majority of clinical FeL cases and are a major participant in the pathogenesis and outcome of infection. The LeishVet group proposes a simplified preliminary staging system, compared to the one used in CanL, to support case evaluation and management. The treatment of FeL remains empirical as pharmacokinetic studies of anti-Leishmania drugs in cats are still lacking. Studies of feline treatment with immunomodulatory substances that can help to prevent disease progression in healthy infected animals are lacking and future studies are needed. The role of cats should be considered in contributing to the Leishmania reservoir for other hosts, including humans. Prevention of sand fly bites by the application of topical insecticides containing molecules with repellent or antifeeding effects is an effective strategy to reduce the risk of Leishmania transmission to and from cats.
