Using LLMs and Essence to Support Software Practice Adoption
Sonia Nicoletti, Paolo Ciancarini

Abstract
Recent advancements in natural language processing (NLP) have enabled the development of automated tools that support various domains, including software engineering. However, while NLP and artificial intelligence (AI) research has extensively focused on tasks such as code generation, less attention has been given to automating support for the adoption of best practices, the evolution of ways of working, and the monitoring of process health. This study addresses this gap by exploring the integration of Essence, a standard and thinking framework for managing software engineering practices, with large language models (LLMs). To this end, a specialised chatbot was developed to assist students and professionals in understanding and applying Essence. The chatbot employs a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system to retrieve relevant contextual information from a curated knowledge base. Four different LLMs were used to create multiple chatbot configurations, each evaluated both as a base model and augmented with the RAG system. The system performance was evaluated through both the relevance of retrieved context and the quality of generated responses. Comparative analysis against the general-purpose LLMs demonstrated that the proposed system consistently outperforms its baseline counterpart in domain-specific tasks. By facilitating access to structured software engineering knowledge, this work contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and practical application, potentially improving process management and the adoption of software development practices. While further validation through user studies is required, these findings highlight the potential of LLM-based automation to enhance learning and decision-making in software engineering.



I
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly advanced in recent years, demonstrating significant potential in various domains, including software engineering. These models, such as GPT-4o and Llama 3, have been extensively used for tasks ranging from code generation and bug detection to documentation support. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into software engineering has primarily focused on coding-related activities, leveraging LLMs to assist developers in writing, reviewing, and optimising code. However, software engineering is a broader discipline that encompasses not only coding but also other critical aspects such as methodology adoption, team collaboration, and the application of development practices.

Despite the growing application of AI in software engineering, process-oriented tasks remain underexplored in terms of LLM integration. While research has investigated AI-driven tools for issue tracking, scheduling, and process automation

, the potential of LLMs to assist with software engineering practices, particularly through the Essence standard, remains largely unexamined. Essence provides a structured way to describe software engineering methods, offering a framework for teams to assess and adapt their processes. However, there is a lack of research on how LLMs can effectively retrieve and present Essence-related knowledge to support software development tasks.

This paper addresses this gap by developing and evaluating a chatbot designed to assist software practitioners in managing and improving their processes using Essence. The chatbot leverages a RAG system to enhance its responses by retrieving relevant context from a curated knowledge base. By combining the generative capabilities of LLMs with targeted information retrieval, the system aims to provide more accurate and contextually relevant answers than general-purpose models

.

In this study, we investigate three key research questions:

RQ1:
How effective is the chatbot’s information retrieval system in providing relevant information on Essence and software engineering practices?

RQ2:
To what extent does retrieval augmentation improve the quality of LLM responses for Essence-related queries compared to general-purpose LLMs?

RQ3:
Which types of Essence-related questions benefit most from retrieval augmentation across different model configurations?

To answer these questions, we conducted a series of experiments evaluating the chatbot’s ability to retrieve relevant information. We measured its performance using both automated metrics, such as BERTScore, and human evaluation criteria, including relevance, accuracy, and completeness. Our findings indicate that the RAG-based chatbot consistently outperforms general-purpose LLMs in domain-specific queries, highlighting the benefits of integrating structured retrieval methods with LLMs.

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We developed a specialised chatbot to support software development activities, paired with a curated dataset of Essence-related documents. This represents a novel application of large language models in software engineering, aimed at assisting with the selection, application, and adaptation of software engineering practices, as well as facilitating reflection on team workflows and process health.

•

We conducted a comparative analysis of four state-of-the-art LLMs across three question types and two configurations (with and without RAG). This analysis provides empirical evidence for the effectiveness of a domain-specific retrieval-augmented approach, based on both automated semantic evaluation and human-assessed quality (relevance, accuracy, completeness).

II
Background and Related Works

II-A

The Essence Standard

Essence is a software engineering standard developed by the SEMAT initiative and maintained by the Object Management Group (OMG)
[
12
]
. It provides a common framework for defining, adapting, and combining software engineering practices. Its goal is to improve communication across teams, encourage flexibility in methods, and support continuous improvement.

At the core of Essence are two components: the Essence Kernel and the Essence Language. The language allows practitioners to describe practices in a modular, visual, and reusable way. It introduces structured elements such as Alphas (key progress areas in a software endeavour), Work Products, Competencies, Activities, and Patterns.

The Essence Kernel contains the universal elements relevant to any software project. It is structured into three Areas of Concern: Customer, Solution, and Endeavour. Within these areas lie seven Kernel Alphas (Opportunity, Stakeholders, Requirements, Software System, Work, Team, and Way of Working), each tracking critical dimensions of project health and progress.

The process of
“essentialising”
a practice means expressing it using the Essence Language to distil its essential components, such as activities and artefacts. This allows for better adoption, adaptation, and integration of practices like Scrum or Kanban within diverse team contexts.

Essence games use physical or digital cards to represent Essence elements and improve collaboration. Examples include Progress Poker (team alignment on Alpha states), Chase the State (retrospective analysis across Alphas), and Objective Go (goal setting). These games help teams visualise progress and facilitate structured discussions during meetings.

Essence has seen practical application in both industry and academia. In industry, it supports the integration of various methodologies (e.g., Agile, DevOps), offering flexibility and enhancing process health monitoring. In academia, Essence is used as a pedagogical tool to teach core software engineering principles. Its modular design allows students to engage with practices critically and adapt them to specific problems. Essence cards help make abstract concepts more tangible.

II-B

LLMs for Software Development

LLMs have rapidly become a transformative technology in software engineering. While their most widely known application is code generation, their usage has begun to expand significantly across various stages of the software development lifecycle. A comprehensive systematic literature review by Hou et al.
[
11
]
highlights how LLMs are being integrated into tasks such as code summarisation, automated testing, bug fixing, code review, and natural language documentation, in addition to code synthesis. Similarly, observations from real-world software engineering practices reveal that practitioners are leveraging LLMs like ChatGPT not only to generate code but also to explore design alternatives, clarify requirements, and seek architectural guidance
[
15
]
.

In educational contexts, LLMs are reshaping how software engineering is taught. The work by Kirova et al.
[
16
]
argues that software engineering education must rapidly adapt to the LLM era, embracing these tools as assistants in learning rather than treating them as threats to academic integrity. An observational study by Rasnayaka et al.
[
26
]
shows how students were seen to use LLMs as interactive collaborators throughout their workflow, rather than merely as one-shot code generators.

Beyond individual learning, LLMs are increasingly used to support collaborative practices. The development of AI-powered Scrum Masters
[
2
]
and autonomous agents in agile environments
[
25
]
suggests a future in which LLMs may play a persistent role in project facilitation, planning, and retrospectives. These innovations underscore that LLMs are not merely tools for code generation but are evolving into broader socio-technical actors within software engineering teams.

Several recent studies explore how LLMs can assist in specific software engineering roles or artefacts. For example, ChatGPT has been applied to tailor documentation for different stakeholders
[
3
]
, suggest software design patterns
[
20
]
, and even emulate software process models for code generation
[
19
]
. These cases illustrate the breadth of tasks LLMs can support, ranging from communication and decision-making to formal process adherence.

Despite these advancements, the literature is still relatively sparse when it comes to using LLMs to support the methodological foundations of software engineering. This is particularly true for work centred on the Essence standard, which aims to provide a common ground for describing and analysing software development practices. Although Essence is gaining traction in academia and industry, relatively little work has focused on integrating it with AI-driven systems. A notable exception is the work of Noreña-Cardona et al.
[
23
]
, who used Essence as a visual and analytical framework to improve understanding of DevOps practices. Their work suggests that Essence can act as a meta-language for modelling and comparing diverse development approaches, and its integration with intelligent systems could provide significant pedagogical and organisational value.

Our work builds on these ideas by using an LLM-powered chatbot to mediate interaction with Essence-related concepts, supporting users in exploring software practices described in the Essence language. To our knowledge, this is one of the first attempts to operationalise Essence within an interactive LLM system using a RAG architecture.

RAG is an increasingly popular technique to overcome the limitations of LLMs when answering domain-specific questions. Standard LLMs are often trained on general-purpose corpora and may hallucinate or return imprecise answers when confronted with specialised content. RAG addresses this issue by retrieving relevant documents from a curated knowledge base and using them as context during inference
[
17
]
. This technique has proven effective across domains such as medicine, law, and finance, where factual correctness and domain alignment are critical. In the software engineering domain, RAG has been used to improve the accuracy of answers to highly technical queries, particularly when model knowledge may be out-of-date or too general
[
14
]
.

In summary, our research is situated at the intersection of several emerging trends: the growing use of LLMs in non-coding software engineering tasks, the limited but promising integration of the Essence framework into intelligent systems, and the application of RAG techniques to improve LLM reliability in specialised domains. This synthesis forms the foundation for our proposed system and experimental evaluation.

III
Method

III-A

Dataset

The foundation of a RAG system lies in the quality and structure of the data it retrieves
[
36
]
. For Essence Coach, the system retrieves its information from a curated dataset of 22 documents sourced from diverse materials, covering topics such as the Essence standard, software engineering practices, and process management. These documents were gathered from various sources, including academic publications, books, guides, presentations, and online articles, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the Essence standard and its applications in software development
[
32
,
27
]
. The dataset aims to provide a broad understanding of different aspects of Essence, including its theoretical foundations, practical implementations, and educational resources. To facilitate retrieval and processing within the RAG system, all documents were converted into Markdown format. This decision was made due to Markdown’s lightweight structure, ease of parsing, and compatibility with text-processing tools, making it well-suited for structured document storage and retrieval
[
8
,
18
]
. The conversion process involved extracting textual content from various formats, including PDFs, PowerPoint presentations, and web pages, ensuring that the structure and readability of the documents were preserved.

The dataset covers four major topics related to Essence, as shown in Figure
1
. The largest portion of the dataset is dedicated to
“Essentialising Software Engineering Practices”
, which includes materials discussing how common software engineering practices can be described using the Essence language. The second-largest category focuses on the
“Essence Kernel and Language”
, which contains materials that explain the core elements of Essence, including its concepts, principles, and visual language. The dataset also includes documents on
“Essence Games”
, which are interactive learning approaches designed to help software practitioners engage with Essence concepts in a practical and playful manner. Finally, the
“Essence Cards”
category comprises materials that describe the content of several Essence elements as shown in their respective cards.

In addition to covering different topics, the dataset also includes various types of documents, as illustrated in Figure

. The largest portion of the dataset consists of presentations, which include seminar slides and lecture materials, commonly used in academic and professional training. These materials provide structured explanations of Essence concepts and are often designed for instructional purposes. The second-largest document type is guides, which offer step-by-step instructions on how to apply Essence in different contexts. Guides are particularly useful for practitioners looking to integrate Essence into their workflows. The dataset also includes books, which provide in-depth discussions on Essence and its applications in software engineering with several examples. Another portion of the dataset consists of research articles, which include peer-reviewed papers discussing the theoretical and empirical aspects of Essence. These articles provide valuable insights into how Essence has been studied and applied in different settings. RAG Pipeline

The application comprises three main components: the chat interface, the retrieval mechanism, and the generation module. Figure
3
presents a high-level overview of the system architecture, illustrating how these components interact to process user queries and generate responses.
The following sections detail the strategies used to optimise each stage of the pipeline.

Fig. 3:
Overview of the system’s architecture.

III-B
1
Embedding Strategy

To enable efficient retrieval, the documents were split into chunks based on their Markdown headers. This semantic chunking approach ensured that each chunk represents a cohesive unit of information rather than arbitrary text fragments
[
24
]
. A total of 461 chunks were obtained from the 22 documents.

For vector embeddings, the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model was used to generate 384-dimensional vector representations of each chunk. The embedding process involved tokenising text into smaller units, generating embeddings using the transformer model, and storing the embeddings in ChromaDB, a vector database
[
21
]
.

III-B
2
Retrieval Strategy

The retrieval strategy for the chatbot was developed after evaluating multiple approaches to ensure the most relevant and contextually accurate information was retrieved. The final method chosen is an ensemble retriever that combines vector search using cosine similarity with keyword-based search using BM25, as shown in Figure
4
. This approach was selected because it leverages both semantic understanding and exact term matching, which is particularly valuable in the context of the Essence standard due to its many domain-specific terms
[
29
]
.

Fig. 4:
Ensemble Retriever.

Vector search is performed using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, the same model used for the embedding. Cosine similarity is then used to measure the angle between these embedding vectors, with smaller angles indicating greater similarity. This allows the retriever to find documents that are semantically related to the query, even if they do not contain the exact keywords
[
22
]
. BM25, on the other hand, is a probabilistic ranking function that scores documents based on term frequency and inverse document frequency, ensuring that documents containing the exact query terms, especially rare and meaningful ones, are ranked higher
[
31
]
.

To fully exploit the strengths of both methods, the ensemble retriever assigns equal weights (0.5 each) to cosine similarity and BM25 scores when ranking results. Each retrieval method returns the two most relevant documents, which are then combined, resulting in up to four unique contexts that are appended to the user query before being sent to the language model. We selected four as the target number of contexts based on preliminary tests to determine a suitable balance between relevance and input length. In some cases, the number of final contexts may be two or three instead of four if both retrieval methods return overlapping results, though this was relatively uncommon.

III-B
3
LLM for Generation

After retrieving the relevant contexts, the augmented prompt, consisting of the user’s original query and the retrieved information, is sent to the chosen LLM to generate a response. The output is then returned to the user as the chatbot’s answer. To ensure consistency and relevance, a system prompt was given to the LLM, explicitly defining the chatbot’s role as an
“Essence Coach”

[
34
]
. This prompt provided a short definition of the Essence standard and instructions on how to combine the retrieved context with prior knowledge.

Multiple LLMs were tested to determine the most suitable model for response generation. These included Llama-3.3-70B-Versatile, GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Haiku, and DeepSeek-V3. These models were selected based on their strong performance in benchmarks, broad contextual understanding, and suitability for handling technical and structured information like the Essence standard. Llama-3 was accessed through the Groq API, while the other models were used via their respective web applications.

In addition, a version of the chatbot with a graphical user interface was developed using Llama-3. This web-based implementation includes more features, such as the ability to summarise previous messages and allows users to select predefined roles (Scrum Master, Product Owner, Developer) and specify the event they are attending (Sprint Planning, Retrospectives, etc.). While these features enhance usability, they are not directly within the scope of this research and are therefore not discussed in this paper.

III-C

Initial User Observations

Before designing the experiment to evaluate the different model configurations, we provided the version of the chatbot with a user interface to students and gathered informal feedback as they used it during a course project involving the Essence framework.
The evaluation is based on usage logs and anecdotal student comments. Two different classes were involved:

•

undergraduate students in Computer Science (CS), attending a project course including substantial teamwork for software development; and

•

graduate students in Social Sciences (SS), attending an introductory course on digital transformation projects based on agile collaboration with a focus on the requirements phase.

CS students posed practical and sometimes ambiguous questions, such as “If I create an Essence card for a tool like GitLab or SonarQube, what card is it? Is it an Alpha?”, “How can I show technical debt?”, “How can I draw a diagram to show technical debt?”.

The chatbot responded with consistent, domain-appropriate answers. For example, it correctly identified GitLab as best modeled as a Work Product in Essence, and provided multiple visualization strategies (bar charts, matrices, burndown charts) for representing technical debt.

Students reported that the chatbot was especially useful for clarifying ambiguous or cross-cutting concepts, such as mapping real tools to Essence elements or writing structured reports using Alpha states. One student said that the chatbot “helped me understand how to reflect Essence in the project report more clearly than the official guide”.

Despite some verbosity and occasional repetition, students found the retrieval-augmented version more informative than base LLMs. Questions involving practical course activities, such as retrospectives or team reports, were answered with helpful structure and aligned terminology.

This informal log-based evaluation confirms that the chatbot can assist CS students in applying Essence concepts to real-world scenarios.
A formal usability study is planned for future iterations.

We also piloted the chatbot with a group of SS graduate students enrolled in a course on digital product development.
These students had limited programming experience but were engaged in agile project work where they used Essence to structure team processes.

Analysis of the logs shows that students often asked high-level or reflective questions such as “Can Essence be used for team building?”, “I am not able to program, so how can I use Essence?”, “Is Essence useful for retrospectives?”.

The chatbot provided contextually appropriate answers. For example, to the question on non-programmers using Essence, it explained that Essence is a framework for describing and evolving ways of working, not code, and gave examples of its use in organizing teamwork and managing progress.

Students appreciated that the chatbot acted as a “non-judgmental guide” in navigating an unfamiliar formalism.
One typical feedback was “Now I understand why we work using sprints: Essence gave a clear explanation”. Others used the bot to improve their team reports, asking how to describe activities, alphas, or work products in plain language.

However, some struggled with terminology, and the chatbot occasionally returned answers that were too abstract or jargon-heavy. Overall, the logs show that the chatbot helped demystify Essence for non-technical users, supporting their reflection and report writing. These findings suggest that LLM-based assistants can lower the barrier for framework adoption in interdisciplinary contexts.

III-D

Experiment Design

Each model was tested in two configurations: one using RAG and one without it, which meant the model would respond purely from its default knowledge base. To evaluate their performance, all models were given the same set of 30 questions related to the Essence standard and software engineering practices. While the questions were inspired by an earlier test involving students, their queries were not sufficient to construct the full dataset, so we came up with additional questions to ensure a more comprehensive and balanced set. These questions were designed to assess different aspects of the models’ capabilities and were divided into three categories:

•

Information:
General questions about Essence, such as
“What are the Alphas of the Essence Kernel?”
.

•

Decision-Making:
Questions that required recommending the most suitable practices based on a given scenario, such as
“What practices would you recommend to a group of students who have to develop a mobile game for a university project?”
.

•

Translation:
Questions that involved describing known software engineering practices using Essence terminology, such as
“Describe the pair programming practice using the Essence language.”
. These are examples of what is referred to as
“essentialisation”
.

Each model was tested in a single chat session, receiving all 30 questions consecutively. This setup ensured that contextual carryover (if any) remained consistent across models. Additionally, to maintain comparability, responses were constrained to a predefined word limit ranging from 100 to 250 words, ensuring that differences in verbosity did not affect the evaluation
[
5
,
28
,
13
]
.

Some of the questions had known answers within the RAG system, particularly those in the
“Information”
category, which were formulated directly from the Essence documentation. In contrast, the
“Decision-Making”
questions were intentionally crafted without a clear answer in the dataset, allowing us to observe how well the models reasoned and adapted when precise information was unavailable. A subset of the
“Translation”
questions also had known answers within the database. This approach helped evaluate whether retrieved contexts contributed meaningfully to the response quality when it did not directly answer the question.

III-E

Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation of the experiment was divided into two main aspects: assessing the retrieved contexts and evaluating the responses generated by the chatbot. This dual approach is commonly used when evaluating LLMs with a RAG system, as there is no strict standard for such evaluations
[
33
]
.

The first part of the evaluation focused on how well the retrieval mechanism selected relevant information. To do this, we used Precision@K, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), and Mean Average Precision (MAP)
[
30
]
. Precision@K measures the proportion of relevant documents among the top-K retrieved results and is defined mathematically as:

\text{Precision@K}=\frac{\text{Number of relevant items in top K}}{\text{K}}

This metric was chosen to evaluate how effectively the retrieval system prioritises relevant contexts. To evaluate the ranking of the retrieved contexts, we used MRR, which considers the position of the first relevant document in the ranked list. It is computed as:

\text{MRR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\text{rank}_{i}}

where N represents the number of queries. A higher MRR score indicates that relevant contexts appear earlier in the list. Finally, we used MAP, which evaluates ranking quality across multiple queries by computing the mean of precision values at each relevant document’s rank position. It is defined as:

\text{MAP}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{AP}_{i}

\text{AP}_{i}=\frac{1}{\text{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\text{Precision@j}\cdot\text{relevance}(j)

where AP(i) represents the Average Precision for a given query, which is calculated by averaging the precision at every rank where a relevant document appears. To compute these metrics, we manually reviewed the retrieved contexts for each question, counting how many of the four retrieved results were truly relevant. The order of the retrieved documents was also recorded to determine whether the most relevant ones were retrieved first.

The second part of the experiment focused on evaluating the chatbot responses. This was done using two complementary approaches: an automated metric, BERTScore, and a human evaluation process. BERTScore compares the generated responses with the reference answers using contextual token embeddings. It computes precision, recall, and F1-score
[
35
]
. Precision measures how much of the generated response consists of words that correctly match the meaning of words in the reference answer. Recall, on the other hand, evaluates how much of the reference answer’s meaning is present in the generated response. A high precision score means the generated response is mostly accurate and does not include incorrect information. A high recall score means the generated response contains most of the important details from the reference answer, even if it also includes extra or less precise wording. The F1-score balances both measures as their harmonic mean. Unlike traditional token-based metrics, BERTScore leverages contextual embeddings, making it more suitable for assessing semantic similarity in open-ended responses
[
10
]
.

To complement the automated evaluation, three experts in the Essence framework manually scored each response based on three criteria, relevance, factual accuracy, and completeness, without knowing which model generated the response, as the answers were presented in random order and without attribution
[
1
]
. Relevance measures whether the response directly addresses the question as expected. Factual accuracy ensures that the response contains correct information. Completeness assesses whether the response provides sufficient detail and includes references to Essence, even when the question did not explicitly mention it. Each criterion was rated from 1 (low) to 3 (high). The BERTScore and human evaluation results were analysed separately for the three question categories (Information, Decision-Making, and Translation) and the overall scores across all 30 questions were also calculated.

By combining BERTScore and human evaluation, we aimed to mitigate biases from both methods. BERTScore offers an objective measure based on semantic similarity, while human evaluation provides a qualitative assessment that captures aspects such as reasoning, clarity, and explanatory depth. Human evaluation also allows for a fair assessment of questions that can have multiple correct answers, ensuring that valid but different responses are not unfairly penalised
[
1
]
.

IV
Results

IV-A

RQ1: Information Retrieval Effectiveness

The effectiveness of the chatbot’s information retrieval system was assessed by evaluating the retrieved contexts using three key metrics: Precision@K, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), and Mean Average Precision (MAP). The results of this evaluation, based on the 30 questions used in the experiment, are presented in Table
I
.

Precision@K, which measures the proportion of relevant documents retrieved among the top K results, was found to be 0.731. In our case, K = 4, indicates that, on average, 73.1% of the top four retrieved results were relevant to the given question, typically with three out of the four being considered relevant.

The Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), which evaluates the rank position of the first relevant document in the retrieved list, was measured to be 0.653. This metric accounts for the order in which relevant contexts appear, with higher scores indicating that relevant documents tend to appear earlier in the ranked list. An MRR score of 0.653 suggests that in most cases, the first relevant context was ranked in a relatively high position, either first or second.

The Mean Average Precision (MAP), which captures both the precision at different recall levels and the ranking of relevant documents, was recorded at 0.769. MAP provides a more comprehensive measure of retrieval performance by considering the relevance of all retrieved contexts across the dataset. A score of 0.769 reflects that the retrieval system was effective in not only retrieving relevant contexts but also ranking them appropriately.

RQ2: Models Comparison

IV-B
1
BERTScore Evaluation

To assess the quality of the chatbot-generated responses, we utilised BERTScore, which evaluates the semantic similarity between a generated response and a reference answer. The evaluation considered three key metrics: Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Each metric was computed for all 30 questions and further categorised into three types: Information, Decision-Making, and Translation questions. The experiment was conducted using four different LLMs: Llama 3.3, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude 3.7, both with and without RAG.

Table
II
presents the results of this evaluation. In general, models equipped with RAG demonstrated improved precision, recall and F1 values, indicating that incorporating the retrieved context into the user query enhanced the model’s ability to produce responses with higher semantic similarity to the reference answers.

Among all models with RAG, Claude 3.7 achieved the highest overall F1-score, followed by Llama 3.3, DeepSeek-V3, and finally GPT-4o. Claude also attained the highest precision score, whereas DeepSeek-V3 excelled in recall. Conversely, GPT-4o had the lowest precision score, while Claude had the lowest recall. These results suggest that Claude prioritises generating responses with high accuracy relative to the reference, whereas DeepSeek-V3 captures more of the reference content but at the cost of slightly lower precision.

Examining the models without RAG, Llama 3.3 performed best overall, followed by DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o, and finally Claude 3.7. This is particularly notable since Claude, which ranked the lowest without RAG, became the best-performing model once RAG was introduced. This observation suggests that Claude benefited the most from this technique, demonstrating a significant performance improvement. Conversely, Llama 3.3, which was already the top-performing model without RAG, exhibited the smallest improvement, indicating that its responses were already relatively strong even without external retrieval.

Across all models, Precision increased slightly more than Recall with the inclusion of RAG. This trend implies that retrieval augmentation primarily aids in refining the accuracy of generated responses rather than expanding their completeness. The likely explanation is that retrieved context helps models formulate more precise answers while constraining them to relevant information, thereby limiting over-generation.

For example, Precision decreased for Translation questions in both Llama 3.3 and DeepSeek-V3 when RAG was applied. Additionally, both Precision and Recall slightly declined for Decision-Making questions in Llama 3.3 with RAG compared to its performance without RAG. These anomalies suggest that while RAG generally enhances response quality, it may introduce minor inconsistencies depending on the nature of the question.

Regarding question types, the general trend observed in the BERTScore evaluation was confirmed: Information questions received the highest scores, followed by Decision-Making and Translation. V
Discussion

V-A

Implications

The experimental results confirm that integrating RAG leads to a noticeable improvement in response quality across all tested models. Both BERTScore and human evaluation indicate that models equipped with RAG consistently outperform their non-RAG counterparts. The Precision@4 and MRR values reflect how the system was able to consistently retrieve relevant documents among the top ranked results and the MAP value further demonstrates that the system was effective in maintaining high precision across different recall levels. However, improvements vary across models, with some benefiting more than others. Notably, Claude 3.7 and DeepSeek-V3 showed the greatest improvement and were also the best-performing models according to the BERTScore and human evaluation respectively.

Our results indicate that Information questions consistently achieved the highest scores, followed by Decision-Making and then Translation. This pattern was observed across both automated and human evaluations. Despite overall performance gains, Decision-Making questions exhibited the least improvement following the integration of RAG. The lower performance gain on Decision-Making questions suggests that the models struggled to incorporate Essence principles into subjective or open-ended reasoning tasks. This contrasts with Information and Translation questions, where Essence elements were explicitly mentioned in the query, making it easier for the model to retrieve and utilise relevant knowledge. The improvements in Translation questions with RAG were particularly pronounced, suggesting that retrieval plays a crucial role in helping the model relate Essence terminology to natural language explanations.

A key finding is the emergence of two different
“best”
models depending on the evaluation metric: Claude 3.7 performed best in BERTScore evaluation, while DeepSeek-V3 achieved the highest overall human evaluation scores. Interestingly, both models were initially among the worst performers without RAG but improved the most after its integration. This suggests that RAG is particularly beneficial for models that struggle with domain-specific knowledge, allowing them to significantly enhance their completeness and accuracy. The divergence in which model is considered the best may be due to differences in how they process language and context. Claude 3.7’s answers were likely more similar to the ground truth because it may prioritise generating responses that closely match the wording and phrasing of reference texts, boosting its semantic similarity scores. In contrast, DeepSeek-V3 might produce more interpretative or contextually rich responses that resonate better with human evaluators but differ more from the exact reference wording.

Additionally, the fact that Decision-Making questions benefited the least from RAG across all models suggests that merely retrieving relevant Essence content is insufficient for this kind of question. Unlike Information or Translation tasks, which explicitly require Essence knowledge, Decision-Making questions often require reasoning beyond what is present in the retrieved context. This highlights a potential limitation of RAG when applied to complex decision-based queries.

These findings have several implications for developing AI-powered assistants in software engineering education and practice. First, the effectiveness of RAG in improving chatbot responses suggests that integrating structured knowledge sources can enhance domain-specific LLM applications. However, the variability in improvements across different models indicates that choosing the right base LLM is crucial. Models with weaker intrinsic knowledge may benefit more from RAG, while already strong models may show only marginal improvements.

Furthermore, the difficulty of Decision-Making questions highlights a challenge in designing AI systems that assist with software engineering frameworks like Essence. While RAG helps in retrieving relevant knowledge, reasoning about its application remains a challenge.

Finally, the significant improvements in Translation tasks suggest that RAG can play a critical role in bridging the gap between formal software engineering concepts and their natural language interpretations. This could be particularly useful in educational contexts, where students may benefit from using Essence to relate abstract frameworks to practical implementations.

V-B

Threats to validity

While this study employs both automated and human evaluation methods to assess the quality of chatbot-generated responses, certain limitations must be acknowledged. One key threat to validity is the inherent limitations of the evaluation metrics. Although BERTScore and human assessment were used to complement each other, they each have distinct weaknesses. BERTScore, as an automated metric, captures semantic similarity but does not fully account for factual accuracy or the contextual appropriateness of responses. In contrast, human evaluation, while more nuanced, is inherently subjective and may introduce variability in scoring due to differences in individual judgement. The combination of these methods mitigates some biases but does not eliminate them entirely.

Another limitation stems from the restricted question set and sample size used in the evaluation. The study employed a fixed set of 30 questions, which, while diverse, may not fully represent the range of queries users might pose in real-world applications. Notably, the evaluation did not include follow-up questions, which are common in interactive settings. As a result, the findings may be influenced by the specific characteristics of these questions rather than being generalisable to a broader spectrum of software engineering inquiries. A larger and more diverse dataset would strengthen the reliability of the conclusions drawn.

Furthermore, there is a risk of overfitting to the evaluation dataset. The LLMs assessed in this study may perform well on the predefined set of questions but struggle with novel queries beyond this controlled setting. Since the RAG-enhanced chatbot retrieves information from a fixed set of documents, the responses might reflect patterns that are optimised for the given dataset rather than demonstrating a true improvement in reasoning and adaptability.

VI
Conclusion

This study presented a specialised chatbot that leverages retrieval-augmented generation to support software practitioners and students in understanding and applying the Essence standard. Unlike general-purpose LLMs or applications focused on code generation, our work targets a less explored area: providing automated support for the adoption and tailoring of practices, as well as coordinating and evolving ways of working.

By integrating a curated knowledge base of Essence documents and employing an ensemble retrieval strategy, the chatbot provides contextually rich and accurate responses. Across 30 different queries, the RAG-enhanced chatbot consistently outperformed baseline LLM responses, especially in Information and Translation tasks. These findings highlight the potential of combining LLMs with structured retrieval to bridge the gap between abstract software engineering standards and practical application. While promising, further work is needed to validate the chatbot in real-world educational or professional settings. Future directions include expanding the knowledge base, incorporating user interaction data, and conducting longitudinal studies on metrics such as task completion per sprint or learning outcomes.

This work lays the groundwork for the development of intelligent assistants that can improve software engineering education by enabling students to more intuitively understand software development practices, and by supporting development teams in reflecting on, adapting, and improving their ways of working.
