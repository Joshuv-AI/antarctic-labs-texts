Does the reported learning curve of artificial intelligence–guided point-of-care ultrasound reflect operator competency or study design?
Authors: Thirunavukkarasu Arun Babu, Vijayan Sharmila
Published: 2026

Point-of-care ultrasound is rapidly becoming an integral component of modern bedside medicine, and the integration of artificial intelligence has the potential to be a transformative advance by enabling even non-expert healthcare providers to acquire clinically useful images with minimal training.1,2 We read with great interest the article by Wright et al. evaluating artificial intelligence (AI)–guided point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) performed by non-expert healthcare workers in rural and remote Australia.3 The study addresses an important challenge in contemporary cardiovascular care by exploring whether AI-assisted image acquisition can expand access to echocardiography where expert sonographers are scarce.4 The finding that diagnostic-quality parasternal images were obtained substantially more often than apical views provides valuable insights for future implementation of AI-assisted echocardiography.

However, we believe that the interpretation of the reported learning curve warrants further discussion. The principal conclusion that image quality improved after the first 10 scans and among users performing at least 20 examinations may not solely represent the acquisition of operator competence. Exposure to scanning was highly unequal across only 10 participants, with physicians performing most examinations, and the authors themselves acknowledge substantial collinearity between physician status and scan experience (variance inflation factor 9.1). Although separate multivariable models were presented, the mixed-effects model accounting for operator clustering found that neither scan count nor physician status independently predicted diagnostic image quality. This discrepancy suggests that the observed learning curve may reflect differences in operator background and case distribution rather than cumulative scanning experience alone.5

Another potential source of bias is the non-standardized educational intervention. Sites received additional online mentoring only when requested, and the frequency, duration, and content of this feedback were neither quantified nor incorporated into the analysis. Consequently, some operators may have received substantially greater educational reinforcement than others. This introduces performance variability that cannot be distinguished from genuine learning through repeated scanning. Future studies should prospectively standardize mentorship or treat ongoing feedback as a measurable covariate when modelling competency development.

Patient characteristics also deserve greater consideration when interpreting operator performance. Hypertension and larger body surface area were independently associated with reduced diagnostic image quality, particularly for apical windows. If less experienced operators encountered a greater proportion of technically challenging patients, apparent differences in learning could partly reflect case mix rather than operator proficiency. Risk-adjusted learning curves or cumulative sum analyses incorporating patient complexity would provide a more robust assessment of competency acquisition.

Finally, the study evaluates image acquisition quality rather than downstream diagnostic accuracy or clinical decision-making. Although obtaining interpretable images is an essential prerequisite, implementation of AI-guided POCUS ultimately depends on whether novice-acquired examinations lead to accurate diagnoses, appropriate referrals, and improved patient outcomes. The reported 28% prevalence of echocardiographic abnormalities offers an opportunity for future analyses examining lesion-specific diagnostic performance, particularly for clinically important valvular disease, where AI-guided screening is expected to have the greatest impact.

AI should be viewed as an adjunct to, rather than a substitute for, operator knowledge and clinical reasoning. While AI guidance may facilitate image acquisition by novice users, structured education in ultrasound physics, cardiac anatomy, image interpretation, and the limitations of AI remains essential for safe implementation and appropriate clinical decision-making.5 Furthermore, AI algorithms should be validated within specific disease settings before widespread adoption. Recent studies have demonstrated that automated echocardiographic measurements may exhibit clinically important inaccuracies in conditions such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, illustrating that algorithm performance cannot be assumed to generalize across all patient populations.6,7 Disease-specific validation is therefore necessary to minimize unrecognized errors, prevent silent performance drift, and ensure that AI-assisted POCUS remains reliable across diverse clinical scenarios.

This important study provides encouraging evidence that AI-guided POCUS may help reduce disparities in echocardiographic access. Nevertheless, distinguishing true operator learning from differences in clinical background, mentoring intensity, patient complexity, and the quality of structured pre-implementation training will be essential before defining competency thresholds or recommending widespread task shifting. AI-assisted POCUS should undergo rigorous disease-specific validation to ensure consistent performance across diverse clinical settings before widespread implementation. Addressing these issues in future implementation studies will strengthen the evidence supporting AI-assisted echocardiography in underserved settings.

Lead author biography

Dr Thirunavukkarasu Arun Babu is a Professor of Paediatrics at AIIMS Mangalagiri, India, and a leading advocate for point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in paediatric practice. He is the author of the book titled Pediatric point of care ultrasound. His work centres on integrating bedside ultrasound into routine clinical care, medical education, and research. He has contributed extensively to the development of paediatric POCUS through original research, scholarly publications, book chapters, training programmes, and institutional implementation initiatives. His primary interests include critical care ultrasound, lung ultrasound, haemodynamic assessment, procedural guidance, and the broader application of POCUS to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes in children.
