Strengthening Prehospital Emergency Care in Somalia: Current Barriers and Priorities for System Reform
Authors: Hassan Adan Ali Adan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective prehospital emergency care is a core component of an emergency care system because assessment, initial stabilization, communication, and transport begin before definitive hospital treatment. In Somalia, prehospital services remain fragmented, with substantial reliance on informal transport and limited access to trained responders, standardized protocols, dispatch, and coordinated referral. Somalia-specific reports have documented major gaps in organized prehospital care, including limited ambulance capacity and a lack of a unified national system. This Perspective examines these barriers and proposes a pragmatic pathway for reform that prioritizes governance, workforce development, community first response, dispatch and communication, ambulance standards, and linkage with receiving hospitals. Rather than proposing an expensive high-resource model, the paper advocates phased development beginning with context-appropriate first-responder and basic ambulance-provider training, followed by regulated expansion of advanced prehospital care as workforce, financing, and clinical governance mature. Existing Somali experience with WHO-supported emergency-care training provides a foundation for local capacity building, while regional experience from Kenya illustrates the value of policy, regulation, data, and coordinated dispatch. Mobile communication technologies may offer a feasible bridge where conventional dispatch infrastructure is limited. The immediate priority should be to establish a nationally coordinated, measurable, and sustainable prehospital system that connects communities, responders, ambulances, and hospitals.Keywords: prehospital care, emergency medical services, Somalia, trauma, emergency medical technicians, health systems strengthening

Introduction: The Prehospital Gap and the Somali Context

Emergency care is a continuum that begins at the scene of illness or injury, continues during transport, and extends through emergency-unit and early inpatient care. The World Health Organization (WHO) Emergency Care System Framework therefore treats prehospital functions as integral to the wider health system rather than as an isolated ambulance service.1,2

The traditional “golden hour” concept has helped emphasize the importance of early trauma care, but a fixed 60-minute threshold should not be interpreted as a universal rule for every injured patient. Evidence suggests that the relationship between prehospital time and outcome varies according to injury severity, physiology, and the interventions provided during the prehospital interval. The more useful policy message is that time-sensitive threats should be recognized and treated as early as possible while avoiding unnecessary delays to definitive care.3,4

Somalia faces a particularly challenging prehospital environment because conflict-related injuries, road traffic injuries, mass-casualty incidents, geographic inequity, limited resources, and fragmented emergency services intersect. WHO reports have described the absence of an effective nationwide prehospital system and documented that, after emergencies in Mogadishu, patients may be transported using private vehicles or carried by relatives. In 2022, WHO reported that Aamin Ambulance was one of only a small number of prehospital providers in the country and that its service was concentrated in Mogadishu.5,6

Importantly, the Somali system is not starting from zero. WHO-supported basic emergency care training in Somalia has already produced national trainers and trained hundreds of frontline health workers, demonstrating that locally delivered emergency care education is feasible. WHO has also supported work toward strengthening emergency services and trauma response in Somalia.7,8

This article is a narrative Perspective rather than a systematic review or an empirical study. It draws on Somalia-specific reports from WHO, peer-reviewed literature on prehospital and trauma-system development in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and regional policy experience, particularly from Kenya. The sources were selected to anchor the principal claims about system gaps, workforce development, governance, communication, and feasible reform options; no formal systematic search, meta-analysis, or primary data collection was undertaken. The purpose is to translate this evidence into a practical Somali reform agenda.9–12

Current Barriers to Effective Prehospital Care in Somalia

Fragmented Service Delivery and Limited Coverage

The most fundamental problem is fragmentation. Somalia lacks a unified prehospital system that consistently links emergency activation, dispatch, trained response, transport, receiving-facility communication, and quality assurance. WHO has described the absence of an effective prehospital system beyond Mogadishu and the limited availability of organized trauma response. Such fragmentation increases dependence on informal transport and means that access to timely prehospital intervention can vary substantially according to location, security conditions, and the availability of individual providers.5,6,9

Workforce and Training

An ambulance is not, by itself, a prehospital care system. Effective services require personnel with defined competencies, clinical protocols, supervision, continuing education, and mechanisms for certification and regulation. LMIC literature repeatedly identifies shortages of trained responders, limited continuing education, inadequate equipment, weak coordination, and inconsistent clinical protocols as major barriers to prehospital care.9,10

Somalia already has a useful platform on which to build. The WHO-supported Basic Emergency Care program trained 354 frontline health workers and established 16 provisional national trainers in 2020–2021. This experience suggests that a phased national prehospital curriculum could be developed through Somali universities, professional societies, hospitals, and the Ministry of Health, rather than relying indefinitely on externally delivered training.7

Dispatch, Communication, and Hospital Coordination

Prehospital care depends on more than moving a patient. A functioning system must allow the public or a first responder to activate assistance, dispatchers to identify and prioritize incidents, responders to communicate with each other, and receiving facilities to prepare for incoming patients. WHO guidance identifies governance, communication, referral, and information exchange as essential components of prehospital systems.1,2

For Somalia, a conventional high-cost emergency call center should not be considered the only possible starting point. A phased model could combine a nationally recognized emergency access number with radio communication and mobile-phone-based dispatch, using simple location and incident information before more sophisticated digital systems are introduced. This approach is consistent with emerging LMIC experience showing that mobile technologies can support dispatch and coordination where conventional infrastructure is limited.2,12

Transport, Infrastructure, and Security Constraints

Mogadishu’s traffic congestion, road conditions, security requirements, and variable access to facilities can delay transport and complicate conventional ambulance deployment. However, the solution should not be reduced to acquiring more large ambulances. Fleet composition, dispatch location, road access, referral destinations, maintenance, fuel, staffing, and security procedures must be planned together. In selected urban areas, smaller response vehicles or motorcycles may be considered as rapid first-response platforms, provided that their scope, safety, equipment, supervision, and referral arrangements are formally defined.2,9,12

Hemorrhage and Other Time-Sensitive Threats

The prehospital interval is clinically important when immediately reversible threats are present. Severe external hemorrhage, airway compromise, respiratory failure, and shock can deteriorate before hospital arrival if no trained responder is available. Evidence from LMIC settings emphasizes the importance of context-appropriate hemorrhage control and early recognition and management of life-threatening conditions.10,13

From “Transport” to an Integrated Prehospital System

The central policy shift should be from viewing prehospital care as transportation to viewing it as a coordinated clinical and public-health function. The WHO framework identifies essential functions at the scene, during transport, and at the receiving facility. Somalia can adapt these functions to its resource and security context without reproducing the cost structure of high-income EMS systems.1,2

A practical model would have four linked layers: community recognition and first aid; basic ambulance response; advanced prehospital care where feasible; and coordinated receiving-facility care. The first two layers should be developed first because they are likely to provide the broadest coverage at comparatively lower cost. Advanced interventions should be introduced only when adequate training, clinical governance, equipment, supervision, and medical oversight are available.2,12,14

Strategic Priorities for Somalia

Establish Governance and Regulation First

The Federal Ministry of Health and relevant state authorities should define EMS as a component of the national emergency-care system and establish minimum standards for ambulance providers, personnel, equipment, clinical protocols, dispatch, documentation, and referral. A regulatory pathway should define scopes of practice and credentialing for community first responders, basic ambulance providers/EMTs, and advanced providers. Governance should include representation from government, hospitals, professional societies, training institutions, ambulance operators, and community stakeholders.1,9,11

Build a Phased Workforce

Rather than immediately attempting to create a large paramedic workforce, Somalia could begin with standardized basic ambulance-provider training based on WHO-compatible competencies, including scene safety, patient assessment, basic airway and breathing support, bleeding control, shock recognition, splinting, safe transport, handover, and communication. The WHO Basic Ambulance Provider course provides a useful reference for such a foundation. Advanced training can then be introduced for selected providers as systems for medical oversight, credentialing, equipment, medications, and quality assurance become established.2,14

Make Community First Response Part of the System

Community first responders can provide the earliest lifesaving actions while formal EMS capacity is developing. Training should focus on a small number of high-impact skills recognition of emergencies, bleeding control, basic airway support, cardiopulmonary resuscitation where appropriate, safe movement, and activation of emergency services. Police, security personnel, transport workers, community volunteers, and other groups routinely present at high-risk locations could be prioritized. Such programs should be linked to formal EMS rather than operating as isolated projects.12,15

Develop Dispatch and Referral Coordination

Somalia should establish a nationally recognized emergency access mechanism and a simple dispatch architecture that can expand over time. A minimum system should record the caller’s location, event type, patient priority, responding unit, destination, and key clinical information. Receiving hospitals should have a defined pre-alert process so that resuscitation, surgery, blood services, or other time-sensitive resources can be mobilized before arrival.1,2,11

Finance for Sustainability, Not Only Start-Up

The principal feasibility challenge is sustainability. Ambulance procurement without recurrent funding for fuel, maintenance, staffing, training, communications, equipment replacement, and supervision risks creating nonfunctional fleets. Somalia should therefore develop a costed EMS package with clearly assigned budget responsibilities, while allowing regulated partnerships with nongovernmental and private providers. External partners can support start-up capacity, but long-term service standards and financing should progressively be embedded within national and subnational health planning.1,11,12

Measure Performance and Learn Locally

A small national EMS dataset should be introduced from the beginning. Core indicators could include emergency call volume, dispatch time, response time, scene time, transport time, proportion of patients receiving defined prehospital interventions, pre-alert frequency, destination, and short-term outcomes where feasible. These indicators would allow Somalia to identify bottlenecks, compare service models, and justify future investment. The system should also support operational research on what works in different security, geographic, and urban contexts.1,2,9,12

A Phased Reform Roadmap

Reform should proceed incrementally, beginning with governance, minimum standards, basic ambulance-provider training, community first response, and essential communication and referral systems. Subsequent phases should strengthen dispatch, infrastructure, equipment, quality assurance, and data systems, with advanced prehospital care introduced as training, regulation, financing, and clinical governance capacity develops. Conclusion

Somalia’s prehospital emergency-care gap is a health-system problem that cannot be solved by ambulances alone. The priority is to build a coordinated pathway connecting communities, trained responders, dispatch, transport, and receiving hospitals. A feasible reform strategy should begin with governance, basic provider training, community first response, communication, standardized equipment, and measurable quality indicators, followed by carefully regulated expansion of advanced prehospital care. The country already has experience in emergency-care training and trauma-system strengthening on which to build. A phased, locally governed approach offers a realistic route toward a more reliable prehospital system that is responsive to Somalia’s security, financial, workforce, and infrastructure constraints.
