Threat appraisal, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking: a study on the behavior of not eating wild mushrooms in China
Authors: Si Chen, HuiJie Zou, Han Liu, Zhenyi Li, Juan Du, Ning Xu, Jiani Li, Yibo Wu, Fangmin Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe applied and extended the Protection Motivation Theory to investigate the predictive factors of not eating wild mushrooms to find ways to prevent food poisoning accidents.MethodsA total of 322 residents from poisoning risk areas in China completed a self-administered questionnaire based on Protection Motivation Theory, assessing perceived susceptibility, rewards, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking. Data were analyzed using the partial least squares method.ResultsPerceived susceptibility (β = 0.221, P < 0.001), self-efficacy (β = 0.194, P < 0.001), and risk information seeking (β = 0.245, P < 0.001) were positively correlated with the intention of not eating wild mushrooms. In contrast, intrinsic rewards (β=-0.168, P = 0.039) were negatively correlated with the intention of not eating wild mushrooms. ConclusionPerceptual susceptibility, intrinsic rewards, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking to predict the behavioral intention of not eating wild mushrooms. The results of this study support the theory of protection motivation and its extended applicability, providing evidence that public health crises can promote changes in resident behavior related to food safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25459-1.Keywords: Wild mushrooms, Threat appraisal, Perceived rewards, Self-efficacy, Risk information seeking

Introduction

Poisonous fungi (poisonous mushrooms) are increasingly harmful to human health and have become a prominent global public health problem [1]. Poisonous fungi refer to the species in which the fruiting bodies of large fungi produce toxic reactions to people, livestock, and poultry after eating [2]. Edible and poisonous mushrooms are very similar in macroscopic characteristics, so they are easily confused in the wild [3]. This poses a significant threat to countries and regions that rely heavily on wild mushrooms for food. Coping measures are also noticeable, including analyzing the epidemiological characteristics of wild mushroom poisoning events [4–6], identifying suspected wild mushrooms, and detecting toxins [7, 8]. Many European countries have formulated laws and regulations to ensure the edible safety of wild mushrooms [9]. In addition, many scholars believe that the most effective way to avoid wild mushroom poisoning is to prevent the public from eating wild mushrooms [10]. To this end, the China Municipal Government has repeatedly reminded people not to eat wild mushrooms by setting up warning signs, posting posters, and hanging propaganda slogans and loudspeakers in towns, villages, markets, restaurants, and mountain entrances [11]. With the gradual change in knowledge about the risk of poisonous mushrooms, residents may have the motivation to choose not to eat wild fungi to protect their health. However, it is not clear what factors prompted residents to give up eating wild mushrooms.

It is a healthy behavior not to eat wild mushrooms. Health behavior refers to the behaviors that individuals take to prevent diseases and maintain their health, including changing health risk behaviors and adopting positive health behaviors [12]. Only the behavior intervention based on theory can explain the individual’s problem behavior more scientifically, diagnose the individual’s behavior, and formulate the corresponding intervention plan [13]. However, at present, the research on eating poisonous mushrooms by mistake mostly stays in the epidemiological stage, lacking the guidance of healthy behavior theory, and behavioral intervention overemphasizes the management and propaganda role of the government, lacking systematic empirical research on the mechanism and process of influencing the main behavior. In addition, most of the previous interventions on healthy eating behavior were in developed countries such as Europe and America. Whether the intervention strategies can be applied and effective in other cultural situations needs more research to verify [14]. For the development and perfection of the theory, it is very important to empirically test the validity of the theoretical model in different cultural backgrounds and people.

Therefore, this study attempts to explore the predictive factors that affect residents’ willingness not to eat wild mushrooms in China. There are many theories of healthy behavior, and the protective motivation theory is adopted in this study because it is the theoretical basis for the research of health activities promotion and healthy eating behavior [15, 16], and this theory is often used to study the choice of protective eating, and other similar healthy behavior theories do not directly measure the threat assessment [17]. Threat appraisal can explain how people seek self-protection (willingness) from harmful or stressful lives [18]. Threat appraisal and self-efficacy are important components of the Protection Motivation Theory. In addition, it is considered that risk information seeking is an important predictor in the field of food safety [19, 20]. However, previous studies did not integrate threat appraisal, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking. Therefore, this study will expand the Protection Motivation Theory, increase the risk information seeking, and provide empirical evidence for explaining the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

This study systematically examines threat appraisal, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking by providing empirical data on the cultural background of China. First of all, since few scholars have studied how Chinese people view the risks associated with food poisoning, we are one of the first studies to provide new insights into how people view the risks associated with eating wild mushrooms. These findings advance our understanding of risk perception by providing first-hand data in the context of China because, in most existing literature, risk perception and health behavior are mainly constructed in the Western context. Secondly, we identified the determinants of healthy behavior, such as perceived susceptibility, perceived intrinsic rewards, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking. Thirdly, we extend the theoretical discussion of healthy behavior to the background of China and emphasize the unique social and cultural characteristics of the collective behavior of eating wild mushrooms in China. It is helpful to the theoretical development of Protection Motivation Theory and risk information seeking. At the same time, this study provides some important practical insights for health and food safety risk management institutions and practitioners.

Theoretical background

The relationship between threat appraisal, self-efficacy, and not eating wild mushrooms

Rogers put forward the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), which conceptualizes the motivation of individuals to participate in protective behavior under the threat of stimulation [21]. The decision whether to participate in protective behavior is first controlled by two different cognitive processes: threat appraisal and coping appraisal [22]. The theory of protection motivation provides a framework to understand whether people perceive the threat first and whether they believe that they can fight this threat, rather than focusing on the reasons for their feeding behavior. This perception of effectiveness is very important because if people don’t believe that this kind of behavior is effective, they usually don’t establish protective behavior [21]. Taking protective actions against health threats depends on the individual’s self-protection motivation, and people will form protective motivation decisions through the evaluation of threat assessment [23]. When the public perceives that individuals’ lives and health are vulnerable to food safety problems, a higher degree of threat appraisal can enhance their protection motivation. Threat appraisal, an assessment process, will affect people’s motivation to protect themselves from specific threats [24]. The introduction of threat appraisal into this study will promote the subconscious assessment of the feeding behavior of wild mushrooms, because people generally do not calculate risks and benefits rationally and cannot protect themselves from threats, especially for wild mushroom poisoning, which may focus more on explaining the irrationality of protection behavior and the importance of more subjective factors.

Threat appraisal refers to the assessment of unhealthy behaviors or diseases [21, 24]. Threat appraisal includes three important elements: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and perceived rewards, which are considered the antecedents of individual adaptive actions [23]. According to this theory, the perception of high susceptibility and severity will trigger individuals to take risk-prevention actions [23].

In this study, we investigated residents’ threat appraisal of eating wild mushrooms. People think that the more serious the threat of a certain behavior to their health, and feel that they are vulnerable to this threat, the more likely they are to take health protection actions [25]. Some scholars combined the Suan Tang Zi accident with threat appraisal and found that the perceived severity and susceptibility have a significant positive impact on consumers’ food safety risk prevention [26].

Perceived severity refers to the individual’s belief in the seriousness of the threat, and whether people perceive the threat first depends on the perception of the seriousness of the threat [24]. Jan et al. used threat appraisal to determine consumers’ food shopping, food safety, and online food distribution behavior during COVID-19 and found that the perceived severity significantly predicted hand hygiene habits after shopping [27]. As the public’s awareness of the risks associated with contact with contaminated surfaces and COVID-19 infection increases, they wash their hands and food packaging after shopping and wash their hands more frequently before eating or preparing food [28]. To sum up, we predict that the more residents perceive that eating wild poisonous mushrooms by mistake is a threat to their health, the more likely they are to refuse to eat wild mushrooms.

Perceived susceptibility refers to an individual’s estimation of their chances of experiencing threats [24]. Threats should not only be regarded as a serious problem, but also be recognized that individuals themselves are vulnerable to threats. When individuals don’t feel that they may face a real threat, they have no incentive to protect themselves, even if the threat itself is very serious. A person’s protection opportunities are enhanced by their susceptibility to the threat of food safety scandals [29]. Runge and others believe that susceptibility is an important variable in preventing alcoholism among the elderly [30]. In the aspect of eating wild mushrooms, this means that even if individuals think that eating poisonous mushrooms by mistake is a serious problem, they still need to feel vulnerable to the threat of eating poisonous mushrooms by mistake before taking protective actions. Perceived rewards refer to the perceived rewards of maintaining practices that are currently considered risky, including intrinsic rewards and extrinsic rewards. Intrinsic rewards refer to the positive influence of perceived behavior; Extrinsic rewards refer to the perceived positive social reaction or consequence [31]. High severity and susceptibility promote healthy behaviors, but it is known that high reward perception will reduce risk-prevention behaviors [23]. In the research of Penny Peng and others, it is found that increasing susceptibility and severity and reducing intrinsic rewards and extrinsic rewards are conducive to the formation of protective motivation and the promotion of individual healthy behavior [32]. MacDonell et al. found that the intrinsic rewards and extrinsic rewards in threat appraisal factors are significantly related to teenagers’ smoking intention [33]. A study on tobacco use among Iranian teenagers also shows that there is a significant negative correlation between intrinsic rewards and tobacco use [34]. In this study, intrinsic rewards refer to the positive physiological and psychological effects perceived by residents when they eat wild mushrooms, such as eating wild mushrooms is delicious. Extrinsic rewards refer to the positive social reaction that residents feel when they eat wild mushrooms. For example, eating wild mushrooms can help me make more friends. Intrinsic rewards and extrinsic rewards will weaken the individual’s perception of threats. Therefore, this study puts forward the following assumptions:

H3: Intrinsic rewards were negatively associated with the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

H3: Intrinsic rewards were negatively associated with the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

H4: Extrinsic rewards were negatively associated with the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

Coping appraisal is described as an individual’s ability to perform protective actions in the presence of threats [35]. The main factor of coping appraisal is self-efficacy. Some scholars believe that self-efficacy is the most powerful predictor of intention [36]. Self-efficacy is defined as the perceived ability of an individual to perform recommended behavior [22]. This is an important predictor of health prevention behavior [37]. The higher a person’s sense of self-efficacy, the more active their efforts to take protective actions [36]. Laato et al. found that perceived severity and self-efficacy are the antecedents of non-purchase intention [38]. Therefore, this study puts forward the following assumptions:

H5: Self-efficacy was negatively associated with the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

Influence of risk information seeking on not eating wild mushrooms

Correct healthy eating behavior originates from a reasonable decision-making process and is influenced by many factors. In addition to several elements contained in the Protection Motivation Theory, risk information seeking also plays an important role. In addition, the theory of protective motivation is an open theory, which allows different disciplines to increase and expand other variables according to the specific situation of the research object.

Risk information seeking refers to the way individuals seek information about their health, diseases, and various health risks, as well as health protection behavior [39]. When individuals feel threatened by potential harm and are uncertain about the course of action, they will participate in the information-seeking process [40]. Researchers have studied individuals’ risk information seeking for specific environmental or health hazards, such as eating contaminated fish from the Great Lakes [20] and participating in cancer clinical trials [41]. Previous studies have shown that there is a correlation between information-seeking and health behavior [42]. For example, in the study on the influence of unvaccinated black Americans’ willingness to vaccinate, it is found that risk information seeking has a significant influence on their willingness to vaccinate, and they are more likely to vaccinate [43]. Regarding the risk information seeking of wild mushrooms, previous studies have found that for 84% of mushroom pickers, the main source of knowledge about wild mushrooms is their parents, 26% of mushroom pickers learn about wild mushrooms from wild mushroom maps, and the rest obtain knowledge about wild mushrooms through friends, the Internet, and other means [44]. Zhili Xiao and others found through a questionnaire survey among residents in Hunan Province that at present, residents mainly obtain popular science information about wild mushroom poisoning through “propaganda slogans or posters” (66.82%), “mobile phone information such as WeChat and small videos” (66.88%) and “word of mouth from relatives and friends” (61.95%) [45]. Up to 90% of the respondents believe that it is necessary to publicize and educate the knowledge of wild mushroom poisoning [46]. At the same time, the government often played a very important role in the prevention and control of wild mushroom poisoning in China [47]. The main source of risk information measured in this study is the propaganda and popular science activities organized by the government to prevent wild mushroom poisoning. To sum up, this study puts forward the following assumptions:

H6: Risk information seeking was positively associated with the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms.

Materials and methods

Subject investigated

The inclusion criteria for the participants in this study were: ① Age is greater than or equal to 18 years old; ② Having the nationality of the People’s Republic of China; ③ As a resident population; ④ voluntarily participate in the study and fill out the informed consent form; ⑤ The questionnaire can be completed by itself or with the help of the investigator; ⑥ understands the meaning of each item of the questionnaire. The exclusion criteria for this study were: ① confusion and mental abnormalities; ② cognitive impairment; ③ participating in other similar studies; ④ unwilling to collaborate. All the participants signed a written informed consent form and voluntarily accepted the survey for this study. The study complied with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki [48]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China National Center for Food Safety Risk Assessment (Record number: [2022] 050).

Data source

The study was designed and organized by researchers working in collaboration with the China National Center for Food Safety Risk Assessment (CFSA). The self-report data were collected from several cities in China from June 2022 to November 2022, including Ganzhou District of Zhangye City, Gansu Province, Chengxian County of Gansu Province, Lichuan City, and Xuanen County of Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Hubei Province. The study used a three-stage cluster sampling method and conducted stratified sampling by street. In the first stage of sampling, the first provinces at risk of poisonous mushroom poisoning were found from the Chinese foodborne disease surveillance and reporting system, and Gansu and Hubei provinces were randomly selected with the probability proportional to the scale. In the second stage of sampling, according to the counties and districts with many toxic mushroom poisoning incidents announced by the food safety committees and health commissions of Hubei and Gansu provinces, Ganzhou District of Zhangye City and Cheng County of Longnan City and Lichuan City, and Xuanen County of Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture of Hubei Province were randomly selected. In the third stage of sampling, two interviewers visited each selected village or neighborhood in street order, assisted by local government staff, and households were selected using a compact segmented sampling method. If the family was empty, the interviewer would revisit it. Such attempts were made up to three times before respondents were treated as non-responders. We would not replace non-responders or non-contributors. This sampling process ensured that families/respondents were equally selected, which means that unbiased results could be estimated without access weights.

The collected questionnaires were screened according to the following principles: (1) the missing key variables were deleted; (2) the whole page of the questionnaire was deleted; (3) the forward terms and the reverse terms were deleted, and 322 valid questionnaires were obtained.

Regarding the sample size, the minimum sample size was determined using G * Power software. Because the model contains six predictors (for behavioral intention to not eat wild mushrooms), the effect size was set to moderate (0.15) with a required efficacy of 0.80 and an A level of 0.05. Based on the calculation results, each group should contain 98 respondents. Therefore, the total sample size of 142 recovered and 180 recovered from Gansu and Hubei amounted to 322 was sufficient for data analysis. The final sample consisted of 148 females (46%) and 174 males (54%), respectively, and other demographic data are shown in Table 1.

Measuring tool

Using a questionnaire as a survey tool, mainly based on the framework of protective motivation theory, it mainly included four core contents, namely perceived threat, perceived reward, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking. All questionnaires were prepared by the research group based on the review of the literature and the previous study. To ensure the validity of the surface and content of the questionnaire, we invited seven experts from the fields of health management, epidemiology, psychology, and communication to review the questionnaire three times. After completing the questionnaire design, 50 subjects were invited to conduct a small-scale pre-experiment, and the scale content was streamlined and improved according to the pre-test results.

Perceived threats

According to the scale of Lin [31] and others, the perceived threat was designed. The perceived threat included two dimensions: perceived susceptibility and perceived severity. Perceived severity was measured by three items: “People who eat wild mushrooms are more likely to be poisoned than those who don’t pick and eat wild mushrooms”, “People who eat wild mushrooms are more likely to die than those who don’t pick and eat wild mushrooms” and “People who often eat wild mushrooms are more likely to be poisoned”. Perceived susceptibility was measured by three items: “If I eat wild mushrooms, I will be poisoned”, “If I eat wild mushrooms, my health will be threatened” and “If I eat wild mushrooms, I may die”; Likert 5 was used to score the measurement, and the options ranged from very different (1 point) to very agree (5 points). The higher the score, the stronger the perceived susceptibility and severity. As shown in Fig. 2.

Related perceived threats items in the questionnaire

Perceived rewards

According to the scale of Lin [31] and others, the perceived reward was designed, which included two dimensions: intrinsic rewards and extrinsic rewards. Intrinsic rewards were measured by three items: “Wild mushrooms are delicious and I like to eat them”, “Wild mushrooms are nutritious and good for my health” and “Picking wild mushrooms can bring me happiness”; Extrinsic rewards were measured by three items: “Eating wild mushrooms can make more friends”, “Eating wild mushrooms can make my family happy” and “People who eat wild mushrooms live happier lives than those who don’t eat wild mushrooms”. Likert 5 was used to score the measurement, and the options ranged from very different (1 point) to very agree (5 points). The higher the score, the higher the perceived intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. As shown in Fig. 3.

Related perceived rewards items in the questionnaire

Risk information seeking

According to the interview results and literature [49], risk information seeking was measured by three items: “You were willing to take the initiative to learn about activities to prevent wild mushroom poisoning”, “You will support activities to prevent wild mushroom poisoning”, and “You can participate in activities to prevent wild mushroom poisoning”. Likert 5 was used to score the measurement, and the options ranged from very different (1 point) to very agree (5 points). The higher the score, the more active the respondents were in seeking risk-seeking information. As shown in Fig. 4.

Related risk information-seeking items in the questionnaire

Self-efficacy

According to the interview results and literature [50], Self-efficacy was measured by three items: “If I don’t want to eat wild mushrooms, no one can convince me”, “Even if everyone around me eats wild mushrooms, it doesn’t mean I want to eat wild mushrooms” and “Even if my relatives and friends let me eat wild mushrooms, I can refuse”. Likert 5 was used to score the measurement, and the options ranged from very different (1 point) to very agree (5 points). The higher the score, the higher the individual’s self-efficacy. As shown in Fig. 5.

Related self-efficacy items in the questionnaire

The willingness not to eat wild mushrooms was the dependent variable of this study. It was measured by three items: “I plan to eat fewer wild mushrooms in the next five years”, “I plan to continue to eat wild mushrooms in the next five years”, and “I plan not to eat wild mushrooms shortly”. Likert 5 was used to score the measurement, and the options ranged from very different (1 point) to very agree (5 points), with the second question scored in reverse. The higher the score, the stronger the willingness of the respondents not to eat wild mushrooms. As shown in Fig. 6.

Related “The willingness not to eat wild mushrooms” items in the questionnaire

Data processing

As this study was a cross-sectional study with a small sample size, the partial least squares method (partial least squares, PLS), which required low sample numbers and had no special requirements for data distribution, was selected as the study method. In addition, because the PLS method could effectively overcome the collinearity problem among the observed variables, it could remove the effect of the unprofitable noise of the regression, which could make the model more robust [51]. The method used a series of unary or multivariate linear regression iterative solutions [52]. It could deal with the index structure at the same time, which had a good application effect in the comprehensive evaluation of small sample data [53]. To achieve empirical data analysis, the software SmartPLS 3.0 was used to perform reliability and validity tests on scales, including consistency reliability, aggregate validity, and discriminative validity tests. In terms of model and hypothesis testing, we applied partial least squares (PLS) and Bootstrapping algorithms.

Structural model analysis

Measurement model

According to the suggestion of Hair and others, the multicollinearity problem was evaluated. All the variance inflation factor (VIF) values were lower than the recommended value of 5, showing no signs of collinearity problems, Table 8. Next, the model fit was assessed by evaluating the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) [59]. As the SRMR value for this research model was 0.063, lower than the threshold value of 0.08, it could be concluded that the model was a reasonable model fit.

Discussion

Analysis of the characteristics of each variable

The study found that residents’ perceived severity, self-efficacy, and willingness not to eat wild mushrooms have significant differences in education. Generally speaking, residents with higher education levels tend to show higher levels of perceived severity, self-efficacy, and willingness to avoid eating wild mushrooms. This may be because lower educational levels may lead residents to be less likely to pay attention to or engage with health information [60]. Previous studies have also found that most people who eat wild poisonous mushrooms by mistake are people over 50 years old in rural areas. They have a low level of education, rely on their own life experience, and are reluctant to change their eating habits [5]. However, people with high academic qualifications may have more information about food safety and healthy eating, and are more likely to perceive the health threat caused by eating wild mushrooms than people with low academic qualifications, and will exercise stronger control over risky behaviors and adopt more proactive coping strategies. Therefore, according to the current results, it is necessary to design intervention measures to improve the cognition of people with low education levels.

In addition, the scores of perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy, risk information seeking, and willingness not to eat wild mushrooms in Enshi, Hubei Province, were higher than those in Gansu Province. This may be because Enshi Prefecture, Hubei Province has taken various measures to prevent eating poisonous mushrooms by mistake in recent years, such as using monitoring and evaluation platforms and reporting to medical institutions for monitoring; Monthly epidemic situation analysis and risk assessment and all-round, multi-level and three-dimensional health education were carried out during the period of frequent wild mushroom poisoning, which improved the risk awareness of residents to some extent [11]. Moreover, Enshi Prefecture was selected as the comprehensive experimental area of food safety and nutrition health of the National Health and Wellness Commission in 2021 [61]. This can not only promote the implementation of relevant measures but also take advantage of the national pilot zone initiative to integrate various resources and cultivate among consumers the habit of avoiding wild mushroom consumption. This shows the importance of the government’s attention to the prevention of wild poisonous mushrooms in various regions. Therefore, strengthening government regulation of food safety plays a crucial role in improving individual participation in healthy behaviors.

Analysis of influencing factors on behavior willingness to not pick or eat wild mushrooms

The regression analysis showed that perceived susceptibility has a significant positive effect on the intention to avoid eating wild mushrooms. In other words, the higher the perceived probability of mistakenly eating poisonous mushrooms, the stronger the intention to refrain from eating them. This may reflect residents’ own evaluation of the risks and benefits of eating wild mushrooms. Personal knowledge of risk is beneficial for explaining and predicting human health behaviors [62]. In contrast to previous studies, Plutnikov et al. found that perceived susceptibility did not influence health behaviors among cardiac patients. Instead, the same study reported an association between perceived susceptibility and adopting a low-fat diet, possibly because a low-fat diet is an easier behavior to adopt [63]. In this study area, eating wild mushrooms has been a local tradition and cultural practice for thousands of years. Many eaters have little chance of eating poisonous mushrooms due to the experience of identifying poisonous mushrooms handed down from their ancestors. Therefore, future health education should emphasize that changes in the environment and mushroom species have made ancestral identification experience unreliable. It is necessary to reshape previous beliefs about safely eating wild mushrooms.

The relationship between perceived severity and behavioral intention was not significant, consistent with the conclusions of Zahra’s study on fall protection behavior in older adults, and Zahra suggested that the reason for this result may be a low level of perceived severity, that people knew what to do but were not motivated to do it [64]. Another explanation could be that a high perceived threat may produce different outcomes, and in some cases, increased perceived severity leads to preventive behavior, but in others to rejection and denial [23].

It was also found that intrinsic rewards significantly negatively influenced the willingness not to eat wild mushrooms. That is, individuals obtain positive effects and intrinsic benefits in the process of eating wild mushrooms. For example, the greater the emotional value they attach to eating wild mushrooms, the less likely they are to intend to avoid them. This negative effect is similar to the existing results [34]. Yan et al. also found that although students were fully aware of the dangers of smoking, they were not willing to quit because they liked the comfort of smoking and believed that smoking brings many intrinsic benefits [65]. The effect of extrinsic rewards on willingness not to eat was not significant, consistent with the findings of Lin et al. [31]. Therefore, correcting residents’ misconceptions about the positive effects of eating wild mushrooms may be more effective than an extrinsic reward intervention derived from eating wild mushrooms.

Thirdly, risk information seeking significantly positively affects the behavioral intention not to eat wild mushrooms, which indicates that the more individuals actively seek information about poisonous mushrooms, the more likely they are to have the behavioral intention not to eat wild mushrooms. These findings are consistent with the previous studies [66, 67]. And risk information seeking is the strongest influencing factor for the willingness not to eat. According to the current results, we should enhance the risk information-seeking ability of residents in the risk areas with toxic mushroom poisoning. In particular, acquiring knowledge about poisonous mushrooms requires considerable cognitive effort and access to information that is not always readily available. For example, someone might want more information about the dangers of poisonous mushrooms and how to deal with them. However, collecting this information often requires travel to county governments or hospitals and interpretation of bureaucratic forms and medical data sheets, tasks that may hinder access to information, especially in mushroom poisoning risk areas, mostly in rural areas. Therefore, local governments could deliver information about poisonous mushrooms directly to villages in simple and easy-to-understand formats.

This study also showed that self-efficacy was positively associated with not eating wild mushrooms. Self-efficacy involves the perceived ability of individuals to exhibit coping responses. A higher level of self-efficacy helps to improve the intention of not eating wild mushrooms. These findings are consistent with previous studies [68]. These studies have also employed the PMT as their theoretical framework. The individual’s belief in his ability to perform these behaviors appears to be a prerequisite for the intention to adopt such behavior [69]. Eating wild mushrooms is a culture and custom in many parts of China, while China is a collectivist society, where individuals rely on and listen to the opinions of groups or families. When facing relatives and friends, individuals who remain determined not to eat wild mushrooms are more likely to successfully maintain this behavior. In future prevention and control activities, the influence of Chinese collective culture on individual self-efficacy should be taken into account, so that residents can learn communication skills when refusing people around them to persuade them to eat wild mushrooms and enhance their self-confidence and control to resist the pressure of culture and customs.

Conclusion

This paper introduces the core concept of protective motivation theory into the study of protective behavior in a healthy diet, expands the application field of protective motivation theory, combines protective motivation and risk information seeking, and enhances the explanatory power of protective motivation theory in health behavior research. Furthermore, this study examines conservation motivation and risk information seeking by highlighting a very unique cultural context in which people pick and eat wild mushrooms as part of a cultural habit, and where wild mushroom poisoning has been identified with high fatality rates in certain parts of China in the past decades. To understand the determinants of risk information seeking and intent to change this behavior, we validated several important influencing factors, such as threat appraisal, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking. The results found that perceived susceptibility, intrinsic rewards, self-efficacy, and risk information seeking could predict the behavioral intention of residents to not consume wild mushrooms. Our study contributes to the current understanding of risk perception and related behaviors and extends to the discussion in collective cultural valence situations. We call for further research to examine risk perception and health behaviors, move beyond cultural traditions centered on Western paradigms, and develop cross-cultural perspectives that emphasize the uniqueness of local cultural situations.

In addition, this study also has some limitations. First, the behavioral intention that can best affect the behavior was selected as the measurement object, but it cannot comprehensively present and predict the factors that affect the behavior of not eating wild mushrooms. Second, this was a cross-sectional study that, although appropriate to the research hypothesis we proposed, did not allow us to draw causal conclusions. In terms of causal interpretation, future studies can conduct intervention experiments on the feeding population to further verify the actual effect of the influencing factors.
