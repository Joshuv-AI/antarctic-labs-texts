Clinical characteristics and risk factors of recurrence for Pseudomonas aeruginosa-associated fracture-related infection following surgical treatment
Authors: Hongri Wu, Shengpeng Yu, Dong Sun, Shuling Wang, Xiaohua Wang, Jing Fu, Zhao Xie, Jie Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), a prevalent Gram-negative pathogen in fracture-related infections (FRIs), presents huge therapeutic challenges due to biofilm formation and multidrug resistance (MDR). Evidence-based guidelines for PA-associated FRI (PA-FRI) remain limited. The aim of the present study was to delineate the clinical characteristics of PA-FRI and associated risk factors of recurrence, and to provide actionable insights for optimizing antibiotic stewardship, refining surgical protocols, and reducing the burden of PA-FRI recurrence.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study examined 147 cases of PA-FRI involving limb fractures treated at our centre from January 2012 to December 2023. Demographic, microbiological, and clinical parameters were evaluated with ≥ 24-month follow-up. Multivariate regression analysis was conducted to identify risk factors for recurrence.ResultsThe cohort (mean age 45.06 years (SD 13.83)) showed a predominance of tibial fractures (69.4%; 102 out of 147). MDR-PA prevalence increased from 33.3% (2012 to 2014) to 58.6% (2021 to 2023), accounting for 44.9% (n = 66) of isolates. Coinfection with Staphylococcus aureus occurred in 17 cases (11.6%). Initial debridement and antibiotic therapy achieved infection control in 132 cases (89.8%), with persistence in 10.2% (n = 15). At a median 36-month follow-up (IQR 30 to 48), recurrence occurred in 15.6% (n = 23) after documented resolution of infection. Nine patients (6.1%) required amputation, while the remaining 14 achieved infection-free union. Multivariate logistic regression identified the following risk factors for recurrence: post-debridement internal fixation (odds ratio (OR) 4.85 (95% CI 1.00 to 23.36)); multiple prior debridements (two or more) (OR 6.00 (95% CI 1.02 to 35.37)); MDR-PA infection (OR 11.33 (95% CI 1.95 to 65.91)); long-term polymethylmethacrylate spacer retention (OR 9.32 (95% CI 2.08 to 41.80)); and shorter duration (less than four weeks) of systemic antibiotics (including intravenous and oral) (OR 7.84 (95% CI 2.02 to 30.40)). Delayed surgical intervention (more than ten weeks from the time of initial injury to the index debridement surgery) was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence compared with acute/sub-acute interventions (OR 0.16 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.63)).ConclusionThe high MDR prevalence and recurrence rates underscore persistent challenges in PA-FRI. This study identifies key modifiable risk factors and establishes a risk-stratified framework to guide preoperative optimization, antimicrobial stewardship, and surgical decision-making.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(9):1194–1203.

Introduction

Fracture-related infection (FRI) occurs in 1% to 30% of open fractures and 1% to 5% of closed fractures, representing a devastating complication in orthopaedic trauma and a substantial health-related economic burden.1-4 Despite advances in treatment techniques and strategies, recurrence rates exceed 20% in some cases.5 The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens and immunodeficiency (e.g. HIV) has further exacerbated therapeutic challenges.6,7 Literature suggests that Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA),8-10 characterized by its intrinsic biofilm-forming capacity, adaptive antibiotic resistance, and propensity for medical device colonization, accounts for 23.3% to 39.3% of Gram-negative FRI isolates.11-13 Alarmingly, the prevalence of MDR-PA in FRI has surged in recent cohorts,8,14 consistent with global trends in antimicrobial resistance. The biofilm-forming nature of PA complicates eradication efforts, as extracellular polymeric substances protect the bacteria from immune clearance and reduce antibiotic penetration by factors of ten- to 1,000-fold.3,15 These factors collectively contribute to prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and heightened risks of limb loss or chronic osteomyelitis.

Current management of PA-associated FRI (PA-FRI) relies on radical surgical debridement coupled with culture-guided antibiotic therapy. However, consensus on optimal treatment protocols remains elusive. Guidelines from the Fracture-Related Infection Consensus Group emphasize biofilm disruption, but lack PA-specific recommendations regarding antibiotic duration, debridement timing, and surgical strategies.16,17 When treatment protocols designed for general FRI are applied to PA-FRI, some studies have reported relatively higher rates of infection recurrence in the PA-FRI group. For example, an early study by Tice et al10 reported on 454 bone infection patients following debridement and antimicrobial therapy, including 20 cases of PA infection. Their results demonstrated that patients with PA infections had twice the recurrence risk compared with those with Staphylococcus aureus infections. Similarly, a cohort study by Wang et al9 found a 28% recurrence rate (7 out of 25) among PA infections, identifying it as a significant risk factor for recurrence when treated with the induced membrane technique. In another study, Yalikun et al18 reported on 149 bone infection patients treated with the Ilizarov bone transport technique. Their univariate analysis also revealed PA infection as an independent risk factor for infection recurrence (odds ratio (OR) 6.055; p = 0.012; Wald test). Given the complexity and high recurrence burden associated with PA-mediated orthopaedic infections, there is a pressing need for targeted investigation of this subgroup to better understand their infection patterns and refine clinical management, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

This 12-year retrospective cohort study addresses these gaps by analyzing 147 cases of PA-FRI to delineate its clinical characteristics and associated risk factors of recurrence, with the aim of establishing a risk-stratified intervention framework. With a large sample size and long-term follow-up, this study integrates longitudinal microbiological profiling, surgical variables, and patient-specific factors. By integrating epidemiological surveillance with therapeutic decision-making, this study seeks to provide actionable insights for optimizing antibiotic stewardship, refining surgical protocols, and reducing the burden of PA-FRI recurrence.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study examined patients with limb infections from 2012 to 2023, treated at a high-volume Level 1 bone infection centre within Southwest Hospital of Army Medical University in China. Our multidisciplinary team, comprising orthopaedic surgeons, infectious disease specialists, and clinical pharmacists, manages approximately 300 bone infection cases annually, reflecting the institution’s extensive expertise. Diagnosis of FRI required fulfillment of at least one of the following criteria:2,16,17 the presence of fistulae or sinuses, wound dehiscence with purulent drainage, intraoperative pus identification; concordant microbial growth from two or more independent deep-tissue specimens; or histopathological confirmation of infection. All intraoperative samples were taken from deep tissue or implants, with at least three separate specimens collected using clean instruments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and all procedures adhered to ethical guidelines and regulatory standards.

Upon diagnosis of FRI, the multidisciplinary team stratified disease severity using the Cierny-Mader classification19 and determined the appropriateness of conservative versus surgical management. Surgical candidates underwent comprehensive preoperative optimization, involving both systemic and local evaluations. Orthopaedic surgeons finalized operative plans through shared decision-making with patients. Of the initial cohort of 147 patients meeting inclusion criteria (limb osteomyelitis, PA-FRI, ≥ 24-month follow-up), exclusions were applied for palliative/amputation interventions, incomplete records, and comorbid malignancy.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

From 2012 to 2023, a total of 1,631 patients with a discharge diagnosis of FRI were surgically treated in our centre. Of these, all 147 patients with PA-FRI met the inclusion criteria, had completed the final follow-up, and were included in the final analysis. The median follow-up period was 36 months (IQR 30 to 48). The mean age of the cohort was 45.06 years (SD 13.83). Age stratification revealed that the highest incidence of PA-FRI occurred in the 40 to 59 years age group (61.2%; n = 90) followed by the 18 to 39 years age group (24.5%; n = 36). The majority of patients were male (82.3%; n = 121). A total of 90 patients (61.2%; n = 90) had open fractures, and initial osteosynthesis was performed using a plate in 64 patients (43.5%), and a nail in 38 patients (25.9%). The majority of patients (91.8%; n = 135) were classified as Cierny-Mader host type B, indicating a compromised host status. Clinical features such as sinus tract formation were observed in 45.6% (n = 67) of patients. The infection duration was more than ten weeks in 37.4% (n = 55) of cases. Definition and data collection

Clinical outcomes, including infection control, reoperations, and related complications, were followed up in the cohort of patients with PA-FRI. Infection control was evaluated by categorizing cases as either infection persistence or recurrence. Infection persistence was defined as an inadequate response to index debridement and antibiotic therapy,10,20 evidenced by residual signs of infection and imaging or pathological confirmation, or the need for repeat debridement. Infection recurrence was following FRI diagnostic criteria and defined as re-emergent infection at the original site following documented resolution after treatment.16,17 For this, documented infection resolution requires all of the following: no local inflammation (swelling, erythema, warmth, sinus discharge); normal ESR and CRP; no radiological infection signs (e.g. no progressive osteolysis or sequestrum); and negative deep-tissue cultures if clinically indicated. MDR was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories.6 Continuous follow-up was performed at two, six, 12, 18, and 24 months post-treatment. A dedicated clinical researcher (S.W) conducted structured follow-up through scheduled outpatient visits and telephone interviews. Secondary outcomes included reoperation rates, complication profiles, and antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Demographic and clinical variables (e.g. sex, age, infection characteristics, comorbidities, trauma history, tobacco use) as well as treatment parameters (e.g. prior debridements, antibiotic regimens, debridement timing, fixation strategies, flap coverage, and bone reconstruction techniques) were extracted from electronic health records. Microbiological data were obtained from institutional databases, with PA identification based on characteristic colony morphology on blood agar and antimicrobial susceptibility testing via broth microdilution according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.21 Although CLSI criteria for in vitro susceptibility changed in 2023, we confirm that we consistently used previous CLSI standards for each isolate’s analysis throughout the study period. Based on the consistency of data standards, the 2023 CLSI breakpoint revisions do not affect our results or conclusions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v. 22.0 statistical software (IBM, USA). Intergroup differences in demographic factors, clinical characteristics, treatment protocols, infection cure rates, and postoperative complications were statistically assessed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables, with normality confirmed via Shapiro-Wilk testing. To elucidate the role of demographic, microbiological, antibiotic, and surgical treatment-related variables as potential risk factors for recurrence, we compared patients with apparent cure to those experiencing recurrence. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions. Variables exhibiting p-values < 0.25 in univariate screening were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis to evaluate their independent associations after adjustment for confounders. Where applicable, 95% CIs and p-values were calculated to determine statistical significance. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Microbiological profile and antimicrobial resistance

In terms of microbial aetiology, PA was the sole pathogen in 72 (49.0%) of cases, while coinfection with S. aureus occurred in 17 (11.5%) of cases. Coinfection with other pathogens accounted for 58 (39.5%) of cases. Notably, the prevalence of PA among all Gram-negative bacilli FRI isolates was 34.6% (n = 147 out of 425), which was slightly increased from 28.4% (n = 29 out of 102) in 2012 to 2014 to 39.3% (n= 33 out of 84) in 2021 to 2023 (Figure 1a). Among these, the total prevalence of MDR-PA was as high as 44.9% (n = 66 out of 147) in this study period, significantly increased from 33.3% (n = 11 out of 33) in 2012 to 2014 to 48.0% (n = 24 out of 50) in 2018 to 2020, and to 58.6%(n = 17 out of 29) in 2021 to 2023 (Figure 1b). For specific antimicrobial agents, piperacillin, cefazolin, cefuroxime, Chemitrim, and minocycline resistance levels in PA were nearly 100% (n = 147 ). The proportion of PA infections resistant to carbapenems (e.g. imipenem, meropenem) was also of concern, reaching as high as 31%(n = 45 out of 147). Treatment methods and clinical outcomes

Initial debridement and antibiotic therapy were the mainstay of treatment. The median duration of intravenous antibiotic therapy was four weeks (IQR 3 to 5 weeks). Some patients also received oral antibiotics, which were administered following the completion of intravenous therapy as sequential therapy. The median duration of oral antibiotic therapy was two weeks (IQR 1 to 3 weeks). All antibiotic therapy is individualized by a multidisciplinary team based on culture and susceptibility results, as well as the patient’s clinical response. Duration was calculated from the first day of effective intravenous antibiotics to the cessation of all systemic antibiotics (intravenous or oral). Additionally, 80.3% (n = 118) of patients were treated with local antibiotics via antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) (Table III and Figure 2). Ceftazidime and piperacillin/tazobactam were the most commonly used intravenous antibiotics, while 5 g of vancomycin was added to 40 g PMMA (containing 0.5 g gentamicin) for local treatment. Initial infection control was achieved in 132 patients (89.8%), with 10.2% (n = 15) experiencing infection persistence after the first treatment, and 48 patients (32.7%) needed additional flap coverage. Following initial infection control, 123 patients (83.7%) underwent reconstruction surgery for larger bone defects. Autografts were used in 64 patients, bone transport in 37 patients, and permanent PMMA retention in 22 patients. However, 23 patients (15.6%) experienced recurrence (Table III and Figure 3), 13 (56.5%) within six months, seven (30.4%) between six and 12 months, and three (13.1%) beyond 12 months, after documented resolution of infection. Among the 23 patients with recurrence, 11 had a positive culture for PA again, while 12 were culture-negative. At the end of the follow-up, nine patients (6.1%) required amputation (seven due to refractory infection and two due to malignancy), while the remaining 14 patients achieved infection-free bone union.

Risk factors of recurrence

Univariate analysis revealed that factors such as age, sex, tobacco use, prior fractures, previous debridement, prior implant use, Cierny–Mader host type, sinus formation, bone reconstruction, and use of local antibiotics were not associated with infection recurrence (Table IV). Fisher’s exact test showed MDR infection (p = 0.002), use of less than four weeks of systemic antibiotics (p < 0.001), flap coverage (p = 0.014), post-debridement internal fixation (p = 0.009), and long-term PMMA retention (p = 0.008) were potential influencing factors for recurrence. Multivariate logistic regression analysis (Figure 4) revealed that post-debridement internal fixation (OR 4.85 (95% CI 1.00 to 23.36); p = 0.049) and prior multiple debridements (two or more) (OR 6.00 (95% CI 1.02 to 35.37); p = 0.048) were associated with an increased risk of recurrence in PA-FRI. Additionally, MDR-PA infection (OR 11.33 (95% CI 1.95 to 65.91); p = 0.007), long-term PMMA spacer retention (OR 9.32 (95% CI 2.08 to 41.80); p = 0.004), and use of less than four weeks of systemic antibiotics (OR 7.84 (95% CI 2.02 to 30.40); p = 0.003) were identified as strong predictors of recurrence. Conversely, surgical intervention in the late infection phase (more than ten weeks) (OR 0.16 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.63); p = 0.009) was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence compared with interventions in the acute or sub-acute phase.

Discussion

PA-FRI presents a formidable clinical challenge due to the pathogen’s intrinsic biofilm-forming capacity,22 adaptive antibiotic resistance,23 and ability to colonize medical devices.3 Our 12-year retrospective cohort study analyzing 147 PA-FRI cases underscored the persistent threat posed by antibiotic resistance and the high recurrence rates associated with this infection. Results demonstrated that 44.9% (n = 66) of isolates were identified as MDR, including carbapenem-resistant PA, with a recurrence rate of 15.6% (n = 23) despite aggressive debridement and antimicrobial treatment. These findings were consistent with global trends and present huge therapeutic challenges due to limited treatment options and increased morbidity.14,24

In our cohort, tibial fractures were most prevalent (69.4%; n = 102), likely due to compromised soft-tissue coverage and delayed vascularization, which provide a conducive environment for adhesion of biofilm bacteria (e.g. PA).15,24 Most patients had undergone multiple surgeries and repeated antimicrobial treatments, which inevitably aggravates the development of drug resistance. Those patients with multiple revision surgeries (two or more) faced a six-fold higher risk of recurrence, underscoring the complex nature of these cases. The recurrence may stem from hidden infection niches and compromised local immunity due to repeated surgeries, consistent with previous reports.9,18,25 Coinfections were also common in our study. In particular, these coinfections may enhance the resilience of PA biofilms via extracellular matrix cross-talk.16,26-28 However, no significant correlation was found between coinfections and recurrence in subsequent analyses. MDR-PA was found to be a strong predictor of recurrence, highlighting the critical need for effective antimicrobial stewardship and the development of novel therapeutic strategies to address MDR-PA infections.

The combination of appropriate surgical intervention with antimicrobial therapy remains the standard approach in orthopaedic infection management. Current evidence suggests that both oral and intravenous antibiotics are effective in treating these infections.3,29-31 The general recommendation is for systemic antibiotics to be administered for at least four to six weeks post-surgery, but the duration of treatment often depends on clinician experience, therapy response, and patient-specific factors such as compliance and tolerability. Prolonged antibiotic therapy, however, increases the risk of hepatorenal toxicity, gut dysbiosis, secondary infections, and accelerated development of resistant strains.14,22,27,31 Some authors have previously suggested that shorter courses of systemic antibiotics, especially when combined with local antibiotic therapy, can achieve good outcomes.31-33 In our study, the use of systemic antibiotics for less than four weeks was identified as a significant risk factor for recurrence, emphasizing the importance of adequate and appropriate antibiotic therapy in the management of PA-FRI.

To date, there is no universally accepted classification of FRI. Some scholars have attempted to classify infection duration in relation to fractures into early infection (within two weeks), delayed infection (three to ten weeks), and late infection (more than ten weeks).3,34,35 In our study, we observed that debridement performed during the late infection phase significantly reduced the risk of recurrence compared with interventions in the early or delayed phases (p = 0.009). This may be explained by the fact that, during the late phase, the infection is often more localized, allowing for better delineation of necrotic tissue, which can be more thoroughly excised.3,15,36 Clinically, this may support a staged approach to treatment: initial antibiotic therapy or drainage, followed by definitive debridement during the chronic phase. However, this finding must be interpreted cautiously: optimal timing remains a case-by-case decision, depending on patient stability and soft-tissue condition.

In the management of FRI, stabilization and reconstruction following debridement are often considered necessary steps for most patients. While external fixation has long been the standard method for treating orthopaedic infections, there is an increasing trend towards using antibiotic-loaded bone cement in combination with internal fixation,3,17,37 primarily as a temporary solution. The theoretical basis for this approach lies in the coating strategies that enhance local antimicrobial therapy and reduce bacterial adhesion. Bone grafting, the induced membrane technique,9 and bone transport remain the primary methods for managing large bone defects.18 However, with the widespread use of antibiotic-loaded bone cement (PMMA), some patients may opt for permanent PMMA spacers for bone defects.38 In our study, both post-debridement internal fixation and long-term PMMA spacer retention were identified as significant risk factors for recurrence in PA-FRI. These findings suggest that the use of internal fixation and long-term retention of PMMA spacers should be approached with caution.

There are several limitations in this study. First, selection bias may have been introduced by the single-centre nature of the study. Second, not all confounders that could influence antibiotic response, such as socioeconomic factors and first line antibiotic choice, were included in the analysis. Third, the relatively small number of recurrence events (n = 23) limited statistical power for multivariable analysis, and some statistically significant differences have wide CIs. Despite these limitations, this study remains the largest cohort of PA-FRI cases with extended follow-up, providing valuable insights into recurrence patterns and management.

This 12-year analysis of 147 PA-FRI cases highlighted the growing prevalence of MDR and substantial recurrence risks, underscoring the therapeutic complexity inherent in PA-FRI. Several non-modifiable and modifiable risk factors were identified as being associated with infection recurrence, contributing to management for preoperative optimization, antimicrobial stewardship, and surgical decision-making.

Take home message

- This study identified that systemic antibiotics < four weeks, post-debridement internal fixation, and retained polymethylmethacrylate spacers are modifiable risk factors for recurrence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) fracture-related infection.

- High multidrug-resistant PA prevalence (44.9%) independently predicted recurrence, highlighting the need for enhanced antimicrobial stewardship and tailored surgical decision-making.
