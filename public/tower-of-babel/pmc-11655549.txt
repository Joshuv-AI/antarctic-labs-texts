GARD-LENS: A downscaled large ensemble dataset for understanding future climate and its uncertainties
Authors: Samantha H. Hartke, Andrew J. Newman, Ethan Gutmann, Rachel McCrary, Nicholas D. Lybarger, Flavio Lehner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This article introduces the Generalized Analog Regression Downscaling method Large Ensemble (GARD-LENS) dataset, comprised of daily precipitation, mean temperature, and temperature range over the Contiguous U.S., Alaska, and Hawaii at 12-km, 4-km, and 1-km resolutions, respectively. GARD-LENS statistically downscales three CMIP6 global climate model large ensembles, CESM2, CanESM5, and EC-Earth3, totaling 200 ensemble members. GARD-LENS is the first downscaled SMILE (single model initial-condition large ensemble), providing information about the role of internal climate variability at high resolutions. The 150-year record of this large ensemble dataset provides ample data for assessing trends and extremes and allows users to robustly assess internal variability, forced climate signals, and time of emergence at high resolutions. As the need for high resolution, robust climate datasets continues to grow, GARD-LENS will be a valuable tool for scientists and practitioners who wish to account for internal variability in their future climate analyses and adaptation plans.

Background & Summary

As decisionmakers around the world increasingly seek out information about the next several decades under a changing climate, global climate models (GCMs) provide attractive datasets by simulating the atmosphere, ocean, land surface, and cryosphere under historical and potential future conditions. The latest generation of such models, often referred to as earth system models (ESMs)1, also incorporate atmospheric chemistry, biogeochemistry, and other earth processes that influence the evolution of the atmosphere and long-term climate. By simulating the earth system using known physical laws, equations, and parameterizations describing processes such as turbulence and evaporation, GCMs provide estimates of both historical and future precipitation, temperature, and other variables2,3. However, GCMs are limited by the computational resources they require to simulate the dominant energy, momentum, and water fluxes across the Earth System components for the hundreds to thousands of years required to spin-up oceans, ice sheets, terrestrial carbon pools, and attempt to quantify system uncertainties, typically limiting GCM outputs to a coarse scale (100–250 km).

Future climate uncertainty arises in GCM data from several sources4; firstly, GCMs often apply different mathematical approximations of processes such as radiative transfer, evapotranspiration, clouds, and other processes. The resulting model uncertainty includes uncertainty due to the parameterizations and other decision-making involved in tuning a GCM. Next, internal variability describes the inherent chaos of atmospheric processes, and the fact that near identical atmospheric conditions can progress to very different conditions over the course of days to decades5. Large GCM ensembles seek to capture this internal variability by initializing simulations at offset times from a stable control simulation or by slightly perturbing the initial conditions provided to a model. Lastly, scenario uncertainty describes the unknown forcings in a future climate (e.g., the amount of anthropogenic emissions in coming decades), and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs)6 and Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs)7,8 attempt to bracket this uncertainty by portraying several possible emission scenarios, including cases of decreasing and increasing global emissions. For further background on the challenges of GCM simulations, see9,10.

The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP) has been used for decades to compare various GCMs’ historical simulations and future projections. The CMIP6 suite of GCMs includes several single model initial-condition large ensembles (SMILEs), which are uniquely useful for (1) quantifying internal variability of GCMs, (2) robustly calculating climate signals, and (3) assessing the time of emergence, or the moment in time when the climate has substantially departed from the historical climate11–15. Large ensembles allow scientists to differentiate the signal of a changing climate from interannual variability (e.g., ‘noise’), and, as Mankin et al.15 note, “Large ensembles have revealed an underappreciated source of uncertainty in regional climate projections—that of internal climate variability.”

Downscaling methods are used to map coarse GCM data to higher resolutions, providing historical data and future projections at scales that are useful for researchers, government agencies, decisionmakers, and other adaptation practitioners that seek to understand climate impacts at a local scale. There are two main classes of downscaling methods: empirical statistical downscaling (ESD), which captures statistical relationships between historical GCM and observation data and applies these relationships to GCM projections, and dynamical downscaling, which uses GCM projections as the boundary conditions for a regional climate model (RCM)16,17. A growing third class of downscaling methods is machine learning, such as CarbonPlan’s Deep SD dataset, which uses a convolutional neural network to learn the relationship between historical GCM data and observations and applies the trained neural net to GCM projections18. See Kotamarthi et al.19 for a further overview of downscaling methods and available datasets.

However, downscaling methods introduce additional layers of uncertainty into the data they generate, in addition to the uncertainty already present in GCMs (e.g.20,21). For instance, regression-based statistical downscaling models attempt to accurately capture the linear relationship between observations and GCM data but naturally still contain uncertainty when such a fit is imperfect22. Training data presents another source of uncertainty23; depending on which observational dataset is used for training, or the dataset period and length (e.g., using 1960–1980 vs. 1980–2010), the downscaling results may be quite different, often resembling the training dataset. Another nontrivial source of uncertainty is the assumption of statistical stationarity made in ESD approaches; that is, ESD methods assume that the statistical relationship presented by historical observations and GCM data will be the same in the future, an assumption that has been shown to have limits when applied to downscaled maximum temperature data24. Uncertainties in downscaled datasets are often found to be an underlying source of error in hydrologic, agriculture, and other models that use downscaled data as input25,26. Even though understanding of this uncertainty is limited, downscaled datasets provide valuable insight for future plans and adaptation strategies under a changing climate and are central to efforts such as the National Climate Assessment27.

Due to computational limits and other resource constraints, downscaling schemes are often only applied to a limited number of GCM ensemble members; this is a form of sampling uncertainty, such that only a few of the equally plausible climate futures modeled by GCMs are being translated to high resolution datasets that risk analysts, for example, or other decision-makers will use. No single model initial-condition large ensemble (SMILE) has thus far been downscaled and released as a publicly available dataset. Even though forced responses in the climate require a large ensemble to robustly calculate, currently available downscaled datasets such as LOCA2 downscale ten or fewer members of individual GCM28, often due to computing, resource, and data availability constraints. It is easily shown that the range of possible future trends can vary substantially when calculated based on a limited ensemble instead of a full large ensemble (Fig. 1a,b). This is because trend estimates vary substantially between ensemble members, a byproduct of internal variability (Fig. 1c). As an additional issue, areas outside of the contiguous United States (OCONUS) are frequently excluded from CONUS-centric climate studies and datasets, even though OCONUS is also experiencing extreme climate hazards29. One obstacle to OCONUS downscaling efforts is a relative scarcity of robust, long-term observations which are needed for training or calibrating downscaling models, although a few downscaled Hawaii and Alaska projections have notably been made available in recent years30–33.

This work introduces a statistically downscaled daily large ensemble dataset, GARD-LENS, covering the contiguous U.S., Alaska, and Hawaii. GARD-LENS downscales three CMIP6 single model initial-condition large ensembles, CESM2, CanESM5, and EC-Earth3, to predict high resolution daily precipitation (pcp), mean temperature (t_mean), and temperature range (t_range). This dataset allows researchers to better partition uncertainties in downscaled data and can help those who use this data understand where many downscaled products lie relative to the expected range of internal variability, which, up until now, has been difficult to quantify at high resolution.

While several studies have started to quantify the impact that downscaling method and even training datasets can have on downscaled data applications, including hydrologic modeling (e.g.34,35), there has been no comparison with the range of uncertainty represented by internal variability. Without downscaling large ensembles, there is little to no understanding of how downscaling impacts internal variability and how the future climate’s internal variability may differ at high resolutions compared to the coarse scale of GCMs. GARD-LENS will allow scientists and adaptation practitioners to better understand the sources of uncertainty in downscaled climate projections, particularly because it allows them to directly calculate the range of internal variability, which is considered an irreducible, but often poorly quantified, source of uncertainty36,37.

Continued analysis of the GARD-LENS dataset is ongoing and will be detailed in future publications. This analysis will include additional time of emergence analysis, uncertainty decomposition, spatial and object-based analysis, and comparison of GARD-LENS fields with a suite of other downscaled products. As the need for high resolution, robust climate dataset grows, GARD-LENS will be a valuable tool for scientists and practitioners who wish to account for internal variability in their future climate analyses and adaptation plans.

Due to the coarse scale of GCMs, many coastal cells are mainly influenced by ocean and atmosphere dynamics, without influence from land areas (including islands within those cells). This can contribute to poorer performance of downscaled data in coastal areas24. To avoid using GCM data which may not accurately simulate land-influenced changes in underlying coastal regions, CESM2 (CanESM5) coastal cells with land fractions less than 0.9 (0.5) were replaced with data from the nearest land-covering GCM cell. The land fraction threshold was selected based on GCM resolution, since using too high of a land fraction threshold with a coarse GCM (e.g., 2.8° CanESM5) would result in replacing most of California, for example, with inland grid cells. Due to EC-Earth’s relatively high resolution, no land fraction thresholding was performed for this GCM data.

Reanalysis and observation datasets

Several reference datasets are used in this work to train and verify GARD-LENS. While individual datasets may exhibit limitations, especially in complex terrain46, using a comprehensive suite of observation datasets provides greater confidence in validation results. Additionally, statistically downscaled datasets have often been shown to reflect the characteristics (and limitations) of the training datasets used to generate them23; we can better understand the extent to which the performance of downscaled data is tied to training data by including the observation datasets used to generate each downscaled product (e.g., GMET for GARD LENS and Livneh for LOCA2). GMET was selected for evaluating and training GARD-LENS due to its ability to capture historical extremes and its availability over Alaska and Hawaii at high resolution47–49 (available at https://www.earthsystemgrid.org/dataset/gridded_precip_and_temp.html). Before using GMET for training or evaluation over Alaska, daily precipitation was capped at 500 mm/d since GMET Alaska data has been noted to overestimate high rainfall rates, and daily mean temperatures were capped at 30° C since several unrealistic daily temperatures were noted in interior Alaska49. The Alaska GMET dataset was regridded from a curvilinear 2-km grid to a roughly 4-km rectilinear grid using conservative interpolation to reduce the computational resources required to downscale this region. Variables from the ERA5 Reanalysis dataset, available at daily, 0.25° resolution50 (available at https://rda.ucar.edu/datasets/d633000/) are also used to train GARD after being upscaled to 1.0°. See the section “GARD Predictor Selection” for more details on how GMET and ERA5 variables are used in training GARD. The daily 1/16° Livneh “unsplit” dataset51,52 (available at https://psl.noaa.gov/data/gridded/data.livneh.html) was selected for comparison since it is a training dataset for several downscaled products (e.g., LOCA, MACA, & DBCCA), and 1/24° PRISM is included for its high resolution and general accuracy53 (available at https://prism.oregonstate.edu/recent/).

GARD

The Generalized Analog Regression for Downscaling (GARD) method presents a flexible framework for statistically downscaling GCM fields which allows users to specify configurations such as general methodology (regression and analog approaches) as well as parameters such as the number of analog days and predictor variables. En-GARD, the ensemble version of GARD, was introduced in Gutmann et al.22 to generate probabilistic downscaled estimates of climate projections by estimating an error term during the regression fitting or analog selection process, which is later stochastically added to the output downscaled fields. Like nearly all statistical downscaling methods, GARD assumes the stationarity of the statistical relationship between GCM and observed climate data. Dixon et al.24 showed that this assumption cannot always be expected to hold true, depending on the season and extent of climatic changes.

GARD-LENS uses the GARD analog regression method with 100 analog days selected for regression fitting at each grid cell and time step (Fig. 2). The training data, which can include any desired predictor variables such as winds, temperature, precipitation, and specific humidity, is used by the model to (1) select analog days with similar variable values as the day being predicted and (2) train a linear regression model that predicts the high-resolution variable of interest (e.g., daily precipitation or temperature) based on the suite The final step in the GARD downscaling process is to apply a post-processing perturbation (Fig. 2, Supplemental Fig. 7), which reverses any transforms applied to the predictand (e.g., cube root transform for precipitation) during the regression fitting process and adds a random perturbation of the calculated error field. The error term is calculated by combining a spatially correlated random field (SCRF; consisting of uniform noise) over the study domain with the estimated error distribution of each downscaled variable. The SCRF is created by replicating the spatiotemporal correlation structure of the GARD mean field, using the Space-Time Rainfall Error and Autocorrelation Model (STREAM), described in further detail in Hartke et al.58. In the case of precipitation, which is modeled logistically within GARD, the uniform noise values from a SCRF are first compared to the estimated probability of precipitation to determine if rain will occur in the downscaled field. Further details on GARD methodology can be found in Gutmann et al.22, and the general GARD methodology schematic is included as Supplemental Fig. 7. All GARD simulations were completed on the NCAR Derecho and Campaign computing systems, supported by the NCAR Computational and Information Systems Laboratory59.

GARD predictor selection

A variety of atmospheric variables from ERA5 as well as precipitation, mean daily temperature, and daily temperature range from GMET, all coarsened to 1° to match the resolution of the CESM2 data, were included in the predictor selection process. Predictor selection was based on which predictor set demonstrated the best regression fit and the best fit to historical trend and annual statistics (Supplemental Fig. 1). Table 3 summarizes the selected predictors for estimating daily precipitation and mean temperature using GMET as the training product during the period 1980–2014. For daily precipitation, coarsened precipitation from GMET and coarsened 700 mb winds, temperature, and potential vorticity were found to produce downscaled estimates closest to historical observations in terms of annual totals, historical trends, and ENSO correlation patterns (see Supplemental Fig. 1 for results from the predictor selection process). We chose to use coarsened GMET data in the training process based on the assumption that, once bias corrected to the GMET distribution, GCM data is accurately portraying coarse scale climate. When GARD trains a regression to predict 1/8° GMET precipitation from 1° GMET precipitation, it is learning only the relationship between coarse scale averages and the fine scale impacts of topography, coast lines, etc. without any additional bias correction that may occur if, for instance, 1° ERA5 precipitation was used to train the regression. An argument could be made for almost any predictor set used during the predictor selection process, depending on which performance metrics were prioritized, except for ‘pcp_puv’ (using convective precipitation as a predictor) and ‘prect_uv’ (using ERA5 total precipitation as a predictor; Supplemental Fig. 1). For downscaling precipitation, we selected the ‘pcp_tuv’ predictor set, which uses GMET total precipitation and ERA5 700mb level temperature (T700), and u and v 700mb winds, due to its balanced ability to capture historical trends and annual mean and 95th percentile precipitation relative to other predictor sets. We also note that 700mb variables for EC-Earth3 are not publicly available so U850, V850, and tas are used in place of U700, V700, and T700, respectively; however, based on predictor sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1), this should not significantly alter results. Following a similar rational, we selected predictor sets for downscaling daily t_mean and t_range (Table 1). For CanESM5 member r24i1p2f1, atmospheric level data was not saved, so only pr and tas were used as predictors in the r24i1p2f1 GARD simulations.

En-GARD allows users to specify the transform period used to empirically quantile map GCM data for future predictions, a common mode of bias correction22. Often, the historical period when observations overlap with the GCM simulation being downscaled will be used; however, we used the historical period of ten randomly selected members of each GCM ensemble, with the intention of including a more representative climatological distribution for quantile mapping. A similar ensemble version of bias correction is used for LOCA228. We found that adjusting this transform period to include additional ensemble members (from the same GCM as the member being downscaled) altered the resulting GARD output more than using different predictor sets, particularly for end of century daily temperatures (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Finally, we observed that unreasonably high future daily mean temperatures were occasionally predicted by the GARD regression in interior Alaska (e.g., in excess of 40 °C), particularly towards the end of the century. These values are considered unreasonable as they correspond to change signals far greater than what the GCMs predict. To calculate a reasonable upper limit on predicted future daily temperatures, we calculated the maximum historical daily temperature for each grid cell based on all GARD LENS members and added the maximum change signal for future daily temperatures across all raw GCM members (Supplemental Fig. 3). All Alaska GARD LENS daily temperature predictions were then capped according to the grid cell-specific maximum for each GCM. This correction affected roughly 0.01–0.03% of GARD-LENS Alaska daily temperatures from 2050–2100, depending on the specific ensemble member.

Data Records

The GARD-LENS dataset is available at the Research Data Archive (RDA) hosted by NSF NCAR60 (https://oidc.rda.ucar.edu/datasets/d619000/). The dataset is roughly 12TB in size, though several summary files provide annual statistics for GARD-LENS in each study region at much smaller size (less than 100 GB). The downscaled data for each variable is located in the directories pcp, t_mean, and t_range, respectively, and the summary files are available in the summary_files directory. The file naming format is GARDLENS_{GCM}_{ensemble number} _{variable}_1950_2100_{study area}.nc, and within each file, the downscaled variable is defined by coordinates (lat, lon, time). In python, we recommend accessing these files using xarray61. In the summary_files directory, annual mean, maximum, and standard deviation for all GCM ensemble members in a given study area are provided in files with naming format GARDLENS_{variable} _stats_{study area}.nc. Specific downscaled GCM simulations can be obtained from this file by selecting the specified GCM and ensemble number using xarray’s.sel function (e.g., ds.sel(gcm=‘canesm5’, ens=‘r1i1p1f1’)).

Technical Validation

Here we verify the general accuracy of GARD-LENS and demonstrate the range of interesting analyses that it may be used for. Validation focuses on the mean bias of the GARD-LENS ensemble as well as the containing ratio (CR). The containing ratio (CR)62,63 measures the proportion of instances for which a predictive ensemble “brackets” the ground truth. This probabilistic performance metric has been used by previous work in the precipitation, hydrologic modeling, and forecast verification communities to assess ensemble performance63–66. An ideal CR is 1.0 in this case because it indicates that the GARD-LENS ensemble brackets observed climate statistics for all grid cells in the study area and gives confidence that the ensemble range will also bracket the (unknown) climate statistics of the future. The Wasserstein Distance, also known as the earth mover distance, describes the “effort” used to move one probability distribution to match another and is used to quantify the difference between GMET and GARD-LENS historical probability density functions67–69. Several additional analyses are completed to demonstrate the utility of the GARD-LENS dataset, including annual precipitation trends, time of emergence, and changes to the climate signal. Time of emergence for a given climate metric is calculated by identifying the year when the magnitude of the climate change signal for the metric exceeds the historical (1980–2010) standard deviation of the metric (e.g., the expected interannual variability, or noise, of that metric70–72)

Bias in annual means (Fig. 3) and maximums (Supplemental Fig. 4), a common metric for evaluating climate datasets, is calculated to demonstrate the GARD-LENS dataset’s ability to capture annual climate characteristics at the high resolution of the training dataset, GMET. The bias in historical mean daily t_mean and t_range is low across most of CONUS for all GARD-LENS GCMs, with a CR of 0.95 or greater (Fig. 3e,f,h,i,k,l). A negative bias in historical t_mean is observed across the northern Midwest and Great Plains for GARD-LENS CESM2 and CanESM5, but this higher bias relative to the rest of CONUS is due to a colder climatological t_mean in this region, which amplifies the percentage bias. For daily pcp, the relative bias in GARD-LENS is highest in western CONUS, particularly in dry, topographically complex, and mountainous regions. However, this bias is limited to within 25% of the mean daily pcp recorded by GMET, and the CR is very high (0.96 or higher) for all GARD-LENS GCMs (Fig. 3d, g,j). Figure 4 shows the Wasserstein Distance (WD) calculated for the three GARD-LENS variables over the historical period (1980–2014) between GMET and (1) Livneh (regridded to 1/8°), (2) 10 random GARD-LENS CESM2 members, (3) 10 random GARD-LENS EC-Earth3 members, and (4) 10 random GARD-LENS CanESM5 members. For the GARD-LENS results, the WD is calculated for each of the 10 members of each GCM and averaged afterward. Most noticeably, the WD between GMET and GARD-LENS members is lower than that between GMET and Livneh, meaning that there is a greater difference between the historical distributions of two observation products (i.e., observational uncertainty) than between that of GMET and GARD-LENS. While this might be surprising, it is not unbelievable, given that GARD-LENS was calibrated using historical GMET data and that GMET and Livneh display some of their highest differences at high elevation and in complex topography, areas where the producers of these datasets had to make unique methodological decisions due to sparse gauge locations and extreme precipitation gradients. Vissio et al. (2020) reported a similar result when a reanalysis product displayed a higher WD than CMIP5 models when compared to global observation products. Figure 8 demonstrates how GARD-LENS can be used to calculate time of emergence. Time of emergence (ToE) describes the point in time when climate change emerges from a background reference state (generally the historical climate characterized by data from the 20th century)14. The time of emergence of different climate characteristics (e.g., mean annual temperature, max daily temperature, precipitation variability) can vary, due to the ability of annual means to change at a different rate than annual extreme, for example. Since large ensembles are uniquely useful for their ability to provide robust time of emergence estimates, ToE is calculated for two climate metrics, annual maximum daily temperature and maximum daily precipitation, using the CESM2 large ensemble and the downscaled CESM2 ensemble from GARD-LENS. The historical standard deviation, or “noise,” for annual maximum daily values is substantially higher (relative to the signal; Fig. 8e,f) for precipitation than for temperature (Fig. 8a,b), which leads to time of emergence estimates for annual maximum daily precipitation that occur later in the century. GARD-LENS shows good performance in CONUS, Alaska, and Hawaii, with the ensemble mean following historical observations and the ensemble range bracketing observed interannual variability and trends (Figs. 3, 5, 6, 7, Supplemental Figs. 4–6). GARD-LENS appears to preserve the substantial range of internal variability in the original SMILEs that it downscales, even if it does not precisely match the SMILEs’ original estimates of local precipitation trends (Fig. 5). Datasets which downscale fewer ensemble members of GCMs (e.g., LOCA2 includes 10 CESM2 members) do not display the same range of the full large ensemble, which is not surprising (Figs. 1, 5), but points to the importance of having available downscaled SMILEs for robust decision making in climate adaptation studies.

To summarize, the GARD-LENS ensemble demonstrates the ability to bracket historical climatology and has comparable trend estimates to other downscaled datasets in CONUS. In Alaska and Hawaii, GARD-LENS presents an appealing alternative to coarser CMIP6 products and shows improved performance relative to global products when estimating historical climate at high resolutions. This initial analysis shows that estimates of internal variability and climate signals from GARD-LENS at high resolution can differ substantially from the respective GCMs (Figs. 6, 7). By resolving time of emergence at a higher resolution, some signals emerge earlier than when calculated using a GCM alone (e.g., Fig. 8a,b or south Texas in Fig. 8g,h). Not only does downscaling alter the projected climate signal (Figs. 6, 7), but it appears to alter historical estimates of interannual variability as well (e.g., the historical standard deviation in Fig. 8a,b,e,f).

The only other available downscaled datasets for Hawaii, Deep-SD and NASA-NEX, are at a relatively coarse resolution for adaptation and planning purposes and do not capture historical climate over Honolulu well (Fig. 6). GARD-LENS could be particularly useful in Hawaii where its predictions account for coastal and orographic features that coarser products cannot resolve, but which are crucial for accurate predictions of local climate. Validation work has found that Alaska and Hawaii GARD-LENS simulations slightly underestimate historical annual maximum daily temperature; while the bias is slight (e.g., less than 10%), this will be explored in future work.

Usage Notes

The data can be read using any software that is able to link to netcdf4 libraries.

One of the main sensitivities in the GARD-LENS simulations was found to be the selection of the transform period that the GCM predictor data is quantile mapped to (Supplemental Fig. 2). Using a single GCM ensemble member during the historical period as the transform data resulted in (unrealistically) high end of century maximum daily temperatures in some locations; using ten GCM ensemble members for the transform was found to be a reasonable solution, with much smaller changes in the output observed when increasing the number of ensemble members in the transform beyond ten. While investigating the sensitivity of the GARD model to different configurations, we found one of the greatest sensitivities to be the end of century maximum daily temperature (Supplemental Fig. 2). Whether adjusting the number of analog days or increasing the number of ensemble members in the transform period, the 2070–2100 annual maximum daily temperature consistently showed the highest amount of change across CONUS; end of century mean daily temperatures were often unaffected.
