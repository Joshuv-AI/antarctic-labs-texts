Aggregation operators based on interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic sets for medical waste disposal planning
Authors: Serdar Kuzu, Murat Kirişci, Ali Kablan, Volkan Öngel, Ismail Erkan Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a novel decision-making framework based on interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic sets, a new theoretical model designed to capture linguistic uncertainty, interval-valued hesitation, and Fermatean membership characteristics simultaneously. The primary objective is to construct the mathematical foundations of interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic sets, develop corresponding aggregation operators, propose a new score function, and formulate a complete multi-criteria decision-making algorithm. The research begins by defining interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic sets and exploring their fundamental algebraic and ordering properties. Several aggregation operators (Weighted Aggregation and Geometric Weighted; Ordered Weighted Average and Ordered Geometric Weighted; Generalized Weighted Averaging and Generalized Weighted Geometric; Generalized Ordered Weighted Averaging and Generalized Ordered Weighted Geometric; and Linguistic Hybrid) tailored to the interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic structure are proposed, and their properties are rigorously proven. A novel score function is introduced to provide a meaningful ranking mechanism for interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic evaluations. Building on these components, a new multi-criteria decision-making algorithm is developed. The algorithm is applied to selecting medical waste disposal techniques, a domain characterized by high uncertainty and conflicting criteria. The robustness of the results is examined using the interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy-COPRAS method, and comparative analyses are conducted with previously established fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making approaches. A comprehensive sensitivity analysis is also performed to validate the stability of the proposed framework. The empirical results indicate that the proposed interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic-based multi-criteria decision-making framework effectively handles linguistic imprecision and interval-valued hesitation, producing reliable and interpretable rankings. Robustness and sensitivity analyses confirm the stability of the ranking across different parameter settings and weighting schemes. Comparative results show that the proposed method provides more consistent and resilient outcomes than traditional fuzzy and classical decision-making models. Introduction

Medical waste refers to waste generated in healthcare facilities that can pose risks to human health and the environment. The basic categories shown in Fig. 1 are:

Classification of medical wastes.

1. Infectious Waste: Waste that carries the risk of microorganisms and transmitting disease. Risks of infectious waste include outbreaks and healthcare worker infections.

2. Pathological Waste: Biological materials such as human or animal tissues, organs, and amputation fragments. Risks of this type of waste include biological contamination, ethical issues, and hygiene concerns.

3. Sharps Waste: Waste that can cause injury, such as needles, scalpels, and broken glass ampoules. Risks of sharps waste include puncture wounds and bloodborne transmission (Hepatitis, HIV, etc.).

4. Pharmaceutical Waste: Expired or unused medications and drug residues. Risks include water and soil pollution, as well as antibiotic resistance.

5. Chemical & Toxic Waste: Chemicals used in hospitals, disinfectants, substances containing heavy metals, etc. Risks include poisoning and environmental toxicity1.

6. Radioactive Waste: This refers to waste containing radioactive material generated during nuclear medicine and radiology procedures. Risks include radiation exposure and long-term environmental damage.

The purpose of this classification is to ensure that the appropriate and safe disposal method is selected for each type of waste, as each has a different risk profile.

Figure 1 shows the classification of medical waste, and Fig. 2 shows the disposal/processing strategies that can be applied to this waste2,3.

Waste strategies.

In Fig. 2, the “Waste Strategies” section generally refers to alternative disposal methods. The alternatives selected in this study using COPRAS are based on this. The most common strategies are:

(A) Incineration: Waste is burned at high temperatures to reduce volume and eliminate pathogens. Advantages include eliminating the risk of infection and significantly reducing volume. Disadvantages include the potential formation of harmful gases such as dioxins/furans, as well as high investment and operating costs4.

(B) Autoclaving: Sterilization is performed using pressurized steam. It is generally suitable for infectious waste. The advantages of this technique include its lower environmental impact compared to incineration and its ability to provide safe sterilization. The disadvantages are its limitations in pathological or chemical waste and the need for subsequent shredding/transportation of the waste.

(C) Chemical Disinfection: Waste is purified from microorganisms using chemical substances. Advantages include its effectiveness for some liquid wastes and its relatively low cost. In contrast, disadvantages include the potential for chemical residues to harm the environment and the suitability of the system for all waste types.

(D) Landfilling: Waste is buried in regulated landfill sites. The advantage is that it is simple and relatively inexpensive. The disadvantage is the risk of long-term environmental pollution and its unsuitability for infectious and toxic waste.

Figures 1 and 2 support the study’s basic logic. Since waste types differ, their risks also differ. Because the risks differ, the criteria have different levels of importance. Therefore, the decision problem becomes a multi-criteria structure. The most appropriate strategy is selected using methods like COPRAS5–7.

Due to its numerous criteria and possibilities, a MW method selection challenge may be categorized as a MCDM problem. However, experts who evaluate these kinds of problems can only evaluate the qualitative norms linguistically. MCDM techniques transform these judgments into a numerical structure, even though fuzzy logic can manage the ambiguity in these verbal words8.

Motivation

FFSs are a more flexible extension of classical FSs and PFSs. If and are expressed as intervals rather than single values, an IVFFS is obtained. Linguistic sets, on the other hand, convert linguistic terms (low, medium, high) into numerical form, and this conversion is done not as a single value but as a range of values. If we examine IVFFLSs, we see that these sets are a combination of linguistic variables with a Fermatean structure. IVFFLSs can model uncertainty more flexibly because they simultaneously represent both linguistic uncertainty and Fermatean flexibility. Thanks to the requirement of , they can capture decision-makers’ uncertainty across wide ranges, thus enhancing the level of hesitation. Because different assessments from different experts can be easily integrated across intervals, they are well-suited for group decisions. These explanations form the primary motivation for our study of IVFFLSs. In addition, although FFLSs have been studied, the lack of IVFFLSs is also an important source of motivation.

Concerns regarding human and animal health are reasonable, given the world’s growing population and the rise of pandemic diseases9. One of these difficulties is the proper disposal of the enormous amounts of waste generated by medical establishments such as hospitals and laboratories. Managing the MW generated by healthcare facilities’ operations is one of the most serious difficulties they confront worldwide10. Given the contagious nature of MW, improper disposal of various hazardous goods, such as used Personal Protective Equipment and needles, can be risky. Inadequate management of MW can significantly endanger the community and hasten the spread of numerous pandemic diseases. It is important to think carefully about the issue of MW elimination in this scenario11. This motivation stems from applying the algorithm derived from the new clusters and the aggregation operators to be developed in this study to MW disposal planning.

Necessity

FFS play a vital role in various fields, as it is an advantageous and powerful concept for dealing with uncertain information. Combining FFSs with interval-valued linguistic sets can model the “uncertainty” in human judgments, and decision-makers’ evaluations often cannot be reduced to a single point; uncertainty is more accurately represented as an interval. It would be beneficial to define a new interval-valued linguistic set and its aggregation operators that will more realistically reflect uncertainty, enhance flexible interpretation, and provide better group decision support.

Municipal authorities have struggled in recent years to choose the most appropriate MW disposal strategy to handle MW properly, particularly in developing countries. It is possible to frame this process as a MCDM problem, which entails evaluating many tangible and intangible criteria for MW disposal alternatives and choosing the optimal technique.

However, there are two main challenges in DMs on MW disposal plans: (a) The research’s basis is how to characterize this data because the evolution of MW disposal planning has left decision information ambiguous. Determining objective DMs, allocating attribute weights, and considering decision-makers preferences are critical steps in the MW disposal decision-making process. (b) Even though the multicollinear problem has evolved, the number of variables, attributes, and decision-makers has increased, which must be addressed12.

MW disposal is a complex process. Healthcare personnel need help because there are multiple regulatory bodies, different kinds of waste, and different threats. It should be noted that regulatory bodies are always looking for solutions to management concerns and problems related to MW disposal13. These questions are fundamental: How can we prevent these dangers? How do we overcome the difficulties in avoiding dangers?

Even though there is research on MW disposal planning, the subject’s sensitivity and significance calls for reexamining these studies. It is evident from the data gathered from the literature that healthcare workers require a solid, dependable, and valuable scientific framework that can manage highly complicated uncertainties. Particularly in developing countries, a group of hospital managers, a chief physician, and physicians –most of whom lack expertise in MW management–uses their subjective judgments and opinions to assess the logistical service providers. To stay within their allotted annual budget, hospital management primarily considers economic and financial factors14. As a result, choices about how to dispose of MW might not always be logical and reasonable. Furthermore, practitioners frequently base their conclusions on incomplete and inaccurate information under real-world circumstances. Originality

The environment, employees, patients, and the general public can all be impacted by the MW produced by healthcare services. One of the most important decisions healthcare companies must make is how to dispose of MW. This problem is made more difficult by several conflicting demands and choices. Experts in DM could feel self-conscious when evaluating these options. This research presents new fuzzy-type MCDM techniques for evaluating MW disposal alternatives. For the approach, IVFFLS is identifed, and new operators are developed. The essential characteristics of the defined IVFFLSs and comparison laws were investigated, and score and accuracy functions were determined. Additionally, MCDM processes based on IVFFLS have been developed. An algorithm is presented by giving a scenario explaining the concept of MCDM in IVFFLS.

Research gap

Various types of linguistic sets exist in the literature. The FFLS has been defined and studied in multiple contexts. While interval-valued linguistic sets exist, IVFFLSs have not been reported in the literature. IVFFLSs need to be added to the literature. We must provide their aggregation operators and score-accuracy functions and prove their fundamental properties.

Choosing a suitable and efficient treatment technology is essential in managing MW, which can be considered a classic MCDM problem.

Handling MW is a significant problem, particularly in underdeveloped nations. In this situation, selecting the optimal management technology is complex and unresolved. MW can be disposed of in several ways. It is crucial to evaluate which of the options is the finest. Many factors such as economic, public health, technological, psychological, environmental and operational should all be considered while evaluating each option. Choosing the optimal solution for treating MW is a challenging MCDM problem with various qualitative and quantitative aspects. The suggested methodology tackles the pressing problem of MW disposal planning. This work uses score-accuracy functions based on IVFFLSs and aggregation operations to rate candidates, estimate the associated uncertainty, and maximize their benefits.

The approach established by this study attempts to address the research problems that we see as the following gaps:

1. Which standards will be applied when evaluating the efficacy of the techniques employed in planning MW disposal?

2. Is using IVFFLSs the best option for eliminating MW?

3. Is the MCDM approach, which uses IVFFLS to effectively display fuzzy information, appropriate for resolving the healthcare waste technology selection problem?

4. How consistent are the results in comparison to other well-known MCDM techniques?

We describe a novel integrated FF-based MCDM technique incorporating linguistic term sets and aggregation operators to handle the aforementioned issues accurately.

Contribution

This study proposes a novel, integrated multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework for medical waste (MW) disposal planning in highly uncertain, linguistic evaluation environments. Although fuzzy linguistic sets and their extensions have been widely investigated, existing approaches generally represent uncertainty through linguistic terms, interval-valued uncertainty, or advanced fuzzy membership structures. However, a systematic decision-making framework that simultaneously incorporates linguistic assessment, interval ambiguity, and the Fermatean fuzzy membership–nonmembership structure remains limited. To address this gap, this paper introduces an interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic environment and develops a complete decision-support model.

The significant contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

Introduction of a new uncertainty representation model (IVFFLS). This study formally defines the Interval-Valued Fermatean Fuzzy Linguistic Set (IVFFLS), which integrates linguistic term sets with interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy membership and nonmembership degrees. This representation provides a richer uncertainty space compared with classical fuzzy linguistic sets, intuitionistic fuzzy linguistic sets, and Pythagorean fuzzy linguistic sets, since it preserves expert hesitation and ambiguity through interval bounds while benefiting from the extended expressive capability of the Fermatean structure.

Problems in the actual world are frequently highly intricate. Complexity can be attributed to ambiguities, randomness, or limited understanding caused by a lack of data or low-quality information. Using linguistic terminology to identify the parameters of the problem statement poses the most difficulty. Understanding decision-maker’s knowledge of confusing facts will result in more accurate predictions and valuable solutions. Fuzzy modeling is a mathematical technique for expressing uncertainty in human systems that mainly relies on Zadeh’s enology. Understanding decision-makers knowledge as ambiguous data will result in more exact forecasts and advantageous solutions (Zadeh’s FS concept). Even while the study of partial membership required a significant divergence from conventional logic, it is evident that FS theory cannot determine the satisfaction or dissatisfaction of human judgment on an individual level. Atanassov32 devised the IFSs. Since then, IFSs have been employed by many scholars in a range of optimization-related disciplines. PFSs were created by Yager33. Senapati and Yager34 designed the FFSs. Numerous studies have been undertaken on FS, IFS, and PFS35–37,33. FFS studies quickly became established in the literature34–40. In Ref.41, information from IVFFS is obtained by developing IVFF Dombi weighted averaging (geometric) operators, which utilize Dombi operations that are combined. Seikh and Chatterjee42 introduced an innovative approach using SWARA, BWM, and VIKOR in an IVFF environment to assess the challenges and find effective strategies for e-waste management. In Ref.43, the triangular divergence measure has been utilized to propose a modified distance metric for IVFFSs and a proposed modified TOPSIS method. Seikh and Chatterjee42 presented a new decision-making method combining the SWARA and ARAS methods using IVFFSs. In the paper by Mandal and Seikh44, a hybrid multi-attribute group decision-making methodology is introduced, based on the entropy method, deviation-based method, and MABAC method with IVSF sets, to select the most effective plastic waste management process when the decision experts’ and attribute weights are completely unknown. Chatterjee and Seikh43 developed a robust multi-attribute group decision-making framework that combines circular Fermatean fuzzy numbers with the Schweizer-Sklar weighted aggregation operator, as well as RANCOM, OWCM, and AROMAN, to capture the uncertainty and imprecision in expert judgments.

Atanassov and Gargov45 were the first to describe the IVIFS. The closed subinterval of the interval [0,1] was used to express the degree of membership and nonmembership. Peng and Yang46 and Zhang47 further proposed the IVPFS, whose membership function and nonmembership function are represented by an interval number. Jeevaraj defined the IVFFS48. The theory of IVFFS, which is a generalization of the FFS, is a more solid and trustworthy instrument to deal with the ambiguous and partial data in the MCDM process. As a result, it can be used in more applications49.

These investigations are capable of handling ambiguous data that is limited to quantitative definitions. However, in real life, many DM scenarios occasionally involve qualitative features of portraying imperfect information and uncertainty. Decision-makers, for example, favor using language such as “very low,” “low,” “medium,” “high,” “very high,” “perfect,” etc. when evaluating someone’s “intelligence.” In that case, a decision-maker can use language variables to indicate his thoughts about the objects50. Xu51 proposed a continuous linguistic term set to describe it. By merging the ideas of the linguistic approach and IFS, Zhang52 created the LIFS, which describes the degree of membership and nonmembership reflected by the linguistic terms.

However, because of the ambiguity of human cognition and the complexity of real-world DM situations, it is not easy to characterize the decision information by exact values. In some cases, a qualitative evaluation of numerous criteria is required. For example, since language evaluation closely reflects human cognition, linguistic information helps evaluate a company’s performance when an expert concludes that the performance is excellent. Preliminaries

A mathematical framework called the IVFFS is utilized in DM and modeling situations with imprecision and uncertainty. When exact s are challenging to assign, IVFFS adds more flexibility and expressiveness to regular FSs. It is advantageous in the following situations: IVFFS is frequently used in MCDM problems with ambiguous or imprecise decision criteria. By depicting and as intervals, it facilitates handling subjective assessments. IVFFS offers a framework for modeling and reasoning with interval-valued and when working with datasets that contain inherent uncertainty or partial information. Expert systems and human-centric decision models are examples of systems that can benefit from IVFFS since they depend on human input. Instead of giving exact values, humans frequently give ambiguous or interval-based evaluations. By taking into account interval-valued degrees of agreement and disagreement, IVFFS may compile a variety of viewpoints in scenarios involving several stakeholders or experts. Systems with unclear or ambiguous operating parameters, such as process control or robotics, can be modeled using IVFFS. Applications such as image processing and data clustering that require robust handling of ambiguous or overlapping patterns utilize IVFFS. In fields where data uncertainty is standard, such as sustainability and environmental management, IVFFS facilitates DM.

The following succinctly describes the reasons for selecting IVFFS over alternative fuzzy models: By enabling the expression of , , and hesitation degree as intervals, IVFFS offers a more adaptable structure. Better suited for situations in which exact membership values are not known. Beneficial in situations when ambiguity is a natural part of the situation. IVFFS offers a more adaptable paradigm where DEs lack complete information or have concerns about the veracity of data from several sources. It is particularly favored when it is necessary to communicate the uncertainty in expert opinions in intervals. IVFFS provides a more adaptable representation for every criterion when many criteria need to be assessed. In situations when human judgments or preferences are ambiguous, working with interval values yields more realistic outcomes. Intervals can more accurately portray fluctuating situations when information is ambiguous because of a system’s time-varying nature. IVFFS simulates uncertain data in data analysis procedures, intensive learning, and artificial intelligence systems that rely on fuzzy logic. Interval values can be used to express uncertainty in systems that operate with incomplete data. IVFFS applies when a thorough expression of uncertainty is required in financial analysis, project management, or security risk assessment.

IVFFS offers a strong mathematical framework for handling complex uncertainty by fusing the benefits of interval-valued representations and FFSs, which permit larger ranges for and than IFSs. This strategy makes more robust and trustworthy decisions possible, enabling DEs to communicate and manage uncertainty across a broader spectrum.

Methodology

Interval-valued fermatean linguistic sets

IVFFLS provides a hybrid information representation that goes far beyond classical fuzzy set theory, modeling uncertainties more richly. The fundamental pillars of this structure are:

Fermatean Fuzzy Sets: Offer a structure in which the sum of the cubes of membership and non-membership degrees is constrained to . This extension defines a broader range of uncertainty than classical and intuitive fuzzy sets.

Interval-Valued Structure: By expressing a value as an interval rather than a single definite number, it directly accounts for decision-makers’ hesitations and uncertainties.

Linguistic Representation: Mental evaluations of human experts are often expressed using linguistic terms such as “high,” “medium,” and “low.” IVFFLS transforms these expressions into a consistent mathematical model.

Combining these three components creates not only a theoretical extension but also an uncertainty model that has not yet been systematically addressed in the literature. Such a combination allows for a more flexible and realistic representation of uncertainty in decision models.

IVFFLS models uncertainties more powerfully. In classical fuzzy systems, uncertainty is modeled with a single membership value. This may not fully reflect the experts’ mental uncertainty. In IVFFLS, however, the expert provides the linguistic term “high,” which is then converted into an interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy structure. Both hesitation and the interval of uncertainty represent the result. This allows for more accurate modeling of expert uncertainty, especially in multi-criteria and risk-intensive decision problems such as medical waste management. Single-valued memberships cannot adequately represent the nature of real-life expert statements.

In a decision support method, the higher the expressive power, the better it can reflect the decision-maker’s intentions, uncertainty, risk perception, and expert hesitations. IVFLS, by increasing linguistic ambiguity to a “range” level, utilizing the wide ambiguity field of the Fermatean structure, and preserving the mathematical representation of linguistic expressions, transfers the assessments of human experts into the mathematical model with much higher expressive power. This directly reflects what decision-makers are actually thinking, especially in cases such as medical waste disposal. Thus, the model reduces errors caused by uncertainty.

Let us explain the innovative contributions of IVFFLS. First, let us examine the theoretical contribution. IVFFLS introduces a new class of uncertainty that integrates both linguistic evaluation and interval ambiguity. The use of fermatean structures allows for the modeling of a broader membership-non-membership relationship. There is no systematic theory in the literature that brings these three components together under a single framework. Secondly, let us look at the methodological contribution. New aggregation operators and score/accuracy functions suitable for IVFFLS have been defined. These operators enable more efficient uncertainty measurement. Finally, let us look at the practical contribution. It produces more realistic and usable results for multi-criteria, risk-intensive problems based on linguistic expert statements, such as medical waste disposal methods. It improves the quality of decision-making for healthcare administrators and public authorities (especially under uncertain conditions).

When comparing IVFFLS with other models, it is easy to see that IVFFLS is not just an extension: IVFFLS provides a much wider range of expressions than classical FFLS. IVFFLS takes into account hesitation/interval ambiguity (classical heuristics or fermatean sets cannot do this). The operators and score functions developed with IVFFLS offer new features not found in the literature.

IVFFLS is not merely an extension of traditional fuzzy set theory; it is a new class of uncertainty that offers a systematic and robust model of information representation, combining interval ambiguity of linguistic expressions with Fermatean extended membership relations. This model provides a representational capability that significantly surpasses the expressive power of classical approaches by directly accounting for expert hesitation in multi-criteria decision problems.

Aggregation operators

Let us examine the fundamental role of aggregation operators. In multi-criteria decision-making processes, a set of criteria is evaluated; decisions are made by transforming values obtained from different sources into a single “composite decision value.” Aggregation operators perform this combining process. However, classical aggregations are based on fixed numerical values, may not directly handle linguistic ambiguities, and do not consider expert hesitation or interval representation.

In the IVFFLS environment, aggregation operators take the criterion values as linguistic terms interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy numbers an ambiguous and wide range of expressions. Therefore, classical weighted summation or averaging operators are insufficient. New aggregation operators suitable for the IVFFLS environment not only account for numerical values but also consistently consider linguistic ambiguities, hesitation intervals, and the wide ambiguity field of the Fermatean fuzzy structure, thereby giving the decision model mathematical meaning.

In this section, we will explain the innovative role of aggregation operators, whose definitions and theoretical structures will be provided:

They enable combining uncertainty with mathematical consistency. In IVFFLS, each criterion evaluation is given linguistically by an expert. These linguistic terms (e.g. “excellent,” “average,” “low”) are then converted into IVFF numbers. However, conversion alone does not eliminate uncertainty. What is then needed is to weight these interval values and consolidate multiple expert opinions into a single result. The aggregation operators developed specifically here, beyond classical summation and averaging, preserve the membership/external membership power of the Fermatean fuzzy structure, do not disrupt interval ambiguity with operations, and mathematize the expressive power of linguistic terms. Therefore, these operators produce not only summation but also consistent information synthesis.

Simply summing the numerical values will not be meaningful. Many traditional methods sum numerical values, take weighted averages, and normalize criterion scores. However, the information in IVFFLS is expressed in both interval form and in terms of Fermatean fuzzy membership/external membership, and it also contains linguistic ambiguity. Therefore, classical summation distorts this expressive power and does not adequately reflect expert uncertainty. Aggregation operators, therefore, offer a combination operation that prevents information loss.

At the same time, summation is performed with integrated weights. In MCDM, criterion weights express the relative importance of the criteria. In the IVFFLS environment, the degree of uncertainty of each criterion may be different; some criteria may be expressed more “clearly,” while others may be more “hesitant,” and the weight multiplier interacts not only with numerical values but also with uncertainty ranges. The aggregation operators proposed in this study process criterion weights in an uncertain environment and calculate the weighted sum in a way consistent with Fermatean linguistic expressions, thereby effectively reflecting both weight and uncertainty simultaneously. This is a step beyond classical weighted summation.

The new operators provide a solid foundation for scoring and rating. In MCDM methods like COPRAS, the final ranking is done by combining criterion scores, calculating positive/negative effect sums, and normalizing them. Without aggregation operators, the weighting balance between criteria is inconsistent, uncertainty transfer is lost, and comparable results cannot be obtained in the literature. However, this study, using aggregation operators specifically defined for IVFFLS, produces reliable scores that combine both positive and negative criteria while preserving uncertainty, hesitation, and linguistic expressiveness. Thus, decision-makers see not only the ranking result but also more stable results at different levels of uncertainty.

The theoretical significance of these new operators can be explained as follows: Many aggregation operators have been developed in the literature for classical numerical environments, and for intuitionistic fuzzy or Pythagorean fuzzy environments. However, measurable, mathematically consistent aggregation operators for IVFFLS have remained mainly undefined. This study introduces a “new class of operators” in the literature by defining aggregation operators that both support the IVFFLS environment and can be used in MCDM processes. This is not merely an extension, but a new information synthesis methodology.

The practical benefits of aggregation operators can be summarized as follows: they accurately combine different levels of uncertainty among experts, synthesize diverse expert opinions into a single model, preserve the linguistic ambiguity ranges of criteria, and prevent the exclusion of ambiguity from the model.

The proposed aggregation operators are not merely mathematical tools that combine linguistic expressions; they offer a novel information synthesis methodology that consistently integrates ambiguity, hesitation, and the Fermatean fuzzy information structure into MCDM processes. These operators enable the efficient evaluation of critical criterion weights in an uncertain environment and significantly enhance the expressive power and reliability of the decision model, going beyond classical aggregations.

Proposed method

We can explain the novelty and importance of the method we will present in this section as follows:

Let us answer the question, “Why is such a method needed?” In high-risk decision problems like medical waste disposal, decision-makers make linguistic assessments instead of precise numerical data (“high risk,” “medium cost,” etc.), assessments often involve interval ambiguity (uncertainty about whether it is 0.6 or 0.8), there is simultaneous consideration of both membership and non-membership (an alternative might be good from an environmental perspective but bad from a cost perspective). Differences and hesitations are seen among expert opinions.

Classical MCDM methods (AHP, TOPSIS, COPRAS, etc.) generally work with precise values and therefore cannot fully represent this uncertainty. Classical fuzzy or intuitive fuzzy models also have limitations in terms of expressive power in some cases. This study addresses precisely this gap in the literature and meets the need for a decision model that provides a stronger representation in decision problems involving “linguistic, interval, and high-degree ambiguity.”

The novelty of the method and its contribution to uncertainty representation lie in its not addressing uncertainty at only one layer. First, there is linguistic representation. Linguistic terms are the most natural form of information for decision-makers. This study directly incorporates decision-makers’ natural expressions into the model. When using an interval-valued structure, the expert’s “high” evaluation is not a single value, so the question “high, but how high?” remains ambiguous. The interval-valued structure incorporates this hesitation into the model. A wider range of uncertainty can be examined with a fermatean fuzzy structure. The fermatean fuzzy approach offers a broader range of expression than classical intuitionistic or Pythagorean models. Especially in problems with high-risk and contradictory criteria, this broader range offers a more suitable mathematical framework for the decision-maker’s mental evaluation. As a result, the method combines uncertainty in three layers: linguistic, interval, and FFS. This combination is the fundamental basis of the study’s innovation.

The methodological innovation lies in the operators and score functions. The innovation in this study lies not only in the data implementation but also in establishing the mathematical infrastructure required for COPRAS to function within the IVFLS environment. In this context, new aggregation operators have been defined, their fundamental properties have been provided, consistent processing rules have been developed to combine linguistic-interval-FFS information, and score/accuracy functions have been used to sort IVFLS values. This section constitutes the theoretical backbone demonstrating that the method is not merely an “implementation.”

The adaptation of COPRAS to the IVFLS environment is also an innovation. The COPRAS method typically uses a sum of benefit criteria, a sum of cost criteria, and relative importance and performance indices. However, for COPRAS to be applicable in the IVFLS environment, it is necessary to maintain range uncertainty, process membership/non-membership values together, and consistently convert linguistic data into numerical values for calculation. Therefore, this study is not merely about “using” COPRAS, but about restructuring COPRAS in a new uncertainty environment. This is a significant methodological contribution.

Given the method’s practical importance and its suitability for critical decision-making problems, such as medical waste management, it is evident. Problems such as selecting medical waste disposal methods simultaneously involve criteria including environmental risk, human health risk, cost, legal compliance, and technological feasibility, and there is significant conflict among them. In such problems, classical numerical models ignore uncertainty, oversimplify expert evaluation, and compromise the realism of the results. The proposed method, because it operates without excluding uncertainty, improves decision quality, produces more reliable rankings, and provides the decision-maker with more explainable results. Therefore, the method has high value not only theoretically but also practically.

If we examine the significance of the results, we see that more reliable rankings and robustness are obtained. One of the strongest arguments supporting novelty in such studies will be the answer to the question, “Does the proposed method produce more stable results under different uncertainty conditions?” If the study includes sensitivity analysis, comparative analysis, or ranking comparisons across different methods, this underscores the importance of the method. In this way, the method can be positioned not only as “new,” but also as a framework that provides “more robust” decision support.

In terms of contribution to the literature, a new decision-making framework is important. The point to emphasize here is that this study not only proposes a new set but also a complete MCDM framework, including set definition, operators, score functions, a COPRAS-based decision algorithm, implementation, and analysis. This is a significant contribution to the literature in the “method development” category.

In summary, this study is not merely a simple extension of existing fuzzy linguistic sets; it presents a novel IVFFLS-based decision-making framework that combines interval-valued uncertainty, Fermatean fuzzy membership structure, and linguistic evaluation under one roof. Furthermore, unique aggregation operators and sorting mechanisms have been developed to solve decision problems in this environment, and an integrated MCDM approach has been created by adapting the COPRAS method to the IVFFLS environment. In this respect, the study provides a significant contribution to filling the gap in uncertainty representation in the literature, both theoretically and practically.

COPRAS method

COPRAS is often used to rank alternatives based on specific criteria. It is particularly popular in engineering, management, supply chain, and sustainability. It is ratio-based. Thus, evaluating alternatives is based on the proportional effect of the normalized values and the criteria weights for each criterion. It distinguishes between advantage and disadvantage criteria. In other words, benefit (to be maximized) and cost (to be minimized) criteria are considered separately. It ranks the alternatives. In the final stage, a “priority rating” is calculated for each alternative, with the highest value being the best option. It has several advantages: It considers both benefit and cost criteria. The calculation steps are relatively simple. It provides a precise ranking among alternatives. It is particularly effective when uncertainty is low and numerical data is available.

Instead of the precise numerical values used in classical COPRAS, the technique obtained when IVFFS are used to process evaluations expressed in uncertain, linguistic, or interval form is IVFS-COPRAS. This new technique makes it easier to capture uncertainty. It produces more reliable results in situations where decision-makers are uncertain. It easily processes linguistic data. Concepts such as “high quality” and “low cost” are quantified, resulting in a more flexible model. It produces more realistic results than the classic COPRAS technique because it accounts for hesitancy in decision-making environments.

Application and results

Waste management is a crucial aspect of healthcare. When MW is not adequately managed, there is a danger of infections, adverse effects, injuries, and environmental contamination for waste handlers, the general public, and healthcare professionals. Managing MW needs considerable attention and fidelity to avoid detrimental health impacts from inadequate practices, such as exposure to toxic compounds and infectious pathogens57. Government support and dedication are necessary for universal, long-lasting, and sustained progress2. MW affects the ecosystem because it carries infectious diseases that could spread to other species. These MWs put hospital staffs, at risk for infection, which could harm their health. Planning for MW management is a key issue that must consider several critical aspects as well as practical solutions58.

One of the most often used disposal techniques is incineration since it is practical, safe, and easy to utilize, particularly in developing countries59. The temperature at the incinerator’s exit is higher than 800 C. This high temperature can potentially burn most organic materials, converting them into inorganic dust and eliminating germs. Solid waste could be decreased by 85–90% after incineration60, 61, 62. MWs other than explosive and radioactive wastes could be burnt. In addition to burning hospital waste, several facilities provide waste processing, incineration, and flue gas purification. Standard incineration technologies include pyrolysis vaporization, plasma, and rotary kilns.

Chemical disinfection has been around for a while and has several uses. Mechanical and chemical crushing procedures are commonly applied to hospital trash. Crushed hospital waste is typically combined with chemical disinfectants (such as calcium, sodium, or chlorine dioxide) and given the proper time to sit. Chemical disinfectants are excellent at eliminating bacteria and germs because of their broad sterilizing range, fast action, consistency, and low effective concentration. Chemical disinfection techniques may be considered when hospital waste volumes are small.

Garbage is immobilized through encapsulation, which covers the waste in a solid matrix. The preferred technique for managing low- and intermediate-level radioactive waste in the nuclear industry has long been encapsulation in cement or its composites. Medical sciences use encapsulation in polyethylene or iron barrels partially filled with inert fillers such as plastic foam, bituminous sand, lime, cement mortar, or clay before disposing them in landfills or geological locations.

When burned garbage is thrown into the environment, it releases phthalate and other heavy metals, including lead, cadmium, and tin, and the dioxin created during cremation. Before being burned, MW is separated from other municipal solid waste. Not only is it detrimental to the environment, but sending MW to incineration is more expensive than sending it to landfills. Landfill disposal is another option for MW treatment, not necessarily an alternative to incineration. However, it is against the law to dump biohazardous waste. Landfilling MW is still common in many areas, especially in developing nations. MW is separated in a landfill cell using this method.

Microwave technology uses steam and wet heat as a low-heat, steam-based thermal disinfection method. Some people use infrared heaters, forced convection, or heated air circulation to heat the waste. Because of this, reverse polymerization occurs between 177 and 540 C in microwave technology. High-energy microwaves break down organic molecules. Electromagnetic waves (wavelength 1 mm-1 m, frequency 300–3000 MHz) induce the bonds between molecules to vibrate or rub against one another, increasing internal energy. In contrast to high-temperature disinfection, oxygen cannot burn in the N2 atmosphere. This reduced energy and temperature prevent heat loss and pollution of the environment because the disinfection process leaves behind a nontoxic residue.

Discussion

Incineration using MCDM was the most suitable MW Disposal technique. In this case, it would be appropriate to discuss the Burning technique in general.

One of the few solutions that can effectively handle all MW is controlled incineration at high temperatures (over 1000C). It offers the benefit of drastically lowering the weight and volume of the treated wastes.

In some instances, hydrochloric acid (which contributes to acid rain), dioxins, furans, and other harmful airborne pollutants are produced, especially when garbage is burned at low temperatures (less than 800C) or when polymers containing polyvinyl chloride are incinerated. Emissions, residual ash, other airborne ash, and effluent gases emitted by incinerator chimneys contain them. Public health may be harmed by exposure to dioxins, furans, and other coplanar polychlorinated biphenyls. Dioxins and furans can occur in more relaxed pockets at the start or finish of the combustion process, even in high-temperature incinerators (over 800C). Suppose, for instance, that incineration occurs only at temperatures above 800C and that the formation of combustion gases is inhibited at 200–400C. In that case, process optimization can lessen the formation of these compounds.

Modern large-scale processing facilities, including high-temperature incinerators, are not a good option for hospitals in impoverished nations. We should investigate whether such infrastructure exists in the area. Using a home waste incinerator is an additional option. Typically, this type of plant operates at temperatures exceeding 850C. However, we must supply MW straight into the kiln hopper instead of going through the bunker. Although not usually advised for MW incineration (because of an unsecured waste loading system and untreated emissions), cement works incinerators or blast furnaces used in the metal industry can also offer a suitable local alternative. Simple incinerator models are available for handling modest amounts of MW. Some can be purchased, while others need to be constructed on-site using locally sourced materials, with relatively straightforward blueprints. A discharge chimney and one or two combustion chambers (the primary and secondary chambers) make up these incinerators. The method for controlling combustion and airborne emissions is straightforward; however, it may not always exist67.

Assume infectious MW is handled on-site in tiny, single- or dual-chamber incinerators. In that instance, this kind of facility cannot treat waste fractions like medications, chemicals, halogenated materials, or wastes with a high concentration of heavy metals (such as batteries, broken thermometers, etc.). This study includes an MCDM algorithm based on IVFFL evaluations. Because MCDM results can be susceptible to criterion weights, it is necessary to see how the ranking changes in different scenarios where the weights are changed. Monte-Carlo weight sensitivity analysis does precisely this. Monte Carlo analysis is a global sensitivity analysis performed to model the uncertainty in criterion weights. The aim is to answer the question, “To what extent does the final ranking remain constant when the criterion weights change?” In this case, the weights are sampled using the Dirichlet distribution. The Dirichlet distribution is used to ensure that all weights are positive, that their sum is 1 (normalized), that the weights are equally likely to occur with a given parameter , and to control for sparseness or density.

We can explain what is done in each Monte-Carlo example as follows: A random weight vector is generated from the Dirichlet() distribution. For , a uniform random distribution (equal chance for all weight combinations) is found, while for , sparse/more extreme weights are found (e.g. some criteria are minimal, some are very large). Further, when , the weights are balanced, and the weights are close to equality. Using the generated weight vector, alternatives include reaggregation using the IVFFLWA operator in Theorem 4.8, calculating the score using the SC(D) score function (Eq. 9) in Definition 4.4, and obtaining the score ranking for each sample. This process is repeated N times (e.g. ), and the results are combined. The highest score obtained with the score function indicates the best alternative. This process is repeated 3k, 5k, and 10k times, and each sample generates new weights, new IVFFL aggregation, new scores, and a new ranking. Comparison

The continuum of selecting and evaluating alternatives from a small or big pool based on relevant characteristics is known as MCDM. As part of MCDM, a wide range of options are assessed based on several criteria. MCDM methods are implemented to help DMs find the best and most desirable solution to these types of problems. As a result, researchers have initiated many MCDM strategies. We experimented with two MCDM techniques in the FFS framework. To confirm and correct the findings of the offered method, we contrasted the results with the recommended system.

An analysis will be conducted to verify the validity of the suggested methodology. The methods used in research53,68–70 and Ref.71 will be applied to the data from the example given in Sect. 5.

In Ref.53, the concept of FFLSs is introduced, and its basic properties are examined. New score and accuracy functions are also presented. The study also introduces aggregation operators and distance measures for FFLSs (Definitions are in the Appendix.).

Du et al.’s work68 has defined IVPFLSs with a new weighted distance based on IVPFSs. This work presents IVPFLS-based aggregation operators and examines their fundamental properties, such as monotonicity, commutativity, and idempotency (Definitions are in the Appendix.).

Garg and Kumar69 developed a new method by integrating LIFS with set pair analysis methods and defined the linguistic connection number. This study examines various operational laws for linguistic connection numbers and proposes new aggregation operators (Definitions are in the Appendix.).

Mandal and Seikh70 defined new computational rules and score and accuracy functions for an MADM approach in the IVFF environment. After introducing a new distance measure, they employed the TOPSIS technique to solve the MADM problem in the IVFF environment. They also used the Fermate fuzzy entropy method to determine feature weights.

Mandal and Seikh71 presented a score function developed in the q-step orthodouble fuzzy (q-ROF) environment and identified several suitable properties of the given score function. They used the new score function to establish a q-ROF EDAS method for solving MADM problems. For comparison, we will use q = 3 when using this method.

A comparison analysis will be performed to ensure that the offered technique is valid. The data from the example presented in Section 5 will be analyzed using the techniques found in studies53, 68–70 and Ref.71.

Advantages of method

When total squares are equal to or less than 1, PFS is defined by the degree of satisfaction that members and nonmembers have with the criteria. The expert rarely gives the and a particular property so that the sum of the squares is greater than 1. The PFS cannot effectively handle this occurrence. FFS is one of the most comprehensive methods for overcoming this limitation because it can deal with partially unknown and inconsistent data, which are common in real-world scenarios.

It may be inferred from the current investigation and comparison that the outcomes of the offered strategy overlap with those of the available techniques. The primarily benefit of the suggested method over easily accessible DM techniques, however, is that it incorporates additional data and tackles data uncertainty by considering the membership and nonmembership of subattributes considered in the characteristics. The item’s information might be examined more accurately and objectively. In the DM process, it is also a valuable technique for handling inaccurate and imprecise data. As a result, the expected information loss will occur since the purpose behind the score value given to one parameter will not affect the other values.

Conversely, there is no discernible loss of information with our proposed method. By detecting the degree of discrimination and similarity between observations, the intended methodology has an advantage over existing methods, stopping incorrect judgments from being formed. Combining inaccurate and ambiguous information is helpful in the DM process.

Limitations

There are still various issues with this study. First, there is a difference between uncertainty and risk. This study primarily focuses on the effects of risk choice instead of uncertainty preference. Risk aversion is an essential form of uncertainty aversion because it can be challenging to pinpoint the precise likelihood of a MW disposal service and its technology. This work operationalized risk preference using prospect theory. However, a more thorough survey must be developed to identify potential problematic aspects of MW disposal. As a result, the benefit and loss domains associated with MW disposal could not be identified. Future studies should concentrate on integrating specific MW disposal risk indicators with general risk preference standards.

Notwithstanding the benefits of the proposed IVFF-based technique, its application in particular DM scenarios is constrained by its inability to evaluate the options thoroughly. Furthermore, when there are many criteria and possibilities, creating IVFFSs might become difficult. To get over these restrictions, we want to broaden our research into the following areas:

The applicability of the suggested method is more comprehensive than remanufacturing challenges. To expand its application range, we plan to examine more complex disease management scenarios in life, including business, construction management, and medicine.

Implications

The adverse effects of its unplanned disposal are making MW a serious environmental concern. Medical facilities and healthcare providers need to understand the adverse impact of MW and take appropriate steps to mitigate it. Given the urgent need to dispose of MW and the currently accessible treatment techniques, this study offers a method to help health workers decide which MW disposal options to retain. The study’s implications for healthcare management are as follows: the techniques for MW disposal analysis provide a quick and reliable means of evaluating potential options72. Administrators and practitioners in the healthcare sector may find this very helpful. By using the practical, straightforward techniques provided, healthcare facilities can manage the challenge of choosing a waste-disposal strategy. The suggested methods provide a reliable and valuable means of quickly assessing MW disposal options within this framework. This aids healthcare administrators and practitioners by prioritizing feasible MW disposal alternatives based on the necessary components and resources available to that hospital unit.

Our understanding of MW disposal planning, both theoretically and practically, is improved by this study. MW is a significant cause of health and environmental problems73. Medical device adoption has also increased MW, especially among individuals and healthcare professionals. Globally, even with a strong infrastructure, controlling MWe poses significant challenges. The study’s conclusions thus provide a theoretical perspective on the problem of MW and how to address it. The risk analysis in this paper connects the main challenges and complexity of sustainable MW disposal planning. Managerial insights

The interval-valued Fermatean fuzzy linguistic COPRAS-based decision support model developed within the scope of this study offers significant managerial implications for healthcare administrators and local governments in decision problems with high levels of uncertainty, such as the selection of medical waste disposal technology.

(1) More reliable decision-making by representing uncertainty more realistically

Hospital administrators and municipal/waste management authorities often evaluate criteria not with precise numerical values, but with linguistic expressions such as “high cost,” “medium risk,” and “very safe.” The proposed IVFFLS-based model, rather than reducing these linguistic evaluations to a single point, treats them as intervals, directly reflecting decision-makers’ hesitations during the decision-making process. Thus, decisions become more realistic and reliable compared to classical fuzzy or exact methods.

(2) More balanced strategic evaluation among conflicting criteria

In medical waste disposal, criteria such as cost, environmental impact, public health risk, technical feasibility, and legal compliance often conflict with each other. This study, thanks to its COPRAS-based approach, simultaneously evaluates both benefit and cost criteria, preventing managers from making erroneous decisions that focus solely on cost, and thus supporting a more balanced strategic choice.

(3) Clearly showing the strengths and weaknesses of alternatives

Due to the structure of the COPRAS method, the positive (benefit) and negative (cost) aspects of each alternative are calculated separately. Conclusion

This study proposed a comprehensive DM framework grounded in IVFFLs, a newly developed mathematical structure designed to model complex forms of linguistic uncertainty. By establishing the algebraic foundations and ordering principles of this framework, introducing a wide family of tailored aggregation operators, and formulating an effective score function, the study provides a complete methodological toolkit for MCDM problems characterized by ambiguity, hesitation, and conflicting expert judgments.

The application to the medical waste disposal problem demonstrated the practical relevance and robustness of the proposed approach. The resulting ranking is stable across diverse parameter conditions, as confirmed through extensive robustness checks, sensitivity analysis, and comparison with the IVFFL–COPRAS method. The consistency of the results relative to established fuzzy and classical DM techniques further validates the reliability and interpretability of the proposed model.

Overall, the study advances the frontier of fuzzy MCDM by integrating interval-valued reasoning with Fermatean fuzzy linguistic modeling, enabling a more nuanced representation of expert knowledge. The findings highlight the potential of the proposed framework to support decision-making in domains where linguistic vagueness and hesitation are inevitable. Future research may extend this framework to group decision-making environments, dynamic decision processes, or hybrid models that incorporate probabilistic or evidential reasoning.
