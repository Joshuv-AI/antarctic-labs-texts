Targeted FcRn to deliver epitope-optimized RBD confers broad-spectrum mucosal protection against SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Xinlan Chen, Yan Wu, Yanhai Wang, Chenshu Zhao, Lijuan Fang, Xuerui Zhu, Jia Lu, Xiao Gao, Zhongfa Zhang, Haoyu Li, Yingrui Yan, Pengfei Zhou, Xiaoyan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current respiratory vaccines face two major obstacles: limited breadth of protection and insufficient induction of mucosal immunity. Here, we present a neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-targeted mucosal delivery strategy integrating computational antigen design to address both challenges. Using severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) as a model virus, we employed Epigraph to generate T cell epitope-optimized consensus sequences for the receptor-binding domain (RBD) and incorporated them into a modified human IgG1 Fc framework engineered for enhanced FcRn binding, yielding a single fusion antigen. In vitro, this antigen efficiently binds FcRn, facilitating epithelial transcytosis and prolonging mucosal retention. In vivo, it elicits robust cellular, humoral, and mucosal immune responses against SARS-CoV-2 and its major variants in both respiratory and systemic compartments, with evidence of tissue-resident memory responses. Following intranasal administration, the antigen conferred complete cross-protection against lethal challenge with representative SARS-CoV-2 and its variants. Notably, a low antigen dose of 0.2 μg in a two-dose regimen achieved full protection. Immunomics further revealed that this design induces a broader T and B cell repertoire. Collectively, this study establishes a generalizable framework that combines epitope-optimized antigen design with FcRn-mediated mucosal delivery, providing a promising strategy for broad-spectrum vaccine development against rapidly evolving respiratory viruses.

Introduction

Although severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has evolved from a highly pathogenic virus with an approximately 3% fatality rate into a seasonal influenza-like virus that primarily attacks the upper respiratory tract,1,2,3 its acute infection is associated with long COVID symptoms affecting the lungs and the brain, manifesting as lung nodules, fatigue, loss of smell and taste, brain fog, and psychological abnormalities,4 which have not been fully evaluated and recognized. In consideration of the rapid spread and ongoing mutation of SARS-CoV-2, it remains unclear how the virus will continue to evolve and whether novel coronaviruses may emerge in the near future. Therefore, vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 as well as related technologies remain matters worthy of attention. Current COVID-19 vaccines face two major limitations: frequent breakthrough infections occur largely due to the narrow antigenic coverage5,6 and limited transmission-blocking efficacy due to insufficient induction of respiratory mucosal immune responses.7 Addressing these issues through antigen-design strategies will be critical for controlling SARS-CoV-2 as well as emerging respiratory pathogens.

To address rapid viral mutation and immune escape, our study employed both computational antigen design and protein engineering rather than the commonly used simple mixing or tandem-fusion strategies.8,9,10 Among computational approaches, the Epigraph algorithm offers a scalable framework for optimizing T cell epitope coverage across large viral sequence datasets. Its core principle is to maximize inclusion of high-frequency potential T cell epitopes within the mutational landscape while minimizing incorporation of rare or low-frequency epitopes. Unlike traditional mosaic algorithms,11 Epigraph’s graph-based framework efficiently processes large-scale sequence data and enables rapid generation of consensus antigen sequences that preserve key immunological features. This computational method provides a practical strategy for rapidly generating optimized consensus antigens targeting highly variable respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses.

To establish local immunity in the respiratory tract, various strategies have been developed, mainly through in situ antigen delivery or by leveraging the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which can transport the IgG1 Fc-fused antigens across epithelial barriers to distal sites through transcytosis.12 Additionally, FcRn-mediated recycling prolongs IgG1 half-life and is expected to similarly prolong the persistence of Fc-fused antigens, allowing slower antigen release and enhancing immunogenicity. Our previous studies,13,14,15,16 along with the work by Zhu et al.,17 have demonstrated that Fc-fused receptor-binding domains (RBDs) induce strong mucosal immunity and effectively block viral transmission.

Inspired by antibody scaffolding strategies, our previous study presents a versatile IgG1 heavy-chain-based scaffold by modifying both the Fab and Fc domains and displays natural coronavirus RBDs in bivalent and tetravalent formats.16 Here, we provide an example using the trivalent RBD antigen design incorporating in-silico-optimized RBDs, aiming to evaluate whether computationally designed antigens can be effectively integrated into this FcRn-targeted vaccine framework and achieve broad protection. Using combined in vitro and in vivo assays, the epitope-optimized trivalent RBD antigen was characterized with respect to FcRn binding, mucosal penetration, and half-life extension. In BALB/c mice, it elicited enhanced cellular and mucosal immune responses, while studies in human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2)-transgenic mice demonstrated broad-spectrum protection against representative SARS-CoV-2 and its variants. Immunomic profiling, including analyses of T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR) repertoires, provided additional insights into the immune responses induced by this vaccine design. Overall, this study integrates Epigraph-based antigen optimization with FcRn-mediated delivery, offering a promising antigen candidate for SARS-CoV-2 and other emerging respiratory viruses.

Results

Optimized RBDs broaden T cell epitopes without compromising spatial conformation

The Epigraph algorithm was applied to analyze 3,997 high-quality, non-redundant SARS-CoV-2 RBD sequences from the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID) database by February 2024. Three consensus sequences, namely Epigraph-0 (EG0), Epigraph-1 (EG1), and Epigraph-2 (EG2), were subsequently generated. To examine their evolutionary relationships with the SARS-CoV-2 variants, we constructed a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree, which showed that the three consensus sequences were distributed across different sublineages covering all six recently defined SARS-CoV-2 serotypes18 (Figure 1A). Sequence similarity analysis with 20 representative variants revealed that EG0 maintained high similarity across all variants, indicating its greater conservation within the highly variable RBD sequences (Figures 1B and S1A). According to Epigraph predictions, EG0 alone achieved 75% epitope coverage, increasing to 92% when allowing a single amino acid mismatch. To assess T cell epitope optimization, MHC class I and II epitopes were predicted for EG0 + EG1 + EG2 using NetMHCpan-4.1, with the prototype (PT) RBD sequence as the reference. EG0 alone did not substantially increase the number of predicted epitopes relative to the PT, whereas EG0 + EG1 and EG0 + EG1 + EG2 enhanced both strong- and weak-binding MHC class I/II epitopes by approximately 2- and 3-fold, respectively (Figure 1D). Analysis of the top 100 strong-binding MHC class I/II epitopes showed that EG0 and the PT exhibited similar epitope distributions, characterized by high diversity but heterogeneous binding affinities, whereas EG0 + EG1 + EG2 preserved epitope diversity while being markedly enriched for high-affinity epitopes (Figures 1E and S1B).

For B cell epitope evaluation, we used AlphaFold3 to predict the monomeric structures of EG0, EG1, and EG2, using the reported RBD structure (PDB: 8SGU) as a reference (Figure 1F). All three consensus RBDs exhibited high structural similarity to the native RBD, with root-mean-square deviations (RMSDs) of 0.466, 0.453, and 0.536, respectively, suggesting that conformational B cell epitopes might be largely preserved.

Design, preparation, and characterization of the antigens based on the optimized RBDs

To further broaden the protective spectrum and promote mucosal protection, we incorporated EG0, EG1, and EG2 into a modified human IgG1 heavy chain with several modifications. Specifically, the L234A, L235A, and G237A mutations were introduced to silence FcγR-mediated effector functions—including antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC)—thereby reducing potential adverse effects. Meanwhile, the M252Y, S254T, and T256E mutations were introduced to extend FcRn-mediated half-life. By embedding EG0, EG1, and EG2 into this framework, the bivalent EG01-Fc and trivalent EG0120-Fc constructs were successfully generated (Figure 2A). AlphaFold3 predictions indicated that EG0120-Fc adopts the expected conformation without steric hindrance between the RBDs and the Fc domain, leaving all relevant regions exposed (Figure 2B). Reduced and non-reduced sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) analyses confirmed that EG0, EG1, and EG2 exist as monomers with a molecular weight of ∼35 kDa, whereas EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc form dimers of ∼180 kDa (Figures 2C and S2B). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays revealed that all three consensus sequences bind hACE2, with EG0 exhibiting higher affinity (0.900 nM) than the native PT RBD (3.424 nM). EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc further increased the binding affinity to 0.797 and 0.266 nM, respectively (Figure 2D). Flow cytometry analysis using human (hFcRn)- and mouse (mFcRn)-overexpressing cell lines demonstrated dose-dependent binding of EG0120-Fc, with stronger binding to hFcRn than to mFcRn (Figure 2E). Together, these results indicate that the antigens generated from this platform display the expected conformations and are suitable for further investigation.

EG0120-Fc penetrates mucosal epithelial barrier and extends half-life

To assess Fc-mediated mucosal epithelial barrier penetration, a polarized mFcRn- and hFcRn-expressing Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell monolayer transwell model was employed (Figure 3A). EG0120-Fc efficiently crossed to the basolateral side in a time-dependent manner, compared with the non-Fc RBD, which exhibited only minimal late-phase leakage, indicating that Fc enables effective epithelial barrier penetration via FcRn-mediated transcytosis. Next, AF647-labeled EG0120-Fc and AF647-labeled non-Fc PT RBD dimers were administered intranasally to the mouse respiratory tract to assess their ability to penetrate the mucosal barrier in vivo (Figure 3B). EG0120-Fc rapidly localized to the bronchial surface and subsequently distributed into the lung parenchyma, with fluorescence intensity peaking at approximately 3 h and remaining readily detectable at 72 h. In contrast, the non-Fc PT RBD dimer exhibited rapid signal decay after entering the lung, with almost no visible fluorescence by 72 h. Moreover, antigen persistence in the mouse lung was evaluated by intranasal administration of equal amounts (10 μg) of Cy5-labeled EG0120-Fc or EG0120-Fc (I253A, H310A, and H435A [designated IHH]), followed by in vivo imaging to monitor antigen attenuation (Figure 3C). EG0120-Fc (IHH) was used as a control, as it carries the I253A, H310A, and H435A mutations, which have been reported to abolish FcRn binding.19 In vivo imaging revealed that comparable amounts of EG0120-Fc and EG0120-Fc (IHH) were detected at 3 h post-administration. However, the signal from EG0120-Fc (IHH) declined rapidly over time, whereas significantly higher levels of EG0120-Fc persisted in the lung at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. The amount of EG0120-Fc retained in the lung was consistently greater than that of EG0120-Fc (IHH) throughout the observation period. By 96 h, the pulmonary protein signal for both groups had decreased to undetectable levels. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the designed antigen in our study can efficiently penetrate epithelial cells and extend its half-life, which may enhance its in vivo immune response.

EG0120-Fc elicits broad systemic and mucosal immune responses in mice

Subsequently, the capacity of the antigens to elicit systemic and mucosal immune responses was evaluated. BALB/c mice were intranasally administered with EG01-Fc or EG0120-Fc formulated with a novel stimulator of interferon gene (STING) agonist-based candidate adjuvant, CF501.20 Sera, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), nasal lavage fluid (NALF), and spleens were collected after two-dose vaccination to assess both antibody and cellular responses (Figure 4A). Both antigens elicited strong systemic antibody responses, with serum IgG titers reaching up to 1:107 and serum IgA titers reaching up to 1:104 against representative variant RBDs. Notably, high levels of antigen-specific IgA were detected in BALF and NALF, demonstrating robust mucosal antibody responses in both upper and lower airways (Figure 4B). Neutralization assays showed that the antigens elicited cross-neutralizing antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 variants, whereas EG5.1 and JN.1 exhibited a certain degree of immune escape (Figure 4C). To assess cellular immune responses, ex vivo splenocytes were restimulated with peptide pools derived from EG0 + EG1 + EG2. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that both EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc robustly induced specific helper type 1 (Th1) and helper type 2 (Th2) CD4+ T cells, as well as central memory T (Tcm) cells, effector memory T (Tem) cells, and tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells (including CD4+ and CD8+) (Figure 4D). To evaluate the breadth of T cell responses, splenocytes were restimulated with peptide pools derived from RBDs of distinct viral serotypes (PT, BA.2.75, BA.2.86, and XBB).21,22 Robust Th1 and Th2 responses were observed under stimulation with any of the peptide pools, suggesting broad T cell epitope coverage (Figure 4E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the antigens, particularly EG0120-Fc, induce broad systemic and mucosal immune responses.

EG0120-Fc vaccination protects mice against lethal challenge by SARS-CoV-2 variants

To further evaluate the in vivo protection efficacy of the antigens, hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6 mice were intranasally vaccinated with CF501-adjuvanted EG0120-Fc or EG01-Fc (10 μg), using equimolar amounts of EG0, EG1, and EG2 as controls (Figure 5A). EG0, EG1, and EG2 were included as single-consensus antigen controls to assess the benefit of integrating multiple consensus RBDs into a single multivalent antigen. Sera collected after two-dose vaccination were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), showing that EG0120-Fc, EG01-Fc, and EG0 elicited high-titer cross-reactive IgG and IgA against all tested RBDs, ranging from the original one to the recently emerging one (Figure 5B). Neutralization assays demonstrated that EG0120-Fc elicited robust and broad-spectrum neutralizing antibody responses, with geometric mean titers (GMTs; log10) exceeding 3.0 against the PT, Delta, and BA.5 and reaching 2.5 against XBB.1.16 and JN.1 (Figure 5C). EG01-Fc also displayed cross-neutralization against these variants, although the potency was slightly lower than that of EG0120-Fc. By contrast, sera from mice immunized with monomer antigens showed limited neutralizing activity, with EG1 and EG2 groups exhibiting no detectable neutralization, while EG0 displayed strong neutralization against BA.5 and XBB.1.16.

To further assess the protective efficacy of the Epigraph-designed antigens, BA.5, Delta, and XBB.1.16, representing the viral strains phylogenetically closest to EG0, EG1, and EG2, respectively, were selected for lethal challenge experiments. EG0120-Fc, EG01-Fc, and EG0 all conferred complete protection, with no weight loss or deaths observed (Figure 5D). In contrast, mice in the EG1 and EG2 groups exhibited continuous weight loss and succumbed between days 5 and 8 post-challenge. Viral loads in the brain, lung, and turbinate were quantified by real-time qPCR, indicating that almost no detectable virus was present in these tissues in the fully protected groups, consistent with the survival results (Figure 5E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that EG0120-Fc is a candidate antigen with broader cross-protection against SARS-CoV-2 and its variants.

EG0120-Fc confers complete cross-protection at an extremely low dosage

Subsequently, we evaluated the optimal dose of EG0120-Fc for complete protection using the same hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6J mouse model. Mice were intranasally administered escalating doses of EG0120-Fc (0.2–10 μg) (Figure 6A). Sera collected after the second dose were analyzed by ELISA, showing broad cross-reactive IgG and IgA antibody responses, with antibody levels increasing in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6B). Discussion

In this study, consensus sequence computation was integrated with FcRn-targeted delivery to address two major limitations of current COVID-19 vaccines: limited breadth of protection and insufficient induction of respiratory mucosal immunity. Consequently, the candidate antigen EG0120-Fc confers broad protective coverage across all six SARS-CoV-2 serotypes while eliciting robust mucosal immune responses, providing an alternative strategy to comprehensively overcome existing challenges in COVID-19 vaccine development.

The RBD is an optimal antigen for inducing neutralizing antibodies, as it directly mediates viral attachment to host receptors. However, this functional importance also renders the RBD highly variable, thereby posing substantial challenges for vaccine design. In the post-COVID-19 era, vaccine research has increasingly focused on addressing the issue of antigenic diversity and achieving broad-spectrum protection. Influenza virus vaccines face similar challenges that also remain unresolved. To tackle this shared problem for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses, multiple strategies have been extensively explored, including antigen mixtures, intramolecular concatenation, mosaic nanoparticles, conserved proteins or epitopes, consensus sequence design, and sequential vaccination.23 Nevertheless, achieving robust and broad protection using a single molecular antigen remains a central objective in vaccine development, as it simplifies vaccine composition, streamlines manufacturing processes, and improves the controllability of potential side effects.

Thus, consensus sequence analysis represents a promising strategy for the development of broadly protective vaccines, as it not only confers coverage against currently circulating strains but may also extend protection to antigenically divergent variants that emerge over time.24 Building upon our previously established Fc-based vaccine platform,16 the present study investigated whether computationally designed consensus antigens could be incorporated into this framework to achieve broad protection while simplifying antigen discovery and optimization. The integration of this computational strategy with FcRn-targeted mucosal delivery extends the platform toward a more rational and scalable antigen-design paradigm. Previous studies have applied alternative computational algorithms to antigen design,25,26 whereas the present study employed the Epigraph algorithm. Epigraph explicitly accounts for T cell epitope coverage, thereby enabling the identification of antigen combinations with enhanced immunological relevance and improved potential for in vivo efficacy. This strategy has been extensively explored and validated in the field of influenza vaccine development.27,28,29,30 In 2022, Janack and Stephen Alexander Beck explored the application of the Epigraph algorithm to generate artificial peptide antigens based on the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein, and reported this work in a master’s thesis. Given the timing of this work, antigen design did not include the Omicron variant, which was first reported in November 2021. In addition, immunization was performed via intramuscular administration, and the potential of Epigraph-derived sequences to induce mucosal immunity or confer protection against authentic SARS-CoV-2 challenge was not investigated.

To address these remaining aspects, we implemented Epigraph in a comprehensive framework encompassing antigen design, structural validation, and immunological evaluation. Guided by this framework, Epigraph was applied to all available SARS-CoV-2 RBD sequences deposited in the GISAID database as of February 2024. All sequences were decomposed into a unified 9-mer peptide library, from which Epigraph identified optimal antigen combinations that collectively represent the global sequence landscape. This process yielded three consensus antigens, designated EG0, EG1, and EG2. EG0 alone captured approximately 75% of all 9-mer peptides, while the inclusion of EG1 and EG2 increased exact-match coverage to 94%. Allowing for a single amino acid mismatch further expanded coverage to 99.6%. This marked improvement underscores the advantage of consensus-based antigen design in accommodating viral sequence drift and enhancing recognition of conserved immunogenic regions. Consistent with these findings, epitope prediction analyses revealed increased diversity and abundance of high-affinity MHC class I and class II epitopes across the EG0/1/2 antigen set, and AlphaFold3 modeling confirmed preservation of the native RBD structural integrity. Finally, the immunological characteristics associated with this antigen design were evaluated through antibody and T cell analyses, along with BCR and TCR repertoire profiling, which revealed distinct immune repertoire features induced by EG0120-Fc immunization.

One major limitation of recombinant protein vaccines lies in their limited ability to induce robust mucosal immune responses. Physical barriers of the respiratory mucosa, including the mucus layer, mucociliary clearance, and epithelial tight junctions, restrict the penetration and retention of soluble protein antigens, thereby constraining vaccine efficacy.31 Although live-attenuated or replicating viral vectors are capable of eliciting mucosal immunity, their performance is often compromised by pre-existing immunity and variable immunogenicity.32 Given the well-established safety profile of recombinant protein antigens, strategies to enhance their mucosal delivery have attracted increasing attention. Among these, targeting the FcRn was first proposed in 201112 and has since been successfully translated into clinical recombinant protein vaccines,33,34 underscoring the feasibility and effectiveness of this approach. In addition to Fc-based designs, human serum albumin has also been explored as an FcRn-targeting carrier,35 further highlighting the broad applicability of FcRn-mediated delivery strategies.

Previous studies have demonstrated that FcRn mediates the transcytosis of IgG Fc across polarized mucosal epithelial cells, a process that is absent in FcRn-knockout mice.12 Subsequently, Li et al. reported that a SARS-CoV-2 S protein with complement-binding sites ablated elicited strong mucosal immune responses in both the upper and lower respiratory tracts; however, this monovalent antigen was not evaluated for cross-variant protection.17 More recently, Zhang et al. employed an IgG4 Fc with silenced effector functions to deliver bivalent RBDs (PT and BQ.1.1), successfully inducing mucosal protection.36 In contrast to these studies, we introduced the YTE mutation into an IgG1 Fc backbone to enhance FcRn binding and further extend antigen half-life. Moreover, Fcγ receptor-binding motifs were abolished through L234A/L235A/G237A (LALAGA) substitutions, resulting in a novel Fc-based construct optimized for mucosal delivery while minimizing Fc-mediated effector functions.

In this study, we constructed a trivalent RBD Fc-fusion antigen, EG0120-Fc. Both in vitro and in vivo analyses demonstrated that intranasal administration of EG0120-Fc facilitated efficient epithelial transport and resulted in detectable antigen accumulation in the lungs. Robust IgA responses in BALF and nasal wash samples further indicate strong humoral immunity across the respiratory tract. Compared with monomeric EG0, EG1, or EG2, EG0120-Fc elicited antibody responses of greater magnitude and breadth, including enhanced binding and neutralizing activities, reflecting an overall improvement in immunogenicity conferred by Fc fusion. Notably, administration of EG0120-Fc at a dose as low as 0.2 μg conferred complete protection against lethal PT and JN.1 challenges while maintaining stable body weight throughout the 11-day post-challenge period, demonstrating robust protective efficacy that cannot be achieved by non-optimized RBD antigens alone. Because adjuvants play an important role in protein vaccine efficacy, the contribution of CF501 should be considered when interpreting the immune responses observed in this study. Our previous work16 demonstrated that Fc-fused antigens can induce robust systemic and mucosal immune responses when formulated with different adjuvants, including alum, AS03, and CF501, suggesting that the observed immune responses are unlikely to be attributable solely to CF501.

Interestingly, complete protection was achieved in the 0.2 μg vaccination group despite the absence of detectable serum neutralizing activity against either PT or JN.1. This observation suggests that protection may not rely solely on neutralizing antibodies. Antigen-binding IgG responses remained detectable in this group, and EG0120-Fc also elicited robust mucosal IgA and T cell responses in immunogenicity studies, suggesting that multiple immune mechanisms may contribute to protection.

This study has several limitations. First, although Fc domains have been widely applied in vaccine design and related studies, the specific contribution of Fc-mediated FcRn targeting to the induction of respiratory mucosal immune responses, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms, remains incompletely understood. This uncertainty arises in part because Fc interacts with multiple receptors in addition to FcRn. Therefore, the benefits and potential risks associated with Fc-based strategies require further investigation and should be addressed in future studies. Second, the expression patterns and binding affinities of murine Fcγ receptors differ substantially from those in humans, limiting the mechanistic interpretation of Fc-fusion protein vaccines in conventional mouse models.37 In addition, species-specific differences in FcRn biology should be considered. Human IgG1 Fc exhibits different binding properties toward murine FcRn and hFcRn,38 which may affect FcRn-mediated antigen transport, retention, and recycling in vivo. Therefore, further validation in hFcRn-transgenic mouse models and non-human primates will be important for assessing the efficacy and translational potential of this vaccine platform.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that integrating consensus sequence computation with FcRn-targeted delivery generates a trivalent RBD-Fc vaccine capable of inducing broad mucosal cross-variant protection at low intranasal doses. This combined strategy offers a promising blueprint for developing mucosal vaccines with enhanced breadth, durability, and transmission-blocking potential—applicable not only to SARS-CoV-2 but also to other rapidly evolving respiratory viruses.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals of China and institutional guidelines. All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ethics nos. WIVAF25202302 and WIVA25202313). Mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All SARS-CoV-2 challenge experiments were performed in an Animal Biosafety Level 3 (ABSL-3) facility in compliance with institutional biosafety regulations.

Cells and viruses

The mammalian cell lines utilized in this research, including Vero E6, MDCK, and HEK293T, were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and propagated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA). These cultures were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and maintained under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). For protein expression, HEK293F suspension cells were employed using OPM-293F Hi-Exp medium (OPM Biosciences, Shanghai, China) within a shaking incubator (120 rpm) at 37°C.

Clinical isolates of SARS-CoV-2 were obtained from the National Virus Resource at the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The viral panel comprised the ancestral strain (PT, IVCAS6.7512) and the Beta (IVCAS6.7552) and Delta (IVCAS6.7585) variants, as well as multiple Omicron sublineages, including BA.2 (IVCAS6.7617), BA.5 (IVCAS6.8981), XBB.1.9 (IVCAS6.9084), XBB.1.16 (IVCAS6.9083), EG.5 (IVCAS6.9086), and JN.1 (IVCAS6.9355).

Construction of FcRn-overexpressing MDCK cell lines

Stable MDCK cell lines expressing either hFcRn or mFcRn were generated using a lentiviral transduction system as previously described,16 with minor modifications. Briefly, recombinant lentiviral plasmids encoding hFCGRT-P2A-hB2M or mFCGRT-P2A-mB2M were cloned into the pCDH-CMV-EGFP-EF1α-Puro backbone (MiaoLing Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfection of the transfer plasmid together with the packaging plasmid pCG8.91 and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G at a 2:3:1 mass ratio. 48 h post-transfection, viral supernatants were harvested, filtered, and used to transduce MDCK cells. Transduction efficiency was initially assessed by monitoring EGFP expression under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Stable cell populations were selected using puromycin (2–4 μg/mL, determined by MDCK sensitivity) for approximately 7 days until EGFP-positive cells exceeded 99%. Expression of hFcRn or mFcRn was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometric analysis prior to downstream experiments.

Consensus RBD generation by Epigraph algorithm

SARS-CoV-2 S protein sequences were downloaded from the GISAID database, encompassing 16,528,797 sequences available up to February 2024. Sequences that were incomplete or redundant were removed, resulting in 293,441 high-quality, non-redundant S protein sequences. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of these sequences was performed with multiple alignment using fast Fourier transform (MAFFT v.7.505), and the RBD region was subsequently extracted. After removing duplicate RBD sequences, a total of 3,997 unique RBD sequences were obtained.

All RBD sequences were uploaded to the Epigraph Vaccine Designer platform to generate consensus sequences. The parameters were set as follows: epitope length = 9 amino acids and number of sequences in the vaccine pool = 3. This calculation produced three consensus sequences: EG0, EG1, and EG2.

Phylogenetic and conservation analysis

To evaluate evolutionary relationships, the 3,997 RBD sequences along with the three consensus sequences were subjected to MSA using MAFFT, followed by ML phylogenetic analysis with IQ-Tree2 (v.2.2.0.3) under the model selection option -m MFP, with support assessed using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-bb 1000) and -bnni. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using Figtree v.1.4.3.

For conservation analysis, 20 representative SARS-CoV-2 RBD sequences (PT, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Lambda, BA.1, BA.2, BA.2.75, XBB, XBB.1.5, EG.5, XBB.1.16, BA.2.86, JN.1, BA.1.2.1, BA.3, BA.5, BQ.1.1, and BF.7) were aligned and analyzed using ESPript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/). Sequence logos were generated with WebLogo (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/create.cgi). Heatmaps illustrating RBD sequence conservation across representative variants were produced using GraphPad Prism (v.9.0; GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).

T cell epitope prediction

T cell epitopes of PT, EG0, EG1, and EG2 sequences were predicted using NetMHCpan-4.1. For MHC class I epitopes, the source species was set to Homo sapiens, and 38 high-frequency HLA-A and HLA-B alleles recommended by the server were selected. For MHC class II epitopes, 27 common HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, and HLA-DP alleles were used. Peptides were scored according to the percentile rank (%Rank) provided by NetMHCpan: for MHC class I, %Rank < 0.5 defined strong binders, and %Rank < 2.0 defined weak binders; for MHC class II, %Rank < 2.0 defined strong binders, and %Rank < 10.0 defined weak binders.

The total numbers of strong and weak binders for each sequence were quantified and visualized using GraphPad Prism. To further assess epitope quality and diversity, the top 100 strongest binding peptides from each sequence were selected and compared. Epitope distribution and comparative analysis were visualized with RAWGraphs 2.0 (https://app.rawgraphs.io/).

Protein structure prediction and analysis

Monomeric EG0, EG1, and EG2 proteins, as well as the candidate vaccine construct EG0120-Fc, were subjected to structural modeling using AlphaFold 3 (DeepMind, London, UK) via the AlphaFold Server (accessed July 2025). For each protein, the top-ranked model based on the confidence score was selected for downstream analysis and visualized using PyMOL (v.2.6; Schrödinger, New York, NY, USA). To assess structural conservation, the predicted RBD structures were aligned to the experimentally resolved SARS-CoV-2 RBD structure (PDB: 8SGU) using Cα atoms, and RMSD values were calculated to quantify conformational differences.

Plasmid construction and protein preparation

Codon-optimized EG0, EG1, and EG2 genes were synthesized and cloned into the eukaryotic expression vector pCAGGS. The fusion constructs EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc were cloned into the expression plasmid pXC17.4 (LONZA, Basel, Switzerland), which is derived from a modified human IgG1 heavy-chain backbone. To eliminate FcγR-mediated effector functions, L234A, L235A, and G237A mutations were introduced into the Fc domain. To enhance FcRn binding, the M252Y, S254T, and T256E (YTE) mutations were incorporated. EG0120-Fc is a heterodimer composed of one EG01-Fc chain and one EG20-Fc chain. To enable heterodimer formation, a “knobs-into-holes” design was applied, including the CW (S354C and T366W) mutation set on one chain and the CSAV (Y349C, T366S, L368A, and Y407V) set on the other. Additionally, an H435R and Y436F (RF) mutation was introduced into the EG0-EG1-Fc chain to abrogate protein A binding, and a C-terminal 6×His tag was added to facilitate purification of the heterodimeric protein. As a result, the two EG0120-Fc chains consisted of EG0-EG1-Fc-AAA-YTE-CW-RF-His and EG2-EG0-Fc-AAA-YTE-CSAV, whereas EG01-Fc forms a homodimeric molecule with the composition EG0-EG1-Fc-AAA-YTE. Consensus (EG0, EG1, and EG2) and Fc-fusion (EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc) proteins were transiently expressed in Harvest 293F cells by plasmid transfection and cultured in serum-free medium for 7 days. Cell supernatants were harvested and purified by protein A affinity chromatography (GenScript, Nanjing, China), followed by further purification using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden). The purity and molecular weight of the recombinant proteins were confirmed by SDS-PAGE under reducing and non-reducing conditions.

BLI analysis

Recombinant hACE2 proteins were conjugated with biotin at a molar ratio of 1:10 for 30 min at room temperature using EZ-Link NHS-PEG4-Biotin (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Excess biotin was removed by buffer exchange with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Biotinylated hACE2 proteins were diluted to 25 μg/mL in PBS supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA and 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20, then immobilized onto the streptavidin sensors, which were hydrated in PBS containing 0.1% BSA and 0.02% Tween 20 for 20 min at room temperature prior to use. Association and dissociation kinetics (Kon and Koff) were measured by exposing the sensors to serially diluted analytes: PT, EG0, EG1 (3-fold dilution series from 200 to 2.5 nM), EG2 (3-fold dilution series from 2,000 to 25 nM), and EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc (3-fold dilution series from 1,200 to 5 nM). BLI measurements were performed using an Octet RED system (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Global kinetic analyses were performed using a 1:1 binding model, and the equilibrium constants (KD = Koff/Kon) were calculated using the data analysis software DataAnalysis (v.11.1.1.8; FortéBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA).

Flow cytometry to detect binding capacity

The binding capacity of EG0120-Fc to FcRn-expressing cells was evaluated by flow cytometry, following a previously established protocol with minor modifications.16 Briefly, recombinant EG0120-Fc protein was conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 NHS ester (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate buffer (pH 8.3) at room temperature for 1 h using a 1:5 protein-to-dye molar ratio. Excess dye was removed by ultrafiltration prior to use. hFcRn- or mFcRn-overexpressing MDCK cells were harvested and incubated with AF647-labeled protein in PBS (Gibco) at 4°C for 30 min to allow surface binding. After incubation, cells were washed thoroughly to eliminate unbound protein and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Following additional washes, fluorescence intensity was quantified using flow cytometry to assess cell-surface binding.

FcRn-mediated transcytosis in a transwell system

FcRn-dependent transcytosis was evaluated using a polarized MDCK transwell model as previously described,16,39 with minor modifications. Briefly, MDCK cells stably expressing hFcRn or mFcRn were seeded at 1.0 × 105 cells per insert onto collagen-coated 0.4 μm pore-size transwell membranes (1.12 cm2; Corning Costar, NY, USA). Wild-type MDCK cells were included as controls. Monolayers were maintained for 4–6 days to allow full polarization, and integrity was verified by measuring transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER; 120–150 Ω × cm2) and apparent permeability coefficients (Papp < 5 × 10−7 cm/s). Prior to the assay, cells were equilibrated in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; Gibco) for 1 h. The apical chamber buffer was adjusted to pH 6.0 using MES buffer to facilitate FcRn binding. Recombinant EG0120-Fc protein (200 nM) was applied to the apical compartment, while the non-Fc RBD dimer (200 nM) served as a negative control. At indicated time points (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h), samples were collected from the basolateral chamber. Transported protein levels were quantified using a double-antibody sandwich ELISA with mouse polyclonal capture and detection antibodies (SinoBiological, Beijing, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis of mouse lung mucosal barrier penetration

AF647-labeled EG0120-Fc protein (10 μg in 30 μL PBS) was intranasally administered to anesthetized 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c mice by inhalation of isoflurane (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). Mice were euthanized at 3, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-administration, with an additional group euthanized immediately as a 0-h control. Lungs were perfused with PBS, harvested, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4°C. Tissues were then dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness using a freezing microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking, sections were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Panoramic MIDI 3D; 3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

In vivo imaging of antigen half-life period in mouse respiratory tract

Prior to the formal experiment, an FcRn-binding-abolished construct, EG0120-Fc (IHH), was generated. Recombinant EG0120-Fc (IHH) proteins were produced using the same procedure as EG0120-Fc. To evaluate the persistence of EG0120-Fc in the mouse respiratory tract, EG0120-Fc and EG0120-Fc (IHH) were conjugated with the near-infrared fluorescent probe Sulfo-Cyanine 5 (Cy5) NHS ester (Shanghai Yingxin Laboratory Equipment, Shanghai, China). Cy5-labeled proteins (10 μg in 30 μL PBS) were intranasally administered to 6- to 8-week-old BALB/c nude mice. Fluorescence images were acquired at 3, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-administration (n = 3 per time point) using an IVIS Spectrum Living Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) under isoflurane anesthesia. Animals were sourced from GemPharmatech and housed in an SPF facility. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 640 and 680 nm, respectively. Additional images acquired at 605 nm excitation and 680 nm emission were used for background subtraction. Fluorescence images were processed and quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Mouse vaccination and challenge

Animals were sourced from GemPharmatech and housed in an SFP facility. Adult BALB/c mice 6–8 weeks old (n = 5) or hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6J mice (n = 5) were adopted to a two-dose regimen with a 3-week interval. Vaccines were formulated with a constant dose of CF501 adjuvant (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) with different doses of recombinant proteins in a total volume of 50 μL. For the immunogenicity experiment, BALB/c mice were intranasally administered an equal dose of EG01-Fc and EG0120-Fc (10 μg). For the broad-spectrum protection experiment, hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6J mice were intranasally immunized with antigen doses containing an equivalent molar amount of RBD, corresponding to 6.4 μg of EG0, EG1, or EG2 and 10 μg of EG01-Fc or EG0120-Fc. For the dose-optimization experiment, hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6J mice were intranasally administered variable amounts (0.2, 1, 5, and 10 μg) of EG0120-Fc. Blood samples were collected from the ophthalmic vein 28 days after the first immunization, placed at 4°C overnight, and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum. Alternatively, BALF and NALF were collected by lavage with PBS, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to remove cells. Spleens were dissected for splenocyte isolation.

Upon completion of vaccination, hACE2-transgenic C57BL/6J mice were transferred from an SPF facility to an ABSL-3 facility and challenged with SARS-CoV-2 after isoflurane-induced anesthesia. SARS-CoV-2 inoculum doses were 1 × 103 plaque-forming units (PFUs) of PT and Delta variants and 1 × 104 PFUs of BA.5, XBB.1.16, and JN.1 variants. Body weight and survival were monitored throughout the experiment. At the experimental endpoint, brain, lung, and turbinate tissues were dissected for viral load determination or histopathological analysis.

ELISA for specific antibody detection

Antigen-specific antibody responses were quantified using an indirect ELISA established in our laboratory and routinely applied in previous studies.13,14,16 Briefly, 96-well high-binding plates (Greiner, Frickenhausen, Germany) were coated with recombinant variant RBD proteins (1 μg/mL, 100 μL per well) at 4°C overnight. After removal of the coating solution, plates were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) and blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 h at 37°C to minimize nonspecific binding. Serial dilutions of serum or BALF/NALF samples were then applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following extensive washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were added and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Color development was achieved using tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate, and the reaction was terminated after 15 min by adding 2 M H2SO4. Optical density at 450 nm was determined using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Samples with absorbance values exceeding 2-fold of background controls were defined as positive.

Plaque reduction neutralization test

Neutralizing antibody titers were determined using a plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) following the standardized protocol established in our laboratory and described previously.13,14,16 Briefly, Vero E6 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight to achieve a confluent monolayer. Heat-inactivated serum samples were serially diluted and incubated with 300 PFUs of SARS-CoV-2 or indicated variants for 1 h at 37°C to allow antibody-virus interaction. The serum-virus mixtures were then transferred onto Vero E6 monolayers and incubated for 1 h to permit viral adsorption. After removal of inoculum, cells were overlaid with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 0.9% methylcellulose to restrict viral spread. Plates were maintained for 72 h, then fixed with 10% formaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Visible plaques were enumerated manually, and PRNT50 titers were calculated using GraphPad Prism software.

Flow cytometry analysis of cellular immune responses

Splenocytes harvested from BALB/c mice were isolated and seeded at 1 × 107 cells per well in 96-well U-bottom plates (200 μL/well). Cells were stimulated with 50 μL of peptide pools in the presence of 5 μg/mL Brefeldin A (Absin, Shanghai, PRC). Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; BioLegend, CA, USA) and RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Overlapping peptide pools from variant RBDs (PT, BA.2.75, BA.2.86, and XBB) and consensus sequences (EG0, EG1, and EG2) were de novo synthesized at Wuhan Bioyeargene Biotechnology (Wuhan, China) and dissolved in PBS. Cells were restimulated with the peptide pools at 10 μg/mL for each peptide, and the co-incubation was conducted at 37°C for 8 h in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

After that, cells were subjected to antibody staining, with washes performed at each step interval. Cells were collected by centrifugation and labeled with Zombie Aqua viability dye (BioLegend) at 4°C for 15 min, followed by Fc receptor blocking using TruStain FcX (anti-mouse CD16/32, BioLegend) at 4°C for 15 min. Surface marker antibodies (BioLegend), diluted in PBS containing 0.5% BSA, were added and incubated at 4°C for 30 min. Cells were then fixed with 100 μL fixation buffer (BioLegend) at 4°C for 30 min, permeabilized with Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (BioLegend), and stained with intracellular antibodies at 4°C for 45 min. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in fixation buffer and detected by flow cytometry. The data were analyzed by FlowJo software (v.10.2; Treestar, Ashland, OR). CD3+CD4+IFN-γ+ cells were defined as Th1 cells, whereas CD3+CD4+IL-4+ cells were defined as Th2 cells. Tcm cells were defined as CD44+CD62L+CD69−, and Tem cells were defined as CD44+CD62L−CD69+. TRM (CD4+TRM) cells were defined as CD4+CD44+CD103+CD69+, and CD8+ TRM (CD8+TRM) cells were defined as CD8+CD44highCD103−CD69high.

Histopathology analysis

Mouse lungs harvested from the challenge experiment were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 7 days. The tissues were subsequently dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological examination. After dehydration and mounting, slides were either imaged using a Nikon Digital Sight DS-FI2 microscopy system (Nikon).

BCR/TCR repertoire sequencing

BALB/c female mice (6–8 weeks old) were intranasally immunized with 5 μg EG0120-Fc or PT2-Fc, formulated with 20 μg CF501 adjuvant, following the immunization schedule described above. Mice were euthanized on day 28, and spleens were collected for downstream analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from spleen tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, cat. no. 15596018) according to the protocol of Chomczynski and Sacchi (1987)40. Approximately 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was used for BCR and TCR library preparation using the KC-Digital Stranded BCR-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Seqhealth Technology, Wuhan, China, cat. no. DT0811-02) and the KC-Digital Stranded TCR-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina 150 (Seqhealth Technology, cat. no. DT0813-02), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

These kits minimize PCR and sequencing duplication bias by incorporating an 8-base unique molecular identifier (UMI) to label pre-amplified cDNA molecules. Libraries corresponding to 250–500 bp were size selected, quantified, and sequenced on a high-throughput Illumina platform.

BCR/TCR repertoire analysis

Raw sequencing data were first processed with fastp (v.0.23.4) to remove low-quality reads and reads containing adaptor contamination. Clean reads were then subjected to an in-house UMI-based deduplication pipeline to eliminate amplification and sequencing biases.

Briefly, clean reads were clustered according to their UMI sequences, with reads sharing an identical UMI grouped into the same cluster. Within each cluster, pairwise sequence alignment was performed, and reads exhibiting >95% sequence identity were further grouped into sub-clusters. After all sub-clusters were generated, MSA was conducted to derive a consensus sequence for each sub-cluster. These UMI-collapsed consensus sequences were used for downstream BCR/TCR repertoire analysis.

Consensus sequences were aligned to the international ImMunoGeneTics (IMGT) database using MiXCR (v.3.0.3) to assign V, D, and J gene segments, determine rearrangement information, and extract CDR3 sequences.

Data analysis

Data are presented as mean (SD). Normality and equal variance assumptions were verified. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed t tests were applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests was used. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism. Significance is indicated as follows: ns p > 0.05, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
