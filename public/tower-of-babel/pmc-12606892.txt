Advanced nitric oxide-activated calcium overload and zinc ion-amplified oxidative imbalance in ferroptosis/apoptosis
Authors: Hongmei Zhou, Yujia Liang, Lijuan Liu, Yulin Liang, Jiaying Tang, He Ding, Shufang Ning, Piaoping Yang, Chen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis, a programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, relies on an oxidative imbalance. However, tumor cells counteract the effects of oxidative imbalance through adaptive defense mechanisms and metabolic reprogramming, thereby limiting the therapeutic efficacy of ferroptosis.ResultsAn antitumor strategy was designed to synergistically induce ferroptosis and apoptosis by targeting the release of nitric oxide (NO) and zinc ions (Zn2+), effectively disrupting the redox homeostasis and antioxidant capacity of tumor cells. ZIF-8 degradation in tumor cells triggers Zn2+-mediated mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibition and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 1 activation, significantly increasing endogenous reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. NO-derived reactive nitrogen species exhibit strong oxidative activity, amplifying ROS-mediated oxidative imbalance, and promoting lipid peroxidation by depleting glutathione and inhibiting glutathione peroxidase 4 activity, inducing ferroptosis and apoptosis. Furthermore, NO disrupts intracellular redox equilibrium by activating endoplasmic reticulum stress and inhibiting the mitochondrial electron transport chain, resulting in calcium overload and mitochondrial malfunction.ConclusionsMulti-pathway disruption of intracellular adaptive defense mechanisms and metabolic reprogramming homeostasis can be achieved via NO synergistic Zn2+, offering the potential for developing highly effective combined ferroptosis and apoptosis antitumor therapies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03793-1.Keywords: Ferroptosis, Oxidative imbalance, Nitric oxide, Calcium overload, Apoptosis

Introduction

Ferroptosis, a novel mode of programmed cell death, differs from traditional apoptosis, necrosis, and pyroptosis owing to its unique molecular mechanisms and pathophysiological features [1–3]. As an iron-dependent version of the regulated cell death process identified in 2012, ferroptosis is initiated by the build-up of lipid peroxidation (LPO), which subsequently leads to cell membrane degradation and death [4–6]. Oxidative imbalance is a key driver of LPO and plays a central role in the molecular regulatory network of ferroptosis [7–9]. Specifically, oxidative imbalance triggers the accumulation of LPO by increasing the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which further triggers ferroptosis [10, 11]. ROS are generated mainly by the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (reduced form) (NADPH) oxidase pathways, and initiate chain reactions by attacking polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane, ultimately leading to excessive accumulation of LPO and impairment of cellular function [12–15]. However, tumor cells interfere with the effects of ROS through a complex array of adaptive defense mechanisms and metabolic reprogramming, limiting the onset and efficacy of ferroptosis and posing a significant challenge to antitumor therapy [16–20]. In addition, the chemical nature of ROS determines their short half-life and limited range of action, making it difficult for ROS to achieve a broad and long-lasting oxidative imbalance in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [21]. Therefore, the development of multimodal antitumor therapies that induce more efficient oxidative imbalance is crucial for advancing research on the induction and enhancement of ferroptosis.

Abnormal zinc ion (Zn2+) distribution and metabolism can induce tumor cell death through various mechanisms, the most significant of which is the disruption of intracellular redox homeostasis by promoting the generation of endogenous ROS, which in turn triggers oxidative imbalance and cellular damage [22–24]. Zn2+ homeostatic imbalance significantly increases intracellular ROS levels, mainly by inhibiting the function of mitochondrial ETC complexes I and III [25, 26]. In addition, the homeostatic imbalance of Zn2+ also promotes the generation of ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and superoxide anion (O2•−), through the activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 1 (NOX1). Specifically, Zn2+ controls the activity of NOX1, an ROS-generating enzyme found in the cytoplasmic membrane. Zn2+ enhances the ability of NOX1 to catalyze NADPH oxidation by modulating the subunit assembly and enzymatic activity [27, 28]. Tumor cells increase their resistance to ferroptosis by metabolic reprogramming, of which NADPH production is a key component. NADPH is the primary intracellular source of reducing power and inhibits LPO and ferroptosis by maintaining the reduced state of glutathione (GSH) and supporting the function of antioxidant enzymes [29, 30]. Zn2+-mediated NADPH depletion leading to GSH depletion and disruption of antioxidant defenses can effectively drive ferroptosis [31, 32]. Targeting Zn2+ to block the mitochondrial ETC and activate NOX1 may be a therapeutic breakthrough in causing oxidative imbalance and ferroptosis by interfering with the metabolic reprogramming process of tumor cells.

Nitric oxide (NO), a crucial gaseous signaling molecule, enhances the susceptibility of tumor cells to apoptosis through various molecular mechanisms, including the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction and triggering of DNA damage [33, 34]. L-arginine (L-Arg), a natural NO donor, is of interest because of its excellent biocompatibility [35–37]. The guanidinium group of L-Arg has excellent cell-penetrating activity and endosomal escape ability, allowing it to enter tumor cells and efficiently release NO intracellularly [38]. L-Arg effectively produces NO when inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) or ROS is present, resulting in its biological action in the TME [39]. NO can react with O2•− in TME to form highly toxic RNS such as peroxynitrite (ONOO−) [40–42]. RNS are much more oxidizing and nitrifying than most ROS and are capable of damaging key biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and DNA through oxidative and nitrative reactions [43–45]. In particular, RNS was able to deplete GSH, the main intracellular antioxidant, and inhibit the activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), leading to a significant increase in intracellular LPO levels, and ultimately to ferroptosis [46, 47]. In addition, RNS drives a vicious cycle of oxidative imbalance by inhibiting ETC activity, leading to reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis and further accumulation of ROS. RNS also promotes apoptosis in tumor cells by activating the mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway [48–50]. NO offers unique advantages in treatment approaches that jointly induce ferroptosis and apoptosis by impairing antioxidant capacity and increasing the susceptibility of tumor cells to apoptosis.

The maintenance of intracellular calcium homeostasis depends on the synergistic regulation of the ER, mitochondria, and plasma membrane calcium channels [51]. As the major intracellular calcium reservoir, the ER regulates the release of calcium ions (Ca2+) with precision through calpain channels [52, 53]. Meanwhile, through the uptake of Ca2+, the mitochondria can take part in the regulation of energy metabolism and apoptosis [54, 55]. Notably, NO plays a pivotal role in this regulatory network. Specifically, NO promotes the release of Ca2+ from the ER into the cytoplasm by activating ER calcium channels, resulting in a significant increase in cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration [56, 57]. However, the endogenous release of Ca2+ is tightly regulated, and cells have a strong capacity for calcium buffering [58, 59]. Therefore, many studies have proposed that a rapid increase in intracellular cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration through exogenous Ca2+ supplementation drives the onset of calcium overload [60, 61]. Calcium overload directly disrupts the function of the mitochondria, leading to reduced ATP synthesis and high levels of ROS production, thereby resulting in oxidative imbalance [62]. Meanwhile, calcium overload in the mitochondria disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential and induces the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c from the mitochondria, which ultimately triggers apoptosis [63, 64]. Introducing exogenous Ca2+ and combining it with NO-mediated release of endogenous Ca2+ can effectively trigger calcium overload and break through the limitations of endogenous calcium regulation, which provides a new direction for efficient therapeutic strategies to induce apoptosis.

Herein, the three-dimensional porous structure of zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) enabled the efficient encapsulation of L-Arg within its internal cavities, followed by mineralization with a calcium phosphate (CaP) coating and further surface modification with hyaluronic acid (HA) and polydopamine (PDA), ultimately yielding the multifunctional nanoparticle L-Arg@ZIF-8@CaP@HA/PDA (LZCHA). PDA is an excellent and widely studied photothermal material for surface modification and enhancement of photothermal conversion efficiency. Coating nanoparticles with PDA on the surface can lead to excellent pH sensitivity and high photothermal conversion efficiency. HA enables active targeting by specifically recognizing CD44 receptors on the surface of tumor cells, while significantly improving biocompatibility and reducing the immunogenicity of the material. ZIF-8 exhibited excellent pH-responsive properties for targeted release under acidic conditions. Upon cellular uptake, LZCHA is degraded in an acidic lysosomal environment, releasing Zn2+, Ca2+, and L-Arg and initiating subsequent cascade reactions (Fig. 1). Specifically, Zn2+ interferes with electron transport by inhibiting the function of ETC complexes I and III, resulting in elevated levels of endogenous ROS. The cellular antioxidant defense system and redox balance were disrupted. At the same time, Zn2+ activates NOX1 to catalyze the oxidation of NADPH to produce O2•−, which provides the basis for the disruption of the metabolic reprogramming process and generation of RNS. L-Arg generates NO via iNOS and O2•− catalysis, which activates the ER calcium channel to promote Ca2+ release into the cytoplasm. Ca2+ released by LZCHA acts synergistically with Ca2+ released by the ER to trigger a calcium overload storm, leading to depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential, which in turn inhibits ATP production and enables apoptosis. Notably, RNS, derived from the reaction between NO and O2•−, not only enhances the oxidative imbalance induced by ROS accumulation but also directly depletes GSH and inhibits GPX4 activity, leading to the accumulation of LPO, which in turn induces the onset of ferroptosis and apoptosis. In addition, LZCHA exhibits good photothermal transfer efficiency under 1064 nm near-infrared irradiation, increasing the local temperature, which enhances the overall catalytic reaction efficiency. The synergistic strategy of dual mechanisms disrupting metabolic reprogramming and redox homeostasis provides new insights and an important rationale for the development of efficient combined ferroptosis and apoptosis antitumor therapies.

Synthesis of L-Arg@ZIF-8@CaP@HA (LZCHA)

To prepare LZ, Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O (1.17 g) was dissolved in 176 mL of solution A (ddH2O), while 10 mL of L-Arg (100 mg mL–1) was added to solution A with thorough stirring for 10 min. Then, 2-MIN (22.70 g) was thoroughly mixed with solution A and stirred for 30 min. L-Arg@ZIF-8 (LZ) was collected by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 10 min) after cleaning three times in succession using anhydrous ethanol and deionized water. Next, Ca(OH)2 (66.5 mg) and poly(acrylic acid) (140.0 mg), along with the obtained LZ (12.0 mg), were added to 150 mL of deionized water. Isopropanol (280 mL) was mixed with (NH4)2HPO4 (87.5 mg) while stirring vigorously and then added dropwise to the solution, which was stirred for 24 h. Centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 8 min) was used to collect L-Arg@ZIF-8@CaP (LZC), which was subsequently dried for 24 h. To improve biocompatibility and photothermal conversion efficiency, LZCHA was prepared by stirring LZC, HA, and PDA at a weight ratio of 20:1:1 for 12 h. ZIF-8 was synthesized using the same procedure as above, but without the addition of L-Arg.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, GER) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (FEI TALOS G2 F200X, CZ) were used to assess the morphology and elemental composition of LZCHA. A zeta potential analyzer (Zetasizer-Nano-ZS, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) was used to determine the surface zeta potential, polymer dispersion index (PDI), and dynamic light scattering (DLS). Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) (TRSTAR-II 3020, Micromeritics, Norcross, GA, USA) was used to analyze the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore distribution of LZCHA. The elemental composition and content of LZCHA were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Escalab XI +, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS20, Thermo Scientific) was performed after loading the functional groups. A thermogravimetric analyzer (HCT-1, Henven) was used to perform thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). To assess compositional changes, UV-vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-2600, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) was performed. The Ca2+ and Zn2+ release rates were assessed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (ICAP 7400, Thermo Fisher Scientific) under different conditions. An infrared thermal imaging camera (NEC AVIO Inf Rec R300SR-HD; Nippon Avionics, Kanagawa, Japan) was used to assess the photothermal characteristics of the samples. Cell images were captured using the confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Leica SP8, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). A flow cytometer (BD Accuri C6; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used to monitor apoptosis.

NO generation

LZCHA was dissolved in PBS at different pH values (5.0, 6.5, and 7.4), and an appropriate amount of H2O2 (1 mM) was added. The Griess reagent kit was used to detect NO production at various time intervals. NO concentration was determined using a standard curve and absorbance at 540 nm, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

L-Arg concentration detection

First, the indicator solution was composed of 1 mL of sodium hydroxide (40.0 mg mL−1), 1 mL of 1-naphthol/propanol (86.5 mg mL−1), and 1 mL of diacetyl/propanol (0.5 mL L−1). After adding the L-Arg gradient standard solution to the indicator solution, the mixture was allowed to react for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm. A standard curve was established based on the absorbance values. LZCHA and H2O2 (1 mM) were incubated for different durations, and the amount of remaining L-Arg was assayed according to the above procedure [65].

pH-Responsive therapeutic release properties

LZCHA was dissolved in PBS (20 mL) at ambient temperature. Centrifugation was performed at different time intervals (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), and 100 µL of supernatant was removed, which was diluted with 8 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide. The supernatant was then removed and replaced with an equal volume of PBS. The released Ca2+ and Zn2+ ions were quantified by ICP-OES.

Evaluation of the photothermal effect of LZCHA

The photothermal properties of the synthesized LZCHA samples were characterized using an infrared thermal imaging camera. The effect of 1064 nm laser power density was investigated by varying the laser power density (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 W cm−2) at 1064 nm and recording the temperature of the LZCHA solution (150 µg mL−1) under near-infrared irradiation. Similarly, the effect of concentration was tested by recording the temperature of LZCHA solutions at different concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, and 200 µg mL−1) under 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2). Three laser on/off cycles were performed to measure the photothermal stability, and the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of the LZCHA was calculated based on previous studies.

Cell culture

L929 fibroblast cells (RRID: CVCL_0462) were acquired from Zhejiang Mason Cell Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The mouse colon cancer cell line CT26 (RRID: CVCL_7256) was acquired from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Not all the cell lines were contaminated. All cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated in a standard cell culture incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Cellular uptake assay

CT26 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured overnight. FITC-LZCHA (150 µg mL−1) was then added, and incubation with the cells was continued for different time intervals (1, 2, 3, and 4 h). Three PBS washes were performed on CT26 cells. Before CLSM fluorescence imaging, cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342.

Invitro cytotoxicity evaluation

The cytotoxicity of LZCHA was analyzed in CT26 cells, and the biosafety of LZCHA was studied in L929 cells. Briefly, the CT26 cells (4 × 103 cells/well) were cultured for 24 h in 96-well plates. The incubation of CT26 cells was continued for 24 h after treatment according to the different groupings (control, ZIF-8, LZ, ZC, LZCHA, and LZCHA + Laser) and various concentrations of the nanoparticles (0, 25, 50, 100, 150, and 200 µg mL−1). Among them, the LZCHA + Laser group received 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 10 min) after incubation. Then, 100 µL of MTT solution (1 mg mL−1) was added after the medium was changed, and the combination was incubated for 4 h. Similar to the experimental steps described above, the biosafety evaluation of L929 cells was performed.

Invitro production of Ca2+ and Zn2+

CT26 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates and cultured overnight. After washing with PBS, incubation was continued for different times (1, 2, 3, and 4 h) by adding a new medium containing LZCHA (150 µg mL−1). Among them, the LZCHA + Laser group received 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 10 min) after incubation. The culture solution was then removed and washed with PBS. 1 mL of HNO3 was added, and the ion concentration was determined using ICP-OES.

Detection of intracellular NO

NO production in cells was visually detected using a DAF-FM DA probe. Briefly, CT26 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates and incubated overnight. The cell culture medium was removed, and the incubation was continued for 4 h by adding 150 µg mL−1 of different materials. The LZCHA + Laser group received 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 10 min). Following incubation, 1 mL of the DAF-FM DA staining solution was applied to the cells after cleaning with PBS. The cells were then incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. The staining solution that did not enter the cells was removed by washing with PBS at the end of the incubation.

Induction of intracellular calcium overload

A fluorescent Ca2+ probe, Fluo-4 AM, was used to measure intracellular Ca2+ levels. CT26 cells cultured overnight were treated with various substances and incubated for 4 h. After staining the cells for 20 min with the Fluo-4 AM probe, they were rinsed three times with PBS. Hoechst 33,342 staining for 10 min, followed by CLSM imaging, showed the intracellular calcium concentration.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

CT26 cells were cultured overnight in six-well culture plates. After various treatments, 1 mL of JC-1 staining solution was added to the cells, which were then incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C for 20 min. Finally, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were observed under a CLSM microscope.

Detection of intracellular ROS levels

To detect ROS generation in the CT26 cells, the cells were stained with a DCFH-DA probe after 4 h of treatment. The DHE probe is commonly used to detect intracellular O2•− production. CT26 cells were subjected to different treatments for 4 h before the DHE staining solution was applied. Cell fluorescence images were obtained using CLSM (Ex/Em = 535/610 nm).

Live/Dead cell staining assay

After culturing CT26 cells in six-well culture plates, the cells were subjected to various treatments (nanoparticle concentration fixed at 150 µg mL−1) for 4 h. The cells were then sequentially treated with PBS, Calcein AM, PI, PBS, 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and PBS in the dark.

Apoptosis detection assay

Six groups of CT26 cells were injected into the 96-well culture plates: (1) control, (2) ZIF-8, (3) LZ, (4) ZC, (5) LZCHA, and (6) LZCHA + Laser. The CT26 cells were incubated with different materials (150 µg mL−1) for 4 h each, and the LZCHA + Laser group received 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2) after incubation. Subsequently, each well was analyzed according to the guidelines provided by the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit. The assay was performed using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer.

Intracellular ONOO− levels

CT26 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates and subjected to different treatments. The intracellular ONOO− content was then detected by staining with 1 mL of the ONOO− detection probe for 30 min.

Assessment of intracellular LPO

CT26 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates, cultured overnight, and exposed to different treatments. After incubation with BODIPY 581/591 C11 dye for 30 min, the cells were imaged by CLSM.

Intracellular MDA/GSH/ATP content assay

CT26 cells were added to 96-well culture plates and incubated overnight. After 4 h of treatment, the cells were harvested and assayed for intracellular MDA/GSH/ATP content using an MDA/GSH/ATP assay kit.

Western blot

The experiments were divided into six groups: (1) control, (2) ZIF-8, (3) LZ, (4) ZC, (5) LZCHA, and (6) LZCHA + Laser. The expression levels of GPX4, Bcl-2, and cleaved Caspase-3 target proteins were determined according to standard protocols.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of LZCHA nanoparticles

Considering the central role of oxidative imbalance and energy metabolism in the regulatory network of ferroptosis, multifunctional LZCHA nanoparticles integrating Ca2+, Zn2+, and NO donors were prepared using a three-step synthesis strategy to construct a therapeutic platform capable of maximizing the efficacy of ferroptosis and apoptosis (Fig. 2a). Specifically, the surface potential of L-Arg was altered by anionic surface modification to enhance its adsorption capacity for Zn2+, thereby providing nucleation sites for in situ ZIF-8 development. As a typical metal-organic framework material, ZIF-8 has unique porous structural features, including a uniform pore size distribution and high specific surface area, which provide an ideal support platform for subsequent functionalization and modification. After the formation of the ZIF-8 shell layer structure, the porous nature of ZIF-8 was utilized to load CaP into the pores. To further enhance the tumor targeting and biocompatibility of the nanoparticles, surface modification with HA was employed. HA on the surface of LZCHA specifically recognizes the CD44 receptor overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells, enabling the active delivery of nanoparticles.

With an average size of 165 ± 3.23 nm, the synthesized LZ nanoparticles show a consistent hexagonal morphology, a distinct change from the octahedral ZIF-8 (Fig. 2b; Figure S1, Supporting Information). The regular hexagonal structure and surface flatness indicated that Zn2+ was uniformly adsorbed on the L-Arg surface and formed a well-crystallized ZIF-8 shell layer. CaP, formed by the chelation of Ca2+ with poly (acrylic acid), was loaded into the pore structure of LZ. Compared with LZ, the surface of LZC showed obvious graininess, while the overall morphology remained intact (Fig. 2c). The uniformly dispersed granular material on the surfaces of the LZC nanoparticles was further confirmed as loaded CaP by elemental mapping (Fig. 2d). After HA and PDA surface modification, LZCHA nanoparticles retained a good hexagonal morphology with a slight increase in size to 180 ± 2.85 nm, which remained appropriately sized for cellular uptake after multiple modifications (Fig. 2e; Figure S2, Supporting Information). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) clearly shows the uniform distribution of the two characteristic elements, Zn and Ca, in the LZCHA nanoparticles, with an atomic content ratio of approximately 2.23:1, revealing unique spatial colocalization characteristics among the elements (Fig. 2f). XRD patterns showed that LZ was in perfect agreement with the characteristic peaks on the simulated ZIF-8 lattice surface, such as (011), (002), (112), (022), (013), (222), (114), and (233) (Fig. 2g) [66]. Remarkably, after modification with CaP and HA, LZC and LZCHA retained crystalline structures highly consistent with that of LZ. The entire synthesis process did not destroy the crystalline integrity of the nanoparticles. XPS was used to accurately determine the relative contents of Zn2+ and Ca2+ on the nanoparticle surfaces. As shown in Fig. 2h, the average mass fractions of Zn2+ and Ca2+ in LZCHA were 7.07% and 2.44%, respectively, which not only confirmed the efficient loading of CaP into the ZIF-8 pores but also provided important experimental evidence to elucidate the ion release behavior of LZCHA in the TME. The characteristic absorption peak of LZ at 2944 cm−1 observed by FTIR was attributed to the amino vibration of L-Arg (Fig. 2i) [67]. The CaP modification of LZC resulted in the appearance of asymmetric telescoping vibrations of PO4 −3 ions at 1067 and 560 cm−1, respectively [68]. The characteristic peaks at 1038 and 2885 cm−1 in the infrared spectrum of LZCHA corresponded to the carbonyl and C-H stretching vibrations of HA, respectively [69]. The mass loss of the nanoparticles during heating was determined by TGA to accurately quantify the arginine loading. As shown in Figure S3 (Supporting Information), a significant mass loss of LZ occurred in the temperature range of 200–400 °C, which was mainly attributed to the thermal decomposition process of L-Arg (17.87% L-Arg loading was calculated). The evolution of the porous properties of the nanoparticles was revealed by BET (Fig. 2j; Figure S4, Supporting Information). The specific surface area of LZ reached 735.41 m2 g−1, with an average pore size of 23.15 nm and pore volume of 0.43 cm3 g−1. After CaP encapsulation, the specific surface area of LZC decreased to 635.39 m2 g−1, the average pore size decreased to 6.88 nm, and the pore volume decreased to 0.22 cm3 g−1. The significant changes in the structural parameters confirmed the deposition of CaP inside the pores of the LZ. Meanwhile, LZC maintained sufficient porosity to provide favorable conditions for subsequent HA modification. After modification, the zeta potential of L-Arg shifted from + 7.38 ± 0.39 mV to − 6.74 ± 0.38 mV, and after capping with ZIF-8, the potential was positively shifted to + 10.56 ± 0.27 mV. After CaP and HA modifications, the zeta potentials became − 13.52 ± 0.37 and − 8.34 ± 0.21 mV, respectively (Fig. 2k). Notably, the considerable changes in the zeta potential at the surface of the material particles and the development of distinctive absorption peaks of HA in the FTIR spectra provide strong experimental evidence for the surface modification of HA. DLS showed that the hydrated particle sizes of LZ, LZC, and LZCHA were 169.66 ± 2.37, 176.19 ± 3.37, and 180.61 ± 2.96 nm, respectively, which were generally consistent with the results of TEM (Fig. 2l). The nanoscale size of LZCHA enables it to accumulate in tumor tissues owing to the high permeability of the tumor vasculature and its retention at the tumor site. The average nanoparticle size of the material remained stable over 6 days, and the PDI was consistently < 0.3, demonstrating the excellent stability of LZCHA (Fig. 2m). In addition, LZCHA showed acceptable PDI in PBS, DMEM, and DMEM containing 10% FBS, demonstrating that it can maintain good biostability over a long period. This property helps reduce side effects during transport under complex environmental conditions in vivo, while allowing safe and effective access to the tumor site for antitumor therapy (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Overall, LZCHA has an optimal nanoscale size for efficient accumulation at the tumor site through enhanced permeation and retention, and its good colloidal stability ensures prolonged residence time in the bloodstream. ZIF-8, L-Arg, and CaP acted synergistically to construct a unique cascade mechanism that amplified ion interference and oxidative stress, ultimately activating the dual death pathways of ferroptosis and apoptosis.

NO and ion release characteristics and photothermal conversion performance

LZCHA integrated the pH-responsive properties of ZIF-8 with the CD44-targeting function of HA to achieve precise accumulation and responsive release in the TME. Under the weakly acidic conditions of the TME and acidic environment of lysosomes, the LZCHA nanoparticles were biodegraded to release Zn2+, Ca2+, and L-Arg. Among them, L-Arg was efficiently converted to NO and L-citrulline under the effect of overexpression of iNOS and O2•− in tumor cells. By targeting the release of NO and metal ions, LZCHA effectively disrupted the redox balance and inhibited the antioxidant capacity of tumor cells, thereby maximizing the synergistic activation of ferroptosis and apoptosis (Fig. 3a). To investigate the NO and ion release behavior of LZCHA in detail, an in vitro simulation system was established using H2O2 and various buffers (pH = 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4).

The concentration of NO produced by LZCHA in the presence of H2O2 was determined using the Griess method and quantitatively analyzed in conjunction with the NO standard curve (Figure S6, Supporting Information). After 10 h incubation at 37 °C, the NO concentration released by LZCHA reached 45.30 and 55.49 µM at pH = 5.0 and 6.5, respectively, which was significantly higher than the 26.58 µM at a normal physiological pH of 7.4 (Fig. 3b,c). Meanwhile, LZCHA can produce NO in response to an acidic TME. The mechanism of NO release was investigated in detail by plotting a standard curve of L-Arg and monitoring the consumption kinetics of L-Arg in real-time using UV-vis spectroscopy with 1-naphthol and diacetyl as chromogens (Figure S7, Supporting Information). In acidic solutions (pH = 5.0), the concentration of L-Arg decreased significantly within 2–4 h, and its rate of consumption correlated significantly with a rapid increase in NO concentration (Fig. 3d). L-Arg, a precursor molecule for NO generation, was efficiently encapsulated in ZIF-8. The coordination bond between Zn2+ of ZIF-8 and the 2-methylimidazole ligand dissociates under acidic conditions, leading to disruption of the backbone structure. Notably, the ZIF-8 structure remained comparatively constant, and the rate of L-Arg release greatly decreased under typical physiological conditions (pH = 7.4). This pH-dependent release behavior endowed LZCHA with excellent tumor-targeting properties, allowing it to be preferentially degraded in an acidic TME while reducing nonspecific damage to normal tissues. Meanwhile, LZCHA nanoparticles feature a hierarchical structure for tumor-specific NO release. The HA outer layer ensures CD44-mediated tumor targeting. In the acidic tumor microenvironment, the CaP shell degrades, exposing the ZIF-8 core. Subsequent endocytosis and lysosomal acidity trigger ZIF-8 disintegration and L-arginine release, which is converted to NO by high ROS/iNOS in tumor cells, minimizing systemic toxicity. The release behavior of Ca2+ and Zn2+ under various pH conditions was quantified using ICP-OES. Incubation of LZCHA in solution (pH = 7.4) for 24 h resulted in a 23.91% and 25.69% cumulative release of Ca2+ and Zn2+, respectively. In contrast, at pH = 6.5 and 5.0, the Ca2+ release rate increased to 62.03% and 88.12%, and the Zn2+ release rate reached 66.17% and 89.54%, respectively (Fig. 3e, f). The pH gradient response characteristic perfectly matched the acidic gradient environment from the tumor tissue to lysosomes, enabling the controlled delivery of LZCHA from tumor-target accumulation to precise intracellular release. In addition, the photothermal conversion properties of LZCHA nanoparticles were investigated. Even at a low concentration (150 µg mL−1), the temperature of LZCHA rose rapidly to the range required for mild photothermal therapy (42–45 °C) when the temperature change was monitored under 1064 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 3g). LZCHA at 150 µg mL−1 reached the temperature needed for mild photothermal therapy in 10 min when exposed to a 1.5 W cm−2 near-infrared laser (Fig. 3h). The temperature increase of the LZCHA solution was positively correlated with its concentration, and the infrared thermal imaging camera results visualized the temperature difference at different concentrations (Fig. 3i). Owing to the localized surface plasmon resonance effect and the photothermal characteristics of the PDA coating, LZCHA exhibited excellent photothermal conversion properties. Superior photothermal conversion increases the local temperature by converting light energy into heat. The increased temperature increases the kinetic energy of the reactant molecules and accelerates the chemical reaction. To calculate the photothermal conversion efficiency (η), the LZCHA was continuously irradiated with a 1064 nm near-infrared laser until the temperature reached a steady state. The laser was then turned off, and the sample was cooled to room temperature. The photothermal conversion efficiency of LZCHA was calculated to be 26.68% based on the heating–cooling curve (Fig. 3j). To verify the photothermal stability of the material, three laser switching cycle experiments were performed, which demonstrated that LZCHA exhibited good photothermal stability and could provide continuous and effective localized heating (Fig. 3k). In summary, LZCHA integrated the dual functions of pH-responsive release and efficient photothermal conversion, realizing the synergistic effects of targeted nanoparticle delivery and precise photothermal therapy.

In vitro validation of lzcha-mediated anti-tumor efficacy

LZCHA nanoparticles achieved efficient targeted delivery through HA-specific binding to tumor cell surface CD44 receptors, significantly improving cellular uptake efficiency. The intracellular Zn2+ released from LZCHA degradation directly induced elevated levels of endogenous ROS and disrupted metabolic reprogramming homeostasis. In addition, the calcium overload storm, synergistically activated by NO and CaP, led to mitochondrial dysfunction, which in turn promoted a burst of ROS generation and exacerbated oxidative imbalance. It is noteworthy that NO not only synergistically induced a more severe oxidative imbalance with endogenous ROS, but also synergistically regulated the ferroptosis and apoptosis signaling pathways with released metal ions to achieve efficient anti-tumor effects.

Effective uptake of LZCHA by tumor cells is critical for subsequent biological experiments. The cellular uptake of FITC-labeled LZCHA nanoparticles was monitored in real time using the CLSM. As shown in Fig. 4a, a time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed in CT26 cells, indicating that LZCHA could effectively enter and accumulate in the tumor cells. As observed by biotransmission electron microscopy (bio-TEM), LZCHA nanoparticles encapsulated in cell membrane invaginations were detected in CT26 cells after 0.5 h of incubation (Fig. 4b). The enrichment of LZCHA in intracellular lysosomes increased significantly when the incubation time was extended to 4 h, which was attributed to HA-mediated CD44 receptor targeting. The specific binding of HA to the CD44 receptor overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells significantly enhanced the tumor-targeting and intracellular internalization efficiencies of the nanoparticles. In the development of biomaterials and drug delivery systems, low cytotoxicity is the primary requirement to ensure the safety of their application. The biosafety of LZCHA against L929 cells was assessed using a standard MTT assay. The cell viability remained within the normal range after the cells were treated with various doses of LZCHA for 12 and 24 h (Fig. 4c). Even at the maximum dosage of LZCHA (200 µg mL−1), cell viability remained > 85%, demonstrating that LZCHA has good biosafety. The cytotoxic effect of LZCHA on tumor cells was verified using CT26 cells. The ZIF-8, LZ, ZC, and LZCHA groups exhibited varying degrees of cytotoxicity compared with that of the control group. Even when incubated with a low concentration of LZCHA (50 µg mL−1), the cellular activity of CT26 cells under 1064 nm near-infrared laser irradiation was < 45% (Fig. 4d). Thus, LZCHA demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and selective cytotoxicity, enabling efficient therapeutic effects.

The degradation behavior of LZCHA in tumor cells and its role in the regulation of ion homeostasis were further investigated. Quantitative ICP–OES analysis revealed that intracellular Zn2+ and Ca2+ concentrations showed a significant time-dependent increasing trend with increasing incubation time in LZCHA with CT26 cells (Fig. 4e, f). After effective uptake by endocytosis into CT26 cells, the nanoparticles underwent structural dissociation of the ZIF-8 backbone induced by an acidic environment. LZCHA sustained the release of Zn2+ and Ca2+, disrupting ionic homeostasis by interfering with the intracellular calcium–zinc balance. Intracellular Ca2+ and NO levels were visualized using Fluo-4 AM and DAF-FM DA fluorescent probes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4g, a significant Ca2+ green fluorescence signal enhancement was detected in CT26 cells in the LZ group without the addition of exogenous Ca2+. In contrast to the ZIF-8 group, which showed no significant green fluorescence signal, the NO produced in the LZ group caused an imbalance in the intracellular calcium homeostasis. This is exactly because NO modifies the calcium channel in the ER, causing a decrease in the stability of the ER calcium pool and leading to the massive release of Ca2+ from the ER into the cytoplasm. Notably, in the LZC group with the addition of exogenous Ca2+, the intensity of intracellular Ca2+ fluorescence signals was significantly elevated compared with that in the LZ group. The introduction of exogenous Ca2+ amplifies the calcium signaling pathway by activating a positive feedback mechanism, ultimately leading to the onset of a calcium overload storm.

To verify whether LZCHA induced the creation of a suitable oxidative environment within the tumor cells, the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA was used to detect changes in intracellular ROS levels. Weak green fluorescence intensity was observed in the ZIF-8 group, confirming that Zn2+ interfered with the oxidative phosphorylation process by inhibiting the activity of mitochondrial complexes I and III, leading to significant accumulation of ROS (Fig. 4h). Compared with the ZIF-8 group, intracellular ROS levels were significantly elevated in the LZ group, which was attributed to GSH-restricted redox regulation. In the LZ group, the introduction of NO not only exacerbated oxidative stress by inhibiting ETC but also reduced intracellular GSH reserves, thereby disrupting the antioxidant defense system and promoting the activation of ferroptosis. Zn2+ also significantly increased intracellular O2•− levels by activating NOX1 and catalyzing the oxidation of NADPH to produce NADP+. By monitoring the changes in the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio in real time, it was observed that the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio gradually increased with the prolongation of the incubation time of LZCHA with CT26 cells. Changes in the NADP+/NADPH ratio confirmed the ability of the LZCHA nanoparticles to activate NOX1 and further disrupt metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells (Figure S8, Supporting Information). For the specific detection of intracellular O2•− levels, a DHE fluorescent probe was used. As shown in Fig. 4i, the LZCHA + Laser group exhibited significant enhancement of the red fluorescence signal, a phenomenon that confirms the ability of LZCHA to effectively induce a large amount of intracellular O2•− production in tumor cells under the synergistic effect of photothermal effects. The accumulation of O2•− can directly attack the unsaturated fatty acids of the cell membrane, triggering the LPO chain reaction, a central molecular event in the onset of ferroptosis. More importantly, the NOX1-mediated O2•− burst depletes a large amount of NADPH, leading to impaired GSH regeneration and a virtuous circle of oxidative stress and antioxidant defense imbalance. Maintaining cellular homeostasis depends on the dynamic equilibrium of the mitochondrial membrane potential. Oxidative imbalance and its disruption create a positive feedback loop for bidirectional control. The fluorescent probe JC-1 was used to detect changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential. As shown in Fig. 4j, the red/green fluorescence ratio was significantly reduced in the LZCHA + Laser group, confirming depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential. Together with the elevated ROS levels described above, this suggests that the mitochondria of CT26 cells were affected by changes in the oxidative imbalance after LZCHA + Laser treatment. In particular, the sustained decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential could further activate the caspase-dependent apoptotic pathway, which may be one of the key mechanisms of LZCHA-induced tumor cell death. The Calcein AM/PI double staining assay showed that the LZCHA + Laser group showed the brightest red fluorescence and barely detectable green fluorescence, indicating the highest number of dead cells and the lowest number of live cells, respectively (Fig. 4k). The high level of cell death and low level of cell survival visually confirmed the high efficiency of LZCHA in killing tumor cells under 1064 nm laser activation. Apoptosis was further measured by Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining flow cytometry, which revealed that the apoptosis rate of the LZCHA + Laser group was 55.30%, which was significantly greater than that of the control group and other treatment groups (Fig. 4l). Quantitative analysis of the flow data was highly consistent with the Calcein AM/PI fluorescence imaging results, which confirmed that LZCHA induced intense tumor cell apoptosis upon laser triggering. The highly potent antitumor efficacy of LZCHA nanoparticles occurs because the release of NO and metal ions synergistically triggers a cascade reaction that disrupts the intracellular redox balance by continuously increasing the level of oxidative stress, ultimately activating ferroptosis and apoptosis.

Investigation of LZCHA-Induced ferroptosis and apoptosis pathways

Inhibition of the mitochondrial ETC and activation of NOX1 by Zn2+ released by LZCHA increased endogenous ROS levels. Notably, NO is essential for the dual-death mechanism that causes tumor cells to undergo apoptosis and ferroptosis. Specifically, NO promotes Ca2+ leakage from the ER into the cytoplasm, which acts synergistically with exogenous Ca2+ released from nanoparticles to trigger a calcium overload storm. In addition, NO disrupts intracellular redox homeostasis by inhibiting mitochondrial function. NO-derived RNS also promoted LPO accumulation by depleting GSH and inhibiting GPX4 activity. LZCHA maximized the efficacy of the antitumor therapy by inducing an intracellular oxidative imbalance (Fig. 5a).

O2•− is not only an important member of ROS but also serves as a key substance in the rapid reaction with NO to form RNS. As the most oxidizing molecule in RNS, ONOO− has more significant cytotoxic effects compared with those of common ROS. The specific fluorescent probe O56 was used to monitor intracellular ONOO− levels. As shown in Fig. 5b, the weak ONOO− signal in the ZC group results from Zn2+/Ca2+ synergy. They induce mitochondrial O2•− overproduction and activate endogenous nitric oxide synthase for NO generation, leading to ONOO− formation. An enhancement of the specific fluorescence signal of apparent ONOO− was observed in LZCHA-treated cells, indicating that LZCHA nanoparticles can effectively achieve the conversion of NO to RNS. LZCHA nanoparticles triggered an amplifying effect on the oxidative stress cascade in tumor cells by synergistically upregulating ROS and RNS levels, resulting in a severe imbalance in intracellular redox homeostasis. This dual oxidative stress not only significantly upregulated the key regulators of the ferroptosis pathway but also realized the efficient synergistic induction of ferroptosis and apoptosis by activating the apoptosis signaling pathway. Specifically, RNS efficiently reduced the intracellular GSH levels through direct redox reactions. Depletion of GSH, a key regulator of ferroptosis and a crucial intracellular antioxidant, leads to the breakdown of the cellular antioxidant defense system, which in turn triggers LPO and ultimately drives ferroptosis. The results of the GSH assay showed that the intracellular GSH level in the LZCHA + Laser group was significantly reduced by 76.07% compared with that in the control group, which directly confirmed the therapeutic mechanism of LZCHA in inducing ferroptosis through GSH depletion (Fig. 5c). GSH depletion in both LZ and ZC groups had distinct mechanisms. In the LZ group, NO and Zn2+ synergistically oxidized GSH and inhibited its synthesis. In the ZC group, Ca2+-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion impaired synthesis, while Zn2+ collaborated with Ca2+ to further inhibit synthesis and promote GSH consumption. To further verify the occurrence of ferroptosis, changes in the LPO levels in CT26 cells were detected using a BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe. CLSM imaging results showed that in LZCHA-treated CT26 cells, the fluorescent signal of the probe underwent a significant shift from red (reduced state) to green (oxidized state), confirming that LZCHA can effectively induce the intracellular accumulation of LPO (Fig. 5d). MDA is a key marker of polyunsaturated fatty acid breakdown in LPO. The detection of MDA content can help visualize the extent of LPO. Quantitative analysis showed that the MDA level in the LZCHA + Laser group was significantly higher than that in the other groups, including being approximately 7.5 times higher than that in the control group (Fig. 5e). This is consistent with the LPO accumulation detected by the BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe. Combining the experimental results of GSH depletion with elevated LPO levels, it was shown that LZCHA triggers the ferroptosis pathway by disrupting intracellular redox homeostasis.

Western blotting was used to assess the expression levels of important regulatory proteins involved in ferroptosis and apoptosis to clarify the molecular processes of cell death (Fig. 5f, g). GPX4 activity was significantly reduced in the LZCHA group compared with that in the control group, and the reduction was even more pronounced in the LZCHA + Laser group. Decreased activity of GPX4, a key enzyme in the intracellular clearance of LPO, combined with GSH depletion, resulted in the inability of cells to clear LPO efficiently. In addition, expression of the classical apoptosis-related protein Bcl-2 was significantly downregulated, whereas that of cleaved caspase-3 was notably upregulated (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Downregulation of Bcl-2, an anti-apoptotic protein, weakens cellular resistance to apoptosis, whereas upregulation of cleaved caspase-3 directly confirms activation of the apoptosis execution phase. Western blot analysis provided molecular evidence that LZCHA exerted its antitumor effects through a dual mechanism (ferroptosis and apoptosis). Mitochondria are the regulatory centers of apoptosis and activate the caspase cascade reaction by releasing pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c. It is also an important regulatory node in ferroptosis, and its dysfunction can lead to ROS accumulation and LPO exacerbation. In addition, ATP supplied by the mitochondria is the main source of energy for tumor cells to meet their abnormal proliferative and metabolic demands. The ATP levels in cells under various treatments were further assessed because ATP is a crucial chemical in cellular energy metabolism that controls cell death. Compared with the control group, the ZIF-8 group showed only a slight decrease in ATP content (by 27.09%), whereas the ATP content of the LZCHA + Laser group was drastically reduced (by 74.06%) (Fig. 5h). This significant difference confirms that LZCHA can effectively target the energy metabolism pathway of tumor cells and exert potent antitumor effects by dramatically reducing ATP levels while synergistically activating the ferroptosis and apoptosis signaling pathways. Ultrastructural changes in mitochondria were observed by bio-TEM, and it was found that mitochondria in healthy cells showed a typical filamentous or thread-like structure with visible cristae (Fig. 5i). In contrast, the mitochondria in the LZCHA + Laser group were smaller, the cristae were reduced or disappeared, and the membrane density was increased, showing typical changes characteristic of ferroptosis. These morphological changes further confirmed that LZCHA effectively induced mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to ferroptosis. The roles of different cell death pathways in LZCHA-induced cytotoxicity were evaluated using the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK. The enhanced cell viability after the use of inhibitors indicates that LZCHA can co-activate intracellular ferroptosis and apoptosis (Figure S10, supporting information). In conclusion, the multi-mechanism synergistic therapeutic strategy of LZCHA, based on a calcium overload storm, oxidative stress amplification, and metabolic regulation, effectively activated the dual death pathways of ferroptosis and apoptosis in tumor cells.

Transcriptomics of the regulatory network of LZCHA-induced ferroptosis and apoptosis

LZCHA enables synergistic antitumor therapy through the combination of ferroptosis and apoptosis by creating a specific oxidative stress microenvironment in tumor cells. Specifically, NO can modify the calcium channel proteins on the ER membrane through nitrosylation, thereby increasing the probability of channel opening and promoting Ca2+ leakage from the ER into the cytoplasm. The Ca2+ released by LZCHA assists NO in inducing calcium overload. Calcium overload increases ROS production by inhibiting mitochondrial function, and ROS/RNS further damages the ER and mitochondria, leading to increased Ca2+ release in a vicious cycle. In addition, RNS modifies key antioxidant enzymes and mitochondrial complexes through nitrosylation, inhibiting their functions and leading to a decrease in the ROS scavenging capacity. RNS directly depletes GSH and inhibits the activities of antioxidant enzymes, such as GPX4, which weakens cellular ROS scavenging and perpetuates the amplification of oxidative stress. This multilevel synergy ultimately leads to a cascading amplification effect of oxidative stress, which drives cell death (e.g., ferroptosis and apoptosis).

The mRNA expression profile of CT26 cells was comprehensively analyzed by RNA sequencing to investigate the therapeutic mechanism of the LZCHA + Laser therapy strategy and its molecular regulatory network at the gene level. RNA sequencing is a high-throughput sequencing technology that can comprehensively and accurately reflect changes in gene expression levels, providing a reliable technical tool for elucidating the mechanism of action of nanoparticles. To guarantee the dependability and statistical validity of the experimental data, two groups were created for the experimental design in this study: the LZCHA + Laser and blank control groups. Each group comprised three biological replicates. The biological reproducibility of the samples was assessed using principal component analysis. The groups of samples showed clear clustering characteristics in two-dimensional space, with samples within groups being closely spaced and samples between groups showing good differentiation (Fig. 6a). Principal component analysis showed that the experimental design was reasonable and the sample processing conditions were stable, which provided a reliable basis for subsequent differential expression analysis. Heat map analysis based on whole transcriptome sequencing data confirmed significant differences in gene expression patterns between the LZCHA + Laser and control groups, suggesting that LZCHA + Laser treatment may exert antitumor effects by modulating specific gene expression networks (Fig. 6b). Through thorough differential expression analysis, 2,338 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 1,038 downregulated and 1,300 upregulated genes (Fig. 6c, d).

To fully elucidate the biological significance of these DEGs, multiple bioinformatic analyses were performed for in-depth mining. First, DEGs were found to be considerably enriched in the ferroptosis pathway using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, confirming the essential function of ferroptosis in LZCHA treatment (Fig. 6e). Notably, several classical apoptosis-related pathways were also significantly enriched, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathways. Previous studies have shown that calcium overload effectively activates the TNF signaling pathway, triggering a downstream apoptosis cascade [70, 71]. ER stress can initiate an unfolded protein response, which, in turn, induces tumor cell apoptosis. Oxidative stress leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage, ultimately resulting in cell death. In summary, LZCHA exerts its therapeutic effects through a dual stress response that drives the dual cell death mechanisms of ferroptosis and apoptosis: (1) LZCHA causes a severe imbalance in intracellular redox homeostasis by downregulating antioxidant molecules such as NADPH and GSH, and upregulating ROS and RNS levels. (2) NO triggers calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cell death by regulating ER calcium homeostasis and stress signaling pathways.

In vivo evaluation of safety and anti-tumor efficacy of LZCHA

These results demonstrated that LZCHA significantly increased intracellular oxidative stress and induced ER stress, providing an ideal microenvironment for ferroptosis and apoptosis. Based on the excellent performance of LZCHA in in vitro experiments, CT26 cells were used to establish tumor-bearing mice to evaluate the biosafety and antitumor effects of LZCHA in vivo (Fig. 7a). The hemocompatibility of LZCHA was evaluated using an erythrocyte hemolysis assay. The hemolysis rate of LZCHA was < 5% at different concentration gradients (0–200 µg mL−1), which is in line with biomaterial safety standards and confirms its good hemolysis rate (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Pharmacokinetic studies were performed to evaluate the in vivo distribution of LZCHA. The blood half-life of LZCHA exhibited typical biphasic elimination characteristics, with a distribution phase half-life t1/2α of 0.14 h and an elimination phase half-life t1/2β of 3.69 h (Fig. 7b). Kinetic analysis showed that the first-phase elimination rate constant of LZCHA was − 0.55 µg mL−1 h−1, which decreased to − 0.04 µg mL−1 h−1 after 1.93 h, and this unique pharmacokinetic profile facilitated drug accumulation at the tumor site (Fig. 7c). ICP–OES was used to study the distribution of Zn2+ and Ca2+ in various organs and tumor tissues to further evaluate the tumor-targeting ability of LZCHA (Fig. 7d; Figure S12, Supporting Information). The results showed that LZCHA was primarily distributed in the reticuloendothelial system, such as the liver and spleen, which is consistent with the typical distribution characteristics of nanomedicines. Notably, the Zn2+ concentration at the tumor site peaked at 6 h after intravenous injection (15.60% ID g−1) and remained at 12.89% ID g−1 after 36 h, confirming the significant tumor-targeting accumulation capacity of LZCHA.

To systematically evaluate the in vivo antitumor effect of LZCHA, 30 CT26 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): control, ZIF-8, LZ, ZC, LZCHA, and LZCHA + Laser. Each group was injected intravenously with the corresponding preparation on days 1, 5, and 9, and subjected to 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W cm−2, 10 min) 6 h after injection. The mice were monitored for changes in body weight and tumor volume throughout the treatment period. No significant weight changes were observed in any of the experimental groups, indicating that the treatment protocols were biologically safe (Fig. 7e). Tumor growth curves showed that the LZCHA monotherapy group exhibited moderate tumor suppression, which was primarily attributed to the synergistic effects of ferroptosis and apoptosis (Fig. 7f, g). Notably, the LZCHA + Laser group demonstrated the most significant tumor-suppressive effect, confirming that photothermal therapy significantly enhanced the antitumor activity of LZCHA. This conclusion was further validated by a photograph of the resected tumor and its weight at the treatment endpoint (Fig. 7h; Figure S13, Supporting Information). The tumor inhibition rate was calculated based on tumor weight to evaluate the tumor suppression efficacy across different groups. The LZCHA + Laser group exhibited a remarkable tumor inhibition rate of 91.26%, demonstrating the potent antitumor efficacy of LZCHA (Figure S14, Supporting Information). By monitoring the temperature changes at the tumor site, it was found that the LZCHA + Laser group could achieve local heating of 42–45 °C under laser irradiation, confirming the photothermal conversion ability of LZCHA in vivo (Fig. 7i; Figure S15, Supporting Information). To assess the clinical translational potential of the treatment regimen, systematic testing of hematological and biochemical indices was performed. As shown in Figure S16 (Supporting Information), the liver and kidney function indices, as well as the hematological parameters of the mice in each treatment group, were maintained within the normal range compared to the control group, indicating that the treatment regimen had a good systemic safety profile. H&E staining revealed no obvious pathological changes in major organs, confirming the excellent histocompatibility of LZCHA (Figure S17, Supporting Information). Histopathological examination of tumor tissue sections stained with H&E showed that the LZCHA + Laser treatment group exhibited significant inhibition of tumor growth, demonstrating strong antitumor efficacy (Fig. 7j). Finally, tumor cell apoptosis and proliferation were assessed using TUNEL and Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining. The images showed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells and a significant decrease in Ki67-positive cells in the tumor tissues of the LZCHA + Laser group, consistent with the results of H&E staining, confirming that this treatment option could effectively inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 7k). In summary, LZCHA exhibited significant tumor targeting, excellent photothermal conversion efficiency, and good biosafety.

Conclusion

Overall, LZCHA constructs an oxidatively imbalanced microenvironment by targeting and regulating the endogenous defense system and metabolic reprogramming processes in tumor cells. LZCHA achieves active targeted delivery by exploiting the specific recognition of the CD44 receptor on the surface of tumor cells by HA. First, ZIF-8 acts as a pH-responsive carrier and is specifically degraded in the TME, releasing Zn2+, Ca2+, and L-Arg, and triggering a series of cascade reactions. Zn2+ released by ZIF-8 significantly increases intracellular ROS levels by inhibiting the function of mitochondrial complexes I and III, laying the foundation for the onset of ferroptosis and apoptosis. Meanwhile, Zn2+ activation of NOX1 further exacerbates ROS accumulation and induces intense oxidative stress. L-Arg generates NO catalyzed by iNOS, which activates calcium channels in the ER and promotes Ca2+ release. ER-released Ca2+ acts synergistically with LZCHA-released Ca2+ to trigger a calcium overload storm, leading to intracellular oxidative stress, depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential, inhibition of ATP production, and exacerbation of ROS accumulation. NO-derived RNS not only enhances the oxidative stress effect of ROS but also promotes LPO accumulation by depleting GSH and inhibiting GPX4 activity, thereby inducing and enhancing ferroptosis and apoptosis. In addition, the photothermal effect of PDA resulted in LZCHA demonstrating excellent photothermal conversion performance (η = 26.68%) under 1064 nm near-infrared laser irradiation, which further enhanced the response efficiency of the local treatment mechanism. Through the synergistic action of multiple mechanisms, we created a tumor-specific oxidative imbalance microenvironment that significantly enhances the selective killing of tumor cells. The LZCHA therapeutic strategy offers a novel approach for the development of antitumor therapeutic regimens by leveraging the synergistic effects of ferroptosis and apoptosis.
