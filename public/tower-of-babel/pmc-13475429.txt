Knowledge and Attitudes Toward Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Among Physicians and Senior Medical Students in Jordan
Authors: Redab Al-Ghawanmeh, Mohanad Odeh, Tamara Kufoof, Mohammad Al-Qudah, Mohammad Hassan Al-thnaibat, Salma Burayzat, Sara Suwan, Hayfaa A Y Alhazami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder that physicians are expected to recognize across diverse clinical settings, as early identification and appropriate referral depend on both clinical knowledge and professional attitudes. Although ADHD is addressed during medical training, less is known about whether greater knowledge translates into more positive professional attitudes.MethodsA national cross-sectional study was conducted in Jordan between June and November 2023. A validated questionnaire was administered to licensed physicians and fifth- and sixth-year medical students. Overall, 553 participants (263 physicians and 290 senior medical students) completed the survey. Knowledge and attitude scores were calculated using a predefined scoring system. Associations were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square tests and ordinal logistic regression.ResultsOverall, 55.2% of participants demonstrated good ADHD-related knowledge, while 52.8% had positive attitudes. Higher knowledge levels were significantly associated with more positive attitudes (p < 0.001). Among medical students, completion of a pediatric rotation independently predicted higher knowledge (OR = 3.37, 95% CI: 1.07–10.56, p = 0.037). Among physicians, higher knowledge was independently associated with personal experience with ADHD (OR = 2.51, p = 0.004) and having children (OR = 3.22, p = 0.049), whereas physicians working in primary healthcare centers had lower odds of higher knowledge than those working in central hospitals (OR = 0.42, p = 0.002).No participant characteristics independently predicted attitudes.ConclusionPhysicians and senior medical students in Jordan demonstrated generally good ADHD-related knowledge but persistent misconceptions and mixed attitudes. Although greater knowledge was associated with more positive attitudes, the observed knowledge–attitude gap suggests that factual knowledge alone may be insufficient to improve professional attitudes toward ADHD. ADHD education should therefore combine diagnostic teaching with longitudinal clinical exposure, reflective learning, and stigma-focused educational strategies to strengthen both ADHD-related knowledge and professional attitudes.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders and is characterized by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that impair functioning across multiple settings.1 Although ADHD was historically considered a childhood disorder, it is now widely recognized as a lifespan condition associated with substantial educational, occupational, and psychosocial consequences.2

ADHD has become increasingly relevant to medical education because physicians across multiple specialties are expected to recognize early symptoms, initiate appropriate referrals, and contribute to the longitudinal management of affected individuals. Failure to recognize ADHD may contribute to delayed diagnosis, impaired academic performance, psychiatric comorbidity, and reduced quality of life among affected individuals.3

Epidemiological studies from the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region report ADHD prevalence estimates ranging from 1.3% to 16%, with pooled estimates approaching 10%.4,5 In Jordan, reported prevalence rates among children and adolescents vary considerably according to diagnostic criteria and study populations.6–9

Healthcare professionals play a critical role in the recognition and management of ADHD. However, multiple studies have demonstrated persistent gaps in ADHD-related knowledge among medical trainees and physicians, particularly regarding diagnostic criteria, inattentive symptoms, and treatment approaches.10,11 Previous educational research has further emphasized that improvements in factual knowledge do not necessarily translate into positive attitudes toward neurodevelopmental disorders.12,13

From a medical education perspective, understanding the relationship between clinical exposure, educational experiences, knowledge acquisition, and attitudes toward ADHD is essential for curriculum development. Undergraduate clerkships, clinical rotations, and continuing medical education programs may all contribute to improving ADHD competency among medical students and practicing physicians.

Recent educational literature has highlighted the importance of structured ADHD teaching strategies and innovative educational interventions. Salmon et al (2019) described the development of an ADHD-focused e-learning resource for medical students and emphasized the need for reinforcement of ADHD teaching prior to clinical exposure. Similarly, Im and Tamarelli discussed the broader educational implications of ADHD within medical learning environments and advocated for increased awareness and educational support systems.14 In Jordan, Al-Habashneh et al recently reported generally good ADHD-related knowledge among clinical medical students, while also identifying persistent gaps in diagnostic understanding and symptom recognition.15 However, that study focused primarily on students and knowledge, leaving the relationship between knowledge, attitudes, and clinical experience among both students and physicians insufficiently explored.

Despite increasing recognition of ADHD within medical education, evidence from Jordan remains limited, particularly regarding the relationship between educational exposure and attitudes toward ADHD. Therefore, this study aimed to assess knowledge and attitudes toward ADHD among physicians and senior medical students in Jordan, identify educational and experiential factors associated with knowledge, and explore the relationship between knowledge and attitudes with implications for undergraduate and postgraduate medical education.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A national cross-sectional study was conducted in Jordan between June and November 2023 to assess knowledge and attitudes toward attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder among physicians and senior medical students.

Participants and Sampling

The target population included licensed physicians practicing in Jordan and fifth- and sixth-year medical students enrolled in Jordanian medical schools. These academic levels were selected because students at this stage had completed or were undertaking core clinical rotations, including pediatrics and psychiatry.

A minimum sample size of 382 participants was calculated using standard sample size estimation methods for cross-sectional studies, assuming a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error.16 To improve statistical power and facilitate subgroup analysis, 553 participants were recruited, including 263 physicians and 290 medical students.

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. The survey was distributed electronically through university communication channels, professional networks, and social media platforms. Medical student participants were recruited from multiple Jordanian universities. Physicians from different practice settings and specialties were invited to participate through professional communication groups. Participation was voluntary, and individuals who agreed to participate completed the anonymous online questionnaire after providing electronic informed consent.

Because the survey was distributed through multiple overlapping electronic communication channels, the total number of individuals who received the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated.

The questionnaire assessed participants’ understanding of ADHD symptoms, diagnostic criteria, associated impairment, and treatment concepts, in addition to perceptions and beliefs related to ADHD.

Validity and Reliability

Content validity was evaluated using Lawshe’s Content Validity Ratio methodology.19 Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The coefficients were 0.77 for the knowledge domain, 0.71 for the attitude domain, and 0.78 for the overall questionnaire, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Scoring System

Knowledge and attitude scores were calculated using a predefined scoring system in which correct responses were assigned +1, incorrect responses −1, and “not sure” responses 0. Knowledge scores were categorized into poor, fair, and good levels based on predefined cutoffs. Attitude scores were categorized into positive and negative groups.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Associations between participant characteristics and knowledge or attitude levels were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Effect sizes were evaluated using Cramér’s V according to established thresholds.20 Cramér’s V values were interpreted as follows: 0.00 = no association, >0.05 = weak, >0.10 = moderate, >0.15 = strong, >0.25 = very strong, and 1.00 = perfect association. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Ordinal logistic regression using the cumulative logit model was performed because knowledge level was categorized into three ordered groups (poor, fair, and good). Separate multivariable models were fitted for medical students and physicians. Variables demonstrating significant associations in the bivariate analyses (p < 0.05) were entered into the corresponding multivariable models. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model fit was assessed using the omnibus likelihood-ratio test and goodness-of-fit statistics, and the proportional odds assumption was evaluated using the test of parallel lines.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Hashemite University (approval ID: 27/4/2023/2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and no identifiable information was collected.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 553 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 263 physicians (47.6%) and 290 senior medical students (52.4%). The median age was 24 years (IQR: 23–27), and 52.4% of participants were female. Most participants were single (88.1%) and did not have children (90.6%). The majority reported no family history of behavioral disorders (84.1%).

Among physicians, 60.8% were general practitioners, 48.3% worked in central hospitals, and 52.9% worked in governmental hospitals. Only 17.5% reported previous ADHD-specific training, and 15.2% indicated that ADHD was included in their specialty training program.

Among medical students, 57.2% were in the sixth academic year, 49.7% had completed pediatric rotations, and 60.0% had completed psychiatry rotations. Knowledge of ADHD

Knowledge scores were categorized into poor, fair, and good levels based on predefined score thresholds. Overall, 55.2% of participants demonstrated good knowledge regarding ADHD, while 37.6% demonstrated fair knowledge and 7.2% demonstrated poor knowledge. The overall mean knowledge score was 11.5 ± 7.13, with a median score of 13 (range: −13 to 25).

Among physicians, the mean knowledge score was 11.5 ± 6.96, whereas among medical students the mean score was 11.5 ± 7.29.

Most participants correctly recognized the impact of ADHD on school performance (92.2%) and the importance of obtaining information from both parents and teachers during assessment (84.8%) (Table S1). Most participants also correctly identified multimodal treatment as the preferred management approach (75.8%).

However, important knowledge gaps persisted. Only 33.8% correctly identified the number of ADHD subtypes, and recognition of inattentive symptoms was inconsistent. In addition, misconceptions remained regarding the role of neuroimaging and blood tests in ADHD diagnosis. Detailed responses to knowledge items are presented in (Table S1).

Attitudes Toward ADHD

The overall mean attitude score was −0.52 ± 3.33, with a median score of 0 (range: −9 to 7) (Table S2). Physicians demonstrated a mean attitude score of −0.36 ± 3.35, while medical students demonstrated a mean score of −0.67 ± 3.31.

Overall, 52.8% of participants demonstrated positive attitudes toward ADHD, whereas 47.2% demonstrated negative attitudes. Most participants agreed that confirming the diagnosis of ADHD is helpful for affected children (86.8%) and that diagnosis relieves family stress and supports problem-solving (76.3%).

Responses to several attitude items indicated persistent negative perceptions regarding ADHD. For example, 20.8% considered ADHD a “fashionable” disorder and 29.8% believed that ADHD may be used as an excuse for poor behavior. Furthermore, 50.6% of participants perceived that parents are reluctant to administer ADHD medication to their children. Detailed responses to attitude items are presented in (Table S2).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is the presence of a knowledge–attitude gap toward attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) among physicians and senior medical students in Jordan. Although higher knowledge levels were associated with more positive attitudes overall, the educational and experiential factors that predicted knowledge did not consistently predict attitudes. This suggests that ADHD-related education may improve factual understanding without necessarily producing parallel changes in beliefs, perceptions, or stigma-related attitudes.

This finding is important for medical education because ADHD competency requires more than recognition of symptoms or familiarity with diagnostic criteria. Physicians and medical students must also develop professional attitudes that support appropriate communication, referral, counseling, and shared decision-making with patients and families. In this context, the present findings suggest that knowledge-based teaching alone may be insufficient if it is not accompanied by learning strategies that address misconceptions, stigma, and clinical confidence.

The moderate knowledge level observed in this study is broadly consistent with previous research showing variable ADHD-related knowledge among healthcare professionals and medical trainees. Studies from Saudi Arabia and Indonesia have reported persistent gaps in diagnostic understanding, symptom recognition, and treatment knowledge among medical students and healthcare providers.10,21 The present findings also extend recent evidence from Jordan by Al-Habashneh et al (2025), who reported generally good ADHD-related knowledge among clinical medical students but identified persistent gaps in diagnostic understanding and symptom recognition15 Unlike that study, the current work included both physicians and senior medical students and examined attitudes in addition to knowledge, allowing a more complete assessment of ADHD-related educational competency across different stages of training and practice.

Several specific knowledge gaps identified in this study have direct educational relevance. Although most participants recognized the academic impact of ADHD and the importance of obtaining information from multiple informants, fewer correctly identified ADHD subtypes, and recognition of inattentive symptoms was comparatively weaker. This is clinically important because inattentive presentations may be less disruptive and therefore more easily missed in educational and clinical settings. Previous work has similarly shown that ADHD recognition can be affected by symptom presentation, with inattentive symptoms often receiving less attention than hyperactive or impulsive behaviors.3 Medical curricula should therefore ensure that ADHD teaching includes the full clinical spectrum of the disorder rather than focusing primarily on the more visible hyperactive-impulsive presentation.

Among medical students, completion of pediatric rotation was the only independent predictor of higher knowledge after adjustment. This finding suggests that pediatric clerkships may provide particularly meaningful exposure to ADHD and related developmental or behavioral concerns. The association is consistent with experiential learning theory, which emphasizes that professional learning develops through the transformation of clinical experience into knowledge through reflection and application.22 Pediatric rotations may allow students to encounter developmental histories, parental concerns, school-related impairment, and referral decisions in a clinical context, making ADHD knowledge more concrete and clinically applicable.

In the adjusted model, completion of psychiatry rotation was not independently associated with higher knowledge. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence that psychiatry teaching lacks value. Instead, ADHD-related knowledge may develop through cumulative exposure across several clinical contexts, including pediatrics, psychiatry, family medicine, and primary care. The significant bivariate association with psychiatry rotation may therefore reflect broader clinical progression rather than an isolated effect of that rotation alone. Pediatric rotation may have retained independent significance because it offers more direct and repeated exposure to developmental, behavioral, and school-related concerns, while psychiatry placements may vary in their emphasis on child and adolescent mental health. This finding supports longitudinal integration of ADHD teaching across the curriculum, with repeated reinforcement across relevant clerkships rather than reliance on a single specialty rotation.

Among physicians, knowledge level was associated with personal experience with ADHD, parental status, and workplace. These findings suggest that experiential and contextual factors continue to shape ADHD knowledge after graduation. Physicians in central hospitals may have greater exposure to complex neurodevelopmental cases, multidisciplinary care pathways, and continuing professional development opportunities. However, the lower knowledge observed among physicians in primary healthcare settings is particularly important because primary care physicians are often the first point of contact for children with behavioral, academic, or developmental concerns. Targeted continuing medical education for physicians in primary healthcare settings may therefore be especially valuable. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously because some physician subgroups, particularly participants with children, were relatively small, which may have contributed to instability between crude subgroup proportions and adjusted regression estimates.

The knowledge–attitude gap identified in this study deserves particular attention. Although knowledge and attitudes were statistically associated, the predictors of knowledge did not translate into predictors of attitude. This distinction suggests that attitudes toward ADHD may be shaped by factors that are not fully addressed through factual instruction, including personal beliefs, cultural interpretations of child behavior, prior clinical experiences, and stigma. Similar concerns have been raised in mental health education, where learners may demonstrate adequate theoretical knowledge while continuing to hold stigmatizing or skeptical attitudes toward psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions.13,23,24

This finding may also reflect the influence of the hidden curriculum in medical training. Learners do not acquire professional attitudes only through formal lectures and written curricula; they also absorb values and assumptions from clinical environments, role modeling, informal comments, and institutional culture.25 If ADHD is informally framed as overdiagnosed, behaviorally exaggerated, or less legitimate than other medical conditions, such messages may weaken the effect of formal teaching. Addressing the knowledge–attitude gap therefore requires attention not only to what is taught, but also to how ADHD is discussed and modeled in clinical learning environments.

The persistence of misconceptions despite moderate knowledge further supports this interpretation. Some participants continued to endorse views suggesting that ADHD may be overdiagnosed or used to justify problematic behavior. These beliefs are not merely factual errors; they reflect attitudes that may influence clinical communication, referral thresholds, and family counseling. Anti-stigma literature suggests that educational interventions are more effective when they combine accurate information with social contact, reflection, and opportunities to challenge stereotypes.23,24,26 For ADHD education, this may involve structured encounters with patients and families, discussion of lived experience, and supervised reflection on common misconceptions.

Approximately half of the participants perceived that parents are reluctant to administer ADHD medication to their children. Unlike the preceding attitude items, this response should not necessarily be interpreted as a misconception. Rather, it may reflect awareness of recognized barriers to pharmacological treatment. Previous studies have shown that parental decisions regarding ADHD medication are influenced by concerns about adverse effects, stigma, treatment acceptability, and broader barriers to engagement with mental health services. Therefore, this finding may represent participants’ awareness of real-world challenges to ADHD treatment rather than an inaccurate belief.27,28

From a curriculum-development perspective, the present findings suggest that ADHD education should address both cognitive and attitudinal learning outcomes. Although the effectiveness of specific educational strategies was not evaluated in this study, previous medical education research supports the use of case-based learning, simulated clinical encounters, reflective practice, and supervised discussions with patients and families to promote knowledge acquisition and attitude development.29–32

The present findings are also consistent with prior publications in the medical education literature. Salmon et al,33 described the development of an ADHD-focused e-learning resource for medical students and emphasized the need for structured reinforcement of ADHD learning Im and Tamarelli highlighted the educational implications of ADHD within medical learning environments and advocated for greater awareness and educational support.14 The current study adds to this literature by showing that ADHD-related educational needs are not limited to knowledge deficits. Professional attitudes and stigma-related beliefs should also be included in educational design and evaluation.

For practicing physicians, the findings support the development of continuing professional education that moves beyond diagnostic updates alone. Continuing medical education should include practical approaches to discussing ADHD with families, addressing concerns about diagnosis and treatment, and recognizing how clinician attitudes may affect care. This is particularly relevant for primary healthcare physicians, who may encounter ADHD early but may have fewer opportunities for specialist-supported learning. Educational programs that include case discussion, multidisciplinary input, and family-centered communication training may be especially useful.

This study has several strengths. It included both physicians and senior medical students, allowing comparison across different stages of professional development. The sample size was relatively large, and the use of multivariable ordinal regression allowed the analysis to account for potential confounding factors. The study also examined both knowledge and attitudes, which provides a broader assessment of ADHD-related educational competency than knowledge assessment alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference regarding the relationship between educational exposure, knowledge, and attitudes. Second, participants were recruited using convenience sampling through social media platforms, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample. Although participants were recruited from multiple Jordanian universities and a variety of physician practice settings, the sample may not fully represent all physicians and senior medical students in Jordan. Furthermore, because the survey was distributed through multiple overlapping electronic communication channels, the total number of individuals who received the invitation was unknown, and a response rate could not be calculated. Finally, the use of self-reported questionnaire responses may have been subject to social desirability and recall bias. In addition, the study assessed educational exposure by rotation completion and selected experiential variables but did not evaluate the content, duration, quality, or teaching methods of those rotations. Future longitudinal and interventional studies should evaluate the impact of structured ADHD educational interventions on both knowledge and professional attitudes.

Overall, the findings indicate that improving ADHD-related medical education requires more than expanding factual content. A stronger curriculum should combine diagnostic knowledge, experiential learning, reflective practice, stigma reduction, and patient-centered communication. Such an approach may help bridge the knowledge–attitude gap and better prepare future and practicing physicians to recognize, discuss, and manage ADHD in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Physicians and senior medical students in Jordan demonstrated generally good ADHD-related knowledge with persistent misconceptions and mixed attitudes. Clinical exposure, particularly completion of pediatric rotations, was independently associated with higher ADHD-related knowledge; however, greater knowledge did not consistently translate into more positive attitudes.

These findings highlight important gaps in undergraduate and postgraduate medical education related to ADHD. Educational strategies should therefore move beyond traditional knowledge-based instruction and incorporate longitudinal clinical exposure, reflective learning, case-based teaching, and stigma-reduction strategies. Strengthening ADHD-related teaching within medical curricula and continuing professional education may improve diagnostic competence, professional attitudes, and ultimately the quality of care provided to individuals with ADHD.
