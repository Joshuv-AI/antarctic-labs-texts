Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Safety of ET-26 in Subjects with Mild to Moderate Renal Impairment
Authors: Fan Yang, Chao-Zhuang Shen, Pan-Pan Ye, Wen-Shuo Lv, Li-Ze Li, Jie Shao, Lei Diao, Bo-Wen Ke, Yi Zheng, Xiao-Ran Yang, Wei Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesET-26 is a novel etomidate analog with reduced adrenal suppression. This study evaluated its pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and safety in subjects with mild to moderate renal impairment.MethodsIn this phase I trial, 24 subjects (normal renal function, mild renal impairment, moderate renal impairment; n = 8/group) received a single intravenous bolus ET-26 dose. PK parameters (Cmax, AUC0–∞, CL), urinary excretion, PD (time to unconsciousness, area under the curve [AUC] of the Bispectral Index [BIS]), and safety were assessed.ResultsSystemic exposure (AUC0–∞) differed by < 16% between renal impairment and normal groups (geometric mean ratios: 84.70–86.85%). Cmax was ~ 46% lower in renal impairment groups, but PD responses were comparable. Renal clearance was minimal (CLr ≤ 0.0085 L/h; urinary excretion ≤ 0.02%) with no correlation between estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and PK parameters. Safety profiles were similar across groups; mild myoclonus (25% per cohort) was the most common adverse event. No renal impairment-specific safety concerns emerged.ConclusionMild-to-moderate renal impairment minimally affects ET-26 exposure or efficacy due to predominant non-renal elimination. No dosage adjustment is needed for ET-26 0.8 mg/kg in these patients. However, patients with severe renal impairment or those receiving dialysis were not studied, and dedicated investigations are required before extrapolating dosing recommendations to these populations. Background

ET-26 is a novel analog of etomidate designed to preserve etomidate's characteristic rapid onset, brief duration of action, and hemodynamic stability, while minimizing adrenal suppression and associated adverse events [1, 2]. Etomidate has long been valued in clinical anesthesia for its cardiovascular safety profile, especially in hemodynamically unstable patients, but its inhibitory effect on 11β-hydroxylase and subsequent cortisol suppression limits its widespread use, particularly in critically ill or septic patients where adrenal function is critical [3–5].

Preclinical studies demonstrated that ET-26 retains the desirable anesthetic profile of etomidate but with significantly reduced adrenal steroidogenesis inhibition and lower incidence of myoclonus in animal models [6–8]. A first-in-human phase I trial in healthy volunteers confirmed linear pharmacokinetics over the therapeutic dose range, rapid achievement of loss of consciousness, and predictable, reversible sedation with minimal cardiovascular impact [2]. However, these studies were conducted exclusively in individuals with normal renal function.

Renal impairment is characterized by reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR). This decrease in GFR directly impairs the clearance of drugs predominantly eliminated by the kidneys. Furthermore, renal impairment can induce systemic pathophysiological changes, including reductions in plasma protein levels and potential alterations in hepatic metabolism [9–12]. These secondary effects can significantly impact the pharmacokinetics of highly protein-bound drugs and drugs primarily metabolized by the liver. While ET-26 is primarily metabolized by hepatic esterases, a small fraction is excreted unchanged in urine. Even minimal renal elimination may lead to drug accumulation or altered pharmacodynamics in patients with reduced GFR, potentially affecting both efficacy and safety. Regulatory guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) recommends dedicated renal impairment studies to characterize any necessary dose adjustments and ensure safe use across the spectrum of renal function [13–15].

This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and safety of a single 0.8-mg/kg intravenous dose of ET-26 in subjects with normal renal function, mild renal impairment, and moderate renal impairment. By enrolling matched cohorts based on estimated GFR categories, we sought to determine whether mild to moderate reductions in renal function necessitate dosage modifications, and to support evidence-based dosing recommendations for future clinical use in patients with varying degrees of renal impairment.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This phase I clinical trial employed a single-center, non-randomized, open-label design to evaluate the PK and PD profiles of ET-26. All subjects received a single intravenous bolus dose of ET-26 at 0.8 mg/kg. Subjects included 24 adults (aged 18–45 years; BMI 19–26 kg/m2) stratified by renal function into three cohorts based on eGFR: normal (≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2), mild impairment (60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2), and moderate impairment (30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2), with eight subjects per group. All individuals provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study implemented predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Qianfoshan Hospital before initiation (key inclusion/exclusion criteria and ethical standards are presented in the electronic supplementary materials [ESM]).

Pharmacokinetic Assessment

Plasma concentrations of ET-26 at various time points were measured using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method. Pharmacokinetic samples were collected according to the planned schedule (Table S1, see ESM), and the bioanalytical methods are provided in the supplementary materials. Protein binding was determined via equilibrium dialysis. Plasma protein binding was measured at 1 minute to capture the early/distribution phase near peak concentration (Cmax) and at 4 h to capture a later redistribution/near-steady state to evaluate the potential time-dependent changes in the unbound fraction.

Data from the normal, mild, and moderate groups were analyzed to generate individual as well as mean (mean ± standard deviation [SD]) plasma concentration–time (c–t) curves and semi-logarithmic c-t curves. The key PK parameters of ET-26 include Cmax, time to Cmax (Tmax), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC0–t, AUC0–∞), percentage extrapolated AUC (AUC_%Extrap), elimination half-life (T½), apparent clearance (CL), renal clearance (CLr), apparent volume of distribution (Vz), and mean residence time (MRT), which were calculated via a non-compartmental analysis using WinNonlin software (version 8.1), based on the actual sampling times and administered dose. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using non-compartmental analysis with the linear trapezoidal rule for area under the curve (AUC) calculations and linear interpolation for concentration–time point estimation. For each group, the arithmetic mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation were computed.

After the log transformation of the primary PK parameters (Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞), analysis of variance and two-sided tests were performed to calculate the geometric mean ratios and 90% confidence intervals. If the 90% confidence intervals fall within the 80.0–125.0% range, it is concluded that there is no significant difference in the PK profiles between the normal, mild, and moderate groups.

An exploratory linear regression analysis assessed correlations between individual eGFR and key PK parameters for ET-26 (CL, CLr, Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞) based on log-transformed PK values. For mild and moderate impairment cohorts, mean eGFR values were used.

Urinary Excretion Analysis

Urinary excretion of the unchanged parent drug ET-26 was quantified through cumulative urine collection over 0–24 h post-dose using SAS 9.4 software. Concentrations in pooled urine samples were determined using a validated LC-MS/MS method. The cumulative excretion amount (μg) was defined as the total analyte mass eliminated in urine during the 24 h. The cumulative excretion rate (%) was calculated as 100 × (total urinary excreted amount)/(administered dose normalized by molecular weight for ET-26).

Pharmacodynamic Assessment

Following the Technical Guidelines for Clinical Evaluation of Intravenous General Anesthetics (CDE, 2022) [16], the pharmacodynamic evaluation in this study focused on two primary endpoints: the time to loss of consciousness and the AUC of the Bispectral Index (BIS). Loss of consciousness was assessed by continuously monitoring the Modified Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scale, with the endpoint defined as the interval from administration to the first instance when the MOAA/S score reached ≤ 1. Simultaneously, BIS values were recorded via electroencephalography to determine the minimum BIS level. The resulting BIS curves were subsequently fitted, and the AUC was calculated using WinNonlin v8.1 or SAS version 9.4. Descriptive statistics, including sample size, mean, median, standard deviation, maximum, and minimum were tabulated for BIS AUC within each renal function group (normal, mild impairment, moderate impairment).

Safety Analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize laboratory test results, vital signs, 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) findings, physical examinations, and other safety-related parameters. Adverse events (AEs) were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 25.1), and their severity was graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0; see Table S2 in the ESM). Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), and TEAEs resulting in study discontinuation were systematically evaluated. All events were further analyzed by renal function group, severity, and their assessed relationship to the investigational drug.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 24 subjects were enrolled in the study, including 8 subjects with mild renal impairment, 8 with moderate renal impairment, and 8 subjects with normal renal function. The median age was 42.0 years (range 28–64 years). The cohort comprised 22 males and 2 females, all of whom were of Han Chinese ethnicity. The median height was 168.5 cm (range 151.0–182.5 cm), the median weight was 66.05 kg (range 57.4–84.9 kg), and the median body mass index (BMI) was 24.30 kg/m2 (range 19.5–29.6 kg/m2) (Table 1). Subjects with normal renal function were matched 1:1 by gender to those with mild and moderate renal impairment. Additionally, the mean age of subjects with normal renal function was within ±10 years of the mean ages of both the mild and moderate renal impairment groups, and their mean weight was within ±15 kg of the mean weights of both impairment groups.

Pharmacokinetic Analyses

Following a single intravenous injection of ET-26 0.8 mg/kg in 24 subjects (8 with mild renal impairment, 8 with moderate renal impairment, and 8 with normal renal function), pharmacokinetic analysis revealed comparable mean Tmax (2.45–2.96 min) across groups. However, the Cmax in the normal renal function group (2560 ng/mL) was significantly higher than that in the mild and moderate renal impairment groups (1370 ng/mL and 1350 ng/mL, respectively), with geometric mean ratios (GMRs) of normal renal function/mild renal impairment and normal renal function/moderate renal impairment of 54.61% (90% CI 33.94–87.86) and 54.21% (90% CI 33.69–87.22). Notably, renal impairment had limited impact on systemic exposure—GMRs of normal renal function/mild renal impairment and normal renal function/moderate renal impairment for AUC0–∞ were 84.70% (90% CI 72.63–98.78) and 86.85% (90% CI 74.47–101.29), respectively, and the difference between the normal group (854 h·ng/mL) and renal impairment groups (719 or 759 h·ng/mL) was < 16%. Moreover, CL increased with declining renal function (normal: 61.4 L/h vs mild/moderate: 73.5–78.0 L/h), while T½ and Vz remained similar across groups. Critically, the cumulative urinary excretion of unchanged ET-26 over 24 h was minimal (≤ 0.02%) without intergroup differences, and renal clearance (CLr: 0.0049–0.0085 L/h) was negligible. Most importantly, no significant linear correlations were observed between eGFR and the CL, CLr, or exposure metrics (Cmax, AUC) of ET-26 (p > 0.05). Given the small sample size (n = 8 per group), these correlative analyses are underpowered to detect small to moderate effects and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Collectively, the limited changes in systemic exposure of ET-26 in mild-to-moderate renal impairment patients, combined with the non-renal elimination pathway, suggest that dosage adjustment may be unnecessary in this population (Table 3, Figs 1, 2).

Pharmacodynamic Analysis

Following a single intravenous injection of ET-26 0.8 mg/kg across renal function groups, pharmacodynamic analysis demonstrated comparable median durations of unconsciousness (mild renal impairment: 1.93 min; moderate: 1.92 min; normal: 1.92 min). Concurrently, the BIS area under the curve was 720.7 (range 545.1–886.8) for mild, 782.0 (range 580.5–1204.7) for moderate, and 731.3 (range 509.4–904.0) for normal renal function groups, with no statistically significant differences among them.

Collectively, these results indicate that the anesthetic induction efficacy of ET-26 is similar between mild-to-moderate renal impairment patients and those with normal renal function (Table 4).

Safety Outcomes

In this study, nine TEAE events were reported in six subjects. Among these, six events were myoclonus, occurring in 25.0% (2/8) of subjects per cohort, all Grade 1. Cohort-specific events were as follows: injection-site pain (25.0%, 2/8) and elevated triglycerides (25.0%, 2/8) in mild impairment; alanine aminotransferase increase (12.5%, 1/8), hyperlipidemia (12.5%, 1/8), cough (12.5%, 1/8), and vomiting (12.5%, 1/8) in moderate impairment; and dizziness (12.5%, 1/8) exclusively in normal function. None of these participants required any pharmacological or non‐pharmacological intervention, and all recovered (Table 5).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of the PK, PD, and safety profile of ET-26 in patients with mild-to-moderate renal impairment. Critically, renal dysfunction had a minimal impact on systemic exposure, as evidenced by an AUC0–∞ difference of < 16% compared with subjects with normal renal function. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed between eGFR and CL, CLr, or other exposure metrics (p > 0.05).

The observed 46% lower Cmax in renally impaired subjects, despite comparable AUC0–∞, suggests an alteration in the initial distribution phase of ET-26. This may be attributed to potential changes in the apparent volume of the central compartment or plasma protein binding in patients with renal impairment [10, 14]. This negligible clinical effect is mechanistically explained by the minimal renal elimination of unchanged ET-26 (CLr ≤ 0.0085 L/h; urinary excretion ≤0.02%), indicating that non-renal clearance pathways, such as hepatic metabolism, predominate. However, given that the AUC and clearance were not substantially altered, this effect on Cmax is likely transient and limited to the immediate post-bolus period. Critically, this observed difference in Cmax did not translate into clinically meaningful differences in pharmacodynamic outcomes. The times to loss of consciousness and the depth of anesthesia as measured by BIS AUC were comparable across all groups. This indicates that for a single bolus dose of 0.8 mg/kg, the lower Cmax in renally impaired patients is not sufficient to impact the onset or efficacy of anesthesia. Therefore, the Cmax difference is not considered clinically relevant for dose recommendation. The observed lack of a significant need for dose adjustment in mild to moderate renal impairment aligns ET-26 with the pharmacokinetic behavior of other intravenous anesthetics like remimazolam, dexmedetomidine, and propofol [17–19]. The minimal renal elimination of these agents, which are cleared primarily by non-renal pathways (esterase hydrolysis and hepatic metabolism), collectively supports the well-established clinical practice of foregoing dose modifications in patients with renal impairment.

Regarding safety, the profiles of TEAEs considered related to the study drug were similar between subjects with renal impairment and those with normal renal function. Myoclonus was the most common drug-related TEAE, occurring in 25.0% of subjects (2/8) in each cohort. All myoclonus events were Grade 1 in severity, self-limiting, and resolved without intervention. This myoclonus represents a known class effect of the parent compound etomidate and its analogues, and its occurrence was independent of renal function status [20, 21]. Compared with historical reports of etomidate-induced myoclonus, commonly 50–80% in untreated bolus induction, the 25% incidence observed here is numerically lower but must be interpreted cautiously given the small cohort size and potential differences in dose, injection technique, pre-treatment, and subject population [22, 23]. Therefore, larger, matched head-to-head studies are needed to confirm a true safety advantage for ET-26. Importantly, no renal impairment-specific toxicity signals (such as electrolyte disturbances or drug accumulation suggestive of altered clearance) were identified. It must be noted that this study excluded patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or those requiring dialysis. The PK characteristics of ET-26 in these populations, therefore, require further validation. Of particular interest, ET-26, as an etomidate derivative, retains the core advantage of hemodynamic stability inherent to the parent drug. However, structural modifications may potentially reduce the risk of adrenocortical suppression associated with etomidate. Etomidate transiently inhibits 11β-hydroxylase, leading to decreased cortisol levels—a safety concern previously raised in critically ill patients [24–26]. In contrast, no ET-26-related reductions in cortisol levels or events of adrenal insufficiency were observed in this study. This may be attributable to molecular optimization in ET-26, which potentially reduces interference with adrenal steroidogenesis.

However, vigilance is warranted regarding the reduced Cmax observed in these patients, as this could theoretically impact the speed of induction in clinical practice, although equivalent PD efficacy was demonstrated here. Study limitations include the relatively small cohort size (n = 8 per group) and the exclusion of patients with severe renal impairment. Future studies should expand cohort sizes to validate the implications of Cmax variability and investigate the specific hepatic enzyme phenotypes governing the non-renal clearance of ET-26.

Conclusion

This study confirms that mild-to-moderate renal impairment has a limited effect on the systemic exposure (AUC) of ET-26. Clearance is predominantly non-renal (urinary excretion ≤ 0.02%), with no significant correlation observed between eGFR and PK parameters. Anesthetic onset time and depth were comparable between patients with renal impairment and those with normal renal function, supporting the clinical equivalence of the current dose (0.8 mg/kg). Based on the stability of exposure, the predominant non-renal clearance mechanism, and consistent safety and efficacy data, no dosage adjustment is required for ET-26 in patients with mild-to-moderate renal impairment.
