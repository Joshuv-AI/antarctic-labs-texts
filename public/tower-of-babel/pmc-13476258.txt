Report from the inaugural 2025 Asia-UK Maternal-Neonatal Infection and Immunity Network (AMMuNet) symposium
Authors: Suraj Bhattarai, Pritha Adhikari, Kalpana Subedi, Anjila Ghimire, Simon B Drysdale, Kirsty Le Doare, Gaurav Kwatra, Paul T Heath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Asia-UK maternal-neonatal infection and iMMunity Network (AMMuNet) convened its first international symposium in Kathmandu, November 2025, to address global and regional burden of maternal-neonatal infections. Primary objective of the symposium was to share the latest research and evidence on perinatal infections and immunization, alongside a focused workshop to develop an outline for potential studies targeting priority pathogens: group B Streptococcus, respiratory syncytial virus, Bordetella pertussis, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

In an ongoing effort to improve global pediatric outcomes, the burden of maternal and neonatal infections remains a formidable obstacle, with South Asia bearing a disproportionately high burden1–3. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD 2021), South Asia reported 3 million stillbirths (≥22 weeks’ gestation) in 2021 alone, contributing almost one-third of the total stillbirths worldwide4. A South Asian cohort study reported total perinatal deaths (stillbirths and early neonatal deaths) reaching as high as 61 per 1000 births5. Similarly, the region reportedly contributes 36% of global newborn deaths, with nearly 4 million cases of neonatal sepsis and other infections annually6. In Nepal, the stillbirth rate (≥22 weeks’ gestation) is roughly 15 per 1000 births and neonatal mortality rate is 21 per 1000 live births7,8.

Neonatal infections stand as a primary threat to infant survival, despite Nepal’s commitment to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3), as well as the Safe Motherhood and Newborn Health Road Map 2030 which aims to reduce both stillbirth rate and neonatal mortality rate to below 12 per 1000 births9.

Notwithstanding substantial progress in clinical care and health infrastructure, infections in early life continue to be a leading cause of avoidable illness and death in Nepal2,3. This burden is significantly intensified by the escalating global crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), including the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterobacter species in neonatal units across the Kathmandu Valley10,11.

Furthermore, while Nepal’s National Immunization Program has achieved strong pediatric coverage, the integration of “life-course immunization” specifically maternal vaccines remains limited. Although Nepal reports optimal coverage of maternal Tetanus-diphtheria (Td) vaccine (68–72%, 2021–24), the adoption of globally accepted pertussis-containing vaccines and the newer one targeting respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) has not been a priority yet. This represents a critical missed opportunity to address existing “immunity gaps” in the country’s diverse federal provinces12.

To address these multifaceted systemic issues, the Global Health Research and Medical Interventions Institute (GlohMED) took the initiative to facilitate an academic gathering in Kathmandu, which would act as a critical bridge between clinical practice, advanced scientific research, and public health policy. By integrating local clinical data into a broader global perspective, the symposium aimed to reposition maternal immunization as a key strategy for reducing perinatal mortality and improving maternal-neonatal health outcomes. We prioritized pathogens for which there is a suspected significant burden of disease in our population and where there are other current or proposed maternal vaccines.

Symposium overview

The Asia-UK Maternal-Neonatal Infection and IMMunity Network (AMMuNet) convened its 1st international symposium in Kathmandu, from 6 to 7 November 202513. The network is jointly led by GlohMED, Nepal and City St. George’s, University of London (CSGUL), United Kingdom (UK), and is supported by the Academy of Medical Sciences in the UK. It aims to advance regional research in the field of maternal-neonatal infection and immunity14.

The symposium served as a high-level scientific platform, bringing together clinicians, researchers, laboratory experts, immunization officers, policy makers, and representatives from international organizations including UNICEF, PATH, GIZ GmbH, and Jhpiego. Participation from national stakeholders including Nepal Government’s Ministry of Health, National Immunization Advisory Committee and the National Public Health Laboratory (NPHL), professional medical societies such as Nepalese Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (NESOG), Perinatal Society of Nepal (PESON), and the private sector ensured strong alignment with policy and implementation priorities. Approximately 40 participants attended the symposium, with the nationalities and residents from the UK, USA, Nepal, Indonesia, and India.

The 2-day symposium included focused sessions on RSV, group B Streptococcus (GBS), maternal vaccine policies, immunology of neonatal infections, and AMR. The 2nd day emphasized synthesis and future research priorities, with targeted discussions on key pathogens in the national context.

The scientific program commenced with a documentary “Maternal immunization in Nepal: Triumphs and Targets”, developed by GlohMED and supported by the IMPRINT Network, which outlined the current state of maternal and neonatal health in Nepal15. Revealing the context of 600,000 annual pregnancies and a neonatal mortality rate of 21 per 1000 live births, the documentary highlighted significant burdens of maternal infections (10% of all pregnancies), stillbirths (5% of all pregnancies), and early-onset infections (14% of all live births). In addition, detailed discussions were held regarding the Td vaccine—the nation’s sole maternal vaccine—noting its 68% coverage, existing delivery challenges, and ongoing strategies to improve uptake15.

Opening keynote

The symposium featured a keynote lecture by Professor Paul T. Heath (CSGUL, London) on “Maternal Immunization: current state and the future”. The lecture highlighted the critical role of maternal immunization in “closing the window of vulnerability” in early infancy. He placed special emphasis on vaccines against RSV, Influenza and Tdap vaccines, underscoring their efficacy, safety profiles, and optimal timing for administration during pregnancy16–18. He highlighted the need for, and potential of, a GBS vaccine, with two candidates in advanced clinical trials. Prof. Heath highlighted the role of regional academic networks like AMMuNet to advance research and advocacy in maternal immunization, sharing successful examples of other similar networks like the Immunizing Pregnant Women and Infants (IMPRINT) and the International Neonatal and Maternal Immunization Society (INMIS)19,20.

Scientific sessions

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

The first major topic discussed was RSV with the invited talk delivered by Dr. Simon B. Drysdale (Oxford Vaccine Group, Department of Paediatrics, University of Oxford, UK), highlighting the global prevalence of the virus, which includes every part of the world, with 33 million cases each year, 3.6 million admissions, and 100,000 deaths annually—97% of which occur in developing countries21. Two major prevention strategies that have been recently approved were highlighted: maternal vaccination and infant monoclonal antibodies.

The pre-fusion RSV vaccine developed by Pfizer (ABRYSVO) and approved for use by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in August 2023, showed a vaccine efficacy (VE) of approximately 80% within the first 90 days of life (DOL), according to the MATISSE trial (Maternal Immunization Study on Safety and Efficacy). The speaker highlighted at least 14 days are needed post-vaccination for pregnant women to generate adequate antibodies to protect the baby22.

Two RSV-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that have been approved for use in the USA and the European Union region—nirsevimab and clesrovimab, were also discussed. Though the binding sites of these mAbs differ, it was highlighted that the efficacy is very high for both agents, as high as 75% in decreasing PICU admissions and 80% in decreasing hospitalizations and emergency visits23,24. These developments have sparked the possibility of a cost decrease due to competition between two equally effective monoclonal antibodies25. But at the same time, the participants were hinting that these products will unlikely be available to Nepal in the near term due to cost and UNICEF/GAVI procurement issues.

This session was followed by a presentation on national RSV surveillance by Mr. Balkrishna Awal (National Public Health Laboratory, Nepal), where he emphasized Nepal Government’s efforts to detect RSV in children under 2 years of age, through routine surveillance from four sentinel sites and conducting regular testing of clinical samples. Among 461 tests conducted in the fiscal year 2024/25, a total of 139 RSV positive cases were detected (both RSV A and B) using both singleplex and multiplex PCR. Nepal’s plan is to conduct gene sequencing locally by 2026.

Dr. Rupesh Shrestha (Institute of Medicine, Tribhuvan University, Nepal) delivered a research presentation highlighting the prevalence of RSV in Nepal as identified in the RSV GOLD—ICU Network Study, a multi-country study involving ten nations26. He underscored the utility of point-of-care testing that yielded a 56.5% positivity rate and noted that RSV accounted for a significant portion of Severe Acute Respiratory Infection cases, with approximately 69% of these patients being under 6 months of age and a case fatality rate of around 3%. He also detailed the seasonal variability of these outbreaks and indicated that RSV infection is a public health priority in Nepal16.

The RSV session concluded with a research presentation by Dr. Ram Hari Chapagain (National Academy of Medical Sciences and Kanti Children’s Hospital, Nepal) who indicated that the cost of RSV illness can have a huge impact in low-income settings, with evidence that the cost in Nepal is very high compared to neighboring countries27.

Group B Streptococcus (GBS)

This session began with an invited talk by Ms. Sreska Shrestha (National Public Health Laboratory, Nepal) who highlighted the global GBS colonization rate (~20–30%), South Asian rates (~5–35%), and specific rates in Nepal (~12–20%), although with only limited studies28–30. The session underscored GBS as a leading cause of neonatal sepsis worldwide though detection remains hindered by limited laboratory infrastructure and the high costs of molecular testing. To improve diagnostic accuracy, the speaker recommended using rectovaginal swabs and selective enrichment media (LIM Broth). The presentation called for an urgent multisectoral approach to support maternal GBS vaccine trials and establish regional policies for their future use.

Another insightful invited talk by Ms. Olwenn Daniel (CSGUL, UK) outlined laboratory methods for assessing systemic and mucosal immune responses to GBS in pregnant women and infants. Her presentation detailed the application of multiplex immunoassays (Luminex), opsonophagocytic killing assays (OPkA), and flow cytometry to evaluate antibody functionality and cellular immunity. She also introduced advanced in vitro modeling using human intestinal cell lines and neonatal gut organoids to quantify GBS adhesion and invasion, highlighting that these models may be a pathway for the development of new vaccines.

The GBS session concluded with a research presentation by Dr. Suraj Bhattarai (GlohMED) who is leading a GBS pilot study in Nepal in collaboration with Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital and with the support of CSGUL and Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, Ohio, USA. With 224 mother-infant enrollments to date, the culture yield was found to be very low, but molecular analysis of the swabs preserved in Lim broth will now be conducted at a partner laboratory in Kathmandu. Additionally, detection and quantification of anti-GBS antibody in maternal serum and cord blood is being undertaken at CSGUL. The chair of the session, Dr. Gaurav Kwatra (Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, Ohio, USA), summarized the issues and further shared many similar challenges faced by him while investigating GBS prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa.

Vaccine policy and planning

The next session began with the second keynote lecture of the symposium by Prof. Kirsty Le Doare (Professor of Vaccinology and Immunology at CSGUL), who emphasized the global burden of GBS infection: 21.7 million cases of maternal colonization, with 300,000 sepsis cases, 90,000 infant deaths, and 57,000 stillbirths, as well as causing maternal deaths29,30. Since active GBS testing or intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis are unlikely to prevent maternal deaths or stillbirths, she emphasized the need for a vaccine to cover the entire spectrum of outcomes. With the GBS hexavalent polysaccharide protein conjugate vaccine expected to be available by 2029, Prof. Le Doare recommended that scientists and local stakeholders start advocating for its adoption by all countries, also stressing its importance for covering neonatal meningitis18,31,32.

The second presentation of the policy session, given by Dr. Suraj Bhattarai (GlohMED), showcased research relating to antenatal care (ANC) attendance of pregnant women across six countries, including four African countries, Nepal, and Bangladesh. He highlighted that yearly fluctuating maternal (Td) vaccination coverage in Nepal could be attributed to poor attendance for routine antenatal care, with data indicating that 24–36 weeks is the best gestational window for vaccine administration as the attendance is highest at around 77% during this period33.

Vaccine immunology

As the final keynote lecture of the symposium, Dr. Gaurav Kwatra (Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, Ohio, USA), presented recent advances in the sero-epidemiology and vaccine development of GBS. He highlighted findings from a multi-country study demonstrating substantial variation in serotype-specific maternal IgG concentrations across countries and serotypes. Bangladesh consistently demonstrated the highest geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) for multiple serotypes whereas Bhutan and Ethiopia reported the lowest levels. Serotype II demonstrated the most consistent and efficient transplacental transfer while cord to maternal geometric mean ratios (GMRs) were generally lower in settings such as Kenya, and particularly low for serotype V in Nigeria and South Africa. Correspondingly, the proportion of newborns achieving serotype-specific protective antibody thresholds for serotypes Ia and III was highest in Bangladesh and lowest in Bhutan and Ethiopia34. Dr. Kwatra further discussed the establishment of quantitative immune correlates of protection using the Gaston assay. Threshold concentrations of infant cord blood anti-capsular polysaccharide (CPS) IgG associated with an 80% reduction in invasive disease risk were estimated at 0.302 µg/mL for serotype Ia and 0.198 µg/mL for serotype III35. However, a substantial proportion of newborns did not naturally achieve these protective levels, highlighting a significant immunity gap. Dr. Kwatra concluded that these findings provide compelling evidence for the need to develop and implement maternal vaccination strategies capable of inducing adequate, serotype-specific antibody levels in infants through effective transplacental transfer, thereby ensuring optimal protection against invasive GBS disease.

Infection and antimicrobials

The day concluded with a highly engaging session on MDR infections in neonates. Studies from three tertiary hospitals revealed striking consistency: the most common organisms isolated were Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii, both critically resistant to carbapenems but still sensitive to tigecycline, polymyxin B, and colistin. However, sensitivity to aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones varied, with Dr. Anjila Ghimire (Neonatologist, Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital, Kathmandu) noting very low sensitivity rates in her data. Dr. Suchita Joshi (Neonatologist, Mediciti Hospital, Kathmandu) added a major concern that in late-onset neonatal sepsis, over 80% of cases showed cephalosporin resistance. Both presenters emphasized the need for individualized antibiograms and rigorous infection prevention protocols. The session highlighted an essential requirement for a national NICU network to establish a proper database for infection surveillance, which would document the resistance crisis and provide critical data for future vaccine development.

Discussion on future research directions on maternal-neonatal infection and immunity

The second day of the symposium commenced with a focused review of the previous day’s discussions, transitioning into an open, targeted session on potential research directions for several major pathogens in the national context for which vaccines are in development or already available. This round-table session was moderated by Dr. Pritha Adhikari (GlohMED) and chaired by the AMMuNet co-leads, attended by around 20 invitees which included neonatologists and obstetricians from our current and prospective study sites, professional societies, and immunization technical advisers to the government.

Discussions on RSV emphasized the critical need to generate robust burden estimates to inform national policy. Participants proposed a large-scale, hospital-based study using samples from a central tertiary pediatric facility to provide actionable epidemiological data. Emerging evidence suggested that high acceptability of RSV vaccination among pregnant women was likely and that implementation was feasible. Determining optimal gestational timing for vaccine delivery was identified as an operational priority.

For GBS, discussions focused on the challenges of accurately assessing disease burden, particularly due to the dynamic nature of GBS colonization and limitations in diagnostic capacity. Insights from ongoing study by GlohMED at Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital, in collaboration with CSGUL, underscored difficulties in bacterial isolation and the need to strengthen laboratory approaches, including the use of PCR-based diagnostics. Global protocols, contrasting universal versus risk-based screening, were debated, bringing to light some influential research such as the GBS3 study in the UK, which has the potential to reshape existing screening guidelines.

The discussion on pertussis highlighted a critical gap in surveillance data. A proposition was made to use the samples being collected for ongoing studies to screen for pertussis, particularly in infants under 6 months, using a multiplex test, after obtaining appropriate regulatory approvals. With regards to participant recruitment, a consensus was reached on sampling children before the completion of the third pertussis vaccine dose to accurately reflect pre-vaccination vulnerability. Such data could provide a compelling argument for transitioning from Td to Tdap maternal vaccination to protect newborns during the early vulnerable period.

The session also addressed the urgent threat of MDR Klebsiella in neonatal intensive care units. The lack of reliable burden data and the organism’s outbreak potential were highlighted as major concerns. The possibility of linking maternal Klebsiella pneumoniae colonization with ongoing GBS prevalence study was also explored. Priority actions included strengthening laboratory capacity, standardizing antimicrobial susceptibility testing including minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination and characterizing circulating strains through K and O antigen typing to inform vaccine development. All participants agreed the necessity of establishing a formal neonatal ICU network to enable real-time infection surveillance, support antimicrobial stewardship, and to generate data to guide both clinical management and vaccine policy decisions.

Conclusion

The “AMMuNet 1st International Symposium: Maternal-neonatal Infection and Immunity”, convened in Kathmandu (2025) fostered multidisciplinary dialog and defined a clear evidence-based agenda. The meeting highlighted critical gaps in pathogen burden data, surveillance systems, and implementation of maternal immunization strategies, while identifying actionable priorities to inform policy and practice.

By strengthening regional collaboration and aligning research with national needs, the symposium underscored the role of maternal vaccination and integrated surveillance in reducing neonatal morbidity and mortality. Moving forward, sustained investment, strategic partnerships, and coordinated research efforts will be essential to translate these priorities into impact and advance maternal and child health outcomes in Nepal and the wider South Asian region. The network prepares to host the second international meeting in 2027.

As a limitation, we mention that Nepal’s sudden political shift and uncertainties in late 2025 affected the organization of this meeting, and some collaborators from neighboring South Asian countries (India, Bangladesh) initially willing to attend the event decided not to attend. This immunization-focused event also lacked the industry presence, and we ensure that our future meetings will invite regional manufacturers.
