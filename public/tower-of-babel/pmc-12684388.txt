Telemedicine: a substitute or complement for home-based medical care? the experience of Medicare enrollees with dementia
Authors: Amresh D Hanchate, Stephanie K Nothelle, Bruce Kinosian, Michael P Bancks, Emilie D Duchesneau, Lindsey Abdelfattah, Jianing Guo, Mia Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) progress, many individuals become homebound and increasingly depend on home-based medical care (HBMC), such as clinician house calls. The COVID-19 pandemic spurred a rapid expansion of telemedicine under Medicare, but its impact on outpatient care by modality—HBMC, telemedicine, and office-based—among enrollees with ADRD remains unclear.MethodsUsing national Medicare claims, we examined changes in primary care visits—categorized as office-based, HBMC, and telemedicine—between the pre-expansion period (March 2019–February 2020) and the late post-expansion period (March–November 2021), when telemedicine use had stabilized. Analyses were stratified by regions with low vs high HBMC provider availability.ResultsRegions with high HBMC availability saw a 13.6% greater increase in HBMC visits and a 5.0% greater increase in all visits compared to low-availability regions. However, gains were concentrated among non-Hispanic White enrollees, with no significant changes for non-Hispanic Black or Hispanic enrollees.ConclusionThese findings suggest that Medicare's telemedicine waivers expanded, rather than replaced, HBMC. Sustaining these policies may support aging in place for individuals with dementia, though disparities in access remain a critical concern.Keywords: telemedicine expansion, home-based medical care, outpatient visits, people living with dementia, office-based visits, telemedicine visits

Introduction

More than seven million Medicare beneficiaries are either completely or partially homebound due to limitations in their ability to leave home independently.1 Among these individuals, approximately 7% to 10% receive home-based medical care (HBMC), defined as clinician-provided medical services delivered in the home or an assisted living facility.2 Persons living with dementia (PLWD) comprise a disproportionately large share of the completely homebound population—over 80%—and are therefore more likely to utilize HBMC.1 Despite this need, the supply of HBMC providers remains insufficient to meet demand.2-6 Moreover, the geographic distribution of HBMC providers is uneven, resulting in significant disparities in access to care.7,8

The COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed a dramatic shift in outpatient care delivery. In response to the rapid imposition of infection mitigation policies and the introduction of telemedicine waivers, telemedicine use among Medicare enrollees surged from under 1% in 2019% to 48% in 2020, before stabilizing at 15%-18% in 2021.9-13 While this expansion improved access for many, its longer-run implications for HBMC users remain unclear.14

Persons receiving HBMC—often older adults with complex needs—may experience telemedicine differently. For some, telemedicine may substitute for HBMC visits, though many patients and caregivers value the personal connection and comprehensive assessment that house calls provide.15,16 For others, particularly those unable to access HBMC due to provider shortages, telemedicine may serve as a complementary service, potentially increasing overall access. Alternatively, expanded telemedicine flexibilities may allow HBMC providers to reduce reliance on office-based visits and extend both telemedicine and HBMC services to a broader population of homebound enrollees. Given known differences in HBMC use by residential setting and race and ethnicity,2,17 the effects of telemedicine on HBMC use may also differ. As telemedicine continues to play a central role in care delivery, it is essential to clarify its function relative to HBMC among community-living, homebound Medicare beneficiaries.18-20

Using administrative data from a nationally representative cohort of PLWD enrolled in traditional Medicare, we analyzed longitudinal trends in outpatient visits—categorized as office-based, home-based medical care (HBMC), and telemedicine—before and after the expansion of telemedicine. Given that telemedicine waivers were implemented simultaneously nationwide, we considered the subsequent volumes of office-based, home-based medical care (HBMC), and telemedicine visits as interdependent (endogenous) variables. Our analysis examined the differential changes in visit volumes across regions characterized by low vs high availability of HBMC providers. Our analysis examined whether HBMC use broadened to include more beneficiaries and whether the intensity of HBMC use changed among existing users. We also assessed variation in outcomes among enrollees by residential setting (private residence vs assisted living facility) and race and ethnicity.

Methods

Data sources

From the national population of 19.3 million eligible Medicare enrollees aged 66 and older (as of 1/1/2019) with continuous TM coverage from 2018 to 2021 (or until death), we identified a random sample of 932 494 enrollees stratified by hospital referral region (HRR) and race and ethnicity (Section A).21 Our primary data source was the claims data for all inpatient and outpatient services (ie, MedPAR, carrier, and outpatient claims records) received by the sample during 2019-2021.22 We obtained HRR-level measure of the number of HMBC providers per capita using data on national counts of HBMC providers and TM enrollees in 2018.23,24 Data on COVID-19 cases and deaths were obtained from the Dartmouth Atlas,25 broadband and mobile data plan access from the American Community Survey,26,27 and primary care provider availability from the Area Healthcare Resource File.28 This study was approved by the institutional review board at [institution omitted]. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.29

Study design and cohorts

From the sample cohort, we identified the target population of those aged 66 and older with a diagnosis of ADRD as of 3/1/2019 (baseline date; “ADRD cohort”) using the Chronic Conditions Warehouse algorithm (Figure S1).30,31 The Chronic Condition Warehouse ADRD indicator is based on a validated algorithm that utilizes diagnosis codes identified in inpatient and outpatient records from the previous three years.32 To evaluate the differences in the post-telemedicine expansion change in outpatient visits by HBMC use, we compared the experience of ADRD enrollees in regions differing in HBMC availability. To adjust for corresponding regional differences unrelated to the needs of the homebound population, we included a comparison cohort of enrollees without a diagnosis of ADRD and aged 66 to 74 (“non-ADRD cohort”). We excluded those with missing data on key study measures.

Pre- and post-telemedicine expansion periods

Using longitudinal individual claims data from 3/2019 to 11/2021 (the study period), we defined 3-month intervals (quarters) as the unit of time. Treating 3/1/2020 as the start of the COVID-19 pandemic and rapid telemedicine expansion, we defined the seven quarters from 3/2020 to 11/2021 as the post-expansion period and the four quarters from 3/2019 to 2/2020 as the “pre-expansion” period. We subdivided the post-expansion period into the first four quarters (“early post-expansion period”) and the last three quarters (“late post-expansion period”).

Outcomes

Our main outcomes were the quarterly number of evaluation and management (E&M) visits by mode—home-based, telemedicine, and office-based—and overall visits provided by primary care clinicians. E&M visits by mode were identified using billing (HCPCS/CPT) codes (Section B).2,5,11,33 Primary care clinicians were identified as physicians specializing in internal medicine, family medicine, geriatric medicine, and general medicine, and nurse practitioners and physician assistants (Section C). As secondary outcomes, we obtained measures of HBMC visits in private residences separately and assisted living facilities

Low and high HBMC provider availability areas

By design, our study cohort includes enrollees from all 306 HRRs in the country. To characterize the study cohort based on the area-level availability of HBMC providers, we obtained the number of HBMC providers per 10 000 TM enrollee population by HRR in 2018, and identified the HRRs in the lowest quartile (“low-HBMC regions”) and the remaining HRRs (“high-HBMC regions”) (Figure S2 and Table S1).23

Cohort characteristics and subgroups

We defined patient age (66–74, 75–84, and 85+) and sex (male/female) at baseline. We identified four race and ethnicity categories: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Others. The Others primarily included Asians and Pacific Islanders. To characterize comorbidities at the baseline date, we utilized the 27-category Chronic Conditions Warehouse indicators (excluding ADRD and Alzheimer's disease indicators) and defined a composite measure based on the count of indicators (0-3, 4-7, and 8+).30 We used the Kim frailty index to define a 3-category indicator (non-frail, mildly frail, and moderately to severely frail) using pre-expansion claims data.34-36 As a socioeconomic indicator, we defined an indicator of Medicaid eligibility in the pre-expansion period.22 As time-varying determinants of the outcomes, we obtained quarterly HRR-level rates of COVID-19 cases and deaths and annual county-level measures of access to broadband internet or a mobile data plan and availability of primary care providers.25,27 So as to examine change in HBMC visits in terms of increased intensity among former HBMC users (“intensive margin”) and broadening of HBMC use across new users (“extensive margin”), we defined former HBMC users as enrollees who had two or more HBMC visits during the pre-expansion period among those who survived at least 6 months after the baseline date, and as those who had one or more HBMC visits among those who died within 6 months after the baseline date.2

Estimation

We compared the characteristics of the ADRD and non-ADRD cohorts residing in low- and high-HBMC regions and among former HBMC users and non-users. We obtained summary longitudinal trends of outpatient visits in low- and high-HBMC regions by mode among survivors, since inclusion of decedents may confound unadjusted comparisons over time due to baseline differences in levels of outpatient visits. To estimate the differential change in outcomes associated with expansion in low- vs high-HBMC regions, we used linear regression models with a triple difference-in-differences specification and individual fixed-effects to contrast the within-individual change in outcomes between (a) the pre-expansion and each of the post-expansion periods in high-HBMC regions relative to low-HBMC regions in the ADRD cohort, and (b) the corresponding changes in the non-ADRD cohort.37-40 Standard errors were clustered at the HRR level. Similar models were estimated for HBMC by patient location (private residence and assisted living facility) and race and ethnicity. We estimated the change in the number of HBMC visits among the subgroup of former HBMC users (“intensive change”). Using a dichotomous indicator of one or more HBMC visits as the outcome, we estimated the change in the proportion of all enrollees using HBMC (“extensive change”). To assess the validity of the difference-in-differences design, we conducted falsification tests of similarity in the pre-expansion trends in outcomes in low- and high-HBMC regions (“parallel trends tests”). We examined the sensitivity of our findings to (a) alternative categorization of HBMC provider availability into four groups (quartiles), (b) use of an alternative ADRD algorithm to identify the ADRD and non-ADRD cohorts,41 (c) use of an alternative non-ADRD cohort including all ages 66 and older, (d) combining specialty visits to the outcome measure, and (e) adjustment for selection into low- and high-HBMC groups from observed covariates. See Appendix Section D for additional estimation details.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The study population included 77 028 and 519 188 enrollees in the ADRD and non-ADRD cohorts (Table 1 and Table S2). Before telemedicine expansion, 12.2% (N = 9452) of the ADRD cohort used HBMC. HBMC users were older, with more comorbidities and frailer, and had higher mortality relative to non-users. Grouped by HBMC provider availability, enrollees in low-HBMC regions (N = 19 735) and high-HBMC regions (N = 57 293) were broadly similar in demographics, comorbidities, frailty, and mortality. Nearly half of the enrollees with ADRD died by the end of the study period. Longitudinal trends in COVID-19 cases and deaths, broadband availability, and provider availability were similar in the low- and high-HBMC areas in the pre-expansion and late post-expansion phases (Figure S3).

Observed trends in outpatient visits by mode

Pre-expansion, HBMC use was higher in high-HBMC regions relative to low-HBMC regions (0.40 and 0.15 visits in the first quarter, respectively), while office visits were similar (1.0 visits in the first quarter) (Figure 1). Between pre-expansion and the late post-expansion periods, the mean number of telemedicine and HBMC visits increased, while office visits and all visits decreased in low- and high-HBMC regions (Section E). These changes were larger among enrollees who survived the study period (Figure S4). Change in outpatient visits associated with telemedicine expansion by HBMC availability

From the triple difference in differences estimates of the change in outpatient visits associated with telemedicine expansion among ADRD enrollees, our focus is on the difference in the post-expansion change between high- and low-HBMC regions, given by the coefficient of the three-way interaction of the indicators of high-HBMC regions, late post-expansion phase, and the ADRD cohort (Table 2 and Table S4). The estimate of 0.056 (95% CI, 0.019 to 0.093) for HBMC visits indicates a larger increase in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions relative to low-HBMC regions, equivalent to a 13.6% increase over the pre-expansion number of visits in high-HBMC regions. Further, the estimate of the two-way interaction of the indicators of the late post-expansion phase and ADRD cohort indicates an additional increase of 0.063 HBMC visits (95% CI, 0.034 to 0.092) for the ADRD cohort across all regions. The estimate of the two-way interaction of the indicators of the late-post expansion phase and high-HBMC regions indicates an additional increase of 0.003 visits (95% CI, 0.001 to 0.005) among ADRD and non-ADRD cohorts. The three-way interaction estimates of the differential change in the late-post expansion period in high-HBMC regions indicate an increase in telemedicine visits (0.021; 95% CI, 0.004 to 0.038) and all visits (0.066; 95% CI, 0.001 to 0.132) and no change in office-based visits.

Assessing the composition of the larger increase in HBMC use in the late post-expansion period, we found that telemedicine expansion was associated with a broadening of HBMC use (Table S5). High-HBMC regions experienced a larger increase in the proportion of enrollees using HBMC (0.014; 95% CI, 0.005 to 0.024), equivalent to a 10.35% increase. Among existing HBMC users, the difference in the change in HBMC visits between high- and low-HBMC regions was not significant (0.215; 95% CI, −0.093 to 0.523).

Change in outpatient visits among subgroups

Stratified analysis by race and ethnicity indicated that the larger increase in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions in the late-post expansion period was experienced by non-Hispanic White enrollees (0.062; 95% CI, 0.021 to 0.104) and not by non-Hispanic Black enrollees (<0.001 [−0.045, 0.046]) and Hispanic enrollees (0.018, 95% CI, −0.018 to 0.055) (Table 3). Expansion was associated with a larger increase in telemedicine use in high-HBMC regions among non-Hispanic White enrollees (0.20; 95% CI, 0.001 to 0.039) and non-Hispanic Black enrollees (0.037; 95% CI, 0.020 to 0.054), and a similar increase among Hispanic enrollees. The corresponding change in office visits and all visits was similar across all race and ethnic groups. Larger increases in high-HBMC regions were observed both for HBMC visits in private residences (0.073; 95% CI, 0.037 to 0.110) and assisted living facilities (0.060; 95% CI, 0.025 to 0.095) (Table S6). Stratified analysis by frailty, we found larger increases in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions among non-frail adults (0.067; 95% CI, 0.019 to 0.115) but similar change among mildly to severely frail adults (Table S7).

Secondary and sensitivity analyses

Falsification tests of the difference-in-differences model indicated that before expansion, the longitudinal trends in outcomes were similar in low- and high-HBMC regions (Table S8). Alternative categorization of HRRs into four categories by HBMC provider availability (quartiles) showed larger increases in HBMC visits in the late post-expansion period in all three higher availability quartiles, relative to the lowest availability quarter, with an increasing trend in differences across higher availability quartiles (Figure S5 and Table S9). The finding of larger increase in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions was not sensitive to (a) an alternative identification of ADRD and non-ADRD enrollees using the Bynum-Standard algorithm (Table S10), (b) broadening of the comparison non-ADRD cohort to include all ages (Table S11), use of combined primary and specialty outpatient visits as the outcome measure (Table S12), and (d) an alternative difference-in-differences specification using propensity score weighting to adjust for potential selection on observed covariates into low- and high-HBMC regions (Tables S13 and S14). We note that the increase in telemedicine visits in the high-HBMC regions was not significant for the ADRD cohort using the Bynum-Standard algorithm.

Discussion

Among community-living TM enrollees with ADRD, the expansion of telemedicine in March 2020 was associated with a greater increase in HBMC visits in regions with higher HBMC provider availability, compared to regions with lower availability. This increase was primarily driven by a broader uptake of HBMC among new users, rather than increased visit frequency among existing users. However, this pattern was not consistent across all subgroups; notably, no significant increase in HBMC visits was observed among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic enrollees.

Nationally, nearly 28% of PLWD are estimated to be homebound.42 However, the proportion of PLWD utilizing HBMC—12.3% in the pre-expansion period in our study cohort—is much smaller, highlighting the incongruence in need-vs-availability of HBMC providers.2-6 HBMC use varied significantly by geography, ranging from 0.147 to 0.409 visits per quarter between the low- and high-HBMC regions.

To identify the potential impact of telemedicine expansion on HBMC use, we compared regions with high- vs low-HBMC provider availability since the comparison of HBMC users and non-users may be confounded by unobserved differences and longitudinal changes in need (eg, functional limitations, family support). In contrast, PLWD living in high- and low-HBMC regions are similar in demographics, comorbidity, frailty, and survival risk and this is a methodological strength of our study. Since part of the variation in HBMC use is impacted by HBMC availability, rather than confounding factors, regional HBMC availability serves as a measure of HBMC use that can be utilized to support causal inference. While telemedicine expansion was associated with similar increase in telemedicine visits and decrease in office visits in high- and low-HBMC regions, we found a larger increase in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions relative to low-HBMC regions. The larger increase in HBMC visits arose primarily from a broadening of HBMC use across those who were not frail and had not used HBMC before expansion. Nationally, with an estimated 1.4 million PLWD in TM, and based on rates of HBMC use in our study, an estimated 143 800 used HBMC before expansion in high-HBMC regions, and telemedicine expansion was associated with expanding access to 1493 new users by 2021, relative to the change in low-HBMC regions.43

To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has examined the impact of telemedicine expansion on HBMC use. Prior work found that expansion was associated with increased telemedicine and reduced office-based visits among PLWD44 and all TM enrollees.40 Previous research has assessed the potential effects of telemedicine expansion by considering variations in telemedicine utilization across health systems and providers immediately after the implementation of telemedicine waivers (2020) as exogenous factors influencing patient outcomes, including total outpatient visits. {Nakamoto, 2024 #12692}{Zeltzer, 2024 #12677} In contrast, our study conceptualizes telemedicine utilization following waiver implementation as an endogenous outcome which may be interdependent with HBMC and office-based visits. We specifically investigated the differential changes in post-expansion visits in regions characterized by either low or high availability of HBMC providers (prior to telemedicine expansion). The Independence at Home Demonstration project found that telemedicine's share of overall primary care visits was higher among high-need TM enrollees in HBMC practices relative to similar high-need enrollees not in HBMC practices (26% vs 17%).45

Our findings indicate the larger increase in HBMC visits in high-HBMC regions was found among non-Hispanic White enrollees and not among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic enrollees. In contrast, a larger increase in telemedicine visits was found among non-Hispanic Black enrollees and not among the other groups. A plausible explanation is differential access to HBMC providers among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic enrollees, as evidenced by the lower prevalence of HBMC use among these groups, relative to non-Hispanic White enrollees, in high- and low-HBMC regions.

Although substantial and persistent regional disparities in the availability of HBMC providers have been extensively documented, the underlying causes remain poorly understood {Harrison, 2020 #13810;Liu, 2022 #12418;Zimbroff, 2021 #12411}. Rural and sparsely populated regions may experience more pronounced provider shortages, attributable to increased travel distances and longer commute times required for HBMC providers {Rogers, 2024 #13811}. The limited number of provider training programs specifically designed for HBMC may also play a role {Cornwell, 2019 #12839}. The composition of the HBMC workforce has evolved in recent years, characterized by a growing presence of nurse providers and a declining proportion of physicians {Perloff, 2024 #13800;Yao, 2017 #13483}. Moreover, there has been a rapid increase in HBMC visits across patients of all ages, including those who are not chronically homebound {Nova One Health, 2025 #13642}. While these developments have the potential to enhance overall access to HBMC, their implications for regional variation in service availability remain uncertain.

A key implication of our study is that telemedicine expansion has the potential for expanded access to HBMC among homebound PLWD. The legislative waivers that enabled flexibility in telemedicine may have led to the adoption or expansion of telemedicine services among HBMC providers and their joint use to expand the number of homebound patients served.44,46-48 However, telemedicine flexibilities are not permanent, despite the continuation of waivers through short-term extensions.49 Making the flexibilities permanent, particularly for the homebound population, may enable expanded HBMC access, as the need for HBMC continues to increase.6,8 As the reimbursement for HBMC remains lower than that for office-based visits in TM, there may be limited potential for growth in HBMC availability.48,50,51 Telemedicine flexibilities provide a unique opportunity to increase HBMC access and advance the current priorities of promoting community living and care coordination among PLWD.52-54

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, since the introduction of telemedicine waivers was triggered by the outbreak of COVID-19 pandemic, our difference-in-differences estimation approach may be confounded by unobserved changes arising from the COVID-19 emergency, including differential changes by regions in the patterns of healthcare delivery. Our use of a comparison non-ADRD cohort was designed to adjust for such regional differences and mitigate the potential confounding. Further, our findings were not sensitive to alternative categorization of regions by HBMC provider availability. Secondly, our dataset did not permit identification of individuals who moved into long-term nursing home care or whether this transition occurred before or after the baseline date (3/2/2019). Because of this limitation, we could not compare study enrollees in low vs high HBMC provider regions to determine if there were systematic differences in transitions to long-term nursing home care during the post-expansion period. Third, our study lacked data on provider and practice level volumes of outpatient visits by mode and how these changed after telemedicine expansion. Fourth, our study is limited to traditional Medicare enrollees, and the experience of telemedicine expansion may differ in Medicare Advantage and other populations. Finally, our study included two years of post-telemedicine expansion data, and may not capture the longer run changes in the delivery and utilization of outpatient care for homebound enrollees.

Conclusion

The expansion of telemedicine beginning in March 2020 was associated with a larger increase in HBMC visits in regions with higher HBMC provider availability among community-living PLWD. However, a larger HBMC increase was not experienced by non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic subgroups. Establishing permanent telemedicine flexibilities, especially for the homebound population, may improve access to HBMC and promote care coordination and community living. Further research is needed to understand and address the additional barriers to HBMC and telemedicine access among diverse subgroups.
