Barriers and facilitators to reducing sedentary behaviour in stroke survivors: a scoping review based on the theoretical domains framework
Authors: Yaqin Li, JieKai Yang, Yuting Yang, Lili Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo synthesise barriers and facilitators to reducing sedentary behaviour among stroke survivors and map them to the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF).DesignA scoping review conducted following Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, Scoping Review extension (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.Data sourcesNine electronic databases (PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI and WanFang) were searched from inception to May 2025. Grey literature was identified through Google Scholar and ProQuest. Updated research was conducted in July 2026.Eligibility criteriaStudies focusing on barriers and/or facilitators to sedentary behaviour among stroke survivors were included, with no restrictions on study design.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data and mapped identified barriers and facilitators to the Theoretical Domains Framework. Frequencies of domains were calculated to summarise distribution patterns.Results49 studies were included. A total of 32 barriers and 17 facilitators were identified and mapped to 12 TDF domains. Barriers were reported more frequently than facilitators. The most frequently represented domains included Skills, Environmental Context and Resources, Beliefs about Capabilities, Beliefs about Consequences, Social Influences, Knowledge and Emotion. Common barriers included stroke-related physical and functional limitations, fatigue, inadequate knowledge, low self-efficacy, fear of falling, limited social support and environmental constraints. Key facilitators included sedentary behaviour education, higher self-efficacy, supportive social and physical environments, inpatient rehabilitation and behaviour change strategies that promoted more active lifestyles.ConclusionsBarriers to reducing sedentary behaviour among stroke survivors include limited knowledge, low motivation, insufficient social support, negative emotions and beliefs and physical limitations. Facilitators include social support, positive beliefs and supportive environmental conditions. Future interventions should comprehensively address these multilevel factors to develop more tailored and feasible behaviour change strategies.Registration numberThis study was registered with the Open Science Framework website (osf.io/ma8qp).

Background

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and a major cause of disability globally, characterised by high incidence, mortality, disability and recurrence rates. In 2021, approximately 11.95 million new cases were diagnosed, and 7.25 million people died from stroke worldwide.1 The risk of recurrence increases substantially over time, with rates of 3.1% at 30 days, 11.1% at 1 year, 26.4% at 5 years and 39.2% at 10 years after the initial stroke, potentially resulting in severe disability or death.2 3 Modifiable lifestyle behaviours are increasingly recognised as important targets for secondary stroke prevention. Among these behaviours, sedentary behaviour (SB) has received growing attention. SB, defined as waking activities with an energy expenditure ≤1.5 metabolic equivalents while sitting, reclining or lying,4 is associated with increased risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer, type 2 diabetes and body composition/adiposity.5 6

In individuals with stroke, SB is particularly relevant during the chronic and long-term stages of recovery, when prolonged SB is highly prevalent after hospital discharge. High levels of SB have been consistently reported across the stroke care continuum, with patients spending approximately 94% of waking time sedentary in acute care,7 74% during rehabilitation8 and 63–87% in community settings. Excessive SB among stroke survivors has been associated with adverse health outcomes, including higher levels of depression and cognitive impairment,9 10 as well as an increased risk of stroke recurrence and mortality.11 12 Although increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and reducing SB are both relevant for secondary stroke prevention, stroke survivors remain highly sedentary, spending an average of 10.9 hours per day in SB and only 4.9 min in MVPA.13 A meta-analysis in The Lancet suggested that 60–75 min of moderate-intensity activity per day may offset the increased risks associated with prolonged sitting (>8 hours/day) in general populations.14 Although this estimate was not derived from stroke-specific populations, it provides a useful reference for understanding the magnitude of activity required to offset prolonged sedentary time. However, achieving such activity levels may be particularly challenging for stroke survivors due to persistent neurological impairments, reduced exercise capacity and functional limitations, with only approximately 20% reaching this level of physical activity. Therefore, reducing SB, particularly by breaking up long sedentary bouts, may represent a more feasible behavioural target in stroke rehabilitation and secondary prevention.13 15 16

To design effective interventions, a systematic understanding of the barriers and facilitators to reducing SB is needed. Previous studies have identified various influencing factors, including health status,17 environment18 and social support.19 However, existing evidence remains fragmented, as individual studies have generally identified only a limited range of factors, resulting in an incomplete understanding of the complex influences on SB reduction among stroke survivors. Furthermore, differences in study populations, stroke recovery stages and conceptual perspectives have contributed to heterogeneous descriptions of these factors. A scoping review is appropriate for synthesising this diverse evidence and identifying the range of factors influencing SB reduction. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), which integrates constructs from 33 theories into 14 domains, offers a comprehensive framework for categorising these factors.20 21 The TDF has been widely applied to investigate determinants of behaviour change across different health contexts, including exercise participation22 and pregnancy weight management.23 Within stroke populations, the TDF has also been used to examine factors influencing healthy lifestyle behaviours.24 However, SB involves behavioural processes that may differ from broader lifestyle modifications. Although previous studies have explored SB prevalence and intervention approaches among stroke survivors, evidence regarding the systematic identification, categorisation and theoretical interpretation of barriers and facilitators to SB reduction using the TDF remains limited.

In conclusion, this study aims to (1) comprehensively review the barriers and facilitators to reducing SB in stroke survivors and (2) map these barriers and facilitators to the TDF, providing a basis for interventions to reduce SB.

Methods

This review was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for Scoping Reviews25–27 and was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, Scoping Review extension (PRISMA-ScR).28 This study was registered with the Open Science Framework website (osf.io/ma8qp).

Identify the review question

The research question was confirmed based on PCC elements, including: P (population): stroke survivors aged 18 years or older who had experienced SB; C (concept): SB; C (context): evidence from any contextual setting. The research question was: What are the barriers and facilitators to reducing SB among stroke survivors?

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy was developed following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews. An initial search of MEDLINE (Ovid) and CINAHL was conducted to identify relevant keywords and index terms, followed by a comprehensive search across all included databases and screening of reference lists of included studies. Nine databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Wan Fang, were searched from inception to May 2025. Grey literature was searched through Google Scholar and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses using combinations of keywords related to stroke, SB, barriers, facilitators and influencing factors. Detailed search strategies are provided in online supplemental material 1. An updated search of all databases and grey literature sources was conducted in July 2026 using the same search strategy and eligibility criteria.

Source of evidence selection

After a systematic search, all records were imported into EndNote software, and duplicate references were removed using the software’s deduplication function. Subsequently, one researcher manually checked the records to identify and remove any remaining duplicates. Two researchers independently screened the title and abstract of all identified studies against eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant studies were then retrieved for full-text assessment. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved in consultation with the third researcher. The reasons for excluding studies were detailed on a PRISMA flow chart.

Data extraction

The charting form was developed a priori to record the key information, including author(s), publication year, country, aims, methods and key findings related to the review question. The data extraction form was piloted using three included studies by two researchers to ensure consistency and refine the extraction process. Following refinement of the extraction form, two researchers independently extracted data from all included studies. Extracted data were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third researcher.

Analysis and presentation of evidence

Extracted barriers and facilitators related to SB reduction were first identified from the included studies using an inductive approach. The identified barriers and facilitators were deductively mapped to the 14 TDF domains according to the theoretical definitions of each domain. When a factor could be assigned to more than one domain, researchers discussed and reached a consensus on the most appropriate domain.

Result

Search result

A total of 17 415 records were identified through database searching. After removing 8209 duplicates, 9206 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 8900 records were excluded based on the eligibility criteria, leaving 306 articles for full-text assessment. Four full-text articles could not be retrieved, and 254 articles were excluded after full-text review for predefined reasons. Grey literature, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses, and the reference lists of included studies were also searched, resulting in the inclusion of one additional study. In total, 49 studies were included in this scoping review. A PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the study selection process is presented in figure 1.

Among the 49 studies, 46 were peer-reviewed articles and 3 were doctoral theses.29–31 There are 41 quantitative studies,8 17 19 30–63 5 qualitative studies64–68 and 3 mixed-method studies.18 29 69 Studies were conducted in Australia (n=10),33 34 39 40 44 46 47 49 56 67 China (n=6),30 31 53 54 63 70 the UK (n=5),17 18 29 65 69 the Netherlands (n=6),32 41 45 60 68 71 Japan (n=5),50 55 59 61 72 the US (n=3),19 57 58 Canada (n=3),35 36 64 Sweden (n=3),8 43 66 Norway (n=2),38 62 South Africa (n=2),37 42 South Korea (n=2),51 73 Israel (n=1)52 and Spain (n=1).48 A summary of the 49 included studies is presented in online supplemental material 2.

Barriers and facilitators

SB, sedentary behaviour.

Within the Knowledge domain, a lack of knowledge about the health risks of SB, strategies to reduce sedentary time and the importance of breaking up prolonged sitting was a commonly identified barrier. Limited knowledge and skills among healthcare professionals were also reported. Facilitators included SB education, individualised feedback and information provision.

In the Skills domain, stroke severity and stroke-related physical and functional limitations, pain and fatigue were the most frequently reported barriers, whereas greater functional ability, enhanced behavioural skills and better sleep were identified as facilitators.

Within the Beliefs about Capabilities domain, low self-efficacy, lack of confidence and negative beliefs about the ability to stand and move were common barriers, while higher self-efficacy facilitated SB reduction.

In the Beliefs about Consequences domain, fear of falling, recurrent stroke, uncertainty about recovery outcomes and negative beliefs about standing were identified as barriers. In contrast, fear of becoming wheelchair-dependent and greater recurrence risk perception were reported as facilitators.

The Environmental Context and Resources domain and Social Influences domain highlighted the importance of contextual factors. Barriers included physical and clinical constraints, organisational cultures and routines associated with SB, healthcare resource shortages, lack of outpatient rehabilitation, financial difficulties, adverse weather conditions, limited social support and inadequate support or supervision from healthcare staff. Facilitators included supportive home environments, positive local physical environments, inpatient therapy sessions, support from family, friends and healthcare professionals, social participation, peer experience sharing and living with a spouse.

Additional barriers were identified in the Social/Professional Role and Identity, Memory, Attention and Decision Processes, Emotion, Intentions and Behavioural Regulation domains, including occupational sitting, changes in social and family roles, cognitive impairment, mental fatigue, habitual SB, anxiety, depression, adaptation to sedentary activities, lack of motivation and poor behavioural regulation. Facilitators in these domains included greater autonomous motivation, motivation to improve health, returning to an active lifestyle, previous activity habits, increased self-care activities and the provision of reinforcing cues or stimuli.

Overall, the identified barriers were related to stroke-related impairments, psychological factors and environmental constraints, whereas facilitators primarily involved enhanced knowledge and self-efficacy, supportive social and physical environments and behavioural strategies that encouraged active lifestyles.

Discussion

In this study, we examined barriers and facilitators to reducing SB in stroke survivors. A total of 49 studies were included, the majority of which were conducted in high-income countries, with a smaller number undertaken in upper-middle-income countries, including China30 31 53 54 63 70 and South Africa.37 42 The review revealed that the barriers and facilitators were multifaceted, reflecting the multidimensional nature of physical, psychological, social and environmental factors.

Barriers and facilitators at the individual level

Skills, memory, attention and decision processes

With the Skills and Memory, Attention and Decision Processes domains, stroke-related physical, functional and cognitive impairment were barriers to reducing SB. Greater stroke severity and physical impairments, muscle weakness, reduced physical capacity and disability limited patients’ ability to replace sitting with standing or light-intensity activities.17–19 29 33 35 36 40 42 48 49 52 58 62–66 68 In addition, cognitive impairments, including deficits in memory and attention, were associated with reduced awareness of prolonged inactivity and difficulties in self-monitoring of SB, which may be related to longer sedentary periods.45 64 65

Fatigue emerged as another barrier across both domains. Post-stroke fatigue, particularly mental fatigue manifesting as a lack of motivation and apathy, was reported to make even light activity challenging and was associated with prolonged periods of sitting or lying down to recover.34 41 42 59 64–66 Consequently, some participants gradually adapted to their circumstances and reported enjoyment of sedentary activities despite expressing a desire to become more active.29

Although some factors, such as stroke severity and specific functional impairments, are not directly modifiable, their identification is essential to understanding the constraints that influence SB. These factors may inform future research exploring tailored approaches, including compensatory strategies, environmental adaptations and additional support for individuals experiencing persistent limitations.

Knowledge

Although stroke-related physical and functional limitations are important barriers to reducing SB, they do not fully account for the persistently high levels of SB after stroke. Even among stroke survivors who have independent mobility, SB remains prevalent (≥10 hours per day),13 and longitudinal studies have reported limited reductions in SB over follow-up periods.17 19 49 This finding suggests that factors beyond physical capability may also be associated with persistent SB. Within the Knowledge domain, insufficient awareness of SB emerged as an important barrier. Stroke survivors often lacked knowledge regarding the health risks associated with SB18 29 31 46 55 65 67–69 and did not perceive the importance of interrupting prolonged SB.29 Furthermore, some individuals prioritised increasing physical activity rather than reducing SB, suggesting a limited understanding of the distinct health implications of these two movement behaviours.29

Although individuals who were informed about the risks expressed willingness to change, they lacked practical strategies and reported receiving insufficient guidance from healthcare professionals.18 67 This gap may partly reflect healthcare professionals’ limited knowledge and skills regarding SB reduction, which may influence their ability to provide appropriate education and behavioural support.69 Conversely, education on SB, feedback regarding sedentary patterns and information about strategies for reducing sedentary time were identified as facilitators.30 67 These findings highlight knowledge gaps among both stroke survivors and healthcare professionals and suggest that future research should further explore how educational approaches can support SB reduction during stroke rehabilitation.

Beliefs about capabilities and consequences

Within the Beliefs about Capabilities domain, self-efficacy was identified as both a barrier and a facilitator. Stroke survivors with higher self-efficacy were more likely to replace sitting with standing,63 whereas those with lower self-efficacy struggled to reduce SB.53 Reduced confidence following stroke was associated with negative beliefs regarding one’s ability to stand, walk or move safely, irrespective of actual physical function. This finding suggests that perceived capability may influence SB independently of objective functional limitations, highlighting the importance of addressing psychological barriers alongside physical rehabilitation.

With the Beliefs about Consequences domain, perceptions of potential negative consequences were commonly identified as barriers. Fear of falling, recurrent stroke or movement-related injury, as well as uncertainty about recovery outcomes, were reported to be associated with avoidance of standing and moving activities and prolonged sedentary time.29 30 32 40 67 Conversely, perceptions of adverse long-term consequences of inactivity, such as becoming dependent on a wheelchair or experiencing recurrent stroke, were perceived as reasons for reducing SB by some individuals.66 70 Taken together, these findings suggest that beliefs about consequences may operate in different directions depending on whether individuals perceive movement or continued inactivity as the greater threat. This distinction may be important when interpreting patients’ reluctance to interrupt sedentary time and when identifying appropriate targets for future intervention research.

Motivation, intention, reinforcement and behavioural regulation

Reducing SB involved not only the capability to move but also psychological and behavioural factors related to motivation, intention and self-regulation. Low motivation to reduce SB was frequently reported among stroke survivors,18 57 64 whereas greater autonomous motivation, determination and a desire to improve health were identified as facilitators associated with greater engagement in standing and moving.57 64 In addition, having meaningful personal goals, such as returning to pre-stroke activity levels or resuming valued daily activities, was reported to support efforts to reduce sedentary time.64

Behavioural regulation also influenced SB. A lack of behavioural regulation and the habitual nature of sedentary routines was identified as a barrier to reducing SB.68 Conversely, previous active lifestyles and increasing participation in self-care activities facilitated SB reduction.59 64 Reinforcing strategies, such as prompts, cues and feedback, were also identified as potential facilitators.50 These findings highlight the importance of considering motivational and self-regulatory processes when understanding SB after stroke. Future research may explore whether approaches targeting goal setting, self-monitoring, prompts, feedback and habit formation can support sustained reductions in SB among stroke survivors.

Emotion

Within the Emotional domain, anxiety32 and depression,39 49 53 58 64 enjoyment of sedentary activities and other negative emotions were identified as barriers. Depression was one of the most consistently reported emotional barriers and was associated with greater SB across several observational studies.39 49 53 58 64 Anxiety was also identified as a barrier, with emotional distress being reported as a factor that may influence stroke survivors’ willingness to engage in activities that interrupt prolonged sitting.32 In addition, stroke-related negative emotions, including shame, embarrassment, frustration, low mood and loss of purpose, were reported to be associated with reduced participation in physical and social activities and greater sedentary time.18 53 Beyond emotional distress, several qualitative studies highlighted that some stroke survivors gradually adapted to prolonged sitting and experienced enjoyment or comfort in sedentary activities, such as watching television, particularly when physical limitations restricted participation in previous activities.29 65 Such adaptation to sedentary lifestyles may represent an additional psychological barrier to changing sedentary habits, as it was associated with reduced perceived need or willingness to interrupt prolonged sitting. Overall, these findings indicate that emotional factors may influence SB after stroke through both negative emotional experiences and adaptation to sedentary routines. Future research exploring SB reduction strategies may need to consider emotional experiences and individual perceptions of sedentary activities alongside physical and environmental factors.

Barriers and facilitators at the environmental and resource level

Environmental context and resources

Environmental and resource-related factors play an important role in shaping opportunities for movement and SB after stroke. In inpatient settings, barriers extend beyond patients’ physical impairments to include the organisation and culture of rehabilitation services. Qualitative evidence suggested that rehabilitation wards often prioritised safety and efficiency, which may limit opportunities for movement outside scheduled therapy sessions and contribute to prolonged sitting or lying.69 Organisational routines, including fixed therapy schedules, fewer opportunities for activity during afternoons and evenings and the requirement for supervised or assisted walking, may unintentionally contribute to prolonged sedentary time.8 46 Sjöholm et al reported that stroke survivors were more active during the morning, when rehabilitation sessions and routine ward activities were concentrated, whereas SB increased later in the day when fewer structured movement opportunities were available.8 Furthermore, physical constraints within the hospital environment, including limited ward space and connections to medical equipment, further restrict patients’ opportunities to stand and move.18 Collectively, these findings suggest that environmental and resource-related factors represent important considerations for future intervention development, with further research needed to evaluate how healthcare, community and environmental contexts can best support SB reduction across the stroke recovery pathway.

Resource availability further shaped opportunities for stroke survivors to translate intentions into behaviour. Shortages of healthcare professionals were reported to limit opportunities for supervised mobility, while heavy workloads may have resulted in staff completing daily tasks on behalf of patients rather than encouraging independent movement.18 In addition, insufficient knowledge and confidence among healthcare professionals regarding SB reduction were reported as potential barriers that may reduce their ability to encourage patients to interrupt prolonged sitting.69 Therefore, healthcare professional training focusing not only on the health consequences of prolonged SB but also on practical strategies to safely promote standing, walking and breaking up sedentary time may represent a potential area for future research. Such approaches may help explore how SB reduction can be integrated into everyday rehabilitation practice rather than restricted to formal therapy sessions.

Beyond the hospital setting, supportive physical and community environments were identified as important facilitators. Home environments equipped with supportive features, such as safety modifications, together with accessible community infrastructure and outpatient rehabilitation services, provide stroke survivors with greater opportunities to remain active after discharge.18 39 65 68 Structured rehabilitation programmes and blended physiotherapist-supported interventions were also reported as potential facilitators by providing continued guidance and feedback.8 69 Conversely, financial difficulties may limit access to home modifications, rehabilitation services and community participation, while adverse weather conditions may present additional challenges to outdoor activity.65 These findings highlight environmental and resource-related factors as important considerations for future SB intervention development, and further research is needed to evaluate how healthcare, community and environmental contexts can best support SB reduction across the stroke recovery pathway.

Barriers and facilitators at the social level

Social influences

Social support was identified as an important factor associated with SB reduction after stroke, with influences extending beyond healthcare professionals to family members, peers and the broader social network. A lack of social support or social isolation may limit opportunities and motivation to interrupt prolonged sitting,19 31 67 whereas extensive support from family, friends, healthcare professionals and fellow stroke survivors facilitates a more active lifestyle.8 60 63–65 67

Healthcare professionals played an important role in encouraging standing and movement during rehabilitation. However, this support was reported to be constrained by staff shortages, time limitations, competing clinical demands and limited knowledge and confidence regarding SB reduction.18 Morton et al reported that healthcare staff sometimes completed routine activities for patients rather than encouraging independent mobility, which may unintentionally reinforce SB.69 Therefore, improving healthcare professionals’ knowledge, confidence and practical skills for integrating SB reduction into routine care represent potential areas for future training and research.31 41 69

Family and friends’ support was consistently identified as an important facilitator of SB reduction, particularly after stroke survivors returned to the home environment. Family involvement may encourage standing and movement and provide practical support for reducing SB.31 65 68 However, qualitative evidence also suggested that family members may lack sufficient knowledge or confidence regarding how to provide effective support for behaviour change. Therefore, future interventions may consider involving family members and exploring ways to provide them with appropriate information and strategies to support stroke survivors’ activity participation. Beyond the family, broader social influences also appeared to play an important role. Social networks, wider social participation and opportunities to share experiences with other stroke survivors were identified as facilitators of SB reduction.31 65 Overall, these findings highlight that SB reduction after stroke may be influenced not only by individual factors but also by the availability and quality of social support from family members, peers, healthcare professionals and the wider social environment.

Implications for future research

An understanding of the multilevel barriers and facilitators identified in this review may inform the development of future SB interventions among stroke survivors. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of these factors, future research should further investigate how individualised, theory-informed approaches can be developed and evaluated across different stages of stroke recovery. Future studies may explore how patient-level factors, including knowledge, motivation, beliefs, emotional status and functional limitations, can be integrated with social and environmental support to promote sustainable behaviour change. In addition, further research is needed to examine how healthcare, community and home environments can provide supportive contexts for reducing SB after stroke.

Conclusion

This scoping review comprehensively summarised 32 barriers and 17 facilitators to reducing SB in stroke survivors. The barriers identified included limited knowledge of SB risks and reduction strategies, low motivation, insufficient social support, negative emotions and beliefs, and stroke-related physical limitations. The facilitators included sufficient social support, positive beliefs and environments conducive to physical activity. Future research should further explore how these multilevel barriers and facilitators can inform the development of tailored approaches to reduce SB among stroke survivors.
