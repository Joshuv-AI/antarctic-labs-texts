Knowledge, attitude, and willingness toward breast magnetic resonance imaging screening among women at high risk of breast cancer in Beijing, China
Authors: Jing Lu, Hongwei Ren, Yuhan Liu, Yuxia Wang, Youzhi Rong, Yahui Wang, Feie Wang, Tianran Li, Liutong Shang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnnual breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is highly recommended to assist mammography for women at high risk of breast cancer (BC). This study explored the knowledge, attitude, and willingness toward breast MRI screening among women at high risk of BC.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled women at high risk of BC between August 2022 and January 2023 in Beijing, China. A structural equation model (SEM) was used to analyze the relationships among knowledge, attitude, and willingness.ResultsA total of 596 questionnaires were collected, and 412 questionnaires (69.13%) were valid. The women’s knowledge and attitude scores were 7.75 ± 2.79 (possible range: 0–12) and 48.53 ± 6.31 (possible range: 13–65). Among the women, 297 (72.09%) were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening. The SEM showed that knowledge had direct effect on attitude [β = 0.77, 95% CI: (0.57, 0.98), P < 0.001], the attitude had direct effect on willingness [β = 0.02, 95% CI: (0.01, 0.02), P < 0.001], knowledge had an indirect effect on willingness through attitude [β = 0.01, 95% CI: (0.01,0.02), P < 0.001], and the direct effect of knowledge on practice was not significant.ConclusionsThe women at high risk of BC had insufficient knowledge and a relatively positive attitude toward breast MRI screening. Most of them were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening. Advertising and public health education programs should be designed to improve their knowledge and attitude, therefore improving their willingness and practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20370-7.

Background

Breast cancer (BC) is one of the most prevalent cancers and the leading cause of cancer-related death in women worldwide [1]. In 2020, China had 416,371 new BC cases and 117,174 BC-related deaths, representing 18% of new cases and 17% of deaths globally [2]. In the early stages of BC, patients mainly exhibit symptoms such as local discomfort and breast nodules without obvious clinical features, which are easily ignored [3]. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment is an effective strategy to prevent BC progression. According to the Chinese guidelines and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, the women at high risk of BC are defined as females meeting at least one of the following [4, 5]: (1) had a family history of BC in immediate family members (first-degree relatives including parents, children, and sisters), (2) had a medical history of BC, (3) had a history of chest radiotherapy (cumulative dose in radiotherapy ≥ 10 Gy before 30 years old), or (4) had a diagnosis of atypical ductal hyperplasia, atypical lobular hyperplasia, or lobular carcinoma in situ before 40 years old.

The selection of screening techniques plays a vital role in early diagnosis and treatment, which can improve patient survival and prognosis [6]. BC screening mainly relies on imaging techniques, such as mammography, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI is the most sensitive imaging technique for BC screening and is widely used for BC diagnosis, especially in women at high risk of BC [7, 8]. Moreover, MRI screening minimizes the risk of missing tumors [9, 10]. Breast MRI can detect BC earlier than mammography among high-risk women [11, 12]. A screening MRI is performed in about 5% of women participating in BC screening in the United States of America [13]. Considering the high prevalence of BC among women at high risk [14], their BC screening behaviors require additional attention.

According to the recommendations from several organizations and guidelines, [including the American Cancer Society (ACS), the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), and the Breast Cancer Screening Guideline for Chinese Women] [15–17], annual MRI is recommended as an additional screening method in conjunction with mammography for women at high risk of BC. Despite these recommendations, the adoption of MRI screening among high-risk women remains low [18, 19].

The knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) survey is based on the theory that motivates human behavioral changes. It is divided into three continuous processes: acquiring knowledge, producing beliefs, and forming behaviors [20]. According to the KAP theory, there is a causal relationship among knowledge, attitude, and practice [20]. A previous study found that insufficient knowledge of healthcare providers about breast MRI screening could impede the application of MRI examination in women at high risk of BC [14]. Despite the benefits of MRI, few studies reported the KAP toward breast MRI screening among women at high risk of BC. MRI-related education and awareness can effectively improve the MRI screening rates of high-risk women [14]. Beijing is the capital of the world’s second-largest populated country [21], but KAP toward breast MRI screening among women in Beijing is still limited. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the knowledge and attitude toward breast MRI screening among women at high risk of BC in Beijing, China. It was hypothesized that (1) knowledge had a positive effect on willingness toward breast MRI screening, (2) knowledge had a positive effect on attitude toward breast MRI screening, and (3) attitude had a positive effect on willingness toward breast MRI screening.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at three medical centers (the First Medical Center, the Fourth Medical Center, and the Sixth Medical Center of the PLA General Hospital) in Beijing, China, between August 2022 and January 2023 and enrolled women at high risk of BC. The three participating hospitals are tertiary academic hospitals, with a total of 9000 beds and 8 million visits annually.

The inclusion criteria were (1) female, (2) aged > 20 years or aged ≥ 16 years with BC history in immediate family members, and (3) at high risk of BC according to Breast Cancer Screening Guideline for Chinese Women [4]. The criteria are supported by the NCCN [5] and were used previously [22, 23]. The questionnaires with incomplete or conflicting responses were excluded.

Ethical considerations

This work has been carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2000) of the World Medical Association. This work was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (2024KY011-KS001) at January, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Procedures

The participants were identified by the physicians and approached by the research team during their re-examination. Immediate family members of BC patients were also invited to participate when they accompanied the patients to their re-examination. The research team member explained the study to them, who then chose to volunteer or not to participate in the study. The participants who agreed to participate were invited to scan the QR code to access the informed consent form, which required signing to grant access to the questionnaire. A web-based questionnaire was conducted using the Sojump website (https://www.wjx.cn/) and distributed to each woman via smartphone. Women who had difficulties completing the online questionnaires were instructed by trained interns.

The questionnaire was designed by the authors according to the Breast Cancer Screening Guideline for Chinese Women [4] and a previous study [20] and reviewed by three experts (a surgeon, a radiologist, and an expert in the field of BC) for content validity. A pilot study was conducted among 95 women, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.79, indicating good internal consistency. The participants of the pilot test were also asked to provide comments about unclear questions (face validity).

The final questionnaire contained three dimensions. The first dimension (13 questions) covered the demographic characteristics and willingness toward breast MRI screening. The demographic characteristics included age, residence, ethnicity, education, occupation, monthly income, marriage, underlying diseases, smoking and drinking, commercial insurance, experience of mammogram, breast ultrasound, and breast MRI screening, and willingness towards breast MRI screening were determined by the question “Are you willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening”. The knowledge dimension comprised 13 questions. Questions #1–12 were assigned 1 point for each correct answer and 0 points for each wrong or unclear answer. Question #13 (not assigned a score) had a conflicting description with question #6, and the questionnaires with the same answer for questions #6 and #13 were considered invalid and were excluded. The knowledge score was obtained by adding the scores of questions #1–12 (range: 0–12). The attitude dimension consisted of 13 questions scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree (5 points) to strongly disagree (1 point). Questions #3, #4, #5, #6, and #8 were reverse-assigned (1 to 5 points). The attitude score was obtained by adding the scores of all 13 questions (range: 13–65).

Based on Bloom’s cutoff, knowledge and attitude scores > 60% were considered relatively sufficient knowledge or relatively positive attitudes, and scores > 80% were considered sufficient knowledge or positive attitude [24, 25].

Sample size

The required sample for this study was calculated based on Cochran’s sample size estimation equation (n = z2pq/e2). In this formula, n is the number of participants required, z is 1.96 in 95% confidence interval, p is the expected proportion, q is 1-p, and e is the 5% margin of error. Since no previous study has reported KAP towards breast MRI screening among the high risk population in China, we have taken 50% as the expected proportion to calculate the sample size, resulting in a sample size of 384.

Statistical analysis

Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) and AMOS 24.0 (IBM, NY, United States) were used for statistical analysis. The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was confirmed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared by independent t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical data were presented as n (%). Characteristics with P < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regressions to explore the characteristics independently associated with sufficient knowledge, positive attitude, and willingness toward breast MRI screening. The goodness-of-fit of the regression models was evaluated by the Hosmer and Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R². A P-value ≥ 0.05 for the Hosmer and Lemeshow test indicated that there was no statistically significant difference between the results of the regression model and the actual situation. A higher Nagelkerke R2 indicates a greater degree to which the model explains the variation in the dependent variables.

A structural equation model (SEM) was used to analyze the interactions among knowledge, attitude, and willingness. Model fitting was evaluated using the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMSR). A post hoc confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to test the construct validity. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 596 questionnaires were collected, but 184 were excluded for providing the same anwser to the questions #6 and #13 in knowledge dimension, resulting in 412 valid questionnaires (69.13%). Forty (9.71%) women had undergone a mammogram examination, 248 (60.19%) had undergone a breast ultrasound, and 34 (8.25%) had undergone a breast MRI. A total of 297 (72.09%) women were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening (Table 1). The CFA showed that the fit indicators were all good (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1).

The knowledge and attitude scores were 7.75 ± 2.79 and 48.53 ± 6.31, respectively. The knowledge scores were likely to be different among women of different ages, education, occupation, monthly income, marriage, smoking habits, and commercial insurance, while the attitude scores were likely to be different among women with different ages, education, and occupation (all P < 0.05) (Table 1). Women who were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening had significantly higher knowledge scores (7.99 ± 2.66 vs. 7.12 ± 3.02, P = 0.016) and attitude scores (49.55 ± 6.29 vs. 45.89 ± 5.57, P < 0.001) than those who were unwilling (Table 1).

The accuracy rate in the knowledge dimension ranged from 30 to 93%. The Question with the lowest rate of correct response was “the common position of MRI is lying flat, but the breast MRI needs to be performed in the prone (prostrated) position” (30%), while the question with the highest correct response rate was “early detection and diagnosis of breast cancer can improve the survival rate and cure rate of the disease” (Supplementary Fig. 2).

About half of the women strongly agreed that “regular MRI screening for breast cancer can help early detection of breast cancer” and that “regular MRI screening for breast cancer can help early detection of other breast diseases” (52% and 48%, respectively). However, 31% of the women disagreed or strongly disagreed that “talking about my breast condition with the medical staff will not cause psychological discomfort to me”, and 23% of the women worried that “breast MRI requires injection of contrast agents, which may cause adverse effects on my body”. In addition, 48% of the women agreed or strongly agreed that “since I am at high risk of breast cancer, I often worry about getting breast cancer” (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that age 31–40 years [OR = 0.34, 95% CI: (0.13, 0.87), P = 0.024], age 41–50 years [OR = 0.23, 95% CI: (0.08, 0.70), P = 0.010], college and undergraduate [OR = 7.91, 95% CI: (1.01, 61.78)], and postgraduate and above [OR = 9.69, 95% CI: (1.15, 81.63), P = 0.049] were independently associated with knowledge (Hosmer and Lemeshow test P = 0.105, Nagelkerke R2 = 0.105; Table 2). Knowledge [OR = 2.09, 95% CI: (1.18, 3.73), P = 0.012], age 31–40 years [OR = 0.52, 95% CI: (0.29, 0.95), P = 0.034], age 41–50 years [OR = 0.40, 95% CI: (0.20, 0.79), P = 0.009], college and undergraduate [OR = 3.06, 95% CI: (1.19, 7.85), P = 0.020], and postgraduate and above [OR = 4.74, 95% CI: (1.66, 13.53), P = 0.004] were independently associated with attitude (Hosmer and Lemeshow test P = 0.535, Nagelkerke R2 = 0.120; Table 3). Positive attitude [OR = 2.98, 95% CI: (1.61, 5.52), P < 0.001] was independently associated with the willingness to undergo regular breast MRI screening (Hosmer and Lemeshow test P = 0.819, Nagelkerke R2 = 0.134; Table 4).

The SEM showed that the knowledge had significant direct effect on attitude [β = 0.77, 95% CI: (0.57, 0.98), P < 0.001], the attitude had significant direct effect on willingness [β = 0.01, 95% CI: (0.01, 0.02), P < 0.001], and the knowledge had significant indirect effect on willingness [β = 0.01, 95% CI: (0.01, 0.02), P < 0.001] (Fig. 1; Table 5). The fitting indexes of the SEM (RMSEA < 0.001, CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, and SRMR < 0.001) all showed that the data fitted the structural model satisfactorily (Table 6).

Discussion

This study showed that the women at high risk of BC had insufficient knowledge and relatively positive attitudes toward breast MRI screening and that their knowledge had a significant indirect effect on their willingness to undergo regular breast MRI screening through attitude. Healthcare providers can use these findings to design more effective educational programs to improve the awareness, attitudes, willingness, and behaviors of the general population of women toward breast MRI screening and ultimately improve the prognosis of BC patients.

Other studies conducted in different countries have also reported poor knowledge of BC and its screening, particularly related to risk factors [26, 27]. As this study focused on women at high risk, it provides valuable insight into their knowledge of breast MRI screening. Given that few studies reported the KAP toward breast MRI screening, the current study provides valuable guidelines and formulation for future research and national management on regular breast MRI screening for women at high risk of BC. A survey of 1000 Asian women discovered that several factors were independent predictors of poor knowledge about BC, including increased age, Malay race, a lower socioeconomic class, and not knowing anyone with BC. Conversely, higher education and knowledge scores were independent predictors of mammogram screening attendance [28]. This study also indicated a link between older age and lower education with insufficient knowledge and negative attitude. Additional public health education programs in BC should be established, especially for older adults with lower education levels.

Although women had a relatively positive attitude towards breast MRI screening, they also reported psychological concerns and worries regarding the adverse effects of MRI. The results were consistent with previous studies showing that the population at high risk of cancer might have baseline anxiety and depression, which hinders further screening [29–31]. Therefore, strengthening psychosocial support for women at high risk of BC may improve attendance for breast MRI screening.

Previous studies showed a high rate of breast MRI screening (58-90%) in women with genetic or familial predisposition [32, 33]. Despite the fact that the majority of women in the present study were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening, only 8.25% had undergone a breast MRI, revealing the inadequate use of MRI. The low MRI examination rate in the present study was consistent with previous studies in the United States of America [18]. These findings highlighted the unmet need for MRI screening in women at high risk of BC and the importance of exploring the factors associated with their willingness. A previous study found that “expected personal health benefit” and “contribution to science” were the most frequent reasons for their willingness to participate in breast MRI screening [34]. The results of this study were consistent with the notion that a positive attitude toward breast MRI screening is a significant predictor of an individual’s willingness to undergo regular screening. Therefore, educational programs to improve attitudes could increase participation rates among women at high risk of BC.

A previous study on BC screening found that knowledge and attitude could predict the behavioral intention of mammography screening [35]. A recent Chinese study also showed that women’s knowledge of risk factors and their attitudes were directly associated with breast self-examination behavior [36]. As the knowledge of women at high risk of BC towards breast MRI screening showed a significant indirect effect on their willingness through attitude in this study, it is also demonstrated that the willingness might be improved by increasing people’s awareness towards MRI screening. Since advertisements promoting awareness about BC and programs or campaigns related to BC have been proven to be effective predictors of the high level of women’s awareness toward BC [37, 38], healthcare providers and governmental policy may need to put more effort into these programs to translate sufficient knowledge into willingness for screening.

This study had strengths. All participants in this study were women aware of their high-risk status and were, therefore, regularly followed up at the three participating hospitals. In China, women at high risk of BC have access to mammography, breast ultrasound, and breast MRI for BC screening [4]. The screening options are comprehensively discussed with all women at high risk of BC, and they can choose the modalities they prefer (and their choice can change with time). Therefore, there might be a high variability in the exposure to knowledge and motivation and in practice.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the women were from three hospitals in Beijing; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable across China, and there is a need for a nationwide study. In China, as MRIs are expensive pieces of equipment kept for specialized examinations, they are usually found in tertiary hospitals. Therefore, all participants were from tertiary hospitals. In Beijing, screening MRI for breast cancer is covered by Social Medical insurance. Indeed, it makes the results non-generalizable where MRI is not covered. Secondly, this study relied on self-reported questionnaire data, which might induce bias. All participants were asked to confirm whether they met one of the four criteria for being high at high risk of BC, but the exact criteria were not recorded. In addition, the questionnaire was distributed through a smartphone, which could exclude individuals who do not use smartphones or are not proficient with electronic devices, like older adults. In addition, there was no guarantee of who really filled out the questionnaire. Such bias could be alleviated by excluding invalid questionnaires, but it could not be eliminated. Although interns helped women who had trouble filling out the online questionnaires, future research should include multiple survey methods, such as face-to-face interviews and discussions, to mitigate this limitation. The goodness-of-fit tests indicated weak contributions of the selected variables to the knowledge, attitude, and willingness outcomes, likely because characteristics such as communication with physicians were not collected. Finally, a SEM can provide a surrogate of causality in cross-sectional studies. Still, the causality is statistically inferred instead of actually observed, and the results must be taken cautiously [39–41].

Conclusions

Women at high risk of BC in Beijing, China, had insufficient knowledge and a relatively positive attitude toward breast MRI screening. Most of them were willing to undergo regular breast MRI screening. The psychological concerns, worries about MRI adverse effects, associated cancer distress, and awareness of this population on breast MRI screening can be improved with strong psychosocial support and BC education. Advertising and public health education programs should be designed to improve their willingness and practice, especially for high-risk women aged 31–50 years or with lower education.
