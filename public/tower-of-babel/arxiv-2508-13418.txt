Identification and Estimation of Multi-order Tensor Factor Models
Zetai Cen

Abstract
We propose a novel framework in high-dimensional factor models to simultaneously analyze multiple tensor time series, each with potentially different tensor orders and dimensionality. The connection between different tensor time series is through their global factors that are correlated to each other. A salient feature of our model is that when all tensor time series have the same order, it can be regarded as an extension of multilevel factor models from vectors to general tensors. Under very mild conditions, we separate the global and local components in the proposed model. Parameter estimation is thoroughly discussed. With strong correlation between global factors and noise allowed, we derive the rates of convergence of our estimators, which can be more superior than those of existing methods for multilevel factor models. We also develop estimators that are more computationally efficient, with rates of convergence spelt out. Extensive experiments are performed under various settings, corroborating with the pronounced theoretical results. As a real application example, we analyze a set of taxi data to study the traffic flow between Times Squares and its neighboring areas.



Key words and phrases:
Dimension reduction, Global factor, Multilevel factor model, Tensor order, Tensor time series.

1
Introduction

With the rapid development of technology over the past few decades, high-dimensional time series are becoming more ubiquitous. This constantly demands methodologies to facilitate interpretation and forecasting. Among many other methods, factor models are found very efficient in dimension reduction and helping researchers to understand the dependence structure in data sets. Credited to these advantages of factor models, they have been applied to various fields, including but not limited to psychology
(
McCrae and John, 1992
)
, biology
(
Hirzel et al., 2002
;
Hochreiter et al., 2006
)
, economics and finance
(
Chamberlain and Rothschild, 1983
;
Fama and French, 1993
;
Stock and Watson, 2002
)
.

More recently, researchers have open up to time series formed by matrices or, more generally, multidimensional arrays or tensors. Not only are such representations often the most natural by how the data is collected, they also provide useful insight that flattening the data might overlook. Thus, the literature has seen increasing work in matrix- or generally tensor-valued factor models. For matrix-valued time series,
Wang et al., 2019
proposed matrix factor models by extending the framework in
Lam et al., 2011
.
Yu et al., 2022
also studied a matrix factor model and proposed a projection estimator with faster rate of convergence than the PCA-type estimator.
Chen and Fan, 2023
proposed
\alpha
-PCA by generalizing the method for vector factor models by
Bai, 2003
. Addressing the interplay between rows and columns,
Lam and Cen, 2024
proposed a main effect factor model, and
Yuan et al., 2023
studied a two-way dynamic factor model. Methodologies are also ample for tensor factor models since the early work by
Chen et al., 2022
, see
e.g.

Barigozzi et al., 2022
,
Han et al., 2022
, and
Han et al., 2024
.

Despite the vast amount of approaches in tensor factor modeling discussed above, all of them consider a single time series with tensor observation of the same order and dimensions. This refrains us to conduct a comprehensive study on data sets containing multiple tensor time series, or to investigate the dependence structure among several tensor time series. In fact, this can be limited even when facing two vector time series that are closely connected in nature for panel data, yet of different dimensions.

To tackle this problem,
Wang, 2008
proposed a multilevel factor model, whose asymptotic theory is later generalized by
Choi et al., 2018
. We also note that such a framework is similar to hierarchical factor models in
Moench et al., 2013
for instance. One benefit of the techniques developed for multilevel factor models is that, the model estimation can be more efficient by accounting for the multilevel or group structure, compared to estimating a very sparse factor loading matrix if all observed panels with unaligned dimensions are directly stacked together. However, re-organizing data sets becomes infeasible for higher-order tensor data (including matrix data which are order-2 tensors), urgently requiring appropriate methods to model the data sets. Given
M
levels in total, for each series of matrix observations with dimension
p_{m,1}\times p_{m,2}
,
m=1,\dots,M
,
Zhang et al., 2025
considered multilevel factor models if either
p_{1,1}=\dots=p_{M,1}
or
p_{1,2}=\dots=p_{M,2}
. Their method decently extended the work in
Wang, 2008
and
Choi et al., 2018
to matrix time series, but satisfying the dimension constraint might be unrealistic to many datasets empirically. Moreover, their Condition 7 requires all row dimensions
p_{m,1}
to be of the same order, and similarly for column dimensions, making their method more restricted in practice.

To address all the above concern, we propose a multi-order tensor factor model to study a joint collection of tensor-valued time series, with at least the following contributions. As far as we are aware, our proposed model is the first work identifying and studying multiple tensor time series, each with potentially different orders and dimensions. Secondly, we adapt both techniques in
Bai, 2003
and
Lam et al., 2011
, and establish theoretical rates of convergence that are often more superior than the results in, for example,
Zhang et al., 2025
, even under very general dependence conditions. We also extend the operator of tensor reshape introduced by
Cen and Lam, 2025a
to facilitate natural representation of global factors. Last but not least, a by-product of our model is a multilevel factor models for general tensor time series, with minor dimension requirements.

2
Multi-order Tensor Factor Models

2.1
Model setup

Under some categorization
m\in[M]
with
M
fixed, suppose a tensor time series
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}\}
,
t\in[T]
, is observed such that each
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}
is a mean-zero order-
K_{m}
tensor of dimension
p_{m,1}\times\dots\times p_{m,K_{m}}
. Without loss of generality, let
K_{1}\leq\dots\leq K_{M}
. By joining
\big\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(M)}\big\}_{t\in[T]}
as a collection (JXC hereafter), we assume JXC follows a
multi-order tensor factor model
such that for each
m\in[M]
, the
m
th
thread

\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}\}
admits the representation for each
t\in[T]
:

\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{k=1}^{K_{m}}A_{m,k}+\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{\ell=1}^{K_{m}}B_{m,\ell}+\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)},

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
is the global core factor with dimension
r_{m,1}\times\dots\times r_{m,K_{M}}
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}
is the local core factor with dimension
u_{m,1}\times\dots\times u_{m,K_{m}}
,
A_{m,k}
and
B_{m,\ell}
(
k,\ell\in[K]
) are respectively the global and local factor loading matrices, and
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}
is the noise. For a non-trivial discussion, we assume
M>1
throughout this paper, and otherwise (
2.1
) boils down to the classical Tucker-decomposition tensor factor model. Note that only JXC is observed and the right-hand side of (
2.1
) is latent.

Model (
2.1
) turns out to be a very general framework. A useful special case is when
K\equiv K_{1}=\dots=K_{M}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}\equiv\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)}
, which is commonly known as a multilevel/group factor model if
K=1

(
Choi et al., 2018
;
Hu et al., 2025
)
or its matrix-valued extension for
K=2

(
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. An immediate importance of (
2.1
) is thus a generalization of multilevel factor models to tensor time series. It might be worth to point out that even when the tensor orders are the same across
m\in[M]
, traditional factor models cannot directly characterize the factor structure in JXC, unless some forms of concatenation are carried out—which inevitably inflates the number of parameters and overlooks the structure along each mode.

Note that in most, if not all, multilevel factor models, the same global factor contributes to different levels, whereas the interplay among
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
(
m\in[M]
) in our formulation (
2.1
) remains unclear. First, it should be straightforward that
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}\neq\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)}
if
K_{m}\neq K_{n}
. More importantly, we choose not to specify, e.g., how
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)}
affects
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(2)}
, and this lack of formulation is seen as an advantage in view of the generality of (
2.1
). On the other hand, an explicit connection among the global core factors can better facilitate interpretation and forecasting on each thread
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}
, and such a discussion is deferred to
Section

4.1
for interested readers.

We refer to
\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}:=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{k=1}^{K_{m}}A_{m,k}
and
\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}:=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{\ell=1}^{K_{m}}B_{m,\ell}
as the global and local components, respectively. Then with (
2.1
), one can model multiple tensor time series with different orders at the same time, with the global component featuring the dependence of observed tensors across threads, which is new to the literature. To separate the global and local components, we impose the following identification conditions.

Assumption 1

(Identification)
.

For all
t\in[T]
,
m\in[M]
, we assume that:

(i)

Any element in
\big\{\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\big\}
has zero mean;

(ii)

Any element in
\big\{\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\big\}
is uncorrelated to those in
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(n)}
for all
n\neq m
;

(iii)

\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
cannot be decomposed as
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}=\mathcal{G}_{t,1}^{(m)}+\mathcal{G}_{t,2}^{(m)}
with nonzero
\mathcal{G}_{t,2}^{(m)}
whose elements are uncorrelated to all elements in
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)}
for all
n\neq m
.

1
is in the same spirit of Assumption 1 in
Choi et al., 2018
and Condition 6 in
Zhang et al., 2025
. Part (i) resolves the indeterminacy in mean. Parts (ii) and (iii) together indicate that the local factor does not carry any dependence structure among the JXC,
\big\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(M)}\big\}
.
These assumptions are very mild in the sense that the identified global factor
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
can be correlated to
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}
. In comparison, such a correlation is prohibited in Assumption 1 in
Choi et al., 2018
for instance, due to their restricted
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)}
which is required to be uncorrelated to the local factors.
1
allows us to conveniently identify the global and local components, and we present the result in the following proposition, with its proof relegated to the supplement.

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then for any
t\in[T]
, the global and local components in (
2.1
) are identifiable in the sense that for any
m\in[M]
, two sets of parameters
(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)},\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)})
and
(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)})
satisfy
\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}=\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}
if and only if
(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)},\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)})=(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)})
.

Proposition

1
serves to separate the global and local components, which is sufficient for the discussion in this article, since both components admit Tucker decompositions and hence identification of parameters within has been well studied. For the same reason, the idiosyncratic noise is not involved in the proposition, as they can be separated from the global and local components by spiking eigenvalues in the unfolding covariance matrix.

Remark 1
.

Fix
m\in[M]
and consider the dynamic of the
m
th thread only. (
2.1
) assumes a sparse tensor factor model in the sense that we can rewrite the model as

\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\times_{k=1}^{K_{m}}(A_{m,k},B_{m,k})+\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)},

(2.2)

where
\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})
denotes the block tensor consisting
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}
on its diagonal. By modeling each observed tensor time series in the above way, more factors are introduced, which does not affect the theoretical results but undermines the estimation performance in practice. A more serious issue is that the dependence between threads is neglected and interpretation lost.

Remark 2
.

With tensors with potentially different orders in JXC, a plausible method to model all tensor time series is to concatenate the vectorized data. In detail, note that for each
m\in[M]
, (
2.1
) implies
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})=\big(\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{m}}A_{m,k},\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{m}}B_{m,k}\big)\big\{\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal},\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}\big\}^{\intercal}+\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})
. We thus have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{1}}A_{1,k}&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{1}}B_{1,k}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{2}}A_{2,k}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{M}}B_{M,k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix},

or, if
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)})=\dots=\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)})
under certain contexts (such as tensor multilevel factor models),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{1}}A_{1,k}&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{1}}B_{1,k}&0&\ldots&0\\
\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{2}}A_{2,k}&0&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{2}}B_{2,k}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{M}}A_{M,k}&0&0&\ldots&\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{M}}B_{M,k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(1)})\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(2)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(M)})\end{pmatrix}.

However, both representations above significantly inflate the number of parameters in the loading matrix, and recovering the non-zero blocks within is also difficult in its own sake.

Remark 3
.

In practice, we also allow for the absence of the global or local components (or both) in the representation (
2.1
) and by
1
, which is more realistic and reasonable from the perspective of data modeling. Although it can be attractive to make this mathematically rigorous in model (
2.1
) such as testing for the factor structure after estimating the components
(e.g.
He et al., 2023
, for matrix time series)
, it is beyond the scope of this paper and hence we refer to
Remark

4
for a practical solution and do not further pursue this line of analysis.

2.2
Estimation of the global and local loading matrices

In this subsection, we discuss the estimation of factor loading matrices in model (
2.1
). First, for any
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
, define
A_{m,\text{-}k}:=\otimes_{j\in[K_{m}]\setminus\{k\}}A_{m,j}
, and similarly
B_{m,\text{-}k}
. By convention, set
A_{m,\text{-}1}=B_{m,\text{-}1}=1
if
K_{m}=1
. Suppose that the factor numbers
r_{m,k}
and
u_{m,k}
are specified; see
Remark

4
for more details. Then for any
t\in[T]
, consider the mode-
k
unfolding of
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}
,

\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})=A_{m,k}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})A_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}+B_{m,k}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})B_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}+\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}),

(2.3)

so that for any
n\neq m
,
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K_{n}})\in[p_{n,1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n,K_{n}}]
, we have the filtered unfolding as

\displaystyle\Omega_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=A_{m,k}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})A_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)}\times_{h=1}^{K_{n}}A_{n,h}\Big)_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Bigg\},

where the last equality used
1
(ii) and
5
(ii) (in
Section

3.1
). Then enlightened by Equation (4) in
Lam et al., 2011
, we introduce a non-negative definite matrix:

\begin{split}\Sigma_{m,k}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[p_{n,1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n,K_{n}}]}\Omega_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}\Omega_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)\intercal},\end{split}

and observe that the
r_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of
\Sigma_{m,k}
span the same linear space as
A_{m,k}
does. Hence for any
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
, we may estimate
A_{m,k}
by
\widehat{A}_{m,k}
which is defined as
\sqrt{p_{m,k}}
times the
r_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of

\begin{split}\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}&:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in[p_{n,1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n,K_{n}}]}\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)\intercal},\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}.\end{split}

(2.4)

To estimate the local loading matrices, first consider
K_{m}>1
. Let
\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp}
be
all but the
r_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}
,
viz.

\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\widehat{A}_{m,k}
. Then from (
2.3
),

\displaystyle\;\quad\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}-A_{m,k}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})A_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}

\displaystyle=B_{m,k}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})B_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}+\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp},

where
\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}:=\otimes_{j\in[K_{m}]\setminus\{k\}}\widehat{A}_{m,j,\perp}
. Intuitively,
\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}
is close to the orthogonal complement of
A_{m,\text{-}k}
, and hence
A_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}
is close to zero. This motivates a PCA-type estimator for
B_{m,k}
, denoted by
\widehat{B}_{m,k}
which is defined as
\sqrt{p_{m,k}}
times the
u_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of

\begin{split}\widehat{\Sigma}_{B,m,k}&:=\frac{1}{Tp_{m}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}^{\intercal}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal},\end{split}

(2.5)

where
p_{m}:=\prod_{k=1}^{K_{m}}p_{m,k}
. Now suppose
K_{m}=1
and the above approach to obtain
\widehat{B}_{m,1}
is thus infeasible. Fortunately, (
2.3
) can be rewritten as

\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})=(A_{m,1},B_{m,1})\big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal},\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}\big\}^{\intercal}+\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}).

Hence by leveraging again the orthogonal complement of the global loading matrix, we may compute
\widehat{B}_{m,1}
as
\sqrt{p_{m,1}}
times the
u_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of

\begin{split}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{B,m,1}&:=\frac{1}{Tp_{m,1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}^{\intercal}\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}^{\intercal}.\end{split}

(2.6)

2.3
Estimation of the core factors and components

With
\widehat{A}_{m,k}
,
\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp}
, and
\widehat{B}_{m,k}
(
m\in[M],k\in[K_{m}]
) obtained in
Section

2.2
, we have for any
t\in[T]
,
m\in[M]
,

\displaystyle\;\quad(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{m})-(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}A_{m,k})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})

\displaystyle=(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}B_{m,k})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})+(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{m}),

where
(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}A_{m,k})
is close to zero, as discussed in
Section

2.2
. Then with the notation
\widehat{C}_{m}:=(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{B}_{m,k})
, the vectorized local factor can be estimated in a least squared manner such that

\begin{split}\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{(m)})&:=(\widehat{C}_{m}^{\intercal}\widehat{C}_{m})^{-1}\widehat{C}_{m}^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp})^{\intercal}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{m}).\end{split}

(2.7)

For any
t\in[T]
,
m\in[M]
, the global factor can also be formulated as in a linear regression form:

\displaystyle\;\quad\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{m})-(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}B_{m,k})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})=(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}A_{m,k})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})+\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{m}).

Then similar to (
2.7
), we may plug in the previous estimators and arrive at

\begin{split}\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{t}^{(m)})&:=\Big\{(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k})^{\intercal}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k})\Big\}^{-1}(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{A}_{m,k})^{\intercal}\Big\{\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{m})-(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{B}_{m,k})\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{(m)})\Big\}.\end{split}

(2.8)

Finally, the global and local common components are respectively estimated by

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}:=\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{k=1}^{K_{m}}\widehat{A}_{m,k},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}:=\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{\ell=1}^{K_{m}}\widehat{B}_{m,\ell}.

Remark 4
.

In (
2.1
), the numbers of global and local factors are unknown, which need to be either pre-specified based on some prior information or appropriately estimated. For the latter, (
2.2
) enlightens a practical approach. In particular,
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}\}
follows a classical tensor factor model with each mode-
k
loading matrix
(A_{m,k},B_{m,k})
with column rank
r_{m,k}+u_{m,k}
(see also
2
(ii) in
Section

3.1
). Hence any consistent factor number estimators can be employed, such as the correlation-thresholding estimator by
Lam, 2021
, the information-criteria approach by
Han et al., 2022
, and the eigenvalue-ratio estimator by
Cen and Lam, 2025b
, etc. Denote the estimated factor numbers by
\widehat{r_{m,k}+u_{m,k}}
.

Inspired by the eigenvalue-ratio estimator in
Zhang et al., 2025
, a practical estimator on the global factor number can be obtained as
\widehat{u}_{m,k}=\argmin_{1\leq i\leq r_{\max}}\lambda_{i+1}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k})/\lambda_{i}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k})
given some upper bounds
r_{\max}
of the factor numbers. The local factor number can thus be estimated by
\widehat{u}_{m,k}:=\widehat{r_{m,k}+u_{m,k}}-\widehat{r}_{m,k}
. We leave the refinement and the corresponding consistency of the factor number estimator to future work.

As briefly discussed in
Remark

3
, there might not be local components for some threads. Presuming the existence of global components,
\widehat{u}_{m,k}=0
would hint on the absence of local component. Following this, estimators for the local loading matrix and local factor can be trivially set as zero in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
, and other procedures therein remain valid.

3
Assumptions and Theoretical Results

3.1
Main assumptions

For each
m\in[M]
, define
p_{m,\text{-}k}=p_{m}/p_{m,k}
,
r_{m}=\prod_{k=1}^{K_{m}}r_{m,k}
, and
r_{m,\text{-}k}=r_{m}/r_{m,k}
. In the following, we present assumptions on the factor structure, noise, and dependence structure.

Assumption 2

(Loading matrices)
.

For any
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
, there is some constant
c

s.t.
:

(i)

\|A_{m,k}\|_{\max},\|B_{m,k}\|_{\max}\leq c<\infty
.

(ii)

\big\|p_{m,k}^{-1}(A_{m,k},B_{m,k})^{\intercal}(A_{m,k},B_{m,k})-I_{r_{m,k}+u_{m,k}}\big\|\leq cp_{m,k}^{-1/2}
for all
p_{m,k}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption 3

(Core factor)
.

For all
m\in[M]
, we assume that:

(i)

Any element in
\big\{\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)},\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\big\}
has bounded fourth moment, and for any
n\in[M]
, there exists some positive constant
c
such that
\big\|\mathbb{E}\big\{\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\otimes\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)})\big\}\big\|_{F}\asymp c
.

(ii)

For any
k\in[K_{m}]
, as
T\to\infty
, we have
T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}\xrightarrow{P}\Sigma_{F,m,k}
,
where
\Sigma_{F,m,k}
is a positive definite matrix with finite eigenvalues.

Assumption 4

(Idiosyncratic noise)
.

For all
m\in[M]
,
t\in[T]
, we assume that:

(i)

Any element in
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}
has zero mean and bounded fourth moment.

(ii)

For all
k\in[K_{m}]
,
t\in[T]
,
i\in[p_{m,k}]
,
j\in[p_{m,\text{-}k}]
, there exists some constant
c
such that

\sum_{s=1}^{T}\sum_{l=1}^{p_{m,k}}\sum_{h=1}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ij}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{lh}\Big\}\Big|\leq c.

(iii)

For all
k\in[K_{m}]
,
i,l_{1}\in[p_{m,k}]
,
j,h_{1}\in[p_{m,\text{-}k}]
, there exists some constant
c
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{p_{m,k}}\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ij}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{l_{1}j},\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{ih_{2}}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{l_{2}h_{2}}\Big\}\Big|\leq c,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{p_{m,k}}\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ij}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ih_{1}},\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{l_{2}j}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{l_{2}h_{2}}\Big\}\Big|\leq c,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{p_{m,k}}\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ij}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{l_{1}h_{1}},\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{ij}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{l_{2}h_{2}}\Big\}\Big|\leq c,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{p_{m,k}}\sum_{h_{2}=1}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big|\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{l_{1}j}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})_{ih_{1}},\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{l_{2}j}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(m)})_{ih_{2}}\Big\}\Big|\leq c.

Assumption 5

(Dependence and moments across threads)
.

For all
m\in[M]
, we assume that:

(i)

Any element in
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}
is uncorrelated to those in
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(n)}
for all
t\in[T]
,
n\neq m
.

(ii)

For all
n\neq m
, there exists some constant
c<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)})\otimes\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(n)})\Bigg)\Bigg\|_{\max}\leq c,

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\otimes\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(n)})\Bigg)\Bigg\|_{\max}\leq c,

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\otimes\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(n)})\Bigg)\Bigg\|_{\max}\leq c,

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\otimes\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)})\Bigg)\Bigg\|_{\max}\leq c.

Assumption 6

(Dependence and moments within threads)
.

For all
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
, we assume that for any deterministic vectors
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m,k}}
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m,\text{-}k}}
,
g\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m,k}}
, and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m,\text{-}k}}
with
\|v\|,\|w\|,\|g\|,\|h\|=1
, there exists some constant
c
such that:

(i)

\mathbb{E}\Big\|T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\big\{v^{\intercal}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})w\big\}\Big\|_{F}^{2}\leq c
.

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\Big\|T^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\big\{g^{\intercal}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})h\big\}\Big\|_{F}^{2}\leq c
.

(iii)

\mathbb{E}\Big\|T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\big\{v^{\intercal}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)})w\big\}\Big\|_{F}^{2}\leq c
.

2
is standard to describing the factor loading matrices. Part (ii) can be seen as an identification condition. It also indicates that the magnitude of each column of the loading matrices grows proportionally to the dimension
p_{m,k}
—hence factors being pervasive—at the rate
p_{k}^{-1/2}
, which is also seen in Assumption 2(ii) in
Barigozzi et al., 2024
. The asymptotic orthogonality between
A_{m,k}
and
B_{m,k}
is arguably very mild, compared to the exact orthogonality between global and local factors in Assumption A1 in
Hu et al., 2025
. Here, our rate of convergence together with the asymptotic orthogonality actually allow the local loading estimator to have faster rate of convergence than the global loading estimator; see
Theorem

2
. It is also worth to remark that weak factors as in
Wang et al., 2019
(
cf.
Condition 4 within) are possible, and we decide to consider strong factors only for improving the reading experience.

Assumption
3
(i) complements
1
by pinpointing the behavior of global factors, while part (ii) describes the local factors and is very common in the factor model literature. We note that Assumption
3
(i) is not the weakest possible in terms of dependency in the global factors, since it requires
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
to be correlated to all
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)}
for
n\neq m
. It is possible to allow
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}
to be independent of global factors in some threads, as long as there is at least one
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(n)}
with
n\neq m
that it is correlated to. However, This relaxation could come at the cost of very restricted dimensionality assumptions, and hence is not pursued here.

4
includes a set of regularity conditions for the idiosyncratic noise. Parts (ii) and (iii) are essentially the same as Assumption 3 in
Barigozzi et al., 2022
and Assumption D in
Yu et al., 2022
for matrix factor models. We point out that requiring elements to have bounded fourth moment in Assumption
4
is weaker than many literature restricting eighth moment to be bounded
(
Choi et al., 2018
;
Chen and Fan, 2023
;
Hu et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.

Lastly, Assumptions
5
and
6
depict general moment conditions between and within threads, respectively.
5
(i) strengthens
1
such that the correlation among different threads is all pooled inside global factors, whereas
5
(ii) is comparable to, for example, Assumption 4 in
Barigozzi et al., 2022
.
6
significantly relax the dependence structure in similar model framework such as multilevel factor models. Overall, it implies the global component, local component, and noise can be pairwise correlated. A more salient feature according to part (iii) is that the correlation between global factor and noise can be quite strong, while still ensuring satisfactory theoretical results.

3.2
Theoretical results

To appreciate the performance of parameter estimators, we first show the rate of convergence for the global loading matrix estimator as follows.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic consistency of global loading estimators)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
5
, it holds for any
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{m,k}}\Big\|\widehat{A}_{m,k}-A_{m,k}\Big\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{T}\Big).

Theorem

1
is an important step for the results hereafter. The approach to obtain
\widehat{A}_{m,k}
leverages the condition that the global factor is uncorrelated to local factors and noise in different threads, which is akin to the approach in
Lam et al., 2011
and
Wang et al., 2019
assuming the noise is white. Hence it is by no accident that our rate of
1/T
coincides with
e.g.
Theorem 1 in
Wang et al., 2019
under strong factors and the same normalization.

Theorem

1
also implies that larger cross-sectional dimension cannot enhance the estimator performance. First note that in a vector factor model (
K_{m}=1
), such a
1/T
rate is at least as good as the rate of convergence for the PCA-type estimator in
Bai and Ng, 2002
, or even more superior when
p_{m,1}
grows in a slower rate than
T
. For higher-order threads, i.e.,
K_{m}>1
, it can be intuited by the difficulty in relating tensor times series with different orders, and the global factor is only identified through the correlation among threads.

In multilevel matrix factor models,
Zhang et al., 2025
estimate the global loading matrix in a similar way and essentially the same result as our
Theorem

1
. However, the same approach is also used in estimating the local loading matrix, resulting in the
1/T
rate again. This overlooks the opportunity to improve the result by involving information from other modes (rows and columns in a matrix-valued data). Our PCA-type loading estimator based on (
2.5
) resolves this. Some results are now in order, providing a theoretical guarantee for the remaining parameter estimators.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic consistency of local loading estimators)
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
hold.

(i)

For any
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
with
K_{m}>1
, let
\widehat{D}_{m,k}
be the diagonal matrix consisting of the first
u_{m,k}
eigenvalues of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{B,m,k}
defined in (
2.5
). Define

\widehat{H}_{m,k}=\frac{1}{Tp_{m}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})B_{m,\text{-}k}^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}\widehat{A}_{m,\text{-}k,\perp}^{\intercal}B_{m,\text{-}k}\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}B_{m,k}^{\intercal}\widehat{B}_{m,k}\widehat{D}_{B,m,k}^{-1}.

Then
\widehat{H}_{m,k}
is asymptotically invertible such that
\widehat{H}_{m,k}\widehat{H}_{m,k}^{\intercal}=I+o_{P}(1)
. Moreover, the PCA-type estimator
\widehat{B}_{m,k}
satisfies

\frac{1}{p_{m,k}}\Big\|\widehat{B}_{m,k}-B_{m,k}\widehat{H}_{m,k}\Big\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{m,k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{Tp_{m,k}}+\frac{1}{Tp_{m,\text{-}k}}\Big).

(ii)

For any
m\in[M]
with
K_{m}=1
, let
\widetilde{D}_{m,1}
be the diagonal matrix consisting of the first
u_{m,1}
eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{B,m,1}
defined in (
2.6
). Define

\widetilde{H}_{m,1}=\frac{1}{Tp_{m,1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\textnormal{{mat}}_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})^{\intercal}B_{m,1}^{\intercal}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}\widehat{A}_{m,1,\perp}^{\intercal}B_{m,1}^{\intercal}\widehat{B}_{m,1}\widetilde{D}_{B,m,1}^{-1}.

Then
\widetilde{H}_{m,1}
is asymptotically invertible, and the estimator
\widehat{B}_{m,1}
based on (
2.6
) satisfies

\frac{1}{p_{m,1}}\Big\|\widehat{B}_{m,1}-B_{m,1}\widetilde{H}_{m,1}\Big\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{m,1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}\Big).

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic consistency of core factor estimators and component estimators)
.

Let assumptions in
Theorem

2
hold. Then for any
m\in[M]
,
t\in[T]
:

(i)

The local factor estimator defined in (
2.7
) satisfies that

\displaystyle\text{for }K_{m}>1,\quad\Big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{(m)})-(\otimes_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{H}_{m,k}^{\intercal})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\Big\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\sum_{k\in[K_{m}]}\frac{1}{p_{m,k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m}}\Big);

\displaystyle\text{for }K_{m}=1,\quad\Big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{(m)})-\widetilde{H}_{m,1}^{\intercal}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)})\Big\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m,1}}\Big).

(ii)

The global factor estimator defined in (
2.8
) is consistent such that
\big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{t}^{(m)})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\big\|^{2}
follows the same rates as in part (i), respectively according to the higher-order case
K_{m}>1
and the vector case
K_{m}=1
.
The same rates of convergence also hold for the squared norm of both global and local component estimators, i.e.,
p_{m}^{-1}\big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)})\big\|^{2}
and
p_{m}^{-1}\big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)})\big\|^{2}
.

Theorem

2
(i) spells out the rate of convergence for the local loading estimator under
K_{m}>1
, and part (ii) under
K_{m}=1
. As mentioned previously, this result is generally better than our
Theorem

1
. In fact, in a balanced-dimension scenario with
T\asymp p_{m,1}\asymp\dots\asymp p_{m,K_{m}}
, the rate in (i) becomes
1/T^{2}
which is better than the result in Theorem 3 in
Zhang et al., 2025
. Until
p_{m,k}
is too small such that
p_{m,k}^{2}\asymp T
, both rates in
Theorem

2
(i) and (ii) boil down to
1/T
.

Compared with the classical rate of
1/p_{m,k}^{2}+1/Tp_{m,\text{-}k}
for PCA-type estimators
(
Barigozzi et al., 2022
, e.g.)
,
Theorem

2
(i) contains an additional
1/Tp_{m,k}
. This is inherited from the error of the global loading estimator, and would have no impact to the result when
p_{m,1}\asymp\dots\asymp p_{m,K_{m}}
and
T
is of order larger than or equal to
p_{m,1}
, which is often the case in practice.

Theorem

3
spells out the consistency of local/global factor/component estimators, and indicate the same rates of convergence. Although we have a superior result in
Theorem

2
, it cannot be translated to
Theorem

3
which suffers from the
1/T
rate in
Theorem

1
. This once again reflects the difficulty in parameter estimation under our model. To compare with multilevel factor models, for the vector case such that
K_{m}=2
for all
m\in[M]
, our result is the same as Proposition 2 in
Choi et al., 2018
. Under the matrix case when
K_{m}=2
and
p_{m,1}\asymp p_{m,2}
for all
m\in[M]
, our derived asymptotic rates would be the same as Theorems 4 and 5 in
Zhang et al., 2025
. Thus, our presented results can be also viewed as pointing out the behavior of parameter estimators in a multilevel factor model for tensor time series with general orders.

4
Further Discussion

4.1
Explicit form of Global Factors

In
Section

2.1
, JXC following a multi-order tensor factor model means each
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}
has the representation (
2.1
). Although the unspecified form of the global factor is more general, it can be of interest to assume explicit connections among
\big\{\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)}\big\}
. If the tensor orders are the same over
m\in[M]
, a direct choice is
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(1)}=\dots=\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)}
. Yet with the potentially different orders, the setup is not so straightforward.

With the notation of tensor map in the supplement, we consider a natural choice that
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)}=\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{G}_{t},V_{m})
for some order-
K_{M}
tensor
\mathcal{G}_{t}
with dimension
r_{1}\times\dots\times r_{K_{M}}
, and some pre-specified channel
V_{m}
.
Without loss of generality, we can let
V_{M}=\big\{\{1\},\dots,\{K_{M}\}\big\}
. Therefore, we may read (
2.1
) as

\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(m)}=\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{G}_{t},V_{m})\times_{k=1}^{K_{m}}A_{m,k}+\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(m)}\times_{\ell=1}^{K_{m}}B_{m,\ell}+\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}.

(4.1)

From the notation of map operator in the supplement,
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{G}_{t},V_{m})
is an order-
K_{m}
tensor with each mode-
j
dimension
r_{m,j}:=\prod_{\theta_{m,j-1}+1<i\leq\theta_{m,j}}r_{i}
. As a simple example, consider
M=2
and
(K_{1},K_{2})=(1,2)
, i.e., the observed JXC consists of a vector time series and a matrix one. Note that
V_{1}=\big\{\{1,2\}\big\}
and
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{G}_{t},V_{1})=\textnormal{{reshape}}(\mathcal{G}_{t},\{1,2\})=\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t})
. Then for each
t\in[T]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(1)}=A_{1,1}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t})+B_{1,1}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(1)}+\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(1)}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1,1}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{t}^{(2)}=A_{2,1}\mathcal{G}_{t}A_{2,2}^{\intercal}+B_{2,1}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(2)}B_{2,2}^{\intercal}+\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(2)}\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2,1}\times p_{2,2}}.

One advantage of (
4.1
) is that the correlation condition in
3
(i) is directly satisfied. Even though all methods and results for (
2.1
) hold for (
4.1
),
Theorem

3
implies the global factor estimator has the rate
\sum_{k\in[K_{m}]}1/p_{m,k}^{2}+1/T+1/p_{m}
. Thus, we should estimate the global factor on
m
th thread such that
\sum_{k\in[K_{m}]}1/p_{m,k}^{2}+1/p_{m}
is minimized, and invert the map operator to estimate
\mathcal{G}_{t}
.

4.2
Fast implementation

When threads in JXC have large dimensions and higher orders, obtaining (
2.4
) can be computationally heavy. To circumvent this, recall from
Theorem

1
that only
T
is involved in the rate of convergence for the global loading matrix estimator. This hints on reformulating (
2.4
) by using a random subset of multi-indices. Indeed, this can be formalized as follows. For each
m\in[M]
,
k\in[K_{m}]
, define

\begin{split}\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}^{S}&:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{M}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)\intercal},\end{split}

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq[p_{n,1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n,K_{n}}]
is a random set drawn independently of JXC, and
\widehat{\Omega}_{k,n,\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}
is the same as in (
2.4
). Then the global loading matrix can be estimated by
\widehat{A}_{m,k}^{S}
, defined as
\sqrt{p_{m,k}}
times the
r_{m,k}
leading eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}^{S}
. More importantly, the performance of such estimator can be made mathematically rigorous, as shown below.

Proposition 2
.

Let all assumptions in
Theorem

1
hold. Fix
m
, and for any
n\in[M]\setminus\{m\}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}\subseteq[p_{n}]
denote the set of row indices of the matrix
\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{n}}A_{n,k}
that corresponds to the rows of
\{A_{n,1},\dots,A_{n,K_{n}}\}
with indices in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
from (
4.2
). Assume

\sigma_{r_{n}}\Big\{\big(\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{n}}A_{n,k}\big)_{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}}\Big\}\asymp\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}|},

where
\big(\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{n}}A_{n,k}\big)_{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}}
represents the sub-matrix of
\otimes_{k=1}^{K_{n}}A_{n,k}
with rows restricted on
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}
. Then it holds for any
k\in[K_{m}]
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{m,k}}\Big\|\widehat{A}_{m,k}^{S}-A_{m,k}\Big\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{T}\Big).

By
2
(ii), the additional assumption is automatically fulfilled if
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\dagger}=[p_{n}]
, i.e.,
\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}^{S}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,k}
. Through our numerical studies in
Section

5.1
Simulation

In the following, we demonstrate the numerical performance of the estimators described in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
using Monte Carlo experiments. As this article includes multilevel factor model as a special case, we will also make comparison with
Zhang et al., 2025
when JXC only contains matrix time series.

To start with, each thread is generated according to (
2.1
), except that we consider the setup in (
4.1
) such that the global factor for each thread is mapped from
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(M)}
. In detail, each entry of the global and local factors is independent AR(1) with coefficient
0.5
and innovation following
i.i.d.

\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The entries of
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(m)}
is constructed similarly, except that the innovation is either
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
or
t_{6}
, which will be specified later. Each global loading matrix is generated as
A_{m,k}=U_{m,k}
for pervasive factors and
A_{m,k}=p_{m,k}^{-0.1}\cdot U_{m,k}
for weak factors, where
U_{m,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m,k}\times r_{m,k}}
consists of
i.i.d.

\mathcal{N}(0,1)
elements. Let
A_{m,k,\perp}
be the half-orthogonal matrix representing the orthogonal complement of
A_{m,k}
. Then the local loading matrix is constructed as
p_{m,k}^{0.5}
(or
p_{m,k}^{0.4}
for weak factors) times a
p_{m,k}\times u_{m,k}
sub-matrix of
A_{m,k,\perp}
. For simplicity, we only consider one factor for all modes and threads.

As the loading matrices are only identified up to an arbitrary rotation, we measure the distance between the column space spanned by the true loading matrix and the estimator. Formally, given any pair of matrices
X
and
Y
, the column space distance is computed as

\mathcal{D}(X,Y):=\Big\|X(X^{\intercal}X)^{-1}X^{\intercal}-Y(Y^{\intercal}Y)^{-1}Y^{\intercal}\Big\|.

Due to the rotational indeterminacy in the loading matrices, performance of the core factor estimator cannot be directly compared, but reflected in the estimated global and local components. We use the (relative) mean squared error to measure the performance of the component estimators:

\displaystyle\text{MSE}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}\big):=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\|\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}-\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}\big\|_{F}^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\|\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}\big\|_{F}^{2}},\quad\text{MSE}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}\big):=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\|\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}-\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}\big\|_{F}^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\|\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}\big\|_{F}^{2}}.

A.1
Introduction of Tensor Map

We extend in this subsection the reshape operator introduced in
Cen and Lam, 2025a
to a
map
operator. Recall first that given any order-
K
tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{K}}
and a set of ordered, strictly increasing elements
v=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\}\subseteq[K]
, the reshaped tensor
\textnormal{{reshape}}(\mathcal{X},v)
is the order-
(K-\ell+1)
tensor resulting from combining all modes indexed by
v
into the last mode. Let
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{L}\}
be a set of ordered vectors where each vector
v_{l}
is the parameter representing mode indices in a reshape operation and all
v_{l}
’s,
l\in[L]
, form a partition of
[K]
. Then we denote the mapped tensor
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V)
as the order-
L
tensor such that the
l
-th mode has dimension
\prod_{j\in v_{l}}I_{j}
and is constructed by combining entries of
\mathcal{X}
with modes in
v_{l}
. This
\textnormal{{map}}(\cdot,\cdot)
operation is more general than permuting the array elements in
\mathcal{X}
as it also allows for stacking elements from different modes into one, and more importantly, preserves the factor structure in the data, by iteratively applying Theorem 1 in
Cen and Lam, 2025a
.

Hereafter, we refer to
V
as
channel
. Without loss of generality, let the data tensor
\mathcal{X}
be pre-processed such that, for
a<b
, elements in
v_{a}
are larger than those in
v_{b}
. Then the procedure of mapping a tensor can be summarized as
Algorithm

1
below.

Algorithm 1

Tensor map

1:

Input:
Tensor
\mathcal{X}
, channel
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{L}\}

2:

Initialize:
Set
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V)\leftarrow\mathcal{X}

3:

for

l\in[L]

do

4:

Set
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V)\leftarrow\textnormal{{reshape}}\big\{\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V),v_{l}\big\}

5:

end

for

6:

Output:
The mapped tensor
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V)

Finally, by
Cen and Lam, 2025a
, the reshape operator can be inverted, meaning that one can recover
\mathcal{X}
from
\textnormal{{reshape}}(\mathcal{X},v)
given the original dimensions of
\mathcal{X}
, we may also recover
\mathcal{X}
from
\textnormal{{map}}(\mathcal{X},V)
.

Fix
m\in[M]
. Then we have
\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}=\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}
. Take any
k\in[K]
, for any
n\neq m
and multi-index
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K_{n}})\in[p_{n,1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n,K_{n}}]
, we have

\displaystyle\;\quad\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)})\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}\Big(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(m)}\Big)\cdot\Big(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)}+\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(n)}\Big)_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}+\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{F,t}^{(m)}\Big)\cdot\Big(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)}+\mathcal{X}_{F,t}^{(n)}\Big)_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)})\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}\Big(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}+[\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}-\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}]\Big)\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\},

where the first and third equalities used Assumption
1
(ii). The above implies
\mathbb{E}\Big\{\textnormal{{mat}}_{k}\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}-\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}\Big)\cdot(\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)})_{\boldsymbol{i}}\Big\}=0
, and together with Assumption
1
(i), suggests that any element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}-\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}
is uncorrelated to any element in
\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(n)}
, which holds true for any
n\in m
. By Assumption
1
(iii), we conclude that
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}_{G,t}^{(m)}=\mathcal{X}_{G,t}^{(m)}
. The global component—hence the local component—is thus identified, which completes the proof of the proposition.

Lemma 4

(Useful inequalities to bound sandwiched terms)
.

We have the following.

(i)

For any square matrix
M
and sequence of matrices
A_{t}
with compatible dimensions for
t\in[T]
, we have

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|M\|^{2}\cdot\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}A_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Note that this inequality is tight, which can be seen by taking
M
as the identity matrix.

(ii)

(Lemma B.16 in
Barigozzi et al., 2025
.) For any sequence of matrices
A_{t}=[A_{t,ij}]
,
B_{t}
for
t\in[T]
, and any matrix
M
with compatible dimensions, we have

\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}MB_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|M\|_{F}^{2}\cdot\sum_{i,j}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t,ij}B_{t}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Part (ii) is a direct quotation of Lemma B.16 in
Barigozzi et al., 2025
, so it suffices to show part (i) below. Expanding the squared Frobenius norm, we have

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2}&=\textnormal{\text{tr}}\left\{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{s=1}^{T}A_{s}M^{\intercal}A_{s}^{\intercal}\right)\right\}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}A_{s}M^{\intercal}A_{s}^{\intercal})\\
&=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{s}^{\intercal}A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}A_{s}M^{\intercal})=:\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{st}MA_{st}^{\intercal}M^{\intercal}),\end{split}

(B.8)

where the second line used the cyclic property of trace, and the definition
A_{st}=A_{s}^{\intercal}A_{t}
. For any
t,s\in[T]
, we may read
\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{st}MA_{st}^{\intercal}M^{\intercal})
as a Frobenius inner product and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality such that

\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{st}MA_{st}^{\intercal}M^{\intercal})=\langle A_{st}M,MA_{st}\rangle_{F}\leq\|A_{st}M\|_{F}\cdot\|MA_{st}\|_{F}\leq\|M\|^{2}\|A_{st}\|_{F}^{2},

(B.9)

where the last inequality used the fact that for any matrix products
AB
, we have
\|AB\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i\cdot}^{\top}B\|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i\cdot}^{\top}\|^{2}\|B\|^{2}}=\|A\|_{F}\cdot\|B\|
.

Combining (
B.8
) and (
B.9
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}MA_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{st}MA_{st}^{\intercal}M^{\intercal})\leq\|M\|^{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\|A_{st}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|M\|^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\textnormal{\text{tr}}(A_{s}^{\intercal}A_{t}A_{t}^{\intercal}A_{s})=\|M\|^{2}\cdot\textnormal{\text{tr}}\left\{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}A_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{s=1}^{T}A_{s}A_{s}^{\intercal}\right)\right\}=\|M\|^{2}\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{t}A_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|_{F}^{2},

as desired. This completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

\displaystyle\;\quad\Big\|\widehat{A}_{m,k,\perp}-A_{m,k,\perp}\Big\|_{F}\leq\frac{8}{\lambda_{p_{m,k}-r_{m,k}}\big(A_{m,k,\perp}A_{m,k,\perp}^{\intercal}\big)}\Big\|\frac{1}{p_{m,k}}\big(A_{m,k}A_{m,k}^{\intercal}-\widehat{A}_{m,k}\widehat{A}_{m,k}^{\intercal}\big)\Big\|_{F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{p_{m,k}}\Big\|\big(A_{m,k}-\widehat{A}_{m,k}\big)A_{m,k}^{\intercal}+\widehat{A}_{m,k}\big(A_{m,k}-\widehat{A}_{m,k}\big)^{\intercal}\Big\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\Big),

where the last equality used the triangle inequality and
Theorem

1
. The second statement in the lemma is straightforward from the above, and this completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{IV}_{2}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\sum_{k\in[K_{m}]}\frac{1}{p_{m,k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m}}\Big)&\text{if $K_{m}>1$,}\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m,1}}\Big)&\text{if $K_{m}=1$;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{IV}_{3}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{m}}\Big).

Putting all together, we hence conclude

\Big\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{t}^{(m)})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{G}_{t}^{(m)})\Big\|_{F}^{2}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\sum_{k\in[K_{m}]}\frac{1}{p_{m,k}^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m}}\Big)&\text{if $K_{m}>1$,}\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{p_{m,1}}\Big)&\text{if $K_{m}=1$.}\end{cases}

The rate of convergence for the local component estimator has been spelled out in (
B.34
) and (
B.35
). The arguments for the global component estimator follow similarly and hence omitted here. This ends the proof of the theorem.
