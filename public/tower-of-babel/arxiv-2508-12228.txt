Can a One-Point Feedback Zeroth-order Algorithm Achieve Linear Dimension Dependent Sample Complexity?
Haishan Ye, Xiangyu Chang

Abstract
We revisit the one-point feedback zeroth-order (ZO) optimization problem, a classical setting in derivative-free optimization where only a single noisy function evaluation is available per query. Compared to their two-point counterparts, existing one-point feedback ZO algorithms typically suffer from poor dimension dependence in their sample complexities—often quadratic or worse—even for convex problems. This gap has led to the open question of whether one-point feedback ZO algorithms can match the optimal linear dimension dependence achieved by two-point methods. In this work, we answer this question affirmatively . By leveraging uniform ball smoothing and a two-stage convergence analysis framework that first establishes rates for the smoothed objective before transferring them to the original function, we show that the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm proposed by Zhang et al., 2022 can achieve optimal sample complexity in several settings. For convex Lipschitz objective functions in deterministic setting, our bound matches the 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ ε − 2 ) \mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{-2}) complexity of the optimal two-point feedback method; for convex smooth functions, we obtain 𝒪 ( d ε − 3 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{-3/2}) ; and for nonconvex smooth functions, we achieve linear dimension dependence with improved constants over prior work. In the stochastic setting, our analysis yields bounds with quadratic dependence on d d but significantly reduced dependence on the value variance compared to Zhang et al., 2022 , leading to sharper guarantees in practical regimes. Moreover, in the important and widely studied least-squares setting, our framework further achieves linear dependence on the dimension, thereby substantially strengthening the practical impact of our results. Our results not only close a long-standing gap in the theory of one-point feedback ZO optimization, but also demonstrate that careful analysis can unlock optimality without modifying the underlying algorithm.

Introduction

This paper considers both deterministic and stochastic formulations of unconstrained optimization problems. We begin by considering the deterministic case, where the objective is to minimize a function over the entire Euclidean space:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(1.1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the decision variable, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued objective function that is assumed to be deterministic and well-defined over the domain.

In many practical applications
(
Malik et al., 2020
;
He et al., 2023
;
Malladi et al., 2023
)
, the objective function depends on uncertain data or stochastic parameters. In such cases, the optimization problem (
1.1
) is naturally formulated as a stochastic program, where the objective function
f
is expressed as an expectation over a random variable
\xi
:

f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[f(x,\xi)\right],

(1.2)

where
\xi
is a random variable representing inherent randomness in the environment, such as data noise, model uncertainty, or fluctuating system parameters.
Due to the expectation operator in Eq. (
1.2
), the exact computation of
f(x)
is often intractable in practice. Instead, one can typically access only stochastic approximations of the objective by sampling from the distribution of
\xi
. That is, for a given
x
, one observes only noisy evaluations
f(x,\xi)
, where
\xi
is drawn from its underlying distribution
(
Berahas et al., 2022
)
.

One straightforward approach to solve (
1.1
) is the (stochastic) gradient descent method
(
Nesterov, 2013
;
Bottou, 2012
)
.
These methods rely on the availability of first-order information, namely the gradient of the objective function with respect to the decision variable. However, in many practical scenarios, such gradient information is unavailable or prohibitively expensive to compute, rendering traditional gradient-based methods inapplicable.
For example, in the context of black-box adversarial attacks against deep neural networks, the attacker does not have access to the internal gradients of the model. Instead, only the inputs and outputs of the model—viewed as a black-box function—are observable
(
Ilyas et al., 2018
)
.
In such a setting, zeroth-order (ZO) optimization techniques
(
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
;
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
;
Berahas et al., 2022
)
, which rely solely on function value evaluations, offer a powerful alternative.
These methods have received significant attention in a variety of domains where gradient information is either inaccessible or costly to compute. Applications include fine-tuning large language models
(
Malladi et al., 2023
)
, solving reinforcement learning problems
(
Malik et al., 2020
;
Mania et al., 2018
;
Salimans et al., 2017
)
, and controlling complex physical systems
(
Chen et al., 2021
;
Luo et al., 2020
;
He et al., 2023
)
.

According to the number of function evaluations queried per iteration, ZO optimization can be broadly classified into two categories:
one-point feedback
and
two-point feedback
methods.
In the one-point feedback setting, the algorithm accesses only one function value at each iteration and uses this value to construct a stochastic approximation of the gradient.

SPALL, 1997
proposes the first one-point feedback ZO algorithm, named SPSA1, for solving (
1.1
).
The gradient estimator is constructed as

G(x)=\frac{f(x+\alpha u)}{\alpha}u^{-1},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

(1.3)

where
u\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
is a random vector whose entries are independently sampled from the Rademacher distribution
(
Sadegh & Spall, 1998
)
, and
u^{-1}
denotes the element-wise inverse of
u
.
Given that
u^{-1}=u
for all
u\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
, the gradient estimator simplifies to

G(x)=\frac{f(x+\alpha u)}{\alpha}u,\quad u\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}.

(1.4)

For stochastic optimization problems as defined in Eq. (
1.2
),
Flaxman et al., 2005
propose the first one-point feedback ZO algorithm under the online bandit optimization framework with the gradient estimator as

G(x,\xi)=d\cdot\frac{f(x+\alpha u,\xi)}{\alpha}u\mbox{ with }\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }u\sim\mathcal{S}(0,I_{d}),

(1.5)

where
u\sim\mathcal{S}(0,I_{d})
means uniformly sampling a random vector from the sphere of a unit ball centered at zero.

Unfortunately, the gradient estimators in Eq. (
1.3
) and Eq. (
1.5
) suffer from high variance.
For instance, the high variance of
G(x)
defined in Eq. (
1.3
) can be illustrated by the following derivation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|G(x)\right\|^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{f(x)+\alpha\langle\nabla f(x),u\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})}{\alpha}u\right\|^{2}\right]

(1.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{f(x)}{\alpha}u+uu^{\top}\nabla f(x)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha u)\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{f(x)}{\alpha}\right\|^{2}\cdot\left\|u\right\|^{2}\right]+3\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|uu^{\top}\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\right]+3\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{O}(\alpha)u\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3d\left(\frac{f(x)}{\alpha}\right)^{2}+3d\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}+3d\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}),

where the final equality follows from the fact that
u\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
and
\mathbb{E}[\left\|u\right\|^{2}]=d
. Since
\alpha
is typically chosen to be a small value dependent on the target precision, the term
\left(\frac{f(x)}{\alpha}\right)^{2}
becomes dominant and leads to large variance.
Likewise, the stochastic one-point gradient estimator in Eq. (
1.5
) also suffers from high variance due to a similar scaling behavior. As a result, the ZO algorithm proposed by
Flaxman et al., 2005
suffers from poor sample complexity, with a convergence rate depending on
\varepsilon^{-4}
and
d^{4}
, where
\varepsilon
denotes the target optimization precision.

In contrast to the one-point feedback estimator in Eq. (
1.5
), two-point feedback ZO algorithms can significantly reduce the variance by utilizing an additional function evaluation per iteration. A widely used two-point feedback gradient estimator is given by
Shamir, 2017
:

G^{\prime}(x)=d\cdot\frac{f(x+\alpha u)-f(x)}{\alpha}u,\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\quad u\sim\mathcal{S}(0,I_{d}).

(1.7)

The variance of
G^{\prime}(x)
can then be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|G^{\prime}(x)\right\|^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|d\cdot\frac{f(x+\alpha u)-f(x)}{\alpha}u\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|d\cdot\left(\langle\nabla f(x),u\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\right)u\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|d\cdot uu^{\top}\nabla f(x)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha)\cdot du\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2d^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|uu^{\top}\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\right]+2d^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{O}(\alpha)u\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}+2d^{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}),

where the final equality follows from Lemma
8
and
\mathbb{E}[\left\|u\right\|^{2}]=1
.
Compared to the variance of
G(x)
in Eq. (
1.6
), the variance of
G^{\prime}(x)
is nearly independent of the choice of
\alpha
, particularly when
\alpha
is small. As a result, two-point feedback ZO algorithms can achieve an order-wise faster convergence rate than their one-point feedback counterparts
(
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
;
Shamir, 2017
;
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
)
.

Due to the high variance of one-point feedback gradient estimators, achieving an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution of (
1.1
) typically requires sample complexity with heavy dependence on the problem dimension. For instance, the sample complexity of the algorithm in
Flaxman et al., 2005
scales at least as
d^{4}
.
In contrast, two-point feedback ZO algorithms with tight convergence guarantees typically exhibit linear dependence on
d

(
Shamir, 2017
)
. As a result,
Larson et al., 2019
raise concerns about the practicality of one-point feedback ZO methods in high-dimensional settings.

To overcome the limitations of one-point feedback ZO optimization, numerous efforts have been made to reduce the variance of one-point feedback gradient estimators in order to accelerate convergence and, in particular, to mitigate the dimensional dependence in sample complexity
(
Saha & Tewari, 2011
;
Dekel et al., 2015
;
Gasnikov et al., 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Chen et al., 2022
)
. One effective strategy is to leverage the high-order smoothness of the objective function
(
Akhavan et al., 2020
;
Bach & Perchet, 2016
)
.
Recently, inspired by two-point feedback ZO methods,
Zhang et al., 2022
propose the
one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm
, which utilizes information from the previous iteration to reduce the variance of the gradient estimator constructed using one-point feedback oracles. Specifically, for deterministic optimization problems, they introduce the following estimator:

g_{t}=\frac{f(x_{t}+\alpha u_{t})-f(x_{t-1}+\alpha u_{t-1})}{\alpha}u_{t},\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\quad u_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),

(1.8)

where
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is the
d
-dimension standard Gaussian distribution.
For stochastic optimization problems, the corresponding estimator is

\tilde{g}_{t}=\frac{f(x_{t}+\alpha u_{t},\xi_{t})-f(x_{t-1}+\alpha u_{t-1},\xi_{t-1})}{\alpha}u_{t},\quad u_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(1.9)

The estimator in Eq. (
1.8
) closely resembles the two-point estimator in Eq. (
1.7
). When
f(x)
is Lipschitz continuous, the value of
f(x_{t-1}+\alpha u_{t-1})
tends to be close to
f(x_{t})
, thereby significantly reducing the variance compared to the standard one-point estimator in Eq. (
1.4
).
Benefiting from this variance reduction,
Zhang et al., 2022
show that the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm achieves improved sample complexity over existing methods. Furthermore, for smooth objective functions,
Chen et al., 2022
introduce a momentum-based scheme to further enhance the efficiency of one-point feedback ZO algorithms by reducing their sample complexity.

(i)

Uniform ball smoothing for tighter gradient approximation.

We utilize the well-known uniform ball smoothing technique for constructing the smoothed objective function

Preliminaries

This section presents the necessary notations and assumptions that will be utilized in this paper, and the definition and property of the uniform ball smoothed objective function. Then the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm is recalled.

2.1
Notation and Assumption

First, we introduce some important notions about objective functions.

Definition 1
.

A function
f(x)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz continuous, if for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L_{0}\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad\mbox{ for }\quad L_{0}>0.

(2.1)

Definition 2
.

A function
f(x)
is
L
-smooth, if for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad\mbox{ for }\quad L>0.

(2.2)

Definition 3
.

A function
f(x)
is
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu>0
, then for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle\partial f(x),y-x\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2},

(2.3)

where
\partial f(x)
is a subgradient of
f(x)
.

In many practice, there exists randomness involving the problem to be solved.
Thus, the objective function is modeled as Eq. (
1.2
).
Next, we will introduce two assumptions related to the randomness of the objective function.

Assumption 1

(Bounded Stochastic Value Variance)
.

We assume that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist
\sigma_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\Big(f(x,\;\xi)-f(x)\Big)^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{0}^{2}.

(2.4)

Assumption 2

(Bound Stochastic Gradient Variance)
.

We assume that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist
\sigma_{1}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\left\|\nabla f(x,\;\xi)-\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}

(2.5)

Assumption
1
is widely used in stochastic one-point feedback ZO optimization
(
Akhavan et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2022
)
.
Assumption
2
is almost a standard assumption for analyzing the convergence rate of stochastic gradient descent
(
Garrigos & Gower, 2023
;
Nemirovski et al., 2009
)
.

Note that the value variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
of function evaluation can be much smaller than the gradient variance
\sigma_{1}^{2}
in many real applications.
We provide the following proposition to show its rationality.

Proposition 1
.

Given
\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{i}^{\top}x^{*},1)
, we define the following least squares problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i}\right)^{2}=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mbox{Uniform}(1,m)}f_{i}(x),\mbox{ with }f_{i}(x)=\left(a_{i}^{\top}x-b_{i}\right)^{2}.

(2.6)

If
\left\|a_{i}\right\|^{2}\geq d
, then it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(f_{i}(x)-f(x))^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\nabla f_{i}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\right].

(2.7)

Proposition
1
shows that for the least squares problem defined above, the function value variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
can be as small as
d^{-1}
times the gradient variance
\sigma_{1}^{2}
.
This observation plays an important role in our subsequent analysis of the stochastic setting.
Since all our stochastic results are expressed in terms of
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, this structural property allows us, in this widely studied and practically important scenario, to replace
\sigma_{0}^{2}
with
d^{-1}\sigma_{1}^{2}
.
Consequently, the sample complexities can be further reduced, achieving improved dimension dependence.
The last row of Table
2
already summarizes all corresponding results, and thus we will not reiterate these implications in Section
4
.

Remark 1
.

The condition
\|a_{i}\|^{2}\geq d
is natural in many statistical learning and deep model settings.
First, many random design matrices satisfy the condition exactly; for instance, when using
\pm 1
(Rademacher) entries in random projection matrices that are adopted in one-bit compressive sensing
(
Li et al., 2018
)
, each row vector has squared norm exactly
d

(
Achlioptas, 2003
)
.
Second, let us consider the sample vectors
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are drawn from an isotropic sub-Gaussian design with independent coordinates and unit variance, then their Euclidean norm concentrates sharply around
\sqrt{d}
.
In particular, by standard norm concentration results for sub-Gaussian vectors
(
Vershynin, 2018

, one has

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\|a_{i}\|-\sqrt{d}\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-ct^{2}/K^{4}\right),

which implies
\|a_{i}\|^{2}=d(1\pm o(1))
with high probability.
Third, by applying

Such normalization is widely used in practice: Batch Normalization
(

)
standardizes layer inputs to approximately zero mean and unit variance, encouraging feature isotropy, while
L_{2}
-normalization layers are standard in metric learning and face recognition
(
Schroff et al., 2015
)
, often followed by scaling by
\sqrt{d}
.

2.2
Subtle Properties of Smoothed Objective Function

The objective of ZO optimization is to estimate the first-order gradient of a function using only function evaluations (zeroth-order oracles).
To achieve this, it is necessary to perturb the function around the current point uniformly in all directions, thereby enabling accurate gradient estimation.
This naturally motivates the smoothed formulation of
f
introduced by
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
.

Zhang et al., 2022
follow the idea and utilize the Guaussian-smoothed
f
to illustrate the nice convergence rate of the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm.
This paper employs the smoothed objective with uniform distribution in a unit ball to refine the convergence analysis of the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm.
We will show that the smoothed objective has several subtle properties that will be fully used in the analysis.

Definition 4
.

Function
f_{\alpha}(x)
is a smooth version of
f(x)
with

f_{\alpha}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{u}}\left[f(x+\alpha\tilde{u})\right],\;\mbox{ with }\;\tilde{u}\sim\mathcal{B}(0,I_{d}),

(2.8)

where
\tilde{u}\sim\mathcal{B}(0,I_{d})
means
\tilde{u}
has uniform distribution in a unit ball.

This smoothing scheme is often referred to as
uniform ball smoothing

(
Hazan et al., 2016
;
Chen et al., 2017
)
, in contrast to the Gaussian smoothing
(
Zhang et al., 2022
)
.
Now we provide several important properties of
f_{\alpha}(x)
.

Lemma 1
.

Letting
f_{\alpha}(x)
be defined in Eq. (
2.8
), then it holds that
\mathbb{E}[g_{t}]=\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{t})
where
g_{t}
is defined in Eq. (
1.8
).
Furthermore,
f_{\alpha}(x)
also have the following properties:

1.

If
f(x)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz continuous, then
f_{\alpha}(x)
is also
L_{0}
-Lipschitz continuous.

2.

If
f(x)
is
L
-smooth, then
f_{\alpha}(x)
is also
L
-smooth.

3.

If
f(x)
is
\mu
-strongly convex, then
f_{\alpha}(x)
is also
\mu
-strongly convex.

Lemma 2
.

The smoothed function
f_{\alpha}(x)
has the following properties:

1.

If
f(x)
is convex, then it holds that
f_{\alpha}(x)\geq f(x)
.

2.

If
f(x)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz continuous, it holds that
|f_{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|\leq L_{0}\alpha
.

3.

If
f(x)
is
L
-smooth, it holds that

\displaystyle|f_{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2},\;\mbox{ and }\;\left\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq L\alpha.

(2.9)

Furthermore, if
f(x^{*})\geq\infty
, then it holds that

\displaystyle f(x^{*})\leq f(\tilde{x}^{*})+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2},

where
x^{*}
and
\tilde{x}^{*}
are the minimal points of
f(x)
and
f_{\alpha}(x)
, respectively.

Remark 2
.

We find that introducing the uniform ball smoothed objective function (
2.8
) can achieve a gradient approximation that satisfies (
2.9
) in Lemma
2
.
In contrast,
Zhang et al., 2022
employ the Gaussian smoothed objective function:

f_{\alpha,\text{Gaussian}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}[f(x+\alpha u)],

and Lemma 2 of
Zhang et al., 2022
shows

\left\|\nabla f_{\alpha,\text{Gaussian}}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq L\alpha(d+3)^{3/2}.

Thus, we indicate that uniform ball smoothing yields a significantly tighter approximation to the true gradient, particularly in high-dimensional settings.
Because the approximation in (
2.9
) does not depend on
d
.
This subtle property of the smoothed objective function has been studied by
Flaxman et al., 2005
;
Chen et al., 2022
and will be fully utilized in the following convergence analysis.

5
Conclusion

This paper considers a long-standing open question in zeroth-order optimization by showing that one-point feedback algorithms can achieve the same optimal sample complexity as two-point methods in several fundamental settings.
By employing uniform ball smoothing and a refined two-stage convergence analysis—first establishing rates for the smoothed objective and then transferring them to the original function—we prove that the one-point residual feedback ZO algorithm of
Zhang et al., 2022
attains
\mathcal{O}(d\varepsilon^{-2})
complexity for convex Lipschitz objectives, matching the optimal two-point bound.
We further establish improved complexities for convex smooth and nonconvex smooth objectives, as well as sharper bounds in the stochastic setting with significantly reduced dependence on the value variance.
Nevertheless, in the stochastic regime, our bounds do not yet fully achieve linear dimension dependence in complete generality, and future research may explore broader conditions or richer problem classes under which such optimal rates can be attained.
Our results demonstrate that optimality in one-point feedback ZO optimization can be achieved without modifying the underlying algorithm.

Lemma 8
.

Letting

Proof.

Lemma 9
.
