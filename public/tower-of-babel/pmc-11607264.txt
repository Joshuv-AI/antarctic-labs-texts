A Response to: Letter to the Editor Regarding “Tafamidis 61 mg Patient Characteristics and Persistency? A Retrospective Analysis of German Statutory Health Insurance Data (IQVIA™ LRx)”
Authors: Sepideh Attal
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

On behalf of my co-authors, I wish to thank Kristen et al. for their thoughts in relation to our recently published article, primarily characterising the adherence and persistence to tafamidis free acid 61 mg in patients with transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) in Germany using the IQVIA LRx prescription database [1, 2]. As a supportive outcome, our study provided brief insight into the most common concomitant medications in patients who were prescribed tafamidis 61 mg [1]. We agree that further characterisation of concomitant heart failure medications in patients with ATTR-CM is warranted. A better understanding of how these medications are utilised, specifically in patients who also take tafamidis, might support treatment-regimen optimisation and improve outcomes [3, 4]. As our primary aim was to explore adherence and persistence with tafamidis, additional analyses to examine concomitant medication use were beyond the planned scope. Further, the IQVIA LRx database offers limited patient data with which to contextualise the observed changes in concomitant prescribing. Due to this, we did not attempt to provide detailed interpretation.

Our study reported on changes in the use of the four most commonly prescribed concomitant medication classes in patients receiving tafamidis in Germany from March 2020 to August 2022 [1]. These classes were loop diuretics, beta-blocking agents (BBs), direct factor Xa inhibitors, and statins. Statistically significant changes in the proportions of patients prescribed BBs, direct factor Xa inhibitors, and statins were observed over the follow-up period (mean of 12 months). The change in the proportion of patients prescribed BBs was − 7%. As Kristen et al. suggest, there are multiple clinical scenarios to consider as potential rationale for this change, as BBs can be beneficial in some cases but detrimental in others [2, 5–7]. The finding that around two-thirds of patients with ATTR-CM in Germany take concomitant BBs is similar to that observed in other regions and not an unexpected finding [8]. Change in the proportion of patients prescribed direct factor Xa inhibitors was + 4%. As noted in the article, we agree that this increase most likely reflects preventative actions to reduce the risk of thromboembolic events, which are common in patients with ATTR-CM [1, 9]. Change in the proportion of patients prescribed statins was − 4% [1]. We also agree that the high proportion of patients taking statins (53%) is expected for the demographic profile of the cohort (elderly [mean age, 78 years] and with various comorbidities) [1, 2, 10].

As mentioned by Kristen et al. treatment decisions in patients with ATTR-CM, who are often elderly and with multiple comorbidities, are not straightforward and typically managed on a case-by-case basis using clinician expertise [2, 6]. Though Kristen et al. caution that our findings could be misinterpreted as suggesting inadequate patient care in Germany, this was not our thought or intent. Data on concomitant medication use in our report are few and the LRx database offers limited clinical and demographic context with which to interpret the findings. Since the study started shortly after the regulatory approval of tafamidis (February 2020), it may be that some patients were diagnosed with ATTR-CM quite a few years previously; however, data to allow determination of disease staging were not available [1, 11]. Additionally, data were not collected on the use of specific concomitant drugs or dosages. The relatively high proportion of patients taking loop diuretics, BBs, direct factor Xa inhibitors, and statins (all > 50%), along with the finding that 54% of patients in the cohort were aged ≥ 80 years at first tafamidis prescription suggests ATTR-CM may have been reasonably advanced in this population, possibly alongside multiple cardiovascular comorbidities [7, 8]. A high suspected comorbidity burden is also supported by the finding that 50% of patients had outpatient visits and 23% had cardiology visits during follow-up. Advanced age, comorbidities, and overall frailty can all complicate prescribing decisions in patients with ATTR-CM.

In summary, while we thank Kristen et al. for highlighting the importance of research into the use of concomitant heart failure medications in patients with ATTR-CM taking tafamidis, we do not feel that the data included in our publication can be used to explore this question further. In particular, the changes observed over follow-up were not able to be fully interpreted due to the lack of clinical context and the reasons behind treatment decisions can never be fully determined from prescription database data. We agree that the evidence supporting clinical decision-making around the use of heart failure medications in patients with ATTR-CM is limited, and this topic deserves further study.
