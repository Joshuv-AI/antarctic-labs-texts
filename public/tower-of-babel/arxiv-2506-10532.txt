Equivariant Neural Diffusion for Molecule Generation
François Cornet, Grigory Bartosh, Mikkel N. Schmidt, Christian A. Naesseth

Abstract
We introduce Equivariant Neural Diffusion, a novel diffusion model for molecule generation in 3 3 D that is equivariant to Euclidean transformations. Compared to current state-of-the-art equivariant diffusion models, the key innovation in Equivariant Neural Diffusion lies in its learnable forward process for enhanced generative modelling. Rather than pre-specified, the forward process is parameterized through a time- and data-dependent transformation that is equivariant to rigid transformations. Through a series of experiments on standard molecule generation benchmarks, we demonstrate the competitive performance of Equivariant Neural Diffusion compared to several strong baselines for both unconditional and conditional generation.



1
Introduction

The discovery of novel chemical compounds with relevant properties is critical to a number of scientific fields, such as drug discovery and materials design
(
Merchant et al. 2023
)
. However, due to the large size and complex structure of the chemical space
(
Ruddigkeit et al. 2012
)
, which combines continuous and discrete features, it is notably difficult to search. Additionally,
ab-initio
Quantum Mechanics methods for computing target properties are often computationally expensive, preventing brute-force enumeration. While some of these heavy computations can be amortized through learned surrogates, the need for innovative search methods remains, and generative models have recently emerged as a promising avenue
(
Anstine and Isayev 2023
)
. Such models can learn complex data distributions, that, in turn, can be sampled from to obtain novel samples similar to the original data.
Compared to other data modalities such as images or text, molecules present additional challenges as they have to adhere to strict chemical rules, and obey the symmetries of the
3
D space.

Currently, the most promising directions for molecule generation in
3
D are either auto-regressive models
(
Gebauer et al. 2019
;
Gebauer et al. 2022
;
Luo and Ji 2022
;
Daigavane et al. 2024
)
building molecules one atom at a time, or Diffusion Models
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
;
Vignac et al. 2023
;
Le et al. 2024
)
that learn to revert a corruption mechanism that transforms the data distribution into noise. As both approaches directly operate in
3
D space, they can leverage architectures designed for machine learned force fields
(
Unke et al. 2021
)
, that were carefully developed to encode the symmetries inherent to the data
(
Schütt et al. 2017
;
Schütt et al. 2021
;
Batzner et al. 2022
;
Batatia et al. 2022
)
.

The success of Diffusion Models has not been limited to molecule generation, and promising results have been demonstrated on a variety of other data modalities
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
. Nevertheless, most existing Diffusion Models pre-specify the forward process, forcing the reverse process to comply with it. A recent line of work has sought to
overcome that limitation and improve generation by replacing the fixed forward process with a learnable one
(
Bartosh et al. 2023
;
Nielsen et al. 2024
;
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
.

Contributions

In this paper, we present Equivariant Neural Diffusion (END), a novel diffusion model for molecule generation in
3
D that (1) is equivariant to Euclidean transformations, and (2) features a learnable forward process. We demonstrate competitive unconditional molecule generation performance on the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs benchmarks. For conditional generation driven by composition and substructure constraints, our approach exhibits a substantial performance gain compared to existing equivariant diffusion models. Our set of experiments underscores the benefit of a learnable forward process for improved unconditional and conditional molecule generation.

2
Background

We begin by establishing the necessary background for generative modeling of geometric graphs. We first introduce the
data representation and its inherent symmetries. We then discuss Diffusion Models (DMs), and more specifically the Equivariant Diffusion Model (EDM)
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
. Finally, we present the Neural Flow Diffusion Models (
NFDM
) framework
(
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
.

2.1
Equivariance

Molecules as geometric graphs in E(3)

We consider geometric graphs embedded in
3
-dimensional Euclidean space that represent molecules. Formally, each atomistic system can be described by a tuple
\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\bm{h})
, where
\bm{r}=(\bm{r}_{1},...,\bm{r}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}
form a collection of vectors in
3
D representing the coordinates of the atoms, and
\bm{h}=(\bm{h}_{1},...,\bm{h}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
are the associated scalar features (e.g. atomic types or charges). When dealing with molecules, we are particularly interested in
E(3)
, the Euclidean group in
3
dimensions, generated by translations, rotations and reflections. Each group element in
E(3)
can be represented as a combination of a translation vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in O(3)
encoding rotation or reflection. While scalar features
\bm{h}
remain invariant, coordinates
\bm{r}
transform under translation, rotation and reflection as
\mathbf{R}\bm{r}+\mathbf{t}=(\mathbf{R}\bm{r}_{1}+\mathbf{t},...,\mathbf{R}\bm{r}_{M}+\mathbf{t})
.

Equivariant functions

A function
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is said to be equivariant to the action of a group
G
, or
G
-equivariant, if
g\cdot f(\bm{x})=f(g\cdot\bm{x}),\forall g\in G
. It is said to be
G
-invariant, if
f(\bm{x})=f(g\cdot\bm{x}),\forall g\in G
.
In the case of a function
f:(\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}\times\mathbb{R}^{M\times D})\rightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}\times\mathbb{R}^{M\times D})
operating on geometric graphs, the function is said to be
E(3)
-equivariant if

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\bm{y}^{(r)}+\mathbf{t},\bm{y}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=f\Big(\mathbf{R}\bm{r}+\mathbf{t},\bm{h}\Big),\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3)\text{ and }\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\bm{y}^{(r)}
and
\bm{y}^{(h)}
denote the output related to
\bm{r}
and
\bm{h}
respectively. There exists a large variety of graph neural network architectures designed to be equivariant to the Euclidean group
(
Schütt et al. 2017
;
Schütt et al. 2021
;
Batzner et al. 2022
;
Batatia et al. 2022
)
.

Equivariant distributions

A conditional distribution
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
is equivariant to rotations and reflections when
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})=p(\mathbf{R}\bm{y}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x}),\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3)
, while
a distribution is said to be invariant when
p(\bm{x})=p(\mathbf{R}\bm{x}),\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3)
. Regarding translation, it is not possible to have a translation-invariant non-zero distribution, as it would require that
p(\bm{x})=p(\bm{x}+\mathbf{t}),\forall\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}
, which would mean that
p(\bm{x})
cannot integrate to
1

(
Garcia Satorras et al. 2021
)
.
However, a translation-invariant distribution can be constructed in the linear subspace
R
, where the center of gravity is fixed to
\bm{0}
(i.e. zero CoM subspace):
R=\{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}:\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{r}_{i}=\bm{0}\}

(
Xu et al. 2022
)
. As
R
can be shown to be intrinsically equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times 3}

(
Bao et al. 2023
)
, we will consider in what follows that
\bm{r}
is defined in
\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times 3}
for ease of notation.

2.2
Equivariant Diffusion Models

Diffusion Models (DMs)
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
are generative models that learn distributions through a hierarchy of latent variables, corresponding to perturbed versions of the data at increasing noise scales. Diffusion Models consist of a forward and a reverse (or generative) process. The Equivariant Diffusion Model (EDM)
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
is a particular instance of a Diffusion Model, where the learned marginal
p_{\theta}(\bm{x})
is made invariant to the action of translations, rotations and reflections by construction. Intuitively, this means that the likelihood of a given molecule under the model does not depend on its orientation.

Forward process

The forward process perturbs samples from the data distribution,
\bm{x}\sim q(\bm{x})
, over time through noise injection, resulting in a trajectory of latent variables
(\bm{z}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, conditional on
\bm{x}
.
The conditional distribution for
(\bm{z}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
given
\bm{x}
, can be described by an initial distribution
q(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x})
and a Stochastic Differential Equation,

\displaystyle\mathop{d\{\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}\}}

\displaystyle=f(t)\big[\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}\big]\mathop{dt}+g(t)\mathop{d\{\bm{w}^{(r)},\bm{w}^{(h)}\}},

where the drift
f(t)
and volatility
g(t)
are scalar functions of time, and
\bm{w}^{(r)}
and
\bm{w}^{(h)}
are two independent standard Wiener processes defined in
\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times 3}
and
\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
respectively. Specifically,
EDM
implements the Variance-Preserving SDE scheme
(
Song et al. 2020
)
, with
f(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)
and
g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)}
for a fixed schedule
\beta(t)
. Due to the linearity of the drift term, the conditional marginal distribution is known in closed-form
(
Särkkä and Solin 2019
)
, and can be reconstructed as

\displaystyle q\big([\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}]\big|[\bm{r},\bm{h}]\big)=q(\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t}|\bm{r})q(\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}|\bm{h})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},|\alpha_{t}\bm{r},\sigma^{2}_{t}\mathbb{I})\cdot\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t},|\alpha_{t}\bm{h},\sigma^{2}_{t}\mathbb{I}),

where
\alpha_{t}=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)\mathop{ds}\big)
and
\sigma_{t}=1-\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)\mathop{ds}\big)
.
It evolves from a low-variance Gaussian centered around the data
q(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x})\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x},\delta^{2}\mathbb{I})
to an uninformative prior distribution (that contains no information about the data distribution), i.e. a unit Gaussian
q(\bm{z}_{1}|\bm{x})\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{1}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})
.

Reverse (generative) process

Starting from the prior
[\bm{z}^{(r)}_{1},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{1}]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})\cdot\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})
, samples from
q(\bm{x})
can be generated by reversing the forward process. This can be done by following the reverse-time SDE
(
Anderson 1982
)
,

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\Big(f(t)\big[\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}\big]-g^{2}(t)\big[\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t}}\log q(\bm{z}_{t}),\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}}\log q(\bm{z}_{t})\big]\Big)\mathop{dt}+g(t)\mathop{d\{\bar{\bm{w}}^{(r)},\bar{\bm{w}}^{(h)}\}},

where
\bar{\bm{w}}^{(r)}
and
\bar{\bm{w}}^{(h)}
are independent standard Wiener processes defined in
\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times 3}
and
\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
, respectively, with time flowing backwards. Diffusion Models approximate the reverse process by learning an approximation of the score function
\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q(\bm{z}_{t})
parameterized by a neural network
s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
. With the learned score function
s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
, a sample
\bm{z}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{0})\approx q(\bm{z}_{0})\approx q(\bm{x})
can be obtained by first sampling from the prior
[\bm{z}^{(r)}_{1},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{1}]\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})\cdot\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})
, and then simulating the reverse Stochastic Differential Equation,

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\Big(f(t)\big[\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}\big]-g^{2}(t)\big[s^{(r)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t),s^{(h)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big]\Big)\mathop{dt}+g(t)\mathop{d\{\bar{\bm{w}}^{(r)},\bar{\bm{w}}^{(h)}\}},

where the true score function has been replaced by its approximation
s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
. In
EDM
, the approximate score is parameterized through an equivariant function:
s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)=\big[s^{(r)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t),s^{(h)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big]
such that
s_{\theta}([\mathbf{R}\bm{z}^{(r)}_{t},\bm{z}^{(h)}_{t}],t)=\big[\mathbf{R}s^{(r)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t),s^{(h)}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big],\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3)
. Practically, this is realized through the specific parameterization,

\displaystyle s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)-\bm{z}_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}},

where the data point predictor
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
is implemented by an equivariant neural network.

Optimization
The data point predictor
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
, or
s_{\theta}
, is trained by optimizing the denoising score matching loss
(
Vincent 2011
)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{DSM}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q(\bm{x},\bm{z}_{t})}\Big[\lambda(t)\big|\big|s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)-\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

where
\lambda(t)
is a positive weighting function, and
u(t)
is a uniform distribution over the interval
[0,1]
.

2.3
Neural Flow Diffusion Models

Neural Flow Diffusion Models
(
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
are based on the observation that latent variables in Diffusion Models, i.e.
\bm{z}_{t}
, are conventionally inferred through a pre-specified transformation – as implied by the chosen type of Stochastic Differential Equation and the noise schedule. This potentially limits the flexibility of the latent space, and makes the learning of the reverse (generative) process more challenging.

Forward process
In contrast to conventional Diffusion Models, Neural Flow Diffusion Models define the forward process implicitly through a learnable transformation
F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})
of injected noise
\bm{\varepsilon}
, time
t
, and data point
\bm{x}
. The latent variables
\bm{z}_{t}
are obtained by transforming noise samples
\bm{\varepsilon}
, conditional on data point
\bm{x}
and time step
t
:

\bm{z}_{t}=F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x}).
. If
F_{\varphi}
is differentiable with respect to
\bm{\varepsilon}
and
t
, and invertible with respect to

\bm{\varepsilon}
, then, for fixed
\bm{x}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}
, samples from
q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
can be obtained by solving the following conditional Ordinary Differential Equation until time
t
,

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})\mathop{dt}\text{ with }f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})=\frac{\partial F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})}{\partial t}\Big|_{\bm{\varepsilon}=F_{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})},

(1)

with
\bm{z}_{0}\sim q(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x})
. While
F_{\varphi}
and
q(\bm{\varepsilon})
define the conditional marginal distribution
q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
, we need a
distribution over the trajectories
(\bm{z}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Neural Flow Diffusion Models obtain this through the introduction of a conditional Stochastic Differential Equation starting from
\bm{z}_{0}
and running forward in time. Given access to the Ordinary Differential Equation in
Eq.

1
and the score function
\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
, the conditional Stochastic Differential Equation sharing the same conditional marginal distribution
q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=f^{F}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})\mathop{dt}+g_{\varphi}(t)\mathop{d\bm{w}}\text{ with }f^{F}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})=f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x}),

(2)

where the score function of
q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
is
\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})=\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\big[\log q(\bm{\varepsilon})+\log\big|J_{F}^{-1}\big|\big]
,
with
\bm{\varepsilon}=F_{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})
, and
J_{F}^{-1}=\frac{\partial F^{-1}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})}{\partial\bm{z}_{t}}
.

Reverse (generative) process
A conditional reverse Stochastic Differential Equation that starts from
\bm{z}_{1}\sim q(\bm{z}_{1})
, runs backward in time, and reverses the conditional forward Stochastic Differential Equation from
Eq.

2
can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}=f^{B}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})+g_{\varphi}(t)\mathop{d\bar{\bm{w}}}\text{ with }f^{B}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})=f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x}).

(3)

As
\bm{x}
is unknown when generating samples, we can rewrite
Eq.

3
with the prediction of
\bm{x}
instead,

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\mathop{dt}+g_{\varphi}(t)\mathop{d\bar{\bm{w}}},\text{ where }\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)=f^{B}_{\varphi}\big(\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big),

(4)

where
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
is a function that predicts the data point
\bm{x}
. Provided that the reconstruction distribution
q(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x})
and prior distribution
q(\bm{z}_{1})
are defined, this fully specifies the reverse (generative) process.

Optimization
The forward and reverse processes can be optimized jointly by matching the drift terms of the true and approximate conditional reverse Stochastic Differential Equations through the following objective,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{NFDM}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\bm{x},\bm{z}_{t})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|f^{B}_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})-\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(5)

which can be shown to be equivalent to minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the posterior distributions resulting from the discretization of
Eqs.

3
and
4

(
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
.

3
Equivariant Neural Diffusion

We now introduce Equivariant Neural Diffusion (END), that generalizes the Equivariant Diffusion Model (EDM)
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
, by defining the forward process through a learnable transformation.
Our approach is a synthesis of Neural Flow Diffusion Models introduced in
Section

2.3
, and leverages ideas of Equivariant Diffusion Model outlined in
Section

2.2
to maintain the desired invariance of the learned marginal distribution
p_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{0})
.
By providing an equivariant learnable transformation
F_{\varphi}
and an equivariant data point predictor
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
, we show that it is possible to obtain a generative model with the desired properties. Finally, we propose a simple yet flexible parameterization meeting these requirements.

3.1
Formulation

The key innovation in Equivariant Neural Diffusion lies in its forward process, which is also leveraged in the reverse (generative) process. The forward process is defined through a learnable time- and data-dependent transformation
F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})
, such that the latent
\bm{z}_{t}
transforms covariantly with the injected noise
\bm{\varepsilon}
(i.e. a collection of random vectors) and the data point
\bm{x}
,

\displaystyle F_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})=\mathbf{R}F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})=\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t},\quad\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3).

We then define
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
as another learnable equivariant function, such that the predicted data point transforms covariantly with the latent variable
\bm{z}_{t}
, i.e.
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t},t)=\mathbf{R}\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
. Finally, we choose the noise and prior distribution, i.e.
p(\bm{\varepsilon})
and
p(\bm{z}_{1})
, to be invariant to the considered symmetry group.

Invariance of the learned distribution
With the following choices: (1)
p(\bm{z}_{1})
an invariant distribution, (2)
F_{\varphi}
an equivariant function that satisfies
F_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})=\mathbf{R}F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})
, and (3)
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
an equivariant function, we have that the learned marginal
p_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{0})
is invariant as desired. This can be shown by demonstrating that the reverse Stochastic Differential Equation is equivariant.
We start by noting that the reverse Stochastic Differential Equation in Equivariant Neural Diffusion is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{d\bm{z}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\mathop{dt}+g_{\varphi}(t)\mathop{d\bar{\bm{w}}}.

As the Wiener process is isotropic, this boils down to verifying that the drift term,
\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
is equivariant, i.e.
\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t},t)=\mathbf{R}\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
. As the drift is expressed as a sum of two terms, we inspect each of them separately. The first term is

\displaystyle f_{\varphi}\big(\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial F_{\varphi}\big(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big)}{\partial t}\Big|_{\bm{\varepsilon}=F_{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t))}.

If
F_{\varphi}
is equivariant, then so is its time-derivative (see
Section

A.3.1
). The same holds for its inverse with respect to
\bm{\varepsilon}
(see
Section

A.3.2
), such that we have
F_{\varphi}^{-1}(\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})=\mathbf{R}F_{\varphi}^{-1}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})=\mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon}
. We additionally have that
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
is equivariant, by definition. As the equivariance of
F_{\varphi}
implies the equivariance of
q_{\varphi}
, from the second term of the drift, we observe that, for
\bm{y}_{t}=\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}\big(\bm{y}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{t},t)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}\big(\bm{z}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\big),\quad\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3).

In summary, in addition to an invariant prior, an equivariant
F_{\varphi}
and an equivariant
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}
ensure the equivariance of the reverse process, and hence the invariance of the learned distribution. In
Section

A.3.3
, we additionally
show that the objective function is invariant, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})=\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\bm{x}),\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
.

We note that alternative formulations for the drift of the reverse process,
\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}
, exist. Most notably, it can be learned directly through an equivariant function, without explicit dependence on
F_{\varphi}
, while maintaining the desired invariance.

3.2
Parameterization

We now introduce a simple parameterization of
F_{\varphi}
that meets the requirements outlined above,

\displaystyle F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)+U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\bm{\varepsilon},

(6)

where, due to the geometric nature of
\bm{x}
,
U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times 3\times 3}
is structured as a block-diagonal matrix where each block is a
3\times 3
matrix, ensuring a cheap calculation of the inverse transformation and its Jacobian. This is equivalent to a diagonal parameterization in the case of scalar features.

Similarly to Equivariant Diffusion Model, our parametrization of
F_{\varphi}
leads to a conditional marginal
q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})
that is a conditional Gaussian with (block-) diagonal covariance, with the notable difference that the mean and covariance are now data- and time-dependent, and learnable through
F_{\varphi}
,

\displaystyle q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{z}_{t}|\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\big),

(7)

where
\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)=U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)U_{\varphi}^{\top}(\bm{x},t)
, such that
\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is also block-diagonal. As
F_{\varphi}
is linear in
\bm{\varepsilon}
, both
\mu_{\varphi}
and
U_{\varphi}
must be equivariant functions whose outputs transform covariantly with
\bm{x}
, in order to ensure the desired equivariance of
F_{\varphi}
,

\displaystyle F_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{x},t)+U_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{x},t)\mathbf{R}\bm{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{R}\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)+\mathbf{R}\big[U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\bm{\varepsilon}\big]=\mathbf{R}F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x}).

We can then readily check that the resulting
q_{\varphi}
is equivariant, as
\forall\ \mathbf{R}\in O(3)
, we have that

\displaystyle q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{z}_{t}|\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\big)=\mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\mathbf{R}\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\mathbf{R}^{\top}\big)=q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x}).

We note that other, and more advanced, parametrizations are possible, e.g. based on normalizing flows with a flow architecture similar to that of
Klein et al. 2024
.

Prior and Reconstruction
While not strictly required, it can be advantageous to parameterize the transformation
F_{\varphi}
such that the prior and reconstruction losses need not be computed. To do so, we design
F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{x},t)
such that the conditional distribution evolves from a low-variance Gaussian centered around the data, i.e.
q(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x})\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{0}|\bm{x},\delta^{2}\mathbb{I})
to an uninformative prior distribution (that contains no information about the data distribution), i.e. a unit Gaussian
q(\bm{z}_{1}|\bm{x})\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{z}_{1}|\bm{0},\mathbb{I})
. Specifically, we parameterize
F_{\varphi}
through the following functions,

\displaystyle\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\bm{x}+t\big(1-t\big)\bar{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),

(8)

\displaystyle U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\big(\delta^{1-t}\bar{\sigma}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)^{t(1-t)}\big)\mathbb{I}+t\big(1-t\big)\bar{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),

(9)

which ensure that (i) in
t=0
,
\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{x}
and
\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},0)=\delta^{2}\mathbb{I}
; (ii) in
t=1
,
\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},1)=\bm{0}
and
\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},1)=\mathbb{I}
; while being unconstrained for
t\in]0,1[
. We note that this is only one possible parametrization for
F_{\varphi}
, and that, by adapting
\mu_{\varphi}
and
U_{\varphi}
, richer priors can easily be leveraged – e.g. harmonic prior given a molecular graph or scale-dependent prior
(
Jing et al. 2023
;
Irwin et al. 2024
)
.

Implementation
In practice,
F_{\varphi}
is implemented as a neural network with an architecture similar to that of the data point predictor
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
, but with a specific readout layer that produces
[\bar{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\bar{\sigma}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\bar{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)]
. The mean output
\bar{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is similar to that of
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
. For
U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
, as
\Sigma_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)=U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)U^{\top}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
should rotate properly,
\bar{\sigma}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is a positive invariant scalar, while
\bar{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is constructed as a matrix whose columns are vectors that transform covariantly with
\bm{x}
.

To ease notation, we introduced all notations in the linear subspace
R
, however in practice we work in the ambient space, i.e.
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 3}
. We detail in
Section

A.5.1
, how working in ambient space is possible. The training and sampling procedures are detailed in
Algorithms

1
and
2
in the appendix.

3.3
Conditional Model

While unconditional generation is a required stepping stone, many practical applications require some form of controllability. As other generative models, Diffusion Models can model conditional distributions
p(\bm{x}|\bm{c})
, where
\bm{c}
is a given condition. Different methods exist for sampling from the conditional distribution, e.g. via guidance
(
Bao et al. 2023
;
Jung et al. 2024
)
or twisting
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
, but the simplest approach consists in training a conditional model on pairs
(\bm{x},\bm{c})
. In such setting,
F_{\varphi}
and
\hat{x}_{\theta}
simply receive an extra input
\bm{c}
representing the conditional information, such that they respectively become
F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x},\bm{c})
, and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{c})
. It is important to note that, compared to conventional Diffusion Models, the forward process of Equivariant Neural Diffusion is now also condition-dependent.

Ablations of Equivariant Neural Diffusion
In addition to baselines from the literature, we compare different ablated versions of Equivariant Neural Diffusion. Dataset and Setup
We perform our experiments on the
QM9
dataset, on
2
different tasks: composition-conditioned and substructure-conditioned generation. Both tasks allow for direct validation with ground-truth properties without requiring expensive Quantum Mechanics calculations, or approximations with surrogate models. In each case, we train a conditional diffusion model as described in
Section

3.3
, i.e. where
F_{\varphi}
and
\hat{x}_{\theta}
are provided with an extra input corresponding to the condition. Additional details are provided in
Section

A.6.4
.

Task 1: composition-conditioned generation
The model is tasked to generate a compound with a predefined composition, i.e. structural isomers of a given formula. The condition is specified as a vector
\bm{c}=(c_{1},...,c_{D})\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, where
c_{d}
denotes the number of atoms of type
d
that the sample should contain. To evaluate the model, we generate
10
samples per target formula, and compute the proportion of samples that match the provided composition. Our results are provided in
Table

3
, where we observe that
cEND
significantly outperforms the baseline, and offers nearly fully controllable composition generation. Additionally, reducing the number of sampling steps has a very limited impact on the controllability. Finally, we perform an ablation whose results are presented in
Table

8
, where fixing the standard deviation is shown to lead to a small decrease in performance, with respect to the full model while remaining significantly better than the baseline with fixed forward.

Task 2: substructure-conditioned generation
We adopt a setup similar to that of
Bao et al. 2023
, and train a conditional Equivariant Neural Diffusion, where the condition is a molecular fingerprint encoding structural information about the molecule.
A fingerprint is a binary vector
\bm{c}=(c_{1},...,c_{F})\in\{0,1\}^{F}
, where
c_{f}
is set to
1
if substructure
f
is present in the molecule, or to
0
if not. Fingerprints are obtained using
OpenBabel

(
O’Boyle et al. 2011
)
.
We evaluate the ability of the compared models to leverage the provided structural information, by sampling conditionally on fingerprints obtained from the test set. We then compute the Tanimoto similarity between the fingerprints yielded by generated molecules and the fingerprints provided as conditional inputs. We compare
cEND
to
EEGSDE

(
Bao et al. 2023
)
, an improved version of
EDM

(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
, that performs conditional generation by combining a conditional diffusion model and regressor guidance. Our results are presented in
Table

4
, along with a handful of samples in
Fig.

2
, where
cEND
is shown to offer better controllability than the compared baselines, as highlighted by the higher similarity.

5
Related Work

The main approaches to molecule generation in
3
D are auto-regressive models
(
Gebauer et al. 2019
;
Simm et al. 2020
;
Gebauer et al. 2022
;
Luo and Ji 2022
;
Daigavane et al. 2024
)
, flow-based models
(
Garcia Satorras et al. 2021
)
, and diffusion models
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
;
Igashov et al. 2024
)
.
A notable exception to the geometric graph representation of
3
D molecules are voxels
(
Skalic et al. 2019
;
Ragoza et al. 2022
;
O Pinheiro et al. 2024
)
, from which the
3
D graph is extracted via some post-processing procedure. Recently, several works have shown that leveraging
2
D connectivity information can lead to improved results
(
Peng et al. 2023
;
Vignac et al. 2023
;
Le et al. 2024
)
. While not incompatible with
END
, we perform our experiments without modeling that auxiliary information, and therefore do not compare to these approaches directly.
Other frameworks have also been tailored to molecule generation, such as Flow Matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2023
;
Irwin et al. 2024
)
or Bayesian Flow Networks
(
Graves et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2024
)
, also showing promises for accelerated sampling.

In the realm of diffusion models for molecules,
EDM-Bridge

(
Wu et al. 2022
)
and
EEGSDE

(
Bao et al. 2023
)
extend upon a continuous-time formulation of Equivariant Diffusion Model
(
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
)
, as Equivariant Neural Diffusion also does. Based on the observation that there exists an infinity of processes mapping from prior to target distributions,
EDM-Bridge
constructs one such process that incorporates some prior knowledge, i.e. part of the drift term is a physically-inspired force term. Equivariant Neural Diffusion can be seen as a generalization of
EDM-Bridge
, where the forward drift term is now learned instead of pre-specified. Through experiments, we show that a learnable forward performs better than a fixed one, even when the latter is physics-inspired.
EEGSDE
specifically targets conditional generation by combining (1) a conditional model score model, (2) a method similar to classifier-guidance (requiring the training of an auxiliary model). In
cEND
, we instead only learn a conditional model.
Finally,
GeoLDM

(
Xu et al. 2023
)
is a latent diffusion model that performs diffusion in the latent space of an equivariant Variational Auto-Encoder, and it can be seen as a particular case of Equivariant Neural Diffusion, where
F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})=\alpha_{t}E_{\varphi}{(\bm{x})}+\bm{\epsilon}\sigma_{t}
, with
E_{\varphi}{(\bm{x})}
denoting the (time-independent) encoder of the Variational Auto-Encoder.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented Equivariant Neural Diffusion (END), a novel diffusion model that is equivariant to Euclidean transformations. The key innovation in Equivariant Neural Diffusion lies in the forward process that is specified by a learnable data- and time-dependent transformation. Experimental results demonstrate the benefits of our method. In the unconditional setting, we show that Equivariant Neural Diffusion yields competitive generative performance across two different benchmarks. In the conditional setting, Equivariant Neural Diffusion offers improved controllability, when conditioning on composition and substructure. Finally, as a by-product of the introduced learnable forward, we also find the sampling efficiency (in terms of integration steps) to be improved, while that property is not actively optimized for in the design of the parameterization nor in the training procedure.

Avenues for future work are numerous. In particular, further leveraging the flexible framework of Neural Flow Diffusion Models
(
Bartosh et al. 2024
)
to constrain the generative trajectories, e.g. to be straight and enable even faster sampling, modelling bond information, or extending the conditional setting to other types of conditioning information, e.g. other point cloud or target property, are all promising directions.

Limitations

From a computational perspective, Equivariant Neural Diffusion is currently slower to train and sample from compared to a baseline with fixed forward with identical number of learnable parameters. Even if convergence is similarly fast, each training (resp. sampling) step incurs a relative
\approx 2.5
x (resp.
\approx 3
x). However, Equivariant Neural Diffusion usually requires much fewer number of function evaluations to achieve comparable (or better) accuracy, and alternative (and more efficient) parameterizations of the reverse process exist. In particular, the drift
\hat{f}_{\theta,\varphi}

could be learned without direct dependence on
f_{\varphi}
, thereby leading to an improved training time and, more importantly, a very limited overhead with respect to vanilla Diffusion Models for sampling. In terms of scalability, Equivariant Neural Diffusion suffers from limitations similar to concurrent approaches. It operates on fully-connected graphs – preventing its scaling to very large graphs, and models categorical features through an arbitrary continuous relaxation – potentially suboptimal and scaling linearly with the number of chemical elements in the modeled dataset. Encodings that scale more gracefully, such as that of Analog Bits
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
(logarithmic) or GeoLDM
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
(learned low-dimensional), are good candidates to better deal with discrete features within END. In terms of data, the presented findings are limited to organic molecules and the metrics, while widely used in the community, also have some evident limitations. To fully assess the practical interest of the generated molecules, thorough validation with Quantum Mechanics simulations would be required.

Broader Impact
Generative models for molecules have the potential to accelerate
in-silico
discovery and design of drugs or materials. This work proposes an instance of such model. As any generative model, it also comes with potential dangers, as this could be misused for designing e.g. chemicals with adversarial properties.

A.3
Equivariance / invariance proofs

A.3.1
Time-derivative of an O(3)-equivariant function

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be a function that is equivariant to actions of the group
O(3)
, such that
\bm{R}\cdot f(\bm{x},t)=f(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x},t),\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
.

Proof sketch

We need to show that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(f(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x},t)\big)=\bm{R}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(f(\bm{x},t)\big),

\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
and
\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(f(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x},t)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\bm{R}\cdot f(\bm{x},t)\big),

\displaystyle=\bm{R}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(f(\bm{x},t)\big),

where the last equality follows by linearity.

A.3.2
Inverse of an O(3)-equivariant function

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be a function that (1) is equivariant to the action of the group
O(3)
, and (2) admits an inverse
f^{-1}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, then
f^{-1}
is also equivariant to the action of
O(3)
.

Proof sketch

We need to show that
f^{-1}\big(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{y}\big)=\bm{R}\cdot f^{-1}(\bm{y}),

\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
and
\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Since
f
is invertible, we have that to any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
corresponds a unique
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, such that
\bm{y}=f(\bm{x})
, and that
f^{-1}(\bm{y})=f^{-1}(f(\bm{x}))=\bm{x}
.
As
f
is equivariant to the action of
O(3)
, we have that
\bm{R}\cdot f(\bm{x})=f(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x}),\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
:

\displaystyle f^{-1}\big(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{y}\big)

\displaystyle=f^{-1}\big(\bm{R}\cdot f(\bm{x})\big),

\displaystyle=f^{-1}\big(f(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x})\big),

\displaystyle=\bm{R}\cdot\bm{x},

\displaystyle=\bm{R}\cdot f^{-1}(\bm{y}).

A.3.3
O(3)-invariance of the objective function

In this section, we show that the objective function is invariant under the action of
O(3)
:
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})=\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\bm{x}),\forall\bm{R}\in O(3)
, provided that
F_{\varphi}
and
\hat{x}_{\theta}
are equivariant.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})q(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-f_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t))+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t))\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})q(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t,\mathbf{R}\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t))+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t))\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\bm{z}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})q(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|\mathbf{R}f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\mathbf{R}f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t))+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\bm{z}_{t},t))\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\mathbf{R}\bm{y}_{t}|\mathbf{R}\bm{x})q(\mathbf{R}\bm{x})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|\mathbf{R}f_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t},t,\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\mathbf{R}\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\mathbf{R}f_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{t},t))+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\mathbf{R}\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{t},t))\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u(t),q_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{x})q(\bm{x})}\Big[\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}\big|\big|f_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t},t,\bm{x})-\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-f_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t},t,\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{t},t))+\frac{g^{2}_{\varphi}(t)}{2}\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log q_{\varphi}(\bm{y}_{t}|\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{t},t))\big|\big|_{2}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{END}}(\bm{x}).

The first equality is obtained by replacing
\bm{x}
by
\mathbf{R}\bm{x}
in the definition of the objective function
Eq.

.
The second is obtained by multiplying by

. The third equality by leveraging that

and
\hat{x}_{\theta}
are equivariant. We then perform a change of variable

As rotation does preserve distances, we obtain the last equality.

A.5
Details about

A.5.1
Working in ambient space

In this section, we omit the invariant features, and consider that
\bm{x},\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
are all collections of vectors.

In opposition to the notations introduced in
Section

3
, we would like
F_{\varphi}
and
\hat{x}_{\theta}
(which ultimately are neural networks) to operate in ambient space directly, i.e. on
\bm{z}_{t},\bm{x},\bm{\varepsilon}\in R
with
R=\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}:\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{0}\}
, instead of
\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)\times d}
as initially presented. To do so, we use the results from
Garcia Satorras et al. 2021
that showed that the Jacobian of the transformation
F_{\varphi}
can be computed in ambient space, for
\bm{z}_{t}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}
that live in the linear subspace
R
, provided that the transformation
F_{\varphi}
(invertible with respect to
\bm{\varepsilon}
) leaves the center of mass of
\bm{\varepsilon}
unchanged.

Considering flat representations of
\bm{z}_{t},\bm{x},\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\cdot d}
, the parameterization of
F_{\varphi}
introduced in
Eqs.

6
,
8
and
9
can be adapted as follows to achieve such property

\displaystyle F_{\varphi}(\bm{\varepsilon},t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)+U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\bm{\varepsilon},

(10)

\displaystyle\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top})\tilde{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

(11)

\displaystyle U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top})\tilde{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)+\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top},

(12)

where
T\in\mathbb{R}^{M\cdot d\times d}=[\mathbb{I}_{d},\mathbb{I}_{d},...]^{\top}
, and
\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top}
corresponds to the linear operator computing the center of mass. In
Eq.

11
, the unconstrained mean output
\tilde{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is simply projected onto the
0
-CoM subspace, thereby inducing no translation. In
Eq.

12
, the unconstrained output of
\tilde{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
is first projected onto the
0
-CoM subspace, before being translated back to the initial center of mass.

With this adapted formulation, we need to be able to (1) compute the latent variable
\bm{z}_{t}
given
\bm{\varepsilon}
, (2) evaluate the Jacobian of the transformation
F_{\varphi}
, and (3) evaluate the inverse transformation
F_{\varphi}^{-1}
.

Computing
\bm{z}_{t}
from
\bm{\varepsilon}

Given that
\bm{\varepsilon}\in R
, obtaining
\bm{z}_{t}\in R
from
\bm{\varepsilon}
simply amounts to (1) computing
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=\tilde{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)+\tilde{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\bm{\varepsilon}
, and then (2) removing the center of mass from
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{t}
– the second term in
Eq.

12
induces no translation as
\bm{\varepsilon}\in R
.

Computing
|J_{F_{\varphi}}|
and
F_{\varphi}^{-1}

In what follows, we shorten the notation, and denote
U_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
by
U
and
\tilde{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
by
\tilde{U}
.
To leverage known identities, we start by reorganizing
Eq.

12
, as

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}+\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top}(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-\tilde{U}).

(13)

The Jacobian of
F_{\varphi}
is given by the determinant of
U
, and the latter can be derived by leveraging the Matrix Determinant Lemma,

\displaystyle\det U

\displaystyle=\det\tilde{U}\cdot\det\big(\mathbb{I}_{d}+\frac{1}{M}T^{\top}(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-\tilde{U})\tilde{U}^{-1}T\big),

(14)

\displaystyle=\det\tilde{U}\cdot\det\big(\mathbb{I}_{d}+\frac{1}{M}T^{\top}(\tilde{U}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d})T\big),

(15)

\displaystyle=\det\tilde{U}\cdot\det\big(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\tilde{U}^{m})^{-1}\big),

(16)

\displaystyle=\det\tilde{U}\cdot\det V,

(17)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\det\tilde{U}^{m}\cdot\det V,

(18)

where
\tilde{U}^{m}
denotes the
m
-th
d\times d
-block in
\tilde{U}
, and
V=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\tilde{U}^{m})^{-1}
is a
d\times d
-matrix. Computing the Jacobian therefore amounts to compute the inverse of
M

d\times d
-matrices and the determinant of
M+1

d\times d
-matrices. In practice,
d=3
such that all computations can be performed in closed-form.

Regarding
F_{\varphi}^{-1}
, we do not need to evaluate the inverse transformation itself, but instead evaluate
\bm{\varepsilon}
given
\bm{z}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=U^{-1}\big(\bm{z}_{t}-\mu(\bm{x},t)\big).

The inverse
U^{-1}
can be obtained via the Woodbury matrix identity,

\displaystyle U^{-1}

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{U}+\frac{1}{M}TT^{\top}(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-\tilde{U})\big)^{-1},

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}^{-1}-\frac{1}{M}\tilde{U}^{-1}TV^{-1}T^{\top}(\tilde{U}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}),

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}^{-1}(\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d}-C),

where
V=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\tilde{U}^{m})^{-1}
as previously defined in
Eq.

18
, and
C=\frac{1}{M}TV^{-1}T^{\top}(\tilde{U}^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{M\cdot d})
. Given the specific structure of
C
, the computation of
\bm{\varepsilon}
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=U^{-1}\bar{\bm{z}}_{t},

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-C)\bar{\bm{z}}_{t},

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}^{-1}\big[\bar{\bm{z}}_{t}-\bm{c}\big],

where
\bar{\bm{z}}=\big(\bm{z}_{t}-\mu(\bm{x},t)\big)
and
\bm{c}=C\bar{\bm{z}}_{t}
acts as a translation operator. We note that computing the inverse transformation requires to invert
M+1

d\times d
-matrices, but as
d=3
in practice, all computations can be performed in closed-form.

A.5.2
Invariant features

For simplicity, we omitted in
Sections

3
and
A.5.1
that molecules are described as tuples:
\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\bm{h})
, as only
\bm{r}
transform under Euclidean transformations. For the invariant features
\bm{h}
, we use the following parameterization

\displaystyle\mu^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=(1-t)\bm{h}+t\big(1-t\big)\bar{\mu}^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),

(19)

\displaystyle\sigma^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\delta^{1-t}\bar{\sigma}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)^{t(1-t)}.

(20)

which ensures that
\mu^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{h}
and
\sigma^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},0)=\delta
; whereas
\mu^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},1)=\bm{0}
and
\sigma^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},1)=\mathbb{I}
.

Implementation

As described in the main text in
Section

3.2
,
F_{\varphi}
is implemented as a neural network with an architecture similar to that of the data point predictor
\hat{\bm{x}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
, but with a specific readout layer that produces the outputs related to
\bm{r}
(
[\bar{\mu}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),\bar{\sigma}_{\varphi},\bar{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)]
). Additionally, it produces
\bar{\mu}^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
and
\bar{\sigma}^{(h)}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
as invariant outputs.

Inverse transformation

The logarithm of the determinant of the inverse transformation
\log|J_{F}^{-1}|
writes

\displaystyle\log|J_{F}^{-1}|=-\log|J_{F}|=-\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{M\times D}\sigma^{(h),i}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)}_{\text{invariant features}}-\underbrace{\sum_{i=m}^{M}\log\big|\det(U^{m}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t))\big|-\log\det|V|}_{\text{vectorial features}},

(21)

where
V
is defined as in
Eq.

18
.

A.6
Experimental details

In addition to the details provided in this section, we release a
public code repository
with our implementation of Equivariant Neural Diffusion.

A.6.1
Evaluation metrics

In this section, we describe the metrics employed to evaluate the different models:

•

Stability:
An atom is deemed stable if it has a charge of
0
, whereas a molecule is stable if all its atoms have
0
charge. We reuse the lookup table from
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
to infer bond types from pairwise distances.

•

Validity and Connectivity:
Validity corresponds to the percentage of samples that can be parsed and sanitized by
rdkit

[
Landrum et al. 2013
]
, after inference of the bonds using the lookup table mechanism
[
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
]
. It should be noted that the metric does not penalize fragmented samples as long as each individual fragment appears valid. This can be problematic when running evaluation on larger compounds such as those in
GEOM-Drugs
, as models tend to generate disconnected structures. To account for that, we also report Connectivity, which simply checks that a valid molecule is composed of exactly one fragment.

•

Uniqueness:
Uniqueness is expressed as the proportion of samples that are valid and have a unique
SMILES
string
[
Weininger 1988
]
among all the generated samples. On
GEOM-Drugs
, we do not report Uniqueness as all generated samples appear unique (as per previous work).

•

Total variation:
The total variation is computed as the
MAE
between the (discrete) marginal obtained on the training data and on the generated samples. For bond types on
QM9
, we compute the ground truth and generated distributions using the lookup table mechanism
[
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
]
.

•

Strain Energy:
The strain energy is expressed as the difference in energy between generated structures and a relaxed version thereof obtained as per
rdkit
’s
MMFF

[
Landrum et al. 2013
]
. From the generated samples, we infer
rdkit

mol
objects using
OpenBabel

[
O’Boyle et al. 2011
]
. We only evaluate the strain energy of valid and connected samples.

•

SA, QED,
\log P
, and MW:
SA denotes the "Synthetic Accessibility Score", which is a rule-based scoring function that evaluates the complexity of synthesizing a structure by organic reactions
[
Ertl and Schuffenhauer 2009
]
. We normalized its values between 0 and 1, with 0 being “difficult to synthesize” and 1 “easy to synthesize”. QED denotes "Quantitative Estimation of Drug-likeness".
\log P
denotes the octanol-water partition coefficient. MW denotes the molecular weight.

We employ the
rdkit
’s implementation of all metrics. To do so, we convert the generated samples to
rdkit

mol
objects using
OpenBabel

[
O’Boyle et al. 2011
]
. We then only evaluate the different metrics for valid and connected samples.

•

MMD:
On
QM9
, we follow the procedure of
Daigavane et al. 2024
, and compute the
MMD

Gretton et al. 2012
between true and generated pairwise distances distributions for the
10
most common bonds in the dataset: ["C-H:1.0", "C-C:1.0", "C-O:1.0", "C-N:1.0", "H-N:1.0", "C-O:2.0", "C-N:1.5", "H-O:1.0", "C-C:1.5", "C-N:2.0"].

A.6.2
Architecture

Our forward transformation
F_{\varphi}
and data point predictor
\hat{x}_{\theta}
share a common neural network architecture that we detail here.
The architecture is similar to that of
EQCAT

[
Le et al. 2022
]
, and updates a collection of invariant and equivariant features for each node in the graph. We choose that architecture because it allows for easy construction of
\bar{U}_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)
by linear projection of the final equivariant layer.

We follow previous work
[
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
]
and consider fully-connected graphs. We initially featurize pairwise distances through Gaussian Radial Basis functions, with dataset-specific cutoff taken large enough to ensure full connectivity.
In opposition to
Hoogeboom et al. 2022
, we do not update positions in the message-passing phase, but instead obtain the positions prediction through a linear projection of the final equivariant hidden states. The predictions for the invariant features are obtained by reading out the final invariant hidden states.

Optimization

For all model variants, we employ Adam with a learning rate of
10^{-4}
. We perform gradient clipping (norm) with a value of
10
on
QM9
, and a value of
1
on
GEOM-Drugs
.

.

A.6.4
Conditional generation

We use
10
layers of message passing for
EDM*
, while the variants of Equivariant Neural Diffusion feature
5
layers of message-passing in
F_{\varphi}
and
5
in
\hat{x}_{\theta}
. The hidden size of the invariant and equivariant features is set to
192

, along with an RBF expansion of dimension
64
with a cutoff of
10
Å for pairwise distances. We train all models for
1000
epochs with a batch size of
64
.

After an initial encoding, the conditional information is introduced at the end of each message passing step, and alters the scalar hidden states through a one-layer
MLP
, that shares the same dimension as the hidden scalar state.

Composition-conditioned generation

The encoding of the condition follows that of
Gebauer et al. 2022
. Each atom type gets its own embedding (of dimension
64
), weighted by the proportion it represents in the provided formula. The weighted embeddings of all atom types are then concatenated and flattened, and the obtained vector (of dimension
64\times\text{number of atom types}
) is processed through a
2
-layer
MLP
with
64
hidden units.

The compositions used at sampling time are extracted from the validation and test sets. For each unique formula, the model gets to generate
10
samples. The reported matching rate refers to the % of generated samples featuring the prompted composition.

Substructure-conditioned generation

The encoding of the condition follows that of
Bao et al. 2023
. Each molecule is first converted to an
OpenBabel
object
[
O’Boyle et al. 2011
]
(solely based on positions and atom types), from which a fingerprint is in turn calculated. The obtained
1024
-dimensional fingerprint is simply processed by a
2-
layer
MLP
with hidden dimensions
[512,256]
, and a final linear projection to
192
, i.e. the hidden size of the invariant features.

The model is evaluated by computing the Tanimoto similarity between the fingerprints obtained from the generated samples and the fingerprints provided as conditional inputs.
