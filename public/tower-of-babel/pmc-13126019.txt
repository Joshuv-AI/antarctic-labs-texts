Improving patient treatment accuracy using transit dosimetry with Electronic Portal Imaging Device images and deep learning☆
Authors: Lorenzo Marini, Carlotta Mozzi, Michele Avanzo, Francesca Lizzi, Icro Meattini, Giovanni Pirrone, Alessandra Retico, Emmanuel Uwitonze, Aafke Christine Kraan, Cinzia Talamonti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and Purpose:Electronic portal imaging devices (EPID) can be utilized for transit in vivo dosimetry (IVD). A major limitation of standard amorphous Silicon EPID detectors for usage IVD is their non-linear detector response to water equivalent dose. Labor-intensive calibration and correction procedures are needed, limiting their practicality for routine clinical use. The purpose of this work was to develop a deep learning (DL) framework, based on data acquisitions and treatment planning system (TPS) dose simulations, which reconstructed water equivalent dose from transit EPID images.Materials and methods:A database of over 200 EPID measurements and corresponding portal dose simulations from the Monaco TPS was created, employing different phantoms and irradiation fields. A 2D U-Net model was developed that transforms EPID transit images into water-equivalent portal dose images. The performance was evaluated through the mean absolute error (MAE) and 2D γ-index analysis with a 3%/3 mm and 5%/5 mm criterion.Results:The mean MAE value over all test cases was 3.8×10−3 cGy. The mean γ-index passing rates were 94.2 ±2.1% and 99.3 ±0.3% for the 3%/3 mm and 5%/5 mm criteria, respectively. Median passing rates were 98.3% for the 3%/3 mm and 100% for the 5%/5 mm criterion, respectively, demonstrating high accuracy in reconstructing portal dose from EPID. The predictions were completed within 1 s.Conclusions:Our DL framework enabled fast and accurate reconstruction of portal dose from transit EPID images. Once trained on clinical data, it may reduce complex corrections, supporting safe and effective treatments that meet modern regulatory standards.Keywords: EPID, Transit dosimetry, Radiotherapy, Deep learning, U-net

Introduction

Electronic Portal Imaging Devices (EPIDs) have been used in various ways in radiotherapy, including pre-treatment quality assurance procedures and in vivo (transit) dosimetry for advanced dose delivery techniques like IMRT and VMAT [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. In vivo dosimetry (IVD) with EPID is recognized as an efficient technique to improve patient safety and treatment accuracy [3], [6], [7], [8]. EPID IVD strategies are broadly categorized into forward and back-projection approaches [3], [9]. The back-projection (or in-patient) method allows for 3D in-patient dose reconstruction, but is intrinsically complex [3], making it less suited for real time error detection [10]. The forward method is more straightforward, as it compares acquired grayscale EPID images to expectations, either directly or after conversion to water equivalent dose at EPID level (portal dose). This has the advantage that direct dose comparison with predictions from a Treatment Planning System (TPS) or Monte Carlo (MC) code is possible, facilitating a timely detection of major treatment errors, such as machine or patient setup errors [3].

The most commonly used type of EPID is the amorphous silicon photodiode (a-Si) flat-panel detector, known for high resolution imaging and rapid acquisition [11]. However, their non-linear responses with water equivalent dose requires labor-intensive and time-consuming measurements involving other dosimeters [12], [13]. Research efforts have been ongoing to create more water-equivalent EPIDs, for example by replacing the metal screen with solid water [14], [15] or plastic scintillators [16], but these designs were not yet clinically adopted. Several academic and commercial frameworks support EPID dose analysis for detection of treatment errors [3]. However, they are often complex and costly, and full integration into routine clinical quality control remains a challenge.

Recently, Artificial Intelligence, particularly Deep Learning (DL), has emerged as promising technique in IVD [4], [17]. DL approaches have been proposed for both planar [18] and 3D back-projection dose reconstruction using EPID data [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], though most rely on simulated data. For forward dosimetry, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been developed to convert EPID gray scale images into water-equivalent dose, but for non-transit scenarios [24]. In one study, an AI model was trained using Monte Carlo simulations and phantom measurements to predict portal dose from transit EPID data, with encouraging results [25]. Given the importance of IVD for treatment quality assurance [2], [3], [4], the worldwide growing adoption of AI in radiotherapy [26], [27], [28], [29], and the stringent European guidelines regarding IVD [30], it is timely to develop accessible and accurate AI-driven frameworks to support and streamline portal dose comparisons.

The goal of this study was to develop an AI-based forward transit dosimetry model that converts grayscale EPID images into water-equivalent portal dose images, Our model removed the need for time-consuming calibrations and thereby supported real-time in vivo dose monitoring. To ensure realism and model generalizability, the model was trained on measured data and TPS predictions of a diverse set of phantoms.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

For developing the DL model we acquired 213 EPID images during the irradiation of 11 types of phantoms, including solid water phantoms (Solid Water HE, Sun Nuclear, Norfolk, VA, USA) with thicknesses of 10, 20, and 30 cm, an anthropomorphic CIRS phantom (CIRS, Norfolk, VA, USA) representing the thoracic region, and a Multiplug phantom (Sun Nuclear, Melbourne, FL, USA) with inserts of titanium, bone, lung, solid water and air. In addition, inhomogeneous slab phantoms were utilized, featuring variations such as air inserts, different bone inserts, and a lung insert. For each phantom, various treatment plans were administered using 6 MV photon beams from a VersaHD Elekta Linac at Careggi University Hospital in Florence. Data were acquired at four Linacs that had been calibrated to provide identical dose delivery. Each Linac was equipped with its own EPID panel, calibrated to provide the same response. The Elekta iViewGT software (version 3.4.1) was used for image acquisition, and the EPID images were 40cm×40cm, with a pixel size of 0.4mm×0.4mm. Each treatment plan included different beam geometries: square (2cm×2cm, 5cm×5cm, 10cm×10cm, and 20cm×20cm), rectangular (1cm×15cm, 2cm×10cm, 4cm×20cm, 15cm×1cm, 10cm×2cm, 20cm×4cm) and also circular and elliptical beams, delivered with 50, 100, 200, 300 and 500 Monitoring Units (MU). The plans were executed in single exposure mode, with gantry angles of 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. The acquired EPID images were referred to as EPIDraw.

Portal dose simulations

In order to obtain a corresponding water equivalent dose image at EPID level (portal dose, PD) for all data acquisitions dose simulations were performed using the Monaco (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) TPS. A research version of the software was utilized, featuring an MC algorithm with uncertainty of 0.2%. The EPID panel was modeled to have a water equivalent thickness of 4.4 cm. To place the scoring layer 60 cm from the isocenter, the detection area was extended beyond the CT. For the other two dimensions, the same field of view (FOV) as the CT was used. The dose plane layer was simulated with a pixel size of black 1mm×1mm. In this way we obtained for each acquisition a corresponding simulated portal dose image from the TPS, PDTPS. The simulations were validated by comparison with Gafchromics EBT3 films [31], [32] and the MapCheck2 detector array. Films were digitized using an EPSON scanner. The agreement between simulations and film measurements, as well as between simulations and MapCheck2 data, was satisfactory. In the former case, a global gamma passing rate above 90% was observed using 5%/5 mm criteria with a 10% threshold. In the latter, the global gamma passing rate reached 100% for 5%/5 mm (10% threshold) and 98.8% for 3%/3 mm (10% threshold).

Deep learning model

We implemented a 2D U-Net [34], aiming at mapping the normalized EPIDprocessed images into normalized PDTPS images. The network was developed using TensorFlow (v2.16.2) and Keras [35]. The architecture (Supplementary Material S1) followed a symmetric encoder–decoder scheme with skip connections between mirrored layers to ensure the retention of fine-grained spatial information. The encoder consisted of four consecutive blocks, each composed of two 2D convolutional layers with kernel size 3 × 3, followed by ReLU activations. Starting from the second block, batch normalization layers were included after each convolution [36]. Max pooling layers with stride 2 × 2 were used to progressively reduce spatial resolution. The number of filters was doubled at each downsampling step in the encoder, and halved symmetrically in the decoder. At the bottleneck, two convolutional layers with the highest number of filters were applied, each followed by batch normalization and ReLU. The decoder mirrored the encoder structure. At the end, a final 1 × 1 convolution with linear activation was used to output a single-channel dose map. The model accepted inputs of shape (256 × 256 × 1) and produced outputs of the same dimensions.

Training and hyperparameter optimization

The dataset was approximately split into a training/validation (90%) and a test (10%) set. The model was trained using a 10 times repeated k-fold cross-validation (CV) approach (k = 5) and, hence, the training/validation set was cyclically divided into 80% (training) and 20% (validation), as shown in Fig. 2. For the training samples, a custom data augmentation strategy was implemented to increase variability and reduce overfitting. Each training sample (paired normalized EPIDprocessed and PDTPS images) was consistently transformed using rotations (90°, 180°, and 270°) and horizontal and vertical flips. We used the RMSprop optimizer with a scheduled learning rate, with patience set to 8 epochs and a minimum learning rate of 5×10−6. Early stopping was not applied; instead, the model weights were saved at the best-performing checkpoints based on validation MAE. To identify the optimal set of hyperparameters, a grid search was performed. The parameters explored were number of filters (8, 16) of the first convolutional layer, learning rate (1×10−2, 1×10−3, 1×10−4) and batch size (4, 8, 16, 32). Each configuration was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, repeated for 10 independent runs (see Section 2.6). Overall, this resulted in 240 training sessions (2×3×4=24 configurations ×10 runs). The training was performed on a NVIDIA V100 GPU hosted on a machine with 2 CPUs Intel Xeon E5-2640v and 64 GB of RAM.

Ensemble inference

The trained DL models were evaluated on an independent test set, consisting of 22 images. The test set was obtained from the entire data sample according to a stratified splitting strategy. To reproduce the heterogeneity of the training set, at least one representative case for each class of samples used during training was included. The output produced by each individual DL model was de-normalized, yielding the portal dose image in physical units (cGy).

To obtain the final portal dose prediction, an ensemble learning strategy was employed [37]. For each EPID image, outputs from the five models in the 5-fold cross-validation (PDDL,m) were averaged pixel-wise. This process was repeated 10 times with different data splits to assess variability, and the final prediction PDDL was computed as the pixel-wise average of these 10 results (Fig. 2).

Performance evaluation

The linearity of the output of DL model with delivered dose was verified by comparing the reconstructed portal dose from EPID images acquired with different MUs and evaluating the R2 values. The final performance of the model was evaluated on the test set of 22 pairs of EPIDraw and PDTPS images. The γ-index [38], [39] was used to compare the individual model predictions (from each training repetition), PDDL,m, as well as the final output of the model, PDDL, with PDTPS. We calculated the γ-passing rate, ΓPR, as the ratio between the number of pixels with a γ-index below 1, Nγ<1, and the total number of pixels, Ntot, using for both a threshold of 10% of the maximum dose in the reference image (PDTPS):

In the evaluation of the ΓPR we used DT/DTA criteria of 2%/2 mm, 3%/2 mm, 3%/3 mm, 4%/3 mm, 4%/4 mm, 5%/3 mm, 5%/4 mm, and 5%/5 mm [3].

Results

On average, training a single model (150 epochs, batch size = 8, k-fold CV with k = 5) required approximately 40 min, leading to a total training time of approximately 120 h (≈5 days) for the entire grid search on a machine with a single NVIDIA V100 GPU. The optimal hyperparameter configuration was identified based on the average validation MAE across all folds and repetitions. It had 16 initial filters, learning rate = 0.001, and batch size = 8 and achieved an average validation MSE of 1.20×10−4 and MAE of 4.96×10−3 over all folds and repetitions. The train and validation metrics were consistently close across the different runs, suggesting good generalization and low risk of overfitting.

Fig. 3 shows the learning curves obtained in a representative training run, where it is evident that in few tens of epochs both metrics reached the plateau, with a loss lower than 10−5 and a MAE around 10−3. The output of the model, PDDL, was verified to be linear with delivered dose for a given sample, with R2 values of the mean portal dose against MUs larger than 0.99. The time it took to predict PDDL from a raw EPID image was less than 1 s. Thus, the inference phase was significantly less resource-intensive compared to the training phase, which was a one-time process.

Discussion

We developed a deep learning–based forward transit dosimetry model that converts grayscale EPID images into water-equivalent portal dose distributions from a single frame. The U-Net, trained on TPS predictions and EPID measurements of a diverse set of phantoms, predicted portal dose in near real time and removed the need for complex calibration and correction steps, such as calibration matrices, flood-field corrections, kernel deconvolution, and back-projection algorithms.

Our work differs from previously published studies on in vivo dose reconstruction using AI methods in several key ways. First, our model was developed using measured EPID data and simulations that were rigorously validated against experimental measurements [31], [32]. This ensured training data that closely reflect real clinical conditions. In contrast, many existing studies in this area relied solely on simulated data [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [24]. Second, our model was based on forward transit dosimetry at the EPID level, offering a direct and robust method for verifying the patient exit dose. This approach was simpler and more direct than back-projection methods employed in recent DL studies [18], [19], [20], [21], that relied on complex computational algorithms, making it harder to implement them into automated analysis frameworks. It also showed higher sensitivity for error detection in some geometries [5]. Third, we employed a different U-Net architecture and training strategy with respect to the aforementioned works. Specifically, we performed a grid search to optimize the training hyperparameter of the U-Net and applied a rigorous CV scheme. Fourth, our model enabled direct comparison with portal dose predictions from the treatment planning system (TPS), unlike the approach used by Zhang et al. [25], who combined independently performed Monte Carlo simulations with measured data. Using TPS predictions supports easier clinical integration, as these predictions can be made readily available in routine workflows. Finally, the model was trained and evaluated on diverse phantoms and field configurations, improving generalizability compared to prior studies.

The model achieved high accuracy, with a mean ΓPR of 94.2% (3%/3 mm), and even higher median values, showing consistent performance across most test cases. Two test cases had a gamma-passing rate below 80% (Supplementary Material Figs. S5, S6). In both cases, the model captured the shape well, but dose deviations up to 7% were seen. This was likely caused by the limited size of the training/validation dataset (191 images) and low representation of these scenarios (five images for the 31 cm solid water phantom and two for Multiplug with PMMA with gantry angle 90°). It is well known that field and phantom dependent factors such as scatter effects, beam spectrum changes, field size, phantom thickness, and inhomogeneities lead to a complex and non-unique relationship between portal dose and EPID pixel values [3], [9]. While the U-Net could in principle learn these dependencies from the EPID images themselves, this required a larger and more diverse dataset.

A limitation of our study was the relatively small dataset, comprising 213 images, which is modest for deep learning applications. While data augmentation was applied to enhance variability and reduce overfitting, additional data were needed to ensure comprehensive representation of the different phantoms and irradiation fields across the training, validation, and test sets. Another limitation was that our model cannot be automatically generalized to other dose delivery systems. In particular, a change of vendor or beam energy would imply a different EPID detector and need to retrain the model. On the other hand, we expect our DL model to generalize to Linacs of the same model at the same energy, as they share the same EPID model. Beam output is not expected to change among Linacs, as it is usually the same but should be checked.

Future work includes the collection of datasets with irregular fields and clinical treatment plans. Research is ongoing to investigate the integration of portal dose predictions from clinical IMRT and VMAT plans into the TPS simulations. At presence, we used single-frame acquisitions from regularly shaped fields. Applying our model to real IMRT or VMAT plans requires multi-frame acquisition, which is currently not supported by the iView EPID image software. We also plan to extend the 2D portal dose reconstruction model to a 3D dose reconstruction in the patient, which was recently demonstrated in simulations to be feasible [19], [20], [21], [22]. This will also facilitate comparisons with commercial EPID IVD frameworks.

In conclusion, we developed a DL-based forward transit dosimetry framework, that mapped raw grayscale EPID images to water-equivalent portal dose distributions using a single-frame acquisition protocol. The model achieved high accuracy: mean ΓPR for the 3%/3mm and 5%/5mm criterion were of 94.2% and 99.3%, respectively, and median passing rates were 98.3% for the 3%/3mm and 100% for the 5%/5mm criterion, respectively. Predicted portal dose distribution could be generated almost instantaneously. Relying on TPS predictions and in-house EPID data, integration into clinical routine should be relatively straightforward. Once trained on clinical data, the method can reduce complex corrections and support safe and effective treatments that meet modern regulatory standards.
