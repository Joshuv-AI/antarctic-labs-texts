The association between social media use and eating disorders in young adults: a systematic review
Authors: Viktorie Fomínová, Martina Sebalo Vňuková, Martin Anders, Eva Kitzlerová, Eva Trávníčková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial media has become an integral part of everyday life and has been suggested as a contributing factor in the development and maintenance of eating disorders (EDs). This systematic review aimed to summarise current evidence on the association between social media use and eating disorders in young adults aged 18–30 years. Only studies in which the authors explicitly declare that they focus on eating disorders as diagnostic entities in relation to social media use were included. A systematic literature search was performed in March 2025 using Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed. In total, 630 records were identified, of which seven studies met the inclusion criteria. Most studies employed cross-sectional designs, with one experimental study. Six out of seven studies reported a positive association between social media use and eating disorder symptomatology or diagnoses, while one study reported no significant association between social media use and eating disorder outcomes. Evidence consistently pointed to the role of appearance-focused content (e.g., thinspiration, fitspiration) and problematic social media use as factors associated with greater symptom severity and related psychological outcomes. Overall, social media use may represent a relevant factor in the development and maintenance of eating disorders in young adults. The findings may contribute to understanding potential risk and protective factors and inform future research directions. However, given the limited number and heterogeneity of studies, further research is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01653-3.Keywords: Social media, Young adults, Eating disorders, Systematic review

Introduction

The global adoption of social media (SM) continues to rise, with the latest data indicating that the number of active user identities has reached 5.24 billion worldwide [21]. This rapid growth is also evident among young adults, who represent one of the most active user groups [26].

Social media has become an integral part of daily life, impacting mental health in both positive and negative ways [22, 33], most notably in relation to depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as broader indicators of psychological well-being [33, 37, 40]. However, comparatively less attention has been paid to the potential role of social media use in eating disorders (EDs). Eating disorders are defined as diagnostic entities within established classification systems such as the DSM-5 and ICD-11, including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, and other specified eating disorders (OSFED; [1, 43].

Some researchers suggest that social media use and social media addiction might be significant determinants in the development and maintenance of eating disorders [29]. Social media algorithms typically expose users to content similar to what they have previously engaged with, which may contribute to eating disorders by repeatedly exposing users to unrealistic stimuli and reinforcing comparison with the “ideal” [5].

Social media is not inherently problematic, and its effects appear to depend on how individuals engage with it; therefore, understanding which specific aspects of use may have negative as well as positive consequences is crucial [41]. Previous research has identified several potential risk factors associated with social media use that may be related to eating disorders. In particular, the systematic review by Padín et al., [30] has shown that greater time spent on social media and specific forms of interaction (e.g., photo sharing, appearance-related feedback, and social comparison) are associated with eating disorder psychopathology. However, evidence also indicates that these associations may be driven less by overall time spent on social media and more by specific types of content and appearance-focused engagement [5]. Studies have also highlighted the potential risk associated with broader patterns of digital media use, including general problematic internet use [18]; however, these findings relate to internet use more broadly rather than to social media specifically.

Although eating disorders most commonly emerge during adolescence (ages 10–19; [17]), symptoms may also develop later [32], and the disorders frequently follow a prolonged course, persisting into young adulthood where they often require clinical attention [6, 28]. This developmental trajectory may partly explain why existing research on the relationship between social media use and eating disorders has predominantly focused on adolescent populations. However, young adults—despite being among the most active users of social media [26]—have received comparatively limited attention. This represents an important gap, as the developmental context of young adulthood may shape both patterns of social media use and vulnerability to eating disorder psychopathology.

A further limitation becomes apparent when considering how eating-related outcomes have been conceptualised in previous research and reviews. Many studies, including systematic reviews, have examined a broad range of eating-related constructs, such as body dissatisfaction, disordered eating, or drive for thinness, rather than clinically defined eating disorders (e.g., [19], [34]). While these constructs are highly relevant, particularly from a prevention perspective, they do not necessarily reflect clinically significant psychopathology. The lack of clear differentiation between subclinical phenomena and formally defined eating disorders may therefore limit the interpretability and clinical applicability of findings.

This limitation is also reflected in previous reviews addressing the relationship between social media use and eating-related outcomes. For example, the systematic review by Padín et al., [30] included a broader range of constructs beyond clinically defined eating disorders and was based on literature published up to January 2020. Similarly, the scoping review by Dane and Bhatia [5] focused on a wider range of eating-related outcomes, and included studies published up to July 2021. Another systematic review by Bajaña Marín and García [2] focused exclusively on the Spanish population, which may limit the generalisability of its findings. In addition, systematic reviews examining social media use and mental health more broadly have tended to address eating disorders only marginally [3, 27]. Taken together, these limitations highlight the lack of a systematic and up-to-date synthesis specifically addressing the association between social media use and clinically defined eating disorders in young adults.

Addressing this gap is important for both theoretical and clinical reasons. A more precise understanding of how social media use relates to eating disorder psychopathology in young adults may help to identify characteristic patterns of social media engagement in individuals with eating disorders, as well as the underlying mechanisms, associated risk factors, and contextual influences shaping this relationship. Such insights may contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how specific forms of social media use are implicated in the development and maintenance of eating disorder symptomatology. Furthermore, a narrower focus on clinically defined eating disorders allows for a more targeted and conceptually coherent synthesis of evidence, facilitating the communication of key findings to professionals working with individuals with eating disorders, including psychologists and psychiatrists. At the same time, this approach may support researchers by providing a clearer and more clinically grounded evidence base upon which more detailed and methodologically refined studies can build.

Current study

This review aims to summarise current findings on the association between social media use and eating disorders, with a particular focus on the young adult population.

Research question: What is the association between social media use and eating disorders in young adults?

As the definition of young adulthood varies across sources [4, 25], for this study, the age range has been defined according to Wilkinson and Dunlop [42] as 18–30 years. Some studies included a broader age range than the predefined one or did not specify the exact age range of participants, only the mean age of the sample. For these reasons, if the mean age of the sample fell within the selected range, the study was also included to ensure that valuable insights were not overlooked.

In this systematic review, eating disorders are conceptualised in a psychopathological sense, referring to diagnostic categories as defined by the DSM-5 or ICD-11. Thus, studies focusing exclusively on broader or preclinical constructs such as disordered eating, body image, drive for thinness, eating concerns, eating disorder tendencies, eating disorder risk, or related subclinical phenomena were explicitly excluded. Only studies in which the authors explicitly declare that they focus on eating disorders as diagnostic entities or focus on clinically significant symptomatology consistent with formal diagnostic criteria were included.

The term social media is broad and encompasses many platforms. Therefore, specific social media platforms included in the keywords of this review were selected based on the most used social media worldwide, according to the latest global active users index published by DataReportal [21]. Social media platforms primarily designed for instant messaging (e.g., WhatsApp, Messenger) were excluded due to their limited relevance for publicly shared appearance-related content—which refers to visually oriented posts (e.g., images, videos) shared within social media feeds that promote self-presentation or body comparison, the main theoretical pathway linking social media use with eating disorder risk.

Methods

This review was not preregistered; however, it was conducted in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [31], following established methodological and reporting standards to ensure a systematic and transparent review process. The article search was conducted in March 2025 using the Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed databases. Grey literature (e.g., dissertations, conference abstracts, and unpublished studies) was not included, as the review aimed to synthesise findings from peer-reviewed empirical studies to ensure methodological quality and comparability. Flowchart of study inclusion

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were empirical quantitative research articles published in English or Czech with full-text access available. Only primary studies were included, while secondary studies (e.g., systematic reviews) and non-research outputs such as books or dissertations were excluded. The review focused on young adults; therefore, only studies including participants aged 18–30 years were considered. To address the aim of the review, studies were required to examine the association between social media use and eating disorders. In line with the conceptual focus of the review, only studies in which the authors explicitly declared that they focused on eating disorders as diagnostic entities were included. This approach was adopted to ensure that the review focused on clinically relevant manifestations of eating disorders and to avoid conflating them with broader or subclinical constructs. Accordingly, studies focusing solely on non-clinical constructs (e.g., body dissatisfaction or disordered eating) were excluded. Studies focusing exclusively on communication-based platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, WeChat), as well as those examining social media as an intervention for eating disorder treatment or as a recruitment tool, were also excluded, as they did not address the association of interest. Questionnaire validation studies were excluded for the same reason.

The study selection process was conducted independently by two reviewers. In cases of disagreement between reviewers, a more conservative approach was applied, and the stricter inclusion criteria were used to determine eligibility. The initial database search identified 630 articles, of which 165 were duplicates and subsequently removed. The remaining 465 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Following the exclusion of 369 records due to non-relevant titles or failure to meet the inclusion criteria, 96 articles remained. The main reason for excluding most studies at this stage was their focus on sub-phenomena related to eating disorders, such as body image or disordered eating, that did not fully align with the review’s defined focus on clinically recognised eating disorders. After full-text inspection, seven articles were considered eligible for inclusion in the review.

Data extracted from each study included: author(s), year of publication, country, study design, sample characteristics (e.g., sample size, age, gender), type of social media platform and usage, measures of social media use, measures of eating disorders or eating disorder symptomatology, key variables examined, and main findings. No relevant missing data were identified in the included studies, and all necessary information for data extraction and interpretation was available. All relevant results pertaining to the association between social media use and eating disorder outcomes were considered as reported in the original studies, without applying additional selection criteria. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; [20]) was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies, as it allows for the appraisal of studies with different methodological designs within a single framework, ensuring consistency and comparability across studies.

Results

The studies were conducted across various countries – Italy (n = 1), USA (n = 1), Australia (n = 2), Germany (n = 1), Lebanon (n = 1), Singapore (n = 1). The included studies showed considerable heterogeneity in study design, sample characteristics, methods of assessing eating disorders, and the operationalisation of social media use. In addition, studies differed in the specific platforms and exposure constructs examined. Most studies were cross-sectional (CS; n = 6), including two descriptive cross-sectional studies. In addition, one study was experimental. Given this heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was conducted, and a meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Studies were grouped for the narrative synthesis according to the primary aspect of the association between social media use and eating disorders they addressed, as well as by key study characteristics where relevant. Across the included studies, six out of seven reported a positive association between social media use and eating disorder outcomes, while one study reported no significant association. Some studies also reported mixed findings depending on the specific type of social media use or content examined. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; [20]). All studies passed the screening questions of the tool and were deemed suitable for full assessment. The quality of the study, using stars, was indicated, as recommended by the authors. Five stars indicate 100% of the criteria were met, four stars represent 80–100%, and three stars indicate 60–80% of the criteria were fulfilled. Responses marked as “Cannot tell” did not meet the criterion. The detailed results of the quality appraisal are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Assessment of eating disorder status

Across the included studies, eating disorder status was determined in two ways. In the majority of studies (n = 5), participants with eating disorders were included based on a pre-existing diagnosis. Although detailed information on how these diagnoses were originally established was not always reported, participants were typically recruited from clinical settings, specialised organisations, or self-identified as having a prior diagnosis (e.g.,, [10], [14], [15], [24], [39]. In the remaining studies (n = 2), eating disorder status was assessed within the study using validated self-report instruments measuring clinically relevant symptomatology (e.g., [11], [36]). Therefore, studies assessing eating disorders through self-report were not excluded; however, only those using validated instruments and meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were included.

Measurement of variables

Data collection methods

Self-report assessments (questionnaires and inventories) were employed as the primary data collection method in all studies, with the majority (n = 6) relying exclusively on this approach. In one study, an additional method involving the analysis of TikTok videos, platform algorithms, and participants’ liking behaviour was utilised.

Social media use measurement

Only two studies used instruments validated and tested for reliability in previous research: the Social Media Disorder Scale [7] and the Instagram Use Questionnaire [23]. The authors developed their items in four other studies specifically for their respective research. One study employed exposure to Instagram posts as an independent experimental variable, the only method used to examine the social media aspect in that study.

Eating disorder measurement

Eating disorder symptoms were assessed exclusively through self-report instruments. Three studies utilised more than one measure. The most frequently used instruments were the Eating Disorder Inventory-2 (EDI-2; [12]) and the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q; [9]), each applied in three studies. Additional measures, each used in two or fewer studies, are detailed in Table 2.

Additional measures

Beyond assessing social media use and eating disorder symptoms, all studies included measures of additional constructs. These varied depending on study objectives and included, for example, the Eating Disorder Quality of Life Instrument (EDQOL; [8]) or the Physical Appearance Comparison Scale-Revised (PACS-R; [35]).

Social media types and key aspects

The studies differed in terms of which social media platforms they focused on. Three studies focused on a single platform: two on Instagram and one on TikTok. One study focused on two platforms: Facebook and YouTube. The remaining studies focused either on multiple specified social media platforms (n = 2) or social media in general without specifying particular platforms (n = 1).

The studies examined different aspects of social media use. However, the majority (n = 5) investigated the content-related aspect, primarily focusing on exposure to material that may act as a potential predictor or maintainer of eating disorder symptoms, such as the “thinspiration” and “fitspiration” phenomena, or other forms of so-called pro-ED content. The concept of thinspiration was described in the included studies as depictions of excessively thin bodies, the glorification of extreme caloric restriction, and the promotion of thinness-oriented attitudes and behaviours, often accompanied by emotional support and validation for individuals striving to maintain these ideals [14]. Similarly, fitspiration was characterised by idealised representations of highly fit and lean bodies with visible muscle tone [14, 24]. More broadly, problematic content, often referred to as pro-eating disorder (pro-ED) content, was conceptualised as exposure to or engagement with appearance-focused material promoting a thin ideal. This includes behaviours such as viewing, following, or posting body image–related content [11], as well as exposure to dieting videos, exercise-related content, food-related content, and other appearance-oriented material [15, 39].

The remaining studies (n = 2) addressed problematic social media use in terms of addictive behaviour and experiences.

Eating disorder types

Almost all studies (n = 6) focused on multiple diagnostic categories of eating disorders, such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, and eating disorders not otherwise specified. In all of these studies, participants with anorexia nervosa constituted the majority. The remaining study (n = 1) focused exclusively on bulimia nervosa.

Research sample

Participants were recruited through hospitals, eating disorder–related organisations, social media, or university-based advertisements. A power analysis for calculating the minimum required sample size was reported in only two studies [24, 36]. Regarding sample size, the smallest sample was reported in the study by Tan et al., [39] with 55 participants, while the largest sample was reported by Fioravanti et al., [10] with 386 participants. Most studies included samples comprising both women and men (n = 4); however, women were significantly overrepresented. The most balanced gender distribution was reported in the study by Sfeir et al., [36], in which women accounted for 61.7% of the sample. In the remaining studies (n = 3), the sample consisted exclusively of women. The majority of studies (n = 5) focused on individuals with a diagnosed eating disorder, with three of them comparing outcomes between individuals with ED and those without. The remaining studies (n = 2) did not include a diagnosed eating disorder as an inclusion criterion for participant selection and assessed the severity of ED symptomatology using their instruments. Detailed information about the study populations, including participant age, is provided in Table 2.

Study focuses

Although all included studies address the relationship between social media use and eating disorders, each approaches the topic with slightly different aims. Three of them aimed to test a theoretically proposed model of the relationship between social media use and eating disorder symptoms, including the examination of potential mediating or moderating variables within this association. The study by Fioravanti et al., [10] aimed to test a model linking problematic Instagram use to eating disorder symptoms, through the mediating effects of appearance comparison tendency, psychological investment in the physical appearance, and body uneasiness. Griffiths et al., [14] aimed to examine a model that proposed relationships between image-centric social media use, exposure to thinspiration and fitspiration content, physical appearance comparisons, and eating disorder symptoms. Specifically, the model proposed that physical appearance comparison mediates the relationship between exposure to thinspiration/fitspiration content and ED symptoms. Sfeir et al., [36] aimed to test a model in which anxiety and depression mediate the association between problematic social media use and bulimia nervosa.

Other included studies had different primary aims. Ladwig et al., [24] aimed to examine the effects of photo-based “fitspiration” and “body positivity” Instagram posts and text-based “body neutrality” Instagram content on state body dissatisfaction and affect in women with ED and women without any mental disorder. Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., [11] aimed to assess the extent to which the symptoms of individuals socially networking about EDs are consistent with a clinical or subclinical ED diagnosis. Tan et al., [39] aimed to assess the pattern of usage of internet, smartphone applications, Facebook and YouTube usage and it´s correlation with severity of ED illness. Griffiths et al., [15] aimed to provide evidence of the potential for the TikTok algorithm to exacerbate ED symptoms.

Key findings related to the association between social media use and eating disorders

Given the predominance of cross-sectional designs, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of associations rather than causal relationships. A relationship between social media use and eating disorders was observed in six of the included studies, whereas one study did not report such an association. Two studies focused on the association between problematic social media use (PSMU) and eating disorders, the both reported a significant relationship [10, 36]. The study by Fioravanti et al., [10] found that patients with eating disorders used Instagram more frequently and exhibited higher levels of problematic Instagram use compared to the healthy control group. Sfeir et al., [36] identified a significant association between problematic use of social media and the presence of bulimia nervosa diagnosis.

Four additional studies explored other social media-related aspects and also confirmed the relationship between social media use and ED psychopathology or symptoms severity [11, 14, 15, 39]. The study by Tan et al., [39] found that the use of smartphone applications, including social media, was associated with greater eating disorder psychopathology. In the study by Griffiths et al., [14], greater exposure to both thinspiration and fitspiration content was associated with more severe eating disorder symptoms. Exposure to thinspiration appeared to be more strongly associated with risk, whereas exposure to fitspiration was more common among participants. Surprisingly, however, more frequent use of image-centric social media was associated with increased exposure to such content, but not directly with eating disorder symptoms. In the study by Griffiths et al., [15], it was found that the more severe the participants’ eating disorder symptoms were, the more biased their TikTok algorithms were toward appearance-oriented, dieting, exercise, and toxic disorder–related videos. These participants were also more likely to like and watch such content, which in turn further increased the likelihood of their algorithms delivering similar videos. However, it should be acknowledged that while “liking” behavior influenced the algorithm’s delivery of such videos, the magnitude of this effect was lower than anticipated. Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., [11] reported that the majority of participants who indicated viewing and/or posting pro-ED online content exhibited symptoms consistent with a clinical or subclinical eating disorder. In terms of subjective perception, participants in the study by Tan et al., [39] reported that social media apps were perceived as contributing to the continuation of their eating disorders.

In contrast to the other studies, Ladwig et al., [24] found no significant differences between individuals with ED and healthy controls regarding the effects of social media exposure. Specifically, exposure to Instagram content related to fitspiration, body positivity, and body neutrality did not appear to pose greater risk or benefit for individuals with ED compared to controls in terms of body dissatisfaction and affect. Griffiths et al., [15] also found no association between the self-reported frequency of TikTok use among users with eating disorders and healthy controls. ED symptoms were also not associated with the number of TikTok videos delivered.

Support for theoretical models

All three studies that tested a theoretical model concerning the relationship between social media use and eating disorders reported support for the proposed model [10, 14, 36]. The study by Fioravanti et al., [10] used structural equation modeling and found that problematic Instagram use predicted both appearance comparison and psychological investment in physical appearance, which subsequently predicted body uneasiness. Body uneasiness, in turn, predicted both eating disorder psychopathology and interpersonal difficulties. In the study by Griffiths et al., [14], the proposed model was supported through path analysis, indicating that physical appearance comparison mediated the relationship between exposure to thinspiration and fitspiration content and eating disorder symptoms. In the study by Sfeir et al., [36], regression-based mediation analysis showed that both anxiety and depression partially mediated the relationship between problematic social media use and bulimia nervosa.

Impact of content exposure

Several studies explored the exposure to different types of social media content. Fitspiration content was examined as a potential risk factor in two studies, which found that its exposure was linked to increased ED symptom severity and more frequent appearance comparisons [14, 24]. Furthermore, exposure to fitspiration was associated with greater body dissatisfaction and reduced positive affect; however, no change in negative affect was observed [24]. In addition to the risks associated with fitspiration content, two studies also highlighted the potential harmfulness of thinspiration and general pro-eating disorder content in relation to the severity of eating disorder symptoms [11, 14]. Ladwig et al., [24] further found that exposure to “body positivity” and text-based “body neutrality” Instagram posts led to a reduction in body dissatisfaction as well as a decrease in negative affect. However, similar to fitspiration, exposure to text-based “body neutrality” posts also resulted in a decrease in positive affect, whereas positive affect remained unchanged following exposure to “body positivity” posts.

Social media behaviour patterns in ED samples

Three studies explored the specific patterns of social media behavior among individuals with eating disorders [11, 15, 39]. For example, individuals with more severe ED symptoms were, in general, significantly more likely to “like” TikTok videos compared to healthy controls. They also reported a significantly higher proportion of viewed content involving diet-related themes, a greater tendency to skip videos that made them feel bad about their appearance, and significantly more difficulty in stopping themselves from using TikTok. Additionally, they were less likely to skip videos due to boredom or lack of interest. They also showed a slightly higher tendency to like problematic video categories such as dieting and exercise-related content [15]. Participants were most frequently exposed to pro-ED online content on these SM platforms – Facebook, Instagram, and Reddit [11]. In the study by Tan et al., [39], less than half of participants with ED reported encountering ED-related content on Facebook, while only a minority reported visiting Facebook groups dedicated to eating disorders. While the study examined reasons for and consequences of visiting ED-related Facebook groups, the very limited number of participants engaging with such groups restricts the interpretability of the findings, which should be viewed as primarily qualitative and not generalizable. Tan et al., [39] further found that respondents did not tend to upload or search for food-related images, or engage with food-related content on Facebook. They primarily shared photos of themselves or with friends and family. On YouTube, the most frequently viewed categories were “Cooking and Food” and “Beauty and Fashion”.

Appearance comparisons

Three studies also examined the association between appearance comparisons—considered a consequence of social media exposure—and the severity of eating disorder symptoms [10, 14, 15]. Women with eating disorders demonstrated a greater propensity for appearance comparisons following exposure to Instagram images compared to women without ED [10]. Similarly, individuals with ED engaged in appearance comparisons on TikTok more frequently than healthy controls, and the frequency of such comparisons was positively correlated with ED symptom severity [15]. Griffiths et al., [14] also found that greater frequency of physical appearance comparisons was associated with more severe ED symptoms. While appearance comparisons were related to increased exposure to both fitspiration and thinspiration content, they were not associated with the frequency of image-centric social media use. Additionally, Tan et al., [39] found that 80% of participants reported comparing their own photos with those of friends or celebrities.

Comorbid depression and anxiety

Two studies also pointed to an association between social media use, depression, and anxiety, as well as between eating disorders and these conditions [11, 36], which is consistent with the most common comorbidities observed in individuals with eating disorders [16].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to investigate the association between social media use and eating disorders. The findings of this systematic review indicate that six out of seven included studies reported a positive association between social media use and eating disorder outcomes in young adults, whereas one study reported no significant association. These findings suggest a relatively consistent pattern across the current evidence base. Nevertheless, it is important to take into account that the included studies were predominantly cross-sectional, and that the strength of the evidence is further limited by the heterogeneity of the studies and the number of studies addressing each specific aspect.

Although the influence of social media on the mental health of the general population has received considerable attention in the research literature, relatively few studies have specifically examined the relationship between social media use and eating disorders. Three databases—Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed—were searched, identifying a total of 630 records, of which seven were included in the review. Aside from duplicate records, the primary reason for exclusion was that many studies focused on broader concepts related to eating disorders, such as body image or disordered eating, rather than on eating disorder diagnoses.

The included studies differed in the specific aspects of social media use they examined, reflecting the heterogeneity of the current evidence base. Furthermore, specific patterns of social media use can be observed among individuals with eating disorders compared to the healthy population. With regard to the aspects of social media that were examined, the focus was mainly on content-related exposure and on problematic use characterized by addiction-like tendencies. None of the studies primarily focused on commonly investigated variables such as time spent on social media, frequency of social media checking, or distinctions between passive and active use. These aspects are frequently explored in relation to other mental health outcomes, such as depression, anxiety, and overall well-being (e.g., [13, 37]). While a few studies in this review included questions about the frequency of social media use (e.g., [10 ], [14]), this variable received limited attention in the overall analysis or discussion.

Across the included studies, female participants were strongly overrepresented, reflecting the gender distribution of diagnosed cases and the overall prevalence of eating disorders [38]. As a result, the findings primarily reflect outcomes among women, and it remains unclear whether similar patterns extend to male populations.

The studies included in this systematic review are relatively recent, which is reflected, for example, in the fact that—with the exception of the study by Tan et al., [39]—none of them were included in any of the previously published reviews on related topics referenced earlier in this article [5, 30]. Therefore, in terms of comparing results with other studies, it is challenging to directly compare the findings with those of the systematic review by Padín et al., [30], as only one study overlaps. Moreover, their review also focused on an adolescent population and on constructs related to, but not exclusively reflecting, eating disorders in a clinical sense. However, in contrast to the present review, their study placed greater emphasis on the risks associated with time spent on social media. Nevertheless, both reviews highlighted the harmful impact of content consumption, particularly when combined with an appearance comparison mechanism. The present review also highlights the risks associated with problematic social media use, particularly addiction-like behaviours, extending prior systematic review findings on problematic internet use in relation to eating disorder pathology to the social media context [18].

As mentioned previously, the number of studies explicitly examining the relationship between social media use and clinically defined eating disorders is quite limited compared to the number of studies focusing on broader related constructs. One possible explanation is the specificity of the target population (i.e., individuals with a formal ED diagnosis), which makes the recruitment process more demanding. Another reason may be that the study of eating disorders requires the use of valid diagnostic tools and, when conducted properly, should involve a complex clinical process, such as the inclusion of structured clinical interviews. This approach is demanding in terms of time, expertise, and organization. As a result, researchers may opt to assess eating disorders using only a single validated questionnaire, which, although more feasible, limits the diagnostic accuracy and may compromise the quality of the findings. However, the fact that the included studies were conducted relatively recently may indicate that interest in this area of research is growing and likely to expand further.

Based on the studies included in this systematic review, some risk factors related to ED symptoms severity can be identified. The studies primarily highlighted the risks associated with exposure to various types of social media content falling into the categories of “pro-ED”, “fitspiration”, and “thinspiration” [11, 24]. Exposure to such problematic content is further reinforced by another risk-related aspect of social media—their algorithms [15]. Another risk factor associated with the severity of ED symptoms is problematic social media use characterized by addiction-like behavior [11, 36]. It should be noted that many theoretically and empirically suggested risk factors have not yet been examined in the included studies.

Although the studies included in this systematic review pointed to an association between social media use and eating disorders in a negative sense, including its role as a potential barrier to the effectiveness of professional treatment [15], they also outlined possible ways in which social media could be used for positive purposes [11, 39]. Participants, for example, viewed social media as a potential tool for connecting with professional help [11] . Given that common barriers to treatment included, for example, fear of stigmatization [11], a large-scale destigmatization campaign could be considered. Such a campaign could incorporate, among other elements, images related to “body positivity” and text-based “body neutrality”, as these have been shown to reduce body dissatisfaction following exposure [24]. Social media platforms could potentially be adapted to prioritise such content through algorithmic promotion, or by linking it to hashtags and keywords commonly associated with pro-ED content. However, such algorithmic interventions would raise ethical concerns regarding user autonomy and content manipulation. Given that most included studies were cross-sectional, these implications should be interpreted with caution.

Compared to previous systematic reviews, the present review provides a more focused and clinically relevant synthesis by concentrating on eating disorders within the young adult population. By adopting a narrower scope and incorporating more recent evidence, it enables a more detailed examination of a topic that has not yet been sufficiently explored. This approach allows for a more specific and nuanced understanding of the relationship between social media use and eating disorders compared to earlier reviews.

Recommendation for future research

Nearly all studies included in the systematic review involved either exclusively female samples or samples in which women were significantly overrepresented. Accordingly, future research should place greater emphasis on male populations with eating disorders, including targeted recruitment strategies to ensure more balanced gender representation. Most studies relied on relatively small sample sizes; therefore, future research should aim to include larger and, ideally, more representative samples to enhance the generalizability of findings. Most of the studies included in this systematic review were cross-sectional and examined associations rather than causal relationships. Future research should employ more experimental designs and, where feasible, randomized controlled trials to better understand the causes and consequences of social media use in relation to eating disorders.

Social media use encompasses many aspects; however, in the context of eating disorders, only a few of these have been explored, with most studies primarily focusing on social media content. Future research should systematically examine additional aspects of social media use, such as intensity, frequency, active versus passive engagement, users’ motivations and purposes for use, the number of followers and followings, as well as the nature and size of the online communities individuals are part of. In addition, further research should aim to deepen the investigation of already studied aspects to allow for more consistent and comparable findings.

The studies included in this systematic review addressed various platforms, primarily Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook. Although some of them focused on a specific platform, future research should move away from general examinations of “social media” as a whole and instead prioritize in-depth investigations of individual platforms. Each social media environment has distinct features, user dynamics, and risks related to ED exposure, and thus requires separate examination. Future studies should focus on the most widely used platforms and those identified as high-risk for pro-ED content, and should also examine where individuals with eating disorders are most likely to engage with such content. Only with this level of specificity can findings be meaningfully generalised.

Limitations

This review includes several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the strict inclusion criteria focused exclusively on clinically defined eating disorders. While this enhances clinical relevance, it excludes studies examining broader constructs such as disordered eating or body image, potentially omitting insights into early risk factors. Second, only quantitative studies were included, which may have limited the depth of understanding of individual experiences that qualitative approaches could provide. Third, the exclusion of grey literature and the lack of preregistration may have increased the risk of publication bias and limited methodological transparency. Finally, the use of the MMAT tool ensured consistent quality assessment across studies; however, it is based on a limited number of relatively broad criteria, which may restrict the depth of methodological evaluation.

Several limitations also relate to the characteristics of the included studies. The studies showed considerable heterogeneity in study design and research aims, which limited comparability and precluded the use of meta-analytic approaches. In addition, the interpretation and integration of findings were complicated by inconsistent use of terminology across studies (e.g., eating disorders, eating disorder symptoms, eating disorder psychopathology), which was not always clearly defined or operationalised. To preserve accuracy, the findings were reported in accordance with the terminology used in each individual study. Another limitation relates to the assessment of eating disorder status across studies. In many cases, more detailed information on how diagnoses were originally established was not reported, and in some studies, eating disorder status was assessed using self-report questionnaires rather than formal diagnostic procedures.

Future systematic reviews may benefit from maintaining conceptual clarity by focusing on clearly defined and homogeneous constructs. Rather than combining clinically defined eating disorders with broader or subclinical phenomena, separate reviews targeting specific constructs (e.g., disordered eating or body image) may allow for more precise synthesis and interpretation of findings. Incorporating qualitative studies and, where appropriate, grey literature may also provide complementary insights into individual experiences and contextual factors. As the evidence base expands, future reviews may be better positioned to apply meta-analytic approaches and examine underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This systematic review examined the relationship between social media use and eating disorders in young adults. The findings of the included studies fairly consistently suggest an association between social media use and eating disorders in this population. These findings indicate that social media may represent a relevant factor in the development or maintenance of eating disorders, or potentially act as a barrier to treatment effectiveness. The findings primarily highlight the potential role of content-related factors and problematic use. However, the evidence remains limited, as the included studies examined only a narrow range of social media use aspects and platforms and were predominantly cross-sectional in design. Further research exploring the relationship between social media and eating disorders is needed in order to gain a deeper understanding of this complex relationship.
