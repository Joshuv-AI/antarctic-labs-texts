Automating the Derivation of Unification Algorithms
Richard Waldinger

Abstract
The unification algorithm has long been a target for program synthesis research, but a fully automatic derivation remains a research goal. In deductive program synthesis, computer programming is phrased as a task in theorem proving; a declarative specification is expressed in logical form and presented to an automatic theorem prover, and a program meeting the specification is extracted from the proof. The correctness of the program is supported by the proof, which also provides an explanation of how the program works. The proof is conducted in an appropriate axiomatic subject-domain theory, which defines the concepts in the specification and the constructs in the target programming language and provides the background knowledge necessary to connect them. For the unification proof, we generalize and automate the manual proof presented in Manna and Waldinger 1981 . The new program unifies two given symbolic expressions (s-expressions) relative to a given “environment” substitution. The proof establishes the existence of an output substitution that is a most-general idempotent unifier of the given expressions and is an “extension” of the environment substitution. If no such substitution exists and the expressions are not unifiable, the program is to produce a failure indicator ⊥ \bot . Initially the environment substitution is the empty substitution, which makes no replacements at all; during execution of recursive calls, the environment substitution records the replacements that have been found so far. Our own unification algorithm employs an environment, and such algorithms appear in the literature [ Luger and Stubblefield 1997 , e.g.,] . We suspect, in addition to being more efficient, the three-argument algorithm with an environment is easier to synthesize automatically than the two-argument version from the Manna-Waldinger paper. The proof is conducted relative to an axiomatic theory of expressions and substitutions. The structure of the derived program reflects the proof from which it was extracted. Conditional expressions in the program are obtained from case analysis in the proof; recursion is introduced using well-founded induction, with respect to a well-founded relation axiomatized in the theory. The proof was obtained automatically by the first-order resolution theorem prover snark [ Stickel et al. 2001 ] . The extracted program, as we remarked, improves on Manna and Waldinger’s and contains some novel elements.

Introduction

Program synthesis, the automatic construction of computer programs, was one of the first applications of automated theorem proving and is still one of the most appealing. Several early researchers
[
Slagle 1965
;
Waldinger and Lee 1969
;
Green 1969
, e.g.,]
exhibited the synthesis of simple programs as demonstrations. The NASA system Amphion
[
Lowry et al. 1994
]
used a theorem prover to construct software for, among other things, the Cassini mission to Saturn. Yet deductive program synthesis has had little impact on the practice of software engineering, even though it seems as if programming and theorem proving are cognitively similar, and that many of the difficulties software engineers have in program construction are like those mathematicians have in proving theorems. Considering the progress theorem provers have made in recent years, it is natural to wonder how well they can now do in program construction.

In deductive program synthesis, we regard the task of constructing a program as one of proving a theorem. A given specification describes the purpose of the desired program without giving any indication of the method by which that purpose is to be achieved. For this reason, a correct specification may be easier to construct than a completed program. The specification is phrased as a conjecture, i.e., a potential theorem to be proved, and is submitted to a theorem prover. For an applicative program, the conjecture typically states the existence of an output entity that, for a given input entity, satisfies the specification. The proof is conducted with respect to a subject domain theory, an axiomatic theory that defines the concepts of the specification language, the constructs of the target programming language, and the background knowledge necessary to connect them. The proof is restricted to be sufficiently constructive that, in proving the existence of a desired output, it is forced to indicate a method for finding such an entity. That method becomes the basis for an algorithm that is extracted from the proof.

We focus on concepts that play a role in the derivation of unification algorithms.
Roughly speaking, unification is the process of finding a way of making two mathematical expressions identical by replacing variables with other expressions. Unification was introduced to the theorem-proving community as part of the resolution principle
[
Robinson 1965
]
. Robinson credited it to Herbrand’s [
Herbrand 1930
] thesis, but added to its conceptualization. Unification is a process of central importance not just for theorem proving, but also for such applications as logic programming, type checking, and natural language understanding. Surprisingly, unification algorithms presented in widely used textbooks by highly qualified authors and incorporated into at least one reasoning system are incorrect
[
Norvig undated
]
.

Unification was an early target in deductive program synthesis research.
Manna and Waldinger 1981
presented a derivation in which a unification algorithm was extracted from an informal manual proof.

Nardi 1989
showed a manual synthesis of a unification algorithm from a proof within Manna and Waldinger’s deductive-tableau formalism, which we also use in this paper.

Paulson 1985
used an interactive proof checking system to show that Manna and Waldinger’s algorithm met its specification, but that argument took
the algorithm as given, so it is a semi-automatic verification rather than a synthesis.
Eriksson 1984
and
Armando et al., 1997
produced partial interactive syntheses. So it seems as if a fully automatic synthesis of an entire unification algorithm is still a research goal.

In this paper, we first provide an introduction to a theory of expressions and substitutions, which serves as the subject domain theory for the derivation. We outline how to adapt a resolution-style theorem prover for program synthesis, with special emphasis on the use of case analysis to produce conditional expressions and mathematical induction to produce recursion. We limit our discussion to the use of a first-order-logic theorem prover to produce applicative (side-effect-free) programs. Our illustrative examples will be taken from the proof-based derivation of a unification algorithm.

snark
was able to produce dozens of unification algorithms for symbolic expressions from the same specification. For this paper we have selected one that was relatively simple and efficient.
We rephrase
snark
’s derivation proof for this algorithm for comprehensibility. We present the algorithm, which contains some novelties discovered by
snark
.

Our hope is that this example may serve as an introduction to deductive program synthesis. It is too terse to provide all the necessary logical background—a more leisurely tutorial appears in, say,
[
Manna and Waldinger 1993
]
—but a reader with some mathematical training should be able to make sense of this paper without having read the earlier work.

A Theory of Expressions, Substitutions, and Unifiers

Our introduction is brisk and semi-formal, and we omit the proof of some known results. We do not attempt to survey the entire theory of expressions; for a more general introduction, see
[
Baader and Snyder 2001
]
. Even people familiar with the theory may want to scan the following section, because we have altered some of the concepts to facilitate the automation of the proofs.

Expressions

T

[

]

(a,b,c,\ldots)
, and
\emph{nil}
and variables
(W,X,Y,Z)
. These are characterized by the relations
\iscnst(e)
and
\isvar(e)
, respectively.
For the unification study, we also include a special constant
\ast

\emph{blackhole}

A nonatomic expression
e_{l}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e_{2}
is the cons (
) of two subexpressions
e_{1}
and
e_{r}

and the variable

Left and Right Functions.

We introduce functions
\lef

\displaystyle\unot({\isatm}(e))\uimplies

\displaystyle\,e={\lef}(e)\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,{\rig}(e),

\displaystyle{\isatm}(e)\uimplies

\displaystyle\unot(e={e}_{l}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,{e}_{r}),

for all expressions

,
{e}_{l}
, and
{e}_{r}

\lef
and

correspond to the functions

and

Two nonatomic expressions

It follows that, for any expressions
e_{l}
and
e_{r}

\lef(e_{l}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e_{r})=e_{l}

For any two expressions

and
e_{2}

e_{1}

occurs properly

if
e_{1}
occurs properly in

e_{1}\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,e_{2}\Leftrightarrow(e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}\uor e_{1}=e_{2}).

We shall also call

and

the
subexpression
and the

relation, respectively. They satisfy the

, that

\displaystyle\isatm(e)\uimplies\unot(d\,\varepsilon\,e)

and the

, that

\displaystyle d\,\varepsilon\,(e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e_{2})\Leftrightarrow(d\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,e_{1}\uor d\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,e_{2}),

for all expressions

,

e_{1}

Size and Vars Functions.

We define the
size
of an expression to be the number of nonvariable symbols, including conses, in an expression; that is

\displaystyle\emph{size}(c)=\,1

\displaystyle\text{if $c$ is a constant}

\displaystyle\emph{size}(v)=\,0

\displaystyle\text{if $v$ is a variable}

\displaystyle\emph{size}(e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e_{2})=\,1+\emph{size}(e_{1})+\emph{size}(e_{2})

\displaystyle\text{if $e_{1}$ and $e_{2}$ are expressions.}

The
\emph{size}
function has been defined so that variables are less than constants. We can also show that

e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}\uimplies\emph{size}(e_{1})<\emph{size}(e_{2}),

for any expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2},

and hence, if

\emph{size}(\lef(e)))<\emph{size}(e)

and

\emph{size}(\rig(e)))<\emph{size}(e).

We denote by
\emph{vars}(e)

, defined by

\displaystyle\emph{vars}(v)=\,\{v\}

\displaystyle\text{if $v$ is a variable}

\displaystyle\emph{vars}(c)=\,\{\}

\displaystyle\text{if $c$ is a constant}

\displaystyle\emph{vars}(e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e_{2})=\,\emph{vars}(e_{1})\cup\emph{vars}(e_{2})

\displaystyle\text{if $e_{1}$ and $e_{2}$ are expressions.}

Because, for a nonatomic expression
e
, we know
e=\lef(e)\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,\rig(e)
, we have the

left-right property
of
\emph{vars}

\emph{vars}(e)=vars(\lef(e))\cup vars(\rig(e)).

We allow ourselves to use
\emph{vars}
as an n-ary function, by the

of
\emph{vars}
,

\emph{vars}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})=\emph{vars}(e_{1})\cup\ldots\cup vars(e_{n}).

We also have the
occurs-subset property
of
\emph{vars}
, namely

e_{1}\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,e_{2}\uimplies\emph{vars}(e_{1})\subseteq\emph{vars}(e_{2}),

for all expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
. The subset relation here is not always proper; for example,
x\,\varepsilon\,(x\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,x)
but

\displaystyle\emph{vars}(x)

\displaystyle=\{x\}

\displaystyle=\emph{vars}(x\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,x).

Imitating Lisp, we sometimes use the
empty tuple

\langle\rangle
as an alternative notation for
\emph{nil}
; the

\langle e\rangle
as an abbreviation for
e_{\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,}\emph{nil}
; the

\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle

e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(e_{2}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,\emph{nil}\,)
; and, in general, the

\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle

e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(e_{2}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(\ldots(e_{n}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,\emph{nil}\,)\ldots))
. Tuples of length three are called

.
We can establish the
vars-union property
of tuples, that

\emph{vars}(\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle)=\emph{vars}(e_{1})\cup\emph{vars}(e_{2})\cup\ldots\cup\emph{vars}(e_{n}).

When regarding symbolic expressions as tuples, it is conventional to use the symbols
\emph{head}
and
\emph{tail}
instead of
\lef
and
\rig
. Thus

\begin{split}&\emph{head}(\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle)=e_{1}\\
&\emph{tail}(\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle)=\langle e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle.\end{split}

We also use
\ufirst

\begin{split}&\ufirst(\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle)=e_{1},\\
&\usecond(\langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle)=e_{2},\end{split}

etc.

It is more common to define unification on functional terms, such as

, but this is more complex and gives us no additional computational power; if we take function symbols to be constants we can encode

(\emph{f}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(e_{1}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(e_{2}\ldots(e_{n}\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,\emph{nil}\,)\ldots))),

We also apply

Substitutions

A
proper substitution

\{{x}_{1}\mapsto e_{1},{x}_{2}\mapsto e_{2},\ldots,{x}_{n}\mapsto e_{n}\}
is a function from expressions to expressions that replaces all occurrences of each variable
{x}_{i}
with the corresponding term
e_{i}
, where we assume that the
{x}_{i}
’s are distinct and that each variable
{x}_{i}
is distinct from its corresponding term
e_{i}
. The replacements are viewed as being performed simultaneously.

We denote by
e\blacktriangleleft\theta
the result of applying the substitution
\theta
to the expression
e
; we shall also say that
e\blacktriangleleft\theta
is an
instance
of
e
under
\theta
. For example,
(X\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(a\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,X))\blacktriangleleft\{X\mapsto Y,Y\mapsto c\}
is
(Y\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(a\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,Y))
. Both occurrences of
X
are replaced and, because the replacements are done simultaneously, the second replacement
Y\mapsto c
has no effect.

The
empty substitution

\{\}
makes no replacements at all. Therefore
e\blacktriangleleft\{\}=e
for any expression
e
.

We introduce one
improper substitution
, the
failure
substitution
\bot
. This substitution maps any expression into the black hole; that is,
e\blacktriangleleft\bot=\ast
, for any expression
e
. We say that the predicate
\emph{is-proper}
holds for any proper substitution; that is,
\emph{is-proper}(\theta)\Leftrightarrow(\theta\neq\bot)
. The improper substitution does not follow the axioms for proper substitutions.

To apply a proper substitution to a nonatomic expression, apply it to the components; that is,
(d\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,e)\blacktriangleleft\theta=(d\blacktriangleleft\theta)\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(e\blacktriangleleft\theta)
. We call this the
distributivity property
for conses. Equivalently,
we have the
apply left-right property

\displaystyle{\unot}\,(\isatm(e))\uand\emph{is-proper}\,(\theta)\uimplies

\displaystyle e\blacktriangleleft\theta=(\lef(e)\blacktriangleleft\theta)\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,(\rig(e)\blacktriangleleft\theta).

Consequently, the result of applying a proper substitution to a nonatomic expression is nonatomic.
We can establish that proper substitutions distribute over tuples and sets; that is, we have the
distributivity property
for substitutions over tuples,

\langle e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle\blacktriangleleft\theta=\langle e_{1}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta,\ldots,e_{n}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta\rangle,

and
distributivity property
for substitutions over sets,

\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}\blacktriangleleft\theta=\{e_{1}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta,\ldots,e_{n}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta\},

for all proper substitutions
\theta
and expressions
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
.
Two substitutions are equal
if they have the same effect on all expressions; that is,
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
provided that, for any expression
e
,

e\,\blacktriangleleft\,\theta_{1}=e\,\blacktriangleleft\,\theta_{2}

To show equality of two proper substitutions
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, it suffices to show, by the
variable equality property
, that they have the same effect on variables; that is, for any variable
x
,

x\blacktriangleleft\theta_{1}=x\blacktriangleleft\theta_{2}.

For example,
\{X\mapsto a,Y\mapsto b\}
and
\{Y\mapsto b,X\mapsto a\}
are equal substitutions.

Applying a substitution to expressions preserves the subexpression relations; that is, for any proper substitution
\theta

\displaystyle e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}\,\uimplies

\displaystyle(e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta)\,\varepsilon\,(e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta)

and, for any substitution
\theta
,

\displaystyle e_{1}\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,e_{2}\,\uimplies

\displaystyle(e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta)\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,(e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta),

for all expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.

We can establish a
distributivity property
of the
vars
function for expressions, namely

\emph{vars}(e_{1},...,e_{n})\!\blacktriangleleft\theta=\emph{vars}(e_{1}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta,...,e_{n}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta).

By an abuse of notation, we call the set of replaced variables
\{{x}_{1},\ldots,{x}_{n}\}
of a substitution
\theta
its
domain
, denoted by
\emph{dom}(\theta)
; and the set of variables that occur in the introduced expressions
e_{1}
, or …, or
e_{n}
its
range
, denoted by
{\emph{range}}(\theta)
. For example, the domain of
\{X\mapsto(W\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,a),Y\mapsto(X\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,b)\}
is
\{X,Y\}
, and its range is
\{X,W\}
.
We define the variables
\emph{vars}(\theta)
of a substitution
\theta
to be those that occur in either its domain or its range; that is,

\emph{vars}(\theta)=\emph{dom}(\theta)\cup\emph{range}(\theta),

and hence

\emph{dom}(\theta)\subseteq\emph{vars}(\theta)

and

\emph{range}(\theta)\subseteq\emph{vars}(\theta).

We define
dom(\bot)=\emph{range}(\bot)=vars(\bot)=\{\}
, where
\{\}
is the empty set.

A substitution
\theta
is said to
miss
an expression
e
, written
\emph{misses}(\theta,e)
, if applying the substitution has no effect on the expression; that is, if
e\blacktriangleleft\theta=e
. This happens if no variable in the domain of
\theta
occurs in
e
. The misses relation applied to tuples of expressions satisfies the property

\emph{misses}(\theta,\langle e_{1},\ldots e_{n}\rangle)\Leftrightarrow[\emph{misses}(\theta,e_{1})\uand\ldots\uand\emph{misses}(\theta,e_{n})],

for all substitutions
\theta
and expressions
e_{1}
, …, and
e_{n}
.
A substitution
\theta
misses an expression
e
if and only if it misses all its variables
; that is

\emph{misses}(\theta,e)\Leftrightarrow\emph{misses}(\theta,vars(e)).

The domain and range of a substitution need not be disjoint.
We have

u\in\emph{dom}(\theta)\Leftrightarrow u\blacktriangleleft\theta\neq u
and
v\in\emph{range}(\theta)\Leftrightarrow(\exists u)[u\in\emph{dom}(\theta)\uand v\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,(u\blacktriangleleft\theta)].
If a variable belongs to the range of a substitution, we also say that the substitution
introduces
the variable.

It can be proved that two substitutions agree
on an expression
e
if they agree on all the variables of the expression; that is
e\blacktriangleleft\theta_{1}=e\blacktriangleleft\theta_{2}
precisely when
v\blacktriangleleft\theta_{1}=v\blacktriangleleft\theta_{2}
for all variables

that occur in

Addition.

The result of adding
two substitutions
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, denoted by
\theta_{1}+\,\theta_{2}
, is the substitution that applies the replacements in parallel. If the same variable occurs in the domains of both substitutions, the replacement in the first substitution is applied; the replacement in the second substitution is ignored. More precisely, for any variable
x
,

\begin{split}x\blacktriangleleft(\theta_{1}+\,\theta_{2})=\,&x\blacktriangleleft\theta_{1}&&\uif x\in\emph{dom}(\theta_{1})\\
=\,&x\blacktriangleleft\theta_{2}&&\uif x\notin\emph{dom}(\theta_{1}).\end{split}

Addition is not in general commutative.

Replacement.

A substitution that replaces a single variable
x
with an expression
e
is itself called a
replacement
, denoted by
\{x\mapsto e\}
. It satisfies the
replacement identity property

x\blacktriangleleft\{x\mapsto e\}=e

and the
replacement nonoccur property

d\blacktriangleleft\{x\mapsto e\}=d\,\uimpliedby\,\unot(x\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,d),

for all variables
x
and expressions
d
and
e
. In other words, a replacement has no effect on an expression if its variable doesn’t occur in that expression. We call
x
the
replaced variable
and
e
the
introduced expression
.

We have the
domain property
,
dom(\{x\mapsto e\})=\{x\}
, and the
range property
,
\emph{range}(\{x\mapsto e\})=vars(e)
, of the replacement function.

If
x
is a variable, we take
\{x\mapsto x\}
to be the empty substitution
\{\}
.

Composition.

Applying the
composition

{\theta}_{1}\blackdiamond\theta_{2}
of two substitutions
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
has the same effect as applying them sequentially; that is, we have a
composition property

e\blacktriangleleft(\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2})=(e\blacktriangleleft\theta_{1})\blacktriangleleft\theta_{2}.

For example,

\{X\mapsto(Y\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,a)\}\blackdiamond\{X\mapsto b,Y\mapsto c\}=\{X\mapsto(c\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,a),Y\mapsto c\}
. Here, the replacement
X\mapsto b
plays no role in the composition, because
X
has already been replaced.

The empty substitution {} acts as an identity
under composition; that is,
\theta\blackdiamond\{\}=\{\}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta
. This holds even if
\theta
is the improper failure substitution
\bot
. The failure substitution acts as an annihilator; that is,
\theta\blackdiamond\bot=\bot\blackdiamond\theta=\bot
.

Composition is associative
; that is,
(\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2})\blackdiamond\theta_{3}=\theta_{1}\blackdiamond(\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3})
, whether or not the substitutions are proper. For this reason, we can write the composition of three or more substitutions without parentheses, as in
\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}
.

The domain and range of the composition of two substitutions
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
satisfy the inclusions

\emph{dom}(\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2})\subseteq\emph{dom}(\theta_{1})\cup\emph{dom}(\theta_{2})
and
\emph{range}(\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2})\subseteq\emph{range}(\theta_{1})\cup\emph{range}(\theta_{2})
, respectively. We call these the
domain
and
range properties of composition
, respectively.

The inclusions cannot be replaced by equalities; consider, for example, the composition
\{X\mapsto Y\}\blackdiamond\{Y\mapsto X\}=\{Y\mapsto X\}.

The domain of
\{X\mapsto Y\}
is
\{X\}

and the domain of
\{Y\mapsto X\}

is
\{Y\}
, but
X
doesn’t belong to
\{Y\}
. Because
X
belongs to

\emph{dom}(\{X\mapsto Y\})\cup\emph{dom}(\{Y\mapsto X\})

but not to

\emph{dom}(\{X\mapsto Y\}\blackdiamond\{Y\mapsto X\}),

the two cannot be equal.
Similarly, the range of
\{X\mapsto Y\}
is
\{Y\}
and the range of
\{Y\mapsto X\}
is
\{X\}
, but
Y
doesn’t belong to
\{X\}
.

Permutation Substitutions and Standardizing Apart.

A
permutation
substitution merely rearranges the variable in its domain. That is, if
\pi
is a permutation substitution

\{{x}_{1}\mapsto e_{1},{x}_{2}\mapsto e_{2},\ldots,{x}_{n}\mapsto e_{n}\},
the expressions
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
are a permutation of the domain variables
\{{x}_{1},{x}_{2}\mathbin{,}\ldots,{x}_{n}\}
.
Every permutation substitution
\pi
has an
inverse
; thus
\pi^{-1}
is

\{e_{1}\mapsto{x}_{1},e_{2}\mapsto{x}_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\mapsto{x}_{n}\}.

This is a legal substitution because the expressions
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
are distinct variables. The inverse has the property that
\pi\blackdiamond\pi^{-1}=\pi^{-1}\blackdiamond\pi=\{\}
. The empty substitution
\{\}
is a permutation.

When we apply a permutation to an expression, we say that we
rename
its variables. Two permutation substitutions
{\pi}_{1}
and
{\pi}_{2}

standardize apart
two expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
if the results of applying the substitutions to the respective expressions, that is,
e_{1}\blacktriangleleft{\pi}_{1}
and
e_{2}\blacktriangleleft{\pi}_{2}
, have no variables in common. It is always possible to standardize apart two expressions. For instance, suppose
{x}_{1}
,
{x}_{2}
,
\ldots

{x}_{n}
is a complete list of all the variables that occur in either expression. Suppose
{y}_{1}
,
{y}_{2}
,
\ldots

{y}_{n}
and
{z}_{1}
,
{z}_{2}
,
\dots

{z}_{n}
are variables that are distinct from each other and from those on the original list of
{x}_{i}
’s. Then, the two permutation substitutions

{\pi}_{1}:\{{x}_{1}\mapsto{y}_{1},{y}_{1}\mapsto{x}_{1},{x}_{2}\mapsto{y}_{2},{y}_{2}\mapsto{x}_{2},\ldots,{x}_{n}\mapsto{y}_{n},{y}_{n}\mapsto{x}_{n}\}

and

{\pi}_{2}:\{{x}_{1}\mapsto{z}_{1},{z}_{1}\mapsto{x}_{1},{x}_{2}\mapsto{z}_{2},{z}_{2}\mapsto{x}_{2},\ldots,{x}_{n}\mapsto{z}_{n},{z}_{n}\mapsto{x}_{n}\}

will standardize
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
apart.
All the variables in
e_{1}\blacktriangleleft{\pi}_{1}
will be
{y}_{i}
’s, and all the variables in
e_{2}\blacktriangleleft{\pi}_{2}
will be
{z}_{i}
’s.
One can even standardize apart two expressions with a single permutation, by renaming all the variables in one of the expressions to variables that don’t occur in the other.

Idempotent Substitutions.

A substitution
\theta
is
idempotent
, written
\idem(\theta)
, if applying it twice (or more) has the same effect as applying it once; that is, if
\theta\blackdiamond\theta=\theta
. For example, the substitution
\{X\mapsto Y\}
is idempotent, because after it has been applied once, all occurrences of
X
have been removed from the expression, so subsequent applications have no effect. On the other hand, the substitution
\{X\mapsto(X{\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,}X)\}
is not idempotent, because each application doubles the number of occurrences of
X
.

A substitution is idempotent
precisely when no variable appears in both its domain and its range.
To see this, suppose
\theta
is such a substitution. Then, for any expression
e
, the result
e\blacktriangleleft\theta
of applying
\theta
to
e
contains no variables in the domain of
\theta
. Hence applying
\theta
a second time has no effect; that is
(e\blacktriangleleft\theta)\blacktriangleleft\theta=e\blacktriangleleft\theta
and hence
e\blacktriangleleft(\theta\blackdiamond\theta)=e\blacktriangleleft\theta
. Since this holds for any expression
e
, we can conclude that it
\theta\blackdiamond\theta=\theta.

To show the inverse, suppose some variable
y
is in both the domain and range of
\theta
. Then, because
y
is in the domain,
y\blacktriangleleft\theta\neq y.
And because
y
is in the range, there is a variable
x
such that
y\,\underline{\varepsilon}\,(x\blacktriangleleft\theta).
Because
y
is in its domain, applying
\theta
again to
x\blacktriangleleft\theta
alters
x\blacktriangleleft\theta
. In other words,
(x\blacktriangleleft\theta)\blacktriangleleft\theta\neq x\blacktriangleleft\theta
; that is,
x\blacktriangleleft(\theta\blackdiamond\theta)\neq x\blacktriangleleft\theta
, and therefore
\theta\blackdiamond\theta\neq\theta.;
that is,
\theta
is not idempotent.

The empty substitution
\{\}
is idempotent, but no other permutation substitution is. The composition of two idempotent unifiers is not necessarily idempotent; for example,

\{X\mapsto Y\}\blackdiamond\{Y\mapsto(X{\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,}X)\}=\{X\mapsto(X{\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,}X),Y\mapsto(X{\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,}X)\},

which has
X
in both its domain and range.

The Vars-Range Subset Properties.

For any expression
e
and proper substitution
\theta
, we can informally establish the
vars-range subset property

\emph{vars}(e\blacktriangleleft\theta)\subseteq\emph{vars}(e)\cup\emph{range}(\theta).

In other words, for a variable to occur in
e\blacktriangleleft\theta
, it must already occur in
e
or be introduced by
\theta.

In general, the subset relation in the property is not necessarily proper; for instance, if
\theta
is the empty substitution
\{\}
,
\emph{range}(\theta)
is the empty set and both sides of the property reduce to
\emph{vars}(e)
. But if
\theta
is a proper idempotent substitution that does not miss the expression
e
, we do have the
vars-range proper-subset property

\emph{vars}(e\blacktriangleleft\theta)\cup\emph{range}(\theta)\subset\emph{vars}(e)\cup\emph{range}(\theta).

For, if
\theta
does not miss
e
, there is a variable
v
that occurs in
e
and belongs to
\emph{dom}(\theta)
. Because
\theta
is idempotent,
v
does not belong to
\emph{range}(\theta)
and hence does not occur in
e\blacktriangleleft\theta
. Thus
v
belongs to the right side of the formula but not the left.

These properties hold as well if
e
is a tuple or set of expressions, rather than a single expression.
They turn out to be useful in the derivation of unification algorithms

Unifiers

A substitution
\theta
is a unifier of two expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
if applying it makes them identical, that is, if
e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta
. For example, the substitution
\{X\mapsto a,Y\mapsto b\}
is a unifier of the expression
(X\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,b)
and
(a\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,Y)
because applying it to either expression maps it into
(a\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,b)
.

Unifiers are not unique. For example,
\{X\mapsto a,Y\mapsto b,Z\mapsto c\}
is also unifier of the expression
(X\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,b)
and
(a\,\,\begin{picture}(-1.0,-3.0)\circle*{3.0}\end{picture}\,\,\,\,Y)
. The third replacement
Z\mapsto c
has no effect on either expression.

The empty substitution
\{\}
is a unifier of two expressions only if they are already identical. The improper failure substitution
\bot
, on the other hand, is a unifier of any two expressions; applying it always yields the black hole
\ast
.

We say that two expressions are
unifiable
if they have a proper unifier, i.e., a unifier other than
\bot
. For example, the two distinct constants
a
and
b
are ununifiable, because no proper substitution can make them identical. A constant and a nonatomic expression can never be unifiable, because the result of applying a proper substitution to a constant is the constant itself, an atom, while the result of applying the same substitution to a nonatomic expression is nonatomic. If one expression
e_{1}
is a proper subexpression of another
e_{2}
, that is, if
e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}
, then

(e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta)\,\varepsilon\,(e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta)
; hence the results cannot be equal and the two given expressions are ununifiable.

If
\theta
is a unifier of two expressions, the result
\theta\blackdiamond\delta
of composing
\theta
with any substitution
\delta
is also a unifier of the two expressions. Hence if two expressions are unifiable, they have an infinite set of unifiers.

For a substitution
\theta
to unify two nonatomic expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, it must unify both their left components and their right components; that is
,

\displaystyle e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left[{\begin{aligned} &\lef(e_{1})\blacktriangleleft\theta=\lef(e_{2})\blacktriangleleft\theta\uand\\
&\rig(e_{1})\blacktriangleleft\theta=\rig(e_{2})\blacktriangleleft\theta\end{aligned}}\right].

This holds even if
\theta
is the improper substitution
\bot
.

We have seen that, if two expressions are unifiable, they have an infinite set of different unifiers. But we want our unification algorithm to yield a unifier that leaves our variables as unconstrained as possible.

More Generality.

A substitution
\theta_{1}
is
strongly more general
than substitution
\theta_{2}
, written
\theta_{1}

\succsim_{gen}

\theta_{2}
, if
\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}=\theta_{2}
. In this case, we can also say that
\theta_{2}
is an
extension
of
\theta_{1}
. For example, the substitution
\{X\mapsto Y\}
is strongly more general than
\{X\mapsto A,Y\mapsto A\}
because
\{X\mapsto Y\}\blackdiamond\{X\mapsto A,Y\mapsto A\}=\{X\mapsto A,Y\mapsto A\}
.

The empty substitution
\{\}
is strongly more general
than any substitution
\theta
, because
\{\}\blackdiamond\,\theta=\theta
. However, no other substitution
\theta
is strongly more general
than the empty substitution, because
\theta\blackdiamond
{}
=\theta\neq\{\}.
On the other hand, the failure substitution
\bot
is not strongly more general
than any proper substitution
\theta
, because
\bot\blackdiamond\,\theta=\bot\neq\theta.
But any substitution
\theta
is strongly more general than the failure substitution, because
\theta\blackdiamond\bot=\bot.

The strongly more general relation has a
composition property
, namely

\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{1}\uimplies\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2},

for all substitutions
\theta_{0}
,
\theta_{1}
, and
\theta_{2}.
For if

\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{1},

that is [by the definition of
\succsim_{gen}
]

\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta_{1}=\theta_{1},

and hence

\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}=\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2},

that is [by the definition of
\succsim_{gen}
, again]

\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2},

as we wanted to show.

If
\theta
is a unifier of two expressions, then clearly any extension
of
\theta
is also a unifier.

Robinson 1965
used the term “most-general unifier” for the output of the unification algorithm, but he did not define what it meant for one substitution to be more general than another.
Manna and Waldinger 1981
said that
\theta_{1}
is more general than
\theta_{2}
if
(\exists\delta)[\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\,\delta=\theta_{2}]
holds; we call this the weakly more-general relation
\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen^{-}}\theta_{2}
. Clearly the strongly more-general relation implies this weakly more-general relation. When
\theta_{1}
is idempotent, the weakly more-general relation also implies the strongly more general relation. To see this, assume that
\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen^{-}}\theta_{2}
; that is, for some substitution
\delta
,
\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\,\delta=\theta_{2}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\,\delta

[by assumption]

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\,\delta

[by idempotence]

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}

[by assumption, again].

Hence
\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{2}
, as we wanted to show.

On the other hand, if
\theta
is the permutation
\{X\mapsto Y,\allowbreak Y\mapsto X\}
, which is not idempotent, we have
\theta\blackdiamond\theta=\{\}
, and thus
\theta\succsim_{gen^{-}}\{\}
; that is,
\theta

is weakly more general than

\{\}
. But
\theta\blackdiamond\{\}=\theta\neq\{\}
, and hence it is not true that
\theta\succsim_{gen}\{\}
; that is,
\theta
is not strongly more general than the empty substitution.

Although the stronger relation is more difficult to grasp, using it allows us to simplify the specification and shorten the proof. In this paper, we will use only the strongly more general relation, sometimes forgetting the “strongly” modifier.

Clearly, a substitution
\theta
is idempotent
precisely when it is (strongly) more general than itself, that is,
\idem(\theta)
is equivalent to
\theta\succsim_{gen}\theta
, because then
\theta\blackdiamond\theta=\theta
. Thus, while the strongly more general relation is not reflexive, it is reflexive
on idempotent substitutions.

The more-general relation can also be shown to be transitive
; that is,

(\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{2}\uand\theta_{2}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{3})\uimplies\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{3}.

For, suppose

\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{2}\uand\theta_{2}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{3}.

Then, by the definition of
\succsim_{gen}
, we have

\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}=\theta_{2}\uand\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}=\theta_{3}.

Hence

\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}=\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}

[by the supposition that [
\theta_{1}\!\blackdiamond\theta_{2}=\theta_{2}
] and thus

\theta_{1}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}=\theta_{3},

[by the supposition that
\theta_{2}\blackdiamond\theta_{3}=\theta_{3}
]. In other words,

\theta_{1}\succsim_{gen}\theta_{3},

as we wanted to show.

Relativizing the Definitions.

It is remarked that, in a proof by induction, it is sometimes easier to prove a stronger, more general theorem, to have the benefit of a stronger induction hypothesis; this is an instance of what
Pólya 1957
termed the
inventor’s paradox
, famously exploited by the Boyer-Moore [
Boyer and Moore 1979
] theorem prover. Had we initially specified that we merely wanted to find a unifier of two expressions, we would have been led to require that the unifier be most general. Then, we would have been led to further require that the unifier be idempotent. We don’t know how to automate the generalization of these specifications; we regard this as a
eureka!
step, as in program transformation
[
Burstall and Darlington 1977
]
. Perhaps a failed inductive proof will give us a hint of how we would like to strengthen the theorem; that is, how would we strengthen the corresponding induction hypothesis to allow the proof to succeed.

We now generalize the specification further. For the current derivation, we have found it better to construct an algorithm that performs unification with respect to an idempotent
environment substitution
. That is, we search for a unifier that is an extension of a given idempotent substitution
\theta_{0}
. This is a strengthening, because, when the environment
\theta
is the empty substitution
\{\}
, it reduces to the original problem, since any substitution is an extension of the empty substitution. Relative unification requires a more general specification, but a simpler proof gives us a more efficient resulting program. Indeed, in the implementation of resolution and other rules,
snark
and other theorem provers perform unification with respect to an environment substitution.

Most-General Idempotence Relation.

To specify the relative unification algorithm, it is convenient for us to introduce the
most-general idempotence relation

{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)
to stand for the condition that
the substitution

\theta

is most-general idempotent for the expressions

e_{1}
and
e_{2}
with respect to the environment substitution
\theta_{0}
, that is

\displaystyle{\mgi}(

\displaystyle\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\forall\theta^{\prime})\left[{\begin{aligned} &e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\,\uimplies\\
&\theta\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\end{aligned}}\right]\end{aligned}

The relation does not require
\theta
to be a unifier of the expressions.

Because the roles of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are interchangeable, the most-general idempotent relation has a
symmetry property

\displaystyle{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{2},e_{1},\theta).

The relation also has a kind of
reflexivity property
; when
\theta_{0}
and
\theta
are equal, the relation clearly holds, because then
\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}
appears in both the antecedent and the consequent of the implication. Hence

\displaystyle[\theta_{0}=\theta]\,\,\uimplies\,\,{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta).

In other words, the environment
\theta_{0}
itself is always most-general idempotent for any expressions.

If the two expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are ununifiable, any substitution
\theta
will satisfy the most-general idempotent relation. When the substitution
\theta^{\prime}
is the improper substitution
\bot
, the consequent
\theta\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}
of the above definition of
\mgi
is true, because any substitution
\theta
is strongly more general than the failure substitution. But if
\theta^{\prime}
is proper, because the two expressions are ununifiable, the antecedent of the definition will be false, and the implication itself will be true. In either case, if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are ununifiable, any substitution
\theta
is most-general idempotent. We call this the
ununifiable property
of
\mgi
.

For instance, if the two expressions are unequal constants, any substitution will be most-general idempotent with respect to any environment. That is,

\displaystyle{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\,\,\uimpliedby

\displaystyle[\iscnst(e_{1})\uand\iscnst(e_{2})\uand e_{1}\neq e_{2}].

Similarly, if one of the expressions is a constant
and the other is nonatomic, any substitution will be most-general idempotent; that is,

\displaystyle{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\,\,\uimpliedby

\displaystyle[\iscnst(e_{1})\uand\unot(\isatm(e_{2}))].

And if one of the expressions is a
proper subexpression
of the other, any substitution will be most-general idempotent; that is,

\displaystyle{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\,\,\uimpliedby\,\,[e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}].

While the improper failure substitution
\bot
unifies any two expressions, it will not be most-general idempotent unless the two expressions are ununifiable with respect to that environment; we call this the
failure property
of
\mgi
. For, if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are unified by a proper substitution
\theta^{\prime}
that is an extension of the environment
\theta_{0}
, the antecedent is true but
it cannot be true that the consequent
\bot\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}
holds; the failure substitution is not more general than any proper substitution. In other words, the implication

\displaystyle e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\,\uimplies

\displaystyle\bot\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}

must be false.

Replacement Property of Most-General Idempotence.

The
\mgi
replacement property

expresses the relationship between replacement and the most-general idempotent relation:

\displaystyle\mgi(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\})\uimpliedby

\displaystyle\isvar(e_{1})

for all substitutions
\theta_{0}
and expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.

To prove this, it suffices (by the definition of the relative
most-general idempotence relation

\mgi
) to
assume that, for some substitution
\theta
, variable
e_{1}
and expression
e_{2}
,

\hypertarget{ass:theta-un}{e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta}

and

{\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta},

that is [by the
definition of the relation
\succsim_{gen}
]

\hypertarget{ass:thet0-moregen}{\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta}.

We show that then

\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\}\succsim_{gen}\theta,

that is [by definition of
\succsim_{gen}
, again]

\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta.

It suffices to show [because
\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta
] that

\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta

or [by properties of equality] that

\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\}\blackdiamond\theta=\theta.

It further suffices [by the
variable equality property
for substitutions] to show that, for any variable
v
,

v\blacktriangleleft\{e_{1}\mapsto e_{2}\}\blackdiamond\theta=v\blacktriangleleft\theta.

When
v
is actually
e_{1},
this reduces [by the
replacement identity property
and the
composition property
of substitutions] to

e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta=e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta,

which we have
assumed
.

When
v
is distinct from
e_{1}
, this reduces [by the
replacement nonoccur property
] to

v\blacktriangleleft\theta=v\blacktriangleleft\theta,

which is true. This concludes the proof.

Instance Property of Most-General Idempotence.

The most-general idempotence relation has an
instance property
that is useful in the derivation of unification algorithms, namely, that

\displaystyle{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta)\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0},e_{2}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0},\theta),

that is,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\forall\theta^{\prime})\left[{\begin{aligned} &e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\,\uimplies\\
&\theta\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\end{aligned}}\right]\end{aligned}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\forall\theta^{\prime})\left[{\begin{aligned} &e_{1}\!\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\,\uimplies\\
&\theta\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}\end{aligned}}\right]\end{aligned}

for any expressions
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, and substitutions
\theta_{0}
and
\theta
. The consequents of the implications on the left and the right side of the equivalence are the same,
\theta^{\prime}\succsim_{gen}\theta
. So it suffices to show that the antecedents are equivalent. If
\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}
is false, both antecedents are false, so we may assume
\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}
, that is
\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}
, is true.

The antecedent of the right side,

e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime},

is equivalent to

e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta_{0}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}

[by the
composition property
and the
definition of
\succsim_{gen}
]
, which is equivalent to

e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}

[because
\theta_{0}\blackdiamond\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}
], which is equivalent to

e_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}=e_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta^{\prime}\,\uand\,\theta_{0}\succsim_{gen}\theta^{\prime}.

But this is the antecedent of the left side,
{\mgi}(\theta_{0},e_{1},e_{2},\theta).

A Brief Introduction to Deductive Program Synthesis

We begin by introducing the notion of the subject domain theory. We do not expect this section to serve as an introduction to logic, but we’ll set down our terminology.

Subject Domain Theory

Program synthesis requires a good deal of knowledge about the domain of application, which is provided here by the subject domain theory. This is expressed by a set of logical formulas in mathematical logic.

Formulas and Terms.

We begin with a vocabulary comprising an infinite set of constants, an infinite set of variables, an infinite set of
n
-ary function symbols, and an infinite set of
n
-ary relation symbols, for each nonnegative integer
n
, as well as some logical connectives and a conditional operator.

The
expressions
comprise
terms
and
formulas
; intuitively, the value of a formula is a truth value, either true or false, and the value of a term is an object, such as a number. We define terms and formulas together inductively. Terms comprise the constants, the variables, the result of applying an
n
-ary function symbol to
n
terms, and the conditional term
\emph{if}\,({\mathcal{P}},\allowbreak\ {{t}_{1}},\allowbreak\ {{t}_{2}})
, where
\mathcal{P}
is a formula and
{t}_{1}
and
{t}_{2}
are terms. We call
{t}_{1}
and
{t}_{2}
the
then-branch
and the
else-branch
, respectively, of the conditional term. (We sometimes write this as

(\uif\mathcal{P}\uthen t_{1}\uelse t_{2})
.

We include the equality symbol
=
as a binary relation symbol in a single term or formula, but we also use it informally in English to relate two terms.

Atomic formulas comprise
\emph{true}
,
\emph{false}
, and the result of applying an
n
-ary relation symbol to
n
terms. Formulas comprise atomic formulas and the results of applying the logical connectives
\uand
,
\uor
,
\unot
,
\uimplies
,
\uimpliedby

Ground

\uor(\mathcal{P},\,\mathcal{Q})
” and “

”) as convenient.

We also use the equivalence symbol
\Leftrightarrow
informally in English to relate two formulas.
Context will let us know if we are regarding
\Leftrightarrow
as part of a single formula or as an English abbreviation for “if and only if” to relate two formulas.
We do not include explicit quantifiers, but, as in resolution theorem proving, we apply skolemization to remove quantifiers, so this will not cost us any expressive power. A detailed treatment of skolemization appears in
[
Manna and Waldinger 1993
]
and other introductions to automated reasoning.

Truth.

A
theory
consists of a finite set of formulas, called the
axioms
of the theory. We do not assume that the axioms are consistent or logically independent. We start by introducing the notion of
truth
of a formula in a theory.

Interpretation.

A (Herbrand)
interpretation
is a possibly infinite list of ground atomic formulas, which are taken to be
true
; those ground atomic formulas not on the list are taken to be
false
. With respect to an interpretation, we can determine the truth value of a nonatomic ground formula, which contains logical connectives, by applying the usual truth tables; for instance,

\uor(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
is true if
\mathcal{P}
is true or
\mathcal{Q}
is true, and false otherwise.

We then say that an arbitrary formula (not necessarily ground) is true under the interpretation if every instance of the formula is true, where an instance is obtained by replacing every occurrence of each variable in the formula by a corresponding term, that is, by applying a proper substitution. An arbitrary formula is false under an interpretation if some instance of the formula is false. A formula is
valid
if it is true under every interpretation.

Equivalence and Equality.

Equivalence and equality are analogous; equivalence applies to formulas and equality to terms.

Equivalence.

Two formulas
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are
equivalent
under an interpretation, written
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\Leftrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, if
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are either both true or both false under the interpretation.
For instance, the double negation sentence
\unot(\unot(\mathcal{P}))
is equivalent to
\mathcal{P}.

The implication sentence
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\uimplies{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is equivalent to the sentence
\uor(\unot({\mathcal{P}}_{1}),{\mathcal{P}}_{2}).

The equivalence sentence
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\Leftrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is equivalent to the sentence

\displaystyle\uand({\mathcal{P}}_{1}\uimplies{\mathcal{P}}_{2},{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\uimplies{\mathcal{P}}_{1}),

to the sentence

\displaystyle\uand(\uor(\unot({\mathcal{P}}_{1}),{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),\uor(\unot({\mathcal{P}}_{2}),{\mathcal{P}}_{1})),

and to the sentence

\displaystyle\uor(\uand({\mathcal{P}}_{1},{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),\uand(\unot({\mathcal{P}}_{1}),\unot({\mathcal{P}}_{2}))).

Equivalence has a
substitutivity property
; namely, for any formula
\mathcal{S}\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\rangle
, if
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are equivalent, so are
\mathcal{S}\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\rangle
and
\mathcal{S}\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\rangle,
where

\mathcal{S}\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\rangle
is obtained from
\mathcal{S}\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\rangle
by replacing zero, one, or more occurrences of
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
with
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
.

Equality.

Two terms
{t}_{1}
and
{t}_{2}
are
equal
under an interpretation, written
{t}_{1}={t}_{2}
, if they satisfy the
substitutivity property
, that is, if, for any formula
\mathcal{F}\langle x\rangle
,
\mathcal{F}\langle{t}_{1}\rangle

\mathcal{F}\langle{t}_{2}\rangle
is true under the interpretation. It follows that equality has the usual properties, such as the
reflexivity property

t=t,

for all terms

symmetry property
,

({t}_{1}={t}_{2})\uimplies({t}_{2}={t}_{1}),

for all terms

and
{t}_{2}
, and the
transitivity property
,

(({t}_{1}={t}_{2})\uand({t}_{2}={t}_{3}))\uimplies({t}_{1}={t}_{3}),

for all terms
{t}_{1}
,
{t}_{2}
, and
{t}_{3}
.

Conditional Terms.

Under a given interpretation, the conditional term

\emph{if}\,({\mathcal{P}},\allowbreak\ {{t}_{1}},\allowbreak\ {{t}_{2}})

is equal to
{t}_{1}
if
\mathcal{P}
is true under the interpretation, and equal to
{t}_{2}
if
\mathcal{P}
is false. Regardless of the truth value of
\mathcal{P}
, if
{t}_{1}
and
{t}_{2}
are equal, the value of the conditional is the value of
{t}_{1}
. Thus the conditional term

is equal to

.)

Our

Expressions versus S-Expressions.

In our section on the theory of expressions and substitutions, we dealt only with S-expressions, which contain occurrences of constants but no function or relation symbols, other than

distributivity property
,

\emph{op}({t}_{1},{t}_{2},\ldots,{t}_{n})\blacktriangleleft\theta\Leftrightarrow\,\emph{op}({t}_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta,{t}_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta,\ldots,{t}_{n}\blacktriangleleft\theta),

and

\uf({t}_{1},{t}_{2},\ldots,{t}_{n})\blacktriangleleft\theta=\,\uf({t}_{1}\blacktriangleleft\theta,{t}_{2}\blacktriangleleft\theta,\ldots,{t}_{n}\blacktriangleleft\theta),

for any terms
{t}_{i}
and proper substitutions
\theta
.
But, as we remarked earlier, conventional expressions can be represented as S-expressions, as in Lisp, so this is not a substantive extension.

Models and Validity.

A
model
of a theory is an interpretation under which every axiom of the theory is true. A formula is
valid in a theory
if it is true under every model of the theory. A theory is
consistent
if it has a model.

As we have mentioned previously, recursive calls are introduced to the program being derived by application of the mathematical induction principle.

Reification.

Although we are working in a first-order logic, it will be useful to quantify over relations and to have functions that apply to relations and produce new ones. To allow this, we employ
reification
;
in other words, we allow variables and complex terms other than predicate symbols to stand for relations.
Because first-order logic does not allow anything other than a predicate symbol at the head of an atomic formula, we can chose to represent a formula
{r}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, where
{r}
is a (perhaps complex) term that stands for a relation, as

wf
​
(r,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, where
wf
is a predicate symbol. In discussing binary relations, and, especially, well-founded relations and orderings, we will use the conventional notation
x{\prec}_{r}\,y
.

Sequences.

A
sequence

\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
is a unary (one-argument) function mapping the natural numbers into terms. We say that a sequence
a^{\prime}=\langle{a}_{0}^{\prime},\,{a}_{1}^{\prime},\,{a}_{2}^{\prime},\,\ldots\rangle
is a subsequence of a sequence
a=\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
if all the elements of
a^{\prime}
occur distinctly in
a
in the same order, perhaps with intervening elements; we say that
a^{\prime}
is an
initial
subsequence of
a
if
{a}_{0}^{\prime}={a}_{0}.
A sequence
a=\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
will be said to
converge
if eventually all its elements are equal; that is, if, for some natural number
j
,
{a}_{j}={a}_{j+1}={a}_{j+2}=\ldots.
If
j
is the least such natural number, the sequence may be said to
converge at
j
.

Well-Founded Relations.

A well-known scientist
(
some say it was Bertrand Russell
)
once gave a public lecture on astronomy …. At the end of the lecture, a little old lady at the back of the room got up and said: “What you have told us is rubbish. The world is really a flat plate supported on the back of a giant tortoise.” The scientist gave a superior smile before replying, ”What is the tortoise standing on?” “You’re very clever, young man, very clever,” said the old lady. “But it’s turtles all the way down!”

—[Stephen Hawking,
A Brief History of Time
, 1988]

The form of induction we use is well-founded induction, that is, induction over a well-founded relation. A relation
{\prec}_{w}
is well-founded if it admits no infinite (strictly) decreasing sequences, i.e., no sequences
a=\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
such that
{a}_{0}\,{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{1}\,{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{2}\,{\succ}_{w}\,\ldots.
(Here,
{\succ}_{w}
is the
inverse
of
{\prec}_{w}
, defined by
x{\succ}_{w}y\Leftrightarrow y{\prec}_{w}\,x.
) For example, the less-than relation
<
is well-founded over the natural numbers (because any infinite decreasing sequence would eventually hit 0), but
<
is not well-founded over all the integers: if we include the negative integers, we can go down forever. Another example of a well-founded relation is the proper subset relation over the finite sets.

A well-founded relation
\prec
is
irreflexive
; that is,
x{\nprec}_{\,w}x
, because if
x\,{\prec}_{w}\,x
for some
x
,
\langle x,x,x,\ldots\rangle
would constitute an infinite decreasing sequence. It is also
antisymmetric
; that is,
\unot(x\,{\prec}_{w}\,y\uand y\,{\prec}_{w}\,x)
because if
x\,{\prec}_{w}\,y
and
y\,{\prec}_{w}\,x
for some
x
and
y
,
\langle x,y,x,y,x,y\ldots\rangle
would constitute an infinite decreasing sequence. We do not assume that well-founded relations are transitive; for instance, the predecessor relation
prec
, defined on the natural numbers by
\texttt{prec}(x,y)
if
x+1=y
, is well-founded but not transitive.

According to the
induction principle
over a well-founded relation

{\prec}_{w}
, if we are trying to prove a proposition
\mathcal{P}[a]
, we can assume inductively that the proposition
\mathcal{P}[x]
holds for any entity
x
such that
x{\prec}_{w}\,a
, that is, such that x is less than
a
with respect to
{\prec}_{w}
. In other words, we prove
\mathcal{P}[a]
under the induction hypothesis
x{\prec}_{w}\,a\,\uimplies\mathcal{P}[x]
.

Well-founded induction is also called
Noetherian induction
. Over the natural numbers, well-founded induction over
<
is usually called
complete induction
. Our reason for using well-founded induction is that it is domain independent and extremely general; the same induction principle applies to nonnegative integers, expressions, substitutions, and other sorts of entities.

We can give an informal justification for the well-founded induction principle as follows. Suppose that, contrary to the principle, we have a proposition
\mathcal{P}[{a}_{0}]
that is false under a given model but for which the induction hypothesis
x{\prec}_{w}\,{a}_{0}\,\uimplies\mathcal{P}[x]
is true. If
\mathcal{P}[x]
were true for every entity
x
such that
x{\prec}_{w}\,{a}_{0}
, the induction hypothesis would imply that
\mathcal{P}[{a}_{0}]
were true too, but it isn’t. Hence there must be some entity
{a}_{1}
such that
{a}_{1}{\prec}_{w}\,{a}_{0}
, that is,
{a}_{0}{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{1}
but
\mathcal{P}[{a}_{1}]
is false. Repeating the same reasoning on
{a}_{1}
, we establish the existence of an entity
{a}_{2}
such that
{a}_{1}{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{2}
but
\mathcal{P}[{a}_{2}]
is false. In this way, we can construct an infinite decreasing sequence
\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
, that is, one such that
{a}_{0}\,{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{1}\,{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{2}\,{\succ}_{w}\,\ldots,
contradicting the well-foundedness of
{\prec}_{w}
.

In a well understood theory, such as the theory of expressions, there will be an arsenal of well-founded relations.

Proper-Subexpression Relation.

The
proper-subexpression
relation
\,\varepsilon\,
is well-founded in the theory of expressions, since we only consider finite expressions. To see this, recall that a proper subexpression of a given expression must be strictly smaller that the expression; in other words,

e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}\uimplies\emph{size}(e_{1})<\emph{size}(e_{2}).

Thus, if there were an infinite decreasing sequence of expressions with respect to the proper-subexpression relation, their sizes would form an infinite decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers, which is impossible.

Constructing nontrivial programs, including the unification algorithm, requires combining known well-founded relations to construct new ones peculiarly appropriate to the program being constructed. We now discuss ways of doing this.

Induced Relation.

For any unary function symbol
g
, we define the well-founded relation
{\prec}_{g(w)}

induced by
g
and
{\prec}_{w}
by the property

x{\prec}_{g(w)}y\Leftrightarrow g(x)\,{\prec}_{w}\,g(y).

If
{\prec}_{w}
is well-founded, so is
{\prec}_{g(w)}.
For, if
{\prec}_{w}
is well-founded but
{\prec}_{g(w)}
is not. there is an infinite sequence
\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
such that

{a}_{0}\,{\succ}_{g(w)}\,{a}_{1}\,{\succ}_{g(w)}\,{a}_{2}\,{\succ}_{g(w)}\,\ldots.

But then, by the definition of the
induced relation
,

g({a}_{0})\,{\succ}_{w}\,g({a}_{1})\,{\succ}_{w}\,g({a}_{2})\,{\succ}_{w}\,\ldots;

that is, there is an infinite decreasing sequence with respect to
{\prec}_{w}
, contradicting the well-foundedness of
{\prec}_{w}
.

This is a case in which we use a complex term to stand for a relation. In the implementation, we use reification to squeeze this into first-order logic.

Vars and Size Relations.

The
vars
relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}(\subset)}
is defined as the relation induced by the function
\emph{vars}
and the subset ordering
\subset.
For two expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, we define the relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}(\subset)}
by

{e}_{1}{\prec}_{\emph{vars}(\subset)}{e}_{2}\Leftrightarrow\emph{vars}({e}_{1})\,\subset\,\emph{vars}({e}_{2}).

Because the subset ordering is well-founded on finite sets, the relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}(\subset)}
is well-founded on expressions.
We abbreviate this ordering as
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}
.

Similarly, the
size
ordering
{\prec}_{\emph{size}(<)}
is defined for expressions
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
by

{e}_{1}{\prec}_{\emph{size}(<)}{e}_{2}\Leftrightarrow\emph{size}({e}_{1})\,<\,\emph{size}({e}_{2}).

Because the less-than ordering is well-founded on the nonnegative integers, the
size
ordering is well-founded on expressions. We abbreviate this ordering as
{\prec}_{\emph{size}}
.

If
e
is a nonatomic expression, we can establish that

\lef(e){\prec}_{\emph{size}}\,e
and
\rig(e){\prec}_{\emph{size}}\,e
. This follows because
\lef(e)
and
\rig(e)
are proper subexpressions of
e
.

Weakly Decreasing Sequences.

For any relation
{\prec}_{w}
, we define its
reflexive closure

\preccurlyeq
by the property

x{\preccurlyeq}_{w}y\Leftrightarrow[x{\prec}_{w}\,y\uor x=y].

For instance, the reflexive closure
{\preccurlyeq}_{\emph{vars}}
of the vars relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}
is defined by

{e}_{1}{\preccurlyeq}_{\emph{vars}}\,{e}_{2}\Leftrightarrow[{e}_{1}{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}\,{e}_{2}\uor{e}_{1}={e}_{2}]\Leftrightarrow[\emph{vars}({e}_{1})\subset\emph{vars}({e}_{2})\uor{e}_{1}={e}_{2}].

A sequence
a=\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
is
weakly decreasing

with respect to a relation
{\prec}_{w}
if

{a}_{0}\,{\succcurlyeq}_{w}\,{a}_{1}\,{\succcurlyeq}_{w}\,{a}_{2}\,{\succcurlyeq}_{w}\,{a}_{3}\ldots.

We can prove that a sequence
a=\langle{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots\rangle
that is weakly decreasing with respect to a relation
{\prec}_{w}
either converges or contains a strictly decreasing subsequence. For suppose that
a
does not converge. We construct a strictly decreasing subsequence
b
as follows.

Take
b_{0}=a_{0}
. Since
a
is does not converge, there must be a subsequent element
a_{j}
such that
a_{0}=a_{1}=\ldots=a_{j-1}\neq a_{j}
.
Because
a
is weakly decreasing, we have
a_{0}{\succcurlyeq}_{w}a_{j}
, that is, because
a_{0}\neq a_{j}
, we have
a_{0}{\succ}_{w}\,a_{j}
. Take
b_{1}
to be
a_{j}
. Thus we have
b_{0}\succ_{w}b_{1}
.

Similarly, if we have found elements of
a
such that

b_{0}\succ b_{1}\succ\ldots\succ b_{k}
,
we can find an subsequent element
b_{k+1}
such that
b_{k}\succ b_{k+1}
.
In this way, we can we can construct an infinite decreasing subsequence of
a
.

In the case in which
\prec_{w}
is well-founded, infinite decreasing sequences do not exist. Therefore, we have the

weakly decreasing sequence property
of well-founded relations, that if
\prec_{w}
is well-founded, all weakly decreasing sequences over
\preccurlyeq
must converge.

Well-Founded Relations on Tuples.

For the synthesis of recursive programs with more than one argument, we form them into a tuple that we treat as a single entity. For this situation, it is necessary to discuss well-founded relations on tuples, which can be constructed from well-founded relations on single arguments. We begin discussing well-founded relations on pairs (2-tuples), with the understanding that this can be extended to triples or general
n
-tuples, i.e., tuples of
n
elements.

If we have a well-founded relation, we can extend it to a well-founded relation on pairs simply by ignoring one of the components. For instance, if
{\prec}_{w}
is a relation, then the relation
{\prec}_{\ufirst(w)}
on pairs induced by the function
\ufirst
satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle{x}_{1},\,{x}_{2}\rangle\,{\prec}_{\ufirst(w)}\langle{y}_{1},\,{y}_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
&\ufirst(\langle{x}_{1},\,{x}_{2}\rangle)\,{\prec}_{w}\ufirst(\langle{y}_{1},\,{y}_{2}\rangle)\Leftrightarrow\\
&{x}_{1}{\prec}_{w}{y}_{1}\end{split}

We have shown that if a relation is well-founded, the relation induced on it by a function is also well-founded. Thus, if
{\prec}_{w}
is well-founded, so is
{\prec}_{\ufirst(w)}
. Intuitively speaking, any infinite sequence of pairs that is decreasing if we ignore the second elements will correspond to an infinite decreasing sequence of individual elements; thus if the sequence of individual elements cannot exist, neither can the sequence of pairs.

Similarly, if we have a well-founded relation on pairs, we can induce a corresponding well-founded relation on triples, and so on.

In the theory of expressions, the
vars
relation, satisfying the property

\langle{d}_{1},{d}_{2}\rangle{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}\langle{e}_{1},{e}_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\emph{vars}(\langle{d}_{1},{d}_{2}\rangle)\,\subset\,\emph{vars}(\langle{e}_{1},{e}_{2}\rangle),

and the
size
relation, satisfying the property

\langle{d}_{1},{d}_{2}\rangle{\prec}_{\emph{size}}\langle{e}_{1},{e}_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\emph{size}(\langle{d}_{1},{d}_{2}\rangle)<\emph{size}(\langle{e}_{1},{e}_{2}\rangle),

are well-founded on tuples of expressions. These are the relations induced on pairs of expressions by the functions
\emph{vars}
and
\emph{size}
and the well-founded relations
\subset
and
<
, respectively.

Because we know that, if the expression
e
is nonatomic, we have
\lef(e)\,\varepsilon\,e
and
\rig(e)\,\varepsilon\,e,
we can establish that

\langle\lef(e_{1}),\lef(e_{2})\rangle{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle

and

\langle\rig(e_{1}),\rig(e_{2})\rangle{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle.

Lexicographic Combination of Well-Founded Relations.

Little man can beat a big man every time if he’s in the right and keeps a-coming.

—Texas Rangers’ creed.

For any two binary relations
{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}
and
{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}
, we define their
lexicographic combination

{\prec}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}
on pairs by the property

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle{x}_{1},\,{x}_{2}\rangle\,{\prec}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}\langle{y}_{1},\,{y}_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}\,\uor\,\\
&{x}_{1}={y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}\,{y}_{2}\end{aligned}\right).\end{split}

Sometimes we find it more convenient to use the logically equivalent

reflexive lexicographic property

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle{x}_{1},\,{x}_{2}\rangle\,{\prec}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}\langle{y}_{1},\,{y}_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}\,\uor\,\\
&{x}_{1}{\preccurlyeq}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}\,{y}_{2}\end{aligned}\right).\end{split}

It is easy to see that the definition implies the reflexive lexicographic property, because
{x}_{1}={y}_{1}
implies
{x}_{1}{\preccurlyeq}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}
.
In the other direction, if the right side of the reflexive lexicographic property holds and the first disjunct
{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}
is true, then the right side of the definition holds.
But suppose the right side of the reflexive lexicographic property holds but the first disjunct
{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}
is false; then the second disjunct

[{x}_{1}{\preccurlyeq}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}\,{y}_{2}]\bigr]

is true and hence [because
{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}
is false]

[{x}_{1}={y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}\,{y}_{2}].

Hence the right side of the definition holds.

We can extend this definition to apply to three or more binary relations. For instance, for three relations the definition is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle{x}_{1},\,{x}_{2},\,{x}_{3}\rangle\,{\prec}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2},{w}_{3})}\langle{y}_{1},\,{y}_{2},\,{y}_{3}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &{x}_{1}{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}\,{y}_{1}\,\uor\,\\
&{x}_{1}={y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}\,{y}_{2}\,\uor\,\\
&{x}_{1}={y}_{1}\uand{x}_{2}={y}_{2}\uand{x}_{3}{\prec}_{{w}_{3}}\,{y}_{3}\end{aligned}\right).\end{split}

If
{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}
and
{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}
are both well-founded relations, so is their lexicographic combination
{\prec}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}
. For suppose, to the contrary, there is an infinite decreasing sequence of pairs
\{\langle{a}_{0},{b}_{0}\rangle,\langle{a}_{1},{b}_{1}\rangle,\langle{a}_{2},{b}_{2}\rangle,\ldots\rangle
, that is, one such that

\langle{a}_{0},{b}_{0}\rangle\,{\succ}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}\,\langle{a}_{1},{b}_{1}\rangle\,\allowbreak{\succ}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}\,\allowbreak\langle{a}_{2},{b}_{2}\rangle\,{\succ}_{\lex({w}_{1},{w}_{2})}\,\ldots.

By the definition of the
lexicographic combination on pairs
, we know that, for each natural number
i
, we have either

\displaystyle{a}_{i}{\succ}_{{w}_{1}}\,{a}_{i+1}\uor

\displaystyle[\text{the ${\prec}_{{w}_{1}}$ case for $i$}]

\displaystyle{a}_{i}={a}_{i+1}\uand{b}_{i}{\succ}_{{w}_{2}}\,{b}_{i+1})

\displaystyle[\text{the ${\prec}_{{w}_{2}}$ case for $i$}].

In either case, we have
{a}_{i}{\succcurlyeq}_{{w}_{1}}\,{a}_{i+1}
for each natural number
i
. But since
{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}
is well-founded, we know (from the
weakly decreasing sequence property
) that the sequence cannot weakly decrease indefinitely and hence must converge at some natural number
j
; thus for each
k
such that
k\geq j,
we have
{a}_{k}={a}_{k+1}.
But then (by the
irreflexivity of well-founded relations
), for each
k
such that
k\geq j,
the
{\prec}_{{w}_{1}}
case for
k
cannot hold, and therefore the
{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}
case for
k
must hold.
Hence, for each
k
such that
k\geq j,
we have
{b}_{k}{\succ}_{{w}_{2}}\,{b}_{k+1},
but this contradicts the well-foundedness of
{\prec}_{{w}_{2}}
. Therefore no decreasing sequence of pairs can exist under the lexicographic combination, so the lexicographic combination must be well-founded.

Lexicographic Combination of Induced Relations.

In the derivation of the unification algorithm, we find it convenient to use a

lexicographic combination of induced relations.
While the construction is quite general, we describe it in a special case that resembles its use in the unification derivation. We define the lexicographic combination
{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}
of the
vars
relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}}
and the
size
relation
{\prec}_{\emph{size}}
by the property

\displaystyle\begin{split}&e_{1}\,{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}\,e_{2}\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &\emph{vars}(e_{1})\subset\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\,\uor\,\\
&\emph{vars}(e_{1})=\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\uand\emph{size}(e_{1})<\,size(e_{2})\,\end{aligned}\right).\end{split}

In other words, with respect to the lexicographic combination,

e_{1}
is less than
e_{2}
if the variable set of
e_{1}
is a proper subset of variable set of
e_{2}
, or if the variable set of
e_{1}
equals the variable set of
e_{2}
and the size of
e_{1}
is less than the size of
e_{2}
.

This is well-founded, because it is the relation induced by a function on simpler well-founded relation. The function is the function
\emph{vars-size}
, defined by

vars\!\!-\!\!size(e)=\langle vars(e),size(e)\rangle,

which maps any expression into the pair of its variable set and its size.
The simpler well-founded relation is the lexicographic relation
{\prec}_{\lex(\subset,<)}
,
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle s_{1},n_{1}\rangle\,{\prec}_{\lex(\subset,<)}\,\langle s_{2},n_{2}\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &s_{1}\subset\,s_{2}\,\uor\,\\
&s_{1}=\,s_{2}\uand n_{1}<\,n_{2}\,\end{aligned}\right),\end{split}

which is the lexicographic combination of the proper-subset relation
\subset
on the finite sets and the less-than relation
<
on the nonnegative integers.

The relation
{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}
is equivalent to the relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars-size}(\lex(\subset,<))}
induced by function

\emph{vars-size}
on the above relation
{\prec}_{\lex(\subset,<)}
, because

e_{1}{\prec}_{\emph{vars-size}(\lex(\subset,<))}e_{2}

is equivalent [by the
definition of induced relations
] to

\emph{vars-size}(e_{1}){\prec}_{\lex(\subset,<)}\emph{vars-size}(e_{2}),

which is equivalent [by the
definition of
\emph{vars-size}
] to

\langle\emph{vars}(e_{1}),\emph{size}(e_{1})\rangle\,{\prec}_{\lex(\subset,<)}\langle\emph{vars}(e_{2}),\emph{size}(e_{2})\rangle,

which is equivalent [by the
definition of
\lex(\subset,<)
] to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\begin{aligned} &\emph{vars}(e_{1})\subset\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\,\uor\,\\
&\emph{vars}(e_{1})=\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\uand\emph{size}(e_{1})<\,\emph{size}(e_{2})\,\end{aligned}\right),\end{split}

which is equivalent [by the
definition of
{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}
] to

e_{1}\,{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}\,e_{2}.

Because the component relations
<
and
\subset
are well founded on finite sets and nonnegative integers, their lexicographic combination is well-founded on the pairs, and so is the relation induced by the function
\emph{vars-size}
onto the lexicographic relation.

In our derivation, we actually use the equivalent
reflexive lexicographic

version of the lexicographic combination of relations,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&e_{1}\,{\prec}_{\lex(\emph{vars},\emph{size})}\,e_{2}\Leftrightarrow\\
&\left(\begin{aligned} &\emph{vars}(e_{1})\subset\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\,\uor\,\\
&\emph{vars}(e_{1})\subseteq\,\emph{vars}(e_{2})\uand\emph{size}(e_{1})<\,size(e_{2})\,\end{aligned}\right).\end{split}

Specifications and Programs

For this paper, we deal with a particular form of specification and program.

Specifications.

We restrict our attention to the synthesis of applicative (side-effect-free) programs from declarative specifications. We begin with a specification of the form

\uf(a)\Lleftarrow\operatorname{find}Z\operatorname{such}\operatorname{that}\mathscr{Q}[a,\,Z],

where
a
is the input parameter and
Z
is the output. In other words, for a given input entity
a
, we want to find output entity
Z
that satisfies the input-output condition
\mathscr{Q}[a,\,Z]
; that entity will be the output produced by the program
\uf(a)
we are synthesizing. The input-output condition
\mathscr{Q}[a,\,Z]
is a formula that contains no variable other than
Z
. (Because we allow tuples of entities, all this extends to allow multiple inputs and outputs, too, or none at all.)

Primitive Symbols.

We assume that logical connectives, the conditional operator, and certain of the constant, function, and relation symbols of the theory have been declared to be
primitive
; intuitively, this means that we assume we know how to compute them, and we allow them to appear in the program we are constructing. A term or formula is said to be
primitive
if every constant, function, or relation symbol it contains is primitive. A primitive term can turn out to be equal to a nonprimitive term, even though we know how to compute one but not the other.

Most skolem functions are not primitive; they yield an entity we know exists but may not know how to find. There may also be other symbols that we include in the specification or in an axiom that we do not expect to appear in a final program. For instance, if we are constructing a program to test if a natural number is prime, we might include the symbol
\emph{prime}
in the specification but expect it to be deconstructed and not to appear explicitly in the final program. Thus, a computable term may be nonprimitive.

Programs.

Our programs are of the form

\uf(a)=t,

where
t
is a primitive term, called the
body
of the program. Such a program is said to satisfy the above specification if, for every model of the theory, the corresponding instance
\mathscr{Q}[a,\,t]
of the input-output condition is true.

We require that the output entries be primitive to ensure that the program is something we know how to compute.
We do not require the body
t
of the program to be ground (variable-free); if the body contains a variable, that variable can be replaced by any primitive ground term. By not instantiating the variable, we leave the program open to a wider variety of subsequent refinements and optimizations.

Deductive Tableaus

Resolution theorem provers such as
snark
are refutation procedures, in which our conjectured theorem, and all its subgoals, are negated and put into a clausal form, in which
\uand
,
\uor
, and
\unot
are the only logical connectives. While this transformation allows efficient implementation, it also makes proofs difficult for people to follow. In our presentation, we introduce a notation, the
deductive tableau
, which retains a distinction between assertions and goals, and allows the implication (
\uimplies
), reverse implication (
\uimpliedby
), and equivalence (
\Leftrightarrow
) in formulas. The tableau also provides a representation for the incremental construction of a program as the proof proceeds.

The Structure.

A derivation from a specification

is carried out with a tableau structure, a set of
assertions
and
goals
, which are formulas, each with an optional associated
output entry
, a term.

We introduce some apparatus to provide the meaning of a tableau with respect to the given specification.

Allowable Outputs.

For any interpretation, a term is an
allowable output
of the tableau if it is primitive and is an instance

of an output entry
{t}_{i}
corresponding to either

\bullet

a goal

of the tableau such that the corresponding instance

\bullet

an assertion

of the tableau such that the corresponding instance

is false under the model.

If a goal row has no output entry, any term is allowable under an interpretation for which the goal is true. If an assertion has no output entry, any term is allowable under an interpretation for which the assertion is false.

A transformation on tableaus preserves allowable output if, for a given interpretation, the allowable outputs before the transformation equal the allowable outputs afterwards.

Correctness Condition of a Tableau.

In a given theory, with respect to a given specification, a tableau is said to satisfy the
correctness condition
for a given model
\mathcal{M}
for the theory if any term that is allowed under
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the input-output condition. A tableau is
correct
for a theory and specification if it satisfies the correctness condition for any model of the theory.

A transformation on tableaus
preserves correctness with respect to a theory and specification
if, for a given model, a term has the correctness condition before the transformation precisely when it has the correctness condition afterwards. A transformation that preserves output entries for a model will also preserve the correctness condition. Consequently, if one tableau is correct in the theory with respect to the specification, so is the other.

Duality.

There is a
duality property
between assertions and goals; in fact, any row

\mathcal{A}

t

can be replaced by the row

\unot(\mathcal{A})

t

while preserving the allowable outputs. For, if there is a term that is an instance

(\unot\,(\mathcal{A}))\blacktriangleleft\theta

\unot(\mathcal{A}\blacktriangleleft\theta)

\mathcal{A}\blacktriangleleft\theta
, is false.

Because of duality, we could have phrased a derivation using only assertions, or only goals. Indeed, the
snark
implementation of the deductive tableau uses only assertions; goals are negated, translated into clausal form, and put into the assertion column. But the distinction between assertions and goals can make a derivation easier to follow. Typically, forward reasoning is conducted in the assertion column and backward reasoning in the goal column.

Splitting Rules.

We can split assertions that are conjunctions and goals that are disjunctions or implications into their components without changing the outputs allowed. For instance, according to the

, a goal row

\mathcal{A}

There is also an analogous

that decomposes an assertion that contains a conjunction into two (or more) assertions, and an

that decomposes a goal that contains a disjunction into two (or more) goals, without altering the output entries. In our

While in implementations, implication (

According to the

Variables in goals behave as if they were existentially quantified, while in assertions they appear to be universally quantified; we use upper-case symbols for variables, to distinguish them. During a proof, in assertions or goals, our rules will be able to replace variables with other terms.

When the substitution is a permutation

which (because

By

Thus, if a tableau contains a goal row, an instance of that row can be added without changing the allowable outputs, and the row may be replaced by a row to which a permutation substitution has been applied to its goal and its output entry.

In presenting a derivation, we freely rename the variables in a row to improve clarity.

Orphaned Output Entries and Valid Assertions.

If the output entry of a goal row

\mathcal{G}

V

consists of a new variable

, one that does not occur elsewhere in that row, then, according to the
orphaned output-entry property
, we can drop the output entry and replace the row with

\mathcal{G}

.

For, if
\mathcal{G}\blacktriangleleft\theta
is true under an interpretation, the new row will allow any primitive term

Since

is t, the replaced row also allows any primitive term.

If a tableau row has multiple output entries, we can drop any of them if it is a variable that does not occur elsewhere in the row (including another output column.)

A formula that is valid in the theory can never be false in any model for the theory; hence that formula can be added to a tableau as an assertion without changing the correctness of the tableau with respect to the theory and given specification. It never allows any new output entries in any model.
Therefore, for a given theory, by the
valid-formula-introduction property
, we may include in our tableau any formula that is a definition or axiom or has been previously proved in that theory. For instance, we have established in the theory of substitutions that a substitution is idempotent precisely when it is more-general-idempotent than itself. Thus, we can include in a tableau for the theory the assertion

\displaystyle\idem(\Theta)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Theta\succsim_{gen}\Theta

.

We have made the substitution
\Theta
a variable so the assertion will behave as if the substitution is universally quantified;
\Theta
is the upper-case
\theta
.

For the derivation of the unification algorithm, we have proved the validity of all the added assertions that were not axiomatic, either by hand or by a separate deductive-tableau proof. Adding a nonvalid assertion compromises the correctness of the final program.

A deductive-tableau program derivation framework applies

Case Analysis and Conditional Program Formation

In a deductive approach, a conditional program structure is introduced as a result of a case analysis in the proof.
We describe the resolution rule and the equivalence and equality replacement rules, all of which perform case analysis and can introduce conditionals.

Resolution Rule.

Once you eliminate the impossible, whatever remains, no matter how improbable, must be the truth.

—Arthur Conan Doyle,
The Case-Book of Sherlock Holmes
, 1927

Paradoxically, our derivation of the unification algorithm requires the resolution and equivalence and equality replacement rules, but these rules depend on the unification algorithm.

We will first describe the resolution rule as it applies to two goals, but, by

Mathematical Induction and Recursive Program Formation

We now see how the well-founded induction principle is integrated into the deductive tableau. In the deductive tableau framework, we begin with the initial tableau

Assertions

Goals

\uf(a)

\mathscr{Q}[a,Z]

Z

In other words, for a given input entity
a
, we are constructing a program
\uf
that will yield an output
Z
that satisfies the input-output condition
\mathscr{Q}[a,Z]
. For a well-founded relation
{\prec}_{w}
, we can conduct the proof under the induction hypothesis that recursive calls to the program
\uf(x)
we are constructing will satisfy the input-output condition
\mathscr{Q}[x,\,\uf(x)]
for any input
x
such that
x\,{\prec}_{w}\,a.
We add this
induction hypothesis
to the initial tableau as an assertion:

Assertions

Goals

\uf(a)

\mathscr{Q}[a,Z]

Z

X{\prec}_{w}\,a\,\uimplies\mathscr{Q}[X,\,\uf(X)]

In other words, instead of proving the desired conclusion itself, we prove the inductive step, which says that the induction hypothesis implies the desired conclusion.

Using the induction hypothesis in a proof can introduce a recursive call into the program being derived. However, including the induction hypothesis in the tableau does not force us to derive a recursive program. If we don’t use the induction hypothesis in the derivation proof, the program we derive won’t use recursion.

Use of the well-founded induction principle provides a termination argument for the derived program. If the program
\uf(a)
invokes a recursive call
\uf(X)
, we require that
X{\prec}_{w}\,a
, that is,
a{\succ}_{w}\,X
. If there were a nonterminating computation in which
\uf({a}_{0})
invokes
\uf({a}_{1})
, which invokes
\uf({a}_{2})
, and so forth, the sequence of arguments
{a}_{0},{a}_{1},{a}_{2},\ldots
would constitute an infinite decreasing sequence, because
{a}_{0}{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{1}{\succ}_{w}\,{a}_{2},\ldots,
contradicting the well-foundedness of
{\prec}_{w}
. The program may still fail to terminate for cases in which the specification does not restrict the output, e.g, if the programs’s input is illegal.

The choice of the well-founded relation is not straightforward. Generally, we cannot anticipate what relation to use until the proof is underway.
Manna and Waldinger 1981
developed a complete proof in which the well-founded relation was unspecified; only then, when one could one see what properties the relation had to satisfy, was it possible to construct the appropriate relation.

For an automatic system, we can expect the formulator of an subject domain theory to provide properties of the most basic well-founded relations in that theory; e.g., for the theory of expressions and substitutions, we would be given properties of the
\emph{vars}
relation
{\prec}_{\emph{vars}(\subset)}
and the
\emph{size}
relation
{\prec}_{\emph{size}(<)}
. But any particular algorithm may require some combination of the basic relations for its derivation and termination proof. We can reasonably hope an automatic system would discover this combination, but we have not yet introduced the necessary apparatus; we regard this as a challenging topic for future research.

Finding these well-founded relations can be challenging for people. For instance, the associative-commutative unification algorithm, which allows us to take into account the associativity and commutativity of function symbols in the unified expressions
[
Stickel 1981
;
Livesey and Siekmann 1976
]
was published (and used) years before the well-founded relation was discovered that allowed its termination to be established
[
Fages 1987
]
.

For the derivation discussed in this paper, we provide a successful well-founded relation as an unautomated “eureka” step.

We only apply induction to the initial tableau; if induction needs to be applied to subsequent rows, this must be done in a separate subsidiary tableau, and the results of that derivation may be added to the main tableau as a new assertion. We have not automated this step; it may be regarded as a topic for future research.

Introduction of the Occurs Check

A unification algorithm must fail if one of its argument expressions is a proper subexpression of the other; unless infinite expressions are allowed, there is no proper substitution that unifies them. We have established that, in any environment, if one expression is a subexpression of another, although no substitution will be a unifier, any substitution will be most-general idempotent. Thus, we can use the assertion

Introduction of the Replacement

T

The SNARK Proof.

The proof we have presented is based on the
snark
proof, but it has been altered for readability.
The original
snark
proof is a refutation, so the conjecture is negated and
snark
derives a contradiction. All the rows are in clausal form, and in
snark
syntax, which is based on Lisp syntax.
The proof was found in some 10 seconds, but this number is not really meaningful. Presumably, more modern theorem provers would be faster, but if we extended the theory with additional axioms to enable the construction of other algorithms, all proof times could be longer.

No changes were made in
snark
for finding the derivation, but some modifications were made to the answer extraction mechanism, to allow it to delete orphaned output entries. We were able to alter its strategy by providing weights on the symbols in the theory’s vocabulary and an ordering between them. The weights determined the which row is worked on next, and the ordering determined which part of a given row was the focus of attention. The ordering on symbols we employed was the
recursive path ordering

[
Dershowitz 1982
]
.

The
final program
was subjected to automatic simplification; e.g., redundant tests were eliminated.
snark
was able to find many proofs for the theorem, each giving a different unification algorithm. By altering the symbol ordering, we were able to influence the algorithm
snark
discovered. For example, in the algorithm we presented, we test if the environment is a proper substitution,
\emph{is-proper}(\theta_{0})
, before we perform the occurs check,
e_{1}\,\varepsilon\,e_{2}
. This is because in the symbol ordering we have declared, the properness symbol
\emph{is-proper}
is less than the occurrence symbol
\,\varepsilon\,
. When we reverse this ordering, we get an algorithm in which we perform the occur check before we test if the environment is proper. Some orderings give us longer proof searches, or no proof at all; some orderings give better programs than others.

Discussion

As we have remarked, the proof from which the program is extracted establishes the correctness of the derived program and also provides its rationale, an explanation of its working. Of course, the correctness of the program depends on the correctness of the specification and the assertions we provide; if these have errors, all bets are off.

The body of knowledge required for the derivation is surprisingly large and assembling it was labor intensive. One may ask what the point is of devoting so much effort toward constructing a program that is already known. Furthermore, the automatic construction of the unification algorithm itself required a unification algorithm.

For one thing, the derivation is useful as a case study; many of the problems that arise, such as the discovery of a well-founded relation or the generalization of the specification appear in other program synthesis problems.

It has been found that theory-specific unification algorithms (e.g. associative-commutative unification
[
Stickel 1981
;
Livesey and Siekmann 1976
]
, higher-order unification
[
Huet, G. 1975
]
) can allow a theorem prover in that theory to find proofs more quickly than if the properties of the relevant relation and function symbols are represented by axioms. If one could construct the appropriate unification algorithm automatically, it would be possible to discard those axioms mid-proof and replace the unification algorithm of the theorem prover with the newly synthesized one, completing the proof with the new algorithm. For some theories there is no single most-general unifier; one must find a set or stream of unifiers. This must be reflected in the specification, and the theorem prover must be prepared to incorporate such an algorithm.

Also, the same knowledge may allow the derivation of many algorithms, including other unification algorithms and, presumably, other algorithms with the same subject domain, such as pattern matchers and anti-unification
[
Plotkin 1970
]
and disunification
[
Comon 1994
]
algorithms. We may look forward to a day in which program synthesis systems improve and specialize themselves in the course of solving a particular programming problem.

The derivation was challenging for
snark
to discover; perhaps more modern theorem provers would have an easier time of it. For instance, in current work, the contemporary theorem prover
Vampire

[
Hozzová, P., et al. 2024
]
is being extended and applied to program synthesis. The rules of Vampire are similar to those of
snark
, but it performs many more inferences per second. Vampire employs the Green [
Green 1969
] answer literal in place of
snark
’s output columns. As we do, it introduces a new assertion to play the role of the induction hypothesis in an induction proof.

Several decisions we made in the design of the theory of expressions were taken to reduce the search space. We specified the more-general idempotence relation so that it would imply idempotence of the unifier—this meant that we did not have to include idempotence as a separate condition. We decided to treat the failure indicator (
\bot
) as a special substitution which yielded the black hole constant (
\ast
) when applied to any expression. This meant that we did not have to treat ununifiable expressions as a special case. All of this led to a shorter specification and a correspondingly shorter proof, which was easier to discover.

We did not attempt to discover the proof from first principles—rather we included lemmas that described the properties of the relations in our specification. (In the same way, a number theorist does not attempt to prove a complex theorem using the basic axioms, such as the Peano postulates.) In proving the main theorem,
snark
did not use the
equality replacement rule
, which coincides with the paramodulation rule in resolution theorem provers. We found that we got better performance when we dispensed with the rule and instead included equality substitutivity properties for the principal function and relation symbols. At this point, all the lemmas we used have been proved, either by
snark
or by hand. In proving the lemmas, we did use the
equality replacement rule
.

Notes on Large Language Models.

Recently, impressive results have been obtained using
large language models

[
Wikimedia Foundation 2023
]
, which apply neural net technology to very large collections of information, including text, programs, and images. Neural-net-based systems are now superior to humans at chess and go. They do a decent job of translating languages. Some of these systems will produce code in a variety of subject domains and programming languages, and are being used to assist professional programmers as a time saver. The systems are particularly effective in recovering known algorithms that have been documented in the literature.

Unfortunately, large language models have been known to pick up erroneous information from their data sources, and confabulate information when it seems opportune. They can invent plausible references to articles that do not exist, which appear in journals that also do not exist. One system was unsure about whether “seven” was greater than “eight”; another insisted that 6 was a prime, even after it was told that 2 times 3 is 6. In our own experiments, a system was unsure whether, say, December 31, 1999 came before or after January 1, 2000. While the capabilities of these systems are constantly improving, we are not at the point at which we can trust their results. Nor do they always explain or justify their findings.

This brings up the question of whether we can combine the virtues of large language models and deductive methods.
One obstacle for a theorem proving approach is collecting and encoding as axioms in logic the knowledge we require to construct a program; perhaps some of this effort could be relegated to a large language model, with the resulting theory tested for inconsistency and otherwise validated by a theorem prover. The task of formulating a specification in logic is an obstacle to all of us; perhaps an LLM could perform this, with the resulting formula translated back into natural language for confirmation by the user. Also, determining the settings a theorem prover requires to be effective is something of a black art; in
snark
we had to determine experimentally the weights we assigned to symbols and the ordering we applied between them. A large language model might be effective at this; it doesn’t seem very different from playing a game.

This is an active area of research; for instance, the system LeanDojo
[
Yang et al. 2023
]
has been successful in applying a large language model to guide the search in the interactive theorem prover Lean
[
Avigad et al., 2015
]
. But anything we say here is already out of date, if we’re lucky.
