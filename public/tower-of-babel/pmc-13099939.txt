Efficacy and safety of mavacamten in non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Muhammad Abdullah, Husna Irfan Thalib, Mustafa Bin Tahir, Noman Khalid, Ahmad Mushtaq, Muhammad Adil Afzal, Michael D Moore, Nagaraja Sharma, Salman Muddassir, Michael Parker Ayers, Mazen Albaghdadi, Wilbert S Aronow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (nHCM) remains a therapeutic challenge, with limited pharmacological options beyond symptomatic management. Mavacamten, a selective allosteric cardiac myosin inhibitor, has shown benefit in obstructive HCM, but its role in nHCM is uncertain.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted up to November 2025 for placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling patients with nHCM. Outcomes included Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) score, peak oxygen consumption (pVO2), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Pooled mean differences (MDs) and risk ratios (RRs) were estimated using random- or fixed-effects models based on heterogeneity.ResultsTwo RCTs (n = 639) met inclusion criteria. Mavacamten showed a nonsignificant improvement in KCCQ (MD: 2.12; 95% CI: –0.98 to 5.22; I2 = 0%) and peak oxygen consumption (MD: 0.36 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: –0.20 to 0.92; I2 = 0%). No significant difference was observed in NYHA class improvement (RR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.93 to 1.43; I2 = 0%). TEAE incidence was similar between groups (RR: 1.11; 95% CI: 0.34 to 3.58; I2 = 40.8%). Atrial fibrillation occurred more frequently with mavacamten (RR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.28 to 1.56), whereas palpitations and dizziness did not differ significantly.ConclusionsMavacamten does not significantly enhance symptoms, functional capacity, or health status in nHCM, though safety is comparable to placebo. Ongoing monitoring for arrhythmias and reduced ejection fraction is essential. Larger, longer-term studies are needed to clarify its role in this population.Keywords: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, non-obstructive HCM, Mavacamten, Cardiac myosin inhibitors, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is an inherited cardiac disease defined by left ventricular hypertrophy in the absence of abnormal loading. Its clinical course is influenced by sarcomeric hypercontractility, diastolic dysfunction, myocardial ischemia, and atrial or ventricular arrhythmias [1], [2]. Pharmacological treatment has improved for obstructive HCM (oHCM), but patients with non-obstructive HCM (nHCM) have limited therapeutic options and remain highly symptomatic [1].

Mavacamten is a selective allosteric cardiac myosin inhibitor that stabilizes the relaxed state of β-cardiac myosin. By reducing actin–myosin cross-bridge formation, it lowers contractility and improves myocardial relaxation [3], [4]. Its clinical benefit was first established in oHCM. In the EXPLORER-HCM trial, mavacamten improved exercise capacity, health status, and LVOT gradients compared with placebo, supporting both its mechanism and therapeutic value in obstructive disease [5].

The effect in nHCM is less certain. Without dynamic LVOT obstruction, disease expression is driven more by diastolic dysfunction, microvascular ischemia, and fibrosis than by excess systolic contractility [1]. In the Phase II MAVERICK-HCM trial, symptomatic patients with preserved ejection fraction and elevated biomarkers showed reductions in NT-proBNP and cardiac troponin I with mavacamten, consistent with lower wall stress. However, the study was designed primarily for safety and dose finding rather than functional outcomes [6]. Further analyses confirmed biomarker and diastolic effects but did not clarify whether these translate into clinical improvement [7].

The Phase III ODYSSEY-HCM trial evaluated the effect of mavacamten on exercise capacity (peak VO2) and health status (KCCQ) in symptomatic nHCM. Baseline characteristics and exploratory echocardiographic results have been reported, showing some favorable changes in diastolic and atrial function, as well as reversible reductions in ejection fraction [8], [9]. However, peer-reviewed reports indicate that the trial did not meet its primary endpoints, raising doubts about the efficacy of myosin inhibition in nHCM [10].

This meta-analysis aims to examine the efficacy and safety of mavacamten in nHCM, focusing on functional capacity, patient-reported outcomes, biomarkers, and adverse events, to provide a clear assessment of its role in the patient population.

Methods

Literature search

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guideline recommendations. PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched systematically up to November 1, 2025. The search strategy combined the terms non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, mavacamten, and randomized controlled trial. References of relevant publications and abstracts from major cardiology conferences were also screened to ensure completeness.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were randomized, placebo-controlled trials enrolling patients with non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Trials were required to report at least one of the following outcomes: Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) score, peak oxygen consumption (pVO2), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, or treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Trials restricted to obstructive HCM or those not reporting relevant endpoints were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary patient-reported outcome was the KCCQ clinical summary score, a validated measure of health status in cardiomyopathy. Functional capacity was evaluated by the change in peak oxygen consumption (pVO2) measured during cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Symptomatic status was assessed by change in NYHA functional class. Safety was assessed by the proportion of patients experiencing at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), as defined by each trial.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators using a standardized form. Extracted information included study design, trial phase, duration of follow-up, sample size, mean age, sex distribution, study setting, and clinical outcomes. Continuous outcomes (KCCQ and pVO2) were recorded as mean changes from baseline with standard deviations. Dichotomous outcomes (NYHA improvement and ≥ 1 TEAE) were extracted as numbers of events and denominators. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included trials was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. Domains assessed included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was judged as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. The overall judgment for each study was determined by the highest level of risk across domains.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using meta package in R (version 4.2.0). For continuous outcomes, pooled mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were estimated. Fixed-effects models were applied when heterogeneity was low, whereas random-effects models were used when heterogeneity was moderate or greater. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. Prediction intervals were calculated to estimate the expected range of treatment effects in future studies. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 906 records were identified through database searches including PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Clinicaltrials.gov. After removing 413 duplicates, 493 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 467 records were excluded due to irrelevance to the research question. The remaining 26 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 24 studies were excluded. Finally, 2 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

MAVERICK-HCM was a phase II randomized controlled trial that enrolled 59 patients with symptomatic non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy across multiple centers in the United States. The mean age of participants was 54 years, and 58% were women. Patients were followed for 16 weeks, and outcomes included changes in Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) scores, peak oxygen consumption (pVO2), New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs).

ODYSSEY-HCM, a phase III global trial, randomized 580 patients from approximately 22 countries to mavacamten or placebo, with follow-up extended to 48 weeks. The mean age of participants was 56 years, and 46% were women. As in MAVERICK-HCM, the primary outcomes included health status assessed by KCCQ, exercise capacity measured by pVO2, NYHA functional class, and safety endpoints.

Across both studies (Table 1), mean changes in KCCQ were numerically higher with mavacamten compared with placebo, though the between-group differences were small and variable. Exercise capacity improved only modestly, with pVO2 changes of less than 1 mL/kg/min in either group. The proportion of patients experiencing NYHA class improvement was similar in the treatment and control arms in both trials. Adverse events were common, occurring in over 80% of patients, but the frequency was comparable between groups. These trial-level findings provided the basis for the pooled meta-analyses reported below.

Efficacy outcomes

Kansas City cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ)

Across MAVERICK-HCM and ODYSSEY-HCM, the pooled mean difference was 2.12 points (95% CI: –0.98 to 5.22), favoring treatment but not statistically significant (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity was negligible (I2 = 0.0%), while prediction intervals (–17.98 to 22.22) suggested wide uncertainty. These results indicate that mavacamten did not confer consistent improvement in patient-reported health status.

Forest plot of KCCQ score change in non-obstructive HCM. The pooled MD was 2.12 points (95% CI: –0.98 to 5.22), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%).

Peak oxygen consumption (pVO2)

Both trials showed minimal increases in exercise capacity. The pooled effect was 0.36 mL/kg/min (95% CI: –0.20 to 0.92) with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 3). The wide prediction interval (–3.27 to 3.98) underscores the lack of reproducible benefit.

Forest plot of peak VO2. The pooled MD was 0.36 mL/kg/min (95% CI: –0.20 to 0.92), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%).

Safety outcomes

Treatment-Emergent adverse events (TEAEs)

Rates of at least one TEAE were high in both trials, reflecting the comorbidity burden in this population. The pooled risk ratio was 1.11 (95% CI: 0.34 to 3.58) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 40.8%) (Fig. 4). This suggests a comparable safety profile between mavacamten and placebo, with no consistent evidence of harm.

Forest plot of ≥ 1 TEAE. The pooled RR was 1.11 (95% CI: 0.34 to 3.58), with moderate but nonsignificant heterogeneity (I2 = 40.8%).

NYHA functional class

The pooled risk ratio for NYHA improvement was 1.15 (95% CI: 0.93 to 1.43) (Fig. 5). No heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0.0%), though the prediction interval (0.28 to 4.67) indicated considerable variability in potential future estimates. Overall, treatment did not significantly improve functional class compared with placebo.

Forest plot of NYHA functional class improvement. The pooled RR was 1.15 (95% CI: 0.93 to 1.43), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%).

Palpitations, atrial fibrillation and dizziness

The pooled analysis demonstrated no significant difference in the occurrence of palpitations between groups (pooled RR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.75–1.43; I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 6a). In contrast, treatment was associated with a significantly increased risk of atrial fibrillation (pooled RR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.28–1.56; I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. 6b). The incidence of dizziness did not differ significantly between groups (pooled RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.52–1.65), although moderate heterogeneity was noted (I2 = 42.6%) (Fig. 6c).

Risk of bias assessment

The Randomized Controlled Trials were assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0. Both included trials were judged to be at overall low risk of bias (Fig. 7), with appropriate randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, and minimal attrition. ODYSSEY-HCM (Desai 2025) was consistently low risk across all domains. MAVERICK-HCM (Ho 2020) showed low risk in most domains but was rated as having some concerns for selective reporting, as certain secondary outcomes were not consistently detailed across publications. Importantly, both studies reported prespecified primary endpoints transparently, thereby, depicting confidence in the reliability of the trial data.

GRADE assessment

The GRADE assessment indicates that mavacamten, compared with placebo in patients with non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, does not provide consistent or clinically meaningful improvements in efficacy outcomes (Table 2). Two randomized trials including 639 participants demonstrated modest, non-significant effects on patient-reported health status (KCCQ) and NYHA functional class, and minimal changes in peak oxygen consumption. Safety analyses showed no significant differences in overall treatment-emergent adverse events. Subgroup analyses revealed no increase in palpitations or dizziness, but a significantly higher risk of atrial fibrillation was observed with mavacamten. Certainty of evidence was rated moderate for KCCQ and NYHA class, low for peak VO2 and most safety endpoints, and very low for dizziness due to imprecision and heterogeneity.

Discussion

Non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (nHCM) is a distinct clinical and pathophysiological entity from obstructive HCM (oHCM), with a higher burden of arrhythmias and myocardial fibrosis. Given the favorable effects of cardiac myosin inhibitors (CMIs) like mavacamten in oHCM, this meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the potential therapeutic role of mavacamten in nHCM [11]. However, it is important to acknowledge that the currently available evidence is limited to only two randomized trials, including one small phase II study, and therefore the overall body of evidence remains preliminary.

In this meta-analysis, pooled results from phase II MAVERICK-HCM and phase III ODYSSEY-HCM trials demonstrated that mavacamten did not have significant clinical benefit in patients with symptomatic nonobstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (nHCM). Across the two trials, the pooled mean difference in Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ-CSS) was 2.12 points (95% CI 0.98 to 5.22), numerically favoring mavacamten but not reaching statistical significance [12]. Similarly, pooled analyses showed no significant improvement in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class or peak oxygen uptake (pVO2) compared with placebo. Heterogeneity was negligible (I2 = 0%), though wide prediction intervals underscore substantial uncertainty regarding the true effect. In the MAVERICK-HCM trial, patients with elevated baseline biomarkers (such as troponin I or E/e′) appeared more likely to benefit from mavacamten; however, the ODYSSEY trial did not demonstrate a clinical benefit, and our pooled analysis similarly showed no significant treatment effect.

The rates of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were high across both trials. The pooled risk ratio for TEAEs was 1.11 (95% CI, 0.34–3.58), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 40.8%), suggesting no consistent evidence of harm. However, protocol-directed treatment interruptions and discontinuations due to reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were more frequently observed in the mavacamten group, particularly in ODYSSEY-HCM. Reduction in LVEF is a well-established adverse effect of cardiac myosin inhibitors. Current guidelines suggest discontinuation of CMI when LVEF decreases to less than 50% [1], [13]. In the ODYSSEY-HCM trial, LVEF declined to < 50% in 21.5% and to ≤ 30% in 2.4% (7 patients) of those receiving mavacamten, versus none in the placebo group. All but 3 patients recovered LVEF ≥ 50% after treatment interruption; among the 7 with LVEF ≤ 30%, 4 recovered, while 3 did not by study end or withdrew before reassessment. Although the decline in LVEF was generally reversible with treatment cessation or dose adjustment, it signifies the need for structured echocardiographic monitoring for patients on CMI therapy.

Atrial fibrillation and rhythm-related adverse effects are important considerations in patients with nHCM, who are already predisposed to arrhythmias due to diastolic dysfunction and atrial remodeling. Atrial fibrillation (AF) represents a major source of morbidity in nonobstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (nHCM), reflecting advanced atrial remodeling, elevated filling pressures, and diffuse myocardial fibrosis [14], [15]. All patients who experienced atrial fibrillation as an adverse effect in the phase II MAVERICK trial had a prior history of atrial fibrillation, whereas at baseline, 33% of participants receiving mavacamten in the ODYSSEY Trial had atrial fibrillation. In the ODYSSEY trial, serious adverse events related to atrial tachyarrhythmias occurred in 4.2% of patients versus 3.4% in the comparator group. Treatment-emergent adverse events included atrial fibrillation in 12.2% versus 8.6%, and atrial flutter in 2.1% versus 1.4% of patients, respectively. In our pooled analysis, mavacamten was associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation, with a pooled relative risk (RR) of 1.41 (95% CI: 1.28–1.56), and no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%), indicating a consistent signal across trials. This signal likely reflects recurrence or unmasking of latent AF rather than a direct pro-arrhythmic effect, considering a substantial proportion of the population had atrial fibrillation at baseline. Cardiac myosin inhibitors do not directly alter atrial ion channels; however, in patients with stiff, nonobstructive ventricles, transient changes in loading conditions or diastolic filling dynamics may increase left atrial wall stress in an already vulnerable substrate. Additionally, intensified rhythm surveillance and protocol-mandated monitoring during mavacamten titration may have contributed to increased AF detection [16]. Nevertheless, this consistent signal across trials underscores the need for careful rhythm monitoring when considering mavacamten in nHCM, particularly in patients with prior AF or significant atrial enlargement. In contrast, no significant differences were observed for palpitations (RR 1.04, 95% CI: 0.75–1.43) or dizziness (RR 0.92, 95% CI: 0.52–1.65; I2 = 42.6%).

MAVERICK-HCM showed significant, dose-dependent reductions in NT-proBNP and cardiac troponin I (cTnI), suggesting improvement in myocardial wall stress. NT-proBNP is an established biomarker in HCM, elevated levels of which are associated with increased all-cause mortality [17], [18]. Furthermore, in patients with HCM, an abnormal serum concentration of hs-cTnT is an independent predictor of adverse outcome, and a higher degree of abnormality in hs-cTnT value is associated with a greater risk of cardiovascular events [19], [20], [21], [22]. It is also a marker of ongoing myocardial fibrosis [23], [24]. However, significant reductions in these biomarkers did not translate into improvements in exercise capacity or health status. This disconnect was reaffirmed in the ODYSSEY-HCM trial, which failed to show significant benefit on pVO2 or KCCQ-CSS at 48 weeks despite favorable trends in NT-proBNP. NT-proBNP and troponin primarily reflect myocardial wall stress and ongoing injury rather than reversibility of established structural disease, and their reduction may therefore confirm biological target engagement without guaranteeing symptomatic improvement. Patients enrolled in ODYSSEY-HCM exhibited features of advanced disease, including marked diastolic dysfunction, atrial enlargement, and a high burden of myocardial fibrosis pathophysiologic substrates that are unlikely to be reversed by sarcomere inhibition over short-term follow-up. Moreover, functional endpoints such as peak oxygen uptake are influenced by multiple non-cardiac factors, including chronotropic competence, atrial fibrillation burden, and peripheral conditioning, which may limit sensitivity to isolated improvements in myocardial mechanics. These biomarkers are a measure of wall stress and injury and not scar regression. Collectively, these findings suggest that while mavacamten favorably modulates myocardial stress biology in nHCM, disease stage, fibrosis burden, and endpoint selection may critically determine whether such biological effects translate into clinically meaningful benefits [25], [26], [27]. Furthermore, in contrast to obstructive HCM (oHCM) where symptoms are largely driven by dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, nHCM is characterized by diastolic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and subendocardial ischemia, none of which are directly targeted by mavacamten [28]. In ODYSSEY-HCM, patients exhibited features of advanced disease, including increased left atrial volume index, elevated E/e′ ratios, and high baseline NT-proBNP and troponin levels, suggesting a burden of diastolic and fibrotic remodeling not easily reversed by sarcomere inhibition alone [13].

Importantly, the choice and optimization of background therapy, particularly β-blockers, may have influenced outcomes. β-blockers remain a Class I guideline-recommended therapy in HCM, based largely on historical trials [29], [30], [31]. Their role in nHCM specifically is less well studied but is thought to improve symptoms via negative chronotropic effects and reduced LV filling pressures. In ODYSSEY-HCM, the majority of patients (78.5% of participants in the mavacamtem arm) were receiving β-blockers at baseline, consistent with contemporary guideline-directed care. Exploratory subgroup analyses in the ODYSSEY-HCM suggested that patients on background β-blocker therapy demonstrated numerically greater improvements in both peak oxygen uptake and health status compared with those not receiving β-blockers, whereas no clear benefit was observed in patients off β-blockade. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the post-hoc nature of subgroup analyses and the absence of formal interaction testing, they raise the possibility that β-blockers may be permissive or synergistic with sarcomere inhibition in advanced nHCM. By stabilizing heart rate, prolonging diastolic filling time, and attenuating fluctuations in filling pressures, β-blockers may mitigate hemodynamic instability and allow safer myocardial unloading with mavacamten. These observations highlight the importance of optimized background therapy when evaluating novel agents in nHCM and warrant prospective evaluation in future trials to clarify the role of add-on mavacamten therapy and its potential benefits.

Management of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is multimodal, with non-pharmacologic strategies such as lifestyle counseling, rhythm surveillance and atrial fibrillation management, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, and septal reduction therapy in obstructive disease forming the foundations of management. In nonobstructive HCM, these approaches primarily address disease complications rather than the underlying myocardial pathology. In this setting, mavacamten would be expected to function as a complementary therapy alongside established management strategies. If future studies demonstrate clinical benefit, its role is likely to be phenotype- and stage-specific, potentially favoring earlier disease or carefully selected patient subsets.

Strengths and Limitations

This analysis has several key strengths: it's the first to combine randomized data in nHCM, uses consistent outcome measures, and shows little variation between the results. However, several limitations remain. The analysis is limited by the small number of available trials and modest sample sizes. The wide prediction intervals emphasize uncertainty regarding generalizability across diverse subgroups, particularly given racial homogeneity in the trials.

Conclusion

In our pooled analysis suggests that mavacamten does not significantly improve symptoms, exercise capacity, or patient-reported health status in patients with nHCM. The overall safety profile appears comparable to placebo, though reductions in LVEF remain an expected class effect requiring careful monitoring. Our analysis did show an increased risk for atrial fibrillation which needs to be studied further in larger trials. Future studies should aim to refine patient selection, incorporate nHCM-specific outcome tools, and evaluate longer-term effects of cardiac myosin inhibitors beyond biomarker modulation. CMIs remain promising agents, but their role in nonobstructive disease requires further clarification before routine clinical adoption.
