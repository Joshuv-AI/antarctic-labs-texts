Evidence Supporting Decrease in Sudden Deaths Due to Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Barry J. Maron, Martin S. Maron
Published: 2026

For over 50 years, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) has acquired a highly visible and grim reputation as a major cause of unexpected sudden death (SD) in young active people, either when undiagnosed in the community (ie, competitive athletes), or patients within conventional hospital and clinic-based cohorts.1,2 In this Commentary, we revisit this timely issue to assess its current status decades later and specifically the effects of contemporary medical initiatives and therapeutic advances intended to decrease the rate of such tragic events in young people.

HCM sudden deaths in competitive athletes

Reports of SDs in young competitive athletes, paradoxically epitomizing the healthiest segment of society, have been part of the public domain for many years, with events often driven by the news and social media.1 Once attributed to a mysterious overtraining effect unique to the athlete lifestyle, these events came to be recognized largely as the result of underlying and usually unrecognized cardiovascular diseases, prominently HCM and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.3

In an unique 30 year national postmortem survey of >2400 competitive athletes from the U.S. National Sudden Death in Athletes Registry, HCM was the single most common cause of SD among 9 major cardiovascular diagnoses accounting for 35% of events at a rate of 9/year3 (Table 1). Indeed, recently assembled data sets tabulating SD frequency in athlete populations uniformly report a lower event frequency attributable to HCM.4, 5, 6,7,8 Specifically, the 5 most recent large clinical-pathologic surveys in young athletes come from diverse populations in the United States, United Kingdom, and Denmark including clinical and/or autopsy data from national and regional registries, as well as the National Collegiate Athletic Association (Table 1). These studies tabulate a total of 49 HCM-athlete deaths over 62 years, an average of only <1 per year (ie, mean 0.8/year).4, 5, 6, 7, 8

Such comparative data raise questions concerning determinants of the apparent downward trend of athlete deaths establishing HCM as a much less frequent cause than previously regarded.2,3 Albeit likely multifactorial and difficult to substantiate statistically in the absence of prospective study data, we nevertheless offer the experienced viewpoint that the current low absolute number of HCM deaths occurring in young competitive student-athletes is likely a direct consequence of the entrenched long-standing 40-year AHA/ACC consensus guidelines for eligibility vs disqualification decision-making in this population.9

Recommendations to the practicing community have consistently advised selective and prudent restriction of student-athletes with underlying HCM (particularly those with outflow obstruction, symptoms, or risk markers),1,2 ie, from the unpredictable environment of intense competitive sports with uncontrollable autonomic, metabolic, ischemic processes that lower thresholds for lethal tachyarrhythmias.

An emerging narrative from the sports medicine community aggressively promotes liberalization of HCM-athlete eligibility based on shared decision-making and prioritizing personal liberties and lifestyle over safety, but also characterizes the prior AHA/ACC guidelines and decision-making for inaccurately athletes as anachronistic, permissive, and paternalistic.10

Sudden death in clinically identified HCM patients

Since disease inception >60 years ago,2 the risk of arrhythmic SD has been a highly visible and feared complication of HCM by young and middle-aged patients.1 Indeed, before year 2000 (and for the initial 40 years of HCM recognition), clinicians were relegated to ineffective antiarrhythmic drugs, and notification that SD events had in fact already occurred.2,11

The implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) and primary prevention of SD were introduced to the HCM patient population 25 years ago most aggressively in the United States, but associated with skepticism that ICDs might not perform reliably on this unique nonischemic arrhythmogenic substrate.11 However, this concern proved unwarranted, and ICD therapy for high risk HCM patients is now accepted in all consensus guidelines and practiced internationally in more than 60 countries on 6 continents in diverse societies, cultures, and health care systems.

With a mature AHA/ACC risk stratification algorithm based on 1 or more generally accepted clinical markers, it is now possible to reliably anticipate and prevent SD in individual patients with 95% sensitivity (annual therapy rate, 3% to 4%), associated with a low rate of device failure (≤0.5%).1,11 Indeed, among cohorts documented in the literature worldwide >1000 individual adults and children have survived with HCM attributable to ICD termination of life threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias. In a large prospective study, only 0.3% of patients not selected for an ICD ultimately experienced arrhythmic SD (including some who refused a device recommendation), with survivability comparable to an age-sex-matched general U.S. population.1,11 Consequently, the reduced incidence of SDs in HCM has contributed substantially to the currently low overall HCM-related mortality rate of ≤0.5%/year,11 less than for most other major cardiac or noncardiac risks to living, affording HCM patients with the opportunity for achieving normal or extended life expectancy and good quality of life.11

The mathematical European Society of Cardiology risk score model based on a formula comprising 7 clinical variables is favored by many European cardiologists for providing higher specificity for implant decisions (about 90%) with the objective to limit device overuse; risk categories are useful in estimating SD risk during interactions with patients and families.1,11

In conclusion, historically, HCM has been considered a major cause of SD in young people.1,11 Consequently, it has been our long held aspiration to create and support innovations that decrease SD risk in this disease.1,9 Such a shifting trend in survival for HCM is reminiscent of the under-recognized 90% decrease in deaths from acute myocardial infarction.12

The initiative introducing primary prevention ICDs to HCM patients represents a paradigm breakthrough by changing disease course and decreasing SD event rates.11 Also, 40 years of selectively removing high risk HCM patients from participation in intense and unpredictable competitive sports environments based on AHA/ACC societal consensus guidelines has likely been a major factor in reducing the frequency of sports-related HCM-SDs as documented here. Therefore, the therapeutic initiatives summarized in this Commentary deserve continued attention and support in both the patient and physician communities to further reduce the devastating SD events that can occur in HCM patients under a variety of clinical circumstances.
