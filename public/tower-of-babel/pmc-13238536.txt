Outcomes of Hepatocellular Carcinoma involving the Portal Vein Main Trunk and/or the Inferior Vena Cava: Experience From a Multidisciplinary Liver Cancer Clinic
Authors: I‐Chia Liu, Atif Zaheer, Chen Hu, Christopher Shubert, Kelly Lafaro, William R. Burns, Richard Burkhart, Jin He, Benjamin Philosophe, Jane Zorzi, Paige Griffith, Alexandra T. Strauss, Amy K. Kim, Robert Liddell, Kelvin Hong, Christos Georgiades, Robert Anders, Kiyoko Oshima, Elsa Hallab, Amol Narang, Won Jin Ho, Mark Yarchoan, Marina Baretti, Jeffrey Meyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVascular invasion by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), particularly involving the main portal vein or inferior vena cava (IVC), is associated with poor prognosis. However, contemporary real‐world outcomes and the clinical benefit of liver‐directed therapies (LDT) in this setting are poorly defined. We evaluated outcomes of patients with vascular invasion managed in a multidisciplinary liver cancer clinic.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed outcomes for patients with HCC who presented to the Johns Hopkins Liver Multidisciplinary Clinic between 2020 and 2024, with radiographic evidence of the tumor thrombus in the main portal vein and/or IVC. Overall (OS) and progression‐free survival and gastrointestinal variceal bleeding incidence were evaluated. Associations between clinical variables and outcomes were analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsFifty‐four patients met our study criteria. Forty‐eight patients (89%) had portal vein main trunk invasion, and 13 (24%) had IVC tumor thrombus. Treatment included systemic therapy alone in 30 patients (56%), systemic therapy and LDT in 15 patients (28%), best supportive care in six patients (11%), and LDT alone in three patients (6%). Median OS was 5.7 months (95% CI: 2.6–12 months). Median survival was shorter among patients with IVC involvement (4.1 months, 95% CI: 0.82–5.7 months) than those without (7.6 months, 95% CI: 2.3–17 months), which was independently associated with worse prognosis in multivariable analysis (HR: 4.0, 95% CI: 1.5–11, p = 0.0051). Other significant findings of multivariable analysis include performance status (HR: 7.2, 95% CI: 2.7–19, p < 0.0001) and viral hepatitis (HR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.79, p = 0.027). Four patients (7%) experienced bleeding esophageal varices requiring intervention.ConclusionsHCC with tumor thrombus involving the main portal vein and/or IVC is associated with poor survival in real‐world clinical practice. Prospective studies are needed to define the optimal management of this high‐risk population.

Introduction

Vascular invasion by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with resulting tumor thrombus is well established as a poor prognostic factor, with high rates of mortality related to the development of extrahepatic metastatic disease, liver decompensation and failure, as well as the consequences of portal hypertension [1, 2, 3]. Invasion of the portal vein (PV)–portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT)–is designated as stage C disease in the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer system, with systemic therapy as the recommended primary treatment approach [4]. Patients with imaging‐diagnosed macrovascular invasion into the hepatic veins and/or inferior vena cava (IVC) are often managed similarly as those with PVTT. Macrovascular invasion has typically precluded patients from eligibility for liver transplantation and also, frequently, for surgical intervention [3].

Location of the PVTT is identified in the Vp classification system, with Vp1 disease representing involvement of peripheral branches of the PV, and Vp4 disease representing involvement of the main trunk of the PV and/or contralateral first branch [5]. There are differences in long‐term oncological outcomes as a function of the Vp category [6]. However, optimal management for patients with any degree of vascular invasion remains uncertain, and many groups have reported on results with liver‐directed/local‐regional therapies, including surgery and various combinations of therapies in efforts to improve survival outcomes [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Combining liver‐directed local‐regional therapies (LDT) and systemic therapy for patients with BCLC stage C disease mirrors the combined modality paradigm common to other advanced cancers, but many questions remain as to the optimal combinations and sequencing of therapies, especially in the modern era of targeted and immunotherapies, and how to safely select patients for these aggressive treatments.

There have been important recent advancements in the systemic management of advanced HCC, with immunotherapy agents demonstrating significant activity and signals of long‐term survival in a minority of patients. The IMbrave 150 trial showed the superiority of combination bevacizumab and atezolizumab, an anti‐PD‐L1 agent, relative to sorafenib with respect to overall survival in patients with advanced disease [13]. In a subset analysis of patients with Vp4 disease, the combination treatment continued to show improved survival outcomes compared to sorafenib [2]. Recent randomized trials have also demonstrated the value of adding LDTs to selected patients with vascular involvement [10, 14, 15].

At our institution, we have a high‐volume multidisciplinary clinic dedicated to patients with primary liver malignancies [16]. In this report, we review outcomes of a large cohort of patients presenting to this clinic with the most advanced degrees of vessel involvement by HCC—main trunk portal vein (Vp4 disease) and/or IVC invasion—and analyze clinical factors and interventions associated with survival outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This is a retrospective review of treatment‐naïve patients with newly diagnosed HCC who presented to our institution's liver multidisciplinary clinic (LMDC). The inclusion criteria for our study cohort included [1]: HCC diagnosed radiographically and/or by biopsy that was newly diagnosed and had not undergone prior treatment [2]; radiographic evidence of macrovascular tumor invasion into the main trunk of the portal vein and/or IVC; and [3] received their treatment primarily at our institution. Exclusion criteria included: [1] fibrolamellar HCC and/or mixed hepatocellular carcinoma‐cholangiocarcinoma [2]; presence of another, synchronous malignancy [3]; prior history of liver transplant and [4]; prior history of HCC, regardless of whether the patient received any treatment. For patients who met the above criteria, we collected their relevant past medical history, baseline clinical characteristics, and treatment details. This study and waiver of consent for participation have been approved by the institution's IRB.

Liver Multidisciplinary Clinic Practice Workflow

The practice of our LMDC has been previously described [16]. To briefly review, providers from multiple medical specialties, including medical oncology, radiation oncology, surgical oncology, diagnostic radiology, interventional radiology, hepatology, and pathology, attend the weekly clinic where patient referrals come from both within and outside of our institution. After LMDC, patients may choose to follow the clinic's treatment recommendations but receive the actual treatment at an institution closer to their home while periodically following the LMDC.

Treatment Strategy for HCC With Main Portal Vein and/or IVC Involvement and Follow‐Up

All patients were considered in a multidisciplinary fashion for the use of systemic therapy and/or LDTs. At our institution, LDTs are considered for patients with advanced HCC either prior to systemic therapy or as consolidation treatment following initial systemic therapy, or as palliation for symptomatic disease, based on multidisciplinary discussion. For this study, LDTs were defined as liver‐directed local‐regional therapies and included the following possible interventions: surgery, thermal ablation, transarterial embolization therapy (such as bland embolization, chemoembolization (TACE), and radioembolization (TARE)), and external beam radiation therapy (EBRT). Patients may also undergo extrahepatic local‐regional therapies (e.g., radiation therapy to a site of distant metastatic disease). Patients were typically followed with clinical assessment, including imaging and laboratory results. Patients planned for treatment with bevacizumab routinely underwent screening endoscopy.

Clinical Endpoints

The clinical endpoints of interest in this study were progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). The date of the LMDC evaluation was the starting time point for both endpoints. Events for our PFS endpoint included any radiographic or clinical evidence of disease progression that warranted a change in systemic therapy as well as death from any cause, with censoring at last documented clinical encounter. An event in OS was death from any cause, with censoring at last documented clinical encounter.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as median with range, mean with standard deviation, and/or percentages for baseline patient characteristics, clinical disease features, and treatment details. The Cox proportional hazard models were performed to determine associations between key clinical characteristics and treatment details with clinical endpoints of interest. Median follow‐up time was calculated with the reverse Kaplan–Meier method [17]. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed for OS, with comparisons between groups made with the log‐rank test. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline Patient and Disease Characteristics

From January 2020 to December 2024, there were 54 patients with newly diagnosed, treatment‐naïve HCC with involvement of the main portal vein and/or IVC who met our inclusion criteria. Baseline patient and disease characteristics are presented in Table 1. Most patients were men (81.5%) and 60 years or older (81.5%). There were 13 patients (24%) with IVC involvement (with or without concomitant portal vein involvement). About 59% of the patients had child‐Pugh class A disease, while 69% of patients had an albumin‐bilirubin (ALBI) score of grade 2 or 3. There were 34 patients (60%) with an AFP greater than 400 ng/mL. Twenty‐two patients (41%) had extrahepatic disease, including nodal disease and distant metastases.

The treatment courses and prescribed systemic therapy regimens are shown in Table 2. The majority of patients (30/54, 56%) were treated with systemic therapy without LDT. There were six patients (11%) who did not receive any cancer‐directed therapy and were offered the best care only because of poor functional status at presentation. Survival Outcomes

The median follow‐up time for OS was 35 months. For the entire cohort, there were 43 deaths (70.6%), median survival was 5.7 months (95% CI: 2.6–12 months), and the 1‐year survival was 35.3% (Figure 1). Median survival for patients with and without IVC involvement was 4.1 (95% CI: 0.82–5.7 months) and 7.6 months (95% CI: 2.3–17 months), respectively. In the subset of patients with ECOG PS 0–1, the median OS was 8.4 months (95% CI: 5.2–20 months) and the 1‐year survival rate was 46.3%. The associations of clinical features with OS are shown in Table 3. In multivariable analysis, IVC involvement was associated with inferior survival outcomes (HR 4.0, 95% CI 1.5–11, p = 0.0051), while the history of viral hepatitis was associated with improved outcomes (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.23–0.79, p = 0.027). Median PFS was 3.4 months (95% CI: 2.2–5.7 months) for all patients, with 1‐year PFS of 25% (Figure 2). We did not model the impact of LDTs on survival as they were delivered in a heterogeneous fashion with respect to the type of treatment and timing (relative to systemic therapy administration) of delivery, with concern in particular for the problem of immortal time bias [18]. We did, however, perform an exploratory survival analysis with patients separated into three groups—those treated with systemic therapy alone (median OS of 4.1 months), those treated with systemic therapy and LDT (median OS of 17 months), and those who did not receive systemic therapy (median OS of 0.69 months) (Figure S1). Inter‐group survival comparisons were not performed because of the concern for bias.

Progression‐Free Survival for All Patients.

Notably, five patients lived for more than 30 months. Their median age was 64 years (range: 63–68) and all had ECOG PS of 0–1 at presentation. Only one of these patients had IVC involvement. Four patients received Bevacizumab/Atezolizumab at some point in their treatment course for systemic therapy. Two patients received LDT in addition to systemic therapy.

Safety and Adverse Events

Four patients developed upper gastrointestinal bleeding related to esophageal varices after treatment initiation and required hospitalization. Two patients received bevacizumab as part of their systemic therapy prior to the variceal bleed, and neither patient had evidence of bleeding varices on endoscopy done at initiation of systemic therapy. In all cases, the varices were successfully banded and there were no deaths related to bleeding.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the poor prognosis of extensive vascular invasion using HCC in a large, real‐world group of patients, with IVC invasion associated with particularly poor outcomes. We also showed that prolonged survival can nonetheless be achieved in a small subset of patients. Systemic therapy is the primary recommendation from the BCLC staging/treatment classification for patients with HCC with macrovascular invasion [4]. However, even with current approaches involving combined treatment with targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors, specifically concurrent Bevacizumab and Atezolizumab as studied in the IMBrave150 trial, median PFS is < 6 months and median OS is < 8 months for the subset of patients with Vp4 disease [2]. This study demonstrated that prolonged survival is possible, as one‐ and two‐year survival in the patients with Vp4 disease were 39% and 29.3%, respectively, for patients treated with Bevacizumab/Atezolizumab. In our current study, median PFS was 3.4 months, and median OS was 5.7 months. These inferior outcomes relative to IMBrave150 may reflect the high proportion of patients in our study with ECOG PS of 2–3 (28%) (patients with ECOG PS score > 1 were excluded from IMBrave150), the related fact that 11% of patients in our series did not receive any cancer‐directed treatment, as well as the inclusion of patients with IVC involvement (24%), which was associated with particularly poor clinical outcomes. When considering only patients who presented with ECOG performance status of 0–1, our survival outcomes are comparable to the findings from IMBrave 150, with a median OS of 8.4 months. The median OS in our study was notably inferior to the results of 16.4 months (treatment with durvalumab and tremelimumab) and 23.7 months (treatment with ipilimumab and nivolumab) in the HIMILAYA and CheckMate 9DW trials [19, 20, 21], respectively, but it should also be noted that macrovascular invasion was seen in a minority of patients in these studies‐ 26.2% in the durvalumab plus tremelimumab arm in HIMILAYA and 23% in the ipilimumab and nivolumab arm in CheckMate 9DW. In our study, variceal bleeding was uncommon and manageable with therapeutic endoscopic intervention.

Multidisciplinary addition of LDTs to patients with HCC with advanced vascular invasion is a rational consideration. LDTs may enhance disease control within the liver, at least complementing the effects of systemic therapy. Even in the setting of very advanced disease with extrahepatic metastases, control of cancer within the liver may prolong survival by preventing malignancy‐associated liver failure. Individual LDTs may be effective on their own or be used to downstage to other local interventions such as surgery or even liver transplantation. One significant concern applying LDTs in patients with vascular invasion is treatment‐induced liver decompensation in patients with already tenuous hepatic function. In our clinical experience, although LDTs were delivered to many patients, there was variability in the type of LDT delivered as well as the timing with respect to the systemic therapy component, and as such, we did not model LDTs as a covariate in our analysis of survival outcomes. This heterogeneity is likely a reflection of uncertain answers to the issues raised earlier. There are indeed data from randomized trials supporting the addition of LDTs for advanced HCC, at least in selected circumstances, in the modern era of systemic therapies. A trial reported by Yoon et al. compared sorafenib with TACE followed by EBRT in patients with HCC and vascular invasion with ECOG PS of 0–1 [14]. Bilateral or main portal vein involvement was present in 36.7% of patients. The combined treatment yielded improved median OS (55 versus 43 weeks). The NRG Oncology/RTOG 1112 phase 3 randomized trial was recently reported and compared 5‐fraction stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) followed by sorafenib versus sorafenib alone in patients with unresectable HCC [15]. Advanced‐stage disease was common, with vascular involvement in 74% of patients, and Vp3, Vp4, or IVC involvement present in 63% of patients. Most of the patients also had multifocal disease. The addition of SBRT improved PFS from 5.5 to 9.2 months (p < 0.001) and OS from 12.3 to 15.8 months (p = 0.06). In multivariable analysis, the use of SBRT was associated with improved survival. These studies provide significant evidence supporting the safe and effective use of LDT intervention in selected patients with HCC with advanced vascular invasion and also provide a foundation for further study of LDTs in the evolving era of immunotherapy and immunotherapy‐based combination approaches. The results of the NRG Oncology/RTOG 1112 trial provide a foundation for the earlier incorporation of LDT (specifically radiation therapy) in the sequencing of treatments. This approach warrants further investigation.

At this time, IVC invasion is not explicitly included as stage C disease in the BCLC categorization [4]. Our results demonstrate that this specific form of advanced vascular invasion has outcomes inferior to even Vp4 disease, and caval invasion may require different, including more aggressive, therapeutic approaches in comparison to patients with PVTT. Caval invasion, with or without PVTT, should be considered very advanced and poor‐prognosis disease.

Our study is limited by the retrospective review and the heterogeneity of treatment courses, and these issues may limit the ability to make strict conclusions regarding prognosis and treatment effect. Nonetheless, this heterogeneity reflects real‐world cancer management considerations. The relatively large number and proportion of patients with caval involvement and analysis of their outcomes add to the understanding of this specific, very poor‐prognosis subgroup of patients, although detailed analysis is limited by the patient numbers. Our results also demonstrate that long‐term survival in the setting of advanced vascular invasion is possible, with a plateauing survival curve.

In summary, our results demonstrate the significant challenges that remain for management of HCC with advanced vascular invasion. We present results with a relatively large subset of patients with IVC tumor thrombus, a population that remains understudied and clinically complex. Although a small proportion of patients will reach long‐term survival, early disease progression and death are common. Multidisciplinary discussion and planning are essential. There is a growing evidence base that the addition of LDTs can provide a meaningful path toward improved survival, both as definitive therapy or possibly as downstaging interventions that ultimately facilitate resection or transplantation.
