Abstract
. In 2023, Greaves et al. constructed several sets of 57 equiangular lines in dimension 18. Using the concept of switching root introduced by Cao et al. in 2021, these sets of equiangular lines are embedded in a lattice of rank 19 spanned by norm 3 vectors together with a switching root. We characterize this lattice as an overlattice of the root lattice A 9 ⊕ A 9 ⊕ A 1 A_{9}\oplus A_{9}\oplus A_{1} , and show that there are at least 246896 246896 sets of 57 equiangular lines in dimension 18 18 arising in this way, up to isometry. Additionally, we prove that all of these sets of equiangular lines are strongly maximal. Here, a set of equiangular lines is said to be strongly maximal if there is no set of equiangular lines properly containing it even if the dimension of the underlying space is increased. Among these sets, there are ones with only six distinct Seidel eigenvalues.

2.
Sets of equiangular lines and affine equiangular sets

In this section, we introduce sets of equiangular lines and affine equiangular sets, and describe the relations between them.
In general, an isometry is defined as a mapping between metric spaces that preserves metric.
In this paper, we treat lines and vectors in a Euclidean space, and hence isometries are compositions of translations and orthogonal transformations.
Furthermore, we will restrict our attention to isometries which fix certain points, such as the origin or a specific vector (e.g., a switching root), and consequently preserve inner products.
For simplicity, we also refer to a function defined on a set of vectors that preserves inner products as an isometry.

First, we introduce concepts related to sets of equiangular lines.
For a set
\Phi
of equiangular lines, denote by
\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
the smallest vector subspace containing all lines in
\Phi
.
Two sets
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
of equiangular lines are said to be
isometric
if there exists an isometry
f:\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\to\langle\Phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(\Phi)=\Phi^{\prime}
.
Here, note that
f(\Phi)
denotes
\{\{f(u):u\in l\}:l\in\Phi\}
.
A set
\Phi
of equiangular lines is said to be
strongly maximal
if there is no set of equiangular lines properly containing
\Phi
even if the dimension of the underlying space is increased.
Also, a
Seidel matrix
is a symmetric matrix with diagonal entries
0
and off-diagonal entries
\pm 1
.
Two Seidel matrices are said to be
switching equivalent
if they are similar under some signed permutation matrix.
For a non-negative integer
n
, denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For a set
\Phi=\{l_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
n
equiangular lines with common angle
\arccos(\alpha)
, there exist unit vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
l_{i}=\mathbb{R}v_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
.
Then, the matrix
(1/\alpha)(I-G)
is a Seidel matrix, where
G
is the Gram matrix of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
.
Although
v_{j}
can be replaced with
-v_{j}
as a unit vector generating
l_{j}
for some
j
, this Seidel matrix is uniquely determined up to switching equivalence.
In particular, the characteristic polynomial of the Seidel matrix
S
is an invariant of the set
\Phi
of equiangular lines.

Next we introduce the concept of affine equiangular sets.
Note that a vector
v
is called a
root
if
(r,r)=2
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
s
be a real number at least
1
.
Let
r
be a root in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
The set of vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is called an
affine equiangular set with norm
s
with respect to
r
if

(i)

(u_{1},r)=\cdots=(u_{n},r)=1
,

(ii)

(u_{i},u_{j})=\begin{cases}s&\text{ if }i=j,\\
0,1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The root
r
is called the
switching root
of the affine equiangular set.

For an affine equiangular set
\omega=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
with norm
s
with respect to a switching root
r
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2u_{i}-r}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{2u_{j}-r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\begin{cases}2s-1&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-1,1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, the lines
\mathbb{R}(2u_{1}-r),\ldots,\mathbb{R}(2u_{n}-r)
are equiangular with common angle
\arccos(1/(2s-1))
in the vector space
\langle u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\rangle\cap r^{\perp}
.
We say that this set of equiangular lines is
induced
by
\omega
.
Any set of equiangular lines with common angle
\arccos(1/(2s-1))
is induced by some affine equiangular set with norm
s
.
Refer to
[
2
]
for details.

Definition 2.2
.

Two affine equiangular sets
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
with respect to a common switching root
r
are said to be
switching equivalent

if there exists a bijection
g:\omega\to\omega^{\prime}
such that either
g(u)=u
or
g(u)=r-u
for all
u\in\omega
.
Denote by
\sim_{\sw}
the switching equivalence, that is,
\omega\sim_{\sw}\omega^{\prime}
if
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
are switching equivalent.
Denote by
[\omega]
the switching class
\{\omega^{\prime}:\omega^{\prime}\sim_{\sw}\omega\}
.

We see that if two affine equiangular sets are switching equivalent, then they induce the same set of equiangular lines.
It should be noted that the concepts above have been essentially introduced in terms of matrices in
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Affine equiangular sets
\omega
with respect to a switching root
r
and
\omega^{\prime}
with respect to a switching root
r^{\prime}
are
isometric

if there exists an affine equiangular set
\omega^{\prime\prime}
switching equivalent to
\omega^{\prime}
and a bijective isometry
g:\omega\cup\{r\}\to\omega^{\prime\prime}\cup\{r^{\prime}\}
such that
g(r)=r^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Affine equiangular sets
\omega
with respect to a switching root
r
and
\omega^{\prime}
with respect to a switching root
r^{\prime}
are isometric
if and only if the set of equiangular lines induced by
\omega
and that induced by
\omega^{\prime}
are isometric.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
be the sets of equiangular lines induced by
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
, respectively.
Assume that
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
are isometric.
Then, there exists an affine equiangular set
\omega^{\prime\prime}
switching equivalent to
\omega^{\prime}
and a bijective isometry
g:\omega\cup\{r\}\to\omega^{\prime\prime}\cup\{r^{\prime}\}
such that
g(r)=r^{\prime}
.
This induces the bijection

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccc}\{2u-r:u\in\omega\}&\to&\{2u^{\prime\prime}-r^{\prime}:u^{\prime\prime}\in\omega^{\prime\prime}\}\\
2u-r&\mapsto&2g(u)-r^{\prime}.\end{array}

Since this preserves inner product,
this mapping can be extended to the isometry

f:\langle 2u-r:u\in\omega\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\to\langle 2u^{\prime\prime}-r^{\prime}:u^{\prime\prime}\in\omega^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}.

Here,
\langle 2u-r:u\in\omega\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
holds, and

\langle 2u^{\prime\prime}-r^{\prime}:u^{\prime\prime}\in\omega^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle 2u^{\prime}-r^{\prime}:u^{\prime}\in\omega^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\langle\Phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

holds by
\omega^{\prime}\sim_{\sw}\omega^{\prime\prime}
.
Since this isometry
f
satisfies
f(\Phi)=\Phi^{\prime}
by definition, we conclude that
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are isometric.

Conversely, we assume that
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
are isometric.
Then, there exists an isometry
f:\langle\Phi\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\to\langle\Phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(\Phi)=\Phi^{\prime}
.
Noting that
u\in\omega
corresponds one-to-one to
\mathbb{R}(2u-r)\in\Phi
, and
u^{\prime}\in\omega^{\prime}
does one-to-one to
\mathbb{R}(2u^{\prime}-r^{\prime})\in\Phi^{\prime}
, we see that for any
u\in\omega
, there uniquely exists
u^{\prime}\in\omega^{\prime}
such that
f(\mathbb{R}(2u-r))=\mathbb{R}(2u^{\prime}-r^{\prime})
.
Then,

f(u)\in\{u^{\prime},r^{\prime}-u^{\prime}\}

holds.
This means that
\omega^{\prime\prime}:=\{f(u):u\in\omega\}
is switching equivalent to
\omega^{\prime}
.
In addition, the mapping
\omega\to\omega^{\prime\prime};u\mapsto f(u)
is a bijective isometry.
Therefore,
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
are isometric.
∎

Definition 2.5
.

An affine equiangular set
\omega
with respect to a switching root
r
is said to be
strongly maximal

if there is no affine equiangular set with respect to
r
properly containing
\omega
, even if the dimension of the underlying space is increased.

Note that an affine equiangular set is strongly maximal if and only if the set of equiangular lines induced by it is strongly maximal.
Also, for a set
S
of vectors, denote by
\langle S\rangle
the lattice generated by
S
, that is, the set of linear combinations of vectors in
S
with integral coefficients.
Although, in general, an additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a

if it is discrete,
all sets written as
\langle S\rangle
in this paper are discrete.

Lemma 2.6
.

An affine equiangular set
\omega
with norm
s
with respect to
r
is strongly maximal
if and only if
there is no vector
v
in the dual of the lattice
\langle\omega\cup\{r\}\rangle
such that
(v,v)\leq s
,
(v,r)=1
and
(v,u)\in\{0,1\}
for every
u\in\omega
.

Proof.

The affine equiangular set
\omega
is not strongly maximal if and only if there exists a vector
w
in some vector space containing
\langle\omega\cup\{r\}\rangle
such that
(w,r)=1
and
(w,u)\in\{0,1\}
for every
u\in\omega
.
Let
v
be the orthogonal projection of such a vector
w
onto the vector space generated by the vectors of
\omega
together with
r
.
Then,
v
is contained in the dual lattice of
\langle\omega\cup\{r\}\rangle
,
and satisfies
(w,u)\in\{0,1\}
for all
u\in\omega
and
(w,r)=1
.
Thus, we obtain the necessary and sufficient condition.
∎
