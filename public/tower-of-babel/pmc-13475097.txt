Exploring patients’ and public perspectives on an oral capsule for gastric submucosal delivery in Denmark and the USA: a mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Caroline Buhl, Lourdes Cantarero-Arévalo, Jesús Sánchez, Silvia E Rabionet, Lene Havsteen, Karsten Lindhardt, Anna Birna Almarsdóttir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery represent a fundamentally new route of administration for biologics, which are currently limited to injection. Involving patients and members of the public to understand their perspectives on this novel pharmaceutical form, distinct from both injections and traditional oral tablets, is essential for the clinical development, regulatory approval, adoption and acceptability. This study aims to identify perceived benefits, concerns and communication preferences regarding oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery, using BIONDD as a case study, to initially document the elements of the perceived value proposition and potential reservations about the oral delivery system from the patient and public perspectives.Methods and analysisThis two-country exploratory sequential mixed-methods study will be conducted in Denmark and the USA. First, focus group interviews with purposively sampled patients and members of the public will explore attitudes towards oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery. Qualitative data will be analysed using thematic analysis, combining manual and artificial intelligence-assisted coding. Insights from the focus groups will inform the development of a structured questionnaire, which will subsequently be distributed to a broader sample in both countries. Survey data will be analysed descriptively, and where possible, exploratory comparisons between country samples will be made. Main outcomes will include perceived benefits, concerns, acceptability and preferred terminology for discussing this novel pharmaceutical form.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the relevant ethics committees at the University of Copenhagen and Nova Southeastern University. All data will be managed confidentially in line with General Data Protection Regulation and local regulations. Informed consent will be obtained in accordance with site-specific ethical procedures, and withdrawal will be possible up to a defined stage of data processing, after which data cannot be linked to individual participants. Results will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals, at conferences and on institutional websites, in accordance with established ethical research and authorship guidelines.

Introduction

The use of injectable biological therapies has increased substantially in recent decades, primarily due to the expanding development and clinical application of complex molecules such as antibodies, peptides and nucleic acids, which cannot efficiently be administered orally due to physiological barriers, including enzymatic degradation and poor absorption across the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.1 2 While innovations in injection devices have improved convenience,3 patients consistently prefer non-invasive administration routes, particularly oral formulations, to avoid needle-associated pain, anxiety and stigma.4 5

Attempts to replace injectable administration with other non-oral alternatives, such as nasal sprays, have encountered challenges, particularly for the delivery of drugs requiring systemic exposure or those with large molecular weights.6 These challenges have driven growing interest in developing oral formulations that can match the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of injectable biologics.2 Recent advances include drug-device capsules that deliver therapeutic agents directly into the gastric submucosa, bypassing traditional absorption pathways.2 7–9 One such emerging capsule is BIONDD, a representative example of this new class of orally administered drug-delivery capsules. On dissolution in the stomach, the BIONDD capsule deploys a small device that administers the drug directly into the gastric tissue, bypassing traditional GI absorption pathways.2 8 10 11 Preclinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach, showing systemic exposure and pharmacokinetic profiles comparable to subcutaneous administration in large animal models.11 Supporting the broader potential of orally ingestible drug-delivery systems, early clinical studies of a different investigational drug-device capsule targeting the intestinal wall have demonstrated bioavailability and pharmacokinetic profiles comparable to subcutaneous administration for selected biologics in humans, while maintaining acceptable safety and tolerability profiles,12 13 supporting the translational relevance of preclinical exposure data generated in large-animal models for related ingestible capsule systems. These delivery systems seek to combine the convenience of oral administration with the pharmacological effectiveness of parenteral administration, while differing fundamentally from conventional oral capsules in both function and mechanism of action.

While preferences toward oral medications over injections are well established, it remains unclear whether such preferences extend to gastric submucosal delivery systems. No published studies have examined patient attitudes, acceptance or usability concerns for this novel route of administration, leaving a critical knowledge gap. In line with user-centred design approaches and regulatory guidance, eliciting patient and public perspectives at early stages of development is required to ensure that emerging innovations align with user needs, expectations and potential concerns.14–17 Because BIONDD may be relevant for multiple disease indications and preventive uses such as vaccines, input from both patients and members of the public is essential. Including individuals with varied injection experience, from chronic therapy users to those familiar only with immunisation, ensures development aligns with real-world needs and expectations. Understanding how patients and the public evaluate this form of drug delivery, distinct from both injections and traditional oral capsules, is essential for guiding early stages of clinical development, regulatory approval and eventual adoption.

Thus, the aim of the study is to explore patients’ and public perspectives on oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery, using BIONDD as a case study. A mixed methods (MMs) design will be used, integrating qualitative focus groups and quantitative questionnaires in Denmark and the USA. This approach enables a comprehensive understanding of preferences, acceptance and potential barriers to use. Additionally, the findings will inform the development of communication strategies for BIONDD, ensuring that information about both benefits and risks is conveyed effectively to support appropriate patient understanding.

Methods and analysis

Study design

Investigating patients’ and the public’s perceptions of a new, innovative administration form is not straightforward. To gain a fuller, more nuanced, and more credible understanding of how and why these perceptions form, a MM approach was adopted. Creswell defines MM research as:

A methodology and method to research in the social, behavioural, and health sciences in which the investigator gathers both quantitative (close-ended) and qualitative (open-ended) data, integrates or combines the two, and then draws inferences (called “metainferences”) from the integration that provides insights beyond what can be learned from the quantitative or qualitative data.18

MM leverages the strengths of both quantitative and qualitative data to address the complexity inherent in studying human perception. This integration will provide a more comprehensive understanding of perspectives.19 20 Findings can be both generalisable (from quantitative data) and actionable or contextually grounded (from qualitative data), making them more useful for policy or practice.18 Qualitative data can uncover subtleties and unexpected themes that numbers alone might miss.

The study will be an Exploratory Sequential Mixed Methods Study that will start with a qualitative (QUAL) study that will inform the quantitative (QUANT) study afterwards, and thereby help develop an informed instrument (figure 1). Two data collection methods will be employed: focus group interviews for the qualitative study and a survey for the quantitative study, addressing the following research questions:

Study setting

Perceptions are often shaped by complex social, cultural or psychological factors. Therefore, the MM study will be conducted in both Denmark and the USA to gain a deeper understanding of the challenges participants might face, given that geographical, cultural, political and health system differences may affect the participants’ perspectives.

In Denmark, the study will be conducted at the Department of Pharmacy at the University of Copenhagen. In the USA, the study will be conducted at the College of Pharmacy at Nova Southeastern University in Fort Lauderdale, Florida.

Patients’ medication experience significantly influences preferences.21 Therefore, the study will include four distinct groups varying by disease type and demographic characteristics (table 1). Demographics within each disease group will also vary, adding further diversity of perspectives. Participants must be residing in the country where the study is conducted (Denmark or the USA) and at least 18 years old. These groups were selected to capture variation in experience with injectable treatments and perspectives on medication administration, which represent key dimensions relevant to the study objectives.

Disease areas were selected for relevance to oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery, such as BIONDD. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) commonly use injections on a weekly or biweekly basis. Patients with type 2 diabetes typically inject more frequently, which may shape different opinions compared with patients with RA. Obesity patients, eligible for chronic injectable treatments since 2021,22 often have recent experiences starting injection therapy. The general public rarely uses injections regularly but may recall vaccination experiences, especially after widespread COVID-19 vaccinations in 2021. Previously, injections were mostly linked to childhood immunisations or annual influenza injections.

Including participants with and without injection experience is essential, as this new pharmaceutical form could apply to multiple indications. Diverse viewpoints will ensure a comprehensive understanding of patients’ and public perspectives.

Theoretical framework

Adopting new innovative solutions or starting a new medication regimen can be complex processes influenced by various factors. A suitable theoretical framework for these situations could integrate elements from several established models. In this case, a combination of two theoretical frameworks will be used: the Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory23 24 and the Health Belief Model (HBM).25 26

The DOI theory, developed by Everett Rogers,23 24 is useful for understanding how new ideas, technologies or practices spread within a society or group. The HBM aids in understanding the process of initiating a new medication regimen by focusing on individual beliefs about health conditions that predict health-related behaviours, such as starting medication.25 26 An integrative framework combining elements from DOI and HBM will help provide a comprehensive understanding of the patients’ views of BIONDD. This approach considers two key aspects: first, that oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery represent a completely new mode of administration; and second, that acceptance and perceptions are influenced by patients’ health beliefs regarding medication.

Study 1: qualitative focus group interview with patients and public

This study will consist of 16 focus groups (FGs), 8 in Denmark and 8 in the USA, with six to eight participants per group. The size of the group will allow each participant time to express their opinions and share their stories.27 FG discussions will be facilitated by trained researchers with experience in qualitative methods. Each session will include a moderator and a co-moderator to guide the discussion and ensure balanced participation.

A semi-structured interview guide has been developed iteratively with input from the research team and patient partners and will be pilot tested prior to data collection (see online supplemental file 1). During the discussions, participants will be presented with a standardised visual explanation (video) of the BIONDD capsule illustrating its mechanism of action and will receive a standardised, evidence-informed description of the investigational capsule, including its proposed mechanism, potential benefits, available preclinical and early clinical evidence and the uncertainty surrounding its comparative clinical efficacy relative to injectable therapy, to support informed and balanced discussion.

Some FGs will consist of participants from similar disease groups, so they can spend less time explaining themselves to each other and more time discussing the topics at hand.27 Other groups will mix participants from different backgrounds to encourage questions, diverse viewpoints and explanations that might not otherwise merge. The use of both homogeneous and mixed groups is intended to encourage diverse perspectives and generate a broader range of discussion topics. Basic descriptive information will be collected for all participants, including age, gender, background characteristics, socioeconomic indicators (eg, income), disease group and prior experience with injectable treatments.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited through multiple channels to ensure the inclusion of individuals from diverse backgrounds. Recruitment strategies will include outreach via patient organisations, social media platforms, online research panels, as well as community-based approaches such as distributing flyers or posters in local shops, doctor offices and community pharmacies. A variety of recruitment methods will be used to maximise reach and enhance the diversity of the study sample.

All participants will receive a gift card equivalent to approximately US$30 as compensation for their participation. In Denmark, the gift card will be provided after the FG session, following recommendations from patient organisations and aligning with cultural norms where tokens of appreciation are typically given post-participation. In contrast, participants in the USA will be informed about the incentive during recruitment, in accordance with local regulatory and ethical requirements that mandate full disclosure of incentives as part of the informed consent process. Advertising the incentive upfront in the USA also supports timely recruitment within a limited time frame.

To minimise barriers and ensure equitable access, parking and related expenses will be reimbursed for all in-person participants. Additionally, participants in both Denmark and the USA will have the option to join sessions online, thereby increasing geographical accessibility and inclusivity. FG sessions will be conducted either fully online or fully in person; hybrid formats will not be used to ensure consistent interaction within each group. The recruitment strategy aims to capture variation in perspectives across participants, particularly in relation to experience with injectable treatments.

Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis will be performed to extract recurring themes and concepts relevant to questionnaire development. Findings will be used to directly inform development of the survey tool, including the selection and wording of questionnaire items. A combination of manual thematic analysis using NVivo, as described by Braun and Clarke,28 and an AI-assisted approach using Microsoft Copilot Enterprise29 30 harnesses the strengths of both human judgement and AI efficiency. This hybrid approach is increasingly adopted to improve both speed and depth in qualitative research, particularly for managing large datasets or enhancing coder triangulation.29 31 32

Coding will follow the hybrid inductive-deductive approach outlined by Fereday and Muir-Cochrane,33 allowing for both theory-driven codes based on existing frameworks and inductive codes emerging from the data. This ensures that the analysis captures both anticipated and novel insights.

The efficacy of the GPT-driven approach depends heavily on the quality of the instructions or ‘prompts’ provided to the model.34 Studies show that tailoring prompts to the nuances of thematic analysis can optimise ChatGPT’s usefulness in this field.32 Therefore, this study will build on the prompts presented by Turobov et al,30 Nguyen-Trung,29 and Sánchez et al (coauthor’s unpublished data), which have previously demonstrated to accurately reflect the results found in the traditional manual analysis.

Study 2: survey with patients and public

This study consists of a structured questionnaire which includes instructions, demographic items and themes emerging from the qualitative study. Prior to completing the questionnaire, participants will receive a stepwise visual and textual explanation of the BIONDD capsule, including its mechanism and comparison with oral and injectable treatments. This information will also explicitly describe the investigational nature of the delivery system to support informed responses. Participants will be informed that comparative clinical efficacy relative to injectable therapy has not yet been established and that available evidence represents early-stage preclinical and clinical investigations.

The survey will measure participants’ perspectives using structured items reflecting key themes identified in the qualitative phase. These themes refer to characteristics related to the oral capsule and its use (eg, perceived benefits, concerns and communication aspects), which are operationalised through survey items derived from the qualitative study. Respondents evaluate these using structured response scales, enabling quantitative analysis. The questionnaire content is informed by the qualitative findings and supplemented through theoretical framework, expert consultation and relevant literature to ensure relevance and clarity.

The questionnaire was developed for this study and is not intended as a fully validated instrument. However, selected items will be assessed for internal consistency and construct validity to support interpretation of the findings. The questionnaire will collect demographic information including age, gender, country of residence, ethnic or cultural background, educational level and socioeconomic indicators (eg, income). In addition, information on participants’ health status, disease background and experience with medication, including injectable treatments, will be collected.

The questionnaire will be developed in English and translated into Danish following the Principles of Good Practice for the Translation and Cultural Adaptation Process for Patient-Reported Outcome Measures.35 The translated version will then be pilot tested in both countries using cognitive debriefing interviews, including a ‘think-aloud’ method, as suggested by Tourangeau,36 to evaluate clarity, wording and response formats. Feedback from participants, including members of the public, will inform revisions to ensure cultural appropriateness, clarity and comparability across Denmark and the USA.

Recruitment

To ensure a diverse and representative sample, the recruitment strategy for the quantitative study will mirror the multifaceted approach used in the qualitative study. Survey participants will be recruited via patient organisations, social media platforms, online research panels and community-based outreach, such as distributing flyers or posters in local shops, doctors’ offices and community pharmacies. Throughout the recruitment period, the composition of respondents will be regularly monitored. If under-represented groups or regions are identified, targeted recruitment efforts will be implemented to achieve a balanced and inclusive sample as delineated in the study objectives.

Participants from the qualitative study will be encouraged to share the survey with their personal and professional networks to facilitate further recruitment via snowball sampling, thereby broadening the participant pool.

The survey will be administered online, enabling rapid data collection from a large and geographically dispersed population, while maximising convenience and anonymity for respondents. This method is cost-effective and efficient, allowing individuals to participate on their preferred devices at their convenience.

Prior to data collection, a sample size calculation will be conducted to estimate the minimum sample size required for reliable and valid analysis. The calculation will be based on the planned multivariable analysis and anticipated effect sizes. Based on preliminary estimates, approximately 200 evaluable participants per country are expected to be required to ensure adequate statistical power, with a recruitment target of approximately 236 participants per country to account for incomplete or ineligible responses. This approach ensures adequate statistical power to identify meaningful associations, while avoiding unnecessary resource expenditure associated with oversampling. All procedures will adhere to ethical guidelines, ensuring participation is voluntary, informed and confidential.

MMs integration

In this exploratory sequential MMs design, integration will occur both during development of the quantitative instrument and during interpretation of findings across phases. Themes identified in the qualitative study will inform questionnaire development, ensuring that the quantitative component is grounded in participants’ perspectives.

Integration of the qualitative and quantitative findings will be achieved through joint displays, matrix summaries and narrative comparisons of results that enable the side-by-side presentation and synthesis of findings from both strands of the study. This process will involve systematically comparing and integrating quantitative findings with the qualitative insights derived from the FGs to identify how quantitative results extend, confirm or contrast with the thematic analysis of the qualitative data.

Through this MMs approach, triangulation will be employed to enhance the credibility and robustness of the study’s conclusions. By examining the research questions from both qualitative and quantitative perspectives, potential contradictions can be clarified and the validity of the findings strengthened. The integration highlights both the breadth (‘how many/how much?’) provided by the quantitative analyses and the depth (‘why/how?’) revealed through the qualitative phase, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomena under study.

This synthesis will draw on descriptive statistics, reliability measures and inferential tests from the quantitative data, alongside the nuanced themes generated by the qualitative analysis. Findings will be interpreted and reported with particular attention to areas where the datasets complement, converge, or diverge, providing nuanced and multidimensional answers to the study’s core research questions.

Patient and public involvement

Patient partners will be involved from the outset as advisers in both qualitative and quantitative phases. They will help define inclusion criteria, review study materials for relevance and clarity and provide guidance on vocabulary and topics. Patient and public insights inform the development of the FG interview guide and the questionnaire. Members of both patient groups and the general public will participate in the study through FGs, the quantitative survey and pilot testing.

Patient organisations will support recruitment, and patient partners will contribute to the development of study materials in an advisory role and assess the burden of participation. Study results will be disseminated in patient-friendly and public-friendly formats through recruitment networks, newsletters and presentations.

Ethics and dissemination

The study is registered under the University of Copenhagen (UCPH) record (Case no.: 514–2024/24-3000) and has received approval from both the Research Ethics Committee for SCIENCE and SUND at UCPH (Case: 504–0542/24-5000) and the Institutional Review Board at Nova Southeastern University (IRB#: 2024–615).

Information collected during this research study will be handled confidentially, within the limits of the law, and access will be restricted to individuals who need to review the information. Only the main author (CB) will have access to personal identification data, while all other researchers will work with anonymised datasets. Data will be securely stored on the University of Copenhagen drives. All data management will comply with national regulations, the General Data Protection Regulation, Danish data protection law (Law no. 502 of 23 May 2018), and guidelines set out on UCPH’s Research Portal. Necessary agreements for data sharing or joint responsibility will be established with external collaborators as required.

Before taking part, all participants will receive comprehensive information about the study. Informed consent will be obtained in accordance with site-specific ethical procedures. For the qualitative study, written informed consent will be obtained from participants in Denmark prior to participation, while in the USA the study will be conducted under an Institutional Review Board-approved waiver of documentation of informed consent. For the quantitative study, informed consent will be obtained electronically when participants access the online questionnaire. Participation in the survey will be anonymous, and no personally identifiable information will be collected.

Participants will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they may withdraw without consequence up to a defined stage of the data processing procedure. Withdrawal of qualitative data is possible, provided that individual contributions can be linked to participants via ID numbers and a secure key held exclusively by the principal researcher (CB). Once the key has been deleted, or after study findings have been published or disseminated, it will no longer be possible to withdraw individual contributions. The study will collect data on experiences with needle injections and perspectives on a new dosage form, requiring careful handling of sensitive health-related information.

During FG sessions, participants will be advised to use only their first names. Audio recordings will be transcribed and assigned ID numbers; personal information such as names will not appear in any reports, publications or conference presentations. Confidentiality will be strictly maintained for interview participants, and questionnaire responses will be fully anonymised, with no personally identifiable information collected. All data will be processed anonymously, and pseudonyms will be used in interview transcripts where appropriate. In addition to these measures, a conscious effort will be made to encourage balanced participation so that all voices are heard, fostering an environment of active listening and ensuring that discussions remain non-judgemental and respectful.

The study began in May 2024 and is expected to continue until April 2027. Study results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at relevant scientific meetings and conferences. Authorship will be determined according to the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors guidelines. The research team is committed to ensuring that all published work is free from plagiarism and research misconduct, and that findings are accurately represented.

Discussion

This MM study will draw on the strengths of both qualitative and quantitative approaches to provide a nuanced, evidence-based understanding of patients’ and the public’s perspectives on BIONDD. By publishing this protocol, we aim to make our methodological choices explicit and transparent. However, a certain flexibility of methods is essential throughout the study, allowing insights gained at each stage to inform subsequent phases and ensuring the full potential of the multistage MM design is realised.18

Quality considerations

Methodological rigour and credibility will be maintained through established criteria and frameworks across qualitative, quantitative and integrative components. In the qualitative study, the quality is addressed through the four criteria of trustworthiness proposed by Lincoln and Guba: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.37 Credibility is ensured through techniques such as member checking, triangulation of data sources and prolonged engagement with the research setting, allowing for an accurate and authentic representation of participants’ perspectives. To enhance transferability, the study provides rich, thick descriptions of the context, participants and findings, enabling others to assess the extent to which results may be relevant in different settings. Dependability is ensured by maintaining a detailed record of all methodological decisions, procedures followed and any changes made during the study. This documentation allows others to understand how the research was conducted step by step. Additionally, the research process is reviewed through discussions with colleagues and, where possible, external reviewers to confirm that the study was carried out consistently and logically. Finally, confirmability is established by clearly linking findings to data, using direct quotations and maintaining reflexive documentation to account for the researcher’s influence on the process.

For the quantitative study, quality is ensured by focusing on validity, reliability, generalisability and replicability.38 Validity is addressed by grounding the development of the quantitative instrument in both the findings from the preceding qualitative study and the study’s theoretical framework. This ensures that the instrument is closely aligned with the constructs that are being examined. Where possible, established measurement tools are used; for newly developed items, pilot-testing is conducted to assess clarity, relevance and alignment with expected outcomes. Operational definitions are clearly defined, and all items are designed to reflect key concepts derived from both theory and empirical qualitative insights. Reliability will be explored through statistical approaches such as internal consistency (eg, Cronbach’s alpha) to support interpretation of the findings. Strict adherence to standardised data collection procedures ensures that measurements are consistent and replicable. Generalisability is supported by employing appropriate sampling strategies and offering detailed descriptions of the sample and research setting, while limitations related to representativeness are clearly identified. Replicability is safeguarded by meticulously documenting instruments, procedures and data analysis methods, allowing other researchers to reproduce the study under similar conditions.

Within the MMs approach, quality is ensured through the meaningful integration of qualitative and quantitative strands, as well as by addressing specific design-related concerns.18 39 In line with Fetters et al,40 ensuring validity in exploratory sequential designs involves three key considerations. First, the quantitative component must be grounded in the findings from the qualitative phase. This study addresses this by using the domains identified in the initial FG discussions to guide the development of the survey instrument in the subsequent quantitative phase. Second, it is essential to develop rigorous and methodologically sound quantitative tools. To meet this, systematic procedures such as pilot testing, expert review and assessment of scale properties are employed to support the robustness and interpretability of the survey findings. Third, care must be taken to avoid overlapping participants across phases to minimise bias. This is addressed by selecting a large, independent sample for the quantitative phase, thereby enhancing both generalisability and representativeness.

Integration is achieved not only at the design stage but also in data interpretation and reporting. This is facilitated through strategies such as joint displays and narrative explanations that illustrate how findings from both phases complement and inform one another. Legitimation strategies, including transparent documentation of integration procedures and engagement in methodological peer review, are employed to address potential validity threats and reinforce the overall credibility of the MMs design.

By systematically applying these principles across all components of the study, the research aims to uphold high standards of quality, trustworthiness and methodological integrity, ensuring that the findings offer valid, reliable and meaningful insights. As BIONDD remains in early-stage development and clinical efficacy has not yet been established, the findings reflect anticipated perceptions within an evidence-informed context rather than direct evaluations of confirmed clinical performance.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

The study’s strengths include the MMs design, enabling both in-depth qualitative exploration and quantitative generalisation, resulting in a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of patients’ and public perspectives on innovative drug-delivery capsules such as BIONDD. The inclusion of patients with diverse disease backgrounds and experiences ensures a broad spectrum of viewpoints, thereby strengthening the validity of the findings. Conducting research in both Denmark and the USA enables valuable comparative analyses between distinct healthcare systems and populations, enhancing the international relevance of the results. The study’s exploratory approach is anchored in established theories of innovation acceptance and medication use, guiding the development of robust frameworks, interview guides and survey instruments. Early and theory-driven patient involvement ensures that insights remain patient-centred and directly applicable to pharmaceutical product development. The multidisciplinary research team, with expertise in both qualitative and quantitative methods, strengthens the study’s design, analysis and interpretation. Embedding the project within real-world combination product development provides actionable insights to industry stakeholders, prescribers and other healthcare professionals.

Recruitment for both studies employs multiple channels to maximise reach and inclusivity, with procedures adapted to local contexts. In Denmark, incentives will be provided after participation, aligning with cultural norms and patient collaborators’ recommendations, which may encourage participation from those genuinely interested in the research topic. In the USA, incentives will be disclosed upfront to comply with Institutional Review Boards requirements and facilitate timely enrolment. These approaches aim to balance ethical, cultural and practical considerations, supporting diversity and engagement.

A notable strength is the hybrid approach to thematic analysis, merging AI-assisted and manual methods. AI tools such as ChatGPT have demonstrated advantages in thematic analysis, including enhanced efficiency, consistency and productivity when handling large qualitative datasets.29 30 32 41 These tools can streamline coding, organise data into clusters and help identify patterns that might be missed in manual analysis.29 32 Its ability to generate human-like text and facilitate rapid initial coding reduces time and labour while mitigating some challenges of researcher subjectivity and inconsistencies in manual theme identification.30 31 42–44 Studies also highlight the tool’s potential to standardise data analysis and support code diversity, making it a valuable supplement to traditional qualitative methods.44–46

Limitations

Despite these strengths, several limitations merit careful consideration. As the study is conducted exclusively in Denmark and the USA, the findings may primarily reflect a ‘Global North’ perspective and may not be generalisable to patients from other geographical or cultural backgrounds. Recruitment challenges could affect sample diversity, and differences in incentive disclosure may introduce bias, such as attracting participants primarily motivated by compensation in the USA or making recruitment more challenging in Denmark. There is also a risk that participants aware of the incentive might tailor their responses to what they perceive as desirable, potentially affecting data quality.

Although efforts are made to include patients from diverse demographic and disease backgrounds, recruitment pathways may still introduce selection bias, as individuals who choose to participate may not fully represent the broader patient population or the general public. Differences in recruitment or response rates between the qualitative and quantitative phases may also influence the study’s findings.

The anonymisation process in the quantitative phase, while essential for confidentiality, may lead to potential overlaps with participants from the qualitative phase; however, the use of a larger quantitative sample is intended to minimise this issue. Additionally, logistical and organisational challenges may arise from the cross-institutional and international nature of the collaboration.

While the hybrid AI-human approach to thematic analysis is innovative, it also introduces limitations, such as the tendency to produce more descriptive than interpretive codes, lack of transparency in the analytic process (‘black box’ issue), and better suitability for deductive rather than inductive analyses.30 45 46 Reliability and validity concerns may arise due to possible randomness, errors or unstructured outputs, especially when absent from a systematic coding framework.30 47–49 Ethical considerations, including data privacy and the risk of generating misleading information, must also be addressed.30 50

To mitigate these issues, AI-generated codes will be treated as provisional analytical suggestions, subject to independent review, refinement and consensus by researchers. Strategies include manual review and refinement of AI-generated codes, use of established prompting frameworks, careful prompt engineering and selective cross-validation.29 30 32 49 Ethical safeguards include data anonymisation, secure handling and utilisation of trusted AI tools.30 50 By combining the computational strengths of AI with rigorous qualitative methodology, the approach aims to maximise efficiency and analytical depth while maintaining the validity, reliability and ethical standards of the research.30

Potential implications of the study

The results of this study will shed light on potential benefits and concerns regarding oral capsules for gastric submucosal delivery. These insights will help identify unmet patient needs and enable developers and clinicians to better understand real-world challenges. This, in turn, can guide drug development toward outcomes that genuinely matter to patients.51

Early patient preference studies, when done well, can support the design of products that are more acceptable and usable for patients, thereby increasing the likelihood of real-world success. Early patient-based evidence also facilitates dialogue and alignment among developers, regulators, payers, clinicians and patients. In recent years, regulatory agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency, alongside major Health Technology Assessment bodies, have increasingly emphasised the importance of structured patient engagement and patient-preference data in early development. These data can strengthen regulatory submissions and reimbursement discussions by demonstrating that a new therapy addresses outcomes that are meaningful from the patient perspective.51

Finally, the lessons from this study will inform the development of a framework for patient engagement in early drug development, addressing gaps in current regulatory guidelines and requirements for effective patient involvement.
