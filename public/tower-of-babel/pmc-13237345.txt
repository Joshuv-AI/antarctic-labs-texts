NATON robotic-assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty achieves superior radiological alignment and comparable one-year functional outcomes to conventional surgery
Authors: Yuan Li, Ju Wu, Ke He, Guan-Jun Sun, Yi Yin, Xu Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To compare the early clinical efficacy and postoperative implant alignment between NATON robotic-assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (RA-UKA) and conventional UKA (C-UKA) in treating severe medial compartment knee osteoarthritis (OA). This retrospective matched cohort study included 40 RA-UKA and 40 C-UKA patients, matched 1:1 by age, gender, and surgical site et al. Primary outcomes included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs): knee society score (KSS), Oxford knee score (OKS), visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain, and forgotten joint score (FJS). Radiological assessments included coronal/sagittal angular deviations of femoral/tibial components, hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, and Kennedy zone distribution. The follow-up time was 15.4 ± 1.6 months. Operative time was significantly longer in the RA-UKA group (95.8 ± 6.8 min vs. 75.3 ± 6.5 min, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in KSS score, OKS score, or intraoperative blood loss between groups (all P > 0.05). RA-UKA achieved significantly better subjective PROMs, with lower VAS score (1.97 vs. 2.5, P = 0.012, Cohen’s d = − 0.60) and higher FJS score (86.9 vs. 84.3, P = 0.025, Cohen’s d = 0.50). The RA-UKA group had smaller femoral component coronal alignment deviation (2.39 ± 1.41° vs. 5.79 ± 1.15°, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = − 2.46) and flexion alignment deviation (3.3° (2.9–4.0) vs. 6.5° (5.9–7.1), P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = − 0.78). No significant differences were observed in tibial component coronal deviation and posterior tibial slope(PTS) deviation between two groups (all P > 0.05). Correlation analysis revealed weak negative correlations between FJS score and femoral coronal/flexion deviations (all P < 0.05). VAS score was weakly positive correlated with PTS deviation (ρ = 0.240, P = 0.032) and moderately positive correlated with femoral coronal deviation (r = 0.311, P = 0.005). No serious complications occurred in either group during follow-up. The NATON RA-UKA achieved more accurate early implant positioning than C-UKA, while short-term clinical outcomes were generally comparable between groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48561-9.

Introduction

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) represents one of the optimal surgical interventions for single-compartment knee osteoarthritis. Compared to total knee arthroplasty (TKA), UKA offers distinct advantages including reduced blood loss, shorter operative time, and faster recovery. However, the success of UKA depends on multiple factors such as patient selection, implant design, component alignment, fixation, and soft tissue balance1–5. Achieving accurate component alignment using conventional instrumentation remains challenging, with studies reporting outliers exceeding 2° from the planned alignment in 40–60% of cases6,7.

Precise component positioning and fixation have been shown to reduce failure rates8,9. Recent advancements in robotic-assisted surgery, incorporating 3D computed tomography (CT), real-time navigation, and robotic arm precision, have demonstrated significant improvements in implant accuracy, more objective soft tissue balancing, and better restoration of knee kinematics10,11. International multicenter studies reveal that RA-UKA reduces component angle deviations by over 50% compared to conventional techniques (coronal plane deviation < 1° vs. 2.5°), with 5-year revision rates decreasing from 8.2 to 3.1%12–14. Compared to C-UKA, RA-UKA also demonstrates superior gait kinematics and improved clinical score15–17. For novice surgeons, RA-UKA shows no learning curve effect in terms of implant accuracy and clinical outcomes10. RA-UKA becomes cost-effective compared to C-UKA when the annual case volume exceeds 94 procedures, though it may not be cost-effective in low-to-medium volume arthroplasty centers18.

The NATON surgical robot represents an emerging active, image-dependent orthopedic platform, with its core design focusing on enhancing the precision and reproducibility of prosthesis implantation in UKA through preoperative 3D planning and intraoperative active robotic-arm assisted bone resection. Current clinical data on domestic NATON RA-UKA are primarily limited to small-sample, short-term observations, lacking high-level evidence-based comparisons with conventional techniques19. Existing studies focus mainly on postoperative functional score without comprehensive radiographic quantification of implant accuracy. Therefore, this study aimed to compare radiographic outcomes and early clinical results between NATON RA-UKA and C-UKA. We hypothesized that robot-assisted UKA would achieve more precise prosthesis positioning, with short-term clinical outcomes comparable to those of C-UKA.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by Ethics Committee of Suining Central Hospital (Approval No. KYLLKS20240207). Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with group assignment (RA‑UKA vs. C‑UKA) as the dependent variable. A 1:1 nearest‑neighbor matching approach was used with a caliper width set at 0.2 standard deviations, a widely recommended value to minimize bias while maintaining a sufficient sample size. Sampling with replacement was not used. Matching variables included age, sex, BMI, side, preoperative knee joint disease duration, last follow‑up time, preoperative HKA angle, and preoperative knee function score. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to evaluate covariate balance before and after matching. Before matching, 55 patients were eligible in the RA‑UKA group and 57 in the C‑UKA group. Using this matching strategy, 40 well‑matched pairs were obtained, resulting in 40 patients in each group. The IRENE Zheng Tian Unique prosthesis is a fixed-bearing unicompartmental knee system with an onlay femoral component. All procedures were performed using the same model of UKA (Unique, IRENE Zheng Tian, Naton Medical Group, Beijing, China). Inclusion criteria were: (1) primary medial compartment knee osteoarthritis graded III-IV on the Kellgren-Lawrence scale; (2) varus deformity < 15° and flexion contracture < 15° with active knee flexion ≥ 90°; (3) functionally intact anterior cruciate and collateral ligaments; (4) age between 50 and 80 years; (5) body mass index < 35 kg/m²; and (6) All included patients completed regular postoperative rehabilitation and follow-up. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inflammatory arthritis, or significant pathology in the lateral or patellofemoral compartments; (2) varus deformity ≥ 15° or flexion contracture ≥ 15°; (3) severe comorbidities contraindicating surgery; (4) active infection; (5) history of prior ipsilateral knee surgery; (6) severe psychiatric or cognitive disorders impairing compliance; and (7) confirmed periprosthetic or systemic infection). RA-UKA procedure

The RA-UKA utilized a fixed-bearing unicompartmental prosthesis. An onlay design was utilized for the femoral component. Patients in the RA-UKA group underwent preoperative lower limb CT scanning. Three-dimensional CT data were used to create a computer-aided design (CAD) model, and preoperative surgical planning was performed using a domestic knee replacement navigation and positioning system (Beijing Naton Medical Robot Co., Ltd.). Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in the supine position. A medial parapatellar approach was utilized to expose the medial femoral condyle and tibial plateau. The NATON robotic system’s tracking arrays were securely fixed to the femur and tibia with pins. Following system guidance, the femoral and ankle centers were registered, and knee kinematics were assessed to determine the rotational center and ligament tension. Bone registration was performed by digitizing key femoral landmarks (knee center, distal femur, posterior condyles) and tibial landmarks (tibial center, lowest point, posterior aspect, medial/lateral margins, and intercondylar eminence). After registration, optimal implant size and position were selected in the system interface. The robotic arm then guided bone preparation using a burr according to the preoperative plan, followed by implantation of the femoral and tibial components and wound closure. C-UKA procedure

The C-UKA utilized a fixed-bearing unicompartmental prosthesis. An onlay design was utilized for the femoral component. With the patient in the supine position, an 8 cm medial parapatellar incision was made. After confirming isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis and ligamentous integrity, the medial meniscus and peripheral osteophytes were resected. Tibial resection was performed using an extramedullary guide, with a bone cut of 6–8 mm maintaining 7° of posterior slope to ensure balanced flexion and extension gaps. The femoral condyle was then prepared using a femoral cutting guide. Trial components were inserted to assess joint mobility and stability. Finally, the tibial and femoral components were cemented in place, a polyethylene insert was implanted, and the wound was irrigated and closed in layers.

Perioperative patient management

Both groups followed identical perioperative protocols. Patients received intravenous 1.5 g cefuroxime sodium and 1 g tranexamic acid 30 min preoperatively. The standardized rehabilitation protocol commenced after anesthesia resolution, initiating ankle pumps and quadriceps isometric contractions. On postoperative day 1, patients began ambulating with a walker and performing straight leg raises. Active knee flexion-extension exercises (range 0° to 100°) were started on day 2. Subsequently, supervised rehabilitation under a physical therapist included progressive knee extension, flexion, and straight leg raise exercises, with ongoing assessment of functional recovery. Patients continued ambulation with walker assistance until discharge, followed by individualized home exercise programs emphasizing moderate, consistent activity while avoiding excessive joint stress. Patients were instructed to avoid overexertion causing joint swelling, pain, or subcutaneous ecchymosis during the first postoperative month.

Follow-up plan and observation indicators

Both groups were documented for operative time and estimated surgical blood loss. All patients were evaluated using validated patient-reported outcome measures. The Knee Society Score (KSS, 0-100) assessed objective knee function, while the Oxford Knee Score (OKS, 0–48) evaluated perceived knee function20,21. Pain was quantified using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10), and joint awareness was measured with the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS, 0-100)22. Standard anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs, as well as full-length standing radiographs of the lower limbs, were obtained. Radiographic measurements included the femoral component coronal deviation, femoral component flexion deviation, tibial component coronal deviation, and PTS deviation (Fig. 4A). The Prosthesis Deviation Score is an exploratory comprehensive radiological assessment indicator (equal weights). The specific calculation method is as follows: Prosthesis Deviation Score = Absolute value of femoral component coronal deviation + Absolute value of femoral component flexion deviation + Absolute value of tibial component coronal deviation + Absolute value of PTS deviation. The hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA) and the distribution of the mechanical axis according to the Kennedy zones were measured from pre- and postoperative full-length standing radiographs. The Kennedy zone distribution, based on the tibial plateau division described by Kennedy and White23, categorizes the tibial plateau into zones 0, 1, 2, C, 3, 4, and 5. The zone through which the line connecting the center of the femoral head and the center of the ankle joint passed was recorded. A result falling within zone 2 or zone C was defined as “Excellent/Good” (Fig. 4B). Radiographic assessment was standardized using full-length standing lower limb radiographs. During imaging, patients stood upright with both feet parallel and toes pointing directly forward. Images demonstrating lower limb rotation (unilateral or bilateral) were excluded from analysis. All radiographic parameters were measured twice by two resident physicians who were not involved in the surgical procedures, with the average value used for analysis. The inter-observer reliability of all radiographic measurements was assessed by two independent orthopedic surgeons who were blinded to the group allocation. The interobserver reliability of all radiographic measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement, all ICC values demonstrated excellent reliability, ranging from 0.85 to 0.96. All measurements were performed using the calibrated digital measurement tools of the INFINITT PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. Normally distributed data with homogeneous variance are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using independent-samples t tests. Non-normally distributed data are presented as the median with interquartile range [M (IQR)] and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed to explore the associations between radiographic alignment deviation parameters and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients according to data distribution. Effect sizes were reported to complement statistical significance. Cohen’s d was used for continuous variables, while the Phi (φ) coefficient or Cramér’s V was used for categorical variables, as appropriate.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary radiographic alignment outcomes using a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 to assess whether the available sample size provided sufficient statistical power. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

All patients in both groups were successfully followed up for a mean duration of 15.4 ± 1.6 months (range: 12–18 months). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in baseline characteristics, including age, sex, side, body mass index (BMI), disease duration, time to last follow-up, and preoperative knee joint function score (FJS, OKS, VAS) (all P > 0.05), indicating good balance and comparability between the groups (Table 1).

Postoperative imaging evaluation showed that the femoral component coronal deviation in the RA-UKA group was significantly smaller than that in the C-UKA group (2.39 ± 1.41° vs. 5.79 ± 1.15°, P < 0.001), and the femoral component flexion deviation was also significantly smaller in the RA-UKA group than in the C-UKA group (3.3 (2.9–4.0)° vs. 6.5 (5.9–7.1)°, P < 0.001). Although the tibial component coronal deviation and PTS deviation in the RA-UKA group were closer to the ideal values, the differences were not statistically significant (both P > 0.05). There were no significant differences in HKA parameters (P = 0.743) or Kennedy mechanical axis distribution between the two groups. Furthermore, the total deviation score of the RA-UKA group was significantly better than that of the C-UKA group (11.29 ± 3.46 vs. 18.81 ± 3.24, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Post hoc power analysis demonstrated that, with 40 patients in each group, the achieved statistical power exceeded 0.90 for femoral component coronal deviation and femoral component flexion deviation. In contrast, tibial component coronal deviation and PTS deviation exhibited small effect sizes and limited statistical power.

The operation time in the RA-UKA group was significantly longer than that in the C-UKA group (95.8 ± 6.8 min vs. 75.3 ± 6.5 min, P < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in intraoperative blood loss between the two groups (P > 0.05). There were no significant differences in postoperative KSS score, OKS score, and ROM between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Patients in the RA-UKA group had lower VAS score (1.97 vs. 2.5, P = 0.012, Cohen’s d=-0.60) and higher FJS score (86.9 ± 6.3 vs. 84.3 ± 3.9), P = 0.025, Cohen’s d = 0.5), both with moderate effect sizes (Table 3). Postoperative radiological images of a typical RA-UKA case are shown in Fig. 5.

In the correlation analysis, none of the prosthesis alignment deviation indices were significantly correlated with KSS score and OKS score (all P > 0.05). The FJS score was weakly negative correlated with the total deviation Score (r=-0.225, P = 0.045), weakly negative correlated with femoral coronal deviation (r=-0.273, P = 0.013), and weakly negative correlated with Femoral component flexion deviation (ρ=-0.263, P = 0.019). The VAS score was weakly positive correlated with the total deviation score (r = 0.294, P = 0.008), moderately correlated correlated with femoral coronal deviation (r = 0.311, P = 0.005), and weakly positive correlated with PTS deviation (ρ = 0.240, P = 0.032) (Table 4; Fig. 6). Since multiple correlation analyses were performed without adjustment for multiple comparisons, borderline significant results should be interpreted with caution. A total of 84 patients were included in the RA-UKA group, and 5 cases were converted to conventional surgery intraoperatively, with a conversion rate of 5.9% (5/84). These 5 patients were not included in the final statistical analysis. All patients completed their last follow-up, with no loss to follow-up or missing data. No serious complications (such as infection, poor wound healing, or periprosthetic fracture) occurred in either group during the entire follow-up period.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that robotic technology improves the accuracy of femoral component positioning while extending operative time. Both techniques demonstrated comparable outcomes in terms of early postoperative clinical function and overall lower limb alignment restoration.

The operative time in the NATON RA-UKA group was significantly longer than that in the C-UKA group (95.8 ± 6.8 min vs. 75.3 ± 6.5 min), with an average increase of 20 min. The operative time for NATON robotic-assisted surgery (95.8 ± 6.8 min) was shorter than that reported by Tan et al.24 for Mako robotic-assisted surgery (127.45 ± 36.72 min). The primary reasons for the longer operative time in the RA-UKA group compared to the C-UKA group include: (1) robot positioning via bone trackers, (2) management of occasional robotic arm malfunctions, and (3) repeated adjustments to ensure precise implant placement. Studies by Lee et al.25 and Mergenthaler et al.26 have demonstrated that robotic assistance does not lead to higher surgical complications despite longer operative times. Furthermore, this time difference is expected to gradually diminish as surgeons progress along the learning curve and robotic workflows are optimized. In this study, the RA-UKA group also exhibited greater blood loss compared to the C-UKA group, which is consistent with the findings of Khan et al.27 Increased intraoperative blood loss may also be attributed to the surgeon’s unfamiliarity with the technique. Additionally, the placement of tracking pins in the femur and tibia in the RA-UKA group may contribute to increased blood loss. Nevertheless, the increased blood loss observed in this study did not result in a higher transfusion rate or affect early patient recovery.

In this study, the RA-UKA group demonstrated superior femoral component positioning accuracy in both the coronal and sagittal planes compared to the C-UKA group, with component flexion deviations of 3.3 ° (2.9–4.0) versus 6.5 ° (5.9–7.1), and component coronal deviations of 2.0°±1.97° versus 5.79°±1.14°, respectively. Wu et al.28 reported a mean coronal plane deviation of 2.59°±5.4° for femoral components using the Mako robotic system, indicating a high level of consistency with the operational accuracy of active robotic platforms like the NATON system. In contrast, handheld systems such as the Navio robot, while also significantly improving precision, place greater emphasis on integrating surgeon tactile feedback with robotic assistance. A review29 noted that Navio-assisted surgeries consistently demonstrated favorable restoration of knee anatomical axis, joint line, femoral angle, and tibial angle, generally slightly surpassing active robotic systems while remaining substantially superior to conventional techniques. No significant difference was observed in tibial component alignment (coronal angle and posterior slope) between the two groups. This is likely attributable to the relative technical simplicity of tibial resection, which allows conventional instruments to achieve sufficient accuracy, whereas the advantage of robotic assistance is more pronounced in the femoral side due to its more complex anatomical geometry and higher demands for three-dimensional positioning. The rate of coronal plane outliers for femoral components was 10% in the RA-UKA group, significantly lower than the 30% observed in the C-UKA group. Compared with published data, our outlier rate is comparable to the 10.9% reported by Park et al.30 using the Mako system, and slightly better than the 12% reported in earlier studies on the Navio system Although the two groups showed comparable rates of tibial coronal plane outliers, the RA-UKA group exhibited fewer extreme deviations31. Regarding overall implantation precision, the RA-UKA group achieved a significantly lower total component deviation score (Exploratory indicators) than the C-UKA group (P < 0.001). This finding may indicate better overall positioning accuracy, although this interpretation is preliminary and requires further validation.

Since UKA aims to preserve the patient’s preoperative physiological alignment and restore balance solely by replacing the worn medial compartment, its principle differs from TKA, making it crucial to avoid over-correction of the mechanical axis32,33. In this study, the postoperative HKA angle was corrected by only approximately 2° in both groups compared to preoperative values, with no significant difference in final HKA between the groups. Furthermore, the RA-UKA group demonstrated smaller HKA deviation and less dispersion than the C-UKA group. This indicates that as long as varus deformity is not over-corrected in UKA, the postoperative HKA typically remains close to the preoperative value, thereby preserving the patient’s unique physiological state. RA-UKA maintains the original ligamentous balance more effectively by preventing unintended ligament laxity or overtightening through precise bone resection, which can occasionally occur in conventional surgery. Consequently, the alignment distribution in the RA-UKA group likely more closely resembles the preoperative condition. However, since ligament balance was largely maintained in the C-UKA group by experienced surgeons, both groups ultimately exhibited similar macroscopic alignment outcomes. Although no statistically significant difference was found in the distribution of the mechanical axis Kennedy zones between the two groups (P = 0.144), the RA-UKA group achieved a higher excellent rate of Kennedy zone distribution (100% vs. 95%) with no cases of varus over-correction. Studies by Sun et al.34 and Negrín et al.31 also support that RA-UKA provides more accurate prosthesis positioning and alignment distribution.

No significant differences in short-term clinical outcomes were observed between the RA-UKA and C-UKA groups, as assessed by KSS and OKS scores, which aligns with the 2-year follow-up clinical results reported by Gilmour et al.35 Although Blyth et al.36 noted significantly greater early pain improvement in the robotic group (with a 55.4% lower median pain score compared to the conventional group, p = 0.004), this difference disappeared by the one-year mark. Conversely, Negrín et al.31supported the superiority of RA-UKA in pain and functional scores, potentially attributable to reduced soft tissue injury through robotic precision osteotomy37,38. In the present study, the RA-UKA group demonstrated a statistically significant lower VAS pain score (1.97 ± 1.10 vs. 2.50 ± 0.60, P = 0.012) and higher FJS score (86.9 ± 6.3 vs. 84.3 ± 3.9, P = 0.025) at short-term follow-up. These findings may be associated with more precise bone preparation and prosthesis positioning achieved by robotic-assisted techniques, which could contribute to improved joint line restoration and ligament balance, thereby reducing abnormal soft tissue impingement and shear stress, alleviating postoperative pain, and promoting a more natural knee sensation. This interpretation is partly supported by our correlation analysis, which showed weak associations between greater femoral alignment deviations and less favorable FJS score. However, it is important to note that the observed mean differences in VAS (0.53 points) and FJS (2.6 points) score were below the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds reported for UKA (approximately 1.6–2.0 points for VAS and approximately 14 points for FJS)39,40. This suggests that while statistically significant, these improvements may not represent clinically meaningful benefits at short-term follow-up. In addition, Hansen et al.41 reported a higher incidence of knee pain in the robotic group at 2-year follow-up in a retrospective cohort of 62 patients undergoing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty using either robotic-assisted or conventional techniques, underscoring the variability of patient-reported pain outcomes and the need for larger, prospective, long-term studies.

In our study, there were no complications or revision cases, resulting in an early survival rate of 100%, consistent with the early survival outcomes of RA-UKA reported by Banger et al.42 and Sun et al.34 Harris et al.43 also identified differences in prosthesis positioning between the two groups, with RA-UKA exhibiting a significantly lower postoperative revision rate. Regarding prosthesis survival, the three-year revision rate for MAKO robotic UKA was reported as 2.5%44. Similarly, Cool et al.45 concluded that RA-UKA has a higher survival rate than C-UKA, whereas Wong et al.46 contended that the survival rates are similar, reaching 97% at two years postoperatively. Due to the short follow-up period in our study, long-term complications have not yet been observed. In terms of postoperative gait kinematics, Motesharei et al.15 found that RA-UKA performed better at one year after surgery. Whether the lower revision rate of RA-UKA should be attributed to the technology itself or to the experience of early adopters remains unclear. However, for less experienced surgeons, the technology effectively enhances surgical accuracy through standardized procedures. In this study, 5 patients (5.9%) were converted from robotic-assisted to conventional surgery due to reasons such as robotic arm malfunction, software issues, significant deviations in bone resection, or loosening of the reference array (these cases were excluded from the final outcome analysis). Currently, no authoritative published literature directly reports the conversion rates for UKA procedures using Mako, Navio, or other robotic systems. Potential causes for such technical problems include: (1) The limited range of motion of the robotic arm may lead to singular positions during extreme joint positioning, resulting in arm lock-up47; (2) The preoperative 3D-CT-based planning requires high image quality, where scanning inaccuracies or patient movement can cause mismatches in anatomical landmark registration48; (3) Inaccurate intraoperative joint surface registration may lead to significant over- or under-resection; (4) Insecure fixation of the reference array (e.g., failure to engage both cortices) makes it prone to loosening during limb manipulation or accidental contact49.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it is subject to potential selection and confounding bias, although 1:1 matching was used to reduce this risk. Second, while the sample size was adequate for the primary radiographic outcome, the overall cohort remained small, limiting the evaluation of rare complications and the feasibility of more advanced statistical modeling. Third, the short follow-up period (mean 15.4 months) precluded assessment of long-term implant survival, osteoarthritis progression in adjacent compartments, and conversion to total knee arthroplasty. The exploratory correlation analyses did not account for multiple comparisons, which may increase the risk of type I error. Therefore, the early advantages and potential relationship of RA-UKA in prosthesis alignment and patient-reported outcomes require further investigation with longer durations and larger sample sizes. Fourth, the Prosthesis Deviation Score was used as an exploratory indicator and lacks standardized validation, and its relationship with long-term outcomes was not assessed. Fifth, Exclusion of converted cases may introduce selection bias. Our results only reflect the performance of the robotic system in successfully completed cases and may not represent the overall effectiveness in a real-world intention-to-treat population. Finally, radiographic evaluation was based on two-dimensional imaging, which cannot fully represent three-dimensional implant position; CT would be more precise but was not used to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure. Further large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and the cost-effectiveness of the NATON robotic system.

Conclusion

The NATON RA-UKA achieved more accurate early implant positioning than C-UKA, while short-term clinical outcomes were generally comparable between groups.
