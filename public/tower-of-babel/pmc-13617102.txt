Tendon-muscle-nerve disconnection in chronic large and massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears: a targeted narrative mechanistic review and hypothesis-generating framework
Authors: Tao Chen, Jinghui Song, Liang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anatomical repair of chronic large or massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears does not invariably restore strength, endurance, or patient-reported function. This targeted narrative mechanistic review synthesizes cuff-specific human studies, rotator cuff animal models, and explicitly labelled non-cuff skeletal-muscle evidence to examine persistent dysfunction after repair. Its primary purpose is to calibrate evidence across mechanical load-transfer failure, selected neural modifiers, and stromal remodeling; its secondary purpose is to propose a falsifiable, hypothesis-generating tendon-muscle-nerve (TMN) framework. Human evidence is strongest for the prognostic association of fatty infiltration, atrophy, muscle volume, and tendon integrity. Suprascapular neuropathy is documented in selected patients but is not present in most chronic massive tears. Cuff-specific evidence for neuromuscular-junction remodeling is sparse and conflicting, whereas fibro-adipogenic progenitor, macrophage, extracellular-vesicle, and metabolic-fate mechanisms remain predominantly animal, ex-vivo, or extrapolative. We therefore propose that mechanical burden, defined neural modifiers, and candidate biological stage be measured separately and initially retained as continuous variables. Proposed categories and quantitative anchors require prospective testing for reliability, longitudinal stability, incremental prognostic value, and treatment-effect heterogeneity. The TMN construct is not a validated diagnosis, staging system, or treatment algorithm. Mechanical restoration remains the clinical foundation; neural and stromal interventions remain selective or preclinical research hypotheses.

Introduction and conceptual framework

The clinical paradox motivating this review is straightforward: tendon healing matters, yet it does not guarantee biological recovery. In chronic large and massive tears, even a healed repair may coexist with persistent weakness, limited endurance, pain-related inhibition, and disappointing function. The more difficult question is not simply whether the tendon can be reattached, but whether the remaining muscle is still contractile, recruitable, metabolically responsive, and capable of transmitting force.

Fatty infiltration, atrophy, and fibrosis provide the clearest clinical clue. The Goutallier classification made fatty degeneration a reproducible imaging feature, and subsequent MRI studies associated fatty degeneration, atrophy, and tear chronicity with reparability, healing, and outcome (Goutallier et al., 1994; Fuchs et al., 1999; Goutallier et al., 2003; Patte, 1990; Thomazea et al., 1997; Zanetti et al., 1998; Nakagaki et al., 1995). Notably, these muscle abnormalities often persist after repair and accompany poorer clinical results and lower force (Gladstone et al., 2007; Gerber et al., 2007). Studies of large and massive tears reach a similar conclusion from another direction: tendon integrity is necessary, but it does not account for all variation in postoperative function (Galatz et al., 2004; Harryman et al., 1991; Gerber et al., 2000).

Here, TMN disconnection is used solely as an organizing research construct for separately measurable failures of tendon load transfer, muscle contractility, neural activation, and stromal homeostasis. It does not denote a uniform lesion, a validated staging system, or a predetermined causal sequence, and it is not intended as a diagnostic label or treatment algorithm.

Our primary focus is chronic large and massive posterosuperior tears involving the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. Definitions of a massive tear differ across studies, variously relying on a diameter greater than 5 cm, involvement of at least two tendons, or topographic criteria (Lädermann et al., 2016). We therefore retain the definition used in each source and use large/massive as a scope descriptor, not as a new threshold. Acute traumatic tears, isolated subscapularis or teres minor disease, postoperative failure, and cuff-tear arthropathy may share elements of this model, but their trajectories should not be assumed to be identical.

The purpose of this targeted narrative review is to synthesize what is established in human cuff disease, identify where cuff-specific animal experiments provide model-limited perturbational evidence, and distinguish these findings from non-cuff skeletal-muscle extrapolation. The proposed framework is intended to generate testable hypotheses and minimum datasets rather than to establish a new diagnosis.

We therefore organize the synthesis around two independent research questions. First, is the observed pattern most consistent with mechanical unloading, a measured neural modifier, or both? Second, do imaging and function suggest candidate early stress, intermediate remodeling, or advanced fibrofatty replacement? These labels are probabilistic research descriptors and require prospective validation.

The subsequent sections proceed from the strongest evidence for mechanical load-transfer failure and human muscle-quality prognosis to selected neural findings, candidate stromal mechanisms, unvalidated temporal descriptors, research phenotyping, and translational boundaries.

Established structural variables remain the starting point for both clinical and experimental work: tear size, tendon involvement, retraction, topography, muscle volume, and fatty infiltration (Patte, 1990; Lädermann et al., 2016). The TMN approach asks a narrower, testable question: do separately measured mechanical, neural, and stromal variables add reproducible prognostic information, and does biological stage modify the response to structural repair?

Fatty infiltration is not merely a radiological descriptor. It signals loss of muscle quality and may contribute directly to poor function. CT and MRI grading systems provided the historical foundation; quantitative MRI, CT density, three-dimensional muscle volume, and regional fat mapping now offer a more detailed view of spatial heterogeneity (Goutallier et al., 1994; Goutallier et al., 2003; Zanetti et al., 1998; Kuzel et al., 2013; Valencia et al., 2018). This distinction matters because mechanical unloading and neurogenic injury may not produce the same distribution of fat.

Natural-history studies show that untreated or enlarging tears may progress both in size and in muscle degeneration. Fatty infiltration and atrophy are not explained by age alone; they also track with chronicity, tendon involvement, retraction, and symptomatic progression (Melis et al., 2010; Mall et al., 2010; Keener et al., 2015; Safran et al., 2011; Yamaguchi et al., 2001). The muscle therefore has a trajectory of its own. A tendon lesion establishes the pathologic setting, but the muscle and interstitium continue to remodel even as symptoms fluctuate.

Clinical outcome studies connect fatty infiltration with function. Fatty degeneration predicts inferior strength and repair integrity, histological studies show myofiber degeneration, fibrosis, and cellular changes in advanced human disease, and biomechanical work links higher fat fraction with reduced force-generating capacity (Gladstone et al., 2007; Gerber et al., 2007; Galatz et al., 2004; Bogd et al., 2021; Bowen et al., 2025; Santago et al., 2016; Gibbons et al., 2017; Valencia et al., 2018). A successful repair consequently has two relevant endpoints: structural tendon continuity and reconnection of a muscle that can generate and transmit force.

Mechanical disconnection: force-transmission failure and muscle remodeling

Loss of force transmission is the most immediate consequence of a chronic full-thickness tear. In an intact cuff, force generated by the supraspinatus and infraspinatus is transmitted through the intramuscular connective tissue and myotendinous unit to the tendon insertion on the greater tuberosity of the humerus. Rupture interrupts that path. Muscle fibers may continue to contract, but their force is no longer conveyed efficiently to bone. This is more specific than simple disuse: the route by which the muscle performs mechanical work has failed.

The biomechanical literature supports this interpretation. The rotator cable and crescent concept explains how load may be redistributed across the cuff, whereas partial-repair strategies seek to restore force couples when complete anatomical coverage is not possible (Burkhart et al., 1993; Burkhart et al., 2003; Burkhart, 1997). The biological corollary is plausible: when force cannot be transmitted, the muscle loses tensile feedback, sarcomere-length homeostasis, and activity-dependent metabolic demand. Thus, even a moving shoulder may contain a chronically under-loaded muscle.

Animal studies provide causal support for this mechanical pathway. Tendon release or tenotomy can produce muscle atrophy, fatty infiltration, and fibrosis without deliberate nerve injury. Across sheep, rat, and mouse models, tendon release, delayed repair, and creation of large tears lead to persistent changes in muscle architecture and composition (Gerber et al., 2004; Meyer et al., 2004; Luan et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2012; Gumucio et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018a; Rowshan et al., 2010). Differences in quadruped anatomy, loading, and chronicity limit direct translation, but the repeated result is important: tendon injury alone can initiate muscle degeneration.

The disruption is also architectural. Shortening, loss of pennation, altered passive tension, and fibrotic stiffening may leave a muscle that can be mobilized surgically but remains physiologically compromised. Once this remodeling is established, restoring tendon length may recover anatomy without normalizing intramuscular strain. This helps explain why early restoration of tension may not be biologically equivalent to late repair.

Neural dysfunction: established peripheral findings and candidate modifiers

Peripheral suprascapular neuropathy

The suprascapular nerve warrants attention because it supplies both the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. Medial tendon retraction may increase traction at the suprascapular or spinoglenoid notch. Nevertheless, anatomical plausibility alone is not evidence of electrophysiologically confirmed neuropathy.

Direct human evidence is available, although chiefly in selected subgroups. Suprascapular neuropathy has been documented in some massive tears, and recovery after repair or decompression has been reported (Boykin et al., 2010; Mallon et al., 2006; Costouros et al., 2007; Lafosse et al., 2007). By contrast, prospective EMG studies have not found neuropathy in most massive tears, and comparative data do not support routine decompression (Collin et al., 2014; Gerber et al., 2020). Peripheral neuropathy therefore needs to be demonstrated, not inferred from tear size or fatty infiltration.

Imaging and experimental studies help define the limits of this association. Chronic cuff tears and suprascapular neuropathy may produce different patterns of atrophy and fat (Beeler et al., 2013); severe neurogenic fatty infiltration can occur despite intact tendons (Leclere et al., 2014); and isolated tendon or nerve injury can independently induce fatty infiltration, atrophy, and fibrosis in animals (Mistretta et al., 2025; Eshima et al., 2023). Taken together, these observations support a neural modifier in a subset of cases, not a universal neural cause.

When present, a peripheral nerve abnormality may intensify degeneration, broaden its distribution, or limit recovery. Compression, traction injury, and denervation need to be examined as component-specific questions. They should not be conflated with pain inhibition, reduced voluntary drive, or remodeling at the neuromuscular junction.

Pain-related central inhibition and motor-unit recruitment

A patient may also fail to activate the cuff fully in the absence of a peripheral nerve lesion. Pain-related central inhibition and altered motor control can reduce voluntary drive, whereas motor-unit recruitment is a separate physiological level that may be assessed through interference-pattern analysis, motor-unit number estimation, or activation testing. Both are plausible explanations for weakness that appears disproportionate to structure, but direct longitudinal evidence in chronic large/massive posterosuperior tears remains sparse.

EMG/NCS is useful for confirming selected peripheral abnormalities, but its interpretive limits matter. A normal result does not exclude mechanical unloading or pain-related inhibition, and an abnormal result does not establish failure of the neuromuscular junction. Future studies need to report these neural levels separately and relate each to objective strength, endurance, pain, and the regional imaging pattern (Boykin et al., 2010; Mallon et al., 2006; Costouros et al., 2007; Lafosse et al., 2007; Collin et al., 2014; Gerber et al., 2020; Beeler et al., 2013; Leclere et al., 2014; Mistretta et al., 2025; Eshima et al., 2023).

Hypothesis-generating NMJ remodeling: general skeletal-muscle extrapolation

Cuff-specific evidence for NMJ remodeling remains sparse and inconsistent, and comparable longitudinal data in human chronic cuff tears are unavailable. Most mechanistic information on motor-endplate remodeling, acetylcholine-receptor organization, and excitation-contraction coupling derives from aging, denervation, or neuromuscular disease outside the rotator cuff (Tintignac et al., 2015; Li et al., 2018; R et al., 2014; Blaauw et al., 2013). NMJ morphology should therefore be treated as a hypothesis-generating research endpoint—not an established driver, a phenotyping criterion, or an inference from conventional EMG/NCS.

Neural-mechanical convergence

Mechanical unloading and reduced activation may ultimately create a similar low-tension, low-demand microenvironment. Studies in general skeletal muscle connect reduced loading and altered growth signaling with the autophagy-lysosome and ubiquitin-proteasome systems, as well as Akt/mTOR and FoxO pathways (Sandri, 2013; Bonaldo and Sandri, 2013; Egerman and Glass, 2014; Schiaffino et al., 2013; Bodine et al., 2001; Sandri et al., 2004; Jorgenson et al., 2020). These findings establish biological plausibility, but they do not show that one convergence pathway accounts for human cuff disease.

One animal study illustrates the point. In a combined massive-tear and suprascapular-nerve-injury model, mTOR-SREBP-1-PPAR-gamma signaling was associated with adipogenesis, and mTOR inhibition reduced fatty infiltration (Joshi et al., 2013). The study identifies a modifiable pathway in that experimental setting; it does not establish a master switch or a validated target in patients.

Tendon-only and nerve-only injuries can each generate fat, while their combination may produce more extensive or differently distributed change. Whether such patterns form reproducible human clusters remains unresolved. Prospective studies will need to account for age, metabolic status, pain, and tear morphology before assigning the difference to neural-mechanical interaction.

Potential early signals include loss of muscle volume, edema-like or altered T2 signal, heterogeneous fat fraction, reduced activation, and weakness out of proportion to the structural lesion (Zbinden et al., 2026). None is specific. Their value is more likely to emerge from a multimodal panel than from use as isolated diagnostic criteria.

Neural-directed strategies: compartment, timing, and combination

Lesion-directed treatment is biologically most plausible when a localizable and potentially reversible neuropathy has been demonstrated. Selected human studies do not support routine suprascapular nerve decompression during cuff repair in the absence of a documented lesion (Collin et al., 2014; Gerber et al., 2020). A mouse compression-and-release study suggests that timely relief can improve reinnervation and aspects of muscle remodeling, but that model did not include a tendon tear or repair (Wang et al., 2018b). Neural management should therefore be lesion-specific rather than prophylactic.

Activity-maintenance strategies—including graded activation and neuromuscular electrical stimulation—could be evaluated as ways to limit disuse or under-recruitment while protecting the repair. They should not be described as synaptic rescue, because postoperative human evidence has not established restoration of cuff NMJ biology or durable strength recovery. Timing and intensity must be tested against tendon-healing constraints.

At a molecular level, preservation of agrin–LRP4–MuSK signaling and acetylcholine-receptor clustering derives mainly from non-cuff NMJ biology (Tintignac et al., 2015; Li et al., 2018; R et al., 2014; Blaauw et al., 2013), whereas denervation-associated IL-6–STAT3 signaling links neural loss to stromal and fibrotic responses in general skeletal muscle (Madaro et al., 2018). These are hypothesis-generating platforms, not cuff-specific therapies. Future experiments should measure proximal neural outcomes together with inflammation, FAP state, ECM remodeling, tendon healing, and function. Neural monotherapy is most plausible for an early documented lesion; mixed phenotypes would generally require mechanical restoration, with neural or activity-directed treatment evaluated as an adjunct.

Stromal and extracellular-matrix remodeling: human observations and candidate cellular mechanisms

Human cuff tissue and imaging studies document myofiber loss, fat accumulation, atrophy, and fibrosis, but they do not identify one cellular pathway as the dominant cause of chronic degeneration (Gladstone et al., 2007; Gerber et al., 2007; Galatz et al., 2004; Bogd et al., 2021; Bowen et al., 2025; Santago et al., 2016; Gibbons et al., 2017; Valencia et al., 2018). Fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) constitute one plausible candidate stromal axis. Their regenerative, adipogenic, and fibrogenic potential is established principally in non-cuff skeletal muscle, whereas cuff-specific support derives largely from animal lineage and perturbation studies (Joe et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2011; Mozzetta et al., 2013; Heredia et al., 2013; Lemos et al., 2015; Madaro et al., 2018; Wosczyna et al., 2019; Biferali et al., 2019; Molina et al., 2021; Contreras et al., 2021; Negroni et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2016; Shirasawa et al., 2017; Agha et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2020a; Davies et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Flores-Opazo et al., 2024). Longitudinal human data linking FAP state to prognosis or reversibility are lacking.

Foundational skeletal muscle studies demonstrated that PDGFR-alpha-positive interstitial progenitors are distinct from satellite cells and can form ectopic fat, that fibrosis and adipogenesis can arise from related mesenchymal progenitors, and that FAP survival or clearance depends on immune and cytokine cues (Joe et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2011; Mozzetta et al., 2013; Heredia et al., 2013; Lemos et al., 2015; Madaro et al., 2018; Wosczyna et al., 2019). These data provide the conceptual basis for interpreting rotator cuff fatty infiltration as a regulated cell-fate process rather than passive lipid deposition.

Cuff-specific animal studies illustrate both biological plausibility and translational distance. In a severe combined mouse model involving suprascapular denervation, full cuff tendon transection, and humeral-head resection, oral imatinib at 30 mg/kg 5 days per week suppressed early adipogenic gene expression, PDGFR-alpha-positive cells, and histological fat by two to 4 weeks (Shirasawa et al., 2017). The model and early treatment timing test prevention of fat development, not reversal of established human disease, and imatinib is not FAP-specific. Comparisons of cuff- and limb-derived murine FAPs further suggest tissue-specific adipogenic behavior, but do not establish intrinsic susceptibility in human cuff muscle (Agha et al., 2021).

Cell number alone may be less informative than cell fate. Some FAP states support myogenesis; others form white adipocytes, deposit collagen-rich matrix, or enter beige/brown-like adipogenic programs with greater metabolic activity. Tear size, chronic unloading, and inflammation may shift this balance (Agha et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2020a; Davies et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Flores-Opazo et al., 2024). Such fate bias is likely to be more biologically meaningful than a simple positive-or-negative FAP marker.

In general skeletal-muscle injury, macrophage states influence debris clearance, satellite-cell activity, and FAP survival or differentiation (Arno et al., 2007; Tidball, 2017; Chazaud, 2014; Wynn and Vannella, 2016). Whether the same temporal sequence governs chronic human cuff degeneration is unknown, and the M1/M2 binary does not capture the full diversity or timing of macrophage states.

Cuff-specific evidence remains preclinical. In a mouse supraspinatus/infraspinatus tendon-transection-plus-denervation model, 2 × 10^9 M0-, M1-, or M2-macrophage-derived exosomes were injected locally at injury and the supraspinatus was assessed 6 weeks later. In this small experiment, M2-derived exosomes were associated with less fatty infiltration, fibrosis, and atrophy and with greater UCP1-related beige differentiation (Liu et al., 2023). The prophylactic, single-model result identifies a candidate signaling route; it does not establish extracellular-vesicle therapy, a universally beneficial macrophage state, or reversal of established disease.

The familiar M1/M2 binary also understates the dynamic and multidimensional nature of macrophage states. Age, metabolic health, mechanical load, and damage signals may all modify macrophage-FAP interactions (Arno et al., 2007; Tidball, 2017; Chazaud, 2014; Wynn and Vannella, 2016; Liu et al., 2023; Greenberg and Reeves, 2021). Future studies will need to combine state-resolved markers with functional assays rather than infer benefit from a single polarization label.

Metabolism may connect these immune signals to the eventual fat phenotype. Beige adipogenesis, UCP1 expression, beta-adrenergic signaling, and mitochondrial activity could indicate a more plastic adipose state than mature white fat. Conversely, low metabolic demand, insulin resistance, aging, and persistent inflammation may favor white adipogenesis and fibrosis. Measuring only a reduction in visible fat would miss this functional heterogeneity.

Fibrosis deserves attention alongside fatty infiltration. Collagen accumulation can alter passive stiffness, intramuscular force transmission, vascular diffusion, satellite-cell behavior, and surgical mobilization. A cuff muscle replaced by both fat and collagen is not simply weak; its mechanical and biological connection to the repair construct has been compromised.

Skeletal-muscle fibrosis reflects dysregulated repair, including TGF-beta-associated signaling, persistent fibroblast/FAP activation, and abnormal extracellular-matrix turnover (Gillies and Lieber, 2011; Lieber and Ward, 2013; Mann et al., 2011; Serrano and Muñoz-Cánoves, 2010; Bentzinger et al., 2012). In chronic large/massive posterosuperior tears, fibrosis may limit restoration of tendon length, increase repair tension, and reduce postoperative adaptability. It may also complicate imaging-based interpretation: two muscles with similar fat fractions may perform differently if one contains substantially more collagen.

The relevant tissue endpoint is therefore fibroadipogenic remodeling: the simultaneous loss of contractile tissue and its replacement by adipogenic and collagen-rich interstitium. Whether cellular state predicts reversibility better than quantitative imaging is an important question for future studies, not an established clinical claim.

Candidate biological stages and limits of reversibility

Biological stage and dominant mechanism are conceptually separate, but the proposed stages remain unvalidated research descriptors. Calendar time alone is insufficient because tear morphology, age, metabolic health, pain, neural involvement, and loading history may alter the rate and pattern of remodeling.

A candidate early-stress phenotype would be expected to show reduced tension or activation with limited tissue replacement, whereas a candidate intermediate-remodeling phenotype would show measurable regional fat or collagen accumulation with residual contractile reserve. FAP or neural changes may accompany either pattern but should not be inferred without direct measurement. No validated human threshold currently separates these states.

Several stromal, immune, and metabolic pathways are modifiable in preclinical systems (Liu et al., 2016; Agha et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Shirasawa et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2020b; Lee et al., 2020c; Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020; Iio et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Stamiris et al., 2024; Rodeo, 2007). Because most treatments were initiated at or near acute injury, those experiments correspond more closely to prevention or early remodeling than to reversal of established chronic degeneration.

At the proposed advanced end of the spectrum, high fat burden, collagen-rich fibrosis, severe atrophy, and low contractile reserve are expected to predominate. Human longitudinal studies generally show little improvement in semiquantitative fatty-infiltration grade after repair (Gladstone et al., 2007; Melis et al., 2010). Selected neural-release and beige-fat experiments modify individual histological endpoints in animals (Lee et al., 2020c; Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020; Iio et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018b), but none establishes reversal of advanced human fibrosis or fibrofatty collapse.

Candidate two-dimensional research phenotyping and validation

A candidate research phenotype should begin with established structural variables and preserve quantitative measures as continuous data. Only after prospective validation should investigators explore separate probabilistic labels for dominant mechanism (mechanical-unloading, neural modifier, or mixed) and biological stage (Table 1). Standard MRI reporting should still include tear size, tendon involvement, retraction, Goutallier grade, tangent sign, and muscle volume, with regional fat distribution and quantitative measures added where available.

A mechanical-unloading-dominant assignment would require concordance between tear/retraction and the regional muscle pattern; if the neural domain has not been assessed, the mechanism should remain indeterminate rather than be assigned by default. A neural-modifier assignment would require an objectively measured, anatomically concordant peripheral or recruitment abnormality. Severe disease, pain-limited recruitment, or exploratory NMJ morphology alone would not define a mixed mechanism.

Electrodiagnostic testing is most informative when used selectively. EMG/NCS can support peripheral neuropathy or a motor-unit abnormality, but it neither measures pain-related central inhibition nor proves NMJ pathology. A normal study does not exclude mechanically driven fatty infiltration, and an abnormal study does not show that decompression alone will restore muscle quality.

Candidate stage should be studied independently using continuous imaging and function, with tissue profiling as an exploratory correlate. PDGFR-alpha-associated stromal populations, adipogenic and fibrotic readouts, macrophage states, mitochondrial markers, and NMJ morphology do not currently define a stage or routine clinical category.

Candidate operational definitions for prospective validation

Future studies should retain electrophysiological and functional measures as continuous variables in primary analyses. A candidate ‘electrodiagnostically supported neural modifier’ could require an anatomically localizing needle-EMG abnormality in the supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus—active denervation, chronic neurogenic motor-unit morphology, or reduced recruitment—together with a quantitative nerve-conduction abnormality relative to laboratory reference limits or the contralateral side. In a normal-subject study, a side-to-side latency difference of 0.4 m was proposed as an acceptable upper limit (Casazza et al., 1998); it should be treated only as an exploratory sensitivity anchor, not a cuff-specific diagnostic cutoff. Deltoid, cervical paraspinal, and other control muscles are needed to distinguish suprascapular neuropathy from radiculopathy, plexopathy, or generalized neuropathy. Pain-limited recruitment should be coded separately, and conventional EMG/NCS should not be interpreted as proof of NMJ pathology.

Quantitative MRI fat fraction should likewise remain continuous in primary analyses. In a prospective six-point Dixon cohort, supraspinatus fat fraction was explored using <8%, 8% to <13.5%, and ≥13.5% bands, with progression defined as an increase of at least 4.5 percentage points (Hochreiter et al., 2024). These cohort-derived values are not TMN-stage thresholds and should not be transferred across sequences, scanners, muscles, or populations without calibration. An advanced phenotype should require convergent evidence—high fat fraction together with reduced normalized or fat-free muscle volume and impaired strength or contractile reserve—rather than fat fraction alone. All categories require test–retest and interobserver reliability, internal derivation, external validation, and incremental value beyond established structural variables.

Modifiers and boundary conditions

Age, diabetes, obesity, sarcopenia, smoking, sex, tear chronicity, pain severity, and rehabilitation exposure can influence muscle volume, fat fraction, neural activation, inflammation, and healing. These are not merely background variables; they may shift both mechanism and stage and should be prespecified as covariates. Metabolic risk and general muscle health are particularly relevant when fat is diffuse or recovery is poor.

We restrict this model to chronic large/massive posterosuperior tears because supraspinatus/infraspinatus retraction, suprascapular nerve anatomy, and the available evidence on muscle degeneration intersect most clearly in this setting. Acute tears, isolated subscapularis or teres minor disease, revision repair, and cuff-tear arthropathy will require separate validation.

Translational hypotheses and clinical boundaries

Mechanical restoration remains the most firmly supported clinical principle. Tendon continuity, appropriate tension, tissue mobility, and the choice of reconstruction or augmentation determine whether a load-bearing muscle-tendon-bone circuit can be re-established. That clinical foundation should be kept distinct from unvalidated claims that established biological remodeling can be reversed.

Neural evaluation is useful when it is selective and component-specific. Documented suprascapular or spinoglenoid compression, traction neuropathy, and postoperative nerve injury may alter management, whereas pain-related inhibition, motor-unit under-recruitment, and NMJ remodeling require different measurements. Available evidence does not justify routine nerve release for all massive tears (Boykin et al., 2010; Mallon et al., 2006; Costouros et al., 2007; Lafosse et al., 2007; Collin et al., 2014; Gerber et al., 2020; Beeler et al., 2013; Leclere et al., 2014; Mistretta et al., 2025; Eshima et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018b; Kong et al., 2016).

Rehabilitation is both a load-and-activation intervention and a postoperative protocol. Excessive early loading may jeopardize tendon healing, whereas prolonged underloading may deepen atrophy and loss of activation. Studies of protected motion, scapular motor control, graded isometrics, and progressive resistance should report repair integrity together with strength, endurance, and muscle quality.

Because apparent efficacy depends strongly on model architecture and treatment timing, candidate biological interventions should be read as experimental probes rather than a therapeutic menu. Imatinib and retinoic-acid-receptor agonists were tested from the early postoperative period in severe combined-injury models (Shirasawa et al., 2017; Shirasawa et al., 2021), and macrophage-derived exosomes were delivered at injury in a tendon-transection-plus-denervation model (Liu et al., 2023). These experiments primarily address prevention of early stromal commitment. By contrast, beige-FAP delivery at delayed repair, delayed beta3-adrenergic agonism, and liraglutide initiated 2 weeks after an unrepaired tear provide limited analogues of intermediate remodeling (Lee et al., 2020b; Wang et al., 2021; Yoon et al., 2026). Reported changes in histological fat, fibrosis, or muscle-related measures do not establish reversal of advanced disease, tendon-healing safety, or human efficacy. Advanced fibrofatty replacement requires realistic goals. When tendon mobility and contractile reserve are poor, salvage reconstruction, tendon transfer, superior capsular reconstruction, reverse shoulder arthroplasty, or compensation-focused rehabilitation may be considered according to established clinical indications. The TMN framework is not validated to select among these options.

Framework validation and future research

Validation should proceed in sequence: measurement reliability, prospective phenotype derivation, incremental prognosis beyond established structural variables, external validation, and only then treatment-effect modification. Mechanism and candidate stage should remain separate variables rather than being collapsed into another severity score.

Prospective cohorts should integrate qMRI fat fraction and regional distribution, muscle volume, edema-like or altered T2 signal, component-specific neural measures, objective strength and endurance, pain and motor-control measures, tendon integrity, metabolic risk, and rehabilitation exposure. Age, sex, diabetes, obesity/sarcopenia, smoking, and chronicity need to be prespecified covariates rather than post hoc explanations.

A minimum dataset makes the proposal falsifiable. Mechanism and stage assignments must be reproducible and must improve calibration, discrimination, or the detection of treatment-effect heterogeneity beyond tear size, retraction, Goutallier grade, and tendon healing alone.

Preclinical designs should distinguish initiating injuries from downstream pathways by including sham, isolated tendon injury, isolated nerve injury, combined injury, delayed repair, and nerve-release groups. Sampling across biological stages should incorporate sex, age, and metabolic context. Supplementary Table S3, provided in the separate Supplementary Material, summarizes the proposed experimental matrix.

Intervention studies could test whether adding a mechanism- and stage-matched adjunct to mechanical restoration improves outcomes. Early experiments should distinguish prevention after disruption of loading or activation from reversal after established remodeling; delayed and repaired models are needed before clinical translation. Relevant outcomes include tendon integrity, objective force, endurance, qMRI fat fraction and muscle volume, fibrosis, and patient-reported function (Zbinden et al., 2026; Lebaschi et al., 2016).

Clinical use would require prospective external validation and evidence that the framework improves decisions or outcomes; the present model is a research agenda only.

Limitations and interpretive boundaries

The searches were structured and documented, but relevance-based selection was not independently duplicated and no formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed. Omission and selection bias therefore remain possible, and the English-language restriction may have excluded relevant evidence. The evidence-provenance categories are interpretive aids rather than formal certainty grades.

Human evidence is strongest for the prognostic relevance of imaging-defined fatty infiltration, atrophy, and muscle volume. Human cuff evidence for fibrosis is less mature and often cross-sectional. Cuff-specific evidence remains limited for NMJ remodeling, pain-related activation deficits, longitudinal FAP states, macrophage–extracellular-vesicle signaling, and biological reversibility. Animal perturbation and non-cuff skeletal-muscle studies support plausibility but cannot establish mechanism or treatment efficacy in patients.

Neither the proposed electrophysiological designation nor the qMRI anchors has been validated for TMN diagnosis, staging, prognosis, reproducibility, or treatment selection. Prospective studies should report variables continuously, prespecify exploratory categories, assess test–retest and interobserver reliability, and perform internal and external validation. Most biological interventions were tested in young rodents using acute or severe combined injury; changes in adipogenic markers or histological fat should not be equated with durable force recovery, tendon healing, or reversal of chronic human disease.

The treatment discussion is therefore a map of experimental questions, not clinical recommendations. Translation requires human safety, dose, delivery, target engagement, durability, and efficacy data, as well as proof that any adjunct does not compromise tendon-to-bone healing.

Conclusion

Chronic large and massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears cannot be explained by tendon discontinuity alone, but their biology should not be compressed into a single disconnection phenotype. The proposed TMN framework separates dominant mechanism from candidate biological stage, distinguishes objectively measurable neural components, and treats possible NMJ remodeling as a hypothesis-level domain. Its value depends on prospective demonstration of reproducibility, incremental prediction, external validity, and treatment-effect modification beyond tear morphology, muscle-quality imaging, and tendon healing. Mechanical restoration remains the clinical foundation; neural evaluation is selective, and stromal or molecular interventions remain preclinical research hypotheses.
