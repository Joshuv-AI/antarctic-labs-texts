Live Airway Prevotella Attenuated Diesel Exhaust-Induced Lung Injury and Neutrophil Activation via Bacterial Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Bojia Wang, Lihua Yang, Jiahua Cai, Qixiao Jiang, Hongna Zhang, Jinmei Piao, Wei Han, Yuxin Zheng, Menghui Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diesel exhaust (DE), as a principal contributor to traffic-related air pollution, poses significant public and occupational health risks. The lung microbiome could be shaped by inhaled pollutants, which play a crucial role in sustaining pulmonary health. Our prior study found a lower abundance of airway Prevotella nanceiensis (P.nan.) in DE-exposed individuals. Here, in this study, live P.nan. and its bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) alleviated lung damage under inhaled DE exposure, accompanied by reduced neutrophil infiltration and activation. Notably, DE triggered lipid droplet accumulation in the lung, mitigated by the live P.nan./BEVs intervention. Additionally, inhibitors targeting lipid oxidation, synthesis, and transport attenuated neutrophil activation in response to diesel exhaust particle (DEP) stimulation. Moreover, the intervention of BEVs resulted in decreased levels of inflammatory mediators, neutrophil recruitment-associated chemokines, and lipid-related genes. This study highlights live P.nan./BEVs’ protective role against DE-induced lung injury, implicating neutrophil recruitment and lipid metabolism as key mechanisms and offering potential therapeutic avenues.

Introduction

The detrimental effects of air pollutants have emerged as a critical public health concern, with diesel exhaust (DE) being one of the principal sources of air pollution, and its adverse impact on human health is multifaceted. DE can penetrate the lung and lead to respiratory damage, including inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs, thickening of small airway walls, and reduced lung function. The inhalation of DE attributes to various lung diseases such as pneumonia, asthma, and even lung cancer. Substantial evidence has illustrated that DE exposure can trigger oxidative stress and an inflammatory response in the lungs, leading to lung injury. As the predominant responders in pulmonary mucosal immunity, neutrophils can not only engulf exogenous particulate matter upon exposure to external environmental stimuli but also secrete plenty of inflammatory factors. Diesel exhaust particle (DEP) exposure can provoke different degrees of mild neutrophilic airway inflammation, eosinophilic infiltration, and chemokine expression in both lean and obese mice, triggering inflammation and airway remodeling. Atmospheric particulate matter, including ultrafine environmental particles (UFP), can cause neutrophil infiltration in the respiratory tract of animals, accompanied by selectively increasing neutrophil inflammation in the lungs of asthmatic mice. , Long-term excessive fluoride exposure can trigger neutrophil iron erosion, release NETs, induce neutrophil extracellular traps, and exacerbate brain inflammation. Excessive neutrophils can exacerbate the progression of inflammation in the lungs and contribute to lung injury and fibrosis.

Accumulated studies have established that the lung constitutes a complex microbial ecosystem colonized by a variety of bacteria, fungi, and viruses, collectively known as the pulmonary microbiome. Among them, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes represent the predominant phyla in the healthy lung microbiome. The most prevalent genera are Prevotella spp., Wechnella spp., and Streptococcus spp. These commensal bacteria play a more pivotal role in maintaining lung health than fungi and viruses. − The lung bacteriome constantly maintains a dynamic equilibrium, and any imbalance is closely related to lung health. For example, Pseudomonas, cocci, and mycoplasma are predominant in the upper respiratory tract of patients with COVID-19 during the recovery phase. In patients with tuberculosis, an increased abundance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Aspergillus is observed in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). External environmental stimuli can alter the lung microenvironment, influencing the bacterial colonization and potentially leading to pulmonary disease. For instance, short-term exposure to PM2.5 can lead to changes in the diversity of the bacterial community in the buccal mucosa and notable differences in the phyla of Spirochaetes and Proteobacteria.

As one of the most predominant commensal bacterial genera residing in the lungs, Prevotella plays an essential role in maintaining lung health. Prevotella has been demonstrated to enhance the clearance rate of pathogens and attenuate the inflammatory response in mice subjected to Streptococcus pneumoniae invasion, thereby safeguarding the respiratory system. Consistent with our previous findings, the relative abundance of P. nan. was markedly lower in workers occupationally exposed to DE, and spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between the abundance of P. nan. and the duration (in years) of DE exposure. Consequently, exogenous P. nan. was supplemented with the exposed animals to evaluate its effect on the host. Prevotella, Gram-negative bacteria, can secrete bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) to communicate with host cells. These BEVs comprise bacterial metabolites, membrane proteins, nucleic acids, peptidoglycans, and other substances to communicate. Recently, BEVs have been demonstrated to exert a protective effect on the respiratory tract by mitigating the inflammatory response in pulmonary epithelial cells.

The lung is an organ rich in lipids, which are believed to be instrumental in preserving alveolar stability, modulating immune responses, maintaining barrier function, facilitating signal transduction, and providing energy. Lipid metabolism is involved in regulating the metabolic state of pulmonary cells, enhancing lung function, and maintaining lung health. Certain lipids, such as sphingolipids and phospholipids, exhibit anti-inflammatory properties that help alleviate an inflammatory response. Cellular activity and function are profoundly influenced by the pulmonary microenvironment, particularly by the regulation of lipid metabolic homeostasis. For instance, neutral free fatty acids (NEFA) in the lung microenvironment drive the acquisition of activated neutrophil phenotypes by modulating the expression of activation markers such as CD11b and CD62L, as well as by reshaping membrane phospholipids. Neutrophils rely on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) as a critical energy source to sustain their survival and proinflammatory functions within lung tissue, while lipid metabolic reprogramming directly governs their capacity of migration, phagocytosis, and inflammatory mediator release. Alterations in lung lipid metabolism (e.g., excessive synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol, or phospholipids) can modulate the activation, recruitment, phagocytosis, and inflammatory secretion of neutrophils. ,

Consequently, this study investigates the protective role of live P. nan and its BEVs on lung health under DE exposure, which is realized by inhibiting neutrophil recruitment and activation through lipid remodeling. This study concurrently addresses the existing research gaps concerning P. nan. and its BEVs, as well as their role in DE–induced lung injury.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Whole-body DE Exposure

C57BL/6J wild-type male mice aged between 6 and 8 weeks were maintained in a specific pathogen-free environment in the Laboratory Animal Centre of Qingdao Municipal Hospital. This experiment employs a diesel engine exhaust inhalation-type whole-body exposure system, which more closely approximates real occupational exposure. The mice had unrestricted access to standard laboratory food and water in the exposure system, adhering to a 12 h light/dark cycle. During exposure, the exposure system operated without generating any additional noise. The mice were subjected to daily exposures lasting 1 h, with the exposure concentrations being monitored in real-time. Ten independent mice for each group underwent pathological staining, qPCR, and other experimental procedures. Our previous human study showed that the geometric mean of PM2.5 levels in the occupational environment was 430.8 μg/m3. DEP Extraction

DEP was extracted according to the method established previously. In brief, the diesel exhaust was produced by using a model 192 single-cylinder diesel generator, and the particles were obtained by Teflon filter membranes. The membranes were sectioned using non-metal scissors (ceramic), placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, with an adequate volume of water and Tween 80 (3–5 μL), and mixed thoroughly. The mixture underwent ultrasonication for 30 min three times. According to our previous research, the particle size of DEP ranges from approximately 30 to 50 nm, and various metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) were identified in the mixture. The mixture was then stored in a −80 °C refrigerator overnight. The membrane pieces were removed, the supernatant was lyophilized, and the pellets were weighed and subsequently diluted with water to the specified concentration for further application.

Live P.nan. Culture and BEV Isolation

Live P.nan. were purchased from Biobw company (bio-135565) and cultured on EG+5% sterile horse blood agar plate under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h. The bacterial culture was then transferred to broth medium and continued to be cultured anaerobically at 37 °C for 2–3 days. The bacterial culture was subjected to centrifugation at 4000g at 4 °C for 45 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000g at 4 °C for 3 h. The resulting pellets were resuspended in sterile PBS. Purified BEVs were plated on agar to ensure the lack of bacterial contamination, and Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay was performed to quantify endotoxin according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein concentration of BEVs was assessed using a BCA protein assay kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture of BEVs was then adjusted to the desired concentration for subsequent experiments based on the protein concentration assessment. The remaining bacterial culture was preserved at −80 °C in glycerol.

Intranasal Instillation

Live P. nan and its BEVs were suspended in PBS and administered intranasally to the mice. In detail, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injections of sodium pentobarbital. The suspension was gradually instilled into the mice’s nostrils utilizing a pipette, ensuring the mice were maintained in an upright position until complete inhalation by the nasal cavity was achieved to prevent any liquid leakage. Each mouse in the intervention group received three intranasal inoculations of 7 × 105 CFU P. nan or 100 μg BEVs on Days 0, 7, and 14. −

Collection of Blood, Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF), and Lung Tissue

Within 24 h after the final exposure, animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). The mice were euthanized by eyeball puncture. The blood was stored at 4 °C for 2 h. Separate plasma by centrifugation at 1000 r/min for 15 min and store at −80 °C. Perform thoracotomy, intubate, and perform triple lung lavage to collect BALF. Centrifuge BALF at 300g for 10 min to separate the cell pellet and supernatant. Harvest lung tissue was harvested for histopathological examination, gene expression analysis, and other experiments.

Histology and Immunohistochemistry

The lungs were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin or OCT, and sectioned into slices of 5 μm thickness for subsequent pathological evaluation. The paraffin-embedded sections underwent sequential immersion in ethanol of varying concentrations for paraffin removal, followed by treatment with hematoxylin staining solution, 1% hydrochloric acid to remove excess hematoxylin, 0.6% ammonia–water for nuclear bluing, eosin staining solution, and ethanol and xylene in different concentrations for dehydration and clearance. Ultimately, the sections were mounted using neutral balsam medium for histological examination with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining. For Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining (PAS staining oxidizes polysaccharides in tissues using periodic acid, generating aldehyde groups that subsequently react with Schaffner’s reagent to form a pink or purplish-red stain, thereby visualizing glycogen, mucinous substances, and other components). The sections were then incubated with LY6G (a marker for neutrophils) primary antibody and corresponding enzyme-linked secondary antibody to facilitate the identification of neutrophils. (Used to mark areas of positive immunostaining for neutrophils in the airways under optical microscopy and analyze the obtained images using ImageJ software. Extract the positive granular areas, set the positive area threshold, measure the optical density values to obtain the percentage of positive area, and derive the airway neutrophil staining score. The OCT-embedded sections were incubated in BODIPY493/503 (labeling neutral lipid content) working solution for 30 min, stained with DAPI for 10 min, and subsequently sealed using an antifading mounting medium.

RNA Isolation and QPCR

Total RNA was extracted employing TRIzol reagent and subsequently reverse-transcribed cDNA using the mRNA selective PCR Kit (TOLOBIO). The cDNA templates were then subjected to amplification via real-time PCR using an SYBR qPCR Master Mix (TOLOBIO). The gene expression was quantified using the 2­(−ΔΔCt) method. The sequences of the primers utilized were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and are documented in Table S1.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α, etc., were measured using ELISA kits (FANKEW) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm utilizing a microplate reader, and the levels of IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α, etc., were calculated based on the standard curve.

Cell Culture

The femur and tibia of mice were collected, flushed with sterile PBS, and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The bone marrow cell suspension was centrifuged in a gradient Percoll solution (1.119 and 1.077 g/mL separation solution) at 800 g for 30 min at 4 °C, with both brake and accelerator set to 0. The purified primary neutrophils were isolated from the intermediate layer between 1.119 and 1.077 g/mL Percoll solution. The MLE-12 cell line (obtained from HyCyte TM) and primary mouse marrow-derived neutrophils were cultured in DF-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) mixture. The MLE-12 cell line were seeded into 96-well plates, and following cellular attachment, DEP was added and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for the indicated time, and the absorbance of the cell supernatant was detected at 450 nm. Mouse bone marrow–derived neutrophils were seeded into the upper compartment of Transwell inserts, while MLE-12 cells were plated in the corresponding lower chamber at a density of 2.6 × 105 cells per well, maintaining a neutrophil-to-MLE-12 cell ratio of 1:1. In the coculture experiment, neutrophils and MLE-12 cells were cocultured at a for 24 h, and DEP (100 μg/mL) and BEVs (10 μg/mL) were introduced into the culture of MLE-12 cell line in the lower chamber.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The size of the BEVs was detected using a nanoparticle tracking analyzer (ZetaView). After the instrument is automatically calibrated, the instrument is purged to ensure particle counts are reduced to single digits. The sample is diluted to an appropriate concentration with a clean PBS solution, and it is observed if the number of particles displayed at the detection site falls within the range 50–400. The instrument performs detection and generates a test report, providing information about the number, size distribution, and concentration of particles.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Once the BEVs are adjusted to an appropriate concentration based on sample conditions, they are placed on a copper grid to stand for 1 min. A filter is employed to dry the sample on the copper grid, followed by the absorption of approximately 15 μL of 2% acetic acid peroxide uranium staining solution using a pipette to stain at room temperature for 1 min. Filter paper is used to dry the sample on the copper grid, and the stained sample is allowed to dry naturally. A TEM is employed to identify a suitable field of view for photography.

16S rRNA

Initially, the DNA of mouse lungs was extracted using a (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA). The V3–V4 (400–500 bp)­16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers (F: ACTCCTACGGGAGGGCAGCA, R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT), and the PCR product was purified through a column purification kit. The purified PCR product was constructed into a library that was sequenced via high-throughput sequencing. USEARCH (v10.0.240) was utilized to generate amplicon sequence variants, while VSEARCH (v2.22.1) was applied to calculate α and β diversity indexes following quality control and sequence redundancy reduction; the species were annotated based on the RDP database. Plotting and statistical analysis were performed by using R software (4.4.2). The species stacking map was drawn on the Weikemeng Shengxin Cloud Network.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise noted, statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 Software. Group comparisons were performed based on a one-sample t test, ordinary one-way ANOVA tests, or Dunnett’s comparison test with a single pooled variance. A p-value of *p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Result

Live P.nan. Exert a Protective Effect on the Lungs under DE Exposure

Based on our prior occupational population study, the relative abundance of sputum live P. nan. is lower in workers exposed to DE. Consequently, 7 × 105 CFU of live P.nan. (Figure S1) solution was administered intranasally to mice during DE exposure for 30 days (Figure A). The H&E staining of lung tissues indicated that DE exposure contributed to thicken alveolar walls, destruction of alveolar structures, and alveolar collapse, which was attenuated following the intervention of live P.nan. (Figure B). Quantitative assessments of inflammatory lesions were conducted on H&E-stained sections. The results showed that intervention with live P. nan significantly alleviated lung inflammation induced by DE exposure (Figure C). The PAS staining revealed a reduction in mucus secretion and fibrosis following the administration of live P.nan. (Figure D), with this reduction reaching statistical significance (Figure E). These results indicate that live P.nan. may provide a protective effect against lung injury resulting from DE exposure.

Microbiota Sequencing Analysis

16S rRNA sequencing of the lung tissue was conducted to analyze the variations of microbiota following live P.nan. intervention. α diversity analysis presented the richness and evenness of the bacterial community, including Chao1, Observed Species, and Shannon and Simpson indices. These indices showed no significant differences under DE exposure and live P.nan. intervention (Figure A). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) converted the Bray–Curtis distance matrix to graphically visualize the variation of β diversity in the bacterial community. In our study, PCoA exhibited analogous bacterial clustering patterns in the DE-exposed and live P.nan. groups (Figure B). As a significant respiratory pathogen, Bordetella contributed to respiratory inflammatory responses and disrupted the respiratory tract’s defense. The stacking maps illustrated the relative abundance of Bordetella increased under DE exposure but decreased in live P.nan. intervention groups (Figure C). Subsequently, the relative content of Prevotella in the lungs was quantified using QPCR. Consistently with our previous human study, the relative abundance of Prevotella diminished in DE-exposed groups (Figure D). Furthermore, the relative abundance of Prevotella was restored in live P.nan. intervention groups (Figure D). The results indicated that DE exposure contributed to reduced Prevotella, and supplemented live P.nan. could colonize the lungs.

P. nan.-derived BEVs Alleviated Lung Injury under DE Exposure

BEVs function as mediators of communication between commensal bacteria and their hosts. Therefore, the BEVs were successfully extracted from P.nan. culture by ultracentrifugation. By negative staining, transmission electron microscopy revealed their spherical particles with bilayer lipid structure (Figure A). Transmission electron microscopy analysis revealed that the particle size distribution of major BEVs (96%) ranged from 124.7 to 289.6 nm (Figure B). The number of BEVs in the P.nan. culture was 7.3 × 104 particles per milliliter (data not shown). The BEVs solution was verified having no bacterial contamination by agar plating and no endotoxin contamination (Figure S2). To further explore the protective role of BEVs, live P.nan. and its BEVs were administered intranasally during DE exposure. The results showed that both live P. nan. and BEVs intervention significantly attenuated lung injury (Figure C,D) and mucus secretion (Figure E,F) induced by DE exposure.

Live P.nan. and Its BEVs Attenuated Neutrophil Recruitment in the Lung

As the first-line effector cells in the immune response, neutrophils are rapidly recruited and release various inflammatory factors in response to an external environmental stimulus. Excessive activation of neutrophils contributes to lung damage, including alveolar injury and fibrosis. Therefore, the infiltration of neutrophils in the lung tissue was identified by the LY6G marker through IHC staining. The results indicated that neutrophils were recruited into the lungs under DE exposure, and the recruitment was inhibited following intervention by live P.nan. and BEVs (Figure A). The areas of infiltrating neutrophils in lung tissue were quantified by ImageJ software, and the reduction of neutrophil infiltration in live P.nan. and BEVs intervention groups was significant (Figure B). The mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines were assessed in lung tissue and cell pellets in BALF, respectively. The mRNA levels of TNF-α in lung tissue and the cell pellets from BALF were dramatically reduced after live P.nan./BEVs intervention, whereas the mRNA levels of IL-12 and IL-6 displayed a decreased trend (Figure C). Compared to the DE exposure groups, the expression levels of chemokines responsible for recruiting neutrophils (CXCL1, CXCL2, and CCL6) were reduced following the intervention of live P.nan. and BEVs (Figure D). The protein levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines were determined in BALF by ELISA. The protein levels of TNF-α, IL-12, IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CCL6 in BALF were significantly reduced in live P.nan./BEVs intervention groups (Figure E). These results indicated that the intervention of live P.nan. and BEVs could attenuate the inflammatory response and neutrophil recruitment in lung tissue under DE exposure.

BEVs Attenuated Neutrophil Activation by Regulating Lipid Metabolism Pathways

The CCK-8 assays indicated a dose-dependent decline in MLE-12 cell viability upon DEP stimulation, with 80% viability deemed the maximum nontoxic dose (100 μg/mL) (Figure A). Therefore, this dose (100 μg/mL) was used for subsequent experiments. The bone marrow neutrophils were isolated and exposed to DEP and BEVs for 24 h. Post-DEP exposure, the mRNA levels of IL-12 and IL-6 increased significantly, whereas BEV treatment dramatically reduced this elevation (Figure B). Subsequently, bone marrow-derived neutrophils and MLE-12 cells were cocultured at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h, with DEP (100 μg/mL) and BEVs (10 μg/mL) and BEVs added directly to the MLE-12 cells. The mRNA levels of IL-12 and IL-6 in neutrophils, as well as CXCL1 and CCL6 in MLE-12 cells, reduced notably in the BEV-supplemented group under DEP exposure (Figure C). The protein levels of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE) in the supernatant of neutrophils were examined by ELISA. We found that DEP stimulated the production of MPO and NE in neutrophils, while BEV intervention significantly reduced their secretion (Figure D). Accumulated studies emphasize the correlation between lipid metabolism and neutrophil activation. Here, to speculate about the role of lipid metabolism in our study, the inhibitors of lipid oxidation (GW6471), synthesis (TVB-3166), and transportation (SSO) pathways were supplemented. The inhibitors of lipid oxidation and transportation notably reduced the mRNA level of TNF-α in neutrophils (Figure E–G). The results suggest that BEVs diminish neutrophil activation, whereas DEP promotes activation by modulating lipid oxidation and transportation pathways.

The lipid droplets were examined with BODIPY493/503 (a stain for neutral lipid and oil) in the lungs after exposure for 30 days. A significant increase in lipid droplets was observed in the DE exposure groups, which was attenuated by live P.nan. and BEVs intervention (Figure A). To further elucidate the lipid metabolism pathways, the levels of genes related to lipid synthesis, transportation, lipolysis, and oxidation in neutrophils were examined by QPCR within the coculture system (Figure B). The mRNA levels of genes related to lipid synthesis (ACC1, FASN, SCD1), lipid transportation (CD36, FATP1, FATP2), and lipid oxidation (ACOX1, PPARα, LCAD) were significantly elevated following DEP exposure, whereas these genes were markedly reduced after BEVs intervention (Figure C–E). Discussion

DE exposure contributes to airway remodeling and may lead to lung damage. Our previous study identified that the relative abundance of live P.nan. in sputum was markedly lower in the DE exposure group compared to the control group. Consequently, the current study explored the role of live P. nan in the lungs under DE exposure. We elucidated that live P.nan. and its BEVs exert a protective function by suppressing excessive neutrophil recruitment and activation in the lungs, thereby mitigating hyperinflammatory responses. This research broadens the comprehension of the role of the airway commensal bacteriome and its BEVs in the lung, offering promising targets for intervention in lung injury.

This study employs PM2.5 as the primary exposure indicator, given that it represents the principal constituent and displays a well-characterized dose–response relationship with lung function. This facilitates precise control of the exposure levels and ensures experimental reproducibility. The exposure concentrations for the animal experiments were determined based on occupational PM2.5 concentrations in diesel-related workplaces, thereby improving the external validity and extrapolative value of toxicological findings. In addition, the concentrations of NO and O2 in the exposure chamber were continuously monitored. Nevertheless, DE is a complex mixture containing not only PM2.5 but also other pollutants, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and nitrogen oxides. Achieving continuous prolonged daily exposure was challenging because of equipment constraints and stress responses in animals including noise, reductions in food and water consumption, and increased thermoregulatory demands. To address these concerns, we implemented a 1 h/day exposure protocol specifically designed to optimize dose equivalence. Although the calculated pulmonary deposition dose was comparable to that observed in occupationally exposed populations, the use of short-term, high-concentration exposure paradigms may underestimate tissue adaptation processes and/or long-term cumulative effects under chronic exposure conditions. According to established dosimetric models, a 1 h inhalation exposure to 1.5 mg/m3 diesel exhaust is predicted to yield an estimated pulmonary deposition of approximately 0.3–0.5 μg DEP. Given the reported volume of epithelial lining fluid (VELF, 1.6 ± 0.5 μL) in healthy mice, the resulting local DEP concentration at the epithelial surface is estimated to reach approximately 143–454 μg/mL. Furthermore, DE exposure has been shown to promote mucus secretion and may consequently increase the VELF volume, which would dilute the deposited particles and thereby reduce the effective local concentration at the epithelial surface. Under such conditions, the actual epithelial surface concentration is likely lower and may be reasonably approximated as being on the order of 100 μg/mL.

The lung bacteriome contributes to the maintenance of lung health by regulating the host immune responses. , For example, certain commensal bacteria can facilitate the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and inhibit the hyperinflammatory response. , Moreover, the lung bacteriome enhances the function of the lung barrier by competitively excluding pathogenic bacteria. As one of the significant constituents of the indigenous airway bacteriome, Prevotella has been shown to play a pivotal role in sustaining lung health and pulmonary immune regulation. For instance, the Prevotella melanogenic supplement inhibited the colonization of pathogen Streptococcus pneumoniae and reduced inflammation in mice, indicating a potentially beneficial role of Prevotella in pulmonary health. In our previous occupational human study, the relative abundance of the airway P.nan. was reduced in DE-exposed workers. Consistently, in the present study, we found that the relative abundance of P.nan. was reduced in DE-exposed mice. Therefore, we supplemented live P.nan. to mice intranasally and found that live P.nan. intervention alleviated DE-exposure-induced lung injury. However, under DE exposure supplemented, P. nan. increased its relative abundance compared to that in the sham control group. We speculate that this phenomenon is associated with DE exposure, reshaping the pulmonary niche induced. DE exposure markedly increased the relative abundance of the putative pathogen Bordetella while concomitantly suppressing or eliminating other competing microbial taxa, thereby generating vacant ecological niches. This dynamic reduced resistance to colonization by the exogenous supplemented P. nan., granting it an amplifying advantage. Moreover, DE exposure can alter the composition and rheological properties of airway mucus, potentially enhancing the adhesion of Prevotella to epithelial cells. Collectively, these conditions contributed to P. nan.’s colonization advantage.

A growing amount of research has shown that both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria could secrete nanosized BEVs, which contain numerous components derived from the parental bacteria, including proteins, lipoproteins, DNA, RNA, and lipids. BEVs mediate bacterial communication with host cells and other bacteria by delivering bacterial biological molecules to targets, thereby exerting potential immunomodulatory capabilities. BEVs could suppress and activate the immune response, which depends on the parental bacteria. , Some pathogenic bacteria release BEVs carrying LPS, peptidoglycan, and other molecules that can be recognized by host immune cells, thereby activating the host immune response. BEVs derived from certain probiotic bacteria can stimulate host immune cells to secrete anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby suppressing the host’s inflammatory response. For example, BEVs derived from Bacteroides species can induce dendritic cells to secrete IL-10, thereby suppressing inflammatory responses. Probiotic Bifidobacteria-derived BEVs promote antitumor immune responses primarily through the activation of T lymphocytes, whereas BEVs produced by pathogenic Acinetobacter baumannii aggravate inflammatory processes by transporting toxins and other proinflammatory mediators. Interestingly, the protection against lung injury of live P.nan.-derived BEVs was more potent than that of live P.nan. alone. In fact, because of negligible endotoxin contamination and lower malignancy, BEVs are safer and more biocompatible than their parental bacteria in biomedical applications. Therefore, compared to live P.nan., its BEVs are more favorable in clinical applications. The particle size of BEVs ranges from 124.7 to 289.6 nm, significantly smaller than live bacteria (0.5–2 μm). This enables BEVs to penetrate deeply into the alveoli and traverse the pulmonary surfactant layer, thereby promoting their more homogeneous distribution throughout the lung and enhancing their uptake by host cells for efficient delivery of cargo to intracellular targets. Furthermore, specific bioactive components within BEVs may undergo an actively regulated and selective sorting process, leading to their preferential enrichment in BEVs than in the corresponding parental bacteria. Consequently, the protective efficacy of BEVs is superior to that conferred by live P. nan.

Neutrophils, as core members of the innate immune system, are capable of rapidly identifying and phagocytosing foreign substances, leading to excessive inflammation. In the study, we found that DE exposure contributed to the recruitment of neutrophils and the expression of related chemokines in mice. In fact, accumulating studies have illustrated the interaction between bacteria and immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells. For example, administering live Lactobacillus during bronchopulmonary dysplasia reduces neutrophil infiltration, alleviates pulmonary inflammation, and consequently improves lung structure and function. Probiotic interventions such as Lactobacillus plantarum and Lactobacillus acidophilus have also been demonstrated to reduce neutrophilic activation and recruitment in pneumonia models, decrease inflammatory markers, and thereby improve lung structure and function. , BEVs can directly modulate the activity of neutrophils. For example, Bacteroides-derived BEVs can alleviate colitis by suppressing neutrophil activation, while other BEVs induce neutrophil activation and recruitment. For instance, Escherichia coli-secreted BEVs triggered intense pulmonary inflammatory responses and infiltrated neutrophils into the lungs. In our study, both live P. nan. and its BEVs can mitigate neutrophil overactivation and recruitment, thereby promoting inflammation resolution and tissue repair. According to the immunohistochemical staining results, DE exposure significantly increased neutrophil infiltration in lung tissue, thereby suggesting enhanced activation and proliferation of neutrophils rather than the induction of apoptosis. Under DE exposure, neutrophils are able to phagocyte foreign particulate matter, the abnormal activation or dysfunction of the phagocytic process may aggravate the local inflammatory response. Therefore, further investigations focusing on the phagocytic function of neutrophils under DE exposure are needed.

A healthy lung is an organ rich in lipids, which are crucial in sustaining lung health. The pulmonary surfactant lipids, such as phosphatidylglycerol and phosphatidylinositol, are involved in regulating immune responses, preventing alveolar collapse, and providing the energy for breathing. In the study, we found that DE exposure contributed to the accumulation of lipid droplets in the lung, indicating the disorder of lipid metabolism in the lung microenvironment, which could affect the activation of immune cells in the lung. Numerous research has shown that the activation of neutrophils is modulated by lipid metabolism, including chemotaxis, activation, inflammatory mediator production, apoptosis, immunomodulation, and antibacterial effects. − For example, the migration of neutrophils requires lipid uptake and oxidation (e.g., ACC1, FASN) to supply energy for the cell membrane reconfiguration. , β-oxidation can furnish a significant amount of ATP to facilitate neutrophil activation and migration at inflammatory sites. BEVs selectively suppressed DEP-induced upregulation of genes involved in lipid synthesis, transport, and oxidation while exerting no detectable effect on the expression of lipolysis-associated genes (HSL/MGL) (HSL/MGL), indicating pathway-specific metabolic regulation. The observed reduction in lipid droplet accumulation upon P. nan./BEVs intervention is thus more likely attributable to diminished lipid uptake and/or de novo synthesis, rather than to enhanced lipid degradation, reflecting finely tuned metabolic reprogramming. Future lipid flux analysis (e.g., 13C-labeled fatty acid tracing) could validate whether BEVs limit ATP supply through the inhibition of FAO, thereby contributing to the suppression of neutrophil activation. However, excessive lipid accumulation can lead to heightened activation of neutrophils, potentially aggravating the inflammatory response by promoting the release of various inflammatory mediators. In our study, we found lipid imbalance in neutrophil under DE exposure, and the inflammatory response was abrogated when specific inhibitors of lipid metabolism were supplemented. Importantly, in this study, BEV supplementation restored the lipid metabolism of neutrophils under DE exposure. In fact, recent studies reveal that BEVs can exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating lipid metabolism and even modulate the microenvironment to achieve immunotherapy. For example, proteases in BEVs derived from P. gingivalis altered lipid oxidation in the host, leading to hepatic fat deposition and thereby influencing the progression of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease. The high crude protein content in BEVs derived from Lactobacillus paracasei can regulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes in lipid metabolism, thereby influencing lipid catabolism and improving metabolic syndrome.

This study has several limitations. First, because workers occupationally exposed to diesel exhaust are predominantly male, only male mice were included in the experimental design. Consequently, the exclusive use of male animals constrains the external validity of the findings, and caution is required when the results are extrapolated to broader occupational populations that include females. Second, there was a failure to identify key effector molecules within the BEVs. While this study verified the global protective effects exerted by BEVs, it did not incorporate omics-based approaches to further characterize, identify, and functionally validate specific core molecular effectors. This methodological constraint limits our ability to translate these findings into targeted and mechanistically grounded intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that live P.nan. and its BEVs confer protection to the lungs from DE exposure by suppressing neutrophil recruitment and activation and regulating lipid metabolism. This study expanded our understanding on the function of the airway commensal bacteriome and its BEVs within the pulmonary system, offering promising targets for therapeutic intervention in lung injury.
